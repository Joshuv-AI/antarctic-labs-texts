Spectrally Resolved (Colour-Coded) Fundus Autofluorescence Using the EIDON System in Vitreoretinal Lymphoma
Authors: Alessandro Marchese, Riccardo Sacconi, Matteo Menean, Alessandro Bordato, Marco Battista, Giulio Modorati, Francesco Bandello, Giuseppe Querques, Elisabetta Miserocchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study evaluates colour-coded fundus autofluorescence (FAF) in vitreoretinal lymphoma (VRL), which still represents a diagnostic challenge because of the variety of ocular manifestations and difficulties in obtaining a definite diagnosis.MethodsRetrospective cross-sectional analysis of consecutive patients with VRL studied with colour-coded FAF. For comparative analysis, the patients also underwent conventional blue and green fundus FAF. Detection and appearance of typical VRL lesions in macular and extramacular locations on colour-coded FAF were studied and compared with other imaging techniques, including optical coherence tomography.ResultsWe evaluated 20 eyes from 10 patients affected by VRL. The agreement among readers on the presence of VRL lesions using colour-coded FAF was excellent. Lesion-specific comparative analysis showed that retinitis-like lesions were identified in 2/2 eyes using colour-coded FAF versus 0/2 eyes using blue FAF and green FAF, while lymphomatous retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) detachments were detected in 4/4 eyes using colour-coded FAF versus 1/4 eyes using blue FAF and green FAF. The classic granular appearance was similarly detected in 13/13 eyes across all FAF modalities. Comparative analysis revealed that colour-coded FAF appeared to better delineate retinitis-like lesions and lymphomatous RPE detachments than other types of FAF. Conversely, green FAF was more informative within the macula, while colour-coded FAF was also able to detect minor fluorophores and showed RPE atrophic areas, sub-RPE deposits, and perivascular infiltrates.ConclusionColour-coded FAF can recognize the classic features of VRL. As a complementary imaging modality, colour-coded FAF may facilitate the recognition of retinitis-like lesions, lymphomatous retinal pigment epithelium detachments, and minor fluorophores compared with other FAF types.

Introduction

Vitreoretinal lymphoma (VRL) is a rare, severe subtype of intraocular neoplasia associated with the presence of malignant B-cell lymphocytes in most cases [1]. VRL still represents a clinical challenge because of protean ocular manifestations, difficulties in obtaining a definite diagnosis, possible involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) with high morbidity and mortality, and variable response to therapy [2, 3]. The diagnosis of VRL is often delayed as it can masquerade as uveitis, leading to improper treatment with corticosteroids and reduced sensitivity of vitreous cytology. To increase the diagnostic yield for VRL, various molecular tests have been introduced, including the detection of the MYD88 L265P mutation and the measurement of the interleukin (IL) 10/IL6 ratio in ocular specimens [2, 4, 5]. Also, multimodal ocular imaging reveals multiple features suggestive of VRL, guiding more invasive analyses and helping monitor treatment response [6–14]. The main ocular manifestations of VRL are observed in the vitreous, retina, and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Optical coherence tomography, fluorescein angiography, and fundus autofluorescence (FAF) provide details on posterior segment involvement. Specifically, FAF allows a non-invasive analysis of retinal and RPE fluorophores, which are commonly altered in VRL [6, 15]. Patients with VRL often show mottled hypo- and hyper-FAF in the involved areas [6].

Recently, a device to study retinal and RPE fluorophores has become available (EIDON, CenterVue, Padova, Italy). This platform provides a device-specific spectrally resolved fundus autofluorescence (FAF) implementation, also known as colour-coded FAF, in which autofluorescence emission is simultaneously separated into a short-wavelength green-emission fluorescence component (GEFC) and a long-wavelength red-emission fluorescence component (REFC). These two emission spectra are then reconstructed into a single colour-coded image, allowing visualization of both major and minor fluorophores that may not be as evident with conventional FAF modalities. This distinction is important for differentiating colour-coded FAF from standard blue FAF, green FAF, or conventional colour fundus photography [16]. This FAF is elicited by a lower (~ 450 nm) blue wavelength light-emitting diode, and emitted fluorophores are decoded through a colour sensor, providing a broad emission spectrum of “colour-coded” FAF [17, 18].

Promising results have been obtained in different retinal disorders through its application.

In this study, we evaluated the use of colour-coded FAF in VRL, describing the phenotypes and information obtained as compared with other imaging techniques.

Methods

This was a retrospective cross-sectional observational study that included consecutive patients with VRL imaged with colour-coded FAF (EIDON, CenterVue, Padova, Italy) between December 2019 and February 2020. This retrospective study involves human participants and was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, and its later amendments. All subjects provided informed consent to participate in the study and publication of the information. The ethics committee of IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele was notified about this study. According to Italian law, retrospective studies require the ethics committee to be notified, but do not require its approval. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study. The diagnosis of VRL was obtained through cytopathologic analysis of ocular or CNS specimens demonstrating the presence of malignant large B-cell lymphocytes, CD20 positive. Additional molecular analyses revealing MYD88 L265P mutation in the aqueous or vitreous have been obtained in selected cases.

Fundus autofluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal LED fundus imaging system (EIDON, CenterVue, Padua, Italy) with a blue illumination wavelength (range 440–475 nm, peak at 450 nm) and emission detection between 500 and 700 nm.

The examination was performed as previously described [16, 17]. In brief, the device performs autoalignment, autofocus (range − 12 to + 15 diopters), autoexposure, and autocapture of the images. The autofluorescence emission spectrum is simultaneously measured over a short-wavelength range (500–560 nm, green fluorescence component; GEFC) and a long-wavelength range (560–700 nm, red fluorescence component; REFC) using a line-scanning principle. The resultant FAF image can be displayed as a colour-coded image, in which each pixel has a colour dependent on the autofluorescence intensity of the two captured spectra/channels.

Data analysed were the detection and appearance on colour-coded FAF of VRL lesions in macular and extramacular locations. These lesions included mottled areas of RPE atrophy, sub-RPE deposits, lymphomatous RPE detachments, perivascular infiltrates, and retinitis-like lesions. Optic disc involvement by VRL was also evaluated. To confirm the findings observed on colour-coded FAF, we also reviewed the charts and imaging of each patient, which included a complete ophthalmic evaluation with measurement of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), anterior segment evaluation at the slit-lamp, dilated fundus examination, and optical coherence tomography (OCT; Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg, Germany). For qualitative comparative analysis, we reviewed blue FAF (Heidelberg Spectralis HRA, Heidelberg Spectralis Heidelberg, Germany; 488 nm excitation light) and green FAF (Optos, Dunfermline, Scotland, UK; 532 nm excitation light) if available for the same day as colour-coded FAF. Two masked graders (AM and AB) evaluated VRL lesions on colour-coded, blue, and green FAF and the agreement was then calculated. After open adjudication, comparative analysis was based on predefined qualitative assessment of lesion visibility, lesion delineation, and reader agreement across colour-coded, blue, and green FAF. Each lesion was evaluated for whether it was equally appreciated across modalities or for whether one modality appeared to provide additional qualitative information. This study was not designed as a formal diagnostic accuracy analysis, and conclusions were therefore interpreted as descriptive rather than quantitative superiority claims. Inter-rater agreement was calculated using Randolph's free-marginal multi-rater kappa, providing an overall agreement estimate and a 95% confidence interval (CI). Descriptive statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). For all the tests, a p < 0.05 was considered significant, and the data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

This study evaluated 20 eyes from 10 patients with VRL (6 males [60%] and 4 females [40%], mean age = 73 ± 14; range 48–88 years). Among these patients, six (60%) had isolated ocular involvement, while four (40%) had associated CNS lesions at presentation. The mean BCVA was 20/40 Snellen equivalent (0.33 ± 0.40 LogMAR). Colour-coded, blue, and green FAFs were successfully obtained in all but two eyes because of significant cataracts. Blue FAF in one patient and green FAF in another patient were unavailable. The agreement among readers on the presence of VRL lesions using colour-coded FAF was excellent (free-marginal kappa = 0.89 with 95% CI of 0.67, 1.00), with an overall agreement of 94.4%.

In extramacular locations, qualitative comparative analysis revealed that colour-coded FAF appeared to better disclose retinitis-like lesions (2 out of 2 eyes) and lymphomatous RPE detachments (4 out of 4 eyes) than other types of FAF. Specifically, green and blue FAF did not reveal retinitis-like lesions, and only one out of four eyes showed evidence of lymphomatous RPE detachments on both green and blue FAF. On colour-coded AF, retinitis-like lesions appeared as regions with a reduction in both red- and green-emission autofluorescence components. Lymphomatous RPE detachments were identified as bright yellow regions because of a reduced concentration of green-emitting fluorophores (Fig. 1). Moreover, colour-coded FAF identified mottled areas of RPE atrophy, sub-RPE deposits, and perivascular infiltrates (Fig. 2). On colour-coded FAF, RPE atrophic areas appeared as regions with reduced red-emission fluorophores and variable reduction of green-emission FAF components, presumably depending on the extent of photoreceptors loss. Green-emission FAF signals seemed associated with better preserved photoreceptor layers on OCT (Fig. 3), and similar components were not visible on blue and green FAF. Optic disc involvement by VRL was not present in any of the eyes during the analysis, but on colour-coded FAF, it was easier to observe the contour and profile of the optic disc compared with green and blue FAF.

During the analyses, different artefacts were observed on colour-coded FAF, including reduced image quality associated with media opacity and interference by non-ophthalmic fluorophores on the instrument lens due to blinking during the examination (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, we analysed patients with vitreoretinal lymphoma using different fundus autofluorescence types to identify retinal and RPE alterations. We observed that the use of a novel subtype of FAF, providing a colour-coded reconstruction of retinal fluorophores, was effective in detecting the classic features of VRL. VRL is rare and still poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [19]. The average time from the onset of ocular symptoms to the diagnosis can reach 12–24 months [20].

In this setting, multimodal imaging can raise the suspicion of VRL and prompt more invasive investigations to establish the diagnosis [9, 21–24]. Many alterations have been reported in association with VRL on different ocular imaging modalities. For instance, OCT can show vertical hyperreflective lesions in VRL presenting as hyperreflective columns involving the inner and outer retina [13]. These lesions may easily remain undetected without OCT, as they are not clearly visible on fundus photography, infrared pictures, or fundus autofluorescence (unless colocalizing with hyperautofluorescent sub-RPE deposits). Other specific features of VRL include focal sub-RPE deposits and large lymphomatous RPE detachments, which appear as elevated yellowish lesions on fundus examination and show highly reflective homogeneous content on OCT (as illustrated in Fig. 1) [10, 21]. These RPE detachments are thought to contain lymphomatous cells, as resulted from retinal biopsies with aspiration of subretinal fluid and analysis of autopsied eyes with VRL, showing lymphomatous cells between the RPE and Bruch’s membrane [25, 26]. VRL can eventually present with retinitis-like lesions, and OCT can help in the differential diagnosis with infectious retinitis [8].

Fundus FAF can help to recognize other features of VRL. VRL often presents mottled areas of RPE atrophy alternating with sub-RPE hyperautofluorescent deposits, producing a granular appearance on FAF [6]. This presentation typically shows a leopard-spot appearance on fluorescein angiography [6]. Hyperautofluorescent lesions may represent focal areas of RPE dysfunction related to VRL, while hypoautofluorescent lesions are areas of atrophic RPE, often developing after resolution of retinal and sub-RPE infiltrates associated with VRL. Large RPE apertures have also been reported in VRL and are more readily detected on fundus FAF [27].

The potential clinical relevance of this modality lies in improving recognition of highly suggestive lesions, increasing diagnostic suspicion for VRL before invasive procedures such as vitreous biopsy, and supporting the differential diagnosis with inflammatory or infectious retinitis. However, this study did not directly evaluate reduction in diagnostic delay or impact on patient management, and future prospective studies are needed.

Recently, novel subtypes of FAF have been developed and include colour-coded FAF, also known as spectrally resolved fundus autofluorescence, since the emission spectrum is reconstructed based on the emitted wavelength [18]. In this device-specific implementation, autofluorescence emission is separated into green- and red-emission components and reconstructed into a colour image, allowing visualization of minor fluorophores that may not be as evident with conventional FAF platforms. Specifically, its application in VRL seemed to better delineate some specific lesions qualitatively compared with other types of FAF, including retinitis-like lesions and lymphomatous RPE. Both these lesions are considered associated with lymphomatous cells. Interestingly, a previous in vitro study observed that malignant B cells show an intrinsic autofluorescence, which can be useful for their recognition [28]. In this study, colour-coded FAF seemed to provide a better contrast to detect retinitis-like lesions associated with VRL, which appeared as areas with hypo-FAF. We speculate that retinitis-like lesions appear mostly hypoautofluorescent because retinal swelling and thickening may reduce the transmission of excitation light to the RPE and consequently decrease the visibility of lipofuscin-related autofluorescence. Similarly, lymphomatous RPE detachments likely appear hyperautofluorescent because of the accumulation of lymphomatous material between the RPE and Bruch’s membrane, altering the distribution of fluorophores and enhancing signal contrast on colour-coded FAF. Large lymphomatous RPE detachments were also better recognized on colour-coded FAF, although partially visible on other types of FAF, and appeared hyperautofluorescent. Furthermore, colour-coded FAF highlighted the presence of minor green emission components of FAF in affected areas. Preserved photoceptors have been previously related to the emission of minor fluorophores [16]. In case of extensive chorioretinal atrophy, green-emitting fluorophores colocalized to areas of visible sclera.

There are several limitations to the present study and include the small number of subjects, the absence of follow-up analysis, and the absence of histological correlation for the lesions observed. Some of the lesions described in the eyes with VRL in previous studies were not present in our patients, and information on their appearance on colour-coded FAF could not be provided. Furthermore, two eyes could not be analysed because of significant media opacities, and the presence of artefacts may have precluded the analysis of some lesions associated with VRL. Additional limitations include the qualitative nature of intermodality comparisons, potential observer bias despite masked grading, the device-specific nature of the findings related to the EIDON platform, and the absence of direct assessment of diagnostic confidence or management changes. Furthermore, media opacity and blinking-related artefacts with interference from non-ophthalmic fluorophores may reduce image quality and affect interpretation in real-world clinical practice. However, to the best of our knowledge, colour-coded FAF has not yet been applied to VRL, and this study provided the base for its use in this disease.

Conclusion

Colour-coded FAF can be used to recognize the typical features of VRL and help to identify highly specific lesions for VRL. Also, colour-coded FAF provided additional details to identify retinitis-like lesions, lymphomatous RPE detachments, and minor fluorophores in VRL compared with other types of FAF. Further studies are necessary to confirm these findings on a large scale, determine their response to therapy, and change over the course of the disease.
