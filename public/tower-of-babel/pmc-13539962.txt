Mutual antagonism between auxin and diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 regulates high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation
Authors: Mohinur Khamzaaliyeva, Punita Lalchand, Didier‐Deschamps Ashley, Xue Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Cell elongation is a fundamental process in plant growth and development, and it is significantly affected by temperature changes. While the signaling mechanisms driving high‐temperature‐induced cell elongation have been extensively studied, the strategies plants employ to counteract excessive temperature‐induced elongation remain poorly understood.Using Arabidopsis thaliana hypocotyls as a model, we show that high temperature induces the triacylglycerol biosynthetic enzyme DGAT2, which acts as a growth‐restraining regulator that counterbalances auxin‐driven elongation. Auxin, in turn, downregulates DGAT2 expression, establishing a feedback loop that limits excessive hypocotyl elongation under high temperature.Thermo‐ and auxin‐responsive regulation of DGAT2, and its role in modulating membrane lipid order, are conserved between Arabidopsis and Camelina, indicating a shared DGAT2–auxin regulatory module.Our study reveals how lipid metabolism, auxin homeostasis, and membrane dynamics intersect to shape hypocotyl adaptive responses, thereby optimizing growth under high‐temperature conditions. Keywords: auxin, DGAT2, high temperature, hypocotyl elongation, membrane dynamics

Introduction

Understanding how cells adapt to temperature changes is a fundamental aspect of biological research (Sengupta & Garrity, 2013; de Mendoza, 2014). One of the most studied and critical aspects of temperature adaptation is the alteration of membrane fluidity in response to temperature changes (D. K. Ma et al., 2015). To maintain optimal fluidity, cells adjust their membrane lipid composition, primarily by modifying the levels of unsaturated fatty acids (de Mendoza, 2014; Holthuis & Menon, 2014). Increasing membrane unsaturation can compensate for the reduced fluidity caused by low temperatures, whereas decreasing membrane unsaturation can counteract the increased fluidity caused by high temperatures. This process, known as homeoviscous adaptation (HVA), has been reported across the tree of life (Sinensky, 1974; Cossins & Prosser, 1978; de Mendoza, 2014; Ernst et al., 2016; Hayes et al., 2021). However, the precise molecular mechanisms regulating lipid composition in response to elevated temperatures remain poorly understood in eukaryotes.

In plants, the saturation levels of membrane lipids are dynamically regulated through interconnected mechanisms (Yu et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2024). Fatty acid desaturases play a central role in this process by directly introducing double bonds into fatty acid chains, leading to increased membrane unsaturation (Browse et al., 1993; Shanklin & Cahoon, 1998). Since phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the primary site of fatty acid desaturation, PC acyl remodeling is another critical mechanism. This process involves the selective removal of fatty acids from membrane lipids and their replacement with others, allowing rapid adjustments in saturation levels (Lands, 1960; Stymne & Stobart, 1984; Pan et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2021). Additionally, membrane lipid and triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis share several precursors, such as fatty acyl‐CoAs and diacylglycerols (DAGs). Specific TAG synthesis reactions, catalyzed by enzymes such as phospholipid:diacylglycerol acyltransferase1 (PDAT1) (Stahl et al., 2004; Pan et al., 2013) and diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2) (Zhou et al., 2013), selectively incorporate unsaturated fatty acids into TAGs, potentially reducing the proportion of these fatty acids in membrane lipids. Although all five Arabidopsis TAG acyltransferases, DGAT1/2/3 and PDAT1/2, have been implicated in temperature stress responses (Shomo et al., 2024), their regulation and specific roles in cellular adaptation to temperature changes remain largely unexplored.

Plants are highly responsive to temperature changes. A range of morphological and architectural changes in response to moderately elevated ambient temperatures, including hypocotyl (the embryonic stem) and stem elongation, primary root growth, leaf hyponasty, and early flowering, are collectively known as thermomorphogenesis (Casal & Balasubramanian, 2019). Temperature‐induced elongation of Arabidopsis hypocotyls is a strong and easily scorable phenotype for studying signaling pathways for thermomorphogenesis in plants. Research has identified several mechanisms by which plants regulate cell elongation under high‐temperature conditions (Casal & Balasubramanian, 2019). A core mechanism involves the plant hormone auxin, which drives apoplastic acidification, leading to cell wall loosening and subsequent elongation in a dose‐dependent manner (Cosgrove, 2005). Elevated temperatures have been shown to increase intracellular auxin levels by influencing its biosynthesis, transport, and signaling pathways (Gray et al., 1998; Franklin et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2012; Hanzawa et al., 2013). While temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation may help seedlings adapt to warmer conditions by distancing sensitive apical tissues from heat‐absorbing soil and enhancing air movement (Gray et al., 1998), excessive elongation produces fragile tissues prone to lodging and yield loss, necessitating tight control of thermomorphogenic growth. Here, we show that DGAT2 antagonizes auxin effects, potentially by modulating plasma membrane (PM) lipid order, while auxin suppresses DGAT2 expression, forming a regulatory loop that limits excessive hypocotyl elongation under high temperature. As climate change accelerates, insights into these regulatory mechanisms may inform future efforts to develop climate‐resilient crops.

Materials and Methods

Growth conditions and plant materials

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) was used as the wild‐type (WT) reference background. The following mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC): dgat1‐1(AS11), pdat1‐2 (SALK_065334C), pdat2‐like (SALK_010854C), pin3‐4 (SALK_038609C), pin4‐3 (CS9368), and pin7 (SALK_048791C). Camelina sativa var. DH55 seeds were obtained from the Canadian National Plant Genetic Resources System. Additional seeds were kindly provided by Dr Rebecca Roston (dgat2‐salk; SALK_067809C), Dr Jiří Friml (pin3‐3 pin4‐101 pin7‐102), and Dr Minoru Nagano (SP‐sfGFP‐D4L). The dgat2‐c CRISPR mutant was generated in the Col‐0 background (see subsequently). Overexpression (p35S::GFP‐DGAT2) and complementation (pDGAT2::GFP‐DGAT2) lines were generated by transforming Col‐0 and dgat2‐salk, respectively. The auxin reporters R2D2 (pGreenIIM RPS5A‐mDII‐ntdTomato/RPS5A‐DII‐n3Venus; Addgene #61629) and DR5 (pGreenIIM DR5v2‐ntdTomato/DR5‐n3GFP; Addgene #61628) were transformed into Col‐0 to generate homozygous lines. The R2D2 x dgat2‐salk, pDR5v2∷ntdTomato‐pDR5∷n3EGFP x dgat2‐salk, SP‐sfGFP‐D4L x dgat2‐salk, and pPIN3::PIN3‐GFP x dgat2‐c were generated by genetic crossing. PIN3‐GFP experiments were performed using the pPIN3::PIN3‐GFP x dgat2‐c line because repeated attempts to generate a pPIN3::PIN3‐GFP x dgat2‐salk line were unsuccessful due to the inability to recover kanamycin‐resistant F2 progeny expressing PIN3‐GFP. Plants were maintained in a growth chamber under a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle at 22°C : 18°C, day : night, with a light intensity of c. 110 μmol m−2 s−1 photosynthetically active radiation.

Vector construction

To generate the pDGAT2::GFP‐DGAT2 construct for complementation tests, the 896‐bp promoter fragment upstream of the DGAT2 start codon was amplified from Col‐0 genomic DNA and used to replace the 35S promoter in pGWB506. The DGAT2 coding sequence (CDS) was subsequently cloned into this vector via Gateway LR recombination. For the overexpression construct (p35S::GFP‐DGAT2), the DGAT2 CDS was amplified from Col‐0 cDNA, cloned into pDONR207, and subsequently recombined into pGWB506.

The promoter‐reporter construct (pDGAT2::NLS‐3xmVenus) was generated by assembling the 896‐bp DGAT2 promoter into XbaI/HindIII‐digested pGWB501 using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly. The NLS‐3xmVenus sequence, amplified from the R2D2 plasmid, was introduced via Gateway BP and LR reactions into the pGWB501/pDGAT2 intermediate.

For CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome editing, two target sites were selected using CRISPR‐P (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR/) and introduced into sgRNA expression cassettes driven by the AtU3d and AtU3b promoters. These cassettes were then inserted into a modified pYLCRISPR/Cas9 vector containing the egg‐cell‐specific EC1.2p promoter driving Cas9 (Wang et al., 2015; X. Ma et al., 2015).

All constructs were introduced into A. thaliana via Agrobacterium‐mediated floral dipping. All primers used in this study are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Auxin‐induced hypocotyl elongation assays

Surface‐sterilized seeds were sown on solid ½MS medium, stratified at 4°C for 3 d, and grown vertically for 4 d (16 h : 8 h, light : dark photoperiod, 22°C, 70 μmol m−2 s−1). Seedlings were then transferred to freshly prepared solid ½MS medium supplemented with varying concentrations of indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) or an equimolar ethanol mock treatment. IAA stock solutions were prepared in ethanol and diluted in water before use. Following 48 h of incubation under identical growth conditions, hypocotyl lengths were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ).

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 7‐d‐old Arabidopsis shoots or 5‐d‐old Camelina hypocotyls using the HiPure Plant RNA Plus Kit (GeneBio Systems, Burlington, ON, Canada). First‐strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the GB‐Script III cDNA Synthesis Kit (GeneBio Systems).

Reverse transcription PCR (RT‐PCR) was conducted on a SimpliAmp™ Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green chemistry. For experimental treatments, 7‐d‐old Arabidopsis seedlings were subjected to high‐temperature treatments (28°C) for either 4 h (following 7 d at 22°C) or 3 d (following 4 d at 22°C). Characterization of DGAT2 expression in hypocotyl epidermal cells

To analyze DGAT2 expression in hypocotyl epidermal cells, we generated a promoter‐reporter line expressing a triple mVenus fluorochrome fused to a nuclear localization signal under the control of the DGAT2 promoter (pDGAT2::NLS‐3x mVenus). This construct was stably transformed into A. thaliana Col‐0 plants, and homozygous T3 seedlings were used for all experiments. Seven‐day‐old seedlings were grown under control conditions (22°C) and subsequently exposed to either elevated temperature (28°C) or 5 μM IAA for 4 h; control groups received a 22°C incubation or mock treatment, respectively. Confocal imaging was performed using a Leica STELLARIS 5 microscope (×25 objective) focusing on the upper hypocotyl, spanning c. 350 μm basipetally from the cotyledon–hypocotyl junction. mVenus was excited at 488 nm with emission collected at 490–530 nm, employing Tau‐gating to minimize Chl autofluorescence. Maximum intensity projections were generated from z‐stacks consisting of 10 optical sections acquired at 1 μm intervals using Fiji (ImageJ). Background signal was corrected by subtracting the mean gray value of a representative background region (free of plant tissue) from the entire frame. Nuclei were segmented via Otsu local auto‐thresholding using the ‘Particle Analysis’ plugin in ImageJ. For quantification, at least 15 hypocotyls per condition were analyzed in each of three independent biological replicates, representing a minimum of 304 nuclei per replicate. For each hypocotyl, the average nuclear fluorescence intensity was calculated per nucleus, and these values were averaged to yield the mean fluorescence intensity for each hypocotyl. In graphs presenting mean fluorescence intensities, ‘n’ denotes the number of hypocotyls analyzed.

Auxin reporter line imaging and quantification

The R2D2 and DR5v2 reporters were imaged using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser scanning microscope with a ×25 objective. GFP and Venus were excited at 488 nm and detected at 490–530 nm, while tdTomato was excited at 561 nm and detected at 571–630 nm. Fluorescence was collected from the upper hypocotyl region (c. 350 μm below the cotyledon–hypocotyl junction). Image processing was performed as described above for promoter‐reporter analysis: z‐stack projections were generated in Fiji, followed by background subtraction and Otsu‐based nuclear segmentation.

For DR5v2, mean ntTomato fluorescence intensity was calculated by averaging nuclear intensities across all nuclei in each hypocotyl. For R2D2, ratio images were generated by dividing the mDII‐ntdTomato signal intensity by the DII‐nVenus signal intensity using the Image Calculator function in ImageJ. The ntdTomato channel was used as a master mask to segment nuclei, and the mean gray value was extracted for each nucleus from the ratio image. Ratios were then averaged across all nuclei per hypocotyl to obtain a single value per seedling. These seedling‐level values were then used for statistical comparison between genotypes (Col‐0 vs dgat2) and temperature treatments.

Hypocotyl cell length measurement

Seedlings were incubated in 20 μg ml−1 propidium iodide (PI) for 20 min to stain cell walls and imaged on a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope with a ×20 objective. Z‐stacks were acquired at 1.5 μm intervals. PI was excited at 536 nm, and emission was collected between 585–620 nm. Images were taken from the middle region of the hypocotyls. For seedlings grown at 22°C, a single confocal field (c. 582 μm in length) was imaged. For high‐temperature‐treated seedlings, two adjacent fields were acquired and stitched using the tile scan function to generate a composite image (c. 1106 μm in length). For each genotype, ≥ 10 hypocotyls were analyzed in each of two independent experiments. Cell lengths were measured in ImageJ, and mean cell length per hypocotyl was calculated by averaging all cells within the imaged region.

Transient expression and colocalization imaging in Nicotiana benthamiana

Transient co‐expression of pDGAT2::GFP‐DGAT2 and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker p35S::HDEL‐mCherry was performed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated infiltration, as previously described (Li, 2011). Imaging was performed using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope in sequential scanning mode to minimize spectral crosstalk. GFP was excited at 488 nm with emission collected at 490–530 nm, and mCherry was excited at 543 nm with emission collected at 620–630 nm.

Protoplast isolation, transformation and imaging

To examine the role of DGAT2 in membrane lipid order, mesophyll protoplasts were isolated from 3‐wk‐old A. thaliana leaves using previously described methods (Yoo et al., 2007). Briefly, protoplasts (c. 2 × 105 per transfection) were transformed with 10 μg of p35S::AtDGAT2‐CFP, p35S::CsDGAT2‐CFP, or p35S::HDEL‐CFP (control) using polyethylene glycol (PEG)‐mediated transfection and incubated for 15 h at room temperature.

For lipid order analysis, transformed protoplasts were stained with 5 μM di‐4‐ANEPPDHQ (Cat # D36802; Invitrogen) in ½MS for 5 min at room temperature, and imaged on a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope with a ×25 objective. Di‐4‐ANEPPDHQ was excited at 488 nm, and emission was collected in two channels: 545–565 nm (ordered phase) and 635–655 nm (disordered phase). CFP was excited at 405 nm, with emission collected at 460–500 nm. Generalized polarization (GP) values were calculated from emission ratios as described previously (Owen et al., 2012).

Total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy imaging

For sfGFP‐D4L and PIN3‐GFP puncta imaging, hypocotyls of 8‐d‐old seedlings were mounted in ½MS medium and imaged using a Quorum Technologies TIRF microscope with a ×100 Apo oil‐immersion objective (NA 1.47; pixel size 0.1018 μm). PIN3‐GFP time‐lapse movies were acquired over 100 frames (c. 25 s) using a 256 × 256‐pixel region of interest, with a 40 ms exposure time and a 250 ms frame interval. Photobleaching was negligible over the duration of imaging. GFP was excited with a 488 nm solid‐state laser diode, and fluorescence emission was collected through a 525/50 nm band‐pass filter using an EMCCD camera.

sfGFP‐D4L puncta density quantification

TIRF images were processed using the TrackMate plugin (v.7.13.2) in Fiji/ImageJ via a custom Python script. sfGFP‐D4L puncta were identified using the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) detector with an estimated diameter of 0.5 μm and a median filter for noise reduction. To ensure consistency across datasets, a fixed Quality Threshold of 250 was applied to all datasets. Total puncta counts were extracted per field of view and normalized to the total area to calculate puncta density (puncta μm−2).

Puncta detection and full width at half maximum (FWHM) quantitation

TIRF images were analyzed using custom MATLAB script. Puncta were detected with a LoG filter, optimized for identifying blob‐like structures. The LoG kernel was defined with a characteristic scale (σ) related to the estimated puncta diameter (d) by σ = d/2.8. Local maxima were identified in the LoG‐transformed image to determine puncta centroids. To distinguish molecular signals from background noise, a dynamic quality threshold was applied, defined as T=medianP+k·MADP, where P represents the intensities of all detected peaks, MAD is the median absolute deviation, and k is a sensitivity factor (set to 1.5).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Shoots from 7‐d‐old Arabidopsis seedlings grown at 22°C or treated at 28°C (4 h or 3 d) were harvested, frozen, and ground in liquid nitrogen. Proteins were extracted in lysis buffer and quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. GFP‐DGAT2 fusion proteins were detected using a rat monoclonal anti‐GFP antibody (ChromoTek, Planegg‐Martinsried, Germany) followed by an HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rat IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). Ponceau S staining of the RUBISCO large subunit served as a loading control. Three independent experiments were performed.

In silico promoter analysis

Putative Auxin Response Factor (ARF) binding sites were identified within the promoter regions of DGAT2 homologs using the binding site prediction tool available in PlantRegMap (Tian et al., 2020). Promoter sequences were defined as the 3‐kb region upstream of the transcription start site (TSS). When the upstream intergenic region was shorter than 3 kb, the entire intergenic region was used.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed in R (v.4.3.3) or Graphpad Prism 10. Normality was assessed using D'Agostino–Pearson, Shapiro–Wilk, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests; datasets were considered normal if at least one test was passed (P = 0.05). For two‐group comparisons, t‐tests (paired or unpaired with Welch's correction) were used. For multiple groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post hoc tests was applied. The number of biological replicates and the statistical tests used are detailed in the figure legends.

Results

DGAT2 is a negative regulator of high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation

To investigate the role of lipid metabolism, particularly TAG biosynthesis, in high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation, we examined the hypocotyl elongation phenotypes of previously characterized loss‐of‐function mutants in key acyltransferases involved in the final step of TAG synthesis, including dgat1‐1 (AS11 mutant with 147 bp repeat insertion), dgat2‐salk (SALK_067809C), pdat1‐2 (SALK_065334C), pdat2‐like (SALK_010854C) (Fig. 1a–d). These mutants have been shown to affect TAG accumulation and reduce tolerance to freezing or heat stress (Shomo et al., 2024). Under our control conditions (22°C), hypocotyl length in the WT did not differ significantly from any mutant, whereas significant differences were observed among the mutant lines (Fig. 1b,c). To account for genotype‐specific differences in baseline hypocotyl length, we calculated normalized hypocotyl elongation ratios as (individual hypocotyl length at 28°C – mean hypocotyl length at 22°C)/mean hypocotyl length at 22°C. This normalization allowed comparison of thermomorphogenic responses among genotypes by expressing hypocotyl elongation relative to each genotype's corresponding 22°C control. As shown in Fig. 1(d), WT seedlings have a normalized hypocotyl elongation ratio of c. 1, indicating a twofold increase in hypocotyl length at 28°C relative to the 22°C baseline. The dgat2‐salk and pdat1‐2 mutants exhibited a significantly greater hypocotyl elongation ratio at 28°C than the WT, whereas the dgat1‐1 and pdat2‐like mutants showed elongation comparable to the WT. These findings suggest that DGAT2 and PDAT1 may act as negative regulators of high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation. To further prioritize candidates, we examined the tissue‐specific expression patterns of DGAT1, DGAT2, PDAT1, and PDAT2 using the eFP Browser from the Bio‐Analytic Resource for Plant Biology (BAR) (https://bar.utoronto.ca/) (Winter et al., 2007). Among these genes, DGAT2 showed the highest expression in the hypocotyl and was preferentially expressed in the hypocotyl compared to roots and cotyledons (Fig. S1). Together with the mutant phenotype, this expression pattern led us to focus our subsequent analyses on dissecting the mechanistic role of DGAT2 in regulating high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation.

To confirm that the observed hypocotyl phenotype in the dgat2‐salk mutant results from the loss of DGAT2 function, we generated complementation lines by introducing a pDGAT2::GFP‐DGAT2 construct into the dgat2‐salk mutant background. The N‐terminal GFP fusion did not interfere with DGAT2 localization, as GFP‐DGAT2 colocalized with the ER retention marker HDEL‐mCherry when transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf epidermal cells (Fig. S2). Expression of the transgene in the dgat2‐salk mutant rescued the hypocotyl phenotype (Fig. 1e,f), confirming that the phenotype observed in dgat2‐salk is attributable to the loss of DGAT2 function. To further validate the role of DGAT2, we generated an independent CRISPR‐Cas9–edited dgat2 line (designated dgat2‐c). This line carries a 2‐base‐pair deletion located 17 nucleotides downstream of the start codon (Fig. 1g,h), introducing multiple premature stop codons (Fig. S3). Similar to dgat2‐salk, the dgat2‐c mutant grows normally like Col‐0 under control conditions but shows significantly enhanced hypocotyl elongation at 28°C compared to Col‐0 (Fig. 1i,j). However, the hypocotyl phenotype in dgat2‐c was weaker than in dgat2‐salk. This difference suggests that dgat2‐c is likely a hypomorphic (partial loss‐of‐function) allele with residual DGAT2 activity, possibly due to low levels of a truncated transcript. Supporting this, RT‐PCR showed no detectable DGAT2 transcripts in dgat2‐salk, whereas dgat2‐c retained reduced DGAT2 transcript levels relative to the WT (Fig. 1i). In contrast to dgat2 mutants, overexpression of DGAT2 in the Col‐0 background suppresses high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 1i,j). Together, these findings demonstrate that DGAT2 acts as a negative regulator of hypocotyl elongation in response to elevated temperature.

High temperature stimulates DGAT2 gene expression and protein levels

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of DGAT2 in high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation, we first examined whether elevated temperature affects DGAT2 expression. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) revealed that both short‐term (4‐h) and long‐term (3‐d) exposure to high temperature (28°C) significantly upregulated DGAT2 expression compared to the control condition (22°C) in Arabidopsis seedling shoots (Fig. 2a). A similar temperature‐responsive expression pattern was observed for DGAT1, but not for PDAT1. No detectable PDAT2 amplification was observed by RT‐qPCR, suggesting that PDAT2 is either not expressed or expressed at very low levels during the seedling stage. This result is consistent with publicly available RNA‐seq expression data (Fig. S1). To more precisely assess DGAT2 expression in hypocotyl cells, we generated a promoter‐reporter line (pDGAT2::NLS‐3x mVenus) in which a triple mVenus fluorochrome fused to a nuclear localization signal is expressed under the control of the DGAT2 promoter. Consistent with transcriptomic data from the Plant eFP Viewer, mVenus fluorescence was detected throughout the seedling (Fig. S4). Notably, strong expressions were observed in the epidermis of the cotyledon and hypocotyl, whereas in the root, the signal was predominantly restricted to the vascular tissues. To evaluate the responsiveness of the DGAT2 promoter to high temperature, seedlings were subjected to the 28°C treatment for 4 h. This resulted in a marked increase in mVenus fluorescence in the hypocotyl epidermal cells (Fig. 2b), supporting the conclusion that elevated temperature enhances DGAT2 promoter activity. We next evaluated if high temperature affects DGAT2 protein accumulation in seedling shoots using western blotting. As shown in Fig. 2(c), both 4‐h and 3‐d high temperature treatments increased DGAT2 protein levels, with a more pronounced effect at the long‐term 3‐d treatment. Together, these results demonstrate that high temperature stimulates both DGAT2 transcription and protein accumulation.

Since PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) is a central thermal regulator mediating high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation (Quint et al., 2016), we next investigated whether the high‐temperature‐induced expression of DGAT2 depends on PIF4. To this end, we used the previously reported pif4‐2 mutant (Koini et al., 2009), which, consistent with earlier studies, failed to exhibit high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 2d). Comparing DGAT2 expression between Col‐0 and pif4‐2 revealed that high‐temperature‐induced upregulation of DGAT2 was significantly greater in pif4‐2 mutants after a 4‐h shift to 28°C (Fig. 2e). These findings suggest that PIF4 negatively regulates high‐temperature‐induced DGAT2 expression. Given the absence of predicted PIF4 binding sites within the DGAT2 promoter, this negative regulation is likely indirect.

Loss of DGAT2 activity enhances high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl cell elongation and auxin responses

High‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation is primarily driven by cell elongation (Gray et al., 1998; Bellstaedt et al., 2019). To determine whether the elongated hypocotyl phenotype in the dgat2 mutant is due to enhanced cell elongation, we imaged and measured epidermal cell length in the middle region of the hypocotyls. As shown in Fig. 3(a), WT and dgat2‐salk mutants displayed similar cell lengths at 22°C, with mean values of 92.8 and 94.8 μm, respectively. At elevated temperature (28°C), cell length increased in both genotypes, but the increase was significantly greater in dgat2‐salk mutants (mean values of 179.5 μm in WT and 254.2 μm in dgat2‐salk). These results suggest that DGAT2 acts as a negative regulator of high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl cell elongation.

Given that auxin is a key hormone driving high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl cell elongation (Gray et al., 1998), we asked whether the increased cell length observed in the dgat2 mutant is associated with altered auxin levels or signaling. To test this, we utilized the ratiometric reporter R2D2 to monitor auxin input by comparing an auxin‐degradable reporter (DII‐n3Venus) with an auxin‐insensitive reference (mDII‐ntdTomato) (Liao et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 3(b), shifting to 28°C for 6 h increases the mDII : DII ratio in both Col‐0 and dgat2. The highest ratio was observed in dgat2 at 28°C, suggesting that the loss of DGAT2 activity exacerbates temperature‐induced auxin accumulation.

To determine if these elevated auxin input translated into increased signaling output, we employed the auxin‐responsive double reporter line pDR5v2::ntdTomato‐pDR5::n3EGFP, which expresses nuclear‐localized tdTomato and EGFP driven by synthetic auxin‐inducible DR5v2 and DR5 promoters, respectively (Liao et al., 2015). Because the modified DR5v2 reporter is more sensitive than DR5, the DR5‐driven EGFP signal was weak in our experiments; therefore, we focused on nuclear tdTomato quantification. While fluorescence intensity was comparable between genotypes at 22°C, it was significantly higher in dgat2 than in Col‐0 at 28°C (Fig. 3c), indicating an enhanced transcriptional response in the dgat2 mutant under high temperature.

Collectively, these results indicate that DGAT2 negatively regulates high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl cell elongation, likely through the attenuation of auxin accumulation and subsequent transcriptional responses.

DGAT2 contributes to a more ordered PM environment

Since DGAT2 preferentially incorporates unsaturated fatty acids into TAGs (Zhou et al., 2013), its activity likely sequesters these acyl chains from membrane lipid synthesis, thereby increasing membrane lipid saturation and order. To test this, we used the membrane environment‐sensitive dye di‐4‐ANEPPDHQ, which shifts its emission spectrum based on lipid packing (Owen et al., 2012). This shift was quantified by a GP value, where higher values indicate increased membrane order. We initially stained hypocotyls with this dye. However, di‐4‐ANEPPDHQ labeling was patchy despite sectioning and vacuum infiltration, likely due to the waxy cuticle limiting dye penetration. To overcome this limitation, we performed staining in isolated leaf protoplasts. As shown in Fig. 4(a,b), transient overexpression of CFP‐DGAT2 significantly increased PM GP values compared to the HDEL‐CFP (an ER‐localized marker) control, suggesting that DGAT2 activity promotes membrane ordering.

To validate these findings in hypocotyls, we used TIRF microscopy to analyze the nano‐organization of the sterol biosensor SP‐sfGFP‐D4L (Ukawa et al., 2022). Since sterols preferentially associate with saturated lipids and are enriched in liquid‐ordered domains (Bacia et al., 2005; Martinez‐Seara et al., 2010), we reasoned that this biosensor could serve as an indirect proxy for PM lipid order in living cells. As shown in Fig. 4(c), SP‐sfGFP‐D4L forms discrete PM nanodomains with substantial heterogeneity in fluorescence intensity and size. To quantitatively characterize these structures, we analyzed two parameters: puncta density and domain size. For puncta density, we applied a stringent Quality Threshold (250) in ImageJ TrackMate to selectively identify high‐intensity puncta, which likely correspond to the most highly ordered, sterol‐enriched nanodomains. To assess domain size, we quantified the FWHM using a lower Quality Threshold (1.5), allowing inclusion of all statistically significant puncta while maintaining rigorous filtering against background noise. The FWHM, reflecting the lateral width of the fluorescence intensity profile at 50% of its peak height, provides a robust readout of the spatial extent of these nanodomains (McKenna et al., 2019). Different thresholds were used because puncta density and domain size quantify distinct features of membrane organization: puncta density measures the abundance of the brightest nanodomains, whereas FWHM measures the size distribution across the broader nanodomain population.

As shown in Figs 4(c,d) and S5, in Col‐0 at 22°C, the mean density of high‐intensity sfGFP‐D4L puncta was c. 1 punctum μm−2. Shifting to 28°C resulted in a significant decrease in puncta density and a slight increase in median FWHM, consistent with thermally induced membrane disordering and the resulting broadening of sterol‐enriched nanodomains. Notably, dgat2 mutants exhibited a significantly lower density of high‐intensity puncta even at 22°C. While density remained stable at 28°C in the mutant, FWHM increased significantly, reaching the highest values observed. Given that lipid unsaturation has been suggested to regulate domain size through its effect on line tension at domain boundaries (Heberle et al., 2013; Wongsirojkul et al., 2022), the increased domain size and reduced density of compact sfGFP‐D4L puncta in dgat2 are consistent with a less ordered PM state, where lower line tension permits the lateral expansion of ordered nanodomains.

Together, these GP measurements and sterol‐containing nanodomain analyses support a role for DGAT2 in promoting PM lipid order.

DGAT2 constrains PIN3 nanodomain size at the PM

Our findings suggest that DGAT2 promotes PM lipid ordering and suppresses high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation, at least in part, by attenuating auxin accumulation and signaling. To investigate how increased membrane order influences auxin levels in epidermal cells under elevated temperatures, we focused on the behavior of PIN3, a key PM‐localized auxin efflux carrier. PIN3 has been shown to form PM nanodomains (McKenna et al., 2019), making it a relevant candidate for examining how DGAT2‐mediated changes in membrane order affect auxin transport. Consistent with a role of PIN proteins in thermomorphogenesis, the pin3 pin4 pin7 triple mutant has been reported to display markedly reduced hypocotyl elongation in response to high temperature (Bellstaedt et al., 2019). We therefore examined pin3‐4, pin4‐3, and pin7‐1 single mutants. As shown in Fig. S6, pin3 and pin4, but not pin7, exhibited reduced high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation. The pin3 pin4 pin7 triple mutant had shorter hypocotyls at 22°C and a more severe reduction in thermal response than either single mutant, indicating that these PIN proteins function partially redundantly in mediating high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation.

Given that membrane ordering influences protein lateral organization at the PM (Levental & Lyman, 2023), we investigated whether DGAT2 modulates the nanoscale organization and dynamics of PIN3 using TIRF imaging and single‐particle tracking in 8‐d‐old hypocotyls. While PIN3–GFP diffusion coefficients and bimodal population dynamics were unaffected by temperature or the loss of DGAT2 (Figs 5a–d, S7, Video S1), we observed significant shifts in nanodomain spatial organization (Figs 5e–g, S8). Notably, our measured PIN3–GFP diffusion coefficients were lower (D = 0.004 ± 0.0005 μm2 s−1 vs D = 0.012 ± 0.001 μm2 s−1), while FWHM measurements were higher (396 ± 27 nm vs 166.7 ± 31.1 nm), than those previously reported for 5‐d‐old seedlings (McKenna et al., 2019); these differences likely arise from variations in seedling age, growth conditions, and image processing parameters (Fig. S9). Despite these baseline differences, shifting seedlings to 28°C for 24 h increased PIN3 nanodomain FWHM in both genotypes, an effect significantly more pronounced in dgat2‐c (Figs 5e, S8). Gaussian Mixture Modeling revealed that high temperature increases the fraction of ‘large’ PIN3 nanodomains (FWHM ≥ 480 nm), with dgat2‐c reaching the highest proportion (Figs 5f,g, S10). These data suggest that DGAT2 constrains high‐temperature‐induced lateral expansion of PIN3 nanodomains, potentially influencing PIN3‐mediated auxin export and contributing to the regulation of hypocotyl elongation under high temperatures.

Auxin feedback regulates DGAT2 expression

Since auxin commonly regulates its own homeostasis through feedback mechanisms (Ruiz Rosquete et al., 2012), we next investigated whether auxin could in turn regulate DGAT2 expression. Given the biphasic effect of auxin on hypocotyl elongation, promoting elongation at low to moderate concentrations and inhibiting it at higher, supra‐optimal levels (Collett et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2025), we first tested a range of concentrations of indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA; 100 nM to 50 μM) to identify the dose that most effectively promoted elongation. As shown in Fig. S11, maximal hypocotyl elongation was observed between 2.5 μM and 10 μM IAA. Based on this dose–response analysis, we selected 5 μM IAA for subsequent experiments.

To test the effect of auxin on DGAT2 expression, 7‐d‐old Col‐0 seedlings were treated with 5 μM IAA, 5 μM benzoic acid (weak acid control), or a mock control (0.005% ethanol) for 4 h. After treatment, roots were excised and hypocotyls along with cotyledons were harvested for RT‐qPCR analysis. As shown in Fig. 6(a), IAA treatment reduced DGAT2 transcript levels by c. 32% compared with the mock control, whereas benzoic acid did not differ from the mock control. By contrast, DGAT1 transcript levels were not significantly affected by IAA. Consistently, the DGAT2 promoter‐reporter line (pDGAT2::NLS‐3 x mVenus) exhibited a significant decrease in mVenus fluorescence in hypocotyl epidermal cells after 4 h of IAA treatment (Fig. 6b), further supporting that auxin negatively regulates DGAT2 expression in the hypocotyl. These results are aligned with in silico promoter analysis showing that DGAT2, but not DGAT1, contains multiple predicted Auxin Response Elements (AuxREs) in its promoter, which are putative binding sites for ARFs, the key transcriptional regulators of auxin signaling (Fig. 6c). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that auxin negatively regulates DGAT2 expression and suggest that the predicted AuxREs may contribute to this transcriptional response. However, whether ARFs directly bind to the DGAT2 promoter and regulate its transcription remains to be experimentally validated.

DGAT2–auxin crosstalk for thermal adaptation is shared between Arabidopsis and Camelina

To assess whether the thermoresponsive regulatory mechanism identified in A. thaliana extends to a crop species, we examined its presence and functional relevance in Camelina sativa var. DH55, an oilseed crop in the Brassicaceae family. C. sativa shares key molecular components with A. thaliana, including homologs of PIF4, ARFs, PIN3, and DGAT2, which play critical roles in high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation. Similar to Arabidopsis, high temperature significantly promoted hypocotyl elongation in Camelina seedlings (Fig. 7a). We then examined whether the expression of CsDGAT2 in hypocotyls is regulated by high temperature and auxin. Notably, due to a whole‐genome triplication in the Camelina lineage, each Arabidopsis gene often corresponds to three homologous copies in the Camelina genome. This triplication was observed for the CsDGAT2 genes, which were designated as CsDGAT2A (Csa04g037310), CsDGAT2B (Csa06g025650), and CsDGAT2C (Csa09g058550) (Lee et al., 2024). Previous attempts (Lee et al., 2024) to use homoeolog‐specific primers for RT‐qPCR were unsuccessful due to the high sequence similarity among CsDGAT2 homologs (over 97.8% identity in the CDS and 90.8% in the 3′UTR regions between CsDGAT2B and CsDGAT2C). Therefore, we designed a single set of primers to quantify the combined transcript levels of all three CsDGAT2 genes. As shown in Fig. 7(b,c), RT‐qPCR showed that expression of CsDGAT2s was strongly induced after short‐term exposure to high temperature (28°C for 4 h) but significantly suppressed by auxin treatment (5 μM IAA for 4 h) in hypocotyls, paralleling the regulatory pattern observed for AtDGAT2. Consistent with this auxin‐dependent regulation, putative ARF‐binding sites were identified in the promoters of all three CsDGAT2 genes (Figs 7d, S12).

Given that Camelina DGAT2, like its Arabidopsis counterpart, preferentially incorporates unsaturated fatty acids into TAGs (Lager et al., 2020), we asked whether CsDGAT2 similarly affects membrane lipid order. To address this, we transiently overexpressed CsDGAT2C in Arabidopsis leaf protoplasts and observed a marked increase in membrane lipid order (Fig. 7e,f), mirroring the effects of AtDGAT2 expression. The conserved thermally induced and auxin‐repressed expression of DGAT2, together with its enzymatic activity that modulates membrane properties, supports the conclusion that crosstalk between DGAT2‐mediated lipid metabolism and auxin signaling is shared between Arabidopsis and Camelina.

To evaluate the broader phylogenetic scope of this mechanism, we scanned DGAT2 promoter regions across 23 angiosperm species spanning monocots and eudicots, together with two representative bryophytes. Strikingly, putative ARF‐binding sites were consistently detected in DGAT2 promoters across both angiosperms and bryophytes (Table S2), suggesting that the transcriptional regulation of DGAT2 by auxin signaling is not limited to Brassicaceae but may represent an evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanism that contributes to thermal adaptation in land plants.

Discussion

In this study, we uncovered a previously unrecognized layer of regulation in thermomorphogenesis: a mechanistic link between DGAT2‐mediated TAG synthesis and auxin homeostasis in Arabidopsis hypocotyls. Our model proposes that elevated temperature activates two antagonistic pathways (Fig. 8): (1) a canonical growth‐promoting arm that stimulates auxin biosynthesis and signaling to drive cell elongation, and (2) a growth‐restraining arm in which DGAT2 enhances membrane lipid order and thereby constrains elongation. Crucially, these pathways engage in reciprocal inhibition: auxin suppresses DGAT2 expression, while DGAT2 attenuates auxin accumulation and signaling, potentially by modulating PIN3 nanodomain organization at the PM of hypocotyl epidermal cells, thereby opposing auxin‐driven elongation. Whether DGAT2‐mediated changes in membrane lipid order influence PIN3 function through additional mechanisms, such as regulating PIN3 endocytic trafficking, protein turnover, or transport activity, remains an important question for future investigation. In addition, direct examination of TAG and membrane acyl unsaturation in hypocotyl epidermal cells will be important for elucidating the biochemical basis by which DGAT2 modulates PM lipid order.

Proposed regulatory model of auxin and DGAT2 in controlling high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl cell elongation. Based on its established substrate preference, increased DGAT2 is proposed to promote the incorporation of unsaturated fatty acids into triacylglycerol (TAG), thereby contributing to the maintenance of plasma membrane lipid order. Auxin promotes cell elongation, whereas DGAT2 counteracts this effect, potentially by maintaining plasma membrane lipid order and thereby modulating PIN3 nanodomain organization and function. In turn, auxin represses DGAT2 expression. This dynamic interplay between auxin signaling and DGAT2 helps fine‐tune the thermomorphogenic response, preventing excessive hypocotyl cell elongation under elevated temperatures. This model significantly refines the prevailing view of thermomorphogenesis. The canonical model emphasizes a unidirectional cascade in which high temperature inactivates phytochrome B (phyB), stabilizes PIF4, and enhances auxin biosynthesis and transport, thereby promoting cell elongation (Fan et al., 2025). While this explains growth activation, it lacks intrinsic mechanisms to prevent over‐elongation. Our findings introduce a homeostatic counterbalance: as auxin levels rise, they suppress DGAT2, but sustained warmth eventually induces DGAT2 through a mechanism that is likely independent of PIF4, which then curbs auxin accumulation and elongation. Consistent with this model, the DGAT2 promoter lacks canonical PIF4‐binding G‐boxes but contains multiple predicted AuxREs, and our data demonstrated that auxin represses DGAT2. Thus, PIF4 likely influences DGAT2 expression indirectly, potentially through its role in auxin biosynthesis or signaling. Notably, this DGAT2–auxin antagonism is distinct from other known inhibitory interactions in thermomorphogenesis, such as the ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5)‐PIF4 and phyB‐PIF4 axes (Delker et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2017; Park et al., 2018; Nieto et al., 2025). Whereas HY5 primarily acts transcriptionally to repress PIF4 expression and competes with PIF4 for target gene promoters, phyB inhibits PIF4 post‐translationally by directly interacting with PIF4 and promoting its phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation in the light. By contrast, DGAT2 operates metabolically and post‐transcriptionally, modulating auxin responses through effects on membrane properties. Thus, our model does not replace existing frameworks but complements and deepens them, revealing a multi‐layered control system that integrates transcriptional, hormonal, metabolic, and biophysical inputs.

A key unresolved question is how elevated temperature induces DGAT2 expression. Temperature, by definition, increases molecular kinetic energy, leading to elevated membrane fluidity (Yadav et al., 2025). To maintain membrane functionality, cells must adjust lipid saturation. In yeast, the ER‐localized transcription factor Mga2 senses membrane lipid packing and activates desaturation genes upon thermal perturbation (Covino et al., 2016; Ballweg et al., 2020). Plants possess analogous ER‐anchored bZIP transcription factors (bZIP17, bZIP28, and bZIP60) that translocate to the nucleus and positively regulate high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation in a PIF4‐dependent manner (Zhang et al., 2025). Although the DGAT2 promoter lacks binding motifs for these ER‐tethered bZIPs, it does contain sites for nucleus‐localized bZIPs (e.g. ATB2/AtbZIP53/GBF5), which are implicated in both stress responses (Weltmeier et al., 2009; Weiste et al., 2017) and auxin signaling (Jiang & Li, 2025). Nevertheless, the upstream regulator(s) that directly induce DGAT2 expression in response to elevated temperature remain unknown. It will therefore be interesting to investigate whether these nucleus‐localized bZIP transcription factors, or an as‐yet‐unidentified membrane property sensor, mediate temperature‐dependent DGAT2 induction. Intriguingly, DGAT2 is also upregulated during acute heat stress, during which polyunsaturated TAGs accumulate (Yang et al., 2024). Together with its expression across tissues and developmental stages, this suggests that DGAT2 may play additional roles in thermoadaptation beyond hypocotyl elongation, although this remains to be tested.

Beyond DGAT2, our genetic data suggest that PDAT1 may also act as a negative regulator of high‐temperature‐induced hypocotyl elongation. Like DGAT2, PDAT1 preferentially incorporates unsaturated fatty acids into TAGs (Stahl et al., 2004), but it does so by transferring them directly from membrane phospholipids, potentially resulting in a more immediate effect on membrane composition. Although PDAT1 expression is not upregulated by high temperature, it may be regulated post‐translationally. Interestingly, like DGAT2, the PDAT1 promoter also contains putative ARF‐binding sites, which are absent in the promoters of DGAT1 and PDAT2. Future work should examine whether PDAT1 is also regulated by auxin and whether it acts in concert with DGAT2 to fine‐tune membrane composition during thermal adaptation.

Plants possess several biochemical mechanisms that reduce membrane unsaturation by lowering the proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). These include suppression of ω‐2 and ω‐3 fatty acid desaturases, the key enzymes that produce linoleic acid (18:2) and α‐linolenic acid (18:3), through reduced transcription, translational repression, or increased protein turnover (Horiguchi et al., 2000; Matsuda et al., 2005; O'Quin et al., 2010; Menard et al., 2017); acyl‐editing reactions mediated by lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferases (LPCATs), which facilitate the exchange of PUFA‐containing acyl groups for more saturated fatty acids in phospholipids (Lager et al., 2013); and degradation of PUFA‐rich membrane lipids via lipases or lipoxygenases (LOX) (Singh et al., 2022). Notably, desaturase downregulation affects only newly synthesized fatty acids and does not alter pre‐existing PUFA content in membranes, whereas LOX‐mediated degradation is irreversible and potentially wasteful or damaging because oxidative cleavage can generate cytotoxic aldehydes (e.g. malondialdehyde and hexenal) that exacerbate cellular stress (Singh et al., 2022). By contrast, DGAT2 and PDAT1 provide a reversible mechanism for modulating membrane unsaturation by channeling PUFAs into TAG. This TAG‐based sequestration enables the removal of the most fluidity‐promoting fatty acids from membrane lipid pools without carbon loss or oxidative damage, preserving them in cytosolic lipid droplets for potential remobilization. Thus, DGAT2‐ and PDAT1‐mediated TAG sequestration may represent a distinctive membrane‐stabilizing mechanism that provides the dual benefits of membrane protection and conservation of energy‐rich fatty acids during thermal adaptation.

In summary, our study reframes thermomorphogenesis as a dynamically balanced process governed not only by transcriptional activators such as PIF4 but also by metabolic feedback from lipid remodeling. By linking membrane biophysics to auxin transport and homeostasis, the DGAT2–auxin antagonistic loop may enable plants to fine‐tune growth under elevated temperature while avoiding maladaptive over‐elongation. Importantly, this lipid–hormone crosstalk appears to be evolutionarily conserved. Several key regulatory and biochemical features of DGAT2, including thermal induction and auxin‐mediated repression of DGAT2 expression, as well as DGAT2‐mediated membrane lipid ordering, are conserved in Camelina. In addition, the widespread presence of DGAT2 genes, together with the conserved occurrence of ARF‐binding sites in their promoters across angiosperms and bryophytes, suggests that DGAT2–auxin crosstalk may represent a broadly conserved regulatory mechanism underlying thermal adaptation in land plants.
