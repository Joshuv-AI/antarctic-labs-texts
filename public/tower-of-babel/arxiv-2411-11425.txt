Non-equilibrium steady states with a spatial Markov structure
Frank Redig, Berend van Tol

Abstract
We investigate the structure of non-equilibrium steady states (NESS) for a class of exactly solvable models in the setting of a chain with left and right reservoirs. Inspired by recent results on the harmonic model [ 4 ] , [ 5 ] , we focus on models in which the NESS is a mixture of equilibrium product measures, and where the probability measure which describes the mixture has a spatial Markovian property. We completely characterize the structure of such mixture measures, and show that under natural scaling and translation invariance properties, the only possible mixture measures are coinciding with the Dirichlet process found in [ 4 ] in the context of the harmonic model.

Introduction

The structure of the stationary state of stochastic interacting particle systems driven out of equilibrium, for instance via boundary driving, is a challenging problem, and very few exactly solvable models are available.
A famous example of an exactly solvable model in this context is the boundary driven exclusion process, solvable via the matrix ansatz solution, see
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
.

Recently, new explicit representations of non-equilibrium steady states (NESS) were discovered in the context of the boundary driven KMP model

[
14
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
7
]
, and in the context of the boundary driven harmonic model
[
4
]
,

[
5
]
.
For a general class of mass transport models, including both the KMP and the harmonic model on general graphs, such representations were found via intertwining in

[
13
]
.
The integrable structure of the harmonic model was discovered in
[
11
]
, and explicit formulas for the factorial moments in the non-equilibrium stationary state are given.

An important further development is the discovery that in all these models the NESS can be written as a stochastic mixture of product measures with equilibrium marginals. E.g. for the KMP model the equilibrium measures are products of exponentials with identical parameters. If we then consider the KMP model on a chain with
n
sites and with left reservoir parameter
\theta_{L}
and right reservoir parameter
\theta_{R}
the NESS reads

\mu_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}^{(n)}=\int\left(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{\theta_{i}}\right)\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}(d\theta_{1},\ldots d\theta_{n}).

(1)

Here
\nu_{\theta_{i}}
is an exponential distribution with parameter (i.e., expectation)
\theta_{i}
, and

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
, the so-called mixture measure, is a probability measure on
[0,\infty)^{n}
.
This mixture measure describes the distribution of the “hidden parameters”
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
.
We then recover the equilibrium measures of the KMP model when
\theta_{L}=\theta_{R}
because in that case the mixture measure
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
becomes a Dirac measure.
Moreover, it turns out that the mixture measure
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
in (
1
)
is itself the stationary measure of a Markovian dynamics on the space of parameters
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
which is called the “hidden temperature” model
[
7
]
or in more general setting, the “hidden parameter” model
[
13
]
.
Other models where the NESS can be written as a stochastic mixture of product measures include the boundary driven symmetric exclusion process
[
10
]
, and the boundary driven inclusion process
[
13
]
.
In both these models (as well as in the KMP model), the mixture measure cannot be obtained explicitly as it is the case for the harmonic models (on the chain), but it can only be characterized implicitly as the stationary measure of the hidden parameter model.

In the context of the (both discrete and continuous) harmonic model, a representation as in (
1
) exists, and in that context, the measure

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
is available in closed form and given by the joint distribution of the order statistics of
n
independent uniforms on the interval
[\theta_{L},\theta_{R}]
(where we assume
\theta_{L}<\theta_{R}
).
In
[
4
]
, a class of generalized harmonic models is introduced, where also the mixture measure
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
is explicit and given by an ordered Dirichlet distribution.

In
[
13
]
we provide a probabilistic understanding of the fact that for those harmonic models the mixture measure can be obtained explicitly, by showing that under the measure

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
, the conditional distribution of
\theta_{i}
, given all
\theta_{j},j\not=i
is uniform on
[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i+1}]
, which is exactly the Markov structure of the joint distribution of order statistics of independent uniforms. As we showed in
[
4
]
, this spatial Markov structure of the measure

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
also leads naturally to the additivity principle for the pressure and the corresponding large deviation rate function (cf.
[
3
]
). Therefore, we can think of the spatial Markov structure
of the mixture measure
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
as a microscopic counterpart of the additivity principle
[
3
]
.

In this paper, we want to focus
on the precise consequences of the Markov structure of the mixture measure

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
. As a first result, we prove that in such a setting
the measures
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}

are completely determined by the system consisting of a single site with left and right reservoirs, i.e., the measures

\Lambda^{1}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
. As a second result we characterise those single site systems which are “extendable”, i.e., give rise to a Markov family
\{\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Finally, as a third result, under natural assumptions of scaling and translation invariance, we prove that the only possible measures

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
are the ordered Dirichlet distributions which appeared in the generalized harmonic models
[
4
]
.

The measures
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
are concentrated on the set of ordered
n
-tuples

\theta_{L}<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\ldots<\theta_{n}<\theta_{R}
. Because of this ordering restriction, the standard theory of Markov specifications from

[
12
]
chapter 10 and 11, or the Hammersley-Clifford theorem
[
6
]
is not directly applicable as both assume non-null specifications. We can think of the measures

\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}

as the joint distribution of a Markov bridge, i.e., a joint distribution of the form

X^{(n)}_{1}=\cdot,\ldots,X^{(n)}_{n}=\cdot|X^{(n)}_{0}=\theta_{L},X^{(n)}_{n+1}=\theta_{R}

where
X^{(n)}_{1}=\cdot,\ldots,X^{(n)}_{n}
are the first
n
steps of a Markov process. However, because of the ordering restriction,
this Markov bridge depends on
n
and therefore cannot be thought of as the conditioning of an infinite-volume Gibbs measure with nearest neighbor interaction.

The rest of our paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we define the two-sided Markov property and a notion of symmetry for the measures
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
. In section 3 we study their support properties. In section 4 we provide a way to reconstruct the measure
\Lambda^{n}_{\theta_{L},\theta_{R}}
corresponding to
n
sites from the measure corresponding to a single site. Finally, we find two properties concerning the shift and scale invariance of the measures to characterize the Dirichlet distributions.

2
Structure of the
n
-site densities

In this section we first introduce the necessary notation and give a formal definition of the Markovian structure of the mixture measure
\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}
. We also define a notion of symmetry which we will assume throughout this text. Next we prove that the Markov property implies a product structure for the
n
-site density and subsequently investigate consequences of the symmetry property.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We consider a family of probability density functions
\{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
corresponding to random vectors taking values in
(I_{a,b})^{n}
, where
I_{a,b}=(a\wedge b,a\vee b)
. That is, for fixed
a
,
b
and
n
we have random variables
\Theta_{1,n},...,\Theta_{n,n}
with joint density
\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}
:

\mathbb{P}_{n}((\Theta_{1,n},...,\Theta_{n,n})\in A)=\int_{A}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}d\lambda_{n},

(2)

for each Borel set
A\in\mathscr{B}((I_{a,b})^{n})
and
n\geq 1
and where
\lambda_{n}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We assume that, for all values of
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
n\geq 1
, we have a corresponding density
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
. This then defines the map
\Lambda^{n}:(a,b)\rightarrow\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
and we refer to this map as the
n
-site density. Moreover, we refer to
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
as the the
n
-site density with left parameter
a
and right parameter
b
.

Throughout this text we often use the densities of marginals and conditional densities of the random variables
\Theta_{i,n}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, which we denote as follows,

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(\theta_{i}):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{i},...,\theta_{n})d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{i-1}d\theta_{i+1}...d\theta_{n}

(3)

for the
i
-th marginal and, for
\theta_{i}\in\text{supp }(\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i})
,

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{i}=\theta_{i}):=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{i},...,\theta_{n})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(\theta_{i})}.

(4)

We now define what it means for the family
\Lambda^{n}
to be two-sided Markov. For notational simplicity we omit the right index
n
in
\Theta_{1,n},...,\Theta_{n,n}
whenever
n
is fixed, i.e. we write
\Theta_{1},...,\Theta_{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

The family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is called

1.

Two-sided Markov
if the corresponding densities
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
satisfy the following two properties

(i)

Two-sided Markov property:

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{j}=\theta_{j})=\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j-1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{j-1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{j},b}^{n-j}(\theta_{j+1},...,\theta_{n})

(5)

for all
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{a,b}
. Here we define
\Lambda_{a,b}^{0}:=1
.

(ii)

Support restriction:
\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}
has support
I_{a,b}
. Here we define the support of a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\text{supp }(f):=\{x\in X:f(x)\neq 0\}
.

2.

Symmetric
if, for all
n\geq 1
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{a,b}

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\Lambda_{b,a}^{n}(\theta_{n},...,\theta_{1}).

(6)

Remark 2.2
.

In this paper we are interested in densities
\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}
which concentrate on the set of ordered
n
-tuples
a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{n}<b
. This is guaranteed via the requirement on the support of
\Lambda^{1}
, as we will prove in proposition
3.2
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the joint probability density of the order statistics of
n
i.i.d. uniform random variables on
(a,b)
with
a\leq b
. More precisely, for
n\geq 1
we consider
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}\sim U(a,b)
and denote the corresponding order statistics by
\Theta_{1}=X_{1:n},\Theta_{2}=X_{2:n},...,\Theta_{n}=X_{n:n}
. The joint distribution of
\Theta_{1},...,\Theta_{n}
is given by

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=n!\cdot|b-a|^{-n}\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{n}<b).

(7)

The marginals are given by

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(\theta)=\frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i)!}|b-a|^{-n}|b-\theta|^{n-i}|\theta-a|^{i-1}\mathds{1}(\theta\in I_{a,b}).

(8)

The two-sided Markov property now follows from

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{i}=\theta_{i})=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(\theta_{i})}=\frac{(i-1)!(n-i)!}{|b-\theta_{i}|^{n-i}|\theta_{i}-a|^{i-1}}\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{n}<b)

(9)

and

\Lambda_{a,\theta_{i}}^{i-1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{i-1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{i},b}^{n-i}(\theta_{i+1},...,\theta_{n})=\frac{(i-1)!(n-i)!}{|b-\theta_{i}|^{n-i}|\theta_{i}-a|^{i-1}}\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{n}<b).

(10)

We generalize the definition of the order statistics for the case
b\leq a
in such a way that the family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
becomes symmetric. I.e. we define

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\begin{cases}n!\cdot|b-a|^{-n}\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{n}<b)\quad\text{if }a<b\\
n!\cdot|b-a|^{-n}\mathds{1}(a>\theta_{1}>\theta_{2}>...>\theta_{n}>b)\quad\text{if }a>b\end{cases}

(11)

One can show the two-sided Markov property for the case
a>b
in the same way as is done above for
a<b
.

2.1
Product form for two-sided Markov families

In this paper we aim to fully characterize the symmetric families which satisfy the two-sided Markov property. A first step in this direction is given by the proposition
2.4
below. It states the equivalence between the two-sided Markov property and the density being a product of marginals in two distinct ways. These product forms can directly be derived by repeatedly applying the two-sided Markov property. The converse can be derived by combining the two product representations.

Proposition 2.4
.

For notational convenience we write
\theta_{0}:=a
and
\theta_{n+1}:=b
. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is two-sided Markov.

2.

For all
n\geq 1
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{a,b}
we have

\begin{cases}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{n}=\theta_{n})=\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n}}^{n-1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n-1})\\
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{1}=\theta_{1})=\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{n-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{n}).\end{cases}

(12)

3.

For all
n\geq 1
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{a,b}
,

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{\theta_{i},b}^{n-i,1}(\theta_{i+1})=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n-i+1}}^{n-i,n-i}(\theta_{n-i}).

(13)

Proof.

1 implies 2: This follows immediately from the definition of the two-sided Markov property.

2 implies 3: We use induction. (
13
) obviously holds for
n=1
. Assume (
13
) holds for
n=k
, then we have, using (
12
),

\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k})=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k-i+1}}^{k-i,k-i}(\theta_{k-i})

(14)

and

\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k+1})=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{\theta_{i},b}^{k-i,1}(\theta_{i+1}).

(15)

3 implies 1: We have the following two expressions for
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}

\displaystyle(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=[\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{n-1,1}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{j-1},b}^{n-j+1,1}(\theta_{j})]\cdot[\Lambda_{\theta_{j},b}^{n-j,1}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{j+1},b}^{n-j-1,1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{n-1},b}^{1,1}(\theta_{n})]

(16)

and

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}

\displaystyle(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=[\Lambda_{a,\theta_{2}}^{1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{3}}^{2,2}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j-1,j-1}(\theta_{j})]\cdot[\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j,j}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j+1}}^{j+1,j+1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,n}(\theta_{n})].

(17)

Notice both expressions factor into a part which does not depend on
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{j-1}
and a part which does not depend on
\theta_{j+1},...,\theta_{n}
. As a consequence

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{n-1,1}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{j-1},b}^{n-j+1,1}(\theta_{j})}{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{2}}^{1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{3}}^{2,2}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j-1,j-1}(\theta_{j})}=C(a,\theta_{j},b)=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j,j}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j+1}}^{j+1,j+1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,n}(\theta_{n})}{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j,j}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j+1}}^{j+1,j+1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,n}(\theta_{n})}

(18)

for some function
C
. We tacitly used that the denominators above are non-zero. However if one of the factors in the denominator of the equation above is zero, then the two-sided Markov property holds automatically because both left and right hand side of the defining property (
5
) are equal to zero. One can now relate the factors of the first and the second expression via
C
. We use this to show the two-sided Markov property.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}

\displaystyle(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=[\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{n-1,1}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{j-1},b}^{n-j+1,1}(\theta_{j})]\cdot[\Lambda_{\theta_{j},b}^{n-j,1}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{j+1},b}^{n-j-1,1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{n-1},b}^{1,1}(\theta_{n})]

(19)

\displaystyle=C(a,\theta_{j},b)\cdot[\Lambda_{a,\theta_{2}}^{1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{3}}^{2,2}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j-1,j-1}(\theta_{j})]\cdot[\Lambda_{\theta_{j},b}^{n-j,1}(\theta_{j+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{j+1},b}^{n-j-1,1}(\theta_{j+2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{n-1},b}^{1,1}(\theta_{n})]

\displaystyle=C(a,\theta_{j},b)\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{j}}^{j-1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{j-1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{j},b}^{n-j}(\theta_{j+1},...,\theta_{n}).

Integrating both sides of this equation with respect to
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{j-1},\theta_{j+1},...,\theta_{n}
yields
C(a,\theta_{j},b)=\Lambda^{n,j}_{a,b}(\theta_{j})
, which then gives us the two-sided Markov property in (
2.1
).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

Proposition (
2.4
) will play an important role in later applications since it shows the whole family is determined by its left and right-side marginals, i.e.
\{\Lambda^{n,1}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{\Lambda^{n,n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. More precisely, suppose one has a family up to some
m\geq 0
,
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{m\geq n\geq 1}
. Then we can construct a valid density
\Lambda^{m+1}
if we can find
\Lambda^{m+1,1}
and
\Lambda^{m+1,m+1}
such that

\Lambda^{m+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m+1}):=\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{m}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m+1})=\Lambda_{a,\theta_{m+1}}^{m}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m})\cdot\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}(\theta_{m+1}).

(20)

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m+1}\in I_{a,b}
. Indeed, one can substitute the product forms in (
13
) for
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m}
. Then
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1}
again has the product form

\Lambda^{m+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m+1})=\prod_{i=0}^{m}\Lambda_{\theta_{i},b}^{m+1-i,1}(\theta_{i+1})=\prod_{i=0}^{m}\Lambda_{a,\theta_{m+2-i}}^{m+1-i,m+1-i}(\theta_{m+1-i}),

(21)

and hence satisfies the two-side Markov property.

2.2
Implications of the symmetry property

In this subsection we examine some of the consequences of the symmetry property defined in (
6
). First, we will relate the
j
-th and the
n-j+1
-th marginal. This is done in proposition
2.6
. Next we show that the symmetry of a family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is completely determined by the symmetry of
\Lambda^{1}
, provided that the family is two-sided Markov. This result is the content of
2.7
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be symmetric. Assume
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\geq 1
, then

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,j}(\theta)=\Lambda_{b,a}^{n,n-j+1}(\theta)

(22)

for all
j\in\{1,...,n\}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,j}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta,...,\theta_{n})d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{j-1}d\theta_{j+1}...d\theta_{n}

(23)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{b,a}^{n}(\theta_{n},...,\theta,...,\theta_{1})d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{j-1}d\theta_{j+1}...d\theta_{n}=\Lambda_{b,a}^{n,n-j+1}(\theta)

∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}\}_{n\geq 1}
be two-sided Markov with
\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}=\Lambda_{b,a}^{1}
, then the whole family
\{\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is symmetric.

Proof.

We use induction. The symmetry of
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
holds for
n=1
by assumption. Suppose the symmetry holds for
n=k
and that
(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})\in\text{supp }\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}
. Notice that, due to the two-sided Markov property,

1=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})}.

(24)

We obtain

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})}.

(25)

We will use this equation to show the symmetry for
n=k+1
. Notice that we have symmetry if

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1}(\theta_{k+1},...,\theta_{1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{b,\theta_{1}}^{k}(\theta_{k+1},...,\theta_{2})}=1.

(26)

By the induction hypothesis this is equivalent to

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{1})}=1

(27)

We will prove (
27
) to show symmetry for
n=k+1
. We start from (
25
) and observe that

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})}=\Bigg(\frac{\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})}\Bigg)^{-1}=\Bigg(\frac{\Lambda_{b,\theta_{1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{\theta_{k+1},a}^{k,k}(\theta_{1})}\Bigg)^{-1}=\frac{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{k+1})}.

(28)

For the third equality we use the induction hypothesis and for the last equality we use (
25
). One can now separate the equation into a
\theta_{1}
-dependent and a
\theta_{k+1}
-dependent side. That is

\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})}{\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{k+1})}=c

(29)

for some
c
independent from both
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{k+1}
. Integrating
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})=c\cdot\Lambda_{b,a}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{1})
over
\theta_{1}
yields
c=1
. This yields (
27
) and hence concludes the proof.
∎

3
Support of the densities

In this section we study the support of the densities
\{\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}\}_{n\geq 1}
. More precisely, we show that the support of the first marginal
\Lambda^{n,1}_{a,b}
is given by
I_{a,b}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The product form from the previous section then implies that
\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}
is supported on the set of ordered
n
-tuples in the interval
I_{a,b}
.

For two-sided Markov families
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
one can find transition operators transforming on the marginals. More precise, for suitable
i
, one can find a raising operator
P_{n,i+1}
and a lowering operator
Q_{n,i-1}
transforming
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}
to
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i+1}
and
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i-1}
respectively. The proposition below states these transition operators explicitly.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be two-sided Markov. For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
n\geq 1
,
i\in\{1,...,n\}
we have

\begin{cases}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i+1}=P_{n,i+1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}\quad\text{ for }i\in\{1,...,n-1\}\\
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i-1}=Q_{n,i-1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}\quad\text{ for }i\in\{2,...,n\}\end{cases}.

(30)

Here
P_{n,i+1}:L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)\rightarrow L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
and
Q_{n,i-1}:L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)\rightarrow L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
are given by

\begin{cases}P_{n,i+1}f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\cdot\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i,1}(x)dy\\
Q_{n,i-1}f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{i-1,i-1}(x)dy\end{cases}.

(31)

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that transition operators
P_{n,i+1}
and
Q_{n,i-1}
map
L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
functions to
L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
functions. Indeed, let
f\in L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
, then

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|P_{n,i+1}f(x)|dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(y)|\cdot|\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i,1}(x)|dydx\leq||f||_{1}<\infty.\

(32)

A similar argument can be used to show
Q_{n,i-1}
map
L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
functions to
L^{1}(-\infty,\infty)
.

We now show that
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i+1}=P_{n,i+1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}
. On can use an analogous proof to show
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i-1}=Q_{n,i-1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}
.

We use the notation
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}
to denote the joint distribution of
\Theta_{i}
and
\Theta_{i+1}
, i.e.

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(\theta_{i},\theta_{i+1})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})d\theta_{1},...,d\theta_{i-1}d\theta_{i+2}...d\theta_{n}.

(33)

We write
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(y,\theta_{i+1}|\Theta_{i}=y)
for the joint distribution under the condition
\Theta_{i}=y
. Suppose we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(y,\theta_{i+1}|\Theta_{i}=y)=\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i,1}(\theta_{i+1}),

(34)

then the result follows directly:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i+1}(\theta_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(y)\cdot\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(y,\theta_{i+1}|\Theta_{i}=y)dy

(35)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(y)\cdot\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i,1}(\theta_{i+1})dy

\displaystyle=P_{n,i+1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,i}(\theta_{i+1}).

To prove (
34
) we compute
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(y,\theta_{i+1}|\Theta_{i}=y)
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,(i,i+1)}(y,\theta_{i+1}|

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}=y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{i-1},y,\theta_{i+1},...,\theta_{n}|\Theta_{i}=y)d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{i-1}d\theta_{i+2}...d\theta_{n}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{a,y}^{i-1}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{i-1})\cdot\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i}(\theta_{i+1},...,\theta_{n})d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{i-1}d\theta_{i+2}...d\theta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda_{y,b}^{n-i}(\theta_{i+1},...,\theta_{n})d\theta_{i+2}...d\theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{a,b}^{n-i,1}(\theta_{i+1}).

∎

In the following proposition we make use of the transition operators introduced in
3.1
in order to prove that the support of
\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}
determines the support of the marginals
\Lambda^{n,1}_{a,b}
and hence via the product structure the support
\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be two-sided Markov and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then

\text{supp }(\Lambda_{a,b}^{1})=I_{a,b}\quad\text{implies}\quad\text{supp }(\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1})=I_{a,b}

(37)

for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

We use induction. For
n=1
the statement holds by assumption. Assume
\forall m\leq k
: supp
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m,1}=I_{a,b}
. First we show that supp
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}\subset I_{a,b}
. This part of the argument doesn’t rely on the transition operators.

Step 1: the support of
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
is a subset of
I_{a,b}

Suppose
\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
is in the support of
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
, i.e.
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\neq 0
. Then,there exist
\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k+1}
such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,1}(\theta_{2})...\Lambda_{\theta_{k},b}^{1,1}(\theta_{k+1})

(38)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,k+1}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,k}(\theta_{k})...\Lambda_{a,\theta_{2}}^{1,1}(\theta_{1})\neq 0.

Suppose
\theta_{1}<b
, then
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{k+1}\leq b
by the induction hypothesis and the first line of (
38
). The second line of (
38
) tells us that either
a<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<...<\theta_{k+1}
or
a>\theta_{1}>\theta_{2}>...>\theta_{k+1}
. Combining the information from line one and line two gives
a<\theta_{1}<b
. Similarly we can assume
\theta_{1}<b
and derive
b<\theta_{1}<a
. We conclude
\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b}
.

Step 2:
I_{a,b}
is the support of
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}

We notice that
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
is a fixed point for
Q_{k+1,1}P_{k+1,2}
by virtue of proposition
3.1
,

Q_{k+1,1}\Big(P_{k+1,2}\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}\Big)=Q_{k+1,1}\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,2}=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}.

(39)

We simplify notation by writing
f
for
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
,
g
for
P_{k+1,2}\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
, i.e.
f:=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
and
g:=P_{k+1,2}\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
a\leq b
. Then,

g(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\cdot\Lambda_{y,b}^{k,1}(x)dy=\int_{a}^{b}f(y)\cdot\Lambda_{y,b}^{k,1}(x)dy.

(40)

Note that under the induction hypothesis supp
\Lambda_{y,b}^{k,1}=[y,b]
for
y\in[a,b]
and that supp
f\subset I_{a,b}=[a,b]
. We see that

\text{supp }g=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\text{ess inf}(\text{supp }f)<x<b\},

(41)

where
\text{ess inf}(\text{supp }f):=\inf\big\{z\in\mathbb{R}:\lambda\big((-\infty,z]\cap\text{supp}f\big)\neq 0\big\}
with
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure. Analogously, we have

f(x)=Q_{k+1,1}g(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(y)\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{1}(x)dy=\int_{a}^{b}g(y)\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{1}(x)dy

(42)

and

\text{supp }(f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<\text{ess sup}(\text{supp }g)\}

(43)

with
\text{ess sup}(\text{supp }g):=\sup\big\{z\in\mathbb{R}:\lambda\big([z,\infty)\cap\text{supp }g\big)\neq 0\big\}
. Notice that
\text{supp }g
can not be empty since
f
is a fixed point of
Q_{k+1,1}P_{k+1,2}
. Indeed, if
\text{supp }g
would be empty then
f
would be zero, which is not possible since
f=\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}
is a density function. This implies ess sup(supp
g
)
=b
since the support of
g
is the interval in (
41
). We conclude supp
f
= supp
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1,1}=(a,b)=I_{a,b}
.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

The support of the first marginals determines directly the support of the joint density. Indeed, the product expressions for
\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}
in proposition
2.4
directly yields that

\text{supp }\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}=\begin{cases}\{\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{n}<b\}\quad\text{for }a<b\\
\{\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:a>\theta_{1}>...>\theta_{n}>b\}\quad\text{for }b<a\end{cases}.

(44)

4
Uniqueness and Recursive construction

In this subsection we attempt to construct families
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfying the two-sided Markov property. In particular, we are interested in identifying which “one-site systems”
\Lambda^{1}
extend to a full two-sided Markov family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. We call those
extendable
. So far, in section 3, we have seen that both symmetry and the support of the first marginal

\Lambda^{n,1}
are determined by
\Lambda^{1}
. One of the results in this section gives uniqueness for extensions of
\Lambda^{1}
which has support on
I_{a,b}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we will use proposition
2.4
to identify the form of the
\Lambda^{1}
which are extendable.

4.1
Uniqueness

In the previous subsection we found transition operators with the marginals as fixed points. Since these operators are defined in terms of marginals of smaller order, one could be tempted to show uniqueness recursively via a fixed point theorem (e.g the Krein-Rutman theorem). However, in our setting it appears to be non-trivial to satisfy the condition for such theorems. Therefore we give a more direct approach below, relying strongly on our knowledge about the support of the marginals.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a two-sided Markov family. Then
\Lambda^{1}
uniquely determines
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
.

Proof.

Suppose we have two families
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
which are both two-sided Markov. Moreover assume

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{1}_{a,b}.

(45)

We show

\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}=\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}.

(46)

using induction. For
n=1
, the density functions are equal by assumption. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+},a<b
, be arbitrary and assume that
\Lambda^{k}=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k}
. For any
a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{k+1}<b
we have

\displaystyle 0\neq\Lambda^{k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})

(47)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})

and

\displaystyle 0\neq\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})

(48)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{k+1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{k}}^{k-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{k})\cdot\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1}).

We find

\frac{\Lambda^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{k+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{k+1}}^{k,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{k,k}(\theta_{k+1})}=\frac{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})}{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(\theta_{k+1})}.

(49)

Let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary and pick
\theta_{k+1}=b-\epsilon
. For all
\theta_{1}\in(a,b-\epsilon]

\frac{\Lambda^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(b-\epsilon)}=\frac{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})}{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(b-\epsilon)}.

(50)

Integration of both sides over
\theta_{1}
yields that
\Lambda^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(b-\epsilon)=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,k+1}_{a,b}(b-\epsilon)
and hence we have
\Lambda^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(x)=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,1}_{a,b}(x)
for
x\in(a,b-\epsilon)
. Since this holds for arbitrary
\epsilon
, we can conclude that
\Lambda^{k+1,1}_{a,b}=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k+1,1}_{a,b}
. Moreover, we have
\Lambda_{a,b}^{k+1}=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{a,b}^{k+1}
. For
b\leq a
a similar argument holds.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The theorem above essentially says that it is impossible for multiple two-sided Markov families to have the same single site density. However, which
\Lambda^{1}
correspond to a two-sided Markov family, i.e., which
\Lambda^{1}
are extendable is at this point an open question. This will be the subject of the next subsection, in particular in theorem
4.5
below we give a full characterization.

4.2
Recursive construction

By virtue of proposition
2.4
we can attempt to recursively construct a family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
from a suitable, symmetric,
\Lambda^{1}
. In the proof of the theorem below we find a construction as described in remark
2.5
, i.e. we find suitable left and right marginals to construct
\Lambda^{n+1}
from
\Lambda^{n}
. before we state this theorem, we first prove two preliminary results.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric two-sided Markov family. For arbitrary
n\geq 1
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, there exist
y
-dependent functions
f_{n},g_{n}
and
h_{n}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
such that

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(x)=f_{n}(a,b)\cdot g_{n}(b,x)\cdot h_{n}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(51)

for all
a,x,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
x\in I_{a,b}
and
b\in I_{a,y}
. If a density is of this form we will refer to it as factorizable.

Proof.

By conditioning on
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{n+1}
we find

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{n+1}}^{n-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{n})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},y}^{n,n}(\theta_{n+1})=\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n+1}}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},\theta_{n+1}}^{n-1}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{n})\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,n+1}(\theta_{n+1}),

(52)

where both sides of the equation are equal to
\Lambda^{n+1}_{a,y}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n+1})
.
To proceed we need information on the support of
\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1}
. To this end we assume
a<y
. As a consequence, we can pick
a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{n+1}<y
, then

\frac{\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{y,a}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{n+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,n+1}(\theta_{n+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n+1}}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{\theta_{1},y}^{n,n}(\theta_{n+1})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n+1}}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{y,\theta_{1}}^{n,1}(\theta_{n+1})}.

(53)

The first and third equality use the symmetry property. The second equality follows from (
52
). The same equation can be derived for
y<a
and
\theta_{1}>\theta_{n+1}
, hence we continue with general
a,y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\theta_{1}\in I_{a,\theta_{n+1}}
,
\theta_{n+1}\in I_{a,y}
. Notice that in the case
a<y
we have
a<x<b<y
, whereas in the case
y<a
we have
y<b<x<a
. That is, in the first case
y
acts as an upper bound for the interval
I_{a,b}
and in the second case it acts like a lower bound. We observe that

\Lambda_{a,\theta_{n+1}}^{n,1}(\theta_{1})=[\Lambda_{y,a}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{n+1})]^{-1}\cdot\Lambda^{n,1}_{y,\theta_{1}}(\theta_{n+1})\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(\theta_{1}).

(54)

One can now take
\theta_{1}=x
and
\theta_{n+1}=b
to obtain

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(x)=[\Lambda_{y,a}^{n+1,1}(b)]^{-1}\cdot\Lambda^{n,1}_{y,x}(b)\cdot\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(x).

(55)

We can then define the functions
f_{n},g_{n}
and
h_{n}
as follows

f_{n}(a,b):=[\Lambda_{y,a}^{n+1,1}(b)]^{-1},\quad g_{n}(b,x):=\Lambda^{n,1}_{y,x}(b),\quad h_{n}(x,a):=\Lambda_{a,y}^{n+1,1}(x).

(56)

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric two-sided Markov family. For arbitrary
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
z_{1}\leq z_{2}
, there exist functions
f,g
and
h
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f(a,b)\cdot g(b,x)\cdot h(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(57)

for all
a,b\in(z_{1},z_{2})
. Moreover, the functions
f
and
g
can be chosen to be symmetric.

Proof.

Let
Z_{1}>0
and
Z_{2}>0
be such that
Z_{1}<z_{1}
and
z_{2}<Z_{2}
. Let
a,b\in(Z_{1},Z_{2})
and assume
a\leq b
. Then

b\in I_{a,Z_{2}}
, hence proposition
4.3
gives us functions
\widehat{f},\widehat{g}
and
\widehat{h}
such that

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=\widehat{f}(a,b)\cdot\widehat{g}(b,x)\cdot\widehat{h}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b}).

(58)

Similarly we can assume
b\leq a
, then
b\in I_{Z_{1},a}
, hence there are functions
\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g}
and
\widetilde{h}
such that

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=\widetilde{f}(a,b)\cdot\widetilde{g}(b,x)\cdot\widetilde{h}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b}).

(59)

hence we can define
f,g
and
h
as

f(a,b)=\begin{cases}\widehat{f}(a,b)\quad\text{if }a<b\\
\widetilde{f}(a,b)\quad\text{if }b<a\end{cases}

g(b,x)=\begin{cases}\widehat{g}(b,x)\quad\text{if }x<b\\
\widetilde{g}(b,x)\quad\text{if }b<x\end{cases}

h(x,a)=\begin{cases}\widehat{h}(x,a)\quad\text{if }a<x\\
\widetilde{g}(x,a)\quad\text{if }x<a\end{cases}

This show the first part of the lemma, we proceed with general
a,b\in I_{Z_{1},Z_{2}}
and
x\in I_{a,b}

Now we only have to show that we can choose
f
and
g
symmetric. We define
\psi(a,x)=\sqrt{g(a,x)\cdot h(x,a)}
and
\phi(a,b)=\sqrt{f(a,b)\cdot f(b,a)}
. Notice that, using the symmetry of the family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)\cdot\Lambda_{b,a}^{1}(x)}

(60)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{f(a,b)\cdot f(b,a)}\cdot\sqrt{g(a,x)\cdot h(x,a)}\cdot\sqrt{g(b,x)\cdot h(x,b)}

\displaystyle=\phi(a,b)\cdot\psi(a,x)\cdot\psi(b,x).

We now restrict
a,b
to

(z_{1},z_{2})
and claim that

\frac{\psi(y,x)}{\psi(x,y)}=\frac{c_{2}(y)\mathds{1}(x<y)+c_{1}(y)\mathds{1}(y<x)}{c_{1}(x)\mathds{1}(x<y)+c_{2}(x)\mathds{1}(y<x)}

(61)

for some functions
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
and
x\neq y
,
x,y\in(z_{1},z_{2})
. Before we prove this claim we demonstrate how we will use it. If the claim holds, then we have

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y):

\displaystyle=[c_{1}(x)\mathds{1}(x<y)+c_{2}(x)\mathds{1}(y<x)]\cdot\psi(y,x)

(62)

\displaystyle=[c_{2}(y)\mathds{1}(x<y)+c_{1}(y)\mathds{1}(y<x)]\cdot\psi(x,y)=\Psi(y,x)

for all
x,y\in(z_{1},z_{2})
with
x\neq y
. As a consequence

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=\phi(a,b)\cdot\psi(a,x)\cdot\psi(b,x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})=\phi(a,b)\cdot\Psi(b,x)\cdot\gamma(a,x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b}).

(63)

Here we define
\gamma
, for
x\in I_{a,b}
, as

\displaystyle\gamma(a,x):

\displaystyle=[c_{1}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(a<x)+c_{2}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x<a)]^{-1}\psi(a,x)

(64)

\displaystyle=[c_{1}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x<b)+c_{2}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(b<x)]^{-1}\psi(a,x).

Notice that the last equality relies on the fact that
x\in I_{a,b}
. Indeed, for
x\in I_{a,b}:

a<x\iff x<b
and
b<x\iff x<a
. We can pick
f=\phi
,
g=\Psi
and
h=\gamma
. Since
\phi
and
\Psi
are symmetric, this completes the proof.

Finally, we prove the claim (
61
).
Recall that (
60
) holds on the whole interval
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
rather than only on the subinterval
(z_{1},z_{2})
. Let
x,y\in I_{z_{1},z_{2}}
. If we have
x<y
then

\frac{\Lambda^{2,1}_{z_{1},z_{2}}(x)}{\Lambda^{2,1}_{z_{2},z_{1}}(y)}=\frac{\Lambda^{1}_{z_{1},y}(x)}{\Lambda^{1}_{z_{2},x}(y)}=\frac{\phi(z_{1},y)\cdot\psi(z_{1},x)\cdot\psi(y,x)}{\phi(z_{2},x)\cdot\psi(z_{2},y)\cdot\psi(x,y)}.

(65)

Define
C_{1,2}(x):=\frac{\psi(z_{1},x)}{\Lambda^{2,1}_{z_{1},z_{2}}(x)\cdot\phi(z_{2},x)}
and
C_{2,1}(y):=\frac{\psi(z_{2},y)}{\Lambda^{2,1}_{z_{2},z_{1}}(y)\cdot\phi(z_{1},y)}
. The equation above reads then

\frac{C_{2,1}(y)}{C_{1,2}(x)}=\frac{\psi(y,x)}{\psi(x,y)}.

(66)

Now assume
y<x
, similarly we find

\frac{C_{1,2}(y)}{C_{2,1}(x)}=\frac{\psi(y,x)}{\psi(x,y)}.

(67)

Combining these two expressions yields

\frac{\psi(y,x)}{\psi(x,y)}=\frac{C_{2,1}(y)\cdot\mathds{1}(x<y)+C_{1,2}(y)\cdot\mathds{1}(y<x)}{C_{1,2}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x<y)+C_{2,1}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}(y<x)}

(68)

We define
c_{1}=C_{1,2}
and
c_{2}=C_{2,1}
to obtain the required form. This proves (
61
).
∎

We can now state a characterization of the
\Lambda^{1}
which generate a symmetric two-sided Markov family.

Theorem 4.5
.

The following two statements are equivalent,

1.

The family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is symmetric and two-sided Markov

2.

\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
a,b\in I_{z_{1},z_{2}}
there exists a symmetric
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(x)=f(a,b)\cdot g(b,x)\cdot g(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(69)

with normalization

f(a,b)=\Bigg(\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}g(b,y)\cdot g(y,a)dy\Bigg)^{-1}.

(70)

Proof.

1 implies 2.

Lemma
4.4
states that we can write

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(x)=\widetilde{f}(a,b)\cdot\widetilde{g}(b,x)\cdot\widetilde{h}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(71)

with
\widetilde{f}
and
\widetilde{g}
symmetric. Using the symmetry of
\Lambda^{1}
, we can deduce that
\widetilde{h}
is of the form
\widetilde{h}(x,a)=c(x)\cdot\widetilde{g}(x,a)
for some function
c
. Indeed, the symmetry gives

\widetilde{f}(a,b)\cdot\widetilde{g}(b,x)\cdot\widetilde{h}(x,a)=\widetilde{f}(b,a)\cdot\widetilde{g}(a,x)\cdot\widetilde{h}(x,b)

(72)

for
x\in I_{a,b}
. Using the symmetry of
\widetilde{f}
, be obtain

\frac{\widetilde{g}(b,x)}{\widetilde{h}(x,b)}=\frac{\widetilde{g}(a,x)}{\widetilde{h}(x,a)}=:c^{-1}(x).

(73)

This then gives us
\widetilde{h}(x,b)=c(x)\cdot\widetilde{g}(b,x)
. We define
g(a,x):=\sqrt{c(x)\cdot c(a)}\cdot\widetilde{g}(a,x)
and
f(a,b):=\widetilde{f}(a,b)/\sqrt{c(a)\cdot c(b)}
. Notice that
f
and
g
are symmetric. We can now to write
\Lambda^{1}
in the form

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(x)=f(a,b)\cdot g(a,x)\cdot g(x,b).

(74)

Since
\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}
is a probability density,
f
must indeed have the form (
70
).

2 implies 1.

We define the family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
recursively. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and assume that for some
m\geq 1
, the first marginals
\Lambda^{1,1},...,\Lambda^{m,1}
have the form

\Lambda_{a,b}^{k,1}(x)=f_{k}(a,b)\cdot g_{k}(b,x)\cdot h_{k}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(75)

for
a,b\in(z_{1},z_{2})
, and
1\leq k\leq m
with
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
symmetric. Notice that this is true for
\Lambda^{1}
with
f_{1}(a,b):=f(a,b)
,
g_{1}(b,x):=g(b,x)
and
h_{1}(x,a)=g(x,a)
. We define
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}
and
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}
as

\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}(x)=f_{m+1}(a,b)\cdot[f_{m}(b,x)]^{-1}\cdot h_{m}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(76)

and

\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}(x)=\Lambda_{b,a}^{m+1,1}(x)=f_{m+1}(b,a)\cdot[f_{m}(a,x)]^{-1}\cdot h_{m}(x,b)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(77)

with
f_{m+1}
the normalization

f_{m+1}(a,b)=\Bigg(\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}[f_{m}(b,x)]^{-1}\cdot h_{m}(x,a)dx\Bigg)^{-1}.

(78)

We show that
f_{m+1}
is symmetric. Assume
a\leq b
. Then, using the recursion,

\displaystyle[f_{m+1}(a,b)]^{-1}

(79)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}g(a,x_{1})\int_{x_{1}}^{b}g(x_{1},x_{2})...\int_{x_{m}}^{b}g(x_{m+1},b)dx_{m+1}...dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{x_{1}}^{b}...\int_{x_{m}}^{b}g(a,x_{1})\cdot g(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot...\cdot g(x_{m+1},b)dx_{m+1}...dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}...\int_{a}^{b}\mathds{1}(a\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq...\leq x_{m+1}\leq b)\prod_{i=0}^{n}g(x_{i},x_{i+1})dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{m+1}

and

\displaystyle[f_{m+1}(b,a)]^{-1}

(80)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}g(b,x_{m+1})\int_{a}^{x_{m+1}}g(x_{m+1},x_{m})...\int_{a}^{x_{2}}\cdot g(x_{1},a)dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{x_{m+1}}...\int_{a}^{x_{2}}g(b,x_{m+1})\cdot g(x_{m+1},x_{m})\cdot...\cdot g(x_{1},1)dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}...\int_{a}^{b}\mathds{1}(a\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq...\leq x_{m+1}\leq b)\prod_{i=0}^{n}g(x_{i},x_{i+1})dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{m+1}

where it is understood that
x_{0}=a
and
x_{m+2}=b
. This immediately gives the symmetry of
f_{m+1}
. We proceed to show that
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}
and
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}
indeed yield a density
\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1}
which is two sided Markov. For arbitrary
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we calculate

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot

\displaystyle\Lambda^{m}_{\theta_{1},b}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m+1})=\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})\cdot\Lambda_{\theta_{1},b}^{m,m}(\theta_{m+1})

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(a,b)\cdot[f_{m}(b,\theta_{1})]^{-1}\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{1},a)\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle f_{m}(b,\theta_{1})\cdot g_{m}(\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1})\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{m+1,b})\cdot\mathds{1}(\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b},\theta_{m+1}\in I_{\theta_{1},b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(a,b)\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{1},a)\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle g_{m}(\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1})\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{m+1},b)\cdot\mathds{1}(\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b},\theta_{m+1}\in I_{\theta_{1},b})

and

\displaystyle\Lambda^{m}_{a,\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m})\cdot

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}(\theta_{m+1})=\Lambda_{a,\theta_{m+1}}^{m,1}(\theta_{1})\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})\cdot\Lambda_{a,b}^{m+1,m+1}(\theta_{m+1})

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{m}(a,\theta_{m+1})\cdot g_{m}(\theta_{m+1},\theta_{1})\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{1},a)\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(b,a)\cdot[f_{m}(a,\theta_{m+1})]^{-1}\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{m+1},b)\cdot\mathds{1}(\theta_{1}\in I_{a,\theta_{m+1}},\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(a,b)\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{1},a)\cdot\Lambda^{m-1}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1}}(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle g_{m}(\theta_{1},\theta_{m+1})\cdot h_{m}(\theta_{m+1},b)\cdot\mathds{1}(\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b},\theta_{m+1}\in I_{\theta_{1},b}).

Observe that these two are equal. As is explained in remark
2.5
, one can use proposition
2.4
to construct a
\Lambda^{m+1}
for which the two-sided Markov property holds. Since
\Lambda^{1}
is clearly symmetric, the whole family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is symmetric as is shown in proposition
2.7
. This concludes the proof.

∎

We explicitly state the recursion from the proof of theorem
4.5
. This is done in the definition below, alongside the introduction of some terminology.

Definition 4.6
.

\Lambda^{1}
is called a generating density if it is of the form in (
69
) for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
z_{1}<z_{2}
and
a,b\in(z_{1},z_{2})
. The unique symmetric two-sided Markov family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with first marginals

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(x)=f_{n}(a,b)\cdot g_{n}(b,x)\cdot h_{n}(x,a)\cdot\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b})

(83)

is called the generated family. The functions
f_{n},g_{n}
and
h_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
are defined via the recursion

\begin{cases}f_{n+1}(a,b)=\big(\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}[f_{n}(b,x)]^{-1}\cdot h_{1}(x,a)dx\big)^{-1}\\[8.61108pt]
g_{n+1}(b,x)=[f_{n}(a,x)]^{-1}\\[8.61108pt]
h_{n+1}(x,a)=h(x,a)=g(x,a)\end{cases}

(84)

with
f_{1}=f
and
g_{1}=g
. For fixed
z_{1},z_{2}
we call
(f_{n},g_{n},h_{n})
the
n
-th order factors generated by
\Lambda^{1}
. The functions
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
are symmetric for all
n\geq 1
, as is shown in the proof of theorem
4.5
.

Remark 4.7
.

Notice that in the definition above, the
n
-th order factors are all constructed from just one function. Indeed, given only
g_{1}
, we know
h_{1}
and
f_{1}
since
h_{1}
is simply defined to be equal to
g_{1}
and
f_{1}(a,b)
is the normalization of
g_{1}(b,x)\cdot h_{1}(x,a)
. The recursive construction gives the higher order factors.

Example 4.8
.

We verify that the density of the order statistics are indeed recursively generated according to (
84
). (
8
) evaluated in
i=1
yields

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(x)=n\cdot|b-a|^{-n}|b-x|^{n-1}\mathds{1}(x\in I_{a,b}).

(85)

This expression is clearly factorizable for
x\in I_{a,b}
, we can take

f_{n}(a,b)=n!|b-a|^{-n},\quad g_{n}(b,x)=\frac{|b-x|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!},\quad h_{n}(x,a)=1.

(86)

We immediately see that
f_{1}(a,b)=|b-a|^{-1}
and
g_{1}(b,x)=1
are symmetric, hence
\Lambda^{1}
is indeed generating. Moreover
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
are symmetric and satisfy the recursion,

f_{n+1}^{-1}(a,b)=\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}h_{1}(x,a)\cdot f^{-1}_{n}(b,x)dx=\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}\frac{|b-x|^{n}}{n!}dx=\frac{|b-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}

(87)

and

g_{n+1}(b,x)=[f_{n}(b,x)]^{-1}=\frac{|b-x|^{n}}{n!}.

(88)

We conclude that
f_{n},g_{n}
and
h_{n}
are indeed the
n
-th order factors generated by
\Lambda^{1}
.

Example 4.9

(gapped order statistics)
.

In example
4.8
we reconstructed the joint density corresponding to the order statistics of uniform random variables. In this case we had
g_{1}(a,b)=1
, defining the whole family. Since
g_{1}
is indeed symmetric strictly positive, we could have directly seen that the order statistics are symmetric and two-sided Markov. In fact we recover related families if we take
g_{1}(a,b)=[(s-1)!]^{-1}\cdot|b-a|^{s-1}
for any integer
s\geq 1
. For this choice of
g_{1}
we obtain the so-called
gapped

n\geq 1
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we define
N=s(n+1)-1
and consider
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{N}

\sim U(a\wedge b,a\vee b)
. Then
\Theta_{1}:=X_{s:N},\Theta_{2}:=X_{2s:N},...,\Theta_{n}:=X_{ns:N}
are the gapped order statistics with gap size
s
. For
a<b
, their joint densities and first marginals are known
[
15

to be

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\frac{N!}{[(s-1)!]^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{|b-a|^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{i-1})^{s-1}\cdot\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{n}<b)

(89)

and

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n,1}(\theta)=\frac{(s(n+1)-1)!}{(s-1)!(sn-1)!}\cdot\frac{1}{|b-a|^{s(n+1)-1}}\cdot|b-\theta|^{sn-1}\cdot|b-\theta|^{s-1}\cdot|\theta-a|^{s-1}\mathds{1}(\theta\in I_{a,b})

(90)

where it is understood that
\theta_{0}=a
and
\theta_{n+1}=b
. Akin to the density for
s=1
in example
2.3
, the indicator is replaced by
\mathds{1}(b<\theta_{n}<...<\theta_{1}<a)
in the case where
b<a
. One can check that this family is symmetric and two-sided Markov by directly verifying definition
2.1
. We demonstrate the recursion in (
84
). Notice that
\Lambda^{n,1}_{a,b}
is indeed factorizable for

f_{n}(a,b)=\frac{(s(n+1)-1)!}{|b-a|^{s(n+1)-1}},\quad g_{n}(b,\theta)=\frac{|b-\theta|^{sn-1}}{(sn-1)!},\quad h_{n}(\theta,a)=\frac{|\theta-a|^{s-1}}{(s-1)!}.

(91)

Take
n=1
. We can immediately see that
f_{1},g_{1}
and
h_{1}
define a generating density.
f_{n}
also follows from the recursion since
g_{n}=f_{n-1}^{-1}
.

Example 4.10

(Dirichlet processes)

Notice that we can in fact generalize the example above by taking
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

f_{n}(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(s(n+1)))}{|b-a|^{s(n+1)-1}},\quad g_{n}(b,\theta)=\frac{|b-\theta|^{sn-1}}{\Gamma(sn)},\quad h_{n}(\theta,a)=\frac{|\theta-a|^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s)}.

\Lambda_{a,b}^{n}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\frac{\Gamma(s(n+1))}{[\Gamma(s)]^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{|b-a|^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{i-1})^{s-1}\cdot\mathds{1}(a<\theta_{1}<...<\theta_{n}<b)

5
Characterizing properties of Dirichlet densities

In this section we find characterizing properties for
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
corresponding to an ordered Dirichlet distribution, i.e., the family given in (
93
). In theorem
4.5
, we provided a characterization of those
\Lambda^{1}
which are part of a two-sided symmetric Markov family

\{\Lambda^{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Due to this characterization, it is easy to generate a large class of symmetric two-sided families. Indeed, we can perform the construction in definition
4.6
for an arbitrary integrable and symmetric function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. However, we will prove that under natural assumptions such as scaling and shift invariance (detailed below), the only two-sided Markov families are the Dirichlet processes of example
4.10
.

Definition 5.1
.

The family
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is

1.

Scale invariant
if for all
a,b,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
n\geq 1
and
\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b},\theta_{2}\in I_{\theta_{1},b},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{\theta_{n-1},b}
,

\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\gamma^{n}\cdot\Lambda_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}^{n}(\gamma\cdot\theta_{1},...,\gamma\cdot\theta_{n}).

(94)

2.

Shift invariant
if, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\gamma>-(a\wedge b)
,
n\geq 1
and
\theta_{1}\in I_{a,b},\theta_{2}\in I_{\theta_{1},b},...,\theta_{n}\in I_{\theta_{n-1},b}

\Lambda^{n}_{a,b}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})=\Lambda_{a+\gamma,b+\gamma}^{n}(\theta_{1}+\gamma,...,\theta_{n}+\gamma).

(95)

We start with a useful lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous symmetric function which satisfies the following scale and shift properties

\begin{cases}\phi(\gamma\cdot x,\gamma\cdot y)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)\cdot e^{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y)\\
\phi(x+\gamma,y+\gamma)=\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)\cdot e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y)\end{cases}

(96)

for some continuous
functions
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\widetilde{\lambda}_{2},\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\widehat{\lambda}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then
\phi
is of the form

\phi(x,y)=t\cdot|x-y|^{\sigma}

(97)

with
\sigma,t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)=\gamma^{\sigma}
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}=1
and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}=\widehat{\lambda}_{2}=0
.

Proof.

First we will show that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(a)=a^{\sigma}
and

\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)=u(a-1)
.

We have the following restriction on
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}
. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(a)\cdot\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(b)\cdot e^{-[\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)\cdot b+\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)]\cdot|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y)=\phi(a\cdot b\cdot x,a\cdot b\cdot y)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a\cdot b)\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a\cdot b)|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y).

(98)

Since this expression must hold for all
x
and
y
, we have the two equations for
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}
,

\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(a\cdot b)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(a)\cdot\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(b)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)\cdot b+\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a\cdot b).

(99)

This shows that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
is of the form
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)=\gamma^{\sigma}
, for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. The second equation tells us that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)=u\cdot(\gamma-1)
for
u\in\mathbb{R}
. To see this, use (
99
) to write

\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)\cdot b+\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a\cdot b)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)\cdot a+\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a).

(100)

Assume that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)\cdot\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)\neq 0
. We divide by
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)\cdot\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)
and separate the equation in
a
and
b
,

u^{-1}:=\frac{a}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(a)}=\frac{b}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(b)}.

(101)

This implies that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}
is of the form
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)=u\cdot(\gamma-1)
or
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)=0
. Since
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)
is continuous, we have
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)=u\cdot(\gamma-1)
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}
, which might be zero. We can perform a similar procedure as we did above for the shift property. That is, we start from the requirement

\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(a)\cdot\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(b)\cdot e^{-[\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(a)+\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(b)]\cdot|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y)=\phi(a+b+x,a+b+y)=\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(a+b)\cdot e^{-\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(a+b)|x-y|}\cdot\phi(x,y).

(102)

Again, we can argue that this equation should hold for all
x
and
y
to obtain

\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(a+b)=\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(a)\cdot\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(b)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(a)+\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(b)=\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(a+b)

(103)

for arbitrary
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The first equation shows that
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)=\exp(v\cdot\gamma)
,
v\in\mathbb{R}
while the second equation gives
\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)=w\cdot\gamma
,
w\in\mathbb{R}
. Now that we know the general form of
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\widetilde{\lambda}_{2},\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{2}
, we find an explicit form for
\phi
and argue that
u,v,w=0
Assume
y>x
,then

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(x)\cdot e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(x)|y-x|}\cdot\phi(0,y-x)

(104)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\lambda}_{1}(x)\cdot e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{2}(x)|y-x|}\cdot\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(y-x)\cdot e^{\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(y-x)|y-x|}\cdot\phi(0,1)

\displaystyle=t\cdot e^{v\cdot x}\cdot e^{w\cdot x(y-x)}\cdot(y-x)^{\sigma}\cdot e^{u(y-x-1)(y-x)},

where
t:=\phi(0,1)
.
We will then show that
u=v=w=0
.

By the scaling property,

\displaystyle\phi(\gamma\cdot x,\gamma\cdot y)

(105)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\cdot\gamma^{\sigma}\cdot(y-x)^{\sigma}\cdot\exp{[\gamma(vx-u(y-x))+\gamma^{2}(wx(y-x)+u(y-x)^{2})]}

and

\displaystyle\phi(\gamma\cdot x,\gamma\cdot y)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(\gamma)\cdot\exp{[\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\gamma)(y-x)]}\cdot\phi(x,y)

(106)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\sigma}\cdot\exp{[u(\gamma-1)\cdot(y-x)]}\cdot\phi(x,y)

\displaystyle=t\cdot\gamma^{\sigma}\cdot\exp{[u(\gamma-1)\cdot(y-x)]}\cdot(y-x)^{\sigma}\cdot\exp[(vx-u(y-x))+(wx(y-x)+u(y-x)^{2})].

These two expressions should be equal for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, which is only possible if
u,v
and
w
are zero. In that case we find
\phi(x,y)=t\cdot(y-x)^{\sigma}
. Clearly this expression satisfies the shift property. Due to the symmetry assumption on
\phi
we have, for general
x,y
, we finally obtain

\phi(x,y)=t\cdot|y-x|^{\sigma}.

(107)

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric, two-sided Markov family, i.e.,
\Lambda^{1}
is of the form (
69
)

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f(a,b)\cdot g(b,x)\cdot g(a,x)

(108)

with

f(a,b)^{-1}=\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}g(a,x)\cdot g(b,x)dx

(109)

for some symmetric function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
. We assume
g(x,.)
to be differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\{x\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

If
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is both scale and shift invariant, then, for some
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f(a,b)\cdot(t\cdot|b-x|^{s-1})\cdot(t\cdot|x-a|^{s-1}).

(110)

That is, we recover the density corresponding to the Dirichlet process with parameter
s
from example
4.10
.

Proof.

Notice that for
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in I_{a,b}

\frac{\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(\theta_{2})}=\frac{\Lambda^{1}_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}(\gamma\cdot\theta_{1})}{\Lambda^{1}_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}(\gamma\cdot\theta_{2})}.

(111)

Bringing all the factors containing
\theta_{1}
to the left hand side and all the terms containing
\theta_{2}
to the right hand side shows that for any
\theta\in I_{a,b}
,

\frac{g(a,\theta)\cdot g(b,\theta)}{g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)\cdot g(\gamma\cdot b,\gamma\cdot\theta_{1})}=\frac{\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(\theta)}{\Lambda^{1}_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}(\gamma\cdot\theta)}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}(a,b,\gamma).

(112)

Define
\widetilde{g}(a,\theta):=\log[g(a,\theta)/g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)]
. We differentiate with respect to
\theta
and obtain

\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)+\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(b,\theta)=0,

(113)

where
\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}
denotes the derivative of
\widetilde{g}
in the second entry. As a consequence

\begin{cases}\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)=-\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(b,\theta)\quad&\text{for }\theta\in I_{a,b}\\
\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)=\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(b,\theta)\quad&\text{for }\theta\notin I_{a,b}.\end{cases}

(114)

This is easily seen as follows. Consider the case
a,b<\theta
and let
c>\theta
. Then
\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)=-\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(c,\theta)
and
\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(b,\theta)=-\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(c,\theta)
, so we indeed have
\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)=\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(b,\theta)
. A similar argument can be made for the case where
a,b>\theta
. We can now directly infer that

\widetilde{g}^{(0,1)}(a,\theta)=\begin{cases}-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\quad&\text{for }\theta<a\\
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\quad&\text{for }\theta>a.\end{cases}

(115)

As a consequence,

\widetilde{g}(a,\theta)=\begin{cases}-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta+\widetilde{\kappa}_{3}(a,\gamma)\quad&\text{for }\theta<a\\
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta+\widetilde{\kappa}_{4}(a,\gamma)\quad&\text{for }\theta>a.\end{cases}

(116)

We now use the explicit form of
\widetilde{g}
to find a scaling property for
g
. If
a<\theta
, then

\log\frac{g(a,\theta)}{g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{4}(a,\gamma)+\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta.

(117)

Likewise, if
\theta<a
, then

\log\frac{g(a,\theta)}{g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{3}(a,\gamma)-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta.

(118)

For now we assume
a<\theta
. Using the symmetry of
g
we obtain the following relation

\widetilde{\kappa}_{3}(a,\gamma)-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta=\widetilde{\kappa}_{4}(\theta,\gamma)+\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot a.

(119)

We do separation of variables in
a
and
\theta
. This yields the following expression which is independent of
\theta
and
a

\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma):=\widetilde{\kappa}_{3}(a,\gamma)-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot a=\widetilde{\kappa}_{4}(\theta,\gamma)+\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot\theta.

(120)

We rewrite
\log g(a,\theta)/g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)
as

\log\frac{g(a,\theta)}{g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)+\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)(\theta-a).

(121)

Under the assumption
\theta<a
we find

\log\frac{g(a,\theta)}{g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)+\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)(a-\theta).

(122)

Now we have the following relation between
g(a,\theta)
and
g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)

g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)=e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)}\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot g(a,\theta)

(123)

Notice this scaling property for
g
is derived from just the scale invariance. Moreover, one can mutatis mutandis repeat the procedure above to derive a shift property. This leaves us with the following expressions for scaling and shifts

\begin{cases}g(a+\gamma,\theta+\gamma)=e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)}\cdot e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot g(a,\theta)\\
g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)=e^{-\kappa_{5}(\gamma)}\cdot e^{-\kappa_{2}(\gamma)\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot g(a,\theta)\end{cases}.

(124)

Here
\widehat{\kappa}_{5}
and
\widehat{\kappa}_{2}
are some continuous function of
\gamma
analogous to
\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}
. By virtue of lemma
5.2
, we can conclude the proof.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

A close inspection of the proofs of lemma
5.2
and theorem
5.3
reveals that it is not enough to only assume one of the properties in
5.1
. That is, if one only assumes either shift or scale invariance, then there is a much wider class of symmetric two-sided Markov families. To illustrate this, we recover the specific form of the generating densities which give rise to scale, respectively shift, invariance.

Scale invariance:

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric two-sided Markov family as introduced in the statement of theorem
5.3
which only satisfies scale invariance. The proof of the theorem then shows that
g
satisfies (
123
) i.e.,

g(\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot\theta)=e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)}\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot g(a,\theta)

(125)

for some continuous functions
\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}
. The proof of lemma
5.2
gives candidates for the form of these two functions. Indeed, (
99
) gives
\exp{\{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}}(\gamma)\}=\gamma^{s}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)=u\cdot(\gamma-1)
for some
s,u\in\mathbb{R}
. Now we define
\varphi(x):=g(1,x)=g(x,1)
, for
x\in(0,1]
and find

g(a,\theta)=e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{5}(a\vee\theta)}\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(a\vee\theta)\cdot|1-\frac{a\wedge\theta}{a\vee\theta}|}\cdot g\Big(\frac{a}{a\vee\theta},\frac{\theta}{a\vee\theta}\Big)=(a\vee\theta)^{s}\cdot e^{u(a\vee\theta-1)\cdot|1-\frac{a\wedge\theta}{a\vee\theta}|}\cdot\varphi\Big(\frac{a\wedge\theta}{a\vee\theta}\Big)

(126)

This yields the following generating density

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(\theta)=f(a,b)\cdot\theta^{s}\cdot e^{u(\frac{a\wedge b}{\theta}+\frac{\theta}{a\vee b})}\cdot\varphi\Big(\frac{a\wedge b}{\theta}\Big)\varphi\Big(\frac{\theta}{a\vee b}\Big)\cdot\mathds{1}(\theta\in I_{a,b}).

(127)

Here,
f(a,b)
is the normalization of
\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}
. It is straightforward to check that this density is indeed scale invariant.

Shift invariance:

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric two-sided Markov family as introduced in the statement of theorem
5.3
which only satisfies shift invariance. As was mentioned in the proof of theorem
5.3
,
g
also obeys a shift property of a form similar to(
123
). That is,

g(a+\gamma,\theta+\gamma)=e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)}\cdot e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot g(a,\theta).

(128)

(
103
) in the proof of lemma
5.2
yields that
\widehat{\kappa}_{5}(\gamma)=-v\cdot\gamma
and
\widehat{\kappa}_{2}(\gamma)=-w\cdot\gamma
, where
v,w\in\mathbb{R}
. Define
\varphi(x):=g(0,x)=g(x,0)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then

g(a,\theta)=e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{5}(a\wedge\theta)}\cdot e^{-\widehat{\kappa}_{2}(a\wedge\theta)}g(a-a\wedge\theta,\theta-a\wedge\theta)=e^{v\cdot a\wedge\theta}\cdot e^{w\cdot a\wedge\theta\cdot|a-\theta|}\cdot\varphi(|\theta-a|).

(129)

This gives the following generating density

\Lambda^{1}_{a,b}(\theta)=f(a,b)\cdot e^{v\theta+w(a+b)\theta-w\theta^{2}}\cdot\varphi(|\theta-a|)\varphi(|\theta-b|).

(130)

A short calculation reveals that
\Lambda^{1}
is indeed shift invariant.

5.1
Densities depending on distances

In this subsection we discuss a special case of shift and scale invariant densities where the factor
g_{1}(b,x)
is of the form
g_{1}(b,x)=\Phi(|b-x|)
for some differentiable function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Notice that one automatically has shift invariance for densities of this form, whereas the scale invariance imposes an extra restriction on
\Phi
. We provide then a simpler proof to show that such
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
correspond to the Dirichlet processes of example
4.10
. We start with
the a lemma which is a direct consequence of theorem
4.5
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be integrable. Suppose
\Lambda^{1}
is of the form

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f_{1}(a,b)\cdot\Phi(|b-x|)\cdot\Phi(|x-a|)

(131)

with

f_{1}(a,b)^{-1}=\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}\Phi(|b-x|)\cdot\Phi(|x-a|)dx

(132)

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then there exists a symmetric, two-sided Markov family
\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{1}=\Lambda^{1}
.

Proof.

Clearly
\Lambda^{1}(a,b)
is symmetric and factorizable for
f_{1}
,
g_{1}
and
h_{1}
, where the latter two are given by

g_{1}(x,y)=h_{1}(x,y)=\Phi(|x-y|)

(133)

and
f_{1}
is as defined in the statement of the lemma. Theorem
4.5
yields the existence of
\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a symmetric, two-sided Markov family where
\Lambda^{1}
is of the form

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f_{1}(a,b)\cdot\Phi(|b-x|)\cdot\Phi(|x-a|)

(134)

with

f_{1}(a,b)^{-1}=\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}\Phi(|b-x|)\cdot\Phi(|x-a|)dx

(135)

for some differentiable function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Moreover, assume that
\{\Lambda^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is scale-invariant. Then, for some
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f_{1}(a,b)\cdot(t\cdot|b-x|^{s})\cdot(t\cdot|x-a|^{s}).

(136)

Proof.

We start from the following observation, for
a\leq\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\leq b
,

\frac{\Phi(\theta_{1}-a)\cdot\Phi(b-\theta_{1})}{\Phi(\theta_{2}-a)\cdot\Phi(b-\theta_{2})}=\frac{\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(\theta_{2})}=\frac{\Lambda_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}^{1}(\gamma\cdot\theta_{1})}{\Lambda_{\gamma\cdot a,\gamma\cdot b}^{1}(\gamma\cdot\theta_{2})}=\frac{\Phi(\gamma\cdot(\theta_{1}-a))\cdot\Phi(\gamma\cdot(b-\theta_{1}))}{\Phi(\gamma\cdot(\theta_{2}-a))\cdot\Phi(\gamma\cdot(b-\theta_{2}))}.

(137)

For the second equality we use the scale invariance. We bring the
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
dependent factors to the left- and right hand side respectively. This yields

\frac{\Phi(\theta_{1}-a)\cdot\Phi(b-\theta_{1})}{\Phi(\gamma\cdot(\theta_{1}-a))\cdot\Phi(\gamma\cdot(b-\theta_{1}))}=\frac{\Phi(\theta_{2}-a)\cdot\Phi(b-\theta_{2})}{\Phi(\gamma\cdot(\theta_{2}-a))\cdot\Phi(\gamma\cdot(b-\theta_{2}))}=c_{a,b}(\gamma).

(138)

Here
c_{a,b}(\gamma)
is a constant which depends only on
a,b
and
\gamma
. We introduce a new function
\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
as
\widetilde{\Phi}(x)=\log(\Phi(x)/\Phi(\gamma\cdot x))
. The equation above can be rewritten as

\widetilde{\Phi}(\theta-a)+\widetilde{\Phi}(b-\theta)=\log(c_{a,b}(\gamma)).

(139)

Differentiation with respect to
\theta
yields

\widetilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(\theta-a)-\widetilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(b-\theta)=0.

(140)

Given arbitrary
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we can find
a,b
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
a\leq\theta\leq b
and
x=\theta-a
,
y=b-\theta
. We find that
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(x)=\widetilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(y)
. Since
x
and
y
are arbitrary, this means that
\Phi^{\prime}
is constant and hence that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is of the form

\widetilde{\Phi}(x)=\alpha(\gamma)\cdot x+\beta(\gamma)

(141)

which gives

\Phi(\gamma\cdot x)=e^{-\alpha(\gamma)\cdot x-\beta(\gamma)}\Phi(x).

(142)

In what follows we absorb the minuses in
\alpha(\gamma)
and
\beta(\gamma)
. Next we differentiate with respect to
\gamma
, this gives

[\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma)\cdot x+\beta^{\prime}(\gamma)]\cdot\Phi(\gamma\cdot x)=x\cdot\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma\cdot x)

(143)

or, substituting
x/\gamma
for
x
,

[\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma)\cdot x+\gamma\cdot\beta^{\prime}(\gamma)]\cdot\Phi(x)=x\cdot\Phi^{\prime}(x).

(144)

The expression above is an ODE which has solution

\Phi(x)=k\cdot e^{\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma)\cdot x}\cdot x^{\gamma\cdot\beta^{\prime}(\gamma)},

(145)

with
k\in\mathbb{R}
an arbitrary constant. Since
\Phi
can not depend on
\gamma
, we must have that
\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma)=\alpha
and
\gamma\cdot\beta^{\prime}(\gamma)
are constants. Hence we must have the following form for
\Phi
,

\Phi(x)=k\cdot e^{\alpha\cdot x}\cdot x^{\beta}.

(146)

It is straightforward to check that for a function
\Phi
of this form the whole generated family satisfies the homogeneity condition. One should notice that we can set
\alpha=0
because

\Lambda_{a,b}^{1}(x)=f_{1}(a,b)\cdot\Phi(|x-a|)\cdot\Phi(|b-x|)=f_{1}(a,b)\cdot k^{2}\cdot|x-a|^{\beta}\cdot|b-x|^{\beta}

(147)

∎
