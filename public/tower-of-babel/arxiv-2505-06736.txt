Supersonic Flow Past an Obstacle in a Quasi-Two-Dimensional Lee–Huang–Yang Quantum Fluid
G. H. dos Santos, L. F. Calazans de Brito, A. Gammal, A. M. Kamchatnov

Abstract
A supersonic flow past an obstacle can generate a rich variety of wave excitations. This paper investigates, both analytically and numerically, two types of excitations generated by the flow of a Lee–Huang–Yang quantum fluid past an obstacle: linear radiation and oblique dark solitons. We show that wave crests of linear radiation can be accurately described by the proper modification of the Kelvin original theory, while the oblique dark soliton solution is obtained analytically by transformation of the 1D soliton solution to the obstacle’s reference frame. A comparison between analytical predictions and numerical simulations demonstrates good agreement, validating our theoretical approach.

I
Introduction

The study of Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) has attracted a significant scientific interest due to its quantum fluid behavior. In the condensate of weakly interacting atoms, most problems can be described within the mean-field approximation by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation (GPE)
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
. However, when the mean-field interspecies attraction in Bose-Bose mixtures exceeds the average repulsive forces, the GPE predicts a collapse of the system. In the case of quantum droplets, this issue can be resolved by incorporating quantum fluctuation effects, leading to a correction of the GPE with a quartic nonlinear term—the Lee-Huang-Yang (LHY) correction—thus opening the study of the beyond-mean-field effects
Lee et al. 1957
;
Petrov 2015
(see also
Lavoine et al. 2021
;
Bulgac 2002
).

By manipulating the interaction strength and the number of atoms, it is possible to create a carefully designed mixture of a two-component BEC in which the cubic mean-field term vanishes, allowing quantum fluctuations to be studied through the quartic nonlinear term
Jörgensen et al. 2018
. This leads to the LHY equation in the form

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\Psi+U_{ext}\Psi+g_{LHY}|\Psi|^{3}\Psi,

(1)

where
U_{ext}
represents the external potential, which can account for effects such as traps and obstacles, among others. The parameters
g_{LHY}
and
m
denote the three-dimensional inter-particle interaction constant and the atomic mass, respectively. The quantum fluid governed by the LHY equation exhibits both nonlinear and dispersive effects. These properties enable the propagation of various atomic excitations, including solitons, vortices, dispersive shock waves, and linear radiation. As in the case of the GPE, a supersonic flow past an obstacle in the LHY fluid is expected to generate stationary coherent wave patterns satisfying the Mach-Cherenkov-Landau resonant radiation condition. These wakes are referred to as Kelvin-like wakes due to their similarity to Lord Kelvin’s water wave structure generated by a moving ship (commonly known as the Kelvin wake pattern)
Kelvin 1887
;
Whitham 1974
;
Johnson 1997
;
Kolomeisky et al. 2022
. More specifically, for supersonic flow velocities, the GPE predicts two distinct wave structures: oblique dark solitons inside the Mach cone and linear radiation outside it. In the case of the GPE, these structures have been extensively studied, experimentally
Cornell 2005
;
Amo et al. 2011
and through theoretical investigations, particularly regarding linear radiation
Gladush et al. 2007
;
Khamis and Gammal 2013
and oblique dark soliton wakes
Kamchatnov and Salerno 2009
;
El et al. 2006
. However, for Eq. (
1
),
this problem remains unsolved.

In this work, we develop an analytical framework for the investigation of a supersonic flow past an obstacle in a quasi-2D quantum fluid described by Eq. (
1
). We restrict our analysis to flow velocities that support the propagation of two distinct wake patterns: oblique dark solitons and linear radiation. The paper is structured as follows: Section II presents the analytical theory of linear radiation outside the Mach cone. Section III focuses on oblique solitons inside the Mach cone. In Section IV, we compare analytical predictions with numerical simulations. Finally, we conclude with a discussion in Section V.

II
Dimensional Reduction and the Quasi-2D Hydrodynamical Model

We will consider the model of quasi-2D BEC tightly confined in the
z
-direction by the external potential, so that the condensate cloud acquires a “pancake” configuration. This means the harmonic potential corresponds to much greater frequency
\omega_{z}
in
z
of atomic motion direction than the frequency
\omega_{\perp}
of oscillations in the “pancake” plane,
\omega_{z}/\omega_{\perp}\gg 1
. Consequently, the atoms condense into the ground state
\phi(z)
of the motions in the
z
-direction for which the weak interatomic interaction can be neglected, whereas the atomic motion in the
(x,y)
-plane is governed by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation with account of the interatomic interaction. If number of atoms
N
in the condensate is large enough, so that the radius
R_{0}
of the pancake-shape density distribution is much greater than the characteristic harmonic length
a_{z}
in the axial direction, then we can factorize the 3D condensate wave function in the following way,

\Psi(x,y,z,t)=\Psi(x,y,t)\,\phi(z),

(2)

where

\phi(z)=\frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}a_{z}^{1/4}}e^{-\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2a_{z}}\right)}

(3)

is the ground-state wave function in the axial direction. Substitution of this expression into Eq. (
1
) yields after integration over the
z
-direction the quasi-2D LHY quantum fluid equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}+U_{\perp}+g_{LHY}^{\prime}|\Psi|^{3}\right)\Psi(\mathbf{r},t),

(4)

where we keep the notation
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
for the effective 2D condensate wave function with
\mathbf{r}=(x,y)
. In particular, the number of atoms can be estimated as
N\approx|\Psi|^{2}\pi R_{0}^{2}
.
U_{\perp}
denotes the potential acting in the
(x,y)
-plane, for our purpose it is convenient to take
U_{\perp}=0
, and
g_{LHY}^{\prime}
is the renormalized LHY interaction constant, which is defined as

g^{\prime}_{\text{LHY}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{5\pi^{3/2}a_{z}^{3}}}g_{\text{LHY}}.

(5)

Making the transformation

\mathbf{r}\rightarrow\xi_{0}\mathbf{r},\quad t\rightarrow\tau t,\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi\rightarrow\Psi_{0}\Psi

(6)

where
\xi_{0}=\hbar/\sqrt{m|\Psi_{0}|^{3}g^{\prime}_{LHY}}
,
\tau=\hbar/|\Psi_{0}|^{3}g^{\prime}_{LHY}
and
\Psi_{0}=1/|\Psi_{0}|^{3}g^{\prime}_{LHY}
, we transform Eq. (
4
) to non-dimensional variables. As a result, we arrive at the equation

i\frac{\partial\Psi(x,y,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\Psi(x,y,t)+|\Psi|^{3}\Psi(x,y,t).

(7)

To obtain basic properties of a uniform BEC we make the so-called Madelung transformation,

\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)=\sqrt{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}e^{i\int\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}}.

(8)

Then Eq. (
8
) transforms to the Euler-like hydrodynamic equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\rho}\nabla\rho=\nabla\left[\frac{\nabla^{2}\rho}{4\rho}-\frac{(\nabla\rho)^{2}}{8\rho^{2}}\right],

(9)

where, in the context of the Bose gas theory,
\rho
and
\mathbf{u}
represent the density and velocity fields of the hydrodynamic system, respectively. These equations admit a simple solution
\rho=\rho_{0}=\mathrm{const}
,
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathrm{const}
which represents the flow of BEC with a uniform density and constant velocity. We are interested in wave patterns generated by such a flow past an obstacle which can be modelled by an additional potential
U_{\perp}
localized around the origin
(x=0,y=0)
of our coordinate system.

III
Linear radiation in LHY fluid flowing past an obstacle

At first, we have to find the dispersion relation for linear waves propagating along the uniform BEC with
\rho=\rho_{0}=\mathrm{const}
,
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathrm{const}
. To perform the linearization, we separate the smooth background parameters from an oscillatory perturbation, that is, we assume

\mathbf{u}=(u_{0}+u^{\prime},v^{\prime}),\quad\rho=\rho_{0}+\rho^{\prime},

(10)

where
u^{\prime}
,
v^{\prime}
,
\rho^{\prime}
denote small perturbations and we take the background flow directed along
x
-axis,
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},0)
. In the first order approximation with respect to small variables, we get from the system (
II
) the linearized system:

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial t}+u_{0}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\rho_{0}\left(\frac{\partial u^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v^{\prime}}{\partial y}\right)=0,\\
&\frac{\partial u^{\prime}}{\partial t}+u_{0}\frac{\partial u^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x}=\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial^{3}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x^{3}}+\frac{\partial^{3}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial x\partial y^{2}}\right),\\
&\frac{\partial v^{\prime}}{\partial t}+u_{0}\frac{\partial v^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial\rho^{\prime}}{\partial y}=\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial^{3}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial y^{3}}+\frac{\partial^{3}\rho^{\prime}}{\partial y\partial x^{2}}\right).\\
\end{split}

(11)

The dispersion relation is obtained by assuming that the perturbation terms take the form of a plane wave solution,

u^{\prime},v^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}\propto\exp\left[i\left(k_{x}x+k_{y}y-\omega t\right)\right],

(12)

so the substitution of this
ansatz
into Eq. (
11
) yields

\omega=u_{0}k_{x}+k\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{k^{2}}{4}}.

(13)

For a fluid at rest with
u_{0}=0
, this equation is recognized as the Bogoliubov dispersion relation
Bogolyubov 1947
. In the long wavelength limit
k\ll 1
, this relation reduces to
\omega\approx c_{0}k
, where

c_{0}=\frac{\omega}{k}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\rho_{0}^{\frac{3}{4}}

(14)

is called the ‘sound velocity’. Introduction of this characteristic flow velocity allows one to rewrite the dispersion relation, making the transformation in dimensionless variables

\omega\rightarrow c_{0}^{2}\omega,\quad k\rightarrow c_{0}k,\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{0}\rightarrow c_{0}M,

(15)

so we get

\omega\equiv G(k_{x},k_{y})=Mk_{x}+k\sqrt{1+\frac{k^{2}}{4}},

(16)

where
M
denotes the Mach number and
k
is the modulus of the wave vector
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
. This form of the dispersion relation coincides with that for linear waves in usual BEC, whose dynamics is described by the standard GPE (see, e.g. Ref.
Gladush et al. 2007
), so we can use the method of Refs.
Whitham 1974
;
Gladush et al. 2007
for finding the Kelvin-like wake pattern.

Geometry of the stationary wave crest of linear radiation outside the Mach cone. The group velocity

of the wave is compensated by the flow velocity
\mathbf{u}
. The angle
\eta
determines the direction of the wave vector
\mathbf{k}
and
r
is the radius-vector of the point
P(x,y)
.

It is clear that the stationary wave pattern can only be generated by a supersonic flow
M>1
(or
u_{0}>c_{0}
), when the corresponding component of the flow velocity can compensate for wave propagation. A typical wave crest is shown in Fig.
1
for the flow incoming from the right. In a stationary wave generated by the flow past an obstacle, the wave vector is equal to the gradient of the phase
\theta
of the wave, whereas the frequency must be equal to zero:

\mathbf{k}=\nabla\theta,\quad G(k_{y},k_{x})=\omega=0.

(17)

Consequently, the equality of the cross-derivatives gives the equation

\frac{\partial k_{y}}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial k_{x}}{\partial y}.

(18)

The function
k_{x}=f(k_{y})
is defined in implicit form by the second Eq. (
17
), so we can rewrite Eq. (
18
) as an equation for
k_{y}
:

\frac{\partial k_{y}}{\partial x}-f^{\prime}(k_{y})\frac{\partial k_{y}}{\partial y}=0.

(19)

It is known as the Hopf equation and its theory is well developed (see, e.g., Ref.
Whitham 1974
). It is clear that
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
are constant along characteristic curves which satisfy the equation

\frac{dy}{dx}=-f^{\prime}(k_{y}).

(20)

We are interested in the wave pattern far enough from a small obstacle, so we can take an obvious self-similar solution of this equation with characteristics stemming from the origin,

\frac{y}{x}=-f^{\prime}(k_{y}).

(21)

Then the definition of the derivative of the function defined implicitly yields

\frac{y}{x}=\frac{\partial G/\partial k_{y}}{\partial G/\partial k_{x}}.

(22)

At last, according to the definition of the angles in Fig.
1
, we have
y/x=\tan{\xi}
, and then straightforward algebra (see Ref.
Gladush et al. 2007
) yields

\tan{\xi}=\frac{\left(2M^{2}-1-\tan^{2}\eta\right)\tan\eta}{\left(M^{2}+1\right)\tan^{2}\eta-\left(M^{2}-1\right)}.

(23)

Consequently, Eq. (
17
) gives the expression

k=2\sqrt{M^{2}\cos^{2}\eta-1}

(24)

for the wave number.

Another convenient parameter
\mu
(see Fig.
1
) can be found from the relation

\tan\mu=\frac{2M^{2}}{k^{2}}\sin 2\eta.

(25)

where
\xi+\mu+\eta=\pi
. The line of a constant phase can be obtained by integration the wave vector (
17
),

\theta=\int_{0}^{r}\mathbf{k}\cdot d\mathbf{r}.

(26)

Since the wave vector is a gradient of the phase, the path of the integration can be chosen arbitrarily, and it is convenient integrate along the line with
\xi=\mathrm{const}
, so we get

\theta=kr\cos\mu,

(27)

where
r
is the modulus of the radius vector. Then with the use of Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) we find that the modulus of the radius vector can be expressed as a function of
\eta
,

r=\frac{4\theta}{k^{3}}\sqrt{M^{2}(M^{2}-2)\cos^{2}\eta+1}.

(28)

At last, we obtain a parametric form of the line of a constant phase as

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=-r\cos\xi=-\frac{4\theta}{k^{3}}\left(1-M^{2}\cos 2\eta\right)\cos\eta,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=r\sin\xi=\frac{4\theta}{k^{3}}\left(2M^{2}\cos^{2}\eta-1\right)\sin\eta.

(29)

To obtain the equation for wave crests in notation of the LHY equation,
we performed the inverse of the transformation (
15
). According to the definition in Eq. (
26
) we find that the transformation of the phase satisfies the relation
\theta\rightarrow\theta/c_{0}
, so

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{4c_{0}^{2}\theta}{k^{3}}\left[M^{2}\cos 2\eta-1\right]\cos\eta,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\frac{4c_{0}^{2}\theta}{k^{3}}\left[2M^{2}\cos^{2}2\eta-1\right]\sin\eta.

(30)

In the last equation, we maintain the Mach number
M
to simplify the notation. The angle
\eta
varies in the interval

-\arccos\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)\leq\eta\leq\arccos\left(\frac{1}{M}\right).

(31)

These formulas show that this linear “ship-wave” pattern is located outside the Mach cone
which corresponds to the lines with
\eta=\pm\arccos(1/M)
.

IV
Oblique solitons in LHY fluid flow past an obstacle

Another wave pattern observed within the Mach cone consists of two oblique dark solitons located symmetrically with respect to the
x
-axis, as is shown in Fig.
2
. In this section, we propose an analytical theory of this wave pattern. It is clear that these oblique solitons are developed from the “shadow” behind the obstacle and they attain a stationary form far enough from the obstacle. Therefore, we first find this stationary form assuming infinite length of a soliton. Such a soliton can be considered as a 1D solution of the generalized nonlinear Schrödinger equation, so that its velocity is directed normally to the soliton’s dip.

IV.1
DARK SOLITONS IN A 1D LHY FLUID

Figure 2:
Geometry of dark oblique solitons inside the Mach cone.

The soliton solution can be found following the general theory developed in Ref.
Kamchatnov 2024
;
Kamchatnov and Salerno 2009
. The 1D LHY equation has the form

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\tilde{x}^{2}}+|\psi|^{3}\psi,

(32)

where it is assumed that
\tilde{x}
is a coordinate normal to the soliton. We suppose for simplicity that the soliton moves with constant velocity
V
through the condensate at rest. We make again the Madelung transformation

\psi(\tilde{x},t)=\sqrt{\rho(\tilde{x},t)}e^{i\int\tilde{u}(\tilde{x})\,d\tilde{x}},

(33)

and obtain the hydrodynamic-like system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial(u\rho)}{\partial\tilde{x}}=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tilde{x}}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\rho}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tilde{x}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}}\left[\frac{1}{4\rho}\frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial\tilde{x}^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\rho^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tilde{x}}\right)^{2}\right].

(34)

The standard assumption that the variables
\rho
and
u
only depend on the variable
\xi=\tilde{x}-Vt
transforms this system to two ordinary differential equations which must satisfy the boundary condition

u\rightarrow u_{0},\quad\rho\rightarrow\rho_{0}\quad\text{ for }\quad|\xi|\rightarrow\infty,

(35)

that is the soliton excitation is localized and the condensate is at rest far enough from the soliton.

Integration of the first equation of the system (
IV.1
) gives at once

u(\rho)=V\left(1-\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}\right).

(36)

Then the second equation yields

\frac{1}{4\rho}\frac{d^{2}\rho}{d\xi^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\rho^{2}}\left(\frac{d\rho}{d\xi}\right)^{2}=\rho^{\frac{3}{2}}-\rho_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{V^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{0}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-1\right).

(37)

This equation can be transformed with the use of the identity

\frac{1}{4\rho}\frac{d^{2}\rho}{d\xi^{2}}-\frac{1}{8\rho^{2}}\left(\frac{d\rho}{d\xi}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho}}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\rho}\right),

(38)

so that, after multiplication of Eq.(
37
) by
d(\sqrt{\rho})/d\xi
and easy integration, we get

\frac{d\rho}{d\xi}=\pm 2\sqrt{R(\rho)},

(39)

where

R(\rho)=2\rho\left(\frac{3}{5}\rho_{0}^{\frac{5}{2}}-\rho_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}\rho+\frac{2}{5}\rho^{\frac{5}{2}}\right)-V^{2}\left(\rho_{0}-\rho\right)^{2}.

(40)

The dark soliton solution corresponds to
\rho
changing between the two roots of
R(\rho)
, where
R(\rho)\geq 0
, so these roots represent the maximal
\rho_{0}
and minimal
\rho_{m}
values of the density, respectively. In the center of the soliton we have (
\xi\rightarrow\xi_{0}
)
\rho\rightarrow\rho_{m}
, so integration of Eq. (
40
) gives us the dependence
\rho=\rho(\xi)
in implicit form,

\xi(\rho)=\xi_{0}\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{\rho_{m}}^{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{\sqrt{R(\rho)}}.

(41)

The relationship between the velocity
V
of the soliton and the minimal value of the density
\rho_{m}
at the soliton’s center can be found directly from the equation
R(\rho_{m})=0
,

V^{2}=\frac{2\rho_{m}\left(\frac{3}{5}\rho_{0}^{\frac{5}{2}}+\frac{2}{5}\rho_{m}^{\frac{5}{2}}-\rho_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}\rho_{m}\right)}{(\rho_{m}-\rho_{0})^{2}}.

(42)

IV.2
OBLIQUE DARK SOLITON IN A QUASI-2D SYSTEM

To extend our theory to the quasi-2D oblique dark soliton, we change the reference frame to the one where the obstacle is located at the origin of the coordinate system and the condensate flows from the right with the flow velocity
u_{0}<0
. We suppose that the soliton’s dip lies along
y^{\prime}
-axis rotated by the angle
\alpha
with respect to the first reference frame,

x^{\prime}=x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha\text{, \quad and \quad}y^{\prime}=x\sin\alpha-y\cos\alpha.

(43)

In this rotated coordinate system the flow velocity has the components
u^{\prime}_{0}=(u_{0}\cos\alpha,u_{0}\sin\alpha)
, and the soliton’s velocity is compensated by the normal component of the flow velocity, so the soliton becomes stationary. This means that
V=-u_{0}\cos\alpha
and we get the oblique soliton solution in the form

x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha=\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{\rho_{m}}^{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{\sqrt{R(\rho)}},

(44)

where we took into account that the soliton stems from the origin where the obstacle is located, so
\xi_{0}=0
. Then, for a given value of
x
, the soliton’s profile in
y
-direction is given by the formula

y(\rho,x)=x\cot\alpha\pm\frac{1}{2\sin\alpha}\int_{\rho_{m}}^{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{\sqrt{R(\rho)}}.

(45)

The angle
\alpha
is influenced by several parameters, including the flow velocity and the obstacle’s size and geometry. Its calculation lies beyond the predictive scope of our analytical approach. In our study,
\alpha
was determined numerically using the relation
\alpha=\arctan(x_0/y_0)
, where
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
denote the coordinates of some point in the center of the soliton line.

V.1
NUMERICAL METHOD

V.2
Numerical Method

The numerical approach employed in our simulations involves three transformations of the governing equation (
7
), similar to the method used in Refs.
Khamis and Gammal 2013
. The first transformation, applied to the homogeneous system, is used to fix the boundary conditions as
\Psi=\Psi_{0}e^{-i\rho_{0}t}
. Next, we introduce the obstacle potential term,
U_{\text{obs}}=U(x-vt,y)
, and perform a change of reference frame:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=x-vt;\quad y^{\prime}=y;\quad t^{\prime}=t;

\displaystyle\phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\psi(x,y,t)\,e^{-\frac{iv}{2}\quantity(x - \frac{1}{2} v t)}.

Finally, we apply a transformation to recover fixed boundary conditions:

\varphi=\phi\,e^{\frac{iv}{2}\quantity(x' + \frac{1}{2} v t')}.

This leads to the equation derived in
Hakim 1997
:

\begin{split}i\partialderivative{\varphi}{t'}=&-\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\frac{\partial^2 \varphi}{\partial x'^2} +\frac{\partial^2 \varphi}{\partial y'^2} )+iv\partialderivative{\varphi}{x'}-\rho_{0}\varphi\\
&+U_{obs}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\varphi+\absolutevalue{\varphi}^{3}\varphi,\end{split}

(46)

which is solved numerically.

Given the two-dimensional nature of the system, we use the Peaceman-Rachford (PR) method
Quinney 1987
for spatial derivatives, combined with the split-step operator method for the ordinary terms. The PR method is well known for being unconditionally stable when applied exclusively to the dispersion terms. In our implementation, it proved to be effective for evolving the system in time while maintaining numerical accuracy and stability. Additionally, suitable discretizations techniques were applied to ensure that both the boundary conditions and the presence of the obstacle were accurately incorporated into the numerical framework. The typical spatial and temporal discretizations employed are
\Delta x=\Delta y=0.20
and
\Delta t=0.001
, respectively.

V.3
LINEAR RADIATION

As is shown in Fig.
3
, wave crests of linear radiation can be calculated analytically with good precision
from Eq. (
III
). Moreover, Eq. (
III
) predicts that just ahead of the obstacle, in the limit
y\rightarrow 0
,
the wavelength vanishes as the flow velocity
|u_{0}|
increases. This becomes clear by assuming
\psi\rightarrow 0

and expanding Eq. (
III
) in a Taylor series. This calculation results in the system:

\begin{split}&x\approx\frac{\theta}{2\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}}}+\frac{\theta(c_{0}^{2}-5u_{0}^{2})}{\left(u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\psi^{2},\\
&y\approx\frac{\theta\left(2u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\right)}{2\left(u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\psi,\end{split}

(47)

which can be rewritten as a function
x=x(y)
,

x\left(y\right)\approx\frac{\theta}{2\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}}}+\frac{4\left(c_{0}^{2}-5u_{0}^{2}\right)\left(u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}{\theta\left(2u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}}y^{2}.

(48)

Finally, from Eq. (
26
), we find that
\theta\approx kx
. Substituting this into Eq. (
48
), we obtain, in the limit
y\rightarrow 0
, the wavelength

L\approx\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}}},

(49)

which decreases as a function of the velocity
u_{0}
. This “shortening” of the wavelength can be seen when we compare the wave patterns for
M=2
in Fig.
3
with the other one for
M=5
in Fig.
4
. If we increase
M
even more, then we observe in numerical simulations a “continuous” density distribution in the region of for small
\psi
. This means that the wavelength becomes here smaller than the size of the obstacle, so the interference of generated harmonics becomes destructive and it does not lead to formation of a regular wake wave pattern.

V.4
OBLIQUE DARK SOLITON

The oblique dark solitons produced by the LHY fluid inside the Mach cone attains a stationary form only when the critical flow velocity is reached. Below this critical velocity, the soliton is unstable and, similar to the case of the Gross-Pitaevskii equation, it breaks into vortex-antivortex pairs due to the snake instability. A discussion of this instability in the context of the Gross-Pitaevskii equation can be found in Ref.
Kamchatnov and Pitaevskii 2008
and qualitatively similar behavior is expected for the case of LHY dynamics.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated analytically and numerically two types of wave excitations generated by a supersonic flow past an obstacle in a quasi-2D LHY quantum fluid. Numerical simulations confirmed that the wave crests of linear radiation, formed outside the Mach cone, are accurately captured by linear theory (see Fig.
3
). The structure of oblique dark solitons is well described by a quasi-one-dimensional analytical model (see Fig.
5
), demonstrating excellent agreement with the numerical results.

From an experimental perspective, the supersonic flow past an impenetrable barrier, as modeled in this work, offers a useful approximation for BEC systems past strong laser beams. The features identified here—such as oblique solitons and linear wakes—are potentially observable in current experimental setups, particularly in quantum droplets or strongly interacting mixtures where LHY corrections play a dominant role. As a direction for future research, we propose extending this framework to penetrable, weak obstacles, where the local flow velocity varies along the soliton line. This generalization is relevant for describing experiments in which a weak laser beam moves through a dilute BEC
Engels and Atherton 2007
. This framework may also serve as a tool to measure and analyze experimentally critical velocities associated with the emission of collective excitations as proposed in Ref.
Lyu et al. 2022
.
