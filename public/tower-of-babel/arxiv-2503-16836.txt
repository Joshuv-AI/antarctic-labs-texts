A Flexible Fairness Framework with Surrogate Loss Reweighting for Addressing Sociodemographic Disparities
Wen Xu, Elham Dolatabadi

Abstract
This paper presents a new algorithmic fairness framework called 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} - 𝜷 \bm{\beta} Fair Machine Learning ( 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} - 𝜷 \bm{\beta} FML), designed to optimize fairness levels across sociodemographic attributes. Our framework employs a new family of surrogate loss functions, paired with loss reweighting techniques, allowing precise control over fairness-accuracy trade-offs through tunable hyperparameters 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} and 𝜷 \bm{\beta} . To efficiently solve the learning objective, we propose Parallel Stochastic Gradient Descent with Surrogate Loss (P-SGD-S) and establish convergence guarantees for both convex and nonconvex loss functions. Experimental results demonstrate that our framework improves overall accuracy while reducing fairness violations, offering a smooth trade-off between standard empirical risk minimization and strict minimax fairness. Results across multiple datasets confirm its adaptability, ensuring fairness improvements without excessive performance degradation.

Introduction

Sociodemographic disparities—driven by unequal access to education, healthcare, and employment—remain one of the most urgent challenges of our time
Zajacova and Lawrence 2018
. Addressing these inequities demands more than equal treatment; it requires confronting historical injustices and systemic barriers. Amartya Sen’s “The Idea of Justice”
Sen 2008
and Matt Cavanagh’s “Against Equality of Opportunity”
Roemer and Trannoy 2015
advocate for a justice framework that is adaptive and context-sensitive, ensuring fairness is measured by tangible improvements in people’s lives rather than abstract ideals.

This need for a more nuanced and contextual approach to fairness is especially critical in machine learning (ML) due to its increasingly influence on decision-making in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, finance, and criminal justice. The widespread adaoption of ML models raises urgent concerns about their potential to either mitigate or reinforce existing sociodemographic disparities
Barocas et al. 2019
;
Mehrabi et al. 2021
;
Juhn et al. 2022
. At the core of this challenge lies the dual task of
defining
and
achieving
fairness in algorithmic systems. While formal fairness criteria offer structured approaches to bias mitigation, they often fail to account for broader sociodemographic contexts, limiting their ability to address deeper systemic inequities
Barocas et al. 2019
;
Mehrabi et al. 2021
.

Existing fairness-aware ML approaches primarily address bias through statistical constraints—such as demographic parity and equalized odds—applied at various stages of the pipeline, including pre-processing transformations, in-processing optimization frameworks, and post-processing adjustments
Zafar et al. 2017
;
Hashimoto et al. 2018
;
Lahoti et al. 2020
;
Baharlouei et al. 2020
. While these methods provide measurable fairness guarantees, they often frame fairness as a rigid optimization constraint rather than an adaptable principle that evolves with changing contexts. This static approach risks overlooking the broader structural factors that contribute to disparities
Buyl et al. 2024
. Strategies that prioritize improving outcomes for historically marginalized groups frequently introduce trade-offs that may compromise performance for others, leading to ethical and practical tensions
Selbst et al. 2019
. Bridging this gap requires fairness frameworks that move beyond static parity metrics, incorporating a
flexible
, context-aware understanding of equity while
preserving model utility
—one that aligns with Sen’s and Cavanagh’s vision of justice as an active and corrective force
Noble 2018
;
Dolata et al. 2022
.

In this work, we propose a novel fairness-aware optimization framework that dynamically balances fairness and accuracy, adapting to the specific socioeconomic context of an application. Drawing inspiration from fair resource allocation in network congestion control
Mo and Walrand 2000
and fairness-aware learning in federated settings
Li et al. 2020
, our framework generalizes existing fairness paradigms, including minimax fairness proposed in
Hashimoto et al. 2018
;
Mohri et al. 2019
, which prioritizes the most underrepresented groups. By embedding fairness directly into the optimization process, our approach advances the broader goal of developing fairness-aware models that account for ethical and policy implications across diverse domains such as healthcare, criminal justice, and economic decision-making.
The key
contributions
of this work are as follows:

•

We propose a novel model-agnostic framework, called
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
Fair Machine Learning (
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
FML), for in-processing fair prediction through the utilization of a family of surrogate loss functions with loss reweighting techniques. These functions incorporate reweighting the polynomials of loss functions on different subgroups to calibrate the distribution of performance among protected features or imbalanced labels. The
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
FML has two sets of hyperparameters
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
, which enable an arbitrary level of fairness on model performance.

•

We introduce a distributed stochastic optimization algorithm, referred to as Parallel Stochastic Gradient Descent with Surrogate Loss (
P-SGD-S
), designed to train ML models using our newly proposed loss functions. We also provide convergence guarantees for
P-SGD-S
for both convex and nonconvex loss functions.

•

We validate the flexibility of our framework in achieving fairness across three classification tasks: two based on sociodemographic attributes and one on label-partitioned fairness. Our approach adapts to different fairness definitions, balancing accuracy and fairness trade-offs in both convex and nonconvex settings.

Related Work

There is no uniform definition of fairness in ML, but two main levels of fairness are generally recognized in the literature: individual-level fairness and group-level fairness. Individual level fairness aims to achieve fairness for each unique entity in the dataset
Dwork et al. 2012
;
Zemel et al. 2013
;
Louizos et al. 2016
, as well as counterfactual fairness
Kusner et al. 2017
and PAC-learnable individual fairness
Yona and Rothblum 2018
. On the other hand, group fairness is concerned with metrics such as demographic parity
Zafar et al. 2017
and equality of opportunity
Hardt et al. 2016
. These recognitions have led to the adoption of three types of methods for achieving fair predictions: pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing methods.

Pre-processing methods
modify input data before training, such as
fairness through unawareness

Grgic-Hlaca et al. 2016
, advocating for the removal of protected features before model training. Another approach, presented in
Calmon et al. 2017
, introduces convex optimization for learning a data transformation. This method aims to limit distortion and control discrimination while simultaneously preserving utility.

In-processing methods
integrate fairness considerations directly into the learning process. In
Zafar et al. 2017
;
Baharlouei et al. 2020
, the authors proposed incorporating fairness constraints, such as the Pearson correlation and Rényi correlation between the protected features and the predictions, to impose fairness. Reweighting-based methods have also been proposed
Lohaus et al. 2020
;
Bendekgey and Sudderth 2021
;
Yao et al. 2024
, where fairness objectives are incorporated by combining empirical loss with fairness constraints, focusing on metrics like demographic parity and equality of opportunity. Building on Rawlsian distributive justice
Rawls 2001
, distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
Rahimian and Mehrotra 2019
;
Duchi and Namkoong 2021
has been explored as a fairness mechanism, where minimax optimization problems are solved to ensure robust model performance
Hashimoto et al. 2018
;
Sagawa et al. 2020
. DRO-based methods have also been applied in distributed ML
Mohri et al. 2019
, highlighting their applicability to federated settings. Some works in distributed ML have considered fairness notions such as proportional fairness
Zhang et al. 2023
and
\alpha
-fairness
Li et al. 2020
. However, proportional fairness, as defined in
Zhang et al. 2023
, focuses on the relative improvement in each client’s performance, which is unrelated to our objective. The most related work to ours is
Li et al. 2020
, where the authors introduce a
q
-fair federated learning (
q
-FFL) framework with surrogate loss functions. However, their approach lacks theoretical convergence analysis. Besides, the model’s hyperparameter
q
was uniform among all clients, i.e., groups, and only the fairness on model accuracy was investigated. Unlike much of the algorithmic fairness literature
Rabonato and Berton 2024
, which relies on auxiliary fairness constraints or regularization techniques, our approach directly integrates fairness into the learning process through a novel loss function, making it fundamentally distinct from existing literature.

Post-processing methods
mitigate discrimination by adjusting model outcomes. For instance,
Hardt et al. 2016
introduced an optimal approach for equality of opportunity, relying solely on the joint statistics of the predictor, the target, and the protected feature. Moreover,
Fish et al. 2016
proposed shifting decision boundaries for protected groups in various models.
Dwork et al. 2018
offered a transfer learning-based decoupling algorithm for fair adjustments across black-box models, and
Alghamdi et al. 2022
used information projection to post-process classifier outputs. However, these methods often incur additional computational overhead, limiting their practicality in resource-constrained settings.

Preliminaries

In this section, we provide essential technical groundwork on fairness in ML to underpin the development of our theory.

We consider a supervised learning scenario, where each data point consists of a feature vector
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, with
{\mathcal{Z}}
as the feature space, and a label
Y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, with
{\mathcal{Y}}
representing the outcome space. To simplify our presentation, we focus on the binary classification problem, i.e.,
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{0,1\}
. However, the concepts and findings can be readily extended to multi-class classification problems or regression tasks. It is important to note that some features in
Z
should be treated carefully, concerning fairness and bias in ML. For instance, under the Civil Rights Act of 1964 in the United States
Act 1964
, features such as age, gender, race, and religion are classified as protected features for individuals. Thus, we categorize all features into protected features
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
and non-protected features
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
such that
Z=(A,X)\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{Z}}
. The output prediction from the trained model is represented by
\hat{Y}
.
In the remaining parts of the paper, we use uppercase symbols to denote random variables or vectors while we use lowercase symbols to represent unspecified realizations of them. If these lowercase symbols represent realizations of random vectors, they are presented in bold font. Specifically, we use the lowercase
\bm{x}
,
\bm{a}
,
\bm{z}
,
y
, and
\hat{y}
to represent a realization of the random variable/vector
X
,
A
,
Z
,
Y
, and
\hat{Y}
, respectively.

We consider the following well-known definition for group-level fairness, which entails equivalent model performance across different groups. Here, we make an additional assumption that the protected feature
A
is a single, binary feature and use
A=a
to represent the protected feature taking a scalar value of
a
. The definitions can be extended to include multiple protected features and categorical ones beyond binary.

Definition 1

(Equality of Accuracy (EA))
.

A model satisfies equality of accuracy if

\displaystyle P(\hat{Y}=Y|A=0)=P(\hat{Y}=Y|A=1).

(1)

Achieving definition
1
implies equal accuracy of predicted outcomes for the two groups, categorized based on the protected feature,
A
.

Definition 2

(EA violation)
.

The EA violation is defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\text{EA}}=|P(\hat{Y}=Y|A=0)-P(\hat{Y}=Y|A=1)|.

(2)

A smaller EA violation
\epsilon_{\text{EA}}
indicates a more uniform model performance across the two groups. In addition, we incorporate other widely recognized fairness criteria, such as demographic parity (DP)
Zafar et al. 2017
and equality of opportunity (EO)
Hardt et al. 2016
, as discussed in the Appendix.

Fair Machine Learning Formulation

We begin by formalizing the
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
fair machine learning (FML) under the assumption that all available
N
feature-label pairs
\{(\bm{z}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independently drawn from an unknown distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
. Here, a learner is represented by a hypothesis
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
, where
{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the hypothesis class. For any
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, the predicted outcome of
h
is
\hat{Y}=h(Z)
. We further assume that each learner
h
is parameterized by
\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We define the loss function as
\ell:{\mathcal{W}}\times{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that the loss incurred by a data point
(\bm{z},y)
with respect to a model
w
is given by a non-negative value
\ell(\bm{w},(\bm{z},y))
. For convenience, we represent the loss incurred by a data point
(\bm{z}_{j},y_{j})
using a model
\bm{w}
by
\ell_{j}(\bm{w})
for any
j\in[N]
, where
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
.

A canonical approach to solving a learning problem involves empirical risk minimization (ERM)
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
;
Mohri et al. 2018
, where the objective is to identify a learner that minimizes the average loss incurred by the provided training examples. This optimization can be formulated as the following finite sum minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ell_{j}(\bm{w}).

(3)

We propose a new family of surrogate loss functions that facilitate the incorporation of diverse levels of fairness, inspired by the utility function in resource allocation
Mo and Walrand 2000
and fair resource allocation in FL
Li et al. 2020
. To differentiate this approach from previous works, we name this family of loss functions as
\beta
-fair surrogate loss functions.

Definition 3

(
\beta
-fair Surrogate Loss Function)
.

The family of
\beta
-fair loss functions for any
(\bm{z},y)\in({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and for any
\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}}
is

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(\bm{w},(\bm{z},y))=\frac{(1+\ell(\bm{w},(\bm{z},y)))^{{}^{1+\beta}}}{1+\beta},

(4)

where
\beta\geq 0
and
\ell
is any given nonnegative loss function.

Remark 1
.

Our proposed
\beta
-fair surrogate loss function can be interpreted as placing larger penalties on data points with higher losses when
\beta>0
. Furthermore, it serves as an upper bound for the original loss, which contrasts with the surrogate loss function used in
Li et al. 2020
. Notably, their approach does not include a bias term (such as
+1
in the numerator of equation
4
), making this upper-bound property not always guaranteed in their formulation.

We further define our surrogate empirical loss for the learning problem. As previously mentioned, we focus on a scenario where the protected feature
A
is a single binary attribute, assuming values in
\{0,1\}
. We denote
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
for the set of samples with
A=0
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
for the set of samples with
A=1
. The cardinality of
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
are
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, respectively.

Definition 4

(
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
Fair Surrogate Loss Function)
.

The group-aware
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
fair surrogate loss is defined as

\displaystyle L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}(\bm{w})=\alpha_{0}F_{\beta_{0}}(\bm{w})+\alpha_{1}F_{\beta_{1}}(\bm{w}),

(5)

where
F_{\beta_{0}}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{S_{0}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}}f_{\beta_{0}}(\bm{w},(\bm{z}_{i},y_{i}))
is the average
\beta_{0}
-fair surrogate loss on
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
,
F_{\beta_{1}}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{S_{1}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}}f_{\beta_{1}}(\bm{w},(z_{i},y_{i}))
is the average
\beta_{1}
-fair surrogate loss on
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})\in\Delta_{1}
, i.e., the
1
-dimensional probability simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For convenience, we let
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1})
.

In our proposed framework, the objective is to minimize the
\bm{\alpha}
-
\bm{\beta}
fair surrogate loss in equation
5
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}}}L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}(\bm{w}).

(6)

Remark 2
.

Note that our formulation has two sets of tunable hyperparameters:
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
. The hyperparameter
\bm{\alpha}
captures the explicit weights assigned to each group of the data, while the hyperparameter
\bm{\beta}
steers the surrogate loss for data sample from each group. Common choices for
\bm{\alpha}
include equal group weights, i.e.,
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=1/2
, and equal sample weights, i.e.,
\alpha_{0}=S_{0}/N
and
\alpha_{1}=S_{1}/N
, making the weights proportional to the number of data points in each group.

Remark 3
.

Our formulation subsumes many previous works as special cases. When setting
\alpha_{0}=S_{0}/N
,
\alpha_{1}=S_{1}/N
, and
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=0
, our formulation in equation
6
is equivalent to the original ERM formulation in equation
3
. If we set
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=q>0
, and reset the
+1
bias term in the numerator to be
0
, our formulation in equation
6
aligns with the optimization formulation of q-FFL in
Li et al. 2020
with
2
clients. Additionally, by letting
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
be positive while
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
approach positive infinity, min-max fairness
Hashimoto et al. 2018
;
Mohri et al. 2019
is imposed, where the data point with largest loss dominates. As such, tuning
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
allows for imposing any level of fairness between the extreme case of ERM and the min-max fairness.

Remark 4
.

Note that equation
6
can be adapted for scenarios involving protected or unbalanced labels instead of protected features. In this case, assuming binary labels, we can redefine
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
as the index set of samples with label
Y=0
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
as the index set of samples with label
Y=1
. We refer to the scenario of grouping samples by values of
Y
as the label-partitioned scenario and the scenario of grouping samples by values of protected features
A
as the feature-partitioned scenario. In our experiments, we demonstrate the flexibility of our proposed methods in both scenarios.

Convergence Analysis

In this section, we provide the convergence analysis of
P-SGD-S
. To maintain the clarity of our explanations, we assume without loss of generality that
\gamma_{i}^{(t)}=\gamma,\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}},t\in{\mathcal{T}}
. We begin by making the following assumptions
Bottou et al. 2018
.

Assumption 1

(Smoothness)
.

There exists some positive
L_{0}
such that
\left\|\nabla L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}(\bm{w}_{0})-\nabla L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}(\bm{w}_{1})\right\|\leq L_{0}\left\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{1}\right\|,\forall\bm{w}_{0},\bm{w}_{1}\in{\mathcal{W}}.

Remark 5
.

The smoothness
L_{0}
is actually dependent on
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{\beta}
, and the smoothness of the bounded original loss function
\ell
. For simplicity, we represent the smooth of surrogate loss
L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}
by
L_{0}
without explicitly writing down the dependence.

Assumption 2

(Unbiased Local Stochastic Gradient)
.

The stochastic gradient
\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w};\xi)
is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient for any group-specific loss function
F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w})
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w};\xi)]=\nabla F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w}),\,\forall\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}},\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}},\forall\xi\in\Xi
, where
\Xi
denotes the set of all possible mini-batches (i.e., the set of randomness in the sampling process).

Remark 6
.

The assumption holds since in
P-SGD-S
, a mini-batch
\xi_{i}^{(t)}
is chosen uniformly at random for all
i\in{\mathcal{I}},t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, i.e., we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w}^{(t)})]

\displaystyle\!=\!{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i\in\xi_{i}^{(t)}}|\xi_{i}^{(t)}|^{-1}(1\!+\!\ell_{i}(\bm{w}^{(t)}))^{\beta_{i}}\nabla\ell_{i}(\bm{w}^{(t)})]

\displaystyle\!=\!\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}_{i}}(S_{i})^{-1}(1\!+\!\ell_{i}(\bm{w}^{(t)}))^{\beta_{i}}\nabla\ell_{i}(\bm{w}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\!=\!\nabla F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w}^{(t)}).

Assumption 3

(Bounded Stochastic Gradient Variance)
.

There exists a positive
\sigma^{2}
such that the variance of the stochastic gradient
\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w};\xi)
is bounded as
{\mathbb{E}}[\|\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w};\xi)-\nabla F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w})\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2},\,\forall\bm{w}\in{\mathcal{W}},\,\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

We first provide an important lemma to facilitate our analysis of
P-SGD-S
.

Lemma 1

(Equivalence and Expectation of Model Update)
.

An equivalent expression of the per round update in equation
9

\bm{w}^{(t+1)}=\bm{w}^{(t)}-\gamma\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\alpha_{i}\tilde{\nabla}F_{\beta_{i}}(\bm{w}^{(t)};\xi_{i}^{(t)}),

(10)

whose expectation is

{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{w}^{(t+1)}]=\bm{w}^{(t)}-\gamma\nabla L_{(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}(\bm{w}^{(t)}).

(11)

Proof.

Substituting the update rule in equation
8
into the aggregation rule in equation
9
, we can obtain equation
10
. Taking expectation on both sides of equation
10
and applying Assumption
2
, we obtain equation
11
.
∎

Convex Loss

8
Conclusion

Reweighting techniques for both convex and nonconvex settings, effectively addresses sociodemographic disparities in classification tasks while maintaining strong predictive performance. By tuning fairness parameters, our method provides a flexible trade-off between no fairness constraints and strict fairness enforcement, allowing practitioners to adapt fairness interventions to specific contexts without imposing overly rigid constraints. Future work will extend this framework to intersectional fairness analysis, more complex multimodal datasets, and integration with state-of-the-art deep learning models, including transformers, to further enhance its applicability in real-world decision-making systems.

Definition 5
