Radiographic outcomes and prognostic factors after protocol-defined prolonged abduction containment for Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease: a 427-hip retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tiantian Xia, Fengjie Zhang, Jiaru Liao, Xiaoqiang Yang, Fan Yang, Wei He, Ziqi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on nonoperative treatment for Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease remains limited by heterogeneity in treatment exposure, including variable combinations of observation, physiotherapy, bracing, casting, activity restriction, and weight-bearing restriction. We evaluated radiographic outcomes and risk factors after a protocol-defined prolonged cast-based abduction containment regimen, thereby establishing benchmark outcome estimates for this specific nonoperative pathway.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included children with Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease treated with the institutional protocol between 2009 and 2022 and followed radiographically to healing. The protocol consisted of continuous cast-based abduction containment and strict non-weight-bearing until radiographic healing. The primary outcome was an adverse radiographic result at healing, defined as modified Stulberg grade III to V. Hip-level associations were evaluated using generalized estimating equations with an independence working correlation structure and patient-clustered robust standard errors to account for correlation between bilateral hips.ResultsA total of 398 children with 427 hips were included; 110 hips (25.8%) had an adverse radiographic outcome. Adverse outcome rates increased across age groups: 15.8% in children younger than 6 years, 24.1% in children aged 6 to < 8 years, and 39.8% in children aged 8 years or older. In the primary multivariable model, compared with children younger than 6 years, the odds of adverse outcome were higher in children aged 6 to < 8 years (OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.13 to 4.15; p = 0.020) and in those aged 8 years or older (OR 5.69, 95% CI 2.77 to 11.71; p < 0.001). Compared with Herring A/B hips, Herring B/C hips (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.07 to 4.49; p = 0.032) and Herring C hips (OR 10.28, 95% CI 5.50 to 19.22; p < 0.001) had higher odds of adverse outcome. Sensitivity analyses showed the same overall pattern.ConclusionsWithin this defined nonoperative pathway, older age at diagnosis and greater Herring lateral pillar involvement were associated with a higher risk of adverse radiographic outcome at healing. These protocol-specific estimates provide a reference for prognostic counselling within this treatment pathway and a benchmark for future comparative studies of nonoperative strategies.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2600120879, 20 March 2026, retrospectively registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10291-4.

Background

Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease is an idiopathic osteonecrosis of the capital femoral epiphysis in skeletally immature children [1, 2]. During the active phase of the disease, ischemia followed by fragmentation and reossification may lead to femoral head deformity, hip incongruity, and early degenerative change [1, 3]. The main treatment goal is therefore to preserve femoral head sphericity and containment throughout disease progression [4].

Age at onset and disease severity remain central to treatment decisions [5, 6]. Younger children are often managed nonoperatively, whereas older children and those with more severe lateral pillar involvement are more likely to be considered for containment surgery [5, 7]. However, expected outcomes after nonoperative treatment remain insufficiently defined when treatment exposure is not clearly specified [8, 9].

A major reason is that previous studies have grouped markedly different management pathways under the broad label of nonoperative treatment [5, 8]. These pathways have included observation, physiotherapy, bracing, casting, activity modification, and varying degrees and durations of weight-bearing restriction [5, 8, 10]. Outcome endpoints have also differed across studies, ranging from early radiographic deformity measures to modified Stulberg classification after healing or skeletal maturity [8, 10, 11]. This heterogeneity limits the interpretability and clinical transferability of aggregated nonoperative outcome estimates [8, 11].

Our institution has used a prolonged abduction containment immobilization protocol characterized by continuous cast-based containment and strict non-weight-bearing until radiographic healing. To address this evidence gap, we analyzed a large cohort treated under a sustained, protocol-defined regimen of cast-based abduction containment and strict non-weight-bearing until radiographic healing. We aimed to quantify radiographic outcomes after this standardized treatment pathway and to identify independent predictors of adverse radiographic outcome. We hypothesized that age at diagnosis and radiographic severity would stratify outcome risk even under a uniform nonoperative treatment exposure [5, 6, 12].

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of children with Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease treated with a protocol-defined prolonged abduction containment regimen. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guideline for observational studies [13]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (approval number: PJ-XS-20251023-001). The requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Ethics Committee because of the retrospective design and use of de-identified clinical and radiographic data. The study was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.

Participants

We reviewed all consecutive children with Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease who presented to our institution between September 2009 and August 2022. Diagnosis was confirmed independently by two senior orthopedic surgeons on the basis of clinical and radiographic findings. All 418 patients initially commenced the institutional nonoperative protocol. Eligibility for the analytic cohort required completion of the protocol and radiographic follow-up to the Waldenström healing stage. Six patients were lost to follow-up during treatment before reaching radiographic healing; subsequent management after loss to follow-up, including possible treatment elsewhere, was unavailable. Operative containment before radiographic healing was a prespecified exclusion criterion; however, no patient crossed over to surgery before reaching the radiographic endpoint. The remaining 14 exclusions comprised initial Waldenström remodeling stage at diagnosis (n = 8) and concomitant arthritis (n = 6).

Treatment protocol

After diagnosis, patients were treated with a non-removable hip spica cast that positioned the affected hip in approximately 30 degrees of abduction and moderate flexion, with a rigid inter-limb bar to maintain containment (Fig. 1). Strict non-weight-bearing was prescribed throughout the active disease stage, with cast replacement and radiographic reassessment scheduled every two to three months. The cast was worn continuously and was removed only briefly for scheduled replacement, usually every two to three months. Supervised range-of-motion exercises were performed during these intervals. Treatment was continued until radiographic healing was confirmed by restoration of trabecular continuity in the weight-bearing region and homogeneous epiphyseal density [14]. Treatment duration was calculated in days from the earliest recorded radiographic date at Waldenström stage 1, 2a, or 2b to the date of first documentation of stage 3a. This measure therefore represented the duration of the recorded nonoperative treatment pathway to the prespecified radiographic endpoint.

Radiographic assessment and outcome definition

Standardized anteroposterior and frog-leg lateral radiographs were reviewed independently by two blinded pediatric orthopedic surgeons, with disagreements resolved by a senior author. Variables recorded for analysis included Waldenström stage at diagnosis, Catterall classification [15], and Herring lateral pillar classification during fragmentation [16]. Adverse radiographic outcome was defined as modified Stulberg grade III to V, reflecting loss of femoral-head sphericity rather than direct clinical failure. Favorable outcome was defined as modified Stulberg grade I or II. In a random sample of 100 hips, interobserver reliability for the Herring classification and modified Stulberg grade was assessed at the hip level using percentage agreement and weighted kappa coefficients.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. The unit of analysis was the hip because radiographic severity, treatment duration, and the modified Stulberg outcome were assessed at the hip level. As bilateral hips were included, univariable and multivariable logistic models were estimated using generalized estimating equations with an independence working correlation structure and patient-clustered robust standard errors. This approach retained hip-specific information while accounting for within-patient correlation between bilateral hips. Age at diagnosis was categorized as younger than 6 years, 6 to < 8 years, and 8 years or older. Herring groups A and B were combined as the reference category in the primary model because of the small number of hips classified as Herring A. Sex, bilaterality, Waldenström stage at diagnosis, Catterall classification, and Herring classification were also entered into the model. Given the conceptual overlap between Catterall and Herring classifications, collinearity was assessed before multivariable modeling and estimates for overlapping severity variables were interpreted in context. Sensitivity analyses were performed by modeling age as a continuous variable, restricting the analysis to unilateral cases only, and refitting the model using the original four-level Herring classification. The unilateral-only analysis used ordinary logistic regression because each patient contributed only one hip. For interobserver reliability, percentage agreement and weighted kappa coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the Herring classification and modified Stulberg grade in the random 100-hip sample [17]. Confidence intervals for weighted kappa were estimated by bootstrap resampling with 5,000 iterations.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of the 418 patients who initiated the protocol, six were lost to follow-up before radiographic healing and no patient underwent operative containment before healing. The remaining 14 exclusions comprised initial Waldenström remodeling stage at diagnosis (n = 8) and concomitant arthritis (n = 6). The final analytic cohort comprised 398 children with 427 affected hips (Fig. 2). Treatment duration was 323.3 ± 190.1 days (mean ± SD), with a median of 294 days (interquartile range, 183.5 to 442 days; range, 33 to 989 days) (Table 1). Twenty-nine patients contributed bilateral hips (58 of 427 hips); the primary analyses therefore accounted for within-patient correlation. The mean age at diagnosis was 6.48 years. There were 146 hips in children younger than 6 years, 158 hips in children aged 6 to < 8 years, and 123 hips in children aged 8 years or older. The cohort included 367 hips in boys and 60 hips in girls. At diagnosis, 51 hips were in the Waldenström initial stage and 376 were in the fragmentation stage. Herring classification during fragmentation was A in 7 hips, B in 250 hips, B/C in 65 hips, and C in 105 hips. Demographic data and outcomes are shown in Table 1. In the random 100-hip sample, interobserver agreement was 90.0% for the Herring classification, with a weighted kappa of 0.90 (95% CI 0.84 to 0.96), and 91.0% for the modified Stulberg grade, with a weighted kappa of 0.92 (95% CI 0.86 to 0.96) (Fig. 3).

Radiographic outcomes

At final radiographic assessment, modified Stulberg grades were distributed as follows: grade I in 92 hips, grade II in 225 hips, grade III in 69 hips, grade IV in 14 hips, and grade V in 27 hips. Overall, 110 hips were classified as having an adverse radiographic outcome, corresponding to an adverse outcome rate of 25.8%. The rate of adverse radiographic outcome increased stepwise with age at diagnosis: 15.8% in children younger than 6 years, 24.1% in children aged 6 to < 8 years, and 39.8% in children aged 8 years or older (Fig. 4).

Multivariable analysis

In the primary multivariable model estimated with patient-clustered robust standard errors, age at diagnosis and Herring classification were independently associated with adverse radiographic outcome. Compared with children younger than 6 years, those aged 6 to < 8 years had higher odds of adverse outcome (OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.13 to 4.15; p = 0.020), and the odds were substantially higher in children aged 8 years or older (OR 5.69, 95% CI 2.77 to 11.71; p < 0.001). Using Herring groups A and B as the reference category, Herring B/C hips were associated with increased odds of adverse outcome (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.07 to 4.49; p = 0.032), while Herring C hips showed a markedly stronger association (OR 10.28, 95% CI 5.50 to 19.22; p < 0.001). Sex, bilaterality, Waldenström stage at diagnosis, and Catterall classification were not independently associated with adverse outcome. The wide confidence intervals for Catterall categories should be interpreted cautiously because of overlap with Herring classification and sparse reference strata. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the overall robustness of the primary findings. When age was modeled continuously, each one-year increase in age at diagnosis remained associated with higher odds of adverse outcome (adjusted OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.82; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1). In the unilateral-only analysis of 369 hips, the associations for age 8 years or older and Herring C hips remained statistically significant, while the estimates for age 6 to < 8 years and Herring B/C hips remained directionally consistent but were no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2). The model retaining the original four-level Herring classification showed a similar overall pattern (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This study describes radiographic outcomes in a large single-center cohort of children with Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease treated with a standardized prolonged abduction containment immobilization protocol. The principal finding was that, within this uniform nonoperative pathway, older age at diagnosis and greater lateral pillar involvement were the factors most strongly associated with adverse radiographic outcome [5, 6, 12, 18, 19].

The central contribution of this study is the provision of protocol-specific outcome data for a large cohort treated with sustained cast-based abduction containment and strict non-weight-bearing. By defining the treatment exposure explicitly, the study allows its outcome estimates to be interpreted against a reproducible clinical pathway. This is clinically relevant because previous nonoperative cohorts often combined observation, physiotherapy, bracing, casting, activity modification, and variable weight-bearing restrictions under a single treatment label [8, 10, 20–22]. Such heterogeneity limits the use of historical nonoperative cohorts as a uniform comparator [8, 11]. By analyzing a sustained, protocol-defined pathway, the present study establishes a clearer clinical context for outcome estimation and risk stratification.

The adverse radiographic outcome rate in the present cohort was 25.8%, defined as modified Stulberg grades III to V. This rate was lower than those reported in the combined nonoperative arms of Herring et al. (54.2%) and the long-term cohort of Larson et al. (58.6%) [5, 32]. It was broadly comparable with the rates reported by Rosenfeld et al. (20.1%) and Rich and Schoenecker (20.8%) [34, 35]. Lower rates were reported in selected younger cohorts, including Canavese and Dimeglio (15.0%) and Singh et al. (7.7%) [23, 36]. In contrast, Cheok et al. reported an aspherical femoral head in 59.05% of non-surgically managed hips [19]. Direct comparisons remain limited by differences in age, disease severity, treatment selection, follow-up duration, and adverse-outcome definition (Table 3).

The association between increasing age at diagnosis and adverse radiographic outcome was strong and clinically coherent [5, 6, 12, 18, 19, 23]. In the primary multivariable model, children aged 6 to < 8 years and those aged 8 years or older had progressively higher odds of adverse outcome than children younger than 6 years, and the same overall pattern was observed when age was modeled continuously. These findings are consistent with the longstanding view that remodeling potential decreases with age and that older children have less biological reserve during the active phase of disease [6, 23–25].

Radiographic disease severity was also strongly associated with outcome [5, 6, 16, 18, 24, 26]. After combining Herring groups A and B as the reference category in the primary model, both Herring B/C and Herring C were associated with higher odds of adverse radiographic outcome, with the strongest association observed in Herring C hips. This pattern indicates that more severe lateral pillar involvement remains closely linked to final femoral head shape even when treatment exposure is relatively uniform [5, 6, 18, 26, 27].

These findings show that protocol standardization improves interpretability while preserving the prognostic importance of baseline biological and radiographic risk. Although a standardized pathway reduces treatment heterogeneity, femoral head remodeling remains strongly conditioned by age-related remodeling capacity, the severity of vascular insult, and structural involvement [6, 11, 20, 24, 25, 28]. The present data support risk-stratified counselling: while the protocol provides a consistent nonoperative pathway, prognosis differs substantially according to age at diagnosis and lateral pillar involvement.

The external validity of the present findings should therefore be considered carefully. The protocol involved prolonged hip-spica abduction containment with strict non-weight-bearing until radiographic healing, which differs substantially from the physiotherapy-based, activity-modification, or brace-based pathways commonly used in other centers [8, 20–22]. Accordingly, the present estimates should not be interpreted as directly applicable to centers using less restrictive contemporary nonoperative protocols. Rather, they provide outcome benchmarks for this specific protocol-defined treatment pathway.

Radiographic healing was selected as the primary endpoint because it was consistently available for the full cohort and directly reflects the central anatomical goal of Perthes treatment: preservation of femoral head sphericity and hip congruency. Modified Stulberg classification is clinically meaningful because residual femoral head shape is associated with later hip symptoms, osteoarthritis, and functional limitations [3, 30, 32]. However, it is not a direct patient-centered outcome and cannot fully characterize pain, hip-specific function, activity participation, or the social and emotional impact of the disease on children and their families [30, 31], or later salvage procedures in individual patients. The radiographic findings should therefore be interpreted as an anatomic outcome rather than proof of long-term functional benefit.

Several design features should be considered when interpreting these findings. Because of the retrospective design and absence of an internal comparator, this study does not support causal comparison with other treatment strategies. Selection bias may have arisen because eligibility required completion of the institutional protocol and radiographic follow-up to healing. Out-of-hospital adherence to non-weight-bearing and treatment burden were not directly quantified. The primary analysis used hip-level generalized estimating equations with patient-clustered robust standard errors; the unilateral-case sensitivity analysis yielded a similar overall pattern. Finally, the study assessed radiographic outcome at healing and did not systematically capture patient-reported function, pain, subsequent salvage surgery, or long-term degenerative change.

The study has several strengths that distinguish it from many previous nonoperative cohorts. It includes a large cohort managed at a single center under a clearly defined and sustained nonoperative protocol, with follow-up through radiographic healing and consistent outcome assessment. Interobserver reliability for the key radiographic classifications was high. In a field where nonoperative treatment is often reported as a broad and heterogeneous category, these protocol-specific data improve the interpretability of this treatment pathway and provide a stronger basis for future comparative research [8, 11, 19].

Conclusions

In this cohort of 427 hips managed according to a defined protocol of prolonged cast-based abduction containment and strict non-weight-bearing, 74.2% achieved modified Stulberg grade I or II at radiographic healing. Adverse radiographic outcomes occurred in 25.8% of hips, with risk increasing progressively with older age at diagnosis and greater Herring lateral pillar involvement. These factors showed the strongest associations with radiographic outcome. The findings provide protocol-specific benchmarks for radiographic prognosis and risk stratification within this treatment pathway, as well as a reference for future comparative studies of nonoperative treatment strategies.
