Violacein: a natural antibacterial agent empowered by film-forming sprays for topical applications
Authors: Kampanart Huanbutta, Pornsak Sriamornsak, Parapat Sobharaksha, Natta Thuphabut, Chaiyawat Rachagrai, Narabhorn Sangnak, Suwisit Manmuan, Tanikan Sangnim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe rise of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms poses a significant public health threat. Exploring natural compounds with antibacterial properties offers a potential solution. Violacein, derived from Chromobacterium violaceum, has shown promise for its antibacterial and antioxidant effects. This study aims to develop and evaluate a violacein-loaded film-forming spray (FFS) to enhance patient adherence through a flexible, peelable skin film, while maintaining antibacterial efficacy.MethodsThe antibacterial and antioxidant activities of violacein crude extract were evaluated. Then a central composite design was employed to optimize the FFS formulation by varying the concentration of the film-forming agent (Eudragit® RS 100), solvent ratio (ethanol), and diethyl phthalate (plasticizer). The properties of both liquid and film forms were characterized.ResultsViolacein exhibits both antibacterial and antioxidant effects. Altering the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 and the ethanol-to-water ratio impacts viscosity, drying time, and toughness, affecting the spray radius. Increasing diethyl phthalate concentration reduces stress (the force the film can withstand before deformation) and Young’s modulus (a measure of stiffness) but increases strain (the film’s ability to stretch before breaking). The optimized formulation consists of 17.41%w/w Eudragit® RS 100, 20%w/w diethyl phthalate (DEP), and a 4:1 (w/w) ethanol-to-water ratio, producing a flexible, durable film with a wide spray radius and viscosity ranging from 0.025 to 0.045 Pa·S. Importantly, the violacein-loaded FFS exhibits superior antibacterial activity compared to a commercial clindamycin solution.ConclusionThe developed violacein-loaded FFS successfully generated a flexible and durable film, with significant in vitro antibacterial activity. This evidence suggests its potential for further development as an effective topical formulation that may enhance patient adherence.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05191-4.

Introduction

Infections of the skin and subcutaneous tissues are defined by microbial invasion of the dermis and underlying tissues. In the United States, the incidence of skin and soft tissue infections, which are among the most common diseases encountered in both ambulatory and hospital settings, has risen rapidly over the past few decades [1]. Despite the fact that variousmedications have been used to treat infections of the skin and soft tissues, antibiotic resistance is becoming an increasing problem worldwide [2]. In recent years, relatively limited progress has been made in finding new and effective antibiotics, which exacerbates this dangerous condition. Recent research has focused on a group of natural antimicrobials, such as herbal and microbial extracts, because they have a high promise for treating antibiotic-resistant pathogens [3–7].

Many bacteria naturally produce antimicrobial compounds as a survival strategy to compete with other microorganisms in their environment. These bioactive substances inhibit or eliminate competing species, enabling the producing organisms to thrive in resource-limited or densely populated ecosystems [8]. Among these compounds, violacein a purple pigment synthesized by various free-living environmental bacteria such as Chromobacterium violaceum [9], Janthinobacterium lividum [10], and Duganella violaceinigra [11] has attracted considerable scientific interest. Violacein exhibits a broad spectrum of biological activities, including anticancer [12], antioxidant [13], trypanocidal [14], antifungal [15], antiviral [4], antibacterial [16], and antimalarial effects [17]. Owing to these diverse properties, violacein is increasingly being explored for commercial applications in the pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and textile industries. Structurally, violacein consists of three structural units: a 5-hydroxyindole, an oxindole, and a 2-pyrrolidone. These units are linked together to form the characteristic structure of violacein as shown in Fig. 1. Violacein has a molecular formula of C₂₀H₁₃N₃O₃ and a molecular weight of approximately 343.34 g/mol. It is known for its poor solubility in water but dissolves well in organic solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and methanol. The conjugated double bond system in its structure is responsible for its characteristic violet coloration and strong ultraviolet–visible (UV-Vis) absorption, which also facilitates its analytical detection. These structural features are closely associated with its antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anticancer properties, as demonstrated in various preclinical studies [18, 19]. In addition to active ingredients to treat infections of the skin and subcutaneous layer, topical dosage formulations that maximize therapeutic efficacy are essential. In general, ointments, creams, solutions, and gels are the topical dosage forms used to treat bacterial infections, despite their numerous limitations. For instance, semisolid preparations such as creams and ointments do not remain in contact with the skin’s surface and are easily removed by clothing or contact. Similarly, while modern formulations like hydrogels are increasingly used, they can also present challenges; they may exhibit lower mechanical stability, have longer drying times, and can be easily displaced by perspiration or friction, potentially reducing contact time with the skin. Therefore, these formulations must be administered frequently for persistent diseases or symptoms. The film forming systems (FFS) have been developed as a solution to these constraints, as an alternative to conventional topical and transdermal formulations [20, 21]. The FFS have been developed as a solution to the constraints of current topical pharmaceutical dosage forms, as an alternative to conventional topical and transdermal formulations [22]. The FFS is a non-solid dosage form, but it can create a film on the epidermis after spraying or application [23]. The forming film is transplantable, flexible, and adhesive to skin for a period of time; therefore, it is suitable for transdermal drug delivery systems [24].

This research aims to develop and evaluate a film-forming spray containing violacein for topical antibacterial application. First, Chromobacterium violaceum was incubated, and violacein was extracted. The minimal bactericidal concentration, minimal inhibitory concentration, and antioxidant activity of violacein were examined. Then, a film-forming spray was formulated by using Eudragit® S100, E100 and RS100 as film forming agent. A Design of Experiment (DoE) approach, specifically a central composite design, was applied to systematically evaluate how variations in formulation components (e.g., polymer, solvent ratio, plasticizer) influence key product properties such as viscosity, drying time, film strength, and spray radius. Finally, the optimized formulation was calculated, prepared, and evaluated.

Materials and methods

Materials

Eudragit® RS 100 (Lot. B170608591), Eudragit® S 100 (Lot. B180705205), and Eudragit® E100 (Lot. B171101671) were obtained from Evonik Nutrition & Care GmbH, Germany. Ethanol was purchased from Impress Ethanol (Bangkok, Thailand). Diethyl phthalate (DEP) (Lot. H7LYLFP) was received from TCI (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Violacein crude extract Preparation

Violacein is a bacterial pigment produced by Chromobacterium violaceum. To obtain violacein crude extracts, a culture of C. violaceum (ATCC 12472), obtained from the Department of Medical Sciences, Ministry of Public Health, Nonthaburi, Thailand, was incubated in nutrient broth at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 45 °C with agitation at 100–250 rpm for 24 to 168 h. Following incubation, the bacterial cells were harvested. The incubation parameters including temperature, duration, and agitation rate were optimized to maximize violacein production. Violacein was subsequently extracted from the culture broth using ethyl acetate in three sequential steps. For the first extraction, 60 mL of ethyl acetate was added to the culture broth. This was followed by the addition of 40 mL and 30 mL of ethyl acetate for the second and third extractions, respectively. The combined organic phases were concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Laborota 4011, Heidolph®, Germany) at 50 °C to remove residual solvent. The resulting violacein crude extract was then freeze-dried using a freeze dryer (Alpha 1–2 LD Plus, CHRIST®, UK) for 72 to 96 h. Finally, the dried violacein crude extracts were weighed and stored at 4 °C for subsequent evaluation and formulation development. The obtained violacein crude extract was identified by wavelength scanning and quantified by measuring its absorbance at 575 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV1800, Shimadzu, Japan) [25, 26]. The incubation and extraction methods were adapted from the methodology described in the previous study by Leelaudomlipi et al. [27].

Violacein crude extract characterization

Antibacterial activity

Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC6538) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCC14990), obtained from the Department of Medical Sciences, Ministry of Public Health, Nonthaburi, Thailand, were selected for testing due to their prevalence as Gram-positive bacteria on human epidermis and mucosal surfaces. The bacterial stock cultures were incubated on nutrient agar at 37 °C for 16–18 h. The bacterial suspensions were compared to a 0.5 McFarland standard, and subsequently diluted by a factor of 100.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC of the violacein crude extract was determined using the macro-broth dilution method in order to assess the appropriate dosage of the herbal extract for film-forming spray application. The microbial samples were prepared by inoculating Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis into Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 16–18 h. The incubated bacterial cultures were then diluted to achieve a turbidity equivalent to the 0.5 McFarland standard. This diluted MHB was further diluted 100-fold with 0.85% NaCl solution. Prior to testing, the violacein crude extracts were exposed to UV radiation for 2 h., and all sample preparation procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions to minimize the risk of contamination prior to antimicrobial testing. To determine the MIC, the concentrated violacein crude extract was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to create a stock solution. This stock solution was then diluted with DMSO to achieve the desired concentrations of 25.00, 12.50, 6.25, 3.13, and 1.56 mg/mL. Subsequently, 80 µL of each violacein crude extract concentration was transferred to 5 mL of the prepared MHB containing the test bacteria. DMSO was used as the negative control, sterile distilled water served as the blank control, and clindamycin at a concentration of 1 µg/mL was employed as the positive control. The violacein crude extract and microbe mixtures were thereafter subjected to incubation for a duration of 16 to 18 h at a temperature of 37 °C. The turbidity of the mixture in the test tubes following the incubation period was utilized as an indicator for assessing the proliferation of bacteria. The MIC was ascertained as the minimum concentration of violacein crude extract capable of inhibiting the development of microorganisms [5].

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MBC of the violacein crude extract was investigated by applying the cloudy mixture obtained from the MIC test to the Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) surface. DMSO was used as the negative control, sterile distilled water served as the blank control, and clindamycin at a concentration of 1 µg/mL was employed as the positive control. The inoculated agar plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 to 18 h. The absence of bacterial colonies indicated that the MBC had been reached [28].

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of violacein crude extract was assessed using 2,2’-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radicals in a radical-scavenging assay. The violacein crude extract was prepared at concentrations ranging from 7.8125 to 250 µg/mL. The gallic acid standard was used to benchmark the antioxidant activity of violacein crude extract in the concentration range of 12.5–0.1953 mg/mL. The anti-oxidation property is expressed as the IC50 value, which is the concentration of a substance that can inhibit free radical activity by 50%.

The ABTS radical-scavenging assay is a swift, straightforward, and sensitive technique for quantifying the antioxidant activity of various compounds. This study employed the technique as modified by Arumugam et al. (2014) [29]. A 7 mM solution of ABTS was prepared and stored in the dark at 25 °C for 18 h. Functional stability assessment of the extract

The violacein crude extract was stored at ambient temperature for 60 days to assess its functional stability, with a focus on bioactivity retention rather than chemical content. To evaluate efficacy-related outcomes, the following parameters were monitored on days 1, 30, and 60: physical appearance, antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, and antioxidant activity using the DPPH and ABTS assays. The antimicrobial activity was determined using the agar diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA), with the diameter of the inhibition zone recorded to evaluate the impact of storage duration on bioactivity. A clear zone method was employed to sensitively track the gradual decline in antimicrobial efficacy during the study period. Antioxidant activity was measured according to the procedures described in Sect. 2.3.2. This approach reflects a functional stability assessment, focusing on the retention of biological activity over time. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure data reliability.

FFS formulation

Preliminary study

The preliminary study aimed to explore the design space for subsequent experimental design by investigating the following parameters: the type and quantity of film-forming polymer, the ethanol-to-water ratio, and the amount of plasticizer. Three distinct polymer types, namely Eudragit® S100, E100, and RS100, were solubilized in 95% ethanol at varying concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, 10%, and 15% (w/w). The solubility and film-forming characteristics of these solutions were rigorously assessed. Subsequently, the chosen polymer was dissolved in ethanol, with concentrations ranging from 47.5% to 95% (w/w). Diethyl phthalate (DEP), a plasticizer, was incorporated into the formulation at concentrations spanning from 10% to 50% (w/w).

Design of experiment (DOE)

A central composite design (CCD) was employed to structure the experimental runs, as detailed in Table 1, to investigate the influence of three independent variables: Eudragit® RS 100 concentration (A), DEP concentration (B), and the ratio by weight of ethanol to water (C). Then 17 experimental runs (3 center points) were generated by Design Expert Program version 11 (Table S4). The design encompassed three factors, each examined at three different levels. All runs in the design of experiments (DOE) were monitored for seven parameters of the FFS products. These properties include spray radius (R1), liquid viscosity (R2), film stress (R3), film strain (R4), Young’s modulus of film (R5), film toughness (R6), and time to 50% drying (R7). Following the completion of the CCD experimental trials, partial least squares (PLS) models were constructed, and an optimization formulation was computed. FFS evaluation

Spray radius (R1)

The FFS solution sample was transferred to a spray bottle with a capacity of 30 mL. The sample was sprayed in a horizontal direction onto a white background that was A4-sized. The distance between the spray nozzle and the white background was 10 cm. The sample solution was sprayed at five distinct locations. After spraying, the spayed white background was left to dry. Once the drying was complete, the spray radius was determined. Finally, the average value was calculated based on the obtained measurements [31].

Viscosity (R2)

The rheological properties and viscosity of the FFS solution sample were assessed using a cone and plate rheometer (KINEXUS Lab+, MS603S/01, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Surrey, UK). Rheological flow curve measurements were performed at a controlled temperature of 25 °C, with the shear rate varied systematically between 0.1 and 1 s⁻¹. Subsequently, a flow curve was constructed, and the viscosity at a shear rate of 1 s− 1 was determined [23].

The optimized FFS formulation was also evaluated using amplitude sweep analysis to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) and to assess the formulation’s structural integrity under oscillatory stress. The measurements were conducted at 25 °C, with a constant angular frequency of 5 rad/s while varying the applied strain from 0.01% to 100%. This test provided insight into the formulation’s resistance to deformation and its ability to maintain structural consistency under small deformations, which is critical for film formation and application performance [32].

Physical properties of films

The physical properties of the dried films (prepared from casting method) including film stress (R3), film strain (R4), Young’s modulus (R5), toughness (R6), and ultimate tensile strength were evaluated using a texture analyzer (TA.XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, UK) equipped with a 5 kg load cell in tensile mode. The FFS solutions from all 17 central composite design (CCD) runs were cast onto glass plates and left to dry and solidify at room temperature for 18 h. After drying, uniform films were peeled and cut into rectangular specimens of 1 cm × 8 cm. The thickness of the dried films was measured and found to be in the range of 0.12–0.15 mm which can ensure consistency across all samples. Transparent adhesive tape was applied at both ends (1 cm each) to ensure grip consistency, resulting in a measurement area of 1 cm × 6 cm. Each film sample was mounted between two tensile grips, with a fixed initial gauge length (L₀) of 6 cm, and the test was performed at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/s. Time to 50% drying (R7)

The drying time of the FFS formulation was monitored in order to determine which formulation dries most rapidly, thereby facilitating the application. The method by which the drying time was ascertained was by weight change. A pristine Petri dish was placed on a four-digit electronic balance for this experiment. Following that, the liquid FFS, which had been prepared in accordance with the CCD design for a total of 17 runs, was transferred to the Petri dish via a dropper. The weight was monitored at the following time intervals: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. The weight loss over time was graphed, and the time at which a 50% reduction in weight occurred was determined and reported [31].

Antimicrobial activity of FFS loaded with Violacein crude extract

The agar-well diffusion method was utilized to examine the antimicrobial activity of an optimized violacein crude extract laden with FFS against two pathogenic bacteria in order to validate the antimicrobial properties of the FFS product [28]. A total of seven punctures were incised onto the surface of the MHA using a sterile pipette. Following that, 0.1 mL drops of different sample solutions, including a blank FFS solution, FFS solutions containing violacein crude extract at concentrations of 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/mL, clindamycin at a concentration of 25 mg/mL (positive control), distilled water (negative control), and the commercial product of clindamycin, were dispensed into these punctures. Following the drying process (about 30 min), the samples went through incubation at 37 ˚C for a duration of 16–18 h. The diameters of the inhibition zones (areas without bacterial growth) were measured in millimeters and recorded. All samples were tested in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of variance test and Levene’s test for the homogeneity of variance were performed using SPSS version 10.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Post-hoc testing (p < 0.05) of the multiple comparisons was performed in the dissolution test result by performing either the Scheffé or Games-Howell test, depending on whether Levene’s test for homogeneity is insignificant or significant.

Results and discussion

Violacein crude extract Preparation

The optimal cultivation conditions for Chromobacterium violaceum American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 12,472 that yielded the highest production of violacein crude extract were determined to be an agitation rate of 200 revolutions per minute (rpm), an incubation temperature of 30 °C, and an incubation period of 3 days. Under these conditions, the maximum dry weight of violacein crude extract obtained was 2.59 ± 0.02 g per mL of culture medium, as shown in Tables S1–S3. The violacein crude extract obtained after rotary evaporation and subsequent freeze-drying was observed to be dry and powdery in appearance, with no detectable odor of organic solvent. The absence of any residual solvent odor, together with the complete solidification of the extract, indicates that ethyl acetate used during the extraction process was effectively eliminated.

Agitation rate was found to significantly influence violacein production. The highest yield was observed at 200 rpm (2.87 ± 0.03 g/mL for dry crude extract and 0.02 ± 0.01 µg/mL of violacein extract concentration), (Table S1). These results suggest that adequate aeration and mixing are critical factors for violacein biosynthesis, likely due to enhanced oxygen transfer and uniform nutrient availability throughout the culture [34]. Temperature also played a key role in violacein production. The maximum yield was observed at 30 °C (2.14 ± 0.01 g/mL for dry crude extract and 0.02 ± 0.01 µg/mL of violacein extract concentration), while production decreased considerably at 25 °C (1.51 ± 0.01 g/mL for dry crude extract) and was nearly undetectable at 40 °C (0.04 ± 0.02 g/mL) (Table S2). These findings indicate that violacein biosynthesis is highly sensitive to temperature, with optimal production occurring at 30 °C. This observation is consistent with a previous study on C. violaceum strain VB.1, which reported that the bacterium can grow within a temperature range of 25–37 °C, with 30 °C being the most favorable for both growth and violacein production. In addition, the incubation period had a pronounced effect on violacein production. The peak yield was achieved after 3 days of incubation (2.59 ± 0.02 g/mL for dry crude extract and 0.03 ± 0.01 µg/mL of violacein extract concentration), after which a gradual decline was observed. This reduction in yield at extended incubation times may be attributed to nutrient depletion, metabolic shifts, or degradation of the pigment during prolonged cultivation (Table S3) [27].

In comparison to the study by Sasidharan et al. [35], which reported a yield of 0.6 g violacein per gram of dry biomass under conditions of pH 7, 25 °C, 200 rpm, and 24 h incubation, the present study achieved a significantly higher yield. The enhanced yield can be attributed to the longer incubation period, higher temperature, and potential strain-specific metabolic advantages of C. violaceum ATCC 12,472 over other Chromobacterium strains [36].

The violacein crude extract was identified using ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy. The spectrum exhibited strong absorption in the visible region, with a prominent peak observed around 575 nm, which is consistent with the characteristic absorbance range of violacein. This result aligns with previous findings reported by Antonyraj Matharasi Perianaika Anahas and colleagues, who observed strong absorption spectra between 500 and 700 nm, with a maximum absorbance at 575 nm specifically for Chromobacterium violaceum. The spectral data confirm the presence of violacein and further validate the efficacy of the extraction and identification process employed in this study [26].

Antibacterial activity of Violacein crude extract

The MIC of violacein crude extracts was determined by broth dilution, and their MBC was measured by subculturing the broths used for MIC determination onto fresh agar plates. The MIC and MBC of the extract against Staphylococcus epidermidis were both 0.5 mg/mL. And the MIC and MBC of the extract against Staphylococcus aureus were 0.25 mg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively. The findings may suggest that violacein crude extracts exhibit potent antimicrobial properties against Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis. Furthermore, this result implies that violacein crude extracts could demonstrate greater efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus in comparison to Staphylococcus epidermidis. The MIC and MBC appear high compared to purified antimicrobial agents. However, these values reflect the use of a crude violacein extract, which contains a mixture of active and inactive components, thus requiring higher concentrations to achieve antibacterial effects. These outcomes are consistent with a prior report that showed the antibacterial properties of violacein against Staphylococcus aureus and other gram-positive bacteria [9, 37].

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant capacity of the violacein crude extract was evaluated using both ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging assays, as presented in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. The calculated IC50 values defined as the concentration required to achieve 50% free radical inhibition were 98.30 µg/mL for the ABTS assay and 177.51 µg/mL for the DPPH assay, determined using a four-parameter logistic (4PL) model. These values indicate that the extract possesses a moderate level of antioxidant activity, particularly when benchmarked against the standard antioxidant compound gallic acid, which demonstrated significantly lower IC50 values of 0.47 µg/mL (ABTS) and 1.18 µg/mL (DPPH). The stronger scavenging effect observed in the ABTS assay compared to DPPH suggests that the violacein crude extract may be more effective in quenching aqueous-phase radicals, as ABTS is more suitable for both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants, whereas DPPH primarily evaluates hydrophobic antioxidant capacity. The data also exhibit a dose-dependent increase in antioxidant activity, supporting the functional potential of the extract in redox modulation.

The observed antioxidant activity of the violacein crude extract may be attributable to phenolic compounds previously reported in similar extracts, known for their hydrogen-donating and radical-scavenging properties [38, 39]. However, since the exact phenolic profile of the crude extract has not been chemically characterized in this study, further phytochemical analysis is essential to confirm the presence and contribution of specific antioxidant constituents. Additionally, these results suggest potential applications of the extract in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical formulations, especially where oxidative stress mitigation is relevant. Future work should aim to isolate and identify active compounds to better understand their mechanism of action and optimize their application in targeted delivery systems or stability-enhancing formulations.

Stability of the extract

To monitor the stability of the violacein crude extract, its physical appearance, antimicrobial activity, and antioxidant activity were examined on days 1, 30, and 60. As shown in Table 3, a notable divergence in stability was observed between the two biological activities. The antioxidant capacity, evaluated via DPPH and ABTS assays, remained highly stable, with only a minor reduction of 2.2–2.5% over the 60-day period. In contrast, the antimicrobial activity against both bacterial strains showed a gradual but significant decline. By day 60, the efficacy had decreased by approximately 23.8% for Staphylococcus aureus and 13.2% for Staphylococcus epidermidis. This discrepancy strongly suggests that different compounds within the crude extract are responsible for these two effects. The consistent antioxidant activity is likely attributable to chemically robust molecules, such as phenolic compounds. Bacterial extracts, including those from Chromobacterium species, are known to contain various phenolics that are stable and potent radical scavengers [38]. On the other hand, the antimicrobial activity is primarily driven by violacein and potentially other more labile secondary metabolites. While some studies report violacein as being relatively stable across certain pH and temperature ranges, it is also known to be susceptible to degradation from environmental factors like prolonged light exposure and oxidation [39]. Therefore, the observed decline in antibacterial potency is most likely due to the gradual degradation of these less stable antimicrobial components, while the more resilient phenolic compounds persist, maintaining the extract’s high antioxidant capacity. This highlights the complex nature of crude extracts and supports the need for future chemical characterization to identify the specific molecules responsible for each bioactivity.

FFS formulation

The preliminary study was conducted on the FFS formulation in order to identify any significant excipients, solvents, or factors that influence the properties of the FFS products. The concentration of Eudragit® RS 100, the ratio of ethanol to water, and the DEP concentration were determined as critical variables that significantly impacted the physical characteristics of FFS in both solution and film. As shown in Table S1, these critical parameters were thus varied in accordance with the experimental design of the CCD. Furthermore, the outcomes of every CCD experimental run are detailed in Table S1.

Following the completion of all experimental runs based on the design of experiments (DOE), Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression models were constructed, as presented in Table 4. These models quantitatively illustrate the influence of three critical formulation variables—Eudragit® RS 100 concentration (A), diethyl phthalate (DEP, B), and the ethanol-to-water ratio (C), on seven key response parameters: spray radius (R₁), viscosity (R₂), film stress (R₃), film strain (R₄), Young’s modulus (R₅), film toughness (R₆), and time to 50% drying (R₇). Each equation encapsulates the strength and direction of these relationships, while accompanying statistical metrics—p-value, F-value, adjusted R², and predicted R²—evaluate the models’ significance and predictive robustness. A detailed interpretation of each model is provided in the subsequent sections.

Spray radius (R1)

The association between the spray radius of the FFS (R1) and the formulation factors is depicted by the PLS model and a contour plot, as shown in Table 4; Fig. 3a, respectively. The spray radius was found to be influenced only by the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 (A) and the ratio of ethanol to water (C), with a linear relationship observed between these variables (p-value = 0.0045). The spray radius was shown to decrease with an increase in the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100, whereas a higher amount of ethanol resulted in an extended spray radius. The reason for this phenomenon is that the polymer Eudragit® RS 100 increase in viscosity of the formulation, leading to a reduction in the spray radius [28, 31]. Ethanol functions as a solvent, hence reducing the viscosity of the formulation. Consequently, this leads to an increase in the spray radius [31].

Viscosity (R2)

Table 4; Fig. 3b illustrate the impact of formulation parameters on the viscosity of the formulation (R2). The relationship between the variables was quadratic, with only the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 (A) and the ratio of ethanol to water (C) having significant effects on the viscosity (p-value < 0.0001). The findings are consistent with the outcome seen for the spray radius (R1), indicating that an increased concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 and a reduced proportion of ethanol in the solvent lead to a decrease in the viscosity of the formulation.

Figure 4a depicts the flow curve of the optimized FFS formulation, showing the relationship between shear viscosity and shear rate. The curve demonstrates a clear shear-thinning behavior across the entire measured range (0.001–0.1 s⁻¹), characterized by a continuous and monotonic decline in viscosity with increasing shear rate. This behavior is typical of pseudoplastic materials and indicates the progressive alignment and disentanglement of polymer chains under shear. This is likely attributable to the semi-rigid, water-insoluble nature of Eudragit® RS 100, which forms loosely entangled polymer networks at rest. Under shear, these chains disentangle and align in the flow direction, reducing resistance and thus lowering viscosity. This shear-thinning behavior is beneficial for topical applications: the lower viscosity under shear facilitates easy spraying, while the higher viscosity at rest promotes uniform film formation and reduces runoff or dripping [40].

Figure 4b shows the amplitude sweep analysis, illustrating how the storage modulus (G′) and complex modulus (G*) vary with increasing oscillatory strain (γ*). The data reveal a Linear Viscoelastic Region (LVR) up to approximately 0.01% strain, where both moduli remain nearly constant. Beyond this point, both G′ and G* begin to decrease markedly, indicating structural degradation of the internal polymer network and a transition to non-linear viscoelastic behavior. The steep drop in G′, in particular, reflects the breakdown of elastic structure and suggests a transition toward a more fluid-like response. This rheological profile confirms that the film maintains its mechanical integrity under small deformations but yields under higher strain desirable for topical film formation, allowing resilience during application and flexibility post-drying.

Physical properties of films (R3-R6)

Figure 5 depicts the graphical representations that demonstrate the relationship between the formulation factor and various physical attributes of films, including film stress (R3), film strain (R4), and the Young’s modulus of film (R5). The relationship between the variables is characterized by linearity, with only the concentration of DEP exhibiting a statistically significant effect on them, as presented in Table 4. Increased DEP concentrations decreased both the film stress and the Young’s modulus. However, the inclusion of DEP in the formulation increased the film strain. This implies that DEP functions as a plasticizer, resulting in a softer and more flexible film. Plasticizers commonly function by enhancing the elastic properties of the polymer chains and diminishing intermolecular interactions by introducing free volume into the polymer matrix. As a consequence of this decline in intermolecular forces, both film stress and Young’s modulus decrease. On the contrary, the incorporation of DEP into the formulation resulted in an increase in film strain. This suggests that DEP improves the film’s resistance to deformation prior to failure when subjected to stress. The enhanced ductility induced by DEP facilitates the elongation of polymer chains, leading to an increased strain on the film [41].

In the context of film toughness (R6), it is noteworthy that the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 (A) and the ethanol-to-water ratio (C) exert significant influence on the toughness of film, as discerned from the PLS model (Table 4) and the contour plot (Fig. 3C) (p value = 0.0024). To enhance film toughness, it is advisable to elevate both the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 and the proportion of water in the solvent. This might be because Eudragit® RS 100 is a high-molecular-weight polymer that forms a strong intermolecular network within the film matrix. Increasing the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 enhances the density and strength of this network, leading to improved film toughness [42]. On the other hand, water acts as a plasticizer for Eudragit® RS 100, enhancing its flexibility and chain mobility. A higher proportion of water in the solvent promotes the plasticization effect, allowing the polymer chains to slide past each other more easily. This improved chain mobility contributes to the film’s ability to absorb and dissipate energy, further enhancing its toughness [43]. The synergistic effect of Eudragit® RS 100 concentration and the ethanol-to-water ratio on film toughness suggests that optimizing both factors can lead to the development of films with exceptional mechanical properties.

Time to 50% drying (R7)

The correlation between the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 (A) and the ratio of ethanol to water (C) on the film formation time or time to 50% drying is shown in Fig. 3D (contour plot) and Table 4 (PLS model). Film formation time was considerably prolonged when the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 was increased (p-value = 0.0005), whereas it was extensively shortened when the ethanol ratio in the formulation was raised (p-value = 0.0332). This might be because Eudragit® RS 100, a water-insoluble polymer, forms a denser matrix when present in higher concentrations, hindering the evaporation of solvents and prolonging film formation [44]. Conversely, ethanol, a volatile solvent, facilitates solvent evaporation and accelerates film formation [31]. Furthermore, the interaction between Eudragit® RS 100 and ethanol could play a role. At lower ethanol concentrations, Eudragit® RS 100 chains may be more tightly entangled, impeding solvent diffusion and delaying film formation. However, as the ethanol concentration increases, the polymer chains become more solvated and mobile, allowing for faster solvent evaporation and quicker film formation [28].

Optimization of FFS formulation

After all CCD experimental runs were completed and all PLS models were generated, the optimized FFS formulation was calculated, prepared, and evaluated. The optimized FFS formulation for the concentration of Eudragit® RS 100 (A), DEP concentration (B), and the ratio of ethanol to water (C) were found to be 17.41% (w/w), 20.00% (w/w), and a 4:1 ratio, respectively, which provided a desirability value of 0.566 out of 1. The predicted responses from the PLS regression models were compared with the actual test values, and the prediction ability percentage of each response was calculated as presented in Table 5. The partial least squares regression models from all responses demonstrated high to low prediction ability, with percentages ranging from 34.80% to 81.25%. The high prediction ability was associated with R1 and R7, which were the spray radius and the time to 50% drying of the formulation, respectively. This might be because the spray radius and time to 50% drying dry are directly influenced by the formulation composition (polymer concentration and solvent), while mechanical property responses are also influenced by factors such as environmental conditions and measurement techniques. The lowest predictive ability percentage was R5 (Young’s modulus of film), which might be explained by the inherent variability in the measurement of mechanical properties, such as Young’s modulus, which can be affected by factors such as sample preparation and testing conditions. Additionally, the Young’s modulus of the film is a complex property that is influenced by both the polymer and solvent components of the formulation, making it more challenging to predict accurately using PLS regression [45].

The impact of violacein extract (2.5 mg/mL) on the rheological and physicochemical characteristics of the optimized film-forming spray (FFS) formulation is detailed in Table 6. The addition of the extract resulted in notable alterations across several parameters, reflecting its interaction with the formulation matrix. The spray radius (R₁) exhibited a modest reduction from 1.60 to 1.57 cm (− 1.88%), indicating minimal influence on sprayability [46]. In contrast, the viscosity (R₂) increased substantially by 43.79% (from 0.0169 Pa·s to 0.0243 Pa·s), suggesting enhanced internal resistance to flow, likely due to increased molecular interactions or the structuring effect of the extract. This elevated viscosity may improve film cohesion and uniformity upon application [47]. Conversely, mechanical performance metrics demonstrated mixed outcomes. The film stress (R₃) and film strain (R₄) declined by 19.26% and 22.10%, respectively, implying a reduction in tensile strength and elongation capacity. These changes point toward a mild softening effect, possibly due to disruption of the polymeric matrix or plasticizing action of the extract components [48]. Despite this, the Young’s modulus (R₅) showed a slight increase of 2.96%, denoting a marginal enhancement in stiffness, potentially attributed to specific interactions or alignment between polymer chains and the incorporated extract [49]. Remarkably, the film toughness (R₆) increased significantly by 63.56% (from 13.94 to 22.80 MJ/m³), reflecting a marked improvement in the film’s ability to absorb energy before rupture. This suggests enhanced durability and mechanical resilience, particularly beneficial for skin application under mechanical stress [50]. One of the most pronounced changes was observed in the time to 50% drying (R₇), which decreased dramatically by 58.49% (from 0.053 min to 0.022 min). This accelerated drying can be attributed to potential modifications in solvent evaporation dynamics or better film-forming capacity due to the extract’s influence [51]. Collectively, these findings underscore that violacein extract incorporation enhances key functional properties, particularly viscosity, toughness, and drying time while preserving acceptable mechanical attributes, making it a promising bioactive additive for topical FFS systems.

Antimicrobial activity of FFS

Figure 6 illustrates the antibacterial activity of the optimized film-forming spray (FFS) formulations containing various concentrations of violacein crude extract (2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/mL) against Staphylococcus epidermidis and Staphylococcus aureus. All violacein-loaded FFS formulations demonstrated clear zones of inhibition ranging from approximately 10 to 11 mm in diameter, indicating effective suppression of bacterial growth. Notably, no significant difference in antibacterial activity was observed among the three violacein concentrations, suggesting that even the lowest tested concentration (2.5 mg/mL) is sufficient to achieve antibacterial efficacy in the FFS system. This dosage corresponds to approximately 10–20 times the MBC (0.5 mg/mL), a strategy employed to account for potential drug loss, variations in film thickness, and diffusion limitations imposed by the polymeric matrix. These factors are known to affect drug availability at the site of action in film-based delivery systems, thus justifying the higher incorporated concentrations [52]. In contrast, the commercial clindamycin solution (10 mg/mL), commonly used for topical acne treatment, failed to produce a measurable inhibition zone under the same experimental conditions. This may be attributed to insufficient contact time and rapid diffusion or absorption into the agar surface. Unlike film-forming systems, liquid dosage forms tend to have lower residence time, reducing their ability to maintain prolonged antimicrobial action [20].

Conclusions

The study’s findings indicate that Chromobacterium violaceum can be effectively utilized to extract violacein with a very high yield, given the specific conditions employed in this study. The extract obtained showed both antioxidant and antibacterial properties. The FFS, formulated using Eudragit® RS100, DEP, and a solvent mixture of water and ethanol, was developed to achieve broad spray coverage, appropriate mechanical film characteristics, and rapid film drying following spraying. The optimized FFS was used to load the violacein crude extract, which resulted in a product that showed superior antibacterial action compared to the topical antibacterial commercial product. The study’s findings are particularly significant given the growing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Violacein, a natural compound with potent antibacterial activity, could potentially serve as an alternative to conventional antibiotics that are becoming increasingly ineffective. The development of effective FFS topical formulations of violacein could revolutionize the treatment of bacterial skin infections, a common and often difficult-to-treat skin condition. However, further studies are warranted to evaluate the long-term safety, long-term stability and efficacy of these formulations in clinical settings. Moreover, chemical characterization of the violacein crude extract has to be conducted in future studies to identify and quantify the various compounds present in the crude extract.
