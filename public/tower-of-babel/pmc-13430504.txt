Scientific assessment of the 2024 post‐market environmental monitoring report on the cultivation of genetically modified maize MON 810 in the EU
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Antoine Messéan, Yann Devos, Fernando Álvarez, Ana M. Camargo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt the request of the European Commission, EFSA assessed the 2024 post‐market environmental monitoring (PMEM) report on the cultivation of genetically modified maize MON 810, which expresses the Cry1Ab protein. Using a weight‐of‐evidence approach and considering information provided by the consent holder and the Competent Authorities of Portugal and Spain, EFSA concluded that the PMEM report provides no evidence of unexpected adverse effects on human and animal health, or on the environment associated with the cultivation of maize MON 810 in 2024. Refuge compliance was generally high in Spain and fully achieved in Portugal. However, uncertainty remains in situations where clusters of small maize MON 810 fields may collectively exceed the 5‐ha threshold requiring refuge implementation. No evidence of practical resistance to Cry1Ab was identified in European corn borer populations sampled in north‐eastern Spain in 2024. Nevertheless, the consistently low Cry1Ab susceptibility observed in Mediterranean corn borer populations from this area between 2022 and 2024, together with unexpected field damage reported in Girona in 2021 as well as indications of resistance alleles detected in 2024, indicate that a decrease in susceptibility cannot be excluded. EFSA also considers that the current resistance monitoring strategy lacks sufficient sensitivity for the early detection of resistance evolution. General surveillance activities, including farmer questionnaires in Spain and annual literature searches, did not identify unanticipated adverse effects. However, uncertainty remains regarding the potential exposure of highly sensitive non‐target Lepidoptera to maize MON 810 pollen due to the lack of implementation data on isolation distances toward protected habitats. EFSA also identified methodological and reporting shortcomings in both case‐specific monitoring and general surveillance, while welcoming the continued efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain to address data gaps and support refinement of monitoring and risk management measures. After more than two decades of cultivation without evidence of unexpected adverse environmental effects, EFSA considers that general surveillance could be streamlined to focus on two core elements: (1) a strengthened farmer complaint system, complemented where appropriate by existing plant health and pest monitoring networks; and (2) transparent and comprehensive annual literature searches.

SUMMARY

At the request of the European Commission (Directorate‐General for Health and Food Safety), EFSA assessed the 2024 post‐market environmental monitoring (PMEM) report on the cultivation of genetically modified (GM) maize MON 810, which expresses the Cry1Ab protein. The report covers both case‐specific monitoring (CSM), consisting of insect resistance management (IRM), and general surveillance (GS). EFSA's assessment also took into account Member State comments and additional information provided by the Competent Authorities of Spain, including reports on teosinte, outcomes of the stakeholder meeting held on 15 December 2025 and updates on actions taken by the Competent Authorities of Spain in response to previous EFSA recommendations.

CSM comprised: (1) data on compliance with refuge requirements in Spain and Portugal; and (2) insect resistance monitoring of European corn borer (ECB) and Mediterranean corn borer (MCB) populations collected in north‐eastern Spain, the main area of concern for resistance evolution due to the high‐adoption rate of maize MON 810.

Refuge compliance was reported to be generally high in Spain and complete in Portugal. In Spain, 96.8% of farmers cultivating more than 5 ha of maize MON 810 reported implementing a refuge. However, uncertainty remains regarding refuge implementation in situations where clusters of nearby small maize MON 810 fields may collectively exceed the 5‐ha threshold. EFSA reiterates the importance of full compliance with refuge requirements, particularly in areas of high maize MON 810 adoption, and welcomes the efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain to identify such clusters and strengthen farmer awareness and communication in those areas.

Resistance monitoring was based on diagnostic concentration bioassays using F1 progeny of field‐collected larvae. In 2024, corrected moult inhibition values for ECB were in line with the expected > 99% threshold. For MCB, however, values remained below the expected threshold in all three populations tested. These findings continue the pattern observed since 2022 and, together with the unexpected MCB damage reported in Girona in 2021 and indications of resistance alleles in individuals collected in 2024, mean that reduced Cry1Ab susceptibility in MCB populations from north‐eastern Spain cannot be excluded.

EFSA identified several methodological limitations affecting the robustness and sensitivity of resistance monitoring. These include insufficient sample sizes, high pre‐imaginal mortality during rearing, weaknesses in assay design and reporting, uncertainty regarding the appropriateness of the diagnostic concentration used for MCB, and shortcomings in confirmatory testing. Particularly for ECB, the monitoring strategy remains insufficiently sensitive to detect the recommended 3% resistance allele frequency. EFSA reiterates the need to increase monitoring sensitivity and precision, for instance, through the use of more sensitive approaches such as periodic F2 screens. In this context, EFSA welcomes the applicant's confirmation that F2 screens will replace annual diagnostic assays for MCB populations in north‐eastern Spain from 2025 onwards, starting with Girona, but maintains that ECB populations should also be subjected to resistance allele frequency estimation.

The situation in Girona remains of particular concern. Monitoring data from 2022 to 2024 show persistently low susceptibility of MCB populations from this area, and the 2024 follow‐up assays provided additional indications consistent with the possible presence of resistance alleles. EFSA considers the follow‐up actions implemented in 2024 insufficient and reiterates the need to strengthen both resistance monitoring and communication in Girona, including targeted field scouting and efforts to select a resistant MCB population for further characterisation.

The GS dataset included farmer questionnaires conducted in Spain and data collected through a farmer complaint system as well as annual literature searches. No farmer questionnaire was conducted in Portugal, where the consent holder relied instead on information provided by the Competent Authorities. Based on the information available, no unanticipated adverse effects of maize MON 810 cultivation on human or animal health or the environment were identified in 2024.

EFSA acknowledges improvements made to the farmer questionnaire in recent years but notes that important limitations remain regarding its scope and representativeness. In particular, the questionnaire still provides limited information on aspects relevant to environmental risk assessment, such as the surrounding environment, farm‐level uptake patterns and changes in the receiving environment. EFSA also notes that Portuguese data remain less informative and less comparable than those collected in Spain.

The farmer complaint system may provide a useful complement to laboratory‐based resistance monitoring as well as support the detection of potential unanticipated effects, but its current design does not yet allow EFSA to assess its effectiveness. The requirement to report damages ‘higher than expected’ remains too vague, and the system lacks sufficient information on thresholds, communication mechanisms and farmer training. Strengthening farmer awareness, improving reporting criteria and enhancing integration with existing plant health and pest monitoring networks would increase its overall value within PMEM.

The literature search did not identify any new hazards or modified exposure pathways that would alter previous risk assessment conclusions. Nevertheless, EFSA identified several areas for improvement, including the transparency of article selection, justification of exclusions, clarification of uncertain cases and explicit inclusion of ‘teosinte’ as search term in future search strategies.

Regarding teosinte, no new evidence was provided in the 2024 PMEM report that would substantially alter previous EFSA assessments. Available information from Spain indicates that teosinte remains locally persistent but geographically contained, mainly within cultivated fields and that current management measures continue to limit its spread. EFSA welcomes the ongoing research project in Spain and France addressing remaining uncertainties related to hybridisation and hybrid fitness, and reiterates the importance of continued data exchange, monitoring and targeted research.

With respect to non‐target Lepidoptera (NTL), uncertainty remains regarding the potential exposure of highly sensitive species to maize MON 810 pollen in protected habitats, as no information was reported on the implementation of isolation distances in 2024. EFSA welcomes the efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain to improve the characterisation of the distribution of sensitive species, relevant protected areas and the management measures applied in protected areas hosting NTL species of interest. EFSA considers that these activities can help refine future risk assessments and support the evaluation of whether additional more targeted risk mitigation measures may be warranted.

EFSA also acknowledges the limitations of existing environmental monitoring networks as standalone GS tool for maize MON 810. However, EFSA considers that plant health and pest monitoring systems may provide useful complementary information and encourages continued work to explore how these sources might better support PMEM.

After more than two decades of cultivation without evidence of unexpected adverse environmental effects, EFSA considers that GS could be streamlined to focus on two core elements: (1) a strengthened farmer complaint system, complemented where appropriate by existing plant health and pest monitoring networks; and (2) transparent and comprehensive annual literature searches.

Using a weight‐of‐evidence approach, EFSA concludes that the 2024 PMEM report provides no evidence of unexpected adverse effects associated with the cultivation of maize MON 810. However, uncertainties remain regarding: (1) the Cry1Ab susceptibility of MCB populations from north‐eastern Spain; and (2) the potential exposure of highly sensitive NTL species to maize MON 810 pollen in the absence of implementation data on isolation distances.

EFSA also identified methodological and reporting shortcomings in both CSM and GS that should be addressed in future annual PMEM reports. Several recommendations from previous assessments remain unaddressed, although EFSA acknowledges the constructive efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain, notably in relation to refuge compliance, teosinte management, environmental monitoring networks and measures relevant to NTL protection.

EFSA considers that priority should be given to the following actions: Increase the sensitivity of insect resistance monitoring, particularly for ECB and MCB populations in north‐eastern Spain;Strengthen resistance monitoring and communication efforts in Girona;Revise the remedial action plan to clearly specify measures to be implemented in the event of confirmed resistance;Evaluate the need and feasibility of isolation distances in high maize MON 810 cultivation areas near protected habitats hosting sensitive non‐target lepidopteran species; andHarmonise farmer questionnaires across Spain and Portugal to ensure consistency in data collection.

INTRODUCTION

Genetically modified (GM) maize MON 810 expresses the Cry1Ab protein from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), providing protection against specific lepidopteran pests, notably the European corn borer (ECB) (Ostrinia nubilalis) and Mediterranean corn borer (MCB) (Sesamia nonagrioides), which remain among the most damaging maize pests in Europe.

The cultivation of maize MON 810 is authorised in the European Union (EU) under Directive 90/220/EEC (Commission Decision 98/294/EC). 1 Since 2003, the event has been incorporated into multiple maize varieties. In 2024, maize MON 810 was cultivated on 69,411 ha in Spain and 906 ha in Portugal (70,317 ha in total), representing a ~46% increase compared with 2023 (48,225 ha), alongside a 13% increase in Spain's total maize area (DGAV, 2025; MAPA, 2024).

To delay resistance evolution in target pests, the consent holder (Monsanto Europe S.A. 2 ) has implemented an insect resistance management (IRM) plan, initially based on the EuropaBio harmonised plan for single lepidopteran‐active Bt maize events (Alcalde et al., 2007) and updated in 2025 (Appendix 6). 3 This plan relies on the high‐dose/refuge strategy (Gould, 1998; Tabashnik et al., 2013), combining high Bt expression to eliminate heterozygotes with structured non‐Bt maize refuges that maintain susceptible pest populations.

Resistance monitoring and refuge compliance are central to IRM. Monitoring aims to identify early signs of reduced susceptibility to Cry1Ab, enabling timely mitigation. The consent holder applies two complementary approaches: (1) laboratory bioassays on field‐collected ECB and MCB populations; and (2) a farmer complaint system capturing potential loss of product efficacy. Effective refuge compliance remains critical, as resistance cases in other Bt crops have been linked to partial or inadequate implementation (Tabashnik et al., 2023; Tabashnik & Carrière, 2017). Farmer training and communication are therefore essential components of IRM (Andow, 2008; Bates et al., 2005; Glaser & Matten, 2003; Head & Greenplate, 2012).

The consent holder initiated general surveillance (GS) voluntarily in 2005, ahead of mandatory post‐market environmental monitoring (PMEM) under Directive 2001/18/EC and Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. GS aims to detect unanticipated adverse effects and includes farmer questionnaires and systematic literature searches.

Since 2005, the consent holder has annually reported both case‐specific monitoring (CSM) (including IRM and resistance monitoring) and GS results. EFSA has evaluated annual PMEM reports for the 2009–2023 seasons (EFSA, 2018, 2019a, 2020, 2021, 2022a, 2023, 2024, 2025; EFSA GMO Panel, 2011a, 2012a, 2013, 2014a, 2015a, 2015b, 2016, 2017). Overall, findings indicate no greater risk to human or animal health or the environment compared with conventional maize. However, EFSA has consistently identified methodological limitations in both CSM and GS and issued recommendations to improve monitoring design and reporting, some of which have been progressively incorporated in recent reports (see also EFSA, 2015a).

Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

On 21 November 2025, the European Commission (DG SANTE) received the annual PMEM report for the 2024 growing season of maize MON 810 (hereafter referred to as the ‘2024 PMEM report’) from the consent holder, covering the period July 2024 to June 2025.

On 30 January 2026, EFSA was mandated to evaluate the findings of the monitoring activities, taking into account Member State comments and, where relevant, to assess any changes in monitoring methodology.

On 27 February 2026, EFSA was further requested to consider additional information provided by the Competent Authorities of Spain, including reports on the presence and management of teosinte in 2025, outcomes of the stakeholder meeting held on 15 December 2025, and updates on the actions taken or explored by Spain in response to EFSA's previous recommendations.

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

Data

EFSA based this statement on the 2024 PMEM report, 4 comments from EU Member States, and information provided by the Competent Authorities of Spain, including reports on teosinte, stakeholder discussions and actions taken or explored by Spain in response to EFSA's previous recommendations. Further information on CSM was also provided by the consent holder upon request.

Methodologies

EFSA evaluated the evidence and monitoring methods presented in the 2024 PMEM report in line with Annex VII of Directive 2001/18/EC and relevant guidance on PMEM of GM plants (EFSA GMO Panel, 2011b).

The assessment of the literature search followed EFSA methodological principles (EFSA, 2010, 2019b), and the overall evaluation applied a weight‐of‐evidence approach (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2017). Comments from EU Member States were also considered and are addressed in Annex A.

ASSESSMENT

Case‐specific monitoring

Compliance with refuge requirements 5

Consent holder's assessment

In 2024, 250 farmers growing maize MON 810 in Spain completed the farmer questionnaire that included questions assessing compliance with refuge requirements. Overall, 97% reported compliance: 196 farmers implemented refuges, while 46 were exempt due to cultivating < 5 ha of maize MON 810. However, eight farmers cultivating > 5 ha of maize MON 810 did not implement refuges, often citing lack of perceived need or relying on neighbouring non‐Bt fields.

In Portugal, no farmer questionnaires were completed. Instead, the consent holder relied on inspections performed by the Competent Authorities of Portugal on 10 farms where maize MON 810 was grown, which reported full compliance (DGAV, 2025).

Based on these data, the consent holder concluded that refuge compliance was high and proposed linking refuge implementation to direct payments under the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) or other national rules.

Information provided by Spain

The Competent Authorities of Spain reported increased stakeholder engagement and awareness‐raising activities (e.g. training, workshops, communication tools) to promote compliance with refuge requirements.

The exact location of maize MON 810 plots is recorded annually through CAP questionnaires managed by the Fondo Español de Garantía Agraria (FEGA). Spain plans to do a historical analysis of these data to identify recurrent clusters of small maize MON 810 fields (< 5 ha) and to target there awareness‐raising actions, including practical field sessions, to improve refuge implementation.

Furthermore, Spain indicated that additional information on refuge compliance may be collected through inspections conducted under other GMO‐related control activities.

EFSA's assessment

EFSA considers that refuge compliance in Spain was generally high but not complete (96.8% for farms growing > 5 ha of maize MON 810). Uncertainty remains in clustered areas where individual small fields collectively exceed the 5‐ha threshold, in which case refuges should also be implemented.

EFSA notes the practical constraints associated with implementing coordinated (community) refuges, in particular the absence of a clear legal framework and the need for effective cooperation among multiple farmers. In this context, EFSA welcomes the efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain to raise awareness and to identify recurrent clusters of small maize MON 810 fields, as these actions can help target areas where refuge compliance may be more challenging.

EFSA acknowledges that refuge compliance cannot be attributed solely to farmers, as decisions to implement non‐Bt refuges are also influenced by external factors, including the roles and actions of other stakeholders such as regulatory authorities and seed companies (Reisig & Dentzman, 2026). Ensuring full compliance therefore requires strengthened coordination and collaboration among all actors involved.

Conclusion on refuge requirement compliance

Despite the overall high level refuge compliance in 2024, EFSA emphasises the need to achieve full compliance with refuge requirements and encourages further efforts to strengthen farmer awareness, particularly in regions with high maize MON 810 adoption and in clusters of small fields exceeding 5 ha. Such progress relies on strengthened coordination and collaboration among all actors involved, given their shared role in supporting effective refuge implementation.

Insect resistance monitoring 6

Consent holder's assessment

Following the IRM plan, resistance monitoring in 2024 focused on north‐eastern Spain (Ebro basin), where maize MON 810 adoption has averaged around 60% in the last years (Appendix B). Populations of ECB and MCB were collected in Huesca (Zone 1) and Girona (Zone 2), and MCB also in Navarra (Zone 3). Insect collection included fields in Girona close to the location where unexpected MCB damage was reported in 2021 (Appendix 7). Susceptibility to Cry1Ab was assessed using diagnostic concentration and plant bioassays (Table 1).

European corn borer monitoring Field sampling and laboratory rearing

Field sampling and laboratory rearing

Field sampling and laboratory rearing

In 2024, 458 late‐instar ECB larvae were collected from four sites in north‐eastern Spain (refuge areas or non‐Bt maize fields), while 32 additional sites yielded insufficient larvae. The collected larvae were reared in the laboratory following a standardised protocol (Thieme et al., 2018), and 166 (36.2%) reached adulthood, with their F1 progeny tested for Cry1Ab susceptibility. The estimated detection limit 7 for resistance alleles was 7.8%.

A laboratory‐maintained susceptible population, originally obtained from the Institut National de la Recherche Agronomique (INRA, France) and maintained under laboratory conditions for over 8 years, was used as a control in the bioassays. This population had been reared without exposure to maize MON 810 or the Cry1Ab protein. bMonitoring assays

bMonitoring assays

Monitoring assays

The following bioassays were performed (Table 1): (1) a diagnostic bioassay to detect reduced susceptibility to Cry1Ab; (2) a plant tissue bioassay using maize MON 810 and non‐GM leaves; (3) a confirmatory assay on survivors; and (4) concentration–response assays with a susceptible laboratory strain.

Diagnostic bioassay: F1 neonates were exposed to a diagnostic concentration of 28.22 ng Cry1Ab/cm2 in an artificial diet assay. 8 A total of 1716 larvae were tested (with buffer controls), and mortality/moult inhibition was assessed after 7 days. Corrected moult inhibition exceeded the expected 99% in both zones (99.2–99.9%; mean 99.5%), while control mortality was low (Table 2). The reference strain showed 100% moult inhibition at the diagnostic concentration.

Plant tissue bioassay: To complement the diagnostic bioassay, an additional assay was conducted with F1 larvae from the field‐collected populations using maize MON 810 leaves. In total, 6349 larvae were fed maize MON 810 leaves (with verified Cry1Ab expression), all of which died within 7 days. In the control group (non‐Bt maize leaves), mortality was 4.4%, with normal larval development observed.

Confirmatory assay: The eight larvae surviving the diagnostic assay were retested on maize MON 810 leaves to confirm that they were not potentially resistant to Cry1Ab. All larvae died within 7 days, but no information was provided by the consent holder on whether any of the larvae moulted further in this period.

Concentration–response assays: Susceptibility of the reference strain was assessed across a range of Cry1Ab concentrations. Larval mortality and moult inhibition were assessed after 7 days of exposure. MIC50 and MIC90 values were estimated by probit analysis (Robertson et al., 2007) and were within the range of previous years, albeit at the lower end (Appendix D).

Mediterranean corn borer monitoring Field sampling and laboratory rearing

Field sampling and laboratory rearing

Field sampling and laboratory rearing

In 2024, 1732 MCB late‐instar larvae were collected from 12 sites (refuge or non‐Bt maize fields) across three zones in north‐eastern Spain; 24 additional sites yielded insufficient numbers. Larvae were reared under standardised protocols (Farinós et al., 2004; González‐Núñez et al., 2000), and 1115 (64.4%) reached adulthood. Of these, 1036 adults (94 cages) produced F1 progeny used in diagnostic bioassays, corresponding to a detection limit of 3.1%.

Confirmatory assays: Of 138 larvae reaching the second instar in the diagnostic assay, two larvae originating from the same oviposition cage (number 33) from Zone 2 moulted to third instar and were alive after 10 days feeding on Bt maize. When these larvae continued feeding on Bt leaves, none of them continued moulting or completed the larval stage.

The results of additional testing indicated reduced Cry1Ab susceptibility, with 92.3% moult inhibition in diagnostic assays (and three larvae progressing to third or fourth instar in subsequent confirmatory assays with maize MON 810 leaves) and one F2 larvae moulting to second instar after feeding on maize MON 810 leaves for 10 days. European and Mediterranean corn borer resistance monitoring Laboratory reference strains

Laboratory reference strains

Laboratory reference strains

Between 2018 and 2022, the MCB laboratory strain originated from Galicia (north‐western Spain), where maize MON 810 is not cultivated and thus selective pressure is expected to be low. Over this period, the strain was replaced four times due to Nosema sp. infections and ‘discrepancies in susceptibility to Cry1Ab’ observed in laboratory assays. In 2023, the strain was again replaced for similar reasons, including infection and high natural variability in Cry1Ab susceptibility, which has previously been documented in Galician MCB populations (González‐Núñez et al., 2000). In 2024, a new MCB population collected in Coimbra (central Portugal) was introduced. However, the absence of information on local maize MON 810 uptake raised uncertainty regarding potential selection pressure. Consequently, the consent holder replaced this population again in 2024 with a newly collected strain from Galicia.

The MIC90 and 95% confidence interval obtained in 2024 fell within the historical range (Appendix 7, Section 3.2). Given the historically high variability in MIC90 values, potentially linked to frequent strain replacement, the absence of a temporal trend is not unexpected.

EFSA considers that reference strains should display stable and high Cry1Ab susceptibility over time. The repeated replacement of MCB laboratory strains from different regions, combined with their inherent variability in Cry1Ab susceptibility, suggests that recently used strains may not represent a robust reference population.

EFSA further reiterates that comparisons with laboratory strains should serve only as quality control and cannot be used to dismiss low response values observed in field populations tested in susceptibility bioassays. bField sampling and laboratory rearing

bField sampling and laboratory rearing

Field sampling and laboratory rearing

In line with the EU IRM sampling scheme, ECB and MCB larvae were collected from three zones in north‐eastern Spain in 2024, the main resistance hotspot due to sustained high maize MON 810 uptake (~60% of maize area) and two pest generations per year (Alfaro, 1972; Cordero et al., 1998; Appendix B).

In 2024, sampling covered Huesca and Navarra, regularly monitored in previous seasons (Candasnos, Lanaja, Sariñena, Mendigorría), and Girona (Cataluña), which was included for the third consecutive year due to high maize MON 810 uptake and pest pressure. Girona remains highly relevant, as unexpected MCB damage in 2021 suggested resistance allele frequencies sufficient to affect field performance; 2024 results support its continued inclusion (see Section 3.1.2.2.c). EFSA agrees with this selection and reiterates the need to sample the area where a resistance allele was detected previously (Camargo et al., 2018).

Larvae were collected from non‐Bt maize fields within 700 m of maize MON 810 fields in Zones 1–2 and up to 6.5 km in Zone 3 (Appendix 7, Tables 3, 4). As the IRM plan requires sampling within dispersal range of nearby Bt maize fields (Appendix 6), these unusually larger distances (attributed to low infestation near Bt maize fields) highlight practical constraints. EFSA recommends improved coordination with field technicians and regional monitoring networks (e.g. RedFAragon, Gencat) to better identify infested fields near maize MON 810 cultivation.

Finding fields infested with MCB and especially ECB larvae was challenging, as in 92% (ECB) and 69% (MCB) of 36 sites sampled, none or very few larvae were detected. While the target population size (1000 larvae; 2000 genomes) was exceeded for MCB (1732 larvae), only 458 ECB larvae were collected. EFSA acknowledges that it is a challenge to collect large amounts of larvae due to factors such as natural fluctuation in pest density and regional pest suppression (Dively et al., 2018). High pre‐imaginal mortality during rearing (64% ECB; 56% MCB) further reduced sample sizes, despite optimised protocols. EFSA acknowledges the inherent challenges (e.g. parasitism, pathogens) contributing to mortality before susceptibility testing.

As a result, the recommended detection limit of 3% resistance allele frequency was not achieved in 2024, limiting early detection. This is particularly critical for ECB, where the 3% threshold has never been met since diagnostic concentration assays were first adopted in 2016. The 2024 upper‐bound estimate for ECB resistance alleles (7.8%) remained high (7.8% in 2023), and likely overestimates detection power due to assumptions on mating contribution, since single‐pair crosses would be required for accurate estimation. EFSA recommends more detailed reporting on breeding design (e.g. number of contributing adults per zone) for ECB assays.

EFSA reiterates the need to optimise ECB sampling to achieve the 2000‐genome target and 3% detection limit, enabling early detection of resistance evolution. To this end, EFSA recommends: (1) strengthening collaboration with field technicians and seed company scouts; (2) increasing the use of regional phytosanitary data (e.g. from pest monitoring networks such as RedFAragón and GenCat); and (3) better integrating farmer‐reported information (e.g. complaints and questionnaires). Clearer guidance in Technical User Guides is also needed, particularly in Portugal, to promote regular and active scouting of maize MON 810 fields and systematic reporting of unexpected damage caused by ECB or MCB. Improved farmer training remains essential, as 86% of farmers reporting high corn borer pressure were unable to distinguish between ECB and MCB (Appendix 1).

Given the increasing difficulties in locating infested fields, EFSA reiterates the need to implement more sensitive alternative monitoring strategies. cMonitoring assays

cMonitoring assays

Monitoring assays

Since the 2016 growing season, the consent holder has assessed Cry1Ab susceptibility using diagnostic bioassays with F1 larvae from field‐collected populations instead of concentration–response assays. While EFSA previously accepted the rationale for this shift, it has consistently raised concerns regarding its implementation, especially assay design, diagnostic concentration selection and confirmatory testing (EFSA, 2018, 2019a, 2020, 2021, 2022a, 2023, 2024). Despite repeated recommendations to revise the IRM plan and consider alternative approaches, only partial progress has been made.

Design of diagnostic assays: EFSA reiterates that reference populations in diagnostic assays should serve solely as quality controls, not as comparators for field populations (EFSA, 2022a and earlier). Moult inhibition should therefore be assessed against the expected > 99% response, without statistical comparison to reference strains. This is especially relevant given the frequent replacement of ECB and MCB reference strains, which undermines their consistency across years. In addition, the moult inhibition of populations sampled in different zones in north‐eastern Spain should be compared against the expected > 99% independently, as these represent distinct high‐adoption areas where resistance may emerge locally (EFSA GMO Panel, 2012a). Although this approach reduces statistical power, it allows for a more accurate assessment of resistance evolution at finer geographical scales.

Selection of diagnostic concentrations: Uncertainty remains regarding the appropriateness of the diagnostic concentration for MCB, which appears to have been derived from highly exposed populations and may therefore be overestimated (EFSA, 2021). If diagnostic assays continue to be used, the diagnostic concentration should be recalculated using data from less‐exposed populations and validated to demonstrate > 99% moult inhibition in susceptible strains.

In this context, EFSA recommends that the consent holder consider applying the Bayesian Benchmark Dose (BMD) modelling tool 10 to derive the diagnostic dose. This approach, recently developed for estimating specific response levels (such as the diagnostic dose) from dose response data, fits multiple statistical models to the data and combines them through model averaging, thereby improving the robustness of the final estimate.

Testing strategy: The 2024 testing strategy further raises concerns. A substantial proportion of ECB assays were conducted without controls (681/877 larvae in Zone 1; 799/838 in Zone 2; 56/248 in the reference population), severely limiting the interpretability of the results (Appendix 8). EFSA stresses the need for robust quality assurance, appropriate laboratory training and harmonised methodologies across ECB and MCB assays, including consistent statistical analyses and detailed reporting (Appendix F). Given the well‐established experience with MCB susceptibility monitoring and the progressive optimisation of its protocols, EFSA strongly recommends the consent holder to implement the methodological framework used for MCB also for ECB susceptibility assays.

For MCB, corrected moult inhibition values in 2024 (96.8%, 91.8% and 97.5% in Zones 1–3; mean 95.5%) were below the expected > 99% and among the lowest recorded since 2016. This continues the pattern observed in 2022–2024 and suggests reduced Cry1Ab susceptibility in north‐eastern Spain. The trend is most pronounced in Girona, where values declined from 95.7% (2022) to 93.1% (2023) and 91.8% (2024), following the report of unexpected MCB damage in maize MON 810 fields in 2021.

Follow‐up studies with offspring of larvae surviving diagnostic assays provide additional indications of reduced susceptibility. In particular, offspring from oviposition cage 33 (Girona) showed low susceptibility to both diagnostic Cry1Ab exposure and maize MON 810 leaf tissue assays across two generations. Although confirmatory analyses were constrained by Nosema infection and colony collapse, the results (together with consistently low susceptibility) suggest the presence of resistance alleles in the Girona MCB population. Taken together with the 2021 field incident and the progressive decrease in susceptibility observed over recent years, these findings suggest that resistance to Cry1Ab may be evolving in this area. EFSA therefore recommends intensified monitoring, targeted communication and increased efforts to establish a resistant population for further characterisation. The implementation of an F2 screen in MCB populations from Girona in 2025 represents an important first step toward clarifying the resistance status in this region.

EFSA also notes inconsistencies in confirmatory testing, as larvae from the new MCB laboratory strain that survived diagnostic exposure were not further analysed. All surviving individuals should be subjected to harmonised confirmatory procedures. More broadly, moult inhibition values below the expected > 99% should systematically trigger further investigation. EFSA again recommends alignment with the step‐wise approach of the US EPA (2010, 2018; Appendix E), including testing progeny of siblings to assess heritability. In addition, confirmatory plant assays should move from leaf tissue to whole‐plant systems to better reflect field exposure, considering larval boring behaviour and lower Cry1Ab expression in stalk tissues (Kaçar et al., 2023; Székács et al., 2010). Finally, EFSA notes that the methodology provided by the applicant for the leaf tissue confirmatory assays with ECB populations is incomplete, as it does not clearly specify the bioassay endpoints nor the duration of the exposure. These critical methodological details should be clearly defined.

Alternative test methods: EFSA reiterates that the current resistance monitoring strategy remains insufficiently sensitive to detect the recommended 3% resistance allele frequency (EFSA, 2015a, 2019a). While increasing sampling effort or reducing mortality could improve sensitivity, practical constraints persist. EFSA therefore considers that more sensitive approaches, particularly periodic F2 screens (Andow & Alstad, 1998), represent the most effective solution. Such screens, combined with a strengthened farmer complaint system (Section 3.2.3.3), could replace annual diagnostic assays and provide more robust estimates of resistance allele frequencies. To ensure adequate sensitivity, at least 100 isolines should be tested, and results should inform updated resistance modelling to guide future resistance monitoring (Andow & Alstad, 1998; Huang et al., 2012). Resistance evolution models should be rerun after each F2 screen, using the most recent estimates of resistance allele frequency and reflecting any relevant updates to model parameters (e.g. uptake of maize MON 810 and refuge compliance). The resulting allele frequency estimates and simulation outputs will help determine whether resistance is increasing relative to previous assessments and, in turn, inform the timing of subsequent F2 screens.

In this context, EFSA welcomes the planned implementation of F2 screens for MCB populations from 2025, starting with Girona and followed by Navarra and Huesca (EFSA, 2023, 2024, 2025). However, EFSA maintains that ECB populations in north‐eastern Spain should also be included, as no resistance allele frequency estimates exist for this species despite emerging evidence of resistance evolution elsewhere (Smith & Farhan, 2023). A modified F2 screen (Santiago‐González et al., 2023) could also be considered, particularly if resistant MCB strains are established.

Supplementary susceptibility bioassays with plant tissue: Regarding supplementary plant bioassays, EFSA notes that ECB assays were affected by excessive control mortality (71.2%; up to 100% across replicates), exceeding accepted quality thresholds and compromising validity. These data should have been excluded. EFSA therefore recommends stricter quality control criteria prior to analysis.

Reporting of monitoring data: EFSA also reiterates the importance of transparent and complete reporting (Appendix F). In particular, the confirmatory testing framework for ECB remains unclear and inconsistent, in contrast to the more clearly defined criteria used for MCB. All methodological aspects, endpoints and follow‐up procedures should be fully specified.

Follow‐up of unexpected damage in Girona in 2021: Actions undertaken in 2024 were limited to routine resistance monitoring and stewardship activities. EFSA considers these measures insufficient, particularly in light of the decreasing Cry1Ab susceptibility observed in MCB populations from this area (moult inhibition: 95.7% in 2022; 93.1% in 2023; 91.8% in 2024). Resistance monitoring should therefore be strengthened through targeted sampling and active scouting in both conventional and maize MON 810 fields. Farmers in the region should be explicitly informed and encouraged to regularly inspect maize MON 810 fields for signs of MCB damage and to report any findings. In addition, efforts to establish a resistant population should be intensified to test the hypothesis that the reduced susceptibility observed in the offspring of oviposition cage 33, together with the 2021 incident, is associated with resistance alleles and to enable further characterisation. In this context, the planned F2 screen in Girona in 2025 represents a critical step. Finally, the consent holder should consider deploying sentinel plots in Girona and other areas where signs of potential resistance evolution have been reported (e.g. where a resistance allele was identified by Camargo et al. (2018)). The assessment of target pest presence and damage in sentinel plots has been shown to align with the results of laboratory susceptibility bioassays, indicating that sentinel plots can serve as a useful complementary tool for the early detection of resistance evolution (Smith et al., 2026).

Remedial action plan: While the updated CropLife Europe IRM plan (Appendix 6) clarifies certain aspects of the definition and investigation of suspected resistance, it still lacks sufficient detail on available IPM options, specific remedial measures and the criteria for their implementation. EFSA considers that illustrative examples of remedial actions are necessary to ensure preparedness and a timely, proportionate response in case of confirmed resistance.

Conclusions on insect resistance monitoring

Diagnostic concentration bioassays with progeny of field‐collected populations showed moult inhibition values in line with the expected > 99% for both ECB populations, whereas all three MCB populations displayed lower than expected inhibition. Complementary plant assays using maize MON 810 leaves showed that none of the tested MCB larvae completed development. However, as these assays reflect worst‐case exposure (high Cry1Ab expression), their relevance to field conditions, where larvae are exposed to lower toxin levels after boring into stalks, remains uncertain. Overall, the consistently low moult inhibition for MCB over three consecutive years, together with the 2021 damage incident in Girona and evidence of resistance alleles in the sampled populations in 2024, indicates that reduced Cry1Ab susceptibility in MCB populations from north‐eastern Spain cannot be excluded.

Following the 2021 incident, monitoring in Girona continued through inclusion in annual resistance monitoring. Results for 2022–2024 consistently showed Cry1Ab susceptibility below the expected > 99%. EFSA recommends maintaining and strengthening monitoring in this region, including enhanced farmer vigilance (regular field inspections) and communication. The consent holder is also encouraged to attempt selection of a resistant MCB strain from this area and to intensify grower education.

EFSA further recommends adopting a step‐wise approach to resistance confirmation, similar to US EPA guidance and updating the harmonised IRM plan accordingly. This should include testing progeny of siblings of larvae surviving diagnostic assays to assess heritability, and conducting plant assays on whole plants to better reflect field conditions. Where resistance is suspected, thorough investigation and, if confirmed, implementation of remedial measures are required. These measures should be clearly specified in the remedial action plan of CropLife Europe's harmonised IRM plan, which should be updated.

Based on the number of larvae tested, the 2024 monitoring was insufficiently sensitive to detect the recommended 3% resistance allele frequency for either ECB or MCB (EFSA, 2015a). Given the difficulty of consistently achieving this detection limit, EFSA reiterates the need for more sensitive methods. In particular, EFSA has long recommended replacing diagnostic assays with periodic F2 screens. EFSA now considers it timely to implement F2 screens for MCB populations in north‐eastern Spain, including sampling from both the area where a resistance allele was previously detected (Camargo et al., 2018) and the Girona area affected in 2021. EFSA welcomes the consent holder's intention to introduce F2 screens for MCB from 2025 onwards (2025–2028), starting with Girona. EFSA also recommends conducting F2 screens for ECB populations in north‐eastern Spain, for which resistance allele frequencies have not yet been estimated.

A comprehensive list of recommendations for improving IRM is provided in Table 6 of Section 5.

No unintended adverse effects were identified in Spain in 2024 based on the information gathered through the farmer questionnaires. Farmer complaint system

Consent holder's assessment

The consent holder, together with other companies marketing maize MON 810 seeds, has established a farmer complaint system that enables the reporting of product performance issues which may signal the emergence of resistance. This includes, for example, unexpected damage to maize MON 810 caused by target pests, potentially indicating reduced efficacy of the trait. In parallel, the consent holder has proposed replacing the farmer questionnaire with this complaint‐based system.

In 2024, no complaints related to reduced efficacy of maize MON 810 were reported through this system. None of the 436 recorded complaints were attributed to Bt maize performance; instead, they mainly concerned general agronomic aspects, such as seed quality or yield. Reported complaints are compiled by the National Spanish Breeders Association (ANOVE).

The consent holder also refers to regional plant health and pest monitoring networks (e.g. Red Fitosanitaria Aragón, RAIF Andalucía), which support integrated pest management and may facilitate the reporting of unusual observations, including potential product failure.

Information provided by Spain

The Competent Authorities of Spain noted that regional plant health and pest monitoring networks remain heterogeneous and are not yet systematically connected to the consent holder for reporting relevant observations. Efforts are underway to explore their potential integration into GS. While these structural limitations persist, training initiatives led by the Competent Authorities of Spain aim to improve famers' awareness and use of these networks in the cultivation of maize MON 810.

EFSA's assessment

EFSA considers that the farmer complaint system could complement IRM by providing first‐hand information on pest pressure and product performance. However, its effectiveness in serving these purposes cannot be assessed due to insufficient detail on its design, performance and supporting grower education. EFSA therefore reiterates the need for greater transparency.

The system's reliability is further limited by gaps in farmer knowledge, as most Spanish farmers reporting high corn borer pressure could not distinguish between ECB and MCB. This highlights the need to strengthen practical training on pest identification, field scouting and damage characterisation. EFSA welcomes ongoing efforts by the Competent Authorities of Spain to expand awareness and field‐based training.

Although the regional plant health and pest monitoring networks and related surveillance systems (e.g. regional alert systems and phytosanitary bulletins) cited by the consent holder do not specifically address resistance, some routinely collect data on pest incidence could support the early detection of changes, including in non‐target lepidopteran pests showing lower susceptibility or resistance to maize MON 810. However, it remains unclear whether and how these data are currently used for resistance monitoring purposes. EFSA therefore recommends strengthening collaboration with these networks to facilitate the timely exchange of information and enable rapid notification of events relevant to resistance monitoring.

Conclusions on the farmer complaint system

The farmer complaint system provides a potentially useful but currently limited contribution to PMEM. While no signals of reduced efficacy were reported in 2024, the system lacks the specificity, transparency and supporting framework (e.g. clear reporting thresholds, farmer training and data integration mechanisms) required to effectively support early detection of resistance. Strengthening farmer awareness, improving reporting criteria and enhancing integration with existing plant health and pest monitoring networks would increase its overall value within PMEM.

Existing (environmental) monitoring networks 13

Consent holder's assessment

The consent holder identified four types of monitoring networks (governmental, academic, conservation and professional) but concluded that they are not suitable for GS, as they do not monitor GM crop cultivation and cannot establish cause‐effect relationships (Smets et al., 2014). Additional limitations include delayed technical constraints, restricted access and lack of harmonisation. These findings are consistent with previous EFSA assessments (EFSA GMO Panel, 2014b; see also Henrys et al., 2014).

Information provided by Spain

An assessment of the suitability of EEMNs operating in north‐eastern Spain and gathering data on pollinators conducted by the Competent Authorities of Spain in 2025 concluded that no EEMNs are currently suitable for PMEM of maize MON 810. Key limitations include the absence of maize cultivation data, insufficient spatial resolution and the inability to link environmental observations to maize MON 810 cultivation. It further noted that EEMNs would need to be specifically designed to support PMEM objectives. Consistent with this, ongoing work is assessing whether selected EEMNs, particularly those targeting pollinators, could be adapted to support GS.

EFSA's assessment

EFSA recognises the limitations of using EEMNs to detect potential adverse effects of GM crops, particularly the difficulty of attributing environmental changes to GM crop cultivation in the presence of multiple confounding factors, as well as issues related to data quality, accessibility and interoperability.

Nevertheless, EFSA considers that EEMNs can provide useful information on environmental trends and may help detect signals of potential adverse effects, which could trigger further investigation to assess whether maize MON 810 cultivation may have contributed to the observed changes.

EFSA welcomes the efforts of the Competent Authorities of Spain and encourages further development of EEMNs, while noting that this would require broader coordination beyond the current GMO regulatory framework.

Conclusions on EEMNs

EFSA considers that EEMNs may provide useful information for identifying unanticipated adverse effects. However, due to methodological limitations, EEMNs are not currently suitable as standalone tools for GS of maize MON 810. EFSA therefore encourages continued collaboration between relevant stakeholders to further explore and strengthen their potential utility for GS.

Teosinte 15

Consent holder's assessment

The consent holder concluded, based on the information gathered through the farmer questionnaires and literature searches, that no adverse effects are associated with the potential occurrence of teosinte in maize MON 810 fields. Despite EFSA's previous recommendations, the consent holder maintains that teosinte is not relevant for the environmental risk assessment of maize MON 810 and that additional reporting would provide limited added value.

Information provided by Spain

Monitoring based on systematic annual surveys indicates that teosinte remains locally persistent but geographically contained in Aragon, Cataluña and Navarra, mainly within maize fields (both MON 810 and conventional), with no evidence of stable populations in non‐cultivated habitats. Regional trends show limited spread, with some reductions due to intensified monitoring. No hybridisation between maize MON 810 and teosinte has been detected under field conditions.

Management relies on agronomic measures (e.g. crop rotation, avoiding maize in heavily infested areas, silage use), supported by monitoring and farmer engagement. Farmer awareness and identification capacity remain variable.

A research project launched in 2025 (Spanish and French partners) aims to address knowledge gaps on teosinte identified by EFSA (2016, 2022b), including teosinte distribution, hybridisation and hybrid fitness, using monitoring, experiments and genomics. The project is expected to generate targeted evidence to support the environmental risk assessment and risk management of maize MON 810.

EFSA's assessment

Potential hybridisation between maize MON 810 and teosinte could, in principle, lead to environmental risks (Bauer‐Panskus et al., 2020; Lohn et al., 2021; Trtikova et al., 2017). However, EFSA considers these risks negligible due to the low likelihood of the required sequence of events (EFSA, 2016, 2022b). Recent studies confirm (e.g. Arias‐Martín et al., 2024) that vertical gene flow can occur, but uncertainties remain regarding its ecological relevance and potential fitness advantages.

No teosinte‐specific data were collected through the 2024 farmer questionnaire, which has not yet been updated to capture teosinte‐specific occurrence and infestation levels. While general weed pressure is captured, explicit inclusion of teosinte would improve data relevance. In addition, the current literature search strategy may miss relevant studies, as it does not explicitly include teosinte as search term.

Therefore, EFSA reiterates the need to: Include teosinte‐specific questions in the farmer questionnaire;Incorporate ‘teosinte’ as search term in literature searches;Maintain data exchange with the Competent Authorities of Spain;Strengthen farmer training and awareness on teosinte identification and management;Continue and expand targeted research and monitoring.

Conclusions on teosinte

No new evidence on teosinte was provided in 2024, and uncertainties remain regarding some assumptions underpinning the established pathways to harm (EFSA, 2016, 2022b). Available data indicate that teosinte is locally persistent but contained, mainly within cultivated fields and that current management limits its spread. Non‐target Lepidoptera

Consent holder's assessment

The 2024 PMEM report does not provide specific information on potential adverse effects of maize MON 810 cultivation on NTL.

Information provided by Spain

The Competent Authorities of Spain assessed the feasibility of implementing isolation distances between maize MON 810 fields and protected areas potentially hosting sensitive NTL species and whose life cycle overlaps with pollen shed.

The assessment identified major data gaps, particularly regarding the distribution and Cry1Ab sensitivity of NTL species, as well as uncertainties in identifying which protected areas (e.g. Natura 2000 sites) are relevant in relation to maize cultivation. Consequently, although such measures would in principle target specific protected areas hosting relevant NTL species, their practical implementation is currently not feasible. The absence of legal requirements on isolation distances, together with the lack of a specific prohibition on cultivating GM plants within protected areas, further constrains the implementation of this recommendation.

The Competent Authorities of Spain further assessed the presence of protected Lepidoptera species in Natura 2000 sites compatible with maize cultivation, considering conservation measures and data availability across regions. Data coverage and robustness depends on the legal protection category of the 13 NTL EU‐relevant species present in Nature 2000 areas, and management approaches vary by region, with no harmonised framework in place. Despite this variability, it was confirmed that conservation measures may impact agricultural practices but do not impose restrictions on maize or MON 810 cultivation within protected areas.

EFSA's assessment

The ingestion of Bt maize pollen by NTL larvae on host plants near fields is a potential environmental risk. EFSA modelling (EFSA, 2009; EFSA GMO Panel, 2011c, 2012b, 2015c; Perry et al., 2010, 2011, 2012, 2013) estimated larval mortality and indicated that species with very high or extreme sensitivity to Cry1Ab could be at risk, although no such species have been identified to date due to limited data, particularly for non‐pest species, making the risk largely hypothetical. Under a conservative worst‐case approach, EFSA recommended a 20 m isolation distance between maize MON 810 fields and protected habitats where susceptible NTLs may occur. This recommendation remains valid, but no information on its implementation was provided in the 2024 PMEM report.

EFSA welcomes ongoing efforts by the Competent Authorities of Spain to address data gaps and notes that improved information on NTL distribution and maize cultivation patterns could support more targeted risk mitigation measures. Continued work is encouraged to identify where measures may be needed and feasible.

Conclusions on non‐target Lepidoptera

In the absence of implementation data on isolation distances, uncertainty remains regarding potential exposure of highly sensitive NTL species to harmful amounts of maize MON 810 pollen.

EFSA recommends that risk managers consider, on a case‐by‐case basis, the implementation of isolation distances where relevant (e.g. in areas of high maize MON 810 cultivation near protected habitats hosting sensitive NTL species), or alternatively that the consent holder provides evidence demonstrating the absence of highly sensitive NTL species in areas adjacent to maize MON 810 fields.

EFSA welcomes ongoing efforts by the Competent Authorities of Spain and emphasises the need to further address data gaps and refine risk assessment and risk management measures to prevent harmful exposure of highly sensitive NTL species to maize MON 810 pollen.

Considerations for streamlining GS in future annual PMEM reports on maize MON 810 cultivation

After more than two decades of cultivation without reported evidence of unexpected adverse environmental effects, EFSA considers that the accumulated evidence supports a more streamlined GS approach for maize MON 810. This reflects both the experience gained and the recognised limitations of current monitoring systems, while focusing GS efforts on generating relevant data.

Although GS was not required at the time of authorisation, the consent holder has implemented it since 2005, comprising farmer questionnaires, annual literature searches and a farmer complaint system. EFSA has acknowledged the value of this approach, which has generated a substantial body of information without indicating any unexpected adverse environmental effects. However, methodological limitations remain, and EFSA has recommended targeted improvements.

Subject to the improvements outlined below, GS for maize MON 810 cultivation could be streamlined in future annual PMEM reports to focus on two core elements: A robust and well‐functioning farmer complaint system, complemented where appropriate by existing plant health and pest monitoring networks;Transparent and comprehensive annual literature searches.

RECOMMENDATIONS

In the context of the annual assessment of PMEM reports for maize MON 810 cultivation, several of EFSA's recommendations from previous PMEM assessments remain unaddressed, and additional issues were identified in the 2024 PMEM report. Given the critical nature of some of these points, their implementation is essential to ensure the continued safety of maize MON 810 cultivation for human and animal health and the environment.

Outstanding priorities include the need to: Increase the sensitivity of the resistance monitoring strategy to enable early detection of reduced Cry1Ab susceptibility in ECB and MCB populations in north‐eastern Spain (e.g. by replacing current diagnostic tests with more sensitive approaches such as F2 screens);Strengthen resistance monitoring and communication efforts in Girona to ensure effective detection of potential Cry1Ab resistance evolution;Revise the remedial action plan to clearly specify measures to be implemented in the event of confirmed Cry1Ab resistance;Evaluate the need and feasibility of isolation distances in high maize MON 810 cultivation areas near protected habitats hosting sensitive NTL species;Implement farmer questionnaires across Spain and Portugal to ensure consistency in data collection.In addition, EFSA identifies several non‐critical recommendations that would further improve the scientific robustness and transparency of the PMEM plans and reports (Table 6), including recurring and newly identified issues.
