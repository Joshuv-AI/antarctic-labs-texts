Evaluation of postoperative pain and antibacterial efficacy of nanochitosan and sodium hypochlorite in endodontics: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: L. Krishna Prasad, Safiya Nikhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The long-term success of root canal treatment depends on effective elimination of microorganisms from the root canal system using chemical irrigants before obturation. Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is the most commonly used irrigant in endodontic therapy; however, it has certain limitations, such as high surface tension that restricts penetration into lateral canals and isthmuses. Therefore, newer irrigants with improved antimicrobial properties are being explored. Recently, nanoparticles have been introduced as potential antimicrobial agents for root canal disinfection.Aim and Objective:The aim and objective of this study was to evaluate the postoperative pain (POP) and antibacterial effectiveness of 0.2% nanochitosan and 2% NaOCl irrigating solutions during routine endodontic procedures in young adults.Subjects and Methods:A randomized clinical trial was conducted on 40 single-rooted teeth. After rubber dam isolation, access cavity preparation and working length determination were performed. Participants were randomly divided into two groups: Group I – 2% NaOCl and Group II – 0.2% nanochitosan as irrigants during cleaning and shaping. Pre- and postirrigation microbial samples were collected, and bacterial load was assessed by counting colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL). POP was evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h.Results:Both irrigants showed a significant reduction in bacterial counts. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of antibacterial effectiveness or POP.Conclusion:Within the limitations of this study, 0.2% nanochitosan demonstrated antibacterial effectiveness and POP outcomes comparable to 2% NaOCl, suggesting its potential as an alternative irrigant in routine endodontic procedures.

INTRODUCTION

The success of endodontic therapy largely depends on the effective elimination of these microorganisms and their by-products from the root canal system.[1] Persistent infection within the canal space has been strongly associated with post-treatment apical periodontitis and endodontic failure.[1] Studies have demonstrated that the presence of microorganisms at the time of obturation significantly influences long-term treatment outcomes.[2] Therefore, microbial control remains the biological foundation and cornerstone of successful root canal therapy.[3]

Irrigation plays an indispensable role in modern endodontics. An ideal irrigant should possess potent antimicrobial activity, tissue-dissolving capability, low surface tension for better penetration, minimal toxicity to periapical tissues, and the ability to disrupt biofilms.[4] Despite strict adherence to clinical protocols reported success rates of endodontic treatment range between 86% and 98%, indicating that persistent microorganisms remain a concern.[5]

Postoperative pain (POP) is one of the most common and undesirable sequelae following root canal treatment. It may result from extrusion of infected debris, microorganisms, or irrigants into the periapical tissues during instrumentation.[6]

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) has long been regarded as the gold standard irrigant in endodontics due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and ability to dissolve necrotic and vital organic tissues.[4]

Several studies have confirmed the strong antibacterial efficacy of NaOCl against a wide range of endodontic pathogens. In addition, activation of NaOCl using sonic, ultrasonic, laser, or heat-assisted techniques enhances its penetration, tissue dissolution, debris removal, and antimicrobial efficacy, especially in complex root canal systems. However, despite its effectiveness, NaOCl has certain limitations. It is cytotoxic when extruded beyond the apical foramen. High concentrations have been associated with adverse effects on dentinal collagen and a reduction in the mechanical properties of dentin. Attempts to reduce its concentration to improve safety may compromise antimicrobial efficacy.[78]

Because of these limitations, researchers have explored alternative irrigating solutions with improved biocompatibility and enhanced antibacterial properties.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have opened new avenues for improving root canal disinfection.

Nanoparticles have demonstrated superior antibiofilm activity compared to conventional antimicrobial agents.

Among the various nanomaterials studied, chitosan nanoparticles have gained considerable attention in dentistry. Chitosan is a natural polysaccharide derived from chitin through deacetylation and is composed of glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine units. It is widely recognized for its biocompatibility, biodegradability, bioadhesive properties, and low toxicity, making it suitable for biomedical applications.[9]

The nanoparticulate form of chitosan further enhances its antibacterial efficacy by improving surface interaction and penetration into dentinal tubules. Studies have demonstrated that chitosan nanoparticles exhibit significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against Enterococcus faecalis. Moreover, nano-chitosan has shown the ability to significantly reduce bacterial colony-forming unit (CFU) counts in infected root canals.[1011]

Although in vitro studies have shown promising results for nanochitosan, clinical evidence regarding its effect on POP and antibacterial efficacy remains limited. Considering the limitations of NaOCl, further clinical evaluation is necessary. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial was conducted to compare POP, and antibacterial effectiveness, assessed by CFU reduction, between 0.2% nanochitosan and 2% NaOCl during routine endodontic treatment of single-rooted teeth in young adults.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted in the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics at K.V.G. Dental College and Hospital, Sullia, Dakshin Kannada, after obtaining ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref No: IECKVGDCH/PG21/2024-25). The trial was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2024/08/072666) on 19/08/2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients requiring root canal treatment were included in the study after obtaining written informed consent, irrespective of gender and socioeconomic status. Participants aged 18–45 years with single-rooted permanent teeth diagnosed with chronic irreversible pulpitis and in good general health were selected. Teeth associated with intraoral or extraoral sinus, swelling or abscess, history of antibiotic use in the previous 2 months, open apex, abnormal anatomy, calcified canals, long-standing open access cavities, periodontal involvement, or vital inflamed pulp tissue, periapical lesion were excluded. Psychologically compromised patients were also excluded.

Participants were randomly allocated into two groups using the paper slip method: Group I received NaOCl as the irrigant, while Group II received nanochitosan.

0.2% nanochitosan preparation

A 0.2% nanochitosan solution was prepared using the ionotropic gelation method, in which 2 g of chitosan was dissolved in 100 ml of 1% acetic acid under continuous magnetic stirring, followed by dropwise addition of sodium tripolyphosphate to facilitate ionic cross-linking and nanoparticle formation. The solution was freshly prepared before use (Nano research lab Jharkhand).

Clinical procedure

All clinical procedures were performed under local anesthesia using 2% lignocaine with adrenaline (1:200,000) and rubber dam isolation to maintain aseptic conditions. The operative field was disinfected using 3% hydrogen peroxide (VLP Products, Lokmanya Tilak Marg, Mumbai, India) followed by 2% NaOCl (Hyposol®, Prevest DenPro Limited, Jammu, India). Access cavity preparation was carried out using sterile burs. Initial minimal instrumentation was performed using no. 10 and 15K file; pulp extirpation was done. Working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (Root ZX, J. Morita USA, Irvine, CA, USA) and confirmed radiographically, standardized at 1 mm short of the apex [Figures 1 and 2].

Sample collection

Microbiological sampling was carried out before and after irrigation. For the preirrigation sample (Sample A), a sterile paper point was inserted into the canal up to working length for 60 s and transferred to a sterile container containing normal saline. The irrigant was delivered using a 30-gauge side-vented irrigation needle attached to a disposable syringe, inserted passively up to 1 mm short of the working length. Biomechanical preparation was then completed using rotary instruments along with the assigned irrigant, with a total volume of 10 ml used. The irrigant was allowed to remain in the canal for 5 min to ensure adequate antimicrobial action, after which the canal was flushed with sterile saline and dried using paper points. A second sterile paper point was then inserted for 60 s to obtain the postirrigation sample (Sample B), which was similarly transferred to a sterile container. The access cavity was temporarily sealed after sample collection.

Microbiological testing

All samples were transported immediately to the microbiology laboratory (Research Cell and Development Unit, KVGDCH) under aseptic conditions. The samples were vortexed and inoculated onto blood agar plates using the streak plate method. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Following incubation, colony morphology and growth characteristics were observed, and the number of viable bacterial colonies was quantified using a colony counter. The bacterial load was expressed as CFU/ml, calculated using the formula: CFU/ml = (Number of colonies × Dilution factor)/Volume of sample inoculated [Figures 3 and 4].

Pain assessment was performed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Preoperative pain was recorded before treatment. POP was assessed at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Patients were instructed to record their pain scores and were contacted via telephone to ensure compliance. Pain was categorized as no pain (0), mild (1–3), moderate (4–6), severe (7–9), and unbearable (10). Patients experiencing moderate-to-severe pain were advised to take ibuprofen 400 mg and record the number of analgesic tablets consumed. Those with persistent pain were instructed to report to the clinic for further evaluation.

The primary outcome measure was reduction in bacterial load (CFU/ml), and the secondary outcome was POP intensity.

RESULTS

Following irrigation with NaOCl (S2), the mean bacterial load decreased substantially to 3,575.0 CFU/mL with a standard deviation of 2,299.5 CFU/mL. The median postoperative value was 2,750.0 CFU/mL, with a minimum of 1400 CFU/mL and a maximum of 9700 CFU/mL. For the Nanochitosan group at baseline (S1), the mean bacterial load was 15,140.0 CFU/mL, but the standard deviation was notably higher at 14,548.7 CFU/mL, reflecting greater variability in the data. This high standard deviation was attributed to the presence of an extreme outlier, specifically Sample 26, which had a bacterial count of 71,000 CFU/mL. The median value of 11,100.0 CFU/mL provides a more accurate representation of the typical preoperative bacterial load in this group, as it is less influenced by extreme values. The bacterial counts ranged from a minimum of 5100 CFU/mL to a maximum of 71,000 CFU/mL. After irrigation with nanochitosan (S2), the mean bacterial load decreased to 3,815.0 CFU/mL with a standard deviation of 2,540.5 CFU/mL. The median postoperative value was 2,650.0 CFU/mL, with counts ranging from 1300 CFU/mL to 9000 CFU/mL. These descriptive statistics demonstrate that both irrigants produced substantial reductions in bacterial load, although the presence of an outlier in the Chitosan S1 group necessitated the use of nonparametric statistical tests for subsequent analyses to ensure robust and valid comparisons [Table 1 and Graph 1].

POP was assessed using VAS scores at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Both groups showed a progressive reduction in pain over time [Graph 2]. DISCUSSION

The present randomized clinical study evaluated the antibacterial effectiveness and POP associated with two irrigating solutions – 2% NaOCl and 0.2% nanochitosan – during routine endodontic treatment.

Reduction in intracanal bacterial load measured by CFU count and POP assessed using the VAS were selected as the primary clinical parameters to determine the effectiveness of the irrigants.

NaOCl is a standard irrigant in endodontic practice and is widely used during root canal treatment. It is commonly used in concentrations ranging from 0.5% to 6%, with higher concentrations generally showing greater antibacterial activity and tissue-dissolving capacity. High concentration of NaOCl may cause ultrastructural damage to the dentin and could harm apical and periapical tissues. Therefore, we had chosen 2% concentration for our study as it is less toxic and commercially available.[4]

Among the various alternatives investigated, chitosan nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention because of their unique biological and physicochemical properties.[12]

The results of the present investigation demonstrated that both irrigating solutions produced a significant reduction in intracanal bacterial load, as indicated by the decrease in CFUs between preoperative and postoperative samples. The NaOCl (Group I) showed a mean reduction of 12,805 CFU/mL (78.2%), whereas the nanochitosan (Group II) demonstrated a reduction of 11,325 CFU/mL (74.8%). Although the reduction was slightly greater in the NaOCl group, the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.527). These findings indicate that nanochitosan exhibits antimicrobial activity that is comparable to NaOCl, which is widely regarded as the standard irrigant in endodontic practice.

The antimicrobial activity of NaOCl is due to the release of hypochlorous acid and free chlorine, which oxidize bacterial enzymes and disrupt metabolic functions. Its high alkaline pH further enhances bacterial destruction by denaturing proteins and damaging cell membranes. In addition, chlorine ions aid in dissolving organic tissue and disrupting biofilms, improving root canal disinfection.[1314]

A study conducted by Memon K et al. reported that the antimicrobial effectiveness of nano-chitosan was nearly comparable to that of 5.25% NaOCl.[15]

The antibacterial activity of chitosan is primarily attributed to its polycationic structure, where positively charged amino groups interact with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, disrupting permeability and causing leakage of intracellular components, ultimately leading to cell death. Chitosan also exhibits chelating properties, binding metal ions essential for microbial enzymatic activity and thereby interfering with bacterial metabolism. At the nanoscale, its antibacterial efficacy is further enhanced, as nanoparticles can penetrate bacterial cells and interact with DNA, disrupting transcription and protein synthesis.[11] Studies have shown that chitosan nanoparticles possess superior antibacterial properties due to their ability to interfere with intracellular processes.[12] The reduction in intracanal bacterial load may thus be attributed to combined mechanisms of membrane disruption, intracellular interference, and metal ion chelation.[16]

These results are consistent with the findings reported by Roshdy et al. and Moukarab, who observed that chitosan nanoparticles exhibit antimicrobial activity comparable to NaOCl against intracanal microorganisms. Similarly, other investigations have demonstrated that nanoparticle-based antimicrobial agents can effectively reduce bacterial contamination within root canals due to their enhanced interaction with microbial cell surfaces.[1718]

The results of the present study also support the findings of Cakici and Cakici, who reported that chitosan demonstrates significant antimicrobial activity and may serve as a potential alternative to NaOCl in endodontic practice.[19] Their systematic review highlighted the antibacterial effectiveness and favorable biological properties of chitosan, emphasizing its potential application in root canal disinfection.[19]

Previous investigations evaluating different concentrations of chitosan have demonstrated that lower concentrations, such as 0.2%, can still provide significant antimicrobial activity, indicating that effective disinfection can be achieved without using higher doses of the material. This observation is clinically relevant because the use of lower concentrations may help maintain the balance between antimicrobial efficacy and biological safety.[20] In the present study, 0.2% nanochitosan was selected as the irrigating solution, and the favorable outcomes obtained in terms of bacterial reduction suggest that this concentration is adequate for clinical use. Although increasing the concentration might potentially enhance antimicrobial activity, it could also influence the physicochemical properties of the solution or increase the likelihood of undesirable biological effects. These findings are consistent with previous research that reported strong antibacterial and antibiofilm properties of cationic nanoparticulates used for root canal disinfection.[10]

Another important aspect evaluated in the present study was POP following endodontic treatment. POP after endodontic treatment remains an important concern in clinical practice because it directly affects patient comfort and the perceived success of the procedure. Even with considerable improvements in endodontic instrumentation and treatment protocols, factors such as residual microbial infection, inflammatory reactions in periapical tissues, and limitations associated with conventional irrigation techniques may still lead to post-treatment discomfort.[21]

In the present investigation, POP was assessed using the VAS, which is widely recognized as a reliable and validated method for measuring subjective pain intensity in clinical research.[2223]

Analysis of pain scores revealed a statistically significant difference between the two groups at the 24-hour time interval. Patients treated with NaOCl (Group I) reported a mean VAS score of 1.30, whereas patients treated with nanochitosan (Group II) reported a significantly lower score of 0.45 (P = 0.010). However, no statistically significant differences were observed at the 6-, 12-, 48-, or 72-h time intervals.

The reduced POP observed in the nano-chitosan group may be explained by its favorable biological compatibility. Chitosan has been shown to be well-tolerated by biological tissues and has demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity in various biomedical applications.[20] Silva et al. reported that chitosan solutions used in endodontic procedures can effectively remove the smear layer while maintaining favorable biological characteristics. Because of its natural origin and biodegradable properties, chitosan is less likely to induce inflammatory reactions when compared with synthetic chemical agents.[24]

In contrast, NaOCl is known to possess significant cytotoxic potential when it comes into contact with vital tissues. Accidental extrusion of NaOCl into periapical tissues may lead to irritation and inflammatory responses, which may contribute to postoperative discomfort. Therefore, the lower pain scores observed in the nanochitosan group may reflect the improved tissue compatibility of this irrigant.[25]

The study demonstrates several strengths. Its randomized controlled design minimized selection bias and ensured comparable groups. Standardized measures, including CFU counts and VAS scores, enabled objective assessment of antibacterial efficacy and POP. Use of appropriate non-parametric tests and both inter- and intragroup comparisons improved statistical reliability. Multiple postoperative time points allowed detailed pain evaluation, and inclusion of analgesic consumption provided an additional measure of patient discomfort.

However, certain limitations should be considered. Bacterial assessment was based on culture methods, which may not detect all microbial species; molecular techniques like polymerase chain reaction could provide more comprehensive analysis. Parameters such as smear layer removal, debris elimination, and effects on dentin were not evaluated, and only a single irrigant concentration was tested, limiting generalizability. Future studies should include larger multicenter trials with longer follow-up, evaluation of different concentrations, assessment of dentin effects and residual antimicrobial activity, and exploration of combination protocols, patient-reported outcomes, and cost-effectiveness.
