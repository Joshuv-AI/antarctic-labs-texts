Establishing editorial standards for qualitative research studies at BMC Global and Public Health
Authors: Ben Cranfield, Gen Li, Gerrit John-Schuster
Published: 2025

BMC Global and Public Health reviews a wide range of topics using diverse methodologies. This breadth reflects our mission to publish globally relevant, locally grounded research. Qualitative research studies, especially interviews and focus groups, require a distinct editorial assessment focused on context, narrative, and relevance to public health practice.

We are committed to transparency in both publishing and editorial decision-making. This means clearly communicating how we assess qualitative research manuscripts and ensuring our evaluations are consistent and informed by best practices. To deliver impactful, globally relevant insights, we must ensure that the qualitative research studies we publish adhere to the highest standards of rigor and transparency. To support this, we recently conducted internal discussions with three members of our editorial board (Dr. Amrita Daftary—York University, Canada, and Centre for the AIDS Programme of Research, South Africa; Dr. Petra Gronholm—London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, UK; and Dr. Jill Thompson—The University of Sheffield, UK) to refine our approach to the initial review of interview and focus group studies and to support decisions about peer review. Below we summarise the outcomes of that exercise and outline the key criteria we consider when assessing these types of articles.

Evidence-base

We expect the authors to clearly situate the evidence produced by their research within existing literature and frameworks. This includes applying relevant theories, models, epistemological and ontological notions, and methodological guides that informed data interpretations—especially for exploratory studies. If the approach is novel, the authors would be expected to demonstrate the rationale and value.

Sample size

We aim to understand whether the sample size is appropriate given the study’s aims, methodology, and context. While qualitative research does not require statistical power, it does require sufficient depth and, often, diversity to support meaningful analysis. The authors should discuss and justify their sample size and approach to sampling in relation to considerations around data adequacy, saturation, scope, and feasibility. Transparent and critical considerations of sample size sufficiency enable the assessment of the collected data and insights in view of the research question [1]. Smaller sample sizes may not generate enough data to identify all relevant themes, and larger sample sizes might be an indication that interview questions or focus groups lack sufficient depth or dynamism to capture rich data. The authors are obligated to justify decisions around sample sizes and address potential limitations within the discussion.

Transparency

A step-by-step breakdown of the qualitative procedure is essential to assure readers of the robustness of the findings. This includes how participants were recruited; how data were collected, coded, and analysed; and how themes were developed. The use of relevant methods to support these steps must be provided and rationalised, such as any tools/methods used for recruitment, evidence of how interview guides were developed and administered, and subsequent coding or other analytic frameworks. Transparency enables readers to understand and trust the research process and production of evidence. Qualitative results must be presented clearly and systematically so readers can understand theme generation and draw meaningful insights. Thus, we encourage the use of comprehensive reporting guidelines such as the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [2]. Qualitative researchers should also be transparent in describing their roles and backgrounds; a reflexivity statement is highly recommended.

Quality

We look for evidence that the methods used are appropriate, well-referenced, and support sound qualitative practice. This includes techniques for ensuring confirmability, reliability, and credibility, such as triangulation (the use of multiple methods, data sources, or perspectives), contextualisation (situating data, findings, or interpretations within the wider context in which they occur), member checking (sharing findings with participants or representatives to confirm accuracy), or reflexive journaling (researchers recording and discussing their thoughts and decisions during the study to reflect on assumptions and influences). The authors should demonstrate how they maintained methodological integrity throughout the study.

Narrative

We value results sections that can “stand alone”—meaning the narrative should be coherent and compelling even without direct quotes. Quotes are essential to convey the evidence supporting the authors’ interpretations; but they should enrich the story, not carry it. The results should be structured by themes and contextualised for nuance. Although the authors are not limited by word count, we discourage overuse of quotes and overly detailed narratives.

Reflection

The discussion should be closely linked to the study’s aims and findings in relation to the study methods, especially the study setting and population. We encourage authors to reflect not only on methodological limitations but also on study contributions and broader implications, including ethical, contextual, and policy-related considerations.

Ethics

Ethical approval must be clearly stated, and patient or participant safety must be addressed. This includes informed consent, confidentiality, and sensitivity to vulnerable populations. Dataset availability should be clarified in relation to ethical protocols. Ethical rigor and adherence to relevant guidelines are non-negotiable. Audio or video recording and data protection must be specified.

Conclusion

We apply similarly rigorous editorial standards to mixed methods and rapid qualitative studies. While these designs may differ in structure, methods, and urgency, they must still demonstrate methodological clarity, transparency, and ethical integrity. Mixed methods studies should show clear integration between qualitative and quantitative components, and rapid approaches must still provide sufficient detail and scientific rigor to ensure credibility. Regardless of format, we expect all submissions to uphold the same level of scientific quality as traditional interview and focus group studies.

Qualitative research offers rich insights into public health challenges and solutions, particularly in global contexts where lived experience and social dynamics are central. At BMC Global and Public Health, we are committed to supporting highly robust qualitative research studies and ensuring that our editorial standards are upheld.

We thank our editorial board members for their thoughtful contributions to this internal project and for their ongoing support in shaping the journal’s approach to qualitative research.
