Development and validation of an interpretable machine learning model for osteoporosis prediction using routine blood tests: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qipeng Wei, Jinxiang Zhan, Xiaofeng Chen, Qingyan Huang, Hao Li, Weijun Guo, Zihao Liu, Shiji Chen, Dongling Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) remains the gold standard for osteoporosis diagnosis, its clinical utility is constrained by cost and accessibility challenges. This study aims to develop a predictive model for osteoporosis using routinely available clinical blood biomarkers, thereby providing an innovative and accessible approach for early detection.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 8,144 orthopedic inpatients who underwent DXA scans at Panyu Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine between January 2022 and December 2023. Demographic characteristics and first 24-hour admission blood parameters were collected. Potential predictors were identified through univariate analysis, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and Boruta algorithm. Ten supervised machine learning algorithms were employed to construct predictive models. Model performance was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), calibration plots, decision curve analysis (DCA), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in the test cohort. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis provided interpretable visualization of feature contributions.ResultsThe cohort was randomly divided into training (n = 5,702) and testing sets (n = 2,442). Feature selection convergence across three methods identified 11 key predictors. The logistic regression model demonstrated superior performance in the testing set (AUC = 0.800), outperforming other algorithms in calibration and clinical utility assessments. SHAP analysis revealed age, gender, uric acid concentration, alkaline phosphatase levels, hemoglobin levels, and neutrophil count as the six most influential predictors. An accessible web-based risk calculator has been deployed at:https://op-lm.shinyapps.io/osteoporosis/.ConclusionWe developed an easy-to-use online calculator based on machine learning, which outperforms traditional models, enabling patients to preliminarily screen for osteoporosis using routine blood test results from their health check-ups. This interpretable machine learning model demonstrated promising performance and may assist in improving osteoporosis screening and risk stratification in clinical settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03292-1.

Introduction

Osteoporosis(OP), a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by diminished bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, imposes a substantial global health burden through its association with fragility fractures [1]. Epidemiological data reveal a striking 32.1% prevalence among Chinese adults over 50 years old, with postmenopausal women demonstrating particularly heightened susceptibility. The condition’s clinical impact is magnified by 50% disability and 20% mortality rates following fragility fractures, projected to escalate healthcare expenditures to ¥132 billion by 2035 [2]. While dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) remains the diagnostic gold standard, its widespread implementation is hindered by equipment costs, radiation exposure, and geographic accessibility constraints [3, 4].

The emerging paradigm of blood-based osteoporosis prediction leverages routine laboratory biomarkers - an approach particularly relevant in China’s healthcare context where annual health screenings exceed 500 million participants [5]. Although quantitative ultrasound (QUS) dominates current bone density assessments due to operational convenience, its diagnostic accuracy remains suboptimal [6]. In contrast, standardized blood parameters offer superior reproducibility and widespread availability, presenting an untapped resource for risk stratification.

Mounting evidence underscores intricate connections between bone metabolism and immune-inflammatory pathways, crystallized in the evolving field of osteoimmunology [7, 8]. Key mechanisms include lymphocyte-mediated cytokine regulation of osteoclast-osteoblast equilibrium and RANKL signaling activation under chronic low-grade inflammation [9–12]. Previous literature has found that predictive potential in metabolic-inflammatory composite indices such as monocyte-to-HDL ratio (MHR) and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, which demonstrate stronger bone density correlations than individual biomarkers [13, 14]. Nevertheless, conventional statistical models fail to capture complex nonlinear relationships within multidimensional hematological data, while insufficient interpretability and sample size limitations impede clinical translation [15, 16].

This study aimed to develop and validate osteoporosis prediction models by integrating immune, inflammatory, and lipid metabolism biomarkers using interpretable machine learning. We conducted systematic comparisons of ten machine learning algorithms to select the optimal model, employing the SHAP framework to generate personalized predictive interpretations. Subsequently, an interactive screening tool was implemented via the Shiny platform. The Osteoporosis Logistic Regression Prediction Model (OP-LM) will effectively harnesses China’s extensive health screening blood test data to broaden osteoporosis screening accessibility. This tool delivers an economical, non-invasive strategy for early osteoporosis detection, demonstrating particular utility for large-scale screening in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study designs

This single-center retrospective study consecutively enrolled patients admitted to the Department of Orthopedics at Panyu Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine between January 2022 and December 2023, all of whom underwent DXA assessments. Bone mineral density measurements were performed using DXA system with standardized acquisition protocols, capturing lumbar spine (L1–L4), left femoral neck, and total hip measurements. Participants were classified into osteoporosis (T-score ≤ -2.5 SD) and non-osteoporosis(T-score >-2.5 SD) groups according to WHO diagnostic criteria, as detailed in Fig. 1.

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Panyu Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine in strict compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. The requirement for written informed consent from patients was waived due to retrospective design. All personal information of the patients had already been de-identified during the analysis.

Patients and data

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent DXA during hospitalization. Inclusion Criteria: ① Age ≥ 45 years; ② Completion of standardized DXA assessment; ③ Availability of complete electronic medical records (including age, gender, and routine blood test data). Exclusion Criteria:① Secondary osteoporosis;②Hematological disorders; ③ History of malignancy; ④ Severe hepatic/renal insufficiency; ⑤ Acute infectious diseases; ⑥ Incomplete clinical data.

Baseline demographic characteristics (age and gender) and standardized laboratory data were collected through the following protocol: Fasting morning venous blood samples were obtained within 24 h of admission for all participants. Complete blood cell analysis — including hemoglobin (HGB), neutrophil count (NEUT), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), lymphocyte count (LYMPH), and monocyte count (MONO) — was performed using a Mindray BC-6800Plus hematology analyzer. Biochemical profiling (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], alkaline phosphatase [ALP], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], albumin [ALB], uric acid [UA], fasting blood glucose [FBG], calcium [Ca], phosphorus [Pi], and creatinine [Cr]) was conducted with a Cobas 8000 biochemical analyzer. Both laboratory assessments and DXA were completed during the same hospitalization period.Before model development, several preprocessing steps were performed to ensure data quality and consistency. Duplicate records and entries with apparent input errors were excluded. For variables with missing values less than 20%, imputation was performed using the mean for continuous variables and the mode for categorical variables. Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded from model construction.

Feature selection and model construction

In this study, the caret package in R was used to randomly divide the included patients into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). The training set was used for model construction, and the testing set was used to evaluate model performance.

To screen potential predictors in the training set, three independent methods were applied to analyse the baseline variables: univariate analysis, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Lasso regression, and Boruta algorithm [21]. Univariate analysis is a traditional screening method based on P-values, with a threshold of P < 0.05. Lasso regression identifies features with non-zero coefficients as potential predictors, effectively solving multicollinearity issues and avoiding model instability caused by excessive intervariable correlations [22]. In this study, Lasso regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to analyse baseline high-dimensional data and screen variables. The Boruta algorithm is a feature selection method based on variable importance measures, identifying significant features by comparing the Z-values of candidate features with those of “shadow features”. During each iteration, Z-values of real features were computed using a random forest (RF) classifier, while shadow feature Z-values were generated by randomly shuffling real features [23]. This algorithm iteratively eliminates features showing lower-than-random relevance.

Overlapping variables selected by univariate analysis, Lasso regression, and the Boruta algorithm were used to construct predictive models. A 10-fold cross-validation was applied to the training set: in each iteration, 90% of the data were used to train the model, while the remaining 10% were used for internal validation.Hyperparameter optimization for all machine learning models was performed via grid search combined with repeated 10-fold cross-validation within the training set to minimize overfitting and avoid information leakage. Detailed hyperparameter settings for all models are summarized in Supplementary Material. Ten supervised machine learning algorithms were implemented: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network (NN), random forest (RF), XGBoost, k-nearest neighbours (KNN), AdaBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost.

Model assessment

The optimal model was determined through comprehensive evaluation of discrimination capacity, calibration performance, and clinical applicability across all 10 candidate models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to visualize discriminative power, with the area under the curve (AUC) quantified as the primary metric. Complementary predictive performance was assessed through accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. Clinical validity and practical utility were further examined via decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration plots, respectively.

Model interpretation

By analyzing the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values, we quantified the contribution and significance of each feature to the final classification outcome. Higher SHAP values indicate a stronger influence on the model’s output. We present the feature importance analysis used to interpret the optimal model [24, 25].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software(version 4.4.3). Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as the median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage). Differences in continuous variables were assessed using an independent sample t-test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Lasso regression and Boruta analysis were conducted using the R packages “glmnet” and “Boruta,” respectively, while model training was performed with the “caret” package. By default, model hyperparameter tuning was automatically executed using the standard grid search method in the “caret” package. Additionally, SHAP value analysis was carried out using the “shapviz” package.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2022 and December 2023, a total of 11,241 orthopedic inpatients at Panyu Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine underwent DXA examination. After excluding 2,992 patients with missing test indicators and 105 patients with malignancies, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or acute infections, a final cohort of 8,144 patients was included. Among them, 4,933 were diagnosed with osteoporosis, while 3,211 were classified as non-osteoporotic. The detailed patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 5,702) and a testing set (n = 2,442) in a 7:3 ratio. A summary of the patient characteristics is presented in Table 1, indicating no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups.

Development of model

In the training dataset, 26 variables, including Age, Gender, HGB, NEUT, RBC, PLT, LYMPH, TC, TG, HDL_C, LDL_C, ALP, ALT, ALB, UA, Cr, Ca, Pi, MHR, NHR, PHR, LHR, SII, TyG, NHHR, and CHG, showed significant statistical differences between the osteoporosis and non-osteoporosis groups (P < 0.05). In contrast, MONO, AST, and FBG did not show significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05), as detailed in Table 2.

The 26 variables identified through univariate analysis were first subjected to Lasso regression for feature selection, followed by further optimization using the Boruta algorithm. The optimal lambda value for Lasso regression was 0.015, resulting in the selection of 11 key predictive factors, including demographic variables (age and gender) and laboratory indicators (HGB, ALP, UA, LYMPH, ALT, ALB, TyG, NHHR, and NEUT). The Boruta algorithm identified the same 11 key variables, fully aligning with the Lasso results (Fig. 2). Ultimately, age, gender, HGB, ALP, UA, LYMPH, ALT, ALB, TyG, NHHR, and NEUT were established as the core predictive factors for the model.

Evaluation of model

This study employed ten machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network (NN), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and categorical boosting (CatBoost), to predict osteoporosis based on variables identified through univariate analysis, LASSO regression, and the Boruta algorithm (including Gender, Age, HGB, ALP, UA, LYMPH, ALT, ALB, TyG, NHHR, and NEUT). Performance evaluation results revealed that the OP-LM exhibited the most robust performance in the testing set (Table 3).The logistic regression (OP-LM) model can be expressed as the following predictive equation:

As shown in Fig. 3, OP-LM demonstrated the highest discriminatory power in the testing set (AUC = 0.800, 95% CI: 0.783–0.818), achieving a balanced sensitivity (0.719) and specificity (0.740), with an F1 score of 0.761. Compared to AdaBoost (AUC = 0.696, 95% CI: 0.676–0.716) and XGBoost (AUC = 0.772, 95% CI: 0.754–0.791), OP-LM showed superior classification performance. While CatBoost exhibited slightly better specificity (0.827) and precision (0.844), its lower sensitivity (0.612), F1 score (0.710), and test AUC (0.794, 95% CI: 0.777–0.812) suggested limited generalization ability. GBM showed strong stability, with the smallest decrease in AUC from the training set (0.818, 95% CI: 0.807–0.829) to the testing set (0.797, 95% CI: 0.779–0.814), and performed well in specificity (0.835), precision (0.851), and F1 score (0.715), making it a strong alternative candidate model. The calibration curve demonstrated that OP-LM’s predicted probabilities closely matched the actual risk, with calibration curves for both the training and testing sets tightly following the ideal diagonal. In conclusion, OP-LM was established as the optimal model due to its superior discriminatory power, stable calibration performance, and resistance to overfitting. Model interpretability analysis and online application

Based on the SHAP framework, this study analyzes the decision-making mechanisms of the OP-LM model from both global and local perspectives. Figure 5A shows the SHAP summary plot, which ranks the feature importance from high to low as follows: Age, Gender, UA, ALP, HGB, NEUT, TyG, LYMPH, ALT, ALB, and NHHR. Figure 5B presents the standard bar chart of the mean absolute SHAP values for each predictor, arranged in descending order. Figure 5C and D further illustrate how SHAP values can be used to interpret individual model predictions. Age is the most important feature in the OP-LM, and Figs. 6A-F further elucidate the interactions between age and other features.

To enhance the model’s applicability in clinical settings and assist clinicians in making efficient decisions, we optimized the input variables by retaining only 11 key features, including several routine blood test indicators. To further improve the model’s accessibility and usability, we developed a dedicated website (https://op-lm.shinyapps.io/osteoporosis/).This user-friendly platform allows clinicians to input relevant patient data and quickly assess the probability of osteoporosis in their patients.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a machine learning-based model for predicting osteoporosis. Given the 32.1% prevalence of osteoporosis among individuals over 50 years old in China and the rapidly growing healthcare burden, the establishment of efficient screening tools has become an urgent need [26].A recent systematic review found that AI models achieved 99% accuracy in predicting osteoporotic fractures, significantly outperforming traditional methods like DXA and FRAX, which have accuracies around 80% [27]. OP-LM demonstrated consistent accuracy in both the training and validation cohorts (AUC 0.79–0.80). In terms of predictive performance, OP-LM showed a high AUC, indicating its accuracy and stability in predicting osteoporosis. Additionally, calibration curves and DCA further confirmed the model’s good calibration and clinical net benefit.

With over 500 million health checkups performed annually in China, bone density screening primarily relies on QUS [6, 28], whose diagnostic accuracy is limited by equipment calibration differences and site specificity [29, 30]. In contrast, OP-LM requires only routine blood indicators for osteoporosis screening and is highly compatible with the current health checkup process.

In this study, feature selection was used to improve the model’s interpretability and clinical utility. First, univariate analysis was used to preliminarily select potential associated variables. Then, Lasso regression addressed multicollinearity issues and reduced non-essential features, and finally, Boruta algorithm was used to verify feature stability based on the importance scores from random forest [6, 26, 28]. By reducing the number of predictive variables, OP-LM becomes easier to understand and apply, thereby simplifying the data collection process in clinical practice.

Notably, the concurrent inclusion of neutrophil and lymphocyte counts highlights the critical role of the chronic inflammatory microenvironment in bone loss. Neutrophils promote bone resorption directly by releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) [31–33]. Lymphocyte imbalance, such as Th17/Treg ratio disruption, may regulate osteoclast activation through the RANKL/OPG pathway, which aligns with the “bone immunology” theory [11]. Studies have shown that IL-17 secreted by Th17 cells can activate RANKL signaling to promote osteoclast differentiation, while Treg cells suppress bone resorption by secreting IL-10 [34]. Furthermore, the inclusion of the TyG index and NHHR confirms the disruptive effect of lipid metabolism on bone homeostasis [35, 36]. Hypertriglyceridemia can inhibit osteoblast differentiation, while abnormal HDL-C function may weaken its anti-inflammatory and bone-protective effects [37]. These findings expand the traditional understanding of osteoporosis mechanisms centered around calcium-phosphorus metabolism [38–40], providing new directions for targeted interventions.

SHAP analysis further validated the biological plausibility of the model. The primary contributions of age and gender align with the epidemiological characteristics of osteoporosis [41]. The significant SHAP values for alkaline ALP and HGB are consistent with their known biological functions [42, 43]. The bidirectional effect of uric acid, promoting oxidation at low levels and acting as an antioxidant at high levels, may explain its nonlinear risk association, which corroborates the findings of Lin [44]. The inclusion of alanine ALT may reflect the comorbid mechanism between non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and osteoporosis [45]. Liver-derived pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α inhibits osteogenesis through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a hypothesis partially confirmed by basic experiments conducted by Nagao [46]. NHHR is an emerging marker of systemic inflammation and cardiovascular risk, which could indirectly influence bone health through inflammatory pathways [47]. Although NHHR contributions are modest compared to primary predictors, including such features can improve model robustness by capturing subtle metabolic or inflammatory signals.This strategy of exploring multi-organ interactions from routine tests provides a solid foundation for the biological credibility of low-cost screening tools.

Interestingly, in our study, logistic regression outperformed more complex machine learning models such as Random Forest and LightGBM on external validation data, despite some complex models achieving perfect AUC in the training set. This discrepancy likely reflects overfitting, a common issue when models with high complexity are trained on limited datasets, allowing them to memorize the training data rather than generalize to new data. Logistic regression, as a simpler and more regularized model, is less prone to overfitting and can provide more stable predictive performance in practice. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies comparing simple and complex models for clinical prediction tasks, where simpler models sometimes outperform complex algorithms in external validation due to better generalizability [48, 49].

In this study, we integrated novel metabolic-inflammatory composite indicators such as TYG and NHHR with related indicators like lymphocyte and neutrophil counts from routine blood tests, revealing the complex interactions between bone metabolism and immune-metabolism-inflammation. Our findings suggest that OP-LM may help identify early-stage osteoporosis patients, enabling clinicians to perform initial screening, promote further tests, and support treatment decisions. Although direct comparison with QUS was not possible in our dataset, previous studies have reported that QUS often shows lower sensitivity, ranging from 65% to 75%, compared with DXA [50], which highlights the potential of OP-LM to provide more reliable early risk stratification.We developed a web-based calculator for preliminary osteoporosis screening using routine blood tests, enabling practical use in clinical settings. Potential barriers include clinician training, patient education, and integration with electronic health record systems. With proper training and seamless integration, this tool can be effectively implemented in real-world practice. Particularly in primary healthcare institutions, OP-LM could serve as an intelligent pre-screening tool before DXA testing, reducing the number of patients requiring imaging diagnosis, making full use of blood-related indicators, and significantly optimizing healthcare resource allocation—representing the model’s greatest clinical value.

However, our study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, so selection bias cannot be entirely avoided. Second, due to the lack of an external validation cohort, we relied solely on an internal testing set for model validation, which may limit the generalizability and robustness of the findings. Third, although a 10-fold cross-validation and independent test set were used to mitigate overfitting, we did not employ conduct formal statistical tests for model performance comparison. These omissions may lead to a slight overestimation of internal performance and limit the statistical rigor of algorithmic comparisons. Fourth, some data were incomplete because of the retrospective design, including height, weight, and medical history, which were therefore excluded from the analysis. Additionally, the study population consisted of orthopedic inpatients, more than half of whom were diagnosed with osteoporosis, which may differ from community or primary care populations. Despite these limitations, our preliminary study provides a foundation for future research and highlights the potential clinical utility of the OP-LM calculator, particularly as a pre-screening tool in broader healthcare settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed and validated an interpretable prediction model for osteoporosis risk based on routine blood tests. This study demonstrates the potential of machine learning models, particularly the logistic regression model (OP-LM), to serve as a convenient prescreening tool. The accompanying online calculator could, after further validation, help increase preliminary screening rates and optimize the allocation of DXA resources by identifying high-risk individuals. Future prospective and multi-center studies are needed to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility in real-world settings.
