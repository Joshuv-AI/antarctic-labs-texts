Asymptotics of Non-Convex Generalized Linear Models in High-Dimensions: A proof of the replica formula
Matteo Vilucchio, Yatin Dandi, Matéo Pirio Rossignol, Cedric Gerbelot, Florent Krzakala

Abstract
The analytic characterization of the high-dimensional behavior of optimization for Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) with Gaussian data has been a central focus in statistics and probability in recent years. While convex cases, such as the LASSO, ridge regression, and logistic regression, have been extensively studied using a variety of techniques, the non-convex case remains far less understood despite its significance. A non-rigorous statistical physics framework has provided remarkable predictions for the behavior of high-dimensional optimization problems, but rigorously establishing their validity for non-convex problems has remained a fundamental challenge. In this work, we address this challenge by developing a systematic framework that rigorously proves replica-symmetric formulas for non-convex GLMs and precisely determines the conditions under which these formulas are valid. Remarkably, the rigorous replica-symmetric predictions align exactly with the conjectures made by physicists, and the so-called replicon condition . The originality of our approach lies in connecting two powerful theoretical tools: the Gaussian Min-Max Theorem, which we use to provide precise lower bounds, and Approximate Message Passing (AMP), which is shown to achieve these bounds algorithmically. We demonstrate the utility of this framework through significant applications: (i) by proving the optimality of the Tukey loss over the more commonly used Huber loss under a ε \varepsilon contaminated data model, (ii) establishing the optimality of negative regularization in high-dimensional non-convex regression and (iii) characterizing the performance limits of linearized AMP algorithms. By rigorously validating statistical physics predictions in non-convex settings, we aim to open new pathways for analyzing increasingly complex optimization landscapes beyond the convex regime.

Introduction

High-dimensional optimization problems are ubiquitous in modern machine learning and statistics, with applications ranging from sparse regression, to kernels, to neural networks. In statistics, mathematics, and theoretical machine learning, a large body of works have been dedicated to the characterization of the analytical asymptotic performance with synthetic data (see e.g.
(

;

;

(

;

)
or random feature regression
(
Mei and Montanari, 2022b
;

)
, the non-convex landscape remains largely unexplored despite its practical significance.

We shall be interested here in the

(2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}_{d}

In the rest of the manuscript we will consider the Gaussian case where the samples

.

The convex setting is well understood, as powerful theoretical tools have emerged to analyze these high-dimensional problems. Two such important tools, in particular, are the Gaussian min-max theorem
(
Gordon, 1988
;
Stojnic, 2009
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2015
;
Loureiro et al., 2021a
)
, and the analysis of fixed-points of the approximate message passing algorithms (AMP)
(
Donoho et al., 2010
;
Bayati and Montanari, 2011
;
Bolthausen, 2014
;
Loureiro et al., 2021b
)
. Another, different, approach, has been through analyses based on random matrix theory
(
Dobriban and Wager, 2018
;
Richards et al., 2021
;
Bach, 2024
)
. However, extending these results beyond convex setting has remained a fundamental challenge, as the existing techniques heavily rely on convex analysis.

Yet, there are strong indication that these limits are not fundamental. Indeed powerful heuristic have been developed for these problem by the statistical physics community though the use of the replica and cavity methods
(
Mézard et al., 1987
;
Mezard and Montanari, 2009
;
Advani et al., 2013
;
Zdeborová and Krzakala, 2016
)
. These methods predict a whole range of formula
very similar
to the ones appearing in convex cases, but it is notably difficult to establish their validity
(
Talagrand, 2003
)
. In particular, convexity does not play a fundamental role in these predictions, which instead rely on the so-called
replica symmetric
assumption.

In this work, we bridge this gap, and provide
a rigorous proof
of the statistical physics predictions for the loss and the generalization predicted by these formulae in the non-convex case, that matches exactly the validity range of the replica symmetric assumption predicted by physicists. The originality of our approach is that we use a combination of both the Gaussian min-max theorem, and the message passing techniques. Specifically we use Gaussian min-max theorem as a
lower bound for the true minimum of the optimization problem
. This true minimum can be characterized as the solution of a low dimensional problem. Later,
we show how this bound can be achieved through a carefully designed AMP algorithm
that depends only on the solution of the low dimensional problem, and whose convergence can be checked from a low dimensional integral depending on the solution of the low dimensional problem. This framework not only provides rigorous mathematical guarantees but also validates numerous predictions from statistical physics that have long suggested the existence of such characterizations beyond the convex regime.

These low-dimensional fixed-point equations we provide emerge naturally from both the Gaussian min-max theorem analysis and the state evolution of AMP algorithms.
Furthermore, our analysis reveals that the validity of these characterizations does not depend on convexity. Instead, it relies on a more subtle stability condition (known in physics as the
replicon
or
de Almeida-Thouless
condition
(
de Almeida and Thouless, 1978
;
De Dominicis and Kondor, 1983
)
) that can be verified directly — and trivially — from the low-dimensional equations. Interestingly, this condition turns out to be the same as the point-wise stability convergence criterion of the message passing algorithm
(
Bolthausen, 2014
)
. The replicon condition, which we fully characterize, determines when the high-dimensional optimization landscape is sufficiently well-behaved for our analysis to hold. Notably, this condition can be satisfied even in clearly non-convex settings, explaining the empirically observed success of certain optimization procedures in non-convex high-dimensional problems.
The validity of our results are precisely those conjectured to be necessary by statistical physics: when this condition is not satisfied
the replica symmetric formula are known to be wrong (and needs to be replaced by the more involved replica symmetry broken ones
(
Talagrand, 2006
)
, which are beyond the scope of this paper).

We demonstrate the power of this approach through some applications that have attracted significant recent attention: negative regularization in high-dimensional regression for generic losses
(
Tsigler and Bartlett, 2023
;
Kobak et al., 2020
)
, the performance of spectral algorithms

(
Mondelli and Montanari, 2018
;
Lu and Li, 2020
;
Maillard et al., 2022
)
, and the study of non convex losses
(
Loh, 2017
;
Chang et al., 2018
)
in robust training
(
Vilucchio et al., 2024
;
Adomaityte et al., 2024
)
. Our results open new pathways for analyzing increasingly complex optimization landscapes in high-dimensional statistics, offering both theoretical insights and practical algorithmic implications for modern machine learning problems.

1.1
Further related works

Our work synthesizes and extends several fundamental theoretical frameworks in high-dimensional statistics and optimization, as well as in the mathematical spin glass litterature.

The Gaussian Min-Max Theorem (GMT) —

The GMT
(
Gordon, 1988
)
, later strengthened to the Convex GMT
(
Thrampoulidis et al., 2015
)
, is a powerful tool for analyzing high-dimensional problems.
While it has proven highly effective for convex problems
Thrampoulidis et al. (2018)
;
Javanmard and Soltanolkotabi (2022)
;
Loureiro et al. (2021a)
, its application to non-convex settings has remained limited.
Our work demonstrates how GMT can be extended beyond convexity through careful analysis of the associated low-dimensional optimization landscape.

Approximate Message Passing (AMP) —

Originating in spin glass theory as the Thouless-Anderson-Palmer (TAP) equations
(
Thouless et al., 1977
)
for the Sherrington-Kirpatrick model
(
Mézard et al., 1987
)
, these message passing algorithms have witnessed a burst of popularity after the work of
Bolthausen (2014)
and its extension to compressed sensing in
Donoho et al. (2010)
. They have become a cornerstone of high-dimensional inference with synthetic data. The precise analysis of AMP dynamics
(
Bayati and Montanari, 2011
)
revealed that these algorithms can often achieve fundamental statistical limits. Recent work has extended the analysis of AMP to a broader class of inference problems through oriented graph representations
(
Gerbelot and Berthier, 2023
)
.
Related to us are the works of
Montanari and Richard (2015)
, who characterized phase transitions in estimation error for non-negative PCA, and
Celentano et al. (2023)
;
Celentano (2024)
, who established local convexity of the so-called TAP free energy and finite-sample convergence guarantees for Z2-synchronization.
Their approach also relies on a combination of Gaussian comparison inequalities and message passing iterations, albeit for a restricted class of problems.
In our approach, we use AMP across a much broader class of non-convex problems.

Spin glass theory —

Our work builds upon fundamental results in spin glass theory. The mathematical treatment of replica symmetry was pioneered by
Talagrand (2003)
.
This was later extended to the full Parisi solution
(
Talagrand, 2006
;
Panchenko, 2013
)
, establishing the exactness of the replica method predictions. In this work, we focus on the simpler replica symmetric regime.

Applications —

Our framework also yields new insights in two important areas that have attracted significant recent attention.
The first concerns negative regularization, which has been primarily studied in the specific context of ridge regression
(
Hastie et al., 2022
;
Wu and Xu, 2020
;
Richards et al., 2021
)
, and the analysis can be carried out because of the quadratic loss.
Our results extend these insights to general loss functions, providing an interest in robust statistics where non-convex losses such as Tukey’s biweight and Cauchy loss functions are used for handling outliers
(
Lozano and Meinshausen, 2013
;
Loh, 2024
)
.
Another application provides a comprehensive analysis of linearized AMP algorithms, and the performance of spectral method, where we show we can recover directly the results from
(
Montanari and Richard, 2015
;
Mondelli and Montanari, 2018
;
Lu and Li, 2020
;
Maillard et al., 2022
)
, without resorting to random matrix theory tools.

3.1
Non Convex Loss Function for Robust M-Estimation

A fundamental challenge in high-dimensional statistics is developing estimators that are robust to outliers while maintaining statistical efficiency. Classical robust statistics has extensively studied this problem in the low-dimensional regime
(
Huber, 1981
;
Maronna et al., 2019
)
, with particular focus on M-estimators.

The design and analysis of loss functions that effectively handle outliers in regression has been central to robust statistics. The classical literature has introduced numerous such functions, emphasizing non-convex alternatives to least squares estimation. Notable examples include Tukey’s biweight function
(
Tukey, 1960
)
and the Cauchy loss
(
Lehmann and Casella, 2006
)
, which offer superior robustness properties compared to convex alternatives. Recent work by
Lozano and Meinshausen (2013)
and
Loh (2017)
has renewed interest in this area, studying stationary points of nonconvex functions for robust linear regression within a local neighborhood of the true regression vector.

The theoretical analysis of these estimators in the proportional high-dimensional regime has remained challenging due to their non-convex nature. Our theorem provides a framework to address this challenge, yielding sharp asymptotic characterizations of both convex and non-convex robust regression estimators.

To illustrate our results, we consider the performance of the ERM estimator from
Eq.

4
with L2 loss
\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{L2}}(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{2}
, the Huber Loss function
\mathscr{L}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{H}}(u)=\xi^{2}\int_{0}^{\absolutevalue{u}/\xi}\min(1,x)\differential{x}
, the Tukey biweight loss function
\mathscr{L}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{T}}(u)
, and the Cauchy loss function
\mathscr{L}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{C}}(u)
defined as

\mathscr{L}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{T}}(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}\quantity[1 - \left(1 - \frac{u^2}{\xi^2}\right)^3],&\text{if }|u|\leq\xi,\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}+\varrho\absolutevalue{u - \xi}^{3},&\text{if }u>\xi,\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}+\varrho\absolutevalue{u + \xi}^{3},&\text{if }u<-\xi.\end{cases}\,,\quad\mathscr{L}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{C}}(u)=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right)\,,

(20)

where
u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=y-z
and with
\ell_{2}
regularization
\mathscr{R}_{\lambda}(w)=\frac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}
.
The Tukey loss includes an additional coercive term with parameter
\varrho
, required for our theoretical framework to apply. In practice, for sufficiently small
\varrho
, this modification has negligible effect on the estimator’s behavior.

In

Since our theory shows that the estimation error converges to a deterministic function of the model parameters, optimizing the hyperparameters

reduces to a two-dimensional optimization problem. While a rigorous justification of this hyperparameter optimization, which requires strengthening our statement from pointwise to uniform convergence, could likely be obtained using techniques similar to those in

Nonetheless, we observe strong agreement between our asymptotic predictions and finite-dimensional simulations.

3.2
Phase Transitions in Negative Regularization for Generalized Linear Models

We will now consider the phenomenon of negative regularization in high-dimensional GLMs. While this effect has been extensively studied for ridge regression
(
Hastie et al., 2022
;
Wu and Xu, 2020
;
Richards et al., 2021
)
, its behavior for non-quadratic loss functions and
\ell_{2}
regularization remains poorly understood.
This is a technical hurdle as the previous results strongly rely on Random Matrix Theory.
We provide a characterization of this phenomenon for a broad class of loss functions.

For a convex
\mathscr{L}(y,z)
with sub-quadratic growth at infinity, setting
\lambda<0
leads the minimization objective to diverge to
-\infty
as
\norm{\w}\rightarrow\infty
. However, up to a threshold, the objective admits a local minimizer.
While
Theorem

2.1
does not directly hold for the case of negative regularization we characterize the training landscape by studying the problem defined in
Eq.

2
with
a=b=q
, as a function of
q
.

For a non-negative regularization strength (
\lambda\geq 0
), convexity of the loss ensures the existence of a unique minimizer whose normalized squared norm converges almost surely to a finite value.
As we move into negative regularization, there exists a critical threshold
\lambda_{c}<0
that separates the case with and without a local minima.
For
\lambda\in(\lambda_{c},0]
, the loss landscape admits at least two distinct minimizers: a local minimizer at finite norm and a global minimizer whose norm diverges to infinity.
Below the critical threshold (
\lambda<\lambda_{c}
), the landscape no longer presents any finite-norm minimizer, and the objective function is minimized through a sequence of solutions with diverging norms.

These theoretical predictions are validated by numerical experiments shown in
Figure

2

(Left)
for the Huber loss
\mathscr{L}_{H}^{\xi}
, which presents the limiting training objective
{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}
as a function of the norm of the solution
q
for different values of
\lambda
.
For
\lambda>0
the loss objective presents a single minima because of convexity.
For
\lambda<0
the training loss develops both a local minimum at finite
q
(which continuously extends from the global minimum at positive
\lambda
) and a global minimum at diverging
q
.
The minima exists only for values of
\lambda<0
not too negative.
Crucially, these two minima are separated by a local maximum, suggesting that local optimization algorithms initialized at small norm will converge to the non-trivial local minimum.
Additionally, in
Figure

2

(Right)
we consider gradient descent initialized at different norms in the training profile for

3.3
Spectral Method in Generalized Linear Models

Last but not least we now present interesting results in the analysis of spectral methods. A standard question is how many observations are required to obtain a better-than-random prediction within a class of algorithms, also known as
weak learnability
. This can be studied both
statistically
(within the class of all, including exponential time algorithms) or
computationally
(restricted to a particular computational class, such as first-order algorithms). For generalized linear models, often called single-index model in this context, weak learnability has been heavily studied. The statistical threshold for learnability has been characterised by

Barbier et al. (2019)

when the covariate dimension
d
is large. Optimal computational thresholds for the class of first-order iterative algorithms were also derived in
(
Mondelli and Montanari, 2018
;
Luo et al., 2019
;
Celentano et al., 2020
;
Maillard et al., 2020
)
. A natural question is wether spectral methods can capture this optimal threshold, and several works have considered this problem, in particular,
Lu and Li (2020)
and
Mondelli and Montanari (2018)
considered the problem with random matrix theory tools and discussed the weak recovery phase transition. More recently,
Maillard et al. (2022)
connected these spectral methods to approximate message passing algorithms.

The traditional analyses of such spectral methods often rely on intricate random matrix theory arguments, requiring careful control of both the bulk distribution of eigenvalues and the behavior of isolated eigenvalues. We shall show that our framework provides a more direct route to analyzing these estimators. Specifically, consider the analysis of spectral estimators for matrices of the form

A=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}(y_{\mu})\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}^{\top},

(21)

where
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a preprocessing function and the observations follow a GLM as
y_{\mu}=\varphi(\frac{1}{d}\langle\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}},\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\rangle)
. The classical spectral method estimates the signal by computing the leading eigenvector of
A
. By the Courant-Fischer-Weyl min-max principle, this is equivalent to solving

\lambda_{\mathrm{true}}=\min_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{K}_{1,1}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}(y_{\mu})\quantity(\w^\top\x_\dataidx)^{2}\,,\quad\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{K}_{1,1}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}(y_{\mu})\quantity(\w^\top\x_\dataidx)^{2}\,,

(22)

where
\mathcal{K}_{1,1}
denotes the unit sphere.

Consider the estimate
\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
obtained from
Algorithm

1
and its associated eigenvalue estimate
\lambda_{\mathrm{amp}}=\langle\hat{{\mathbf{w}}},A\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}\rangle/\norm{\wminimizer}_{2}^{2}
. In the high-dimensional limit where both the dimension
d
and sample size
n
grow to infinity at a fixed ratio
\alpha=n/d
, we prove that
\lambda_{\mathrm{amp}}
converges almost surely to the true spectral estimate
\lambda_{\mathrm{true}}
. This convergence result relies on showing that the AMP algorithm converges to a fixed point that necessarily satisfies the first-order optimality conditions of the original spectral optimization problem.

Specifically, let
m=\langle\tilde{{\mathbf{w}}},\hat{\mathbf{w}}\rangle/d
denote the overlap of the solution with the teacher vector. In the high-dimensional limit, for
\rho=\lim_{d\to\infty}\norm{\wteacher}_{2}^{2}/d=1
, our main theorem proves that
m
behaves as the solution of the following system of equations

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\frac{a(m,\tau)}{\sqrt{\alpha a^{2}(m,\tau)+b(m,\tau)}}\,,

\displaystyle\,a(m,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\quantity[\qty(s - \frac{m h}{\sqrt{1 - m^2}} ) P(m,\tau)]\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha\sqrt{\alpha a^{2}(m,\tau)+b(m,\tau)}}\,,

\displaystyle\,b(m,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathbb{E}\quantity[\qty(s - \frac{m h}{\sqrt{1 - m^2}} - P(m,\tau) )^2]\,,

(24)

where the expectation is taken over
s,h\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
y\sim P_{\mathrm{out}}\quantity(\cdot\mid s)
, and where
P(m,\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{P}_{\tau\mathscr{L}(y,\cdot)}\quantity(s - \sfrac{m h}{\sqrt{1 - m^2}})
with
\mathscr{L}(y,z)=\mathcal{T}(y)z^{2}
.

These equations fully characterize the performance of both the spectral method and the AMP algorithm in the high-dimensional limit.
This result provides a precise analytical description of the spectral estimator’s performance without requiring random matrix theory tools.

Our analysis reveals that the algorithmic and statistical aspects of spectral estimation are intimately connected through the state evolution of AMP.
In particular, the fixed point equations above allow us to precisely characterize the phase transition boundary between successful and unsuccessful recovery, as well as the asymptotic correlation achieved in the recovery phase.

Our proof technique’s originality lies in synthesizing two established methodologies in high-dimensional statistics.
While each approach has been studied separately, their combination yields a framework that mirrors the intuition from statistical mechanics. Specifically, our approach first derives equations for the order parameters through comparison inequalities, analogous to the replica method, and then verifies their physical realizability through the stability analysis of the corresponding dynamics.

The methodology developed in this paper opens several promising research directions.
A natural extension would be to problems exhibiting replica symmetry breaking, where the current Gaussian comparison bounds would need to be generalized to account for multiple pure states.
Such an extension could provide insights into problems like sparse PCA or community detection in the hard phase.
Furthermore, our proof technique suggests a general recipe for analyzing non-convex optimization landscapes: first establishing information-theoretic limits through comparison inequalities, then constructing explicit algorithms that achieve these bounds.

We will use the following definition extensively.

Definition 5.1

(Pseudo-Lipschitz function)

A function

+ \abs{x}^{k-1}
+ \abs{y}^{k-1}
)\absolutevalue{x - y}\,.

(30)

We also define the following two proprierties of a function

Definition 5.2

(Prox-Coercive function)
.

A function

is said to be prox-coercive if for any
r,\delta\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto f(r)+\frac{(\delta-r)^{2}}{2}
is coercive in

i.e.

\lim_{|r|\rightarrow\infty}f(r)+\frac{(\delta-r)^{2}}{2}=\infty\,.

(31)

Definition 5.3

(Locally lower bounded)
.

A function
f
is said to be locally lower bounded if, for any point
r\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a neighborhood

(32)

Definition 5.4

(Weakly convex function)
.

Fix

. A function

is said to be
V_{0}
-weakly convex (resp. strictly
V_{0}
-weakly convex) if the function

A function

is said to be weakly convex if it is
V_{0}
-weakly convex for some

.

Remark 5.1
.

From this definition three remarks are in order

•

f
is convex if and only if it is
V_{0}
-weakly convex for any
V_{0}\in[0,\infty)
.

•

For a given

is

-weakly convex if and only if
V_{0}f
is
1
-weakly convex.

•

If

is strictly

Remark 5.2
.

We additionally note that the notable example of the negative perceptron, where the considered function is the negative step function is not considered in our framework.

(45)

Similarly, we can obtain the corresponding result for

Lemma 5.2

(Coercivity of Moreau Envelopes)
.

For any coercive function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
lower bounded by
C
, the Moreau envelope
\mathcal{M}_{f(\cdot)}
is coercive.

Proof of
Lemma

5.2
:

By the definition of Moreau Envelope form

\mathcal{M}_{f(\cdot)}(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{f(y)+\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{x - y}^{2}\right\}\,.

(46)

The coercivity of
f
implies that for any

,
\exists R(M)>0
such that:

\forall\absolutevalue{x}>R(M)\implies f(x)>M.

(47)

Now, set
\tilde{R}=R(M)+\sqrt{2(M+|C|)}
. Suppose that
\absolutevalue{x}>\tilde{R}
. Then, for any

with
\absolutevalue{y}>R(M)
, we have:

f(y)+\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{x - y}^{2}\geq f(y)>M\,,

(48)

while for any

with

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{x - y}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{x - y}^{2}-C\geq\frac{1}{2}(\absolutevalue{x}-\absolutevalue{y})^{2}-C\geq\frac{1}{2}(\absolutevalue{x}-R(M))^{2}-C>\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{2(M+|C|})^{2}-C\geq M\,,

(49)

where the last inequality follows since
\absolutevalue{x}>\tilde{R}=R(M)+\sqrt{2M}
. Therefore, for any

\mathcal{M}_{f(\cdot)}

∎

The other two tools that are needed in the subsequent analysis concern the characterization of proximal selections under weak convexity conditions.
The following theorem is the main technical tool that allows us to translate weak convexity assumptions on the loss and regularization functions into Lipschitz continuity properties of their proximal operators.

Theorem 5.3

(Main Characterization of Proximal Selections)
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous and prox-coercive.
Then the proximal operator
\mathcal{P}_{f}
of
f
is never empty-valued, that is,
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
. When this condition holds,
any proximal selection
r_{\min}
of
f
is non-decreasing,
almost everywhere differentiable, and the following equivalences are satisfied:

(i)\qquad r_{\min}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})\iff f\text{ is strictly }1\text{-weakly convex},

(50)

(ii)\qquad r_{\min}\text{ is }1\text{-Lipschitz continuous over }\mathbb{R}\iff f\text{ is convex},

(51)

and for any
L\in(1,\infty)
,

(iii)\qquad r_{\min}\text{ is }L\text{-Lipschitz continuous over }\mathbb{R}\iff f\text{ is }\frac{L}{L-1}\text{-weakly convex}.

(52)

Furthermore, when any of these conditions hold,
r_{\min}
is uniquely determined by the set equality

\forall\delta\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)=\{r_{\min}(\delta)\}\,.

(53)

In this case,
r_{\min}
is onto, and for
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the interval over which
r_{\min}
takes the constant value
r_{0}
is given by

[r_{0}+f^{\prime}_{-}(r_{0}),r_{0}+f^{\prime}_{+}(r_{0})]\,.

(54)

In particular,
r_{\min}
is increasing if and only if
f
is differentiable.

The following corollary establishes that

Corollary 5.4

Moreover under

(respectively

5.2
Technical Framework

Our analysis relies on two theoretical frameworks: Generalized Approximate Message Passing (GAMP) and the Gaussian Min-max Theorem (GMT).

GAMP is a class of iterative algorithms characterized by coupled iterations, as in

In the high-dimensional limit of

\displaystyle\beta_{t}

(56)

\displaystyle\Omega_{s,t}

Theorems

5.3
and
5.4
.

As this appendix may be of broader interest beyond the setting of the main paper, we slightly modify the notation. We will work, whenever possible, with a generic function
f\,:\,\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and denote
E_{f}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
as the set of inputs
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\cdot)
is non-empty, where
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\cdot)
is here defined as :

\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\argmin_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{(r-\delta)^{2}}{2}+f(r).

(180)

Similarly, the Moreau enveloppe is here defined as :

\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{(r-\delta)^{2}}{2}+f(r).

(181)

Throughout, we denote by
r_{\min}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
an arbitrary proximal selection defined on
E_{f}
, i.e.
\forall\,\delta\in E_{f},r_{\min}(\delta)\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
. When the proximal operator is singleton-valued, the proximal selection is uniquely determined.

Before starting we also recall a fundamental property of convex functions that will be used repeatedly.

Lemma A.1

(Properties of Convex Functions)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex function. The set of points where
g
is finite is an interval
J
. On the interior
\mathring{J}
, the left and right derivatives
g^{\prime}_{-}
and
g^{\prime}_{+}
exist everywhere and satisfy:

(i)

g^{\prime}_{-}
and
g^{\prime}_{+}
are non-decreasing functions with
g^{\prime}_{-}\leq g^{\prime}_{+}
.

(ii)

g^{\prime}
is defined almost everywhere on
J
and is non-decreasing.

(iii)

For all
x\in\mathring{J}
,
g^{\prime}_{-}(x)=g^{\prime}(x^{-})
and
g^{\prime}_{+}(x)=g^{\prime}(x^{+})
.

(iv)

g
is differentiable at
\delta\in\mathring{J}
if and only if
g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta)=g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta)
, which holds if and only if
g^{\prime}
is continuous at
\delta
.

A.1
Existence and Basic Properties of Proximal Operators

Although it is a necessary condition, asking that
f
is lower semi continuous (l.s.c.) does not guarantee non-emptiness of the proximal operator of
f
. Indeed,
r\mapsto\exp(-r^2)-\frac{r^{2}}{2}
is l.s.c. but its proximal operator at
\delta=0
is empty because the minimizer has fled to infinity. The purpose of the prox-coercive property that we impose on
f
is to prevent this from happening.

We first recall key properties of proximal operators for weakly convex functions.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose
f
is lower semi-continuous and prox-coercive. For
V\in[0,1]
,
E_{Vf}=\mathbb{R}
and in particular the Moreau envelope of
Vf
is always finite. Furthermore, for any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{P}_{Vf}(\delta)
is a compact set.

Proof of
Proposition

A.2
:

Fix
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and
V\in(0,1]
. Consider a minimizing sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

\frac{(\delta-r_{n})^{2}}{2}+Vf(r_{n})\to\mathcal{M}_{Vf}(\delta)\in[-\infty,\infty).

Suppose by contradiction that
(|r_{n}|)_{n}
is unbounded. Then it has a subsequence with
|r_{n_{k}}|\to\infty
. Since
f
is prox-coercive,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left[(\delta-r_{n_{k}})^{2}/2+f(r_{n_{k}})\right]=\infty
.
However, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{(\delta-r_{n})^{2}}{2}+Vf(r_{n})=V\left[\frac{(\delta-r_{n})^{2}}{2}+f(r_{n})\right]+(1-V)\frac{(\delta-r_{n})^{2}}{2}.

Since
V\in(0,1]
and the second term is non-negative, taking
\limsup
along the subsequence yields a contradiction to the convergence of the left-hand side.

Thus
(r_{n})_{n}
is bounded. Let
r
be an accumulation point. By lower semi-continuity, taking
\liminf
along a convergent subsequence yields

(\delta-r)^{2}/2+Vf(r)\leq\mathcal{M}_{Vf}(\delta)
.
The reverse inequality holds by definition of the Moreau envelope, establishing that
r\in\mathcal{P}_{Vf}(\delta)
.

The same argument shows
\mathcal{P}_{Vf}(\delta)
is closed and bounded, hence compact.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

The following function is convex:

g:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}-\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}.

Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is almost everywhere twice differentiable on the interval
I_{f}:=\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}\mid\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)>-\infty\}
.

In addition, for
\delta\in I_{f}
,

\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\subset[g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta),g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta)].

If
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is differentiable at
\delta\in E_{f}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{f}
is continuous at
\delta
), then
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
is a singleton.

Furthermore, any proximal selection of
f
is non-decreasing on
E_{f}
. If there exists a non-trivial interval
I\subset I_{f}
such that
I\subset E_{f}
, this selection is continuous at
\delta
if and only if
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is differentiable at
\delta
, and differentiable almost everywhere on
I
.

Proof of
Proposition

A.3
:

We can rewrite
g(\delta)
as

g(\delta)=\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\left[\delta r-\frac{r^{2}}{2}-f(r)\right].

Since
\delta\mapsto\delta r-\frac{r^{2}}{2}-f(r)
is convex for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
g
is convex as a supremum of convex functions. By
Lemma

A.1
,
g
is differentiable almost everywhere on
I_{f}
, hence so is
\mathcal{M}_{f}
.

Now fix
\delta\in I_{f}
and suppose
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
is non-empty. Take
r_{\min}(\delta)\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
. For
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta^{\prime}\neq\delta
,

\frac{\delta^{\prime 2}}{2}-\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta^{\prime})\geq\frac{\delta^{\prime 2}}{2}-\frac{(\delta^{\prime}-r)^{2}}{2}-f(r)=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}-\frac{(\delta-r)^{2}}{2}-f(r)+(\delta^{\prime}-\delta)r.

At
r=r_{\min}(\delta)
, this gives

g(\delta^{\prime})-g(\delta)\geq(\delta^{\prime}-\delta)r_{\min}(\delta).

Dividing by
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\neq 0
yields
r_{\min}(\delta)\in[g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta),g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta)]
.

By
Lemma

A.1
,
g
is differentiable at
\delta
if and only if
g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta)=g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta)
, which implies
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
is a singleton.

For any proximal selection
r_{\min}
defined on
E_{f}
and
\delta<\delta^{\prime}
in
E_{f}
,

r_{\min}(\delta)\leq g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta)=g^{\prime}(\delta^{+})\leq g^{\prime}(\delta^{\prime-})=g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta^{\prime})\leq r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime}),

where we used
Lemma

A.1
. Thus
r_{\min}
is non-decreasing.

For continuity, suppose
\delta\in\mathring{I}
and
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is differentiable at
\delta
. Since
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{f}
is continuous at
\delta
, there exist sequences
(\delta_{n,\pm})_{n}\subset I
with
\delta_{n,\pm}\to\delta^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{M}_{f}
differentiable at each
\delta_{n,\pm}
. Then

r_{\min}(\delta_{n,\pm})=\delta_{n,\pm}-\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{f}(\delta_{n,\pm})\to\delta-\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{f}(\delta)=r_{\min}(\delta),

establishing continuity. Conversely, the above chain of inequalities yield at
\delta\in\mathring{I}

r_{\min}(\delta^{-})\leq g^{\prime}_{+}(\delta^{-})\leq g^{\prime}_{-}(\delta^{+})\leq r_{\min}(\delta^{+}).

If
r_{\min}
is continuous at
\delta
then so are
g^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is differentiable at
\delta
.

Almost everywhere differentiability follows from Lebesgue’s theorem on monotone functions.
∎

Remark A.1
.

In particular, if
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
, such as when
f
is lower semi-continuous and prox-coercive (by Proposition
A.2
), then any proximal selection of
f
is non-decreasing.

A.2
Characterization via Chord Slope Functions

Proposition A.4
.

Take
r_{\min}
to be a proximal selection of
f
defined on
E_{f}
. Then we have

E_{f}=\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}\mid\exists r_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\,s_{f}(r_{0})\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{0})\},

(182)

and for any
r_{0},\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,

r_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\iff s_{f}(r_{0})\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{0}),

(183)

where
s_{f}
and
i_{f}
come from
Definition

5.6
.

Proof of
Proposition

A.4
:

For any
r_{0},\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, by the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
,

r_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\iff\forall r\in\mathbb{R},\quad\delta(r-r_{0})\leq f(r)-f(r_{0})+\frac{r+r_{0}}{2}(r-r_{0}).

Separating the cases
r>r_{0}
and
r<r_{0}
, this is equivalent to

\forall r>r_{0},\quad\delta\leq\frac{f(r)-f(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}+\frac{r+r_{0}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\forall r<r_{0},\quad\delta\geq\frac{f(r)-f(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}+\frac{r+r_{0}}{2}.

By the definitions of supremum and infimum, we obtain
Eq.

183
.
To establish
Eq.

182
we notice the following. We have that
\delta\in E_{f}
if and only if
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\neq\emptyset
, which occurs if and only if there exists
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with
s_{f}(r_{0})\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{0})
.
∎

Remark A.2
.

Proposition

A.4
implies that for
\delta\in E_{f}
, the left chords of
f
at
r_{0}
are uniformly bounded above, and the right chords are uniformly bounded below. It also implies

f^{\prime}_{-}(r_{0}):=\limsup_{r\to r_{0}^{-}}\frac{f(r)-f(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}\leq f^{\prime}_{+}(r_{0}):=\liminf_{r\to r_{0}^{+}}\frac{f(r)-f(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}.

Proposition A.5
.

The following equivalence holds:

\forall r\in\mathbb{R},\quad s_{f}(r)\leq i_{f}(r)\iff f\text{ is }1\text{-weakly convex},

where
s_{f}
and
i_{f}
come again from
Definition

5.6
.
Furthermore, when this is the case,
s_{f}=f^{\prime}_{-}+\mathrm{id}
and
i_{f}=f^{\prime}_{+}+\mathrm{id}
are non-decreasing.

Proof of
Proposition

A.5
:

Define
F:x\mapsto f(x)+\frac{x^{2}}{2}
. For
r\neq r_{0}
,

\frac{f(r)-f(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}+\frac{r+r_{0}}{2}=\frac{F(r)-F(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}.

If
F
is convex, then by
Lemma

A.1
,

s_{f}(r_{0})=F^{\prime}_{-}(r_{0})=F^{\prime}(r_{0}^{-})\leq F^{\prime}(r_{0}^{+})=F^{\prime}_{+}(r_{0})=i_{f}(r_{0})
.
Conversely, if
s_{f}(r_{0})\leq i_{f}(r_{0})
for all
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then for
r<r_{0}<r^{\prime}
,

\frac{F(r)-F(r_{0})}{r-r_{0}}\leq s_{f}(r_{0})\leq i_{f}(r_{0})\leq\frac{F(r^{\prime})-F(r_{0})}{r^{\prime}-r_{0}}.

This chain of inequalities characterizes convexity of
F
.

When
F
is convex, we observe that
s_{f}=F^{\prime}_{-}=f^{\prime}_{-}+\mathrm{id}
and
i_{f}=F^{\prime}_{+}=f^{\prime}_{+}+\mathrm{id}
are indeed non-decreasing.
∎

Corollary A.6
.

Suppose
f
is
1
-weakly convex. The following equivalences hold:

s_{f}\text{ is increasing}\iff f\text{ is strictly }1\text{-weakly convex}\iff i_{f}\text{ is increasing},

(184)

where
s_{f}
and
i_{f}
are from
Definition

5.6
.
Furthermore,
s_{f}
is constant on a non-trivial interval if and only if
i_{f}
is also constant, with the same value, on that interval.

Proof of
Corollary

A.6
:

By
Proposition

A.5
,
s_{f}=F^{\prime}_{-}
and
i_{f}=F^{\prime}_{+}
where
F:r\mapsto f(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}
is convex.

Suppose
F
is strictly convex. Fix
r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}<r_{4}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Strict convexity yields

\frac{F(r_{2})-F(r_{1})}{r_{2}-r_{1}}<\frac{F(r_{3})-F(r_{2})}{r_{3}-r_{2}}<\frac{F(r_{4})-F(r_{2})}{r_{4}-r_{2}}<\frac{F(r_{4})-F(r_{3})}{r_{4}-r_{3}}.

Taking the limit
r_{1}\to r_{2}^{-}
yields
s_{f}(r_{2})\leq\frac{F(r_{3})-F(r_{2})}{r_{3}-r_{2}}<\frac{F(r_{4})-F(r_{2})}{r_{4}-r_{2}}
. Then taking
r_{3}\to r_{4}^{-}
gives
\frac{F(r_{4})-F(r_{2})}{r_{4}-r_{2}}\leq s_{f}(r_{4})
. Combined, we deduce
s_{f}(r_{2})<s_{f}(r_{4})
, so
s_{f}
is increasing. The proof for
i_{f}
is analogous.

Conversely, if
F
is not strictly convex, there exists a non-trivial open interval
J
on which
F
is linear. On
J
,
F^{\prime}
is constant, hence
s_{f}|_{J}
and
i_{f}|_{J}
are constant and equal.
∎

Remark A.3
.

If
f
is strictly
1
-weakly convex, then
r\mapsto f(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}
has at most one minimum, and hence
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is singleton-valued or empty. If
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is singleton-valued everywhere.

A.3
Coercivity and Continuity of Proximal Selections

Proposition A.7
.

Suppose there exists
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)>-\infty
. Then
f
is locally lower-bounded.

Proof of
Proposition

A.7
:

Suppose by contradiction that
f
is not locally lower-bounded at some
r_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Thus, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists
r_{n}\in(r_{\infty}-\frac{1}{n},r_{\infty}+\frac{1}{n})
with
f(r_{n})\leq-n
. Thus
r_{n}\to r_{\infty}
and

\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)\leq\frac{(r_{n}-\delta)^{2}}{2}+f(r_{n})\leq\frac{(r_{n}-\delta)^{2}}{2}-n\to-\infty,

which contradicts
\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta)>-\infty
.
∎

Remark A.4
.

In particular, if there exists
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)\neq\emptyset
, then
f
is locally lower-bounded.

Proposition A.8
.

Suppose there exists a sequence
(\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset
and
|\delta_{n}|\to\infty
.

Then any proximal selection
r_{\min}
of
f
satisfies
|r_{\min}(\delta_{n})|\to\infty
.

Proof of
Proposition

A.8
:

Suppose by contradiction that
|r_{\min}(\delta_{n})|
does not diverge. Then there exists
M>0
such that a subsequence (still denoted
(\delta_{n})
) satisfies
|r_{\min}(\delta_{n})|\leq M
for all
n
and converges to some
r_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
.

By
Proposition

A.7
,
f
is locally lower-bounded. Thus there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
containing
r_{\infty}
with
\inf_{U}f>-\infty
. Without loss of generality, assume
r_{\min}(\delta_{n})\in U
for all
n
.

For
n
such that
|\delta_{n}|\geq M
, by the triangle inequality,

\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta_{n})\geq\frac{(|\delta_{n}|-M)^{2}}{2}+\inf_{U}f.

However, for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta_{n})\leq\frac{(\delta_{n}-r)^{2}}{2}+f(r).

Setting
r_{n}=\mathrm{sign}(\delta_{n})(M+1)
and defining
M^{\prime}=\frac{(M+1)^{2}}{2}+\max(f(M+1),f(-(M+1)))
, we obtain

M^{\prime}\geq\mathcal{M}_{f}(\delta_{n})-\frac{\delta_{n}^{2}}{2}+r_{n}\delta_{n}\geq\frac{M^{2}}{2}-M|\delta_{n}|+r_{n}\delta_{n}+\inf_{U}f=|\delta_{n}|+\frac{M^{2}}{2}+\inf_{U}f\to\infty,

which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary A.9
.

Suppose
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
r_{\min}
be a proximal selection of
f
. Then

r_{\min}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})\iff f\text{ is strictly }1\text{-weakly convex}.

Furthermore, when this is the case,
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is singleton-valued and
r_{\min}
is an onto function.

Proof of
Corollary

A.9
:

With
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is finite everywhere and Proposition
A.3
shows
r_{\min}
is non-decreasing, and Proposition
A.8
shows
|r_{\min}|
is coercive. Hence
r_{\min}
is continuous if and only if it is onto.

Suppose
r_{\min}
is onto. Proposition
A.4
guarantees
s_{f}\leq i_{f}
. By Proposition
A.5
, this implies
f
is
1
-weakly convex. Since
r_{\min}
is continuous, Proposition
A.3
shows
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is differentiable and thus
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is singleton-valued.

Suppose by contradiction that
f
is not strictly
1
-weakly convex. By Corollary
A.6
, there exist
r_{1}<r_{2}
with
s_{f}(r_{1})=s_{f}(r_{2})=s_{f}(r)=i_{f}(r)
for
r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
. In particular,
\delta=s_{f}(r_{1})
satisfies
s_{f}(r_{1})\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{1})
and
s_{f}(r_{2})\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{2})
. By Proposition
A.4
,
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta)
, which contradicts singleton-valuedness.

Conversely, suppose
f
is strictly
1
-weakly convex. By Remark following Corollary
A.6
,
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is singleton-valued. Define

\delta:r_{0}\mapsto\frac{s_{f}(r_{0})+i_{f}(r_{0})}{2}.

By Proposition
A.5
,
s_{f}(r_{0})\leq\delta(r_{0})\leq i_{f}(r_{0})
. By Proposition
A.4
,
r_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\delta(r_{0}))=\{r_{\min}(\delta(r_{0}))\}
. Thus
r_{0}\in\mathrm{Im}(r_{\min})
, proving
r_{\min}
is onto.
∎

Remark A.5
.

In particular, when
f
is lower semi-continuous and prox-coercive, this equivalence holds, since these hypotheses imply
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
by
Proposition

A.2
.

Remark A.6
.

If
f
is strictly
1
-weakly convex,
r_{\min}
is the only function such that

\forall\delta\in\mathbb{R},s_{f}(r_{\min}(\delta))\leq\delta\leq i_{f}(r_{\min}(\delta))
.

A.4
Lipschitz Continuity from Weak Convexity

Lemma A.10
.

Suppose
f
is
V_{0}
-weakly convex for some
V_{0}>1
. Then,
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, let
r_{\min}
be a proximal selection of
f
. Then
r_{\min}
is
\frac{V_{0}}{V_{0}-1}
-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof of
Lemma

A.10
:

Define
G:r\mapsto V_{0}f(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}
, which is convex by
V_{0}
-weak convexity. For any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
F_{\delta}:r\mapsto f(r)+\frac{(\delta-r)^{2}}{2}
can be written as

F_{\delta}(r)=F_{0}(r)+\left(-\delta r+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{V_{0}}G(r)+\left(1-\frac{1}{V_{0}}\right)\frac{r^{2}}{2}\,.

(185)

Since
V_{0}>1
, both terms on the right are convex, hence
F_{0}
is convex, and consequently
F_{\delta}
is convex.
Furthermore, if
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
G
is differentiable at
r_{0}
, we have that, for any
r\in\mathbb{R},\>G(r)\geq G(r_{0})+G^{\prime}(r_{0})(r-r_{0})
. Thus,
F_{\delta}
is coercive in
r
, and
f
is prox-coercive. Since it is weakly convex, it also is continuous and hence
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
.

Since
r_{\min}(\delta)
minimizes the convex function
F_{\delta}
, the first-order optimality condition gives
0\in\partial F_{\delta}(r_{\min}(\delta))
. From the expressions above,

\partial F_{\delta}(r)=\frac{1}{V_{0}}\partial G(r)+\left(1-\frac{1}{V_{0}}\right)r-\delta.

Therefore, at
r=r_{\min}(\delta)
,

V_{0}\delta-(V_{0}-1)r_{\min}(\delta)\in\partial G(r_{\min}(\delta))=[G^{\prime}_{-}(r_{\min}(\delta)),G^{\prime}_{+}(r_{\min}(\delta))].

For
\delta<\delta^{\prime}
, using that
r_{\min}
is non-decreasing (
Proposition

A.3
) and
G
is convex (hence
\partial G
is monotone),

V_{0}\delta-(V_{0}-1)r_{\min}(\delta)\leq G^{\prime}_{+}(r_{\min}(\delta))\leq G^{\prime}_{-}(r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime}))\leq V_{0}\delta^{\prime}-(V_{0}-1)r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime}).

Rearranging yields

0\leq r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime})-r_{\min}(\delta)\leq\frac{V_{0}}{V_{0}-1}(\delta^{\prime}-\delta).

which is the definition of Lipschitzness that we were looking for.
∎

Before proving the reciprocal, we recall a well known characterization of convex functions over intervals in our specific case.

Lemma A.11
.

Let
F\,:\,\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function.

F\text{ is convex}\iff F\text{ is locally Lipschitz continuous with }F^{\prime}\text{ non-decreasing}

(186)

Remark A.7
.

A Lipschitz continuous function on a compact set is differentiable almost everywhere on this compact set. Thus, in the above equivalence,
F^{\prime}
non-decreasing must be understood as
F^{\prime}
is non decreasing over the set where it is defined.

Proof of
Lemma

A.11
:

Take
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose
F
is convex. We know that
F
is almost everywhere differentiable with
F^{\prime}
non-decreasing.
For
r^{\prime}<r
elements of
[a,b]
,

F^{\prime}_{-}(a)\leq\frac{F(r)-F(r^{\prime})}{r-r^{\prime}}\leq F^{\prime}_{+}(b).

(187)

Thus,
F
is
\max(|F^{\prime}_{+}(a)|,|F^{\prime}_{-}(b)|)
-Lipschitz continuous on
[a,b]
.
Conversely, if
F
is Lipschitz continuous on
[a,b]
, then it is absolutely continuous on
[a,b]
and for
r^{\prime}<r\in[a,b]
,

F(r)-F(r^{\prime})=\int_{r^{\prime}}^{r}F^{\prime}.

(188)

Now, since
F^{\prime}
is non-decreasing, then

F^{\prime}(r^{\prime+})=\inf_{[r^{\prime},r]}F^{\prime}\leq\frac{F(r)-F(r^{\prime})}{r-r^{\prime}}\leq\sup_{[r^{\prime},r]}F^{\prime}=F^{\prime}(r^{-})\,.

(189)

This suffices to show that
F
is convex. Indeed, take
r^{\prime\prime}\in[a,b]
such that
r^{\prime\prime}<r^{\prime}
. Applying
Eq.

189
to both pairs
(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime})
and
(r^{\prime},r)
, and using the fact that
F^{\prime}(\cdot^{-})\leq F^{\prime}(\cdot^{+})
because
F^{\prime}
is non-decreasing, yields

\frac{F(r^{\prime})-F(r^{\prime\prime})}{r^{\prime}-r^{\prime\prime}}\leq F^{\prime}(r^{\prime-})\leq F^{\prime}(r^{\prime+})\leq\frac{F(r)-F(r^{\prime})}{r-r^{\prime}}.

(190)

This is a well known characterization of convex functions.
∎

Corollary A.12
.

Suppose that
E_{f}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
r_{\min}
be a proximal selection of
f
. For
L\in(1,\infty)
, we have

r_{\min}\text{ is $L$-Lipschitz continuous}\iff f\text{ is $\frac{L}{L-1}$-weakly convex},

(191)

and
r_{\min}
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous if and only if
f
is convex.

Proof of
Corollary

A.12
:

Suppose
r_{\min}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous.
In particular, it is continuous and the
Corollary

A.9
yields the fact that
f
is strictly
1
-weakly convex.

From
Lemma

A.11
we know that
r\mapsto f(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}
and
r\mapsto-\frac{r^{2}}{2}
are locally Lipschitz continuous, therefore
f
has the same property.

Let us denote by
F:r\mapsto\frac{L}{L-1}f(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}
. Our aim is to show that
F
is convex,
i.e.
locally Lipschitz continuous with non-decreasing derivative by
Lemma

A.11
. We already know that
F
is locally Lipschitz continuous, since it is the case of
f
.

By
Corollaries

A.9
and
A.3
and continuity,
r_{\min}
is non-decreasing and onto. Thus it suffices to check that
G\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=F^{\prime}\circ r_{\min}
is non-decreasing wherever it is defined.

Take
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G
is defined at
\delta
. Then,
f
is differentiable at
r_{\min}(\delta)
, which minimizes the function
r\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\frac{(\delta-r)^{2}}{2}+f(r)
.

Therefore, the extremality condition yields

\delta-r_{\min}(\delta)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}(r_{\min}(\delta)),

(192)

and thus

G(\delta)=\frac{L\delta}{L-1}-\frac{r_{\min}(\delta)}{L-1}.

(193)

However, for
\delta<\delta^{\prime}
real numbers,

0\leq r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime})-r_{\min}(\delta)\leq L(\delta^{\prime}-\delta),

(194)

and thus

-r_{\min}(\delta)\leq-r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime})+L(\delta^{\prime}-\delta)

(195)

Fix
\delta_{0}<\delta_{1}
in the domain of
G
.

Using
Eqs.

193
and
195
,

G(\delta)\leq\frac{L\delta}{L-1}-\frac{r_{\min}(\delta^{\prime})}{L-1}+\frac{L}{L-1}(\delta^{\prime}-\delta)=G(\delta^{\prime}).

(196)

This is the desired result.

Conversely, if
f
is
\frac{L}{L-1}
-weakly convex, since
\frac{L}{L-1}>1
and

\frac{\frac{L}{L-1}}{\frac{L}{L-1}-1}=L,

(197)

Lemma

A.10
yields that

r_{\min}\text{ is $L$-Lipschitz continuous}.

(198)

Now for the second equivalence, if
r_{\min}
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous, it is
L
-Lipschitz continuous for any
L>1
.

Hence,

r\mapsto f(r)+\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)\frac{r^{2}}{2}\text{ is convex for any }L>1.

(199)

This family of convex functions converges pointwise to
f
as
L\rightarrow 1^{+}
, which is thus a convex function.

Conversely, if
f
is convex, it is
V_{0}
-weakly-convex for any
V_{0}>1
. Therefore,

r_{\min}\text{ is }\frac{V_{0}}{V_{0}-1}\text{-Lipschitz continuous}.

(200)

Taking the point wise limit
V_{0}\rightarrow\infty
yields that
r_{\min}
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous.
∎

Appendix B
Applications

B.1
Examples of Weakly Convex Losses

We verify
Assumption

2.4
for standard robust losses found in the statistics literature.

Example B.1

(Huber Loss)
.

The Huber loss is defined as

\mathscr{L}_{H}^{\xi}(y,z)=\xi^{2}\int_{0}^{|y-z|/\xi}\min(1,x)\,dx.

Its second derivative with respect to
z
is

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathscr{L}_{H}^{\xi}}{\partial z^{2}}(y,z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|y-z|\leq\xi\\
0&\text{if }|y-z|>\xi\end{cases}\geq 0.

Thus
z\mapsto\mathscr{L}_{H}^{\xi}(y,z)
is convex, hence
V_{0}
-weakly convex for any
V_{0}\geq 0
.

Example B.2

(Tukey Biweight Loss)
.

The Tukey biweight loss is defined as

\mathscr{L}_{T}^{\xi}(y,z)=\begin{cases}\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{(y-z)^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right)^{3}\right]&\text{if }|y-z|\leq\xi\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}+\varrho|y-z-\xi|^{3}&\text{if }y-z>\xi\\
\frac{\xi^{2}}{6}+\varrho|y-z+\xi|^{3}&\text{if }y-z<-\xi\end{cases}

where
\varrho>0
is a coercivity parameter. For
u:=y-z
with
|u|\leq\xi
, the second derivative is

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathscr{L}_{T}^{\xi}}{\partial z^{2}}(y,z)=\left(1-\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right)\left(1-5\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right).

This is negative when
\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\in(\frac{1}{5},1)
. To find the minimum, set
t=\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}}
and minimize
(1-t)(1-5t)=1-6t+5t^{2}
. Taking the derivative yields
-6+10t=0
, giving
t=\frac{3}{5}
. At this point, the second derivative equals
(1-\frac{3}{5})(1-3)=-\frac{4}{5}
. For
|u|>\xi
, we have
\frac{\partial^{2}\mathscr{L}_{T}^{\xi}}{\partial z^{2}}=6\varrho|u-\xi|>0
.

Taking
V_{0}=\frac{5}{4}
ensures

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\left(V_{0}\mathscr{L}_{T}^{\xi}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)\geq\frac{5}{4}\cdot\left(-\frac{4}{5}\right)+1=0.

Thus
\mathscr{L}_{T}^{\xi}
is
\frac{5}{4}
-weakly convex.

Example B.3

(Cauchy Loss)
.

The Cauchy loss is defined as

\mathscr{L}_{C}^{\xi}(y,z)=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{(y-z)^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right).

Its second derivative with respect to
z
is

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathscr{L}_{C}^{\xi}}{\partial z^{2}}(y,z)=\frac{1-\frac{(y-z)^{2}}{\xi^{2}}}{\left(1+\frac{(y-z)^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right)^{2}}.

Setting
t=\frac{(y-z)^{2}}{\xi^{2}}
and minimizing
h(t)=\frac{1-t}{(1+t)^{2}}
gives
h^{\prime}(t)=\frac{t-3}{(1+t)^{3}}=0
at
t=3
, with minimum value
h(3)=\frac{-2}{16}=-\frac{1}{8}
.

Taking
V_{0}=8
ensures

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\left(V_{0}\mathscr{L}_{C}^{\xi}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)\geq 8\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{8}\right)+1=0,

so
\mathscr{L}_{C}^{\xi}
is
8
-weakly convex.

Example B.4

(
\ell_{2}
Regularization)
.

The
\ell_{2}
regularization is defined as

\mathscr{R}_{\lambda}(w)=\frac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}
.
Its second derivative is

\mathscr{R}_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(w)=\lambda\geq 0

for
\lambda>0
. This is convex, hence
V_{0}
-weakly convex for all
V_{0}\geq 0
.

B.2
Negative Regularization in a Spherical Shell

To study the landscape of the problem at hand we slice it in spherical shells of radius squared equal to
q
. We have that

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{d}\quantity[
\sum_{\dataidx=1}^n
\lossfun\qty(y_\dataidx, \frac{\w^\top\x_\dataidx}{\sqrt{d}} )
]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\norm{\w}_{2}^{2}}{d}

\displaystyle=\min_{q\geq 0}\min_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{K}_{q,q}}\frac{1}{d}\quantity[
\sum_{\dataidx=1}^n
\lossfun\qty(y_\dataidx, \frac{\w^\top\x_\dataidx}{\sqrt{d}} )
]+\frac{\lambda}{2}q

(201)

\displaystyle=:\min_{q\geq 0}h(q)+\frac{\lambda}{2}q

(202)

where the second minimization can be studied with the help of
Theorem

2.1
. We now are left with the study of a function in a single variable
h(q)
.

We consider the specific form of the optimization objective obtained from
Corollary

6.5
, where for
(m^{\star},q^{\star},\tau^{\star},\kappa^{\star},\nu^{\star},\eta^{\star})\in\mathcal{S}^{\star}
we have that

h(q)=\alpha\mathbb{E}\quantity[
\lossfun\qty(y, \mathcal{P}_{\frac{\tau^\star}{\kappa^\star}\lossfun(y,\cdot)} \qty(\frac{m^\star}{\sqrt{\rho}} s + \sqrt{q - \frac{(m^\star)^2}{\rho}} h))
]\,,

(203)

and where
q^{\star}=q
since in the problem definition we have that
a=b=q
. We also note that because of the assumption on
\mathscr{L}

h(q)
is also convex.

We study first the derivative of the first term. We notice three facts: first, the value of the derivative in zero is non-positive, secondly it is bounded from above by a constant and third for
q\to\infty
the derivative reaches zero from above.

We thus define the value of
\lambda_{c}
as

\lambda_{c}=\min\quantity{\lambda\in\RR\mid\exists q \geq 0 \quad\partial h(q) = \frac{\lambda}{2} }\,,

(204)

The presence of a single minimizer for
\lambda\geq 0
follows from the convexity of the optimization objective.
For
\lambda_{c}\leq\lambda<0
follows from the fact that for
\lambda\in[\lambda_{c},0]
there are two values of
q
such that
\partial h(q)=\frac{\lambda}{2}
.
If we consider
\lambda<\lambda_{c}
we have that there is no stationary point for
h(q)
and thus it is a decreasing function of
q
and its minimum is attained at
q\to\infty
.
