Regularity and pointwise convergence for dispersive equations on ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2}
Utsav Dewan

Abstract
. In the prototypical setting of non-Euclidean geometry, the 2-dimensional Real Hyperbolic space ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} , we consider the Carleson’s problem for the Schrödinger equation and improve the best known result until now by proving that the Sobolev regularity threshold β ≥ 1 / 2 \beta\geq 1/2 for the initial data, is sufficient to obtain pointwise convergence of the solution a.e. on ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} . In fact, we prove the same bound for a wide class of dispersive equations that include the fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase, the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, also known as the fourth order Wave equation. For the Schrödinger equation, we improve the result of Wang-Zhang (Canad J Math 71(4), 983-995, 2019) and for the fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase, we improve the result of Cowling (Lecture Notes Math 992, 83-90, 1983).

1.
Introduction

One of the most celebrated problems in Euclidean Harmonic analysis is the Carleson’s problem: determining the optimal regularity of the initial condition
f
of the
Schrödinger equation given by

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u=0\>,\>\>\>(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\>,\end{cases}

in terms of the index
\beta
such that for all
f
belonging to the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
H^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the solution
u
of (
1.1
) converges pointwise to
f
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}u(x,t)=f(x)\>,\>\>\text{ almost everywhere }.

In 1980, Carleson studied this problem for
n=1
in
[
Car80
]
and obtained the sufficient condition
\beta\geq 1/4
for the pointwise convergence (
1.2
) to hold. In 1982, Dahlberg-Kenig
[
DK82
]
showed that
\beta\geq 1/4
is also necessary. This completely solved the problem in dimension one and subsequently, posed the question in higher dimensions.

In 1983, Cowling
[
Cow83
]
studied the problem for a general class of self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}(X)
for a measure space
X
and obtained
\beta>1
, to be a sufficient condition for the associated Schrödinger operator. For
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, in 1987, Sjölin
[
Sjö87
]
improved the bound to
\beta>1/2
, by means of a local smoothing effect. This improvement was also independently obtained by Vega
[
Veg88
]
in 1988. Then special attention was focused towards the case
n=2
and developing decoupling techniques, continuous improvements were made
[
Bou92
,
MVV96
,
TV00
,
Lee06
]
. These attempts reached fruition recently in 2017, when Du-Guth-Li
[
DGL17
]
obtained the bound
\beta>1/3
, for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by using polynomial partitioning and decoupling. Then in 2019, Du-Zhang
[
DZ19
]
obtained the bound
\beta>n/2(n+1)
, for dimensions
n\geq 3
, by using decoupling and induction on scales. This bound is sharp except at the endpoint
\beta=n/2(n+1)
, due to a counterexample by Bourgain
[
Bou16
]
. Thus in the Euclidean setting, the Carleson’s problem has been almost fully resolved.

In the case of a Riemannian manifold, where the volume measure neither satisfies any doubling property nor does it admit dilations, the above mentioned Euclidean machineries work no longer and thus it offers a fresh challenge. The prototypical example of such a manifold is the
2
-dimensional Real Hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. On
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, the Schrödinger equation corresponding to the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
, is given by,

(1.3)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\Delta u=0\>,\>\>\>(x,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,\text{ on }\mathbb{H}^{2}\>.\end{cases}

In fact, the equation (
1.3
) is just a special case of the family of fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase of degree
a>1
:

(1.4)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{a}{2}}u=0\>,\>\>\>(x,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,\text{ on }\mathbb{H}^{2}\>.\end{cases}

It is natural to study the Carleson’s problem for the family of equations (
1.4
) in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, for initial data belonging to the Sobolev spaces:

(1.5)

H^{\beta}\left(\mathbb{H}^{2}\right):=\left\{f\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{H}^{2}\right)\mid\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}f\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{H}^{2}\right)\right\}\>,\>\beta\geq 0\>.

The only known result in this direction is the sufficiency of the bound
\beta>a/2
and is due to Cowling
[
Cow83
]
. In the particular case of the Schrödinger equation (that is,
a=2
) however, Wang-Zhang have recently improved the bound down to
\beta>1/2

[
WZ19
, Theorem 1.1]
.

The purpose of this article is to improve the above results down to
\beta=1/2
. In fact, we will obtain the bound
\beta=1/2
, for general dispersive equations of the form,

(1.6)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\Psi(\sqrt{-\Delta})u=0\>,\>(x,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,\>\text{ on }\mathbb{H}^{2}\>,\end{cases}

such that the phase function of the corresponding multiplier
\psi(\lambda):=\Psi\left(\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+1}\right)
is radial, real-valued and its (radial) derivatives satisfy the following high frequency asymptotics for
|\lambda|>1
and some
a>1
:

(1.7)

\begin{cases}|\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)|\asymp|\lambda|^{a-1}\>,\\
|\psi^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)|\asymp|\lambda|^{a-2}\>,\\
|\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)|\lesssim|\lambda|^{a-3}\>.\end{cases}

We note that for
\Psi(r):=r^{a}
, with
a>1
, we recover the fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase (
1.4
). Some other examples are given by
\Psi(r):=r\sqrt{1+r^{2}}
and
\Psi(r):=\sqrt{1+r^{4}}
corresponding respectively to the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, also known as the fourth order Wave equation (for more details see
[
FX24
,
GPW08
]
), which often appear in Mathematical Physics.

We now present our main result:

Theorem 1.1
.

For Dispersive equations (
1.6
), with phase satisfying (
1.7
), the pointwise convergence of the solution to its initial data,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}u(x,t)=f(x)\>,

holds for almost every
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, whenever the initial data
f\in H^{\beta}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
with
\beta\geq 1/2
.

To address the problem of pointwise convergence, the key is to consider the corresponding maximal function,

S^{*}_{\psi}f(x):=\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<1}\left|u(x,t)\right|\>,

where
u
is the solution of (
1.6
) with initial data
f
. By standard arguments in the literature (for instance, see the proof of Theorem 5 of
[
Sjö87
]
), Theorem
1.1
then follows from the maximal estimate:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f
be an
L^{2}
-Schwartz class function on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
B\left(x_{0},1/2\right)
denote the geodesic ball centred at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
with radius
1/2
. Then for Dispersive equations (
1.6
), with phase
\psi
satisfying (
1.7
), the maximal estimate

(1.8)

{\|S^{*}_{\psi}f\|}_{L^{1}\left(B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)}\lesssim{\|f\|}_{H^{\beta}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\>,

holds for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
and all
\beta\geq 1/2
.

Remark 1.3
.

We now briefly summarize the novelties of this article:

•

In
[
Cow83
]
, Cowling’s arguments only involved abstract Hilbert space theory. In
[
WZ19
]
, the authors obtained the bound
\beta>1/2
, by means of a local smoothing effect, building up on Doi’s ideas
[
Doi96
]
on the interaction between smoothing effects of the Schrödinger evolution group and the behaviour of the geodesic flow.

•

The main idea in our article however is completely different: utilizing the underlying Riemannian Symmetric space structure of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\cong SU(1,1)/SO(2)
, our approach is based on the notion of Helgason-Fourier transform, arising from the representation theory of semi-simple Lie groups. Then crucially using a local Bessel series expansion of the spherical function
\varphi_{\lambda}
(given by Lemma
2.1
), we estimate the joint oscillation afforded by
\varphi_{\lambda}
and the Fourier multiplier, to improve the bound down to
\beta=1/2
.

This article is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the necessary preliminaries and fix our notations. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.2
. Finally, in Section
4
, we conclude by sketching some possible directions for further research.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some preliminaries and fix our notations. Throughout, the symbols ‘c’ and ‘C’ will denote positive constants whose values may change on each occurrence. The enumerated constants
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
will however be fixed throughout.
\mathbb{N}
will denote the set of positive integers. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lceil x\rceil
will denote the smallest integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x\leq m
. For non-negative functions
f_{1},\>f_{2}
we write,
f_{1}\lesssim f_{2}
if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
, so that

f_{1}\leq Cf_{2}\>\>,

and
f_{1}\asymp f_{2}
if

\frac{1}{C}f_{1}\leq f_{2}\leq Cf_{1}\>.

For a real-valued function
f_{3}
, we will also write
f_{3}=\mathcal{O}(f_{1})
if

|f_{3}|\lesssim f_{1}\>.

We now recall the required preliminaries about rank one Riemannian Symmetric spaces of non-compact type and Fourier analysis thereon. The relevant information can be found in
[
GV88
,
Hel00
,
Hel08
]
.

Let
G
be a connected, non-compact, semi-simple Lie group with finite center and
K
be a maximal compact subgroup of
G
. We consider the Riemannian Symmetric space of non-compact type
\mathbb{X}=G/K
. Let
n
be the dimension of
\mathbb{X}
. Thus elements
x\in\mathbb{X}
are of the form
x=gK
, for
g\in G
. We let
o=eK
denote the origin of
\mathbb{X}
and
dx
the left
G
-invariant Riemannian volume measure on
\mathbb{X}
. The group
G
acts on
\mathbb{X}
by isomteries, that is, the geodesic distance function
d(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbb{X}
, is left
G
-invariant:

d\left(g\cdot x,g\cdot y\right)=d(x,y)\>,x,y\in\mathbb{X}\>,\>g\in G\>.

Expressing the Iwasawa decomposition in the form
G=NAK
, any element
g\in G
can be uniquely written as,
g=n(g)\exp\left(A(g)\right)k(g)
, where
A(g)\in\mathfrak{a}
, the Lie algebra of
A
. Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be the centralizer and normalizer of
A
in
K
respectively. Let
\mathbb{B}=K/M
and
db
denote the natural probability measure on it. The finite group
W=M/M^{\prime}
is called the Weyl group. The function
A
defined above actually defines a function on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{B}
via,

A\left(gK,kM\right)=A(k^{-1}g)\>.

The dimension of
\mathfrak{a}
is called the rank of
G
and also of
\mathbb{X}
. Hence, when rank =
1
,
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the real dual of
\mathfrak{a}
and its complexification respectively, can be identified with
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
. From now onwards, we focus only in the rank one case. We fix some order on the non-zero restricted roots. There are at most two roots which are positive relative to this order. These roots will be denoted by
\alpha
and
2\alpha
. Letting
p
and
q
denote the multiplicities of these roots respectively, we set
\rho=(p+2q)/2
.

One also has the Cartan decomposition
G=K\overline{A^{+}}K
. Let
g^{+}
be the
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
-component of
g\in G
, in the decomposition
G=K\left(\exp\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}\right)K
and let
\sigma(g)=|g^{+}|
. For any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{X})
, its integral with respect to the volume measure can be written in the polar coordinate as,

\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(x)\>dx\>=\>\int_{K}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(ka_{s}\cdot o)\>D(s)\>ds\>dk\>,

where
D(\cdot)
is the density function,
ds
is the Lebesgue measure restricted to
[0,\infty)
and
dk
is the Haar measure on
K
.

The spherical function on
\mathbb{X}
has the form,

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}\>e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>db\>,\>\text{ for }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\>,

and satisfies the functional equation:

(2.1)

\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)=\int_{\mathbb{B}}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{(-i\lambda+\rho)A(y,b)}\>db\>,

where
g_{x},g_{y}\in G
such that
x=g_{x}K
and
y=g_{y}K
. Moreover,

\varphi_{\lambda}=\varphi_{-\lambda}\>,\>\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\>,

and thus in particular,

(2.2)

\varphi_{-i\rho}(x)=\varphi_{i\rho}(x)\equiv 1\>,\>x\in\mathbb{X}\>.

We also have

(2.3)

\left|\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\right|\leq 1\>,\>x\in\mathbb{X}\>,\>\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\>.

The spherical function is a radial function, that is,

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(s)\>,

where
s=d(o,x)
, the geodesic distance of
x
from the origin.

Although, in the generality of rank one Riemannian Symmetric spaces of non-compact type, we do not have an explicit expression of
\varphi_{\lambda}
, for points near the origin however,
\varphi_{\lambda}
does admit a Bessel series expansion. But before stating that result, let us define the following normalizing constant in terms of the Gamma functions,

c_{0}=\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\>2^{\left(\frac{q}{2}-2\right)}\>\>\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\>,

and the following functions on
\mathbb{C}
, for all
\mu\geq 0
,

\mathscr{J}_{\mu}(z)=2^{\mu-1}\>\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\>\Gamma\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{J_{\mu}(z)}{z^{\mu}},

where
J_{\mu}
are the Bessel functions
[
SW90
, p. 154]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
ST78
, Theorem 2.1]

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a rank one Riemannian Symmetric space of non-compact type and for
x\in\mathbb{X}
, let
s=d(o,x)
. Then there exist
R_{0}>1,R_{1}>1
, such that for any
0\leq s\leq R_{0}
, and any integer
M\geq 0
, and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\varphi_{\lambda}(s)=c_{0}{\left(\frac{s^{n-1}}{D(s)}\right)}^{1/2}\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{M}a_{l}(s)\mathscr{J}_{\frac{n-2}{2}+l}(\lambda s)s^{2l}+E_{M+1}(\lambda,s)\>,

where

a_{0}\equiv 1\>,\>|a_{l}(s)|\leq C{(4R_{1})}^{-l}\>,

and the error term has the following behaviour

\left|E_{M+1}(\lambda,s)\right|\leq C_{M}\begin{cases}s^{2(M+1)}&\text{ if }\>|\lambda s|\leq 1\\
s^{2(M+1)}{|\lambda s|}^{-\left(\frac{n-1}{2}+M+1\right)}&\text{ if }\>|\lambda s|>1\>.\end{cases}

We will also require the following asymptotic expansion of Bessel functions:

Lemma 2.2
.

[
Sze75
, Eqn (1.71.8), p. 16]

For
\mu\geq 0
, we have for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle J_{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{\pi t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cos\left(t-\frac{\mu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\left\{\sum_{l=0}^{p-1}\tilde{a}_{l}t^{-2l}\>+\>\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-2p}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{2}{\pi t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sin\left(t-\frac{\mu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\left\{\sum_{l=0}^{p-1}b_{l}t^{-2l-1}\>+\>\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-2p-1}\right)\right\}\>,

for
t\to+\infty
, where
\tilde{a}_{l}
and
b_{l}
are constants depending only on
l
and also
\tilde{a}_{0}\equiv 1
.

As a consequence, we have:

Lemma 2.3
.

There exist complex numbers
z_{1},\dots,z_{6}
, such that for
t\to+\infty
,

\displaystyle J_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{it}\left\{z_{1}\>t^{-\frac{1}{2}}+z_{2}\>t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}+\>e^{-it}\left\{z_{3}\>t^{-\frac{1}{2}}+z_{4}\>t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-\frac{5}{2}}\right)\>,

\displaystyle\frac{J_{1}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{5}\>e^{it}\>t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\>+\>z_{6}\>e^{-it}\>t^{-\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-\frac{5}{2}}\right)\>.

Proof.

The result follows at once from Lemma
2.2
, by considering
p=2
for
J_{0}
, and
p=1
for
J_{1}
.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of
G
and
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
denote its universal enveloping algebra. The elements of
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
act on
C^{\infty}(G)
as differential operators, on both sides. Following the notation of Harish-Chandra, we shall write
f\left(D;g;E\right)
for the action of
(D,E)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\times\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
on
f\in C^{\infty}(G)
at
g\in G
. More precisely,

\displaystyle f\left(D;g;E\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{1}}\cdots\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{d}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}\cdots\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{e}}\right)\big|_{s_{1}=\cdots=s_{d}=t_{1}=\cdots=t_{e}=0}

\displaystyle\times f\left(\left(\exp s_{1}X_{1}\right)\cdots\left(\exp s_{d}X_{d}\right)g\left(\exp t_{1}Y_{1}\right)\cdots\left(\exp t_{e}Y_{e}\right)\right)\>,

if
D=X_{1}\cdots X_{d}
and
E=Y_{1}\cdots Y_{e}
, for
X_{1},\cdots,X_{d},Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{e}\in\mathfrak{g}
.

We now come to the definition of
L^{2}
-Schwartz class functions.
\mathcal{S}^{2}(G)
, called the
L^{2}
-Schwartz class on
G
, is the collection of all
f\in C^{\infty}(G)
such that for each
D,E\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
and all non-negative integers
m
,

\left|f\left(D;g;E\right)\right|\lesssim_{m}\varphi_{0}(g)\left(1+\sigma(g)\right)^{-m}\>,\>g\in G\>.

\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
is the sub-collection of functions in
\mathcal{S}^{2}(G)
that are also right
K
-invariant. In particular, for all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
and all non-negative integers
m
,
\Delta^{m}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{X})
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
, the Helgason-Fourier transform of
f
is defined as,

\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(x)\>e^{(-i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>dx\>,\>(\lambda,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{B}\>.

The function
x\mapsto e^{(-i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}
is an eigenfunction of
\Delta
with eigenvalue
-\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)
, and hence for all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
,

(2.4)

\widetilde{\left(\Delta f\right)}(\lambda,b)=-\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\>.

The relevant inversion formula is given by,

f(x)=\frac{1}{|W|}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\>e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db\>,\>x\in\mathbb{X}\>,

where
{\bf c}(\cdot)
denotes the Harish-Chandra’s
{\bf c}
-function. The Plancherel identity is given by,

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>=\>c\>\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\right|^{2}\>{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>,

for some
c>0
. The weight in the Plancherel measure satisfies
[
ST78
, Lemma 4.2]
:

(2.5)

{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\lesssim\left(1\>+\>|\lambda|\right)^{n-1}\>,\>\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\>.

Using the above notion of the Helgason-Fourier transform, for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
, the solution of the equation

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\Psi(\sqrt{-\Delta})u=0\>,\>(x,t)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,\>\text{ on }\mathbb{X}\>,\end{cases}

is given by the formula,

(2.6)

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{|W|}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\>e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it\psi(\lambda)}\>{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db\>.

In view of the Plancherel identity, we can also express the norms for the Sobolev spaces defined in (
1.5
) by,

\left\|f\right\|_{H^{\beta}(\mathbb{X})}\>\asymp\>\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)^{\beta}\>\left|\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\right|^{2}\>{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>.

In this article, we are primarily interested in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\cong SU(1,1)/SO(2)
, where

\displaystyle SU(1,1)\>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\bar{b}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix}\mid a,b\in\mathbb{C},\>|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1\right\}\>,

\displaystyle SO(2)\>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\left\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\
0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\mid\theta\in[0,2\pi)\right\}\>.

In this case, the density function is given by,

D(s)=\sinh(2s)\>,

and hence for
s<1
, satisfies the local growth asymptotics:

(2.7)

D(s)\asymp s\>.

Moreover, the weight of the Plancherel measure is explcitly given by,

{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}=\lambda\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2}\right).

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We start off the proof in the general setting of rank one Riemannian Symmetric spaces of non-compact type and then gradually specialize to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Let
G
be a connected, non-compact, semi-simple Lie group with finite center and real rank one. Let
K
be a maximal compact subgroup of
G
and
\mathbb{X}=G/K
be the corresponding Symmetric space. Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
, the class of
L^{2}
-Schwartz class functions on
\mathbb{X}
and
B\left(x_{0},1/2\right)
denote the geodesic ball on
\mathbb{X}
, with centre
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
and radius
1/2
, with respect to the left
G
-invariant metric
d
on
\mathbb{X}
.

We note that to prove the local maximal estimate (
1.8
), it suffices to prove the following estimate at the end-point:

(3.1)

\left\|T_{\psi}f\right\|_{L^{1}\left(B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)}\lesssim{\|f\|}_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>,\text{ for all }x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}\>,

where
T_{\psi}f
is the linearization given by the formula (
2.6
),

T_{\psi}f(x):=u(x,t(x))=\frac{1}{|W|}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\>e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\>{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db\>,

for any measurable function
t(\cdot):\mathbb{X}\to(0,1).

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(\mathbb{X})
, by (
2.2
), (
2.4
), in view of the growth asymptotic of
{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}
given in (
2.5
), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Plancherel theorem yields for sufficiently large
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
,

\displaystyle\left|T_{\psi}f(x)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\|\Delta^{m_{0}}f\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}}e^{2\rho A(x,b)}\>db\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2})^{2m_{o}}}d\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\|\Delta^{m_{0}}f\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>\varphi_{-i\rho}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2})^{2m_{o}}}d\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty\>,

and hence there exists a complex-valued function
\Theta
on
\mathbb{X}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
,

\left|T_{\psi}f(x)\right|=T_{\psi}f(x)\cdot\Theta(x)\>,\text{ with }\left|\Theta(x)\right|\equiv 1\>.

Next, by Fubini’s Theorem and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we note that

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\psi}f\right\|_{L^{1}\left(B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}T_{\psi}f(x)\cdot\Theta(x)\>dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|W|}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(\lambda,b)\left(\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\Theta(x)\>dx\right){|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}\>d\lambda\>db

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\|f\|}_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\Theta(x)\>dx\right|^{2}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}\>d\lambda\>db\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>,

and hence to prove (
3.1
), it suffices to show that

(3.2)

\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\Theta(x)\>dx\right|^{2}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}\>d\lambda\>db<\infty\>.

In this direction, by Fubini’s theorem and the functional equation (
2.1
), we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\Theta(x)\>dx\right|^{2}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}\>d\lambda\>db

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{it(x)\psi(\lambda)}\>\Theta(x)\>e^{(-i\lambda+\rho)A(y,b)}\>e^{-it(y)\psi(\lambda)}\>\overline{\Theta(y)}

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\times\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}\>dx\>dy\>d\lambda\>db

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\Theta(x)\>\overline{\Theta(y)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t(x)-t(y))\psi(\lambda)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)A(x,b)}\>e^{(-i\lambda+\rho)A(y,b)}\>db\right)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\times\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}d\lambda\>dx\>dy\>

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)\>e^{i(t(x)-t(y))\psi(\lambda)}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{|\lambda|}d\lambda\right|\>dx\>dy\>,

where
g_{x},g_{y}\in G
such that
x=g_{x}K
and
y=g_{y}K
. Now as the spherical function, the phase and
{|{\bf c}(\cdot)|}^{-2}
all are even, the last integral simplies to

2\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)\>e^{i(t(x)-t(y))\psi(\lambda)}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{\lambda}d\lambda\right|\>dx\>dy\>.

Thus to prove (
3.2
), it suffices to prove that

(3.3)

\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)\>e^{i(t(x)-t(y))\psi(\lambda)}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{\lambda}d\lambda\right|\>dx\>dy<\infty\>.

Now as
\varphi_{\lambda}
is radial and the distance function on
\mathbb{X}
is left
G
-invariant, we have

\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)=\varphi_{\lambda}(d(o,g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o))=\varphi_{\lambda}(d(g_{y}\cdot o,g_{x}\cdot o))=\varphi_{\lambda}(d(y,x))\>.

Also as
x,y\in B\left(x_{0},1/2\right)
, We note that

(3.4)

d(y,x)\leq d(y,x_{0})+d(x,x_{0})<1\>.

Then for a fixed pair
(x,y)\in B\left(x_{0},1/2\right)\times B\left(x_{0},1/2\right)
, setting
s=d(y,x)
and
\tau=t(x)-t(y)
, we focus on the
\lambda
-integral in (
3.3
), that is,

I:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(s)\>e^{i\tau\psi(\lambda)}\>\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{\lambda}\>d\lambda\>.

We now specialize to
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Thus plugging
{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}=\lambda\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2}\right)
, the above integral simplifies to

I=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(s)\>e^{i\tau\psi(\lambda)}\>\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2}\right)\>d\lambda\>.

We now estimate the above oscillatory integral by introducing a smooth resolution of identity. More precisely, let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be even, non-negative such that

Supp(\eta)\subset\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}\mid 1<|\xi|<4\right\}\>,\text{ and }\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\eta\left(2^{-j}\>\xi\right)=1\>,\text{ for }\xi\neq 0\>.

This enables us to decompose
I
as,

I=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta\left(2^{-j}\>\lambda\right)\varphi_{\lambda}(s)\>e^{i\tau\psi(\lambda)}\>\tanh\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2}\right)\>d\lambda\>,

which after the change of variable
\lambda\mapsto 2^{j}\lambda
, takes the form

I=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}2^{j}\int_{1}^{4}\eta\left(\lambda\right)\varphi_{2^{j}\lambda}(s)\>e^{i\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}2^{j}I_{j}\>.

We first estimate for low frequency. By the boundedness of
\varphi_{\lambda}
given by (
2.3
) and
\tanh
, we trivially have

|I_{j}|\leq 3\>.

And thus

(3.5)

\left|\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}2^{j}\>I_{j}\right|\lesssim\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}2^{j}\lesssim s^{-1}\>.

We now focus on high frequency, that is, on
j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil
which is equivalent to saying
2^{j}>s^{-1}
. By (
3.4
),
s<1
. Moreover since,
\lambda\in(1,4)
, consequently we have

2^{j}\lambda s>2^{j}s>1\>.

Then by Lemma
2.1
, for
M=1
, we have

(3.6)

\varphi_{2^{j}\lambda}(s)=c_{0}\left(\frac{s}{D(s)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[J_{0}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)\>+\>a_{1}(s)s^{2}\frac{J_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)}{2^{j}\lambda s}\right]+\mathcal{E}_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda,s\right)\>,

where

(3.7)

\left|\mathcal{E}_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda,s\right)\right|\lesssim s^{4}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>.

Now by Lemma
2.3
, we have complex numbers
z_{1},\dots,z_{6}
such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle J_{0}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i2^{j}\lambda s}\left\{z_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}+z_{2}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\>e^{-i2^{j}\lambda s}\left\{z_{3}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}+z_{4}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(2^{j}\lambda s)\>,

where

(3.9)

\left|\mathcal{E}_{2}(2^{j}\lambda s)\right|\lesssim(2^{j}\lambda s)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>.

and

(3.10)

\frac{J_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)}{2^{j}\lambda s}=z_{5}\>e^{i2^{j}\lambda s}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\>+\>z_{6}\>e^{-i2^{j}\lambda s}\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{E}_{3}(2^{j}\lambda s)\>,

where

(3.11)

\left|\mathcal{E}_{3}(2^{j}\lambda s)\right|\lesssim(2^{j}\lambda s)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>.

Therefore, plugging (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) in (
3.6
), we get that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\varphi_{2^{j}\lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i2^{j}\lambda s}\left\{\theta_{1}(s)\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\>+\>\theta_{2}(s)\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\>e^{-i2^{j}\lambda s}\left\{\theta_{3}(s)\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\>+\>\theta_{4}(s)\left(2^{j}\lambda s\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right\}+\mathcal{E}(2^{j}\lambda,s)\>,

where the complex-valued functions
\theta_{j}
(for
j=1,\cdots,4
) satisfy,

|\theta_{j}(s)|\lesssim 1\>,\>\text{ for }s<1\>,

and

\mathcal{E}(2^{j}\lambda,s)=\mathcal{E}_{1}(2^{j}\lambda,s)+c_{0}\left(\frac{s}{D(s)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{2}(2^{j}\lambda s)\>+\>a_{1}(s)\>s^{2}\>\mathcal{E}_{3}(2^{j}\lambda s)\right]\>.

Thus by (
3.7
), (
3.9
), (
3.11
) and the local growth asymptotics of the density function given by (
2.7
), it follows that

(3.13)

\left|\mathcal{E}(2^{j}\lambda,s)\right|\lesssim(2^{j}\lambda s)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>.

Thus for
j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil
, invoking the decomposition (
3.12
) in the definition of
I_{j}
, we write,

\displaystyle I_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(s)\>(2^{j}s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{1}^{4}e^{i\{2^{j}\lambda s\>+\>\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)\}}\>\eta\left(\lambda\right)\>\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda

\displaystyle+\>\theta_{2}(s)\>(2^{j}s)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{1}^{4}e^{i\{2^{j}\lambda s\>+\>\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)\}}\>\eta\left(\lambda\right)\>\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda

\displaystyle+\>\theta_{3}(s)\>(2^{j}s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{1}^{4}e^{i\{-2^{j}\lambda s\>+\>\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)\}}\>\eta\left(\lambda\right)\>\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda

\displaystyle+\>\theta_{4}(s)\>(2^{j}s)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{1}^{4}e^{i\{-2^{j}\lambda s\>+\>\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)\}}\>\eta\left(\lambda\right)\>\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda

\displaystyle+\>\int_{1}^{4}\eta\left(\lambda\right)\mathcal{E}(2^{j}\lambda,s)\>e^{i\tau\psi(2^{j}\lambda)}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>d\lambda\>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{5}I^{m}_{j}\>.

For
I^{5}_{j}
, by the pointwise estimate (
3.13
), we have

\left|I^{5}_{j}\right|\lesssim(2^{j}s)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>,

and hence as
2^{j}>s^{-1}
, we get that

(3.14)

\left|\sum_{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}^{\infty}2^{j}\>I^{5}_{j}\right|\lesssim s^{-\frac{5}{2}}\sum_{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}^{\infty}2^{-\frac{3j}{2}}\lesssim s^{-\frac{5}{2}}\>s^{\frac{3}{2}}=s^{-1}\>.

We next take care of
I^{m}_{j}
for
m=1,\dots,4
, simultaneously, by considering for
l=0
or
1
, the oscillatory integral

\int_{1}^{4}e^{iF_{s}(\lambda)}\>H(\lambda)\>d\lambda\>,

where

F_{s}(\lambda)=\pm 2^{j}\lambda s+\tau\psi\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\>,\text{ and }H(\lambda)=\eta(\lambda)\>\lambda^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>.

We note that

(3.15)

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{s}(\lambda)=\pm 2^{j}s\>+\>2^{j}\tau\psi^{\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\>,

(3.16)

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}_{s}(\lambda)=2^{2j}\tau\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\>,

(3.17)

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime\prime}_{s}(\lambda)=2^{3j}\tau\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\>.

We now recall the properties of the phase function
\psi
given by (
1.7
): for
\lambda\in(1,4)
and
j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil
, there exist
a>1
and positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\geq 1
, such that

(3.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{1}}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-1}\leq\left|\psi^{\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\right|\leq C_{1}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-1}\>,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{2}}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-2}\leq\left|\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\right|\leq C_{2}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-2}\>,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\left|\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\right|\leq C_{3}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-3}\>.

Thus combining (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) with the upper bounds in (
3.19
) and (
3.20
), it follows that

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime\prime}_{s}(\lambda)\right|\lesssim 2^{aj}|\tau|\>,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime\prime\prime}_{s}(\lambda)\right|\lesssim 2^{aj}|\tau|\>.

Setting
\zeta(\lambda)=\eta(\lambda)\>\lambda^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}
, we also have

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime}(\lambda)\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>+\>\left(2^{j-1}\pi\right)\zeta(\lambda)\cosh^{-2}\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>,

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\>+\>\left(2^{j}\pi\right)\zeta^{\prime}(\lambda)\cosh^{-2}\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)

\displaystyle+\>\left(2^{2j-1}\pi^{2}\right)\zeta(\lambda)\cosh^{-2}\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right)\tanh\left(2^{j-1}\pi\lambda\right),

and hence for
\lambda\in(1,4)
and
j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil
,

(3.23)

\left|H^{(p)}(\lambda)\right|\lesssim 1\>,\>\text{ for }p=0,1,2\>,

with the implicit constants being independent of
j
.

We now proceed with the oscillatory integral estimate by first considering

\mathcal{J}_{1}:=\left\{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil\mid 2^{j}s\leq 2^{(aj-1)}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|\right\}\>.

For
j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
, by (
3.15
), the triangle inequality, the lower bound in (
3.18
) and the facts that
\lambda\in(1,4)
and
a>1
, it follows that

(3.24)

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime}_{s}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{j}|\tau|\left|\psi^{\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\right|-2^{j}s

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{j}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-1}-2^{j}s

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2^{aj}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|-2^{j}s

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{aj}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|-2^{(aj-1)}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{(aj-1)}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|\>.

Then by performing integration-by-parts twice and using (
3.21
)-(
3.24
), we obtain

\left|\int_{1}^{4}e^{iF_{s}(\lambda)}\>H(\lambda)\>d\lambda\right|\lesssim\left(2^{aj}|\tau|\right)^{-2}\>,

which yields for
m=1,\dots,4
and
l=0,1
,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}2^{j}\>I^{m}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}2^{j}\>2^{-j\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\left(2^{aj}|\tau|\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}2^{j}\>2^{-j\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\left(2^{j}s\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{5}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}^{\infty}2^{-j\left(\frac{3}{2}+l\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{5}{2}+l\right)}\>s^{\left(\frac{3}{2}+l\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{-1}\>.

Next, we focus on

\mathcal{J}_{2}:=\left\{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil\mid 2^{j}s\geq 2^{(aj+1)}4^{a-1}C_{1}|\tau|\right\}\>.

For
j\in\mathcal{J}_{2}
, by (
3.15
), the triangle inequality, the upper bound in (
3.18
) and the facts that
\lambda\in(1,4)
and
a>1
, it follows that

(3.26)

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime}_{s}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{j}s-2^{j}|\tau|\left|\psi^{\prime}\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{j}s-2^{j}C_{1}|\tau|\left(2^{j}\lambda\right)^{a-1}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2^{j}s-2^{aj}4^{a-1}C_{1}|\tau|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{j}s-2^{j-1}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{j-1}s\>.

Then by performing integration-by-parts twice and using (
3.21
)-(
3.23
) and (
3.26
), while keeping in mind the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{2}
, we obtain

\left|\int_{1}^{4}e^{iF_{s}(\lambda)}\>H(\lambda)\>d\lambda\right|\lesssim\left(2^{j}s\right)^{-2}\>,

which yields for
m=1,\dots,4
and
l=0,1
,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{2}}2^{j}\>I^{m}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{2}}2^{j}\>2^{-j\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\left(2^{j}s\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{5}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil}^{\infty}2^{-j\left(\frac{3}{2}+l\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{5}{2}+l\right)}\>s^{\left(\frac{3}{2}+l\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{-1}\>.

Finally, we consider

\mathcal{J}_{3}:=\left\{j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil\mid 2^{(aj-1)}C^{-1}_{1}|\tau|<2^{j}s<2^{(aj+1)}4^{a-1}C_{1}|\tau|\right\}\>.

From the above definition, it immediately follows that the set
\mathcal{J}_{3}
is finite, moreover its cardinality is bounded by,

(3.28)

\left|\mathcal{J}_{3}\right|\leq\left(\frac{2}{a-1}\right)\log_{2}(2C_{1})\>+\>2\>.

Now for
j>\lceil-\log_{2}(s)\rceil
and
\lambda\in(1,4)
, by (
3.16
) and the lower bound in (
3.19
), we have

\left|F^{\prime\prime}_{s}(\lambda)\right|\geq\left(C^{-1}_{2}\min\left\{1\>,\>4^{a-2}\right\}\right)2^{aj}|\tau|\>.

Combining this with (
3.23
), it follows from Van der Corput (for instance, see the proof of
[
Ste93
, Proposition 2, pp. 332-333]
) that

\left|\int_{1}^{4}e^{iF_{s}(\lambda)}\>H(\lambda)\>d\lambda\right|\lesssim\left(2^{aj}|\tau|\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\>.

The above inequality along with the facts that
2^{j}s>1
and (
3.28
), while keeping in mind the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{3}
, then yields for
m=1,\dots,4
and
l=0,1
,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{3}}2^{j}\>I^{m}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{3}}2^{j}\>2^{-j\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\left(2^{aj}|\tau|\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{3}}2^{j}\>2^{-j\left(\frac{1}{2}+l\right)}\>\left(2^{j}s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{3}}\left(2^{j}s\right)^{-l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle s^{-1}\>\left|\mathcal{J}_{3}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle s^{-1}\>.

So combining (
3.5
), (
3.14
), (
3.25
), (
3.27
) and (
3.29
), we obtain

\left|I\right|\lesssim s^{-1}\>,

which in turn yields

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(g^{-1}_{y}g_{x}\cdot o)\>e^{i(t(x)-t(y))\psi(\lambda)}\frac{{|{\bf c}(\lambda)|}^{-2}}{\lambda}d\lambda\right|\>dx\>dy

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{dx\>dy}{d(x,y)}\>.

By the left
G
-invariance of the distance function and the volume measure, we have

\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{dx\>dy}{d(x,y)}=\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{dx\>dy}{d(x,y)}\>.

Then by Fubini-Tonelli’s theorem, another application of the left
G
-invariance of the distance function and the volume measure and the local growth asymptotics of the density function (
2.7
), it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{dx\>dy}{d(x,y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{dy}{d(x,y)}\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(\int_{B\left(x,1\right)}\frac{dy}{d(x,y)}\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B\left(o,\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(\int_{B\left(o,1\right)}\frac{dy}{d(o,y)}\right)dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 1\>,

and hence, we obtain (
3.3
). This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
. ∎

4.
Concluding remarks

We now sketch some possible directions for further research:

(1)

On
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, Theorem
1.1
establishes the sufficiency of the bound
\beta\geq 1/2
. On the other hand, for radial initial data, the Carleson’s problem for dispersive equations on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
has recently been fully resolved
[
Dew1
,
DR24
,
Dew2
]
and in particular, yields the necessity of the regularity threshold
\beta\geq 1/4
. So it will be interesting to seek the critical Sobolev index
\beta_{0}\in[1/4,1/2]
for solving the Carleson’s problem. For the particular case of the Schrödinger equation at least, keeping in mind the results for
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
Bou16
,
DGL17
]
, a reasonable guess would be to conjecture
\beta_{0}=1/3
.

(2)

It would be interesting to see whether the sufficiency of the bound
\beta\geq 1/2
continues to hold true for higher dimensional Real Hyperbolic spaces or more generally, for the class of rank one Riemannian Symmetric spaces of non-compact type.
