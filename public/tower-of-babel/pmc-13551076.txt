AI‐Based Automated Instrument Tracking During Endoscopic Skull Base Surgery: A Pilot Feasibility Study
Authors: David R Grimm, Dhiraj Pangal, Danyal Z Khan, Margaux Masson‐Forsythe, Kaan Duman, Ayesha Syeda, Jack Cook, Peter H Hwang, Jayakar Nayak, Zara M Patel, Noel Ayoub, Robert Dodd, Juan Fernandez‐Miranda, Daniel Donoho, Michael T Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEndoscopic endonasal approaches (EEA) to skull base tumors are a technically challenging procedure for both surgeons and trainees. Artificial intelligence and computer vision (CV) may offer an objective analysis that provides surgical assessment on technique and enhanced educational feedback for surgeons of all levels.MethodsPatients undergoing EEA from a tertiary care center between January 2022 and January 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. A large, publicly available CV model fine‐tuned on endoscopic surgical video in collaboration with Surgical Data Science Collective (SDSC) was used for frame‐by‐frame detection of 14 different surgical instruments. Tool usage was depicted across different surgical phases of EEA. Dice‐Sørensen Similarity Coefficients (DICE) were used to identify similarities between tool motion patterns across patients.ResultsTen patients (5 male, 5 female, ages 52.1 ± 13.8 years) were included, with over 35 h of video footage analyzed encompassing over 1.9 million individual frames. The sellar phase accounted for the majority of the analyzed frames. Within the sellar phase, the most frequently identified tools included suction (661,785), drill (138,031), Rhoton dissector (116,823), and cotton patties (105,038). Overall, DICE with higher frequency use exhibited substantially increased DICE scores: 0.52 for drill, 0.63 for Rhoton dissector, 0.67 for cotton patties, and 0.74 for suction.ConclusionCV models reliably identified surgical instruments and can be used to capture both high‐frequency patterns and deviations from common patterns in instrument usage across cases of endoscopic surgery.Keywords: artificial intelligence, computer vision, pituitary, rhinology

Introduction

Endoscopic endonasal approaches (EEA) for resection of skull base tumors require navigation of narrow anatomical corridors around critical anatomical structures. When successfully performed, patients experience decreased morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3]; however, the execution of these surgeries relies on highly technical demands [4, 5]. Communicating and teaching the subtleties of these techniques to trainees is often challenging in such a high‐stakes environment [6, 7, 8].

Artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision (CV) may provide insight into some of these surgical technique subtleties in a data‐driven manner. Endoscopic surgery is primed to leverage and develop these CV technologies into decision‐support tools and learning instruments. When implemented as an educational support tool via analytic assessment of trainee surgical performance, high‐quality and personalized feedback may potentially inform more effective communication of advanced surgical techniques. Recent studies have already begun to demonstrate the impacts of AI‐assisted surgical vision in analyzing variable surgical scenarios [9].

In this pilot study, we aim to demonstrate the ability of a large, publicly available CV model in identifying 14 different surgical instruments and tracking their movement patterns amongst patients undergoing EEA to pituitary adenomas. Videos were sourced from a high‐volume advanced endoscopic skull base surgery center. By analyzing spatial and temporal use patterns, we explore the feasibility of quantifying surgical techniques to ultimately develop data‐driven tools to enhance surgical training and improve surgical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Patient Cohort

After receiving approval from the institutional review board, a retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who had undergone an endoscopic endonasal resection of pituitary adenoma at a tertiary care academic center between January 01, 2022, and January 01, 2023. All surgical approaches and resections were performed by right‐handed skull base fellowship‐trained rhinologists and neurosurgeons. Relevant clinical data collected included age, sex, tumor site involvement, revision status, need for septoplasty, cerebrospinal fluid leak, and reconstruction technique.

Inclusion Criteria and Exclusion Criteria

Patient eligibility included age over 18 years, with a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of a pituitary adenoma, who underwent a transsphenoidal approach. Exclusion criteria included those without or with incomplete surgical video recordings, or additional surgical approaches, such as transpterygoid or transcranial approaches.

Data and Model Analysis

For CV detection of surgical tools, we engaged in collaboration with Surgical Data Science Collective (SDSC). We utilized a publicly available, state‐of‐the‐art CV and image detection model: You Only Look Once Version 8 (YOLOV8). Labeled data from ~65 cases of Endonasal endoscopic approaches were utilized for frame‐by‐frame surgical tool labeling. For each frame, tools were labeled, and their X and Y coordinates were recorded. Tool usage was then plotted by location throughout each phase of dissection, as well as plotted by frame time. Baseline YOLOV8 was then fine‐tuned on the labeled EEA dataset. Training metrics included a batch size of 16, a learning rate of 0.01, for a total of 50 epochs with a stochastic gradient descent as the optimizer, with an early stopping patience of 5 epochs. The dataset was split randomly, with 80% utilized for training and 20% for validation. The model was validated using an independent test dataset to evaluate its classification and object detection capabilities. The model demonstrates high overall reliability, achieving a weighted precision of 0.845, a weighted recall of 0.821, and a weighted F1‐score of 0.832. Localization accuracy is also strong, as evidenced by a weighted mAP50 of 0.865. Validation error rate is low, with confusion matrices revealing that the primary source of error is under‐detection. The model occasionally misses an instrument and misclassifies it as background, rather than committing the more critical error of confusing one distinct tool for another. High‐frequency classes show excellent performance, highlighted by a 0.96 true positive rate for the suction category. Metrics on testing sets are included in Supporting Information: S1.

Primary outcome measures included Dice‐Sørensen Similarity Coefficient (DICE) scores calculated from instrument motion plots to compare similarity between samples. A mean score per tool category was generated and reported across patient samples. Tools with less than 1000 frames identified were flagged to note the potential floor effect of near‐zero detection counts in order to avoid interpretation of falsely elevated or low DICE scores.

Identified tools included suction, drill, Rhoton dissector, cotton patties, grasper, Rongeur, scissor, suction coagulator, straight forceps, curette, bipolar forceps, Surgicel, surgical knife, and Doppler. Surgical phases were broken up into nasal, sphenoid, sellar, and closure based on standardized surgical transition points [10].

Results

Ten patients with pathologically confirmed pituitary adenomas comprising 5 females and 5 males with an average age of 52.1 ± 13.8 years (47.8 ± 16.3 for males and 56.4 ± 10.9 for females) were selected for analysis (Table 1, Figure 1). Of this cohort, 3 (30%) were undergoing revision endoscopic skull base surgery, 3 (30%) received a concurrent septoplasty, 4 (40%) experienced a CSF leak, and 6 (60%) reconstructions required a nasoseptal flap. Over 35 total hours of endoscopic footage were analyzed, equating to over 1.9 million frames. Across these 10 patients, 1.23 million tools were identified across 1.9 million frames. Tool usage varied significantly by surgical phase. The highest frequency of tool identification was in the sellar phase (1,140,078), followed by closure (43,193), sphenoid (27,255), and nasal (26,484). Out of the 14 detected instruments, the suction was the most frequently identified tool with 704,978 detections (Figure 2). Its usage was highest in the sellar phase (661,785), but also notable in the sphenoid (17,358), closure (13,400), and nasal (12,435). Other highly utilized tools across phases included the drill (144,077), Rhoton dissector (128,393), and cotton patties (124,505). The tools with the lowest frequency of identification included the Doppler (5), surgical knife (476), and Surgicel (1500). Tool counts by phase ranged 0–12,435 in the nasal phase, 0–17,358 in the sphenoid phase, 0–661,785 in the sellar phase, and 0–13,400 in the closure phase.

Total frame counts for each tool in each surgical phase with a heat map.

Mean DICE scores by phase when evaluated in their totality were relatively low: 0.08 for the nasal phase, 0.11 for the sphenoid phase, 0.29 for the sellar phase, and 0.16 for the closure phase (Figure 3). However, in the sellar phase, when evaluating tools with greater than > 100,000 tool identifications, DICE scores substantially increased to 0.52 for the drill, 0.63 for Rhoton dissector, 0.67 for cotton patties, and 0.74 for suction, with a mean of 0.64 indicating a strong overlap amongst patients for these high‐frequency use tools.

On visual inspection of tool distribution, we note what appears to be a significant overlap of tools with the pituitary adenoma seen in Figure 1 and tool usage (Figures 4 and 5). Additional patterns are notable in tools with > 100,000 identifications, such as the drill, Rhoton dissector, cotton patties, and suction. Both the drill and Rhoton dissector tool usage biases over the site of the pituitary tumor, as one would expect, while the cotton patties and suction are used more diffusely, and in a gravity‐dependent manner, reflecting their roles in maintaining visibility throughout the surgical field and evacuating blood. Discussion

In this pilot study, we focus on the outputs of a fine‐tuned YOLOv8 model for identifying and modeling the pattern of surgical instrument usage throughout endoscopic endonasal resection of pituitary adenomas. In other fields, such as ophthalmology, CV, and deep neural networks have already been shown to be highly effective in detecting phase transitions in real time during cataract procedures [11]. This has been described by Yu et al. [12] with a precision and recall greater than 90%. One thing that must be considered when detecting phase changes is the constellation of tools comprising that phase and in what proportion. For the intents and purposes of this pilot, it can be done by absolute values, but ultimately attempts at phase transition identification, and perhaps micro transitions within phases that have previously gone unnoticed, should be quantified as seen in other surgical specialties [13, 14, 15, 16].

As expected, 14 different surgical tools were identified in 10 different patient cases. And as discussed, we observe that tool usage is expectedly and understandably phase‐dependent. As the Sellar phase of the surgical approach is the longest, we see that the majority of tool identifications happen during this phase. This represents a few additional known aspects of this surgical phase. This phase is highly technical, repetitive, and small motions such as the drilling of millimeters of bone followed by reassessment are commonly seen, which not only contributes to the high identified tool count, but also the higher DICE in the suction, drill, Rhoton dissector, and cotton patties, which comprise the bulk of the tools used during this phase. It is similarly reassuring and expected that higher frequency used tools, as seen within the sellar phase, have increased overlap in their usage. In alternative phases, such as nasal, sphenoid, and closure, decreased time spent in these phases shows decreased tool identifications by absolute numbers and, understandably, poorer DICE scores. This could be due to a lack of data compared to that of the sellar phase, or higher inter‐patient variability that may exist in surgical approaches. In general, if these inter‐patient differences remain despite significant increases in training data, that may indicate variability in surgeon technique and could offer options for quality improvement or procedural standardization based on patient outcomes. To this author's knowledge, while CV has yet to be implemented with a focus on direct patient outcomes, it has frequently been used in assessment. Yang et al. [17] aimed to analyze technical proficiency using the Global Evaluation Assessment of Robotic Skills, focusing on surgeon efficiency and bimanual dexterity, and were able to depict strong correlations between AI assessment with CV and expert analysis evaluation. It is understandable that these CV models may soon be implemented to give personalized surgeon feedback [11, 18, 19, 20]. CV is similarly being used in the surgical setting in many other ways. Kumazu et al. [21] has depicted the use of deep‐learning algorithms to aid in identifying difficult‐to‐discern dissection planes in robotic‐assisted surgery. In a different vein, it is being used to aid in the identification of challenging‐to‐locate abdominal metastasis by Chen et al. [22].

On the education front, CV models have been shown to detect differing levels of surgeon expertise throughout training [23]. Expanded use cases of these models could include individualized resident, fellow, and attending feedback on surgical technique. Surgeon feedback could be broken down by phase as depicted in this study, or with further granularity, identifying microphases or aspects of each surgical phase that trainees of different levels may struggle with as they progress through their surgical training. Data‐driven, analytic feedback from these models may help bridge gaps in surgical training between low and high‐volume institutions by providing the intensive feedback gained through surgical repetition. While there is no substitution for surgical case volume, CV model feedback could help maximize trainee learning when case counts are limited. With their increased adoption, the demand for easy‐to‐use tools with straightforward visualization features and low technical barriers to entry, such as YoloV8s models and outputs, will become increasingly important. Critical next steps to bring these models into clinical practice and training environments include prospective validation on a broader scale and correlation of model feedback with objective surgical assessment tools and patient outcomes.

Ethical and translational implications surrounding automated surgical performance assessment should also not be overlooked. Fundamentally, these models rely on quantitative measurable metrics, which allow for their objectivity. However, operating rooms are dynamic environments where clinician judgment, communication with surrounding personnel, and adaptability are key factors that are impossible to capture through technical performance analysis alone. AI model transparency is also fundamentally flawed, as these assessments are made without clear explanations as to how or why. Finally, when automated assessments are misleading or faulty, contributing to either an adverse clinical outcome or misrepresentation of trainee ability, medicolegal liability with respect to the institution, developer, or clinician may become increasingly unclear.

This pilot study has several limitations. This is a retrospective cohort that is small, so extrapolation of findings is limited in nature. The study design would benefit from a larger cohort. The analysis of frames is additionally heavily skewed towards the sellar phase, leaving relatively limited amounts of data for the nasal, sphenoid, and closure phase. This could be why lower DICE scores are noted versus true diversity of surgical approach in these phases. In the detection of tools with lower frame counts, such as those noted < 1000, sparse detections may imply increased overlap due to higher DICE scores than is true or clinically meaningful. The transition point between surgical phases, though objectively determined, is still subject to surgeon judgment, lending towards misclassification of tools in one phase versus another. In this study, for all videos, these transition points were determined by the same surgeon, so any subjective bias is consistent across samples. Additional confounders in the clinical cohort include differing levels of case complexity, as no uniform surgical approach is ever employed. Differing team dynamics between rhinologists and neurosurgeons, as well as the level of experience, may also impact surgical approaches and use patterns. Finally, pituitary adenoma is treated as a uniform cohort, as no subtyping was performed.

Conclusion

SDSC's YoloV8 enables visualization of surgical tool use by surgical phase, quantification of tool utilization, and mapping of their location within the surgical field. As these models continue to develop, we hope to enhance surgical training and improve surgical outcomes with their implementation as educational feedback and surgical support tools.
