Martingale Projections and Quantum Decoherence
Lane P. Hughston, Levent A. Mengütürk

Abstract
We introduce so-called super/sub-martingale projections as a family of endomorphisms defined on unions of Polish spaces. Such projections allow us to identify martingales as collections of transformations that relate path-valued random variables to each other under conditional expectations. In this sense, super/sub-martingale projections are random functionals that (i) are boundedness preserving and (ii) satisfy a conditional expectation criterion similar to that of the classical martingale theory. As an application to the theory of open quantum systems, we prove (a) that any system-environment interaction that manifests a supermartingale projection on the density matrix gives rise to decoherence, and (b) that any system-environment interaction that manifests a submartingale projection gives rise an increase in Shannon-Wiener information. It follows (c) that martingale projections in an open quantum system give rise both to quantum decoherence and to information gain.



Keywords:
Quantum mechanics, entropy, information, system, environment, decoherence, martingales, supermartingales, submartingales.

1
Introduction

Most physical, biological and social systems observed in nature can be understood in terms of (a) an environment, (b) a set of admissible actions taking place in that environment, and (c) the set of all possible states formed as a manifestation of these actions. In essence, it is viable to consider any such dynamical system as some form of open system that hosts environmental interactions and transformations, which, in turn, evolve and shape the system as a consequence of transmissions.
In light of this, we focus on quantum systems as open systems consisting of states and actions on those states.
More specifically, we shall study
decoherence
, a well-known physical phenomenon that involves the reduction of the off-diagonal coordinates of a quantum density matrix in the basis of an observable as a result of internal and external interactions

[

]
, it has recently been shown in
[

]
that the opposite holds: decoherence implies information

.

The work of reference
[
11
]
motivates the present investigation, in which we take the view that quantum decoherence is a fundamentally important concept that provides deeper insights into the state evolution of a quantum system that interacts with its environment. The original contributions of the our work here are two-fold: (i) the introduction of what we call super/sub-martingale projections as random endomorphisms on path-valued random variables in a broad mathematical setting, and (ii) the proof of a fundamental connection of super/sub-martingale projections with quantum decoherence and Shannon-Wiener information.

Although we believe super/sub-martingale projections could merit a dedicated study in their own right, we adopt a parsimonious presentation of these functionals towards the study of quantum decoherence. Accordingly, we prove (a) that the existence of a supermartingale projection on the diagonal magnitudes of a density matrix implies quantum decoherence, and (b) that the existence of a submartingale projection on the diagonal magnitudes of a density matrix implies increasing Shannon-Wiener information, under conditional expectations. Hence, (c) if a system-environment interaction manifests a martingale projection on the density matrix of an open quantum system, one can expect both decoherence and information gain within that system.

The mathematical framework of the paper is structured as follows. In Sections 2 and 3 we introduce super/sub-martingale projections as a family of endomorphisms on Polish spaces of random variables. Thereafter, we continue in Section 4 within a quantum framework, where we study decoherence and Shannon-Wiener information in terms of super/sub-martingale projections. Our conclusions are summarized in Section 5 and in the Appendix we provide some examples of the structures under consideration.

2
Path Transformations

We work with a probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\nu)
and a nonempty compact set
\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In essence, we ask
\mathbb{T}
to be a totally-ordered set and we choose its superset
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
canonically. We define a sequence of nonempty subsets by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}\,\triangleq\,[r,t]\,\bigcap\,\mathbb{T}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{for any $r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}$}\ni\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}\neq\emptyset,

(1)

where
[r,t]=\{s\,:\,r\leq s\leq t\}
is a closed interval. Note that
\emptyset\subset\mathbb{T}_{[r,s]}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
for any
r\leq s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
. Also, for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, we have
\inf(\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})=r
and
\sup(\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})=t
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\rho=\inf(\mathbb{T})
and
\tau=\sup(\mathbb{T})
, then
\mathbb{T}_{[\rho,\tau]}=\mathbb{T}
. Also, note that
\mathbb{T}_{[t,t]}=\{t\}
for any
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a Polish space – that is to say, a topological space that is separable and completely metrizable. Let
(\mathbb{M},\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{M}))
be its Borel space. Since
\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}
is a Polish space for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, the product
\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}
is also a Polish space – hence,
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]},\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})
is a Borel space. For what follows, we are interested in
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})
-valued random variables for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
.
Note that each
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})
can be considered a
path
space for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, where any
\mathbb{M}
-valued random variable collected over
\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}
is itself a random variable that can be viewed as a path (i.e. trajectory) in
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})
.
Finally, we define the double-union space

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})=\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\,\,\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{T}\,\bigcap\,r\leq t}\left(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}\right),

(2)

which need
not
be a Polish space – we recall that unions of Polish spaces are not neccesarily Polish. Note that
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
is itself a path space, where any
\mathbb{M}
-valued random variable collected over any
\mathbb{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
is a path in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
.

Henceforth, let
X_{t}
be an
\mathbb{M}
-valued random variable for any fixed
t\in\mathbb{T}
. Accordingly, we write the collection over any
\mathbb{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}^{*}}\triangleq\{X_{t}\,:\,\forall t\in\mathbb{T}^{*}\}\,\,\text{for $\mathbb{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{T}$}\ni\mathbb{T}^{*}\neq\emptyset,

(3)

where
\mathbb{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
is totally ordered. Note that
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}^{*}}
in (
3
) is an element of
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{*})\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
for any nonempty
\mathbb{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
. In other words,
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}^{*}}
is an
(\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{*})
-valued random variable, and hence, a
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
-valued random variable.

Remark 2.2
.

If
\mathbb{T}^{*}=\{t\}
for fixed
t\in\mathbb{T}
, then
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}^{*}}=X_{t}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
is a
singleton path
, since we can view it as an
(\mathbb{M}\times\{t\})
-valued random variable.

We are now in position to introduce a family of endomorphisms on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
.

Definition 2.3
.

For any
p\leq r\in\mathbb{T}
and any
s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}\,:\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})

(4)

be an endomorphism such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{T}_{[s,t]}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right),

(5)

with
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{T}_{[s,t]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
. Then we say
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}
is a
path transformation
on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
from
\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}
to
\mathbb{T}_{[s,t]}
.

We refer the reader to the Appendix for various examples. We shall however provide a simple visualization of a path transformation for motivation, below.

Example 2.4
.

Set
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{T}=[0,3\pi]
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[0,2\pi]}}=\boldsymbol{X}_{[0,2\pi]}
be given by
X_{t}=\sin(t)+W_{t}
for every
t\in[0,2\pi]
, where
\{W_{t}\}_{t\in[0,2\pi]}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued Gaussian process under
\nu
. Let
\mathbf{1}(.)
be the indicator function, and define

\boldsymbol{Y}_{[\pi,3\pi]}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(0,2\pi)\mapsto(\pi,3\pi)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{[0,2\pi]}\right)=\begin{cases}X_{t},&\text{if}\ t\in[\pi,2\pi)\\
X_{2\pi}+\sin(t)+\epsilon\mathbf{1}(\tau\leq t),&\text{if}\ t\in[2\pi,3\pi],\end{cases}

(6)

where
\epsilon:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\tau:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are
\mathscr{F}
-measurable random variables.

We emphasize that path transformations in Definition
2.3
can be random transformations – this can also be seen in Example
2.4
through the appearance of random variables
\epsilon
and
\tau
. More specifically, define a subalgebra
\mathscr{G}\subset\mathscr{F}
. Even if
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
is
\mathscr{G}
-measurable, the random variable
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{T}_{[s,t]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
in (
5
) need not be
\mathscr{G}
-measurable. In addition, the statement below highlights that compositions of path transformations are path transformations.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
be an ordered set for an integer
K\geq 2
and let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\left(\alpha^{(i)},\beta^{(i)}\right)\mapsto\left(\alpha^{(i+1)},\beta^{(i+1)}\right)}
be a path transformation for every
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{K\}
. Then, for any
\alpha^{(i)}\leq\beta^{(i)}\in\mathbb{T}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the composition

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\left(\alpha^{(1)},\beta^{(1)}\right)\mapsto\left(\alpha^{(K)},\beta^{(K)}\right)}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\left(\alpha^{(1)},\beta^{(1)}\right)\mapsto\left(\alpha^{(2)},\beta^{(2)}\right)}\,\,\circ\,\,\cdots\,\,\circ\,\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\left(\alpha^{(K-1)},\beta^{(K-1)}\right)\mapsto\left(\alpha^{(K)},\beta^{(K)}\right)}

(7)

is a path transformation on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
from
\mathbb{T}_{[\alpha^{(1)},\beta^{(1)}]}
to
\mathbb{T}_{[\alpha^{(K)},\beta^{(K)}]}
.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from Definition
2.3
, since path transformations are endomorphisms parametrized over every ordered pair of points in
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

In Definition
2.3
, if we set
s=t
in (
5
), we can write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{T}_{[t,t]}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t,t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right)=Y_{t},

(8)

using Remark
2.2
. Hence,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t,t)}
can be viewed as a
projection
on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
for any
p\leq r\in\mathbb{T}
and any
t\in\mathbb{T}
. In addition, if also
p=r
, then we can write

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(r,r)\mapsto(t,t)}\left(X_{r}\right),

(9)

for any
r,t\in\mathbb{T}
. For such transformations, we can ease the notation by writing

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}\,\triangleq\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t,t)}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(r)\mapsto(s,t)}\,\triangleq\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(r,r)\mapsto(s,t)}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(r)\mapsto(t)}\,\triangleq\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(r,r)\mapsto(t,t)}

(10)

for
p,r,s,t\in\mathbb{T}
. We employ this simplification whenever we have a repeated index symbol.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
be an
\mathbb{E}
-valued stochastic process on
\mathbb{T}
and
\rho=\inf(\mathbb{T})
. For some fixed
s\in\mathbb{T}
, if

\displaystyle\left.X_{u}\,\right|_{\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[\rho,s]}}}\overset{\textnormal{law}}{=}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(\rho,s)\mapsto(u)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[\rho,s]}}\right),

(11)

for every
u\in\mathbb{T}_{[s,\tau]}
with
\tau=\sup(\mathbb{T})
, the family of path transformations

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(\rho,s)}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(\rho,s)\mapsto(u)}\,:\,\forall u\in\mathbb{T}_{[s,\tau]}\right\}

(12)

is law-consistent with
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
, given its path
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[\rho,s]}}
. Note that if
s=\rho
in (
11
), then
\mathcal{P}^{(\rho,\rho)}
, if it exists, can generate
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
from the initial state
X_{\rho}
.

3
Martingale Projections

We shall now introduce a special class of path transformations relevant to our study. We start by defining the
\sigma
-algebras

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}}=\sigma\left(\{X_{s}\,:\,\forall s\in\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}\}\right)=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}}\right),

where
\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}}\subset\mathscr{F}
for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
. Clearly, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[r,s]}}\subseteq\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}},\hskip 7.22743pt\text{$\forall r\leq s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}$.}

(13)

Remark 3.1
.

Since
\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]}
is a Polish space for any
r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, there exists a regular conditional distribution for any
(\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{T}_{[r,t]})
-valued random variable given any
\mathscr{G}\subset\mathscr{F}
.

Remark
3.1
is what allows us to work with conditional expectations, which are key functionals for our purposes. For example, if a path transformation is bounded in conditional expectation on a bounded subset of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
, we call this property
boundedness preserving
, as formalized below.

Definition 3.2
.

A path transformation
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}
is said to be boundedness preserving under
\nu
-measure if for a bounded
\boldsymbol{S}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
there exists a bounded
\boldsymbol{S}^{*}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{S}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right.\right]\in\boldsymbol{S}^{*}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}),

(14)

for every
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{S}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
.

If
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}
is boundedness preserving under
\nu
-measure, then we write
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(s,t)}\in\mathbb{B}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}))
. Hereafter, we work with an
\mathbb{E}
that can be equipped with a partial order
\preceq
. Recall that a total order
\leq
is a partial order.

Definition 3.3
.

For some
p\leq r\leq t\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}
be a projection path transformation such that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}\in\mathbb{B}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}))
. Then
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}
is a
supermartingale projection
of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right.\right]\preceq X_{r},

(15)

and a
submartingale projection
of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure if

\displaystyle X_{r}\preceq\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right.\right].

(16)

If
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}
satisfies both
(
15
)
and
(
16
)
, and hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(p,r)\mapsto(t)}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\right.\right]=X_{r},

(17)

then it is a
martingale projection
of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{T}_{[p,r]}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure.

With this definition at hand, let us clarify the connection between martingales from stochastic analysis
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
and martingale projections. Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
be an
\mathbb{M}
-valued process on
\mathbb{T}
and let
||.||_{\mathbb{M}}
be a norm in
\mathbb{M}
. If
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[||X_{t}||_{\mathbb{M}}\right]<\infty,\,\,\,\text{$\forall t\in\mathbb{T}$}\hskip 7.11317pt{\rm and}\hskip 7.11317pt\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[X_{t}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathbb{T}_{[0,s]}}\right.\right]=X_{s},\,\,\,\text{$\forall\,s\leq t\in\mathbb{T}$},

(18)

then
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
is a
(\nu,\mathscr{G}^{\boldsymbol{X}})
-martingale.

Note that the property of being a martingale is a property of a
stochastic process
, since the conditions in (
18
) must hold at
every
element of
\mathbb{T}
. In essence, a martingale projection relaxes this requirement and asks whether there exists a projection from any path (which can be a singleton path) in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
onto a point (which
is
a singleton path) in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
that satisfies the martingale-like conditions given in Definition
3.3
. Such transformations enable us to identify
\mathbb{M}
-valued martingales on
\mathbb{T}
as collections of boundedness preserving endomorphisms that relate
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})
-valued random variables to each other under conditional expectations.

Definition 3.4
.

We introduce the following spaces:

1.

\mathbb{H}^{(-)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}))
: All supermartingale projections on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure.

2.

\mathbb{H}^{(+)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}))
: All submartingale projections on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure.

3.

\mathbb{H}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T}))
: All martingale projections on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}\,,\mathbb{T})
under
\nu
-measure.

From Definition
3.3
and
\preceq
being a partial-order, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T}))=\mathbb{H}^{(-)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T}))\,\,\bigcap\,\,\mathbb{H}^{(+)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T})).

(19)

The generalization brought through
\mathbb{H}^{(-)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T}))
,
\mathbb{H}^{(+)}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T}))
and
\mathbb{H}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbb{M}\,,\mathbb{T}))
, in relation to classical martingales, will be important when studying quantum decoherence, where we would not necessarily ask the open quantum system to satisfy a property throughout its
entire
lifetime, but rather over possibly compartmentalized system-environment interactions – this will be clarified in what follows.

4
Quantum Decoherence

We shall now focus on open quantum systems as in reference
[
11
]
, for which the interactions between quantum systems and their environments can perturb those systems. First, let
\mathcal{Q}=\{1,\ldots,Q\}
for some
Q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and consider a quantum system initially in a pure state
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle
expressed in the form

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{\pi_{0}^{(q)}}\exp\left(\text{i}\theta^{(q)}\right)\left|E^{(q)}\right\rangle,

(20)

where
\pi_{0}^{(q)}\geq 0
for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Here
\{|E^{(q)}\rangle\,:\,q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
is a set of normalized eigenstates of a non-degenerate observable
\hat{E}
and
\{\theta^{(q)}\,:\,q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
is the set of corresponding phases. In this section, it should be understood that
\sqrt{x}=|\sqrt{x}|
for any
x\geq 0
. The initial density matrix of the system is
\Psi_{0}=|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|
, the coordinates of which are given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{(i,j)}=\langle E^{(i)}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|E^{(j)}\rangle=\sqrt{\pi_{0}^{(i)}\pi_{0}^{(j)}}\exp\left(\text{i}\left(\theta^{(i)}-\theta^{(j)}\right)\right),\hskip 7.22743pt\text{$\forall i,j\in\mathcal{Q}$},

(21)

in the
\hat{E}
-basis, so that the overall magnitudes are

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}^{(i,j)}|=\sqrt{\pi_{0}^{(i)}\pi_{0}^{(j)}},\hskip 7.22743pt\text{$\forall i,j\in\mathcal{Q}$}.

(22)

For what follows, we choose
\mathbb{T}=\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{M}\}
as a countable ordered set for some
t_{M}<\infty
and
M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, with
t_{0}=0
. The quantum system then interacts with its environment at
t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
and manifests a transformation that generates a new state

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{t_{0}}\right\rangle\mapsto\left|\psi_{t_{1}}\right\rangle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{\pi_{t_{1}}^{(q)}}\exp\left(\text{i}\theta^{(q)}\right)\left|E^{(q)}\right\rangle,

(23)

at
t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
\pi_{t_{1}}^{(q)}\geq 0
for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, which highlights the fact that we work with phase-homogeneous quantum systems where
\{\theta^{(q)}\,:\,q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
is invariant under transformations. As the system continues to interact with its environment, new states arise as a series of random transformations

\displaystyle\left\{\left|\psi_{t_{0}}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left|\psi_{t_{k-1}}\right\rangle\right\}\mapsto\left|\psi_{t_{k}}\right\rangle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{\pi_{t_{k}}^{(q)}}\exp\left(\text{i}\theta^{(q)}\right)\left|E^{(q)}\right\rangle,

(24)

for any
t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{t_{0}\}
with
\pi_{t_{k}}^{(q)}\geq 0
for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Since an external observer has no information about the details of such transformations, each
\pi_{t_{k}}^{(q)}
for
t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{t_{0}\}
and
q\in\mathcal{Q}
is a random variable for that observer. We define the expected state of the system, given previous states, under
\nu
-measure, by setting

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{[t_{0},t_{k-1}]}}\left[\Psi_{t_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\,\,|\psi_{t_{k}}\rangle\langle\psi_{t_{k}}|\,\,\left|\,\,\pi_{t_{0}}^{(q)},\ldots,\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\,:\,\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}\,\right.\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\,\,|\psi_{t_{k}}\rangle\langle\psi_{t_{k}}|\,\,\left|\,\,\left|\psi_{t_{0}}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left|\psi_{t_{k-1}}\right\rangle\right.\right],\hskip 7.22743pt\text{$\forall t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{t_{0}\}$},

(25)

for
t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{t_{0}\}

Proposition
4.1
shows that the off-diagonal magnitudes of the expected density matrix are smaller than those of the most recently observed density matrix of the given open quantum system. Hence, the existence of a supermartingale projection on the diagonal magnitudes of an observed ensemble of density matrices implies an expected decoherence in that system. Now, as in reference
[
11
]
, we continue by studying the Shannon-Wiener information given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{[t_{0},t_{k-1}]}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\,\,\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{t_{k}}^{(q)}\log\left(\pi_{t_{k}}^{(q)}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\pi_{t_{0}}^{(q)},\ldots,\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\,:\,\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}\,\right.\right],

(51)

for every
t_{k}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{t_{0}\}
, which is essentially the negative of the Shannon-Wiener entropy.
Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{[t_{0},t_{k-1}]}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t_{k-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left[\,\,\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\log\left(\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\right)\,\,\left|\,\,\pi_{t_{0}}^{(q)},\ldots,\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\,:\,\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}\,\right.\right].

(52)

Note that the
\sigma

under conditional expectation. Therefore, we can move the summation from inside the conditional expectation to the outside, writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{[t_{0},t_{k-1}]}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t_{k-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\log\left(\pi_{t_{k-1}}^{(q)}\right).

(53)

Finally, we see that the right-hand side of (

, which allows us to write (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{[t_{0},t_{k-1}]}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t_{k-1}}\right]=\mathcal{S}_{t_{k-1}}.

(54)

We can now present the next result to evaluate the direction of information in the system.

5
Conclusion

It may be helpful if we comment briefly on the significance of the present work. In this paper, we have constructed what we call super/sub-martingale projections as random endomorphisms on path-valued random variables. As part of this objective, we have introduced path transformations on Polish spaces, which form a general framework that allows us to host super/sub-martingale projections within a broader landscape. We envision that the mathematical setting presented concisely in this work can foster further such rigorous analysis in future.

The main goal of this paper has been to study the decoherence and information-theoretic behaviour of open quantum systems, which we have approached through super/sub-martingale projections. We have shown that the existence of martingale projections in an open quantum system implies both expected decoherence and expected information gain in that system.

Towards deepening our understanding of quantum decoherence, supermartingale projections enable us to identify a fundamental condition on system-environment interactions as path-transformations, since they naturally induce expected non-increasing behaviour over possibly compartmentalised time segments. Consequentially, these projections allow us to connect classical supermartingales from stochastic analysis with quantum decoherence under a specific setup. In the same vein, submartingale projections (and in special cases classical submartingales) enable us to connect non-decreasing behaviour with the direction of information in open quantum systems. Accordingly, this paper places martingale projections, and thereby classical martingales, in a central location to merge a deep relation between quantum decoherence and information.

We note in particular that martingales play a pivotal role in energy-based state reduction dynamics in quantum measurement theory
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
22
,
23
,
27
]
. Accordingly, we close this paper by drawing attention to the following observation. In the theory of quantum measurement, martingales have been interpreted as randomized analogues for the conservation law of energy
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
21
]
. In the theory of quantum decoherence, martingales can again be interpreted as randomized analogues for the conservation law of energy. This remark is suggestive of a potentially deeper consolidation of quantum measurement and quantum decoherence through martingale constructs, which we hope to explore in more detail elsewhere.
