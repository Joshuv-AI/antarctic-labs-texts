The supercooled Stefan problem: fractal freezing and the fine structure of maximal solutions
Raymond Chu, Inwon Kim, Sebastian Munoz

Abstract
. We study the supercooled Stefan problem in arbitrary dimensions. First, we study general solutions and their irregularities, showing generic fractal freezing and nucleation, based on a novel Markovian gluing principle. In contrast, we then establish regularity properties of maximal solutions, which are obtained by maximizing a suitable notion of “average” freezing time. Unexpectedly, we show that maximal solutions have a transition zone that is open modulo a low-dimensional set: this allows us to apply obstacle problem theory for a finer regularity analysis. We further show that maximal solutions are in general non-universal, and we obtain sharp stability results under perturbation of each maximal solution. Lastly, we study maximal solutions in both the radial and the one-dimensional setting. We show that in these cases the maximal solution is universal and minimizes nucleation, in agreement with phenomena observed in the physics literature.

1.
Introduction

We study the supercooled Stefan problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\eta_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\eta=(\chi_{\{\eta>0\}})_{t}&\text{in }\;(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
\eta\geq 0,\,\,\eta(0,\cdot)=\mu\quad&\text{on}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
\end{cases}

where
0\leq\mu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
has compact support. The system (
1.1
) models the freezing of supercooled water into ice, where
\eta
denotes the negative temperature of the water
[
38
]
. Initially the water is at temperature
-\mu
, the ice is kept at temperature
0
throughout the evolution, and the transition from water to ice occurs at zero temperature. Thus
\{\eta>0\}
is the region occupied by the water, and the
free boundary

\partial\{\eta>0\}
denotes the interface between ice and water. Letting
U:=\{\mu>0\}
be the space occupied by water at the initial time, we require
\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\}\uparrow U
as
t\downarrow 0
, so that
(\mu,U)
serves as the initial data for the free boundary problem; this will be made precise in Definitions
2.1
.

Unlike the classical Stefan (melting) problem, the supercooled Stefan problem is ill-posed and unstable
[
27
]
, as is typical for solidification from a supercooled state. Even in one dimension, classical solutions can develop discontinuities where the positive set jumps in finite time
[
39
,
25
,
13
]
. When the free boundary is constrained to be a single point, the solution can be continued past the jump
[
16
]
, and there is a unique continuation that minimizes the jump size
[
12
,
33
]
.

Beyond the single-free-boundary-point case, mostly formal/numerical work and well-prepared initial data have been treated
[
26
,
24
,
9
,
14
,
29
]
.

The main analytical challenge is the rich geometry of potential singularities that can form. For general initial data, even global-in-time existence was open until the recent work
[
10
]
, which also introduced the notion of
maximal
solutions.

Our aim is to analyze (
1.1
) for general

\mu
, beginning with weak solutions and then focusing on the class of maximal solutions. Akin to
[
10
]
, our analysis is motivated by the underlying particle dynamics and combines methods from probability and PDE: we present some of the main ideas in Section
1.1
.

To state our results, we first define the largest class of initial data for which global-in-time weak solutions can exist, formulated in terms of the
subharmonic order

\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}
between measures (see Definitions
2.2
–
2.3
). Roughly speaking,
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
with respect to
U
if there exists a stopped Brownian motion in
U
with initial distribution
\mu
and final distribution
\nu
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
U
be a bounded open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and let
\delta\in[0,1)
. We say that
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
if
\mu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is non-negative,
\{\mu>0\}=U
, and

(1.2)

\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu\;\;\textnormal{with respect to}\;U,\textnormal{ for some }\nu\;\textnormal{with }\nu\leq(1-\delta)\chi_{U}.

It is known that a necessary condition for (
1.1
) to have a solution is
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}(U)
(see
[
10
, Thm. 7.2]
).
Conversely,
[
10
]
shows existence whenever
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
for some
\delta>0
,
U
is Lipschitz, and
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
; we discuss how the Lipschitz assumption can be removed in Proposition
4.1
and Remark
4.2
.

Clearly
\mu\leq 1-\delta
implies
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
, but the converse need not hold.
Heuristically, elements of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
may take large values at interior points far from the boundary of
U
, where there is more room to diffuse the mass by Brownian motion. The importance of the critical supercooling threshold
\mu=1
has been long known in the physics literature
[
8
,
35
]
, and will be observed here in several contexts.

Given any weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
), the sets
\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\}
are monotone nonincreasing in
t
, and
\eta
has a unique lower semicontinuous representative that ensures the set
\{\eta>0\}\subset(0,\infty)\times U
is open. One may then unambiguously decompose

(1.3)

U=F_{0}\dot{\cup}\bigcup_{t>0}\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\},

where
|F_{0}|=0
, and
F_{0}
is the set of points that freeze at the initial time. More generally, if one defines the
freezing time

s\in\operatorname{LSC}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};[0,\infty])
by

(1.4)

s(x):=\inf\{t>0:\eta(t,x)=0\},

then
F_{0}=\{x\in U:s(x)=0\}
and, for
t_{0}>0
,
\{s=t_{0}\}
is the set of points that freeze at time
t_{0}
, which equals
\partial\{\eta>0\}\cap\{t=t_{0}\}
if
s
is continuous (see, however, Proposition
1.4
). We also define the
transition zone
of
\eta
as the set of points that freeze in positive time,

(1.5)

\Sigma=\{0<s(x)<\infty\}\subset U.

Our first result shows that, for general initial data, the freezing set may contain any prescribed fractal.

Theorem 1.2

(Programmed fractal freezing)
.

Let
U
be a bounded open set, and let
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, with
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
. Then the following hold:

(i)

Suppose
\mu\leq 1-\delta
. Then for any closed null set
F\subset U
, there exists a weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) such that

F\subset\{x:s(x)=0\}.

(ii)

Let
\eta_{0}
be a weak solution to (
1.1
). Then there exists
0\leq T_{0}=T_{0}(\|\mu\|_{\infty})<\infty
such that the following holds: for any
T>T_{0}
and for any closed null set
F\subset\{\eta_{0}(T,\cdot)>0\}
, there is a weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) with corresponding freezing time
s
, such that

(1.6)

\eta(t,\cdot)=\eta_{0}(t,\cdot),\quad 0\leq t<T,\quad\text{and }\;\;F\subset\{x:s(x)=T\}.

Moreover, if
\mu\leq 1
, then
T_{0}=0
. In particular, for any
T>0
, there exists a weak solution of (
1.1
) with the property

(1.7)

\operatorname{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x\in U:s(x)=T\})=d.

In physical terms, Theorem
1.2
can be viewed as a timed seeding principle: a brief intervention that releases or activates nucleants along a prescribed thin set
F
, forcing a flash-freeze at
F
. The set
F
may be space-filling in the fractal sense, which corresponds to the formation of crystals of arbitrary complexity. This also highlights the extreme non-uniqueness of the model in the absence of regularizing physics such as surface tension. In a similar vein, we also show (see Proposition
4.4
) that, even with smooth initial data, solutions may exhibit
nucleation
(spontaneous appearance of ice) at the initial time, as well as an interior waiting time phenomenon.

Given this ill-posedness, it is natural to ask whether one may select a canonical, physically meaningful notion of solution that admits a well-posedness theory and improved regularity properties. While this has been explored extensively in the one-dimensional case of a single free boundary point
[
12
,
33
]
, very little is known in a more general setting.

To explore these questions, we focus for the remainder of the paper on
maximal
solutions of (
1.1
), first introduced in
[
10
]
(see Definition
2.8
). Informally, these solutions maximize their time to freeze, in a sense analogous to the one-dimensional physical solutions of
[
12
]
. Maximal solutions arise from the stochastic optimization problem

(1.8)

\mathcal{P}(\mu,u):=\inf_{\nu}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)\,d\nu(x):\;W_{0}\sim\mu,\;W_{\tau}\sim\nu\leq\chi_{U},\;\tau\leq\tau^{U}\right\},

where
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
(and thus
U=\{\mu>0\}
),
W_{t}
is Brownian motion,
\tau^{U}
denotes its first exit time from
U
, and
u>0
is a smooth, strictly superharmonic function. Denoting by
\nu
the unique optimizer,
\nu=\chi_{\Sigma}
a.e. for some measurable
\Sigma\subset U
, and there exists a unique solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) with transition zone
\Sigma

[
10
]
. In fact, if
\tau
is the Skorokhod–optimal stopping time (see Section
2
) such that
W_{\tau}\sim\nu
, then
\eta
is precisely the active particle distribution of
W_{t\wedge\tau}
(see Definition
2.4
), and
\eta
is the unique solution to (
1.1
) that postpones solidification as much as possible, in the sense that it maximizes

(1.9)

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}-\Delta u(W_{t})dt\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}-\Delta u(x)\eta(t,x)dtdx.

As we will see in Proposition
2.9
this maximization property uniquely characterizes maximal solutions.
In particular, in the simplest case of
\Delta u\equiv-1
, the maximal solution associated to
u
maximizes the quantity
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\right]
, or equivalently
\|\eta\|_{L^{1}((0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
. Moreover, these solutions have a maximal transition zone, in the sense of the subharmonic order (see
[
10
, Thm. 1.2]
).

As we will see next, maximal solutions also enjoy substantial regularity and stability properties. We first discuss our regularity results for the transition zone (
1.5
). It was conjectured in
[
10
, Rem. 6.2]
that the transition zone of maximal solutions is, in general, not an a.e. open set. Our next result proves this conjecture to be false, showing that the transition zone may only contain a low-dimensional subset of its boundary. In what follows, given
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
S^{\circ}
will denote the topological interior of
S
.

Theorem 1.3

(Fine structure of the transition zone)
.

Let
U
and
\mu
be as given in Theorem
1.2
, and assume that
U
has a Lipschitz boundary. Let
u
be a smooth, positive, and strictly superharmonic function on
\overline{U}
. Let
\eta
be the maximal solution to (
1.1
) associated to
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
, and let the freezing time
s:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
be defined by (
1.4
). Then the transition zone
\Sigma=\{x:0<s(x)<\infty\}
satisfies

(1.10)

\Sigma=\Sigma^{\circ}\cup A,

where
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)\leq d-1
. If
u
is real analytic, then

(1.11)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)\leq d-2.

One also has, for general
u
,

(1.12)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\partial\Sigma\cap\{s(x)>0\})\leq d-1,\;\;\quad\quad|\partial\Sigma|=0,

and the set
\Sigma
is measure-saturated.

We refer to Lemma
5.2
for the definition of measure-saturation. Heuristically, this means that: (i) there cannot exist cracks that freeze in finite time surrounded by water that never freezes, and (ii) there cannot exist cracks that never freeze surrounded by water that freezes in finite time.

Regarding (
1.11
), the gain in regularity when
u
is analytic rather than merely smooth reflects a broader pattern that highlights the inherent instability of the problem (see also the discussion after Theorem
1.5
regarding the converse phenomenon).

In combination with the results of the companion paper
[
15
]
, Theorem
1.3
yields strong regularity results for the freezing time, and precise dimension bounds for the set of singular points that freeze at positive times, outside of which the free boundary is smooth (see Corollary
5.4
and Remark
5.5
). In particular, in the subcritical case when
\mu\leq 1
, Corollary
5.4
implies that fractal freezing never occurs for maximal solutions at any positive time, in contrast with Theorem
1.2
. Moreover, for general
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U),
maximal solutions do not exhibit fractal freezing for most times, namely

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{t:\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x:s(x)=t\})>d-1\})=0.

While Theorem
1.2
suggests that this estimate is close to sharp, the optimality of the estimate and whether maximal solutions can develop fractal freezing when
\sup\mu>1
remain open.

For general maximal solutions, the Hausdorff dimension estimate (
1.12
) for
\partial\Sigma
cannot be improved to include the set of initial freezing
\{s=0\}
. In fact, for any closed null set
F\subset U
(in particular fractals with
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(F)=d
), there exists
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}(U)\cap C(\overline{U})
such that every weak solution (including maximal solutions) of (
1.1
) satisfies
F\subset\{s=0\}\subset\partial\Sigma
(see Proposition
5.6
, compare with Theorem
1.2
(i)). It remains an interesting open question whether for maximal solutions the regularity of
\{s=0\}
can be improved with the slack condition
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
,
\delta>0
.

We also note that, even in the more regular setting of maximal solutions with smooth initial data, it may be difficult to understand the geometry of
\Sigma
beyond Hausdorff dimension bounds. For instance, while it is easy to show that
\partial U\subset\overline{\Sigma}
,
waiting time
phenomena may develop. That is, there may be points near the initial free boundary
\partial U
that never freeze, and where the freezing time

is discontinuous (see Figure
3
for a numerical example). The following construction illustrates this fact.

Proposition 1.4

(Waiting time, Proposition
6.6
)
.

Let
d=2
, let
U=B_{1}\setminus\overline{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}
, and let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
There exists a smooth density
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
such that, for any choice of

, if
\eta
denotes the maximal solution associated to
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
,
then the corresponding transition zone

satisfies

(1.13)

|U_{\varepsilon}\setminus\Sigma|>0\quad\text{for every}\quad\varepsilon>0,

where
U_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in U:d(x,\partial U)<\varepsilon\}
. In particular, the freezing time of any such

is discontinuous. Moreover,
\mu
may be chosen to be arbitrarily close, in any

such that the associated transition zone for

is the open union of two outer annuli of

.

This result also shows that the absence of a waiting time is an unstable phenomenon, since the limiting density
\mu_{*}
does not satisfy the pathology (
1.13
). The construction is robust, in the sense that
\mu_{*}\in(0,1)
can be taken to be any continuous

In hopes of identifying a canonical notion of solution,
[
10
]
conjectured that maximal solutions are
universal
. Namely, while
\eta
is uniquely determined by its maximization of (

), it is natural to ask whether
\eta
is independent of the choice of the superharmonic weight

. The following result shows by counterexample that this conjecture is false in dimensions

, yet universality holds in the radial and one-dimensional cases. We refer to Propositions

Theorem 1.5

(Non-universality of maximal solutions)
.

In dimension
d\geq 2
, and for any
\delta>0
, there exist smooth
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(B_{1})
and smooth weights
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
such that the maximal solutions
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
to (
1.1
) associated to
{\mathcal{P}}(\mu,u_{1})
and
{\mathcal{P}}(\mu,u_{2})
are not equal.

If
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}(U)
,
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
, and either

or

and

are both radial functions, then there exists a unique maximal solution, independent of

. In addition, if
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(U)
for some

, then the set of initial freezing is empty.

The counterexample constructed in

1.5
is fairly general, and highlights the converse phenomenon to Theorem
1.3
: non-analyticity of

on its own can induce irregularities of the transition zone (see Proposition

). In the same spirit, the explicit construction in the proof of Proposition

(see Proposition

) shows that the waiting time pathology arises due to the insufficiently fast decay of the tangential Fourier coefficients of the smooth density
\mu

[
10
, Sec. 7.1]
).

In light of Theorem
1.5
, a natural follow-up question is to ask which weight

u
should be regarded as physically canonical; this remains open and likely requires a further understanding of the thermodynamics behind the problem.

Given the lack of universality, the most one can reasonably expect is stability of maximal solutions with respect to perturbations of
\mu
and

; this is the content of our next result. We now specify the notion of convergence for our domains and transition zones.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
S
be measurable subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We say that
S_{n}\to S
as

if
\lim_{n\to\infty}|S_{n}\Delta S|=0
and, for every open set
W
such that
\overline{S}\subset W
, there exists
N>0
such that
\overline{S_{n}}\subset W
for
n\geq N
.

Now for
\delta>0
,
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)\cap C(\overline{U})
with a Lipschitz domain

,
consider
\mu_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U_{n})\cap C(\overline{U_{n}})
such that

is Lipschitz. We assume that
(\mu_{n},U_{n})
converges to
(\mu,U)
in the following sense.

(1.14)

({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus U_{n})\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus U),\quad\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{n}\cap U)}\to 0\;\text{ as }\;n\to\infty.

We further assume the following on the convergence of the weight function, with an open set
D
containing
\overline{U}
and
\overline{U_{n}}
:

(1.15)

\{u_{n}\}\subset C^{2}(D),\;\;\Delta u_{n}<0\;\hbox{ in }D,\hbox{ and }u_{n}\hbox{ converges to some }u\hbox{ in }C^{2}(D),\;\;\hbox{as }n\to\infty.

In this setting we have the following stability result.

Theorem 1.7

(Stability of maximal solutions, Propositions

and

)
.

For

and

as given above, let

where
\Sigma_{n}

The assumptions of Theorem

Finally, we discuss the free boundary behavior of maximal solutions in dimension
1
and in the radial case, which are universal by Theorem
1.5
. We are interested in the topology of the positive set. Given a radial or one-dimensional solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) for which

is connected, we say that
\eta
exhibits nucleation if for some

, the set
\{\eta(t_{0},\cdot)>0\}
is disconnected.

Theorem 1.8

(Proposition
8.2
, Proposition
8.3
, Proposition
8.4
)
.

Assume that
d=1
with
U=(0,1)
, and let
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(U)
with

. Then the following holds for the maximal solution
\eta
with initial data
\mu
:

(a)

If

, then nucleation does not occur, and the free boundary consists of two smoothly evolving boundary points.

(b)

There exists
\mu\in C^{\infty}((0,1))
with

(c)

If

For

Although the nucleation example above is specific, Section

offers heuristics indicating that nucleation is generic when

. In higher dimensions, it remains an open question whether the occurrence of nucleation depends sharply on the critical supercooling value (see
[

In the two-phase setting, Gurtin
[

interpreted nucleation as an entropy-increasing mechanism that stabilizes the system, providing a thermodynamic rationale for continuing solutions past blow-up. In this context, Theorem

(c) asserts that maximal solutions are

1.1.
Main ideas

After discussing some preliminaries in Section
2
, we obtain, in Section
3
, a useful characterization for subharmonic order in rough domains, and a low-regularity Green-type formula for subharmonically ordered measures. These will be very important throughout the paper, particularly for the fractal freezing results, and to handle the low regularity of the dual optimizers of (
1.8
), as is further discussed below.

In Section
4
, we prove Theorem
1.2
with a two-step strategy. First, by exploiting the connection between solutions to (
1.1
) and so-called subharmonically generated sets
[
29
, Sec. 6]
, we extend the existence theory of
[
10
]
to irregular domains
U
(see Proposition
4.1
). Then, by solving (
1.1
) in a suitably rough subdomain
\tilde{U}\subset U
such that
|U\setminus\tilde{U}|=0
, we produce a solution to (
1.1
) such that the set of initial freezing
\{s=0\}
contains an arbitrary fractal.

The main ingredient in the second step, which is also of independent interest, is a
Markovian gluing principle
for the optimal Skorokhod problem; see Proposition
4.8
. By
[
5
, Rem. 6.19]
, any admissible stopping time that is the first hitting time to a closed, nondecreasing in time set, must be optimal. This characterization of optimal times allows us to glue them: the optimal stopping times associated with two Skorokhod problems can be concatenated at any prescribed deterministic time, and the resulting stopping time is optimal for the suitably glued Skorokhod problem. We stress that this gluing result is stronger than a standard dynamic programming principle, due to the freedom in choosing the continuation after the gluing time.

Due to the connection between (
1.1
) and the optimal Skorokhod problem (
2.4
), it follows then that one can patch together two weak Stefan solutions to obtain a new weak solution (see Proposition
4.10
). The combination of these two steps generates “programmed fractal freezing” at positive times.

In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.3
by making use of the dual formulation of (
1.8
), namely

(1.17)

\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)=-\inf_{\psi}\left\{\int(\psi-u)^{+}-\int\psi\mu:\psi\in L^{1}(U),\;\Delta\psi\geq 0\right\}.

The minimizer
\nu
for (
1.8
) may then be a.e. characterized as

(1.18)

\nu=\chi_{\{\psi>u\}},

where
\psi
is any optimizer for (
1.17
). While one must be cautious about the fact that the sets
\{\psi>u\}
and
\Sigma
only agree up to a null set and cannot be identified, this suggests the natural strategy of obtaining regularity of
\Sigma
by studying the regularity of the optimizers
\psi
.

Indeed, we show that any optimizer
\psi
is harmonic away from the set of initial freezing. This allows us to take advantage of the parabolic obstacle problem formulation of (
1.1
) and precisely describe, through PDE methods, the fine structure of
\Sigma
. The dimension estimate (
1.11
) is obtained through a Weierstrass preparation argument that uses in a critical way the strict superharmonicity of the weight
u
and the measure saturation of the transition zone.

Let us mention that the dual problem (
1.17
) that we study in Section
5
is a generalization of the classical problem of approximating, in the
L^{1}
norm, an arbitrary superharmonic function
u
by a subharmonic function
\psi
(see
[
1
,
2
]
, and see also
[
3
,
28
,
22
]
for the problem with
harmonic

\psi
). Indeed, upon adding a constant, a computation shows that (

) is equivalent to

\inf_{\psi}\left\{\int(\psi-u)^{+}(1-\mu)+\int(\psi-u)^{-}\mu:\psi\in L^{1}(U),\Delta\psi\geq 0\right\},

and, by taking

Even in this special setting, the problem is delicate, and global regularity results for the optimizers only exist in special domains, under strong conditional assumptions. For instance, it was shown in

that if

.

In Section
6
, we prove Theorem

and Proposition

. First, we obtain a complete description of radial and one-dimensional maximal solutions: their transition zone is an outer shell of the domain and there is no initial freezing (Proposition

Section
7
contains the proof of Theorem

and

via the use of potential variables. This is then upgraded to strong convergence by using some of the free boundary regularity implied by Theorem

.

Finally, we prove Theorem

in Section
8
. In the radial and one-dimensional cases, the spatial (or radial) derivative of the potential satisfies a monotonicity property when

(see the arguments of Proposition

This monotonicity rules out nucleation and jumps of the free boundary for the maximal solution in this regime. With nucleation and jumps absent for

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we will use the same symbol to refer to an absolutely continuous measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as to refer to its density function.
We begin by giving the definition of a weak solution to the supercooled Stefan problem.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
0\leq\mu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be of compact support and denote
U=\{\mu>0\}
. We say that
\eta\in L^{2}((0,\infty);H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))

is a weak solution of (
1.1
) if

(a)

the set
\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\}
is nonincreasing in

,

(b)

\eta
vanishes outside
U\times(0,\infty)
, and
\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\}=U
up to a null set,

(c)

for any test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(2.1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big[(\eta-\chi_{\{\eta>0\}})\varphi_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\varphi\Big]\,dt\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\big[(-\mu+\chi_{U})\varphi\big](0,x)\,dx.

An important notion of ordering for establishing existence of solutions to the Stefan problem is the subharmonic order.

Definition 2.2

(Subharmonic ordering)
.

For
0\leq\mu,\nu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with compact support and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mu(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nu

\mu
and
\nu
are subharmonically ordered if for some open set
\mathcal{O}
containing the support of
\mu+\nu

(2.2)

\int\varphi(x)d\mu(x)\leq\int\varphi(x)d\nu(x)\;\;\text{ for every smooth subharmonic function}\;\;\varphi\text{ in }\;\mathcal{O},

and we write
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
.

Subharmonic ordering admits a natural probabilistic interpretation in terms of Brownian motion
[
37
,
36
]
. For our filtered probability space, we adopt the same standard assumptions as in
[
29
, Sec. 2.2.1]
. By
[
18
, Thm. 1.5]
, it is known that
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
if and only if there exists a stopping time
\tau
for Brownian motion such that

(2.3)

W_{0}\sim\mu,\quad W_{\tau}\sim\nu,\quad\mathbb{E}[\tau]<\infty.

We use this probabilistic interpretation to define subharmonic order with respect to a rough domain.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu
,
\nu
be as in Definition
2.2
, let

be a bounded open set such that

with respect to

\tau\leq\tau^{U}
for Brownian motion such that (

) holds.

We now recall the

\mathcal{Q}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\tau}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\tau^{2}]:W_{0}\sim\mu\text{ and }W_{\tau}\sim\nu\right\},

where

are stopping times (for further background on the optimal Skorokhod problem, see the survey
[

[

By

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w-\frac{1}{2}\Delta w=-\frac{1}{2}\nu\chi_{\{w>0\}}\text{ on }(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\

then

(2.6)

\tau^{*}=\inf\{t\geq 0:(t,W_{t})\in\{w=0\}\}\text{ almost surely, where }w\text{ solves }\eqref{obstacle_problem_123}.

Here

Definition 2.4

3.
Properties of relative subharmonic order in rough domains

In this section we obtain, under minimal regularity assumptions on
U
, a characterization of the subharmonic order with respect to
U
. This will be useful in later sections, especially for the fractal freezing results of Section
4
. Along the way we also derive a low-regularity Green-type formula for subharmonically ordered measures that will be important in Section
5
.

We begin with a basic elliptic estimate.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and assume that
v:=\Delta^{-1}h\geq 0
. Then there exists
C=C(d)>0
such that for any
x\in\{v=0\}
and
r>0
,

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}+r\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\leq C\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}r^{2}.

Proof.

The Harnack inequality
[
20
, Thms. 8.17, 8.18]
yields

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\leq C(\inf_{B_{r}(x)}v+r^{2}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r}(x))})=C\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}r^{2}.

On the other hand, by the Calderón-Zygmund estimates
[
20
, Thm. 9.11]
, we have

r\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\leq C(\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r}(x))}+r^{2}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r}(x))})\leq C\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}r^{2}.

∎

For the next lemma, we recall that

(3.1)

U_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in U:\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial U)<\varepsilon\}.

Besides its important role in this section, the other main purpose of the following Green-type formula will be to eventually test it against the optimizer
\psi
of the dual problem (
1.17
), which is merely in
L^{1}(U)
. We stress that both the
L^{1}
regularity of
\psi
and the quadratic decay of the Green potential
v
near
\partial U
are critical in the proof below.

Lemma 3.2

(Green identity for subharmonic order)
.

Let
U
be a bounded open set, let
0\leq\mu,\nu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
vanish outside
U
, and let
v=\Delta^{-1}(\nu-\mu)
. Then the following holds:

(i)

If
v\geq 0
and
v
vanishes outside
U
, then, for every subharmonic function
\psi\in L^{1}(U)
,

(3.2)

\int\psi(x)\nu(x)dx=\int\psi(x)\mu(x)dx+\int v(x)\Delta\psi(dx),

where
\Delta\psi\geq 0
is the distributional Laplacian of
\psi
.

(ii)

If
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
with respect to
U
and
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
, then
v\geq 0
,
v
vanishes outside
U
, and the conclusion of (i) holds.

Proof.

Assume first that
v\geq 0
and
v\equiv 0
outside
U
. Let
\zeta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U)
be such that
0\leq\zeta_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
,
\zeta_{\varepsilon}\equiv 1
in
U\setminus U_{\varepsilon}
,
|D\zeta_{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}
, and
|D^{2}\zeta_{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}}
.
We then have

(3.3)

\left|\int_{U}\Delta(v\zeta_{\varepsilon})\psi-\int_{U}(\Delta v\zeta_{\varepsilon}\psi)\right|=\left|\int_{U}(2\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\psi+\Delta\zeta_{\varepsilon}v\psi)\right|\\
\leq C\int_{U_{\varepsilon}}(|\nabla v\|\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\|\psi|+|\Delta\zeta_{\varepsilon}\|v\|\psi|).

By Lemma
3.1
, since
v
vanishes on
\partial U
, we have
|v|+\varepsilon|\nabla v|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
on
U_{\varepsilon}
, and therefore

(3.4)

\int_{U_{\varepsilon}}(|\nabla v\|\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\|\psi|+|\Delta\zeta_{\varepsilon}\|v\|\psi|)\leq C\int_{U_{\varepsilon}}|\psi|.

Since
\psi\in L^{1}(U)
is a subharmonic function,
\sigma:=\Delta\psi
is a non-negative Radon measure on
U
. From (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we then have the estimate

\left|\int_{U}v\zeta_{\varepsilon}d\sigma-\int_{U}(\Delta v\zeta_{\varepsilon}\psi)\right|\leq C\int_{U_{\varepsilon}}|\psi|.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and using the fact that
\psi\in L^{1}(U)
, we conclude from the monotone convergence theorem and the dominated convergence theorem that

\int_{U}vd\sigma=\int_{U}\Delta v\psi,

which is precisely (
3.2
). This proves (i).

Assume now that
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
with respect to
U
and
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
.
For each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, by approximation, one can test (
2.2
) against
{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot-x)
, which readily yields
v(x)\geq 0
. By Itô’s formula, for any
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{U})
, and any
\tau\leq\tau^{U}
such that (
2.3
) holds,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v\xi=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\nu-\mu)\Delta^{-1}\xi=\mathbb{E}[\Delta^{-1}\xi(W_{\tau})-\Delta^{-1}\xi(W_{0})]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\xi(W_{s})ds\right]=0.

This implies that
v\equiv 0
a.e. outside
\overline{U}
. Since
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
, for any
x\notin U
and any ball
B_{r}(x)
, we must have
B_{r}(x)\setminus\overline{U}\neq\emptyset
. Since
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{U}
is open, we have
|B_{r}(x)\setminus\overline{U}|>0
, and since
v=0
a.e. outside
\overline{U}
, we infer that
x\in\overline{\{v=0\}}
. But
v
is continuous, so it follows that
v(x)=0
, which shows that
v\equiv 0
outside
U
. We may then apply part (i) to deduce (
3.2
).
∎

We finish this section by characterizing the subharmonic order in
U
.

See Remark
6.4
for a counterexample showing that the equivalence can be false if the mild condition
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
is removed.

Proposition 3.3

(Relative subharmonic order characterization)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be as in Definition
2.2
, and assume in addition that
\mu,\nu\equiv 0
outside of a bounded open set
U
such that
U=\overline{U}^{\circ}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(3.5)

\int\varphi(x)d\mu(x)\leq\int\varphi(x)d\nu(x)\;\;\text{ for every subharmonic function}\;\;\varphi\in L^{1}(U),

(3.6)

\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu\quad\text{ and }\quad v:=\Delta^{-1}(\nu-\mu)\equiv 0\quad\text{outside}\quad U,

and

(3.7)

\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu\;\;\;\text{with respect to }U.

Moreover, if one removes the assumption that
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
, then (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) are still equivalent, and they both imply (
3.7
).

Proof.

Assume first that (
3.5
) holds. Since
U
is bounded, it follows that
\mathcal{N}(\cdot-x_{0})\in L^{1}(U)
for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If moreover
x_{0}\in U^{c}
, then
\mathcal{N}(x-x_{0})
is harmonic in
U
. Thus, testing (
3.5
) against
\pm\mathcal{N}(x-x_{0})
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(x-x_{0})\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(x-x_{0})\,d\nu(x),

that is,
v(x_{0})=0
. This proves that
v
vanishes outside
U
, so (
3.6
) holds.

Assume now (
3.6
). As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we see from (
2.2
) that
v\geq 0
. Then, by Lemma
3.2
(i), (
3.5
)
holds. Letting
\tau^{*}
be the minimizer of (
2.4
) we see by (
2.6
) that
\tau^{*}\leq\tau^{U}
. This proves that
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
with respect to

, and thus (

) holds. We have thus far shown that (

) and (

) are equivalent, and they both imply (

).

Finally, assume that
U=(\overline{U})^{\circ}
and (
3.7
) holds. Then, (
3.5
) follows from Lemma
3.2
(ii).

∎

4.
Programmed fractal freezing

Whenever
\eta
is a weak solution to (
1.1
), we recall that the freezing time
s:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is defined by (
1.4
). The freezing set at each time can then be defined as

(4.1)

F_{t}=\{x\in U:s(x)=t\}\quad t\in[0,\infty).

While
F_{0}
can only be a measure zero set (see Definition
2.1
), it can act as a barrier for Brownian particles that affects the pointwise dynamics of ice in significant ways, and is thus worth our attention. We will first see in Section
4.1
that, for
arbitrary initial data
,
F_{0}
can contain any prescribed closed null set. In Section
4.2
, we take advantage of this observation, together with a programmed gluing result (Proposition
4.10
) to obtain solutions for which
F_{t}
includes any prescribed null set at any positive time
t>0
, and prove Theorem
1.2
.

4.1.
Existence result and the geometry of initial freezing

In this section, we study the generic nature of the set
F_{0}
given by (
4.1
). We begin by obtaining an existence result for rough domains with initial data below the critical supercooling threshold, which removes the Lipschitz assumption on
U
from
[
10
, Thm. 1.2]
. Roughly speaking, the main observation is that, by Proposition
2.5
, there is a correspondence between solutions to (
1.1
) and measurable sets
\Sigma\subset U
such that
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\chi_{\Sigma}
with respect to
U
.

Proposition 4.1

(Existence for the supercooled Stefan problem in bounded domains)
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded open set. Assume that
0\leq\mu\leq 1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
and
U=\{\mu>0\}
.

Then there exists a weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
).

Proof.

By Remark
2.6
and Proposition
3.3
, it is enough to show that there exists a measurable set
\Sigma\subset U
such that the pair
(\mu,\chi_{\Sigma})
satisfies (
3.5
).
Given that
U
is open, we may write

U=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\overline{Q_{i}},

where the
Q_{i}
are open, disjoint dyadic cubes. Since, for each
i
,
Q_{i}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain and
\mu\chi_{Q_{i}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(Q_{i})
, Proposition
2.5
(b) implies that there exist measurable sets
\Sigma_{i}\subset Q_{i}
such that

(4.2)

\mu|_{Q_{i}}\leq_{\text{SH}}\chi_{\Sigma_{i}}

in the subharmonic order with respect to
Q_{i}
. Let
\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{i}
, and let
\nu=\chi_{\Sigma}
. Then, for any subharmonic function
\phi\in L^{1}(U)
, we have, by (
4.2
) and Proposition
3.3
, that

\int_{U}\phi d\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{Q_{i}}\phi d\mu\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{\Sigma_{i}}\phi=\int_{U}\phi d\nu,

which proves that
(\mu,\chi_{\Sigma})
satisfy (
3.5
). ∎

Remark 4.2
.

While this is not needed for our purposes, the conclusion of Proposition
4.1
holds for general
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
such that
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
(without assuming that
\mu\leq 1-\delta
), with a refined version of the same proof, if one either: (i) assumes that
U=\overline{U}^{\circ}
or (ii) replaces the assumption
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
by the slightly stronger condition that the pair
(\mu,\nu)
in Definition
1.1
satisfy (
3.5
). This technicality arises due to the non-equivalence of (
3.5
)–(
3.7
) for “punctured” domains.

Next, we show that the closed null set
F_{0}
of points that freeze at
t=0
can be arbitrarily irregular, in the sense that, for any
\mu
, it can contain any given closed null set (and in particular, any closed fractal, including those of dimension
s\in(d-1,d]
).

Proposition 4.3

(Initial freezing can contain any closed null set)
.

Let
U,\mu
be as given in Proposition
4.1
, and let
F\subset U
be any closed set of measure zero. Then there exists a weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) such that
F\subset F_{0}
, where
F_{0}
is the set of initial freezing of
\eta
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{U}=U\backslash F
. Then, by Proposition
4.1
, there exists a weak solution
\eta
to (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu\chi_{\tilde{U}}
. By Proposition
2.5
and Definition
2.1
, we have, for the associated freezing time
s
,

(4.3)

\{s>0\}=\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\{\eta(t,\cdot)>0\}\subset\tilde{U},\;\;|\tilde{U}\cap\{s=0\}|=0.

Since
|U\backslash\tilde{U}|=0
,
\eta
is also a weak solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu
, and, by (
4.3
),
F\subset F_{0}
.
∎

The next example shows that the freezing time
s(x)
may be discontinuous in the interior of
U
, and nucleation (i.e. spontaneous freezing away from the ice boundary) can occur at the initial time. Indeed, in the following construction, there exists
x_{0}\in U
that freezes at
t=0
, and
x_{0}
may be approximated by entire open annuli of points that
never
freeze. The proof will be postponed to Section
6.3
, since the construction involves radial maximal solutions, which have not yet been discussed. This proposition will not be used in the proof of any result.

Proposition 4.4

(Initial nucleation and waiting time)
.

Let
U=B_{1}
and assume that
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
is radial, with
\mu\leq 1-\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Then there exists a solution to (
1.1
) with freezing time
s
such that

s(0)=0,\quad B_{r^{(2)}_{n}}\backslash\overline{B}_{r_{n}^{(1)}}\subset\{s=\infty\},

where

0<r^{(1)}_{n}<r_{n}^{(2)}<1,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}r^{(1)}_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}r^{(2)}_{n}=0.

4.2.
Markovian gluing and programmed freezing at positive times

We now show how to construct weak solutions to (
1.1
) with a prescribed
F_{t}
at any positive time
t>0
by using the Markov property of Brownian motion. Let us first recall the definition of the hitting time.

Definition 4.5

(Hitting time)
.

Let
R\subset[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a measurable set and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process.
The
first hitting time
of
(t,X_{t})
to the set
R
is defined by
\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0:(t,X_{t})\in R\}
. For brevity, we also say that
\tau
is the first hitting time of
X_{t}
to
R
.

We require the following result from
[
5
, Rem. 6.19]
, which implies that the optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu,\nu)
is characterized as the unique admissible stopping time that is also the first hitting time to a closed, nondecreasing set.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
0\leq\mu,\nu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be compactly supported, with
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu
. Suppose that there exists
s^{*}\in\operatorname{LSC}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};[0,\infty])
such that the first hitting time

\tau^{*}
of

(t,W_{t})
to
R^{*}:=\{(t,x):t\geq s^{*}(x)\}

satisfies the admissibility condition
W_{0}\sim\mu
,
W_{\tau^{*}}\sim\nu
,
\mathbb{E}[(\tau^{*})^{2}]<\infty
.
Then
\tau^{*}
is the unique optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu,\nu)
.

We begin with a useful lemma, which is an immediate consequence of (
2.6
) combined with the closedness and monotonicity of the barrier set
\{(t,x):s(x)\leq t\}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be as given in Proposition
4.6
, and let
\tau^{*}
be the optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu,\nu)
, and let
s(x)
be the associated freezing time. Define the active region at time
t
as

(4.4)

U_{\mu,\nu}(t):=\{x:s(x)>t\}.

Then
\{\tau^{*}>t\}=\{W_{t\wedge\tau^{*}}\in U_{\mu,\nu}(t)\}
a.s.

Note from (
1.4
) and Lemma
2.7
one has that
U_{\mu,\nu}(t)=\{x:w(t,x)>0\}
where
w
is the associated potential variable.

We are now ready to show the main technical result of this section, a
Markovian gluing principle
that will later yield the generic non-uniqueness result of Theorem
1.2
. It states that one can concatenate two optimal stopping times associated with two different Skorokhod problems, and the resulting stopping time is optimal for the corresponding glued problem. The key step below will be to show that, if
R_{i}
denote the barriers associated with these Skorokhod problems, then as we will see below, the concatenated stopping time is the hitting time of the glued barrier (
4.11
). By Proposition
4.6
, it therefore suffices to verify that the glued barrier is closed and nondecreasing in time to conclude that this hitting time is optimal for the glued problem. In the context of the Stefan problem,
\nu_{0}
below will correspond to the characteristic function of the transition zone of the first solution. Meanwhile,
\nu_{1}
will be the characteristic function of the transition zone of the second solution combined with the region that has already frozen, at the gluing time, under the first solution
(see Proposition
4.10
).

Proposition 4.8

(Markovian gluing principle)
.

Let
0\leq\mu_{0},\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be compactly supported, with
\mu_{0}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu_{0}
, let
\tau_{0}
be the optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
with respect to the Brownian motion
W_{t}
.

For
t\geq 0
let
\mu_{t}
be the law of
W_{t\wedge\tau_{0}}
, and assume that there exists
t_{1}>0
such that

(4.5)

\mu_{t_{1}}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu_{1},

with the property

(4.6)

U_{\mu_{t_{1}},\nu_{1}}(0)\subset U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1}).

Let
\tau_{1}
be the optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu_{t_{1}},\nu_{1})
with respect to the Brownian motion
\tilde{W}_{t}:=W_{t+(t_{1}\wedge\tau_{0})}
. Then the stopping time

(4.7)

\tau:=\tau_{0}\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}+(t_{1}+\tau_{1})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}

optimizes
\mathcal{Q}(\mu_{0},\nu_{1})
.

Proof.

Note first that, since
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
have bounded densities,
\mu_{t_{1}}
does as well. Moreover, we trivially have
\mu_{t_{1}}\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu_{0}
, and thus testing (
2.2
) against convex functions implies that
\mu_{t}
is supported in the convex hull of
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{0})
. Additionally, the strong Markov property implies that
\tilde{W}_{t}
is a Brownian motion with initial distribution
\mu_{t_{1}}
, so
\tau_{1}
is well-defined.

Since
W_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\tau^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\tau_{0}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(t_{1}+\tau_{1})^{2}]<\infty,
Proposition
4.6
implies that, to show that
\tau
is optimal, it suffices to show that
W_{\tau}\sim\nu_{1}
and that
\tau
is the first hitting time of
(t,W_{t})
to a closed and nondecreasing in time set.

First we will show that
W_{\tau}\sim\nu_{1}
. For any continuous and bounded function
f(x)
, we have

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}[f(W_{\tau})]=\mathbb{E}[f(W_{\tau_{0}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}]+\mathbb{E}[f(W_{t_{1}+\tau_{1}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}]\\
=\mathbb{E}[f(W_{\tau_{0}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}]+\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}].

The second term in the right hand side may be written as

(4.9)

\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}]=\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})]-\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}].

Now from (
4.5
), (
4.6
), and (
2.6
),
one has
\tau_{1}\leq\tau^{U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})}
(the exit time from
U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})
). Hence, Lemma
4.7
implies that
\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}=\{\tilde{W}_{0}\notin U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})\}\subset\{\tau_{1}=0\}
a.s. In particular, in view of (
4.9
),

(4.10)

\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}]=\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})]-\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{0})\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}].

Therefore, we deduce from (
4.10
), (
4.8
), and
\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}}\sim\nu_{1}
that

\mathbb{E}[f(W_{\tau})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{W}_{\tau_{1}})]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)d\nu_{1}(x),

that is,
W_{\tau}\sim\nu_{1}
. Applying Proposition
3.3
to a large ball containing
\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{1})
, we infer that
\mu_{0}\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu_{1}
.

It remains to show that
\tau
is the first hitting time of
(t,W_{t})
to a nondecreasing in time closed barrier. For this purpose, let
R_{i}
be optimal barriers for
\tau_{i}
given by (
2.6
), so that
\tau_{0}
is the first hitting time of
(t,W_{t})
to
R_{0}
, and
\tau_{1}
is the first hitting time of
(t,\tilde{W}_{t})
to
R_{1}
. We define the shifted barrier

\tilde{R}_{1}:=\{(t+t_{1},x):(t,x)\in R_{1}\},

and the glued barrier

(4.11)

R:=(R_{0}\cap([0,t_{1}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))\cup(\tilde{R}_{1}\cap([t_{1},\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

Figure 1.
Illustration of the gluing procedure for stopping times at
t_{1}
.
The ordering of the barriers at time
t_{1}
implies that the glued barrier is closed and is nondecreasing in time.

We first claim that
\tau
is the first hitting time of
W_{t}
to
R
. For a sample
\omega
such that
\tau_{0}(\omega)\leq t_{1}
,
\tau(\omega)=\tau_{0}(\omega)\leq t_{1}
is the first hitting time of
W_{t}
to
R_{0}
, and thus to
R
.

If instead, a sample path satisfies
\tau_{0}(\omega)>t_{1}
, then
\tau(\omega)=t_{1}+\tau_{1}(\omega)
. From
\tau_{0}(\omega)>t_{1}
, we have
(t,W_{t}(\omega))\notin R_{0}
for
t\leq t_{1}
. In addition, along this sample path,
\tilde{W}_{t}(\omega)=W_{t+t_{1}}(\omega)
, so, by definition of
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{1}(\omega)
is the first hitting time of
(t,W_{t+t_{1}})
to
R_{1}
. Hence,
\tau(\omega)=t_{1}+\tau_{1}(\omega)
is the first hitting time of
W_{t}
to
\tilde{R}_{1}\cap([t_{1},\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and thus to
R
.

In summary, for any sample path, we have verified that
\tau
is the first hitting time of
W_{t}
to
R
.

Now, recalling the characterization (
2.6
) for the barriers
R_{i}
, (
4.6
) implies that

R_{0}\cap\{t=t_{1}\}\subset\tilde{R}_{1}\cap\{t=t_{1}\}
. This inclusion, along with the nondecreasing in time property of both
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
implies that
R
is nondecreasing in time. We conclude by observing that
R
is closed, since it is the union of two closed sets. ∎

Corollary 4.9

(Programmed gluing of active particle distributions)
.

Under the assumptions and notation of Proposition
4.8
,

(4.12)

\eta(t,x):=\begin{cases}\eta_{0}(t,x)&t\in(0,t_{1}),\\[3.00003pt]
\eta_{1}(t-t_{1},x)&t\geq t_{1},\end{cases}

is the active particle distribution associated to
(\mu_{0},\nu_{1})
, where
\eta_{0}
and
\eta_{1}
are the active particle distributions associated to
(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
and
(\mu_{t_{1}},\nu_{1})
, respectively.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.8
, the optimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\mu_{0},\nu_{1})
is given by
\tau
from (
4.7
).
Now for any
g\in C_{b}([0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}+\tau_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}\int_{t_{1}}^{\tau_{1}+t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}\wedge t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}\int_{t_{1}}^{\tau_{1}+t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right].

Recalling that
\tilde{W}_{t}=W_{t+(t_{1}\wedge\tau_{0})}
, this yields

(4.13)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}\wedge t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}>t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}g(s+t_{1},\tilde{W}_{s})ds\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}\wedge t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}g(s+t_{1},\tilde{W}_{s})ds\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}g(s+t_{1},\tilde{W}_{s})ds\right].

By Lemma
4.7
, we have

(4.14)

\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}=\{W_{t_{1}\wedge\tau_{0}}\notin U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})\}=\{\tilde{W}_{0}\notin U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})\}\quad\text{a.s.}

On the other hand, from (
4.5
), (
4.6
), and (
2.6
),
we get
\tau_{1}\leq\tau^{U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})}
(the exit time from
U_{\mu_{0},\nu_{0}}(t_{1})
). In view of (
4.14
), this implies that
\tau_{1}=0
a.s. on
\{\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}\}
.
Hence, recalling Definition
2.4
, we deduce from (
4.13
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}\wedge t_{1}}g(s,W_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}g(s+t_{1},\tilde{W}_{s})ds\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}g(s,W_{s})\chi_{\{s\leq t_{1}\}}ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}g(s+t_{1},\tilde{W}_{s})ds\right]=\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g(s,x)\eta_{0}(s,x)dsdx\\
+\int_{t_{1}}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g(s,x)\eta_{1}(s-t_{1},x)dsdx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s,x)\eta(s,x)dsdx.

∎

We see next that this programmed gluing of the active particle distributions implies a gluing of weak solutions of (
1.1
). Recall that the freezing time
s
for a solution
\eta
of (
1.1
) has been given in (
1.4
), with
\{s\leq t\}
describing the frozen set by time
t
.

For the proof, we introduce the following notation. If
(\eta,\rho)
denotes the Eulerian variables associated to
(\mu,\nu,\tau)
(see Definition
2.4
), we disintegrate
\rho

with respect to its spatial marginal
\nu
, interpreting
\rho(ds,x)
as
\nu(x)
times the
conditional law of
\tau
given
W_{\tau}=x
:

(4.15)

\int_{0}^{t}\rho(ds,x):=\nu(x)\,\mathbb{P}[\tau\leq t\,|\,W_{\tau}=x].

Also, by decomposing
\mu_{t}
from Proposition
4.8
into active particles plus frozen particles (see the arguments of
[
29
, Thm. 4.15]
or
[
11
, Lem. 4.11]
), we may write

(4.16)

\mu_{t}(x)=\eta(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\rho(ds,x).

Proposition 4.10

(Programmed gluing for the Stefan problem)
.

Let
U
be a bounded open set, and let
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
with
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
. Let
\eta_{0}
solve (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu
. For any
t_{1}>0
, let
\eta_{1}
solve (
1.1
) with initial data
\eta_{0}(t_{1},\cdot)
. If the freezing times
s_{i}
of
\eta_{i}
satisfy

(4.17)

\{x:s_{1}(x)>0\}\subset\{x:s_{0}(x)>t_{1}\},

then

(4.18)

\eta(t,x):=\begin{cases}\eta_{0}(t,x)&t\in(0,t_{1})\\[3.00003pt]
\eta_{1}(t-t_{1},x)&t\geq t_{1}\end{cases}

is a weak solution of (
1.1
).

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote
\mu_{0}:=\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}_{1}(\cdot):=\eta_{0}(t_{1},\cdot)
. Let
\nu_{0}:=\chi_{\Sigma_{0}}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{1}:=\chi_{\Sigma_{1}}
, where
\Sigma_{i}:=\{0<s_{i}(x)<\infty\}
. By Proposition
2.5
(d),

(4.19)

\mu_{0}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu_{0}\text{ and }\tilde{\mu}_{1}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\tilde{\nu}_{1}.

Then
\eta_{0}
and
\eta_{1}
are, respectively, the active particle distributions associated to
(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
and
(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{1})

[
29
, Thm. 5.6]
. Let
\rho_{0}
denote the stopped particle distribution corresponding to
(\mu_{0},\chi_{\Sigma_{0}})
.
Let
\tau_{0}
and
\mu_{t_{1}}
be as in Proposition
4.8
, and define

(4.20)

\nu_{1}:=\tilde{\nu}_{1}+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\rho_{0}(ds,x).

By (
4.16
), we have
\mu_{t_{1}}=\tilde{\mu}_{1}+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\rho_{0}(ds,x).
Thus, in view of (
4.19
),
\mu_{t_{1}}
satisfies
\mu_{t_{1}}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu_{1}
. Additionally, (
4.6
) is satisfied due to (
4.17
). Thus, by Corollary
4.9
,
\mu_{0}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu_{1}
, and
\eta
is the active particle distribution associated to
(\mu_{0},\nu_{1}).

First, we check that
\nu_{1}\in\{0,1\}
. Indeed, as
\rho_{0}\sim(\tau_{0},W_{\tau_{0}})
and
W_{\tau_{0}}\sim\chi_{\Sigma_{0}}
, we have from (
4.15
) that
\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\rho_{0}(ds,x)=\chi_{\Sigma_{0}}(x)\cdot\mathbb{P}[\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}|W_{\tau_{0}}=x].
Now by (
2.6
) and using that
\{s_{0}\leq t\}
is closed and nondecreasing in time, we have that
\mathbb{P}[\tau_{0}\leq t_{1}|W_{\tau_{0}}=x]=\chi_{\{s_{0}(x)\leq t_{1}\}},
and thus

(4.21)

\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\rho_{0}(ds,x)=\chi_{\Sigma_{0}\cap\{s_{0}(x)\leq t_{1}\}}.

From (
4.17
), we have
\Sigma_{1}\subset\{s_{0}(x)>t_{1}\}
, so (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) yield
\nu_{1}\in\{0,1\}.

Next, we check that
\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{1}-\mu_{0})(x)=0
for
x\notin U
. By Definition
2.1
,
\eta_{0}
vanishes outside
(0,\infty)\times U
, so from (
4.17
),
\eta_{1}
also vanishes outside
(0,\infty)\times U
. Therefore, from (
4.18
),
\eta
vanishes outside
(0,\infty)\times U
. We then infer from Lemma
2.7
that if
w_{i}
is the associated potential variable for
\eta_{i}
, then
w_{i}(t,x)=0
for
x\notin U
. Hence, for any
x\notin U

\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{1}-\mu_{0})(x)=\underbrace{\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{1}-\mu_{t_{1}})(x)}_{w_{1}(0,x)}+\underbrace{\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{0}-\mu_{0})(x)}_{w_{0}(0,x)}+\underbrace{\Delta^{-1}(\mu_{t_{1}}-\nu_{0})(x)}_{-w_{0}(t_{1},x)}=0.

We thus conclude from Proposition
2.5
and Remark
2.6
that
\eta
is a weak solution of (
1.1
). ∎

Finally, we use the above gluing property to extend Proposition
4.3
for positive times, thereby proving the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Part (i) of the statement is Proposition
4.3
. For (ii), note that since
(\chi_{\{\eta_{0}>0\}})_{t}\leq 0
,
\eta_{0}
is subcaloric. Thus, if
t_{1}>0
is sufficiently large, then
\eta_{0}(t_{1})\leq 1-\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Set
U_{1}=\{x:\eta_{0}(t_{1},\cdot)>0\}=\{x:s_{0}(x)>t_{1}\}
, where
s_{0}
is the freezing time of
\eta_{0}
, and let
F\subset U_{1}
.

Proposition
4.3
then yields a solution
\eta_{1}
to (
1.1
) on
(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with initial data
\eta_{0}(t_{1},\cdot)
and transition zone
\Sigma_{1}
, such that its freezing time
s_{1}
satisfies

(4.22)

F\subset\{x:s_{1}(x)=0\}

Since
\{x:s_{1}(x)>0\}\subset\{x:\eta_{0}(t_{1},x)>0\}=U_{1}=\{x:s_{0}(x)>t_{1}\}
, Proposition
4.10
implies that the glued function
\eta
defined by (
4.18
) solves (
1.1
), and by (
4.22
), its freezing time
s
satisfies

F\subset\{x:s(x)=t_{1}\}.

To see that (
1.7
) may occur, it suffices to choose
F
with
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(F)=d
.
∎

5.
Transition zone and regularity of maximal solutions

In this section, we switch gears to show that maximal solutions enjoy substantial regularity properties. Our main goal will be to analyze the fine structure of the transition zone to prove Theorem
1.3
. We then explain (Corollary
5.4
), in light of the local results of
[
15
]
, how our study of the transition zone yields a thorough regularity theory for maximal solutions, and precise dimension estimates on the singular set. In particular, we show that maximal solutions do not generate fractal freezing, except at a Hausdorff zero-dimensional set of times, and only if
|\{\mu>1\}|>0
. Roughly speaking, this says that, while certain maximal solutions could potentially exhibit the irregularities featured in Theorem
1.2
, this cannot happen in the subcritical case
\mu\leq 1
, and would be a worst case scenario in general. We then show by example that, even for maximal solutions, the set
F_{0}
of initial freezing can be arbitrarily irregular.

We recall that the transition zone is the set

(5.1)

\Sigma=\{x\in U:0<s(x)<\infty\}.

Since this section is dedicated to analyzing the fine structure of
\Sigma
, we will be careful to avoid identifying
\Sigma
with sets that agree on a set of measure zero. Throughout,
U
will be a Lipschitz domain, and we will work with a fixed choice for the superharmonic weight
u
.

The dual formulation of
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
plays a central role in the present section. Let
\nu^{*}
be the optimal target measure for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
. By
[
10
, Thm. 6.5]
,
\nu^{*}=\chi_{\Sigma}
, and

(5.2)

|\Sigma\Delta\{\phi<0\}|=0,

with
(\phi,\varphi)
being any maximizer of the dual problem

(5.3)

\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)=\sup_{(\phi,\varphi)\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\int\phi(y)dy-\int\varphi(x)\mu(dx)\right],

\mathcal{F}:=\{(\phi,\varphi)\in L^{1}(U):\phi\leq 0,\varphi\geq\phi-u,\;\Delta\varphi\leq 0\}

where the inequality
\Delta\varphi\leq 0
is interpreted in the distributional sense. Part of our analysis will consist of refining the rough statement (
5.2
), to give a more precise characterization of
\Sigma
in terms of the optimizer of the dual problem. We begin by reducing the dual variables.

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, we have

(5.4)

\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)=-\inf_{\psi\in L^{1}(U),\Delta\psi\geq 0}\left\{\int(\psi-u)^{+}-\int\psi\mu\right\}\\
=-\inf_{\psi\in L^{1}(U),\;\psi\geq 0,\Delta\psi\geq 0}\left\{\int(\psi-u)^{+}-\int\psi\mu\right\},

and
\psi\geq 0
is an optimizer of (
5.4
) if and only if the pair
(\min(-\psi+u,0),-\psi)
is an optimizer of (
5.3
). Moreover, if
\Sigma
is the transition zone, then

(5.5)

|\Sigma\Delta\{\psi>u\}|=0.

Proof.

Let
(\phi,\varphi)
be any optimizer of (
5.3
). Replacing
\phi
with
\min(\varphi+u,0)
preserves admissibility, and only increases the value of the objective. Moreover, replacing, in two steps, first
\varphi
with
\min(\varphi,0)
, and then
\phi
with
\min(0,\varphi+u)
, also preserves admissibility at each step and only increases the value of the objective. This proves that

(5.6)

\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{1}(U),-\Delta\varphi\geq 0}\left\{\int\min(\varphi+u,0)-\int\varphi\mu\right\}\\
=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{1}(U),-\Delta\varphi\geq 0,\;\varphi\leq 0}\left\{\int\min(\varphi+u,0)-\int\varphi\mu\right\}.

The claim now follows by setting
\psi=-\varphi
, noting that (
5.5
) is a restatement of (
5.2
).
∎

In order to characterize the precise relationship between
\Sigma
and the optimizer, we will need the key property that
\Sigma
has no hidden “cracks” of measure zero inside
\{s>0\}
, in the precise measure-saturation sense below. We also observe that some concentration of
\Sigma
occurs at every boundary point of
U
.

Lemma 5.2

(Measure-saturation of the transition zone)
.

Let
\eta
be a weak solution to (
1.1
). The transition zone
\Sigma
is measure-saturated in the following sense. For any open set
V\subset U
the following holds:

(i)

If
|V\backslash\Sigma|=0
and
V\subset\{s>0\}
, then
V\subset\Sigma
.

(ii)

If
|V\cap\Sigma|=0
, then
V\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
and
V\cap\{s=0\}=\emptyset
.

Moreover, we have
|B_{r}(x)\cap\Sigma|>0
for any
x\in\partial U
and any
r>0
.

Proof.

Let
w(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}\eta(s,x)ds
be the potential variable. We note that, since
\eta
is subcaloric in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and compactly supported with bounded initial data, the Poincaré inequality and a standard Gronwall argument show that

\eta
decays exponentially, and, in particular,

(5.7)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\eta(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0.

Assume first that
|V\setminus\Sigma|=0
and
V\subset\{s>0\}
, and let
x_{0}\in V
. Since
|V\setminus\Sigma|=0
, for sufficiently small
r>0
such that
B_{2r}(x_{0})\subset V
,
w
satisfies

(5.8)

w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta w=-\frac{1}{2}\chi_{\{w>0\}}\quad\text{in }\;(0,\infty)\times B_{2r}(x_{0})

in the distributional sense. By the nondegeneracy property of the parabolic obstacle problem
[
7
, Lem. 5.1]
, if
w(t,x_{0})>0
, then, for some dimensional constant
c_{d}>0
,

\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times[t-r^{2},t]}w\geq c_{d}r^{2}.

By (
5.7
), we infer that there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
w(t_{0},x_{0})=0
, which proves that
s(x_{0})<\infty
, and thus
x_{0}\in\Sigma
and
V\subset\Sigma
.

Suppose now that
|V\cap\Sigma|=0
and let
x_{0}\in V
. Then, since
V
is open, we deduce as before that there exists
r>0
such that
w
solves the heat equation distributionally (and thus classically) in
(0,\infty)\times B_{2r}(x_{0})
. Since
w\geq 0
and
w(0,\cdot)>0
a.e. in
B_{2r}(x_{0})
(recall that
\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\{\eta(x,t)>0\}=U
a.e.), the strong maximum principle implies that
w(t,x_{0})>0
for all
t
, namely
x_{0}\in\{s=\infty\}
. This shows that
V\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
and
V\cap\{s=0\}=\emptyset
.

Finally, if
x\in\partial U
, then assuming by contradiction that
|B_{r}(x)\cap\Sigma|=0
for some
r>0
, we infer that
\Delta w(0,\cdot)=-\frac{1}{2}\mu\leq 0
a.e. in
B_{r}(x)
, which contradicts the strong minimum principle
[
20
, Thm. 8.19]
.
∎

We can now return to the dual problem and analyze more precisely how the dual minimizer encodes the geometry of the transition zone. In particular, the following result already shows that
\Sigma
is a.e. equal to an open set, providing a negative answer to the conjecture in
[
10
, Rem. 6.2]
.

Proposition 5.3

(Regularity of dual minimizers and structure of
\Sigma
)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, for a minimizer
\psi
of (
5.4
), its unique USC representative is harmonic in the open set
E=\{x:s(x)>0\}
. Letting
f:=(\psi-u)|_{E}
, the transition zone
\Sigma
satisfies

(5.9)

\{f>0\}\subset\Sigma\subset\{f>0\}\cup\{f=0,\nabla f=0\}.

Moreover, if
x_{0}\in\partial\Sigma
satisfies an exterior ball condition, then
x_{0}\notin\Sigma
.

Proof.

By duality
[
10
, Thm. 4.5]
, we have
\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)=\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
, that is,

-\int(\psi-u)^{+}dy+\int\psi d\mu=\int u(y)d\nu(y),

where
\nu=\chi_{\Sigma}
. In view of (
5.2
), this may be rewritten as

\int_{\Sigma}(-\psi(y)+u(y))dy+\int\psi(x)d\mu(x)=\int u(y)d\nu(y),

which means

\int\psi d\nu=\int\psi d\mu.

Letting
v=\Delta^{-1}(\nu-\mu)=w(0,\cdot)
, since
U
is a Lipschitz domain, Lemma
3.2
(ii) yields

\int_{U}(v\Delta\psi)dx=0.

Since
v\geq 0
and
\Delta\psi\geq 0
, this proves that
\psi
is harmonic in the open set
E=\{v>0\}=\{s>0\}
.

Recall that
|\{f>0\}\Delta\Sigma|=0
due to Lemma
5.1
. Note that, by definition,
\{f>0\}\subset\{s>0\}
, and the sets
\{f>0\}
and
\{f<0\}
are open since
f
is continuous in
E
. Hence we infer from Lemma
5.2
that

(5.10)

\{f>0\}\subset\Sigma\subset\{f\geq 0\}.

Suppose now that there exists
x_{0}\in\Sigma
such that
f(x_{0})=0
and
\nabla f(x_{0})\neq 0
. Then the set
\{f=0\}
is a smooth hypersurface in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
, and, in particular, the set
\{f<0\}
has an interior ball
B
at
x_{0}
. Since
x_{0}\in\Sigma\subset\{f\geq 0\}
, this ball
B
is exterior to
\Sigma
at
x_{0}
, namely
B\subset U\backslash\Sigma
and
x_{0}\in\partial B\cap\partial\Sigma
. We will show now that the exterior ball condition at
x_{0}\in\partial\Sigma
implies
x_{0}\notin\Sigma
, from which we can conclude. Note that the potential variable
w
is positive and caloric in
B\times(0,\infty)
. In particular, for each
t_{0}>0
,

(5.11)

w(t_{0},\cdot)>0,\quad-\Delta w(t_{0},\cdot)=-2w_{t}(t_{0},\cdot)\geq 0\;\;\;\;\text{ in }B.

On the other hand, if we had
w(t_{0},x_{0})=0
, then
\nabla w(t_{0},x_{0})=0
due to its
C^{1,\alpha}_{x}
regularity, which by (
5.11
) would contradict Hopf’s lemma. Thus
w(t,x_{0})>0
for all
t>0
, that is,
x_{0}\not\in\Sigma
.
∎

We may now prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The measure-saturation property is simply Lemma
5.2
. Letting
f
be defined on
E=\{s>0\}
as in Proposition
5.3
, we have
\Delta f=-\Delta u>0
in
E
, and thus

(5.12)

\{f=0,\nabla f=0\}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\{f_{x_{i}}=0,f_{x_{i}x_{i}}>0\},

with the right hand side being the union of
d
smooth hypersurfaces. Since
\{f=0,\nabla f\neq 0\}
is a smooth hypersurface, we infer that

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{f=0\})\leq d-1.

By (
5.9
), we have
\Sigma\backslash\Sigma^{\circ}\subset\partial\Sigma\cap\{s>0\}\subset\{f=0\}
, and
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\partial\Sigma\cap\{s>0\})\leq d-1
is proven. A posteriori, we infer that
|\partial\Sigma|=0
from the fact that, by Definition
2.1
,
|U\backslash\{s>0\}|=0
.

Assume now that
u
is real analytic, and let
x_{0}\in\Sigma\backslash\Sigma^{\circ}
. We note first that, by the saturation property of Lemma
5.2
, we must have
|B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\{f<0\}|>0
for every
r>0
, which implies that
x_{0}\in\overline{\{f<0\}}
. Therefore, by (
5.9
) and (
5.12
), it is enough to show that,
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}(S)\leq d-2
, where

S=\{f=0,\nabla f=0,\;f_{x_{d}x_{d}}>0\}\cap\overline{\{f<0\}}.

We fix
x_{0}\in S
, and write
x^{\prime}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})
. With no loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{0}=0
. Since
u
is analytic and
\psi
is harmonic in
\{s>0\}
,
f=\psi-u
is analytic in
\{s>0\}
.

By the Weierstrass preparation theorem
[
30
, Thm. 6.1.3]
, we may write, for some open rectangle
R\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}
centered at
0
,

f(x^{\prime},x_{d})=h(x^{\prime},x_{d})(x_{d}^{2}+b(x^{\prime})x_{d}+c(x^{\prime})),\;\;(x^{\prime},x_{d})\in R\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),

where
h
,
b
, and
c
are real analytic functions, with
h>0
(because
f_{x_{d}x_{d}}(x_{0})>0
). Let
D(x^{\prime}):=(b^{2}-4c)(x^{\prime})
on
R
, and suppose that
(y^{\prime},y_{d})\in S\cap(R\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon))
. Since
h>0
and
f=|\nabla f|=0
at
(y^{\prime},y_{d})
,
y_{d}
is a double root of the one-variable polynomial
x\mapsto x^{2}+b(y^{\prime})x+c(y^{\prime})
. That is,

D(y^{\prime})=0,\quad y_{d}=-b(y^{\prime})/2.

We therefore have

S\cap R\subset\{(x^{\prime},x_{d})\in R\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon):D(x^{\prime})=0,\;x_{d}=-b(x^{\prime})/2\}.

It is then enough to show that

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}(\{D=0\})\leq d-2.

Since
D
is a real analytic function on
R\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
, it is enough to prove that
D\not\equiv 0
. But if we had
D\equiv 0
, then

f(x^{\prime},x_{d})=h(x^{\prime},x_{d})(x_{d}+b(x^{\prime})/2)^{2}\geq 0\;\text{ for every }\;(x^{\prime},x_{d})\in R\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),

contradicting the fact that
x_{0}\in S\subset\overline{\{f<0\}}.

∎

Next, we apply the regularity results of
[
15
]
for decreasing solutions to the parabolic obstacle problem, to derive some important consequences of Theorem
1.3
for the free boundary regularity of maximal solutions, the size of their singular set, and the time frequency of high-dimensional fractal freezing.

Corollary 5.4

(Free boundary regularity and size of the singular set)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, the freezing time
s(x)
is
C^{1}
in
\Sigma^{\circ}
, and is
C^{\infty}
in the set
\Sigma^{\circ}\cap\{\nabla s\neq 0\}
. Moreover, we may write

\partial\{(t,x):\eta(t,x)>0,\;s(x)>0\}=R\dot{\cup}S,

where:

(i)

R\subset(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the set of regular points, is relatively open in
\partial\{\eta>0\}
. The free boundary is a
C^{\infty}
hypersurface advancing with finite speed near every point of
R
.

(ii)

S\subset(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the set of singular points where
\partial\{\eta>0\}
either attains infinite speed or has a discontinuous freezing time. One has
\dim_{\operatorname{par}}S\leq d+1
and, if the generating weight
u
is analytic,
S
satisfies the sharp estimate

(5.13)

\dim_{\operatorname{par}}(S)\leq d.

Moreover, one has

(5.14)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{t:\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x:s(x)=t\})>d-1\})=0,

and, if
\mu\leq 1
, then the above set of times is empty.

Proof.

If
x_{0}\in\Sigma^{\circ}
, then, recalling (
2.5
), there exists a neighborhood
V=(t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}+r^{2})\times B_{r}(x_{0})
such that the associated potential
w
solves the parabolic obstacle problem

(5.15)

\begin{cases}w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta w=-\frac{1}{2}\chi_{\{w>0\}}\\
w\geq 0,\;w_{t}\leq 0,\;\{w>0\}=\{w_{t}<0\}\end{cases}

in
V
. The regularity result for
s
then follows from
[
15
, Thm. 1.2]
, and, by
[
15
, Thm. 1.1]
, one may write

\partial\{(t,x):\eta(t,x)>0,x\in\Sigma^{\circ}\}=R\dot{\cup}S_{1},

where
R
satisfies (i), and
S_{1}=\{(s(x),x)\in\partial\{\eta>0\}:x\in\Sigma^{\circ},\nabla s(x)=0\}
, the set of points where the boundary attains infinite speed, satisfies

\dim_{\operatorname{par}}(S_{1})\leq d.

On the other hand, let
S_{2}:=\{(t,x)\in\partial\{\eta>0\}:x\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma^{\circ}\},
be the set of free boundary points where the freezing time is discontinuous. By the definition of parabolic Hausdorff dimension, we have

\dim_{\operatorname{par}}(S_{2})\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\Sigma\backslash\Sigma^{\circ})+2.

Letting
S:=S_{1}\dot{\cup}S_{2}
, (
5.13
) follows from Theorem
1.3
.

Similarly,
[
15
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 1.3]
implies that

(5.16)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{t:\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x:t=s(x),(t,x)\in R\cup S_{1}\})>d-1\})=0,

with the above set being empty if
\mu\leq 1
. Since
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\Sigma\setminus\Sigma^{\circ})\leq d-1
, we trivially have

(5.17)

\{t:\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x:t=s(x),(t,x)\in S_{2}\})>d-1\}=\emptyset.

Hence, (
5.14
) follows from (
5.16
) and (
5.17
), as does the upgraded statement when
\mu\leq 1
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

We note that, by Theorem
1.2
, (
5.13
) is sharp for weak solutions to (
1.1
). In fact (
5.13
) is also sharp for local solutions to (
5.15
), since it is attained, for any
t_{0}>0
, by the simple example

w(t,x)=(t_{0}-t)^{+}.

See, however,
[
15
, Thm. 1.3]
.

Let us also mention that, from the point of view of optimal Skorokhod embeddings (see Section
2
), the freezing time
s
is precisely the Root‑type barrier associated with the optimal stopping time realizing
\mu\rightsquigarrow\chi_{\Sigma}
. The
C^{1}
regularity result of Corollary
5.4
may therefore be understood as a partial high-dimensional analogue of the one-dimensional continuity theorem for Root’s barrier obtained in
[
4
]
.

Note that the above theorem excludes the discussion of the initial freezing set
\{x\in U:s(x)=0\}
. In fact, the initial freezing of a maximal solution can be as pathological as wanted, as long as it is a measure zero set, as we show next (see Remark
2.10
).

Proposition 5.6

(Fractal freezing at
t=0
in the class
\mathcal{S}_{0}(U)
)
.

Let
U
be an open bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary and let
F\subset U
be a closed set with
|F|=0
. Then there exists an initial datum
\mu\in C(\overline{U})\cap\mathcal{S}_{0}(U)
with
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
such that there exists a unique weak solution
\eta
of (
1.1
). The unique solution
\eta
is also a maximal solution for any weight
u
, and if
s
denotes its freezing time, then

F\subset\{x\in U:s(x)=0\}.

Proof.

We first construct
\mu
. For
\tilde{U}:=U\setminus F
, there exist open disjoint cubes
Q_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(a^{(i)}_{j},b^{(i)}_{j})
such that

\tilde{U}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\overline{Q_{i}}
. Letting

\psi_{i}(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(x_{j}-a^{(i)}_{j})^{4}(b^{(i)}_{j}-x_{j})^{4},

we have
\psi_{i}\geq 0
with
\psi_{i}(x)=|\nabla\psi_{i}(x)|=\Delta\psi_{i}=0
on
\partial Q_{i}
. Choose
\varepsilon_{i}>0
sufficiently small such that
\varepsilon_{i}\Delta\psi_{i}<1
on
Q_{i}
, and define

\mu_{i}(x):=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon_{i}\Delta\psi_{i}(x)&\text{ if }x\in Q_{i},\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Observe that
|\{\mu_{i}=1\}|=0
by analyticity of
\psi_{i}
. If
\varphi\in L^{1}(Q_{i})
is a subharmonic function over
Q_{i}
, then

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi\mu_{i}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi-\int_{Q_{i}}\varepsilon_{i}\Delta\psi_{i}\cdot\varphi\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi\chi_{Q_{i}}.

The last inequality is justified as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, by approximating
\psi_{i}
with smooth, compactly supported functions in
Q_{i}
, and using the definition of the distributional Laplacian (together with
\psi_{i}=|\nabla\psi_{i}|=0
on
\partial Q_{i}
). By Proposition
3.3
, we infer that
\mu_{i}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\chi_{Q_{i}}
over
Q_{i}
. We then define
\mu\in C(\overline{U})
by

\mu(x):=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}(x)&\text{ if }x\in Q_{i}\\
1&\text{ if }x\in\overline{U}\setminus\tilde{U}.\end{cases}

Since
\mu_{i}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\chi_{Q_{i}}
over
Q_{i}
, Proposition
3.3
implies that
\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{Q_{i}}-\mu_{i})\equiv 0
outside
Q_{i}
. Therefore,

(5.18)

\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{U}-\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{Q_{i}}-\mu_{i})\equiv 0\text{ outside }\tilde{U}.

Proposition
2.5
and Remark
2.6
thus imply the existence of a weak solution
\eta
of (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu
and transition zone a.e. equal to
U
. By (
5.18
), the associated freezing time
s
satisfies

F\subset\{x\in U:s(x)=0\}.

We now show uniqueness. Suppose that
\tilde{\eta}
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu
. If
\tilde{\Sigma}
denotes the transition zone of
\tilde{\eta}
, then Proposition
2.5
(d) implies that
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
. In particular, as
\mu({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=|U|
, we see from
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
that
|U|=|\tilde{\Sigma}|
. But as
\tilde{\Sigma}\subset U
due to Definition
2.1
, we conclude that
\tilde{\Sigma}=U
a.e. That is
\tilde{\eta}
has the same initial data and transition zone a.e. as
\eta
, so
[
29
, Cor. 9.10]
implies that
\tilde{\eta}=\eta
.

Finally, we show that
\eta
is a maximal solution for any weight
u
. Since
\mu({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=|U|
the only admissible target measure for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
is
\nu(x)=\chi_{U}(x)
. Thus,
\nu
is the optimizer for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
, so
\eta
is the maximal solution associated to
u
.

∎

6.
Non-universality and waiting time

Our main goal in this section will be to prove Theorem
1.5
. We first show that, in the radial and one-dimensional cases, maximal solutions enjoy a universal transition zone that we characterize explicitly. We also show that with the slack condition
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
for
\delta>0
, the solutions exhibit no initial freezing.

Next, we prove that in dimensions
d\geq 2
, maximal solutions with non-radial data are not universal, as they may depend on the weight
u
. We also exhibit an additional instability phenomenon for maximal solutions, by constructing, in Proposition
6.6
, solutions that display waiting time near the initial free boundary.

6.1.
Universality of radial and one-dimensional solutions

We begin by proving universality of the primal problem
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
when
d=1
, or when both
\mu
and
u
are radial, by identifying the optimal target measure
\nu
. We note that the argument below covers the case
\delta=0
, which falls outside the scope of the results of
[
10
]
, where well-posedness of the primal problem was studied. However, the result is new even in the case
\delta>0
, since uniqueness was only known for a
fixed choice
of
u
.

Proposition 6.1

(Universality for one-dimensional or radial solutions)
.

Assume that

U=B_{1}\quad\text{or}\quad U=A_{\rho}:=\{\rho<|x|<1\},\;\;\;\rho\in(0,1)\quad\text{or}\quad U=(0,1),\;\;\;d=1.

Then for any
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(U)
and any weight
u\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
, both assumed radial if
d\geq 2
,
the unique optimal target measure for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
is

(6.1)

\nu^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}\chi_{(0,a)}(x)+\chi_{(b,1)}(x),&\text{if }U=(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
\chi_{\{\tilde{r}<|x|<1\}}(x),&\text{if }U=B_{1},\\[6.0pt]
\chi_{\{\rho<|x|<r_{1}\}}(x)+\chi_{\{r_{2}<|x|<1\}}(x),&\text{if }U=A_{\rho},\end{cases}

where
(a,b)
are from (
A.4
),

\tilde{r}
is from (
A.3
),
and
(r_{1},r_{2})
are from (
A.1
).

Proof.

We prove the case
U=A_{\rho}
; the remaining two cases follow from a similar argument.

Since
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(A_{\rho})
, the admissible set for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
is nonempty. We first show that there exists a radial competitor. Given any competitor
\nu
, we consider its symmetrization

(6.2)

T(\nu)(x):=\int_{O(d)}\nu(Ax)\,dA,

where
O(d)
is the orthogonal group equipped with the Haar probability measure. Then
T(\nu)
is radial, and since
\nu\leq\chi_{A_{\rho}},
we immediately obtain
T(\nu)(x)\leq\chi_{A_{\rho}}(x).

Next, we verify that
\mu\leq_{\mathrm{SH}}T(\nu)
on
A_{\rho}
.
Let
\varphi\in L^{1}(A_{\rho})
be subharmonic on
A_{\rho}
, and similarly consider the symmetrization
T(\varphi)
. Then, by Fubini’s theorem and the radiality of
\mu
,
T(\varphi)\in L^{1}(A_{\rho})
is subharmonic on
A_{\rho}
, and

(6.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}T(\varphi)(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\,d\mu(x)\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}T(\varphi)(x)d\nu(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi(x)T(\nu)(x)dx.

Hence, we obtain from
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu
over
A_{\rho}
and Proposition
3.3
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}T(\varphi)(x)\,d\mu(x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}T(\varphi)(x)\,d\nu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)T(\nu)(x)dx,

and thus
\mu\leq_{\mathrm{SH}}T(\nu)
on
A_{\rho}
. That is,
T(\nu)
is a radial competitor for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
.

As equality in (
3.5
) holds for harmonic functions over
A_{\rho}
, we may apply it to
\varphi(x)=1
and
\varphi(x)=\mathcal{N}(x)
to obtain that

\int_{\rho}^{1}r^{d-1}\mu(r)dr=\int_{\rho}^{1}r^{d-1}T(\nu)(r)dr\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\rho}^{1}\phi(r)\mu(r)dr=\int_{\rho}^{1}\phi(r)T(\nu)(r)dr,

where
\phi
is as in Proposition
A.1
.
Thus, Proposition
A.1
implies

(6.4)

T(\nu)\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu^{*}\text{ on }A_{\rho},

and hence
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu^{*}\text{ on }A_{\rho},
which means
\nu^{*}
is admissible for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
.
Now if
\nu_{r}
is an admissible radial target measure, then by (
6.4
),
T(\nu_{r})=\nu_{r}\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu^{*}
on
A_{\rho}
, so that

(6.5)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)d\nu^{*}(x)\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)d\nu_{r}(x),

because
u
is superharmonic on
A_{\rho}
. To upgrade this to any admissible target measure, we observe from Fubini’s theorem and the radiality of
u
that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)d\nu(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)T(\nu)(x)dx\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)d\nu^{*}(x),

where we used (
6.5
). Hence,
\nu^{*}
is an optimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
.

To obtain uniqueness, let
\tilde{\nu}
be another optimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
. Then the above arguments imply that
T(\tilde{\nu})\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\nu^{*}
, so that
v(x):=\Delta^{-1}(\nu^{*}-T(\tilde{\nu}))\geq 0
. Optimality and Lemma
3.2
then imply

0=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(x)\Delta v(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Delta u(x)\cdot v(x)dx=0.

Thus, since
\Delta u<0
and
v(x)\geq 0
, recalling that
v
is continuous, we obtain
v\equiv 0
. This implies that
\nu^{*}=T(\tilde{\nu})
. Hence,
T(\tilde{\nu})\in\{0,1\}
and, since
\tilde{\nu}\leq 1
, (
6.2
) forces
\tilde{\nu}=T(\tilde{\nu})
, and thus
\tilde{\nu}=\nu^{*}
.∎

When
\delta>0
, the above result is enough to uniquely characterize the maximal solution, and to identify the transition zone
\Sigma
a.e. We show next that, in fact,
\Sigma
can be identified exactly, and the set of initial freezing can be shown to be empty.

We first prove that,
conditional
on
\Sigma
being a.e. equal to an outer shell, the set of initial freezing is empty. For a later application, we do not assume below that
u
is radial.

Lemma 6.2

(No initial freezing in a.e. annular transition zones)
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
, with
\delta>0
,
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
, and with
\mu
radial if
d\geq 2
. Let
\Sigma
be the transition zone for the maximal solution associated to some weight
u
, and assume that
\nu^{*}=\chi_{\Sigma}
is a.e. given by one of the three cases in (
6.1
). Then the freezing time satisfies

U\cap\{s=0\}=\emptyset.

Proof.

The problem is equivalent to showing that
v:=\Delta^{-1}({\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu})
satisfies

v>0\quad\text{in}\quad U.

Assume first that the second case in (
6.1
) holds. Note that since
\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu
is a.e. equal to a radial function,
v
must be radial, so we may write
v=v(r)
, and we then have

(r^{d-1}v^{\prime}(r))^{\prime}=r^{d-1}(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu).

Integrating twice from
r
to
1
, we obtain

v(r)=\int_{r}^{1}\frac{1}{s^{d-1}}\int_{s}^{1}t^{d-1}(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)(t)dtds.

Letting
\sigma_{d-1}
be the surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
N(|x|):=\mathcal{N}(x)
(with
\Delta\mathcal{N}=\delta_{0}
), and

\phi_{r}(x):=({\mathcal{N}}(x)-N(r))_{+}
in
U
, integration by parts yields

(6.6)

v(r)=\sigma_{d-1}\int_{r}^{1}(N(s)-N(r))(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)s^{d-1}ds=\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}(N(|x|)-N(r))(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)dx\\
=\int_{B_{1}}\phi_{r}(x)(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)dx.

Since
\mathcal{N}
is subharmonic in
U=B_{1}
, so is
\phi_{r}
for every
r\in(0,1)
, with
\phi_{r}\in L^{1}(U)
. Since
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
, letting
\nu_{0}
be as in Definition
1.1
, we have, by Proposition
3.3
,

\int_{U}\phi_{r}\mu\leq\int_{U}\phi_{r}\nu_{0}\leq(1-\delta)\int_{U}\phi_{r}.

Hence, if
r\in[\tilde{r},1)
,
\chi_{\Sigma}=1
a.e. in the support of
\phi_{r}
, and (
6.6
) yields

v(r)=\int_{B_{1}}\phi_{r}(x)(1-\mu)dx\geq\delta\int_{B_{1}}\phi_{r}(x)dx>0.

On the other hand, we have
\Delta v=-\mu\leq 0
in
B_{\tilde{r}}
with
v>0
on
\partial B_{\tilde{r}}
, so the minimum principle yields
v>0
in
B_{\tilde{r}}
, completing the proof.

The third case of (
6.1
) can be argued similarly. The fact that
v>0
in the outer shell
B_{1}\setminus\overline{B}_{r_{2}}
can be shown with identical arguments. As for the inner shell
B_{r_{1}}\setminus\overline{B_{\rho}}
, one obtains again through integration by parts that

v(r)=\int_{B_{r}\setminus B_{\rho}}(N(r)-N(|x|))(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)dx=\int_{U}\varphi_{r}(x)(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)dx,

where
\varphi_{r}(x):=(N(r)-{\mathcal{N}}(x))_{+}
in
U
. The key observation here is that, since in this case
0\notin U
,
\mathcal{N}
is harmonic (instead of merely subharmonic) in
U
, which makes
-\mathcal{N}
subharmonic. Therefore,
\varphi_{r}
is subharmonic in
U
. The proof that
v>0
in
\{\rho<|x|\leq r_{1}\}
concludes as in the first case by testing
\varphi_{r}
in (
3.2
). On
\{r_{1}<|x|<r_{2}\}
we have
\Delta v=-\mu\leq 0
, and
v>0
on
\partial B_{r_{1}}\cup\partial B_{r_{2}}
. By the minimum principle, it follows that
v>0
in
\{r_{1}\leq|x|\leq r_{2}\}
, which concludes the proof.

Finally, for the first case in (
6.1
), after translation and rescaling we may assume that
U=(1/2,1)
, and after reflection, we may assume that
\mu
is even and
U=(-1,-1/2)\cup(1/2,1)
. But this is just a particular instance of the third case in (
6.1
).
∎

We now establish the key structural result about radial and one-dimensional maximal solutions, proving that their transition zone is a pure outer shell of the domain (see Figures
2
and
3
for examples of non-radial transition zones), and no freezing occurs at the initial time.

Proposition 6.3

(Transition zone and no-initial-freezing of radial solutions)
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
be radial with
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
and
\delta>0
. Let
\Sigma
be the transition zone of the unique maximal solution to (
1.1
) associated with a weight
u
. Then, under the notation and assumptions of Proposition
6.1
,

(6.7)

\Sigma=\begin{cases}{\{0<x<a}\}\cup\{b<x<1\}&\text{if}\quad U=(0,1),\\
\{\tilde{r}<|x|<1\}&\text{if}\quad U=B_{1},\\
\{\rho<|x|<r_{1}\}\cup\{r_{2}<|x|<1\}&\text{if}\quad U=\{\rho<|x|<1\}.\end{cases}

In all cases, the set of initial freezing
U\cap\{s=0\}
is empty.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1
,
\Sigma
is a.e. equal to the desired, uniquely determined open annular region, which we denote by
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Thus, by Lemma
6.2
, the set
U\cap\{s=0\}
is empty. Finally, we may upgrade the a.e. equality to exact equality as follows: by Lemma
5.2
, we have
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Sigma\subset\overline{\Sigma^{\prime}}
. Moreover, by Proposition
5.3
, no point of
\Sigma\cap\partial\Sigma
may satisfy an exterior ball condition: thus
\Sigma\subset\Sigma^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

One may now see that the three-way equivalence concluded in Proposition
3.3
is false without the assumption that
U=\overline{U}^{\circ}
. Indeed, let
d\geq 2
, let
(\mu,u)
be any radial pair of initial data with
\{\mu>0\}=B_{1}
, and let
v=\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)
, where
\Sigma
is the unique annular transition zone given by Proposition
6.3
. Since
B_{1}
and
U:=B_{1}\setminus\{0\}
have the same exit time
\tau^{U}
a.s., we have
\mu\chi_{U}\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\chi_{\Sigma}
with respect to
U
. On the other hand,
v(0)\neq 0
(since the set of initial freezing
\{x\in B_{1}:v(x)=0\}
is empty), so
v=\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu)=\Delta^{-1}(\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu\chi_{U})
does not vanish outside of
U
. Thus (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) are not equivalent for the pair
(\mu\chi_{U},\chi_{\Sigma})
.

6.2.
Non-universality of maximal solutions for dimensions
d\geq 2

Having shown that maximal solutions are universal when the data is radial or when
d=1
, we prove below that universality is false without one of these assumptions.

Figure 2.
Transition zone (yellow) of maximal solutions illustrated for
\mu=\frac{1}{2}\chi_{B_{1}}
, and weight functions
u(x,y)=2-(x^{2}+y^{2})
and
u(x,y)=2-300x^{12}y^{12}-10^{-4}(x^{2}+y^{2})
, respectively.

Proposition 6.5

(Non-universality of maximal solutions)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
U=B_{1}
. Consider a radial
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)
, with
\delta>0
and
|\{\mu=1\}|=0
. Let
\eta
be the maximal solution to (
1.1
) with weight
u
, and let
\Sigma
be its transition zone. Then the following holds:

(i)

If
u
is radial, then
\Sigma=B_{1}\backslash\overline{B_{\tilde{r}}}
for some
\tilde{r}\in(0,1).

(ii)

(Robust non-analytic counterexample)
If the restriction
u|_{\partial B_{\tilde{r}}}
is not real analytic for some
0<\tilde{r}<1
, then
\Sigma\not=B_{1}\backslash\overline{B_{\tilde{r}}}
.

(iii)

(Explicit analytic counterexample in
d=2
)
Let
d=2
,
\mu\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}\chi_{U}
, and define

(6.8)

u(x,y):=1-15x^{4}y^{4}-\varepsilon(x^{2}+y^{2}),\quad(x,y)\in U\quad\hbox{ for a sufficiently small }\varepsilon>0.

Then
u
is an admissible weight, and
\Sigma\not=B_{1}\backslash\overline{B_{\tilde{r}}}
for any
\tilde{r}\in(0,1)
.

In particular, in any dimension
d\geq 2
, maximal solutions depend on the choice of
u
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from Proposition
6.3
.
Assume now that
\Sigma=B_{1}\setminus\overline{B_{\tilde{r}}}
for some
\tilde{r}\in(0,1)
. By Lemma
6.2
,
U\cap\{s=0\}=\emptyset
. Thus, by Proposition
5.3
, any optimizer
\psi
to the dual problem (
5.4
) is harmonic in
B_{1}
, and

\overline{\Sigma}=\overline{B_{1}\setminus B_{\tilde{r}}}=\{\psi\geq u\}.

In particular,
\psi=u
on
\partial B_{\tilde{r}}
, implying that
u
is analytic on
\partial B_{\tilde{r}}
, which shows (ii) by contra-positive.

For case (iii), we note first that
u
is an admissible weight: indeed the strict superharmonicity of
u
is immediate for every
\varepsilon>0
, and the positivity for small
\varepsilon
follows because
\sup_{(x,y)\in B_{1}}x^{4}y^{4}=\frac{1}{16}.
Finally, assume by contradiction that
d=2
,
\mu\equiv\frac{1}{2}\chi_{U}
, and
u
is given by (
6.8
). Note that, since
\mu
and
\chi_{\Sigma}
must have the same mass, we have
\tilde{r}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Since
\psi
is harmonic, by uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem on the ball, this determines
\psi
uniquely on
B_{1/\sqrt{2}}
. Furthermore, by unique continuation of harmonic functions, this actually determines
\psi
uniquely on all of
B_{1}
. A posteriori, since
u
is a polynomial,
\psi
is a harmonic polynomial that can be computed exactly in polar coordinates by solving the Dirichlet problem on
B_{1/\sqrt{2}}
. One then obtains

(\psi-u)(1,0)=-(45/2048)+\varepsilon/2<0

for
\varepsilon<\frac{90}{2048}
. However, this contradicts the fact that
(1,0)\in\overline{\Sigma}=\{\psi\geq u\}
.

∎

See Figure
2
for a different polynomial example. This proposition illustrates a sort of converse phenomenon to what occurs in Theorem
1.3
: while analyticity of the weight improves the regularity of
\Sigma
, part (ii) shows that non-analyticity of
u
already forces the transition zone to deviate from the simple annular geometry. In this sense, non-analyticity of
u

by itself
can generate geometric irregularities and ill-posedness in the class of maximal solutions. The explicit example of part (iii) shows, however, that analyticity of
u
is not enough to guarantee universality (this polynomial example is adapted from a similar construction in harmonic
L^{1}
approximation theory
[
22
, Sec. 4]
).

We may now show the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By Proposition
6.5
, universality fails in dimension
d\geq 2
.

In dimension
1
, any open bounded set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
is a disjoint union of countably many bounded intervals. Hence, it suffices to establish universality on a single interval, and by translation and scaling we may reduce to the unit interval
(0,1)
. Similarly, in the radial case, it suffices to consider the case where
U
is an annulus or a ball.

By Proposition
6.1
, for any strictly superharmonic weight
u
, the optimal target measure
\nu^{*}
of
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
is independent of the choice of
u
(radial
u
if
d\geq 2
). Hence, all such weights induce the same active particle distribution, and therefore the corresponding maximal solutions coincide. ∎

6.3.
Waiting time and initial nucleation

Before discussing waiting times for maximal solutions, we first prove Proposition
4.4
, which earlier provided an example of a non-maximal solution with waiting time and initial-time nucleation.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

For each
k\geq 1
, let
A_{k}:={B_{1/k}}\backslash\overline{B}_{1/(k+1)}
. Let
\eta_{k}
be the maximal solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu|_{A_{k}}
associated to any radial weight (e.g. let
u(x):=2-|x|^{2}
). By Proposition
6.3
(applied to a rescaled solution),
\eta_{k}
is a radial function, the transition zone
\Sigma_{k}
of this solution is the union of two outer annuli in
A_{k}
, and the set of initial freezing for
\eta_{k}
is empty. By the arguments of Proposition
4.1
, it follows that there exists a solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
\mu
, given by the glued function

\eta(t,x)=\eta_{k}(t,x),\quad x\in A_{k}.

By construction, using the fact that the set
\{s=0\}
is closed, the solution
\eta
satisfies the required properties.
∎

We now show by example that maximal solutions, too, can exhibit waiting times. In fact, our example will show that this pathology can occur for smooth measures
\mu
that arise as arbitrarily small perturbations of the well-behaved radial solutions of Section
6.1
, displaying the inherent instability of the problem.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
d=2
,
\delta>0
, and
U:=B_{1}\backslash\overline{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}
. Consider a radial
\mu_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
, with
\mu_{0}\leq 1-2\delta
. For
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small, we define in polar coordinates

(6.9)

\mu(r,\theta):=\mu_{0}(r)+\delta_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{k}}\cos(k\theta)\in(0,1-\delta),\quad(r,\theta)\in(1/2,1)\times[0,2\pi).

Then
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
, and, for any weight
u
, the transition zone
\Sigma
for the maximal solution associated to
u
satisfies

(6.10)

|U_{\varepsilon}\backslash\Sigma|>0\;\;\;\text{ for every }\;\varepsilon>0.

Proof.

Note first that
\mu
is smooth by the Weierstrass M-test. Since
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\chi_{\Sigma}
, Proposition
3.3
yields, for any harmonic function
h\in C(\overline{U})
,

\int_{U}hd\mu=\int_{\Sigma}h.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
h_{k}
be the harmonic function defined in polar coordinates by

h_{k}(r,\theta):=\operatorname{Re}((re^{i\theta})^{k})=r^{k}\cos(k\theta),\quad(r,\theta)\in(1/2,1)\times[0,2\pi).

Then, by the
L^{2}
orthogonality of the sequence
\{\cos(k\theta)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
,

(6.11)

\int_{U}h_{k}d\mu=\delta_{0}e^{-\sqrt{k}}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{k+1}\cos^{2}(k\theta)d\theta dr=\frac{\delta_{0}\pi}{k+2}e^{-\sqrt{k}}(1-2^{-(k+2)}).

Assume that, for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
,
|U_{\varepsilon}\backslash\Sigma|=0
. Then
|(B_{1}\backslash B_{1-\varepsilon})\backslash\Sigma|=0
, and

\int_{\Sigma\cap(B_{1}\backslash B_{1-\varepsilon})}h_{k}=\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{k+1}\cos(k\theta)d\theta dr=0,

which implies that

(6.12)

\left|\int_{\Sigma}h_{k}\right|=\left|\int_{\Sigma\cap B_{1-\varepsilon}}h_{k}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{k+1}d\theta dr\leq\frac{2\pi}{k+2}(1-\varepsilon)^{k+2}.

We then conclude from (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) that

\delta_{0}(1-2^{-(k+2)})e^{-\sqrt{k}}\leq 2(1-\varepsilon)^{k+2}=2e^{\ln(1-\varepsilon)(k+2)}.

Since the right hand side decays exponentially, and the left hand side decays sub-exponentially, this yields a contradiction for sufficiently large
k
.
∎

Proposition
6.6
, specialized to the case where the weight
u
is radial, yields, in particular, the waiting time example stated in Proposition
1.4
.

The proof of Proposition

displays clearly how waiting time irregularity arises as a direct consequence of quantitative non-analyticity of

in the tangential direction, that is, the insufficiently fast decay of the Fourier coefficients.

A simple variant of the same argument also shows that, for any fixed

, even finite modes of the form

if

is sufficiently large (see Figure

).

7.
Stability of maximal solutions

In this section we prove Theorem
1.7
.
The main difficulty is that the primal problem
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
depends not only on
\mu
but also on its positivity set
U
. As we will see, the set-convergence condition imposed in Definition
1.6
is sharp for stability (see Remark
7.6
). We first prove an upper semicontinuity result for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
. Combined with the regularity of the dual optimizers from Section
5
, this yields stability of the transition zone. We then obtain strong
L^{p}
stability of the solution
\eta
as a consequence of the transition zone stability and the free boundary regularity of Corollary
5.4
. Throughout this section, we assume that
\delta\in(0,1)
, and that
U
is an open and bounded Lipschitz set.

7.1.
Stability of the transition zone

We begin by stating an a priori
L^{1}(U)
estimate for the dual optimizers, which follows directly from the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 5.3]
:

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(U)
and
u\in L^{1}(U)
. Then any optimizer
\psi^{*}
of
\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)
satisfies

\int_{U}|\psi^{*}(x)|dx\leq\delta^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{1}(U)}.

Next, we show a boundary estimate for continuous
\mu
that follows from the subharmonic order,

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose that
\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U)\cap C(\overline{U})
for some
\delta>0
. Then

(7.1)

\sup_{x\in\partial U}\mu\leq 1-\delta.

Proof.

Let
\nu
be as in Definition
1.1
, and let
v:=\Delta^{-1}(\nu-\mu)
. Since
\mu\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu
with respect to
U
, Proposition
3.3
implies that
v\geq 0
and
v\equiv 0
outside of
U
. Assume, for contradiction, that
\mu(x_{0})>1-\delta
for some
x_{0}\in\partial U
. By continuity of
\mu
, there exists
r>0
such that
\Delta v=\nu-\mu\leq 1-\delta-\mu<0
a.e. on
B_{r}(x_{0})\cap U
. Since
v
vanishes outside
U
, this means that
\Delta v\leq 0
a.e. in
B_{r}(x_{0})
. But
v(x_{0})=0=\min_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v
, which contradicts the strong minimum principle.
∎

Next, we prove that the primal problem is upper semicontinuous with respect to
(\mu,u)
.

Proposition 7.3

(Upper semicontinuity)
.

For
\mu,\mu_{n}
,
u_{n}
, and
u
as given in Theorem
1.7
,

(7.2)

\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(\mu_{n},u_{n}).

Proof.

We will show that
\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})
, which, by duality, is equivalent to (
7.2
). Let
\psi_{n}
be a dual optimizer for
\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})
. By Lemma
7.1
, (
1.14
), and (
1.15
) we have

(7.3)

\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{1}(U_{n})}\leq 2\delta^{-1}|U|\cdot\sup_{n}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}

for
n
sufficiently large. Because
\psi_{n}\geq 0
and is subharmonic, we have the Caccioppoli estimate

\|\nabla\psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(V)}\leq C(\operatorname{dist}(V,\partial U_{n}))\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{1}(U_{n})}\hbox{ for any }V\subset\joinrel\subset U_{n}.

On the other hand, due to (
1.14
), for any open set
V\subset\joinrel\subset U
, we have
V\subset\joinrel\subset U_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. The Caccioppoli estimate thus yields, up to a subsequence,
\psi_{n}\rightarrow\psi
in
L^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(U)
, where
\psi\geq 0
and is subharmonic. Note also that
\psi\in L^{1}(U)
due to (
7.3
).

By Lemma
7.2
and the continuity of
\mu
, there exists a compact set
K\subset U
such that
\mu<1-\delta/2
on
U\backslash K
. Due to the mode of convergence (
1.14
), we have
K\subset U_{n}
and
\mu_{n}<1
on
U_{n}\backslash K
for large
n
. Hence

(7.4)

\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})=\int_{U_{n}}-(\psi_{n}-u_{n})^{+}+\psi_{n}\mu_{n}\leq-\int_{K}(\psi_{n}-u_{n})^{+}+\psi_{n}\mu_{n}\\
+\int_{U_{n}\backslash K}u_{n}+(\mu_{n}-1)\psi_{n}\leq\int_{K}-(\psi_{n}-u_{n})^{+}+\psi_{n}\mu_{n}+\int_{U_{n}\setminus K}u_{n}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and using (
1.14
)–(
1.15
) we obtain

(7.5)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})\leq\int_{K}-(\psi-u)^{+}+\psi\mu+|U\setminus K|\cdot\sup_{n}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

By inner regularity of measurable sets and dominated convergence we conclude

(7.6)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})\leq\int_{U}-(\psi-u)^{+}+\psi\mu\leq\mathcal{D}(\mu,u).

∎

Remark 7.4
.

The proof of Proposition
7.3
shows that any subsequential limit
\psi
of the optimizers
\psi_{n}
for
\mathcal{D}(\mu_{n},u_{n})
is an optimizer for
\mathcal{D}(\mu,u)
.

Next we obtain the main stability result for the transition zone.

Proposition 7.5

(Stability of transition zones)
.

Under the notation and assumptions of Theorem
1.7
, we have
\Sigma_{n}\to\Sigma
in the sense of Definition
1.6
.

Proof.

First observe that, letting
\nu_{n}=\chi_{\Sigma_{n}}
,

\|\nu_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|\nu_{n}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|\mu_{n}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq(1-\delta)|U|.

In the equalities above, we used the assumption that
\mu_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}(U_{n})
and the fact that
\nu_{n}\in\{0,1\}
from Proposition
2.5
(c). Hence, up to extracting a subsequence,
\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\nu}
in
L^{2}(U)
for some
\tilde{\nu}
. From Proposition
7.3
,

(7.7)

\int_{U}ud\nu\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{U_{n}}u_{n}d\nu_{n}=\int_{U}ud\tilde{\nu}

By Proposition
2.5
(b), there exists a unique optimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
, so if
\tilde{\nu}
were admissible, we would obtain
\tilde{\nu}=\nu
. To show admissibility, first observe that from the weak convergence we have
0\leq\tilde{\nu}\leq\chi_{U}
, so it remains to check that
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\tilde{\nu}
over
U
. By the uniform
L^{\infty}
bounds and compact supports of
\mu_{n},\nu_{n}
, the weak convergence and the Calderón-Zygmund estimate imply that
\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{n}-\mu_{n})
converges uniformly to a limit
g(x)
, which solves

\Delta g=\tilde{\nu}-\mu\text{ on }{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad\quad\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}g(x)=0.

Hence, by Liouville’s theorem, this limit is
\Delta^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}-\mu)
. Therefore, we have the uniform limit

\Delta^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}-\mu)(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{n}-\mu_{n})(x).

By Proposition
2.5
(a) and the definition of weak solution,

\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{n}-\mu_{n})\geq 0
and
\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{n}-\mu_{n})\equiv 0
in
U_{n}^{C}
. Thus we have
\Delta^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}-\mu)\geq 0
.
Moreover, for any
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\overline{U}
and any
r>0
such that
B_{r}(x_{0})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\overline{U}
,

|\{x\in B_{r}(x_{0}):\Delta^{-1}\nu_{n}(x)-\Delta^{-1}\mu_{n}(x)\neq 0\}|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

since
|U_{n}\Delta U|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
. By uniform convergence and continuity, it follows that

\Delta^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}-\mu)\equiv 0\text{ in }U^{C}.

Since
v:=\Delta^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}-\mu)
is nonnegative, and vanishes outside
U
, we conclude from Lemma
3.2
(i) and Proposition
3.3
that
\mu\leq_{\textnormal{SH}}\tilde{\nu}
with respect to
U
, which shows that
\tilde{\nu}
is admissible for
\mathcal{P}(\mu,u)
. Hence,
\tilde{\nu}=\nu
.

Now we show the symmetric difference convergence of
\Sigma_{n}
to
\Sigma
, or equivalently the strong convergence of
\nu_{n}
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. To upgrade from weak to strong convergence, by the Hilbert space structure of
L^{2}
, it is enough to show that the
L^{2}
norms converge. The latter holds because, from the subharmonic ordering and the fact that
\nu(x),\nu_{n}(x)\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\|\nu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|\nu\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1/2}=\|\mu\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1/2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\mu_{n}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1/2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\nu_{n}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1/2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\nu_{n}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

Hence,
\nu_{n}\rightarrow\nu
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Lastly, we show that if
\overline{\Sigma}\subset W
for some open set
W
, then
\overline{\Sigma_{n}}\subset W
for sufficiently large
n
. Let
K:=\overline{U}\backslash W
, and let
\psi_{n},\psi\in L^{1}(U)
be, respectively, optimizers for (
5.4
). By the last statement in Lemma
5.2
, we have
\partial U\subset\overline{\Sigma}
, and thus
K\subset U
. Observe also that
K\cap\overline{\Sigma}=\emptyset
and therefore, by part (ii) of Lemma
5.2
,
K\subset\{s=\infty\}^{\circ}
. In particular, noting that
K
is compact,
\psi
is harmonic in a neighborhood of
K
.
By Proposition
5.3
, since
\psi-u
is smooth and strictly subharmonic in
\{s>0\}
, we must have
\{s=\infty\}^{\circ}\cap\{\psi\geq u\}=\emptyset
, and thus

(7.8)

K\subset\{\psi<u\}.

Since
K\subset\{s>0\}=\{\Delta^{-1}(\nu-\mu)>0\}\subset U
,
we have
K\subset\{\Delta^{-1}(\nu_{n}-\mu_{n})>0\}\subset U_{n}
, and
\psi_{n}
is harmonic in a fixed neighborhood of
K
for sufficiently large
n
. Recalling Remark
7.4
, and using the compactness of harmonic functions, up to a subsequence we may assume that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
uniformly in
K
and thus, for sufficiently large
n
,

(7.9)

K\subset\{\psi_{n}<u_{n}\}.

A second application of Proposition
5.3
then yields
K\subset\{s_{n}=\infty\}^{\circ}
, and thus
\overline{\Sigma_{n}}\subset W
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

By the notion of convergence in Definition

, we assumed in Proposition

that for every compact set

7.2.
Stability of the temperature

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
, it remains to show stability of the
\eta
variable. The weak convergence of the
\eta
variable is a direct consequence of the transition zone’s stability. To upgrade the weak convergence to strong convergence, we use the results of Section

\partial\{\eta>0\}
is a set of space-time measure zero, which enables upgrading the weak convergence of

.
We begin by noting some basic stability properties of the potential variable

, which follow from energy estimates and the Calderón-Zygmund estimate.

Lemma 7.7

(
L^{2}
stability of potentials)
.

Let
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\subset(L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))^{2}
be a sequence of uniformly compactly supported and bounded densities, with
\mu_{n}\leq_{\operatorname{SH}}\nu_{n}
.
Let

solve (
2.5

\mu=\mu_{n}
and
\nu=\nu_{n}.
Then

(7.10)

\|w_{n}(t)-w(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\Big(\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\|\nu_{n}-\nu\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\Big),

where
w
is the unique solution of (
2.5
). In addition, if
\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mu
and
\nu_{n}\rightarrow\nu
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then
w_{n}\to w
in
C^{1,\alpha}_{x}C_{t}^{\beta}([0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for any
0<\alpha,\beta<1
.

This strong convergence of

implies weak convergence of
\eta_{n}:

Lemma 7.8
.

For
\mu_{n},\nu_{n},\mu,\nu
given as in Lemma

, let
(\eta_{n},\rho_{n})
and
(\eta,\rho)
be respectively the Eulerian variables associated to
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
and

. Then

\eta_{n}\rightharpoonup\eta\;\;\hbox{ weakly in}\;L^{2}((0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Proof.

By Lemma
7.7
, for any
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and any fixed time

,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi(x)w_{n}(t,x)\,dx\rightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi(x)w(t,x)\,dx.

Recalling that
w_{n}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}\eta_{n}(s,x)\,ds
(Proposition

), this implies that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)\chi_{[t,\infty)}(s)\eta_{n}(s,x)\,ds\,dx\rightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)\chi_{[t,\infty)}(s)\eta(s,x)\,ds\,dx.

This convergence holds for all test functions of the form
g(s,x)=\psi(x)\chi_{[t,\infty)}(s)
. By a density argument, it follows that
\eta_{n}\rightharpoonup\eta
in
L^{2}((0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
∎

Now we upgrade the above to strong convergence:

Proposition 7.9

(Stability of the temperature)
.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(U)\cap C(\bar{U})
and
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(U_{n})\cap C(\bar{U_{n}})
satisfying (
1.14
), and
u_{n},u
satisfying (
1.15
), let
\eta_{n}
and
\eta
be respectively the maximal solutions of (

) with initial data
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
, and with weight

. Then one has, for any

,

\eta_{n}\to\eta\quad\text{in }L^{p}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

Since the

are bounded, by interpolation we may take

. We recall from (

) that

(7.11)

\int_{T}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\eta-\eta_{n}|\leq\int_{U}w(T,\cdot)+\int_{U_{n}}w_{n}(T,\cdot)\xrightarrow{T\to\infty}0\;\;\text{ uniformly in $n$}.

Note that
\eta
and
\eta_{n}
are uniformly bounded, and their supports are uniformly bounded in measure. Thus, to conclude it suffices to show

convergence in any fixed finite time interval
(t_{0},T)
,
0<t_{0}<T
. By Corollary
5.4
(recalling that
|\{x\in U:s(x)=0\}|=0

) we have

. Therefore, by inner regularity of measurable sets, for any

there exists a compact set

and Lemma

and

8.
Regularity and nucleation of radial and one-dimensional maximal solutions

In this section, we analyze the topology of the positivity set of maximal solutions in the radial and one dimensional settings. Although one-dimensional solutions have been extensively investigated, the existing
theory primarily concerns unbounded intervals, where the free boundary is constrained, by definition, to consist of a single point, avoiding any topological changes or nucleation phenomena. This theory does not apply to our setting of compactly supported initial data. In particular,
previously proposed selection principles, such as the notion of physical solutions

[
12
]
, do not cover this regime.

Building on the universality result established in Proposition
6.1
for radial and one-dimensional solutions,
we show that maximal solutions serve as a natural selection principle for compactly supported data. Indeed, the universal maximal solution minimizes nucleation: its support may only exhibit topological changes if every other weak solution does as well (Proposition
8.4
). We recall that
nucleation has been interpreted as a continuation mechanism for the evolution of solutions

[
23
]
.

Our analysis further shows that (1) nucleation does not occur when
\mu
is below the critical supercooling threshold (
\sup\mu\leq 1
),
and (2) nucleation may occur in general otherwise, as demonstrated by an
instructive example.

Because of the universality established in Proposition
6.1
, given a radial initial data
\mu
, we simply refer to the maximal solution associated with any (radial if
d\geq 2
) weight
u
as the maximal solution.

8.1.
The subcritical case:
0\leq\mu\leq 1

We show next that if
\mu
does not surpass the critical threshold
1
, the evolution of the free boundary is smooth over time. A crucial ingredient is the following monotonicity property, which will ensure that the free boundary does not nucleate or jump over time. Under the slack condition
\delta>0
(which we drop in this section), we showed already in Corollary
5.4
that even non-radial maximal solutions do not jump below the critical supercooling threshold, but it remains open whether such non-radial solutions may nucleate in dimensions
d\geq 2
.

Proposition 8.1

(No nucleation or jumps)
.

Assume that

U=B_{1}\quad\text{or}\quad U=A_{\rho}:=\{\rho<|x|<1\},\;\;\;\rho\in(0,1)\quad\text{or}\quad U=(0,1)\text{ with }d=1,

Let
0\leq\mu\leq 1
be supported on
U
, and assume
\mu
is radial if
d\geq 2
. Assume further that
\mu
is not a.e. equal to one in any neighborhood of
\partial U
. Then the (radial if
d\geq 2
) maximal solution
\eta
with initial data
\mu
exists and the freezing time
s(x)
satisfies

(8.1)

\begin{cases}\textnormal{$s(x)$ is strictly increasing on $(0,a)$ and strictly decreasing on $(b,1)$}&\textnormal{if }U=(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
\textnormal{$s(|x|)$ is strictly decreasing on $(\tilde{r},1)$}&\textnormal{if }U=B_{1},\\[6.0pt]
\textnormal{$s(|x|)$ is strictly increasing on $(\rho,r_{1})$ and strictly decreasing on $(r_{2},1)$}&\textnormal{if }U=A_{\rho}.\end{cases}

where
(a,b)
are from (
A.4
),

\tilde{r}
is from (
A.3
),
and
(r_{1},r_{2})
are from (
A.1
).
In particular, there is no initial freezing, nucleation, or jump of the free boundary, and the transition zone
\Sigma
is given by
\{\nu^{*}>0\}
with
\nu^{*}
from Proposition
6.1
.

Proof.

For concreteness, we assume
U=A_{\rho}
, with the other two cases being similar. Consider the radial function
w(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}\eta(s,x)ds
, where
\eta
is the active particle distribution associated to
(\mu,\nu^{*})
. Then from Lemma
2.7
and (
1.4
), we have

s(x)=\inf\{t>0:w(t,x)=0\}.

Thus, as
\partial_{t}w\leq 0
, it suffices to show that
\partial_{r}w(t,r)>0
on
(\rho,r_{1})
and
\partial_{r}w(t,r)<0
on
(r_{2},1)
to obtain (
8.1
).

We first show that
w(t,\cdot)
is subharmonic on
\Sigma
for every
t\geq 0
. By Proposition
2.5
, we have
\Delta w(t,x)=\chi_{\Sigma}-\mu_{t}(x)
, where
\mu_{t}(x)
is the law of
W_{t\wedge\tau^{*}}
so it suffices to show
\mu_{t}\leq 1
. For this purpose, note that, since
\mu\leq 1
and
\eta
is subcaloric,
\eta\leq 1
by the comparison principle. Thus, using (
4.15
) and (
4.21
) we get from (

) that

\mu_{t}(x)=\eta(t,x)+\chi_{\Sigma\cap\{s(x)\leq t\}}\leq\eta(t,x)+\chi_{\{\eta(t,x)=0\}}\leq 1.

Hence,
w(t,\cdot)
is subharmonic on
\Sigma
for any
t\geq 0
.

Since
w\geq 0
and
w(t,\cdot)=0
on
\partial B_{\rho}\cup\partial B_{1}
due to Proposition
3.3
, we have
0=\partial_{r}w(t,\rho)=\partial_{r}w(t,1)
, and,

(8.3)

8.2.

We now focus on nucleation in the case

The key point is that if

may no longer be monotone over the transition zone.

We first illustrate the heuristics behind our example. Fixing

, we consider a density

\mu=\chi_{(0,1/2)}+\chi_{(3/4,1)}-\varepsilon\psi^{\prime\prime}

is chosen small enough so that

Then

,

. Hence

. For small times

, a first-order in time approximation for

Since

and

Now if

hits zero in interior points of its positivity set from the past times. Since

, we therefore expect at least

\eta
nucleates.

Based on the above heuristics, we rigorously show that there exists a polynomial

for which the corresponding maximal solution with initial data

nucleates:

Proposition 8.3

(Nucleation can occur)
.

There exists a polynomial initial density
\mu
on
(0,1)
such that
|\{\mu>1\}\cap(0,1)|>0
, and for the maximal solution with initial data

where

is defined in (

The proof of Proposition

can be found in Appendix

.

Next we show that maximal solutions nucleate only in cases where nucleation is unavoidable. Namely, if the maximal solution nucleates, then so does every other weak solution.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let

be a weak solution of (

) with initial data

, where

[
