Machine learning application in the prediction of postoperative delirium among elderly patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Qing Cheng, Wenyuan Zhang, Xiangyang Yu, Shiyu Chen, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis meta-analysis systematically evaluates machine learning (ML) applications for predicting postoperative delirium (POD) among elderly patients. This study compares predictive performance across models, assesses generalizability and evaluates bias risk. The goal is to provide clinicians with evidence-based guidance for selecting and implementing relevant prediction tools.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines we searched PubMed Web of Science and the CochraneLibrary for studies published between July 17 2020, and July 17, 2025. We included studies that developed ML models to predict POD in elderly surgical patients. Model performance metrics were extracted. A random-effects model was used for meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses and risk of bias assessments were performed using PROBAST.ResultsA total of 14 studies comprising 49 prediction models were included, collectively enrolling 874,485 patients with a pooled POD incidence of 9.86%. The pooled Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) for ML models predicting POD was 0.756 (95% CI: 0.740–0.772), indicating moderate predictive performance. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 95.7%). Among individual algorithms, Logistic Regression (LR) (AUC = 0.794), XGBoost (AUC = 0.773), and LightGBM (AUC = 0.772) showed superior discriminative performance, whereas Decision Trees (DT) performed poorly (AUC = 0.641). In validation analyses, models showed better performance in internal validation (AUC = 0.760) compared with external validation (AUC = 0.745). In external validation, Support Vector Machines (SVM) achieved excellent performance (AUC = 0.808), while XGBoost maintained stable performance (AUC = 0.755). A significant negative correlation was observed between sample size and model performance in external validation (ρ = -0.613, p = 0.015).ConclusionML models demonstrate moderate predictive performance for POD among elderly patients. LR and XGBoost emerged as the top-performing algorithms, whereas DT exhibited relatively poor discriminative ability. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies. Notably, external validation revealed a significant negative correlation between sample size and model performance. Future research should enhance methodological quality, conduct rigorous external validation to confirm model generalizability, and prioritize clinical interpretability to promote clinical use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-026-04084-5.

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome [1, 2]. Its incidence ranges from 10% to 32% across different types of surgery [3–6]. POD seriously affects quality of life and imposes psychological and economic burdens [1, 2, 4, 7–12]. It is also one of the most common postoperative complications in elderly patients, affecting up to 50% of hospitalized patients aged 65 years and above [7]. As the elderly undergoing surgery rise, the long-term adverse outcomes and cognitive damage associated with POD have become pressing clinical concerns [13, 14]. Therefore, early identification and accurate prediction of POD are essential for improving outcomes in elderly surgical patients.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has gained increasing traction in predicting postoperative complications [15, 16]. ML demonstrates superior capability in processing and integrating large-scale, multi-source clinical data, including patient medical history, laboratory and imaging results, intraoperative vital signs, and medication records [17–19]. This robust data-processing ability provides a foundation for developing prediction models for POD. Moreover, ML algorithms exhibit robust generalization to unseen data, thereby enhancing predictive performance. Methodological safeguards, including regularization techniques, cross-validation, and early stopping, effectively mitigate overfitting and ensure model reliability for clinical application [18, 20].

This study systematically reviews ML-based prediction models for POD in the elderly. It evaluates predictive performance, compares algorithmic approaches, examines generalization in clinical settings, and assesses bias risks. The objective is to provide clinicians with evidence-based guidance for selecting and implementing appropriate prediction tools.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251162106). This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency [21]. In this study, literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment were performed independently by two researchers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher when necessary.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across three major electronic databases: PubMed Web of Science and the CochraneLibrary. The search covered publications from July 17 2020, to July 17, 2025. The search strategy incorporated both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords to optimize sensitivity and specificity. Search terms were categorized into three thematic clusters: (1) ML-related terms, including “Artificial Intelligence”, “Deep Learning”, “Machine Learning”, “Algorithms”, “Neural Networks”, “Computer Vision System”, “Machine Intelligence”, “Decision Trees” and “Random Forest”; (2) risk prediction model terms, including “Risk Assessment”, “Risk Management”, “Prediction”, and “Risk Stratification”; (3) postoperative delirium terms, including “Postoperative Delirium” and “Emergence Delirium”. Terms within each category were combined using the Boolean operator “OR,” while the three categories were intersected using “AND”. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study eligibility was determined based on the PICOS framework: (1) Participants (P): elderly surgical patients (aged >= 60 years); (2) Intervention/Exposure (I): studies utilizing artificial intelligence, machine learning, or deep learning-based prediction models; (3) Comparison (C): not applicable, as the focus was on model performance rather than comparative interventions; (4) Outcome (O): postoperative Delirium (POD); (5) Study Type (S): original research articles reporting relevant outcomes. We excluded reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and studies without full-text availability or extractable data. Studies conducted in non-surgical populations were also excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed in accordance with the TRIPOD-AI statement, and encompassed the following domains [22]: (1) Study and participant characteristics: country, publication year, study objective, data source, sample size, inclusion criteria, surgical type, outcome definition, diagnostic criteria for delirium, and POD incidence; (2) Model development details: feature selection methods, missing data handling, data preprocessing procedures, machine learning algorithms employed, hyperparameter tuning, validation approach, and code availability; (3) Model performance metrics: Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and other relevant performance indices. Methodological quality was assessed using the Prediction Model Study Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), which evaluates risk of bias across four domains: Participants, Predictors, Outcome, and Analysis [23]. Each domain was classified as low, high, or unclear risk based on predefined criteria.

To prioritize the evaluation of model generalizability and minimize the influence of overfitting on performance estimation, we adhered to a hierarchical approach for extracting model performance metrics. Data from external validation were prioritized. If external validation was unavailable, performance metrics from the test set in internal validation were utilized. Performance data derived from training or development sets were excluded.

Pooled analysis was conducted using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Logit-transformed AUC values were pooled and subsequently back-transformed to the original scale for presentation. Separate meta-analysis was conducted for the overall cohort and for the subgroup of studies incorporating external validation. For models represented by at least two independent studies, meta-analysis was performed using either a random-effects or fixed-effects model, contingent upon the degree of statistical heterogeneity (random-effects for I2 > 50%, fixed-effects otherwise). Forest plots were generated to visualize the pooled estimates and heterogeneity across studies.

Subgroup analysis

To explore sources of heterogeneity, pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted by:

Validation Type: internal validation vs. external validation.

Model Type [27–29]: Ensemble models (XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Light GBM, Stacking, Stochastic Gradient Boosting (SGB)) vs. Single models (Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Neural Networks (NN), Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Decision Tree (DT), Naive Bayes Classifier (NBC), Elastic-Net Penalized Logistic Regression (PLR), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Logistic Regression (LASSO-LR), Transformer-based Deep Learning (DL) Models).

Within subgroups, model selection was based on heterogeneity: random-effects models were employed when I2 exceeded 50%, and fixed-effects models were used otherwise.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s linear regression test (p < 0.05 indicating significant bias). Funnel plots were constructed to visually examine potential publication bias.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the relationship between sample size and model performance (AUC).

Heterogeneity assessment and sensitivity analysis

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the Q statistic, I2statistic, and the Tau2. I2 values were interpreted as follows: less than 50% indicated low heterogeneity, 50–75% indicated moderate heterogeneity, and greater than 75% indicated high heterogeneity.

To examine the robustness of the pooled estimates, we conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by systematically excluding each study in turn and recalculating the pooled effect size and I2value based on the remaining studies [30–32]. This approach allowed us to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall estimates.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3) with the following packages: “dplyr”, “metafor”, “ggplot2”, “ggpubr”, “tidyverse”, “grid”, and “patchwork”. Analyses were performed at both the overall dataset level and the external validation dataset level.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our search retrieved 190 articles. After removing 34 duplicates, 135 studies were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Of the remaining 21 studies assessed for eligibility, 7 were excluded following full-text review for the following reasons: not focused on postoperative patients (n = 1); model performance not evaluated using AUC (n = 1); ML models without reported AUC values (n = 1); ML used solely for feature selection (n = 1); no ML model developed (n = 1); invalid model performance (n = 1); and significant heterogeneity in predictor type (involving only cerebrospinal fluid protein, precluding meta-analysis) (n = 1). As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the 14 included studies employed 17 distinct algorithms to develop 49 prediction models, of which 15 underwent external validation. The most frequently utilized algorithms were XGBoost (n = 9 studies), RF (n = 6), GBM (n = 6), SVM (n = 5), LR (n = 4), DT (n = 3), and AdaBoost (n= 3). Regarding feature selection, studies employed diverse methodological approaches, including univariate analysis, algorithm-embedded feature importance assessment, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), LASSO-LR, multicollinearity analysis, multivariate logistic regression, stepwise regression, Boosting algorithms, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, and the Boruta algorithm combined with Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Model validation approaches included random split, cross-validation, external validation, Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV), temporal validation, and Bootstrap methods. Furthermore, 10 studies reported hyperparameter tuning strategies in detail. The specific methods included k-fold cross-validation, GridSearchCV, Bayesian optimization, Bootstrap tuning, and LOOCV. Study code was publicly available for only 2 studies.

Risk of bias assessment

The PROBAST tool was used to assess the risk of bias for the 14 included studies (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, 6 studies (42.9%) were rated as low risk of bias, 1 study (7.1%) as unclear risk, and 7 studies (50%) as high risk of bias.

Participants and Predictors: Most studies demonstrated low risk of bias in this domain, indicating relatively rigorous methodology regarding participant selection and predictor definition.

Outcome: 2 studies (14.3%) did not use standardized scales (e.g., CAM, DSM) for POD diagnosis; instead, outcomes were derived from medical records or postoperative antipsychotic medications, potentially introducing measurement bias. Furthermore, only 5 studies (35.7%) explicitly reported blinding of outcome assessors to predictor information. The absence of blinding in the remaining studies may have resulted in additional measurement bias.

Analysis: This domain exhibited the most prominent risk of bias. The main issues included: (1) Risk of overfitting: 4 studies (28.6%) were at high risk due to fewer than 10 events per variable in the development cohort or fewer than 100 POD events in the validation cohort. (2) Missing data handling: 3 studies (21.4%) did not report methods for addressing missing data, and 5 studies (35.7%) directly excluded cases with missing data. (3) Predictor selection: 3 studies (21.4%) used only univariate analysis for predictor selection, and 1 study did not clearly describe the selection method. (4) Other issues: 1 study failed to account for optimism correction in model performance metrics, and 1 study excluded patients who died after enrollment, potentially introducing selection bias.

Heterogeneity assessment and sensitivity analysis

After excluding any single study, the magnitude of change in the I2 value ranged only from -1.8% (decrease) to +0.2% (increase), with the I2 remaining in the range of substantial heterogeneity. Exclusion of the LightGBM study yielded the largest reduction in heterogeneity (I2 = 93.8%), though this value still indicated considerable heterogeneity. For the majority of studies (32 of 49), exclusion resulted in absolute change in I2 of <= 0.1%, suggesting negligible influence on overall heterogeneity.

Supplementary analysis of external validation datasets

The types and quantities of external validation models were shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. A total of 15 models were externally validated, comprising 5 XGBoost models, 2 SVM models, and 1 model each of Transformer-based DL model, RF, LightGBM, LASSO-LR, GBM, PLR, DT, and AdaBoost. The distribution of AUC values was shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. Compared with the overall dataset (Fig. 3), externally validated models exhibited a modest general reduction in discriminative performance. Notably, SVM and XGBoost maintained relatively superior performance in external validation. However, wide confidence intervals for some models indicated substantial uncertainty in generalization performance.

Overall performance: The pooled AUC was 0.745 (95% CI: 0.715–0.774), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 95.70%, Tau2 = 0.072, Q = 702.490, p < 0.001).

Performance by Algorithm: SVM demonstrated the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.808, 95% CI: 0.689–0.889, I2 = 81.0%). However, this estimate was based on only 2 models with high heterogeneity, necessitating cautious interpretation. XGBoost models exhibited slightly lower but more stable performance (AUC = 0.755, 95% CI: 0.728–0.780, I2 = 0%). Other algorithms had insufficient representation for independent meta-analysis.

Subgroup analysis by model type:

Ensemble models (9 records) yielded an AUC of 0.743 (95% CI: 0.704–0.778, I2 = 98.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 7), whereas single models (6 records) yielded an AUC of 0.753 (95% CI: 0.695–0.803, I2 = 92.8%) (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Publication bias: Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias (p = 0.060) (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Sample size and performance: Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant negative correlation between sample size and AUC (ρ = -0.613, p = 0.015), with larger sample sizes associated with modestly lower discriminative performance in external validation (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Heterogeneity assessment: Exclusion of individual studies resulted in changes to I² ranging from -6.1% to +0.6%, with values remaining in the range of substantial heterogeneity. Exclusion of the LightGBM study yielded the largest reduction (I² = 89.6%), though this still indicated considerable heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis: Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the pooled AUC varied between -0.010 and +0.007, indicating relative robustness (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized 49 ML models from 14 studies predicting POD in elderly patients. The pooled AUC of 0.756 indicates moderate discriminative performance. However, substantial heterogeneity (I² = 95.7%) and limited external validation warrant cautious interpretation. Although ML models demonstrate advantages in processing complex clinical data, their effectiveness in predicting POD in elderly patients requires further validation.

Algorithm performance differences and selection

Our meta-analysis shows significant performance variation across algorithms. LR (AUC = 0.794), XGBoost (AUC = 0.773), and LightGBM (AUC = 0.772) demonstrated superior discriminatory performance, whereas DT demonstrated limited performance (AUC = 0.641). This indicates that not all ML models perform well in predicting POD.

The superior performance of LR may be attributed to the limited sample sizes and restricted clinical predictors. Under such conditions, LR generally outperforms more complex ML models [48, 49]. Previous research indicates that LR achieves stable performance with 20–50 events per variable (EPV), whereas ML models like RF, SVM, and NN exhibit substantial performance variation and optimism bias even with EPV exceeding 200 [48]. Moreover, LR offers advantages including model parsimony and strong interpretability, with direct estimation of odds ratios. Therefore, in scenarios with limited sample size or where interpretability is paramount, LR represents a robust candidate. Our findings align with prior studies reporting comparable or superior performance of LR relative to complex ML models for POD prediction [35, 36]. Although some research suggests that ML models offer better discrimination [50–52], and we observed strong performance from models like SVM in external validation (AUC = 0.808), the current evidence is insufficient to establish significant superiority of ML over LR-based clinical prediction models [53]. Indeed, LR has demonstrated comparable performance to ML in predicting other major chronic diseases [49]. The strong performance of LR likely reflects its stability with smaller sample sizes and the predominant use of simple clinical predictors in the included studies.

This study found no significant performance difference between ensemble and single models. This finding may reflect the capacity of certain single models, such as NN and SVM, to perform well on specific datasets. Additionally, the theoretical advantages of ensemble models may not be fully realized with current data scale and quality. However, ensemble models, including RF and XGBoost, typically exhibit “black-box” characteristics with limited interpretability. The combination of multiple base learners creates collective decision-making processes that are difficult to trace and fail to provide transparent, interpretable outputs. In contrast, single models such as LR offer greater transparency and structural simplicity [54].

Model selection should therefore be guided by specific clinical environment requirements rather than algorithmic complexity alone [14, 54, 55]. For limited datasets or applications prioritizing interpretability, LR remains a strong candidate. For maximizing predictive performance with large, high-dimensional datasets, XGBoost represents a viable option despite its reduced transparency. Future research should focus on delineating the optimal application conditions for different ML models rather than conducting simplistic performance comparisons.

Key predictor variables

Beyond algorithm comparison, the choice of predictors is equally critical. However, the types and number of predictors varied considerably across the 14 included studies. Among the 14 studies, predictors such as age, ASA class, preoperative cognitive impairment, dementia, and prolonged surgery were widely used. The number of predictors ranged from 6 to 141, with some studies failing to clearly report the specific predictors included in their final models [39, 47]. This substantial heterogeneity precludes subgroup analysis or meta-analysis at the predictor level. Going forward, efforts should be made to establish a standardized core set of predictors and ensure transparent reporting, thereby facilitating clinical application and enhancing cross-study comparability. Generalization ability

Models performed better in internal (AUC = 0.760) than external validation (AUC = 0.745). External validation results also showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98.0%), indicating limited generalizability to novel populations. Furthermore, we observed a significant negative correlation between sample size and discriminative performance in external validation. This indicates that model performance estimates tend to be more conservative in larger validation cohorts. Potential explanations for this result are outlined here. Performance estimates in small-sample external validation are prone to random variation, whereas large-sample external validation provides more accurate and conservative estimates of average performance in broader populations. This pattern suggests that many current models may inadequately account for population heterogeneity during development, consequently limiting their applicability across centers or regions.

External validation is essential for assessing the generalizability of POD prediction models and determining their translational potential from research settings to clinical practice [56]. However, only 7 studies (encompassing 15 models) conducted external validation, reflecting limited external validation in this field. This paucity may compromise comprehensive evaluation of true model generalizability. Clinicians should recognize that internal validation performance may overestimate true effectiveness and prioritize tools with demonstrated stable performance in independent, multicenter datasets. Future research should enhance standardized implementation and reporting of external validation, particularly through evaluation of model robustness and clinical applicability in larger, more representative cohorts.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

PROBAST identified high-risk situations in the analysis domain, specifically regarding insufficient events per variable, inappropriate missing data handling, and suboptimal predictor selection methods. These methodological deficiencies, alongside unclear reporting, likely contribute to study heterogeneity. Additional concerns including overfitting and optimism bias were inadequately addressed in some studies. Previous research has highlighted prevalent methodological limitations in comparative studies of LR and ML, including incomplete reporting, ambiguous results, and flawed validation procedures [53, 57]. Our study corroborates these concerns. The absence of methodological transparency undermines the credibility of reported performance metrics. We recommend that clinicians prioritize models adhering to TRIPOD-AI guidelines. Future research should emphasize methodological transparency and standardized reporting to enhance reproducibility and reliability.

Sources of heterogeneity

The substantial heterogeneity (I² = 95.7%) likely originates from several factors. These include variations in study populations, surgical types, POD definitions, and model development methodologies. Moreover, differences in feature selection, missing data handling, and validation strategies further contribute, alongside the unclear information and high-risk ratings identified through PROBAST. Future research needs to improve data standardization and methodological transparency to reduce heterogeneity.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the limited number of included studies and substantial heterogeneity may affect the stability of meta-analysis estimates. Second, although our pooled analysis demonstrates moderate performance, evidence-based tool selection requires consideration of specific model characteristics. Subgroup analyses stratified by similar populations and predictors would be valuable. However, substantial cross-study heterogeneity precluded such analyses. As the field matures, future research should prioritize these stratified analyses. Third, incomplete reporting of performance metrics (e.g., sensitivity, specificity) and limited code availability among included studies increased data extraction difficulty and reduced reproducibility. Fourth, although publication bias was not statistically significant in this study, its complete exclusion cannot be assured, particularly given the limited number of external validation studies.

Practical recommendations for clinicians

Based on our findings, we offer the following practical recommendations. First, model selection should align with the clinical data patterns. Our data reveal that LR delivers reliable performance when sample size is limited or interpretability is paramount. XGBoost may offer additional predictive gains when abundant data are available. Physicians can learn to match their data volume and clinical context against the evidence to identify which model’s performance profile best fits their situation. Second, considering internal validation performance may be overoptimistic, physicians should learn to choose models with demonstrated stable performance after external validation in independent, multicenter datasets. Third, physicians should be aware of overfitting risk of ML predictive models for POD. Our data indicate that models developed from small samples or with a low EPV may have optimistic performance estimates. Physicians can learn to scrutinize the ratio of events to predictors, and maintain caution toward combinations of “limited data plus exceptional performance”. Fourth, it’s important to value transparency and reproducibility of prediction models. The data suggest that models adhering to TRIPOD-AI reporting guidelines, fully disclosing key methodological details (e.g., missing data handling, variable selection), and sharing code publicly demonstrate higher real-world reliability. Physicians can apply these specific benchmarks to filter prediction tools worthy of clinical piloting, rather than relying solely on AUC metrics for decision-making.

Conclusion

ML models show moderate performance in predicting POD among elderly patients. LR and XGBoost emerged as the top-performing algorithms. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies. Notably, external validation revealed a significant negative correlation between sample size and model performance. Future research should prioritize methodological rigor, conduct comprehensive external validation to establish generalizability, and improve clinical interpretability to facilitate practical adoption.
