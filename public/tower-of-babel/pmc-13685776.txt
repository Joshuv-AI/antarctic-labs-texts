Australian women’s perspectives on gendered vulnerability, commercial power, and resistance to the commercialization of menopause
Authors: Samantha L Thomas, Simone McCarthy, Hannah Pitt, Katrina Wood, Tina D Purnat, Sarah L White, Martha Hickey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA range of commercial actors actively shape how menopause is understood, experienced, and managed. While existing research highlights concerns about commercial influence, much less is known about how women themselves interpret, negotiate, and respond to these commercialized menopause environments. Using an online qualitative survey, this study explored the opinions of n = 362 Australian cisgender women (45–64 years) about the commercialization of menopause. Data were interpreted using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis, with three themes constructed from the data. (i) ‘Commercialization as awareness raising, knowledge and choice’—in which women viewed the growing commercial interest in menopause as a positive development and evidence that women’s experiences were being recognized. (ii) ‘Profiting from gendered vulnerability’—where women felt that the rapid commercialization of menopause created at least some level of financial or emotional risk. They questioned whether commercial interests were genuinely motivated by concern for women’s well-being, or financial gain. (iii) ‘Navigating crowded menopause information environments’—in which women stated that the sheer volume of menopause content available online and particularly through social media platforms, often contributed to feelings of confusion, information fatigue, and disengagement. Commercial attention to menopause blurs the boundaries between information and marketing and shifts responsibility for navigating risk onto individuals. A critical approach to menopause must address the commercial systems that shape what women know, what they are encouraged to celebrate or fear, what they are invited to buy, and what forms of care and support are made available to them.

Introduction

An increased public focus on menopause as a significant life transition

Almost half of the world’s population will experience the menopause transition (Hickey et al. 2024b). While menopause is most commonly experienced by cisgender women in midlife (45–64 years), it can also be experienced earlier in life by people assigned female at birth who no longer identify as women (Drysdale et al. 2026), those with primary ovarian insufficiency, or those who experience medically induced menopause following ovariectomy or cancer treatment (Hickey et al. 2024a). Menopause experiences are diverse. For some, menopause is extremely challenging, for others liberating, and for most a rather uneventful life event with few disruptions to their daily lives (Jean Hailes for Women’s Health 2023, Hickey et al. 2024b, Thomas et al. 2024b). Menopause experiences are also diverse across global contexts. Much of the existing evidence has been generated with women in high-income countries (Wood et al. 2025c, Bell and Friedman 2026), yet women in low- and middle-income countries (Islam et al. 2025) or those from different ethnic backgrounds (Prasad 2023, Endara-Mina et al. 2025) navigate menopause within very different social, cultural, economic, and health system environments. Researchers and women’s health advocates have argued that the menopause transition should be understood as a biological transition mediated by social and cultural norms (Orgad and Rottenberg 2024a, Thomas et al. 2024b, Bell and Friedman 2026). This includes recognizing that experiences are shaped not only by physiological changes but also by gendered expectations and ageism, normative parameters around labour participation and work, intergenerational caregiving responsibilities, and social relationships (Riach and Jack 2021, Rowson et al. 2023, Wood et al. 2025b, Rottenberg and Gilchrist 2026).

Until recently women’s experiences of menopause were often silenced, stereotyped, and stigmatized (Dahlgren et al. 2023, Orgad and Rottenberg 2024a, Kirby 2025, Peters et al. 2026). In recent years, menopause has become the subject of growing public attention (Orgad and Rottenberg 2024b, Thomas et al. 2024b). This has included increasing visibility in media reporting, social media conversations, workplace initiatives, policy debates, and popular culture (Steffan 2025, Thomas et al. 2025). This public visibility has occurred within rapidly changing information environments in which a diverse range of actors contribute to the framing of menopause (Thomas et al. 2024b). Healthcare professionals, advocacy organizations, media commentators, celebrities, influencers, commercial organizations, and women themselves all participate in public conversations about menopause and shaping menopause discourses (Thomas et al. 2024b). As a result, menopause is increasingly understood through multiple and sometimes competing narratives which seek to serve specific (sometimes vested) interests (Randle et al. 2024). This dynamic is arguably a key outcome of an information environment that can both construct and exploit gendered vulnerability. Researchers have identified that the meanings attached to menopause are actively constructed, contested, and negotiated within broader social, cultural, commercial and political contexts (Randle et al. 2024, Jermyn 2025, Winett and Dalingwater 2026). For those experiencing the menopause transition, this means navigating a complex landscape of information, advice, lived experiences, and commercial messages (Thomas et al. 2025, Orgad et al. 2026, Purnat et al. 2026). This raises important questions about who shapes public understandings of menopause, the type of information that is trusted, whose interests are served by particular framings of menopause, and how these framings may influence consumption behaviours (Thomas et al. 2024b, Purnat et al. 2026, Riach 2026). The Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH) focus on how commercial actors shape health and equity through a range of tactics used to promote the consumption of products and resist the regulation of products and marketing strategies that may be harmful to health (Friel et al. 2023, McCarthy et al. 2023, McNamara and Crosbie 2026). This includes how commercial actors may deliberately engineer the digital landscape, including exploiting architectures of platforms, user data, and targeted marketing online to capture the menopause market (Purnat et al. 2025).

The ‘hot flush gold rush’—the rise of the menopause marketplace

The growing visibility of menopause has coincided with its emergence as a significant commercial opportunity (Onculer and Onculer Yayalar 2025, Wood et al. 2025a). The global menopause market is projected to be worth billions of dollars in the coming decade—sometimes described as the ‘hot flush goldrush’ (Thomas and Hickey 2026). Researchers have argued that a range of commercial actors, including pharmaceutical and wellness companies, clinicians, and social media influencers, actively shape how menopause is understood, experienced, and managed. This includes influencing which experiences are given attention, whose expertise is seen as credible, and which responses, treatments, or products are presented as desirable or necessary (Schneider Dallolio et al. 2025, Takhar et al. 2025, Thomas et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a, Nickel et al. 2026). One way this occurs is through the problematization of menopause as something that requires management or intervention, while simultaneously positioning products and services (including pharmaceuticals, wellness products and services, workplace interventions, books, and podcasts) as pathways to empowerment, healthy relationships, productivity, and well-being (Krajewski 2019, Thomas et al. 2024b, Mccartan 2025, Onculer and Onculer Yayalar 2025, Schneider Dallolio et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a, Nickel et al. 2026). Concerns have been raised that commercial interests may promote products with limited evidence of effectiveness, encourage women to feel responsible for managing menopause, attribute a broad range of midlife experiences to menopause, and reinforce expectations and anxieties about ageing (Corus et al. 2025, Mccartan 2025, Schneider Dallolio et al. 2025, Orgad and Gilchrist 2026).

One example of this process is ‘menowashing’—which we define as a commercial marketing strategy whereby wellness, health, or beauty products (such as teas, cooling sprays, supplements, sleep aids, intimate care products, and weight-loss products) are rebranded and marketed as ‘solutions’ for symptoms attributed to menopause. Such marketing capitalizes on concerns associated with the menopause transition and may rely on claims that lack robust scientific evidence. Menowashing therefore illustrates how commercial actors may not only respond to existing concerns and vulnerabilities but also intensify them by expanding what is framed as a menopause-related problem and positioning consumption as part of managing menopause or ageing ‘successfully’ (Krajewski 2019, Orgad and Gilchrist 2026, Rottenberg and Gilchrist 2026). Understanding how women perceive and respond to these commercial influences is important for examining the relationships between commercial power, gendered vulnerability, health equity, and agency within contemporary menopause discourse.

Women’s engagement with and resistance to menopause information

As menopause information proliferates across digital platforms, traditional sources of trusted information (e.g. from healthcare professionals, researchers, and governments) are increasingly challenged by those seeking to profit from menopause. This occurs within an attention economy in which visibility, engagement, and emotional resonance are rewarded (Van Bavel et al. 2024, Thomas et al. 2025). These commercial actors (including those who make social media content for profit) frequently seek to cultivate authenticity, positioning themselves as trusted guides through menopause. Women may turn to commercial and peer (lived experience) sources because they feel that clinical menopause care has been inadequate or inaccessible—so the gap in care is what partially pushes women away from the health system. Commercial content may also operate through ‘click funnels’, where social media posts, stories, or apparent educational content direct users through a series of links towards commercial websites, product pages or purchases. In this way, information and advice can become part of a pathway designed to convert attention and engagement into consumption. Recent research shows that between 2005 and 2025, the proportion of Google searches that were related to commercial menopause products and services (particularly supplements and digital apps) increased by 15%–20% in the UK, Australia, and the USA, with the researchers concluding that individuals may be increasingly seeking non-clinical ‘solutions’ for menopause (Farina et al. 2026) [Noting that you cannot solve menopause, you can only treat symptoms or (sometimes) intervene to optimize long-term health].

Women may also be confused when their clinician does not endorse what they hear or see on social media platforms. Riach (2026) notes that this may contribute to the rise of ‘menocynicism’, arguing that when menopause intersects with gendered ageism, vulnerability, mistrust of institutions, and online polarization, some women may ‘turn away from mainstream health information and toward increasingly partisan communities and alternative sources of knowledge’ (p. 6). However, women may also be critical of this increased commercial influence—particularly in relation to the interests of the pharmaceutical and wellness industries and social media influencers (Wood et al. 2025a). Rather than passive consumers of information, women may actively piece together fragmented forms of knowledge, including scientific evidence, lived experience, and peer testimony, to navigate gaps in formal health care. This may include questioning commercial motives, challenging dominant framings of menopause, seeking alternative sources of knowledge, or resisting expectations that menopause should be managed primarily through consumption (Thomas et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a). Their resistance is therefore an active information practice through which women seek to make sense of menopause.

While existing research has documented the rapid expansion of menopause markets and highlighted concerns about commercial influence much less is still known about how women themselves interpret, negotiate, and respond to these commercial environments. This study aimed to explore Australian women’s perspectives on the commercialization of menopause and was guided by three research questions:

Materials and methods

Approach

This paper draws on data from a larger online qualitative survey that built on a previous programme of research which explored the public framing of menopause (Thomas et al. 2025) and the commercialization of menopause (Wood et al. 2025a). Focusing again on the specific experiences of Australian cisgender women in midlife (aged 45–64), this new study aimed to understand their opinions about societal attitudes towards women as they age; their views about menopause information, products, and services; and their opinions about how to better support women during midlife and the menopause transition. One paper has been published from this dataset focusing specifically on the intersection between menopause and midlife (Wood et al. 2026). With the exception of socio-demographic characteristics, there are no overlaps between these two papers, with the present paper focusing specifically on women’s opinions about the commercialization of menopause.

Qualitative surveys are an increasingly used research data collection tool which enable individuals to provide their experiences and opinions about a range of health and social phenomena (Braun et al. 2021). They have practical benefits for participants because they can be completed at a time, place, and online device (i.e. phone and laptop) convenient to them; they are anonymous; and they do not require direct interaction with researchers (Thomas et al. 2024a). This may be particularly beneficial for individuals who lack access to private spaces for interviews, have competing caregiving or work responsibilities, feel uncomfortable discussing sensitive topics with researchers, or prefer greater autonomy over how and when they share their experiences (Thomas et al. 2024a). For researchers, the larger sample sizes associated with the survey mean they are able to gather a ‘wide angle’ lens on an issue from a diverse range of participants (Toerien and Wilkinson 2004, Braun et al. 2021). Braun et al. (2021) argue that this is where qualitative surveys can ‘shine’ because they emphasize diversity rather than typicality which is particularly important in providing opportunities for marginalized or often-overlooked groups to participate in the research (p. 643). We took an interpretivist and reflexive ‘Big Q’ approach to the study which acknowledges researcher subjectivity and considers how researchers’ experiences may have shaped their interpretations and sense making of the data (Kidder and Fine 1987, Braun and Clarke 2025). Ethical approval was received by the low-risk Deakin University Health Human Ethics Advisory Group (2025/HE001009).

Sample and recruitment

We sought to recruit about n = 350 participants to the study, who were born female and identified as a woman, were aged between 45 and 64 years, lived in Australia, and had experience of the menopause transition (meaning they were either peri- or post-menopause). They needed to have sufficient English proficiency to complete the survey and have adequate internet access to complete the survey online. While menopause may occur outside of this age range and with those with different gender identities, we intended specifically to recruit cisgender women in midlife, as they are typically the focus of commercial menopause marketing strategies (including social media influencers).

Participants were recruited through Qualtrics, which invites individuals to participate in the research from online panel providers whose members receive a small number of points for completing surveys that can be redeemed for online purchases. Soft quotas were applied for geographical region and age. Qualtrics managed the recruitment and data collection by sending the survey link to people who broadly met the demographics of the inclusion criteria. The researchers did not have access to any identifiable information about the participants. Participants were asked to read an information sheet prepared by the researchers to determine if they would like to participate in the study and were asked to provide their consent (yes/no tick box) at the start of the survey.

Data collection

Data were collected between October and December 2025. The survey was initially piloted with 42 participants. The responses were reviewed by the research team and used to refine the clarity of questions, add questions where areas required further exploration, and removing some questions that were repetitive. Pilot data were removed before the launch of the full survey. The survey collected socio-demographic information, including age, location, area of residence, education, employment status, income bracket, relationship status, parental status, and caregiving responsibilities. It collected discrete information about whether they had ever found it confusing to navigate information about menopause or know where to go for trusted information. Because of the inability to prompt or explain concepts to participants, each question provided a broad context-setting statement about the topic of the question before the question was asked. Reflexivity statement

We recognize that our beliefs, experiences, and professional interests influenced the study design, interpretation of the data, and conclusions drawn. Our analysis is informed by our research, clinical and public health expertise in women’s health, and theoretical expertise in the social and CDoH. Most members of the research team have lived experience of the menopause transition and are exposed to commercialized menopause information through digital and social media environments, informing our awareness of the growing visibility of menopause products, services, and messaging. We also approached this research from the position that women’s own perspectives and opinions about their health should be central to decisions affecting their health and well-being. Guided by a health equity perspective, we were attentive to how commercial practices may affect women differently, particularly those experiencing vulnerability during the menopause transition.

Data interpretation

S.L.T. led the data interpretation with reflexive feedback and critical engagement from co-authors. Once all the data had been collected the data was read and re-read in a process of data familiarization by the S.L.T., S.M., and K.W. Two participants who had provided nonsensical responses to questions such as random letter responses and responses that had no relationship to the questions asked were subsequently removed from the analysis. This led to a total of n = 362 participants who were included in the final dataset for analysis. Socio-demographic data were analysed using basic descriptive statistics.

Qualitative analysis followed an iterative approach to reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2022, 2024, 2025). This involved familiarization with the data through repeated reading of participants’ responses and the generation of initial analytic observations. Given the volume and structure of qualitative survey data, responses were managed in Microsoft Excel to support systematic familiarization across the dataset. Worksheets were used to organize responses by question, record initial codes and analytic observations, and enable researchers to move iteratively between individual responses, groups of responses, and the dataset as a whole. Initial codes were developed across the dataset and used to construct preliminary themes. These themes were subsequently reviewed, refined, and renamed to ensure they were conceptually distinct, internally coherent, and grounded in the data. The final themes were then developed into a coherent analytic narrative, with participant quotations selected to illustrate key themes and concepts. Throughout the analytic process, members of the research team met regularly and provided written feedback sharing reflections, brainstorming ideas, and challenging our assumptions.

Results

Sample characteristics

A table of the overall sample characteristics has been published in Wood et al. (2026). Of the n = 362 women who completed the survey, the mean age was 55 years (range 45–64 years), two-thirds were residents in one of Australia’s three largest states of Victoria, New South Wales, and Queensland (n = 243, 67.1%), and over a third lived in regional, rural, or remote areas (n = 137, 37.8%). Over three quarters were born in Australia (n = 280, 77.3%), and 11 (n = 3.0%) identified as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. Those born outside Australia were from a range of countries including Malaysia, Vietnam, Japan, India, Germany, Italy, England/UK, Macedonia, Zimbabwe, Philippines, Niue Island, Singapore, Indonesia, Hong Kong, Greece, New Zealand, Fiji, Canada, Lebanon, Belgium, China, Argentina, Cyprus, Fiji, and Slovenia. About two-thirds of participants had some form of educational qualification outside of high school (n = 245, 67.7%), including further certificates of education, diplomas, bachelor’s degrees, or postgraduate degrees. Over half of participants were working full time (n = 111, 30.7%) or part time (n = 101, 27.9%). Three quarters had children (n = 276, 76.2%), and 214 (59.1%) stated they had caregiving responsibilities for children under the age of 18, adult children, partners, parents, other family members, or family friends. Just under a third stated they found it confusing to navigate menopause information online or to know where to go to find trusted information (n = 113, 31.2%), with two-thirds (n = 249, 68.8%) stating they did not find this confusing.

Theme 1: commercialization as awareness raising, knowledge, and choice

Some participants viewed the growing commercial interest in menopause as a positive development. They interpreted increased commercial attention as evidence that women’s experiences were finally being recognized by industries that had historically overlooked this life stage. For these women, the expansion of menopause products, services, and information from influencers reflected a broader social acknowledgement of menopause as a legitimate health and well-being issue which contributed to making menopause more visible and acceptable. Participants also expressed interest in trying menopause products, particularly when they believed claims were supported by scientific evidence and demonstrated benefits for symptom management. They suggested that companies were increasingly focused on supporting women through what was often described as a difficult life transition, and some viewed this attention as helping to create more positive narratives around menopause and ageing:‘It’s a more widely accepted natural part of ageing and companies are trying to make it into a more positive experience.’—57 years old, Victoria

‘It’s a more widely accepted natural part of ageing and companies are trying to make it into a more positive experience.’—57 years old, Victoria

‘I love to see new information (and) new companies bring out new products for menopause because I believe we as women need to have more options and care about the whole range of experiences that we might have. It’s all different but basically leading into the same thing which is menopause. I don’t believe that all the advertisements and things lead women to thinking that they have a mid-life crisis.’—45 years old, Western Australia

Positive perceptions of commercial products were closely linked to expectations of scientific credibility and effectiveness. Participants consistently emphasized that the value of commercial products in menopause value depended upon the trustworthiness of the claims being made about the products. Products were viewed unfavourably when they were perceived as not being ‘genuine’, ‘scientific’, or evidence-based:

‘Good if they actually have the interest of women at heart. Bad if it’s just snake oil.’—53 years old Victoria

Women who expressed positive views towards commercial involvement also frequently framed commercial actors as important sources of information and awareness about menopause. Rather than viewing companies solely as profiting from menopause, they still perceived them as contributing information to public knowledge about menopause and raising awareness about symptoms and available management options. They believed that such information was inherently beneficial, regardless of whether products were ultimately purchased (or effective) because overall this new interest in menopause contributed to knowledge for women. Women described themselves as active decision makers who could engage with, reject or selectively adopt commercial products and advice according to their own needs and preferences:

‘I don’t mind it’s all knowledge, you don’t have to buy it.’—51 years old, Victoria

These participants also emphasized their ability to assess information, evaluate products, and determine for themselves whether various products or services were relevant to their circumstances. Information from companies or social media influencers selling products, was understood as one component of a broader information environment that could help and support women as they navigated the menopause transition. Women who were positive about commercial interest in menopause positioned themselves as knowledgeable consumers who had control over their engagement with commercial offerings. In this way, commercialization was not only associated with the provision of products and services but also with the production and circulation of information that participants perceived could enhance their understanding of menopause and expand the range of options available to them. However, a few also admitted that despite this they did sometimes get caught up in the ‘hype’ about menopause products:

‘I believe the hype sometimes and try to do as much research as possible. If I buy something for menopause, I usually have done some reading about it.’—49 years old, Western Australia

Theme 2: profiting from gendered vulnerability

Many participants felt that the rapid commercialization of menopause created at least some level of financial or emotional risk for women, and expressed cynicism about the profit motivations of companies operating within the menopause marketplace. They questioned whether commercial interests were genuinely motivated by concern for women’s well-being, or financial gain through pushing unnecessary products at a vulnerable time in midlife. Participants frequently described companies as willing to do ‘anything for profit’, reflecting broader distrust of commercial involvement in menopause, which they described as playing on ‘emotion and not on functional facts’. However, concerns extended beyond the sale of specific products. Some participants felt that commercial interests were actively shaping how menopause itself was understood and experienced. They suggested that menopause was increasingly portrayed and positioned as a condition requiring intervention or management rather than as a normal life transition:

For some women, the proliferation of menopause products, treatments, and marketing messages reinforced the idea that menopause represented a ‘problem’ to be solved. Participants described this as creating pressure on women to monitor and manage their natural menopause experience via ‘pushing’ a range of products that they ‘just don’t need’:‘I think products claiming to relieve menopause symptoms help make menopause seem like something that has to be fixed.’—63 years old, Western Australia

They criticized products marketed as ‘quick fixes’ or ‘cures’, viewing these as attempts to capitalize on women’s concerns while offering limited evidence of benefit. There were broader concerns about the commodification of a life stage that many participants described as a particularly vulnerable period for women. Participants perceived that commercial actors actively sought to capitalize on women’s hopes for relief from the symptoms they were experiencing by promoting products and services that were unnecessary or exaggerated. Some participants described a sense of information overload within contemporary menopause information environments. They stated that the sheer volume of menopause content available online and particularly through social media platforms, often contributed to feelings of confusion, information fatigue, and disengagement. Some reported actively avoiding menopause content because they felt overwhelmed by the constant flow of information. Others described becoming increasingly sceptical of the information they encountered, finding it difficult to distinguish between independent education, personal experiences, and advertising. Discussion

This study examined how Australian women understand and respond to the commercialization and commodification of menopause. Figure 1 provides an overview of the key findings and implications of the study. In particular, the study shows that the volume of commercialized menopause content available online contributed to feelings of confusion, information fatigue, and disengagement. The study showed that commercial attention to menopause may blur the boundaries between information and marketing, and shifts responsibility for navigating risk onto individuals. The commercialization of menopause may produce epistemic harms by undermining trust in information and shaping what is understood as credible knowledge.

Australian women’s perceptions of the commercialization of menopause.

There are three points for discussion based on the research questions.

First, this study shows that women’s understandings of the commercialization of menopause are shaped by the wider digital and social contexts in which menopause has become both more visible and more commercially valuable. This is significant because the growing visibility of menopause has coincided with its emergence as a lucrative commercial opportunity, in which products, services and technologies are increasingly marketed to women (and their ‘problematic bodies’) in midlife (Onculer and Onculer Yayalar 2025, Takhar et al. 2025). These findings show how the perceived positive aspects of what women view as ‘awareness raising’ can operate as a source of commercial legitimacy. Commercial actors may use the language of empowerment and choice to legitimize their involvement, while obscuring the commercial benefits they derive from shaping how women understand and respond to menopause (Thomas et al. 2024b). This points to an important form of epistemic power, whereby commercial actors can influence not only what women are encouraged to buy, but what counts as relevant knowledge, credible expertise, and an appropriate response to menopause.

Similar to other studies (Wood et al. 2025a), while many women were sceptical of commercial claims, they positioned themselves as active and responsible decision makers who could assess claims, seek evidence, and reject products that appeared exaggerated, unevidenced or untrustworthy. This may also partly explain why over two-thirds of women said they did not feel confused about where to find trusted information about menopause. However, developing on the findings of our previous studies we would argue that this self confidence in the ability to individually evaluate commercial messages, products, and practices sits uneasily within a marketplace designed to capture attention, generate need, and encourage consumption. Commercial actors frame menopause as an issue that requires ongoing management through products, services, or interventions (Krajewski 2019, Mccartan 2025, Nickel et al. 2026). Women’s accounts therefore reveal a tension between perceived agency and commercial persuasion. While women may feel they are freely choosing what information, products or services to engage with, they are doing so within an information environment shaped by commercial interests—including algorithms and click funnels, targeted marketing, and persuasive narratives. We would argue that agency in this context is commercially conditioned—women make choices, but the information, options, and problems made most visible to them are not produced on a level playing field. While media and health literacy are important, they are insufficient on their own against a commercially engineered menopause marketplace (Takhar et al. 2025). Women’s agency does not remove the structural asymmetry between individuals and commercial actors who have considerably greater resources, data, and capacity to shape information environments. Instead, it may place additional responsibility on women to continually identify commercial motives and protect themselves from misleading or unnecessary claims.

Second, women’s concerns reflected significant unease about how commercial actors may profit from vulnerability. Women were particularly concerned about commercial practices that appeared to exploit women who were seeking support, relief, or reassurance. These concerns were directed at products that promised quick fixes, exaggerated the seriousness or universality of symptoms, or made claims that were not clearly supported by evidence. Women’s critiques were about how commercial actors may produce and intensify vulnerability by positioning menopause as something that requires the consumption of solutions. This suggests that vulnerability to the commercialization of menopause is not just about susceptibility to marketing. Women were concerned that ineffective products, unsupported claims, and persuasive marketing could leave women feeling misled, disappointed, overwhelmed, or less trusting of menopause information more broadly. Commercialization occurs in health care environments where trust is already fragile because of gaps in clinical care, inconsistent advice, limited access to specialist support, and uncertainty about where to find reliable menopause information (Purnat et al. 2026). These gaps are important commercially because they create spaces in which commercial actors can position themselves as filling unmet informational, emotional, and healthcare needs. Commercial actors can cultivate authenticity, relatability, and trust, positioning themselves as guides through menopause while simultaneously benefiting from women’s uncertainty and unmet needs. In doing so, this study develops on the findings of other studies by providing further insight into how commercialized menopause practices both construct and exploit vulnerability (Purnat et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a, Orgad and Gilchrist 2026). This is particularly concerning in an attention economy where emotionally resonant content, lived experience narratives and easily accessible ‘solutions’ may be highly visible, even when the evidence base is weak (Van Bavel et al. 2024). Women’s concerns therefore point to the need to understand menopause commercialization not only as a marketplace of products but as a commercial system that shapes how menopause is problematized, which vulnerabilities are made visible, and which solutions are positioned as necessary. This includes how responsibility for navigating menopause is shifted onto individuals rather than shared by health systems, clinicians, and regulators (Schneider Dallolio et al. 2025, Orgad and Gilchrist 2026).

Finally, this study develops our previous research on women’s views of the strategies used by commercial actors (Thomas et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a) by showing how women attempted to navigate increasingly commercialized menopause information environments. Women described two broad strategies—disengaging from menopause content or actively curating and trying to making sense of information from multiple and often intersecting sources. This supports emerging research on the responsibilization of menopause, whereby women are increasingly expected to become informed, engaged and proactive consumers of menopause information (Corus et al. 2025). Our findings begin to show what this responsibilization may look like in practice. While women did not necessarily see themselves as passive recipients of commercial information, some women recognized that women were also not operating on a level playing field with commercial actors. They were required to continually assess who and what to trust, compare competing claims, identify commercial interests, and decide which information to accept or reject. For some women, resisting commercial menopause information became a form of labour, requiring women to remain informed, sceptical and vigilant, or completely disengage with an increasingly difficult information environment. This study contributes to existing research by showing that responsibilization is not just about how women should ‘successfully manage menopause’ but also how they should manage the commercial risks associated with menopause.

These findings move understandings of the commercial determinants of menopause beyond discrete products, actors, or marketing practices (Onculer and Onculer Yayalar 2025, Takhar et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025a) towards a broader ecosystem of influence in which commercial actors help shape the information women encounter, the concerns that are amplified and their responsibility for navigating information and evidence about the solutions available to them. Conceptualizing commercialization as an ecosystem also draws attention to how these influences reinforce one another to shape what becomes visible, credible, and commercially valuable.

Limitations

This study draws on an Australian sample of cisgender women who were proficient in English. We did not distinguish between women who were experiencing perimenopause and those who were postmenopause and did not look intersectionally in seeking to understand women’s experiences. We also did not ask women specifically about their level of engagement in social media spaces, and did not seek to measure their health, digital or media literacy. However, Australia is a revealing case of a globally circulating commercial phenomenon. Commercialized messages about menopause increasingly move across national borders through digital platforms. The findings therefore offer insight into how globalized commercial menopause markets may be interpreted within a comparatively high-income, digitally connected context, while also highlighting the need for future research with more diverse populations and settings. Our study was designed specifically to explore women’s views about the commercialization of menopause, and the open-text questions were therefore intentionally focused on commercial products, practices and actors. We do not position neutrality as an achievable or necessarily appropriate goal within qualitative research. Consistent with a reflexive qualitative approach, we recognize that researchers are involved in shaping the research questions, the data generated and their interpretation.

Conclusion

Commercial attention to menopause blurs the boundaries between information and marketing and shifts responsibility for navigating risk onto individuals. Women’s responses are often active, critical and resistant, but they occur within an information environment shaped by commercial power. Greater visibility of menopause may be perceived as being valuable, but visibility alone does not guarantee agency or empowerment. When commercial actors participate in the production and circulation of menopause knowledge, they may shape not only what women buy, but also how menopause is understood, which experiences are recognized or platformed, and what forms of support are positioned as necessary. A critical approach to menopause must move beyond encouraging women to make better choices, or providing them with more ‘trusted’ information. It must address the commercial systems that shape what women know, what they are encouraged to celebrate or fear, what they are invited to buy, and what forms of care and support are made available to them.
