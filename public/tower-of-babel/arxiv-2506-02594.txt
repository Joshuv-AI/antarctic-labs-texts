EALG: Evolutionary Adversarial Generation of Language Model–Guided Generators for Combinatorial Optimization
Ruibo Duan, Yuxin Liu, Xinyao Dong, Chenglin Fan

Abstract
Generating challenging instances is crucial for the evaluation and advancement of combinatorial optimization solvers. In this work, we introduce EALG (Evolutionary Adversarial Generation of Language Model–Guided Generators), a novel framework that automates the co-evolution of optimization problem instances and their corresponding heuristic solvers using large language models (LLMs). EALG leverages a mutation-based adversarial approach that dynamically evolves instance generation procedures to create increasingly difficult problems, while simultaneously synthesizing adaptive heuristic algorithms through interactions with LLMs guided by algorithmic structure. Unlike existing approaches that focus solely on static benchmark creation or manual solver design, EALG provides a seamless pipeline from instance generation to solver synthesis. Experimental results demonstrate that EALG generates significantly harder instances than current benchmarks, and its synthesized solvers generalize effectively across a broad spectrum of combinatorial tasks. This work explores a new paradigm for combinatorial optimization that integrates instance generation with solver design, resulting in state-of-the-art performance.



1
Introduction

Combinatorial optimization (CO) problems are ubiquitous across scientific and industrial domains, including logistics, scheduling, network design, and circuit layout. However, the combinatorial explosion of the solution space, combined with the structural diversity of these problems, makes the design of effective solvers a persistent and formidable challenge
[
7
,
8
]
. Traditional approaches often rely on handcrafted heuristics, which are time-consuming to develop and typically lack generalizability across different problem types. Recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities in code generation and complex reasoning, opening a promising new direction for the automated design of heuristic algorithms tailored to CO problems
[
5
,
16
]
.

Existing studies typically rely on fixed datasets or uniform instance generation protocols, resulting in limited diversity across problem settings. Moreover, most approaches focus on incremental enhancements to existing solvers rather than introducing fundamentally novel frameworks
[
39
,
3
,
28
,
23
]
. This static evaluation paradigm restricts our ability to assess solver generalization and robustness. As a result, two critical challenges remain underexplored: (1) how to generate increasingly challenging problem instances, and (2) how to design solvers that can co-evolve alongside rising problem complexity. Addressing these challenges requires a fully automated, mutually reinforcing framework that can simultaneously synthesize harder instances and adapt solver strategies in response.

To address these challenges, we introduce
EALG
(Evolutionary Adversarial Generation of Language Model–Guided Generators), a novel framework that leverages large language models to co-evolve optimization problem instances and their corresponding heuristic solvers.
EALG
iteratively mutates instance-generation programs to produce increasingly complex and difficult problems, while concurrently synthesizing more adaptive solver code. This adversarial co-evolution loop enables the solver and problem to continuously challenge and improve each other.

Experimental results show that
EALG
can generate problem instances that are significantly more challenging than existing benchmarks, while simultaneously synthesizing heuristic solvers with strong performance and generalization capabilities—all achieved without manual intervention. This framework paves a new path for LLM-driven benchmark generation and solver co-evolution in combinatorial optimization.

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

•

We introduce
EALG
, a novel framework that enables fully automated co-evolution of optimization instances and solver algorithms via large language models.

•

We develop a mutation-based adversarial mechanism that adaptively modifies instance generation procedures to yield increasingly challenging optimization problems.

•

We propose a structured generation process that leverages large language model to automatically synthesize adaptive, high-quality heuristic algorithms without human intervention.

•

We empirically validate
EALG
across multiple combinatorial optimization tasks, demonstrating the generation of more challenging problem instances and the synthesis of solvers that achieve state-of-the-art performance.

3
LLM-Based Hard Instance Generator

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the space of instance generators
g:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is a latent seed space and
\mathcal{I}
represents the space of TSP instances. Each
g\in\mathcal{G}
is an executable heuristic function by a language model
\mathcal{L}_{\text{inst}}
, conditioned on a natural language prompt
\mathcal{P}_{\text{inst}}
. Formally,

g\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{inst}}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{inst}}),\quad I=g(z),\;z\sim\mathcal{Z}.

(1)

The resulting generator
g
defines a programmatic distribution over instances via algorithmic logic, spatial sampling patterns, and geometric transformations.

To quantify the difficulty of generated instances in a normalized and scale-invariant manner, we used the
relative optimality gap
as:

\text{Gap}(I;h)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{i=1}^{n}\left[f(h(I_{i}))\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{i=1}^{n}\left[f^{*}(I_{i})\right]}-1,

(2)

where
\{I_{1},\dots,I_{n}\}
are instances generated by the instance generator
g
,
f(h(I_{i}))
is the cost of the heuristic solution, and
f^{*}(I_{i})
is the cost of the optimal (or reference) solution.

A larger Gap indicates that the heuristic performs poorly relative to the best-known solution, and thus that the instance is harder with respect to
h
.

4
LLM-Based Heuristic Generator

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the space of heuristic functions
h:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the instance space and
\mathcal{S}
is the solution space (e.g., feasible TSP tours). Each heuristic
h\in\mathcal{H}
is an executable decision procedure synthesized by a language model
\mathcal{L}_{\text{heur}}
, conditioned on a natural language prompt
\mathcal{P}_{\text{heur}}
:

h\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{heur}}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{heur}}).

(3)

The prompt encodes prior knowledge such as algorithmic paradigms (e.g., greedy search, local refinement, metaheuristics), problem constraints, or domain-specific design priors. Once synthesized, the resulting heuristic is evaluated behaviorally by applying it to instances
I\in\mathcal{I}
and measuring its performance under the objective function
f(h(I))
.

Importantly, the heuristic is treated as a black-box symbolic policy: no structural assumptions or internal modifications are made. Selection and refinement are driven purely by performance signals, enabling the discovery of non-trivial and potentially unconventional algorithmic behaviors.

5
Co-Evolution Through Symbolic Adversarial Interaction

We formalize the learning process as a symbolic co-evolutionary game between an instance constructor
g\in\mathcal{G}
and a heuristic solver
h\in\mathcal{H}
. This interaction is guided by a minimax formulation:

\min_{h\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{heur}}}\;\max_{g\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{inst}}}\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{Z}}\left[\mathcal{H}(g(z);h)\right],

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}(g(z);h)
denotes the relative hardness of the instance
I=g(z)
with respect to solver
h
. This framework captures the adversarial dynamics between instance generation and solution strategies: the generator evolves to reveal vulnerabilities in current heuristics, while the solver adapts to counter increasingly complex instance structures.

Interaction Protocol.

The co-evolution proceeds through iterative interaction in the algorithmic function space:

1.

Heuristic synthesis
: Sample a solver
h\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{heur}}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{heur}})
from the heuristic model via symbolic prompt programming;

2.

Hard instance constructor synthesis
: Sample an instance generator
g\sim\mathcal{L}_{\text{inst}}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{inst}})
, which defines a distribution over problem instances via structured algorithmic composition;

3.

Evaluation
: Apply
h
to a batch of instances
\{I_{i}=g(z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, compute the relative hardness
\mathcal{H}(I_{i};h)
, and identify instances exhibiting maximal challenge;

This adversarial loop induces an emergent curriculum over the symbolic policy space: instance constructors synthesize increasingly diverse and non-trivial spatial structures, while solvers develop greater resilience and generalization capabilities. Notably, this process operates entirely without gradient-based optimization, relying instead on behavioral evaluation over executable algorithmic trajectories.

6
Adversarial Co-Evolution of Instances and Solvers

We propose
EALG
, a dual-loop adversarial co-evolution framework that enables the simultaneous evolution of hard combinatorial optimization instances and their corresponding solver heuristics. As illustrated in Figure
1
,
EALG
consists of two tightly coupled evolutionary processes: heuristics evolution and instance evolution, each orchestrated by large language models (LLMs) and driven by adversarial feedback. Figure
1(a)
outlines the overall structure of the system, where both heuristics and hard instance generators are iteratively mutated, reflected upon, and regenerated based on symbolic signals extracted via evolutionary reflection. This co-evolutionary loop allows both components to adapt in response to the other’s progress, promoting mutual escalation. Figure
1(b)
presents concrete examples of this process: it shows how heuristics evolve from naive strategies into spatially informed ones augmented with reflection-driven improvements, and how instance generators grow in complexity through the incorporation of adversarial patterns such as spirals or clustered regions.

Heuristics generator.
The heuristics generator in
EALG
is an LLM-based component that synthesizes candidate solver programs as executable code fragments. These solvers are conditioned on problem descriptions and prior performance reflections, and are sampled from an open-ended algorithmic space without relying on predefined templates. This allows for the emergence of novel search strategies and scoring mechanisms throughout evolution.

Hard instance generator.
The hard instance generator plays a symmetric role in
EALG
by producing instance-generation programs. Each generator defines a procedural method to construct challenging combinatorial problem instances, such as spatially clustered or structurally irregular TSP variants. These generators are optimized to exploit solver weaknesses, gradually shaping a landscape of increasing difficulty. This, in turn, causes solvers to exhibit significantly different performance on the generated hard instances, providing valuable guidance for subsequent optimization by highlighting robustness gaps and informing targeted improvement strategies.

Heuristics mutation.
Given a fixed hard instance,
EALG
selects a previously generated solver and mutates it via the heuristics generator. Mutations may include algorithmic rewrites, strategy switching, or hyperparameter changes. The resulting solver is then evaluated on the current instance and compared to its predecessor to determine its relative effectiveness.

Hard instance mutation.
Conversely, given a fixed solver,
EALG
mutates an existing instance generator using the hard instance generator. The objective is to synthesize instances that are more difficult for the solver, thereby increasing selection pressure and encouraging solver robustness.

Evolutionary reflection.

EALG
adopts evolutionary reflection, a symbolic diagnostic mechanism wherein LLMs evaluate behavioral shifts between original and mutated programs to generate high-level feedback—such as failure modes, inefficiencies, or refinement cues. These insights are embedded into future prompts, enabling implicit memory and long-term adaptation. Through this mechanism,
EALG
selectively produces mutated heuristics or hard instances based on task-specific objectives, allowing the co-evolutionary process to dynamically align with either solver optimization or instance difficulty escalation.

Adversarial co-evolution process.
The two evolutionary paths in
EALG
are tightly coupled through adversarial interaction. As solvers improve, instance generators are pressured to synthesize harder instances; in turn, more difficult instances expose limitations in solver logic. This continuous feedback loop creates an arms race between the two components, driving the emergence of increasingly generalizable solvers and increasingly sophisticated benchmark distributions.

EALG

EALG
across multiple combinatorial optimization tasks and solver paradigms. With

EALG
’s generality and effectiveness. Experimental details and implementation specifics are provided in the future.

8
Conclusion

We present
EALG
, a novel framework that redefines combinatorial optimization through the adversarial co-evolution of problem instances and solvers, orchestrated via large language models. Moving beyond static datasets and manually crafted heuristics,
EALG
establishes a symbolic, feedback-driven process wherein instance hardness and solver competence co-amplify in an iterative evolutionary loop. This paradigm enables the autonomous emergence of algorithmic strategies finely tuned to the shifting complexity of the problem space.

Empirical results across diverse combinatorial tasks confirm that
EALG
not only generates significantly harder instances, but also synthesizes solvers that achieve state-of-the-art performance, outperforming leading LLM-based baselines. The framework’s ability to induce structural diversity, exploit solver vulnerabilities, and evolve high-performing heuristics without human supervision marks a decisive advance toward fully automated, co-adaptive optimization systems.
EALG
opens a new frontier for research at the intersection of symbolic reasoning, automated code synthesis, and dynamic hard instance generation.
