Protocol for the infant growth and development (iGrowUp) study: the role of food and non-food self-regulation in children’s obesity-related risk
Authors: Jessica M. Dollar, Esther M. Leerkes, Lenka H. Shriver, Sally G. Eagleton, Laurie Wideman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood obesity remains a significant public health crisis. Despite growing evidence that a) self-regulation and appetitive traits impact childhood obesity-related risk, and b) obesity-related risk is determined by the interplay between biological, psychological, and home environment factors, research has occurred largely within disciplines, hindering the interpretability and application of findings. We describe the rationale and methods of the Infant Growth and Development (iGrowUp) Study which tests two aims: (1) Determine the extent to which child self-regulatory and appetite constructs are distinct versus overlapping in the preschool period, and (2) Test a comprehensive model of biopsychosocial predictors (age 3.5 years) of childhood obesity-related risk (age 5 years) to determine the extent to which self-regulation in food and non-food contexts buffers children from child and home environment risk factors.MethodsiGrowUp is an interdisciplinary, longitudinal study that leverages the iGrow study, consisting of 299 diverse mother-infant dyads, followed from the prenatal period until children were 2 years old. We aim to recruit these children into the iGrowUp study and re-assess them at ages 3.5 and 5 years old, in addition to recruiting an additional 67 children. Key measures include (a) children’s self-regulation (i.e., observed emotion regulation, attentional regulation, physiological regulation, inhibitory control, executive function in response to food and non-food stimuli in comparable situations; maternal report of effortful control, emotional over-eating, satiety responsiveness), (b) food cue reactivity (i.e., motivation to eat, food responsiveness, enjoyment of food, eating in the absence of hunger), (c) parenting behaviors (i.e., maternal sensitivity, non-supportive emotion socialization, negative parenting practices, parent–child relationship quality), (d) child negative emotionality, (e) child hormonal risk from urine assays, (f) maternal reports of demographics, obesogenic food environment, and health, and (g) anthropometric measures of children. Demographic and other potential covariates will be included in structural equation models.DiscussionThis study has critical implications for prevention since it aims to identify the most influential mechanisms in infancy and preschool that may mitigate obesity-related risk. By combining the approaches of developmental and nutritional sciences, this project enhances the rigor applicable to basic and applied research that assesses food and non-food reactivity and regulation.

Background

Childhood obesity remains a serious public health concern. Approximately 1 in 5 children and adolescents have obesity in the U.S. [1] and it is projected that 33% of school-aged children will have obesity by 2030 [2]. Children with obesity are at a greater risk for developing several co-morbidities in early life, such as type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, elevated blood pressure, and mental health challenges [3, 4], and for a notable proportion of children with obesity, obesity will continue into adulthood [5]. Thus, identifying the most robust early life predictors of obesity amenable to intervention is essential.

A growing literature suggests that increased obesity-related risk may be rooted, at least in part, in poor mastery of self-regulatory skills and heightened appetite (i.e., food cue reactivity, food approach traits) [6–11]. Yet, little research has comprehensively examined these associations across time. Thus, many have called for developmentally-informed, interdisciplinary, longitudinal research to delineate the components of self-regulation in food and non-food contexts, and appetite-related constructs that matter most for childhood obesity [10–12]. In direct response, the Infant Growth and Development (iGrowUp) Study uses novel methodology to test a longitudinal model of biopsychosocial predictors of childhood obesity-related risk by age 5. Importantly, we consider the interplay between specific types of child self-regulation and key biological (e.g., hormones related to energy metabolism), psychological/behavioral (e.g., child food cue reactivity, negative emotionality), and social/environmental processes (e.g., parent–child relationship quality, obesogenic home environment) during the preschool period.

Preschool is a unique time to identify the interplay between these processes given that significant gains in self-regulatory skills occur [13], children’s health behaviors are established, and parents remain primary gatekeepers of the child’s environment. By measuring these constructs at ages 3.5 and 5 years, we will test two overarching aims (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4) to: (1) Determine the extent to which child self-regulatory and appetite-related constructs are distinct versus overlapping in the preschool period, and (2) Test a comprehensive model of biopsychosocial predictors (3.5 years of age) of obesity-related risk at age 5 to determine the extent to which self-regulation in food and non-food contexts buffers children from specific child and home environment risk factors over time. To achieve these aims, we aim to leverage our sample of 299 diverse mother-infant dyads from the iGrow study that have been followed from the prenatal period until children were 2 years old. Primary aim 1: distinguishing self-regulatory and appetite-related constructs

Children’s self-regulation, including the ability to control one’s inner states or behavioral responses to cope effectively with environmental demands [14, 15], is an essential skill. The greatest increases in self-regulation occur in early childhood, and self-regulation crosses physiological, attentional, emotional, behavioral, and cognitive domains of functioning [14–17]. Growing research shows that distinct forms of self-regulation are associated with lower obesity-related risk in childhood [6, 8, 18], adolescence [7, 19, 20], and adulthood [21]. To better inform obesity prevention science, however, several existing gaps require attention.

In most developmental studies examining predictors of childhood obesity, children’s self-regulation in response to non-food stimuli is considered, and delay of gratification tasks are predominantly employed. In the rare instances in which self-regulation in response to food and non-food are both measured, the conclusions drawn may be overstated because of the methodology (e.g., food and non-food regulation tasks differ based on parent’s presence [7] and/or task duration [6]; focus on few self-regulation indicators/domains). In direct contrast, nutrition research largely measures self-regulation in response to food (e.g., eating in the absence of hunger, satiety responsiveness, delay of gratification in response to food, food responsiveness, emotional overeating, rewarding value of food) [6, 10, 11, 22, 23] and largely fails to embed the findings within the developmental self-regulation literature. Further, no consensus exists on which of these measures reflect regulatory behaviors (i.e., top-down processes) and which correspond to trait-like/food cue reactivity (i.e., bottom-up processes) [11], which impedes the synthesis and replication of findings. Integrating developmental and nutrition science perspectives is needed to: (a) ensure methodologically rigorous and similar protocols are employed that assess multiple domains of self-regulation in food and non-food tasks, (b) promote clarity about which behaviors reflect appetite self-regulation vs. food cue reactivity, and (c) provide empirical evidence about which constructs most robustly predict obesity-related risk.

Here we apply the principles of temperament theory to organize the child characteristics proposed as predictors of obesity-related risk. Temperament is often defined in terms of reactivity (i.e., a child’s arousability, excitability, and responsivity of affect and motor activity) and regulation (i.e., processes that modulate reactivity that facilitate/inhibit the response) [24, 25], both of which are biologically based, develop across time, and are open to environmental influence [26]. Despite being intertwined, constructs more closely aligned with one’s behavioral/emotional tendencies are considered as reactivity, whereas control of these tendencies is considered regulatory.

Aim 1A: factor structure

Our first goal is to identify the factor structure to best discern among self-regulatory and appetite-related constructs. To this end, we administer a comprehensive, multi-method battery of measures and will use confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to determine the factor structure of self-regulatory and appetite-related (i.e., food cue reactivity) constructs among preschoolers. Based on existing theory and empirical findings [7, 24, 25, 27], we hypothesize that 3 correlated, but distinct, factors will emerge: general self-regulation, appetite self-regulation, and food cue reactivity (Fig. 1). General self-regulation, defined as the ability to control one’s behavior in response to non-food challenges, will encompass the constructs of emotion regulation, delay of gratification, attentional regulation, physiological regulation, inhibitory control, effortful control, and executive function when exposed to non-food related challenges. Appetite self-regulation, defined as the ability to control one’s behavior in the context of food, will encompass emotion regulation, delay of gratification, attentional regulation, physiological regulation, and inhibitory control when exposed to food stimuli, as well as emotional over-eating (reversed) and satiety responsiveness. Food cue reactivity (i.e., bottom-up traits), defined as children’s interest in food and the extent to which children eat in response to external food cues (e.g., sight, smell, taste of food) regardless of hunger, and potentially to excess [28], will encompass the constructs of food responsiveness, motivation to eat, enjoyment of food, and eating in the absence of hunger. Although based on existing empirical evidence and theory, it is possible that other factor structures will emerge (Fig. 1). For instance, a two-factor structure may best fit the data with Factor 1 encompassing general self-regulation and Factor 2 composed of food cue reactivity and appetite self-regulation, or Factor 1 may encompass self-regulation (in response to food and non-food) and Factor 2 composed of food cue reactivity. For simplicity, the remaining models build from our proposed 3-factor structure but can be adjusted based on the results from Aim 1A.

Aim 1B: unique antecedents

Our second goal is to determine the extent to which preschoolers’ self-regulation in food and/or non-food contexts and food cue reactivity have unique early life antecedents. We focus on the independent and interactive effects of infant characteristics and aspects of maternal behavior in infancy (Fig. 2).

Predictors of general self-regulation

Children who experience more sensitive parenting during infancy, characterized by prompt, consistent, and supportive responses to their cues, develop more adaptive self-regulation [29–35]. Infant negative emotionality, characterized by frequent, intense, and longer bouts of distress, is associated with poorer subsequent self-regulation [36–39], likely because heightened negativity constrains a child’s ability to develop and effectively deploy regulatory strategies [40] and elicits more negative parenting, undermining chances to learn adaptive regulation skills [41]. The negative association between negative emotionality and self-regulation is often attenuated by more sensitive parenting [42–45].

Predictors of appetite self-regulation

The etiology of appetite self-regulation is less elucidated. Obesogenic feeding practices (e.g., restrictive, pressuring) thought to disrupt children’s ability to self-regulate their energy intake, are linked to decreased appetite self-regulation. For instance, parental use of food to soothe and controlling feeding are associated with higher emotional overeating [46–48] and the perception that infants are not able to regulate intake [49], respectively. High negative emotionality is associated with greater emotional overeating [48, 50–53] and lower delay of gratification in the context of food [54]. The association between high child negative emotionality and poorer appetite self-regulation may be attenuated by sensitive parenting [55, 56] and exacerbated by food to soothe [50]. It is also important to consider that circulating levels of appetite-regulating hormones (high leptin, insulin; low adiponectin), or infant hormonal risk, may act as a metabolic signal that manifests behaviorally [57–59].

Predictors of food cue reactivity

Obesogenic feeding practices are also associated with higher food cue reactivity [60–63]. Parents’ indulgent feeding style and feeding to soothe are associated with greater parent-reported enjoyment of food and food responsiveness. A sizeable literature indicates positive associations between restriction and eating in the absence of hunger (EAH) among children [63], but the cumulative effect of restrictive feeding across infancy on later EAH is unknown. Greater infant negative emotionality is associated with higher EAH, food responsiveness, and relative food reinforcement [64–67], and infant negative affect may strengthen the effect of controlling feeding on EAH [64]. The links between infant hormonal risk and food cue reactivity are largely unknown. Also, limited research suggests a positive association between leptin and food responsiveness in boys [68] and insulin and leptin with EAH in older children [69] which may indicate that EAH has a genetic component [63].

Based on this literature, we hypothesize that: (1) controlling for the other predictors, infants prone to high negative emotionality will have poorer general self-regulation at 3.5 years, but this association will be buffered among infants whose mothers are higher in maternal sensitivity (red arrows); (2) infants prone to high negative emotionality will have poorer appetite self-regulation at age 3.5, particularly if they experienced (a) lower maternal sensitivity and/or (b) more obesogenic feeding practices during infancy, (c) elevated infant hormonal risk will predict poorer subsequent appetite self-regulation beyond concurrent hormonal risk, and (d) this association will be stronger among children who experienced more obesogenic feeding during infancy (blue arrows); and (3) obesogenic feeding behaviors in infancy will moderate the associations between (a) infant negative emotionality and food cue reactivity, and (b) infant hormonal risk and food cue reactivity; associations will be stronger among infants who experienced more obesogenic feeding practices during infancy (green arrows).

Aim 1C: Independent contributions to the prediction of subsequent obesity-related risk

Although it is established that high food cue reactivity, low general and/or appetite self-regulation are related to excessive adiposity, accelerated weight gain, and/or overweight/obesity status, the extent to which these constructs predict unique vs overlapping variability in preschoolers’ obesity-related risk remains unknown. Thus, our third goal is to fill this gap (Fig. 3). Based on work that has considered two or more of these constructs together [6, 7, 27, 70], we hypothesize that (1) general and appetite self-regulation will be negatively associated with later obesity-related risk, whereas food cue reactivity will be positively associated with later obesity-related risk; and (2) effect sizes will be greater for associations between obesity-related risk and appetite self-regulation and food cue reactivity than for general self-regulation.

We will assess obesity-related risk with a combination of body mass index (BMI)-for-age percentile, total and central adiposity, and inflammatory markers. This approach is optimal because higher fat mass, central/visceral fat distribution, and elevated inflammatory markers are better indicators of obesity and related health risks than BMI alone [71, 72].

Primary aim 2: test the role of preschoolers’ self-regulation in food and non-food contexts as buffers in a comprehensive biopsychosocial model predicting obesity-related risk in childhood

We also will consider child (negative emotionality, food cue reactivity, hormonal risk, growth or weight-related pattern) and home environment (obesogenic food environment, poor health related routines, negative parenting) factors that are known to negatively impact obesity-related risk and propose that general and appetite-related self-regulation skills may ameliorate these associations with obesity-related risk at age 5 (Fig. 4).

Child risk factors

In addition to food cue reactivity, negative emotionality is associated with maladaptive weight outcomes, likely because children with heighted negative emotions may use food to alleviate distress [73]. Obesity-related risk at age 3.5 also likely predicts subsequent obesity given the significant stability of weight status across developmental periods [5, 74], and that preschool BMI predicts subsequent weight status [75]. Finally, given the known role of hormones in weight management, adiposity, and inflammation among adults and emerging evidence among youth [76–79], we propose that preschool hormonal risk (i.e., elevated insulin, leptin and resistin, and lower adiponectin) will predict age 5 obesity-related risk. To date, the extent to which each predicts obesity-related risk independent of one another is unknown.

Home environmental risk factors

The home environment also influences children’s lifestyle behaviors and thus, obesity-related risk [73, 80–83]. We focus on two home environment constructs: (1) food/eating-related risk factors (i.e., low household availability of healthy food, abundance of high-fat and high-sugar foods in the home, low parental modeling of healthy food intake [80, 84–88], infrequent/poorly structured family meals [89–92], permissive and controlling feeding practices, and using food as a reward or to manage negative emotions [93–95]) and (2) non-food related unhealthy practices and routines (i.e., low availability, accessibility and modeling of physical activity [96–98]; screen use [98–100]; inconsistent bedtime, sleep location, and bedtime routine [101–105]).

It is also important to consider the parent–child relationship and negative parenting behaviors (i.e., insensitive, negative, and autonomy restricting interactions [106–108], non-supportive emotion socialization practices [109], poor monitoring, limit setting and discipline [110], and negative parent–child relationships), likely because they undermine key factors, including children’s sense of self, self-regulation, and emotional well-being, that may prompt engagement in unhealthy behaviors [111]. Also, a recent review concluded that longitudinal studies of feeding styles, general parenting styles, and measures of child characteristics are needed in obesity-related literature [112].

Self-regulation as a moderator

Despite the known association between self-regulation and children’s health [6, 10], there are mixed effects in obesity interventions targeting child self-regulation. However, missing from existing interventions is a consideration of general or appetite-related regulation as mediators of intervention effects, as well as little to no consideration that self-regulation interventions may be effective within specific child and/or home environment risk factors.

Aim 2 will examine the extent to which general vs. appetite self-regulation moderate associations between the above risk factors and subsequent obesity-related risk. We hypothesize (1) that better appetite self-regulation will buffer children with high food cue reactivity, high hormonal drive to eat, elevated early obesity-related risk, and obesogenic home environments from acting on internal and external pressures to consume excessive calories, thereby reducing their subsequent obesity-related risk [78]. We also hypothesize that (2) general and appetite self-regulation will buffer children with high negative emotionality and negative parenting from developing obesity-related risk by reducing eating as a response to distress [8]. Although a growing literature supports these hypotheses [19, 105, 113–115], this work has relied on single indicators of self-regulation, none examined general and appetite self-regulation as simultaneous moderators, and only a few considered preschoolers specifically, underscoring the value of this study.

Methods/design

The iGrowUp study aims to leverage and expand upon data collected for the iGrow study, a prospective, longitudinal, multi-method study of infants and their primary caregivers (all but two were biological mothers; henceforth referred to as “mothers”) aiming to identify prenatal (3rd trimester of pregnancy) and early life predictors (2 months, 6 months, 14 months post-partum) of childhood obesity-related risk at age 2 years. The iGrowUp new data collection will occur with re-enrolled dyads when children are approximately 3.5 and 5 years old, as well as 67 new dyads that are being recruited at age 2 to adjust for existing and anticipated attrition from both studies. Employed methods include biological assays, physiological assessments, anthropometrics, dietary intakes, surveys, and direct observations with behavioral coding.

Setting

The iGrowUp study is being conducted in Greensboro, North Carolina, a mid-size southeastern city in Guilford County (population 549,866). According to Census data, the racial/ethnic composition of the county is 54% White alone, 36% Black or African American alone, and 10% other/multiple races (10% Hispanic/Latino). In 2022, the median income was $62,880 and the poverty rate was 16.4%. Approximately 10% of individuals ages 25 + had not completed high school/GED and 37.9% had earned a bachelor’s degree or higher. In 2018, 31.2% of 2-to-4-year-olds in the county were obese or overweight at their Well Child Visits.

Recruitment and retention

The iGrow study recruited 299 pregnant women. Inclusion criteria were mothers 18 or older, expecting a singleton, fluent in English, and planned to remain in the region for 3 years. Infants with birth defects, metabolic disorders, and/or gestational age < 32 weeks were excluded. Expectant mothers were recruited during their 3rd trimester from (a) childbirth education classes at local hospitals and the Public Health Department, (b) prenatal breastfeeding classes provided by the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC), (c) advertisements in waiting rooms of OB/GYN practices and stores/events targeting expectant parents, (d) via social media (e.g., listservs, Facebook, Twitter), and (e) via paid advertising in regional print and online magazines focused on pregnancy and parenting. Recruitment occurred between February 2019 and April 2022, with a 7-month pause after COVID-19 was declared a pandemic. The sample generally reflects the racial composition of the surrounding county described above. Specifically, 55.2% of mothers identify as non-Hispanic White, 29.4% Black or African American, 6.7% multiracial, 5.0% identified as another racial group, and 3.7% of mothers did not report their race; 7.7% of mothers self-identified as Hispanic. Thirty-four (11.37%) participants formally withdrew or became ineligible for continued participation in the iGrow study; 72% provided data from at least 1 postnatal wave. Except for maternal age (i.e., mothers with more complete participation tend to be older), there is no evidence of selective attrition/completion based on maternal race/ethnicity, income, education, or parity [116].

All remaining dyads in the parent study are being invited to participate in the proposed study when children are 3.5 and 5 years old via phone/email/text. We are also recruiting an additional 67 dyads via childcare centers, local parks and museums, WIC offices, local parent newsletters, and social media to address attrition. One staff member serves as the liaison with participating families to promote rapport and continuity and is the primary recruiter and schedules most assessments. Childcare and transportation (within a 25-mile radius) are provided for participants as needed. If families are unable to participate in lab visits at any time point, they are given the option of completing surveys. Mothers are mailed regular newsletters and personalized cards. Mothers provide information for up to 5 contacts so that we may reach out to in the future should we have difficulty reaching them for follow-up assessments. Participating mothers receive $75 per visit at age 3.5 ($150 total) and $100 per visit at age 5 ($200 total) and children receive a gift at each wave. Mothers and children also receive small gifts with the iGrowUp logo at each assessment.

Laboratory visits were intended to be scheduled ± 5 days of the infant’s 2-month birthday and ± 2 weeks of children’s 6, 14, and 24-month birthdays; however, the COVID 19 pandemic began midway through the project requiring a temporary suspension of in-person data collection. This resulted in a more liberal window and visits occurred on average when children were: 2.26 months (SD = 0.49), 6.90 months (SD = 1.16), 14.70 months (SD = 1.15) and 25.80 months (SD = 1.94). Mothers completed online questionnaires via secure links. Infant urine was either collected at each visit using a urine bag (Mabis Healthcare) or with a home collection kit using sterile cotton balls. If children were potty trained, a urine catch was completed at the laboratory. Mothers and infants engaged in a feeding episode (breast, bottle, or baby food based on mothers’ typical feeding mode at 2, 6, 14 months; standard snack tray at 24 months), free play, and distress eliciting tasks (Still Face at 2 and 6 months [117], arm restraint at 6 months [118, 119], attractive toy in a locked jar and novel character approach at 14 months [120–122], attractive toy in a locked box [123–125], and spider approach at 24 months [121, 126]). Each visit was 2 + hours [116].

Infant Negative Emotionality was assessed using four measures at each wave. Coders rated infant negative affect during each distress eliciting interactive task using the 7-point global negative mood scale adapted from the NICHD SECCYD [127], noted the peak level of distress displayed and the latency between the start of each task and when infants first displayed distress [125]. Each observed indicator will be averaged across distress tasks within a wave. Mothers completed the negative affectivity subscale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire-Revised Very Short Form (VSF) [128, 129] at 2, 6, and 14 months and of the Early Childhood Behavior Questionnaire-VSF [130] at 24 months. An infant negative emotionality composite will be created from these measures.

Infant Hormonal Risk (adiponectin, insulin, leptin levels) was derived from urine samples collected at each wave. Urine osmolality was assessed on each sample using an osmometer (gold standard assessment method; OSMO1, Advanced Instruments, Norwood MA, USA), so that hormone values can be adjusted for hydration status, if warranted. Infant biomarker assessments were completed using commercially available assays (single or multiplex) and the MESO QuickPlex SQ120 (MSD, Rockville MD). All samples from a child (2, 6, 14, and 24 months) were assayed after the 24-month wave in a single assay to minimize inter-assay variability. An infant hormonal risk composite will be created from these measures.

Maternal sensitivity was rated from videotapes of free-play, distress-eliciting, and feeding tasks. Maternal behavior during the first two types of tasks was rated on dimensions using a 7-point scale adapted from the NICHD SECCYD [127, 131]: sensitivity to distress, sensitivity to non-distress, intrusiveness, and detachment, and positive regard and negative regard were rated a single time across the free-play and distress-eliciting tasks given their brevity. The coding system was adapted for the feeding task by modifying descriptors and replacing intrusiveness with sensitivity to fullness cues based on other feeding observation assessments [132, 133]. Sensitivity will be calculated as the average of each rating across tasks with intrusiveness, detachment, and negative regard reversed.

Presuming moderate or higher stability in each of the above indicators over time and across tasks, as in prior research [134–136], each construct will be averaged across the 4 infancy waves yielding highly reliable estimates of infant risk and maternal sensitivity over time and across multiple contexts. These will be manifest variables in analyses.

Maternal obesogenic feeding practices

The obesogenic feeding practices (OFP1) risk index utilized nine risk indicators occurring from birth to approximately 6 months of infant age [116]. Information used to measure these risk factors came from the Infant Feeding Practices Survey-II [137], the Infant Feeding Styles Questionnaire [138], and the Feeding to Soothe Scale [139], which were administered at 2 and 6 months. The proportion cumulative risk (PCR) method was used to create the index [140, 141]. Most indicators (n = 6) were based on the average score across 2 and 6 months. Exceptions were the “early introduction of complementary foods (prior to 4 months of age) and “months of not breastfeeding” in which case data from either 2 months or 6 months were used and “bottle propping,” which was only considered at 2 months given that most infants have the motor skills to hold a bottle themselves by 6 months. The PCR scores for the nine indicators were summed to create the early infancy OFP1 risk index, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating more risk.

The obesogenic feeding practices risk index developed for when children were between 14 and 24 months (OFP2) utilized thirteen risk indicators that consider age-appropriate changes in feeding practices (e.g., eating solid foods, self-feeding), with possible scores ranging from 0 to 13. Information used to measure these risk indicators came from maternal-reported questionnaires including Food to Soothe [142], Infant Feeding Style Questionnaire [138], Family Mealtime Q-sort and Feeding Practices and Structure Questionnaire [143–145], the Infant Feeding Practices Questionnaire-II [137], and the Food Frequency Questionnaire [146]. Each indicator was assessed at both time points, except for family mealtimes which was assessed only at 24 months. The principal component regression method was used to create the index. Multiple imputation was used with missing data on the individual indicators before creating the risk index. OFP1 and OFP2 are significantly correlated (r = 0.73, p < 0.001) and will be summed to create the overall OFP index.

iGrowUp new procedures and measures

Two laboratory visits are scheduled within 2 weeks of each other when the children are ± 6 months of 3.5- and 5-years of age. Food and non-food tasks are spread across visits to ensure no comparable tasks (e.g., gift delay and snack delay) occur during the same visit. Two staff members conduct the visits; the first visit lasts about two hours, and the second visit is a little over one hour. Maternal consent is obtained prior to data collection.

During each visit, staff place pediatric ECG electrodes to collect heart rate during resting baselines and multiple challenging tasks, using the Biolog (UFI, Morro Bay, California). Mothers are sent a secure link via email to complete questionnaires on a device prior to their first visit. Alternatively, they can complete these on a computer in Visit 1. Mothers complete paper questionnaires at each visit about their child’s sleep and diet that day and in the recent past in a room adjacent to the child. Mothers are told at the start of each visit that they can end a task at any point if they desire (i.e., if it makes her uncomfortable, child is upset). Visits are recorded for later coding. At the end of the first visit, an Actigraph® accelerometer (Pensacola, FL) is placed on the child. Instructions are given to the mother, as well as a log for her to complete. They are instructed to (a) have the child wear the accelerometer continuously except when they are in water for 7 days after the visit, (b) record sleep and wake time and any times the device is removed and why (e.g., bathing, swimming), and (c) bring the log and accelerometer back at the second visit.

3.5- and 5-year visit 1

Upon arrival at the lab, a urine sample is procured from the child using a urine collection cup. Consistent with iGrow procedures, children are weighed in light clothing on a calibrated high-precision pediatric scale (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). Child height is measured to the nearest 0.1 cm, using a SECA 214 (Perspective Enterprises, Portage, MI) portable stadiometer, shoes removed; averaged values will be used to calculated BMI (kg/m2). Waist circumference is measured to the nearest 0.1 cm with a Gullick tape using standard procedures [147]. Five skinfolds are taken on children’s tricep, subscapular, suprailiac, thigh, and bicep [148]. Each measure is duplicated for accuracy, and a third measurement is taken if the first two measures disagree by previously established maximum values. Children then sit in the BODPOD using a pediatric insert for a body composition assessment [149]. At the 5-year visit, children lay on a mat and are connected to a device that measures body composition and hydration using bioelectrical impedance (BIA; i.e., electrodes connected to the right foot and right hand [150, 151]) using the BodyStat QUADSCAN4000 which non-invasively measures the flow of current through the body at four different frequencies: 5, 50, 100 & 200 kHz. The BIA device sends a low-level current through their body to estimate the amount of lean body mass, fat mass and water. The measurement itself takes approximately 3–5 s.

Children then go to a room and the mother is asked to complete questionnaires in an adjacent room where she can watch through a 1-way mirror. Mothers are only in the room with the child during the mother–child challenging task. Children are fit with pediatric electrodes on their right collarbone, lower left rib, and navel to collect heart rate using the Biolog (UFI, Morro Bay, California) and engage in a resting baseline (passive video watching for 2 min) to calculate vagal withdrawal scores [152]. Next, children engage in a delay task that involves food (Snack Delay task; M&Ms), in which children are asked to not touch or eat a small container of M&Ms placed in front of them (3 min at 3.5-year, 5 min at 5-year) [153]. Children are allowed to eat the M&Ms after the task. This is followed by a small meal (unsweetened apple juice, string cheese, apple sauce, chicken nuggets, carrots, and ranch/ketchup), which the child can eat until they report being full (~ 10 min). This serves as the pre-load for the EAH task [63, 154, 155], a snack period where children have free access to six highly palatable, energy-dense snack foods (total available kcal = approx. 460–520 kcal Oreo minis™ (18–20 g), Fudge stripe minis™ (18–20 g), Circus animal® cookies (18–20 g), potato chips (10–12 g), cheese puffs (10–12 g), and Cheez-It® crackers (18–20 g), in addition to alternative activities (e.g., toys, crayons). Children are told that they can eat any of the snacks and play with any of the toys while the researcher does some work in the next room (7 min).

Next, children complete a computerized Go No-Go task with the experimenter in the room that involves the presentation of either sweet bakery treats (Food Go-No Go task) or zoo animals (Zoo Go-No Go [non-food] task [156–158]). The order of these tasks (visit 1 vs visit 2) are counterbalanced across participants. In both Go No-Go tasks, children are instructed to respond via button press on most trials (go stimuli) but inhibit responding on a subset of trials (no-go stimuli). Two blocks of 40 trials are presented after the child completes practice trials. Each image appears in the center of the screen. In the Zoo Go No-Go task, children are told that animals from the zoo have escaped from their cages and that the zookeeper needs help catching the animals. Children are told to not catch the monkey because it is the zookeeper’s assistant helping him catch the animals. Children are instructed to press a button to catch all the animals (go trials), and to not respond for the monkey who helps the zookeeper catch the animals (no-go trials). The Zoo task was modified such that in the new Food Go-No Go task, children are told that the Baker needs help sorting the treats (e.g., brownies, cookies, donut) in her shop. Children are told not to box up the birthday cake because it needs to be taken home for the Baker’s daughter’s birthday party. Children are instructed to press a button to put away all the treats (go trials), and to not respond when they see the birthday cake (no-go trials).

Next, mothers and children engage in a challenging task where they work together (8 min). At 3.5 years, the mother and child work to complete a series of images in an Etch-a-Sketch [159, 160] task while each is controlling one of the knobs (i.e., right or left). The dyad is given two images (a rectangle, a house) they must try to replicate in a certain amount of time, while each is only able to move one knob and prohibited from moving the other person’s nob (8 min). At 5 years, mothers and children complete a modified Tangrams shapes [161] task where they must use 7 small shapes to create a larger two-dimensional shape/object on a laminated piece of paper that become increasingly more difficult (i.e., missing an increasing number of internal lines on the larger shape/object).

Finally, children complete a task designed to elicit frustration/distress that involves an appealing toy (non-food) in a Locked Box task. At age 3.5 years, children are presented with three toys (light up unicorn robot, remote controlled car, cell phone) and asked to select their favorite, which is then locked in a transparent box [125, 162]. Children are shown how to use a key to open the lock and then given a set of keys (none of which open the box) and encouraged to open the box for 3 min. After the task, the box is opened and the child is allowed to play with the toy. At age 5, the widely used distress eliciting Not Sharing task [163, 164] has been modified to establish a new Not Sharing (Non-food) task to assess children’s response when coins, not candy, are divided unequally. Specifically, a prize box and bag of pretend coins are brought into the room. The experimenter and child are told that the coins should be divided equally and then can be traded in for a prize from the box. However, the experimenter does not share the coins evenly and asks the child repeatedly how this makes them feel. At the end of the task, the experimenter acknowledges that they were not sharing the coins evenly and gives the child two coins to trade in for a prize.

3.5- and 5-year visit 2

During the second visit at each wave, the child again comes to the campus laboratory. For the visit, mothers sit in an adjacent room to the child and can watch through a 1-way mirror. First, electrodes are placed on the child again and they complete a 2 min seated, resting baseline, mirroring procedures from visit 1. Then, children complete a liking assessment in which the child is asked to taste each food and rate their liking using three smiley faces that represent “yummy”, “yucky”, or “just okay” [165]. Children select their favorite low-energy-dense and their favorite high-energy-dense food for subsequent use in the Relative Reinforcing Value of food (RRV) task [166–168]. Next, in the Gift Delay task the experimenter brings a small, wrapped gift into the room and places it on the table in front of the child and explains that it is a gift for the child, but that they forgot a bow for the gift; the child is told to not touch/open it until the experimenter returns. The child is left in the room for 3 (3.5-year) or 5 (5-year) mins. After the given amount of time, the experimenter comes back to the room, places the bow on the gift, and allows the child to open it [158, 164, 169].

Next, the child completes the Go No-Go (Food Go-No Go or Zoo Go-No Go) task that they did not complete in Visit 1, followed by a frustration/distress eliciting task that mirrors the Locked Box task but involves food, not a toy, as a stimulus. Specifically, at the 3.5-year visit, children choose a desirable food (Food in Unopenable Container task; Dum Dum lollipopsTM Pirates Booty puffsTM). Unbeknownst to the child, the snack is given to them in a container that is unopenable, and the child is told to open the container while the experimenter goes to get them a drink (3 min). When the experimenter returns, the child is given the chosen snack. At the 5-year visit, children engage in the Not Sharing (Food) task, mirroring the Not Sharing (Non-food) task, where children are presented with a situation that they deem unfair (4 min) [163, 164]. Although the experimenter is told to share candy equally with the child, the candy is not split evenly throughout the task and the experimenter continually asks the child how this makes them feel. At the end of the task, the experimenter acknowledges that they were not sharing the candy evenly and gives the child two pieces of candy.

Next, children complete two academic readiness tasks (~ 10 min; [170–173]) that are unrelated to the primary aims to increase the time between food-related tasks. Finally, children complete the RRV task, a computer-based choice paradigm in which children work to earn a food reward (small portions of low energy-dense food [i.e., canned mandarin oranges 1 piece), peaches (~ 1/2 slice), and pineapples (1 piece)] and high energy-dense foods [i.e., gummy bears (~ 2 gummy bears), chocolate kisses (~ 1 kiss), mini chocolate chip cookies (~ 1 cookie)]). Children are instructed that they can earn the low-energy dense food (i.e., fruit) by pressing the mouse to their right and can earn the high-energy dense food (i.e., sweet treat) by pressing the mouse to their left. A picture of the food that the child selected for the task is placed above its corresponding mouse and a light is positioned next to each mouse, which lights up when the specific food reward is earned. Both mice, connected to hidden computers, are on independent, concurrent progressive-ratio schedules of reinforcement. The schedules begin at 4 clicks and double each time a reward is earned, with a maximum of 8 trials (i.e., 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512 clicks). Within the two food options (fruit and sweet treat), weight in grams was balanced as closely as possible based on the weight of one portion of each food. Matching within, but not across food categories, prevents a potential portion size effect [174], since matching across categories will result in larger portions of low-energy dense vs high-energy dense foods. Children indicate when they are done, or the task is ended after 20 min. After the task, the experimenter weighs the food, the child is allowed 10 min to eat as many of the earned snacks as they want, and then the final snacks are weighed again.

General self-regulation

Children’s general self-regulation will be indexed by 7 indicators: Delay of gratification non-food (Gift Delay composite rating (mean of individual z-scores): latency to touch gift, frequency of touches (reverse scored), extent of touches [1 (No touch) – 5 (Pulls gift from bag/box) rating scale]) (reverse scored); percent correct on the Non-Food No-Go trials (inhibitory control); standardized composite of percent of time looking at the gift (reverse scored) in the Gift Delay task and interest in the toy in the Locked Box task (attentional focus on toy is important for task demand) and distraction in both tasks (attentional regulation); global emotion regulation rating in the Locked Box/Not Sharing (Non-food) task (5-point scale); mean RSA withdrawal score from baseline to Gift Delay and to Locked Box/Not Sharing (Non-food); mothers’ reports of effortful control on the Child Behavior Questionnaire-VSF [130]; and global executive function on the Behavioral Rating Inventory of Executive Function preschool [175].

Appetite self-regulation

Children’s appetite self-regulation will be indexed by 7 indicators: Delay of gratification-food (Snack Delay composite rating (mean of individual z-scores): latency to touch snack, frequency of touches (reverse scored), extent of touches [1 (No touch) – 5 (eats an M&M) rating scale]) (reverse scored); standardized composite of percent of time looking at the gift (reverse scored) in the Snack Delay task and interest in the food in the Food in Unopenable Container task (attentional focus on food is important for task demand) and distraction in both tasks (attentional regulation); global emotion regulation rating during Food in Unopenable Container/Not Sharing (Food) task [176], mean RSA withdrawal from baseline to Snack Delay and to Food in Unopenable Container/Not Sharing (Food) [177]; percent correct on the Food No-Go trials (inhibitory control), mother’s reports of satiety responsiveness and emotional overeating (reversed) on the Children’s Eating Behavior Questionnaire [178].

Food cue reactivity

Children’s food cue reactivity will be assessed by 5 indicators: the amount of calories consumed in the EAH task [179]; the RRV of food, operationalized as the breakpoint (the highest trial in which responses are made) for high-energy dense in proportion to the breakpoint for low-energy dense food [high-energy dense breakpoint/(high-energy dense breakpoint + low-energy dense breakpoint)]), the response rate for the high-energy dense food reward [average number of responses (button presses) per minute across all high-energy dense trials] during the RRV task [166]; and mother’s reports of enjoyment of food and food responsiveness (1–5; the higher the number the higher the behavior), appetitive behaviors indicative of food approach traits that are purported to contribute to overweight, as reported on the Children’s Eating Behavior Questionnaire [178].

Child negative emotionality

A manifest variable composed of 4 indicators parallel to infant negative emotionality will be the indicator of child negative emotionality. Specifically, observed global negative affect (4-point scale) and peak distress (4-point scale) during Gift and Snack delay tasks, Locked Box (3.5-year), Unopenable Snack (3.5-year), and Not Sharing (5-year) tasks, and latency to distress (reverse scored) during Locked Box (3.5-year), Unopenable Snack (3.5-year), and Not Sharing (5-year) tasks; and maternal reported negative affectivity on the CBQ-VSF [130]. Scores will be averaged across tasks at each time point to yield a single measure of global negative affect, peak distress, and latency to distress.

Child hormonal risk

Hormone levels (adiponectin, insulin, leptin, resistin) derived from urine samples collected using the procedures described above for infant hormonal risk will be the indicator of child hormonal risk. All samples from a child (3.5-year and 5-year) will be assayed after the 5-year wave in a single assay kit to minimize inter-assay variability. A manifest variable will be created to reflect child hormonal risk.

Obesogenic home environment

A manifest variable composed of 4 mother-reported indicators will be the measure of obesogenic home environment: low availability of healthy foods/abundance of unhealthy foods in the home (Comprehensive Home Environment Survey); lack of parental modeling of healthy eating (Comprehensive Feeding Practices Questionnaire) [181, 182]; infrequent/poorly structured family meals (a composite of items/subscales from the Feeding Practices and Structure Questionnaire [144] and the Family Mealtime Questionnaire [143–145]); and feeding practice/styles associated with obesity-related risk (a composite of controlling and permissive practices on the Comprehensive Feeding Practices Questionnaire [182]; parent-centered strategies on the Caregiver’s Feeding Style Questionnaire [183]; and using food as a reward or to soothe negative emotions from maternal reports on the Feeding to Manage Child Behavior Questionnaire [184]).

Health-related routines and practices

A manifest variable of 3 indicators will be the measure of health-related routines and practices: mothers’ reports of providing physical activity support for their child (a composite of physical activity availability, accessibility, modeling [reversed]) and engaging in more permissive screen time practices (a composite of media availability, role modeling, monitoring [reversed]) on the Comprehensive Home Environment Survey [98]; and unhealthy sleep routines/practices for their child, derived from the Sleep Hygiene Scale [185] and the Bedtime Routines Scale [186].

Negative parenting

A manifest variable composed of 4 indicators will be the measure of negative parenting. Maternal sensitivity (reversed) during the Etch-a-Sketch and Tangrams tasks will be rated using an age-appropriate version of scales described above. Existing studies have coded maternal sensitivity using similar tasks [187]. Non-supportive emotion socialization will be a composite of mothers’ self-reports of supportive (reversed) and non-supportive emotion socialization on the Coping with Children’s Negative Emotions Scale [188]. Negative parenting practices will be a composite of mothers’ self-reports of positive parenting (reversed), inconsistent parenting, and punitive parenting on the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire-Preschool Version [189] and the autonomy support subscale (reversed) of the Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire [190]. Finally, parent–child relationship quality will be a composite of mothers self-reported closeness (reversed) and conflict on the Child Parent Relationship Scale [191].

Statistical plan

Overview

After examining the psychometric properties of study variables, executing any necessary transformations conducted prior to primary analyses and screening potential covariates for inclusion, the analyses will be conducted using SEM. SEM is ideal for models that include both manifest and latent variables. The model to address Aim 1A will include latent constructs determined by the CFA (Fig. 1). We plan for the models to address Aims 1B, 1C, and 2 to include latent (ovals in Figs. 2, 3, and 4) and manifest (boxes in Figs. 2 and 4) constructs. The measurement models for the latent constructs will be examined, followed by estimations of structural models to test specific hypotheses. Model goodness-of-fit will be evaluated using four fit indices: chi-square goodness of fit (non-significant), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; values ≤ 0.05), comparative fit index (CFI; values ≥ 0.95), and the Tucker-Lewis index (values ≥ 0.95) [205]. Where necessary, the difference in the fit of nested models will be examined using differences in the RMSEA and CFI values. Full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation methods will be used for missing data since these produce less biased estimates [206].

Examining aim 1

Aim 1A will determine the factor structure of self-regulation and appetite-related constructs among preschoolers. Nineteen continuous indicators of general and appetite self-regulation and food cue reactivity at age 3.5 (Table 2) will be employed in a CFA. Four primary models will be tested: (Model 1) a one-factor model (i.e., general and appetite self-regulation, food cue reactivity); (Model 2) a two-factor model, with correlated factors (i.e., appetite self-regulation/food cue reactivity, general self-regulation); (Model 3) a two-factor model, with correlated factors (i.e., general and appetite self-regulation, food cue reactivity); (Model 4) a three-factor model with correlated factors (i.e., general self-regulation, appetite self-regulation, food cue reactivity). If models fail to reach adequate fit, modification indices will be utilized [207, 208] in concert with theory and a priori hypothesized alternatives.

Aim 1B will determine the extent to which general self-regulation, appetite self-regulation, and food cue reactivity have unique versus overlapping early life antecedents. All predictors (infant negative emotionality, infant hormonal risk, maternal sensitivity, and obesogenic feeding practices) will be manifest variables consisting of the mean of the standardized measures. Using the factor solution from Aim 1A, we will estimate a structural model to test the specific hypotheses by examining the statistical significance of hypothesized paths. Moderation will be tested with manifest interaction terms. Follow-up analyses for significant interactions will follow [209].

Aim 1C will determine the extent to which preschoolers’ general and appetite self-regulation and food cue reactivity independently predict later obesity-related risk (Fig. 3). Using the factor solution identified in Aim 1A, we will estimate a structural model to test general self-regulation, appetite self-regulation, and food cue reactivity as simultaneous predictors of obesity-related risk. Obesity-related risk at age 5 will be a reflective, continuous latent construct with four indicators assessed: BMI-for-age z-score based on WHO growth standards and/or 2000 CDC growth references, total adiposity (% body fat), central adiposity (waist circumference-to-height ratio), and inflammatory markers. The same fit indices used to assess the CFA models will be used to evaluate goodness-of-fit.

Examining aim 2

Aim 2 will determine the extent to which each type of self-regulation moderates the associations between specific child and home environment risk factors and obesity-related risk (Fig. 4). The food cue reactivity factor score will be saved from the Aim 1A solution and used to create a manifest variable. The other child-related (negative emotionality, hormonal risk, obesity-related risk) and home environment-related risk constructs at age 3.5 years (obesogenic environment, poor health routines, negative parenting) will be manifest variables created from the mean of the standardized measures. General and appetite self-regulation will be latent variables as determined by results from Aim 1A. Age 5 obesity-related risk will be a latent variable. Three structural models will be tested. The first model will include all child risk factors as direct and interactive effects with self-regulation as predictors of child obesity-related risk at age 5. The second model will include all home environment risk factors as direct and interactive effects with self-regulation as predictors of child obesity-related risk at age 5. The third model will include any child and home environment risk factors that were significant main or interactive effects in models 1 and 2. The benefit of this approach is that the within-domain (child, home environment) risks that matter most, independently and in conjunction with regulatory constructs, will be identified before a more comprehensive model is pursued, ensuring adequate power relative to sample size. Moderation will be tested with latent interaction terms using the XWITH command, although manifest interaction terms can be employed if needed [210]. Significant interactions will be probed following standard procedures.

Discussion

The iGrowUp Study will make several important theoretical, methodological, and translational contributions to our understanding of biopsychosocial pathways, especially related to the role of general and appetite self-regulation and food cue reactivity, in the development of childhood obesity. First, this study is among the first to integrate developmental, nutrition, and biopsychosocial theoretical frameworks and measurement strategies in the key developmental period of early childhood. Second, this study will make innovative and important empirical advances by using comprehensive and comparable self-regulation measures in both food and non-food contexts. Specifically, we are the first to employ parallel protocols equated for duration, nature of the stressor, and behavioral coding systems, to provide methodologically rigorous assessments of children’s self-regulation in response to food and non-food stimuli, safeguarding interpretation and application of findings. This, in some cases, required designing new tasks that mirror established, widely used tasks and measures that have sound psychometric properties. Furthermore, we fill a critical gap in the current literature by examining food cue reactivity, so called bottom-up appetite traits, along with general and appetite self-regulation in our models. Third, this study is among the first to test a comprehensive biopsychosocial model of predictors of obesity-related risk in early childhood by integrating key biological, psychological/behavioral, and home environmental risks, and their interactions with specific self-regulatory buffers to address the multifactorial antecedents of childhood obesity. This is a clear departure from existing literature that include a small subset of these factors and largely test simple main effects and/or are cross-sectional in design.

Fourth, despite growing awareness that metabolic hormones play an important role in the development of obesity-related risk, there is little to no empirical evidence of these associations, and it is almost exclusively based on cross-sectional studies. This study is among the first to examine the role of hormones in obesity in early childhood in a community sample of preschoolers. Further, data collected as part of the iGrow and iGrowUp studies, can be used to identify developmental trajectories of obesity-related hormones from infancy through preschool, providing key information to both basic and applied scientists across disciplines. Finally, our comprehensive measures of obesity-related risk comprise a highly novel approach for early childhood. This is a critical next step to appropriately inform preventive intervention efforts for mitigating poor child health outcomes. By considering these key processes within the context of one another, and aiming to refine key constructs related to obesity, this study is poised to identify specific points of entry and important targets for obesity intervention efforts.
