Abstract
Syntactic NL or succinctly SNL was first introduced in 2017, analogously to SNP, as a “syntactically”-defined natural subclass of NL (nondeterministic logarithmic-space complexity class) using a restricted form of logical sentences, starting with second-order “functional” existential quantifiers followed by first-order universal quantifiers, in close connection to the so-called linear space hypothesis. We further explore various properties of this complexity class SNL to achieve the better understandings of logical expressibility in NL. For instance, SNL does not enjoy the dichotomy theorem unless L = = NL. To express the “complementary” problems of SNL problems logically, we introduce μ ​ SNL \mu\mathrm{SNL} , which is an extension of SNL by allowing the use of μ \mu -terms. As natural variants of SNL \mathrm{SNL} , we further study the computational complexity of monotone and optimization versions of SNL, respectively called MonoSNL and MAXSNL. We further consider maximization problems that are logarithmic-space approximable with only constant approximation ratios. We then introduce a natural subclass of MAXSNL, called MAX τ \tau SNL, which enjoys such limited approximability. keywords: second-order logic, NL, SNL, monotone SNL, optimization problem, MAX-CUT, MAX-UK

Motivational Discussion on Syntactic NL

Since its importance was first recognized in the 1970s, the

From a completely distinct perspective, another significant method in measuring the complexity of
\mathrm{NP}

In the late 1990s, Papadimitriou and Yannakakis

This complexity class

P(\cdot,\cdot)
works as a “function”; namely,
Func(P)\equiv Func_{1}(P)\wedge Func_{2}(P)
, where
Func_{1}(P)\equiv(\forall i)(\exists w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})[P(i,w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})]
and
Func_{2}(P)\equiv(\forall i,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})[P(i,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\wedge P(i,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\rightarrow\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}(u_{i}=v_{i})]
.
Here, the symbol “
\exists^{f}
” is emphatically called the
functional existential quantifier
and the variable
P
(in the scope of
\exists^{f}
) is called a
functional variable
.

Definition 2.3

Let
{\cal V}=\{(S_{i},k_{i}),c_{j},0,n,suc,pred\mid i\in[d],j\in[d^{\prime}]\}
denote a vocabulary. A
syntactic NL sentence
(or an
SNL sentence
) over
{\cal V}
is a second-order sentence
\Phi
of the form:

\displaystyle\Phi\equiv\exists^{f}P_{1}\cdots\exists^{f}P_{l}\>\forall i_{1}\cdots\forall i_{r}\>\forall y_{1}\cdots\forall y_{s}

\displaystyle[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},y_{1},\ldots,y_{s},S_{1},\ldots,S_{d},c_{1},\ldots,c_{d^{\prime}})],

where
l,r,s,t\in\mathbb{N}
and each
\psi_{j}
(
j\in[t]
) is a quantifier-free second-order formula for which no two
\psi_{j}
’s share any common first-order variables, where all variables are listed on the above expression of
\psi_{j}
only for simplicity.
Here,
P_{1},\ldots,P_{l}
are second-order functional variables
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
are first-order variables representing natural numbers, and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}
are also first-order variables representing all other input objects. Each formula
\psi_{j}
should satisfy the following two
second-order variable requirements
.

(i)

Each
\psi_{j}
contains only second-order variables of the form
P_{k}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{1}),P_{k}(suc(i),\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{2}),P_{k}(suc^{2}(i),\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{3}),\ldots
,
P_{k}(suc^{a}(i),\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{a+1})
for a fixed constant
a\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, where each of
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{a+1}
is a
k^{\prime}
tuple of terms for a fixed constant
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

(ii)

\psi_{j}
can be rewritten in the logically-equivalent form of finite “disjunctions” satisfying the following condition: among those disjuncts, there are only at most two disjuncts containing second-order variables and each of them must have the form
(\bigwedge_{k,i,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}P_{k}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}))\wedge(\bigwedge_{k^{\prime},i^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$v^{\prime}$}}\neg P_{k^{\prime}}(i^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$v^{\prime}$}))\wedge R
, where
R
is an appropriate subformula including no second-order variable.

The requirement (ii) was originally introduced as a natural analogue of the formulation of 2SAT. It is significantly important to guarantee that SNL (defined in Definition
2.6
) is contained in NL.

Next, we explain the semantics of
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences.

Definition 2.4

Let
{\cal V}=\{(S_{i},k_{i}),c_{j},0,n,suc,pred\mid i\in[d],j\in[d^{\prime}]\}
denote any vocabulary.

(1) A
relational structure

{\cal S}
over
{\cal V}
is a set of tuples
(U_{i},D_{i},k_{i})
and
(\underline{c}_{j},V_{j})
with finite universes
U_{i}
and
V_{j}
of “input objects” (including natural numbers) and domains
D_{i}
associated with predicate symbols
S_{i}
in
{\cal V}
satisfying
D_{i}\subseteq U_{i}^{k_{i}}
, and constants
\underline{c}_{j}
in
V_{j}
.
The constant symbols
c_{j}
are interpreted as
\bar{c}_{j}
and the predicate symbols
S_{i}
are interpreted as
D_{i}
so that, if input objects
\underline{s}_{1},\underline{s}_{2},\ldots,\underline{s}_{k_{i}}
in
U_{i}
are assigned respectively to variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k_{i}}
used for
S_{i}
, the formula
S_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k_{i}})
is true exactly when
(\underline{s}_{1},\underline{s}_{2},\ldots,\underline{s}_{k_{i}})\in D_{i}
.

(2) Let
\Phi
denote any SNL sentence of the form of Definition
2.3
with variables
P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}
over
{\cal V}
.
A
domain structure

{\cal D}
for
\Phi
is the union of three sets
\{(P_{j},[0,e_{j}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\times U^{\prime}_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times U^{\prime}_{j_{k^{\prime}}},k^{\prime}+1)\}_{j\in[l]}
,
\{(i_{j},[0,e^{\prime}_{j}]_{\mathbb{Z}})\}_{j\in[r]}
, and
\{(y_{j},U^{\prime\prime}_{j})\}_{j\in[s]}
, which provide the scopes of variables of
\Phi
in the following manner for fixed constants
e_{j},e^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Each second-order variable
P_{j}
(
j\in[l]
) ranges over all elements in
[0,e_{j}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\times U^{\prime}_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times U^{\prime}_{j_{k^{\prime}}}
, each first-order variable
i_{j}
(
j\in[r]
) ranges over all numbers in
[0,e^{\prime}_{j}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and each variable
y_{j}
(
j\in[s]
) ranges over all elements in
U^{\prime\prime}_{j}
.

Concrete examples of relational and domain structures will be given in Examples
2.8
and
2.9
.

Definition 2.5

A relational structure
{\cal S}
over vocabulary
{\cal V}
is said to
describe
(or
represent
) an instance
x
of the target computational problem if every input object appearing in
x
has either its corresponding predicate symbol in
{\cal V}
with its universe and domain in
{\cal S}
or its corresponding constant symbol in
{\cal V}
with its universe in
{\cal S}
.

It is important to remark that, when
{\cal S}
describes
x
, since the universes
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
must be completely specified inside
x
, their sizes
|U_{i}|
and
|V_{i}|
should be upper-bounded by
O(|x|)
.

When a relational structure
{\cal S}
and a domain structure
{\cal D}
are given for an SNL sentence
\Phi
, it is possible to determine the
validity
of
\Phi
by interpreting all predicate symbols
S_{i}
and all constant symbols
c_{j}
appearing in
\Phi
as domains
D_{i}
and constants
\underline{c}_{j}
in
{\cal S}
and by assigning input objects in
{\cal S}
and
{\cal D}
to variables appropriately.
This interpretation makes
\Phi
either “true” or “false”.
Notationally, we write
({\cal S},{\cal D})\models\Phi
if
\Phi
is true on
{\cal S}
and
{\cal D}
.
When
({\cal S},{\cal D})
are clear from the context, nevertheless, we further omit
({\cal S},{\cal D})
and simply write
\models\Phi
.

Definition 2.6

Given a decision problem
A
and an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence
\Phi
over vocabulary
{\cal V}
, we say that
\Phi

syntactically expresses

A
if, for any instance
x
to
A
, there are a relational structure
{\cal S}_{x}
over
{\cal V}
describing
x
and a domain structure
{\cal D}_{x}
for
\Phi
satisfying the following condition:
x\in A
iff
\Phi
is true on
{\cal S}_{x}
and
{\cal D}_{x}
.

It is possible to view the syntactical expressibility by an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence as a (non-probabilistic) Merlin-Arthur interactive proof system, in which Merlin provides a polynomial-size “proof” to Arthur who check the validity of the proof by a logarithmically space-bounded algorithm.

Definition 2.7

We denote by
\mathrm{SNL}
the collection of all decision problems
A
such that there exist a vocabulary
{\cal V}
and an SNL sentence
\Phi
over
{\cal V}
for which
\Phi
syntactically expresses
A
.

In
[
28
]
, the parameterized decision problem
(\mathrm{DSTCON},m_{ver})
was shown to be in
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}
, where the size parameter
m_{ver}
indicates the total number of vertices of a given graph.
By reviewing the corresponding proof of this fact, we can conclude that
\mathrm{DSTCON}
belongs to (the “non-parameterized” class)
\mathrm{SNL}
.
As another quick example, we see how to construct an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence to express the decision problem
\mathrm{2COLOR}
, in which one asks whether a given undirected graph is colorable using only two colors. This is the same as checking that a given graph is bipartite.
It is known that
\mathrm{2COLOR}
falls in
\mathrm{L}
(see
[
2
]
with the fact that
\mathrm{SL}=\mathrm{L}
).

Example 2.8

We wish to show that
\mathrm{2COLOR}
belongs to
\mathrm{SNL}
by constructing an appropriate
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence for
\mathrm{2COLOR}
.
Given an instance
x
of an undirected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
, we assume that
V_{G}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. We identify each vertex
v_{i}
with the integer
i
.
Hence,
V_{G}
is viewed as
[n]
and
E_{G}
is viewed as a subset of
[n]\times[n]
.
Let
E
denote a predicate symbol associated with
E_{G}
.
We define
{\cal V}=\{(E,2),0,1\}
and
{\cal S}_{x}=\{(U_{x},D_{x},2),(0,V_{1}),(1,V_{2})\}
with
U_{x}=[n]
,
D_{x}=\{(i,j)\mid(x_{i},x_{j})\in\hat{E}\}
,
V_{1}=\{0\}
, and
V_{2}=\{1\}
. Clearly,
{\cal S}_{x}
describes
x
.
Next, we define a sentence
\Phi
to be
(\exists^{f}C)(\forall i,d,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},d^{\prime},e^{\prime})[\Phi_{1}(C,i,d)\wedge\Phi_{2}(C,E,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},d^{\prime},e^{\prime})]
, where
i,j,i^{\prime},d,d^{\prime},e^{\prime}
are symbols expressing the first-order variables ranging over
[n]
,
\Phi_{1}(C,i,d)\equiv C(i,d)\to 0\leq d\leq 1
, and
\Phi_{2}(C,E,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},d^{\prime},e^{\prime})\equiv E(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\wedge C(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})\wedge C(j^{\prime},e^{\prime})\to d^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime}
. The sentence
\Phi
informally asserts that, for an appropriate coloring of vertices, (i) we use only two colors and (ii) two endpoints of each edge are colored by distinct colors.
By rewriting
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
in the disjunction form, we can show that
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
both satisfy the second-order variable requirements.
To see this fact, we note that
\Phi_{1}
is logically equivalent to
\neg C(i,d)\vee 0\leq d\leq 1
and that
\Phi_{2}
is to
\neg E(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\vee C(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})\vee C(j^{\prime},e^{\prime})\vee d^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime}
. The two last formulas show that the required conditions on the second-order variables are clearly satisfied.
We further define
{\cal D}_{x}=\{(C,[n]\times\{0,1\},2)\}\cup\{(s,[n])\mid s\in\{i,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}\}\cup\{(s^{\prime},[2])\mid s^{\prime}\in\{d,d^{\prime},e^{\prime}\}\}
.
It then follows that
\Phi
is true on
{\cal S}_{x}
and
{\cal D}_{x}
iff
x\in\mathrm{2COLOR}
.

unary 0-1 knapsack problem
(UK), which was discussed by Cook

(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})
of unary strings with
b,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and one asks to determine the existence of a subset
S
of
[n]
satisfying
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}=b
.
It was shown in
[

, called

Example 2.9

We claim that the decision problem
\mathrm{UK}
is also in
\mathrm{SNL}
. To see this, let
x=(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})
denote any instance given to
\mathrm{UK}
. For simplicity, we assume that
a_{i}\leq b
for all indices
i\in[n]
.
We then prepare two predicate symbols
I
and
ADD
for which
I(i,a)
means that
a
is the
i
th input value
a_{i}
of
x
and
ADD(c,a,b)
means
c=a+b
. We set
\Phi\equiv(\exists^{f}P)(\forall i,s,t)[P(0,0)\wedge P(n,b)\wedge(\psi_{1}(P,i,s,t)\to\psi_{2}(P,I,ADD,s,t))]
, where
\psi_{1}\equiv i<n\wedge P(i,s)\wedge P(i+1,t)
and
\psi_{2}\equiv s=t\leq b\vee(s<t\leq b\wedge(\forall z)[I(i+1,z)\wedge z>0\to ADD(t,s,z)])
.
Notice that the formula
\psi_{1}\to\psi_{2}
satisfies the second-order variable requirements since
\psi_{1}\to\psi_{2}
can be rewritten as
\neg P(i,s)\vee\neg P(i+1,t)\vee R
for an appropriate formula
R
containing no second-order variables. We then conclude that
\Phi
is an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence.

We set
{\cal V}=\{(I,2),(ADD,3),0,n,b\}
, where
b
is treated as a constant. We define
{\cal S}_{x}=\{(U_{x},D_{I},2),(U_{x},D_{ADD},3),(\bar{b},U_{x})\}
, where
U_{x}=[0,b]_{\mathbb{Z}}
,
D_{I}=\{(\underline{i},\underline{a})\mid\underline{i}\in[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}},\underline{a}\in U_{x}\}
, and
D_{ADD}=\{(\underline{t},\underline{s},\underline{z})\mid\underline{t},\underline{s},\underline{z}\in U_{x},\underline{t}=\underline{s}+\underline{z}\}
. Moreover, we set
{\cal D}_{x}=\{(P,U_{n}\times U_{x},2)\}\cup\{(i,U_{n})\}\cup\{(u,U_{x})\mid u\in\{s,t,z\}\}
with
U_{n}=[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
It then follows that
x=(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})\in\mathrm{UK}
iff
\Phi
is true on
{\cal S}_{x}
and
{\cal D}_{x}
.

A subclass of
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}
, which is called
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
, was also introduced in
[
28
]
. This subclass has a direct connection to the linear space hypothesis (LSH). Here, we introduce its “non-parameterized” version as follows.

Definition 2.10

The complexity class
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
is composed of all decision problems
A
in
\mathrm{SNL}
that enjoys the following extra requirements. Let
\Phi
denote any SNL-sentence of the form given in Definition
2.3
with
t
quantifier-free subformulas
\psi_{j}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$},S_{1},\ldots,S_{d},c_{1},\ldots,c_{d^{\prime}})
together with (hidden) sentence
Func(P_{i})
for all
i\in[l]
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})
.
Assume that
\Phi
syntactically expresses
A
by a certain relational structure
{\cal S}_{x}
and a certain domain structure
{\cal D}_{x}
associated with each instance
x
given to
A
.
Here, each
\psi_{j}
must satisfy the second-order variable requirements.
We further demand that the sentence
(\bigwedge_{h=1}^{l}Func(P_{h}))
must be “expressed” inside
\Phi
with no use of existential quantifiers “
\exists
” in the following sense:
\exists P_{1}\cdots\exists P_{l}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}[(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j})\wedge(\bigwedge_{h=1}^{l}Func(P_{h}))]
is true iff
\exists P_{1}\cdots\exists P_{l}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}\psi_{j}]
is true, where “
\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}
” and “
\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}
” are respectively shorthands for
\forall i_{1}\forall i_{2}\cdots\forall i_{r}
and
\forall y_{1}\forall y_{2}\cdots\forall y_{s}
.

As in
[
28
]
, let us consider
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
, which is the directed
s
-
t
connectivity problem restricted to directed graphs of degree exactly
3
. In the parameterized setting, it was shown in
[
28
]
, the parameterized decision problem
(\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON},m_{ver})
belongs to
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
. In essence, a similar argument leads to the claim that
\mathrm{exact}2\mathrm{DSTCON}
is in
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
.

3
Structural Properties of SNL

Through Section
2
, we have reviewed from
[
28
]
the logical notion of
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences
and the associated complexity class
SNL
. In succession to the previous section, we intend to study the structural properties of
\mathrm{SNL}
in depth. In particular, we are focused on the closure properties of SNL under Boolean operations.

3.1
Basic Closure Properties and L-m-Reductions

It is known that
\mathrm{NL}
is closed under union, intersection, and complementation. Similarly,
\mathrm{SNL}
enjoys the closure properties under union and intersection.

Proposition 3.1

\mathrm{SNL}
is closed under union and intersection.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
denote two arbitrary decision problems in
\mathrm{SNL}
. Take
\mathrm{SNL}
-sentences
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
that syntactically express
A
and
B
, respectively. Since
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
are
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences, we assume that
\Phi_{A}\equiv\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}]
and
\Phi_{B}\equiv\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i^{\prime}$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y^{\prime}$}[\bigwedge_{j^{\prime}=1}^{s}\xi_{j^{\prime}}]
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}=(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{l})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}=(R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{l^{\prime}})
, and
\psi_{j}
’s and
\xi_{j^{\prime}}
’s are all quantifier-free formulas and that
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
satisfy the second-order variable requirements.
Assume further that each
\psi_{k}
has the form
\bigvee_{k_{j}}\hat{\psi}_{k_{j}}
and each
\xi_{j^{\prime}}
has the form
\bigvee_{l_{j^{\prime}}}\hat{\xi}_{l_{j^{\prime}}}
.
For simplicity, all elements in
(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$})
and
(\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$i^{\prime}$},\mbox{\boldmath$y^{\prime}$})
do not share any common variables.

For the target intersection
C=A\cap B
, we define
\Phi\equiv\Phi_{A}\wedge\Phi_{B}
, which is logically equivalent to
\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i^{\prime}$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y^{\prime}$}[(\bigwedge_{j}\psi_{j})\wedge(\bigwedge_{j^{\prime}}\xi_{j^{\prime}})]
.
Since all
\psi_{j}
’s and
\xi_{j^{\prime}}
’s satisfy the second-order variable requirements, so does the formula
\phi\equiv(\bigwedge_{j}\psi_{j})\wedge(\bigwedge_{j^{\prime}}\xi_{j^{\prime}})
. Hence,
\Phi
is also an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence. By definition,
\Phi
syntactically expresses
C
.

For the case of union,
C^{\prime}=A\cup B
, we cannot simply define a sentence
\Phi^{\prime}
as
\Phi^{\prime}\equiv\Phi_{A}\vee\Phi_{B}
using
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
. Instead, we need to define
\Phi^{\prime}
as follows. Let us introduce a new variable
k
, which is assumed to take either
1
or
2
. This
k
is intended to indicate which of
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
is true.
Let us first define
\Xi_{1}\equiv k=1\to\bigwedge_{j}\psi_{j}
and
\Xi_{2}\equiv k=2\to\bigwedge_{j^{\prime}}\xi_{j^{\prime}}
. We then define
\Phi^{\prime}\equiv\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i^{\prime}$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y^{\prime}$}\forall k[1\leq k\leq 2\to\Xi_{1}\wedge\Xi_{2}]
. Notice that
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
are rephrased as
\Xi_{1}\equiv\bigwedge_{j}(\neg(1\leq k\leq 2)\vee k\neq 1\vee\psi_{j})
and
\Xi_{2}\equiv\bigwedge_{j^{\prime}}(\neg(1\leq k\leq 2)\vee k\neq 2\vee\xi_{j^{\prime}})
.
Clearly,
\Xi
is logically equivalent to the conjunction of
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
.
It is not difficult to check that the formula
1\leq k\leq 2\to\Xi_{1}\wedge\Xi_{2}
satisfies the second-order variable requirements. It thus follows that
\Phi^{\prime}
syntactically expresses
C^{\prime}
.

\Box

Given a decision problem
A
, the notation
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(A)
expresses the collection of all decision problems that are
\mathrm{L}
-m-reducible to
A
. Furthermore, for a given complexity class
{\cal C}
,
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!({\cal C})
denotes the union
\bigcup_{A\in{\cal C}}\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(A)
.
Since
\mathrm{NL}
is closed under
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions,
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{NL})=\mathrm{NL}
follows.
Concerning SNL, we obtain the following.

Proposition 3.2

\mathrm{NL}=\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega})=\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{SNL})
.

Proof.

By definition,
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}\subseteq\mathrm{SNL}
follows. We thus obtain
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega})\subseteq\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{SNL})
.

In the “non-parameterized” setting of this work, it is possible to rephrase this inclusion
as
\mathrm{SNL}\subseteq\mathrm{NL}
by restricting corresponding size parameters to
m_{\|}
.
Therefore, we obtain

\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{SNL})\subseteq\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{NL})=\mathrm{NL}
.

In the parameterized setting, it is shown in
[
28
]
that
(\mathrm{exact3}\mathrm{DSTCON},m_{ver})
is complete for
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
under so-called
short SLRF-T-reductions
and it is also
short L-m-reducible
to
(3\mathrm{DSTCON},m_{ver})
.
It is also shown in
[
28
]
that the decision problem
\mathrm{exact3}\mathrm{DSTCON}
is complete for
\mathrm{NL}
under
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions. These results together imply that
\mathrm{NL}\subseteq\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!\!(\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega})
.

\Box

Actually, we can assert a stronger statement than Proposition
3.2
. Here, we intend to claim that
\mathrm{SNL}
occupies a “structurally” important portion of
\mathrm{NL}
in the sense described in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

For any decision problem in
\mathrm{NL}
, there always exists its
\mathrm{L}

3.2

We have demonstrated in Proposition

We show that not only

Proposition 3.5

\mathrm{SNL}

Proof.

Given an undirected graph

, we prepare two predicate symbols,

corresponds to

\Phi\equiv(\exists^{f}P)(\forall i,k,l)(\forall u,v)[1\leq i<n\wedge(\bigwedge_{m=1}^{5}\Phi_{m})]

\Phi_{1}\equiv P(1,u,k)\to k=1

\Phi_{2}\equiv P(i,u,k)\wedge P(i+1,v,l)\to((l=k\wedge u=v)\vee(l=k+1\wedge u\neq v))

\Box

Next, let us consider the decision problem
\mathrm{DSTCON}

\mathrm{SNL}

[

\mathrm{NL}

\mathrm{SNL}

To tackle this question, we intend to expand the complexity class

by introducing the additional
\mu
-operator
applied to second-order variables. In the definition of SNL, second-order variables are treated as functional variables, indicating “functions” from natural numbers to tuples of various objects.
Given a second-order variable

Definition 3.6

A

-term

-operator.

Definition 3.7

We naturally expand

(iii)

The sentences in this expanded logical system of

Since

\Box

Claim 2

.

\Box

Moreover, we introduce
\Psi_{4}(P,N,C)

\Psi_{4}\equiv B_{1}(w,u,i)\wedge B_{2}(w^{\prime},u,n,i)\to[\neg\tilde{P}(w,u,i,u)\leftrightarrow\tilde{C}(w^{\prime},u,n,i,\mu h.N(w^{\prime},h))
.

Notice that the

The desired sentence

(\exists^{f}P,N,C)[\Psi_{0}(P)\wedge\Psi_{1}(P)\wedge\Psi_{2}(P,N)\wedge\Psi_{3}(P,C)\wedge\Psi_{4}(P,N,C)]

with the above three additional formulas

By induction on

Claim 3

Assume that

Let us recall the notations

.

In what follows, we examine the case of

.

\Box

4
Monotone Variant of SNL

We have discussed the basic structural properties in Section
3
. We next intend to expand the scope of our study on
\mathrm{SNL}
in hopes of making its direct application to other areas of computer science.
In the past literature, there have been intensive studies on a wide range of variations of
\mathrm{SNP}
.
We focus on the monotone restriction of
\mathrm{SNP}
(
\mathrm{MonoSNP}
). In natural analogy, we intend to investigate similar concepts induced from
\mathrm{SNL}
and to study their specific characteristics.

4.1
Monotone SNL (or MonoSNL)

In the polynomial-time setting, Feder and Vardi
[
8
,
9
]
studied structural properties of two restricted versions of
\mathrm{SNP}
, called
monotone SNP
(MonoSNP) and
monotone monadic SNP
(MMSNP). Bodinsky, Chen, and Feder
[
4
]
later gave a characterization of MMSNP under a certain natural restriction.
In a similar fashion, let us consider a natural subclass of
\mathrm{SNL}
, which we call the
monotone SNL
or succinctly
MonoSNL
.
Suppose that our vocabulary
{\cal V}
contains predicate symbols
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{d}
.
Given a formula
\Phi
over
{\cal V}
, we then transform it to its conjunctive normal form (CNF).
This formula
\Phi
is said to be
monotone
if the predicates
S_{j}
’s appearing in this CNF formula are all negative (i.e., of the form
\neg S_{i}(v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{k}})
).
In what follows, we wish to study the expressibility of monotone
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences.

Definition 4.1

The notation
\mathrm{Mono}\mathrm{SNL}
denotes the class of all decision problems that are syntactically expressed by monotone
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences.

It turns out that
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
contains natural
\mathrm{NL}
problems. For example, the problem
\mathrm{2COLOR}
, discussed in Example
2.8
, belongs to
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
. Another example is the problem
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
.

Example 4.2

The problem
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
is in
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
. Consider any instance
(G,s,t)
of
\mathrm{exact3}\mathrm{DSTCON}
with
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
and
s,t\in V_{G}
. For simplicity, we assume that
s
has indegree
0
. We naturally extend
G
by including the edge
(t,t)
and write
G^{(ext)}
for this extended graph.
We then introduce a predicate symbol
E
, which represents the edge set
E_{G}
.
For a second-order variable
P
,
P(i,u)
semantically indicates that a given vertex
u
is the
i
th element of a path of
G^{(ext)}
.
We define an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence
\Phi
to be
(\exists^{f}P)(\forall i,u,v,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})[P(0,s)\wedge P(n,t)\wedge\Phi_{1}\wedge\Phi_{2}\wedge\Phi_{3}]
, where

\Phi_{1}\equiv P(0,s)\wedge P(1,v)\wedge(\bigwedge_{k=1}^{3}E(s,v_{k}))\wedge v_{1}\neq v_{2}\neq v_{3}\neq v_{1}\to\bigvee_{k=1}^{3}(v=v_{k})
,

\Phi_{2}\equiv 0<i<n\wedge P(i,u)\wedge P(i+1,v)\wedge(\bigwedge_{k=1}^{2}E(u,v_{k}))\wedge v_{1}\neq v_{2}\to\bigvee_{k=1}^{2}(v=v_{k})
, and
\Phi_{3}\equiv 0\leq i<n\wedge P(i,t)\to P(i+1,t)
.
Intuitively,
\Phi_{1}
means that the path contains an edge from
s
,
\Phi_{2}
means that, if vertex
u
is in the path, then the path contains an edge from
u
, and
\Phi_{3}
means that, if the path reaches
t
at some point, then the path stays on
t
.
It thus follows by definition that
\Phi
syntactically expresses
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
.
The formula
\Phi
is monotone because
\Phi_{1}
is rewritten as
\neg P(0,s)\vee\neg P(1,v)\vee(\bigvee_{k=1}^{3}\neg E(s,v_{k}))\vee\neg(v_{1}\neq v_{2}\neq v_{3}\neq v_{1})\vee\bigvee_{k=1}^{3}(v=v_{k})
and
\Phi_{2}
is rewritten as

\neg(0<i<n)\vee\neg P(i,u)\vee\neg P(i+1,v)\vee(\bigvee_{k=1}^{2}\neg E(u,v_{k}))\vee v_{1}=v_{2}\vee\bigvee_{k=1}^{2}(v=v_{k})
.

Example
4.2
immediately leads to the following consequence.

Corollary 4.3

\mathrm{NL}=\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(\mathrm{Mono}\mathrm{SNL})
.

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathrm{MonoSNL}\subseteq\mathrm{SNL}\subseteq\mathrm{NL}
follows by definition. We thus obtain
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(\mathrm{MonoSNL})\subseteq\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(\mathrm{NL})=\mathrm{NL}
.
By Example
4.2
,
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
contains the decision problem
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
. Since
\mathrm{exact}3\mathrm{DSTCON}
is complete for
\mathrm{NL}
under
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions (see, e.g.,
[
28
]
), we instantly obtain
\mathrm{NL}\subseteq\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(\mathrm{MonoSNL})
.

\Box

Next, we ask whether the
dichotomy theorem
holds for
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
; namely, every decision problem in
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
is either in
\mathrm{L}
or complete for
\mathrm{NL}
under
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions. We do not know that this is the case. This situation is compared to the case of
\mathrm{SNL}
as shown in Corollary
3.4
. However, if
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
equals
\mathrm{SNL}
, then
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
is unlikely enjoy the dichotomy theorem.

In close relation to the dichotomy theorem, as a simple example, we examine the “monotone” segments of 2SAT (
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
and
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
) founded on the notion of the
polarity
of 2CNF Boolean formulas.
Let us recall that a 2CNF formula
\psi
has the form
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}
with
\phi_{i}\equiv z_{i1}\vee z_{i2}
, where each
z_{ij}
is a literal. If every clause
\phi_{i}
is of the form either
x\vee y
or
\overline{x}\vee\overline{y}
for variables
x
and
y
, then
\psi
is said to have
positive polarity
.
In contrast, if every
\phi_{i}
has the form
\overline{x}\vee y
(or
x\vee\overline{y}
), then
\psi
has
negative polarity
. The problem
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
(resp.,
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
) is then defined as the collection of all satisfiable 2CNF formulas that have positive (resp., negative) polarity. These problems
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
and
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
can be syntactically expressed by monotone
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences.

Lemma 4.4

Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
and
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
are both in
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
.

Proof.

We first consider the case of
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
.
Let
\psi
denote any instance of the form
\bigvee_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{i}
given to the decision problem
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
, where each clause
\phi_{i}
is either
x\vee y
or
\overline{x}\vee\overline{y}
for appropriate variables
x
and
y
.
Let
V=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
denote the set of all variables in
\psi
and write
\overline{V}
for the set
\{\overline{x_{1}},\overline{x_{2}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}\}
of negated variables.
To simplify a later argument, we write
z_{1},z_{2}\ldots,z_{n}
to denote
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
z_{n+1},\ldots,z_{2n}
for
\overline{x_{1}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}
, respectively.

We prepare a predicate symbol
C
and a second-order functional variable
T
. Let
C(i,j)
express that a pair
(z_{i},z_{j})
appears as a clause of
\psi
in the form
z_{i}\vee z_{j}
or
z_{j}\vee z_{i}
. Moreover,
T
behaves as a truth assignment so that
T(i,1)
(resp.,
T(i,0)
) means that literal
z_{i}
is assigned to be true (resp., false).
We set
\Phi\equiv(\exists^{f}T)(\forall i,u,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})[\Phi_{1}(T,i,u)\wedge\Phi_{2}(C,T,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\wedge\Phi_{3}(C,i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})]
, where
\Phi_{1}\equiv(T(i,0)\wedge T(n+i,1))\vee(T(i,1)\wedge T(n+i,0))
,
\Phi_{2}\equiv C(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\rightarrow T(i^{\prime},1)\vee T(j^{\prime},1)
, and
\Phi_{3}\equiv C(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow(1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq n\wedge 1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq n)\vee(n+1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n\wedge n+1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n)
.
Notice that
\Phi_{2}
is logically equivalent to
\neg C(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\vee T(i^{\prime},1)\vee T(j^{\prime},1)
and that
\Phi_{3}
is equivalent to
\neg C(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})\vee(1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq n\wedge 1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq n)\vee(n+1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n\wedge n+1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n)
. Therefore,
\Phi
is monotone.
It is not difficult to see that
\psi
is satisfiable iff there is a domain structure that satisfies
\Psi
.

For the case of
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
, we change the above defined formula
\Phi_{3}
to
\Phi^{\prime}_{3}
of the form
C(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow(1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq n\wedge n+1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n)\vee(1\leq j^{\prime\prime}\leq n\wedge n+1\leq i^{\prime\prime}\leq 2n)
. An argument similar to the case of
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
also works for
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
.

\Box

In the log-space setting, by contrast, positive and negative polarities act quite differently.

Proposition 4.5

Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
is in
\mathrm{L}
and
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
is complete for
\mathrm{NL}
under
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions.

Proof.

Notice by Lemma
4.4
that
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}\in\mathrm{NL}
. To prove that
\mathrm{L}
-m-hardness of
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
for
\mathrm{NL}
, we wish to reduce
\mathrm{2SAT}
to
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
by
\mathrm{L}
-m-reductions.
Let
\psi
be any instance of the form
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}
given to the decision problem
\mathrm{2SAT}
with
\phi_{i}\equiv z_{i1}\vee z_{i2}
, where each
z_{ij}
is a literal. For each clause
\phi_{i}
, if it has the form of either
x\vee y
or
\bar{x}\vee\bar{y}
, then we set
\tilde{\phi}_{i}
to be
\phi_{i}
. When
\phi_{i}
is of the form
x\vee\bar{y}
, we prepare a new variable
z
and define
\tilde{\phi}_{i}^{(1)}\equiv x\vee z
and
\tilde{\phi}_{i}^{(2)}\equiv\bar{z}\vee\bar{y}
. We then set
\tilde{\phi}\equiv\tilde{\phi}_{i}^{(1)}\wedge\tilde{\phi}_{i}^{(2)}
. The case of
\bar{x}\vee y
is similarly treated. Notice that
\phi_{i}
is satisfiable exactly when so is
\tilde{\phi}_{i}
.
Since the formula
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{\phi}_{i}
has positive polarity, it follows that
\phi\in\mathrm{2SAT}
iff
\tilde{\phi}\in Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
. This means that
\mathrm{2SAT}\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
.

Next, we wish to solve
Polar^{(-)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
. If we replace
\bar{x}\vee y
to
x\rightarrow y
and
x\vee\bar{y}
to
y\rightarrow x
, then we obtain a directed graph whose vertices are labeled by variables. In this case, if we assign
1
(true) to all variables, then we can make the instance formula satisfiable. Thus,
Polar^{(+)}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
falls in
\mathrm{L}
.

\Box

Now, we look into
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
and its monotone version,
\mathrm{MonoSNL}_{\omega}
. These complexity classes contain quite natural restrictions of
\mathrm{SNL}
and
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
.
To see this fact, let us first recall that any second-order functional variable, say,
P
used in an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence acts as a function mapping natural numbers to certain “objects” specified by an underlying domain structure
{\cal D}
.
Here, we consider the special case where these objects are taken from the binary set
\{0,1\}
.
In other words, all second-order functional variables represent functions from natural numbers to
\{0,1\}
.
This makes
P
behave like a single argument predicate by interpreting
P(\cdot,1)
(resp.,
P(\cdot,0)
) as “true” (resp., “false”). We call any SNL sentence with this restriction a
binary SNL sentence
.

Definition 4.6

The notation
\mathrm{BSNL}
expresses the subclass of
\mathrm{SNL}
characterized by binary SNL sentences. With the use of
\mathrm{BSNL}
in place of
\mathrm{SNL}
, we define
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
from
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
.

Lemma 4.7

\mathrm{BSNL}\subseteq\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
and
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}\subseteq\mathrm{MonoSNL}_{\omega}
.

Proof.

Let
L
denote any language in
\mathrm{BSNL}
and take a binary
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence
\Phi
that syntactically expresses
L
. This
\Phi
has the form
\exists^{f}P_{1}\cdots\exists^{f}P_{l}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$j$}[\psi(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$j$})]
. Notice that each second-order functional variable
P_{h}
represents a function mapping natural numbers to
\{0,1\}
. Now, we consider the logically equivalent sentence
\tilde{\Phi}\equiv\exists^{f}P_{1}\cdots\exists^{f}P_{l}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$j$}[\psi(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$j$})\wedge(\bigwedge_{h=1}^{l}Func(P_{h}))]
.
Note that the sentence
Func(P_{h})
is logically expressed as
(\forall a)[(P_{h}(a,0)\vee P_{h}(a,1))\wedge(\neg P_{h}(a,0)\vee\neg P_{h}(a,1))]
.
Hence,
\tilde{\Phi}
can be rewritten as
\exists^{f}P_{1}\cdots\exists^{f}P_{l}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$j$}\forall a_{1}\cdots\forall a_{l}[\psi(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$j$})\wedge(\bigwedge_{h=1}^{l}\xi_{h}(a_{h}))]
, where
\xi_{h}(a_{h})\equiv(P_{h}(a_{h},0)\vee P_{h}(a_{h},1))\wedge(\neg P_{h}(a_{h},0)\vee\neg P_{h}(a_{h},1))
. This formula satisfies the second-order variable requirements. We thus obtain
\mathrm{BSNL}\subseteq\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
.

The last part of the lemma follows similarly.

\Box

4.2
Relationships to Constraint Satisfaction Problems

Feder and Vardi
[
9
]
demonstrated that every problem in MMSNP is polynomial-time equivalent to a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP).
We fix a set
V
fo “objects” and a set
\Gamma
of constraint functions
f
mapping
V^{k}
to
\{0,1\}
, where
k
is the arity of
f
.
A
CSP over
(V,\Gamma)
consists of the following items: a set
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of variables and a set
C
of constraints of the form
(f,(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}))
with
f\in\Gamma
and
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}\in X
.
An
assignment

\rho
is a function from
X
to
V
. Given such an assignment
\rho
, we evaluate each constraint
(f,(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}))
by computing the Boolean value
f(\rho(x_{i_{1}}),\rho(x_{i_{2}}),\ldots,\rho(x_{i_{k}}))
.
A CSP
(X,C)
is
satisfiable
if there exists an assignment
\rho
that makes all constraints of the CSP satisfied.
A CSP is said to be
of arity at most
d
if all of its constraints have arity at most
d
.
Given a pair
(V,\Gamma)
, we write
CSP_{d}(V,\Gamma)
for the collection of all satisfiable CSPs over
(V,\Gamma)
of arity at most
d
.
When
V=\{0,1\}
, in particular, a CSP over
(V,\Gamma)
is called a
binary CSP over
\Gamma
.
To express the subproblem of
CSP_{d}(\Gamma)
restricted to only binary CSPs over
\Gamma
, we use the special notation of
BCSP_{d}(\Gamma)
.

Let
\Gamma_{OR}
denote the set of four constraint functions
f
defined by setting
f(x,y)
to be one of
x\vee y
,
\bar{x}\vee y
,
x\vee\bar{y}
, and
\bar{x}\vee\bar{y}
for two variables
x
and
y
.
We first demonstrate that

\mathrm{BCSP}_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
characterizes
\mathrm{NL}
.

Proposition 4.8

\mathrm{NL}=\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR}))
.

Before proving this proposition, we claim the following basic properties.

Lemma 4.9

Assume that
V
and
\Gamma
are finite sets. All CSPs over
(V,\Gamma)
of arity at most
2
belong to
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
and all binary CSPs over
\Gamma
of arity at most
2
belong to
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
.

Proof.

Given a CSP
(X,\hat{C})
over
(V,\Gamma)
of arity at most
2
, let
X
denote a set of variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and, for each
d\in\{1,2\}
, let
\hat{C}_{d}
denote a collection of constraints of arity exactly
d
in
\hat{C}
.
Clearly,
\hat{C}
coincides with
\hat{C}_{1}\cup\hat{C}_{2}
.

For simplicity, we express each variable
x_{i}
as
i
.
For each index
d\in\{1,2\}
, we introduce a predicate symbol
C_{d}
so that
\models C_{d}(f,i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
iff
(f,(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{d}}))\in\hat{C}_{d}
.
We also introduce two more predicate symbols
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
so that, for any
d\in\{1,2\}
and for any tuple
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in V^{d}
,
S_{d}(f,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
is true iff
f(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=0
. Let us consider the following sentence:
\Phi\equiv\exists^{f}P\forall f\forall i\forall v\forall i_{1}\forall i_{2}\forall v_{1}\forall v_{2}[\Phi_{1}(P,C_{1},S_{1},f,i,v)\wedge\Phi_{2}(P,C_{2},S_{2},f,i_{1},i_{2},v_{1},v_{2})]
,
where
\Phi_{1}\equiv 1\leq i\leq n\wedge C_{1}(f,i)\wedge P(i,v)\to\neg S_{1}(f,v)

and
\Phi_{2}\equiv 1\leq i_{1}\leq n\wedge 1\leq i_{2}\leq n\wedge C_{2}(f,i_{1},i_{2})\wedge P(i_{1},v_{1})\wedge P(i_{2},v_{2})\to\neg S_{2}(f,v_{1},v_{2})
. Here,
P
represents an assignment, say,
\rho
such that
P(i,v)
is true iff
\rho(i)=v
.

The formula
\Phi
is obviously a monotone
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence because
\Phi_{1}
is equivalent to
\neg(1\leq i\leq n)\vee\neg C_{1}(f,i)\vee\neg P(i,v)\vee\neg S_{1}(f,v)
and
\Phi_{2}
is equivalent to
\neg(1\leq i_{1}\leq n)\vee\neg(1\leq i_{2}\leq n)\vee\neg C_{2}(f,i_{1},i_{2})\vee\neg P(i_{1},v_{1})\vee\neg P(i_{2},v_{2})\vee\neg S_{2}(f,v_{1},v_{2})
. It also follows by the definition of
\Phi
that
\Phi
is true iff
(X,\hat{C})
is satisfiable.

For the second part of the proposition, we start with a binary CSP of arity at most
2
.
In this case, we need to replace
P(i,v)
in the above argument by
P(i,v)\wedge 0\leq v\leq 1
. Similarly,
P(i_{1},v_{1})
and
P(i_{2},v_{2})
should be replaced. A similar argument as above proves the desired second part.

\Box

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

By Lemma
4.9
,
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma)
is contained in
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
(
\subseteq\mathrm{NL}
) for any
\Gamma
. In particular,
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
belongs to
\mathrm{NL}
.
It therefore suffices to prove that
\mathrm{2SAT}\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
.
Let
\phi
denote any 2CNF formula built from a variable set
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and a clause set
C\subseteq(X\cup\bar{X})^{2}
, where
\bar{X}=\{\overline{x_{1}},\overline{x_{2}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}\}
. Whenever each clause in
C
is a “single” literal, say,
z
, we replace it with the clause
z\vee z
. It thus possible for us to assume that
\phi
has the form
\bigwedge_{(z_{i_{1}},z_{i_{2}})\in C}(z_{i_{1}}\vee z_{i_{2}})
, where
z_{i_{1}}
and
z_{i_{2}}
are literals. Given a clause
z_{i_{1}}\vee z_{i_{2}}
, we choose
f\in\Gamma_{OR}
such that
f(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})
equals
z_{i_{1}}\vee z_{i_{2}}
, where
x_{i_{1}}
and
x_{i_{2}}
are the underlying variables of
z_{i_{1}}
and
z_{i_{2}}
, respectively. Let
C
denote the collection
C
of all such constraints
(f,(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}}))
.

Let
I_{\phi}
denote the CSP made up from
X
and
C
.
It then follows that
\phi
is satisfiable iff
I_{\phi}
is satisfiable. We thus conclude that
\mathrm{2SAT}\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
. Since
\mathrm{2SAT}
is
\mathrm{L}
-m-complete for
\mathrm{NL}
, so is
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
.
Therefore, we obtain
\mathrm{NL}=\;\leq^{\mathrm{L}}_{m}\!(BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR}))
.

\Box

We now turn to the dichotomy theorem for
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
. Recall that the dichotomy theorem for
{\cal C}
means that every decision problem in
{\cal C}
is either in
\mathrm{L}
or
\mathrm{NL}
-complete.
It is not yet known that
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
enjoys the dichotomy theorem; in sharp contrast, however, the dichotomy theorem holds for
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma)
. Allender et al.
[
1
]
showed that, for any set
\Gamma
,
BCSP(\Gamma)
is
\mathrm{AC}^{0}
-isomorphic either to
0\Sigma^{*}
or to the “standard” complete problem (under
\mathrm{AC}^{0}
-reductions) for one of the following complexity classes:
\mathrm{NP}
,
\mathrm{P}
,
\oplus\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{NL}
, and
\mathrm{L}
. In our restricted case, from their result, we obtain the following statement.

Proposition 4.10

For any set
\Gamma
,
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma)
is either in
\mathrm{L}
or
\mathrm{NL}
-complete.

Inspired by the connection between
\mathrm{MMSNP}
and CSPs, we wish to demonstrate a close relationship between
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
and
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma)
.

Theorem 4.11

For any decision problem
\Xi
in
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
, there exists a finite set
\Gamma
such that
\Xi
is
\mathrm{L}
-m-reducible to
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Given a decision problem
\Xi
in
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
, we take a binary
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence
\Phi\equiv\exists^{f}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$i$}\forall\mbox{\boldmath$y$}[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$})]
, as stated in Definition
2.3
, provided that
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}=(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{l})
.
We then construct

\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\bigwedge_{\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
by assigning all possible values
(\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
sequentially one by one.
We wish to convert each of the obtained formulas,
\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
, into an equivalent CNF formula. By the second-order variable requirements (i)–(ii), each conjunct (in the obtained formula) has at most two appearances of
P_{k}(\cdot)
or
\neg P_{k^{\prime}}(\cdot)
. We then evaluate all the other predicates (except for
P_{k}(\cdot)
and
\neg P_{k^{\prime}}(\cdot)
) to be either
0
(false) or
1
(true), resulting in a formula consisting only of
P_{k}(\cdot)
and
\neg P_{k^{\prime}}(\cdot)
.

As a concrete example, recall
\mathrm{2COLOR}
in Example
2.8
. In this case,
\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
is either of the following forms:

\neg C(i,d)\vee 0\leq d\leq 1
or
\neg E(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})\vee\neg C(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})\vee\neg C(j^{\prime},e^{\prime})\vee d^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime}
. Since
(i,d,i^{\prime},d^{\prime},j^{\prime},e^{\prime})
are considered to be fixed in
\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
, the subformulas,
0\leq d\leq 1
,
\neg E(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})
, and
d^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime}
, are all evaluated to be either
0
or
1
. We then remove all such subformulas evaluated to be
0
and remove the entire
\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
if its evaluation is
1
. We then rephrase
\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$i$}},\underline{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
and obtain
\neg C(i,d)
or
\neg C(i^{\prime},d^{\prime})\vee\neg C(j^{\prime},e^{\prime})
.
For each pair
(i,d)
, we introduce a new variable
x_{i,d}
to represent the value of
C(i,d)
.
We then define
I_{n}
to be the set
\{(g,(x_{i,d},x_{j,e}))\mid i,j\in[n],(i,j)\in E_{G},d=e\}
, where
g(x,y)=OR(\bar{x},\bar{y})
for two variables
x
and
y
and
\bar{x}
(resp.,
\bar{y}
) denotes the negation of
x
(resp.,
y
).
It then follows that
\Phi
is satisfiable iff
I_{n}
is in
BCSP_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
. This implies that
\mathrm{2COLOR}
is
\mathrm{L}
-m-reducible to
\mathrm{BCSP}_{2}(\Gamma_{OR})
.

5
Optimization Variant of SNL

We turn our attention to another variant of SNL in the field of optimization problems.
In general, an
optimization problem
has the form
(op,I,S,cost)
, where
op\in\{\max,\min\}
,
I
is a set of instances,
S
is a set of (feasible) solutions, an
cost:I\times S\to\mathbb{N}
is a (partial) cost function. As a concrete example of optimization problems,

\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
asks to find the truth assignment of a given 2CNF Boolean formula that maximizes the number of satisfying clauses of the formula.
Despite a wide-range of studies on
\mathrm{NP}
optimization problems, there have been a few works on logarithmic-space optimization
[
23
,
26
,
27
]
.

5.1
Maximal SNL (or MAXSNL)

Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
[
19
]
were the first to study the computational complexity of an optimization version of
\mathrm{SNP}
, called
\mathrm{MAXSNP}
. In a similar vein, we intend to study an optimization version of
\mathrm{SNL}
for promoting the better understandings of
\mathrm{SNL}
.
Along this line of studies, we further explore
\mathrm{SNL}
optimization problems and log-space approximation schemes based on
\mathrm{SNL}
.

Definition 5.1

We define
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
to be composed of all maximization problems that satisfy the following condition: there exist
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences
\Phi
of the form given in Definition
2.3
with relational and domain structures
{\cal S}
and
{\cal D}
for
\Phi
for which
each maximization problem asks to find a solution
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}=(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{l})
satisfying
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{l}Func(P_{i})
that maximizes the value

\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})=|\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$})\mid\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{S}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{c}$})\}|
of the objective function,
where
\psi_{j}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$},\mbox{\boldmath$S$},\mbox{\boldmath$c$}

are sequences of variables, predicate and constant symbols appearing in
\Phi
, provided that
\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}
are only first-order variables that appear in
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{S}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{c}$}
are elements in
{\cal S}
and
{\cal D}
associated respectively with
\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$},\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\mbox{\boldmath$S$},\mbox{\boldmath$c$}
.
It is important to remember that each
\psi_{j}
must satisfy the second-order variable requirements (i)–(ii).

It is obvious by definition that
\mathrm{MAXSNL}_{\omega}\subseteq\mathrm{MAXSNL}\subseteq\mathrm{MAXSNP}
. Let us see three simple examples of problems in
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
.
The parameterized decision problem
(\mathrm{2SAT},m_{ver})
was shown in
[
28
]
to be in
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{SNL}
by constructing an appropriate
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence for
(\mathrm{2SAT},m_{ver})
, where
m_{ver}(\phi)
denotes the total number of variables appearing in a 2CNF Boolean formula
\phi
. From this
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence, by carefully eliminating the presence of
m_{ver}
, we can conclude that
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{2SAT}
belongs to
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
.
As another concrete example of problems in
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
, we consider
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CUT}
whose goal is to find a set
S
of vertices of a given undirected graph
G=(V,E)
for which the number of edges crossing between
S
and
V-S
is maximized.

Example 5.2

As an instance of
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CUT}
, we take an arbitrary undirected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
with
V_{G}=[n]
and
E_{G}\subseteq[n]\times[n]
for a number
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
We introduce a predicate symbol
E
for which
E(i,j)
means that
(i,j)
is an edge in
E_{G}
.
Given a second-order functional variable
P
,
P(i,1)
(resp,
P(i,0)
) indicates that vertex
i
belongs to a solution set
S
(resp.,
V-S
).
Consider the following
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence indicating the existence of such a set

:
\Phi\equiv\exists^{f}P\forall i\forall j[\phi_{1}(P,i)\wedge\phi_{1}(P,j)\wedge(E(i,j)\to\phi_{2}(P,E,i,j))]
, where

\phi_{1}\equiv(P(i,0)\vee P(i,1))\wedge\neg(P(i,0)\wedge P(i,1))
and
\phi_{2}\equiv(P(i,1)\wedge P(j,0))\vee(P(i,0)\wedge P(j,1))
. Intuitively,
\phi_{1}(P,i)
means that
P

and
\phi_{2}(P,E,i,j)

Since

\Phi

\exists^{f}P\forall i\forall j[Func(P)\wedge\phi_{2}(P,E,i,j)]

\Phi

\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}

The objective function
\Pi(P)
for
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CUT}
is expressed as
|\{(i,j)\mid\phi_{1}(P,i)\wedge\phi_{1}(P,j)\wedge E(i,j)\wedge\phi_{2}(P,E,i,j)\}|

We thus conclude that
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{CUT}

\mathrm{MAXSNL}
.

We discuss another simple example, called
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}

.

Example 5.3

Now, we assert that
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}
belongs to
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
. To verify this assertion, we recall the notation from Example
2.9
. We then introduce a second-order variable
P
so that

means the equality

for a certain fixed solution
S
(
\subseteq[n]
), provided that each instance

\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}

(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})

For convenience, we set
\sum_{i\in S\cap[k]}a_{i}

Following Example
2.9
, we define two formulas:

\phi_{1}\equiv 0\leq i<n\wedge 0<j\leq t\wedge 0\leq s+t\leq b\wedge P(0,0)\wedge P(i,s)\wedge P(i+1,s+t)
and

\phi_{2}\equiv t=0\vee I(i+1,t)

whose variables

satisfy the above formulas
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
.
The objective function
\Pi(\underline{P})
for a solution
\underline{P}
of a
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}

5.2
Subclass of MAXSNL in APXL

We discuss the approximability of optimization problems in
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
.
In the polynomial-time setting, it is known from
[
19
, Theorem 1]
that every optimization problem in
\mathrm{MAXSNP}
falls in APX; namely, it is approximated in polynomial time within a certain fixed approximation ratio.
The log-space approximability of optimization problems was studied earlier in
[
23
,
26
,
27
]
.
Following
[
26
,
27
]
, we use the notation
\mathrm{APXL}
(also denoted
\mathrm{APXL}_{\mathrm{NLO}}
in
[
27
]
) for the collection of
\mathrm{NL}
optimization problems that can be approximated in polynomial time using log space with fixed constant approximation ratios.
Several NL optimization problems are known to be “complete” for APXL under various reductions. Those complete problems include the maximization binary 2-bounded close-to-unary knapsack problem and the maximum fixed-length
\lambda
-nondeterministic finite automata problem
[
27
]
.
Since
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
contains optimization problems of extremely high complexity by Proposition
5.5
, it seems difficult to show that every problem in
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
is in
\mathrm{APXL}
.
Hence, it is natural to ask what subclass of
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
makes its problems fall in
\mathrm{APXL}
.

As an example of such problem, we first recall
\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}
from Example
5.3
.

Proposition 5.8

\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}
is in
\mathrm{APXL}
.

Proof.

Let
x
denote any instance of the form
(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})
given to
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}
. For simplicity, we assume that
0<a_{i}\leq b
for all
i\in[n]
.
Consider the following simple, greedy algorithm. It is possible to enumerate the integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
given in the unary representation in the descending order using log space by scanning each input symbol of
x
back and forth (see, e.g.,
[
34
]
). Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}
in the rest of our argument. We wish to inductively construct a subset
S
of
[n]
, starting with
S=\varnothing
, by executing the following scheme.
By incrementing
i
by one from
i=0
, we pick
a_{i}
and check whether
\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}+a_{i}\leq b
. If so, then we expand
S
by adding
i
to
S
; otherwise, we do nothing. After reading all
a_{i}
’s, we output the value
\sum_{j\in S}a_{i}
.

Next, we intend to verify that the above scheme is indeed an approximation scheme with an approximation ratio of at most
2
. Let
S=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}
be the set constructed by this approximation scheme with
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
.
In the case of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq b
,
S
must be
[n]
, and thus the scheme correctly solves the given problem.
In the other case of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}>b
, we wish to prove that
\frac{b}{2}\leq\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\leq b
. To prove this, on the contrary, we first assume that
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}<\frac{b}{2}
.
If
i_{k}<n
, then we obtain
a_{i_{k}+1}>\frac{b}{2}
because, otherwise, we obtain
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}+a_{i_{k}+1}\leq b
and thus the algorithm should place
i_{k}+1
in
S
, a contradiction to the definition of
S
.
Since
a_{i_{1}}\geq a_{i_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq a_{i_{k}}\geq a_{i_{k}+1}
, it follows that
a_{i_{j}}>\frac{b}{2}
for all indices
j\in[k]
. This is in contradiction with
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}<\frac{b}{2}
.
By contrast, let us consider the case of
i_{k}=n
. Since
i_{k}=n
and
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}<\frac{b}{2}
, we conclude that
a_{i}<\frac{b}{2}
for all
i\in[n]
. Since
\sum_{i\in[n]}a_{i}>b
,
S\neq[n]
follows. We then take the smallest number
i^{\prime}
in
[n]-S
. This means that the algorithm does not choose
a_{i^{\prime}}
. Hence, it follows that
\sum_{i\in S\cap[i^{\prime}]}a_{i}+a_{i^{\prime}}>b
. From this, we obtain
a_{i^{\prime}}>b-\sum_{i\in S\cap[i^{\prime}]}a_{i}\geq b-\frac{b}{2}=\frac{b}{2}
. This contradicts the upper bound
a_{i}<\frac{b}{2}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Therefore,
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\geq\frac{b}{2}
must hold. Since the optimal solution has a value of at most
b
, the approximation ratio cannot exceed
2
.

\Box

.
For convenience, we also define
g_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{l}}$})

to be the value
|\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$})\mid R^{(-)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{S}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{c}$})\}|
.
It then follows that, if
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}P_{j}(e,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{j})
and
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}P_{j}(e+1,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{j})
are true, then
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a+1}=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a}\>+\>g_{a+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{l}}$})
.
This seems to provide a simple, easy way to compute
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a+1}
from
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a}
.

Let us see a concrete example of
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})\lceil_{a}
in Example
5.9
.

Example 5.9

Consider the maximization problem
\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}
.
Let us recall its formulation given in Example
5.3
.
We further define
R^{(-)}
as follows:
R^{(-)}(i,j,z,u,v)\equiv 0<i<n\wedge 0<j\leq z\wedge u+z=v\wedge 0\leq v\leq b\wedge(z=0\vee I(i,z))
.
For each value
a\in[n]
, the restriction
\Pi(\underline{P})\lceil_{a}
thus has the form
\Pi(\underline{P})\lceil_{a}=|\{(i,j,z)\mid 0<i\leq a\wedge P(i,u)\wedge P(i+1,v)\wedge R^{(-)}(i+1,j,z,u,v)\}|
.
Moreover,
g_{a}(u,v)
takes the value
|\{(j,z)\mid R^{(-)}(a,j,z,u,v)\}|
. Clearly, when
P(a,u)
and
P(a+1,v)
are true, we obtain
\Pi(\underline{P})\lceil_{a+1}=\Pi(\underline{P})\lceil_{a}\>+\>g_{a+1}(u,v)
.

As noted, the use of
g_{a}
provides a simple, easy way to compute
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a+1}
from
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a}
. However, this does not seem to immediately guarantees a “log-space” procedure of computing
\Pi(\underline{P})
.
To overcome this difficulty, we wish to expand an underlying vocabulary by appending a new function symbol “
h
”.
We write “
h(i)
” with a variable
i
to mean the outcome of this function on input
i
. We then syntactically replace each formula “
P_{k}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
” in
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}
by “
P_{k}(h(i),\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
”.
Note that, since this is just a syntactical replacement, we keep the same variable sequence
u
as “symbolic” terms. For notational convenience, we hereafter write “
P_{k}^{(h)}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
” instead of “
P_{k}(h(i),\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
”. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{(h)}=(P^{(h)}_{1},P^{(h)}_{2},\ldots,P^{(h)}_{l})
.
The formula obtained from
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}
by this syntactical replacement is succinctly referred to as an
h
-term expansion of

\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}
. Naturally, we further obtain
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})
and
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})\lceil_{a}
from
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})
and
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a}
, respectively.

Example 5.10

We consider an
h
-term expansion of
\Pi(P)\lceil_{a}
given in Example
5.9
. Let
h
denote a newly introduced function symbol, which represents a permutation on
[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\underline{h}(0)=0
. We introduce
P^{(h)}
as a new variable associated with
h
and consider
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{a}=|\{(i,j,z)\mid 0<i\leq a\wedge P^{(h)}(i,u)\wedge P^{(h)}(i+1,v)\wedge R^{(-)}(h(i+1),j,z,u,v)\}|
and
g^{(h)}_{a}(u,v)=|\{(j,z)\mid R^{(-)}(h(a),j,z,u,v)\}|
. It then follows that
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{a+1}=\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{a}\>+\>g_{a+1}^{(h)}(u,v)
if
P^{(h)}(a,u)
and
P^{(h)}(a+1,v)
are true.

Now, let us define the complexity class
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
.

Definition 5.11

A maximization problem is in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
if there exists
a quantifier-free SNL formula
R^{(-)}
having no functional variables, which naturally induces
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})
,
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})\lceil_{a}
, and
g_{a}
as shown above, with the following extra three conditions such that the maximization problem asks to find a solution
P
that maximizes the value
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})
of the objective function.
Let
a
,
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{j}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{j}
,
P
,
P^{\prime}
,
\hat{P}
,
S
, and
c
denote sequences of variables, predicates and constant symbols. Let
h
be any newly introduced function symbol whose interpretation
\underline{h}
is a permutation on
[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\underline{h}(0)=0
. Let
A^{(h)}_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$})
denote the set
\{g^{(h)}_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{l}}$})\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{l}}$}\}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}_{l}}$}
range over all possible elements.

(1)

If both
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}P_{j}^{(h)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$u_{j}$})
and
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}P_{j}^{(h)}(a+1,\mbox{\boldmath$v_{j}$})
are true, then
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})\lceil_{a+1}=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})\lceil_{a}\>+\>g^{(h)}_{a+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{l}}$})
.

(2)

If both
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}P_{j}^{(h)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{j})
and
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{l}{\hat{P}_{j}}^{(h)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{j})
are true, then
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})\lceil_{a}\geq\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{\hat{P}}$}^{(h)})\lceil_{a}
implies
A^{(h)}_{a+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u_{l}}$})\subseteq A^{(h)}_{a+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{l}}$})
.

(3)

(i) For any
P^{\prime}
, there exists a
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{(h)}
such that
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P^{\prime}}$})\leq\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})
.
(ii) For any
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{(h)}
, there exists a
P^{\prime}
such that
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})\leq\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P^{\prime}}$})
.

The symbol “
\tau
” in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
indicates the “transitive” relation of
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$})\lceil_{a}
over all values
a
.
The condition (3), in particular, expresses the invariance of the final outcome of
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{P}$}^{(h)})
over the choice of any permutation
h
.

In what follows, we demonstrate that
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}
is an example problem of
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
.
However, it is not clear that
\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}
is “complete” for
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
under naturally chosen reductions.

Give a formula
\phi
, we set
[\![\phi]\!]=1
if
\phi
is true and
[\![\phi]\!]=0
if
\phi
is false.

Lemma 5.12

\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}
is in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
.

Proof.

Firstly, let us recall the objective function
\Pi(P)
defined in Example
5.3
and
R^{(-)}
,
g_{a}
, and
\Pi(P)\lceil_{a}

defined in Example
5.9
for
\mathrm{MAX}\mbox{-}\mathrm{UK}
.
For the containment of
\mathrm{MAX\mbox{-}UK}
in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
, we argue that this
R^{(-)}
satisfies the desired conditions (1)–(3) of Definition
5.11
.
We immediately obtain
\Pi(P)\lceil_{0}=0
.

Let
x
denote an instance of the form
(1^{b},1^{a_{1}},1^{a_{2}},\ldots,1^{a_{n}})
.
We introduce a new functional variable
P
indicating that, for each

Let

denote a newly introduced function symbol, which represents a permutation on

with
\underline{h}(0)=0

The condition (1) of Definition

Toward the condition (3), this comes from the fact that the inputs

Theorem 5.13

Every maximization problem in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
belongs to
\mathrm{APXL}
.

Proof.

Let
D
denote any maximization problem in
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
and consider an appropriately chosen SNL formula
R^{(-)}
satisfying the aforementioned form (*) with functional variables
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{l})
and an objective function
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})
associated with
D
in Definition
5.11
.
The restriction
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})\lceil_{a}
is obtained from
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$})
by the use of the supplemental function

g_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$})=|\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$})\mid R^{(-)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{i}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{y}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{S}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{c}$})\}|
for each
a\in[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

To proceed this proof further, we take a new term of the form
h(i)
and syntactically replace
P(i,\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
in
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\psi_{j}
by
P(h(i),\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
. We introduce a new functional variable
P^{(h)}
to express
P(h(i),\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
as
P^{(h)}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$u$})
.

Let
P_{opt}
denote an optimal solution of
D
.
Hereafter, we intend to approximate
P_{opt}
by defining an appropriate permutation
h
on
[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
h(0)=0

and searching for an approximate solution
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{(h)}
, which makes
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P$}^{(h)})
close enough to
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P_{opt}$}^{(h)})
. This is possible because the condition (3) of Definition
5.11
helps us replace
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P_{opt}$})
by
\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$P_{opt}$}^{(h)})
.
To simplify the notation in the subsequent argument, we assume
l=t=1
, write
P,i,j
for
\mbox{\boldmath$P$},\mbox{\boldmath$i$},\mbox{\boldmath$j$}
, drop “
j
” from
\psi_{j}
, and omit
S
and
c
entirely.
Note that all variables in the tuple
(i,j,\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$})
are evaluated as logarithmic-size “objects”.

Our goal is to determine
P^{(h)}
that maximizes the value
\Pi(P^{(h)})
by employing the following greedy approximation algorithm, called
{\cal B}
, which is in essence a generalization of the one given in the proof of Proposition
5.8
.
Initially, we set
s_{0}=0
, define
h(0)=0
, and take
\underline{v_{0}}
to satisfy
P(h(0),\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{0}}$})
.
By induction hypothesis, we assume that
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{i},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{0}}$},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i}}$}
are already determined.
Assume also that, for each number
j\in[0,i]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, the value
h(j)
is already determined. We then define
S_{h}=\{h(j)\mid j\in[0,i]_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.
Moreover, we assume that
P^{(h)}(0,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{0}}$}),P^{(h)}(a,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$}),\ldots,P^{(h)}(i,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i}}$})
are all true.
We then choose
a,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v}$}
with
a\notin S_{h}
that maximize the value
g_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v}$})
over all possible elements for
v
. Remember that there are only polynomially many possible values assigned to
v
.
We then define
h(i+1)=a
, include
h(i+1)
to the set
S_{h}
, and write
v_{i+1}
for
v
. We also define
s_{i+1}
to be
s_{i}\>+\>g_{h(i+1)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i+1}}$})
and make
P^{(h)}(i+1,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i+1}}$})
true.
It is not difficult to show that
h
is indeed a permutation on
[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
h(0)=0
and that
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{i+1}=\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{i}\>+\>g^{(h)}_{i+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{i+1}}$})
holds for all
i\in[0,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Now, we wish to prove by contradiction that
P^{(h)}
is an approximate solution of
P^{(h)}_{opt}
with approximation ratio of at most 3; namely,
\Pi(P^{(h)})\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
.
Toward an intended contradiction, we now assume that
\Pi(P^{(h)})<\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
.
This immediately yields
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j}<\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
for all numbers
j\in[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Let us take the smallest number
j_{0}\in[n]
such that
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j}<\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j}
holds for all
j\geq j_{0}
. Such a number
j_{0}
exists because of
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j}<\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
for all
j\in[0,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Since the algorithm
{\cal B}
always chooses elements making the value of
g_{a}
the largest, we obtain, in particular,
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{1}\geq\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{1}
, and thus
j_{0}>1
follows.
Hence, we obtain
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\leq\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\leq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
.

If
P^{(h)}_{opt}(j_{0}-1,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$})
and
P^{(h)}_{opt}(j_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})
are true, then we obtain
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}=\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\>+\>g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})
by the condition (1) of Definition
5.11
.
It then follows that
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})-g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}-1}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}}}$})=\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}-(\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1})=(\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}})+(\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}-1})>0
.
This leads to the inequality of
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}-1}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}}}$})<g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})
.

Next, we further claim that
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})<\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
. Assuming that
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
, since the algorithm
{\cal B}
has chosen
v_{2}
so that
g_{1}^{(h)}(\mbox{\boldmath$v_{1}$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$})
is the maximum among all possible values
g^{(h)}_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$w^{\prime}$})
for any
(a,\mbox{\boldmath$w$},\mbox{\boldmath$w^{\prime}$})
, it follows that

\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{1}=\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{0}\>+\>g^{(h)}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{0}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{1}}$})\geq g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
, a contradiction. As a consequence,
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})
is less than
\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
.

It then follows that
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
since, otherwise,
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})=\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}\leq\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}+g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})+(\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}})<3\cdot\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})=\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
, yielding a contradiction.
Since
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j}\geq\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j}
for all
j\geq j_{0}
, if
P^{(h)}_{opt}(j,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$})
and
P^{(h)}_{opt}(j+1,\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})
are true, then the condition (2) of Definition
5.11
ensures that
A^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$})\subseteq A^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j}}$})
, where
A^{(h)}_{a}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{w}$})=\{g_{a}^{(h)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{w}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{w^{\prime}}$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{w^{\prime}}$}\}
.
Notice that
g^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})\in A^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$})
and

g^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j+1}}$})\in A^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j}}$})
.
Since the algorithm
{\cal B}
chooses
v_{j+1}
so that
g^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j+1}}$})
takes the maximum value in
A^{(h)}_{j+1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j}}$})
, we conclude that
g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{u^{\prime}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v^{\prime}}$})\leq g^{(h)}_{j_{0}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}-1}}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\underline{v_{j_{0}}}$})
for all
j\geq j_{0}
.

For each number
j\geq j_{0}
, we write
t_{j}
for the value
\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j}
. We then calculate the value

as

, provided that
P^{(h)}(j,\mbox{\boldmath$u^{\prime}$})
and
P^{(h)}(j+1,\mbox{\boldmath$v^{\prime}$})
are true.
This implies that

for all

. It then follows that

, which implies

\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}\leq\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{n-1}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}
.
We thus conclude that

\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{n-1}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\geq\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}
for all
j\geq j_{0}
.

Since
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{n}\geq\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{n-1}
, we conclude that
\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\geq\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{n}-\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})\lceil_{j_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
. It then follows that
\Pi(P^{(h)})\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})+\Pi(P^{(h)})\lceil_{j_{0}-1}\geq\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
. This is a clear contradiction with our assumption that
\Pi(P^{(h)})<\frac{1}{3}\Pi(P^{(h)}_{opt})
.

Therefore, the algorithm should approximate
P_{opt}
with approximation ratio of at most
3
.

\Box

6
Brief Conclusion and Open Questions

Turing machines and circuit families have been used in the mainstream of computational complexity theory as basic computational models to solve various computational problems. In sharp contrast, a logical approach has taken to measure the complexity of these problems using the expressibility of specific logical sentences.

For a better understanding of
\mathrm{NL}
, the first logical approach was taken in
[
28
]
using the notion of
Syntactic NL
(or succinctly,
SNL
).
\mathrm{SNL}
sentences are characterized in the form of second-order “functional” existential quantifiers followed by first-order universal quantifiers together with two particular requirements called the
second-order variable requirements
. Those sentences syntactically express certain types of languages.
The complexity class
\mathrm{SNL}
(and its variant
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
), consisting of all languages expressed syntactically by
\mathrm{SNL}
(and
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}
) sentences
have a direct association with a practical, working hypothesis, known as the
linear space hypothesis
(LSH).

In this work, we have continued the study of the structural properties of
\mathrm{SNL}
. In particular, we have focused on three major issues:
(1) the expressibility of complementary problems of
\mathrm{SNL}
problems with an introduction of
\mu\mathrm{SNL}
, which is a variant of
\mathrm{SNL}
,
(2) the computational complexity of the monotone variant of
\mathrm{SNL}
(called
\mathrm{MonoSNL}
) together with its restriction (called
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
), and
(3) the computational complexity of the optimization version of
\mathrm{SNL}
(called
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
) together with its variant (called
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
).

For the interested reader, we wish to raise a few important open questions associated with this work.

1.

Concerning various complexity classes discussed in this work, still unknown is any of the following class equalities:
\mathrm{SNL}_{\omega}=\mathrm{SNL}
,
\mathrm{MonoSNL}=\mathrm{SNL}
,
\mathrm{MonoSNL}=\mathrm{CSP}_{2}
,
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}=\mathrm{BCSP}_{2}
,
\mathrm{MAXSNL}=\mathrm{MAXSNP}
, and
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}=\mathrm{MAXSNL}
. Proving the equalities or the inequalities will significantly deepen our understanding of SNL.

2.

We have shown in Section
3.2
that the complementary problem of a particular
\mathrm{SNL}
problem is in
\mu\mathrm{SNL}
. We thus wonder if the complementary problems of all problems in
\mathrm{SNL}
are in
\mu\mathrm{SNL}
(or more strongly, in
\mathrm{SNL}
).

3.

Given an
\mathrm{SNL}
sentence
\Phi
, we specifically call it an
SNL sentence without comparison symbol
if the equality (
=
) as well as the less-than-or-equal symbol (
\leq
) is not used in
\Phi
.
What is the computational complexity of decision problems expressed by such restrictive sentences?

4.

In Definition
3.6
, we disallow any nested application of the
\mu
-operator for constructing
\mu
-terms. When we allow such a nested application of the
\mu
-operator for
\mu
-terms, then how dose the computational complexity of
\mu\mathrm{SNL}
change?

5.

We have stated in Corollary
4.10
that the dichotomy theorem holds for binary CSPs. In the polynomial-time setting, MMSNP is known to satisfy the dichotomy theorem. Does
\mathrm{MonoBSNL}
also enjoy the same dichotomy theorem?

6.

We also expect a further study on
\mathrm{MAX}\tau\mathrm{SNL}
and other natural subclasses of
\mathrm{MAXSNL}
that are nicely contained in
\mathrm{APXL}
.
