Emerging roles for SNRPB2 in governing the cell cycle and steering tumor immune modulation in breast cancer
Authors: Jiazhennan Zhang, Hu Wang, Yizhen Jia, Adalaiti Yasheng, Xiaoye Ai, Xiuli Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is a common malignant tumor with limited treatment options and poor prognosis. SNRPB2, a core spliceosomal component involved in pre-mRNA splicing, is dysregulated in multiple cancers, but its role in breast cancer remains incompletely understood.Methods and ResultsWe analyzed SNRPB2 expression in breast cancer using TCGA, CPTAC, and HPA databases, and assessed its prognostic value via Kaplan-Meier plotter. The biological function of SNRPB2 was evaluated through in vitro cell line assay, and the underlying molecular mechanisms were determined via RNA-seq, RT-qPCR, and additional GEO datasets. Our findings demonstrated that SNRPB2 was significantly overexpressed in breast cancer tissues and correlated with malignant progression and poor prognosis. Knockdown of SNRPB2 induced G2/M cell cycle arrest in cancer cells, decreased expression of numerous genes related to cell cycle and immune modulation, and triggered alterations in multiple alternative splicing events. Mechanistically, SNRPB2 knockdown might promote cancer cell cycle arrest by regulating HMGA2 splicing and expression, while simultaneously suppressing the expression of immune-related genes such as CSF1, CSF1R, IL6, and CX3CL1. Immune infiltration analysis revealed that SNRPB2 expression correlated with increased infiltration of activated inflammatory cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells.ConclusionsSNRPB2 potentially plays a dual role in promoting breast cancer progression and shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment, partly through HMGA2 splicing modulation and immune-regulatory gene suppression. These findings suggest that SNRPB2 may represent a promising therapeutic target for breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-026-11874-z.Keywords: SNRPB2, Breast cancer, Transcriptomics, Cell cycle, Immunomodulatory

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common female malignant tumors worldwide, with the highest incidence rate of all female malignant tumors, posing a great threat to women’s life and health [1, 2]. Based on molecular typing, breast cancer is subdivided into luminal, HER2 positive, and basal-like types, of which the basal-like type is mostly triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [3]. It is generally recognized that the different subtypes of breast cancer are important prognostic factors, and although this is not absolute, TNBC shows a higher metastatic potential and the most worrying prognosis compared to other subtypes [4]. In recent years, many new treatment regimens have emerged, benefiting the majority of patients; nevertheless, overall outcomes remain far from ideal [5–7]. Therefore, in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying breast cancer development and active search for novel therapeutic targets are of great significance for improving patient prognosis.

Increasing evidence indicates that breast cancer pathogenesis results from interactions between tumor cells and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [8]. Cancer cells interact with tumor-infiltrating immune cells within the TIME by expressing cell surface inhibitory ligands/receptors or by directly secreting cytokines, chemokines, and metabolic substances, which prevents them from recognizing or killing cancer cells, thus achieving immune escape and ultimately driving breast cancer development and progression [9, 10]. Small nuclear ribonucleoprotein polypeptide B2 (SNRPB2) is a spliceosomal component that play a well-established role in pre-mRNA splicing and has been linked to cancer development and progression. SNRPB2 accelerates hepatocellular carcinoma progression by inhibiting ferroptosis [11], promotes esophageal cancer progression via the β-catenin/c-Myc axis [12], and contributes to TNBC progression by controlling alternative splicing of MDM4 pre-mRNA [13]. Most intriguingly, recent studies suggest that SNRPB2 may be closely related to immune markers and tumor immune cell infiltration [14, 15]. However, its oncogenic and immunomodulatory roles in breast cancer have not been fully elucidated and require further evaluation.

In this study, we found that SNRPB2 was significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues and closely associated with adverse clinical-pathological features and poor prognosis. Furthermore, SNRPB2 knockdown modulated differential expression or alternative splicing events in numerous genes associated with cell cycle and immunity, suggesting its potential role in regulating cancer progression and TIME. These findings highlight a crucial oncogenic role for SNRPB2 in breast cancer biology and establish a profound link between SNRPB2, cancer phenotypes, and immune modulation, potentially offering a direction for future targeted therapies against breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and gene knockdown

The MDA-MB-231 cell line was purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China), and has been authenticated by an expert and tested for mycoplasma, indicating that it was free from mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured at 37°C in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (164210, Procell, China) and 100 IU/mL penicillin-streptomycin solution (SV30010, Hyclone, USA) under 5% CO2. All siRNA duplexes were provided by Gemma (Suzhou, China) with the following sequences: Negative control (siNC), sense 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’ and antisense 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’; siSNRPB2, sense 5’-GUGGAACAGACUGCAACAATT-3’ and antisense 5’-UUGUUGCAGUCUGUUCCACTT-3’. Transfecting plasmids and siRNA were executed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent. Cells were harvested for subsequent analysis 48 h post-transfection.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR detection

Total RNA was isolated from MDA-MB-231 cells using TRIzol reagent (15596-018, Ambion, USA). RNA quantity and purity were measured using an Ultrafine spectrophotometer (N50 touch, IMPLEN, Germany), and RNA integrity was further verified by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (R323-01, Vazyme, China) on a thermocycler (T100, Bio-Rad, USA), followed by qPCR on the ABI QuantStudio5. The reaction conditions were: 95 °C denaturation for 10 min, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing and extension at 60˚C for 1 min. Each sample included three technical replicates. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization, and relative expression was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCT) method. The qPCR primers used in this study were as follows:

GAPDH forward primer: 5’-GGTCGGAGTCAACGGATTTG-3’.

GAPDH reverse primer: 5’-GGAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3’.

SNRPB2 forward primer: 5’-CTTACCAGAAGAGACTAATGAG-3’.

SNRPB2 reverse primer: 5’-AATCCCTGTAAAGCATCCC-3’.

CSF1 forward primer: 5’-GCCTTCTAGTCACAGCCTCTAT-3’.

CSF1 reverse primer: 5’-CCTGCTCTGATGCCACCAA-3’.

CSF1R forward primer: 5’-CAGTTCTGCTGAGGAGTTGAC-3’.

CSF1R reverse primer: 5’-TGTTGAGTGAAATGACCGAAGG-3’.

IL6 forward primer: 5’-TCGGTCCAGTTGCCTTCTC-3’.

IL6 reverse primer: 5’-GGTGAGTGGCTGTCTGTGT-3’.

CX3CL1 forward primer: 5’-GCACTTTGTTCTGGCTCTTCC-3’.

CX3CL1 reverse primer: 5’-GATCATCCTTGTCCATGTCCTG-3’.

Western blot analysis

The gathered cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA Buffer (PR20001, Proteintech, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (4693116001, Sigma, USA) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Subsequently, 25 µg of protein per sample was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-β-actin (1:5000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-SNRPB2 (1:1000, 13512-1-AP, Proteintech), after which horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000) was applied for 2 h, and protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime, China). Blots represent three independent experiments, with generated images quantified using specialized software ImageJ.

Cell cycle assay

Transfected cells were harvested and resuspended in ice-cold 70% ethanol, then fixed at 4 °C for more than 4 h. Cells were rinsed twice with pre-cool PBS, followed by incubation with 400 µl propidium iodide (P4170, Sigma) at 4 °C protected from light for 30 min. Cell cycle distribution was determined by flow cytometry (Beckman coulter).

RNA sequencing and data preprocessing

For RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), 1 µg of total RNA was used per sample for library preparation. The VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme) was employed to prepare strand-specific RNA-seq libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final qualified libraries were then processed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system for 150 nt paired-end sequencing. Raw sequencing reads were first assessed using FASTX-Toolkit (v.0.0.13) to trim adapters and low-quality bases. After that, clean reads were then aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome by HISAT2 (v.2.2.1) [16], with uniquely mapped reads utilized for gene read count and FPKM calculations.

Bioinformatic analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v.1.30.1) [17] with criteria of fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were used to determine functional categories, while Hallmark gene sets were applied for pathway-level annotation. Alternative splicing events (ASEs) between samples were defined and quantified using the ABLas pipeline [18]. Based on splice junction reads, these events were categorized as intron retention (IntronR), exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), mutually exclusive 5’UTRs (5pMXE), mutually exclusive 3’UTRs (3pMXE), cassette exon, A3SS&ES, and A5SS&ES. Significantly regulated ASEs (RASEs) were defined as differential splicing ratio > 0.15 and FDR < 0.05. The IGV tool [19] was used to visualize sashimi plots of transcript distribution. The abundance of isoforms in breast cancer and normal breast tissue was retrieved from the TCGA-BRCA dataset using the ASCancer Atlas [20].

Publicly available data collection

Pan-cancer expression profiles and related clinical data were obtained from the TCGA database. Concurrently, protein expression profiles were retrieved from the CPTAC database, with visualization of analysis results provided by the UALCAN online tool (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) [21]. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier plotter was employed to analyze prognostic survival information [22]. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database was consulted to examine the expression of the SNRPB2 protein in breast cancer. Furthermore, additional RNA-seq data from SNRPB2-knockdown MDA-MB-231-LM2 cells were acquired from the GEO database (GSE160952) [13] for validation. GSE176078 [23] was utilized to analyze single-cell expression profiles from TNBC patients, and expression of genes of interest was queried on scCancerExplorer [24]. The TIMER2.0 database was used to explore correlations between SNRPB2 expression and infiltration levels of major immune cell types [25]. This association analysis was performed using seven estimation methods (TIMER, CIBERSORT, quanTIseq, xCell, MCP-counter, EPIC and TIDE) after tumor purity adjustment.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least three times, with data presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism or R software. Comparisons between groups were assessed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests, with p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

SNRPB2 was upregulated expression in breast cancer and is associated with poor prognosis

Based on the samples from TCGA database, we analyzed the expression of SNRPB2 across 24 common cancer types. The results showed that SNRPB2 was significantly overexpressed in most cancers, including breast invasive cancer (BRCA), reflecting its potential oncogenic role (Fig. 1A). Analysis of RNA and protein data from the TCGA and CPTAC consistently revealed that SNRPB2 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues. Moreover, SNRPB2 expression tended to be higher in advanced stages and in the TNBC subtype (Fig. 1B-G), indicating an important link between SNRPB2 expression and cancer development and progression. To determine the prognostic relevance of SNRPB2 in BRCA, we performed overall survival (OS) analysis using Kaplan-Meier plotter, which showed that high SNRPB2 expression was significantly associated with poorer prognosis (Fig. 1H). The HPA database also confirmed higher SNRPB2 protein expression in cancerous tissue than in normal breast tissue (Fig. 1I). Collectively, these findings indicate that SNRPB2 is overexpressed in breast cancer and may server as a reliable prognostic biomarker.

Knockdown of SNRPB2 blocked the cell cycle progression and potential immunomodulatory capacity of breast cancer cells

To investigate the functional role of SNRPB2 in breast cancer, we knocked down SNRPB2 in MDA-MB-231 cells (a TNBC cell line) and confirmed its effective reduction by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A) and Western blot (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 1). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that SNRPB2 knockdown resulted in a remarkable decline in G0/G1 cells and a significant increase in G2/M phase cells, in comparison to the si-NC cells, suggesting that the cell cycle was arrested at the G2/M phase (Fig. 2C). Next, we used RNA-seq to investigate transcriptome-wide changes following SNRPB2 knockdown. A sample correlation heatmap showed high consistency among replicates within each group (Fig. 2D). Through DEGs analysis, 373 upregulated and 794 downregulated DEGs were identified (Fig. 2E). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these downregulated DEGs were significantly associated with immune inflammatory responses, signal transduction, chemotaxis, cell migration, and MHC class II molecules, and participated in cell-cell signaling processes (Fig. 2F). This suggests that SNRPB2 knockdown may contribute to suppressing cellular inflammation and optimizing immunomodulatory capacity. Furthermore, GSEA showed that SNRPB2 activates pathways such as E2F targets, MYC targets, and G2M checkpoint pathways, while inhibiting pathways related to hypoxia and metabolism (Fig. 2G). From the perspective of representative marker expression, SNRPB2 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of cytokines, chemokines, MHC class II, and cell surface proteins (SPs), all of which are important participants in modulating immune cell activity. Meanwhile, it also suppressed the expression of numerous cell cycle-related markers (Fig. 2H). These results suggest that SNRPB2 knockdown not only induces cell cycle arrest in breast cancer cells but may also have potential immunological relevance.

SNRPB2 mediates cell cycle arrest of breast cancer cells through regulating alternative splicing

Given that SNRPB2 is a core spliceosomal component, we next examined its effect on AS in TNBC cells. RNA-seq data revealed ten distinct AS event (ASE) types, primarily A3SS, A5SS, and IntronR events. However, it is noteworthy that among these ASEs, many novel events were also detected (Fig. 3A), which may possess previously uncharacterized functions. A total of 2,038 differentially altered ASEs were identified between siSNRPB2 and siNC groups, with the most abundant types being A3SS, A5SS, ES/cassetteExon and IntronR (Fig. 3B). Further, we found that cell cycle-related genes showed high splicing frequency changes upon SNRPB2 knockdown. HMGA2 was particularly notable because its expression was significantly suppressed, and its AS pattern (like A3SS, 3pMXE etc.) was also markedly regulated by SNRPB2 (Fig. 3C-D). GO enrichment analysis of genes with significantly altered ASEs showed involvement in several cell cycle-related processes, such as DNA repair and mitotic spindle changes (Fig. 3E). Therefore, SNRPB2 likely influences cancer cell cycle progression by regulating alternative splicing of target genes. For the ASEs located on the HMGA2 transcript, we examined the corresponding read mapping distribution, and found that the occurrence of this event most likely led to the loss of the 3’ end of the full-length transcript and the exclusion of middle exon region, generated a shorter protein-coding isoform (Fig. 3F). Upon structural comparison, we found that ENST00000403681.7 was the most frequently observed isoform in the TCGA-BRCA dataset (Fig. 3G). However, in our data, SNRPB2 knockdown could lead to exon 4 skipping and intron fragment truncation, which to some extent reduced the expression of full-length HMGA2 protein and instead produced the putative ENST00000354636.7 or ENST00000425208.6 isoform encoding shorter proteins (Fig. 3H). Additionally, we validated the findings of this study using another siSNRPB2 RNA-seq dataset (GSE160952), focusing on the regulation of HMGA2 pre-mRNA splicing. As expected, both RNA-seq datasets detected clear splicing imprints at the same splicing junctions, resulting in visible exon exclusion and ultimately generating a shorter protein-coding isoform (Fig. 3I). Beyond HMGA2 splicing, we also examined expression changes of selected cytokines, chemokines, and cell cycle regulators (Fig. 3J). The results were highly consistent with our own data, further supporting our findings. Overall, these data suggest that SNRPB2 promotes cell cycle dysregulation and tumorigenesis by controlling HMGA2 alternative splicing in TNBC cells.

Immunological association of SNRPB2 with immune regulatory networks in breast cancer

To explore the relationship between SNRPB2 and the tumor microenvironment, we performed immune infiltration analysis using TIMER2.0 database. SNRPB2 expression was significantly positively correlated with infiltration levels of CD8 + T cells, Th2 cells, Tregs, MDSCs, macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells in BRCA, suggesting its potential involvement in shaping the immune landscape (Fig. 4A). To more comprehensively characterize the immune-related features of SNRPB2, we analyzed a single-cell sequencing dataset of TNBC (GSE176078). SNRPB2 was not only overexpressed in cancer cells but also strongly enriched in immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells. These findings suggest that SNRPB2 may regulate immune effector functions within the TNBC tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4B). Compared with normal epithelial cells or G1/S phase genes, the over-response of G2/M phase genes in cancer cells further confirms that cancer cells are primed for mitosis and cell division, typically reflecting higher proliferative activity and stronger invasiveness (Fig. 4C). Notably, SNRPB2 knockdown potently induced G2/M phase arrest in TNBC cells, which is particularly important for slowing tumor growth. Single-cell data also confirmed that cell cycle regulators, including HMGA2, were highly expressed in cancer cells, and that multiple cytokines/chemokines were extensively enriched in tumor-infiltrating immune cells (e.g., myeloid cells and T cells) (Fig. 4D). This pattern may be closely related to activation of innate and adaptive immune responses, recruiting immune cells into the tumor microenvironment through chemotaxis and thereby regulating anti-tumor immunity. To validate our transcriptomic findings, we performed RT-qPCR analysis in MDA-MB-231 cells, which the results demonstrated that SNRPB2 knockdown significantly decreased the expression of genes such as CSF1, CSF1R, IL6, and CX3CL1 (Fig. 4E). These results further support the immunomodulatory role of SNRPB2 in breast cancer, highlighting its involvement in regulating inflammatory signaling and immune cell recruitment pathways. In summary, these findings suggest that SNRPB2 actively contributes to G2/M cell cycle arrest and shapes the breast cancer immune microenvironment, potentially representing a novel therapeutic target for intervention.

Discussion

Recent pan-cancer studies have identified multiple genes that are dysregulated in breast cancer, playing critical roles in cell cycle control, immune infiltration, and patient prognosis [26–29]. These findings highlight the complexity of molecular drivers in breast cancer and underscore the need to further characterize novel oncogenic regulators. The role of SNRPB2 in breast cancer and its related regulatory mechanisms remain largely unknown. Here, we investigated the expression and prognostic significance of SNRPB2 in breast cancer, as well as its potential effects on tumor cell cycle and immune modification, and explored the corresponding potential regulatory mechanisms.

Increasing evidence suggests that high SNRPB2 expression is closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression [12–14]. By interrogating multiple public databases, we validated the oncogenic potential of SNRPB2. Our analysis showed that SNRPB2 is significantly upregulated in breast cancer and is associated with poor prognosis, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target. To further confirm SNRPB2 as a promising therapeutic target for breast cancer, we conducted in vitro knockdown experiments, which showed that knockdown of SNRPB2 significantly induced G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, supporting its potential role in promoting tumor progression. Abnormal cell cycle activation is a well-established driver of tumorigenesis, and targeting cell cycle regulators to induce arrest is a promising anti-cancer strategy [30]. Regardless, these observations provided new perspectives and clues for deepening our understanding of breast cancer pathogenesis, revealing an emerging role for SNRPB2 in breast cancer development. By investigating SNRPB2 expression and its relationship with the cell cycle, we have further refined the molecular regulatory network of breast cancer and broadened our knowledge of its biological characteristics, laying the foundation for subsequent in-depth mechanistic studies.

Abnormal splicing has been recognized as an important factor in tumorigenesis, and numerous studies have linked AS to breast cancer initiation and progression [31–33]. As a core component of the spliceosome, SNRPB2 is essential for U2 snRNP formation and pre-spliceosome assembly, and its disruption may impair RNA synthesis genome-wide [34]. Our RNA-seq analysis showed that SNRPB2 knockdown causes widespread gene downregulation and aberrant splicing events. More importantly, SNRPB2 appears to mis-splice HMGA2 pre-mRNA and reduce full-length HMGA2 transcript expression. HMGA2 has previously been reported to alter chromatin structure, making certain genes more accessible to transcription factors and thereby regulating gene expression [35]. However, whether the observed splicing events inhibits its downstream regulatory function remains unknown. HMGA2 is a well-known oncogene that primarily promotes cell cycle entry and inhibits apoptosis to increase cancer cell proliferation [36]. Several studies have shown that HMGA2 inhibition also induces G2/M arrest in breast cancer cells [37, 38]. Together with our findings, these results suggest that SNRPB2 knockdown may block progression into mitosis by altering HMGA2 splicing, thereby suppressing tumor progression.

In present study, we also found that SNRPB2 knockdown suppresses many immune-related genes, as confirmed by qPCR for CSF1, CSF1R, IL-6, and CX3CL1. These findings indicate that SNRPB2 may function both as an oncogene and an immunoregulatory factor, highlighting its pathogenic complexity in breast cancer. The CSF1/CSF1R axis controls macrophage production, differentiation, and function [39], while IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine involved in immune responses, inflammation, hematopoiesis, and metabolism [40]. CX3CL1 acts as a chemoattractant for many immune cells and may drive the recruitment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [41]. From the perspective of immune cell infiltration, high expression of SNRPB2 appears to correlate with the infiltration of pro-inflammatory or immunosuppressive cells (likes MDSCs, macrophages, neutrophils, and Tregs), which promote immune tolerance and tumor immune escape [42]. Therefore, we propose that SNRPB2 knockdown may restore immune homeostasis by suppressing the secretion of immune-related mediators, ultimately inhibiting breast cancer progression.

In conclusion, our study reveals SNRPB2 is a significant oncogene and prognostic marker in breast cancer, with dual effects on cell cycle regulation and immune modulation. We demonstrate that SNRPB2 knockdown induces G2/M phase arrest and alters alternative splicing of HMGA2, while also suppressing the expression of key immune-related genes such as CSF1, CSF1R, IL6, and CX3CL1. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the current work primarily relies on bioinformatic analyses and in vitro cellular experiments using a single TNBC cell line. Multi-level validation, including independent clinical cohorts, additional breast cancer cell lines, and in vivo animal models (e.g., xenograft or orthotopic tumor studies), is essential to fully establish the translational potential of SNRPB2-targeted therapy. Future studies will also need to dissect the precise molecular mechanisms by which SNRPB2-mediated HMGA2 splicing controls the cell cycle and how SNRPB2-regulated cytokines shape the tumor immune microenvironment. Despite these limitations, our findings provide a strong foundation for further investigation and open new avenues for SNRPB2 as a promising therapeutic target in breast cancer.
