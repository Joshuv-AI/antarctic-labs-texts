The effect of foam concrete grouting on urban structure vibrations in Tabriz metro lines 1 and 2
Authors: Samaneh Khodaei, Hamid Chakeri, Mohammad Darbor, Amir Hossein Nickjouye Tabrizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The construction and operation of urban train tunnels, especially beneath dilapidated areas, often cause vibrations that disturb residents. A prime example is the Tabriz metro’s lines 1 and 2, where vibrations exceeded permissible limits, leading to significant public dissatisfaction. This necessitated a 20 dB reduction in vibration levels. Consequently, a foam concrete injection plan was laboratory-tested for its quality and performance as a vibration insulation material. The results show that replacing 60% of conventional grout with foam concrete for injection behind segments in EPB (earth pressure balance) tunneling is an innovative approach that leads to a 56.5% reduction in water bleeding, a 71.42% increase in gel time, a 30% increase in viscosity, a 35.8% decrease in compressive strength, and a 48% decrease in wave propagation speed. The one-day uniaxial compressive strength of this mix design is 0.28 MPa, making it suitable for injection behind segments. Ultrasonic test results on foam concrete samples indicate a wave propagation speed of 237.31 m/s. Consequently, among the five tested mix designs, the one containing 60% foam, due to its suitable properties for injection and reduction of wave propagation speed, can effectively reduce urban structure vibrations. Furthermore, to assess the feasibility of foam concrete injection instead of conventional grout, this design was implemented over a 120-meter section of Line 2. Ultimately, the injection results and settlement measurements indicate the successful application of this design.

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of cities and population growth, the need for developing public transportation infrastructure, especially metro systems, is increasingly felt. However, the implementation of tunneling projects in dilapidated urban areas presents specific challenges. One of these challenges is the vibrational effects caused by the movement of metro train locomotives from the tunnel on residents in adjacent areas during construction and operation. Environmental vibrations caused by trains can lead to structural distortion, uneven settlement of track foundations, and tunnel seepage, which affect the safety of the structures and foundations. Underground trains propagate sound and vibrations through air noise and by transferring vibrations through the rails to the surrounding ground. The sound wave propagates at ground level and is transmitted to adjacent buildings through soil-structure interactions. Studies have shown that the suitable frequency range for ground vibrations from underground urban railways is approximately 20 to 100 Hz. Song et al. (2024) examined Line 3 of the Nanchang Metro in China. In this study, a numerical model was used to analyze the environmental vibrations caused by the metro train. They conducted research using the numerical model to investigate the impact of train speed on environmental vibrations, and the characteristics of environmental vibrations caused by the train were analyzed. The results indicated that environmental vibrations from the metro train increase with the increase in train speed, and the vibration peaks consistently emerge around 63 Hz1–3.

Various studies have been carried out on the general properties of grout and the impact of injecting conventional grout into the space behind segments to reduce surface settlements in EPB tunneling. Daniel Pila et al. (2011) examined different types of cement grouts; particularly two-component grout used in the space behind segments. In this research, grout tests were conducted, and samples were tested for compressive strength. The results indicated that grout injection reduces the risk of clogging pipes and pumps and fills the cavities in the space behind segments. Additionally, the strength of the grout significantly increases over time4. Sharghi et al. (2017) investigated the mechanical properties of grout. The results showed that as the cement content increases, the strength of the grout also increases; however, the increase in grout strength has little effect on surface settlement5. Rahmati et al. (2022) examined uniaxial and triaxial compressive strength after studying the mechanical properties of grout. The results indicated that increasing the amount of bentonite and sodium silicate in the grout mixture significantly contributes to its strength6. Giulio et al. (2020) carried out a study on two-component grout that is usually injected behind segments by tunneling machines. The results showed that the properties of bentonite affect the mechanical characteristics of the grout and the hydration time7. Todaro et al. (2021) investigated the shear strength of grout and its evolution over time. This study demonstrated that, in addition to compressive strength, shear strength plays a crucial role in the stability of tunnel lining. Furthermore, the friction angle and cohesion of the grout at different ages depend on the mix design8. Barry et al. (2024) conducted a comprehensive study on the properties of injection grout and the effects of its injection behind segments, utilizing two-component grout in their research. They used numerical modeling to determine the amount of surface settlement in the zone of grout injection. The findings showed that injection pressure can significantly reduce surface settlement. In this study, increasing the injection pressure from 0 to 120 kilopascals reduced surface settlement by 17%9. Khoshzaher et al. (2024) examined the impacts of bentonite and sodium silicate in the composition of injection grout behind segments. In this research, mixed designs with varying amounts of bentonite and sodium silicate were considered. The results indicated that enhancing the quantity of bentonite and sodium silicate in the grout mixture contributes to higher compressive strength, reduced gel time, and increased viscosity. In this study, the UCS (Uniaxial Compressive Strength) of the grout raised to 0.682 MPa at 28 days of age10.

Foam concrete is a cement paste or mortar classified as lightweight concrete in which air voids are trapped by a suitable foaming agent in the mixture. It has high viscosity, low weight, controlled low strength, and excellent thermal insulation properties. With proper control of the foam dosage, a wide range of densities (400–1600 kg/m³) of foam concrete can be achieved for applications in structural grades, partitions, insulation, and fillers11. Many researchers have studied the physical, functional, and microscopic properties of foam concrete over the years. These researches have examined the properties of both fresh and hardened foam concrete, its permeability, and the strength of foam concrete grout. In other research, foam concrete grout has been used for vibration insulation in high-speed railways and twin tunnels. In this context, numerical studies have also been conducted to analyze the transmitted vibrations. Kearsley et al. (2001) conducted a study on the properties of foam concrete. The results showed that the volume of water absorbed by foam concrete is approximately twice that of ordinary cement. Additionally, water vapor permeability increased with increasing porosity12. Fei et al. (2015) performed numerical calculations to assess the damping effect of a foam concrete cushion layer during the passage through high-speed railway tunnels. They evaluated the measured damping effects using indicators such as displacement and acceleration. The results indicated that the foam concrete cushion layer has a good damping effect, which is significant at thicknesses of 5 to 10 cm. They recommended the use of this vibration-reduction design13.

Raj et al. (2019) investigated the physical and functional properties of foam concrete. The findings showed that the consistency of the concrete increased with the amount of water and decreased with the amount of foam, while compressive strength increased with density14. Gencel et al. (2022) studied the fresh, hardened, and physical properties of foam concrete. The results showed that an increase in foam volume significantly decreases consistency, increases air voids, and raises the number of interconnected pores, which affects strength. Additionally, increasing the volume of foam also impacts the density of foam concrete. They further demonstrated that adding fillers such as fly ash positively influences strength properties15. Lin et al. (2022) stated that concrete materials can reduce the transmission of train vibrations to adjacent tunnels during the operation of closely spaced twin tunnels. This study utilized the Discrete Element Method (DEM) and an experimental model (scaled at 1:20) to examine the impact of train vibrations at a speed of 120 km/h. Foam bricks (FB) were used in the gap between the two tunnels. The results showed that this approach effectively prevents the propagation of train vibration waves16. Hao et al. (2022) investigated the effects of noise and vibration from urban rail transit on nearby buildings in Tianjin and proposed mitigation measures. Field measurements showed that while indoor vibration could be reduced by up to 6 dB, the effectiveness of mitigation depends on the attenuation of the main frequency corresponding to the secondary radiation noise of the indoor vibration excitation in the building17. Wang et al. (2023) conducted field measurements to assess the environmental impact of vibrations generated by double-shield TBM tunneling in urban subways. The results showed that vibration levels near residential areas exceeded national limits, suggesting that parameters such as cutter-head torque, thrust, rotation speed, and penetration should be reduced to mitigate the effects18.

Lei et al. (2023) investigated the physical, mechanical, and microscopic properties of foam concrete mixed with soil. The results indicated that excessive soil and foam reduce the overall stability of foam concrete, and the uniaxial compressive strength decreases with increasing foam and soil content19. Priyatham et al. (2023) examined foam concrete in terms of porosity, compressive strength, thermal insulation, and sound absorption. This study evaluated the type of foaming agent, additives, fresh properties, physical properties, and mechanical properties. They noted that the strength of foam concrete, as well as the fresh and hardened properties and the resulting porosity, are directly affected by factors, including the type of foam, cement, and mineral additives20. Şimşek et al. (2024) investigated foam concrete mixtures primarily used for thermal insulation purposes. This study aimed to utilize Construction and Demolition Waste (CDW) as a source of fine aggregates (0–4 mm) in the production of foam concrete grout. The results indicated that mixtures with higher foam content have a greater number of larger pores. These pores form interconnected structures that are porous. Additionally, the incorporation of more Fine Recycled Materials (FRM) reduces overall porosity and increases density. The findings also showed that mixtures with higher foam content provide better thermal insulation21. Tuil et al. (2024) investigated ground vibrations caused by railway traffic and proposed a practical solution using foam concrete blocks to mitigate these vibrations. This study outlined the challenges associated with the long wavelengths of vibrations caused by trains. The results indicated that the fundamental frequency of ground vibrations is approximately 18 Hz, which is not affected by the foam concrete blocks22. Mayhoub et al. (2025) stated that Lightweight Foam Concrete (LFC) stands out as a prominent option due to its beneficial properties, including low density, thermal insulation, and soundproofing. This study examines the advancements made in the development of LFC. Additionally, it analyzes the effects of various production parameters on key properties such as workability, porosity, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity23.

Wang et al. (2025) stated that high-speed trains traveling at 400 km/h generate severe alternating pressure and potential sonic booms when passing through tunnels. In this study, they suggested using Foam Metal Hoods (FMH) to reduce the pressure waves caused by trains passing through tunnels. The results indicated that the FMH inlet can attenuate the expansion wave generated by the train entering the tunnel24. As highlighted in the literature review, the problem of vibrations exists in most projects, for which various solutions have been implemented. Previous research has comprehensively examined the physical, mechanical, and performance characteristics of foam concrete. In some studies, the performance of foam concrete as thermal and sound insulation has been investigated. Additionally, foam concrete has been used as a vibration-damping material in high-speed railway systems and closely spaced twin tunnels. However, this time, an innovative approach has been taken to utilize foam concrete as a vibration and shock insulation material in tunnels beneath deteriorating urban areas during excavation. This research was conducted in three phases: field surveys to confirm the presence of vibrations, laboratory examinations to assess the effectiveness of injecting foam concrete behind segments as a replacement for conventional grout injection, and an evaluation of the reduction in vibrations and the feasibility of implementing the proposed plan. In the course of this study, initial field surveys were conducted to measure the vibrations transmitted to the ground surface.

Subsequently, in the laboratory, several mix designs were selected, and tests related to the grout (viscosity, water bleeding, gel time, uniaxial compressive strength) and ultrasonic testing were performed to evaluate the physical and mechanical properties of the foam concrete mixture. After testing several mix designs of foam concrete, one mix that had suitable characteristics for injection was selected; ultimately, the chosen mix was implemented in Line 2 of the Tabriz Metro over 80 rings (120 m). This study addresses several questions in this area: “Is injecting foam concrete into the areas behind the segments effective in reducing vibrations transmitted to the ground surface?” “Does the injected foam concrete have suitable characteristics, performance, and strength?” “Is the proposed plan for injecting foam concrete into the areas behind the segments for vibration reduction feasible?” The most significant advantage of this innovative approach is the ability to reduce ground vibrations without incurring additional costs during excavation with the EPB machine. Vibration isolation methods

To mitigate vibrations generated during the construction and operation of metro tunnels, various methods have been developed, generally classified into three main categories: source control, propagation path control, and receiver structure protection.

Source control methods

These approaches focus on reducing vibrations at their point of generation. For vibrations caused during operation, optimization of track design such as the use of Floating Slab Tracks (FST) is a common strategy. Although FSTs are highly effective (achieving reductions of 20–30 dB), they are complex, expensive, and often require significant space and structural modifications.

Propagation path control methods

These involve installing barriers in the soil between the vibration source and the receiving structures, including Wave Impeding Blocks (WIB), isolation trenches, and ground improvement techniques such as jet grouting. While these barriers can effectively block surface waves, their implementation in dense urban environments is costly and logistically challenging, often causing major disruptions to traffic and existing underground utilities.

Receiver protection methods

This category involves strengthening or isolating vulnerable structures themselves, for instance, through the use of base isolation systems. However, this method is highly customized and expensive, making it unsuitable as a scalable solution for protecting entire urban areas.

In contrast to the above methods, the use of foam concrete grouting behind tunnel segments, as proposed in this study, provides an innovative and integrated path-control approach. The main advantage of foam concrete grouting lies in its seamless integration into the standard TBM construction sequence. It acts as a continuous, ring-shaped isolation layer around the tunnel lining without requiring separate, disruptive construction activities. This makes it a preventive and cost-effective solution that simultaneously addresses vibrations induced during both construction and future train operations.

While methods such as FSTs are excellent for newly designed lines and isolation trenches are effective but disruptive, foam concrete grouting offers a practical compromise with significant advantages for tunneling projects in vibration-sensitive urban areas.

Introduction to lines 1 and 2 of Tabriz metro

Lines 1 and 2 of the Tabriz Metro projects are part of the urban transport development of Tabriz. The Tabriz metro network includes four lines with a total length of approximately 100 km. The main route and design of the Tabriz metro, which includes four lines and several stations, is illustrated in Fig. 225,26. Line 1 of the Tabriz Metro has a length of 16 km, with 8.5 km consisting of a twin tunnel with a final inner diameter of 6 m, excavated using an Earth Pressure Balance TBM (Tunnel Boring Machine). This line comprises 11 underground stations. Line 1 passes through historical areas of the city and several archaeological sites that are highly sensitive to subsidence caused by underground excavations. Line 2 of the Tabriz Metro is approximately 22.4 km long. It begins in the Qaramalek area in the western part of the city and, after passing through the city center, ends at the Tabriz International Exhibition located in Basij Square in the eastern part of the city. This line includes 20 stations and traverses busy areas of the city, particularly commercial zones. The entire tunnel route is underground, and most areas consist of alluvial soils27.

Laboratory tests

After observing the excessive vibrations transmitted to the ground in areas where conventional grout was injected behind the segments, a new proposal was presented. As an innovative approach, the injection of foam concrete grout was suggested for a section under construction in Line 2 (from kilometer 11 + 885 to kilometer 11 + 760) over a length of 120 m. At this stage of the research, the properties of foam concrete were evaluated, comparing it with conventional grout and assessing the feasibility of substituting one for the other. To investigate the injection of foam concrete behind the segments to reduce vibrations, several laboratory tests were conducted. For these tests, five mix designs with increasing foam content were considered. The foam was produced using a laboratory-made device. Viscosity, water bleeding, and gel time tests were performed on fresh foam concrete grout. Additionally, 15 cylindrical samples were prepared for uniaxial compressive strength and ultrasonic tests. In mix designs with a foam content below 30%, the damping capacity of foam concrete decreases significantly and can be considered roughly equivalent to that of ordinary grout. Conversely, when the foam content exceeds 60%, the strength of the produced foam concrete is greatly reduced, making it unsuitable for backfill grouting behind tunnel segments. Therefore, in this study, foam contents ranging from 30% to 60%, with 10% incremental increases, were investigated.

Materials

The grout mixture used behind the segments comprise water, cement, bentonite, and sodium silicate as an accelerator. For the preparation of the foam concrete sample, the foam was also included in addition to these components. The amount of foam incorporated into the mix designs varies from 30% to 60%. The water utilized in this study is drinking water. The cement used is Type 2 Soufiyan cement with a density of 3050 kg/m³ and a compressive strength of 42 MPa. The bentonite used is an activated type (activated bentonite is made from swelling clays and minerals, with at least 85 to 90% montmorillonite content) sourced from Salafchegan, having a specific gravity of 2132 kg/m³. Liquid sodium silicate, branded as SA-161, with a specific solution weight of 1500 kg/m³, was used as an accelerator. The components of the foam concrete grout are depicted in Fig. 3.

Sample preparation

To prepare the foam concrete mixture, the solid raw materials (cement and bentonite) were first mixed together in dry conditions using a mixer for 2 min. While mixing the dry materials, water was gradually added to ensure uniform mixing and to prevent the aggregation and separation of solid materials. After the specified amount of water was added entirely, the mixing process continued for an additional 2 min to prepare the cement grout. The foam was simultaneously prepared during the production of the cement grout. The prepared foam was added to the cement grout and continuously mixed for 1 min to create a fresh paste. In the final stage, sodium silicate was used as an accelerator and added to the prepared mixture to increase the setting rate. Ultimately, the foam concrete mixture was ready for use in various tests.

Foam generator and foam preparation method

In this study, a laboratory-made foam generator was used to produce the required foam. This device consists of a cylindrical tank with an internal diameter of 15.24 cm and a volume of 10 L. The tank features a ¾-inch inlet for pouring the solution, along with two pairs of 8 mm (one for applying pressure and the other for transferring the solution) and 10 mm (for installing an indicator tube) inlets and outlets. The mixing generator includes a 3-inch steel pipe that is confined at one end by a mixing system and at the other by a 4-bolt flange. Inside this generator, sand that has passed through a No. 8 sieve and residue remaining on a No. 20 sieve is placed. To prevent sand movement into the pipes, sponges are used at both the beginning and the end of the generator. The device is equipped with a pressure regulator to control the inlet air pressure and a 50-liter compressor for generating compressed air. This combination contributes to the production of high-quality foam. A schematic representation of the laboratory foam generator is presented in Fig. 4. The air pressure generated by the compressor performs two essential functions: first, the air is directed toward the flow meter, where the flow rate is adjusted before being sent to the mixing system of air and solution for converting the solution into foam. On the other hand, after passing through the regulator and stabilizing the pressure, the air enters the pressure tank of the device, facilitating the movement of the foam solution from the pressure tank to the solution flow meter. After the flow rate is set, it moves toward the air and solution mixing system to convert the solution into foam. Ultimately, foam with the specified characteristics is produced.

Methodology of experiments

In this study, various tests were conducted to assess the physical, mechanical, and performance properties of both fresh and hardened foam concrete, including the Marsh funnel viscosity test, water bleeding test, gel time test, uniaxial compressive strength test, and ultrasonic test.

Marsh funnel viscosity test

The Marsh funnel viscosity test is performed according to ASTM D691029 on the fresh mixed grout component (without the addition of sodium silicate). First, one liter of the prepared foam concrete mixture is measured. after blocking the outlet of the funnel, the mixture is poured into the funnel through the mesh section. The cone is then placed over a graduated cup, and the outlet is opened to enable the grout to flow from the funnel into the graduated cup until it reaches a specified volume of 946 milliliters. The duration of this process is referred to as the Marsh funnel time.

Water bleeding test

The water bleeding test is conducted in accordance with ASTM C940-1630. After preparing one liter of the fresh grout component and ensuring its uniformity, the mixture is poured into a graduated cylinder. The open surface of the cylinder is sealed with a lid and placed in a stable location free from vibrations. After 24 h, the amount of water accumulated in the mixture is measured and recorded.

Gel time test

The gel time test is conducted by adding the second component of the grout, namely sodium silicate, to the first component. After adding the sodium silicate to the foam concrete mixture, the mixture is transferred from one container to another. This process continues until the initial setting occurs, and the foam concrete mixture attains a gel-like consistency. The time taken for this process is recorded as the gel time. Uniaxial compressive strength test (UCS)

In this study, the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) test was performed to evaluate the mechanical properties of the prepared foam concrete mixture and determine its resistance to pressure. For this purpose, cylindrical samples aged 1 day were prepared for all mix designs. The samples had a diameter of 5.4 cm and a length of 15 cm. To ensure reliable results, three samples were created for testing UCS for each mix design. The uniaxial compressive strength test was performed according to ASTM C109-9931 on the hardened grout.

To prepare the required samples for the uniaxial test, the foam concrete mixture was poured into cylindrical molds. The grout was vibrated to ensure the mixture conformed well to the shape of the mold. The samples were removed from the molds after 24 h had passed. In total, 15 cylindrical samples from the specified mix designs in this study were subjected to the UCS test. Ultrasonic test (UT)

Acoustics is the science of sound, describing the phenomenon of mechanical vibrations and their propagation in solid, liquid, or gas materials. Acoustic waves have various technical applications in both medical and engineering fields. These waves can be used to examine the physical condition of a material32. The ultrasonic test is a type of non-destructive testing that employs high-frequency sound waves to assess materials or objects. In this study, ultrasonic testing was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of adding foam to the grout to reduce vibrations transmitted to the ground surface. Results and discussion

In this study, tests for water bleeding, Marsh funnel viscosity, gel time, compressive strength (UCS), and ultrasonic testing (UT) were performed. Based on the results, the water bleeding in all mix designs after 24 h is less than 5%, which is acceptable according to standards. It is observed that as the percentage of foam in the foam concrete mixture increases, the water bleeding decreases. With the increase in foam content, the number of voids and porosity in the mixture also increases. The presence of air, voids, and pores in the foam concrete mixture leads to a reduction in water bleeding. The measured gel time for the mix designs ranges from 7 to 12 s. The foam consists of water, a foaming agent, and air, which results in the dilution of the grout when this material is added. The more diluted the mixture, the longer the gel time takes. Consequently, with the increase in foam percentage in the mixture, the gel time also shows an upward trend. The measured and recorded Marsh funnel time for the mix designs ranges from 40 to 52 s. It is observed that as the percentage of foam increases, the Marsh funnel time also increases. With the increase in foam content in the mixture, the air-induced voids also increase. As a result, these air-induced voids hinder the flow of the mixture, and thus, with the increase in foam percentage, the Marsh funnel time also increases.

According to the results in Table 6 and the graph in Fig. 7, the compressive strength of the samples decreases compared to the pure grout without foam as the foam percentage increases. This decrease in compressive strength is related to the increased porosity in the samples. As shown in Table 7 and the graph in Fig. 9, the wave propagation velocity decreases with the increase in foam percentage in the mixture. This indicates that the presence of foam in the mixture plays an important role as an acoustic and vibration insulator. With the increase in foam percentage, the performance of foam concrete as an acoustic and vibration insulator improves. By adding foam to the concrete mixture, two primary structural changes occur: a reduction in density and a decrease in elastic modulus. Moreover, as the foam content in the mix increases, the porosity also rises. This increased porosity leads to a decrease in wave propagation velocity and plays a significant role in reducing the transmission of vibrations to the ground surface. According to the graph in Fig. 12, the quantitative results recorded by the settlement pins indicate that the settlements in the conventional grout injection zone range between a maximum of 17 mm and a minimum of 0 mm. In the foam concrete injection zone, the recorded settlements range between a maximum of 14 mm and a minimum of 1 mm. The settlement variation patterns in both zones exhibit a consistent and similar trend, demonstrating that replacing 60% of the conventional grout with foam concrete has no adverse effect on tunnel stability or ground deformation control.

One of the objectives of this study was to select an ideal mix design that possesses both the physical and mechanical properties suitable for grout injection behind segments and reduces vibrations transmitted to the ground surface. According to the results presented, the grout with the mix design 460-F60 exhibits appropriate water bleeding and gel time characteristics. The Marsh funnel time for this design is recorded at 52 s, which is below the maximum allowable time of 60 s. This feature is significant due to the grout’s injectability and pumpability behind the segments. The compressive strength of this design is 0.28 MPa, which falls within the acceptable range of 0.2 to 0.4 MPa according to the tunnel quality control guidelines34.

The ultrasonic test also indicated that this design significantly reduces the velocity of sound and vibrations compared to other designs. Therefore, based on the explanations above and the results presented in this study, the grout 460-F60 possesses the necessary characteristics for injection behind the segments. In other studies and research, foam concrete mixtures have primarily been used as thermal insulation20,21,23. In some cases, foam concrete blocks have been employed to reduce vibrations in high-speed railways13,24. The injection of foam concrete grout behind segments in a tunnel being excavated with an EPB machine has been implemented for the first time in Line 2 of the Tabriz metro. The effectiveness of this design has been demonstrated in the present study.

Conclusion

During the excavation and operation of urban metro rail transport systems, vibrations are generated that are transmitted through the soil, causing vibrations in surface structures. The extent of vibration transmission can be controlled through soil reinforcement and vibration reduction. Many surface structures have been designed in the vicinity of urban rail transport systems. Therefore, the vibrations and noise effects caused by trains must be carefully evaluated to provide a comfortable working or living environment for the residents of the buildings35,36. This study demonstrates the successful implementation of foam concrete as a vibration-damping backfill grout in urban metro tunneling. The key implications of our findings are as follows:
