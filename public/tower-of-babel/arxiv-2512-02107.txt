Generalised 4d Partition Functions and Modular Differential Equations
and Palash Singh

Abstract
We prove the equivalence of a class of generalised Schur partition functions 𝒵 G ​ ( q , α ) {\cal Z}_{G}(q;\alpha) of 4d 𝒩 = 2 {\cal N}=2 superconformal gauge theories to contour integral representations of vector-valued modular forms of the type that arise in 2d rational conformal field theories (RCFT). Concretely, we consider the U ​ S ​ p ​ ( 2 ​ N ) USp(2N) theory with 2 ​ N + 2 2N+2 fundamental hypermultiplets and analytically prove that 𝒵 U ​ S ​ p ​ ( 2 ​ N ) ​ ( q , α ) {\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha) satisfies an order- ( N + 1 ) (N+1) modular linear differential equation (MLDE) with vanishing Wronskian index, explaining how the parameter α \alpha of the former determines the parameters of the latter. Several connections are made to characters of RCFTs including unitary ones. We then propose a two-parameter extension 𝒵 U ​ S ​ p ​ ( 2 ​ N ) ​ ( q , α , β ) {\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta) of the generalised Schur partition function. Finally, we relate the α = − k \alpha=-k specialisation to quantum monodromy traces Tr ​ M k {\rm Tr}\,M^{k} and formulate a conjecture linking their k k -dependence to MLDEs.

Introduction and Summary

The study of four-dimensional
{\cal N}=2
superconformal field theories (SCFT) has revealed a remarkably rich algebraic structure in their protected observables. The superconformal index is one such protected observable that plays a distinguished role in organising protected spectra, testing non-trivial dualities, and revealing rigid algebraic structures
[
1
,
2
]
.

A particularly striking manifestation of such a rigid structure is the 4d/2d correspondence, which associates a vertex operator algebra (VOA) to any 4d
{\cal N}=2
SCFT via a cohomological construction
[
3
]
. For a unitary 4d
{\cal N}=2
SCFT, the associated VOA is necessarily non-unitary and encodes the spectrum and OPE data of a protected subsector of quarter-BPS operators, known as the Schur operators. A key consequence of this correspondence is that the Schur limit of the superconformal index
[
4
,
5
]
, referred to as the
Schur index
, coincides with the vacuum character of the associated VOA. Moreover, in cases where the associated VOA is quasi-lisse
[
6
]
, the Schur indices satisfy a modular linear differential equation (MLDE)
[
7
]
.

Rather surprisingly, the Schur index of a 4d
{\cal N}=2
SCFT can also be computed by an infrared formula as the trace of the inverse of the Kontsevich-Soibelmann monodromy operator
[
8
]
(also see
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
). Within this framework, it has been observed in examples that traces of higher powers, say
k
, of the monodromy operator yield
q
-series that can be identified with vacuum characters of certain vertex algebras
{\cal V}^{(k)}

[
13
,
14
]
. While

k>0
encountered in many examples match the characters of unitary VOAs, including rational and logarithmic 2d conformal field theories.

Independent of these considerations, one can define a one-parameter generalisation of the Schur index of an
{\cal N}=2
superconformal gauge theory
{\cal Z}_{G}(q;\alpha)
that was termed the
generalised Schur partition function

[
15
]
. These are defined via a double scaling limit of the full superconformal index in such a way that
\alpha=1
trivially returns the usual Schur index of the SCFT. Based on empirical observations, it was suggested that the generalised Schur partition functions of certain gauge theories numerically coincide with Schur indices of different SCFTs, with the same rank, for finely tuned positive rational values of
\alpha
.

Our goal in the present work is to take a concrete step towards systematising some of these observations by mapping a family of generalised Schur partition functions to known contour integral representations of vector-valued modular forms (VVMF), possibly of logarithmic or quasi-modular type, that solve an MLDE
[
16
,
17
,
18

[

Concretely, we consider the analytically continued generalised Schur partition function of the

SCFT with
2N+2
fundamental hypermultiplets.

Focusing on the

Subsequently, we formulate an analogous proposition for general

solves a one-parameter sub-family of the order-(

Background review

In this section, we provide a basic review of the various four-dimensional and two-dimensional constructions of interest. We begin by a lightning review of the full superconformal index to set up notation. We then restrict to the Schur limit of the superconformal index and discuss the case of
{\cal N}=2
superconformal gauge theories. We conclude the 4d review by discussing the construction of the generalised Schur partition function. Switching gears, we first discuss the modular linear differential equation approach to RCFT characters, followed by a review of the contour integral representation of these characters in certain cases and their connection to MLDE.

2.1
Superconformal Index

The general superconformal index of a four-dimensional
{\cal N}=2
superconformal field theory is a supersymmetric partition function that counts
1/8
BPS cohomologies
[
1
,
2
]
(also see
[
23
]
). This depends on three fugacities coupled to three different combinations of charges in the superconformal algebra that commute with a chosen supercharge and its conformal superpartner, and thus survive in the BPS cohomology.

Concretely, the superconformal index is the
S^{3}\times S^{1}
supersymmetric partition function

{\cal I}(p,q,t,\mathbf{a})={\rm Tr}_{S^{3}}\,(-1)^{F}\,p^{j_{2}-j_{1}-r}\,q^{j_{1}+j_{2}-r}\,t^{R+r}\prod_{i=1}^{{\rm rk}\,G_{F}}a_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}~,

(2.1)

where
j_{1},j_{2}
are the Cartan generators of
SU(2)\times SU(2)
associated to the
S^{3}
,
R
is the Cartan generator of the
SU(2)_{R}

R
-symmetry,
r
is the charge of the
U(1)_{r}

R
-symmetry, and
\lambda_{i}
are the Cartan generators of the
G_{F}
flavour symmetry. We will only be interested in superconformal indices with no fugacity for the flavour symmetries, so we set all
a_{i}=1
.

The basic building blocks of 4d
{\cal N}=2
Lagrangian SCFTs are the
{\cal N}=2
hypermultiplets and vector multiplets. A general
{\cal N}=2
superconformal gauge theory with gauge group
G
contains
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplets in the adjoint representation of
G
and
N_{l}
hypermultiplets that transform in representations
\mathcal{R}_{l}
of
G

[
5
]

\begin{split}i_{V}(p,q,t,\mathbf{z})&=\left(-\frac{p}{1-p}-\frac{q}{1-q}+\frac{pq/t-t}{(1-p)(1-q)}\right)\chi_{\rm adj}(\mathbf{z})~,\\[5.69054pt]
i_{H}^{\mathcal{R}}(p,q,t,\mathbf{z})&=\frac{\sqrt{t}-pq/\sqrt{t}}{(1-p)(1-q)}\Big(\chi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{z})+\chi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}(\mathbf{z})\Big)~.\end{split}

(2.2)

The full (unflavoured) superconformal index of the
G

N_{l}
hypermultiplets in representations
R_{l}
of
G
can be expressed as

{\cal I}(p,q,t)=\oint[{\rm d}\mathbf{z}]_{G}\,{\rm PE}\left[i_{V}(p,q,t,\mathbf{z})+\sum_{l}N_{l}\,i_{H}^{\mathcal{R}_{l}}(p,q,t,\mathbf{z})\right]~,

(2.3)

where
\oint[{\rm d}\mathbf{z}]_{G}
denotes the integral over the Haar measure of
G
that projects onto gauge invariants of
G
. The symbol
{\rm PE}[\,\cdot\,]
stands for Plethystic exponential defined as

{\rm PE}\big[f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\big]={\rm Exp}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,f(x_{1}^{n},x_{2}^{n},\dots)\right)~,

(2.4)

which generates all symmetric products of the function
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
.

2.2
Schur index

There are many limits of this superconformal index that counts BPS cohomologies that preserve higher amounts of supersymmetry
[
5
]
. One such limit is the Schur limit of the superconformal index, henceforth referred to as the
Schur index
, that receives contributions from states annihilated by more than one supercharge. More precisely, the Schur limit of the superconformal index counts quarter-BPS cohomologies and is defined as

t=q\;,\;\text{ arbitrary $p$.}

(2.5)

It can be easily checked that the charge associated to the fugacity
p
also commutes with the supercharges that annihilate Schur operators, and hence the dependence on
p
drops out. Therefore, the Schur index can be expressed as the following trace over the
S^{3}
Hilbert space

\widehat{{\cal I}}(q)={\rm Tr}_{S^{3}}\,(-1)^{F}q^{\Delta-R}~,

(2.6)

where
\Delta
is the scaling dimension.

2.2.2
Schur indices for gauge theories

Let us now consider a Lagrangian
\mathcal{N}=2
SCFT with gauge algebra
G
and
N_{l}
hypermultiplets in representation
\mathcal{R}_{l}
of
G
. The single-letter indices for the vector multiplet and hypermultiplets simplify in this limit and become

i_{V}(q,\mathbf{z})=-\frac{2q}{1-q}\,\chi_{\rm adj}(\mathbf{z})\;,\;i_{H}^{\mathcal{R}}(q,\mathbf{z})=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{1-q}\left(\chi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{z})+\chi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}(\mathbf{z})\right)~.

(2.10)

Therefore, the Schur index can once again be expressed as an integral over the Haar measure of the Plethystic exponential of the single letter indices by specialising Eq. (

{\cal I}(q)=q^{\frac{c_{\rm 4d}}{2}}\oint\big[{\rm d}\mathbf{z}\big]_{G}\,{\rm PE}\left[-\frac{2q}{1-q}\chi_{\rm adj}(\mathbf{z})+\sum_{l}N_{l}\,\frac{\sqrt{q}}{1-q}\left(\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{l}}(\mathbf{z})+\chi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{l}}(\mathbf{z})\right)\right]~,

(2.11)

where the integral is over the maximal torus of
G
with the standard Haar measure

\big[{\rm d}\mathbf{z}\big]_{G}=\frac{1}{|W|}\prod_{j=1}^{{\rm rk}\,G}\frac{{\rm d}z_{j}}{2\pi iz_{j}}\,\Delta(\mathbf{z})\;,\;\text{ where }\Delta(\mathbf{z})\coloneq{\rm PE}\big[{\rm rk}\,G-\chi_{\rm adj}(\mathbf{z})\big]~,

(2.12)

with
|W|
being the dimension of the Weyl group of
G

c_{\rm 4d}=\frac{2\,n_{V}+n_{H}}{12}\;,\ \text{ where }n_{V}={\rm dim}\,\mathfrak{g},\,n_{H}=\sum_{l}({\rm dim}\,\mathcal{R}_{l})\,N_{l}~.

(2.13)

Through standard identities relating

\begin{split}{\cal I}(q)=\frac{(-i)^{{\rm rk}\,G-{\rm dim}\,G}\,\eta(\tau)^{3\,{\rm rk}\,G-{\rm dim}\,G}}{|W|}\oint\prod_{j=1}^{{\rm rk}\,\mathfrak{g}}\frac{{\rm d}z_{j}}{2\pi iz_{j}}\prod_{\alpha\neq 0}\vartheta_{1}(\alpha(\mathbf{u}),\tau)\prod_{l}\prod_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{l}}\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)}{\vartheta_{4}(\rho(\mathbf{u}),\tau)}\right)^{N_{l}}~,\end{split}

(2.14)

where
\alpha
denotes the non-zero roots,
\rho
denotes the weights of the representation
\mathcal{R}_{l}
, and we define

z_{j}\coloneq e^{2\pi i\,u_{j}}\;,\quad\forall\;j~.

(2.15)

Schematically, the contribution of the vector multiplet and the Haar measure in Eq. (

\eta(\tau)
and the product over

\eta(\tau)
and
\vartheta_{4}(\rho(\mathbf{u}),\tau)

2.3
Generalised Schur partition function

The generalised Schur partition function is defined as the following double scaling limit of the full superconformal index:

p=1-\epsilon\;,\;t=(q\,p)^{1+\frac{\alpha}{{\rm log}\,q}\epsilon}\;,\;\text{ with }\epsilon\rightarrow 0~.

(2.19)

This can be best understood as interpolating between two particular limits of the full index with an order of limits issue.

The first limit is setting
q=t
first and then taking
p=1
. This is consistent with the Schur limit which leaves
p
arbitrary. The second limit corresponds to taking
p\,q=t

q=t

15

2.19

\alpha=1

\alpha=0

The single letter indices of the vector multiplet and hypermultiplets take the following form in this limit:

\begin{split}i_{V}(q,\mathbf{z};\alpha)&=\left(1-\alpha-\frac{2q}{1-q}\right)\,\chi_{\rm adj}(\mathbf{z})~,\\
i_{H}^{\mathcal{R}}(q,\mathbf{z};\alpha)&=\alpha\,\frac{\sqrt{q}}{1-q}\big(\chi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{z})+\chi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}(\mathbf{z})\big)~.\end{split}

(2.20)

[
15

2.4
Modular differential equations and the indicial equation

It has long been known
[
28
,
20
,
29
]
that the characters of 2d Rational Conformal Field Theories (RCFT) satisfy Modular Linear Differential Equations (MLDE). Over the last few decades, this has been used with some success to classify RCFT (see for example
[
30
]
and references therein). MLDEs are characterised by their Wronskian index
\ell
, a non-negative integer labelling the number of zeroes of the leading Wronskian determinant.

The general form of an MLDE is:

\Big({\cal D}^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i}\mu_{k,i}\,\phi_{2k,i}(q){\cal D}^{n-k}\Big)\chi(q)=0

(2.25)

where
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
and
{\cal D}
is the Ramanujan-Serre derivative:

{\cal D}\equiv q\frac{d}{dq}-\frac{k}{6}E_{2}(q)

(2.26)

when it acts on a modular function of weight
2k
. Here
\mu_{k}
are constants and
\phi_{2k,i}(q)
are meromorphic modular functions for
SL(2,Z)
of weight
2k
, with
i
labelling the independent functions for the given weight. The maximum number of poles they can have is given by the Wronskian index. We always normalise the
\phi_{2k,i}
so that their
q
-series starts with 1. In the special case
\ell=0
the
\phi_{2k,i}
are holomorphic and hence modular
forms
that belong to the ring generated by the Eisenstein series
E_{4}(\tau),E_{6}(\tau)
. Even in this simple case, the number of parameters in the equation grows quadratically with
n
, making it increasingly hard to solve this equation.

One of the useful pieces of information that can be extracted from an MLDE is the indicial equation which we now describe. For this we note that the general solution (except in cases where there are factors of
\log q
, which we temporarily ignore) takes the power-series form:

\chi_{A}(q)=q^{\gamma_{A}}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{A,r}\,q^{r}

(2.27)

where the
a_{A,r}
are constant coefficients and the
\gamma_{A}
, known as critical exponents, are determined in terms of the coefficients
\mu_{k,i}
. For MLDEs whose solutions are the characters of an RCFT, we identify the exponents with the conformal data
c,h_{A}
by:

\gamma_{0}=-\frac{c}{24},\qquad\gamma_{A}=\gamma_{0}+h_{A},~~A=1,2,\cdots,n-1

(2.28)

The Wronskian index for the MLDE is the number of zeroes of a Wronskian determinant made from the solutions
\chi_{A}
. For any fixed
\ell
, the exponents satisfy the following equation:

\sum_{A=0}^{n-1}\gamma_{A}=-\frac{n\,c}{24}+\sum_{A=1}^{n-1}h_{A}=\frac{n(n-1)}{12}-\frac{\ell}{6}

(2.29)

If we know the exponents
\gamma_{A}
we can determine the quantities
\nu_{k}=\sum_{i}\mu_{k,i}
in the MLDE, though not the individual values of
\mu_{k,i}
. To see this let us count the number of parameters on both sides. On one side the number of exponents is
n
, while on the other hand the MLDE has a constant
\mu_{k,i}
for every independent modular form of weight
2k
. Since
k
takes
n
values, there are
n
modular weights
2k=2,4,6,\cdots,2n
in the sum. Now the exponents are constrained by Eq. (
2.29
), but the coefficient
\nu_{1}
turns out to be constrained by the same equation as we will see below. Thus given the exponents, we can determine the
n-1
independent quantities
\nu_{k},k=2,3,\cdots,n
. Except at low weights, there are multiple modular forms for a given weight, which is the reason for the additional label
i
. Clearly knowing the
\nu_{k}
is not enough to determine the all the constants
\mu_{k,i}
and hence the equation. Rather, for a given set of exponents there are multi-parameter families of distinct MLDE (with distinct solutions) obtained by varying the individual
\mu_{k,i}
keeping their sums
\nu_{k}
fixed.

We now work out the indicial equation that relates the
\nu_{k}
to the
\gamma_{A}
. For this, we insert the leading power in Eq. (
2.27
) into Eq. (
2.25
) and retain the leading power of the result. This then has to be set to 0 to satisfy the MLDE in leading order. We see that
q\frac{d}{dq}
on
q^{\gamma}
gives back
\gamma\,q^{\gamma}
, so we can replace the former operator by the number
\gamma
. However the derivatives
{\cal D}
in the MLDE are not simply
q\frac{d}{dq}
, but contain factors of Eisenstein
E_{2}
. Since each action of
{\cal D}
on a modular function of fixed weight increases its weight by 2, we have:

{\cal D}^{r}=\prod_{j=r-1}^{0}\left(q\frac{d}{dq}-\frac{j}{6}E_{2}(q)\right)

(2.30)

where the terms are ordered with
j
decreasing towards the right. Applying this operator to
q^{\gamma}
, we find terms where every derivative acts on
q^{\gamma}
and gives back
\gamma\,q^{\gamma}
, but also terms where some of the derivatives act on an
E_{2}
. However the latter have sub-leading (higher) powers of
q
, so they can be ignored as long as we are only keeping the leading power. Thus we may write:

{\cal D}^{r}\left(q^{\gamma}\right)\sim\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(\gamma-\frac{j}{6}\right)q^{\gamma}

(2.31)

To leading order we can also replace each
\phi_{2k,i}
by its leading term, which has been normalised to 1. Thus we get the indicial equation:

\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\gamma-\frac{j}{6}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}\prod_{r=0}^{n-k-1}\left(\gamma-\frac{r}{6}\right)=0

(2.32)

where we used the definition
\nu_{k}=\sum_{i}\mu_{k,i}
. Expanding and retaining the highest powers, we obtain the form:

\gamma^{n}-\frac{n(n-1)}{12}\gamma^{n-1}+\nu_{1}\gamma^{n-1}+\cdots=0

(2.33)

where
\cdots
denotes terms with lower powers of
\gamma
. Now since the leading power of Eq. (
2.33
) has coefficient unity, the LHS of Eq. (
2.32
) can equivalently be written:

P_{n}(\gamma_{A}):=\prod_{A=0}^{n-1}(\gamma-\gamma_{A})=0

(2.34)

where
\gamma_{A}
are the roots. Equating the power
\gamma^{n-1}
between the two forms, we find:

\sum_{A=0}^{n-1}\gamma_{A}=\frac{n(n-1)}{12}-\nu_{1}

(2.35)

which we recognise as Eq. (
2.29
) with
\nu_{1}=\frac{\ell}{6}
. Thus
\nu_{1}
is determined by the Wronskian index. Thereafter, Eq. (
2.32
) can be used to determine the exponents
\gamma_{A}
in terms of the independent parameters
\nu_{2},\nu_{3},\cdots\nu_{n}
, and vice-versa. In fact, determining
\nu_{k}
from
\gamma_{A}
is the simpler direction, as we just have to solve linear simultaneous equations.

As an example, for
n=3
and
\ell=0
we get:

\gamma^{3}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}+\left(\nu_{2}+\frac{1}{18}\right)\gamma+\nu_{3}=0

(2.36)

which tells us that
[
16
]
:

\begin{split}\nu_{2}&=e_{2}(\gamma_{A})-\frac{1}{18}\\
\nu_{3}&=-e_{3}(\gamma_{A})\end{split}

(2.37)

with
e_{k}
being the elementary symmetric polynomials of degree
k
.

2.5
Contour integral representation for RCFT characters

Many of the analyses of MLDE and their solutions have focused on the simplest case,
\ell=0
. However even in this case, it becomes increasingly difficult to explicitly solve the equations beyond working order-by-order in a
q
-series by the Frobenius method. In particular, a closed-form solution is required in order to compute the modular transformations of the solutions and this is not generally available when the number of independent characters
n
(closely related to the number of primaries of the full chiral algebra) is large. This contrasts with the case of two characters where the MLDEs are solved
[
16
,
31
]
by hypergeometric functions whose modular transformations are known.

To redress this problem, a multi-variable contour integral form was put forward in
[
17
]
that is capable of solving MLDEs after they are re-expressed in terms of the modular
\lambda
-function (defined below), even for arbitrarily large numbers of characters. In the above reference it was shown that the contour integrals describe the characters of several infinite families of RCFTs, including the
c<1
minimal models as well as the
SU(2)_{k}
and
SU(N)_{1}
WZW models. This was somewhat surprising as the contour integrals, which we review below, depend on just two independent real constants – so it is not guaranteed that every RCFT can be represented by such integrals. Aspects of the contour-integral proposal for characters were worked out in more detail in
[
18
]
and an algorithm found to explicitly compute the modular
\mathcal{S}
-matrix for these contour integrals.

The contour integral approach of
[
17
]
was adapted from the Dotsenko-Fateev integrals used in
[
32
]
to solve for the 4-point correlators of minimal model CFT. These in turn are adapted from Aomoto-Selberg integrals. It was noted in
[
17
]
that if the cross-ratio
z
for correlators is replaced by the modular
\lambda
-function:

\lambda=\frac{\vartheta_{2}^{4}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{3}^{4}(\tau)}

(2.38)

then the BPZ differential equation for correlators turns into the MLDE for characters, expressed in terms of
\lambda

7
7

7

This use of the
\lambda
function was also studied more recently in
[
33
]
.
. This in turn implies that the solutions of the BPZ equation in terms of Dotsenko-Fateev contour integrals can be mapped on to solutions of MLDEs, providing a formalism in which MLDEs can be solved explicitly without resorting to a
q
-expansion. The calculation of monodromies for conformal blocks then nicely turns into a calculation of the modular
S
-matrix for characters.

The contour integrals in question are multiple integrals over variables
t_{i},i=1,2,\cdots,n-1

8
8

8

There is a generalisation to two families of variables
[
32
,
17
]
that we will not need in the present work.
. They are given by:

J_{A}(a,b|\lambda)={\cal N}_{A}\big(\lambda(1-\lambda)\big)^{p}\int_{{\cal C}_{A}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Big[dt_{i}\,t_{i}\,(t_{i}-1)(t_{i}-\lambda)\Big]^{a}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq n-1}(t_{i}-t_{j})^{b}

(2.39)

with
0\leq A\leq n-1
. Here
a,b
are the only free parameters, while
p
is determined in terms of them as:

p=-\frac{n-1}{3}\Big(1+3a+\frac{b}{2}(n-2)\Big)

(2.40)

The constants
{\cal N}_{A}
are a set of normalisations that are worked out in detail in
[
18
]
. The contour integrals are to be performed in the order
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots t_{n-1}
and the subscripts
{\cal C}_{A}
means the contours for
t_{1},\cdots t_{A}
run from 0 to
\lambda
, while those for
t_{A+1},\cdots t_{n-1}
run from 1 to
\infty
. In particular, this means that for
A=n-1
(the maximum allowed value), all contours run from 0 to
\lambda
.

Now
\lambda
is a Hauptmodul of the subgroup
\Gamma(2)\subset SL(2,Z)
, and it transforms under
SL(2,Z)
as:

S{}:\quad\lambda\to 1-\lambda,\qquad T{}:\quad\lambda\to\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}

(2.41)

Using these one can show that the contour integrals Eq. (
2.39
) transform into a linear combination of themselves under
SL(2,Z)
. This is a key feature that allows them to potentially describe the characters of an RCFT.

The simplest version of the above family of integrals is the case
n=2
. In this case there are no terms of the type
\prod(t_{i}-t_{j})
in the integral, so
b
drops out and we get the one-parameter family:

\begin{split}J_{0}&={\cal N}_{0}\big(\lambda(1-\lambda)\big)^{-a-\frac{1}{3}}\int_{1}^{\infty}dt\,\Big[t(t-1)(t-\lambda)\Big]^{a}\\
J_{1}&={\cal N}_{1}\big(\lambda(1-\lambda)\big)^{-a-\frac{1}{3}}\int_{0}^{\lambda}dt\,\Big[t(t-1)(t-\lambda)\Big]^{a}\end{split}

(2.42)

For small
\lambda
, we have
\lambda\simeq\sqrt{q}
. Hence the leading behaviour of the two integrals above is:

\begin{split}J_{0}&~\sim~\lambda^{-a-\frac{1}{3}}~\sim~q^{-\frac{a}{2}-\frac{1}{6}}\\
J_{1}&~\sim~\lambda^{a+\frac{2}{3}}~\sim~q^{\frac{a}{2}+\frac{1}{3}}\end{split}

(2.43)

These can be identified with a pair of characters of a two-character RCFT of central charge
c
whose non-trivial primary has holomorphic conformal dimension
h
, as follows. We equate the leading behaviours using:

\begin{split}-\frac{a}{2}-\frac{1}{6}&=-\frac{c}{24}\\
\frac{a}{2}+\frac{1}{3}&=-\frac{c}{24}+h\end{split}

(2.44)

This implies:

-\frac{c}{12}+h=\frac{1}{6}

(2.45)

Comparing with the general Riemann-Roch formula for
n
characters Eq. (
2.29
), we find
\ell=0
. Now, any pair of functions transforming into themselves under
SL(2,Z)
and having
\ell=0
satisfies the MMS equation
[
20
]

\Big({\cal D}^{2}+\mu\,E_{4}(\tau)\Big)\chi=0

(2.46)

where
\mu=-\frac{c(c+4)}{576}
. Thus the pair Eq. (
2.42
) satisfies Eq. (
2.46

, with the following identifications arising from Eq. (
2.44

\begin{split}c&=12a+4\\

\mu&=-\frac{(a+\frac{1}{3})(a+\frac{2}{3})}{4}\end{split}

For specific values of

the contour integrals are the characters of the entire MMS series CFTs
[
20
,
16
]
as well as infinitely many additional vector-valued modular forms that have integral but not necessarily positive

Returning now to the contour integral Eq. (

\ell=0
. This is done by noting that the leading behaviour of each integral at small

J_{A}(\lambda)~~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\lambda\to 0}}}~~\lambda^{p+\Delta_{A}}~\sim~q^{\frac{1}{2}(p+\Delta_{A})}

where:

\Delta_{A}=A(1+2a)+\frac{1}{2}b\,A(A-1),\quad A=1,2,\cdots,n-1

(2.49)

Identifying the leading exponents
\frac{1}{2}(p+\Delta_{A})
with
-\frac{c}{24}+h_{A}
, we find that the central charge and conformal dimensions are:

\begin{split}c&=-12p=4(n-1)\left(1+3a+\frac{b}{2}(n-2)\right)\\
h_{A}&=\frac{\Delta_{A}}{2}=A\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{b}{4}A(A-1)\end{split}

(2.50)

Applying Eq. (
2.29
) then gives
\ell=0

[
17
]
.

This tempts us to ask under what conditions the contour integral construction above describes the characters of RCFTs. A key advantage of doing so is that the modular
S
-matrix can be worked out explicitly, as explained in detail in
[
18
]
generalising the analogous construction for the monodromy of conformal blocks in
[
32
]
. To see when this can be done, let us assume a given
\ell=0
RCFT with data

and ask whether its characters can be written as contour integrals as above. For this one needs to identify the leading exponents
-\frac{c}{24}+h_{A}
of the RCFT with the leading exponents
\frac{1}{2}(p+\Delta_{A})
of the contour integrals. But the latter set depends on two parameters
a,b
for
n\geq 3
and one parameter for
n=2
, while the former set has

independent parameters after imposing
\ell=0
. Thus, identification is assured only for

where the number of parameters is equal, while there will be RCFTs with

characters that cannot be identified with the above contour integrals. However,
[
17
]
noted the surprising fact that several well-known infinite families of RCFTs admit an identification of their exponents with the leading

-behaviour of the contour integrals. This was shown to hold for the characters of the
A
,
D
and
E
series

models as well as for the

BPZ minimal models. Additionally, by generalising Eq. (

[

), matching of exponents was achieved for the A, D and E series characters of all

BPZ minimal models.

However matching of exponents is merely a necessary, but not sufficient, condition to identify the characters themselves with the contour integrals. But for
\ell=0
, the exponents completely determine the MLDE for

, therefore in this case the necessary condition is also sufficient. On the other hand for

, even after successfully matching exponents, we have no guarantee that the contour integral matches a given RCFT. In Ref.
[
17
]
it was conjectured that in a large number of cases where the RCFT exponents match those of contour integrals, the latter indeed represent the characters of the RCFT. Non-trivial evidence was provided for this conjecture. However it has not been proved in generality as far as we know, nor is the complete list of RCFT known for which this is true.

We now consider the general case of the generalised Schur partition function for the

SCFT with
2N+2
fundamental hypermultiplets. In particular, we will argue that

{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
can be identified with the contour integral

in Eq. (

N+1
MLDE with vanishing Wronskian index. The proof is a simple generalisation of the

Proof
:

From Eq. (
4.5
), the relevant integrand can be expressed as

I_{USp(2N)}(q,\mathbf{z})=\eta^{2N(N+2)}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(2u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{8}(u_{i})}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{i}\pm u_{j})}{\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{i})\vartheta_{4}^{4}(2u_{j})}~.

(4.26)

As before, we can split this into the following two pieces:

\begin{split}I_{USp(2N)}^{(1)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\eta^{6N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(2u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{8}(u_{i})}~,\\
I_{USp(2N)}^{(2)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\eta^{2N(N-1)}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{i}\pm u_{j})}{\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{i})\vartheta_{4}^{4}(2u_{j})}~.\end{split}

(4.27)

We notice that these are exactly in the same form as the two pieces in the
USp(4)
case.

Therefore, by analogy with Eq. (
4.11
), we define the variables

t_{i}=\lambda\,{\rm sn}^{2}(v_{i},k)\;,\;\text{ where }v_{i}=\pi\,\vartheta_{3}^{2}\,u_{i}\;,\;\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N~,

(4.28)

By using the standard identities discussed before and presented in Appendix
A
, the first piece of the integrand can be represented as

I_{USp(2N)}^{(1)}(q,\mathbf{z})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{t_{i}(1-t_{i})(\lambda-t_{i})}{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\right]~.

(4.29)

Next, we can analyse the second piece by using the theta addition rule Eq. (
A.17
) to obtain

I_{USp(2N)}^{(2)}(q,\mathbf{z})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{\eta^{4}\,\vartheta_{3}^{4}}{\vartheta_{2}^{4}\,\vartheta_{4}^{4}}\left(\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{2}(u_{i})}-\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{j})}{\vartheta_{4}^{2}(u_{j})}\right)^{2}~.

(4.30)

As before, using Eq. (
A.16
) and the definition of
\lambda
, this can be simplified to

I_{USp(2N)}^{(2)}(q,\mathbf{z})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}2^{-\frac{4}{3}}\big[\lambda(1-\lambda)\big]^{-\frac{2}{3}}\big(t_{i}-t_{j}\big)^{2}~.

(4.31)

Under the variable transformation Eq. (
4.28
) the integrals picks up the usual Jacobian factor

\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{{\rm d}z_{j}}{2\pi iz_{j}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}\,\vartheta_{3}^{2N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left[\frac{{\rm d}t_{j}}{\sqrt{t_{j}(1-t_{j})(\lambda-t_{j}))}}\right]~.

(4.32)

Assembling these pieces together, the generalised Schur partition function for the
USp(2N)
SCFT can be expressed as

\begin{split}{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)\propto\frac{\eta^{2N}}{\vartheta_{3}^{2N}}\,\big[\lambda(1-\lambda)\big]^{-\frac{N(N+2)}{3}\,\alpha}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}t_{j}&\prod_{i=1}^{N}\bigg[t_{i}(1-t_{i})(\lambda-t_{i})\bigg]^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\times\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\big(t_{i}-t_{j}\big)^{2\alpha}~.\end{split}

(4.33)

Using the same argument for each variable
t_{i}
as discussed in the proof for
{\cal Z}_{SU(2)}(q;\alpha)
, we get the contour

\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\dots\int_{0}^{\lambda}}_{N\;{\rm times}}~.

(4.34)

Therefore, the generalised Schur partition function of the
USp(2N)
SCFT with
2N+2
fundamental hypermultiplets can finally be expressed as

\begin{split}{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)\propto\big[\lambda(1-\lambda)\big]^{\frac{N}{6}\big(1-2\alpha(N+2)\big)}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}t_{j}&\prod_{i=1}^{N}\bigg[t_{i}(1-t_{i})(\lambda-t_{i})\bigg]^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\times\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\big(t_{i}-t_{j}\big)^{2\alpha}~.\end{split}

(4.35)

This corresponds to
J_{N}
in Eq. (
2.39
) with parameters

(4.36)

which agrees with the exponent of
\lambda

4.3
Two-parameter generalised Schur partition function

In this section, we propose a natural two-parameter extension of the generalised Schur partition function introduced in
[
15
]
. While we are not able to obtain this extension from a particular limit of the full superconformal index, we nevertheless demonstrate that this
two-parameter generalised Schur partition function
satisfies an order
N+1
MLDE with vanishing Wronskian index.

The inspiration for this generalisation follows from the proof of Proposition
2
. Recall that we proved that
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
yields a one-parameter sub-family of the original contour integrals reviewed in Eq. (
2.39
). It is then natural to ask if there is an extension of
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
that is equivalent to the full two-parameter family of the contour integrals Eq. (
2.39
).

To this end, we propose the following as the two-parameter generalised Schur partition function associated to the
USp(2N)
SCFT with
2N+2
fundamental hypermultiplets:

\begin{split}&{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)=\\
&\quad\times\;\frac{\eta^{2N}}{|W|\,N(\alpha)}\oint\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{{\rm d}z_{j}}{2\pi iz_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\eta^{6}\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(2u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{8}(u_{i})}\right)^{\alpha}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\left(\eta^{4}\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{i}\pm u_{j})}{\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{i})\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{j})}\right)^{\beta}~.\end{split}

(4.46)

It is immediately clear that the
\alpha=\beta
case of
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
is equivalent to the generalised Schur partition function,
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
, introduced by
[
15
]
. Therefore for
\alpha=\beta=1
, this furnishes the Schur index of the
USp(2N)
SCFT, and thus
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
can be viewed as a two-parameter generalisation of the Schur index for this gauge theory.

As is natural from our motivation, we make the following proposition:

Proposition 3

The two-parameter generalised Schur partition function
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
, with analytically continued
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, is a solution to the following order-
(N+1)
modular linear differential equation with vanishing Wronskian index:

\left(\mathcal{D}^{N+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\sum_{i}\mu_{k,i}\,\phi_{2k,i}(\tau)\,\mathcal{D}^{N+1-k}\right)\,{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)=0~,

(4.47)

where
\sum_{i}\mu_{k,i}
is determined by Eq. (
2.32
) as before, but with:

\begin{split}\gamma_{0}&=-\frac{N}{12}\Big(6\alpha+2(N-1)\beta-1\Big)~,\\[5.69054pt]
\gamma_{A}&=\gamma_{0}+\alpha A+\frac{\beta}{2}A(A-1)~.\end{split}

(4.48)

Again, it must be emphasised that with additional work it is straightforward to determine all the
\mu_{k,i}
, and hence the MLDE, completely by expanding
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}
in a
q
-series.

Proof
:

This proof is almost identical to the one considered in Section
4.2
and hence we do not provide many details here. The integrand of
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
naturally has two components that can be expressed as

\begin{split}I_{USp(2N)}^{(1)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\eta^{6}\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(2u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{8}(u_{i})}\\
I_{USp(2N)}^{(2)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\eta^{4}\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(u_{i}\pm u_{j})}{\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{i})\vartheta_{4}^{4}(u_{j})}\end{split}

(4.49)

Thus by identical arguments as in the proof of Proposition
2
, after the same variable redefinition

t_{i}=\lambda\,{\rm sn}^{2}(v_{i},k)\;,\;\text{ where }v_{i}=\pi\,\vartheta_{3}^{2}\,u_{i}\;,\;\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N~,

(4.50)

these two pieces in the integrand become

\begin{split}I_{USp(2N)}^{(1)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{t_{i}(1-t_{i})(\lambda-t_{i})}{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\right]\\
I_{USp(2N)}^{(2)}(q,\mathbf{z})&=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}2^{-\frac{4}{3}}\big[\lambda(1-\lambda)\big]^{-\frac{2}{3}}\big(t_{i}-t_{j}\big)^{2}~.\end{split}

(4.51)

The Jacobian from this variable change is the same as in Eq. (
4.32
). Finally, the corresponding contour is identical as well due to identical variable transformations. Therefore, the final expression for the two-parameter generalised Schur partition function
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
can be expressed as

\begin{split}{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)\propto\big[\lambda(1-\lambda)\big]^{\frac{N}{6}\big(1-6\alpha+2\beta-2N\beta\big)}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}t_{j}&\prod_{i=1}^{N}\bigg[t_{i}(1-t_{i})(\lambda-t_{i})\bigg]^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\times\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\big(t_{i}-t_{j}\big)^{2\beta}~.\end{split}

(4.52)

This corresponds to
J_{N}
in Eq. (
2.39
) with parameters

a=\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\;,\;b=2\beta\;\implies\;p=\frac{N}{6}\big(1-6\alpha+2\beta-2N\beta\big)~,

(4.53)

which agrees with the exponent of
\lambda
in Eq. (
4.52
). Thus, as expected, the two-parameter generalised Schur partition function
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
can be identified with the contour integral
J_{A}
and thus solves the order
N+1
MLDE with vanishing Wronskian index. The identification of MLDE parameters

can then be determined by expanding the solution in a
q

3
.
\square

Discussion and open questions

In this section, we summarise the concrete calculations of this work, formulate a conjecture, and outline possible generalisations. While it also serves as a conclusion, it is more than just a summary.

[

5.2
Comments on the two-parameter generalisation

In Eq. (
4.46
), we proposed a two-parameter extension of the generalised Schur partiton function for the
USp(2N)
SCFT with
2N+2
fundamental hypermultiplets. This extension was based on the two-parameter contour integral Eq. (
2.39
) and could be seen as a natural extension of the one-parameter
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
written as an integral over
USp(2N)
. For this to have a consistent interpretation as a partition function of the associated 4d
{\cal N}=2
SCFT, we should be able to express this as a limit of the superconformal index. This was indeed the case for the one-parameter generalised Schur partition function which was obtained as a double scaling limit of the full superconformal index.

However, such a limit from the full superconformal index
{\cal I}(p,q,t)
to
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
is not clear to us. In fact, a closer examination of Eq. (
4.46
) reveals that realising
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
from a specialisation of the variables
p,q,t
alone is unlikely. This can be best understood by noticing that different roots of
USp(2N)
are raised differently by the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
. In particular, the contribution of the roots
\pm 2e_{i}
from the vector multiplets is raised to
\alpha
, while the contribution from the roots
\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}
is raised to
\beta
. At the level of the single letter indices, this would scale these roots differently in the adjoint character, making such a specialisation purely in terms of
p,q,t
unlikely.

Recall that the
\alpha=0
case of
{\cal Z}_{USp}(2N)(q;\alpha)
corresponded to the Schur index after giving all hypermultiplets a mass and turning on all Coulomb branch deformations. Analogously, setting
\beta=0
in the two-parameter extension
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,\beta)
we obtain

{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha,0)\;\propto\;\prod_{i=1}^{N}\,\eta^{2}\oint\frac{{\rm d}z_{i}}{2\pi iz_{i}}\left(\eta^{6}\,\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(2u_{i})}{\vartheta_{4}^{8}(u_{i})}\right)^{\alpha}~.

(5.12)

At the level of
USp(2N)
representation theory, this can be seen as the restriction to the
SU(2)^{\otimes N}\subset USp(2N)
root system. Furthermore, the structure of Eq. (
4.46
) ensures that in the
\beta=0
limit, we obtain the one-parameter generalised Schur partition function of a product of
N
copies of
SU(2)
SCFT with
4
fundamental hypermultiplets.

We are not aware of any such deformation of the
USp(2N)
SCFT and thus do not understand the physical origin, if any, of this statement. Therefore, we leave the proper four-dimensional interpretation of the two-parameter generalisation for future consideration.

5.3
Generalisations in 4d and 2d

In this concluding section, we briefly formulate our expectations for the generalisations of the results we have presented in this work.

A natural question is whether this analysis can be extended to the generalised Schur partition functions of other 4d
{\cal N}=2
gauge theories. The empirical evidence indicates that the relation between
{\cal Z}_{G}(q;\alpha)
and the modular linear differential equation is structural and holds for arbitrary
G
. Therefore, it is reasonable to posit that, for a fixed
G
, the generalised Schur partition function
{\cal Z}_{G}(q,\alpha)
satisfies an MLDE of definite order and Wronskian index, thereby extending Proposition
2
.

The main obstacle to extending our proof to the general case is technical. The key analytical bridge between
{\cal Z}_{USp(2N)}(q;\alpha)
and the MLDE was the contour integral representation of VVMFs that solve an MLDE with vanishing Wronskian index. However, an analogous series of elliptic transformations on the generalised Schur partition functions of other SCFTs does not yield contour integrals of the form Eq. (
2.39
). This is unsurprising, as Eq. (
2.39
) themselves do not furnish the most general solution of the corresponding
\ell=0
MLDE. Moreover, it is known that 4d Schur indices do not always satisfy MLDEs with vanishing Wronskian index. Furthermore, in many cases, the Schur index itself has a half-integer
q
-series expansion, and thus the corresponding MLDE is modular only under the congruence subgroup
\Gamma^{0}(2)
.

Thus, a uniform proof expressing
{\cal Z}_{G}(q;\alpha)
as a contour integral representation of an MLDE solution will likely require more general contour integral representations of MLDE solutions that account for these additional complications. We hope to address this problem in the future.

Here we introduce our notations for the Jacobi elliptic functions

\displaystyle k^{2}=\lambda=\frac{\vartheta_{2}^{4}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{3}^{4}(\tau)},\qquad k^{\prime 2}=1-k^{2}=\frac{\vartheta_{4}^{4}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{3}^{4}(\tau)}

\displaystyle\tau=i\frac{K^{\prime}(k)}{K(k)}

\displaystyle K(k)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}}},\qquad K^{\prime}(k)=K(k^{\prime})

\displaystyle v=2Ku=\pi\vartheta_{3}^{2}(\tau)u

(A.27)

\displaystyle\text{sn}(v,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta_{3}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{2}(\tau)}\frac{\vartheta_{1}(u,\tau)}{\vartheta_{4}(u,\tau)}

(A.28)

\displaystyle\text{cn}(v,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta_{4}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{2}(\tau)}\frac{\vartheta_{2}(u,\tau)}{\vartheta_{4}(u,\tau)}

(A.29)

\displaystyle\text{dn}(v,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta_{4}(\tau)}{\vartheta_{3}(\tau)}\frac{\vartheta_{3}(u,\tau)}{\vartheta_{4}(u,\tau)}

(A.30)

An equivalent way to define the Jacobi elliptic functions is directly via the (incomplete) elliptic integral of the first kind

\displaystyle F(\varphi,k)=\int_{0}^{\varphi}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}}}

\displaystyle\text{sn}(v,k)

\displaystyle=\sin{\text{am}(v,k)}=\sin\varphi

(A.32)

\displaystyle\text{sn}(v+4K,k)=\text{sn}(v+2iK^{\prime},k)=\text{sn}(v,k)

Finally, the derivatives of Jacobi elliptic functions can be obtained using identites relating the derivatives of theta functions. In particular, for the Jacobi sine we have

\displaystyle({\cal D}^{2}+\mu E_{4})\chi=0

(B.1)

Let us first perform the change of variables from
\tau
to
K(\tau):=12^{3}/j(\tau)
. Denoting the differential operator
\theta_{K}=K\partial_{k}
, in the new variables we have the following hypergeometric ODE:

\displaystyle\left(\theta_{K}^{2}-\frac{2K+1}{6(1-K)}\theta_{K}+\frac{\mu}{1-K}\right)\chi=0

(B.2)

Noting that the free parameter
\mu
is expressed in terms of
\alpha
as

\displaystyle\mu=-\frac{(6\alpha+1)(6\alpha-1)}{144}

(B.3)

the two independent solutions for generic values of
\alpha
are given as

\displaystyle\chi_{0}=K^{\frac{1}{12}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{12}+\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{5}{12}+\frac{\alpha}{2},1+\alpha,K\right)

(B.4)

\displaystyle\chi_{1}=K^{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\alpha}{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{5}{12}-\frac{\alpha}{2},1-\alpha,K\right)

(B.5)

Whenever we have
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
, the two independent exponents differ by an integer and we have a resonance. In this case, we have logarithmic solutions. The two independent solutions can also be directly expressed in terms of the Meijer
G
-function as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{0}=G^{{1,1}}_{{2,2}}\left(K\,\,\Bigg|\,\,\begin{matrix}1,\,\frac{2}{3}\\
\frac{1}{12}+\frac{\alpha}{2},\,\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\end{matrix}\,\right)

(B.6)

\displaystyle\chi_{1}=G^{{2,0}}_{{2,2}}\left(K\,\,\Bigg|\,\,\begin{matrix}1,\,\frac{2}{3}\\
\frac{1}{12}+\frac{\alpha}{2},\,\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\end{matrix}\,\right)

(B.7)

These solutions are valid for all values of
\alpha
, including integers, in which case we find that the second solution above exhibits logarithmic behaviour.

In the main text, we have extensively used the modular
\lambda
function. Keeping this in mind, let us also write down the general solutions in this variable. The MMS equation is now

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\lambda}^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{2\lambda-1}{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\partial_{\lambda}+\frac{4\mu(\lambda^{2}-\lambda+1)}{\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}}\right)\chi=0

(B.8)

whose two independent solutions are given, again relating
\mu
to
\alpha
using Eq. (
B.3
) as

\displaystyle\chi_{0}=(\lambda(1-\lambda))^{\frac{1}{6}+\alpha}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}+3\alpha,1+2\alpha,\lambda\right)

(B.9)

\displaystyle\chi_{1}=(\lambda(1-\lambda))^{\frac{1}{6}-\alpha}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\alpha,\frac{1}{2}-3\alpha,1-2\alpha,\lambda\right)

(B.10)

The general solutions expressed in terms of Meijer
G

\displaystyle\chi=\lambda^{\frac{1}{6}-\alpha}(1-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{6}+\alpha}\left(c_{1}G^{{1,1}}_{{2,2}}\left(\lambda\,\,\Bigg|\,\,\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}-\alpha,\,\frac{1}{2}+\alpha\\
0,\,\ 2\alpha\end{matrix}\,\right)+c_{2}G^{{2,0}}_{{2,2}}\left(\lambda\,\,\Bigg|\,\,\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}-\alpha,\,\frac{1}{2}+\alpha\\
0,\,\ 2\alpha\end{matrix}\,\right)\right)
