Risk-sensitive Affine Control Synthesis for Stationary LTI Systems
Yang Hu, Shahriar Talebi, Na Li

Abstract
To address deviations from expected performance in stochastic systems, we propose a risk-sensitive control synthesis method to minimize certain risk measures over the limiting stationary distribution. Specifically, we extend Worst-case Conditional Value-at-Risk (W-CVaR) optimization for Linear Time-invariant (LTI) systems to handle nonzero-mean noise and affine controllers, using only the first and second moments of noise, which enhances robustness against model uncertainty. Highlighting the strong coupling between the linear and bias terms of the controller, we reformulate the synthesis problem as a Bilinear Matrix Inequality (BMI), and propose an alternating optimization algorithm with guaranteed convergence. Finally, we demonstrate the numerical performance of our approach in two representative settings, which shows that the proposed algorithm successfully synthesizes risk-sensitive controllers that outperform the naive LQR baseline.

Introduction

In stochastic systems, optimizing solely for expected performance metrics can yield unsatisfactory outcomes. Instead, it is often necessary to consider higher-order characteristics of the distribution to address deviations from average performance that arises from the inherent stochasticity. Risk-sensitive modeling, originally developed in finance, offers systematic techniques to account for these deviations under uncertainty, with broad applicability across a wide range of engineering fields, including risk-sensitive control

, risk-constrained control

To quantify the risks observed in stochastic systems, various measures are introduced to capture distributional properties of random variables
[

,
10
]
, among which the most widely-used are value-at-risk (VaR)
[

]
, conditional value-at-risk (CVaR)
[

and entropic risk measure
[
13

Worst-Case CVaR (W-CVaR)

[
14
]

As an upper bound of the actual risk measured in CVaR, it is not only capable of inducing risk-sensitive performance in face of limited information, but is also robust against model uncertainty by only requiring the first two moments of the noise—which is standard for stochastic systems
[
15
,
16
]

In stochastic Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) systems, we often optimize cumulative step-wise performance measures which, if designed carefully, also implies favorable properties regarding long-term closed-loop behavior (e.g., asymptotic stability, bounded second moments, etc.). The (stationary) limiting distribution of the process, however, provides a more comprehensive view by capturing this long-term behavior under sustained stochastic disturbances. This viewpoint is not only of theoretical interest
[

,
18
,
19
]

[

,
21
]

[

23

Most recently, the worst-case CVaR measure on the stationary distribution of LTI systems (so-called “stationary LTI systems”) was considered in
[
16
]
, but only under the assumptions of
zero-mean
noise and
linear

Section

A-C

affine

Then naturally, one might wonder whether, similar to the LQR controller synthesis, the optimization over the linear term can be decoupled from the bias term in the risk-sensitive affine control synthesis problem. Unfortunately, this is not the case—there exists a strong coupling between the linear and bias terms of the controller, due to the interaction between the mean and covariance of the stationary distribution in the risk-sensitive synthesis. As shown in the example provided in
Section

II-C

naive affine LQR
controller that, by ignoring the coupling, assigns the bias and linear term separately. This phenomenon also illustrates the increasing coupling between the linear and bias terms as the mean and covariance of the noise grow larger.

\displaystyle\iff\;

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varOmega}_{1}-\bar{\varSigma}^{-1}&\tilde{q}\\
*&\tilde{\omega}_{0}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mu}\\

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varOmega}_{1}-\bar{\varSigma}^{-1}&\tilde{q}&\tilde{\mu}\\

*&*&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0

\displaystyle\iff\;

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varOmega}_{1}&\tilde{q}&\tilde{\mu}&I\\
*&\tilde{\omega}_{0}&1&0\\
*&*&1&0\\
*&*&*&\bar{\varSigma}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0.

(12)

Plugging (

Remark 1
.

Even though all the constraints are relaxed to LMIs, the program itself is still a BMI due to the coupling in the objective function. Furthermore, all the relaxations introduced are actually exact, except for the one in (

III-C

An Alternating Optimization Algorithm

So far, we have shown how to relax (
4
) to (
7
), where the latter has the favorable property that the LMI constraints are linear in the decision variables. However, the relaxed problem (
7
) is still hard to solve since it is still heavily coupled in the following senses: i) the objective (
7a
) involves the product of two decision variables
\tilde{\varOmega}
and
\tilde{M}
, and ii) (
7e
) is coupled and cannot be further relaxed without compromising the invariance property of the stationary mean value. As a result, standard BMI optimization solvers still cannot solve program (
7
) directly.

To compute (
7
), we design the following optimization algorithm that alternates between two subsets of variables,
\bm{\xi}:=(\tilde{\varOmega},\tilde{\mu},\ell)
, and
\bm{\theta}:=(\alpha,M,\bar{\varSigma},Y,H)
, and repetitively executes the following two steps:

•

Step I:

we first optimize over
\bm{\xi}=(\tilde{\varOmega},\tilde{\mu},\ell)
using
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\alpha^{\star},M^{\star},\bar{\varSigma}^{\star},Y^{\star},H^{\star})
obtained in the last iteration:

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{\varOmega},\tilde{\mu},\ell}\quad

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\tilde{\varOmega},\tilde{M}^{\star}\rangle,

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\tilde{\varOmega}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+1}_{\succcurlyeq 0},

(13b)

\displaystyle\bar{\varSigma}^{\star}\tilde{\mu}=H^{\star}\tilde{\mu}+B^{\star}\ell+\mu_{w},

(13c)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{M}^{\star}_{1}&\tilde{p}^{\star}&(Y^{\star})^{\top}&\bar{\varSigma}^{\star}\\
*&\tilde{m}^{\star}_{0}+\alpha^{\star}&\ell^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
*&*&R^{-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
*&*&*&Q^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0,

(13d)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varOmega}_{1}&\tilde{q}&\tilde{\mu}&I\\
*&\tilde{\omega}_{0}&1&0\\
*&*&1&0\\
*&*&*&\bar{\varSigma}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0.

(13e)

•

Step II:
we then use
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\star}=(\tilde{\varOmega}^{\star},\tilde{\mu}^{\star},\ell^{\star})
obtained in (
13
) to optimize over
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,M,\bar{\varSigma},Y,H)
as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha,\tilde{M},\bar{\varSigma},Y,H}\quad

\displaystyle\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\tilde{\varOmega}^{\star},\tilde{M}\rangle,

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\tilde{M}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+1}_{\succcurlyeq 0},~\bar{\varSigma}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succcurlyeq 0},

(14b)

\displaystyle H=A\bar{\varSigma}+BY,

(14c)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\varSigma}-\varSigma_{w}&H\\
*&\bar{\varSigma}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0,

(14d)

\displaystyle\bar{\varSigma}\tilde{\mu}^{\star}=H\tilde{\mu}^{\star}+B\ell^{\star}+\mu_{w},

(14e)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{M}_{1}&\tilde{p}&Y^{\top}&\bar{\varSigma}\\
*&\tilde{m}_{0}+\alpha&(\ell^{\star})^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
*&*&R^{-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
*&*&*&Q^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0,

(14f)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varOmega}^{\star}_{1}&\tilde{q}^{\star}&\tilde{\mu}^{\star}&I\\
*&\tilde{\omega}^{\star}_{0}&1&0\\
*&*&1&0\\
*&*&*&\bar{\varSigma}\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq 0.

(14g)

We point out that both (
13
) and (
14
) are now convex in the decision variables, and are thus solvable using generic convex optimization solvers (e.g., CVX
[
26
]
).

(Numerical considerations)

For numerical stability, practical implementation may also include additional bounds to avoid extra large search spaces (e.g.,

and
\tr(\tilde{\varOmega})\leq B_{\varOmega}

III-D

Convergence Guarantee

We conclude this section by proving a convergence guarantee for the proposed alternating optimization algorithm, which in turn is based on the boundedness of the objective. For this purpose, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For any matrices
M,\bar{\varOmega}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+1}_{\succcurlyeq 0}
satisfying

M\succcurlyeq\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\varSigma}+\bar{\mu}\bar{\mu}^{\top}&\bar{\mu}\\
*&1\end{bmatrix},~\bar{\varOmega}\succcurlyeq\begin{bmatrix}Q+K^{\top}RK&K^{\top}R\ell\\
*&\ell^{\top}R\ell-\alpha\end{bmatrix}

for some
Q\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ 0}
,
R\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ 0}
,
\bar{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\bar{\varSigma}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succcurlyeq 0}
,
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\bar{\varOmega},M\rangle\geq 0.

Proof.

When
\alpha\geq 0
, it is obvious that

\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\bar{\varOmega},M\rangle\geq\alpha\geq 0,

where we use the fact that
\bar{\varOmega},M\succcurlyeq 0
implies
\langle\bar{\varOmega},M\rangle\geq 0
.

Otherwise, when
\alpha<0
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\bar{\varOmega},M\rangle

(15a)

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\varSigma}+\bar{\mu}\bar{\mu}^{\top}&\bar{\mu}\\
*&1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}Q+K^{\top}RK&K^{\top}R\ell\\
*&\ell^{\top}R\ell-\alpha\end{bmatrix}}}\right\rangle

(15b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\langle\bar{\varSigma}+\bar{\mu}\bar{\mu}^{\top},Q\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bar{\varOmega},\begin{bmatrix}K^{\top}\\
\ell^{\top}\end{bmatrix}R\begin{bmatrix}K&\ell\end{bmatrix}}}\right\rangle-\alpha}}\right)

(15c)

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\beta}{1-\beta}\alpha\geq 0.

(15d)

Here in (
15b
) we use the fact that
\langle X_{1},P\rangle\geq\langle X_{2},P\rangle
if
P\succcurlyeq 0
and
X_{1}\succcurlyeq X_{2}
; in (
15c
) we apply the linearity of trace; in (
15d
) we use the positive-semidefiniteness of all the matrices involved and
\beta\in(0,1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4
.

The value sequence produced by the alternating optimization algorithm converges.

Proof.

Denote by
J(\bm{\xi},\bm{\theta}):=\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\langle\tilde{\varOmega},\tilde{M}\rangle
the value of (
7
), and by
\varXi_{i}
,
\varTheta_{i}
the constraint set in (
13
), (
14
), respectively. Let
J^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}
,
J^{\mathrm{II}}_{i}
denote the optimal values obtained in Step I and II of the
i
th
iteration, respecitvely. Then the algorithm ensures that

\displaystyle J^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\xi}_{i}\in\varXi_{i}}J(\bm{\xi}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{i-1})\leq J(\bm{\xi}^{\star}_{i-1},\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{i-1})=J^{\mathrm{II}}_{i-1},

\displaystyle J^{\mathrm{II}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{i}\in\varTheta_{i}}J(\bm{\xi}^{\star}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{i})\leq J(\bm{\xi}^{\star}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{i-1})=J^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}.

Therefore, the value sequence

is monotone decreasing. Meanwhile, by
Lemma

3
we also have

J^{\mathrm{II}}_{i},J^{\mathrm{I}}_{i+1}\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.

Finally, we can conclude the convergence of the value sequence by the Monotone Convergence Theorem.
∎

V
Conclusions

This paper extended risk-sensitive control synthesis in stationary LTI systems to affine controllers, accounting for nonzero-mean noise under the Worst-case CVaR (W-CVaR) risk measure. Unlike the zero-mean case where the optimal controller coincides with the LQR optimal one, we discovered that the linear and bias terms of the affine controller are strongly coupled, preventing decoupling of the optimization. By reformulating the problem as a Bilinear Matrix Inequality (BMI), we developed an alternating optimization algorithm that converges to a suboptimal solution. Simulations on Inverted Pendulum and HVAC systems demonstrated that our approach effectively produces risk-sensitive (suboptimal) controllers for systems under uncertainty, with increasing advantage over the naive affine LQR controller in terms of the W-CVaR measure as the dimension and complexity of the problem grow.

Although our algorithm relies solely on solving convex programs, they involve instances of semi-definite programming (SDPs), and thus may not scale efficiently to very large dimensions. Additionally, while our proposed solution is close to the W-CVaR-optimal one in certain cases, the suboptimality gap has not yet been analytically characterized. It is left as future work to address these limitations.

. Note that

\begin{aligned} \varPhi_{t}&=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(A_{K}x_{t-1}+B\ell+w_{t-1})(A_{K}x_{t-1}+B\ell+w_{t-1})^{\top}}}\right]\\
&=A_{K}\varPhi_{t-1}A_{K}^{\top}+(\nu\nu^{\top}+\varSigma_{w})+\nu\mu_{t-1}^{\top}A_{K}^{\top}+A_{K}\mu_{t-1}\nu^{\top}.\end{aligned}

Take the telescoping sum over
t
, from
0
to
T
, we have

\varGamma_{T+1}-\varPhi_{0}=A_{K}\varGamma_{T}A_{K}^{\top}+T(\nu\nu^{\top}+\varSigma_{w})+\nu S_{T}^{\top}A_{K}^{\top}+A_{K}S_{T}\nu^{\top}.

Divide both sides by
T
and let
T\to\infty
, we conclude that

\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}=A_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}A_{K}^{\top}+(\nu\nu^{\top}+\varSigma_{w})+\nu(A_{K}\bar{\mu})^{\top}+(A_{K}\bar{\mu})\nu^{\top},

where we define
\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\varGamma_{T}
and
\bar{S}_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}S_{T}
, and use
\bar{S}_{\infty}=\bar{\mu}
. Meanwhile, the LQR value is given by

\begin{aligned} V_{\textrm{lqr}}(K,\ell)&=\mathop{\lim\sup}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{t}^{\top}Q_{K}x_{t}+2\ell^{\top}RKx_{t}+\ell^{\top}R\ell}}\right)}}\right]\\
&=\mathop{\lim\sup}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tr(Q_{K}\varGamma_{T})+2\ell^{\top}RKS_{T}}}\right)+\ell^{\top}R\ell\\
&=\tr(Q_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})+2\ell^{\top}RK\bar{\mu}+\ell^{\top}R\ell.\end{aligned}

Finally, by linearity and cyclic property of trace, we have

\begin{aligned} \tr(Q_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})&=\tr\big((P_{K}-A_{K}^{\top}P_{K}A_{K})\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})\big)\\
&=\tr(P_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})-\tr(A_{K}^{\top}P_{K}A_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})\\
&=\tr(P_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty})-\tr(P_{K}A_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}A_{K}^{\top})\\
&=\tr\big(P_{K}(\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}-A_{K}\bar{\varGamma}_{\infty}A_{K}^{\top})\big)\\
&=\tr\big(P_{K}(\nu\nu^{\top}+\varSigma_{w}+\nu(A_{K}\bar{\mu})^{\top}+(A_{K}\bar{\mu})\nu^{\top})\big)\\
&=\tr\big(P_{K}(\varSigma_{w}+\nu\nu^{\top})\big)+2\nu^{\top}P_{K}A_{K}\bar{\mu}.\end{aligned}

Finally, we plug the above equation along with
\nu=B\ell+\mu_{w}
back into the expression of
V_{\textrm{lqr}}
. This completes the proof.
∎
