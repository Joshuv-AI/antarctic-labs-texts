Outflows from Star-Forming Galaxies in the Early Universe: Evolution from Double- to Single-Peaked Lyman- α \alpha Profiles Following Delayed Supernova Feedback Astropy ( Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2018 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022 ) , Matplotlib ( Hunter 2007 ) , MPDAF ( Piqueras et al. 2019 ) , NumPy ( Harris et al. 2020 ) , SciPy ( Virtanen et al. 2020 ) , zELDA ( Gurung-López et al. 2022 ) , BEAGLE ( Chevallard & Charlot 2016 )
James Nianias, Jeremy Lim, Yik Lok Wong, Gordon Wong

Abstract
Lyman- α \alpha emission, which owing to its resonant nature strongly couples the emergent line profile to gas kinematics, has become a key observable for probing outflows from star-forming galaxies in the early universe. Typically, Lyman- α \alpha profiles are explained in terms of idealised expanding shell models, while empirically-motivated interpretations of Lyman- α \alpha profiles that make use of comparisons with kinematics revealed by other spectral features (e.g. metal emission and absorption lines) are limited. Here, we analyse the kinematics of emission/absorption lines in the spectra of 338 gravitationally-lensed Lyman- α \alpha sources revealed via blind spectroscopy of galaxy clusters with the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE). Using metal emission lines to measure systemic redshifts, we confirm that the Lyman- α \alpha profiles are consistent with outflowing gas. In cases where interstellar metal absorption lines are detected, we find their kinematics and line ratios to be indicative of clumpy outflows. We investigate the differences between sources with single- and double-peaked Lyman- α \alpha profiles, finding the latter to drive weaker outflows yet show spectral signatures suggestive of younger stellar ages ( ≲ 4 ​ Myr \lesssim 4\,\mathrm{Myr} compared with ≳ 20 ​ Myr \gtrsim 20\,\mathrm{Myr} for sources with single-peaked Lyman- α \alpha profiles). We argue that double-peaked Lyman- α \alpha profiles may reflect weaker feedback in extremely young starbursts due to the delayed onset of supernova explosions. We also investigate whether outflow properties can be reliably inferred by fitting simple expanding shell models. We find that these models yield unphysical parameters in many cases even when they can reproduce the Lyman- α \alpha profiles to high precision, and urge caution when inferring outflow properties from such models.



I
Introduction

In the hierarchical picture of structure formation, properties of massive galaxies are influenced by feedback processes that take place in the low-mass galaxies from which they form. In particular, outflows in low-mass star-forming galaxies (SFGs) in the early universe influence the subsequent galaxy stellar mass function (e.g.
Kereš et al. 2009
,
Hopkins et al. 2014
,
Pakmor et al. 2023
), mass-metallicity relation (e.g.
Tremonti et al. 2004
,
Ma et al. 2016
), and baryon fraction (e.g.
Papastergis et al. 2012
). Observations of outflows from low-mass SFGs in the early universe are therefore pivotal to our understanding of galaxy evolution more broadly.

The most direct observational signature of outflows from SFGs in the early universe is blueshifted absorption by outflowing gas moving along the line of sight towards the observer (so-called “down-the-barrel” absorption). Rest-frame UV absorption lines (accessible by ground-based observatories at
z\gtrsim 2
) have been used to diagnose outflows in stacked spectra of Lyman Break Galaxies (LBGs)
(
Shapley et al. 2003
;
Steidel et al. 2010
;
Jones et al. 2012
)
, and Lyman-
\alpha
(Ly
\alpha
)-emitting galaxies
(
Berry et al. 2012
)
. However, detections of such absorption signatures in individual high-
z
galaxies are limited to a relatively small number of highly luminous systems (e.g.
Pettini et al. 2002
,
Shibuya et al. 2014
). To search for outflows in individual low-luminosity SFGs in the early universe, observers have instead turned to the Ly
\alpha
emission line. Due to the abundant Ly
\alpha
photons produced in star forming regions via re-processing of extreme UV radiation (
\lambda<912\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
) from young stars, even SFGs that are extremely faint in the continuum can be detected via the Ly
\alpha
line
(
Partridge & Peebles 1967
)
. Moreover, Ly
\alpha
’s large scattering cross-section with neutral hydrogen (H) means that the emergent line profile is strongly coupled to the kinematics of the interstellar medium (ISM) and circumgalactic medium (CGM), and hence encodes information about outflows (see reviews by
Erb 2015
and
Ouchi et al. 2020
). Most Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies display asymmetric Ly
\alpha
peaks that are redshifted relative to the systemic redshift of the host galaxy, sometimes accompanied by additional weaker blueshifted peaks (e.g.
Kulas et al. 2012
,
Rivera-Thorsen et al. 2015
). These distinctive line profiles can be reproduced by radiative transfer (RT) models consisting of expanding shells/haloes of gas (e.g.
Ahn 2004
,
Verhamme et al. 2006
,
Orsi et al. 2012
,
Duval et al. 2014
), thus providing theoretical support for outflows from these objects. Approaches have been developed to estimate outflow parameters such as expansion velocity and neutral column density by fitting observed Ly
\alpha
spectra using grids of these RT models
(
Gronke et al. 2015
;
Orlitová et al. 2018
;
Gurung-López et al. 2019
;
Gurung-López et al. 2022
)
. However, owing to the considerable complexity of Ly
\alpha
radiative transfer, estimation of outflow properties (velocity, column density, geometry, etc.) from Ly
\alpha
spectral profiles using such models is fraught with uncertainty and degeneracies
(
Gronke et al. 2016
;
Li & Gronke 2022
)
. Comparisons between Ly
\alpha
lines and down-the-barrel absorption lines – that could be used to better understand the impact of outflow properties on the emergent Ly
\alpha
line profile – are lacking, particularly in the early universe.

In this paper, we study a large sample (
N=338
) of gravitationally-lensed Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies observed with the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE;
Bacon et al. 2010
), and catalogued by
Richard et al. 2021
(
52
henceforth). Drawing on MUSE observations of 12 lensing clusters, with accompanying deep Hubble Space Telescope (
HST
) observations,
52
uncovered a large number of Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies at
2.9<z<6.7
, the morphologies of which have subsequently been studied in further detail by
Claeyssens et al. 2022
. Thanks to the magnification provided by gravitational lensing, some of these objects are among the faintest and most compact galaxies yet seen in the early universe (see, e.g.,
Vanzella et al. 2017
;
Meštrić et al. 2022
;
Messa et al. 2024
;
Iani et al. 2024
). The boost in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) provided by gravitational lensing also enables the detection of faint spectral features that may otherwise be undetected: UV nebular emission lines (besides Ly
\alpha
) and interstellar absorption lines. We analyse the spectra of these objects to search for evidence of outflows by (i) using nebular metal emission lines (where detected) to trace their systemic velocities, against which the Ly
\alpha
profiles can be compared, and (ii) searching for evidence of blueshifted absorption lines. We investigate the relationship between interstellar absorption profiles and emergent Ly
\alpha
profiles and compare our findings against qualitative predictions of expanding shell RT models. With an empirically-driven understanding of how to interpret Ly
\alpha
profiles in terms of outflow properties, we investigate how outflows are affected by the evolution of the stellar populations that drive them. We complement this analysis by fitting expanding shell RT models to the Ly
\alpha
spectra, and test the ability of such idealized models to infer outflow properties by comparing them against observables obtained from metal emission and absorption lines.

Catalogue

We used the spectroscopic catalogues compiled by
52
as a way to identify high-
z
Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies. Here, we provide a basic overview of how these catalogues were compiled; full details can be found in
52
. The sources were detected either based on continuum emission in stacked HST images using
SExtractor

(
Bertin & Arnouts 1996
)
, or based on line emission seen in the MUSE cubes using
MPDAF
(
the MUSE Python Data Analysis Framework
, which provides a suite of tools for working with MUSE data; see
Piqueras et al. 2019
). The catalogues provide source positions, spectroscopic redshifts, and apparent magnitudes in HST ACS/WFC filters (F435W, F606W, F814W, F105W, F125W, F140W, and F160W) among other information. Using the segmentation maps generated by
SExtractor
,
52
then extract spectra for each source. In separate catalogues,
52
provide information about each spectral line in each spectrum including total flux, central wavelength, and full width at half-maximum (FWHM). They estimate these parameters using the spectral fitting module
Pyplatefit
1
1

1

https://pyplatefit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial.html
, which fits families of lines (or templates), with all lines in each family being fitted simultaneously with the same central velocity and FWHM. In emission, the resonant doublets O VI
\lambda\lambda 1032,1038
, N V
\lambda\lambda 1238,1243
, and C IV
\lambda\lambda 1548,1551
are each fitted as distinct families, while the forbidden N IV
\lambda\lambda 1483,1487
, He II
\lambda 1640
, O III]
\lambda\lambda 1661,1666
, N III
\lambda 1750
, Si III]
\lambda\lambda 1883,1892
, and C III]
\lambda\lambda 1907,1909

\lambda 1260
, O I
\lambda 1302
, C II
\lambda 1334
, and Si IV
\lambda\lambda 1394,1403
. The continuum is fitted using stellar population models of
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
. For more information on
Pyplatefit
, see

. The advantage of this approach is that it can provide accurate redshifts even in cases where the SNR of individual lines is too low for them to be detectable. The downside is that any systematics affecting one or two lines can be carried forward to the rest of the template; this is especially concerning since MUSE spectra inevitably contain some residual atmospheric features, which can masquerade as emission/absorption lines. Any systematics resulting from the continuum subtraction (due to, for example, contamination by light from cluster members, or residual atmospheric continuum) may have a similar effect. For this reason, we perform independent fitting of each spectral line (or doublet) to supplement the results of

III.2
Sample Selection and Spectrum Extraction

We selected all sources from the
52
catalogues that have reported Ly
\alpha
SNR greater than
3.0
. We then placed apertures at the corresponding locations in the MUSE data cubes and extracted 1D spectra along with uncertainties using
MPDAF
. This resulted in
842
spectra in total. We chose to extract spectra from apertures rather than from across the entire segmentation map of each source as in
52
in order to better confine the spectra to the brightest continuum regions of the respective sources, where features such as interstellar absorption lines and optically-thin emission lines may be more easily detected. We set the aperture diameter to be
2\times
the FWHM of the point-spread function (PSF) of the corresponding MUSE field at
7000\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
as listed in
52
. The spectra exhibit a wide range of Ly
\alpha
emission profiles as well as spatial morphologies (some of which are clearly distorted by strong gravitational lensing). The metal emission lines, on the other hand, generally appear to arise from more compact, often spatially-unresolved regions. This is unsurprising given that the Ly
\alpha
halos often extend far beyond the UV continuum of the associated object
(
Claeyssens et al. 2022
)
, though in some cases the apparent compactness of the metal emission regions may also be due to poor SNR. We show examples illustrating the range of Ly
\alpha
spatial morphology and spectral profiles, as well as the compactness of the metal emission line sources in Figure
1
.

III.3
Initial Spectral Fitting

For each spectrum, we fitted analytic profiles to all the lines listed in the
52
catalogues using
scipy.curve_fit
with a Trust Region Reflective algorithm. We started by fitting the Ly
\alpha
lines. As has been shown extensively elsewhere, Ly
\alpha
profiles from galaxies at all cosmic epochs yet probed overwhelmingly show either single-peaked or double-peaked profiles
(see review by
Ouchi et al. 2020
, and references therein)
. Therefore, we initially attempted a fit using two asymmetric gaussian profiles (a “blue” and a “red” peak), as defined in
Shibuya et al. 2014
:

f(\lambda)=A\exp\left(-\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_{c})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{asym}}^{2}}\right)

(1)

where
A
and
\lambda_{c}
are the amplitude and peak wavelength, respectively, and
\sigma_{\text{asym}}\equiv(a(\lambda-\lambda_{c})+d)
where
a
and
d
are, respectively, the asymmetry parameter (positive for skew towards longer wavelengths, and negative for skew towards shorter) and characteristic dispersion. We also included an additive constant to fit the local continuum level. Using the best-fit parameters, we calculated the total flux by integrating under the model profile. We obtained uncertainties in the fitted parameters via bootstrapping, i.e. introducing random gaussian perturbations to the spectra with standard deviations equal to the respective uncertainty in each channel, and then repeating the fitting process as well as the calculation of total flux. We took the median absolute deviation of the distribution in each parameter to be the uncertainty (this is more robust than the mean against extreme outliers, which we found to be commonplace when fitting asymmetric gaussians). In cases where either of the two fitted Ly
\alpha
profiles had SNR
<3.0
or were spectrally unresolved, we re-fitted with a single asymmetric gaussian profile. The definition of “spectrally resolved” for two asymmetric gaussians is not as simple as in the case of two conventional gaussians, where the Rayleigh criterion can be employed, so we instead defined two asymmetric gaussians, B and R, to be spectrally resolved when the following criterion was satisfied:

\lambda_{\text{c,R}}-\lambda_{\text{c,B}}>\max(W_{\text{r,B}},W_{\text{l,R}})

, where
\lambda_{i}
is the peak wavelength of the i
{}^{\text{th}}
asymmetric gaussian, and
W_{\text{i,r}}
(
W_{\text{i,l}}
) is
2\times
the distance from the peak wavelength to the wavelength at which the i
{}^{\text{th}}
asymmetric gaussian obtains half of its maximum value on the right (left) side of the peak; see Figure
2
for illustrative examples.

Figure 2:
Ly
\alpha
profiles demonstrating the criterion by which we consider the red and blue peaks to be resolved (left) or unresolved (right). In both cases, the lines have been fitted with two asymmetric gaussians. The peak wavelengths are shown with dashed lines, while the dark shaded bands indicate the distance between the respective peaks and the points at which the profiles obtain half of their maximum values (the light shaded bands show double this distance). For the two components to be considered resolved, neither of the shaded bands of one component may overlap with the peak wavelength of the other component. In the resolved case, the blue band does not extend to longer wavelengths than the red dashed line, nor does the red band extend to shorter wavelengths than the blue dashed line. In the unresolved case, the red band covers the blue dashed line.

For the other emission and absorption lines, we fitted conventional gaussian profiles along with linear functions to fit the local continuum within
\pm 25\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
of the reported observed wavelength in the
52
catalogues. In the case of doublets such as C III]
\lambda\lambda 1907,1909\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
, we fitted two gaussian profiles simultaneously with the same central velocity and width, though leaving the respective amplitudes as independent parameters. We constrained the central wavelengths of the gaussians to be within
\pm 6.25\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
of the observed wavelength listed in the
52
catalogues. We also constrained the FWHM of the fitted gaussians to be at least
2.40\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
in wavelength space and at most
300\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
in velocity space: the lower bound was chosen to reflect the approximate spectral resolution of MUSE (which is very nearly constant with wavelength) and helps to avoid fitting to narrow features that are occasionally left after sky subtraction, while the upper bound was set so as to avoid fitting to any gradual fluctuations in the spectral baselines that can arise due to contamination by nearby cluster members. We obtain uncertainties in all fitted parameters directly from the covariance matrix produced by the fitting module. We chose this method over the bootstrapping that we employed for the Ly
\alpha
profiles because of its much lower computational cost (as it only required us to run the fitting routine once for each line) and the large number of emission/absorption lines in the sample (
\sim 19\,000
). As this approach does not provide reliable uncertainties, we use this step only to obtain tentative detections which we then follow up with more robust fitting with bootstrapped uncertainties as needed.

III.4
Flagging potential contaminants

Following fitting, we cleaned the results of potential contamination from lower-redshift objects. This can happen either due to (i) the misidentification of the [O II]
\lambda\lambda 3726,3729\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
doublet for Ly
\alpha
in the original catalogues of
52
, or (ii) contamination of the spectrum of a genuine Ly
\alpha
source by emission/absorption lines from foreground galaxies. To identify cases wherein the [O II] doublet masquerades as double-peaked Ly
\alpha
emission, we first searched for putative Ly
\alpha
profiles that are indistinguishable from [O II] both in the ratio of the central wavelengths (which is
3728.8/3726.1=1.0007
for the [O II] doublet) as well as the ratio of the red to blue peak (which can vary between 0.35 and 1.5 for the [O II] doublet, depending on the gas density). If neither of these values was more than
3\sigma
away from the corresponding value (or range) for the [O II] doublet, we then checked for additional spectral lines that could confirm the redshift of the source independently of the Ly
\alpha
line. If no such lines existed, the spectrum was flagged and not included in subsequent analysis. In total we identified just four potential [O II] contaminants in this way.

To identify cases where Ly
\alpha
emitters have their spectral fitting affected by absorption/emission features from cluster members, we first searched the
52
catalogues for foreground stars, cluster members, and/or intermediate redshift galaxies with redshifts below 2.9 (i.e. below all Ly
\alpha
emitters in our sample) whose centroids fall within
10.0\arcsec
of each source. We then identified which of these sources are likely to have the largest impact on the target spectrum by comparing their flux-weighted angular separation

D^{*}\equiv\frac{D_{sky}}{\sqrt{F}}

(2)

where
D_{sky}
is the angular separation from the target in arcseconds, and
F
is the total flux within
1\arcsec
of the object’s centroid. For objects with centroids within
1\times
the PSF FWHM from the targets, while also having
F
with SNR
>10
, we assigned
D^{*}=-1
to ensure that they are given priority over much brighter but more distant sources. We took the object with the lowest
D^{*}
value to be the most likely to contaminate the target spectrum. Having established the most likely contaminating object, we estimated the degree to which it actually contaminates the source. To do this, we generated an image of the object from the MUSE data cube by averaging together all wavelength channels within
[6900,7100]\text{\,\r{A}}
, and then fitted a Sersic profile to the image. We then used the intensity of the Sersic profile at the position of the target source to estimate how much flux is contributed by the potential contaminating object within the aperture used to extract the target spectrum. We then normalised the spectrum
3
3

3

52
data set available upon request from the authors.
of the potential contaminating object by this value, while setting the spectral uncertainties equal to those of the target spectrum. Finally, to determine whether this object indeed contaminates the target spectrum, for each fitted line with SNR
>3
in the target spectrum we attempted to fit the same line to the normalised contaminant spectrum, holding the central wavelength and the sign of the flux (i.e. absorption or emission) the same, but allowing amplitude and width of the fitted gaussians to vary. If statistically significant fits were found, we flagged the corresponding line in the target spectrum as likely contaminated, and did not include it in any further analysis. In total,
60
putative detections of spectral lines across
30
spectra were flagged via this procedure.

III.5
Identifying and Aggregating Lensed Counterparts

Due to the complex nature of galaxy cluster lensing potentials, some sources are lensed into several positions in the image plane. To maximise the SNR of our spectra, as well as to prevent repeated entries in our spectral catalogues, we aggregated together spectra from multiply-lensed images of the same source. We did this by comparing the Ly
\alpha
profiles of our extracted spectra: if two or more spectra from the same cluster were found to have Ly
\alpha
profiles that were not significantly different (i.e. having all fitted parameters including peak wavelengths, i.e. redshifts, within
3\sigma
of each other) then we assumed them to belong to the same source and group them together. Due to the very diverse range of Ly
\alpha
profiles, it is unlikely that two unrelated systems could be matched by this procedure. In each matched group, we then discarded any spectra with emission or absorption lines that were previously identified as likely contaminated (see Section
III.4
) to prevent the contamination being carried forward to the aggregated spectrum. Next, we took the sum of all the spectra in each group and re-fitted all spectral lines using the exact same procedure as described in Section
III.3
, except now using the fitted parameters from the initial round of fitting as initial guesses for lines previously detected at
>3\sigma
. We found that this averaging had a mildly positive impact on the detection rate of faint spectral features overall (as compared with simply discarding the lensed counterparts and retaining only that with the highest magnification), with 16 newly detected lines at
5\sigma
against 6 lost, and 5 newly detected blue Ly
\alpha
peaks against 3 lost. After accounting for multiply-lensed images in this way, we were left with
639
spectra.

Next, we removed from our results all those spectra that could not be confidently identified as true Ly
\alpha
emitters. This included all those spectra with low-confidence spectral redshifts according to
52
(those with redshift confidence below the highest rating of 3), that did not show statistically significant asymmetry in our fitted Ly
\alpha
profiles (i.e the asymmetry parameter
a
was within
3\sigma
of zero), and furthermore had fewer than two high-confidence (
>4\sigma
) detections of other spectral lines. This left us with
524
spectra in total.

Inevitably, there are a few cases of multiple spectra that come from the same object but are not grouped together by our procedure to identify lensed counterparts. This can happen when a single lensed image is highly spatially extended and/or clumpy, in which case there can be multiple catalogue entries from
52
pointing to different parts of the same lensed image. Alternatively, the apertures placed on different lensed images of the same source may cover different parts of the target due to differences in the lensed morphologies/magnifications of each image. When there is sufficient variation in Ly
\alpha
profiles across the source, the resulting fitted Ly
\alpha
profiles can have statistically significant differences. After aggregating our spectra, we cleared any such remaining repeated entries by simply removing spectra from the same cluster with Ly
\alpha
peak wavelengths within
300\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
of one another (retaining whichever spectrum has the higher total SNR in emission plus absorption lines, or, if none are detected, the higher SNR in Ly
\alpha
). This left us with
338
spectra in total.

Though

Statistically significant residual features caused by skylines are, in the overwhelming majority of cases that we visually inspected, narrow when compared with genuine absorption and emission lines, consisting of just one or at most two adjacent channels significantly above/below the local continuum level. We therefore flagged all fitted lines with FWHM equal to or close to the minimum value allowed by our fitting procedure (
2.40\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
-
2.41\,\mathrm{\text{\r{A}}}
, see Section
III.3
). We also flagged any lines in which the SNR of the line peak was greater than the SNR of the total integrated flux of the line, as this indicates that these lines are dominated by just a single channel. Since it is unlikely to have two false detections with precisely the right separation in wavelength to masquerade as a doublet, we removed any flags from doublets in cases where both lines were detected with SNR
>3.0

IV.1
Outflow signatures

In the following section, we establish evidence for widespread outflows among our sample of lensed Ly
\alpha

\alpha

IV.1.1
Systemic redshifts

In this Section, we analyze the interstellar absorption lines found in our sample. As we will show, we find kinematics indicative of ubiquitous outflows in the sources in which absorption is detected and the systemic redshift can be measured (10 sources total). Furthermore, based on absorption line ratios, we find evidence of absorption in optically-thick neutral clumps embedded in these outflows. Surprisingly, we also find evidence of optically-thick absorption in tracers of ionized gas.

To search for signs of outflows, we made use of the interstellar absorption lines Si II
\lambda 1260

\lambda 1334

\lambda 1260

The sources with the strongest absorption signatures allow us to probe the structure of the outflows using ratios between absorption lines of the same ionic species. Line ratios corresponding to the ratio of the oscillator strengths of the respective lines indicate that the absorption is unsaturated (i.e. the gas is optically thin); on the other hand, line ratios approaching unity indicate that the absorption is saturated (i.e. the gas is optically thick). Line ratios approaching unity but with non-zero flux density at the line cores indicate saturated absorption in gas with limited neutral covering fraction – i.e. a clumpy medium. Using this method,
Berry et al. 2012
find evidence that high-ionization absorption lines in LBGs are optically thin, based on equivalent width (EW) ratios of the Si IV
\lambda\lambda 1394,1403
doublet,
\text{EW}(\text{SiIV}1394)/\text{EW}(\text{SiIV}1403)\sim 2.0
, which is approximately the same as the ratio of oscillator strengths. This contrasts with the low-ionization lines which are generally found to be consistent with absorption in optically-thick clumps
(
Shapley et al. 2003
;
Steidel et al. 2010
;
Berry et al. 2012
;
Jones et al. 2012
)
as measured via the Si II absorption doublet, where for optically thin absorption the ratio is
\text{EW}(\text{SiII}1260)/\text{EW}(\text{SiII}1527)\sim 5.0
. We perform the same measurement by re-fitting the strongest absorption lines. To get a more complete picture of the uncertainties in absorption line EW ratios, we again use bootstrapping, taking the ratio of EWs for each of the
3000
noise realizations. We show the resulting distributions in EW ratios of low- and high-ionization absorption lines in Figure
7
. In agreement with past studies of the Si II
\lambda\lambda 1260,1527
doublet, we find EW ratios
\sim 1
, suggestive of optically thick clumps. However, in the Si IV
\lambda\lambda 1394,1403
doublet we find mixed results, with three objects clearly preferring optically thick clumps (SMACS2031 IDP375, SMACS2013 IDP366, MACS2214 IDP573), one preferring optically thin absorption (MACS1206 IDP3301), and the remainder showing ambiguous results. These results suggest that the Si IV
\lambda\lambda 1394,1403
absorption may arise from clumps embedded in a still hotter and more tenuous phase, possibly hot
\sim 10^{6}\,\mathrm{K}
gas as is seen via X-ray emission in outflows in the local universe.

IV.2
Source vs outflow properties

IV.2.1
Origin of emission lines

In this section we analyze the metal emission lines found in our sample. We show that these emission lines likely originate in H II regions, being consistent both with theoretical models and observations of local low-metallicity dwarf SFGs. Furthermore, we present evidence for a positive association between emission line EW and the appearance of blueshifted Ly
\alpha
peaks, indicative of harder ionizing spectra and/or ionization parameters in DP sources.

Having established that the emission lines are consistent with expectations H II regions, we compare the strength of the emission from SP and DP sources. We find evidence of a positive association between double-peaked Ly

\alpha

To understand the relationship between Ly
\alpha

\lambda 1334

We found the best SNR in the absorption profiles was obtained by including only those spectra with

4/45

We tested the robustness of these conclusions by altering the stacking procedure in a number of ways, including:

(i)

Excluding the three SP sources with the strongest absorption lines to ensure that the strongest absorbers do not dominate the result (excluding more sources than this lowers the statistical significance of the SP low-ionization absorption to
<3\sigma

(ii)

Weighting all spectra equally to ensure that the results are not dominated by a few spectra with low spectral uncertainties.

(iii)

Including sources without measured systemic redshifts in the stacks using Ly

Including only sources with

The models are able to simultaneously reproduce all emission line EWs and P Cygni profiles in both cases.

We ran
BEAGLE
assuming a constant SFH, motivated by the fact that that our spectra are composites of many sources inevitably spanning a range of ages (we tested single burst models but found them unable to reproduce both the emission line EWs and P Cygni profiles simultaneously). We set uniform priors on all the parameters in our models as shown in Table
2
. These also include dust attenuation,
\tau_{V}
, which is necessary to reproduce the exact shape of the P Cygni spectra, and total stellar mass,
M
, which affects the level of the continuum. We employed the two-component dust attenuation law of
Chevallard et al. 2013
, assuming the default ratio between the dust optical depth in the diffuse ISM and young star birth clouds of
0.4
. There are two further parameters that affect the emission line EWs and P Cygni profiles. First, the upper-mass cut-off of the Chabrier IMF
(
Chabrier 2003
)
adopted in the models can be either
100\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
or
300\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. We found that only the
300\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
cut-off could reproduce the EW of the DP He II
\lambda 1640
line, while either cut-off could reproduce all the SP emission line EWs (including He II
\lambda 1640
, of which there is a statistically significant though weak detection in the SP stack). We therefore adopt the higher upper mass cut-off of
300\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. We also experimented with different values of the ISM density,
n
, which can be
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, or
10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
. We found
n=10^{3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
to produce the best fitting models for both the SP and DP cases, though there was no qualitative difference between the fitting results for
n=10^{3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
and
n=10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
. Models with
n=10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
were strongly disfavoured, being unable to reproduce all emission line EWs simultaneously.

We show the posterior distributions produced by
BEAGLE
for both the SP and DP spectra along with predicted emission line EWs and P Cygni profiles in Figure
13
. For the sake of clarity we exclude the posteriors in
\tau_{V}
and
M
: the former shows no difference between the SP and DP cases, both being characterised by
\tau_{V}<1.0
, while the latter is unphysical given that we do not correct for lensing magnification or varying luminosity distance when stacking the spectra. The most significant differences between the SP and DP posteriors are in age (i.e. time elapsed since onset of star formation),
\log U
, and
\xi_{d}
. We now address each of these in turn.

Age
: The lower age ascribed to the DP spectra can be attributed to two factors. Firstly, the higher emission line EWs, particularly the He II
\lambda 1640
and C IV
\lambda\lambda 1548,1551
lines, require more EUV photons, which are only produced by the most massive stars that live for just a few Myr. Secondly, the deeper P Cygni profile in the DP spectrum suggests a higher proportion of extremely young massive stars (with ages
\lesssim 4\,\mathrm{Myr}
) relative to slightly older stars (with ages
20-100\,\mathrm{Myr}
). Thus extremely young starbursts seem to drive slower/less turbulent and less massive outflows, suggesting significantly lower mass outflow rates. Moreover, the difference in age between SP and DP sources aligns remarkably well with the expected onset of SNe feedback, which can only begin after the most massive stars begin to undergo core-collapse at
\sim 4\,\mathrm{Myr}
(see, e.g.,
Leitherer et al. 2014
). This suggests that outflows in DP sources may be driven primarily by early SNe from rare very massive stars or even pre-SNe feedback mechanisms such as stellar winds and radiation pressure. Once SNe feedback begins in earnest after a few
10\,\mathrm{Myr}
(
Kim et al. 2020
,
Hopkins et al. 2023
), outflows become more massive, faster, and more turbulent (greater ISM turbulence being a well-studied consequence of SNe shocks – see
Mac Low & Klessen 2004
,
Klessen & Glover 2016
), which in turn tends to give rise to SP Ly
\alpha
profiles.

Ionization parameter
: the posteriors in
\log U
tentatively favour a higher ionization parameter for DP sources. In the
Gutkin et al. 2016
models,
U
is specifically the ionization parameter evaluated at the Strömgren radius, and thus primarily reflects the geometry of the ionized gas, with larger values corresponding to more compact H II regions closer to the ionizing source. Hence lower
U
in SP sources may also be driven by strengthening feedback due to the onset of core-collapse SNe, which disrupt the ISM surrounding star-forming regions and drive it outward, thus decreasing
U
.

Dust depletion factor
: our models tentatively favor a higher dust depletion in DP relative to SP sources. This is likely due to the relatively weak stacked Si III]
\lambda\lambda 1883,1892
emission in DP sources. This may also be due to the very young age of the stellar populations powering DP Ly
\alpha
emission, which may not allow sufficient time for dispersal of Si into the ISM via SNe.

Together, these results suggest that Ly
\alpha
profiles – and hence outflows – may undergo rapid evolution within the first
\sim 10\,\mathrm{Myr}
after the onset of an episode of star formation.

V_{\text{exp}}
: the model expansion velocity is generally higher for SP sources than for DP sources, with the difference in the average values being
\sim 40\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
. This difference aligns well with the measured offset between the respective low-ionization absorption profile centroids found in Section
IV.2
of
\sim 60\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
, suggesting that HTS models may provide useful approximations of outflow velocities.

N_{\text{H}}
: the model neutral column density slightly
higher
in DP sources, which stands in stark contrast with the results obtained from absorption profiles in Section
IV.2
that suggest
N_{\text{H}}
should be much lower in DP sources. Moreover, this appears to contradict theoretical expectations of expanding shell models, in which higher
N_{\text{H}}
tends to suppress blueshifted peaks (all other parameters being equal). This issue may partially stem from limited spectral resolution: while low
N_{\text{H}}
enhances the height of blueshifted peaks, it also decreases their separation from the red peaks. Thus extremely low
N_{\text{H}}
can bring the peaks so close together as to be spectrally unresolved by MUSE, making them less likely to be detected as DP sources by our fitting procedure described in Section
III.3
. While this could explain the relatively low fraction of DP sources with low neutral column density
\lesssim 10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}
, it cannot account for the relatively large fraction with high column density
\gtrsim 10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}
.

\tau_{\text{a}}
: the model dust optical depth is found to be much greater in DP sources on average, with an order of magnitude difference in the mean values. Moreover, there is an unexpectedly large number of sources with
\tau_{\text{a}}\sim 1
, the upper bound of the range covered by the model grid, many of which are DP sources. This is unexpected, as blue peaks are strongly suppressed by dust in expanding shell models (as blueshifted photons generally undergo more scattering within the shell and thus are more likely to be absorbed by dust). In most of the high-dust models,
\tau_{\text{a}}
is poorly constrained. However, in
26
cases,
\tau_{\text{a}}
exceeds
0.1
by more than
2\sigma
. Manual inspection shows that all of these allegedly dusty sources have at least some evidence of blue peaks (though due to limited SNR, our fitting procedure only classified 17/26 as DP).

Taken together, these results suggest that, in many cases, our outflow models yield parameters that are unphysical. To understand why this may be the case, we considered two of the most obvious limitations of HTS models: (i) their assumed homogeneity in
N_{\text{H}}
, and (ii) the use of a single expansion velocity rather than a range as in, e.g.,
Verhamme et al. 2006
. Our analysis of metal absorption features casts doubt on each of these assumptions. Firstly, as demonstrated in Section
IV.1.3
, metal absorption lines show strong evidence for optically-thick clumps with limited covering fraction rather than a homogeneous medium (i.e. patchy outflows). Secondly, the wide velocity range spanned by both individual and stacked SP absorption profiles presented in Sections
IV.1.3
and
IV.2
suggests that SP sources in particular may be poorly approximated by a single expansion velocity. To test whether these factors can lead to inaccurate fitted parameters, we constructed toy models consisting of Ly
\alpha
profiles generated by summing together simulated HTS profiles with (i) two different values of
N_{\text{H}}
to approximate patchy outflows and (ii) two different values of
V_{\text{exp}}
to approximate more complex velocity structure. For the mixed-
N_{\text{H}}
models we tested two values of
V_{\text{exp}}
, one of which produces an DP and the other an SP profile, to see whether DP and SP sources are differentially affected by patchy outflows as our fitting results suggest. For the mixed-
V_{\text{exp}}
models we tested two different mixtures of
V_{\text{exp}}
– one with high and one with low contrast – to see whether the effects on the fitted parameters scale with the velocity range spanned by the outflows. In all cases, we adopted fiducial values of
\tau_{\text{a}}
,
EW_{\text{in}}
, and
W_{\text{in}}
based on typical fitted model values; these and all other input values are given in Table
4
. Our simulated profiles were constructed to match the MUSE observations in terms of spectral resolution and wavelength binning, and we added gaussian perturbations to each channel to simulate noise (scaled so as to give us a peak SNR of
30
in each Ly
\alpha
profile). We then fitted each of these simulated profiles using single-component HTS models in exactly the same way as for the real Ly
\alpha
profiles. As shown in the example in Figure
17
, the mixed Ly
\alpha
profiles can be reproduced convincingly by just a single HTS model. The output parameters, shown in Table
4
, demonstrate that

(i)

DP Ly
\alpha
profiles arising from patchy outflows may indeed prefer extreme values of
\tau_{\text{a}}
when fitted with homogeneous expanding shell models, but the same is not generally true for SP profiles, and

(ii)

Ly
\alpha
profiles arising from outflows consisting of a wider range of velocities may be fitted with underestimated
N_{\text{H}}
. This is likely to have a disproportionate impact on SP sources, which are found to have broader low-ionization metal absorption troughs.

While our toy models go some way towards understanding what factors may feasibly underpin the failure of HTS models to accurately reproduce outflow properties of real Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies, some questions still remain. Firstly, the mixed-
N_{\text{H}}
model leads to
underestimated
values of
N_{\text{H}}
in the DP case but not the SP – an effect that we found to be persistent across a wide range of input parameters. Secondly, the output
V_{\text{exp}}
is overestimated in both the mixed-
N_{\text{H}}
and mixed-
V_{\text{exp}}
SP profiles. This is unsurprising, as our simple models neglect any cross-talk between different components, which are likely to have a significant impact on the emergent Ly
\alpha
profile (e.g. if outflow velocity depends on radius, then Ly
\alpha
photons emerging from slower inner regions must undergo radiative transfer through faster outer regions). Furthermore, real outflows are likely to be better represented by a continuous range in both
N_{\text{H}}
and
V_{\text{exp}}
rather than two distinct values each. A systematic investigation of Ly
\alpha
RT is beyond our current scope: our toy models merely serve to show that deviations from simple HTS geometry can lead to unphysical fitted model parameters in line with what we find when we fit HTS models to real Ly
\alpha
profiles.

We also checked the accuracy of the
zELDA
estimates of
z_{\text{sys}}
by comparing them against the systemic redshift as measured using optically-thin emission lines. Figure

zELDA
in velocity space (relative to the red Ly
\alpha
peak). In general, the differences between the two values are within
3\sigma
of zero; however, the distribution of velocity offsets, shown in the histogram in the lower panel, is centered below zero – with the discrepancy being especially strong for SP sources. This means that expanding shell models tend to underestimate the velocity offset of the red Ly
\alpha
peak relative to the systemic (i.e. placing the systemic too close to the red Ly
\alpha
peak in velocity space). This raises three major potential problems:

(i)

The inferred outflow kinematics may be inaccurate, since the proximity of the redshifted peak relative to the systemic scales with the expansion velocity (e.g.
Verhamme et al. 2006
).

(ii)

If there are also interstellar absorption features present, their blueshift relative to the systemic velocity will be exaggerated, leading to overestimation of outflow velocities.

(iii)

The amount of Ly
\alpha
emission at the systemic velocity may be overestimated. This has important consequences, as it has been found that – at low redshift – systemic Ly
\alpha
emission can be used as a proxy for LyC escape
(
Verhamme et al. 2015
)
. Any future studies employing this relationship and wishing to use expanding shell models to estimate systemic redshifts may consequently overestimate LyC leakage.

V
Discussion

V.1
Effect of the Neutral IGM

Attenuation of spectra bluewards of the Ly
\alpha
line by neutral gas in the intergalactic medium – i.e. the Lyman forest – also affects observed Ly
\alpha
line profiles in addition to intrinsic radiative transfer through a galaxy’s ISM. This effect is reflected in the increasing rarity of DP Ly
\alpha
sources at
z>4
in the redshift distributions of SP and DP sources shown in Figure
3
. Below
z=4
, the distributions show no significant difference, with DP sources constituting
\sim 40\%
of all sources. Consequently, some sources at
z>4
that are intrinsically DP must become classified as SP due to IGM attenuation. Despite this, we find no qualitative changes to our results even when excluding all SP sources at
z>4
from the stacked absorption and emission spectra presented in Sections
IV.2.2
and
IV.2.3
, simply due to the fact that lower-
z
sources dominate the stacked spectra.

V.2
Evidence of Delayed SNe Feedback

Our modelling of stacked emission lines from SP and DP sources in Section
IV.2.3
suggests that starbursts in DP sources tend to be younger, while at the same time also showing weaker outflow signatures in interstellar absorption lines as shown in Section
IV.2.2
. We interpret this as evidence of weaker feedback in the first
\sim 4\,\mathrm{Myr}
of star formation, the time scale aligning well with expectations of the onset of SNe feedback (e.g.
Hopkins et al. 2023
). Similar trends have been reported in low-
z
Ly
\alpha
-emitting galaxies by
Hayes et al. 2023
, who find that galaxies with greater Ly
\alpha
EW tend to have younger stellar ages and weaker interstellar absorption lines. However, they conclude that a greater abundance of ionizing photons from very young stars may be responsible for this correlation by simultaneously powering greater Ly
\alpha
emission and ionizing the ISM, thus reducing the neutral column density along the line of sight to the observer. While this may indeed partially explain the association between young stellar age and low neutral column density, it does not explain why younger sources (i.e. DP sources) drive slower/less turbulent outflows as suggested by our stacked interstellar absorption lines, as well as RT models. Moreover, we do not find any evidence of enhanced high-ionization absorption from Si IV
\lambda\lambda 1394,1403
in DP sources as may be expected if the difference is merely in ionization state of the ISM. Rather, our analysis suggests a relationship between stellar population age and ISM
kinematics
, possibly complementary to any relationship between age and ISM ionization.

Figure 19:
Histograms of luminosity in the HST F160W band for SP (red) and DP (blue) sources, based on photometry from
52
. No statistically significant difference can be seen as may be expected if SP sources are generally more massive than DP sources.

Another factor that may influence the relationship between age and outflow rate is galaxy mass. Lower-mass SFGs will tend to have lower neutral column density simply by virtue of having lower gas mass, drive slower outflows due to lower escape velocities, and yet may also undergo more bursty star formation and hence have younger average ages. Burstiness is also driven primarily by the timescale of SNe feedback, with the shortest feasible burst being set by the age at which SNe feedback kicks in and halts further star formation. Thus star formation episodes in DP sources may simply not survive past
\sim 4\,\mathrm{Myr}
in most cases (as opposed to a scenario in which DP sources evolve into SP as feedback strengthens). As a check on this idea, we compared the distributions in luminosity in the HST F160W filter in SP and DP sources based on photometry in the
52
catalogues. To perform this test, we included only sources with
z<4
to guard against bias towards more luminous galaxies at high-
z
, where attenuation by neutral gas in the IGM makes it more likely for sources to show an SP profile. The resulting distributions, shown in Figure
19
, indicate no difference in luminosity between the two subsamples as may be expected if SP sources are more massive. Of course, rest-frame near-UV luminosities alone are limited as a gauge of overall stellar mass. Robust mass estimates from
HST
and
JWST
photometry will likely shed more light on this issue, which we defer to a future paper.

We also consider the impact of line-of-sight effects on the emergent Ly
\alpha
profile. Radiation-hydrodynamic simulation of a Ly
\alpha
emitting galaxy of
M\sim 10^{9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
by
Blaizot et al. 2023
show that the observed Ly
\alpha
profile can vary dramatically depending on viewing angle due to anisotropic neutral column density/ISM kinematics. While viewing angle likely contributes considerable scatter to the relationship between age and interstellar absorption line profiles, this may be mitigated somewhat by the generally low masses of our target sources (
\sim 10^{6}-10^{9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
; see, e.g.,
Iani et al. 2024
), which may give rise to less collimated, more isotropic outflows (e.g.
Strickland & Heckman 2009
).

Finally, we consider the impact of stellar evolutionary tracks on our results. The stellar population templates of
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
used by default by
BEAGLE
do not include the effects of binary evolution, which may influence the ages we infer in our model star formation histories in Section
IV.2.3
. However, as shown by
Chisholm et al. 2019
, binarity seems to
accelerate
the decay of the N V
\lambda\lambda 1238,1243
P Cygni profile that is the strongest indicator of an age difference between SP and DP sources. Consequently, the inclusion of binary evolution is unlikely to have any qualitative effect on our inference of younger stellar ages in DP sources.

VI
Summary and Conclusion

We analysed the spectra of gravitationally-lensed Ly
\alpha
emitters spectroscopically identified by
52
. We fitted analytic profiles to the Ly
\alpha
lines as well as a number of rest-frame UV metal emission and absorption lines from both high- and low-ionization species. We then generated stacks of these emission and absorption lines from spectra with and without detected blue Ly
\alpha
peaks. We find that

(i)

The sources show spectral signatures suggestive of near ubiquitous outflows. Single Ly
\alpha
peaks are redshifted relative to the systemic velocity as measured using metal emission lines, or, when there is also a blue peak, the two peaks straddle the systemic. Interstellar absorption lines from both low- and high-ionization species are blueshifted relative to the systemic redshift in most cases where the latter can be measured (8/10).

(ii)

Both low- and high-ionization absorption lines exhibit line ratios indicative of saturation, suggesting that the outflows consist of optically thick clumps of neutral gas as well as warm ionized gas with limited covering fractions. The sometimes-saturated nature of the high-ionization absorption lines is particularly interesting as it suggests clumps embedded in a still hotter phase.

(iii)

Sources with double-peaked Ly
\alpha
lines have weaker low- and high-ionization absorption lines relative to those with single-peaks, suggesting they are associated with less massive outflows. They also show smaller absorption line widths than single-peaked sources, suggesting slower/less turbulent outflows. Taken together, the weaker and narrower absorption in double-peaked sources suggests significantly lower mass outflow rates.

(iv)

At the same time, double-peaked Ly
\alpha
sources also show nebular metal emission lines indicative of extremely young ages (just a few Myr), while single-peaked Ly
\alpha
sources are characterized by ages
\gtrsim 20\,\mathrm{Myr}
. This suggests a picture in which outflows strengthen significantly as SNe feedback builds in intensity, consistent with theoretical expectations.

(v)

Though qualitative agreement between Ly

profiles and homogeneous expanding shell RT models is good, the models return unphysical outflow parameters (at least when not constrained by any priors) and tend to overestimate systemic redshifts.
