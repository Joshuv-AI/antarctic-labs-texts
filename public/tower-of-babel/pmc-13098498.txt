Protocol for a pragmatic randomized controlled trial evaluating a care pathway incorporating ultrasound-guided platelet-rich plasma injection plus physiotherapy versus physiotherapy alone in patients with L5/S1 lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Rayhan Islam, Mahmudul Haque Udoy, Md Zahirul Islam, Md Abu Sayeed, Feroz Kabir, Md Zahid Hossain, Sharmila Jahan, Ehsanur Rahman, Mohammad Anwar Hossain, K M Amran Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar disc herniation (LDH) causes low back pain and lower-limb neurological symptoms. Conservative and surgical treatments have similar outcomes and recurrence rates within two years. Anti-inflammatory drugs or opioids reduce inflammation but are unsuitable for long-term use. Physiotherapy improves mobility and function, but has limited evidence for reversing disc herniation. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is emerging as a regenerative therapy for LDH. Combining ultrasound-guided PRP with McKenzie therapy may enhance disc repositioning, tissue repair, and sustained recovery. This pragmatic trial evaluates whether adding an ultrasound-guided PRP injection pathway to standard McKenzie therapy improves clinical and neurophysiological outcomes compared with McKenzie therapy alone in a low-resource setting. In the absence of a sham control, it assesses real-world effectiveness rather than the isolated biological efficacy of PRP.MethodsThis single-center, randomized controlled trial will recruit patients with L5/S1 LDH confirmed by clinical examination and MRI, following North American Spine Society (NASS) criteria. Participants will be randomized via concealed block randomization to receive PRP with McKenzie therapy or McKenzie therapy alone. McKenzie therapy, by a certified practitioner, will be delivered for 30–40 min, three times weekly for four weeks. PRP will be injected once by a spine surgeon under GE LOGIQ P5 ultrasonography. The co-primary outcomes are pain intensity and disc morphology. A hierarchical testing approach will be used, with confirmatory analysis of disc morphology performed only if the between-group difference in pain is significant (p < 0.05); otherwise, it will be considered exploratory. The study is powered for the pain outcome. Pain will be assessed using a 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale and Pressure Pain Thresholds, whereas disc morphology will be evaluated using musculoskeletal ultrasonography. Neurophysiological recovery (Straight Leg Raise, Electromyography, and Nerve Conduction Studies) and functional disability measured by the Oswestry Disability Index will be evaluated as secondary outcomes. Because participants and therapists are not blinded, subjective outcomes are susceptible to expectancy effects. Objective disc morphology is the primary unbiased endpoint. Post-tests will be obtained 4 weeks from baseline, and follow-up will be obtained after three months and six months.SignificanceThis study addresses a real-world evidence gap by comparing a PRP-injection care-pathway with usual physiotherapy alone. The findings will inform clinical decision-making in low-resource settings, without attempting to isolate the specific biological effect of PRP.Keywords: Lumbar disc herniation, Platelet-rich plasma, McKenzie therapy, Musculoskeletal ultrasonography, Electromyography, Nerve conduction studies

Introduction

Background and rationale

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a clinical condition presented as a result of lumbar intervertebral disc displacement, compressing the soft tissues posterior to the spinal and neural canal and mediating inflammation, leading to pressure on exiting spinal nerves [1]. Common symptoms of LDH are low back pain and referred pain and/or neurological symptoms following the dermatome and or myotome of the lumbosacral spinal nerves [2]. The displacement originates from a biomechanical imbalance in the intervertebral disc pressure absorption mechanism; the phenomena cause the release of inflammatory mediators and nociceptive neurotransmitters, resulting in inflammation and consistent pain [3,4]. LDH might occur in any lumbar intervertebral level; hence, the anatomical, physiological, and biomechanical attributes of L4/L5 and L5/S1 levels are more vulnerable to herniation [5]. LDH affects between 14% and 20% of the general population worldwide. Approximately 1–3% experience symptomatic LDH yearly [[6], [7], [8]]. It is most common in adults between 30 and 50 years of age [9]. In the United States, over 2 million people are diagnosed with symptomatic lumbar disc disease every year [10]. Where in Asia the prevalence ranges from 12% to 22.8% [7,11]. Bangladesh, like many other low and middle-income countries, has limited epidemiological data on LDH. However, clinical observations, hospital records, and published reports illustrate a rise in disc-related low back pain cases, especially in urban and semi-urban areas [12]. Heavy lifting at work, poor posture, sedentary lifestyle, high body mass index (BMI), and repetitive lumbar stress are major risk factors of LDH in this population [13]. The clinical diagnosis of LDH usually depends on a combination of patient history, physical assessment, and relevant imaging tests [14]. Patients who experience LDH usually complain about a burning or stinging sensation in the lower back, which may radiate towards the lower limb. During physical examination, the Straight Leg Raise (SLR) test is usually positive, and/or changes in motor functions can be found [15]. The sensitivity of SLR varies in different studies for LDH patients. This is usually around 50-90% above, depending on the patient group. Prior studies stated a higher sensitivity in surgical candidates [[16], [17], [18]]. According to the North American Spine Society (NASS) guideline, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for identifying disc-related conditions such as disc degeneration, bulging, protrusions, extrusions, or any disc-related nerve compression [19,20]. Musculoskeletal Ultrasonography (MSK-USG) is becoming an alternative to MRI for evaluating disc characteristics like disc height, echogenicity, and displacement [21]. Electrophysiological tests like Electromyography (EMG) and Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS) are also helpful in detecting the level of nerve involvement, nerve damage pattern, muscle functioning, and recovery status [22].

Conservative management of LDH usually includes: pain-relieving medicines, muscle relaxants, rest, and physiotherapy. Surgery is considered only when the symptoms become unbearable [8]. Corticosteroid injections may provide fast anti-inflammatory relief, but different studies have found short-term benefits with adverse effects in some patients [23]. McKenzie's approach is the most widely practiced and effective technique for managing LDH. This approach is also referred to as Mechanical Diagnosis and Therapy (MDT). The theory is based on the principle of classification and movement pattern, which helps identify the mechanical issues, helps with pain relief, and promotes self-management [24]. Kilpikoski and colleagues [25] found that McKenzie exercises effectively manage LDH and sciatica, even over a 24-month follow-up period. In recent years, regenerative medicine has investigated Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy as a non-surgical treatment for disc issues and nerve pain [8]. PRP is prepared by centrifuging a patient's blood, extracting a layer enriched with platelets, healing factors stored in platelet granules, and cytokines [26]. These components help to promote tissue repair and regeneration of the damaged tissues [27]. Several studies have explored PRP providing a better result in terms of reducing pain and improving functional outcomes, even long-term benefits in LDH patients, compared to corticosteroid injections or a placebo [28]. Although PRP and McKenzie exercises effectively manage LDH, high-quality randomized controlled trials combining both are still very limited. Most prior studies compared PRP with corticosteroids or evaluated PRP in isolation [29,30].

Furthermore, objective outcome assessments using disc morphology changes using MSK-USG and neurophysiological measures like SLR, NCS, and EMG have been largely overlooked in previous studies. Few trials have integrated these imaging and electrophysiological tools to monitor structural and functional recovery post-intervention. While PRP's safety profile is promising, its dosage, administration route, and follow-up duration standardization remain inconsistent across studies [28]. Also, there is limited information from South Asian countries, especially Bangladesh. Limited resources and a lack of awareness about regenerative therapy make it challenging to use in clinical practice. It is important to collect data from areas with limited resources. This can be supported using affordable and simple tools like MSK-USG and basic methods to monitor patient progress.

To address these research gaps, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of combining MSK-USG-guided PRP injection with McKenzie exercises compared to McKenzie exercises alone in patients with L5/S1 LDH. This Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) will determine the impact of the combined treatment on pain, disc morphology, and neurophysiological recovery. We hypothesize that patients receiving the PRP-injection care pathway (PRP + McKenzie therapy) will show greater improvements in pain, disc morphology, and neurophysiological recovery compared to those receiving McKenzie therapy alone. However, because the study design lacks a sham-injection control, any observed between-group difference will reflect the total effect of the PRP-injection pathway (including non-specific placebo effects, patient expectations, and enhanced therapeutic contact) rather than the specific biological effect of PRP. Therefore, this trial is explicitly framed as a pragmatic effectiveness study comparing two real-world care pathways, not as a mechanistic efficacy trial of PRP as a standalone biological agent.

The study's objectives are: 1) To compare the reduction in pain intensity between the two groups using 10-cm VAS and PPTs; 2) To evaluate changes in disc morphology (disc height, echogenicity, displacement) using MSK-USG; 3) To assess neurophysiological recovery via SLR, NCS, and EMG parameters; and 4) To evaluate functional disability through the ODI.

Methodology

Study design

This study will be an outcome-assessor-blinded RCT.

Study location

The study will take place at a single site, specifically at the outpatient unit of the Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation (PTR) at Dr. M.R. Khan Medical Center, affiliated with Jashore University of Science and Technology (JUST), Jashore-7408, Bangladesh.

Eligibility criteria

The study population meeting the inclusion criteria will be considered for the trial. Adults aged 25–55 years will be included, as the risk of LDH is highest in this group, and disc degeneration accelerates beyond age 55 [15]. LDH will be diagnosed according to NASS guidelines, which include: (i) low back pain with or without leg radiation in a dermatomal pattern, (ii) symptom provocation between 30° and 75° in the SLR test, and (iii) imaging evidence of disc herniation at L5/S1 on MRI [21,31]. Patients will be excluded if they have cauda equina syndrome, calcified or hard disc, spinal infections or tumors, previous lumbar surgery, uncontrolled diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or pregnancy. Patients unwilling to participate or who withdraw within the first week will also be excluded.

Sampling and recruitment procedure

Patients with L5/S1 LDH will be identified at the outpatient unit of the Department of PTR, JUST. All patients will be checked using clinical and imaging criteria, following the NASS guidelines. Eligible patients will be invited to participate in the study, and after providing informed consent, they will be randomly assigned to one of two groups. Group assignment will be done using block randomization with variable block sizes, and the allocation will be kept secret using sealed opaque envelopes to ensure fairness. One group will receive a single ultrasound-guided PRP injection plus McKenzie exercises, while the other group will receive McKenzie exercises only. Outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to reduce bias. All participants will be assessed and followed up at the same time points, and data will be analyzed according to the group they were initially assigned to, even if they do not complete the full treatment. Sample size

The sample size was calculated using ClinCalc Sample Size Calculator (©2025 - ClinCalc LLC., www.clincalc.com) [32]. Pain intensity, measured by the 10-cm VAS, was chosen as the primary outcome for this study on LDH. The VAS is a validated and widely used tool for assessing pain severity in LDH populations. For the sample size calculation, we set the option for Study Group Designs: Comparison of two independent study groups and set the Primary Endpoint: Continuous (Means), due to characteristics of the VAS pain score. Based on previous studies, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for VAS in LDH is 33% on a 10-cm VAS, and the standard deviation (SD) is typically around ±2.29 points [[33], [34], [35]]. In the online software, we entered the anticipated mean, VAS score for the control group as 5.17 with an SD of ±2.29. The intervention group reduced the mean by 33% (MCID). A Type I/II error rate (alpha) of 0.05 (two-sided) and 80% power were set. The software calculated a total sample size of 56 participants, with a 7.14% dropout rate total of 60 participants, with 30 participants required in each group. This sample size is consistent with published evidence and ensures a clinically meaningful difference in pain outcomes between the two groups. We acknowledge that the sample size calculation was based solely on the pain outcome (VAS), as a minimal clinically important difference for disc morphology assessed by MSK-USG in lumbar disc herniation has not been established. Consequently, the study may be underpowered to detect meaningful changes in disc morphology.

Accordingly, disc morphology is retained as a co-primary endpoint within a hierarchical testing framework (Section 6), whereby testing proceeds to disc morphology only if the pain outcome is statistically significant. If this condition is not met, analyses of disc morphology will be considered exploratory. This approach controls the family-wise type I error rate while transparently acknowledging the study's power limitations.

Study procedure

The study will follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines throughout the study. The anticipated flow of participants through the study phases is shown in Fig. 1 (CONSORT 2025). The Principal Investigator (PI) will lead a multidisciplinary team that includes a trial manager, a blinded outcome assessor, a musculoskeletal sonologist, two site coordinators, and four intervention providers. Screening and sampling procedures have been mentioned earlier. After the allocation, the intervention group will receive a single ultrasound-guided PRP injection administered by an orthopedic surgeon, followed by a structured McKenzie exercise program delivered by a certified McKenzie therapist. The control group will receive the same McKenzie exercise program without PRP injection. All interventions will be supervised on-site by trained physiotherapists. Site coordinators will monitor daily adherence to the protocol, and a consulting orthopedic physician will manage any adverse events. Baseline, post-intervention (at 4 weeks), and follow-up assessments (at 3 months and 6 months) will be performed by blinded outcome assessors. All outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study. After each assessment, coded outcome data will be securely stored and independently audited. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, meaning that participants will be analyzed in the groups to which they were initially assigned, regardless of protocol adherence or dropout. Specific methods for handling missing data (multiple imputation, sensitivity analyses) are detailed in Section 6.1.

Interventions

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) + McKenzie group (Experimental group)

Participants in the experimental group will receive a single ultrasound-guided PRP injection at the L5/S1 disc level, followed by a structured McKenzie exercise protocol. The PRP will be derived from 26 mL of the patient's own venous blood, processed using a double-spin centrifugation technique, resulting a 2–5 times baseline platelet concentration. Between 2 and 3 mL of PRP, combined with 0.5 mL of sterile saline, will be injected through a transforaminal route, with real-time ultrasound guidance, utilizing a 22-gauge Quincke Chiba spinal needle. The procedure will be carried out under sterile conditions by a skilled orthopedic interventionist [[36], [37], [38]].

The McKenzie therapy protocol includes repeated lumbar movements such as extension, flexion, lateral gliding, and rotation depending on the patient's directional preference. Supervised sessions will take place three times per week over 4 weeks, totaling 12 sessions, and will be conducted by physiotherapists certified in MDT. A structured home exercise program will also be prescribed for all patients, to be performed every 2–3 h during waking hours [39]. The detailed progression of the intervention is shown in Table 2.

McKenzie group (Control group)

Patients assigned to the control group will follow the McKenzie therapy protocol applied to the experimental group, except that PRP injections will be omitted. Outcome measurement

Primary outcomes

The co-primary outcome measures of this study are pain intensity and disc morphology at the L5/S1 level. Pain will be assessed using a 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Pressure Pain Thresholds (PPTs) obtained with a handheld algometer, whereas disc morphology will be evaluated by musculoskeletal ultrasonography (MSK-USG), focusing on disc height, echogenicity, and displacement. Neurophysiological function, evaluated using the Straight Leg Raise test, Electromyography, and Nerve Conduction Studies, will be considered a key secondary outcome. In confirmatory analysis, the two co-primary outcomes will be tested hierarchically, with VAS as the gatekeeper (see Section 6 for details). PPTs are a secondary supportive measure for pain and will not be included in the formal co-primary testing. To measure pain, the 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) will be utilized. This scale is a horizontal line, marked from 0 cm (representing no pain) to 10 cm (representing the worst imaginable pain), where patients will mark their current pain intensity. Validity of VAS ranging from 0.62 to 0.91 and test–retest reliability between 0.71 and 0.94 [41,42]. Pressure Pain Thresholds (PPTs) using a handheld algometer will be used as an objective tool. This tool has been found to have excellent intra-rater and inter-rater reliability in musculoskeletal conditions [43]. Disc morphology will be evaluated using musculoskeletal ultrasonography (MSK-USG), focusing on disc height, echogenicity, and displacement at the L5/S1 level. MSK-USG is a reliable, radiation-free modality for assessing disc integrity, with intra-rater reliability scores ranging from 0.82 to 0.90 [21,44]. Neurological function will be evaluated using the Straight Leg Raise (SLR) test, Electromyography (EMG), and Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS). The SLR test got a high sensitivity (91%) for detecting lumbosacral nerve root irritation [17]. EMG will be used to evaluate denervation and re-innervation in paraspinal and lower limb muscles, with a sensitivity of 79% and specificity of 88% in lumbar radiculopathy [45]. NCS will be used to measure the conduction velocity and latency of the sciatic and tibial nerves. It has high inter-rater reliability and proven diagnostic value in lumbar disc issues [22]. Given the slower time course of nerve regeneration, EMG and NCS parameters will be considered primarily exploratory at the 4-week assessment and confirmatory for medium-to long-term recovery at 3 and 6 months. These measures will be correlated with functional outcomes (ODI) and pain scores to explore mechanistic relationships between neurophysiological recovery and clinical improvement.

Secondary outcomes

Disability will be assessed as a secondary outcome in this study, alongside neurophysiological measures (SLR, EMG, NCS) that complement the co-primary endpoints of pain and disc morphology. To assess disability, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) will be used. This index consists of ten sections evaluating different daily life activities, including pain intensity, personal care, mobility, sitting, standing, sleeping, sexual activity, social interactions, and travel. Each section is scored from 0 to 5, with a maximum score of 50. The final disability percentage is calculated by doubling the total score. The disability levels are then classified as: 0–20% for minimal disability, 21–40% for moderate disability, 41–60% for severe disability, and 81–100% for patients who are bedridden or potentially overstating their symptoms. The ODI has demonstrated excellent reliability and validity in musculoskeletal studies and will serve as a robust indicator of functional improvement or deterioration following intervention [46].

Trial management

Trial oversight and monitoring

The Principal Investigator (PI) will oversee the overall trial monitoring process to ensure adherence to the approved protocol. A designated trial manager will coordinate daily implementation activities, and site coordinators will be responsible for routine participant engagement and protocol delivery. All complications, such as clinical deterioration, adverse reactions to PRP injections, or lack of response to McKenzie exercises, will be recorded and reported to the PI. Any case with worsening neurological symptoms will be marked for further evaluation. A separate analysis of treatment-related complications will be included in the final report. Safety data will be summarized descriptively by treatment group, including incidence rates of AEs by severity and relationship to intervention.

Safety measures and adverse Event monitoring

To ensure participant safety, all PRP injections and McKenzie therapy sessions will be conducted in accordance with established clinical guidelines [38,47]. Procedures will be performed by licensed orthopedic surgeons (for PRP injections) and certified McKenzie therapists (for physiotherapy). Standard sterile techniques, appropriate patient positioning, and real-time clinical monitoring will be employed throughout all interventions [file:1].

Cost analysis

This study is primarily self-funded by the researcher, with partial financial and logistical support from the Department of PTR, JUST. All participants will receive interventions at no commercial cost, with PRP injections, where applicable, provided at a research-subsidized rate under departmental facilitation. As a non-commercial academic trial, any treatment-related complications arising during the study will be evaluated and managed within the department's capacity. However, costs associated with external surgical referrals or unrelated complications will not be covered by the research team. The economic burden of each intervention, including materials, procedural costs, and session frequency, will be systematically monitored and reported in the final trial publication to provide comprehensive insights into cost-effectiveness.

Data audit and management

Data will be collected using printed questionnaires coded with patient-specific identification numbers to maintain confidentiality. These questionnaires will be securely stored and accessed only by the trial manager and data entry team. Statistical analysis will be conducted by an independent biostatistician, ensuring blinding throughout the process. Both printed and digital data will be preserved as intellectual property under the supervision of the PI and the academic research supervisor. All data handling will follow institutional research ethics and standard data protection regulations.

Data analysis

Data analysis will be performed using the SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences), version 23. Most of the outcome measures, such as VAS, PPTs, ODI, and quantitative data from MSK-USG, EMG, and NCS, are continuous data and will be treated accordingly. Descriptive statistics will be reported as means and standard deviations for data that follows a normal distribution, medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for skewed data, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (e.g., disc displacement type or SLR response). To assess baseline equivalence between groups, an independent samples t-test will be used for normally distributed data, or a Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data. For post-intervention analysis, comparisons between the experimental and control groups will follow the same procedure. Changes within groups from baseline to post-test and subsequent follow-ups will be evaluated using either a paired samples t-test (parametric) or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-parametric). A Repeated Measures ANOVA will be employed for parametric data to analyze changes across three time points (baseline, 4-week post-test, and follow-ups at 3 and 6 months), while Friedman's ANOVA will be used for non-parametric data. ITT approach will be applied to account for dropouts or loss to follow-up, with Bonferroni adjustments made for post-hoc tests to control for multiple comparisons. Baseline compatibility will be deemed acceptable if the p-value exceeds 0.05. The statistical significance for all post-treatment analyses will be set at p < 0.05. For confirmatory hypothesis testing, between-group differences in change in pain intensity (assessed by VAS and PPTs) and disc morphology parameters on musculoskeletal ultrasonography will constitute the co-primary efficacy analyses. Analyses of neurophysiological parameters and Oswestry Disability Index scores will be treated as secondary and interpreted in a supportive manner, with appropriate adjustments for multiple comparisons applied where required. EMG and NCS parameters will be analyzed as exploratory outcomes, with particular emphasis on 3- and 6-month changes given the slower pace of nerve regeneration. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients will be calculated to examine relationships between electrophysiological improvements (EMG amplitudes, NCS conduction velocities) and changes in primary outcomes (pain, disc morphology) and ODI scores. These correlations will provide mechanistic insights into the potential pathways linking structural disc changes to neurophysiological and functional recovery. Safety outcomes will be analyzed descriptively, reporting the incidence, severity, and suspected relationship to intervention for all adverse events by treatment group. Serious adverse events will be tabulated separately. To explore the potential impact of unblinded participant expectation, we will conduct a post-hoc sensitivity analysis comparing the treatment effect on VAS and ODI between patients who report high vs. low baseline expectations of PRP (assessed by a simple 5-point Likert question at enrolment). If expectation significantly modifies the outcome, this will be reported as evidence of bias.

Handling of co-primary outcomes and multiple testing

The trial includes two co-primary outcomes: (1) change in pain intensity measured by the 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and (2) change in disc morphology assessed by MSK-USG, with disc height specified as the primary morphological parameter (echogenicity and displacement as secondary).

To control the family-wise type I error rate at α = 0.05 (two-sided), a fixed sequential testing procedure will be applied. First, the between-group difference in change in VAS from baseline to the primary endpoint will be tested using an appropriate adjusted model. Only if this comparison is statistically significant (p < 0.05) will testing proceed to disc height, which will be analyzed using a similar approach. If the VAS outcome is not significant, disc morphology analyses will be considered exploratory.

Secondary outcomes (PPTs, ODI, SLR, EMG, and NCS) will be analyzed as supportive, hypothesis-generating measures, with appropriate caution regarding multiple comparisons.

This approach maintains overall type I error control. Given that the study is powered for VAS only, findings related to disc morphology will be interpreted cautiously and reported with corresponding effect sizes and confidence intervals.

Handling missing data and intention-to-treat analysis

All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, whereby participants will be analyzed according to their randomized group allocation regardless of protocol compliance, adherence, or subsequent interventions (including surgical referral).

Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), generating 20 imputed datasets assuming data are missing at random (MAR) conditional on observed baseline covariates and follow-up data. The following variables will be included in the imputation model: age, sex, baseline VAS, ODI, disc morphology parameters, EMG/NCS values, treatment adherence, and all available post-baseline outcome measures. Pooled estimates and 95% confidence intervals will be obtained using Rubin's rules.

Discussion and implications of the study

LDH typically affects individuals in their fourth to fifth decade of life, often during their most productive and active years [48]. This condition is a significant contributor to global disability, with symptoms ranging from axial low back pain to radiculopathy, neurological deficits, and loss of function [49]. Conservative treatments, including physiotherapy, medication, and rest, are considered first-line management, with reported recovery rates between 60 and 90% [50,51]. However, recurrence of symptoms and functional limitations is common, with post-surgical recurrence rates reported between 3 and 18% within a year [52]. In low-resource settings like Bangladesh, surgical accessibility remains limited, and physiotherapy-led care is often the most viable treatment option.

Despite the wide use of McKenzie therapy in LDH management, many patients experience partial relief, especially when disc pathology involves structural damage or inflammation. PRP therapy, known for its regenerative and anti-inflammatory potential, has shown promise in spinal disc repair [37,38], yet high-quality comparative trials examining PRP combined with physiotherapy versus physiotherapy alone are scarce. This trial, therefore, aims to address a critical practice and research gap by exploring whether the addition of ultrasound-guided PRP injection to McKenzie therapy enhances outcomes in L5/S1 disc herniation patients.

A major strength of this study lies in its rigorous RCT design, which will use block randomization, alongside allocation concealment and blinded assessment of outcomes. However, as a pragmatic trial, we did not blind participants or therapists. This limitation is addressed in detail in the Limitations section, and it substantially affects the interpretability of patient-reported outcomes. It is among the first control trials in Bangladesh to investigate the combined effect of PRP and structured McKenzie therapy, using objective outcome measures including VAS, PPTs, disc morphology on MSK-USG, and neurophysiological assessments (EMG and NCS). Pain intensity (VAS and PPTs as separate measures) and disc morphology have been designated as co-primary endpoints, while neurophysiological and disability outcomes are secondary, providing a focused hierarchy for confirmatory analyses. Importantly, this study is designed as a pragmatic comparison of care pathways rather than a mechanism-focused efficacy trial. In the absence of a sham-injection control, any observed superiority of the PRP-injection group cannot be definitively attributed to the specific biological effects of platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Such effects may instead reflect non-specific influences, including placebo responses, patient expectancy, and the contextual or procedural aspects of the injection intervention. Accordingly, the interpretation of findings will be framed in pragmatic terms. For example, conclusions will emphasize that “the addition of a PRP-injection pathway to McKenzie-based physiotherapy is associated with improved clinical outcomes,” while avoiding definitive causal claims regarding the intrinsic biological efficacy of PRP. This approach is consistent with the real-world clinical context of Bangladesh, where the primary question is whether incorporating a PRP injection—together with its associated non-specific effects—provides incremental value over physiotherapy alone, rather than isolating the biological effect of PRP in comparison to placebo. The use of standardized, validated assessment tools across multiple time intervals, such as baseline, post-treatment, 3-month, and 6-month follow-ups, improves reliability and enables longitudinal tracking of patient progress.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be considered. As a single-center trial conducted in an academic outpatient setting, external validity may be limited, and findings may not be immediately generalizable across broader populations. Additionally, PRP outcomes may vary depending on centrifugation methods, practitioner skill, and baseline disc health, which are variables difficult to standardize in real-world settings [37]. While EMG and NCS provide objective neurophysiological assessment, their sensitivity to detect meaningful changes at 4 weeks may be limited due to the slow pace of nerve regeneration; these measures will therefore be interpreted primarily as exploratory at early follow-up and confirmatory at 3 and 6 months. The absence of a sham-injection control represents an important limitation, as it precludes definitive attribution of any observed between-group differences to the specific biological effects of platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Accordingly, we have framed the study as a pragmatic comparison of two care pathways. Within this context, any conclusions will pertain to the effectiveness of a PRP-injection pathway in real-world clinical practice, rather than the efficacy of PRP as an isolated biological intervention.

The absence of blinding of both participants and physiotherapists, which introduces potential performance and detection bias. Participants in the PRP plus McKenzie group may have greater expectations of improvement due to receiving an invasive intervention, while therapists’ awareness of group allocation may subtly influence their interactions despite standardized protocols. These biases particularly affect patient-reported outcomes, including the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), which are highly susceptible to expectancy effects. Accordingly, any between-group differences in these measures cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. In contrast, objective outcomes—such as disc morphology assessed by musculoskeletal ultrasonography (MSK-USG) and electrophysiological parameters (EMG/NCS)—are relatively less influenced by such biases, although some dependence on patient cooperation remains (e.g., SLR). Therefore, primary interpretative weight will be given to the objective disc morphology outcome, while subjective measures will be considered secondary and interpreted with appropriate caution. The study is thus positioned to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of a PRP-injection care pathway rather than the isolated biological efficacy of PRP.

Furthermore, the trial was powered only for the pain outcome (VAS) because an MCID for disc morphology on MSK-USG is not established. Consequently, a non-significant result for disc morphology should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect; it may reflect insufficient statistical power.

Given the high burden of LDH in low-resource settings, this study is expected to inform evidence-based, cost-effective treatment protocols that may reduce the long-term need for surgery and improve quality of life for affected individuals. While this study provides a short-term analysis, its findings could serve as a foundation for future multicenter trials with longer follow-up periods and comparisons with surgical outcomes.
