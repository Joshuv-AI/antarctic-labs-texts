What is the impact of the chief resident designation on career direction: a cross-sectional analysis of a US database over 15 years
Authors: Tera Frederick Howard, Daniel Castro, Emily Hall, Miriam Alvarez, William Grobman
Published: 2025

Background

Academic medicine provides an infrastructure through which individuals are recruited, educated, and trained to become independent medical practitioners. At the end of training, graduates have a choice to make about their initial career path. Some may choose a fellowship, where they receive additional training and subspecialize, while others begin independent practice. Although there may be nuances within the types of practice settings, they may be generalized to two categories. In academic practice, individuals are employed by an institution that trains medical professionals, and typically incorporate teaching, research, or other forms of scholarship into their careers. In private practice, individuals are either self-employed or employed by a non-academic entity and typically focus primarily on patient care.

Physicians are leaving academic medicine at high rates, and it is unclear whether recruitment into academia will keep pace [1–3]. Despite prior research, conclusive answers to why, when and how physicians choose careers in academia do not exist [4]. Literature suggests, however, that interest in academic careers wanes during residency [5, 6]. Therefore, the examination of events and opportunities that occur in residency, including the Chief Resident (CR) opportunity, will add to the pre-existing understanding about the proximate step to academic career choice. The CR designation is usually bestowed upon senior residents who are perceived to have demonstrated the ability to thrive in the training environment. Although the CR role can have variable definitions (i.e. administrative, quality, education, outpatient) and responsibilities across specialties, the perception of honor associated with the designation is ubiquitous. Moreover, the chief year is the opportunity to hone administrative and teaching skills, which may be transferrable to a career in academic medicine [7]. The relationship between CR and career choice in academia is unclear. One regional study involving 545 Family Medicine residents found no difference in interest in faculty positions between CRs and other final year residents, while another study with 365 Emergency Medicine residents found a strong relationship between CR status and immediate post residency academic positions [8, 9].

Despite a recognition of the benefits of physician workforce diversity to the educational environment and for patient care, academic medicine has struggled to recruit and retain individuals who identify as racial and ethnic minorities. Black, Hispanic, and American Indians make up only 3.6%, 3.2%, and 0.2% of full-time United States (US) medical school faculty, respectively, which is an underrepresentation compared to both the general physician workforce and the US population [10]. Additionally, because of difficulty with retention, racial and ethnic minorities are underrepresented in higher echelons within academia, as indicated by even lower proportions of minoritized individuals with the rank of associate or full professor and who hold leadership positions such as department chair [11–14].

Given the unknown relationship between the CR designation and career choice, this study seeks to investigate whether the CR designation is associated with the type of intended employment immediately following residency training. Because the continued difficulty recruiting minoritized physicians into academia, this study seeks to investigate if the relationship between CR designation and employment choice varies by race-ethnicity.

Methods

We performed a cross-sectional analysis using individual-level de-identified data from the Graduate Medical Education (GME) Track database, which tracks residents across the United States and contains data from the National GME census. The National GME census is managed by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) and American Medical Association (AMA) and consists of surveys that are completed annually by residency program directors and institutional officials. There are two components to these surveys: the Resident Survey and the Program Survey. Response rates to the GME track are typically very high (e.g., 94.8% in 2017, 94.4% in 2018). The study population for this analysis includes residents in their final year of training in a US-based residency program from 2005 to 2020. Those with missing data were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, those in a non-residency program (e.g., a fellowship program) or a transitional year were excluded from the analysis as they were not eligible to be a CR (Fig. 1). The exposure variable was CR status (y/n). Because the survey question about CR is binary without qualifications, the responses could include CR of any type (e.g., administrative, quality, education, etc.). The outcome variable was intended employment type the following year (fellowship, academic, other, or private practice [referent]). Fellowship (y/n) was defined as the plan to enter another GME program (regardless of accreditation). Academic practice (y/n) was defined as the intent to enter a full-time academic position, part-time academic position, or a non-clinical research position. A resident was defined as having “other” plans (y/n) if they were entering a non-medical career (e.g., business), leaving the country, joining the military, not taking an employed position, or had not specified their intent. Residents were considered to enter private practice if they indicated that they were entering a non- academic US practice.

Potential confounding variables that were considered in the analysis included: citizenship (non-US citizen vs. US citizen), self-identified race-ethnicity (African American or Black; American Indian or Alaskan Native; Asian; Hispanic, Latino or of Spanish Origin; Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander or White, multiracial, or other than the listed categories), self-identified sex (male or female), Alpha Omega Alpha (AOA) status (member vs. nonmember), type of medical school (international medical school, DO granting, MD granting), US geographic region of the residency program (central, northeast, southern or western), and specialty type (surgical, non-surgical primary care, non-surgical specialty; see Appendix). For this analysis, those who indicated they belonged to more than one racial-ethnic group were categorized as “multiracial” and those who did not identify according to any of the given categories self-reported as “other”.

Multinomial logistic regression was used to estimate the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals for the association between designation as CR and career choice the following year after adjusting for potential confounding factors. We then evaluated, through interaction analyses, whether that association differed by racial-ethnic group. The University of Texas at Austin Institutional Review Board (IRB 00001017) reviewed the proposed study, determined it not to be human subjects research and thus deemed it exempt from review.

Results

Characteristics of the study population (N = 450,598) are described in Table 1. Approximately 54% self-identified as White and male. The vast majority (85%) were U.S. citizens, who graduated from MD granting medical schools (64%) and were not members of AOA (93%). The greatest proportion of residents were in non-surgical primary care training programs (45%) and the largest geographic region represented was the Northeast (35%). CRs accounted for 23% (N = 103,204) of the sample. Approximately 45% of the entire sample had plans for fellowship, 11.4% planned for academics, 12.6% had other plans, and the remainder expected to enter private practice. The rates of CR by race-ethnicity were 21%, 19%, 25%, 28%, 24% and 36% for Asian, Black, Hispanic, Multiracial, Other and White residents, respectively.

Overall, compared to those not selected as CR, CRs had an increased odds of pursuing academic positions (OR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.06), and a decreased odds of pursuing fellowship (OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.75–0.78) or other career paths (OR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.62–0.65) when compared to private practice (Table 2). In the interaction analysis, there was a difference by race-ethnicity in the relationship between CR and immediate post-residency plans (p < 0.0001). Discussion

In this large study of over 450,000 senior residents across 15 years, we found that the CR designation was associated with plans after graduation from a training program. Specifically, CRs had higher odds of pursuing academic careers compared to those who were not CR, but lower odds of pursuing fellowship or other career plans. Interestingly the relationship between CR and future plans differed by race-ethnicity, with Black, Asian, and multiracial residents with this designation specifically more likely to pursue academics positions than those without the designation in the same racial-ethnic categories.

While the CR selection process across specialty and institution is variable, the qualities identified as desirable in a CR like leadership, organizational skills and communication, are important to a career in academic medicine [15, 16]. As was found in our study, it makes sense that those who occupy the role would be more likely to enter academic medicine as a career path. Indeed, the responsibilities (e.g., administration, teaching) that are fostered during the appointment are likely transferable to the skills that assist in becoming a successful academic [17]. Moreover, CRs may have better access to mentorship from academic faculty, with greater exposure to program, departmental and sometimes hospital leadership [18].

Yet, research has shown a disparity in who has access to this opportunity [19]. In a study investigating race-ethnic and sex disparities in the selection of CR among a national sample of more than 5,000 obstetrics and gynecology residents, researchers found that Black senior residents were 21% less likely to be selected as CR than White residents, even after adjusting for markers of prior achievement like AOA status. White females were almost 4 times more likely to be selected as chief compared to Black males [20]. In a similar study investigating racial and sex differences in CR selection in Emergency Medicine, Black residents were half as likely as White residents to be selected [21]. In an analysis of over 120,000 residents across all specialties, researchers found that Black, Hispanic and Asian senior residents were 26%, 29% and 28%, less likely to be selected CR than their White counterparts [22].

These racial-ethnic disparities in selection of CR, particularly considering the findings of the present analysis, may influence the population that enters careers in academic medicine. In a study involving over 600 medical students representing over 100 medical schools, researchers found that Black and Hispanic medical students reported concern that they would have difficulty succeeding in academics [23]. As our study shows, the association between CR and choosing academics following residency is strongest for Black residents. As academic medicine grapples with underrepresentation of Black individuals among its faculty, critical examination of the CR role selection as an opportunity to diversify the pipeline is needed.

Despite being well-represented in the physician workforce, literature suggests that Asian physicians are less likely to be selected as CR and are less likely to occupy higher leadership positions in academia as faculty [22, 24]. In the present analysis, Asian residents with the CR designation were more likely to choose academics compared to non-chiefs in the same racial category. In addition to examining CR as an opportunity to recruit into academic medicine, exploration of the role as a pathway into academic leadership is needed, especially for Black and Asian groups.

Notably, our study found that CRs were less likely than those not selected as CR to pursue fellowship. For some programs in some specialties (e.g., Internal Medicine or Pediatrics), being CR requires an additional year beyond the standard training time. If someone is pursuing fellowship, the additional CR year may be regarded as an extra burden in terms of time and finances that trainees may be unwilling to bear. Conversely, individuals may perceive the chief year as a method to gain additional training and preparation for their chosen field without having to undergo a fellowship. For other procedure-based specialties (e.g., General Surgery and Obstetrics and Gynecology), being CR does not require an additional year and may help the resident secure competitive fellowship positions. More research is needed to unpack the factors associated with pursuit of fellowship beyond CR designation. Also, the relationship between fellowship and academic career choice is unknown and is beyond the scope of this present study.

Limitations to this study include that this is observational and thus we were only able to determine associations and not causality. Although this study demonstrates statistically significant findings that correspond with previous research regarding the relationship between race and ethnicity with academic prestige, the overall effect sizes we observed are small. As with all observational data, unmeasured confounding and the chance of omitted variable bias cannot be excluded. Moreover, although the variable “race-ethnicity” was by self-report, combining the two concepts of “race” and “ethnicity” may lose some nuance that examining them separately would provide. The study could also not differentiate between the types of CR (i.e. administrative, quality, education, outpatient). Despite this, the designation of CR of any type is widely regarded as an honor. Also, while some specialties colloquially refer to every final year resident as “chief,” there is a clear distinction between these roles in the GME Track database. For example, for the specialty of General Surgery, there are 33,347 final year residents. Of them, only 13,504 are designated as “chief resident” in the database. This is 40%. So while the GME track does not distinguish the type of chief resident (academic, scheduling, education, etc.), there is a difference between the chief role and a final year resident. In fact, there are no specialties in the database where all final year residents are noted as chief resident.

Another limitation is the clear variability across specialty types as to how they assign CR. We did include specialty in the multinomial analysis to account for this potential confounder; the results of the present analysis demonstrate that the relationship between CR and career plans was seen across all specialties. Lastly, this study accounts for career plans immediately post residency and not the entirety of career choices, including those made after fellowship which may or may not be associated with an academic practice setting. However, when considering the factors impacting one’s decision to enter academia, it is a reasonable assumption to make that one is more likely to be on an academic track long term if he or she chose it initially compared to someone who did not.

Conclusions

The results of this analysis suggest that the selection as CR has implications regarding future career choices and the impact varies by race-ethnicity. Research that should follow these study findings include qualitative research understanding reasons behind chosen career paths, larger studies investigating more about the CR selection process itself and studies examining if modifying the chief resident selection process mitigates bias.
