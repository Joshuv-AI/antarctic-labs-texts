Reply to Shugo Yajima, Soichiro Yoshida, and Yasuhisa Fujii’s Letter to the Editor re: Takuya Oishi, Tohru Yoneyama, Yuki Miura, et al. Integrated Assessment of Percentage α2,3-Linked Sialylated Prostate-specific Antigen and the Prostate Health Index with Magnetic Resonance Imaging for Detection of Clinically Significant Prostate Cancer. Eur Urol Open Sci 2026;83:158–65
Authors: Takuya Oishi, Tohru Yoneyama, Shingo Hatakeyama
Published: 2026

We thank the authors for their thoughtful commentary on our study evaluating percentage of α2,3-linked sialylated prostate-specific antigen (S2,3PSA%), Prostate Health Index (PHI), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings for predicting clinically significant prostate cancer (csPC) [1]. We agree that likelihood ratios provide a useful Bayesian framework for translating biomarker results into patient-level risk estimates and may complement conventional measures such as discrimination and calibration.

In our cohort, S2,3PSA% showed a sensitivity of 85.6% and a specificity of 33.6% for csPC, corresponding to a positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 1.29 and a negative likelihood ratio (LR−) of 0.43. PHI showed a sensitivity of 97.3% and a specificity of 22.6%, corresponding to an LR+ of 1.26 and an LR− of 0.12 [2]. Assuming a pretest probability of 30% in men with equivocal MRI findings, these likelihood ratios yield a posttest probability of approximately 36% after a positive result and 16% after a negative result for S2,3PSA%, and approximately 35% after a positive result and 5% after a negative result for PHI. These findings suggest that both biomarkers, particularly PHI, may contribute more to risk reduction after negative testing than to strong rule-in decisions.

However, likelihood ratios are context-dependent. Estimates from conventional biopsy cohorts may not directly apply to MRI-targeted biopsy populations, where Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) already provides structured pretest risk stratification and where disease spectrum differs. Challenges remain especially for PI-RADS 3 lesions. In our study, csPC detection in PI-RADS 3 was 15.3% and remained only 21.9% even when both biomarkers were positive (S2,3PSA% ≥38.0% and PHI ≥27.2). Thus, fixed universal cutoffs may be suboptimal; PI-RADS–specific thresholds warrant further study [3].

We therefore view likelihood-ratio–based interpretation as complementary to multivariable modeling rather than as an alternative. Our study was designed to evaluate a multivariable risk prediction model, not the standalone performance of individual biomarkers. In this framework, S2,3PSA% and PHI were incorporated as continuous predictors alongside MRI findings and age, allowing patient-specific risk estimation without relying solely on dichotomized thresholds. Such thresholds are useful for clinical communication, but they can obscure the graded information contained in continuous biomarkers.

Future prospective validation should integrate Bayesian risk updating, MRI-based pretest probability, and continuous biomarker values. This may help translate model-based prediction into clearer individualized counseling and more balanced biopsy decision-making.
