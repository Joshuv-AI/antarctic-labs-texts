Metabolic syndrome and associated factors among benign prostatic hyperplasia patients at Jimma University Medical center, Jimma, Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Hundesa Emana Urgesa, Zerihun Assefa Tereffe, Tesfaye Adugna Leta, Bedasa Addisu Nasi, Segni Gemechu Hurisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBenign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) involves the enlargement of epithelial and stromal cells of the prostate gland. Its prevalence is increasing owing in part to the rising rate of metabolic syndrome, which includes dyslipidemia, hypertension, hyperinsulinemia, and central obesity. Despite this trend, there is a paucity of data on BPH and metabolic syndrome in the Ethiopian population. This study aims to assess the prevalence and factors associated with metabolic syndrome among BPH patients in southwestern Ethiopia.Materials and methodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted with 158 BPH patients at Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC) from January 15 to April 15, 2024. Socio-demographic data were collected via a WHO stepwise questionnaire and trained nurses collected 5 ml fasting blood samples. The samples were consequently analyzed by trained laboratory technologists at JUMC.ResultsThe overall prevalence of metabolic syndrome among BPH patients was 38.6% (95% CI: 0.31,0.46). There were significant associations between factors such as age (AOR = 3.43, CI: 1.3, 9), residence (AOR = 3.35, CI:1.35,8.30), total cholesterol (AOR = 3.2, CI: 1.27,8.06), low LDL levels (AOR = 3.54, CI:1.08, 11.5 hyperuricemia (AOR = 3.21, CI:1.02,10.01), BMI (AOR = 3.47, CI:1.44,8.32), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) (AOR = 5.07, CI:2.15,11.98), and metabolic syndrome.ConclusionThe prevalence of metabolic syndrome among patients with BPH is notably high. The authors recommend the need for early assessment of risk factors and timely intervention to prevent potential complications.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Jimma, Benign prostate hyperplasia, Ethiopia, Associated factors

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a noncancerous growth of the prostate gland that affects the aging population [1]. It is associated with recurrent urinary tract infections, chronic kidney disease, and surgery-related complications. The disease affects approximately 94.4 million people [2]. In Ethiopia, affects around 18.9% [3]. Emerging research suggests that metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a significant risk factor for BPH [4, 5].

MetS is a cluster of interrelated health disorders that affect the cardiovascular and metabolic systems. It is an important public health concern, because of its association with several chronic diseases such as cardiovascular and underlying causes. These are myocardial infarction, stroke, and diabetic mellitus [6]. MetS is mainly characterized by a combination of high Triglyceride levels, low HDL, and cholesterol levels, abnormal blood sugar levels, high blood pressure, and obesity [7]. This syndrome, affects a significant portion of the global population, with an estimated prevalence of ∼ 1 billion worldwide, including a notable incidence in Ethiopia. Recently, lifestyle changes have led to an epidemiological transition in sub-Saharan Africa including Ethiopia, where the prevalence of MetS rose to 12.5% in 2011 [8].

Contemporary, in 2019, the prevalence of MetS was 20.3% [9], with a pooled prevalence of 34.89% [10], indicating a significant increase in the disease rate. The overall prevalence and number of deaths due to MetS are the highest in Mediterranean countries followed by nations with low sociodemographic indexes [11]. The exact pathophysiologic linkage between MetS and BPH is not well known, but the main hypothetical mechanism for this is, the growth growth-promoting effects of Hyperglycemia in this case, insulin is a mitogenic factor for prostate epithelial cells a key component of MetS [12, 13]. On the other hand, obesity causes the secretion of bioactive elements such as adipocytokines, which can induce insulin resistance [14].

Identification of the prevalence and associated factors with MetS among these patients has clinical importance in early identification of the risk factors, treatment, and prevention of disability, morbidity, and mortality. So far, several studies have been conducted. However, the majority of the studies didn’t address the prevalence as well as factors associated with MetS in old age groups such as BPH patients. In Ethiopia, no study has been conducted on similar areas. This study is mainly intended to bridge the gap regarding the prevalence and associated factors of metS among the study subjects which will be a valuable insight for future researchers, and clinical practitioners.

Methods

Study design, area, and period

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 158 BPH patients, from January 15 To April 15, 2024, at Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC) which is situated in the South-West region of Ethiopia. Jimma is one of the largest cities in the Oromia regional state, JUMC is the sole teaching hospital in the Southwest region serving over 15 million people. With a bed capacity of over 800, the hospital serves around sixty thousand inpatients and two hundred twenty thousand outpatients per year [15].

Population

Source population

All male populations with cases of BPH at Jimma University Medical Center during the study period.

Study population

All males aged 40 years and older who have been diagnosed with BPH.

Eligibility criteria

All patients with known BPH at JUMC during the study period who were aged above 40 years were included in the study this is due to the occurrence of BPH among those > 40 years. Patients with an ultrasound-proven prostate volume of less than 30 ml were also excluded from the study, it is due to the clinical diagnosis of BPH (enlarged prostate above 30 ml ). Since our study participants were only those patients with BPH, those with enlarged prostate, but suspected of having prostate cancer with ongoing examination were excluded from the study. Additionally, because their prostate volume was taken from the patient chart, those with the prostate volume on ultrasound examination unavailable in their medical charts were excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation and sampling procedures

The sample size was calculated by using the single population proportion formula by taking the previous prevalence of metabolic syndrome among BPHs which was 46.5% [16] considering a nonresponse rate of 5%. , n = 380 calculated sample size. N-is the total number of BPH patients attending the urology and surgical wards and surgical OPD within the last three months before data collection. P is the proportion of MetS in BPH while q is 1-P and 0.67 d is the margin of error which is 0.05. Since the target population is less than 10,000 we used the correction formula accordingly , is the final sample size, N= target population, By adding a 5% nonresponse rate, the final sample size (nf) becomes 158 participants.

Sampling procedures

Study participants were selected via consecutive sampling in which all available and accessible respondents who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were included.

Study variables

The outcome variables of this study encompass metabolic syndrome components such as high-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, fasting blood sugar, waist circumference, and blood pressure. The independent variables were age, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, uric acid level, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), smoking, alcohol consumption, khat use, educational level, and Residence.

Sociodemographic variables data collection

Ultrasound prostate volume data were collected from patients’ charts, other metabolic syndrome risk factor data were collected via WHO stepwise questionnaires. Data collection was carried out by trained professional nurses.

Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements

Waist and hip circumferences were measured via a nonelastic, flexible tape meter and the waist-to-hip ratio was calculated. The height of the study participants was measured with a calibrated stadiometer, while weight was recorded via a calibrated weight scale. Blood pressure was measured with a standard automatic sphygmomanometer, ensuring that patients had abstained from caffeine and heavy exercise before measurement.

Sample collection, lipid profile, serum uric acid, and fasting blood sugar measurements

A 5 ml blood sample was collected by trained nurses after overnight fasting in a test tube. The sample was transferred to the chemistry laboratory of JUMC, and centrifuged after 30 min of clot formation using a 23i centrifuge. The separated serum was then analyzed for lipid profile, serum uric acid, and fasting blood sugar. Following daily quality control and calibration, analysis was performed via Cobas 6000 chemistry analyzer. The enzymatic method was used for blood glucose analysis, an enzymatic colorimetric method for the analysis of LDL, cholesterol, Triglyceride (TG) levels, total cholesterol, and HDL levels, and the uricase method for measuring uric acid levels.

The revised NCEP/ATP III 2005, criteria were used to determine the prevalence of metabolic syndrome among BPH patients. According to these criteria, a patient to have metabolic syndrome if they are confirmed with three of the following five conditions: TG ≥ 150 mg/dl or medication for dyslipidemia, HDL < 40 mg/dl or on medication for dyslipidemia, WC > 40 inches, FBS ≥ 100 mg /dl or on medication and BP > 130/88 mmHG or on medication [17].

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into epi data version 4.6.0.6, cleaned, coded, and imported into SPSS version 26 for analysis via logistic regression. The NCEP/ATPIII 2005 criteria were used to determine metabolic syndrome. All assumptions of logistic regression were checked, and subsequent Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. In Bivariate analysis, A P value of 0.25 was considered the threshold for statistical significance, with all values below this level included in the multivariate logistic regression. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the final analysis and discussion.

Ethical consideration

The study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration, ensuring that informed oral consent was obtained from each study participant, who was fully informed about the purpose of the study. The right of The participants to withdraw from the study without any impact on their treatment or their privacy was fully respected.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents

Among the 158 BPH patients, the mean age was 65.57 ± 11.28 years; 87 (55.1%) were from rural areas. Concerning their occupation, 86 (54.4%) were farmers, 17 (10.8%) were government employees, 11 (7%) were nongovernment employees, 34 (20.9%) worked in private business, and 10 (6.3%) were retired from their job. Regarding their religion, the majority of the respondents were Muslims 105 (66.4%), followed by Orthodox Christians 33 (20.8%), and Protestants 20 (13.1%). Furthermore, 75 (47.5%) were illiterate, 27 (17.1%) had primary education, 15 (9.5%) had secondary education and 17 (10.8%) had attended college and above. The study revealed that 14 (8.9%) of the participants were alcohol drinkers, 28 (17.7%) were former smokers and 15 (9.5%) were current smokers. Most of the participants had no habitual heavy physical activity, with 130 (82.3%) participating in simple physical activity and 115 (72.8%) engaged in basic physical activity (Table 1).

Biochemical results of the study participants

Our results indicate that 66 (41.8%) of the participants had HDL < 40 mg /dl. Prevalence of metabolic syndrome

The overall prevalence of metabolic syndrome was consistent with the criteria outlined in the National Cholesterol Education Program/Adult Treatment Panel III. In our study, 38.6% of the participants with 95% CI (0.31,0.46) manifested at least three components of MetS (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The finding of the study underscores the prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) among BPH patients was 38.6% with 95% CI (0.31, 0.46), which is comparable to the findings from a study conducted in India but lower than the study reported in France and Syria [16, 18]. The most prominent factor associated with metabolic syndrome in our study was low HDL levels. Our study revealed that those aged ≥ 60 years have odds of 3.43 times more likely to contribute to metabolic syndrome than their counterparts (AOR = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.3, 9). This finding is supported by other community-based studies [19, 20]. We speculate that older age is often associated with reduced physical activity, which contributes to the development of MetS. More importantly, aging is associated with mitochondrial oxidative stress and the production of reactive species (REF), which can impair insulin uptake in muscle and adipose tissue with subsequent insulin resistance [21, 22]. It has also been reported that aging is associated with low-grade inflammation Which causes disturbance in conventional metabolic processes and promotes the development of insulin resistance [23].

The results of our study demonstrated that urban residents have a 3.35 times higher risk of developing MetS than rural residents (AOR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.35, 8.3). A finding is supported by a previous meta-analysis [24]. This might be due to several factors including lifestyle differences, with urban residents having relatively lower levels of physical activity, higher stress levels, and exposure to pollution as compared to their rural counterparts. Increased physical activity is known to increase HDL levels suggesting that lower physical activity levels in urban areas may contribute to the higher prevalence of MetS [25]. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that urban dwellers are more likely to seek medical attention more frequently than rural residents are, in part because of accessibility to medical facilities, which means that conditions such as metabolic syndrome may be diagnosed more frequently.

Another substantial finding in this research is that elevated total cholesterol was significantly associated with MetS. Patients who had increased total cholesterol (≥ 200 mg/dl ) were 3.2 times prone to MetS. (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.27, 8.06). Higher cholesterol levels contribute to the development of atherosclerotic plaques in endothelial cells, which can impede the flow of blood and lead to hypertension [26]. Additionally, elevated total cholesterol has a positive effect on central obesity and indirectly reduces HDL levels which is known for its good cholesterol with the reduction role in the development of MetS [27].

Elevated LDL was also found to be significantly associated with MetS. It was observed LDL ≥ 130 mg/dl has a 3.54 times risk of causing MetS. (AOR = 3.54,95% CI:1.08,11.56 ). Increased level of this lipo-protein dysregulates insulin signaling pathways and promotes insulin resistance. Moreover, as LDL accumulates in blood vessels, becomes oxidized; and inflamed and contributes to plaque formation with debilitating effects on blood flow through blood vessels [28].

Both BMI and the waist-to-hip ratio are significantly associated with the risks of MetS with a higher odds ratio observed for the waist-to-hip ratio. (AOR = 3.47, 95% CI: 1.44, 8.32) And (AOR = 5.07, 95% CI: 2.15, 11.98) respectively. This finding is supported by another previous study [29]. Visceral adiposity, which is closely linked to the waist-to-hip ratio, is known to disrupt glucose Metabolism [30]. Moreover, central adipose tissue releases proinflammatory cytokines, leading to Low-grade inflammation [27, 31].

Generally, the mechanistic connection between components of metS and BPH lies, in hyperglycemia there is an increase in free calcium to smooth muscle cells and neural tissue which causes sympathetic nervous activation. The insulin growth factor pathway contributes to the association between BPH and insulin resistance. Insulin has structural similarity with insulin growth factor 1, and insulin combines with its receptor [32]. This ends up in complex pathways and prostate tissue growth and proliferation [33]. Another phenomenon between MetS and BPH is inflammation due to components of metS, the components cause the release of cytokines which ultimately result in prostate tissue remodeling and growth [34, 35].

It was observed that serum uric acid is significantly associated with MetS. Those patients having serum uric acid ≥ 7 mg/dl have 3.21 times the risk of owing Mets. (AOR = 3.21, 95% CI: 1.02, 10.01) This idea is supported by previous studies [36–38]. Hyperuricemia is known to induce low-grade inflammation and endothelial dysfunction and decrease nitric oxide production [39]. Another possibility is through increased GLUT9 transporter, and urate transporter 1 expression as a result of a disturbance in glucose hemostatic and insulin resistance resulting in MetS [39].

One of the limitations of this study is its cross-sectional design, which makes temporal relationships impractical. Despite these drawbacks, our research adds valuable insights into the literature by providing the first prevalence data on MetS in the study area and exploring the potential correlation between uric acid levels and MetS.

Conclusions

Our study revealed a notably high overall prevalence of metabolic syndrome 38.6%. Significant risk factors for this include age, urban residence, high cholesterol, elevated LDL, increased uric acid levels, BMI, WHR, and hyperuricemia. These findings underscore the urgent need for early identification of BPH patients at risk for metabolic syndrome. Proactive management and regular assessment of uric acid levels in patients with metabolic risk factors are essential to prevent complications and improve patient outcomes.
