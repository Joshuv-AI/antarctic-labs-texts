A linear test approach to global controllability of third- and fifth-order nonlinear dispersive equations Thanks: Department of Mathematics & Statistics, Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Kolkata, Mohanpur, Nadia, West Bengal- 741246, India. Email address: wrmarit@gmail.com
Debanjit Mondal

Abstract
. We investigate third- and fifth-order nonlinear dispersive equations of KdV type on the torus and establishes approximate controllability by a fixed four-dimensional control; rather than relying solely on the saturation machinery, the analysis exploits the finite-dimensional controllability of the inviscid Burgers equation linearized around a carefully constructed return trajectory, with the trajectory itself obtained from an observable family. This “linear test” strategy, yields more information about the structure of the control than the standard approach. In particular, the constructed control is shown to depend continuously on the initial and target states, a property that is by no means automatic in nonlinear control problems, and to decompose as a bounded linear operator applied to the data plus a fixed remainder, with the operator part interestingly independent of the order of dispersion.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model Description

In this article, we study the dynamics of nonlinear dispersive waves described by Korteweg–de Vries (KdV)-type equations posed on the one-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. More precisely, we consider the evolution equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+(-1)^{j+1}\partial_{x}^{2j+1}u+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(u^{2})=0,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T},

where
j\in\{1,2\}
and
u=u(t,x)
is a real-valued function. This equations includes two classical dispersive models: for
j=1
, (
1.1
) reduces to the celebrated Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation, while for
j=2
it corresponds to the Kawahara equation.

The KdV equation was derived by Korteweg and de Vries
[
KDV95
]
in 1895 as a model for the propagation of surface water waves in a channel. Later, Kawahara
[
Kaw72
]
in 1972 studied a fifth-order dispersive equation and, through numerical simulations, showed that it admits solitary wave solutions. As in the KdV case, these solutions can be either compressive or rarefactive, depending on the sign of the dispersive term.

1.2.
The control problem

We study the controllability of the KdV-type equation:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+(-1)^{j+1}\partial_{x}^{2j+1}u+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(u^{2})=\eta(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{T},\\
u(0,x)=u^{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where
T>0
,
u^{0}
denotes the initial data, and the external force
\eta
serves as the control input.

To formalize, we introduce the notion of approximate controllability for the system.

Definition 1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
\mathcal{G}
a vector space. The equation (
1.2
) is said to be approximately controllable in
H
with
\mathcal{G}
-valued controls if, for any given
T>0
,
\varepsilon>0
, and
u^{0},u^{1}\in H
, there exist a control
\eta
taking values in
\mathcal{G}
and a corresponding solution
u
of (
1.2
) such that

\|u(T)-u^{1}\|_{H}\leq\varepsilon.

Compared to classical boundary and distributed control strategies, finite-dimensional controls have recently attracted significant attention. This work, we focus on the case where
\mathcal{G}
is a finite-dimensional space.

Considering

\mathcal{G}:=\operatorname{span}\{\sin(x),\cos(x)\},

for the KdV equation (
j=1
),
[
Che23
]
established approximate controllability, while for the Kawahara equation (
j=2
), approximate controllability was established in
[
AM25
]
, in both cases in the sense of Definition
1
.

In this article, we establish approximate controllability of (
1.2
) by exploiting
the finite-dimensional controllability of the inviscid Burgers system, linearized around a suitably constructed return trajectory
. This approach gives some new information about the control structure. For this framework, we require the finite-dimensional control space to be

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\operatorname{span}\{\sin(x),\cos(x),\sin(2x),\cos(2x)\}.

To formulate our main result, we first introduce the relevant function spaces.

1.3.
Function spaces

Equations of the form (
1.1
) possess the conservation of mass, energy, and a Hamiltonian structure. These invariants play a central role in the qualitative and quantitative analysis of nonlinear dispersive equations, particularly in the study of well-posedness, stability, and long-time behavior of solutions.

In view of mass conservation, it is natural to restrict the analysis of (
1.1
) to the subspace of mean-zero functions. Accordingly, we consider initial data in the mean-zero Sobolev space

H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}):=\left\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}):[u]=\int_{\mathbb{T}}u(x)\,dx=0\right\},

where
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
denotes the standard Sobolev space of order
s\in\mathbb{N}
on the torus
\mathbb{T}
. We denote the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
-norm by
\|\cdot\|
, and the
H^{s}
-norm by
\|\cdot\|_{s}
.

1.4.
Main result

We are now in a position to announce the main result of this article.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
(j,s)\in\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{N}
, the equation (
1.2
) is approximately controllable in
H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
at small time using a control that takes values in
\mathcal{H}
. That is, for any
T>0,
any initial state
u^{0}\in H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
and target state
u^{1}\in H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a sufficiently small
\tau>0
and a control function
\eta_{\tau,j}\in L^{2}((0,T\tau);\mathcal{H})
such that a solution
u
of (
1.2
) satisfies

(1.4)

\displaystyle u_{j}(T\tau)\to u^{1}\quad\text{in }H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\text{ as }\tau\to 0^{+}.

Furthermore, the control
\eta_{\tau,j}
can be expressed as

(1.5)

\displaystyle\eta_{\tau,j}=\mathcal{C}_{\tau}(u^{0},u^{1})+\kappa_{\tau,j},

where

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\times H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}((0,T\tau);\mathcal{H})

is a bounded linear operator with a finite-dimensional range, and
\kappa_{\tau,j}\in L^{2}((0,T\tau);\mathcal{H})
. Importantly, both
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
and
\kappa_{\tau,j}
are independent of the specific choices of
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
. Limit (
1.4
) is uniform with respect to
u^{0}
and
u^{1}
in a bounded set of
H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}).

As a direct consequence of the Main Theorem, we also establish approximate controllability in a given time.

Corollary 1.1
.

For any
(j,s)\in\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{N}
, the equation (
1.2
) exhibits global approximate controllability in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
at any given time
T>0
. That is, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any initial and target states
u^{0},u^{1}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a control function
\eta_{j}\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})
such that the corresponding solution
u
of (
1.2
), satisfies

\|u_{j}(T)-u^{1}\|_{s}\leq\varepsilon.

1.5.
Main contributions of the present work

The global controllability result is of significant importance for nonlinear partial differential equations, since it imposes no restrictions on the amplitude of the initial and final states or on the control time
T
. In comparison with
[
Che23
,
AM25
]
, we also establish global approximate controllability using finite-dimensional controls, where the dimension of the control space is independent of both the order of the dispersive term and the choice of initial and final data. This article provides some new information about the structure of the control. The control, given in the form (
1.5
), is uniform with respect to the choice of initial and target states. Moreover, the bounded operator part
C_{\tau}
of the control is independent of the order of dispersion. In addition, the constructed control depends continuously on the initial and final states, a property that is not immediate in the context of nonlinear control problems. Such conclusions do not follow directly from the results in
[
Che23
,
AM25
]
.

1.6.
Literature on dispersive equations

1.6.1.
Well-posedness

Local well-posedness for a class of dispersive equations with more general nonlinearities was studied in
[
KPV94
]
using Bourgain spaces, introduced in
[
Bou93a
,
Bou93b
]
. This approach was further developed in
[
KPV96
]
and
[
Tao01
]
. The global well-posedness of (
1.1
) for
j=1
and
j=2
was established in
[
CKS+03
]
and
[
Kat12
]
, respectively.

1.6.2.
Controllability

Regarding control theory, when
j=1,
the system (
1.2
) exhibits good control properties. The study of controllability for the KdV equation dates back to the late 1980s with the works
[
Rus91
]
and
[
Zha90
]
. Since then, the controllability of the KdV equation has been extensively investigated in the literature; we briefly recall some of these results. For internal controllability of the KdV equation on a periodic domain, we refer to
[
RZ93
,
RZ96
,
LRZ10
]
. In addition,
[
CFPR15
]
studied the null controllability of the KdV equation on a finite domain. In both cases, the control region is an arbitrary open subdomain. Concerning boundary controllability, when the left control input is active,
[
Ros04
]
proved that the KdV equation is locally exactly trajectory controllable; this result was later improved in
[
GG09
]
in terms of regularity of the trajectory. If the first spatial derivative at the right endpoint is taken as the control,
[
Ros97
]
established local exact controllability of the KdV equation provided that the length of the spatial domain does not belong to a critical set. Subsequently,
[
CC04
,
Cer07
,
CC09
]
obtained local exact controllability results on critical domains. More recently, local null-controllability of the nonlinear KdV equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions, using a Neumann boundary control on the right, was established in
[
CKN24
]
, while local controllability of the KdV equation with a right Dirichlet control was studied in
[
Ngu25
]
. For a comprehensive overview of controllability results for the KdV equation, we refer to the survey by Cerpa
[
Cer14
]
and the references therein.

Considering
j=2
, the system (
1.2
) corresponds to the so-called Kawahara equation. To the best of our knowledge, the control problem for this equation was first studied in
[
ZZ12
,
ZZ15
]
, where the authors considered the Kawahara equation on the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}
with a distributed control. In
[
ACFD12
]
, the stabilization problem and the critical length phenomenon for the Kawahara equation were investigated. Furthermore, local and global controllability results for a general higher-order KdV-type operator posed on the unit circle are presented in
[
dACKV22
]
. More recently,
[
ADR26
]
proved that local exact controllability of the Kawahara equation holds provided that the length of the spatial interval does not belong to a countable set of critical lengths.

1.7.
Methodology and related literature

1.7.1.
Controllability of partial differential equations (PDEs) by finite-dimensional control force

The controllability of nonlinear PDEs under the action of an additive finite-dimensional forcing has attracted significant attention in recent years. This line of research was initiated in
[
AS05
,
AS06
]
, where approximate controllability results for the two-dimensional Navier–Stokes and Euler equations on the torus were established. Since then, this framework has become widely known as the Agrachev–Sarychev approach, and their methodology has been successfully adapted to a broad class of nonlinear PDEs. It has been applied, for instance, to the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations
[
Shi06
,
Shi07
]
, as well as to both compressible and incompressible Euler systems
[
Ner10
,
Ner11
]
. Further developments include applications to the viscous Burgers equation
[
Shi14
,
Shi18
]
, the Schrödinger equation
[
Sar12
]
, and, more recently, to the semiclassical cubic Schrödinger equation, where small-time approximate controllability of quantum density and momentum has been established
[
CXZ23
]
. The technique has also been extended to parabolic systems with polynomial nonlinearities
[
Ner21a
]
, through the analysis of suitable asymptotic limits. Using this asymptotic limit approach, global approximate controllability with finite-dimensional controls has been established for a variety of models, including the Camassa–Holm equation
[
CDM25
]
, the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation
[
Jel23
]
, and the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation
[
Gao22
]
.

1.7.2.
Return method

The return method, originally introduced in
[
Cor92
]
for stabilization problems, has since become a powerful tool in the study of control theory for nonlinear PDEs. Its first major application to global exact boundary controllability was demonstrated in
[
Cor96a
]
for the two-dimensional incompressible Euler system. This method was further extended to various fluid models: in
[
CF96
]
, it was employed to establish global exact controllability to trajectories for the 2D Navier–Stokes system without boundary, and in
[
Cor96b
]
, to prove approximate controllability of the 2D Navier–Stokes system with Navier slip boundary conditions, using controls localized in physical space or along the boundary. Subsequent developments include its application to the 3D Euler equations in
[
Gla00
]
, and to the 3D Boussinesq system in
[
FI99
]
, where global exact controllability to trajectories was addressed. Years later, this method was adapted to both nonviscous and viscous Burgers-type equations
[
Cha09b
]
, as well as to the global controllability of a nonlinear KdV equation
[
Cha09a
]
, where the author used the controllability of the nonviscous Burgers equation to obtain controllability results for the KdV equation. For a overview of the return method in control theory, we refer
[
Cor07
, Chapter 6]
and the references therein.

1.7.3.
Our approach

The methodology of this work is primarily inspired by
[
Ner21b
]
, where approximate controllability of the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations was established using a combination of the return method and the notion of observable families. We also draw motivation from
[
Cha09a
]
, where global controllability of a nonlinear KdV equation was obtained via the controllability of the inviscid Burgers equation. In the present work, we adapt the approach of
[
Ner21b
]
by combining it with the return method developed in
[
Cha09a
]
within a finite-dimensional control framework. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the Agrachev–Sarychev framework, combined with the return method, to this class of nonlinear dispersive equations.

The main idea of the proof can be outlined as follows. We start with the inviscid Burgers equation (
2.4
). A suitable finite-dimensional return trajectory
w(t)
(cf. (
3.9
)) is constructed, which starts and ends at the origin over a fixed time interval
[0,T]
, and is associated with a control taking values in a finite-dimensional subspace. The equation is then linearized around this trajectory, and
v(t)
denotes the solution to the resulting linearized system (
2.5
), driven by another control that also takes values in the same finite-dimensional space. During the construction of
w(t)
, the time-dependent components (see (
3.9
)) are chosen to form an observable family (see Definition
3
). This observability ensures that the time-dependent linearized system (
2.5
) is approximately controllable using controls from the same finite-dimensional subspace. A control is then constructed such that, for each
j\in\{1,2\}
, the corresponding solutions
u_{j}(T\tau)
and
v(T)
, issued from the same initial data, remain close in the
H^{s}
-norm as
\tau\to 0^{+}
. The approximate controllability of the linearized system, together with the notion of an approximate right inverse, allows one to conclude the limit relation (
1.4
), with the control explicitly constructed in the form (
1.5
). Finally, the operator
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
serves as an approximate right inverse to the resolving operator of the linearized Burgers system, and
\kappa_{\tau,j}
is explicitly expressed in terms of the trajectory
w
and the corresponding control
\xi
.

1.8.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
, we begin by recalling the well-posedness results for the KdV-type equation and introduce our idea of the linear test. In Section
3
, we study the controllability of a linear transport equation with a specific time-space dependent coefficient using finite-dimensional controls. Section
4
is devoted to establishing the closeness of solutions to the linearized equation and the KdV-type equation. Finally, in Section
5
, we complete the proof of the Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.1
.

2.
Existence and properties

We rewrite the system (
1.2
) as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}_{j}u+\mathcal{B}(u)=f,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{T},\\
u(0,x)=u^{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where the linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{j}
is given by

\mathcal{L}_{j}:=(-1)^{j+1}\partial_{x}^{2j+1},\quad\text{for all }j\in\{1,2\},

and the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{B}
is defined as

\mathcal{B}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(u^{2}).

we recall an existence result for the problem (
2.1
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(j,s)\in\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{N}
, and suppose
T>0
,
u^{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
, and
f\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}))
are given. Then the system (
2.1
) admits a unique solution in
C([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{T}))
.

For each
j
, let
\mathcal{R}_{j}
be the operator that maps the initial condition
(u^{0},f)
to the corresponding solution
u
of (
2.1
). The solution evaluated at time
t
is represented by
\mathcal{R}_{j,t}(u^{0},f)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
T>0
, and
f\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}))
. For any initial data
u^{0},v^{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying
[u^{0}]=[v^{0}]
, there exists a constant
C(\|u^{0}\|_{s},\|v^{0}\|_{s})>0
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\mathcal{R}_{j,t}(u^{0},f)-\mathcal{R}_{j,t}(v^{0},f)\|_{s}\leq C\Big(\|u^{0}-v^{0}\|_{s}\Big),\quad\forall j\in\{1,2\}.

The proofs of Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
for
j=1,2
were carried out in
[
Che23
,
AM25
]
, respectively.

Building on the strategy developed in
[
Ner21b
]
, we aim to establish the approximate controllability of KdV-type equations of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}_{j}u+\mathcal{B}(u)=\eta

by reducing the problem to the controllability of a linearized system derived from the inviscid Burgers equation. As a starting point, we examine the inviscid Burgers equation given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+\mathcal{B}(w)=\xi,

and analyze its linearization along a solution trajectory

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{Q}(v,w)=g,

which defines a time-dependent linear system. Here, the bilinear operator
\mathcal{Q}(v,w)
is defined by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(v,w):=w\partial_{x}v+v\partial_{x}w.

The control functions
\eta,\xi,g
are assumed to take values in a common finite-dimensional subspace of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.

3.
Transport equation with finite-dimensional control

We shall obtain in this section a controllability result for linear transport equations of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{Q}(v,w)=g,

where
g
is a finite-dimensional control and
w
denotes a suitably constructed return trajectory, as defined below in Section 3.2.

3.1.
Saturation

Given a subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\text{span}\{\sin(x),\cos(x)\},

we define a nested chain of subspaces as follows:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{V}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{\psi_{1}+\mathcal{Q}(\psi_{2},\phi)\ :\ \psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{k-1},\ \phi\in\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\},\ k\geq 1,\text{ and }\mathcal{V}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{k}

where
\mathcal{Q}
is the bilinear form defined in (
2.6
). From this construction, it is clear that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0}\subset\mathcal{V}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{V}_{n}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{V}_{\infty}.

Definition 2
.

A space
\mathcal{V}
is said to be saturating if
\mathcal{V}_{\infty}
is dense in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
for all
s\geq 0
.

Proposition 3.1

(Density)
.

The spaces
\mathcal{V}_{n}
satisfy the following properties:

(i)

For
n\geq 1
,
\mathcal{V}_{n}
is a vector space of dimension
2(n+1)
. Specifically,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{\sin(x),\cos(x),\dots,\sin((n+1)x),\cos((n+1)x)\}.

(ii)

The space
\mathcal{V}
is saturating, that is,
\mathcal{V}_{\infty}
is dense in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction. Before proceeding with the induction step, we first derive an identity using trigonometric formulas. For
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
and
k,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\big(a\sin(mx)+b\cos(kx),\,c\sin(x)+d\cos(x)\big)=\frac{a}{2}(m+1)\Big[c\sin((m+1)x)+d\cos((m+1)x)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{b}{2}(k+1)\Big[c\cos((k+1)x)-d\sin((k+1)x)\Big]+\frac{a}{2}(m-1)\Big[d\cos((m-1)x)-c\sin((m-1)x)\Big]

(3.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{b}{2}(1-k)\Big[c\cos((k-1)x)-d\sin((k-1)x)\Big].

Using this identity, the desired result follows by induction. We omit the details, as the argument proceeds in the same spirit as in
[
CDM25
]
.
∎

3.2.
Return trajectories obtained from observable families

We recall the notion of an
observable family
, introduced in
[
KNS20
]
in the study of ergodic properties of randomly forced partial differential equations. This concept was later applied in
[
Ner21b
]
to the controllability of the Navier–Stokes equations.

Definition 3
.

A finite collection
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
is said to be an observable family if the following property holds: for every subinterval
J\subset[0,T]
, and for any function
b\in C^{0}(J;\mathbb{R})
together with functions
a_{i}\in C^{1}(J;\mathbb{R})
, the condition

(3.6)

\displaystyle b(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(t)\varphi_{i}(t)=0\quad\text{in }L^{2}(J;\mathbb{R})

implies that
b\equiv 0
and
a_{i}\equiv 0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
on
J
.

Remark 1
.

As explained in
[
Ner21b
, Section 3.3]
, see also
[
KNS20
]
, one can construct observable families in the sense of Definition
3
. For another explicit construction of such functions, see
[
Rud76
, Remark 4.31]
.

Construction of return trajectory
. Here we prove the following proposition

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
T>0
and let
\mathcal{H}
be the subspace defined in (
1.3
). Then there exist a control function
\xi\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})
and a corresponding solution

w\in W^{1,2}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}))

to equation (
2.4
) such that

(3.7)

\displaystyle w(0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad w(T)=0,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{j}w(t)\in\mathcal{H},\qquad t\in[0,T],\;j\in\{1,2\}.

Proof.

For
T>0
, we fix an observable family
\{\varphi_{s},\varphi_{c}\}\subset L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
and choose

\Theta:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
function such that
\Theta(t)=0
if and only if
t=T
. Define

\vartheta_{s}(t)=\Theta(t)\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}(\rho)\,d\rho,\qquad\vartheta_{c}(t)=\Theta(t)\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{c}(\rho)\,d\rho,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Next, define

(3.9)

\displaystyle w(t,x):=\vartheta_{s}(t)\sin x+\vartheta_{c}(t)\cos x.

Set

\xi:=\partial_{t}w+\mathcal{B}(w).

We claim that the pair
(w,\xi)
satisfies the desired properties.

First, since

\vartheta_{s}(0)=\vartheta_{s}(T)=\vartheta_{c}(0)=\vartheta_{c}(T)=0,

it follows immediately that

w(0)=w(T)=0,

so condition (
3.7
) holds.

Moreover, since
\vartheta_{s},\vartheta_{c}\in W^{1,2}_{0}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
, we have

w\in W^{1,2}_{0}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})).

Next, recall that the space
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is invariant under the operators
\mathcal{L}_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,2\}
. Since
w(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
for
t\in[0,T]
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}_{j}w(t)\in\mathcal{V}_{0}\subset\mathcal{V}_{1}=\mathcal{H}\qquad\text{for }t\in[0,T],\;j\in\{1,2\}.

Hence condition (
3.8
) is satisfied.

It remains to verify that
\xi\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})
. By construction, we have

\partial_{t}w\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H}).

We now examine the nonlinear term
\mathcal{B}(w)
. Since

w(t,x)=\vartheta_{s}(t)\sin x+\vartheta_{c}(t)\cos x,

we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(w)

\displaystyle=w\,w_{x}

\displaystyle=\left(\vartheta_{s}(t)\sin x+\vartheta_{c}(t)\cos x\right)\partial_{x}\left(\vartheta_{s}(t)\sin x+\vartheta_{c}(t)\cos x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta_{s}^{2}(t)-\vartheta_{c}^{2}(t)}{2}\sin(2x)+\vartheta_{s}(t)\vartheta_{c}(t)\cos(2x).

Therefore,

\mathcal{B}(w)\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}).

Consequently,

\xi=\partial_{t}w+\mathcal{B}(w)\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H}),

which completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Existence of finite-dimensional control

Given
T>0,\ w\in W_{0}^{1,2}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}))
consider the equation

(3.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{Q}(v,w)=g,\\
v(0)=0.\end{cases}

Proposition 3.3
.

The equation (
3.10
) is approximately controllable in time
T>0
using controls taking values in
\mathcal{H}
. More precisely, for every target state
v^{1}\in H^{s}_{0}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a control input
g\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})
such that the solution
v\in C([0,T];H^{s}_{0})
of (
3.10
), satisfies

\|v(T)-v^{1}\|_{H^{s}}\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

This approach is inspired by the work of Nersesyan
[
Ner21b
]
, where approximate controllability of the 3D Navier–Stokes system is established. We denote by

\mathbf{R}(t,\delta):H^{s}_{0}\to H^{s}_{0},\qquad v_{0}\mapsto v(t),\quad 0\leq\delta\leq t\leq T,

the associated two-parameter resolving operator for the problem

(3.11)

\displaystyle\dot{v}+\mathcal{Q}(w,v)=0,\quad v(\delta)=v^{0}.

Consequently, the resolving operator

\mathcal{A}:L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}))\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}),

providing the solution to (
3.10
) at time
t=T
by the Duhamel’s principle

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}g=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)\,g(\delta)\,d\delta,\quad g\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}).

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathcal{H}\subset H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
denote the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
\mathcal{H}
with respect to
H^{s}
. We then define the operator

\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}:L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}))\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}),

and aim to show that
\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
is dense in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
. It is equivalent to prove that the kernel of the adjoint operator
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}
is trivial. The adjoint
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}
is given by

\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}((0,T);H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})),\quad(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}z)(\cdot)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathbf{R}(T,\cdot)^{*}z,

where
\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)^{*}
denotes for
\delta\in[0,T]
the adjoint of
\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)
with respect to the
H^{s}
-inner product.

Triviality of
\operatorname{Ker}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}
:
Let us consider an element
z\in\ker\mathcal{A}_{1}^{*}
. Our objective is to demonstrate that
z=0
.

By definition of the kernel, for any
g\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\left\langle g,\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)^{*}z\right\rangle_{s}=0,\quad\text{for almost every }\delta\in[0,T].

Due to the continuity in
\delta
of the map
\delta\mapsto\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)g
in the topology of
H^{s}
, we conclude that the pairing vanishes identically

(3.12)

\left\langle\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)g,z\right\rangle_{s}=0,\quad\text{for all }\delta\in[0,T].

Fix some time
T_{1}\in(0,T)
. Using the semigroup (or flow) property of the resolvent operator, we may write

\mathbf{R}(T,\delta)=\mathbf{R}(T,T_{1})\mathbf{R}(T_{1},\delta),\quad\text{for }\delta\in[0,T_{1}].

Substituting into (
3.12
), we obtain

\left\langle\mathbf{R}(T,T_{1})\mathbf{R}(T_{1},\delta)g,z\right\rangle_{s}=0.

Rewriting this using adjoint properties, define

, so that

\left\langle\mathbf{R}(T_{1},\delta)g,z_{1}\right\rangle_{s}=0,\quad\text{for all }\delta\in[0,T_{1}].

In particular, taking
\delta=T_{1}
, we find

\left\langle g,z_{1}\right\rangle_{s}=0,

which implies
z_{1}\perp\mathcal{H}\quad\text{in }H^{s},

by (
3.4
), we have

(3.14)

Our next objective is to establish that
z_{1}
is orthogonal to the space
\mathcal{V}_{2}
. To proceed, we introduce the following notation

. Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}y(t,\delta)+\mathcal{Q}(w(\delta+t),y(t,\delta))

(3.17)

\displaystyle y(0,\delta)

\displaystyle=g.

\displaystyle Y(0,\delta)

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand, differentiating (

where the first term we have used the identity from the linearized operator (

Differentiating the identity (

) with respect to
\delta

(3.21)

Since
z_{1}
is independent of
\delta

\left\langle\frac{d}{d\delta}\mathbf{R}(T_{1},\delta)g,z_{1}\right\rangle_{s}=0.

Using the identity (

To express

in terms of its time evolution, we integrate equation (

. Using the initial condition

, we obtain

\displaystyle Y(T_{1}-\delta,\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{1}-\delta}\partial_{t}Y(t,\delta)\,dt

Substituting (

Changing variables in the second integral via

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\delta}\int_{0}^{T_{1}-\delta}\left\langle\mathcal{Q}(w(\delta+t),Y(t,\delta)),z_{1}\right\rangle_{s}\,dt-\left\langle\mathcal{Q}(\dot{\varTheta}(\delta)\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\varphi_{s}(t)\sin(x)+\varphi_{c}(t)\cos(x)\right)\,dt,g),z_{1}\right\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{1}r_{1},\mathcal{I}_{1,1}\rangle=2\int_{\mathbb{T}}r_{1}\,\partial_{x}r_{1}\,\partial_{x}^{2}r_{1}\,dx,

\displaystyle\leq C\|r_{1}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|r_{1}(t)\|^{2}+\varepsilon_{\tau}+C\int_{0}^{t}(\|v_{\tau}+w_{\tau}\|_{2})\|\partial_{x}r_{1}\|^{2}\,d\rho+C\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{x}r_{1}\|^{4}\,d\rho,

Combing this inequality with (

5.
Proof of the main result

In this section, we prove the main controllability result. By directly combining the approximate controllability of (
3.10
) (cf. Proposition
3.3
) with the closeness of the solutions to the linear equation (
2.5
) and the nonlinear equation (
2.3
) (cf. Proposition
4.1
), one can obtain the approximate controllability of (
1.2
) using a finite-dimensional control. However, proceeding in this way does not allow us to express the control in the form (
1.5
). To achieve this, we need the notion of an
approximate right inverse
for a linear operator.

Let
F
and
H
be separable Hilbert spaces, and let
\mathfrak{T}:F\to H
be a bounded linear operator. In general, the equation
\mathfrak{T}f=h
, may either admit no solution or possess multiple solutions. However, under suitable assumptions, it is possible to construct an
approximate
solution that depends linearly on the given data
h
. The results below formalizes this idea.

Proposition 5.1
.

[
KNS20
, Proposition 2.6]

Let
\mathfrak{T}:F\to H
be a continuous linear operator between separable Hilbert spaces, and assume that
\mathrm{Im}(\mathfrak{T})
is dense in
H
. Let
V
be a Banach space compactly embedded in
H
. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a continuous linear operator
\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}:H\to F
with finite-dimensional range such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{T}(\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}h)-h\|_{H}\leq\varepsilon\|h\|_{V},\quad\text{for all }h\in V.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Given
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the resolving operator

\mathfrak{R}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\times L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})\to C([0,T];H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})),\quad(v^{0},g)\mapsto v,

which maps the initial datum and forcing term to the solution
v
of the linearized inviscous Burgers equation (
2.5
) with initial condition
v(0)=v^{0}
. We denote by
\mathfrak{R}_{t}
its evaluation at time
t\in[0,T]
.
From Proposition
3.3
, we have that the image of the operator

\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,\cdot):L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})

is dense in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, i.e.,

\operatorname{Im}\left(\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,\cdot)\right)\text{ is dense in }H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}).

Essentially, this operator
\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,\cdot)
same as
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is the proof of Proposition (
3.3
). We apply Proposition
5.1
with the following identification of spaces:

\mathfrak{T}:=\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,\cdot),\quad H:=H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}),\quad F:=L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H}),\quad\text{and}\quad V:=H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}).

Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a continuous linear operator by Proposition
5.1

\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H}),

with finite-dimensional range, such that for all
h\in H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

\left\|\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}h)-h\right\|_{H^{s}}\leq\varepsilon\|h\|_{H^{s+2j+1}}.

Choose initial and target states
u^{0},u^{1}\in H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
. Then there exists a constant
M>0
such that

u^{0},u^{1}\in B_{H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}(0,M),

the ball of radius
M
centered at the origin in
H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
.
We now apply the approximate right inverse estimate established earlier with

h:=u_{1}-\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},0).

Let
\theta>0
, and define

g_{\theta}:=\mathfrak{T}_{\theta}\left(u^{1}-\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},0)\right).

Then, by construction, we have

\left\|\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,g_{\theta})-\left(u^{1}-\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},0)\right)\right\|_{s}\leq\theta\left\|u^{1}-\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},0)\right\|_{{s+2j+1}}.

Using the linearity for the equation (
2.5
), as well as the associated resolving operator
\mathfrak{R}_{T}
, we deduce

\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},g_{\theta})=\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},0)+\mathfrak{R}_{T}(0,g_{\theta}),

so for some constant
C=C(M)>0
depending only on
M
. That is,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},g_{\theta})-u^{1}\right\|_{s}\leq\theta C.

We now apply Proposition
4.1
with the previously chosen initial data
u^{0}\in H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
and control
g:=g_{\theta}\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathcal{H})
. Then, by the convergence result (
4.1
), for any
\delta>0
, there exists
\tau_{1}>0
and a control

\eta_{\tau_{1},j}\in L^{2}((0,T\tau_{1});\mathcal{H})

such that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau_{1}}(u_{0},\eta_{\tau_{1},j})-v(T)\right\|_{H^{s}}\leq\delta,

here
v(T)=\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},g_{\theta})
. Combining (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we obtain the estimate

(5.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau_{1}}(u^{0},\eta_{\tau_{1},j})-u^{1}\right\|_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau_{1}}(u^{0},\eta_{\tau_{1},j})-v(T)\right\|_{s}+\left\|\mathfrak{R}_{T}(u^{0},g_{\theta})-u^{1}\right\|_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\delta+\theta C.

Since both
\delta>0
and
\theta>0
are arbitrary, the right-hand side of (
5.4
) can be made arbitrarily small. Therefore, for sufficiently small
\tau>0
, there exists a control

\eta_{\tau,j}\in L^{2}((0,T\tau);\mathcal{H})

such that

\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau}(u^{0},\eta_{\tau,j})\to u^{1}\quad\text{in }H^{s}\quad\text{as }\tau\to 0^{+}.

This completes the proof of the limit (
1.4
), and the convergence in is uniform for all
u^{0},u^{1}
in bounded subsets of
H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
. Now, for the structure of the control, we use (
4.2
) with
g=g_{\theta}
, and defining
\kappa_{\tau,j}(t):=\xi_{\tau}(t)+\mathcal{L}_{j}w(\tau^{-1}t)
, we obtain (
1.5
).
∎

As a immediate consequence, we get the Corollary
1.1
.

Proof of Corollary
1.1
.

By the density of
H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
with semi-global stability (
2.2
), Theorem
1.1
ensures that for any
u^{0},u^{1}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a control
\tilde{\eta}_{\tau,j}\in L^{2}((0,T\tau);\mathcal{H})
such that

\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau}(u^{0},\tilde{\eta}_{\tau,j})\to u^{1}\quad\text{in }H^{s}\text{ as }\tau\to 0^{+}.

Now, by continuity of the flow in time and stability with respect to initial data (Propositions
2.1
–
2.2
), there exist
\mathfrak{r}>0
,
t^{\prime}>0
such that any trajectory starting in
B(u^{1},\mathfrak{r})
remains
\varepsilon
-close to
u^{1}
for time
t^{\prime}
.

Choosing
\tau_{1}
small so that
\mathcal{R}_{j,T\tau_{1}}(u^{0},\tilde{\eta}_{\tau_{1},j})\in B(u^{1},\mathfrak{r})
, we patch the control with zero beyond
T\tau_{1}
to extend the trajectory. If
T\tau_{1}+t^{\prime}\geq T
, we are done. Otherwise, we iterate this argument finitely many times until reaching time
T
. Thus, the system is approximately controllable at time
T
in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The control
\eta
obtained in this corollary does not maintain the structure described in (
1.5
) throughout the full time interval. In particular, the dependence on
(u^{0},u^{1})
is no longer affine once the zero control is applied in a neighborhood of
u^{1}
. This loss of structure is a consequence of the nonlinearity in the map
\mathcal{R}_{j,t}(u^{1},0)
with respect to
u^{1}
. Nevertheless, for any fixed
\varepsilon,M>0
and for initial and target states
u^{0},u^{1}\in B_{H^{s+2j+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T})}(0,M)
, the part of the control on the interval
[0,T\tau]
still matches the form given in (
1.5
), while the portion on
[T\tau,T]
does not depend on
u^{0}
.
