Revised therapeutic window for vancomycin in pediatric patients: evidence from a retrospective therapeutic drug monitoring study
Authors: Tao Zhang, Jingjing Yi, Hua Cheng, Xinyan Han, Yan Wang, Jiao Xie, Qianting Yang, Sasa Hu, Yalin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections among children are escalating annually. Vancomycin serves as the frontline therapeutic agent against MRSA infections. However, determining the therapeutic window that maximizes efficacy while minimizing the risk of toxicity for vancomycin in pediatric patients remains a challenge. This study aimed to explore a therapeutic window for vancomycin in pediatric patients.MethodsThis retrospective study collected data from hospitalized children aged 1 month to 18 years, who underwent routine therapeutic drug monitoring for vancomycin. We analyzed the distribution patterns of vancomycin concentrations in these patients. Factors influencing clinical outcomes and adverse reaction (nephrotoxicity) were investigated. ROC analysis was used to establish the therapeutic window for vancomycin in pediatric patients.ResultsA comprehensive dataset encompassing 183 pediatric patients with 330 samples was analyzed. The mean trough concentration (Cmin) of vancomycin was 7.6 ± 5.5 mg/L. 74.3% of patients exhibited concentrations below the conventionally recommended therapeutic window of 10–20 mg/L. Patients responding positively to treatment exhibited significantly higher Cmin values (8.4 ± 5.7 mg/L) compared to those with treatment failure (5.9 ± 4.4 mg/L, P = 0.006). Similarly, patients who developed nephrotoxicity had significantly elevated Cmin levels (17.8 ± 5.3 mg/L) compared to those without nephrotoxicity (6.4 ± 3.9 mg/L, P < 0.001). Both univariate and multivariate logistic regressions revealed that the Cmin of vancomycin was the predictor of both clinical outcomes and nephrotoxicity. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis pinpointed that Cmin of vancomycin with 5.9 mg/L (AUC = 0.643) and 14.8 mg/L (AUC = 0.960) associated with clinical effectiveness and safety, respectively. When the therapeutic exposure targets established for adults are used as a reference, a substantial proportion of pediatric patients are found to have subtherapeutic vancomycin levels.ConclusionBased on our findings, we suggest a potential therapeutic window of 5.9–14.8 mg/L for vancomycin in pediatric patients, which may help optimize treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate this range.Keywords: Vancomycin, Pediatrics, Therapeutic drug monitoring, Therapeutic window, Pharmacodynamics

Introduction

Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic, is a first-line treatment for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections in children. While alternative agents such as clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and tetracycline are also used, vancomycin remains the preferred choice for moderate to severe cases and constitutes a major proportion of antibacterial drug costs in pediatric care [1].

The drug exhibits a narrow therapeutic index and considerable interindividual variability. Current guidelines from the IDSA, ASHP, and SIDP recommend targeting an AUC₂₄/MIC ratio of 400–600 mg·h/L [2]. Despite this, trough concentration (Cmin) remains the most commonly used monitoring parameter in clinical practice, with surveys indicating that 93% of participating pharmacists primarily rely on Cmin [3].

The generally accepted Cmin target is 10–20 mg/L, with 10–15 mg/L suggested for uncomplicated infections and 15–20 mg/L for severe cases such as meningitis, osteomyelitis, and bacteremia [4, 5]. However, these targets are largely extrapolated from adult data. Emerging evidence, including from our group, supports a strong correlation between Cmin and AUC₂₄ targets in children [6, 7]. Specifically, Cmin values of 5.0–6.9 mg/L or 9.0–12.9 mg/L may suffice to achieve PK/PD targets depending on MIC [6]. Historical pediatric targets were in fact lower: 5–10 mg/L for most infections and 10–15 mg/L for CNS involvement [4]. Although the IDSA guidelines since 2009 have recommended higher therapeutic targets and dosing regimens, the product monographs have not been updated accordingly [2, 4, 8]. Recent studies report alarmingly low target attainment rates under current dosing—as low as 4–51% even when aiming for a Cmin of 5–15 mg/L [9–12].

The pharmacokinetic challenges of vancomycin in children are compounded by pronounced developmental changes in its clearance (CL) and volume of distribution. These changes are further altered in patients with congenital heart disease, renal impairment, sepsis, or those receiving fluid resuscitation [13]. Consistently, studies indicate that standard dosing often fails to reach current targets, suggesting that adult-derived ranges may be unsuitable for children [9].

The aim of this study is to explore an evidence-based therapeutic window for vancomycin in pediatric patients to maximize efficacy and minimize toxicity. A preprint outlining preliminary findings has been published [14].

Methods

Patient information and collection of clinical blood samples

This study retrospectively collected hospitalized pediatric patients from two hospitals: the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University and the Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The period spanned from January 2017 to December 2018.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) hospitalized patients aged 1 month to 18 years; (2) those diagnosed or suspected with Gram-positive bacterial infection and receiving vancomycin treatment; and (3) patients from whom at least one TDM measurement was available. The following patients were excluded from the study: (1) those receiving vancomycin through non-intravenous routes or by continuous infusion; (2) patients allergic to vancomycin; (3) and patients who died within 24 h of vancomycin administration.

Demographic data, medication details, and physiological and biochemical parameters — including age, sex, weight, serum creatinine (SCr), vancomycin dosing regimen, dose, dates of medication, and indications — were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Blood concentration data of vancomycin in patients was collected from those undergoing routine TDM (samples taken 30 min prior to the next dose after reaching a steady state). The blood concentration of vancomycin was determined using the previously established HPLC-MS/MS method by our research team [15].

This study was not prospectively registered in a clinical trial registry as it is a retrospective observational study.

Therapeutic window of vancomycin in pediatric patients

A univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the correlation between various factors and both clinical outcomes and nephrotoxicity. Subsequently, factors exhibiting a P value less than 0.1 in the univariate analysis were subjected to multivariate logistic analysis to further identify factors potentially linked to clinical efficacy and nephrotoxicity.

Clinical effectiveness were categorized into a four-level scale (cured, improved, ineffective, and relapsed) based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical, laboratory, and microbiological parameters. Specifically, ‘cured’ was defined as resolution of fever (body temperature < 37.3°C for > 48 h), normalization of white blood cell count, and complete resolution of signs and symptoms related to the infection at the end of vancomycin therapy. ‘Improved’ was defined as significant alleviation of clinical signs and symptoms with partial laboratory improvement, but requiring continued antibiotic therapy. ‘Ineffective’ indicated persistence or worsening of clinical signs and/or laboratory parameters, necessitating a change in antibiotic therapy. ‘Relapsed’ referred to the recurrence of infection-related symptoms and signs within one week after discontinuation of vancomycin. For simplicity in analysis, ‘cured’ and ‘improved’ were collectively classified as treatment success, while ‘ineffective’ and ‘relapsed’ were grouped as treatment failure.

The assessment of nephrotoxicity involved measuring SCr levels from the initiation of medication to 72 h post-medication. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria as either an increase in SCr by ≥ 26.5 µmol/L within 48 h or an elevation of SCr to ≥ 1.5 times the baseline level within 7 days [16].

The therapeutic window refers to the spectrum of drug concentration levels that exhibit a significant correlation with clinical outcomes and the incidence of nephrotoxicity.

Statistics and analysis

Continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and median for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were characterized by frequencies and percentages. All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level of α set at 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using SPSS (Version 19.0).

In the assessment of vancomycin exposure–response relationships, the final Cmin value prior to therapy discontinuation was used as the pharmacodynamic index for clinical effectiveness. For nephrotoxicity analysis, the Cmin value measured closest to the date of toxicity onset was selected.

A comprehensive monitoring of 330 blood drug concentrations was undertaken. 53 patients had available pathogen culture results, which revealed the presence of 23 methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and 7 MRSA strains. The average vancomycin Cmin concentration was determined as 7.6 ± 5.5 mg/L. 74.3% of pediatric patients exhibited vancomycin Cmin levels below the recommended therapeutic window of 10–20 mg/L. It is noteworthy that an elevated mean creatinine clearance of 134.3 ± 81.4 mL/min was observed, even though only 24% of the patients in our study were formally admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).

The mean Cmin concentration in patients who responded successfully to treatment was significantly higher than in those who failed (P = 0.006). In detail, the mean Cmin values were 8.4 ± 5.7 mg/L and 5.9 ± 4.4 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 1B). Among patients with nephrotoxicity, the mean Cmin concentration was 17.8 ± 5.3 mg/L, significantly exceeding that of patients without nephrotoxicity (6.4 ± 3.9 mg/L, P < 0.001).

In this study, the overall success rate of vancomycin therapy was 72.1%, while 11.5% (21 patients) developed nephrotoxicity during treatment. In the analysis of nephrotoxicity, baseline SCr level, central nervous system infections, presence of MSSA, and vancomycin Cmin were significantly associated in univariate analysis. Additional analysis of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrated that a Cmin concentration of 5.9 mg/L serves as a predictor for therapeutic effect, exhibiting an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.643 (Fig. 2A). A Cmin level of 14.8 mg/L effectively anticipated the emergence of nephrotoxicity, displaying an AUC of 0.960 (Fig. 2B). Taking into account the temporal influence on nephrotoxicity risk, patients were stratified into two groups based on their Cmin levels (< 14.8 and ≥ 14.8 mg/L) for subsequent Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. As depicted in Fig. 3, a significant difference in the incidence of nephrotoxicity was observed between the two groups over the course of vancomycin treatment.

Discussion

Vancomycin has been in clinical use for over six decades, yet its precise therapeutic window for pediatric patients remains inadequately defined. A key pharmacokinetic parameter, CL, is substantially higher in children—approximately 2.5 times that observed in adults [12, 17]. In Chinese pediatric populations, reported vancomycin CL ranges from 0.11 to 0.17 L/h·kg, markedly exceeding adult values [7, 18]. This likely reflects developmental physiological differences, including higher body water content and enhanced renal function, which collectively contribute to lower vancomycin exposure and poor target attainment rates.

The elevated creatinine clearance observed in our study is indicative of a high likelihood of augmented renal clearance (ARC). As highlighted in the comprehensive scoping review by Rhoney et al., ARC is a common phenomenon in pediatric critical care [19]. The significant prevalence of ARC in our cohort, which included a substantial number of patients outside the intensive care unit (only 24% were ICU-admitted), underscores that the physiological drivers of ARC may be present in a wide spectrum of acutely ill children. Accelerated renal elimination provides a compelling explanation for the subtherapeutic vancomycin trough concentrations frequently detected in our patients. This underexposure poses a significant risk of therapeutic failure, particularly for antimicrobials with concentration-dependent efficacy. Our results, aligned with the broader literature, underscore the imperative to recognize ARC beyond the ICU and adapt dosing strategies accordingly—potentially through higher doses, optimized dosing intervals, or therapeutic drug monitoring—to ensure effective drug exposure across various inpatient care settings.

Given these pharmacokinetic disparities, we re-evaluated the vancomycin therapeutic window in pediatric patients, proposing a revised range of 5.9–14.8 mg/L, consistent with earlier findings from our group [6]. Although current international guidelines recommend AUC₂₄/MIC-guided monitoring (target: 400–600 mg·h/L) [2], its implementation remains limited due to practical barriers such as the need for multiple samples, limited pharmacokinetic expertise, and poorly defined pediatric PK/PD targets [20]. Consequently, Cmin remains the standard in most clinical settings, including large tertiary hospitals [21], underscoring the importance of refining Cmin targets for this population.

Historically, pediatric vancomycin dosing has relied on extrapolation from adult data. However, achievement of conventional adult-derived targets (10–20 mg/L) remains low: only 4%-51% of pediatric patients reach 10–20 mg/L [9–12], and a mere 6.9% attain 15–20 mg/L under standard dosing [22, 23]. Higher doses—up to 80–85 mg/kg/day in children aged 1–6 years—may be necessary to achieve concentrations of 15–20 mg/L, highlighting the need for pediatric-specific exposure targets [24, 25].

Our data indicate that satisfactory clinical outcomes could be achieved with Cmin >5.9 mg/L, aligning with the lower bound of earlier pediatric recommendations [26]. A recent meta-analysis supports targeting Cmin between 10 and 15 mg/L for efficacy, with no additional benefit beyond 15 mg/L [27]. These results are consistent with the 2020 Chinese TDM guidelines, which recommend a Cmin of 5–15 mg/L for children, albeit based on limited retrospective data [28]. Our study, with a substantially larger sample, provides robust support for this range.

Despite recommendations advocating a Cmin threshold of ≥ 10 mg/L to prevent resistance, no concomitant increase in vancomycin resistance among MRSA isolates has been observed [29, 30]. Large surveillance studies indicate MIC values consistently ≤ 2 mg/L—mostly ≤ 1 mg/L—with no vancomycin-resistant MRSA detected in Chinese pediatric bloodstream infections [31–33]. At our institution, the proportion of MRSA isolates with MIC = 1 mg/L declined from 81% (2013) to 30% (2024), while those with MIC ≤ 0.5 mg/L increased from 19% to 70%. National surveillance data have identified no vancomycin-resistant MRSA since 2005, suggesting that current exposure levels in children have not driven resistance development.

It is important to acknowledge that the predictive power of the vancomycin trough concentration for clinical efficacy in our study, while statistically significant, was modest (AUC = 0.643). This limited discriminative capacity may be attributable to several factors. First, clinical outcomes are multifactorial and can be influenced by underlying disease severity, host immune status, source control, and the presence of other concomitant medications, which may dilute the solitary effect of vancomycin exposure. Second, the use of a dichotomized clinical outcome (success/failure), though necessary for analysis, may not fully capture the nuanced spectrum of clinical response. Finally, while trough concentration serves as a practical and widely available surrogate, it is an imperfect substitute for the pharmacodynamically superior index of AUC₂₄/MIC. Despite this limitation, the identified trough threshold of 5.9 mg/L remained a significant predictor in multivariate analysis and offers a valuable, readily applicable clinical reference point. Future studies employing Bayesian forecasting to estimate AUC₂₄/MIC or utilizing larger sample sizes may improve predictive accuracy and help validate this target.

An important caveat is the concentration-dependent risk of nephrotoxicity [5]. We identified a significantly increased risk of AKI in children with Cmin >14.8 mg/L. This is supported by a large pediatric study (n = 859) linking Cmin >15 mg/L to AKI (OR 2.18; 95% CI 1.21–3.92) [34], and a meta-report indicating a 2.7-fold higher nephrotoxicity risk beyond this threshold [27]. These findings argue against aggressive exposure targets without clear clinical benefits, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children with sepsis or multiple comorbidities.

Our proposed therapeutic window (5.9–14.8 mg/L) aligns with a growing international consensus that lower vancomycin exposures may be sufficient for efficacy while higher exposures increase nephrotoxicity risk. This is supported by a recent study of pediatric MRSA bacteremia by Lee, which utilized Bayesian-derived AUC₂₄ monitoring [35]. While their methodology is considered the gold standard, they similarly identified a toxicity threshold: an AUC₂₄ >530 mg·h/L was significantly associated with increased rates of AKI (37.5% vs. 2.1%, P < 0.01). This finding resonates with our trough-based nephrotoxicity threshold of 14.8 mg/L, reinforcing the concentration-dependent nature of vancomycin-induced kidney injury. Interestingly, their study did not find a clinical benefit for an AUC₂₄ >400 mg·h/L for efficacy. Our data similarly suggest that a lower trough concentration (5.9 mg/L) might be sufficient for treatment success in many patients. Additionally, our study addresses the practical reality in many settings where trough monitoring remains the only feasible option. Together, these studies suggest that whether monitored by AUC₂₄ or trough, the principles of avoiding excessively high vancomycin exposures to prevent AKI are consistent across diverse pediatric populations.

Finally, while our study supports the utility of trough-based monitoring, we acknowledge the well-established pharmacokinetic superiority of the area under the curve (AUC₂₄/MIC) as a more precise predictor of vancomycin efficacy and toxicity. The transition to AUC₂₄-guided dosing in resource-constrained environments is a necessary step. Promising, practical pathways exist to bridge this gap without requiring intensive blood sampling. Specifically, the adoption of single-sample Bayesian forecasting represents a highly feasible strategy. This approach utilizes a single, strategically timed trough concentration—data already being collected in routine care—in conjunction with published population pharmacokinetic models to provide an accurate, individualized estimate of AUC₂₄. Future efforts at our institutions will focus on validating and implementing such Bayesian-guided dosing protocols to maximize the precision and safety of vancomycin therapy in our pediatric population.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently carries risks of unmeasured confounding and selection bias. Although efforts were made to adjust for known covariates, residual confounding may persist. Furthermore, the exclusion of patients who died within 24 h of initiating vancomycin may have further introduced selection bias. While this exclusion was necessary to analyze the association between steady-state drug exposure and clinical outcomes in patients who had sufficient time to respond to therapy, it may limit the generalizability of our findings to the most critically ill pediatric patients. Second, the limited number of patients with microbiologically confirmed infections, particularly MRSA, restricted our ability to adjust the effectiveness model for pathogen type and MIC values. This MIC-agnostic approach, while reflecting the empirical nature of initial therapy, may dilute the true exposure-efficacy relationship. Third, the vancomycin dosing regimen presented (38 ± 5 mg/kg/day) represents the final, maintained dose. This was the dose from which the therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) concentrations were derived; it was not the initial starting dose. This consistency reflects the cautious clinical practice at our institution. While this provided a consistent cohort for analysis, our findings may not be generalizable to centers that employ more aggressive, guideline-recommended dosing. Fourth, although we identified a correlation between vancomycin Cmin and AKI risk, we did not account for preadmission nephrotoxic medications or the cumulative burden of other nephrotoxic agents, which may influence renal outcomes. Children with sepsis, multiple comorbidities, or obesity may receive weight-based dosing that further amplifies their risk, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of toxicodynamic thresholds. Fifth, our study focused on total vancomycin concentrations. Measuring free drug concentrations—particularly in critically ill children with altered protein binding—may reflect active exposure more accurately. However, target ranges for free vancomycin remain poorly established, warranting future investigation. Finally, this study utilized trough concentrations rather than the preferred AUC₂₄/MIC ratio for exposure assessment. Although we previously demonstrated a strong correlation between vancomycin trough levels and AUC₂₄ in pediatric patients, and trough monitoring remains the most widely used approach in Chinese clinical practice (as reflected in national guidelines), we acknowledge that AUC₂₄/MIC provides a more precise exposure measure. Despite these limitations, our findings align with previous reports and provide clinically relevant guidance for vancomycin dosing and monitoring in pediatric patients. Larger prospective studies incorporating free drug assays, standardized MIC data, and detailed comorbidity profiles are needed to refine exposure targets and improve safety in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

Based on the observed correlation between vancomycin Cmin and clinical outcomes, our study provides preliminary evidence to suggest a potential therapeutic window of 5.9–14.8 mg/L for vancomycin in pediatric patients. However, given the observational design, lack of microbiological eradication data, and modest predictive power of the efficacy threshold, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and must be validated in prospective studies before clinical implementation. If confirmed, this range may help guide more rational and safer vancomycin use in this vulnerable population.
