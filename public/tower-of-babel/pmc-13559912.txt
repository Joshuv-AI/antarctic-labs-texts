Ménière's disease: the treatment evidence we still do not have
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Europe
Published: 2026

An attack of Ménière's disease involves fullness in one ear, roaring tinnitus, and vertigo lasting between 20 min and 12 h. Patients might vomit, cannot stand or drive, and cannot predict the next attack. Attacks cluster unpredictably and can remit spontaneously for months or years. Over time, the disease can cause permanent hearing loss and chronic imbalance, profoundly affecting quality of life and employment. Treatment can reduce vertigo attacks, but cannot reverse established hearing or balance loss.

Ménière's disease affects an estimated 0.05–0.20% of adults worldwide (including 250,000–750,000 people in the EU), is more common in women, and occurs more frequently in Europe and North America than in east Asia.

More than 160 years after the disease was first described, its cause remains uncertain. Endolymphatic hydrops (build-up of inner-ear fluid that distends the membranous labyrinth) has been associated with Ménière's disease, along with genetic, inflammatory, immune, migraine-related, and anatomical mechanisms. Increasingly, the disease is understood not as a single disorder with one cause, but as a heterogeneous clinical syndrome.

Uncertainty extends across the treatment pathway. Patients are commonly advised to reduce salt and caffeine and might receive betahistine, intratympanic corticosteroids to reduce vertigo, or intratympanic gentamicin that controls vertigo by destroying vestibular function but can also damage hearing. A 2023 Cochrane review found no placebo-controlled randomised trials of salt or caffeine restriction. Betahistine did not outperform placebo whereas intratympanic treatments have mainly been compared with other active treatments, showing which performs better but not their individual efficacy.

The evidence does not become stronger as treatment becomes more invasive. Surgical practice varies between countries, but surgery might be considered when less invasive treatments are unsuccessful. Endolymphatic sac decompression (ESD) removes bone over the sac to relieve pressure whereas a newer procedure called endolymphatic duct blockage (EDB) clips the duct shut to alter endolymphatic fluid dynamics. A 2023 Cochrane review of surgical interventions identified only two randomised trials and judged the evidence for their efficacy to be of low or very low certainty.

Only ESD was compared with sham surgery in a trial of 30 patients in 1981, whereas EDB has not been compared against sham surgery. An unblinded randomised trial in 2015 suggested that EDB was substantially more effective but did not include blinding. Surgical techniques have evolved, but definitive evidence of treatment-specific efficacy has remained elusive.

In this issue, Annejet Schenck and colleagues compare EDB with ESD in a multicentre, double-blind, randomised trial of 75 patients with refractory Ménière's disease. At 12 months, 59% of patients after EDB and 67% after ESD were free from vertigo attacks, but this difference was not statistically significant. Quality of life, hearing, and vestibular function did not differ. Stronger preoperative expectations were also associated with treatment success, underscoring how difficult it is to separate treatment effects from expectation and spontaneous improvement as symptoms fluctuate.

The fundamental question remains: does either operation improve symptoms beyond the effects of expectation and spontaneous fluctuation? Comparing one procedure of uncertain efficacy with another cannot answer that question. This trial strengthens the comparative evidence by the double-blind design, but sham-controlled surgery would provide the most definitive evidence, although exposing participants to procedural risks without expected therapeutic benefit raises ethical concerns. Notably in the Netherlands, ESD has been regarded as a placebo-like procedure and was considered an acceptable control; internationally, however, this interpretation is not uniform, and the specific efficacy remains uncertain.

A 2024 meta-analysis found that 52.5% of participants who received placebo across 15 randomised trials reported improvement in vertigo, highlighting how spontaneous fluctuation and placebo response can make treatment effects particularly difficult to interpret in Ménière's disease.

The wider research landscape reflects the same weakness. A ClinicalTrials.gov search in August, 2026, showed that 66 interventional studies were registered with a median enrolment of 40 participants; 21 used a single-group design; and 13 had been terminated, withdrawn, or suspended. Only 22 studies explicitly included a placebo or sham intervention. More trials will not necessarily produce better evidence if weak comparators cannot distinguish treatment effects from expectation and spontaneous improvement.

Ménière's disease needs fewer small studies and more definitive ones. Future trials should be adequately powered, measure symptoms prospectively, follow-up patients long enough to distinguish treatment effects from spontaneous fluctuation, and use agreed core outcomes that include objective measures (such as hearing thresholds) while accounting for patient expectations. Elucidating the underlying pathophysiology is essential. Ménière's disease might encompass several biologically distinct disorders that present with the same clinical symptoms, and identifying these mechanisms could enable treatments that target the underlying cause rather than merely controlling symptoms.
