Data-driven phenotyping of infective endocarditis using unsupervised machine learning: toward a personalized approach
Authors: Murat Karaçam, Vedat Cicek, Barkın Kültürsay, Almina Erdem, Seda Tanyeri Üzel, Şeref Berk Tuncer, Tezel Kovancı, Feryat İgit, Çağdaş Bulus, Mert Babaoğlu, Muhammet Bulut, Özgür Can Usta, Burak Tay, Muhammed Ensar Aslan, Mustafa Kamil Yemiş, Ahmet Öz, Tufan Çınar, Ulas Bagci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeInfective endocarditis (IE) is a heterogeneous disease with diverse clinical presentations, microbiological profiles, and outcomes. Conventional classification systems may not fully capture this complexity. We aimed to identify data-driven clinical phenotypes in IE using an unsupervised machine learning (ML) approach.MethodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, 335 patients with definite IE from three hospitals were included. A total of 55 clinical, microbiological, and echocardiographic variables were analyzed. Missing data were imputed using the MissForest algorithm. Clustering was performed using spectral clustering based on a Gower dissimilarity matrix and supported by a Bayesian non-parametric approach. Associations between cluster assignment and clinical outcomes were assessed using regression analyses.ResultsTwo distinct clusters were identified, reflecting different clinical profiles. Cluster 1 was characterized by native valve endocarditis, a higher burden of comorbidities, inflammatory activation, and larger vegetations. Cluster 2 was characterized by prosthetic valve and device-related IE, higher rates of atrial fibrillation, and larger cardiac dimensions. Cluster assignment was not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses. Increasing age emerged as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality. In the adjusted model, Cluster 2 was independently associated with longer hospital stay, whereas no significant difference was observed in ICU stay.ConclusionUnsupervised ML identified two clinically distinct phenotypes in IE, reflecting disease heterogeneity. Although these phenotypes were not associated with major clinical outcomes, they provide a data-driven framework for understanding disease heterogeneity and may contribute to future personalized approaches in the management of IE. Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a severe and potentially life-threatening disease characterized by infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, most commonly involving the cardiac valves. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, imaging modalities, and surgical management, IE remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Contemporary studies continue to report considerable in-hospital mortality, reflecting the complex pathophysiology and clinical course of the disease [1–3].

Over the past decades, the epidemiology of IE has evolved considerably. The incidence of IE has increased in many regions worldwide, largely driven by demographic changes and healthcare-related factors [4–9]. Aging populations, the widespread use of intracardiac devices, prosthetic valve implantation, and the increasing burden of comorbidities have contributed to the growing number of susceptible individuals [6–9]. In parallel, healthcare-associated infections and invasive medical procedures have altered the microbiological and clinical profile of IE, further complicating its management [1].

Another defining feature of IE is its marked clinical heterogeneity. Patients with IE present with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, microbiological etiologies, and underlying cardiac conditions [1, 2]. These differences translate into substantial variability in disease severity, complications, and outcomes. Traditional classification systems based on individual clinical variables often fail to fully capture this heterogeneity, limiting their ability to provide precise risk stratification and individualized treatment strategies.

In recent years, data-driven phenotyping approaches based on unsupervised machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful tools to identify clinically meaningful subgroups within heterogeneous cardiovascular diseases. Clustering techniques have been successfully applied in conditions such as heart failure, pulmonary embolism, and cardiogenic shock, revealing distinct phenotypes with different pathophysiological mechanisms and prognostic profiles [10–13]. Recent studies have also explored the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning in infective endocarditis, primarily focusing on supervised models for risk stratification and outcome prediction. For example, the SABIER score was developed to estimate the risk of IE among patients with Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia [14], a machine learning–based model was developed to predict early postoperative mortality after surgery for IE [15], and another study applied machine learning techniques to inflammatory biomarkers to predict mortality in patients with IE [16]. However, despite the complex and heterogeneous nature of infective endocarditis, studies applying unsupervised clustering techniques specifically to patients with IE remain scarce.

Infective endocarditis encompasses a mixture of continuous, categorical, microbiological, and echocardiographic variables, making clustering methods capable of handling mixed-type data particularly relevant. Gower-based similarity measures offer a flexible framework for integrating such heterogeneous variables within a single distance matrix [17], and are well suited to being combined with spectral clustering to capture the complex, multidimensional structure of IE.

In the present study, we therefore applied an unsupervised ML–based clustering approach, based on Gower dissimilarity and spectral clustering, to explore potential clinical phenotypes among patients with IE. By integrating demographic, clinical, microbiological, echocardiographic, and laboratory variables, we aimed to identify data-driven clusters reflecting the underlying heterogeneity of the disease and to characterize their clinical profiles. Cluster membership was subsequently entered into post-hoc regression models to assess whether the identified phenotypes were associated with clinical outcomes, integrating unsupervised phenotyping with supervised outcome modeling within a single analytical framework, while unsupervised phenotyping remained the primary focus of the study.

Materials and methods

Data collection

A total of 393 patients hospitalized with a diagnosis of IE between 2015 and 2025 were initially screened for eligibility across three different hospitals in Istanbul, Turkey. The diagnosis of IE was established according to the modified Duke criteria [1]. Patients younger than 18 years, those with a diagnosis of possible IE without fulfilling the criteria for definite IE and patients with an alternative diagnosis explaining their clinical presentation were excluded. In addition, for patients with repeated admissions, only the index hospitalization was considered for analysis. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 335 patients were included in the final study cohort (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study

Clinical information including demographic characteristics, comorbid conditions, laboratory parameters, microbiological findings, echocardiographic data, and in-hospital outcomes was retrieved from the electronic medical record systems of the participating centers. Clinical diagnoses were determined based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes and subsequently verified through physician notes and laboratory reports to ensure accuracy. Standardized definitions were applied in line with established guidelines: diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as a physician-documented diagnosis and/or use of antidiabetic medication [18]; atrial fibrillation (AF) as documented arrhythmia on ECG or Holter monitoring [19]; hypertension (HT) as a physician-documented diagnosis and/or use of antihypertensive therapy [20]; hyperlipidemia (HL) as a physician-documented diagnosis and/or use of lipid-lowering therapy; chronic kidney disease (CKD) as an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for > 3 months [21]; cerebrovascular disease (CVD) as a history of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke or transient ischemic attack; and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) as a physician-documented chronic airway disease with or without pulmonary function testing. The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic criteria for infective endocarditis

The diagnosis of infective endocarditis was established according to the modified Duke criteria. Definite IE was defined by the presence of two major criteria, one major criterion together with three minor criteria, or five minor criteria. Major criteria included positive blood cultures for typical microorganisms consistent with IE obtained from at least two separate blood samples or persistently positive blood cultures, as well as evidence of endocardial involvement on echocardiography, such as vegetation, abscess formation, new partial prosthetic valve dehiscence, or newly developed valvular regurgitation. Minor criteria comprised predisposing cardiac conditions or intravenous drug use, fever ≥ 38 °C, vascular phenomena (including major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarction, intracranial hemorrhage, conjunctival hemorrhages, or Janeway lesions), immunologic manifestations (such as glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, or positive rheumatoid factor), and microbiological findings not meeting major criteria.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and comparative statistics

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) based on their distribution, which was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics across the identified clusters were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. All p-values were two-sided, and a value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variable selection

To identify distinct clinical phenotypes of infective endocarditis, an unsupervised machine learning approach was employed. The dataset included 115 available variables. Among these, 55 core clinical, microbiological, laboratory, and echocardiographic variables were selected a priori and included in the clustering analysis to represent the multifaceted nature of the disease. Outcome and follow-up variables were not used for clustering and were analyzed separately to assess the clinical relevance of the identified phenotypes. The variables included in the clustering analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Missing data imputation

Among the 55 variables included in the clustering analysis, the overall proportion of missing data was 2.8%; the missingness pattern across the full dataset is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, and per-variable proportions for the clustering variables are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Rather than assuming a Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) mechanism, we considered a Missing At Random (MAR) mechanism more plausible, as missingness was concentrated in specific laboratory and echocardiographic variables that were more likely to be missing because of selective clinical test ordering than entirely at random. Missing values were imputed using the MissForest algorithm, an iterative non-parametric random-forest–based method that accommodates mixed-type data and performs robustly under MAR conditions. Imputation was performed using the missForest package with default settings (100 trees per forest and a maximum of 10 iterations); the algorithm terminated according to its default stopping criterion, i.e., when the difference between successive imputations first increased, evaluated by the normalized root mean squared error for continuous variables and the proportion of falsely classified entries for categorical variables. A formal Little’s MCAR test was not applied, as it assumes multivariate normality and is not well suited to high-dimensional data containing a large proportion of categorical variables.

Dissimilarity matrix construction and normalization

Following MissForest imputation, continuous variables were retained on their original scale; each numeric variable was internally range-normalized to the [0,1] interval as an inherent step of the Gower dissimilarity computation. No separate z-score standardization was applied, as this would have amounted to a redundant double normalization given the range normalization intrinsic to the Gower metric. Given the mixed data types, a Gower dissimilarity matrix was then constructed.

Clustering algorithm benchmarking

We benchmarked four clustering algorithms — Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM), K-Prototypes, agglomerative hierarchical clustering, and spectral clustering — on the Gower dissimilarity matrix. For each algorithm, the number of clusters was varied from k = 2 to k = 6, and the average Silhouette width and Dunn index were computed for each solution. Both metrics were maximized at k = 2 and declined progressively for k ≥ 3 across all algorithms; the two-cluster solution was therefore selected prior to the between-algorithm comparison. At k = 2, spectral clustering yielded the highest Silhouette width (0.1612) and Dunn index (0.1938), outperforming hierarchical clustering (0.1596 and 0.1776), K-Prototypes (0.1269 and 0.1318), and PAM (0.0708 and 0.0779) (Supplementary Table 3), and was selected as the final model given its superior performance in capturing non-linear structure in mixed-type data. Spectral clustering was implemented using the kernlab package with a radial basis function (Gaussian) kernel; the kernel width parameter (σ) was estimated automatically from the data using the package’s median heuristic, and the number of centers was set to the selected number of clusters (k = 2). The resulting similarity relationships between patients were visualized using a network-based affinity representation (Fig. 2), and patients were projected onto the first two principal coordinates of the spectral embedding space to illustrate cluster separation (Fig. 2).

Cluster validation (Dirichlet process mixture model)

To validate the robustness of these phenotypes, a Bayesian non-parametric approach was applied using a Dirichlet Process Mixture Model (DPMM) in the two-dimensional spectral embedding space (dirichletprocess package, R). A multivariate normal base distribution was used, and the concentration parameter α was assigned a Gamma (1,1) hyperprior and updated during sampling, allowing the number of mixture components to be inferred from the data. The posterior was estimated using 1,000 Gibbs sampling iterations, and convergence was assessed from the trace of the number of active components and of α. Per-patient posterior assignment probabilities were obtained, and the DPMM-derived hard assignments were compared with the spectral solution to quantify agreement.

Outcome association analysis

For downstream analyses, final cluster membership was defined using the hard cluster labels obtained from the spectral clustering model. Cluster assignment was encoded as a binary variable, with Cluster 1 coded as 0 (reference) and Cluster 2 coded as 1; accordingly, estimates reported as “Cluster 2 vs. Cluster 1” represent the association of Cluster 2 relative to Cluster (1) The final cluster sizes were 220 patients in Cluster 1 and 115 patients in Cluster (2) The DPMM was used to support the robustness of the cluster structure; DPMM-derived assignment uncertainty was not propagated into the regression models.

Following phenotype identification, post-clustering regression analyses were performed to evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified clusters. Model choice was determined by outcome type: logistic regression was used for the binary in-hospital mortality endpoint, whereas multiple linear regression was used for total hospital stay and ICU stay, which were fully observed continuous durations without right-censoring in this retrospective cohort. Adjustment covariates were pre-specified on clinical grounds and limited to age, sex, and surgical intervention — established prognostic determinants in infective endocarditis and the principal potential confounders of the phenotype–outcome association. This parsimonious set was chosen to remain within accepted events-per-variable limits and to avoid overfitting, and deliberately excluded the clustering variables themselves, which are already summarized by cluster membership. The logistic model used a log-odds linear predictor with cluster assignment and the three covariates; coefficients were exponentiated and reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. For the linear models, both unadjusted and adjusted models were constructed, with the adjusted models including the same covariate set; results are reported as beta coefficients with standard errors. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and model performance was evaluated using discrimination and calibration for the logistic model and the coefficient of determination and residual diagnostics for the linear models (reported in the Results and in the footnote to Table 2).

Software

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) with the following packages: missForest (data imputation), cluster (Gower distance and PAM), clustMixType (K-Prototypes), kernlab (spectral clustering), dirichletprocess (Bayesian non-parametric validation), tableone (baseline comparisons), and rms (logistic regression).

Results

Phenotype discovery and cluster validation

Among the 335 patients included in the study, spectral clustering identified two distinct clusters. Cluster 1 included 220 patients (65.7%), whereas Cluster 2 included 115 patients (34.3%). Among the four benchmarked algorithms, spectral clustering showed the best internal validation performance and was selected as the final model (Silhouette width 0.1612, Dunn index 0.1938; full comparison in Supplementary Table 3).

Bayesian non-parametric validation using a DPMM independently identified two mixture components, confirming the cluster count obtained by spectral clustering. The DPMM-derived assignments were concordant with the spectral solution in 90.4% of patients (303 of 335; Cohen’s κ = 0.80; adjusted Rand index = 0.65), with discordant cases located near the inter-cluster boundary, supporting the robustness of the two-cluster structure. The resulting similarity relationships between patients were visualized using dimensionality reduction and network-based affinity representations (Fig. 2). Regarding comorbidities, diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease were significantly more common in Cluster 1 than in Cluster 2 (46.4% vs. 28.7%, p = 0.003; and 38.6% vs. 27.0%, p = 0.044, respectively). In contrast, prior cardiac device- or valve-related history was more prominent in Cluster 2, with markedly higher rates of pacemaker implantation (12.2% vs. 1.8%, p < 0.001), aortic valve replacement (37.4% vs. 3.2%, p < 0.001), mitral valve replacement (44.3% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001), and combined aortic plus mitral valve replacement (18.3% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001).

Blood culture results did not differ significantly between the clusters. However, some presenting symptoms showed significant variation: weight loss (15.5% vs. 7.0%, p = 0.040) and myalgia (25.0% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.005) were more frequent in Cluster 1, whereas dyspnea tended to be more common in Cluster 2, although this did not reach statistical significance (47.8% vs. 36.8%, p = 0.067).

Important differences were also observed in electrocardiographic and echocardiographic features. Cluster 2 was characterized by a substantially higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation (30.4% vs. 7.7%, p < 0.001) and paced rhythm (10.4% vs. 0.9%, p < 0.001), while sinus rhythm was more frequent in Cluster 1 (91.4% vs. 59.1%, p < 0.001). Cluster 2 also demonstrated larger left-sided cardiac dimensions, including higher LVEDD (5.2 ± 0.8 vs. 5.0 ± 0.6 cm, p = 0.033), LVESD (3.5 ± 0.9 vs. 3.2 ± 0.6 cm, p = 0.002), and left atrial diameter (4.5 ± 0.9 vs. 4.0 ± 0.6 cm, p < 0.001), as well as higher PASP values (42.1 ± 14.0 vs. 37.4 ± 11.8 mmHg, p = 0.002).

Vegetation characteristics differed markedly between the two clusters. Cluster 1 was predominantly associated with native valve involvement, with native valve vegetations observed in 96.8% of patients compared with 9.6% in Cluster 2 (p < 0.001). In contrast, Cluster 2 was strongly associated with prosthetic valve and device-related infective endocarditis, showing much higher rates of prosthetic valve vegetation (73.0% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001), lead vegetation (9.6% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001), catheter vegetation (8.7% vs. 2.3%, p = 0.015), and local complications (6.1% vs. 0.0%, p < 0.001). In addition, larger vegetations (≥ 10 mm) were more frequently observed in Cluster 1 (77.7% vs. 64.3%, p = 0.013).

Medication use also reflected these cluster differences. Oral anticoagulant use was markedly more common in Cluster 2 (35.7% vs. 4.1%, p < 0.001), whereas ASA (22.3% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.021) and clopidogrel (6.8% vs. 0.9%, p = 0.031) were more frequently used in Cluster 1 (Supplementary Table 3). Among antimicrobial therapies, cephalosporin use was higher in Cluster 1 (58.2% vs. 46.1%, p = 0.046), while rifampicin (20.9% vs. 8.2%, p = 0.002) and gentamicin (43.5% vs. 28.6%, p = 0.009) were more commonly used in Cluster 2 (Table 1).

Laboratory analyses showed that Cluster 1 had a more unfavorable inflammatory and renal profile, with lower hemoglobin (9.8 [8.7–10.6] g/dL vs. 10.1 [9.1–10.9] g/dL, p = 0.003), lower hematocrit (30.2 [27.0–32.2] % vs. 31.5 [28.7–33.4] %, p < 0.001), lower albumin (32.0 [28.0–37.0] vs. 34.0 [28.1–38.0] g/L, p = 0.018), higher urea levels (49.5 [32.1–85.2] vs. 41.2 [29.0–61.9] mg/dL, p = 0.015), higher creatinine levels (1.10 [0.80–2.82] vs. 0.92 [0.77–1.43] mg/dL, p = 0.008), and higher BNP concentrations (6965 [4264–12770] vs. 5712 [3065–8776] pg/mL, p = 0.001). Sedimentation rate was also higher in Cluster 1 (65.6 [55.0–76.0] vs. 54.2 [41.5–67.6] mm/h, p < 0.001). By contrast, Cluster 2 had higher lymphocyte counts (1.76 [1.17–2.29] vs. 1.55 [1.10–2.02] ×10⁹/L, p = 0.019), higher INR (2.14 [1.79–2.45] vs. 1.43 [1.20–1.59], p < 0.001), and higher APTT values (32.8 [31.3–34.9] vs. 31.4 [29.5–32.8] seconds, p < 0.001).

Outcome associations of the identified clusters

Despite these baseline differences, in-hospital mortality, 1-year mortality, ICU stay, total hospital stay, surgery rate, and acute kidney injury did not significantly differ between the two clusters in the unadjusted comparisons.

The clinical relevance of the identified clusters was evaluated using logistic regression for in-hospital mortality and multiple linear regression for total hospital stay and ICU stay duration (Table 2). Cluster assignment was not associated with in-hospital mortality in either the unadjusted (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.59–1.54, p = 0.840) or adjusted models (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.59–1.56, p = 0.860). Increasing age was the only independent predictor of in-hospital mortality (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, p = 0.039). Multicollinearity was negligible across all models (variance inflation factors 1.00–1.04). The logistic model for in-hospital mortality showed adequate calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.62) but limited discrimination (AUC = 0.58), consistent with the null association between phenotype and mortality. The linear models explained a small proportion of outcome variance (total hospital stay, R² = 0.023; ICU stay, R² = 0.095). Because residuals departed from normality, the linear models were re-estimated with heteroscedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors; under this specification, the association between Cluster 2 and total hospital stay was attenuated to borderline significance (p = 0.052).

Regarding hospitalization duration, patients in Cluster 2 had a longer total hospital stay. In the adjusted linear regression model controlling for age, sex, and surgery status, Cluster 2 remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization (β = 3.95 days, p = 0.035).

In contrast, cluster assignment was not associated with ICU stay duration in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses. In the adjusted model, older age (p = 0.044), male sex (p = 0.042), and surgical treatment (p = 0.019) were identified as independent predictors of longer ICU stay.

Discussion

In this study, we applied an unsupervised ML approach using spectral clustering to explore potential phenotypes among patients with IE. Our analysis identified two distinct clusters with different clinical and echocardiographic characteristics, reflecting the marked heterogeneity of the disease.

These clusters appear to represent two clinically distinct presentations of IE. Patients in the first cluster were largely characterized by native valve endocarditis, whereas the second cluster included a higher proportion of prosthetic valve and device-related infections. Device-related endocarditis has increasingly emerged as an important subtype of IE due to the expanding use of cardiac implantable electronic devices and prosthetic valves in aging populations [7–9]. The resulting clusters were largely distinguished by valve and device involvement, suggesting that these features contribute substantially to the phenotypic heterogeneity of IE rather than implying that valve type alone defines distinct disease entities.

The modest internal validation metrics should also be considered when interpreting the clustering results. Although spectral clustering showed the best performance among the tested algorithms, the absolute Silhouette and Dunn index values indicated relatively limited separation between clusters. The modest separation metrics likely reflect the biological complexity of IE rather than a failure of the clustering approach, suggesting that IE may exist along a clinical continuum—in which native valve, prosthetic valve, device-related involvement, comorbidity burden, inflammatory activation, and cardiac remodeling overlap to varying degrees—rather than as sharply discrete disease categories. Therefore, the identified clusters should not be interpreted as rigid disease classes. Instead, they represent data-driven, clinically interpretable phenotypic patterns within a heterogeneous IE population. Despite modest separation metrics, the clusters remained clinically meaningful because they captured recognizable differences in valve/device involvement, rhythm profile, vegetation distribution, cardiac dimensions, and antimicrobial treatment patterns. These findings support the use of clustering as an exploratory phenotyping tool rather than as a definitive classification system.

Despite these phenotypic differences, our analysis did not demonstrate significant differences between clusters in mortality or the need for surgery in the unadjusted model, and only a modest, borderline association with hospital stay emerged after adjustment. Several factors may explain this observation. Outcomes in IE are influenced by a complex interplay of variables, including microorganism type, host immune response, timing of diagnosis, antimicrobial therapy and surgical management. Therefore, mortality and other adverse outcomes are unlikely to be determined solely by structural or phenotypic differences captured by clustering algorithms, and the absence of mortality separation may indicate that these phenotypes primarily reflect baseline disease presentation rather than independent short-term mortality risk.

In addition, in our cohort, vegetation size tended to be larger in patients with native valve involvement, who also exhibited a higher burden of comorbidities. Although prosthetic valve–related IE is generally associated with worse prognosis in previous studies [22, 23], the presence of larger vegetations and a higher comorbidity burden in the native valve phenotype in our dataset may partially explain the absence of major outcome differences between clusters. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that the prognostic impact of valve type may diminish after adjustment for baseline clinical characteristics. For instance, in a multicenter analysis by Weber et al., no significant difference in mortality was observed between prosthetic and native valve endocarditis after adjustment [24]. These findings suggest that the prognostic impact of valve type may be context-dependent and influenced by other coexisting factors.

In the adjusted analysis, increasing age emerged as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality. This observation is consistent with prior epidemiological studies, highlighting that outcomes in IE are predominantly driven by patient-related factors, rather than valve type or microbiological etiology alone [6]. In addition, Cluster 2 was independently associated with longer hospitalization, although this association became borderline after robust standard-error estimation and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. If confirmed in larger cohorts, this finding may reflect the greater clinical complexity of the prosthetic valve and device-related phenotype, which often requires more extensive diagnostic evaluation and multidisciplinary management. Although direct cost data were unavailable, prolonged hospitalization may serve as an indirect marker of healthcare resource utilization; accordingly, cluster assignment may help clinicians anticipate prolonged hospitalization and coordinate multidisciplinary care, rather than predict mortality risk.

The absence of an association between cluster assignment and ICU stay further supports the interpretation that ICU utilization may be driven less by baseline phenotypic grouping and more by acute clinical severity and management-related factors. In this cohort, older age, male sex, and surgical treatment were independently associated with longer ICU stay, suggesting that conventional clinical variables reflecting patient vulnerability, perioperative complexity, and short-term clinical management may be more informative for ICU resource use than the ML-derived phenotype itself. This distinction also indicates that the identified clusters may capture broader disease presentation and hospitalization complexity, whereas ICU stay is more closely linked to acute treatment course and procedural factors.

As noted in the Introduction, recent reviews have highlighted the potential of artificial intelligence and ML approaches to improve diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment planning in IE [25, 26], but the available studies have relied predominantly on supervised models aimed at predicting specific outcomes rather than on unsupervised phenotype discovery [14–16].

In contrast, unsupervised ML approaches aim to identify natural groupings within datasets without predefined outcome labels, thereby enabling the discovery of previously unrecognized disease phenotypes and providing further insight into disease heterogeneity. Building on this rationale, the present study applied such an approach directly to an IE cohort, an application that has to date remained uncommon in this specific disease setting.

Although clustering techniques have been used in other infectious diseases and critical care settings, their application in IE remains limited. For example, Guilamet et al. applied cluster analysis to identify clinical phenotypes among septic patients with bloodstream infections using variables related to patient characteristics, clinical presentation, and infection features [27]. However, that study focused on heterogeneous septic patients with bloodstream infections rather than specifically examining IE.

Compared with these previous studies, our work specifically focuses on patients with IE and applies a clustering framework to explore disease phenotypes using a comprehensive set of clinical, microbiological, echocardiographic, and laboratory variables. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the earliest applications of such a data-driven phenotyping approach specifically in an IE population. By doing so, our analysis aims to capture the intrinsic heterogeneity of IE beyond traditional classifications. Our findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and may provide a foundation for future studies investigating personalized risk stratification and phenotype-oriented management strategies in IE.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of the study may introduce selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified clusters and clinical outcomes. Second, although the study included patients from three centers, the findings may still have limited generalizability to other healthcare systems or patient populations with different clinical characteristics and treatment practices. Third, although a comprehensive set of clinical, microbiological, echocardiographic, and laboratory variables was included in the clustering analysis, some potentially relevant factors such as detailed information on treatment timing, antimicrobial treatment duration, and other management-related variables were not incorporated into the model. The clustering results were derived from a single dataset without external validation; therefore, the stability and reproducibility of the identified clusters should be interpreted with caution. The relatively modest internal validation metrics (Silhouette and Dunn index) also indicate that the identified clusters should be interpreted as exploratory phenotypic patterns rather than definitive disease classifications. Furthermore, the overall sample size and number of observed events were relatively modest—particularly for ICU stay, which was available in only a subset of patients (n = 145)—which may have limited statistical power to detect associations between cluster assignment and clinical outcomes, especially for ICU stay duration. In addition, missing data were handled using MissForest imputation; as the validity of imputation depends on the underlying missingness mechanism, and a MAR mechanism was assumed while an MNAR mechanism cannot be entirely excluded, the imputed values should be interpreted with this consideration in mind. Finally, total hospital stay and ICU stay were modeled as continuous outcomes using linear regression. These length-of-stay measures could alternatively be analyzed as time-to-event or count outcomes; because in-hospital death both terminates and shortens hospitalization, it acts as a competing event, and competing-risk or time-to-event models represent a valid alternative analytical framing that future studies may adopt. Larger multicenter and prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to determine their clinical applicability.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study represents one of the earliest applications of an unsupervised clustering approach to patients with IE in a multicenter retrospective cohort. Although cluster assignment was not significantly associated with major clinical outcomes, two clinically distinct phenotypic patterns were identified. These findings highlight the marked heterogeneity of IE and suggest that data-driven phenotyping approaches may contribute to future personalized strategies for disease characterization and risk assessment. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate these phenotypes and determine their clinical applicability.
