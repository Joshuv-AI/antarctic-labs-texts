Exponential sums twisted by general arithmetic functions
Anji Dong, Nicolas Robles, Alexandru Zaharescu, Dirk Zeindler

Abstract
. We examine exponential sums of the form ∑ n ≤ X w ⁡ ( n ) ​ e ⁡ ( α ​ n k ) \sum_{n\leq X}w(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n^{k}) , for k = 1 , 2 k=1,2 , where α \alpha satisfies a generalized Diophantine approximation and where w w are different arithmetic functions that might be multiplicative, additive, or neither. A strategy is shown on how to bound these sums for a wide class of functions w w belonging within the same ecosystem. Using this new technology we are able to improve current results on minor arcs that have recently appeared in the literature of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. Lastly, we show how a bound on ∑ n ≤ X | μ ⁡ ( n ) | ​ e ⁡ ( α ​ n ) \sum_{n\leq X}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(\alpha n) can be used to study partitions asymptotics over squarefree parts and explain their connection to the zeros of the Riemann zeta-function.

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and results

Suppose that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. To put our results in context, we first set the following definition

(1.1)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\in\mathbb{P}\\
p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\leq X\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\alpha p_{1}\cdots p_{r})\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\operatorname{e}(x)=\exp(2\pi ix),

and where
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set of primes.
One of our central results is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Upsilon>0
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}},\quad(a,q)=1.

Then for any
X\geq 2,q\leq X
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle S_{r}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll_{r}\bigg(\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{2r}}}\max\{1,\Upsilon^{\frac{1}{2r}}\}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+X^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}\bigg)(\log X)^{3}.

We shall prove more general versions of this theorem as well as several related results involving exponential sums twisted by different functions of primes in the forthcoming sections. These twisted exponential sums can be thought of as belonging within the same ecosystem and the techniques we present can be repurposed for other types of exponential sums.

Bounds on exponential sums have several useful applications in different areas of number theory. One such notable application of a classic inequality in additive number theory takes place when
\Upsilon=r=1
in Theorem
1.1
and it is as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(Vinogradov estimate)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}

(a,q)=1

Then for any
X\geq 2

(1.4)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)\ll\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q}\right)(\log X)^{3}.

This was established by Vinogradov

1.2.
Discussion on the technique

We have chosen to present the partitions with weights associated to
|\mu|
for two main reasons.

First the function
|\mu|
is obviously multiplicative and a tempting course of action would have been to use the results in
[
24
]
, which are extremely general bounds for exponential sums associated to multiplicative functions with certain growth conditions. Our rationale is that there might be other situations where the exponential sum might be twisted, for example by a function
\beta
, say, that is either additive or neither multiplicative nor additive. In those instances, the strategy put forward is to examine the arithmetic properties of
\beta
to understand if it is the combination of other well understood arithmetic functions such as
\mu,\tau,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}},|\mu|,\log
etc. There are a handful of fundamental estimates, such as Vinogradov’s inequality, from which bounds for exponential sums may be achieved by suitable combinations. In general the results of this strategy are more than good enough to treat a wide range of minor arcs, however more tailored approaches could yield tighter bounds. In addition, our results do not impose growth conditions nor multiplicativity on
\beta
. If one were interested in minor arcs associated to
\mu_{\mathbb{P}}
(neither multiplicative nor additive) or
\omega
(additive), then Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
, respectively, would be the key results to use.

The idea introduced in this paper can be applied to other interesting exponential sums.
For example, let
\Omega
be the total number of prime factors of
n
, and let
P(s)
be the prime zeta function.
Since

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)\sum_{k\geq 1}P(ks),

for
\real(s)>1
, we can write
\Omega(n)=\sum_{k\geq 1}(\mathbf{1}*\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{*k}})(n)
.
We may then apply the ideas from Theorem
1.8
to this decomposition to obtain a bound on
\sum_{n\leq X}\Omega(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)
, see also
[
15
]
.
In this way, it is likely that the technique will allow bounding exponential sums associated with a wide range of additive functions.

Other more exotic examples involve the following functions
[
31
,
\mathsection
1.2]
. Let
f_{k}(n)
denote the number of representations of
n
as a product of
k
factors, each greater than unity when
n>1
, the order of the factors being essential. The Dirichlet series for
f_{k}
is
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}f_{k}(n)n^{-s}=(\zeta(s)-1)^{k}
for
\real(s)>1
. Using the binomial expansion, we see that we can write
f_{k}
as a convolution of
\tau
functions, and therefore we may bound the exponential sum twisted by
f_{k}
. The same can be said about
g(n)
, the number of representations of
n
as a product of factors greater than unity, representations with factors in a different order being considered as distinct. If we let
f(1)=1
, then
f(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(n)
and therefore the Dirichlet series is
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)n^{-s}=(2-\zeta(s))^{-1}
for
\real(s)>\alpha_{0}
where
\zeta(\alpha_{0})=2
. In this case, a fractional binomial expansion would also yield combinatorial sums involving
\mu
, and hence, in principle, we could also bound the exponential sum associated to
f
.

Before discussing the second reason, we also mention that our technology also allows us to bound sums with
\operatorname{e}(\alpha P(n))
where
P
is a suitable polynomial. In particular, we could have chosen to study the partitions
\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|,2}
associated to

(1.21)

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|,2}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{n^{2}})^{-|\mu(n)|}.

The function
\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|,2}(n)
can be interpreted combinatorially as the number of partitions of
n
, where each part is a square of a square free integer, e.g.
4
is allowed but
16
is not.
The critical element for the extraction of these partitions would then be Theorem
1.10
.

The second reason is that unlike
\mu
, the function
|\mu|
only takes values
0
and
1
. Therefore,
|\mu|
does not exhibit the same oscillations as
\mu
. As a result, in
[
1
]
the authors achieved
\log\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}\ll\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{-B}
for any fixed
B>0
and all
n\geq 2
. In our present case, we are now able to obtain partition asymptotics of
\log\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|}
with a main term as well as an error term as seen in Theorem
1.11
.

, and

are equivalent to the statement that

, where

. The notation

\lim_{x\to a}f(x)/g(x)=0
and

\lim_{x\to a}f(x)/g(x)=1
. Dyadic sums are represented by
\sum_{n\sim N}f(n)=\sum_{N<n\leqslant 2N}f(n)
. The divisor function is denoted by
\tau(n)
. The
k
-fold divisor function

is defined by the coefficients of the Dirichlet series

\zeta^{k}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}
for

. The notation

indicates that the arithmetic function

is convolved with itself

\Psi(x)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(x)
. The Greek character
\rho

\varpi
. The Latin character

We begin by proving the

Lemma 2.1

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Upsilon>0
such that
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}}
with
(a,q)=1
. For any
X\geq 2
, one has

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X\max\{1,\sqrt{2\Upsilon}\}}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q}\bigg)(\log X)^{3}.

The same bound holds for
\widetilde{S}_{1}(\alpha,X)
with
(\log X)^{3}
replaced by
(\log X)^{4}
.

Proof.

We only give the proof for
S_{1}(\alpha,X)
since the proof for
\widetilde{S}_{1}(\alpha,X)
is almost identical.
Dirichlet’s approximation theorem asserts that for any real
\alpha
and for any
M\geq 1
, there exists a rational number
a_{1}/q_{1}
, where
1\leq q_{1}\leq M
, and
(a_{1},q_{1})=1
, such that
\lvert\alpha-a_{1}/q_{1}\rvert\leq 1/(q_{1}M)\leq 1/q_{1}^{2}
.
Choosing
M=2q
implies there exists
1\leq q_{1}\leq 2q
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with
(a_{1},q_{1})=1
and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2qq_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{q_{1}^{2}}.

Inserting
a_{1}/q_{1}
into Vinogradov’s bound, i.e Theorem
1.2
(or Theorem
1.3
with
\Upsilon=1
), yields

(2.2)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)=\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{q_{1}}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q_{1}}\right)(\log X)^{3}.

We now can have two cases,
a/q=a_{1}/q_{1}
and
a/q\neq a_{1}/q_{1}
.
If
a/q=a_{1}/q_{1}
, we conclude that
a_{1}=a
and
q_{1}=q
since both are in the reduced form.
This implies that

(2.3)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)=\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{X}\sqrt{q}\right)(\log X)^{3}.

Otherwise, if
a/q\neq a_{1}/q_{1}
, then using (
2.1
), we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{qq_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}-\frac{a}{q}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}-\alpha\right\rvert+\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2qq_{1}}+\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}}.

Subtract
1/(2qq_{1})
from both sides of (
2.4
), we have
1/(2qq_{1})\leq\Upsilon/q^{2}
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{q_{1}}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\Upsilon}}{\sqrt{q}}.

Inserting this into (
2.2
) implies

(2.5)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\alpha,X)\ll\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{2\Upsilon}X}{\sqrt{q}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2q}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}.

Therefore, combining (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), we get our desired result.
∎

Before stating the bounds we wish to show on
S_{r}(\alpha,X)
and
\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)
we need to introduce two key auxiliary results that will be critical in the subsequent proofs.

Lemma 2.2

(Generalized bilinear estimate)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Upsilon>0
such that

(a,q)=1
.
Further, let
(\xi_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be two real sequences with

and
|\eta_{n}|\leq 1
.
Then one has for all

that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq X\\
m>M,n>N\end{subarray}}\xi_{m}\eta_{n}\operatorname{e}(\alpha mn)\ll\left(\frac{X\max\{1,\Upsilon\}}{q}+\frac{X}{M}+\frac{X}{N}+q\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}X^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log X)^{2}.

The proof of Lemma
2.2
for
\Upsilon=1
can be found in
[
18
, Lemma 13.8]
and the extension to
\Upsilon>0
follows from the same principles that appeared in the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Furthermore, we shall introduce in the proof of Theorem
1.1
some helpful parameters
M
and
N
.
After deducing upper bounds for the occurring terms as a function of
M
and
N
, we have to choose these parameters
in a suitable way to minimize the resulting upper bounds. To accomplish this optimization, we use the following useful lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
7
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Let
F
,
G_{0}
,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be continuous, real valued functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
F
is strictly decreasing and all
G_{i}
are increasing.
Further, suppose that for
i=0,1,2

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=\lim_{x\to 0}G_{i}(x)=0\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{x\to 0}F(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty}G_{i}(x)=\infty.

Set
G(x):=\max\{G_{0}(x),G_{1}(x),G_{2}(x)\}
and
H(x):=\max\{F(x),G(x)\}
.
We then have

(2.7)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in(0,\infty)}H(x)=F(\min\{U_{0},U_{1},U_{2}\})=G(\min\{U_{0},U_{1},U_{2}\}),

where
U_{i}
is the solution of the equation

for

.

The parameter

runs in (

) over all values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. However, we have to impose in the proof of Theorem
1.1
that
x\leq X
.
Equation (
2.7
) is also correct in this situation as long as
\min\{U_{0},U_{1},U_{2}\}\leq X
because

G(\min\{U_{0},U_{1},U_{2}\})\leq G(X)
.

Equipped with these tools, we may now prove Theorem
1.1
. We do this in a slightly more general setting.
For this, let
f
be an arithmetic function.
Define for
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

(2.8)

\displaystyle S_{r,f}(\alpha,X):=\sum_{n\leq X}f^{*r}(n)\operatorname{e}(n\alpha).

Assume there exists is an
\eta\geq 0
such that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle f^{*r}(X)\ll(\log X)^{r\eta}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\sum_{n\leq X}|f|^{*r}(n)\ll_{r}X(\log X)^{\eta r}

for
X\geq 2
.
Abel summation immediately implies that we have for all

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}|f|^{*r}(n)n^{-y}\ll_{r}X^{1-y}(\log X)^{\eta r}.

Also assume that for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,

with

and

,
we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle S_{1,f}(\alpha,X)\ll(\log X)^{3+r\eta}(Xq^{-\frac{1}{2}}+X^{\frac{4}{5}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f
be as above.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Upsilon>0
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}},\quad(a,q)=1.

We then have for any
X\geq 2
and
r\in\mathbb{N}

(2.13)

\displaystyle S_{r,f}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll_{r}(\log X)^{3+r\eta}(Xq^{-\frac{1}{2r}}\max\{1,\Upsilon^{\frac{1}{2r}}\}+X^{\frac{2+2r}{3+2r}}+X^{\frac{2r-1}{2r}}q^{\frac{1}{2r}}).

We now give a more compact formulation of (
2.13
).
We define for
r\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\beta_{0}(r)=1,&\quad\beta_{1}(r)=\frac{2+2r}{3+2r},&\quad\beta_{2}(r)=\frac{2r-1}{2r},\\
\gamma_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2r},&\quad\gamma_{1}(r)=0,&\quad\gamma_{2}(r)=0,\\
\delta_{0}(r)=-\frac{1}{2r},&\quad\delta_{1}(r)=0,&\quad\delta_{2}(r)=\frac{1}{2r}.\end{array}

With these, we can rewrite (
2.13
) for
\Upsilon\geq 1
as

(2.17)

\displaystyle S_{r,f}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll(\log X)^{3+r\eta}\sum_{j=0}^{2}X^{\beta_{j}(r)}\Upsilon^{\gamma_{j}(r)}q^{\delta_{j}(r)}.

The advantage of (
2.17
) over (
2.13
) is that it is easier to handle within complex calculations. In particular, one can often handle all three summands in (
2.13
) at once, rather than having to look at each one separately.
This is the case, for example, in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
Further, we will use in the proof that each
\beta_{j}(r)
is an increasing function in
r
and that each
\beta_{j}(r)
fulfils the recurrence relation

(2.18)

\displaystyle\beta_{j}(r+1)=\frac{2+2\gamma_{j}-\beta_{j}(r)}{3+2\gamma_{j}-2\beta_{j}(r)}.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

For
q\geq X
, the bound in Theorem
2.1
is larger than
the trivial bound we get from (
2.9
). Thus we can assume that
q\leq X
.
We prove Theorem
2.1
by induction over

.
The case
r=1
and
\Upsilon=1
is true by (

).
The case
r=1
and
\Upsilon>0
follows with the same argument as in the proof of Lemma

.
Thus, Theorem
2.1
holds for

.

Thus we assume that Theorem
2.1
holds for all
s\leq r-1

.
We now need to show that the theorem also holds for
r
.
It is sufficient to consider the case
\Upsilon=1
, since we can use the same argument as in the proof of Theorem

to derive the case
\Upsilon>0
.
Note that we require within the proof that the theorem hold for

with

Inserting the definition of the Dirichlet convolution, we see that

(2.19)

\displaystyle S_{r,f}(\alpha,X)=\sum_{u\leq X}f^{*r}(u)\operatorname{e}(u\alpha)=\sum_{mn\leq X}f^{*(r-1)}(m)f(n)\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha).

We now split

For this, let

We will determine them below to optimize our estimate.
We write

(2.20)

\displaystyle S_{r,f}(\alpha,X)=S_{r,1}(\alpha,X)+S_{r,2}(\alpha,X)+S_{r,3}(\alpha,X)-S_{r,4}(\alpha,X)

with the

\displaystyle S_{r,1}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq X\\
m>M,n>N\end{subarray}}f^{*(r-1)}(m)f(n)\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha),

\displaystyle\quad S_{r,2}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq X\\

\displaystyle S_{r,3}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq X\\
m\leq M\end{subarray}}f^{*(r-1)}(m)f(n)\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha),

\displaystyle\quad S_{r,4}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq X\\
m\leq M,n\leq N\end{subarray}}f^{*(r-1)}(m)f(n)\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha).

We begin by giving an upper bound for
S_{r,1}(\alpha,X)
with Lemma
2.2
.
To apply Lemma
2.2
, we define

(2.21)

\displaystyle\xi_{m}:=f^{*(r-1)}(m)\mathbf{1}_{m\leq X}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\eta_{n}:=f(n)\mathbf{1}_{n\leq X},

where
\mathbf{1}_{m\leq X}
and

Equation (
2.9
) now implies that

\xi_{m}\ll(\log X)^{\eta(r-1)}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\eta_{n}\ll(\log X)^{\eta}.

Combining this with Lemma

\displaystyle S_{r,1}(\alpha,X)

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{X}{M}+\frac{X}{N}+\frac{X}{q}+q\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}X^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log X)^{2+\eta r}

\displaystyle\ll X(\log X)^{2+\eta r}M^{-\frac{1}{2}}+X(\log X)^{2+\eta r}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}+X(\log X)^{2+\eta r}q^{-\frac{1}{2}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log X)^{2+\eta r}q^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It remains to take care of the first two summands in (

More precisely, we set

\displaystyle S_{r,2,N}(\alpha,X)

N
.
Thus we can optimize

.

\displaystyle S_{r,4}(\alpha,X)

Xq^{-\frac{1}{6}}+X^{\frac{33}{38}}+X^{\frac{5}{6}}q^{\frac{1}{6}}
.
Next we study
S_{3}
. We write

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq M}\widetilde{\mu}_{2}(m)\sum_{m\leq X/n}\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leq M}|\widetilde{\mu}_{2}(m)|\bigg|\sum_{m\leq X/n}\operatorname{e}(nm\alpha)\bigg|

(4.22)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leq M}\min\left\{\frac{X}{n},\frac{1}{\|n\alpha\|}\right\}\ll\bigg(M+q+\frac{X}{q}\bigg)\log X.

We have to solve the equations
XM^{-\frac{1}{2}}=M,XM^{-\frac{1}{2}}=Xq^{-1}
and
XM^{-\frac{1}{2}}=q
.
The solutions are
M_{0}=X^{\frac{2}{3}},M_{1}=q^{2}
and
M_{2}=X^{2}q^{-2}
, respectively.
Inserting these, we get
X^{\frac{2}{3}}+Xq^{-1}+q
.
Combining all these results, we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{6}}}+X^{\frac{33}{38}}+X^{\frac{5}{6}}q^{\frac{1}{6}}+X^{\frac{2}{3}}+\frac{X}{q}+q+\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)(\log X)^{10}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{6}}}+X^{\frac{33}{38}}+X^{\frac{5}{6}}q^{\frac{1}{6}}\bigg)(\log X)^{10}.

While this is certainly a useful bound that could be used to deal with the minor arcs arising from partitions weighted by
|\mu|
as we shall in Section
6
, we can actually substantially improve this bound to (
1.17
), i.e. the one shown in the statement of Theorem
1.9
. To accomplish this improvement we shall first to state and prove need an auxiliary result which is both a generalization as well as an improvement of a lemma of Mikawa
[
22
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
2\leq M,J\leq x
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have that

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle:=M\sum_{m\sim M}\sum_{j\sim J}\tau_{k}(j)\min\left\{\frac{x}{m^{2}j},\frac{1}{||\alpha m^{2}j||}\right\}\ll M^{2}J(\log x)^{k}+x^{3/4}\bigg(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q\bigg)^{1/4}(\log x)^{k+3}.

Proof.

Denote
\log x
by
L
. For
H>2
, we have the Fourier expansion

\min(H,||\theta||^{-1})=\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{h}\operatorname{e}(\theta h),

where
w_{h}=w_{h}(H)\ll\min(\log H,\frac{H}{|h|},\frac{H^{2}}{h^{2}}).
Put
H=x(M^{2}J)^{-1}
. By (
4.8
), we have

\sum_{j\sim J}\tau_{k}(j)\ll J(\log J)^{k-1}\ll JL^{k-1}.

Therefore, if
H\leq 2
, we trivially have that

G\ll M\sum_{m\sim M}\sum_{j\sim J}\tau_{k}(j)\ll M^{2}JL^{k-1}.

Now for
H>2
, we may use the above expansion to obtain

\min\bigg\{H,\frac{1}{||\alpha m^{2}j||}\bigg\}=O(L)+\sum_{0<|h|<H^{2}}w_{h}\operatorname{e}(\alpha m^{2}jh).

Substituting this into
G
, we see that

(4.24)

\displaystyle G\ll M^{2}JL^{k}+M\sum_{0<|h|<H^{2}}|w_{h}|\sum_{j\sim J}\tau_{k}(j)\left\lvert\operatorname{e}(\alpha m^{2}jh)\right\rvert=m^{2}JL^{k}+F.

By
[
22
,
\mathsection
4]
we can write the bound

\left\lvert\operatorname{e}(\alpha m^{2}jh)\right\rvert^{2}\ll M+\sum_{g\leq 2M}\min\bigg\{M,\frac{1}{||\alpha gjh||}\bigg\},

this implies that

\displaystyle F^{2}

\displaystyle\ll M^{2}\sum_{k\leq H^{2}}|w_{k}|\sum_{l\sim J}\tau_{k}(l)^{2}\sum_{h\leq H^{2}}|w_{h}|\sum_{j\sim J}\left|\operatorname{e}(\alpha m^{2}jh)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll M^{2}HL\sum_{l\sim J}\tau_{2k-1}(l)\sum_{h\leq H^{2}}|w_{h}|\sum_{j\sim J}\left|\operatorname{e}(\alpha m^{2}jh)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll M^{2}HJL^{2k-1}\bigg\{HJML+\sum_{h\leq H^{2}}\min\bigg(\log H,\frac{H}{h}\bigg)\sum_{j\sim J}\sum_{g\leq 2M}\min\bigg(M,\frac{1}{||\alpha gjh||}\bigg)\bigg\}

(4.25)

\displaystyle=xL^{2k-1}\bigg\{\frac{x}{M}L+E\bigg\},

where for the
E
term we have

E\ll L\max_{1\leq T\ll H}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{h\leq 2HT}\sum_{j\sim J}\sum_{g\leq 2M}\min\bigg(M,\frac{1}{||\alpha gjh||}\bigg).

Using again
[
22
,
\mathsection
4]
,
E\ll x^{1/2}(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q)^{1/2}L^{6}.
Therefore, combining this with (
4.25
), we get

\displaystyle F^{2}\ll xL^{2k-1}\bigg\{\frac{x}{M}L+x^{1/2}\bigg(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q\bigg)^{1/2}L^{6}\bigg\}\ll\frac{x^{2}L^{2k}}{M}+x^{3/2}\bigg(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q\bigg)^{1/2}L^{2k+5}.

Thus, combining this with (
4.24
), we see that

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\ll m^{2}JL^{k}+\frac{xL^{k}}{M^{1/2}}+x^{3/4}\bigg(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q\bigg)^{1/4}L^{k+3}\ll m^{2}JL^{k}+x^{3/4}\bigg(\frac{x}{q}+\frac{x}{M}+q\bigg)^{1/4}L^{k+3},

which was the last ingredient of the proof.
∎

We shall also make use of the following well-known result
[
18
,
\mathsection
13]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in Z
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}
with
(a,q)=1
, then

\sum_{m\leq M}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{m},\frac{1}{||m\alpha||}\bigg\}\ll\bigg(M+\frac{X}{q}+q\bigg)\log(2qMX).

We are now ready to proceed with the proof of the improved bound of the exponential sum twisted by
|\mu|
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Noticing that
\mu^{2}(x)=\sum_{ab^{2}=x}\mu(b)
we write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}\mu^{2}(n)\operatorname{e}(n\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{ab^{2}=n}\mu(b)\operatorname{e}(n\alpha)=\sum_{b^{2}\leq X}\sum_{a\leq X/b^{2}}\mu(b)\operatorname{e}(ab^{2}\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\leq X^{1/2}}\mu(b)\sum_{a\leq X/b^{2}}\operatorname{e}(ab^{2}\alpha)\ll\sum_{b\leq X^{1/2}}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}.

We break the range of
b
into two parts: for large
b
’s, we will use the bound from Lemma
4.4
, whereas for small
b
’s, we will use Lemma
4.5
. This means that

(4.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{b\leq X^{1/2}}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}=\bigg(\sum_{b\leq B}+\sum_{B<b\leq X^{1/2}}\bigg)\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}.

Applying Lemma
4.5
, we get

(4.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{b\leq B}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leq B^{2}}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{m},\frac{1}{||m\alpha||}\bigg\}\ll\bigg(B^{2}+\frac{X}{q}+q\bigg)(\log X).

Next,by Lemma
4.4
dyadically with
k=1
, we have

(4.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{B<b\leq X^{1/2}}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{B<b\leq X^{1/2}}\sum_{m\leq 2}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}m},\frac{1}{||b^{2}m\alpha||}\bigg\}

(4.29)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(X^{1/2}+\frac{X}{q^{1/4}B}+\frac{X}{B^{5/4}}+\frac{X^{3/4}q^{1/4}}{B}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}.

Using Lemma
2.3
, combining (
4.27
) and (
4.29
), we choose

F(B)=\frac{B^{2}}{(\log X)^{3}},\quad G_{1}(B)=\frac{X}{q^{1/4}B},\quad G_{2}(B)=\frac{X}{B^{5/4}}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad G_{3}(B)=\frac{X^{3/4}q^{1/4}}{B},

then we get the bound

(4.30)

\displaystyle\frac{B^{2}}{(\log X)^{3}}\ll\frac{X^{2/3}}{q^{1/6}\log X}+\frac{X^{8/13}}{(\log X)^{15/13}}+\frac{X^{1/2}q^{1/6}}{\log X},

and thus we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(n\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{X\log X}{q}+q\log X+X^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log X)^{4}+\bigg(\frac{X^{\frac{2}{3}}}{q^{\frac{1}{6}}\log X}+\frac{X^{\frac{8}{13}}}{(\log X)^{\frac{15}{13}}}+\frac{X^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{6}}}{\log X}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{X}{q}+q\bigg)\log X+X^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log X)^{4}+\frac{X^{\frac{2}{3}}}{q^{\frac{1}{6}}}(\log X)^{3}+X^{\frac{8}{13}}(\log X)^{\frac{37}{13}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{6}}(\log X)^{3}

(4.31)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{X}{q}\log X+X^{\frac{8}{13}}(\log X)^{\frac{37}{13}}+q\log X,

as it was to be shown.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

In the statement of Lemma
1.9
, the bound will be worse than the trivial bound if
q\geq X
. Note that in (
4.31
) if
q\in(0,X^{5/13})
, then
X/q
dominates; if
q\in(X^{5/13},X^{8/13})
, then
X^{8/13}(\log X)^{\frac{37}{13}}
dominates; and if
q\in(X^{8/13},X)
, then
q\log X
dominates.
In (
4.27
), if we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{b\leq B}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{b^{2}},\frac{1}{||b^{2}\alpha||}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leq B^{2}}\min\bigg\{\frac{X}{m},\frac{1}{||m\alpha||}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(X\sum_{m\leq B^{2}}\frac{1}{m}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\bigg(B^{2}+\frac{X}{q}+q\bigg)(\log X)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.32)

\displaystyle\ll X^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(B+\frac{X^{\frac{1}{2}}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}+q^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)(\log X)^{2}=\bigg(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B+\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}+X^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)(\log X)^{2}.

Then again using Lemma
2.3
, we will have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(n\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X^{3/4}}{q^{1/8}}+X^{13/18}+X^{5/8}q^{1/8}+\frac{X}{q^{1/2}}+X^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}

(4.33)

\displaystyle\ll\bigg(\frac{X}{q^{1/2}}+X^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log X)^{4}.

Now, in (
4.33
) when
q\in(0,X^{1/2})
the term
\frac{X}{q^{1/2}}(\log X)^{4}
dominates, and when
q\in(X^{1/2},X)
the term
X^{1/2}q^{1/2}
dominates. Therefore, comparing (
4.31
) and (
4.33
), we can see that (
4.31
) is always better when
q\leq X
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.10
and additional results

As we saw in Section
2
, two critically important results were Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
with the additional refinement of being able to take
\Upsilon>0
into account. There exist other instances where being able to accommodate the parameter
\Upsilon
will be very helpful, especially if
\Upsilon
is not constrained to be greater than or equal to
1
. One such instance takes place when bounding exponential sums associated to the Liouville
\lambda(n)
function or the Jordan totient function
J_{k}(n)
.
To that end, we present the following generalization to Weyl’s bound, see
[
18
,
35
]
.

Lemma 5.1

(Generalized Weyl bound)
.

Let
M,N,a,q
be integers such that
(a,q)=1
and
q>0
. If
f
is a real polynomial of degree
k\geq 1
with leading coefficient
\alpha
such that
\lvert\alpha-a/q\rvert\leq\Upsilon q^{-2}
for some
\Upsilon>0
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\sum_{x=M+1}^{M+N}\operatorname{e}(f(x))\ll N^{1+\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{\max\{1,\Upsilon\}}{q}+\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{k-1}}+\frac{q}{N^{k}}\bigg)^{2^{1-k}}.

Proof.

Weyl’s inequality implies the case
\Upsilon=1
and the extension to
\Upsilon>0
follows the same principles as in the proof of Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Similarly, we may show the following stronger result which is only valid for
k=2
.

Lemma 5.2

(Generalized quadratic Weyl bound)
.

Let
M,N,a,q
be integers such that
(a,q)=1
and
q>0
. If
f(x)=\alpha x^{2}+\beta x+\gamma
such that
\alpha
such that
|\alpha-a/q|\leq\Upsilon q^{-2}
for some
\Upsilon>0
, then we have

\sum_{x=1}^{N}\operatorname{e}(f(x))\ll\frac{N}{\sqrt{q}}\max\{1,\Upsilon^{1/2}\}+\sqrt{N\log q}+\sqrt{q\log q}.

Proof.

The
\Upsilon=1
case can be found in
[
23
,
\mathsection
3, Theorem 1]
. The extension to
\Upsilon>0
follows the same guidelines as Lemma
2.1
.
∎

We can use these results to prove a bound for exponential sums twisted by
|\mu|
but with a polynomial in
P(n)
instead of
n
in
\operatorname{e}(\cdot)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Note that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}\mu^{2}(n)\operatorname{e}(n^{2}\alpha)=\sum_{b\leq X^{2}}f(b)\operatorname{e}(b\alpha)\quad\textnormal{where}\quad f(a)=\begin{cases}1,&\quad m=(p_{1}\cdots p_{r})^{2}\\
0,&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This can be seen by considering the Euler product of
f
as

\displaystyle F(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\bigg)\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\bigg)\bigg(1+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\bigg)^{-1}

(5.2)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{4s}}\bigg)\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\bigg)^{-1}=\frac{\zeta(2s)}{\zeta(4s)},

for
\real(s)>\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, we may write
f=g*h
, where

\displaystyle g(m)=\begin{cases}1,&\quad\mbox{if $m$ is a square}\\
0,&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad h(\ell)=\begin{cases}\mu(\ell^{1/4}),&\quad\mbox{if $\ell$ is a fourth power}\\
0,&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then (
5.1
) is equivalent to

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ell\leq X^{2}\\
m,\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha).

For generality, replace
X^{2}
by
Y
. Then, our goal is to bound the exponential sum

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ell\leq Y\\
m\geq 1,\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha),

where
\alpha,q
satisfy the minor arc condition
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq q^{-2}
and
(a,q)=1
. Since we are summing over the hyperbolic region
\Sigma:=\{m,\ell:m\ell\leq Y\}
, for arbitrary
L\geq 1
, let
M=Y/L
and define the following sets:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\{m,\ell:m\leq M,\ell\leq Y/m\},\quad\Sigma_{2}=\{m,\ell:\ell\leq L,m\leq Y/\ell\},\quad\Sigma_{3}=\{m,\ell:m\leq M,\ell\leq L\}.

Then,
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}-\Sigma_{3}
. The sum over
\Sigma_{1}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq M}\sum_{\ell\leq Y/m}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leq M}g(m)\sum_{u\leq(Y/m)^{1/4}}\mu(u)\operatorname{e}(mu^{4}\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll Y^{1/4}\sum_{v\leq\sqrt{M}}\frac{1}{v^{1/2}}\ll Y^{1/4}M^{1/4}.

The sum over
\Sigma_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq L}\sum_{m\leq Y/\ell}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\ell\leq L}h(\ell)\sum_{u\leq\sqrt{Y/\ell}}\operatorname{e}(u^{2}\ell\alpha).

Take
\Upsilon=\frac{\ell}{(\ell,q)^{2}}
in Lemma
5.2
, the above is then bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq L}h(\ell)\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{Y}}{\sqrt{q}}+\frac{Y^{1/4}}{\ell^{1/4}}\sqrt{\log q}+\sqrt{q\log q}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{v\leq L^{1/4}}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{Y}}{\sqrt{q}}+\frac{Y^{1/4}}{v}\sqrt{\log q}+\sqrt{q\log q}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\sqrt{Y}L^{1/4}}{\sqrt{q}}+Y^{1/4}\log Y\sqrt{\log q}+L^{1/4}\sqrt{q\log q}.

The third sum over
\Sigma_{3}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq L}\sum_{m\leq M}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)\ll\sum_{\ell\leq L}h(\ell)\sum_{u\leq M^{1/2}}\operatorname{e}(u^{2}\ell\alpha)\ll\sum_{v\leq L^{1/4}}M^{1/2}=M^{1/2}L^{1/4}.

Now setting
M=Y/L
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ell\leq Y\\
m,\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{Y^{1/2}}{L^{1/4}}+\frac{\sqrt{Y}L^{1/4}}{\sqrt{q}}+Y^{1/4}\log Y\sqrt{\log q}+L^{1/4}\sqrt{q\log q}.

Using Lemma
2.3
,
the final bound is

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ell\leq Y\\
m,\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{Y^{1/2}}{q^{1/4}}+Y^{1/4}\log Y(\log q)^{1/2}+Y^{1/4}q^{1/4}(\log q)^{1/4}.

Substituting
Y=X^{2}
, we finally conclude that

\sum_{n\leq X}\mu^{2}(n)\operatorname{e}(n^{2}\alpha)\ll\frac{X}{q^{1/4}}+X^{1/2}\log X(\log q)^{1/2}+X^{1/2}q^{1/4}(\log q)^{1/4},

as we wanted to show. This ends the proof.
∎

It is worth remarking that other approaches could been undertaken in order to achieve a bound for
\tilde{S}_{|\mu|}
. In
[
1
, Theorem 1.6]
it was shown that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}(\mu*g_{2})(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)\ll\bigg(\frac{X}{q^{1/2}}+X^{5/6}+X^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log X)^{5/2}

where
g_{2}
is the indicator function of squarefull numbers. The Dirichlet series for
\mu*g_{2}
corresponds to
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mu*g_{2})(n)n^{-s}=\frac{\zeta(2s)\zeta(3s)}{\zeta(s)\zeta(6s)}
. One such approach would be to consider
(\mu*q_{1})(n)
or
(|\mu|*q_{2})(n)
where
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{1}(n)n^{-s}=\frac{\zeta(2s)}{\zeta(s)\zeta(4s)}
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{2}(n)n^{-s}=\frac{\zeta(2s)^{2}}{\zeta(s)\zeta(4s)}
. This would involve using the Vaughan identity associated to
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. Alternatively, one could try to get a bound for
\sum_{n\leq X}\tilde{\mu}_{4}(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)
where
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\mu}_{4}(n)n^{-s}=(\zeta(4s))^{-1}
for
\real(s)>\frac{1}{4}
, much like in (
4.18
). This would also likely involve the use of the Vaughan identity. Moreover, one would also need a bound for
\sum_{n\leq X}j_{2}(n)\operatorname{e}(\alpha n)
where
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}j_{2}(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(2s)
for
\real(s)>\frac{1}{2}
. Then, by the use of the hyperbola/
\Upsilon
method, one convolves both bounds to get the desired final bound. These approaches will be the subject of future research.

By using Lemma
5.1
bound we may extend Theorem
1.10
, although the bound will not be as precise since Weyl’s inequality is weaker when dealing with a polynomial
P
of degree

.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}
with
(a,q)=1
. Let
X\geq 2,k>2,
and define

R(P_{k},\alpha,X):=\sum_{n\leq X}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(P(n)\alpha),

where
P_{k}(n)=c_{k}n^{k}+c_{k-1}n^{k-1}+\cdots+c_{0}
over
\mathbb{R}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle R(P_{k},\alpha,X)\ll\frac{X^{1/2+2^{2-k}+\varepsilon}}{q^{2^{1-k}}}+q^{2^{1-k}}X^{1/2+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
. In particular, for
k=3
, we have a slightly sharper bound, which is

R(P_{3},\alpha,X)\ll X^{1/2+\varepsilon}q^{1/6}+\frac{X^{1+\varepsilon}}{q^{1/4}},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1.10
except that we use Lemma
5.1
instead of Lemma
5.2
to bound the sum over
\Sigma_{2}
. We then see that the sum over
\Sigma_{2}
is bounded by

L^{2^{1-k}-1/4}q^{-2^{1-k}}Y^{1/2+\varepsilon}+L^{2^{-k}-1/4}Y^{-2^{-k}+1/2+\varepsilon}+L^{k2^{-k}-1/4}q^{2^{1-k}}Y^{-k2^{-k}+1/2+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Combining all bounds, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\ell\leq Y\\
m,\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}g(m)h(\ell)\operatorname{e}(m\ell\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{Y^{1/2}}{L^{1/4}}+L^{2^{1-k}-1/4}q^{-2^{1-k}}Y^{1/2+\varepsilon}+L^{2^{-k}-1/4}Y^{-2^{-k}+1/2+\varepsilon}

(5.6)

\displaystyle\quad+L^{k2^{-k}-1/4}q^{2^{1-k}}Y^{-k2^{-k}+1/2+\varepsilon}.

Using Lemma
2.3
when
k=3
implies that (
5.6
) is bounded by
Y^{1/4+\varepsilon}q^{1/6}+Y^{1/2+\varepsilon}q^{-1/4}
. When
k\geq 4
, (
5.6
) is bounded by

Y^{1/4}+q^{-2^{1-k}}Y^{1/4+2^{1-k}+\varepsilon}+Y^{1/4+\varepsilon}+q^{2^{1-k}}Y^{1/4+\varepsilon}\ll q^{-2^{1-k}}Y^{1/4+2^{1-k}+\varepsilon}+q^{2^{1-k}}Y^{1/4+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Substituting

, we get our desired result.
∎

As illustrated in Section

, it is likely that the bounds we have presented, while useful for most applications where demanding to very demanding savings are required, could further be improved by decreasing the exponents associated to
X,\log X
and
q
using specifically tailored arguments for the problem at hand. A useful tool in this direction will likely involve the Heath-Brown formula for decomposing
\mu
and
\Lambda
as well as the Selberg identity for
\Lambda_{2}
, see
[
16
]
and
[

,
\mathsection
13]
, both of which are advanced combinatorial decompositions of arithmetic functions. Indeed the Heath-Brown decomposition of
\Lambda
and
\mu
played a key role in the enlarging the size of the mollifier of the second moment of the Riemann zeta-function in
[

,
28
]
. In turn, these enlargements increased the proportion of non-trivial zeros of

on the critical line. Here we present an analogue for
{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}}(n)
that will become useful in future research.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N},x\geq 1
and
V\geq x^{1/k}
. For
n\leq x
, we have

{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}{k\choose j}{\mu_{\leq V}}^{*j}*\mathbf{1}^{*(j-1)}*\omega.

Proof.

Write
\mu=\mu_{\leq V}+\mu_{>V}
, then clearly the convolution

\mu_{>V}^{*k}*\mathbf{1}^{*k-1}*\omega

vanishes on
\mathbb{N}_{\leq x}
. Expanding out
\mu_{>V}=\mu-\mu_{\leq V}
and using the binomial formula, we get

(5.7)

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}{k\choose j}\mu^{*(k-j)}*{\mu_{\leq V}}^{*j}*\mathbf{1}^{*(k-1)}*\omega.

Using the identities
\mu*\omega={\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}}
and
\mu*1=\delta
, where
\delta
is the multiplicative identity of Dirichlet convolution, the
j=0
term of (
5.7
) becomes

\mu^{k}*\mathbf{1}^{*(k-1)}*\omega={\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}}
.
For all other terms of (
5.7
), we use again the identity
\mu*1=\delta
and write

\mu^{*(k-j)}*{\mu_{\leq V}}^{*j}*\mathbf{1}^{*(k-1)}*\omega={\mu_{\leq V}}^{*j}*\mathbf{1}^{*(j-1)}*\omega.

Combining all, (
5.7
) can be rewritten as in the statement of the lemma.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.11

Results regarding weighted or signed partitions can be found in recent literature
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
10
]
. The strategy consists in employing the Hardy-Littlewood circle method to extract the asymptotics of
\mathfrak{p}
. Generally, although not always, this process results in examining a contour integral over three distinct arcs. For brevity we shall provide the hardest ingredient for each arc and the reader is referred to the above mentioned literature for the remaining details.

Equation (
1.18
) can be rewritten as

\Psi(z)=\exp(\Phi(z))\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\Phi(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(n)|}{j}z^{jn}.

We may now use Cauchy’s integral formula to extract
\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|}
as

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|}(n)=\rho^{-n}\int_{0}^{1}\Psi(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha=\rho^{-n}\int_{0}^{1}\exp[\Phi(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))-2\pi in\alpha]d\alpha,

for any
0<\rho<1
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
be large. We choose the radius
\rho
to be
\rho=\rho(x)
so that
x=\rho\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)
.
Furthermore, we we can see that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\rho\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n|\mu(n)|\rho^{jn}.

Therefore,
\rho\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)
is a strictly monotonically increasing function on the interval
[0,1)
and
\lim_{\rho\to 1}\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)=\infty
.
Thus the relationship between
x
and
\rho
is well-defined and injective.

6.1.
Set up of the arcs

We now define the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}
and minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
as follows. For real
A>0
we set

(6.3)

\displaystyle\delta_{q}=q^{-1}X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad Q=(\log X)^{A}.

Moreover, for
1\leq a\leq q\leq Q
with
(a,q)=1
we define
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
to be the open interval

(6.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(q,a):=\bigg(\frac{a}{q}-\delta_{q},\frac{a}{q}+\delta_{q}\bigg).

The major arcs
\mathfrak{M}
and minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
are then defined by

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}=\bigcup_{1\leq q\leq Q}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}(q,a)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathfrak{m}=[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\backslash\mathfrak{M}.

We use the canonical splitting of (
6.1
) as

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|}(n)=\rho^{-n}\bigg(\int_{\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}+\int_{\mathfrak{M}\backslash\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}+\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\bigg)\exp[\Phi(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))-2\pi in\alpha]d\alpha.

For the first integral, we shall use contour integration and the saddle point method, for the second one we will use an adapted Siegel-Walfisz lemma associated to
|\mu|
, and for the third term we will exploit the bounds on exponential sums established earlier.

6.2.
The explicit formula

In order to prove the explicit formula of this section, we will require a contour integration involving the term
1/\zeta(2s)
. To prove our formula unconditionally, we shall use the following result due to the works of Ramachandra and Sankaranarayanan
[
29
, Theorem 2]
(see also
[
17
, Lemma 1]
and
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
). This result provides unconditional upper bounds for
1/\zeta(s)
inside the critical strip along a specific vertical line segment.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
T>0
be a sufficiently large positive number and
H=T^{1/3}
. Then one has

\displaystyle\min_{T\leqslant t\leqslant T+H}\max_{\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\sigma\leqslant 2}|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^{-1}\leqslant\exp(C(\log\log T)^{2})

with an absolute constant
C>0
. In particular, there exists a real
T_{*}\in[T,T+T^{1/3}]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(\sigma+iT_{*})}\leqslant T_{*}^{\varepsilon}\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\sigma\leqslant 2,

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

We shall define a parameter
X=(\log\frac{1}{\rho})^{-1}
so that
\rho=e^{-1/X}
as well as
\Delta:=(1+4\pi^{2}X^{2}\theta^{2})^{-1/2}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
X\geq 1
. If
X\Delta^{3}\geq 1
, then there exists a sequence
T_{\nu}
with
\nu\leq T_{\nu}\leq 2\nu
such that

(6.7)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}+\log\frac{X}{2\pi(1-2\pi iX\theta)}+\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}(\varpi)|<T_{\nu}}f(X,\theta,\varpi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(X,\theta,n),

where the functions
f
and
g
are given by

\displaystyle f(X,\theta,\varpi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\zeta^{\prime}(\varpi)}\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{\varpi/2}\Gamma\bigg(\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(1+\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg),

\displaystyle g(X,\theta,n)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\mathfrak{c}_{1}(n)\log\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}+\mathfrak{c}_{2}(n)\bigg)\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{-n}.

The coefficients
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
are computable constants defined in (
6.15
). The sum is taken over the non-trivial zeros
\varpi
of
\zeta(s)
under the assumption, for notational ease, of simplicity.

Remark 6.1
.

Unlike assuming the Riemann hypothesis, the assumption of the simplicity of the zeros does not represent a technical difficulty and it can be relaxed at the expense of cluttering the expression for
f(X,\theta,\varpi)
to the point of becoming unreadable, see
[
31
,
\mathsection
14.27]
‘obvious modifications are required if the zeros are not simple’.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

Recall that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(n)|}{j}\exp\bigg(-jn\bigg(\frac{1}{X}-2\pi i\theta\bigg)\bigg),

where
\rho=e^{-1/X}
. Applying the inverse Mellin transform of the exponential function we obtain

(6.9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(n)|}{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)j^{-s}n^{-s}\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{s}ds,

for
c>0
. The interchange of summation and integration is legal for any
c>1
and it leads to

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{s+1}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(n)|}{n^{s}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{s}ds

(6.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{s}ds.

We temporarily set
y=\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}
and proceed with a singularity analysis. The path of integration in (
6.10
) is the vertical line
\real(s)=c>1
. The technique is to now push the part
|t|\leq T
of this path to the left using a rectangular contour whose vertices are
c\pm iT
and
-u\pm iT
where
u=N+\frac{1}{2}
, for some large
N\in\mathbb{N}
in order to avoid the trivial zeros. The new contour of integration is therefore
\gamma=\cup_{i=1}^{5}\gamma_{i}
,where
\gamma_{1}=(c-i\infty,c-iT]
,
\gamma_{2}=[c-iT,-u-iT]
,
\gamma_{3}=[-u-iT,-u+iT]
,
\gamma_{4}=[-u+iT,c+iT]
and
\gamma_{5}=[c+iT,c+i\infty]
. Using the Cauchy residue theorem leads to

(6.11)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=R+\sum_{k=1}^{5}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{k}}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}ds=R+\sum_{k=1}^{5}I_{k},

where
R
is equal to the contribution of the residues at the singularities of the integrand in the region enclosed by the two paths of integration. We must now look at each integral separately and bound it and find the value of
R
.

We start with
R
. At
s=1
we have a simple pole coming from
\zeta(s)
for which we have

(6.12)

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{res}}\limits_{s=1}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}=y,

which will serve as leading term. At the non-trivial zeros
s=\varpi/2
we have poles which we assume to be simple for which

(6.13)

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{res}}\limits_{s=\varpi/2}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}=\frac{y^{\varpi/2}}{2\zeta^{\prime}(\varpi)}\Gamma\bigg(\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(1+\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg).

There is also a double pole from
\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)
at
s=0
which yields

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{res}}\limits_{s=0}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}=\log\frac{y}{2\pi}.

For the non-trivial zeros at
s=-n
for
n=1,2,3,\cdots
we also have double poles for which

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{res}}\limits_{s=-n}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n!}\frac{\zeta(1-n)\zeta(-n)}{\zeta^{\prime}(-2n)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\log y-\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{\zeta^{\prime}}(-2n)+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(1-n)+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(-n)+\psi(n+1)\right)y^{-n}

(6.15)

\displaystyle=:(\mathfrak{c}_{1}(n)\log y+\mathfrak{c}_{2}(n))y^{-n},

where, we recall,
\psi(x)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(x)
denotes the digamma function. We now have
R
.

Except for one part of the horizontal integral over
\gamma_{4}
, the rest of the analysis is straightforward. We shall make sure of
|y|^{s}\leq(X\Delta)^{-u}e^{-|t|(\Delta-\pi/2)}
from
[
13
]
.
For instance, to bound the vertical segments
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{5}
, we employ
\Gamma(s)\ll e^{-\pi T/2}
,
\zeta(s+1)\ll 1
,
\zeta(2s)^{-1}\ll 1
and
|y|^{s}\ll(X\Delta)^{\sigma}e^{-T(\Delta-\pi/2)}
. These imply that
I_{1}\ll e^{-T\Delta/2}
, with a similar bound holding for
I_{5}
.
For the bound the vertical integral over the segment
\gamma_{3}
, we use Stirling’s formula
\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\zeta(s)\ll(2\pi)^{2\sigma}|s|^{-\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi|t|/2}
as well as the functional equation of
\zeta(2s)
. These lead to
I_{3}\ll X^{-u}\Delta^{-2u}(u^{-2u-3/2}\Delta^{-1/2})
.
The difficulty resides in the horizontal integrals over the paths
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{4}
. Let us consider
I_{4}
since the treatment for
I_{2}
is nearly identical. One first splits the path into

I_{4}=\bigg(\int_{-u+iT}^{-3/2+iT}+\int_{-3/2+iT}^{-1+iT}+\int_{-1+iT}^{c+iT}\bigg)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}y^{s}ds=I_{4,1}+I_{4,2}+I_{4,3},

say. The integral
I_{4,1}
is actually not difficult and it follows by analytic techniques, i.e. using the functional equation of
\zeta(s)
as well as
(\Gamma(s-1))^{-1}\ll e^{1-\sigma-(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)\log(1-\sigma)+\frac{1}{2}\pi T}
yields
I_{4,1}\ll e^{-T\Delta/2}
. The bound for
I_{4,2}
is also
I_{4,2}\ll e^{-T\Delta/2}
as can be seen by the functional equation. However, to deal with
I_{4,3}
we employ Lemma
6.1
: for
-1\leq\real(s)=\sigma\leq 2
and any
\varepsilon>0
, for sufficiently large
V>0
, there exists some
V\leq T_{*}\leq 2V
such that

(6.16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(2(\sigma+iT_{*}))}\ll T_{*}^{\varepsilon}.

The above bound along with
\Gamma(s)\ll T^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi T/2}
and
\zeta(s+1)\ll T^{\eta}
for some fixed constant
\eta>0
yields
I_{4,3}\ll e^{-T\Delta/2}
, which was the last piece.

The final step is to combine all these estimates and let
N\to\infty
so that

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}+\log\frac{X}{2\pi(1-2\pi iX\theta)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}(\varpi)|<T_{\nu}}f(X,\theta,\varpi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(X,\theta,n)+O(e^{-T_{\nu}\Delta/2}),

where
\nu\leq T_{\nu}\leq 2\nu
with
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
. Letting
\nu\to\infty
and choosing our sequence of
T_{\nu}
appropriately such that (
6.16
) is satisfied we are led to

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}+\log\frac{X}{2\pi(1-2\pi iX\theta)}+\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}(\varpi)|<T_{\nu}}f(X,\theta,\varpi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(X,\theta,n),

which was the last step we needed to show.
∎

Lemma
6.2
can be generalized a little more and we may show without much effort that

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{1+m}+W(X,\theta,m)

(6.17)

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}(\varpi)|<T_{\nu}}f_{m}(X,\theta,\varpi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{m}(X,\theta,n),

for

. Here

is given by the cases

\displaystyle W(X,\theta,m)=\begin{cases}\log\frac{X}{2\pi(1-2\pi iX\theta)},&\quad\mbox{if $m=0$},\\

and
f_{m}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{m}(X,\theta,\varpi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\zeta^{\prime}(\varpi)}\bigg(\frac{X}{1-2\pi iX\theta}\bigg)^{m+\varpi/2}\Gamma\bigg(\frac{2m+\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(1+\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg)\zeta\bigg(\frac{\varpi}{2}\bigg).

If

then
g_{0}(X,\theta,n)=g(X,\theta,n)
and otherwise

\displaystyle g_{m}(X,\theta,n)=\frac{(-1)^{n}y^{-n}\zeta(-m-n)\zeta(1-m-n)}{n!\zeta(-2(m+n))}\quad\textnormal{for}\quad m\geq 1.

We can now use (
6.17
) to determine the asymptotic behaviour of
\rho
.

\rho=e^{-1/X}

Using the above asymptotics, we get

(6.19)

(6.20)

\displaystyle X=x^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(x^{\frac{h}{4}+\varepsilon}).

The non-principal major arcs

Our next result involves

in arithmetic progressions and it as follows.

Lemma 6.3
.

As
X\to\infty
assume that
\ell,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\leq(\log X)^{A}
for
some
A>0
and
(q,\ell)=1
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{A}>0
only depending
A
such that

(6.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q\end{subarray}}|\mu(n)|=\frac{X}{\zeta(2)\varphi(q)\prod_{p|q}(1+p^{-1})}+O(X\exp(-C_{A}\sqrt{\log X}))

as
X\to\infty
. Moreover, the implicit constant in
O(\cdot)
can be chosen independently of
q
and
\ell
.

Proof.

We denote by
\chi
a Dirichlet character modulo
q
.
Using properties of characters, we can write

(6.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\operatorname{mod}q\end{subarray}}|\mu(n)|=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi}\bar{\chi}(a)\sum_{n\leq X}\chi(n)|\mu(n)|,

where the outer sum is over all Dirichlet characters
\chi
modulo
q
.
Thus it is sufficient to show that for the principal character
\chi_{0}
we have

(6.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\end{subarray}}\chi_{0}(n)|\mu(n)|=\frac{X}{\zeta(2)\prod_{p\,|\,q}(1+p^{-1})}+O(X\exp(-C_{A}\sqrt{\log X})),

and for all characters
\chi\neq\chi_{0}

(6.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\end{subarray}}\chi(n)|\mu(n)|\ll X\exp(-C_{A}\sqrt{\log X}),

It is straight forward to see that for
\real(s)>1
one has

(6.29)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)|\mu(n)|}{n^{s}}=\frac{L(s,\chi)}{L(2s,\chi^{2})},

where

is the Dirichlet

\chi
.
Thus Perron’s formula implies

(6.30)

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\end{subarray}}\chi(n)|\mu(n)|=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{L(s,\chi)}{L(2s,\chi^{2})}\frac{X^{s}}{s}\,ds

with

and where the prime indicates that the last term of the sum must be multiplied by
\frac{1}{2}
when

is an integer.
Observe that

has no zeros for

and the integrand has thus at most a pole at

for

.
Using the same argument as in the ordinary Siegel-Walfisz theorem to evaluate this integral, see for instance
[

, we obtain

(6.31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{L(s,\chi)}{L(2s,\chi^{2})}\frac{X^{s}}{s}\,ds=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma(1)}\frac{L(s,\chi_{0})}{L(2s,\chi_{0}^{2})}\frac{X^{s}}{s}ds+O(X\exp(-C_{A}\sqrt{\log X})),

where
\gamma(1)
is a small circle centered at
s=1
. If
\chi
is a non-principal character then
L(s,\chi)
is entire and thus
the residue at
s=1
is
0
.
On the other hand,
L(s,\chi_{0})
has for the principal character a pole at
s=1
with residue
\frac{\varphi(q)}{q}
and

L(s,\chi_{0})=\zeta(s)\prod_{p\,|\,q}(1-p^{-s})
.
Thus the residue at
s=1
is

(6.32)

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{res}}\limits_{s=1}\frac{L(s,\chi_{0})}{L(2s,\chi_{0}^{2})}\frac{X^{s}}{s}=\frac{\varphi(q)}{q}\frac{X}{\zeta(2)\prod_{p\,|\,q}(1-p^{-2})}=\frac{X}{\zeta(2)\prod_{p\,|\,q}(1+p^{-1})}.

This completes the proof.
∎

In
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
, it is shown that the above type of result is the main tool needed to bound the integral over the non-principal major arcs
\mathfrak{M}\backslash\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
. Once Lemma
6.3
is established the rest of the argument is very direct.
The first step is to establish the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma_{1}>0
and
\gamma_{2}\ll\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}
for some
A>0
as
\gamma\to 0
.
Moreover let
q,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\ll(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}
and
(\ell,q)=1
. Define

(6.33)

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q}|\mu(n)|\exp(-n\gamma).

Then one has that

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)\varphi(q)\prod_{p|q}(1+p^{-1})}\frac{1}{\gamma}+O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{C})}\right).

where
\varphi(q)
is the Euler totient function and
C\geq 1
can be chosen arbitrarily.

The main step in this proof is to use Abel’s summation formula and to employ Lemma
6.3
.
Since this calculation is not difficult, we will omit the details.
However, the reader can find for example in
[
7
, Lemma 6.2]
the complete proof of an analogous result. Next, observe that for all
\beta>0
we have the elementary but helpful formula

\displaystyle e^{-\beta j/X}=\int_{\beta}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}dy.

Using this identity, we can write

(6.34)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mu(n)|\left(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)\right)^{jn}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\int_{1}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-yj/X}\sum_{n\leq y}|\mu(n)|\operatorname{e}(jn\alpha)\,dy.

The last step is to combine (
6.34
) with Lemma
6.4
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
A>0
be given.
Further, let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
with

\displaystyle(a,q)=1,\quad q\leq(\log X)^{A}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq q^{-1}X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}.

Then there exists
X_{0}(A)
such that we have for all
X>X_{0}(A)

(6.35)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{X}{q}(1+o(1)).

The proof of this lemma is again standard and the interested reader can find, for example, in
[
7
, Lemma 6.3]
the complete proof of an analogous result.
Lemma
6.5
immediately implies

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\theta\in\mathfrak{M}\backslash\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
. Then we have

(6.36)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))\ll_{A}\frac{3}{4}\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho)

where
A>0
is any fixed real number.

6.5.
The minor arcs

In
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
, it is explained that the forthcoming argument is the main step needed to bound the integral over the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
. We shall give the broad details. Recall that
\rho=e^{-1/X}
. Truncating the
J
-sum of (
6.34
) shows that

(6.37)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j}\int_{1}^{\infty}jX^{-1}e^{-jt/X}S_{|\mu|}(t,j\theta)dt+O\bigg(\frac{X}{J}\bigg),

where
J=(\log X)^{2}
. For each
j\leq J
, Dirichlet’s theorem can be used to choose
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
(a,q)=1
such that
|j\theta-\frac{a}{q}|\leq q^{-1}X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}
and
q<X(\log X)^{-A}
. Next, we set
a_{j}:=a/(a,j)
and
q_{j}=jq/(a,j)
. The definition of
\delta_{q}
from Section
6.1
implies that
|\alpha-\frac{a_{j}}{q_{j}}|\leq\delta_{q_{j}}
. Since
\theta\in\mathfrak{m}
, it then follows that
q_{j}>Q
where
Q=(\log X)^{A}
. Once either bound (
1.17
) or (
4.23
) is established, the rest of the argument follows in a straightforward manner.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\theta\in\mathfrak{m}
. Then we have

(6.38)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\theta))\ll_{A}X(\log X)^{10-A/4}

where
A>0
is any fixed real number.

For the sake of illustration of the weaker method, the bounds on Lemma
6.7
correspond to those from (
4.23
). Lemma
6.7
can be strengthened by using Theorem
1.9
instead but it is immaterial to the rest of the argument.

6.6.
The principal major arcs

Next, we present a common result in partition asymptotics that follows from an application of the saddle point method.

Lemma 6.8
.

Set
\rho=\rho(n)
. One has

(6.39)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|}(n)=\frac{\rho^{-n}\Psi(\rho)}{(2\pi\Phi_{(2)}(\rho))^{1/2}}(1+O(n^{-1/5})).

where
\Phi_{(m)}(\rho)=(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho})^{m}\Phi(\rho)
as given by
(
6.7
)

In order to get the asymptotics we compute the terms in the numerator and denominator. First

(6.40)

\displaystyle\rho^{-n}\Psi(\rho)=\exp\bigg(n\log\frac{1}{\rho(n)}+\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho(n))\bigg).

We again distinguish two situations. One where no assumption is made on the non-trivial zeros, and then one where their real part is upper bounded by a fixed constant
h<1
. We begin with the unconditional case.
Setting
\theta=m=0
in Lemma
6.2
and inserting (
6.19
), we get

(6.41)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho(n))=X+\log\frac{X}{2\pi}+\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}(\varpi)|<T_{\nu}}f(X,0,\varpi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(X,0,n)=n^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(n^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon}).

Similarly, we obtain

(6.42)

\displaystyle n\log\left(1/\rho(n)\right)

\displaystyle=nX^{-1}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(n^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon})\quad\textnormal{and}\quad(\Phi_{(2)}(\rho(n)))^{1/2}=n^{\frac{3}{2}}+O(n^{\frac{5}{4}+\varepsilon}).

On the other hand, if
\real(\varpi)\leq h
for all zeros of
\zeta
then

(6.43)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\mu|}(\rho(n))

\displaystyle=X+O(X^{\frac{h}{2}+\varepsilon})=n^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(n^{\frac{h}{4}+\varepsilon}),

(6.44)

\displaystyle n\log\left(1/\rho(n)\right)

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(n^{\frac{h}{4}+\varepsilon})\quad\textnormal{and}\quad(\Phi_{(2)}(\rho(n)))^{1/2}=n^{\frac{3}{2}}+O(n^{1+\frac{h}{4}+\varepsilon}).

Inserting these expressions into (
6.39
) completes the structure of Theorem
1.11
.
Further, we thus see that the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta
are visible in (
6.39
) if one is working with the exact, but implicit saddle point solution.
On the other hand, if one is trying to determine this solution explicitly and enter it in the above expression then the zeros are absorbed by the error term.

6.7.
Final step

Combining Lemmas
6.6
and
6.7
for the error terms along with Lemmas
6.2
and
6.8
for the main terms yields the proof of Theorem
1.11
.

7.
Conclusion and future work

An attractive application of Theorem
1.1
follows a parallelism with Vinogradov’s original goal. We will show in
[
10
]
, which is a follow up to this paper, that we can obtain results on partitions into three integers with prescribed arithmetic conditions. For instance let
N
be an integer and consider the number
r(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},N)
defined by

r(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},N)=\sum_{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=N}\Lambda^{*r_{1}}(n_{1})\Lambda^{*r_{2}}(n_{2})\Lambda^{*r_{3}}(n_{3}).

Retaining the definition and set up of the major and minor arcs from Section
6.1
, we shall see that Theorem
1.1
will allow us to show that

\displaystyle I_{r}:=\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\widetilde{S}_{r}(\alpha,X)^{3}\operatorname{e}(-\alpha X)d\alpha\ll\frac{X^{2r-1}}{(\log X)^{A/2-(4+r)}}

for any
A>0
and where the implied constant depends only on
A
. This is an important step in obtaining an asymptotic expression for
r(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},N)
. Note that if
r_{1}=r_{2}=r_{3}=1
, then this reduces to

r(1,1,1,N)=r(N)=\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}=N}\log p_{1}\log p_{2}\log p_{3},

the counting function for the number of representations of an odd integer
N
as the sum of three primes
p_{1},p_{2}
and
p_{3}
. Likewise, one could consider the quantity

t(N)=\sum_{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=N}|\mu(n_{1})||\mu(n_{2})||\mu(n_{3})|,

the number of representations of an integer
N
as the sum of three numbers with prescribed
|\mu|
conditions. Bounding

\displaystyle I_{|\mu|}:=\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\widetilde{S}_{|\mu|}(\alpha,X)^{3}\operatorname{e}(-\alpha X)d\alpha

by the use of Theorem
1.9
will be a key aspect in determining
t(N)
.

In
[
10
]
we will also study on these exponential sums can be used in the Hardy-Littlewood method to bound the minor arcs arising from partitions associated to functions of primes or squarefree numbers. For example, let
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a polynomial such that
P(\mathbb{N})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. We then define
\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|,P}(n)
to be the number of partitions of
n
whose parts lie in the set
Q_{P}=\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{N}\textnormal{ and }n\textnormal{ squarefree}\}
. If
\gcd(Q_{P})=1
to avoid undesirable congruence obstructions, then we may extract the value of
\mathfrak{p}_{|\mu|,P}(n)
by employing Theorem
5.1
on the associated minor arcs, see also
[
11
]
. Another example takes place when we define the generating function

\Psi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{p_{1},\cdots,p_{r}\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{1-z^{p_{1}\cdots p_{r}}}=\exp(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(z)),

to study partitions into
r
-full primes. Using Theorem
1.1
we will also be able to prove that

\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\ll X(\log X)^{3-\frac{A}{2^{r}}}

for
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
and
A>0
. This will be the key step in bounding the minor arcs which is usually the hardest ingredient of the Hardy-Littlewood method. An adapted Siegel-Walfisz result will handle the non-principal major arcs
\,\mathfrak{M}\backslash\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
. The main terms of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{r}}(n)
will be produced with difficulty employing integration over a Hankel contour to avoid the essential singularities produced by the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
. This application is lengthy and it is considerably more involved than the treatment in
[
7
]
, so we will not include it in the present paper.
