Oyster shell based indirect carbonation integrated with probiotic encapsulation
Authors: Seonmi Shin, Youjeong Lee, Myoung-Jin Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecycling oyster shells—an abundant industrial waste—is essential to reduce marine pollution. Indirect carbonation is promising; however, is cost-prohibitive. This study is a pioneering endeavor to merge indirect carbonation and probiotic encapsulation technologies using oyster shells. Probiotics were encapsulated in the CaCO3 produced through indirect carbonation with oyster shells, and the performance was evaluated. Confocal laser scanning microscopy certified the survival of a substantial proportion of the encased probiotics. Importantly, the majority of the enveloped probiotics demonstrated robust survivability while passing through gastrointestinal and bile fluids. These findings underscore the applicability of oyster shells as an optimal precursor for probiotic encapsulation which is eco-friendly and addresses the challenges faced in industrial waste recycling. This novel approach overcomes the economic limitations associated with indirect carbonation and mitigates the shortcomings of existing probiotic encapsulation methods. Introduction

Global oyster farming has surged in recent years, with production levels reaching 6 million tons in 20181. Notably, oyster shells—which account for more than 90% of the total oyster weight—are a significant source of waste. These shells are nominally used in seeding, calcium supplementation, and fertilization. In terms of recycling, oyster shells can be processed through other methods, such as preparing cement concrete and artificial reefs. However, some of them are disposed of on coasts, on the ground, or into the ocean, causing significant environmental pollution2–4. Although the accumulation level varies across countries, unprocessed oyster shells, designated as industrial waste, are accumulating owing to technical limitations and lack of appropriate recycling facilities. Therefore, innovative oyster-shell-recycling technologies are urgently required for the development of the marine agricultural industry.

Indirect carbonation—a carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology—has recently drawn attention2,4–7. In this process, Ca or Mg ions are extracted from natural minerals or industrial byproducts and then reacted with CO2 to form stable carbonate minerals. The use of industrial byproducts as Ca sources in indirect carbonation has been actively researched8,9. Although indirect carbonation is an essential technology for CO2 sequestration, it remains economically unviable. Therefore, creative routes must be devised to make this technology cost-effective and apply it in new industries. Oyster shells primarily comprise Ca; consequently, they are suitable as a Ca source in indirect carbonation after being calcined4–7. Therefore, to make indirect carbonation economically feasible and overcome the hindrances associated with treating oyster-shell waste, the development of technologies for producing high-value-added materials through indirect carbonation with calcined oyster shells is critical. To that end, the present study was targeted toward producing CaCO3 via indirect carbonation using calcined shells and then employing it to encapsulate probiotics.

With increasing global wellbeing trends, population aging, and the spread of COVID-19, health and immunity strengthening have garnered considerable attention, leading to a surge in the consumer interest and demand for probiotics. The global probiotics market—valued at $58 billion in 2021—is expected to grow 7.7% annually, reaching ~$111 billion by 203010. Probiotic bacteria are required to be formulated in the range of 108–109 CFU/g for consumption and should maintain a therapeutic efficacy of 106–107 CFU/g in the large intestine11. However, probiotics are remarkably sensitive to environmental conditions during production, which considerably reduces their survival rate and prevents them from readily reaching the intestine in a viable state when ingested, thereby necessitating the use of additional processes11–15. Thus, encapsulation technology is currently applied to most probiotics to improve their intestinal reach.

Probiotic encapsulation has often been performed using various polymeric materials such as polysaccharides (alginate, starch, chitosan, cellulose acetate phthalate, κ-carrageenan, gum arabic, acacia gum, and xanthan); proteins (gelatin, milk protein, soy protein, and gluten); and fats11,12,16. The wall material of encapsulated probiotics must endure while they pass through the stomach and then disintegrate in the gut to release cells17. Additionally, it must be cost-effective and of food grade for incorporation into food products. Probiotics encased in single-walled capsules are generally unstable in the human body; therefore, adding multiple coats or crosslinking with cationic polymers is vital to increase stability. However, these additional treatments complicate the process and cause problems such as prolonged manufacturing and high production costs14,18,19.

In recent decades, probiotics have been encapsulated using diverse methods to maintain their viability and provide health benefits during processing, storage, and digestion12,14,16. In encapsulated probiotics, cells are completely enclosed in the wall material using various techniques such as spray drying, extrusion, and emulsification11,12,14,20. However, the encapsulation efficiency, microbead size, and viability of probiotics may depend on the encapsulation technique14. In addition, all of the previous methods have limitations such as the loss of bacterial cells in dry, high-temperature environments11,20,21; sluggish microbead formation that limits large-scale production20; and a wide size range20. Therefore, advanced probiotic encapsulation technologies such as electrospinning and layer-by-layer methods are being studied; however, further advances could be made by targeting aspects such as improving the probiotic stability and intestinal reach, enabling capsule miniaturization, permitting large-scale production, and achieving cost-effectiveness16. In particular, new and innovative capsules with improved probiotic-protecting ability and functionality must be urgently developed. The method reported herein—production of CaCO3 via indirect carbonation and then using it to encapsulate probiotics—is a completely new method that shows promise in this regard.

In this study, probiotics were encapsulated in a single-walled material—CaCO3—produced by reacting CaO with the CO2 produced by sintering waste oyster shells. Essentially, indirect carbonation and probiotic encapsulation technologies were integrated to obtain a commercially competitive product with a significantly higher intestinal reach rate and lower production cost than those of existing probiotic capsules, with oyster shells leveraged as materials that satisfy the current market needs. Additionally, a CO2 zero-discharge system was implemented using the CO2 generated when calcining oyster shells for carbonation.

Overall, this study was aimed at encapsulating probiotics in the CaCO3 produced via indirect carbonation using shells as a Ca source, and then investigating the morphological and structural properties of the probiotics encapsulated in CaCO3 (PEC) by X-ray diffractometry (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Furthermore, the performance of the PEC was experimentally analyzed by evaluating its intestinal reach rate.

Materials and methods

Materials

The strains (Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum subsp. plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium breve, and Lactococcus lactis subsp. Lactis) used in this study were provided by the Korean Collection for Type Cultures, and the oyster shells were supplied by Company A (Korea). DifoTM Lactobacilli de Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) broth and agar (BD, France) and BL agar (KisanBio, Seoul, Korea) were used as media. Sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.8%, Duksan, Korea), potassium phosphate monobasic (KH2PO4, 99.0%, Duksan, Korea), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, Daejung, Korea), 50% sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH, Daejung, Korea), and pepsin (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) were used for acid and bile resistance tests. All reagents were of analytical grade.

XRD (SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) and XRF (XRF-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) analyses were conducted to determine the components and properties of the solid samples including calcined oyster shell and PEC. XRD was performed at 40 kV/30 mA using Cu Kα radiation in the 2θ range of 10.0°–70.0° at 0.04° intervals. SEM (SUPRA-40VP, ZEISS, Germany) was performed to examine the surface morphologies of the solids. FT-IR spectroscopy (iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and TGA (TGA 7, Perkin Elmer, USA) were conducted to determine the functional groups and weight loss of the solids, respectively. FT-IR spectra were collected in the mid-IR range from 500 to 4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 1 cm−1, whereas TGA was performed by heating the samples from 50 to 900 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/min. Laser scattering particle size analysis (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern, UK) was conducted to determine the particle size distribution.

CLSM (LSM 800, ZEISS, Germany) was performed to assess bacterial viability with the samples being pretreated for fluorescence using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA)22,23. The LIVE/DEAD Kit comprises SYTO9 and propidium iodide (PI); the former is membrane-permeable and stains nucleic acids passing through the cell membrane to exhibit green fluorescence, whereas the latter is membrane-impermeable and stains nucleic acids of bacteria with damaged cell membranes to display red fluorescence24. When stained with an SYTO9–PI mixture, viable and dead bacteria are stained green and red with SYTO9 and PI, respectively.

Probiotic encapsulation

For use in the encapsulation experiment, probiotics were first prepared as follows. Probiotic activation was achieved using MRS broth. To produce strains suitable for encapsulation, MRS broth (55 g) was added to distilled water (1 L). Subsequently, high-pressure sterilization was conducted at 121 °C for 15 min, and the resulting strains were inoculated. The inoculated medium was cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 48–72 h, and the subculture was used thereafter weekly. After the culture was completed, centrifugation was performed at 15,000 rpm and 5 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded and the strains were washed three times with sterile distilled water before use in the experiments. Additionally, MRS agar (70 g) and BL agar (60.23 g)—which were used as media for counting viable probiotic cells—were added to distilled water (1 L), respectively, sterilized in a high-pressure sterilizer at 121 °C for 15 min, and then stored at 4 °C. The number of target probiotics was adjusted to 10.16 Log CFU/g.

Oyster shells were calcined in an electric furnace at 900 °C for 3 h to obtain CaO, which was then mixed with distilled water, yielding a suspension that was subsequently filtered to remove impurities and obtain the Ca eluate. Five strains—Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum subsp. plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium breve, and Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis—were mixed in the same ratio and added to a 200 mL reactor containing a Ca solution, following which 99.9% CO2 was injected at a flow rate of 0.2 L/min until the pH reached 7.5 (Fig. 1). After carbonation, the suspension was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter, and the resulting solid—PEC—was rapidly frozen in a freezer at − 60 °C for 4 h. The PEC was dried at 5 mbar and − 80 °C for 24 h using a freeze dryer (Operon, OFR-FDU-8606, Korea) and then subjected to XRD, XRF, SEM, FT-IR, TGA, and CLSM analyses.

Estimating the number of probiotics reaching the intestine

To determine the number of viable cells released from the PEC that passed through the stomach and reached the intestine, acid and bile tolerance tests were conducted using simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF). SGF was prepared according to the method previously reported25. Essentially, pepsin (0.64 g) was added to a 0.2% (w/v) NaCl solution (200 mL) whose pH was adjusted to 2 using 1 M HCl26. SIF was prepared according to the procedure previously reported27, in which the pH of a 0.05 M KH2PO4 solution was adjusted to 7.5 using 1 M NaOH.

The PEC was added to the SGF, agitated at 200 rpm for 2 h in a shaking incubator at 37 °C, and then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. The resulting solid was immediately added to the SIF and then agitated under the same conditions as those used for the SGF. The solution that had undergone two successive treatments was diluted, spread on agar medium, and then cultured at 37 °C for 48 h to count viable cells. Notably, the intestinal reach rate is related to the number of probiotics released by the PEC in the intestine through gastric juice and bile.

Results and discussion

Oyster shell calcination

The XRD and XRF spectra of untreated oyster shells indicated that their main component was calcite, a type of CaCO3, with a Ca content of ~ 96% (Fig. 2a and Table S1). The uncalcined oyster shells also contained trace amounts of Na, Cl, Si, S, and Sr in addition to Ca. After calcination, the XRD and XRF spectra confirmed that CaCO3 was converted to ~ 99% CaO (Fig. 2b and Table S1), with trace amounts of S and Sr remaining as impurities.

XRD patterns of (a) untreated and (b) calcined oyster shells (C: calcite, O: calcium oxide).

Probiotic encapsulation

In this study, probiotics were encapsulated in CaCO3, which was produced by injecting CO2 into the Ca eluate obtained by hydrating the calcined shells. The carbonation efficiency was 92%, indicating that most of the Ca in the solution was converted into CaCO3. The encapsulation efficiency of the probiotics will be described later.

The morphological, spectroscopic, and thermal properties of the freeze-dried PEC were investigated by XRD, XRF, SEM, FT-IR, TGA, and CLSM analyses. The obtained results helped assess the encapsulation of the probiotics, the survival of the confined probiotics, and the probiotics/CaCO3 ratio.

The XRD pattern of the PEC suggested that its CaCO3 component existed in calcite form (Fig. 3a). Moreover, the PEC particles (D50 = 2.8 μm) were found to be considerably smaller than conventionally produced encapsulated probiotics (Fig. 3b). It is worth noting that the bacterial cell size ranges from 1 to 5 μm11, and the microbead size of encapsulated probiotics obtained via extrusion, emulsification, and spray drying is typically 340–1700, 70–192, and 9–19 μm, respectively14. The size of the produced microbeads generally depends on the encapsulation technique. For example, the size of the microbeads produced by extrusion is affected by factors such as the dimensions of the nozzle or syringe, type and concentration of the hardening solution, and droplet flow rate. Similarly, the size of microbeads generated by emulsification is influenced by parameters such as the type of surfactant used, viscosity between the water and oil phases, and flow rate of water droplets14. Generally, small-sized microbeads, as obtained in this study, are suitable for satisfying the sensory aspects of food-related applications28. Moreover, using PEC in both powder and liquid products is advantageous. In the present study, the PEC capsule size was affected by the carbonation time, with prolonged carbonation yielding larger capsules29,30.

Characteristics of the PEC determined by (a) XRD, (b) particle size analysis, and (c) SEM (C: Calcite).

The PEC was found to have a rough and aggregated surface (Fig. 3c). Surprisingly, unlike other encapsulation materials, the surface of the PEC hardly changed after lyophilization11,31. The outer wall of the PEC (Fig. 3c) evidently acted as an efficient barrier against diffusion of solutions toward the capsule core.

FT-IR spectra of the PEC, CaCO3, and probiotics were acquired (Fig. 4) to confirm that the PEC contained both CaCO3 and probiotics and to ascertain the potential existence of CaCO3–probiotics interactions. In the PEC spectrum, the O–H stretching vibration peak appeared at 3280 cm−1, and the C–H, C = C, and C–O peaks appeared at 2908, 1640, and 1032 cm−1, respectively, all these peaks are characteristic of probiotics32–35. Additionally, the characteristic peaks of CaCO3 were identified in the PEC spectrum at 1386, 878, and 712 cm−1 36–38. Therefore, the FT-IR spectra confirmed that the PEC contained CaCO3 and probiotics. Furthermore, examination of the peak wavenumbers of the PEC revealed no detectable shifts in the peak positions of CaCO3 and probiotics. This implies that no new chemical bonds were created between them and that their attachment was presumably based solely on electrostatic attraction. Therefore, these two entities were connected by an electrostatic attractive force without forming new bonds.

FT-IR spectra of the probiotics, CaCO3, and PEC.

TGA was performed on the PEC, probiotics, and CaCO3 to evaluate the probiotics: CaCO3 ratio for the PEC (Fig. 5). The weight loss of most organic matter and CaCO3 is known to occur at temperatures of 250–500 °C and 500–820 °C, respectively39–41. Therefore, the weight loss data of the probiotics, CaCO3, and PEC were acquired in the aforementioned temperature ranges, and the contents of the components were determined (Table S2). The PEC exhibited weight loss values of 8% and 40% at 250–500 °C and 500–820 °C, respectively. The ratio (w/w) of organic matter (probiotics) to CaCO3, calculated based on this result, was 1:10 (Table S2). The TGA results can be used to determine the amount of probiotics and calcium carbonate required to produce PEC, and consequently, calculate the production cost. The price of the CaCO3 encapsulant used in PEC was found to be considerably lower than that of the encapsulant used previously.

TGA profiles of the probiotics, CaCO3, and PEC.

The FT-IR and TGA data suggested that CaCO3 was wrapped around the probiotics; this result is consistent with those reported previously. According to other study42, the negatively charged bacterial cell wall acts as a nucleation site for CaCO3 during carbonation. Ca ions accumulate on the bacterial cell surface and reach a supersaturated state, thereby enabling CaCO3 deposition on the cell surface. Furthermore, a study reported that an increase in pH and carbonate content during microbial metabolism creates favorable conditions for Ca-ion precipitation around the cell wall42.

In the present study, the surface of the probiotics was negatively charged, which triggered the movement of Ca ions. Moreover, CO2 dissolved in the solution to form carbonate ions, which interacted with Ca ions to precipitate CaCO3. The probiotics likely acted as nucleation sites, and miniscule CaCO3 molecules were formed on their surface. Part of the probiotics adsorbed onto the surface of the expanding CaCO3; these adsorbed probiotics affected crystal growth. The SEM image (Fig. 3c) confirmed the presence of the probiotics on the surface of CaCO3. Notably, the shape of the PEC differed from that of typical CaCO3, because the probiotics were embedded between the produced CaCO3 and precipitated together (Fig. 3c).

According to the XRF analysis (Table 1), the K and Na that were present in the probiotics were not found in CaCO3, but were detected in PEC, providing clear evidence of the probiotic encapsulation in CaCO3. Overall, the FT-IR, TGA, SEM, and XRF results validated the distribution of CaCO3 outside the PEC and the presence of the probiotics inside the PEC, indicating that CaCO3 enveloped the probiotics.

The SEM results were useful for determining the shape of the PEC surface; however, they were ineffective for assessing the survival of the encased probiotics. Therefore, CLSM analysis of the PEC was performed to confirm the survival of the enveloped probiotics (Fig. 6). The results indicated that the probiotics were enclosed in CaCO3. Overall, red coloration (PI, dead cells) was not visible, whereas green fluorescence (SYTO9, live cells) was observed, confirming that most of the encapsulated probiotics survived. In contrast, the probiotics freeze-dried without encapsulation in CaCO3—that is, the control group—exhibited a live cell/dead cell ratio of ~ 1:1 (Fig. 6). These results indicated that the CaCO3 in the PEC functioned as a probiotic encapsulant as well as a lyoprotectant.

Performance evaluation of PEC

The critical parameters reflecting the performance of the probiotics—encapsulation yield and intestinal reach rate—were investigated.

Encapsulation yield

The viable probiotic content of the PEC after freeze-drying and the encapsulation efficiency were determined to be 10.16 ± 0.04 Log CFU/g and 100%, respectively (Table S3). These values are significantly higher than those reported previously. For instance, the encapsulation yield has been found to range from 65–99%14. Specifically, extrusion, emulsification, spray drying, and freeze-drying have provided encapsulation yields of 95–99%, 96–97%, 83–94%, and 65–97%, respectively. However, these previously reported values may be lower because the referenced review paper does not state the encapsulation yield in many cases. Notably, certain studies have obtained considerably low encapsulation efficiencies. For example, an encapsulation yield of only 41% was achieved when encapsulation was performed using phytic acid and chitosan as the main components, with CaCO3 employed used as a co-encapsulant to increase stability26.

Probiotics perish frequently during encapsulation and freeze-drying11,14,20,33. Therefore, ensuring the continued survival of probiotics in these processes is a vital task in the probiotics industry. In the present study, the residual moisture content of the probiotics was lowered to ~ 2% by lyophilization; however, freeze-drying protective agents—such as skim milk, whey milk, sucrose, amino acids, dietary fibers, and prebiotics, which are typically used to improve cell viability during freeze-drying and ensure stability during storage14,31 —were not used. Additionally, freeze-drying can cause structural damage to bacterial cells with only conventional protective-agent-free encapsulation materials, leading to reduced viability and metabolic activity11,31. Surprisingly, after lyophilization of the PEC without freeze-drying protective agents, the capsule shape was not deformed and the probiotics were not damaged (Fig. 6). Notably, the probiotics maintained a high viable cell number of 10.16 Log CFU/g after freeze-drying. Therefore, a feasible technology that hardly kills probiotics during encapsulation and freeze-drying was developed in this study.

Intestinal reach

The viability and stability of the free and encapsulated probiotics were evaluated under simulated gastrointestinal and bile conditions. To provide health benefits to humans, probiotic cells must reach the intestine in sufficient quantities without being susceptible to the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract and bile33. Therefore, in addition to the resistance of the free and encapsulated probiotic cells to SGF, the viability of these cells under SIF conditions was investigated. The results (Table S4) showed an immediate decrease in viability for free cells compared to that of their CaCO3-enveloped counterparts. The viability of the free cells decreased from 10.16 to 5.92 Log CFU/g after the gastrointestinal and bile treatments; in contrast, that of the CaCO3-encased cells barely changed (10.16 to 10.19 Log CFU/g; Table S4). This result is noteworthy compared to those reported previously for probiotics encapsulated using conventional methods, whose survivability in SGF and SIF has been found to range from 0–84%14. Notably, the use of freeze-drying has led to probiotic survival rates of 33–63% after SGF and SIF treatments; however, almost none of the probiotics survived in many cases14,43,44.

The intestinal reach rate is related to the number of probiotics released from the PEC in the intestine through gastric juice and bile. When probiotics enter the body, they first pass through the highly acidic stomach and then move to the intestine. The stomach normally maintains a pH of 0.7–0.9, which increases to ~ 2 when food is introduced. Thus, a substantial number of probiotics perish in gastric juice owing to its low pH38. Bile is secreted from the upper part of the small intestine to promote digestion and absorption of ingested lipids and to act as a detergent for microorganisms, thereby enabling the cell membranes of microorganisms comprising fats and fatty acids to exert bactericidal action45. Therefore, the intestinal reach rate of the PEC is related to both the survival rate in the stomach (acid resistance) and that in bile (bile resistance). Notably, if the probiotics are not released from the PEC in the intestine after passing through the stomach and encountering bile, they are essentially futile to the human body.

Given that the recommended daily probiotic intake levels are 8–9 Log CFU/g, the results of the present study with respect to intestinal reach (10.19 Log CFU/g) highlight the outstanding performance of the PEC (Table S4)11. Overall, encapsulation of probiotics in CaCO3 enhanced the tolerance of probiotic cells to simulated gastrointestinal and bile conditions and did not lead to a loss of viability.

For probiotic encapsulation, the encapsulation method and encapsulation material should typically be compatible with each other and with the encapsulated microorganisms46. In that respect, the encapsulation method and material employed in the present study can be used regardless of the type of strain, highlighting its broad application potential. For example, in this study, a composite strain of five representative strains was adopted to show that the intestinal reach of the probiotics was similar to their intake levels, suggesting that all strains were activated in the intestine.

These results suggested that CaCO3 (calcite) was converted into hydroxyapatite when the reaction expressed below occurred in bile fluid, and that the probiotics were released as the CaCO3 decomposed.

Phosphates, which are the main component of bile fluid, have a high affinity for CaCO348,49. Notably, the solubility product constants of CaCO3 (calcite) and hydroxyapatite (Ksp = 3.31 × 10−9 and 2.34 × 10−59, respectively) underscore the viability of Eq. 250.

In the stomach, a small amount of CaCO3 dissolves in the PEC, increasing the pH around the PEC to protect the encapsulated probiotics; most of the probiotics are released when the CaCO3 in the PEC is converted to the more stable hydroxyapatite by reacting with the phosphate in bile. Therefore, the encapsulated probiotics passed through the stomach, reached the intestines without perishing, and exited the capsule in the small intestine and showed activity.

Conclusion

Encapsulation is an emerging technique that delivers probiotics to target locations in the human body while protecting them from gastrointestinal and bile environments and ensuring their controlled release in the intestine. In this study, a technology was developed for encapsulating probiotics in the CaCO3 produced through indirect carbonation using calcined oyster shells. Specifically, the morphological and spectroscopic characteristics of the PEC were investigated, and the PEC performance was evaluated by determining the number of probiotics reaching the intestine. The obtained results underscored the importance of encapsulation in improving the viability and stability of probiotics during digestion, with CaCO3 confirmed as an effective material for ensuring the survival and targeted delivery of probiotics.

To date, few studies have focused on integrating the disparate technologies of indirect carbonation and probiotic encapsulation. Nevertheless, the results of this study suggest that using CaCO3 as an encapsulation material to coat probiotics as a single layer ensures probiotic survival during encapsulation as well as freeze-drying, and prevents the PEC from expanding and deforming. The PEC exhibits outstanding acid and bile resistances, highlighting its potential to solve most problems associated with existing probiotic encapsulation technologies. Additionally, because the devised process is simple and the calcined oyster shells used for coating are inexpensive, production costs are expected to be considerably reduced. The probiotic encapsulation technology reported herein could permit commercial probiotic capsule production, given its low-cost materials, simple encapsulation processes, and high intestinal reach rate. Additionally, this technology is a net-zero process that does not generate CO2, because the CO2 produced during oyster-shell calcination is captured and utilized for PEC production; consequently, it is an environmentally friendly technology that can recycle waste and even achieve carbon neutrality. This indirect-carbonation-based probiotic encapsulation shows promise as a new frontier in CCUS technology.

Improving the probiotic cell viability during industrial processing and storage are the main concerns that must be addressed to successfully commercialize this technology. Therefore, follow-up research is required to improve the storage stability of the PEC by optimizing the indirect carbonation conditions, verify its efficacy, and commercialize the developed technology.
