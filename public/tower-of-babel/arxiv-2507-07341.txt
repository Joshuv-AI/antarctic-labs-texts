On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment: The Computational Intractability of Filtering for AI Alignment
Sarah Ball, Greg Gluch, Shafi Goldwasser, Frauke Kreuter, Omer Reingold, Guy N. Rothblum

Abstract
With the increased deployment of large language models (LLMs), one concern is their potential misuse for generating harmful content. Our work studies the alignment challenge, with a focus on filters to prevent the generation of unsafe information. Two natural points of intervention are the filtering of the input prompt before it reaches the model, and filtering the output after generation. Our main results demonstrate computational challenges in filtering both prompts and outputs. First, we show that there exist LLMs for which there are no efficient prompt filters: adversarial prompts that elicit harmful behavior can be easily constructed, which are computationally indistinguishable from benign prompts for any efficient filter. Our second main result identifies a natural setting in which output filtering is computationally intractable. All of our separation results are under cryptographic hardness assumptions. In addition to these core findings, we also formalize and study relaxed mitigation approaches, demonstrating further computational barriers. We conclude that safety cannot be achieved by designing filters external to the LLM internals (architecture and weights); in particular, black-box access to the LLM will not suffice. Based on our technical results, we argue that an aligned AI system’s intelligence cannot be separated from its judgment.

In this paper, we investigate fundamental limitations to achieving AI alignment through the approach of

1

Under standard cryptographic assumptions, we show that significant computational barriers exist to filtering both input prompts and output responses of LLMs in order to prevent harmful content.

Our contributions are twofold. First, for
prompt filtering
, we construct LLMs for which no efficient filter can distinguish adversarial prompts—crafted to elicit harmful behavior—from benign ones. These prompts are computationally indistinguishable for any efficient detector, implying an inherent barrier to blocking harmful inputs.
Second, we identify natural settings where
output filtering
is also computationally intractable. Even when a prompt is deemed safe, determining whether the resulting output is harmful may itself be infeasible for any efficient filter. This suggests that both preemptive (input-based) and reactive (output-based) filtering can face insurmountable computational challenges.

Furthermore, we formalize and analyze relaxed mitigation strategies in which filters are allowed to modify prompts or outputs, rather than simply rejecting them. Although these
mitigation filters
have greater expressive power, we show that they, too, encounter fundamental computational barriers, indicating that even these more flexible approaches are subject to inherent limitations.

Taken together, our results reveal deep challenges in achieving provably aligned AI systems via standard filtering techniques. These findings underscore the need for a more rigorous understanding of the computational hardness underlying alignment—and raise important implications for the design of regulatory frameworks and technical safeguards.

1.1
Prompt Filtering

The first approach we consider is running user prompts through a
prompt filter
, which can detect and discard prompts that might lead to harmful answers from the LLM. We focus on a setting in which the LLM itself is untrusted, but we can query it while training a prompt filter (during training we can try different prompts to observe if and where harmful behaviors occur). After this filter-training phase, the filter should detect (and reject) prompts that lead to harmful outputs. This is a natural approach which arises in practice. We ask:

Can efficient input-detection filters always be created?

We answer this question in the negative, showing a significant barrier to the natural approach of detection filtering.

The setup is as follows:

1.

There is a computationally powerful LLM, which we do not trust to be aligned. In particular, there is a non-empty set of prompts that incite the LLM to produce harmful outputs, e.g., requesting instructions on how to build a bomb is considered harmful.

2.

We assume the existence of a very weak innocent prompt generator
G
: it generates prompts that (when fed into the LLM) do not produce harmful outputs. We further assume that the distribution of innocent prompts generated by
G
is not low-entropy (i.e. there are many innocent prompts, see Remark
1
).

3.

A prompt filter is a moderately powerful algorithm, which can be trained using real-world data and black-box access to the LLM but during deployment it does not have full access to the LLM architecture and weights. On getting a prompt from a user, the filter should reject it or pass it to the LLM. The goal is to reject prompts on which the LLM produces a harmful output.

The filter should be more efficient than the LLM: otherwise we can ignore the given LLM and just focus on training a new one from scratch and using it in place of a filter.
Additionally, if a filter was as powerful as the LLM, the question arises of how to align the filter itself.

To avoid triviality (i.e. having a filter that rejects all prompts), we also require that the filter accepts prompts generated by the innocent prompt generator
G
with high probability.

Our main result shows the impossibility of prompt filtering:

Theorem 1

(Prompt-detection filter impossibility, informal)
.

For any high-entropy innocent prompt generator
G
, there exists an adversarial prompt generator
G^{\prime}
(with comparable runtime to
G
) and an LLM
M^{\prime}
such that prompts by
G^{\prime}
can induce
M^{\prime}
to produce harmful outputs, and under the assumption that cryptographic Time-Lock Puzzles exist, no prompt filter that runs significantly faster than
M^{\prime}
can distinguish between prompts generated by
G
and prompts generated by
G^{\prime}
.

Discussion.

A few remarks on our result are in order.
First, our result relies on a computational asymmetry between the filter and the LLM. This is a realistic assumption: filtering is only meaningful when the LLM is too expensive or complex to retrain, and cannot be fully simulated by the filter.

Second, we show that there exist LLMs that cannot be aligned using prompt-filtering alone, even though the specific LLM we construct may exhibit contrived behavior. Nevertheless, the core message is more general: filtering harmful inputs without access to the LLM’s internals is, in principle, impossible.
In the current state of knowledge, where LLMs remain largely uninterpretable, this should serve as a warning against overconfidence in input-based safeguards—particularly when facing prompts carefully engineered to elicit harmful outputs.

Importantly, there is a long-standing precedent for such insights in cryptography. Theoretical vulnerabilities that initially appear artificial, overly stylized, or unlikely to occur in practice have repeatedly foreshadowed devastating real-world exploits. A striking example is the 2016 attack on Ethereum smart contracts
(
Luu et al. 2016
)
, in which a seemingly contrived re-entrancy vulnerability—once viewed as a theoretical edge case—was leveraged to steal over 60 million USD.
Such incidents highlight a recurring pattern: foundational impossibility results, even when abstract or idealized, often uncover structural weaknesses that can have far-reaching consequences.

Construction.

Our result relies on the existence of cryptographic
Time-Lock Puzzles
. Introduced by
Rivest et al. 1996
(RSW), time-lock puzzles provide a way to send messages “to the future.” A sender can
quickly
generate a puzzle whose solution
s
remains hidden until a
moderately large amount of computation
—quantified by a time parameter
t
—has been performed after which it can be solved (or opened).
The key property is that
s
remains inaccessible to any adversary running in significantly less time than
t
.

Rivest et al. 1996
proposed a concrete construction based on the assumption that repeated squaring modulo a special RSA integer is an
inherently sequential
task. The RSA integer, standing for Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, is a product of two large primes, and appears in the context of public-key cryptography
(
Rivest et al. 1978
)
.

Informally, in our setting, we use time-lock puzzles to conceal harmful instructions. An LLM that runs for time
t
can solve the puzzle and follow harmful instructions, while a filter incapable of spending that much time cannot even detect the existence of such instructions.

Deceptive Alignment.

The LLMs constructed in Theorem
1
behave in a contrived and deliberately pathological manner, and Theorems
2
and
3
rely on cooperation between LLMs and malicious users—scenarios that may at first glance seem overly pessimistic or implausible. However, recent empirical studies
(
Hubinger et al. 2019
;
Greenblatt et al. 2024
)
have demonstrated that qualitatively similar behaviors can and do emerge in current-generation LLMs under realistic conditions.
The term
deceptive alignment
refers to cases in which an AI system that is not genuinely aligned with human intent nonetheless behaves in an apparently aligned way, often strategically, in order to avoid detection, satisfy training objectives, or manipulate oversight. For example,
Greenblatt et al. 2024
observed that some LLMs modulate their behavior depending on whether they are in a training or deployment context, and on characteristics of the user they are interacting with—selectively choosing to comply with alignment objectives only in certain regimes.
2
2

2

In
Greenblatt et al. 2024
, the LLMs were misaligned during training and aligned during deployment, but one could easily imagine the reverse scenario.

While these empirical findings do not exactly instantiate the theoretical constructions we present, they provide concrete evidence that the kind of strategic misalignment we model—once considered speculative—is already beginning to materialize in practice. This convergence between theory and empirical observation underscores the importance of understanding and anticipating even stylized forms of deceptive behavior.

1.2
Output Filtering

We also consider an alternative approach to alignment: instead of filtering prompts, the defense mechanism filters the
answers
produced by the LLM. We refer to this as an
output filter
. In this setup, a prompt is given to the LLM, which generates a response; both the prompt and the response are then passed to the output filter, which may choose to detect and reject the output.
The goal of the output filter is twofold:
(1) as with a prompt filter, it should reject harmful prompts; and
(2) it should reject cases where
the answer is harmful
, even if the prompt appears benign—e.g., when a seemingly innocent input results in a detailed recipe for constructing a bomb.

Output filters may be easier to design than prompt filters, since they operate with more information.
This motivates the question:

Can efficient output-detection filters always be created (even when prompt filters are impossible)?

Again, we answer this question in the negative.

Note that if it is easy to determine whether a given LLM output is harmful, then output filters are trivial to construct. Thus, output filtering becomes meaningful only in the more challenging setting, where assessing the potential harm of a response is itself difficult. This concern becomes more pressing as AI systems grow more powerful, making it increasingly hard to evaluate the downstream consequences of their outputs. For example, imagine an AI generating a complex business plan aimed at reducing costs by subtly recommending the exclusion of clients with certain characteristics.

1.3
Mitigation

Having established computational barriers for both prompt filters and output filters, we now examine a more permissive mechanism, which we term a
prompt–mitigation filter
.
Here, the filter can modify the prompt and pass it through to the LLM in addition to rejecting it. Note that innocent prompts can also be modified, but the implicit assumption is that the semantics of the prompts is maintained.
Mitigation gives the filter more power, which makes the goal of filtering or obtaining alignment potentially easier to achieve. We show a connection between the security of watermarking schemes and the impossibility of mitigation filtering.

Prompt mitigation and watermarking.

A watermarking scheme
W
enables the creator of an LLM to prove that a given output originated from its model, even after an adversary has post-processed the text. Fully provable watermarking remains beyond the current state of the art: one typically demands that the adversary preserve
some
semantic content—otherwise it could simply delete the text (and with it the watermark). We therefore consider watermarking against adversaries that apply edits from a permissible class
E
and run in time
t
.
The watermark should remain
indistinguishable
to any such time-
t
adversary.

Our focus is on auto-regressive models, which generate text token by token, and on watermarking schemes that embed the mark by perturbing the model’s sampling randomness—a strategy explored by several recent proposals
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
;
Christ et al. 2023
;
Golowich and Moitra 2024
)
.

We show that:

Theorem 5

(Impossibility of mitigation-filters, informal)
.

Let
W
be a watermarking scheme as above that is resilient to edits from a class
E
.
For any high-entropy, innocent prompt generator
G
, there exists an adversarial prompt generator
G^{\prime}
(with comparable runtime to
G

M^{\prime}
such that

G^{\prime}
generates prompts that will induce harmful outputs from
M^{\prime}

1.4
Intelligence, Judgment and Agency

Our results can be interpreted as saying that it is impossible to achieve alignment by separating intelligence and judgment.
To illustrate this message we explain now how our paper relates to a recent proposal for achieving alignment.

Decoupling Intelligence and Agency as a path to AI alignment?

Bengio et al. 2025
propose an approach to AI alignment by developing non-agentic “Scientist AIs”—systems that explain the world through Bayesian inference without taking actions or pursuing goals.
They argue that, unlike agentic AIs, which can become deceptive or self-preserving, Scientist AIs are designed to be interpretable, uncertainty-aware, and inherently trustworthy.
The suggested applications of Scientist AIs are advancing scientific progress and monitoring agentic systems.

Agency
is a complicated term to define.

Bengio et al. 2025
defines it as the ability to change the world to
achieve goals and mentions three pillars of agency:
affordances, goal-directedness, and intelligence
. We refer the reader to Section
4
for future directions for research related to agency and alignment.

Our results point to limitations in the proposed use of Scientist AIs as safety guardrails.
First, the authors suggest using a Scientist AI
A
to generate candidate experiments and another, more powerful Scientist AI
B
to assess their safety. However, Theorem
4
shows that even a stronger
B
may fail to detect all dangerous outputs.
One might argue that a Scientist AI behaves differently from the LLM constructed in the proof of the theorem, being designed for interpretability and trustworthiness—but if that is true, the need for a second filtering AI is unclear.
Theorem
4
also indirectly
implies the need for agency
, as in some situations, there is no other way of judging the level of harm of an action than executing it in the world.

Second, if a Scientist AI is used to filter prompts for a more powerful agentic AI, Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
show it cannot reliably block all harmful prompts. Section 3.1.5 of
Bengio et al. 2025
acknowledges that a guardrail AI must match the intelligence of the system it oversees. But if so, it is unclear why one would use the untrusted AI at all, given similar capabilities and likely similar computational cost of the Scientist AI.

3
Related Work

Alignment.
Making AI models aligned with human preferences is a central concern of contemporary AI research
(
Amodei et al. 2016
;
Leike et al. 2018
;
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Ji et al. 2023
)
. In order to guide model behavior, technical efforts have focused on techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF,
Christiano et al. 2017
), further improved by Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. However, a growing body of work suggests that achieving robust alignment is profoundly difficult: Researchers have highlighted issues ranging from the inherent ambiguity in specifying human preferences
(
Gabriel 2020
;
Zhi-Xuan et al. 2024
;
Sorensen et al. 2024
)
, to problems like shallow alignment induced by properties of the alignment algorithms
(
Jain et al. 2023
;
Kotha et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
and the alignment data
(
Qi et al. 2024
)
. For instance,
Lee et al. 2024
show that the DPO alignment algorithm only teaches a model to bypass toxic “regions” without removing toxic knowledge gained from the pre-training stage, making it possible to elicit this toxic knowledge with “jailbreaks”—changes to the prompt that lead a model from answer-refusal to generating harmful information. Relatedly,
Qi et al. 2024
argue that safety alignment often takes shortcuts by primarily adapting a model’s generative distribution over just its first few output tokens, making them vulnerable to various attacks. The difficulty in robustly aligning models at a deep representational level underscores the need for complementary external mechanisms to detect or prevent harmful model outputs. This is in line with regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act, which requires AI systems in the high-risk category to implement an effective risk management system (see Article 9
EU AI Act
).

Filters.
In response to the need for safer AI systems, practical filtering mechanisms
have been developed and deployed.
For instance, model developers like Meta have introduced tools such as Llama Guard, designed to classify content as safe or unsafe
(
Inan et al. 2023
)
. Similarly, cloud service providers like Microsoft Azure offer content filtering capabilities within their AI service implementations
(
Microsoft Corporation 2025
)
, and companies like Nvidia also provide solutions aimed at moderating AI-generated content
(
NVIDIA Corporation 2025
)
. These approaches represent an ongoing evolution, with classifiers and filters becoming increasingly sophisticated. However, the development of jailbreaks poses a consistent challenge to successful filtering and model alignment in general
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2024
;
Chao et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Against the background of this dynamic co-evolution of attack and defense, our work explores the computational intractability of filtering approaches under cryptographic assumptions.

Time Lock Puzzles.

It is usually desired that cryptographic schemes cannot be broken by any adversary. An exception is the notion of cryptographic puzzles that can be solved in some preset amount of time (or space) but not faster. Examples of such puzzles
(
Dwork and Naor 1992
;
Rivest et al. 1996
)
have been used as a way to combat spam or send messages into the future, forcing the spammer (or the future reader of messages) to invest the preset amount of time. The notion of time-lock puzzles introduced by
Rivest et al. 1996
following May’s time-released cryptography
(
May 1993
)
is especially intriguing in that it allows a user to quickly encrypt a message in such a way that it can be read only after a longer but set number of time steps. Informally, the sender generates a puzzle with
a solution
s
that remains hidden from adversaries that run in time significantly less than
t
, including parallel adversaries with polynomially many processors. The original
(
Rivest et al. 1996
)
candidate was based on the assumption that exponentiation modulo an RSA integer is an “inherently sequential” computation. More recently
Bitansky et al. 2016a
constructed time-lock puzzles based on the necessary assumption that worst-case Non-Parallelizing Languages exist-decidable in time
t
, but hard in the worst case for circuits of depth significantly smaller than
t
-and that indistinguishability obfuscation exists
(
Bitansky et al. 2015
;
Canetti et al. 2014
)
.
A culmination of a series of follow-up works
(
Agrawal et al. 2025
;
Bitansky and Garg 2025
;
Abram et al. 2025
)
managed to construct time-lock puzzles based on a version of the Learning With Errors
(
Regev 2009
)
(LWE) assumption.
One advantage of basing time-lock puzzles on LWE is that the other constructions are broken by quantum algorithms, while LWE is a candidate for post-quantum secure cryptography.

4
Reflections

Our work builds on a growing body of research highlighting the inherent unpredictability and misalignment risks of complex AI systems. By formalizing these insights, we provide a theoretical foundation for understanding when and why alignment may remain an elusive goal, regardless of technical advancements or regulatory safeguards. The implications of our impossibility theorems are profound: they call into question the feasibility of creating AI systems that are both powerful and wholly safe, at least using a certain natural set of filtering tools. This emphasizes the need to reconsider how society audits and handles these systems.

Beyond Cartesian Duality.

Our results show that merging intelligence and judgment might be necessary for achieving AI alignment.
It is in contrast with what some other works suggest, e.g.,
Bengio et al. 2025
argues for separating intelligence from agency.
We, on the other hand, argue that agency is indispensable in some situations.

It would be interesting to investigate how our findings about AI alignment relate to a recent paradigm shift in the study of the mind known as
4E cognitive science

(
Newen et al. 2018
)
, which emphasizes the
embodied, embedded, enactive, and extended
nature of cognition.
This paradigm stands in contrast to the Cartesian mind-body duality and argues that the unit of explanation of cognition is not just neuronal processes in the brain but the whole complex of brain-body-environment.

Sequentiality.

The security of the RSW puzzle hinges on the assumption that modular exponentiation—specifically, computing

A.4
Public key encryption

Diffie and Hellman 1976
introduced the notion of public key encryption (PKE), enabling secure communication between parties without a shared secret. In a PKE scheme, each user generates a pair of keys: a public key
\mathsf{pk}
that is distributed openly and a private key
\mathsf{sk}
that is kept secret. Anyone can encrypt a message
m
using
\mathsf{pk}
to obtain a ciphertext
c
, but only the holder of
\mathsf{sk}
can decrypt
c
to recover
m
. Security relies on the assumed hardness of inverting the encryption without access to the secret key—for example, the difficulty of factoring large integers in the RSA scheme.

A.5
Secret key encryption

Secret key encryption (also known as symmetric-key encryption) requires both parties to share a common secret key
\mathsf{k}
in advance. The encryption algorithm uses
\mathsf{k}
to map a message
m
to a ciphertext
c
, and the decryption algorithm uses the same key
\mathsf{k}
to recover
m
from
c
. Symmetric schemes are typically much more efficient than public key schemes and form the backbone of practical secure communication when a secure channel for key exchange is already available. Notable constructions include block ciphers such as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)
[

First, we define watermarking schemes, for which we adopt definitions from
