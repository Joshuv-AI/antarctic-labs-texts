A similarity-aware network with contrastive optimization for biomedical image segmentation
Authors: Rongjia Lin, Zhidong Yang, Ziheng Xu, Na Gao, Rui Yan, Xiuhui Shi, Senlin Lin, Bowen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate biomedical image segmentation is crucial for clinical diagnosis. Convolutional neural networks and Transformer-based models have been widely used for biomedical image segmentation and have improved segmentation accuracy across multiple imaging modalities. However, most existing methods rely heavily on supervised learning with large-scale annotated datasets, while professionally annotated biomedical images remain scarce. In addition, the geometric consistency available from augmented and unlabeled images is not always explicitly exploited.MethodsWe proposed a similarity-aware network with contrastive optimization for biomedical image segmentation, termed SimBIS. SimBIS supplements pixel-wise supervised learning with an output-space consistency objective. Specifically, segmentation predictions generated from augmented views of the same object are projected into a seven-dimensional Hu-moment space, and the discrepancy between these projections is minimized during training. The Hu-moment is used as a parameter-free auxiliary descriptor of global mask geometry, which is invariant to translation, isotropic scaling, and in-plane rotation. Fine-grained and irregular boundaries are still optimized through the pixel-level supervised loss.ResultsSimBIS improved mDice and mIoU on both LID and SID datasets across multiple classic segmentation baselines. On publicly available biomedical segmentation tasks, including Kvasir-Seg, CVC-ClinicDB, and ISIC 2018, SimBIS not only improved the performance of the original backbone models but also outperformed other state-of-the-art methods on various metrics. Visual segmentation results further showed that SimBIS can effectively segment small targets and distinguish challenging structures, producing more accurate and consistent contours than existing models.ConclusionsSimBIS improves biomedical image segmentation by incorporating Hu-moment-based global shape consistency into end-to-end optimization while retaining pixel-wise supervision. It effectively leverages augmented and unlabeled data without introducing additional trainable parameters or inference-time cost, demonstrating strong potential for annotation-efficient biomedical image segmentation.

Introduction

Biomedical image segmentation is essential in clinical applications, such as anatomical structure localization and image lesion detection [1–3]. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the tongue is a crucial organ of the human body to reflect diseases, obliging in the diagnosis of the syndrome types, pathological types, lesion location and clinical stages of oesophagal cancer, guiding the treatment of oesophagal cancer and judging the prognosis of oesophagal cancer and prognosis of different treatment methods [4]. Tongue image segmentation is the prerequisite and basis for visualization, objectivity, and quantification of tongue diagnosis since the accuracy of the segmentation method of the tongue images directly impacts the results of the tongue diagnosis. Moreover, high-accuracy segmentation can reduce the miss rate of lesions, and it is significantly instrumental in predicting survival rate at various stages for evaluation of lesions such as polyps and skin cancers [5–7].

Recently, deep neural network (DNN) based methods have attracted considerable attention in computer vision community [8–10], especially biomedical image segmentation, because DNNs have an influential ability to learn features through large-scale iterations. Two main categories of DNN-based segmentation methods are convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer [9, 11]. Both have achieved conspicuous success in various imaging modalities like MRI, CT, tongue detector, endoscope and dermatoscopy.

However, these methods mostly rely on supervised learning with large-scale labels, and professionally annotated medical data are difficult to obtain because expert annotation is time-consuming. Consequently, a large amount of unlabeled data remains underused. In addition, most studies focus on increasingly complicated network architectures, such as dense connections, attention mechanisms, and multi-scale designs [12–16], rather than explicitly regularizing prediction consistency with augmented and unlabeled data. In this work, we focus on a task-relevant form of output consistency: whether the same biomedical object yields stable global shape statistics under admissible transformations, while pixel-level supervision continues to determine local boundary details.

Contrastive learning is a self-supervised learning (SSL) method that can learn image-level features from large-scale data without any manual labels [17, 18]. Thus, contrastive learning has achieved extraordinary performance in the field of computer vision, even comparable to supervised learning [19]. Contrastive learning can utilize diverse data to learn the latent representation and demonstrate the powerful feature representation capability [20]. Although contrastive learning is a promising method, most advanced segmentation models are still based on supervised learning, steadily producing accurate segmentation. Additionally, most of the research on contrastive learning is based on image-level image recognition and classification, and it is relatively rare to extend it to pixel-level prediction and it is hard to locate the region of interest (RoI) [21, 22].

To maintain the segmentation accuracy of supervised learning while exploiting additional augmented and unlabeled images, we propose a similarity-aware network with contrastive optimization for biomedical image segmentation (SimBIS). Unlike existing contrastive segmentation methods that mainly compare intermediate feature representations, SimBIS imposes consistency directly on soft segmentation outputs. Moreover, unlike previous moment-invariant-based methods that use moments as hand-crafted features or supplementary measurements, SimBIS incorporates a fixed Hu moment projection into end-to-end network optimization. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

A similarity-aware framework is proposed to complement supervised biomedical image segmentation with output-space consistency on augmented and unlabeled data. Unlike conventional contrastive segmentation methods that mainly operate on intermediate feature embeddings, SimBIS directly constrains the soft segmentation predictions of positive views in a morphological representation space. The supervised module learns pixel-level localization and local boundary details, while the contrastive optimization module regularizes global mask geometry. Experiments on LID, SID, and three public biomedical segmentation tasks show that SimBIS consistently improves multiple segmentation baselines;A deterministic Hu-moment projection and two contrastive optimization losses are introduced. Unlike previous moment-based segmentation methods that use moments as static input features or supplementary descriptors, SimBIS applies the Hu moment operator directly to soft probability maps and back-propagates the resulting consistency loss through the segmentation network. The seven-dimensional projection provides a compact, parameter-free description of low-order global mask geometry with translation, isotropic-scale, and in-plane-rotation invariance. Importantly, the projection is used only as an auxiliary consistency regularizer to optimal biomedical boundaries.

Related work

In this section, we summarize the most typical CNN-based and transformer-based methods used in biomedical image segmentation and review the existing contrastive learning techniques. Finally, we make an overview of moment invariants.

Biomedical image segmentation

The methods based on CNN and Transformer present excellent performance in bio-medical image segmentation and are extensively applied by researchers.

For convolutional neural networks, the Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) [23] was a milestone in deep-learning-based semantic segmentation models. Inspired by FCNs and encoder-decoder models, ronneberger et al. [24] proposed the U-Net for segmenting bio-logical microscopy images, and now it is widely used for medical and biomedical image segmentation. Nowadays, its variants are extended into many applications, such as UNet++ [16], ResUNet [25], R2U-Net [26], Attention U-Net [27], DoubleU-Net [28], FF-Unet [29], and DHMF-MLP [30] are popular CNN-based methods. Other CNN-based methods like PSPNet [31], DeepLabV3+ [32], and HRNet [33] are also widely used.

Inspired by the success of Transformer [15], in Natural Language Processing (NLP), more and more transformer-based methods are applied to computer vision (CV) tasks. Vision Transformer (ViT) [34] was the first attempt to demonstrate that a pure Transformer based architecture could achieve SOTA performance. Transformer-based methods like PraNet [6], TransUNet [11], MedT [35], TransFuse [36], and SANet [37] employ attention mechanisms and multiscale feature modeling to capture global dependencies and local spatial details. Hybrid architectures have also been developed to combine convolutional operations with Transformer-based representations. For example, MobileUNETR [38] integrates local and global image information, while DAM-Seg incorporates dense associative networks into a Transformer-based architecture to retain cardiac patterns and improve anatomical consistency [39]. More recently, CGQR-Net extracts contours from coarse predictions and encodes them as learnable queries to refine multiscale features for boundary-aware cardiac ultrasound segmentation [40].

Although the above methods perform exceptionally in biomedical image processing, most work concentrates only on supervised learning with large-scale labels. It is necessary to explore alternative ways to improve the model learning ability.

Contrastive learning

Currently, self-supervised contrastive learning methods [19, 20] have been broadly employed in computer vision, and they can utilize unlabeled data to learn the underlying representations. Some improved methods of SwAV [41], MoCo [40], SimCLR [19] were comparable to the state-of-the-art supervised method on ImageNet. The main idea of contrastive learning is to generate positive and negative data pairs, pull the positive data pairs closer, and push the negative data pairs farther in the feature space. Several methods based on contrastive loss were proposed to treat each instance as a class and use instance discrimination based on contrastive loss for representation learning [17–19]. Other works also researched hard negative mining strategies and various approaches using memory banks [42]. In addition [43, 44], showed that only positive examples are effective in the contrastive learning model.

Most of the research on contrastive learning is based on image-level image recognition and classification, and it is relatively rare to extend it to pixel-level prediction. What is more, contrastive learning is mainly employed in pre-training for semantic segmentation tasks. Recently, ConDSeg [45] uses contrastive learning to improve foreground–background feature discrimination, whereas SSMD-UNet combines supervised lesion segmentation with an unsupervised reconstruction task in a multi-decoder architecture, allowing additional unlabeled fundus images to contribute to representation learning [46].

Moment invariants

As a classical theory of pattern recognition and image processing, moment invariants have been confirmed to be invariant to scaling, translation and rotation [47]. It can be measured according to the information provided by the shape boundary and its internal region. Moment invariants are widely applied in computer vision, such as target recognition [48], object classification [49] and image registration [50]. Recently, some works implemented moment invariants to biomedical image segmentation, such as brain segmentation [51] and retinal blood vessel segmentation [52]. In addition, recent medical image segmentation studies have demonstrated that explicit spatial or morphology-aware guidance can improve structural prediction. For example, Umirzakova et al. introduced a force-map-enhanced lightweight model to provide spatial guidance for cervical cell segmentation [53]. Although this method does not use moment invariants, it further supports the value of incorporating explicit structural information into medical image segmentation.

However, most previous moment-based methods use moment invariants as hand-crafted features or supplementary information for segmentation. In this work, we apply Hu moments directly to the soft segmentation predictions and use the resulting invariant representations to measure morphological consistency between augmented views. The corresponding consistency loss is back-propagated through the predictions to optimize the shared segmentation network.

Methodology

The overview of proposed SimBIS. The SimBIS is composed of a supervised module, a contrastive optimization module, a data augmentation module, a segmentation model and loss functions

The SimBIS network is shown in Fig. 1. The SimBIS network contains a data augmentation module, a contrastive optimization module, a supervised module, a segmentation model and loss functions. The data augmentation module generates positive views from labeled and unlabeled images for the contrastive optimization module. The supervised module learns pixel-wise target localization and local boundary details, whereas the contrastive optimization module imposes consistency on the global geometry of the predicted masks. Both modules share the same segmentation model. Finally, the supervised and contrastive losses are combined, and the segmentation model is optimized through back-propagation.

Data augmentation module

The data augmentation module can promote the richness and diversity of images and form an adversarial mechanism with the contrastive optimization module. Specifically, the data augmentation module improves the discriminability of the image. In contrast, the contrastive optimization module decreases the error of the similarity (or increases the similarity) of the image feature mapping through contrastive self-supervision, thereby optimizing the network and improving performance.

Contrastive optimization module

In previous work, contrastive learning is often used as a pre-training task before a downstream segmentation task. When the pre-text and downstream tasks are optimized separately, image-level contrastive objectives may not reliably identify the biomedical region of interest. SimBIS therefore trains the supervised and contrastive objectives simultaneously: the supervised objective identifies the target region and provides pixel-level guidance, while the contrastive objective regularizes the consistency of the predicted target geometry across positive views. In SimBIS, latent feature exploration is an auxiliary optimization process that uses additional augmented and unlabeled images to constrain the representation learned by the segmentation network through prediction-space consistency.

Step 6: Projection into the invariant shape space. Specifically, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{t}}:[\mathrm{0,1}{]}^{H\times\:W}\to\:{\mathbb{R}}^{7}$$\end{document} denote the Hu moment projection operator. For each prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{o}_{i}$$\end{document}, the fixed moment projection is defined as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{z}_{i}=p}_{moment}\:\left({o}_{i}\text{}\right)=[{\varnothing\:}_{1}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{2}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{3}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{4}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{5}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{6}^{i},{\varnothing\:}_{7}^{i}]$$\end{document}

Unlike a trainable projection head, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{t}}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} contains no learnable parameters. It transforms the complete soft prediction map into a compact representation of its global probability distribution. The representation is used only during training to compute the auxiliary consistency loss and introduces no additional parameters or inference-time computation.

The Hu moments are selected because they match the requirements of the present auxiliary objective: (i) their translation, isotropic-scale, and in-plane-rotation invariance directly matches the geometric augmentations used to construct positive views; (ii) they are computed without learnable parameters, avoiding an additional projection network; and (iii) their seven-dimensional form adds negligible storage and similarity-computation cost.

Generally, the predictions inferred by the segmentation model for different augmentations of the same image should be identical or highly similar. After mapping these predictions to the moment invariants, their corresponding feature vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{z}_{i}$$\end{document} are expected to be close in the invariant space. We then maximize the similarity (or minimize the discrepancy) between these moment invariant projections and back-propagate this signal to encourage the network to learn robust, augmentation-invariant feature representations. For transformations that preserve target identity and dominant morphology, the two projections are expected to satisfy:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{moment}\left({f}_{\theta\:}\right({t}_{a}\left({x}_{i}\right)\left)\right)\approx\:{p}_{moment}\left({f}_{\theta\:}\right({t}_{b}\left({x}_{i}\right)\left)\right)$$\end{document}

Supervised module

In the supervised module, the processed task is the target task. In general, the computer vision pipeline for adopting self-supervised learning involves performing the pre-text task and the downstream task. The downstream task is an application-specific task that utilizes the knowledge learned in the pre-text task. But the supervised module is conducted simultaneously with the contrastive optimization module in SimBIS. Thus, it will implicitly provide targets and their segmentation positions for similarity analysis.

In a particular segmentation task, users can choose different segmentation loss functions. Experiment

This section presents available datasets, evaluation metrics and implementation details. Implementation details

All the experiments are implemented on two GPUs using the PyTorch framework. Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4 is adopted, and all models are trained for 100 epochs. The values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{sup}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{con}$$\end{document} are empirically set to 0.6, 1, 0.1. Further, we resize the input images to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:352\times\:352$$\end{document}. A combination of binary cross-entropy and dice loss has been employed as the supervised loss function. The data augmentation is used for contrastive learning in the contrastive module and not for supervised learning in the supervised module. In this paper, the moment invariants we use are the Hu moment invariants [47]. In order to solve the problem that the components of the moment invariants are mostly decimals and flipping, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{log}\left(\left|{\mathcal{z}}_{i}\right|\right)$$\end{document} to represent the features in the computation.

Experimental results

Performance on lingual image dataset and sublingual image dataset with different baselines

First, we investigate the performance of SimBIS without using additional unlabeled data on two datasets, Lingual Image Dataset (LID) and Sublingual Image Dataset (SID) (refer Table 1), across five classic segmentation baselines and two representative methods, namely FCN, UNet, UNet++, AttUNet, ResUNet, MobileUNETR and ConDSeg. It is important to note that during similarity analysis of latent features in the contrastive optimization module, we only utilize the data from LID and SID without using their respective label information. Furthermore, the proportion of data used for similarity analysis will be discussed in 4.4.2.

Performance on Lingual Image D ataset. As seen from the experiment, adding SimBIS increased mDice and mIoU for each evaluated backbone on LID (refer Table 3). There has been a substantial refinement in evaluation metrics in all the baselines using our approach. We denote FCN, AttUnet, ResUnet and SimBIS as FCN8s, Attention UNet, and ResUnet34 and using the SimBIS, respectively. The best is based on ResUnet(SimBIS), and IoU is 0.972. The Data Augmentation Ratio. Excessive data augmentation will lead to over-fitting. However, a moderate amount of data augmentation can improve the performance of the models. In this regard, a data augmentation module is proposed, which can augment the existing datasets according to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}. Comparison with SOTA methods on publicly biomedical segmentation tasks

SimBIS can also be extended to utilize unlabeled data from the same and similar domains. We further compare different state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods on three publicly available biomedical segmentation tasks, namely Kvasir-Seg, CVC-ClinicDB, and ISIC 2018. It should be noted that we additionally leverage unlabeled data for similarity analysis in the contrastive optimization module (as presented in Table 2). We opt to utilize Pranet as the segmentation model for Kvasir-Seg and CVC-ClinicDB datasets, while employing TransUNet as the segmentation model for ISIC 2018 dataset. This decision is made to validate the effectiveness of our approach, as opposed to relying solely on state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods.

Performance on Kvasir-Seg. We demonstrate the quantitative evaluation of SimBIS in Table 4 and qualitative results in Fig. 5(a). From the quantitative results, we can observe that our SimBIS not only significantly improves on all metrics relative to the original model (PraNet) but also outperforms all the other SOTA methods. Specifically, SimBIS achieves improvements of 3.34% on mDice and 4.28% on the mIoU compared to PraNet. Moreover, SimBIS achieves the best segmentation performance among all models and has an improvement of 0.43% and 0.92% over MobileUNETR in mDice and mIoU. As shown in Fig. 5(a), SimBIS can better learn the features of colon polyps, and its segmented regions are more accurate than the original model (PraNet). In addition, the visualization results can also be observed; SimBIS has the capability to segment small targets, which the PraNet model missed.

Performance on CVC-ClinicDB. The quantitative results for the CVC-ClinicDB are exhibited in Table 4. Specifically, SimBIS achieves a mDice of 0.929, mIoU of 0.887. We can observe an improvement of 3.33% and 4.47% over PraNet in mDice and mIoU. Although TransFuse still keep the SOTA performance in mDice (0.934), SimBIS obtains the best balance compared to the other SOTA methods. The qualitative segmentation performance in Fig. 5(b), SimBIS has a stronger ability to identify and segment polyps that are similar in colour and structure to the surrounding intestinal tissues, and the segmentation edge of polyps is also significantly improved.

Performance on ISIC 2018. The quantitative results from Table 4 reveal that our proposed SimBIS achieves SOTA results on mDice and mIoU. Specifically, SimBIS achieves the mDice of 0.909 and mIoU of 0.843 with improvements of 1.67% and 2.55%. As visualized in Fig. 5(c), SimBIS is more conducive to distinguishing diseased skin from normal skin. Additionally, we can obtain predictions with more abundant details, and their contours are more consistent with labels.

The Effectiveness of SimBIS. As seen from Table 5, the model is trained for supervised learning only using the supervised module, obtaining the mDice of 0.899, mIoU of 0.849. However, using the supervised module, data augmentation module and contrastive optimization module simultaneously, namely, SimBIS, can increase mDice and mIoU by 1.4 ~ 3.1% and 1.3 ~ 3.8%, respectively. This improvement is significant and inspiring. These results indicate that the auxiliary output-space consistency objective can extract useful global-shape constraints from augmented data and improve segmentation performance of the supervised model.

The Option of Data Augmentations. To improve samples’ diversity and discriminability, we use combined transformation augmentation and nonlinear transformation augmentation on the data augmentation module. We can see that the combination transform augmentation and CutMix can achieve better segmentation, as shown in Table 5. The reason is that the combination transform augmentation can obtain more diversified augmented images through geometric transformation and Color space transformation, while CutMix can obtain new samples by introducing different backgrounds. In addition, random erasure may break the integrity of the region of interest in the image, resulting in poor segmentation performance.

The Option of Contrastive Optimization Loss Functions. It mainly analyses the performance of two contrastive loss functions in the contrastive optimization module. The cosine similarity loss function may attain the best segmentation performance compared to the error loss function. The result indicates that the cosine similarity loss function is more conducive to measuring the similarity between samples.

The Option of Contrastive Optimization Weights. In addition, by fixing the supervised loss weight (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{sup}=1$$\end{document}) and varying the contrastive loss weight (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{con}$$\end{document}), we observed that even a small value (e.g., (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{con}=0.01$$\end{document})) brings substantial performance gains (e.g., mDice + 2.56%, mIoU + 2.59%), as shown in Table 6. The model remains robust across a wide range of (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{con}$$\end{document}) values (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.01\sim\:0.5$$\end{document}), consistently improving segmentation accuracy without introducing any the number of parameters, peak GPU memory, or inference latency. The only trade-off is a modest training time increase, which is acceptable given the consistent accuracy improvement. This design ensures improved segmentation precision with minimal computational cost, confirming the practical applicability and effectiveness of SimBIS in real-world scenarios.

Computational Cost. For a mini-batch of B soft prediction maps with spatial resolution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\times\:W$$\end{document}, Hu moment extraction requires a fixed number of pixel-wise reductions and algebraic operations. Its theoretical time complexity is therefore \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(BHW\right)$$\end{document}, and the projection introduces no trainable parameters. In our implementation, Hu moments are calculated on the CPU during training. Consequently, the additional computation does not increase peak GPU memory, but the CPU-side calculation and CPU–GPU synchronization introduce additional wall-clock training time. As shown in Table 6, enabling the contrastive optimization branch increases the training time from approximately 150 s to 164 s per epoch, corresponding to an overhead of approximately 9.3%. In contrast, the parameter count remains unchanged at 45.55 M, while the peak GPU memory and inference latency remain unchanged at 44,909 MB and 75 ms per image, respectively. The Hu moment projection and contrastive loss are used only during training and are removed during inference. SimBIS therefore retains the same inference computation graph and inference FLOPs as the corresponding baseline model.

Conclusion

In this work, we propose a novel similarity-aware network with contrastive optimization for biomedical image segmentation. SimBIS applies a fixed, parameter-free Hu moment projection to soft segmentation outputs and minimizes their discrepancy across augmented views. The key methodological distinction is that consistency is imposed on the morphology of the predicted masks rather than only on intermediate feature embeddings. In addition, Hu moments are incorporated directly into end-to-end optimization instead of being used as static hand-crafted features or post-processing measurements. This design integrates moment invariants directly into network optimization, providing additional global shape supervision from labeled and unlabeled data without extra trainable parameters or inference-time cost. Extensive experiments on LID, SID datasets and another three publicly biomedical segmentation tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of SimBIS, and the results manifest that SimBIS achieves better performance than other existing methods.

Despite the promising results of SimBIS, several limitations remain. First, the segmentation performance of SimBIS is inherently bounded by the capacity and architecture of the underlying supervised segmentation network. In other words, SimBIS can effectively enhance latent feature learning but cannot surpass the intrinsic representational limit of the base model. The Hu-moment objective regularizes the representation learned by the base model but cannot replace the intrinsic representational capacity of that model. Second, the contrastive optimization module increases training time, although it introduces no additional model parameters or inference latency. Third, the moment invariants projection used to quantify feature similarity may not fully capture complex spatial or semantic variations in fine-grained biomedical structures. This limitation is mitigated in SimBIS because local details are learned by the high-dimensional backbone features and pixel-wise supervised losses. Finally, SimBIS currently relies on unlabeled data from the same or closely related domains; thus, its robustness under significant domain shifts remains to be systematically evaluated. Future work will focus on designing more expressive feature mapping representations, improving computational efficiency, and extending SimBIS to multi-domain and multi-target segmentation scenarios.

Overall, SimBIS provides a data-driven way to derive an additional global consistency signal from augmented and unlabeled images while retaining conventional pixel-wise supervision. Future work will focus on combining the current interpretable global descriptor with more expressive local or multiscale representations for complex and multi-target segmentation.
