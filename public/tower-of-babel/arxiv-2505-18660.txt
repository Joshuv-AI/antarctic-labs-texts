So-Fake: Benchmarking and Explaining Social Media Image Forgery Detection
Zhenglin Huang, Tianxiao Li, Xiangtai Li, Haiqun Wen, Yiwei He, Jiangning Zhang, Hao Fei, Xi Yang, Bei Peng, Xiaowei Huang, Guangliang Cheng

Abstract
Recent advances in AI-powered generative models have enabled the creation of increasingly realistic synthetic images, posing significant risks to information integrity and public trust on social media platforms. While robust detection frameworks and diverse, large-scale datasets are essential to mitigate these risks, existing academic efforts remain limited in scope: current datasets lack the diversity, scale, and realism required for social media contexts, while detection methods struggle with generalization to unseen generative technologies. To bridge this gap, we introduce So-Fake-Set , a comprehensive social media-oriented dataset with over 2 million high-quality images, diverse generative sources, and photorealistic imagery synthesized using 35 state-of-the-art generative models. To rigorously evaluate cross-domain robustness, we establish a novel and large-scale ( 100K ) out-of-domain benchmark ( So-Fake-OOD ) featuring synthetic imagery from commercial models explicitly excluded from the training distribution, creating a realistic testbed for evaluating real-world performance. Leveraging these resources, we present So-Fake-R1 , an advanced vision-language framework that employs reinforcement learning for highly accurate forgery detection, precise localization, and explainable inference through interpretable visual rationales. Extensive experiments show that So-Fake-R1 outperforms the second-best method, with a 1.3% gain in detection accuracy and a 4.5% increase in localization IoU. By integrating a scalable dataset, a challenging OOD benchmark, and an advanced detection framework, this work establishes a new foundation for social media-centric forgery detection research.

Image Forgery Detection Datasets

The landscape of image forgery detection datasets has evolved alongside generative techniques, beginning with a predominant focus on facial manipulations. Early datasets such as DFFD

established the foundation for deepfake detection research, albeit with a narrow emphasis on GAN-generated facial forgeries

[

and DMimage

, which include diverse content types beyond faces. Furthermore, recent datasets have moved beyond simple real/fake labels to provide richer annotations, including binary masks for localization
[

and generation descriptions for explainability
[
21

40

39

. More recently, researchers
[

compares prominent forgery datasets across key dimensions including generative methods, dataset size, and data source diversity. So-Fake-Set addresses the limitations identified above through its large scale, state-of-the-art generative techniques, diverse data sources spanning multiple content types, and comprehensive annotations supporting multi-class detection, tampered area localization, explanation, and an accompanying OOD benchmark for rigorous cross-domain evaluation.

2.2
Image Forgery Detection, Localization, and Explanation

Recent developments in forgery detection have focused primarily on using deep neural networks to distinguish authentic content from manipulated content. While these methods

, neglecting the diverse manipulation types characteristic of social media images.
In parallel with addressing data limitations, interpretability has emerged as a critical frontier, with recent approaches attempting to generate human-understandable justifications alongside detection.
Motivated by recent vision-language models

In contrast to these approaches, So-Fake-R1 leverages reinforcement learning to discover authentic model reasoning, enabling accurate detection, precise localization, and interpretable explanations without relying on manual annotations, making it well-suited for real-world deployment in social media contents.

2.3
Reinforcement Learning in VLMs

Reinforcement learning has recently shown promise in vision-language tasks by enabling models to develop reasoning capabilities through trial-and-error learning rather than mimicking human-provided explanations. Among recent advances, methods like offline actor-critic
[
65
]
, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
66
]
have shown promise, while Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
25
]
has emerged as particularly effective for tasks requiring minimal human supervision. Specifically, GRPO achieves this through rule-based reward mechanisms that guide models toward desired behaviors without relying on extensive annotations.
This annotation-free approach makes GRPO particularly valuable for vision-language models (VLMs)
[
60
,
67
,
68
]
, where obtaining detailed human explanations for visual reasoning is especially challenging. Notable applications include Seg-Zero
[
69
]
for zero-shot segmentation, Visual-RFT
[
70
]
for visual question answering, and VLM-R1
[
71
]
for robust object detection. Although GRPO has shown success in various vision-language tasks, it has not yet been applied to forgery detection, where both accuracy and unbiased explainability are essential. To our knowledge, So-Fake-R1 is the first framework to use GRPO for forgery detection, eliminating the need for manual annotations while achieving superior performance.

Data Collection

We use distinct data sources for So-Fake-Set and So-Fake-OOD to support in-domain training and cross-domain generalization evaluation, respectively, as detailed below:

We select real images from COCO

So-Fake-OOD.
For OOD benchmark, we deliberately source images from a different distribution by collecting content directly from Reddit using its official API. This ensures that our benchmark reflects authentic social media content not present in standard computer vision datasets, providing a rigorous evaluation of cross-domain generalization. Figure

3.2.2
Synthetic Data Generation

Full Synthetic Images.

We generate full synthetic images using 35 different techniques to ensure comprehensive coverage of forgery generation methods. Of these, 25 are used exclusively for So-Fake-Set, 8 for So-Fake-OOD only, and 2 appear in both splits (see Appendix
A.1
for complete list). While we primarily focus on state-of-the-art methods, we deliberately include limited samples from older techniques to ensure our So-Fake-Set captures the full spectrum of synthetic content that may be encountered in real-world scenarios. These generative methods fall into two main categories: GAN-based and diffusion-based techniques.
For GAN-based methods, we adhere to the official implementation guidelines. For diffusion-based approaches, we employ a text-to-image generation pipeline. First, we use Qwen2.5-VL-3B
[
82
]
to generate detailed captions describing the content of source images. These captions then serve as prompts for various diffusion models to generate synthetic content. After generation, we apply a pretrained ResNet-50
[
83
]
classifier to categorize the generated images into our 12 predefined categories. We conduct rigorous human evaluation to ensure dataset quality and suitability, with detailed assessment protocols provided in the Appendix
A.2
. Figure
2
(b) (left) presents representative process of generating full synthetic images.

Tampered Images.
To generate tampered images, we employ image inpainting techniques that modify specific regions while preserving the rest of the original image. We utilize three state-of-the-art inpainting models for So-Fake-set: Latent Diffusion
[
30
]
, Stable Diffusion-XL
[
84
]
, and FLUX.1-Fill-dev
[
38
]
. For So-Fake-OOD, we employ additional methods including Ideogram 3.0
[
85
]
, GPT-4o
[
41
]
, and Recraft
[
86
]
, ensuring evaluation on unseen generation techniques. Following SIDA
[
15
]
, we obtain object masks from source images using LangSAM
[
87
]
. To maintain semantic consistency, we use the original image captions as prompts when applying inpainting techniques. The inpainting models receive three inputs: the original image, the corresponding mask, and the caption-based prompts. An example of a generated tampered image is shown in Figure

(b), right.

4.1
Architecture

Recently, LLMs
[
15
,
21
,
63
,
64
]
have demonstrated strong capabilities in forgery detection. However, these approaches typically rely on extensive manual annotations and struggle to provide genuine, fine-grained explanations of their reasoning process. To address these limitations, we propose So-Fake-R1, a unified framework that simultaneously performs detection, localization, and explanation within a single architectural design. So-Fake-R1 achieves state-of-the-art performance through reinforcement learning, requiring only limited cold-start data and a few thousand GRPO training steps, as illustrated in Figure
3
(a). Figure
3
(b) illustrates the So-Fake-R1 GRPO training framework, which utilizes two main components: Qwen2.5-VL-7B
[
82
]
as the policy model and SAM2
[
88
]
for segmentation. Given an input image
x_{i}
and a corresponding text prompt
x_{t}
, we feed them into the policy model to generate multiple candidate completions. GRPO then evaluates these completions using a reward model, computes relative advantages based on predefined reward functions (Section
4.2
), and optimizes the policy to maximize rewards while maintaining KL divergence constraints with reference policy.

Unlike SIDA
[
15
]
and FakeShield
[
21
]
, which rely on special tokens and predetermined region annotations for tampered images, So-Fake-R1 generates natural language completions that seamlessly integrate detection, localization, and explanation without requiring special tokens such as
<DET>
or
<SEG>
. When So-Fake-R1 identifies an image as TAMPERED, it generates a textual completion
\mathbf{C}
that includes bounding box coordinates. These coordinates are processed by the prompt encoder
\mathcal{F}_{penc}
, producing the encoded prompt
\mathbf{B}
. Simultaneously, the input image
x_{i}
is processed by the image encoder
\mathcal{F}_{ienc}
. The decoder
\mathcal{F}_{dec}
then combines both the encoded image and the encoded prompt to generate a precise segmentation mask
\mathbf{M}
for the identified tampered region, as follows:

\mathbf{C}=\pi_{\theta}(x_{t},x_{i}),\quad\mathbf{B}=\mathcal{F}_{penc}(\mathbf{C}),\quad\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{F}_{dec}(\mathcal{F}_{ienc}(x_{i}),\mathbf{B})

(1)

4.2
Reward Functions

The reward function plays a crucial role in our method, as it determines the optimization direction of the model. The aim of So-Fake-R1 is to achieve detection, localization, and explanation in a single completion, which requires a carefully designed reward function that balances multiple objectives. We design five reward components:

(1) Thinking Format Reward.
Following DeepSeek-R1
[
25
]
, we encourage structured reasoning by rewarding outputs that contain reasoning within
<think>...</think>
tags and final answers within
<answer>...</answer>
tags.

(2) Classification Reward.
We reward accurate multi-class classification (REAL, TAMPERED, or FULL_SYNTHETIC) based on the label provided within the
<answer>...</answer>
tags.
Inspired by Seg-zero
[
69
]
, we design three segmentation rewards:

(3) Segmentation Format Reward:
The model receives a positive reward if the answer includes properly formatted bounding box coordinates (four numerical values) with relevant keywords.

(4) Bounding Box IoU Reward:
A positive reward is given if the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) between the predicted and ground truth bounding boxes exceeds 0.5.

(5) Bounding Box L1 Reward:
This reward evaluates the L1 distance between predicted and ground truth bounding boxes, with a positive reward for distances less than 10 pixels.

The total reward is calculated as follows:

R_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{\text{fmt}}R_{\text{fmt}}+\lambda_{\text{cls}}R_{\text{cls}}+\lambda_{\text{seg\_fmt}}R_{\text{seg\_fmt}}+\lambda_{\text{IoU}}R_{\text{IoU}}+\lambda_{\text{L1}}R_{\text{L1}}

(2)

where each
\lambda
value is a hyperparameter that controls the relative importance of its corresponding reward component. The specific weight settings, detailed reward values, and scoring criteria are presented in the Appendix
B.1
.

4.3
Training

The training of
So-Fake-R1
consists of two stages:
cold start
and
GRPO training
.

Cold Start.

We construct a dataset of 8,500 examples to guide the model in generating outputs that follow the desired format. To facilitate this, we leverage GPT-4o
[
41
]
to produce feasible chains of thought, enabling the model to learn structured reasoning patterns (
<think>...</think>
,
<answer>...</answer>
). Specifically, we fine-tune
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct

[
82
]
on this dataset for one epoch. The cold start stage is efficient, taking only about 30 minutes on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. This initial training provides the foundation for the model to understand the task of forgery detection before proceeding to the more sophisticated GRPO training. Further details about the cold-start dataset and its construction are provided in the Appendix
A.3
.

GRPO Training.
After cold start, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
25
]
to enhance the model’s ability to generate coherent explanations without requiring additional manually labeled data. For each input query, GRPO generates multiple completions from the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
. These completions are evaluated by a reward model, and the advantage values
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, …,
A_{G}
are calculated through group-based normalization. The policy is then optimized to maximize these advantages while maintaining an appropriate KL divergence with the reference policy.

Experiments

5.1
Experimental Settings

Datasets.

We train So-Fake-R1 in two stages: (1) cold-start training on 8,500 images sampled from So-Fake-Set with chain-of-thought annotations, followed by (2) GRPO fine-tuning on the same dataset to enhance explanation quality, detection accuracy, and localization precision. To evaluate the out-of-distribution capability of models, we test it on the separate So-Fake-OOD benchmark.

Implementation Details.

We use Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
[
82
]
as our policy model and SAM2
[
88
]
for segmentation. We apply LoRA
[
89
]
with
\alpha=32
and dropout=0.05 for efficient training. Learning rates are set to
5\times 10^{-5}
for cold-start and
1\times 10^{-4}
for GRPO stages. We assign
\lambda_{\text{fmt}}
=
\lambda_{\text{seg\_fmt}}
= 0.1, and
\lambda_{\text{cls}}=\lambda_{\text{IOU}}=\lambda_{\text{L1}}=0.9
. GRPO training was conducted on two 40G A100 GPUs and completed in approximately 24 hours. Additional details are provided in the Appendix
B.1
.

Metrics.
We evaluate detection performance using image-level accuracy (ACC) and F1 scores, and localization quality using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 scores. For explanation interpretability, we employ two complementary metrics: (1) Cosine Semantic Similarity (CSS), which captures semantic alignment between embeddings of predicted and ground-truth explanations, and (2) ROUGE-L, which quantifies textual overlap through longest common subsequence matching. Ground-truth explanations are obtained by generating initial reasoning with Claude 3.7 Sonnet
[
90
]
, followed by human experts’ revision and validation. Further details are provided in the Appendix
B.2
.

6
Conclusion and Limitation

Conclusion.
In this work, we present
So-Fake-Set
, a large-scale dataset containing 2 million images across 12 categories, generated by 35 different models to advance social media forgery detection. We also introduce
So-Fake-OOD
, a 100K-image benchmark curated from Reddit content and unseen generative models, aimed at evaluating cross-domain robustness. Additionally, we propose
So-Fake-R1
, a reinforcement learning-based framework that unifies detection, localization, and explanation, achieving state-of-the-art performance across diverse evaluation settings. We hope this work establishes a strong foundation for social media-oriented forgery detection and highlights the potential of reinforcement learning for generating interpretable rationales.

Limitation.
Despite its strengths, our work has certain limitations. So-Fake-R1 may still produce inaccurate localization results, and both the scale and quality of So-Fake-Set can be further improved.

A.2
Human Expert Assessment

We conducted a rigorous human evaluation to ensure the quality and appropriateness of our dataset. Five experts participated in a multi-stage process that included: (1) filtering source images, (2) validating model selection choices, and (3) assessing the quality of generated synthetic content. The overall process is illustrated in Figure
6
.

Source Image Filtering.

To construct a realistic and diverse OOD benchmark, we carefully selected subreddits based on their popularity, topical diversity, and coverage of broad content categories representative of typical social media platforms. Specifically, we selected 13 subreddits, including (1)
pics
, (2)
HumanPorn
, (3)
aww
, (4)
earthporn
, (5)
redditgetsdrawn
, (6)
drawme
, (7)
TheWayWeWere
, (8)
Animal
, (9)
RoomPorn
, (10)
football
, (11)
spotted
, (12)
carporn
, and (13)
cityporn
.
We used the official Reddit API
3
3

3

https://www.reddit.com/prefs/apps
to collect images and adhered to Reddit’s Public Content Policy
4
4

4

https://www.reddit.com/r/reddit/comments/1co0xnu/sharing_our_public_content_policy_and_a_new/
, ensuring that all images were used strictly for non-commercial research purposes.

After collecting the initial dataset, experts manually reviewed the Reddit images and filtered out those deemed inappropriate, harmful, or irrelevant to social media image analysis. This process resulted in a curated set of 100K high-quality source images for building the So-Fake-OOD benchmark.

Model Selection.

Five human experts conducted a thorough evaluation of generative models to determine which should be included in So-Fake-Set and So-Fake-OOD. Each candidate model was reviewed based on its visual output quality, prompt controllability, content diversity, and relevance to current generative trends. The experts considered both widely adopted and emerging models, ensuring a balanced mix of GAN-based and diffusion-based architectures. This process resulted in the selection of 35 models, ranging from early GANs like StyleGAN and AttGAN to state-of-the-art diffusion models such as SD-XL
[
84
]
, FLUX
[
38
]
, and DiT
[
108
]
. By involving domain experts in model evaluation, we ensured that the final selection reflects both high technical quality and representative diversity for real-world image forgery scenarios.

Image Quality Assessment.

Expert reviewers assessed the realism of generated images using a five-point scale (0–5, with 5 indicating the highest quality). Images scoring below 3 underwent secondary review, and confirmed low-quality samples were excluded from the dataset. For So-Fake-Set, we adopted a two-stage filtering process: an initial automated pass using QwenVL-7B
[
82
]
to remove clearly non-photorealistic images, followed by random human spot-checks. In contrast, all So-Fake-OOD images were subjected to a comprehensive manual review to ensure consistently high visual quality and realism.

A.3
Cold-Start Dataset Construction

This section describes how the cold-start data was constructed. We first sampled images evenly across all 27 generative methods and 12 categories from So-Fake-Set. In total, we collected approximately 8,500 images, maintaining a balanced distribution across the three classes: real, fully synthetic, and tampered.
We then used QwenVL-2.5-7B
[
82
]
to generate initial chain-of-thought explanations for these images, using the prompt structure shown in Code

"TAMPERED,<|box_start|>"

A.4
Explanation Ground-Truth Generation

This subsection outlines how textual explanation ground-truths were constructed to supervise the explanation component of So-Fake-R1.
We first provided Claude 3.7 Sonnet
[
90
]
with prompts that included the original image, a tampered region mask (for tampered images only), and the corresponding class label (real, tampered, or fully synthetic). The model then generated chain-of-thought reasoning responses in the desired format.

To ensure the quality and semantic accuracy of the generated rationales, human experts systematically reviewed a sample of 3,000 outputs. This evaluation followed three key criteria:

(1) Accuracy
: The rationale must correctly describe the visual evidence in the image.

(2) Clarity
: The explanation must be concise, unambiguous, and easy to understand.

(3) Consistency
: The reasoning style and conclusions must be coherent across similar prompts and scenarios in the dataset.

All flawed or ambiguous outputs were refined or removed based on expert feedback. The finalized explanation annotations provide high-quality supervision for evaluating the multimodal reasoning capabilities of So-Fake-R1 and other related techniques.

B.1
Reinforcement Learning Configuration

In this section, we describe the configuration of the GRPO training setup, including the specific weight assignments and detailed reward components. The total reward is computed as:

R_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{\text{fmt}}R_{\text{fmt}}+\lambda_{\text{cls}}R_{\text{cls}}+\lambda_{\text{seg\_fmt}}R_{\text{seg\_fmt}}+\lambda_{\text{IoU}}R_{\text{IoU}}+\lambda_{\text{L1}}R_{\text{L1}}

(3)

Based on observations from the ablation study on reward design, we found that the progress of GRPO training is less sensitive to formatting rewards. Therefore, we set
\lambda_{\text{fmt}}
=
\lambda_{\text{seg\_fmt}}
= 0.1, and
\lambda_{\text{cls}}=\lambda_{\text{IOU}}=\lambda_{\text{L1}}=0.9
., encouraging the model to prioritize classification accuracy and precise segmentation outputs during training. Below, we highlight the specific characteristics and design considerations for each reward function:

Thinking Format Reward.
This reward function encourages structured reasoning by requiring the model to format its output using
<think>...</think>
for the reasoning process and
<answer>...</answer>
for the final answer. The model receives a reward of +1 if the output follows this format correctly; otherwise, it receives 0.

Classification Reward.
This reward encourages accurate multi-class classification among
REAL
,
TAMPERED
, and
FULL_SYNTHETIC
, based on the label provided within the
<answer>...</answer>
tags. To be specific, the model receives a reward of +1 for correctly identifying
REAL
or
FULL_SYNTHETIC
images, and a higher reward of +3 for correctly identifying
TAMPERED
, as detecting tampered images is more challenging. Incorrect classifications receive a reward of 0.

Segmentation Format Reward:
This reward encourages the model to produce bounding box predictions in the correct format. The model receives a reward of +1 if its output includes properly formatted coordinates (i.e., four numerical values enclosed between the tags
<|box_start|>
and
<|box_end|>
), such as:

<|box_start|>

(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})

<|box_end|>
.
If the format is incorrect or missing, the reward is 0.

Bounding Box IoU Reward:
This reward encourages accurate localization of manipulated regions in tampered images. For
TAMPERED
images, the model receives a reward of +3 if the predicted bounding box (enclosed in
<|box_start|>
and
<|box_end|>
) achieves an Intersection-over-Union (IoU) greater than 0.5 compared to the ground truth. For
REAL
and
FULL_SYNTHETIC
images, which do not require localization, a reward of +1 is assigned by default. In all other cases, the reward is 0.

(5) Bounding Box L1 Reward:
This reward evaluates the L1 distance between the predicted and ground truth bounding boxes. For
TAMPERED
images, the model receives a reward of +3 if the total L1 distance across all four coordinates is less than 10 pixels. For
REAL
and
FULL_SYNTHETIC
samples, which do not require bounding boxes, a default reward of +1 is assigned. Otherwise, the reward is 0.

Appendix C
Broader Social Impact

To ensure the quality, fairness, and responsible use of our dataset and method, we have implemented several key measures during development. Expert reviewers were involved throughout the data collection and annotation process, including the selection of source content, validation of generative outputs, and refinement of textual explanations. We also followed platform-specific content usage policies and maintained strict adherence to non-commercial, research-only purposes.

So-Fake-Set, So-Fake-OOD, and So-Fake-R1 framework are designed to advance the field of multimodal forgery detection, with a particular focus on social media contexts. By offering large-scale, diverse, and well-annotated benchmarks alongside an interpretable and high-performing model, our work provides valuable resources for the research community. These contributions can support future developments in robust AI systems, foster academic exploration, and assist in building more trustworthy digital media ecosystems.
We believe our dataset and methods will positively impact the broader AI and computer vision communities by encouraging progress in transparent, explainable, and socially beneficial technologies for image authenticity verification.
