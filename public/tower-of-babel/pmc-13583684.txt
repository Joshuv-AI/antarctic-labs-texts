Maintaining smoke-free homes in Germany: a hypothesis-generating qualitative study of women’s experiences across social and household contexts
Authors: Bibiana Barrera, Ariadna Feliu, Carolina Espina, Tilman Brand, Hajo Zeeb, Vaughan W Rees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore how women perceive, negotiate and maintain smoke-free home rules in Germany, and how age (life stage) and educational experiences shape these processes, using a hypothesis-generating qualitative approach.DesignHypothesis-generating qualitative study using semi-structured interviews analysed with thematic content analysis and an interpretive critical discourse layer, informed by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation and Behaviour (COM-B) model.SettingThe German arm of the Qualitative Component of the European Code Against Cancer, 4th Edition study conducted in Bremen, Germany, and coordinated by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.ParticipantsNine women purposively sampled for maximum variation in age (18–30, 31–45, 46–65 years) and educational level (low, secondary, high); all resided in Germany and had no previous cancer diagnosis.ResultsWomen expressed strong motivation to protect family members, particularly children, from secondhand smoke. However, their ability to enforce smoke-free rules varied. Difficulties were greater when household authority was constrained, when a cohabiting person who smokes shaped daily routines or when smoking was socially normalised. In these situations, women often adopted partial restrictions or harm-reduction strategies. Life stage and educational experiences intersected with household roles and relationship dynamics, shaping women’s perceived legitimacy and negotiation strategies.ConclusionsEstablishing and sustaining smoke-free homes is not solely an individual decision but is embedded in social relations, gender roles and structural constraints. These insights suggest that strengthening smoke-free homes in Germany requires approaches that address relational and contextual barriers alongside individual intentions.

Introduction

Secondhand smoke (SHS) exposure remains a major public health concern, particularly in private homes, which are the primary setting for SHS exposure across Europe.1 Women bear a disproportionate burden of SHS exposure in domestic settings, often as partners who do not smoke or caregivers in households where men smoke.2 3 This situation raises equity concerns and highlights the gendered dynamics of tobacco-related harm and household decision-making.

The past two decades have seen substantial progress in reducing the number of people exposed to SHS in German homes. A combination of public smoking bans, educational campaigns and increased social awareness of the harmful effects of SHS4 5 prompted 75% of German households to adopt smoke-free rules by 2018.6 7 The proportion of children aged 0–6 years exposed to SHS at home declined from 24% in the early 2000s to 6%–7% in 2012.8 Despite these improvements, SHS exposure persists. Families with lower socioeconomic status (SES) remain considerably less likely to adopt completely smoke-free rules at home,8 perpetuating unequal health risks for children and adults in socioeconomically disadvantaged households. Less is known about the lived processes through which smoke-free home rules are established, negotiated and sustained in everyday domestic life, where women are often positioned as household health gatekeepers and primary caregivers.

Even in households where residents do not smoke, incidental SHS exposure from visitors or incursions from neighbouring dwellings presents a health risk. A European study found that approximately 13% of homes with residents who do not smoke allowed visitors to smoke indoors,6 indicating potential gaps in awareness or agency. Partial restrictions (such as smoking only in one room or when children are absent) are insufficient because SHS spreads throughout buildings and persists over time, undermining protection efforts.1

Although a 100% smoke-free home policy is optimal, the creation and maintenance of smoke-free homes are shaped by structural factors, relational dynamics and gender roles.9 Women often attempt to establish smoke-free rules at home, yet their ability to enforce them may be constrained by partner behaviour and household power dynamics.10 Understanding how women perceive and navigate these processes across diverse social and household contexts remains limited. Qualitative research is an ideal strategy for understanding these perspectives and informing more equitable tobacco control interventions.

Sustained behavioural change is driven not only by knowledge or motivation but also by the interaction of personal capabilities, social opportunities and contextual factors that enable or constrain action.11 The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation and Behaviour (COM-B) model provides a comprehensive framework for examining how these influences interact to shape household tobacco control and why maintaining smoke-free rules may be straightforward in some settings yet difficult to enact in others.11

Within the COM-B model, capability refers to an individual’s psychological and physical capacity to enact a behaviour, including the necessary knowledge and skills; opportunity refers to the physical and social factors external to the individual that make a behaviour possible or prompt it; and motivation refers to the reflective and automatic processes that energise and direct behaviour.11 These three components are interdependent, so that a deficit in any one can prevent the behaviour even when the others are present. Applied to household tobacco control, the model directs attention to how women’s skills and confidence in setting rules (capability), their physical living arrangements and social environments (opportunity) and their values, habits and emotional states (motivation) jointly determine whether smoke-free rules can be established and sustained. The COM-B model also underpinned the design and analytic framework of the parent QualiECAC4 study, providing continuity between data collection and the present analysis.

This study aimed to explore how women perceive, negotiate and maintain smoke-free home rules in Germany using a hypothesis-generating qualitative approach and to inform the development of more equitable tobacco control strategies by identifying context-sensitive barriers and facilitators to support implementation and maintenance of smoke-free homes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study draws on qualitative data from the Qualitative Component of the European Code Against Cancer, 4th Edition (QualiECAC4) Study,12 a multicountry project designed to examine barriers and facilitators to adopting the recommendations of the European Code Against Cancer, 4th Edition, including ‘Make your home smoke free’ (recommendation #2).13 The study was coordinated by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). The QualiECAC4 Study used an exploratory, cross-sectional, narrative-based, semi-structured interview design guided by the COM-B model of behaviour change.12 14 The present paper reports an analysis of interviews with women recruited in Germany, conducted in Bremen at the Leibniz Institute for Prevention Research and Epidemiology. Interviews followed a standardised semi-structured topic guide that was developed for the parent study and organised around the COM-B domains (online supplemental file 1). The topic guide addressed all 12 recommendations of the European Code Against Cancer, and the present analysis focuses on participants’ accounts relating to recommendations #1 (‘Do not smoke; do not use any form of tobacco’) and #2 (‘Make your home smoke-free’).

Participants

The study sample comprised nine German women representing a range of sociodemographic profiles. Participants were purposively selected using a maximum variation sampling strategy to capture heterogeneity in experiences related to maintaining smoke-free homes. Recruitment followed predefined inclusion criteria, requiring that all participants had no previous cancer diagnosis and resided in Germany during data collection. Age and educational level were used as sensitising characteristics to structure recruitment across a predefined sampling matrix (younger: 18–30 years; intermediate: 31–45 years; older: 46–65 years; low, secondary and high educational levels). Consistent with the study’s hypothesis-generating aims, the sample size was determined by the goal of achieving maximum variation across the predefined age-by-education matrix and analytic depth within each account, rather than by thematic saturation or statistical representativeness, which are not the objectives of in-depth qualitative inquiry of this kind. The nine participants filled the sampling matrix while permitting detailed, context-rich analysis of each case. The sample included both women who smoked and women who did not, as well as women who did and did not cohabit with a person who smokes; because these characteristics were not systematically recorded at screening, they are described qualitatively rather than reported as counts.

Procedures

Participants were recruited from the Leibniz Institute and the Leibniz Living Lab (LLL), a community-based research infrastructure located in a socioeconomically disadvantaged Bremen neighbourhood. Participants had no previous relationship with the interviewers. Before participation, they were informed about the interviewers’ roles, aims and research interests.

They each participated in one in-depth interview and were compensated with a 20-Euro voucher for their time and contribution. All participants provided informed consent before the interviews.

Interviews were semistructured, conducted in person, lasted an average of 40 min and involved only the interviewer and participant. This setting and approach enabled the research team to build trust with participants and collect rich, contextualised narratives about women’s experiences of maintaining smoke-free homes. No participants dropped out, no repeat interviews occurred and all conversations were audio recorded. BB, a research assistant at the Leibniz Institute with qualitative research experience from her master’s studies, and RW, the coordinator of the LLL with previous qualitative research experience, collaborated to train community health workers to conduct the interviews. Female researchers conducted all interviews under the supervision of HZ, the principal investigator and senior scientific supervisor of the German study, to ensure consistency and ethical rigour.

Data analysis

Analysis was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.16 Digitally recorded interviews were transcribed and translated into English. Translation was supported by artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted software (DeepL Pro); all translated transcripts were subsequently reviewed for accuracy and conceptual equivalence by German-speaking members of the research team.

A two-stage analytic approach was conducted. First, thematic content analysis (TCA) was performed,17 18 in which transcripts were imported into ATLAS.ti (V.25) for data management and coding. AI-assisted natural language processing functions within ATLAS.ti were used to support initial code suggestions and data organisation; however, all analytic decisions (code definitions, code application, merging/splitting of codes and theme development) were made by the research team.19 The COM-B model informed the analytic structure: codes were organised within Capability, Opportunity and Motivation domains, and themes were developed through iterative team discussion. Coding and the COM-B-aligned thematic structure were independently reviewed by two researchers (AF and BB), with disagreements resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (CE). Although the COM-B model provided a deductive framework for organising codes, coding remained open to inductively derived sub-themes; relational constructs such as perceived legitimacy, household authority and negotiation self-efficacy were developed inductively from the data and then located within the relevant COM-B domains. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction, and participants did not provide feedback on the findings.

Second, critical discourse analysis was conducted as an interpretative layer to explore discursive practices, social and cultural norms and power dynamics underlying the interview content.20 Whereas the COM-B-informed TCA identified what enabled or constrained smoke-free home practices across capability, opportunity and motivation and provided the organising scaffold, the discourse-analytic layer interpreted how these processes were shaped by gendered power relations, household hierarchies and social norms. The two approaches were integrated sequentially, with discourse analysis used to interpret the relational and power dimensions embedded within the COM-B-coded accounts, thereby enriching rather than replacing the thematic synthesis.

Regarding analytic sufficiency, the parent QualiECAC4 study defined thematic saturation at the European Union (EU) regional level for its cross-national objectives.12 In this analysis, we did not treat saturation as a primary criterion for inference. Instead, consistent with a hypothesis-generating qualitative approach and purposive maximum variation sampling, we aimed for analytic adequacy through depth of accounts, coherence of COM-B-informed themes and the identification of patterned variations across cases. No new COM-B-relevant themes emerged during late-stage team synthesis, supporting the internal consistency of the thematic framework for this subsample.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis followed a structured, theory-driven approach based on the COM-B model. TCA was applied to identify how the three COM-B core components (capability, opportunity and motivation11) manifested in relation to women’s smoke-free home practices. Guided by this framework, codes and themes were organised within COM-B domains to support an explanatory synthesis of the conditions under which smoke-free home practices were enabled or constrained.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This study involved secondary qualitative analysis of interviews conducted for the parent QualiECAC4 study; participants contributed as interviewees by sharing their experiences and perspectives but were not involved in setting the research questions or interpreting the findings.

Results

The study participants reflected diverse social and household circumstances (table 1). Most women lived in urban areas (n=7). Economic conditions varied, ranging from no reported difficulty in paying bills (n=4) to occasional difficulty (n=2), persistent financial strain (n=1); one participant declined to answer and data were unavailable for one participant. Nearly all participants reported a close case of cancer in their immediate personal environment, and most expressed a general belief in the importance of preventive health measures. In contrast, awareness of the European Code Against Cancer was low, with no participants reporting prior explicit knowledge.

Capability domain

All women demonstrated an awareness of the health risks associated with smoking and SHS. However, their perceived capability to establish and sustain smoke-free rules varied in relation to autonomy within the household, communication and negotiation skills and accumulated health knowledge and lived experience.

In some narratives, women described strong cognitive clarity and confidence in setting boundaries, portraying smoke-free rules as straightforward and non-negotiable: ‘It’s not allowed to smoke in my house and if someone wants to smoke, they can go out on the balcony’. These accounts often reflected high perceived agency and a clear sense of entitlement to define household norms.

In analytical contrast, other women described reduced psychological capability to negotiate smoke-free rules when living with persons who smoke or when household authority was perceived as limited: ‘My own husband, for example, smokes at home and that’s not so easy’. Women in such circumstances often framed their efforts as balancing health protection with maintaining harmony, describing uncertainty about how to assert boundaries without escalating conflict.

Furthermore, some women linked stronger confidence and more developed strategies to accumulated health literacy, family responsibilities or salient illness experiences in close circles, experiences that were sometimes described as shaping long-term prevention orientations: ‘Someone in my immediate circle has just been diagnosed with cancer for the fourth time… then you think differently about your health.’

Opportunity domain

Women’s opportunities to enforce smoke-free homes were shaped by physical living arrangements and social environments, with patterns of variation across various narratives.

Some women described supportive opportunity conditions where non-smoking was normative within their close social circles, and smoke-free rules were reinforced by partners, family members or routines that prioritised health. In these contexts, maintaining smoke-free homes required little active enforcement. One woman who lived alone described her family and friends as ‘all in favour of me doing it’, adding that ‘no one would stop me from doing it or advise me against it.’ Practical supports, such as stable routines and clear household expectations, helped sustain consistent rule implementation.

In contrast, women in more constrained contexts described opportunity barriers arising from shared living arrangements, cohabitation with someone who smokes or environments where smoking was normalised. These accounts highlighted limited leverage to enforce smoke-free norms and the social difficulty of enforcing rules, particularly when requests were perceived as challenging established household practices or visitor expectations: ‘When I have visitors or something, it's difficult to say that we don't smoke at home now.’ Several women described adopting mitigation strategies (eg, balcony-only rules, window management and restricting smoking to specific areas) as pragmatic responses when implementing complete indoor bans proved difficult: ‘You can smoke on my balcony, but not inside the flat.’ These narratives demonstrated that opportunities were embedded in material conditions and household social relations rather than in individual choice alone.

Motivation domain

Women’s motivation to maintain smoke-free homes reflected both reflective processes (values, intentions and prevention beliefs) and automatic processes (habit, stress responses and social pull), with patterns of variation shaped by lived experience, relationship dynamics and perceived responsibilities.

Many women expressed strong reflective motivation grounded in health protection and prevention values, often framed as a personal commitment to well-being and a desire for a healthy future. Some narratives emphasised self-improvement and long-term health orientation, while others highlighted relational motivations such as responsibility for family members and the wish to model healthy behaviours: ‘Firstly, because of me, and also because of the children.’ In these accounts, breaches of smoke-free rules were frequently associated with discomfort, guilt or moral tension, reinforcing motivation to re-establish boundaries.

Similarly, contrasting narratives illustrated how motivation could fluctuate when automatic processes or relational pressures interfered with intentions. Some women described social contexts where smoking was common, creating a pull towards tolerance or compromise even when they preferred strict rules. Others described stress-related vulnerability, where emotional strain or conflict made enforcement more difficult or led to rule flexibility despite awareness of SHS harms. These accounts illustrate analytical contrasts between motivation as stable and internalised vs motivation as context-dependent and negotiated.

Intersections of life stage, education and household position

Across the three COM-B domains, patterns in women’s accounts appeared to vary with life stage and educational experience as these intersected with household roles and relationship dynamics. Younger women and those who described limited authority within their household tended to frame smoke-free rules as difficult to assert, expressing uncertainty about their legitimacy to impose boundaries on older relatives, partners or visitors who smoked. In contrast, older women and those with greater household autonomy more often portrayed smoke-free rules as an established entitlement that required little justification. Women with higher educational experiences more frequently invoked health-literacy-based reasoning and prevention narratives when accounting for their rules, whereas women in lower-education or more constrained households more often emphasised relational negotiation and the social costs of enforcement. These were analytical contrasts across a small sample rather than deterministic differences, but they suggest that perceived legitimacy and negotiation strategies were shaped by the interaction of life stage, educational experience and position within the household hierarchy rather than by any single characteristic alone.

Discussion

These findings show that women’s efforts to maintain smoke-free homes are shaped not only by individual choices but also by the intersection of social position, household power dynamics and broader structural conditions.

Across the study participants, women expressed a consistent desire to protect children and family members, echoing European and global evidence that child protection is a primary motivator for maintaining a smoke-free home.1 21 22 Contrasting narratives indicated that in some contexts, particularly where household authority was constrained, where cohabiting persons who smoke shaped everyday routines or where material conditions limited feasible alternatives, women described difficulty enforcing smoke-free rules. This aligns with German national surveillance data showing that SHS exposure and indoor smoking remain concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged families.8 23 24

Smoke-free homes cannot be understood as mere behavioural choices; they are embedded in relational and structural environments that either enable or undermine women’s agency. These contextual constraints align with prior European and international research documenting how structural factors influence the feasibility of maintaining smoke-free homes.21 25 26 Women’s narratives also resonate with studies indicating that the enforcement of smoke-free rules is often mediated by partners’ behaviours and sociocultural norms, particularly where decision-making power around smoking in the home is unevenly distributed.9 27

Life stage and educational experiences appeared to intersect with family hierarchies, caregiving roles and relationship dynamics to shape perceived legitimacy and authority in household negotiations. These analytical contrasts are consistent with evidence on age-related hierarchies within families and the role of household authority in shaping health-related decision-making.25 26 28 They also reflect life-course research suggesting that autonomy, caregiving roles and housing circumstances may influence environmental control and the implementation of health-protective norms within the home.28

Although the motivation to protect family members was prominent across accounts, structural realities often constrained women’s ability to act on it. Previous literature describes ‘teachable moments’, such as pregnancy, as potential catalysts for change, yet indicates that without sustained social and environmental support, smoke-free rules may weaken over time.21 29 This study reinforces the importance of contextual support: even where women described strong prevention intentions, persistent barriers such as entrenched social norms, partner smoking and competing household demands could undermine consistent rule maintenance.

Social norms surrounding smoking emerged as a key contextual influence. This aligns with evidence that higher-SES contexts are often characterised by the denormalisation of smoking, greater exposure to health information and stronger perceptions of personal agency.30 31 Conversely, other accounts described environments where smoking remained socially common or tolerated, making enforcement more socially difficult and increasing the likelihood of partial restrictions. This pattern is consistent with the literature, indicating that pro-smoking norms may be more prevalent in disadvantaged contexts and can undermine smoke-free home practices.26 32

Implications for German tobacco control: structural solutions are required

Germany’s comparatively weak tobacco control framework, permissive smoking culture and limited enforcement of public smoking bans continue to shape domestic smoking norms.31–33 The absence of comprehensive housing-related protections, including smoke-free multi-unit housing policies and tenant safeguards against exposure from neighbouring dwellings, further limits structural support for smoke-free living compared with other EU countries. Consequently, responsibility for maintaining smoke-free environments is shifted to the household level, placing a disproportionate burden on individuals with fewer resources and limited household authority. These findings highlight the need for more robust structural interventions to support smoke-free homes.

In addition, family-based and couple-focused approaches are essential, considering that smoke-free rule implementation is often relational rather than individually controlled. Such approaches have shown promise in supporting behaviour change and reducing conflict around household smoking.34 35

Towards equitable, family-centred approaches

Overall, smoke-free home interventions should extend beyond individual-level counselling or advice delivered to women alone (eg, brief guidance in clinical or community settings) and should also incorporate strategies that address the relational and structural contexts in which smoke-free rules are negotiated and sustained.

Equity-oriented, family-centred approaches are likely to be more effective and sustainable when they explicitly engage partners and other household members, support women’s negotiation and boundary-setting in ways that reduce conflict and address practical constraints such as housing conditions and exposure from neighbouring units.34

Aligned with the EU’s goal of a Tobacco-Free Generation by 2040, such strategies should strengthen women’s capabilities and opportunities to maintain smoke-free homes while addressing broader social and political determinants that shape exposure in private spaces.

Candidate hypotheses and intervention targets

Building on this hypothesis-generating analysis, we identify several candidate mechanisms that can be tested in future qualitative and mixed-methods research. First, women’s ability to sustain smoke-free rules may depend less on risk awareness alone and more on negotiation self-efficacy and perceived legitimacy within household relationships (capability), suggesting that interventions should include practical communication and boundary-setting supports that reduce conflict rather than only delivering information. Second, smoke-free rules may be most difficult to maintain when opportunity constraints are present, such as cohabiting with persons who smoke, visitor norms or housing conditions that limit control over indoor air, indicating the need for structural measures (eg, smoke-free multi-unit housing standards, tenant protections and accessible cessation supports for cohabitants) that reduce reliance on individual enforcement. Third, motivation to protect family members appears widespread but may be undermined by automatic drivers such as stress and habitual routines, implying that family-centred cessation and relapse-prevention supports should address stress regulation and routine disruption and engage partners and other household members rather than focusing on women alone. Collectively, these hypotheses suggest that effective smoke-free home strategies in Germany will likely require multilevel approaches that strengthen women’s capabilities, expand household opportunities and support motivation in ways that are feasible within everyday domestic life.

Strengths and limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample was small and purposively selected, and the analysis focused on women in a single national context, which limits statistical generalisability. Social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ accounts, particularly given the normative expectations surrounding smoking and health protection. Selection bias is also possible, as women who agreed to participate may have been more health-conscious or motivated than those who declined. In addition, educational level was used as a proxy for socioeconomic position, capturing only one dimension of social stratification. Other determinants, such as income, occupation, housing quality and neighbourhood conditions, are known to shape SHS exposure and the feasibility of maintaining smoke-free homes but were not systematically examined in this analysis. In addition, smoking status and cohabitation with a person who smokes were not systematically recorded at screening, which limited our ability to compare accounts formally across these groups.

In addition, this study employed purposive maximum variation sampling to explore variation in women’s experiences across diverse social and household contexts. The findings are therefore not intended to support prevalence estimates or demographic inference but offer conceptually transferable insights into mechanisms that may shape smoke-free home practices, including negotiation capacity, household power dynamics and contextual constraints.

A key strength of this study lies in its theory-informed design. Applying the COM-B framework provided a structured yet flexible lens to integrate behavioural, relational and structural influences on smoke-free home practices. This approach supported an explanatory synthesis that highlights how capability, opportunity and motivation interact within everyday domestic settings. By foregrounding women’s narratives and situating them within broader social contexts, the study contributes to a deeper understanding of household-level tobacco control.

Conclusion

The study reinforces that women’s ability to maintain smoke-free homes reflects not only personal intentions but also the social, gendered and structural contexts in which everyday household decisions are made. Life stage and educational experiences, alongside household composition and relationship dynamics, shaped what women perceived as feasible when setting, negotiating and enforcing smoke-free rules, even when the motivation to protect family members was strong. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest that smoke-free home maintenance depends on relational negotiation capacity and perceived legitimacy, the opportunity structures created by cohabiting persons who smoke and housing conditions, and the extent to which stress and routines support or undermine consistent rule enforcement. Strengthening smoke-free homes in Germany, therefore, requires approaches that move beyond individual awareness and address the relational and structural conditions that can constrain women’s agency.

Aligned with the EU’s goal of a Tobacco-Free Generation 2040, policies should prioritise equity-oriented, family-inclusive strategies, including interventions that engage partners and wider household networks in supporting smoke-free norms. Future research should examine how such approaches, alongside strengthened housing and community supports, may reduce socially patterned SHS exposure and generate more sustainable smoke-free home environments.
