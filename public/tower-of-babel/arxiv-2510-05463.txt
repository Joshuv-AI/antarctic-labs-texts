Robust Pricing and Hedging of American Options in Continuous Time
Ivan Guo, Jan Obłój

Abstract
We consider the robust pricing and hedging of American options in a continuous time setting. We assume asset prices are continuous semimartingales, but we allow for general model uncertainty specification via adapted closed convex constraints on the volatility. We prove the robust pricing-hedging duality. When European options with given prices are available for static trading, we show that duality holds against richer models where these options are traded dynamically. Our proofs rely on probabilistic treatment of randomised stopping times and suitable measure decoupling, and on optimal transport duality. In addition, similarly to the approach of Aksamit et al. 2019 in discrete time, we identify American options with European options on an enlarged space. Keywords: robust pricing and hedging, American options, duality, optimal transport, volatility restrictions, Azéma supermartingale

Introduction

Option pricing is at the origins of modern quantitative finance, with the Black-Scholes formula one of its most iconic manifestations and often billed as one of the equations which changed the world, see
Stewart 2012
. The intuition behind it, going back to the seminal works of
Black and Scholes 1973
;
Merton 1973
, links pricing to hedging: if a hedging strategy can replicate the option’s cashflow, then the two should have the same initial price, or else markets would admit plain arbitrage opportunities. In some probabilistic models for the underlying’s dynamics, such as the Black-Scholes model, we can compute this price by taking risk-neutral expectations. In more general, incomplete models a perfect hedging strategy may not exist but the principle generalises to the so-called pricing-hedging duality: the cost of the cheapest superhedging strategy is equal to the supremum of the payoff’s expectations under all risk-neutral measures, see
(
Föllmer and Schied 2004
, Chp. 7)

(
Karatzas and Shreve 1998
, Chp. 5)
.

The above classical results require us to fix a probabilistic model for the dynamics of the risky assets. This perspective, while allowing for an efficient description of the risk within the chosen model, is silent on the errors related to the model choice itself, errors often referred to as the
Knightian uncertainty
after
Knight 1921
,
model ambiguity or misspecification
or
epistemic uncertainty
, see
Hansen and Marinacci 2016
;
Walley 1991
. This shortcoming became a focal point after the 2008 global financial crisis and led to the development of
robust methods
in mathematical finance. Rich literature emerged tackling the foundational questions on how to best capture, mathematically speaking, diversity of models, how to properly define an arbitrage opportunity if a single model is not specified and to obtain a
robust fundamental theorem of asset pricing
that would characterise absence of arbitrage. And how to formulate, and prove, a
robust pricing-hedging duality
. In discrete time settings, these questions were answered using two points of view. One, the so-called
quasi-sure
perspective, relied on families of probability measures which, while potentially mutually singular, have enough structure to obtain a complete set of results, see
Bouchard and Nutz 2015
and the references therein. The other point of view adopted a pointwise (or pathwise),
\omega
by
\omega
, also leading to complete robust equivalent of the classical results, see
Burzoni et al. 2019
and the references therein. The two approaches are naturally just different ways to see the problem and can be shown to be equivalent, see
Obłój and Wiesel 2021
.
Analogous efforts were undertaken in continuous time settings, with quasi-sure analysis advanced in, e.g,
Denis and Martini 2006
;
Neufeld and Nutz 2013
;
Possamaï et al. 2013
and pathwise arguments used by, e.g,
Mykland 2003
;
Vovk 2012
;
Allan et al. 2023
;
Dolinsky and Soner 2014
;
Hou and Obłój 2018
. The formidable technical challenge in continuous time relates to the necessity to consider suitable classes of admissible trading strategies and to define their outcomes, given by stochastic integrals, simultaneously under many different, often singular, probability measures. To the best of our knowledge, while a lot of progress has been done and many partial exciting results exist, in contrast to the discrete time setting, in continuous time no complete robust theory has been obtained. Our work contributes to the ongoing development of robust mathematical finance in continuous time.

In the context of robust pricing and hedging, if no modelling assumptions are made, the range of possible no-arbitrage prices for a given contingent claim can be impractically large. A natural way to narrow it is to use available market information. In this way, arbitrary constraints coming from modeller’s choices are replaced with observable market prices. The latter would be taken into the account in the classical approach via a reverse-engineering process, in which a particular model is selected, say from a parametric class of models, to best fit observed market prices via
calibration
. In the robust approach instead, such market prices are added as constraints to the original problem. This perspective in fact was behind the foundational works of
Hobson 1998
;
Brown et al. 2001
which solved robust pricing and hedging of lookback and barrier options using probabilistic methods of Skorokhod embeddings, see
Obłój 2004
;
Hobson 2011
;
Cox and Obłój 2011
, based on the classical observation of
Breeden and Litzenberger 1978
that incorporating market prices of call options with a maturity
T
is equivalent to fixing the marginal distribution at
T
under the risk-neutral measure. In subsequent seminal contributions
Beiglböck et al. 2013
;
Galichon et al. 2014
re-wrote the robust pricing and hedging problem as an optimal transport (OT) problem with a martingale constraint, i.e., a
martingale optimal transport
(MOT) problem. These ideas brought rich and powerful techniques of OT to robust mathematical finance, allowing in particular to develop numerical methods and perform empirical studies for robust methods, see
Guo and Obłój 2019
;
Eckstein et al. 2021
, and obtain robust pricing-hedging duality results under general constraints, see
Guo et al. 2017
;
Guo and Loeper 2021
.

Interestingly, as first observed by
Neuberger 2007
, these ideas were not sufficient to consider robust pricing and hedging for American options, and their naive extension could lead to a gap in the pricing-hedging duality. Instead, a weak formulation was suggested by
Neuberger 2007
and further explored in
Hobson and Neuberger 2017
. Another technical solution, via randomised models, was proposed by
Bayraktar et al. 2015
;
Bayraktar and Zhou 2017
. A more comprehensive explanation was proposed by
Aksamit et al. 2019
who analysed the problem through the lens of information — while stock prices are observed, and traded on, dynamically, the constraining market option prices are given statically at time
t=0
and not updated afterwards, limiting the class of exercise policies for the American option holders. They linked duality to a robust version of the dynamic programming principle (DPP), used for classical American option pricing. This also explained why
Dolinsky 2014
, who studied game options, which include American options, in a nondominated discrete time market, did not encounter duality gap issues since his setup had no statically traded options.
Aksamit et al. 2019
then showed that the pricing-hedging duality can be restored by considering a richer class of models where options are traded dynamically as well.

Our work makes a decisive new contribution to this literature by developing robust pricing and hedging duality for American options in continuous time. We show that
\pi^{A}_{g}
, the superhedging price of an American option
Z
using dynamic trading in the stocks and static trading in European options with payoffs
g
, is equal to

\sup_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Q}},\widehat{\tau}\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}}\mathbb{E}^{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}Z_{\widehat{\tau}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
corresponds to risk neutral measures for joint dynamics of stocks and options
g
, and the stopping times
\widehat{\tau}
are allowed to depend on the information about both. The exact statement of that duality is given in Theorem
5.2
and it allows to further restrict models to those with volatility taking values in pre-specified sets. These could be general, but could also be given by a point, in which way our results recover the to classical model-specific pricing-hedging duality for American options, as in
Myneni 1992
, as a special case. When no statically traded options are available,
g=0
, then we can restrict to martinagle measures
\mathcal{Q}
on
\Omega
and exercise policies
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.
To prove the above duality we adopt ideas pioneered in
Aksamit et al. 2019
and show that an American option can be identified with a European option in an enlarged space
\bar{\Omega}
. However, unlike in
Aksamit et al. 2019
, to link the pricing in the two perspectives, we do not go via a dynamic programming representation but instead rely on probabilistic techniques related to randomised stopping times and Azéma supermartingales, see Theorem
3.1
and other results in section
3
. Also on the hedging side, we encounter genuine novel difficulties in the continuous time setup. We lift models to allow for dynamic trading in the European options with given time zero market prices. However, while we work with continuous asset prices, we can not ensure that option prices, defined via conditional expectations, are continuous. In fact, they may be random at time
t=0
. To account for that, we have to consider fictitious markets with trading on
[-\delta,1]
, see section
4
. This allows us to establish a chain of inequalities between potential superhedging and pricing valuations, see Proposition
4.5
. The final piece of the jigsaw is to show the LHS and RHS of this long chain of inequalities are equal, which boils down to establishing pricing-hedging duality for European options on
\bar{\Omega}
. We do this in section
5
using OT-duality methods, in analogy to
Guo and Loeper 2021
;
Tan et al. 2013
.

2.1
The enlarged space

A key idea in the robust hedging of American options is to progressively encode the optimal stopping decision in the probability space and its filtration. It relies on observing that an American option in the original space can be, in a suitable sense, interpreted as a European option in an enlarged space, see
El Karoui and Tan 2013
;
Aksamit et al. 2019
. In this subsection, we will set up the appropriate enlarged space and establish the robust pricing-hedging duality for European options in this space.
Consider the set
\Theta\subset C([0,1],\mathbb{R})
defined by

\Theta:=\{\vartheta\in C([0,1],\mathbb{R}):\vartheta_{t}=\theta\wedge t,\text{ for some }\theta\in[0,1]\}.

The space
\Theta
is isometric to
[0,1]
, with an obvious bijection between
\theta\in[0,1]
and
\vartheta\in\Theta
via
\vartheta=\theta\wedge\cdot
or
\theta=\vartheta_{1}
.
When there’s no confusion, we will simply use
\theta
to denote elements of
\Theta
, while
\vartheta
is reserved when it is necessary to refer to the whole path.
Define the product space
\bar{\Omega}:=\Theta\times\Omega
, which is a subset of
D([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
and inherits its topology. Also extend the canonical process via
\bar{X}_{t}(\bar{\omega})=(\vartheta_{t},X_{t}(\omega))
and the canonical filtration to
\bar{\mathbb{F}}
.
For each
\bar{\omega}=(\vartheta,\omega)\in\bar{\Omega}
, let
\bar{\omega}_{\cdot\wedge t}:=(\vartheta_{\cdot\wedge t},\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})
denote the stopped path at time
t
.
Analogous to
\Lambda
from the original space, we define
\bar{\Lambda}:=\{(t,\bar{\omega}_{\cdot\wedge t}):t\in[0,1],\bar{\omega}\in\bar{\Omega}\}
. As before, for convenience, we will often write elements of
\bar{\Lambda}
as

(t,\bar{\omega}_{\cdot\wedge t})=(t,\vartheta_{\cdot\wedge t},\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})=(t,\theta\wedge t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})

Similar to before, let
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
be the set of Borel probability measures on
\bar{\Omega}
and let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}\subset\bar{\mathcal{P}}
be the set of measures under which
X
is a square integrable martingale.

3
Robust pricing of American options

In this section, we represent the robust price of an American option as the robust price of a corresponding European option in the enlarged space. Put differently, we show that optimising linear functionals on
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{Q}
is equivalent to optimising on the enlarged space
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
.
An American option payoff is given as a measurable function
Z\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
, i.e., if exercised at time
t
, the option pays
Z_{t}(\omega)=Z(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})
, and such an option can be naturally also exercised at a stopping time
\tau
and we write
Z_{\tau}
for its payoff
Z(\tau(\omega),\omega_{\cdot\land\tau(\omega)})
. Note also that
Z
naturally induces a random variable on
\bar{\Omega}
via
\bar{Z}(\bar{\omega})=Z(\theta,\omega_{\cdot\land\theta})
, i.e., the composition of
Z
with the map

\bar{\Omega}\ni\bar{\omega}=(\vartheta,\omega)\longrightarrow(\vartheta_{1},\omega_{\cdot\land\vartheta_{1}})\in\Lambda,

which is continuous with our choice of norms.

For any
E=(E_{1},E_{2})\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{B}(\Lambda)
,
define
\bar{E}_{1}:=\Theta\times E_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\bar{\Omega})
and

\bar{E}_{2}=\{(t,\theta\wedge t,X_{\cdot\wedge t})\in\bar{\Lambda}:(t,X_{\cdot\wedge t})\in E_{2}\}\in\mathcal{B}(\bar{\Lambda}).

Next, define

\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}^{E}&=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{P}(E_{1})=\lambda\otimes\mathbb{P}(E_{2})=1\},\\
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}&=\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}:\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\bar{E}_{1})=\lambda\otimes\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\bar{E}_{2})=1\},\end{split}

(1)

where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure, so
\lambda\otimes\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
and
\lambda\otimes\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Lambda})
.

We state now our main result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
E_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
and
E_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\Lambda)
.
Then for any
Z\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
and bounded from below, we have

\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E},\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\tau}=\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}Z(\theta,\omega_{\cdot\land\theta}).

The set
E
above is a technical device that will allow us to apply Theorem
3.1
to a variety of settings.
The proof of the theorem is split into two main steps. Lemma
3.8
connects measures on
\bar{\Omega}
to measures on
\Omega
and stopping times. Lemma
3.10
ensures that the martingale property is preserved along the way. The latter, we feel, is the more subtle of the two steps. If one does not care about the preservation of the martingale property, then there are much more direct approaches to the first step.

3.1
Convexifying stopping times and measures

We are interested in pairs of the form
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
where
\mathbb{P}
belongs to some subset of probability measures on
\Omega
and
\tau
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
-stopping time,
\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbb{P}}
.
Each pair
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
defines a linear map on the space of jointly measurable functions on
(t,\Omega)
via
\phi\to\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\phi_{\tau})
.
In order to employ convex duality techniques to solve optimisation problems of the form
\sup_{(\tau,\mathbb{P})}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\phi_{\tau})
, we need to identify the convex hull of such pairs
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
or linear maps.
Since many problems have objectives or constraints on
\mathbb{P}
, the convexification should be done in a way that preserves the convexity of
\mathbb{P}
(or the induced linear map on measurable functions on
\Omega
).

A natural approach (see e.g.,
El Karoui and Tan 2013
;
Aksamit et al. 2019
) is to identify
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
with a measure from
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
. However, a general element in
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
usually corresponds to a random time (not even a randomised stopping time, see Example
3.3
). The approach of
Aksamit et al. 2019
overcomes this by explicitly constructing an optimal stopping time via backward induction in discrete time, which cannot be applied in continuous time settings.
Our approach is to use
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
as well and to use the non-anticipative nature of American payoffs together with the martingale property of our pricing measures to show that we can indeed restrict to randomised stopping times.

Definition 3.2
.

A
randomised stopping time
is an
(\Omega,\mathbb{F})
-adapted, right-continuous, increasing process
A
with
A_{0}=0
and
A_{1}=1
.

Randomised stopping times have been studied in

Baxter and Chacon 1977
, see also
Meyer 1978
, and in many works since, see
Beiglböck et al. 2016
for a good discussion in relation to OT methods for Skorokhod embeddings. If
\tau
is an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time, then
A^{\tau}_{t}=\mathds{1}_{\tau\leq t}
is a randomised stopping time which has a particularly simple structure: it is equal to zero and then jumps to one at time
\tau
. More generally,
A_{t}
can be interpreted as the proportion of mass that has been stopped along each path by the time
t
. The non-anticipative property of
A
helps eliminate the possibility of random times that are not randomised stopping times. Our aim is to extract an appropriate non-anticipative
A
from each
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
. First however, we give a simple example to show that naive convexification of pairs
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
would not result in randomised stopping times.

Example 3.3
.

Consider two pairs of stopping times and martingale measures,
(\tau,\mathbb{P})
and
(\tau^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
, where
\tau=0
,
\tau^{\prime}=1
,
\mathbb{P}
is the law of a constant process, and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
is the law of the same constant process on
t\in[0,1/2]
followed by a Brownian motion on
t\in[1/2,1]
. Let
\bar{\mathbb{P}}=\delta_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}
and
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}=\delta_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
be the corresponding measures in the enlarged space. The average
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime\prime}=(\bar{\mathbb{P}}+\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime})/2
is a martingale measure, however its
\theta
component does not correspond to a stopping time or a randomised stopping time, as it requires the knowledge of the process from
t=1/2
to determine the stopping decision at
t=0
. In particular, at
t=0
it will immediately stop all paths that will be constant on
t\in[1/2,1]
. However, at time
t=0
, it is not possible to distinguish those paths from those which will follow a Brownian motion on
[1/2,1]
.

We recall the following simple properties of randomised stopping times.

Lemma 3.4

(
Shmaya and Solan 2014
)
.

Let
A
be a randomised stopping time and for each
r\in[0,1]
, define
\tau_{r}=\inf\{t\geq 0:A_{t}\geq r\}
. Then
(\tau_{r},r\in[0,1])
is a non-decreasing family of stopping times and

A_{t}(\omega)=\lambda({r\in[0,1]:\tau_{r}(\omega)\leq t}),\quad t\in[0,1],

where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure. Consequently, for any
\eta\in L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
,

\int_{0}^{1}\eta(\theta,\omega)\,dA_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{1}\eta(\tau_{r},\omega)\,dr.

We also need to recall the Itô–Watanabe multiplicative decomposition of non-negative supermartingales.

Theorem 3.5

(
Itô and Watanabe 1965
)
.

Let
\xi
be a non-negative right continuous supermartingale with
\xi_{0}>0
defined on a filtered probability space satisfying the usual hypothesis. Then
\xi
has the decomposition

\xi=MA

with a positive local martingale

and a natural decreasing process

. If there are two such factorisations, then they are identical up to
T_{\xi}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\xi_{t}=0\}
.

Furthermore, if there exists a constant
K>0
and an almost surely finite stopping time
T
such that
1/K\leq\xi_{t}\leq K
for
t<T
and
\xi_{t}=\xi_{T}
for

, then the local martingale

in the decomposition is a (true) martingale.

The distinction between local and true martingale in Theorem
3.5
actually creates some difficulties for us, but we will work around it by focusing on the following type of measures in
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})

Definition 3.6
.

For

\mu(\{1\}\times\Gamma)\geq\epsilon\mu^{\Omega}(\Gamma)

is the
\Omega

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})

be the Dirac mass at 1. Then

The following lemma is the first step in the proof of Theorem

with a measure on

Lemma 3.8

Let

\mu^{\Omega}
and a right-continuous, increasing and

A_{1}=1
, such that for every
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
-adapted process
\psi
,

\mu(\psi_{\theta}(\omega))=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{t}\,dA_{t}.

This can be written as

\mu(\psi_{\theta}(\omega))=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{\tau_{r}}\,dr.

for a family of
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
-stopping times
\{\tau_{r}\}_{r}
that satisfies
\tau_{r}=1
for
r>1-\epsilon
.

Proof.

Instead of
\psi
, it suffices to check functions of the form
\psi_{t}=\mathds{1}(t>s,\omega\in\Gamma)
for any fixed
s
and any
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}^{\mu_{\Omega}}_{s}
.

Define the raw IV process
R
from
\mu
(by say, disintegrating in
\omega
and taking the distribution functions with respect to
\theta
), so

\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\mathds{1}(\theta>s,\omega\in\Gamma)\,d\mu=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\Omega}}\big((1-R_{s})\mathds{1}(\omega\in\Gamma)\big),\quad s\in[0,1].

Let
{}^{o}R_{t}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\Omega}}(R_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mu_{\Omega}}_{t})
be the optional projection of
R
. Then
1-{}^{o}R
is a non-negative
\mu_{\Omega}
-supermartingale, and in particular we can and will work with its rcll version,
often known as the Azéma supermartingale after
Azéma 1972
.
Moreover, since
\mu
is
\epsilon
-modified,
1\geq 1-{}^{o}R_{1}>\epsilon
.
By Theorem
3.5
, it admits a multiplicative decomposition
1-{}^{o}R=M(1-A)
, for some positive
(\mathbb{F}^{\mu_{\Omega}},\mu_{\Omega})
-martingale
M
and an increasing
\mathbb{F}^{\mu_{\Omega}}
-adapted process

with
M_{0}=1,A_{0}=0,A_{1}=1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\Omega}}\big((1-R_{s})\mathds{1}(\omega\in\Gamma)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\Omega}}\big((1-{}^{o}R_{s})\mathds{1}(\omega\in\Gamma)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\Omega}}\big(M_{s}(1-A_{s})\mathds{1}(\omega\in\Gamma)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big((1-A_{s})\mathds{1}(\omega\in\Gamma)\big),

where
d\mathbb{P}/d\mu_{\Omega}=M_{1}
.
The last part follows from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The above proposition has the following interpretation.
We showed that every measure in
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
is equivalent to some measure in
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
, when tested only against non-anticipative functions. The measure from
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is then equivalent to a pair
(A,\mathbb{P})
where
A
characterises a randomised stopping time, while
\mathbb{P}
is only uniquely determined until the randomised stopping time finishes. This makes sense since elements of
\Lambda
cannot see into the future.

3.2
Preservation of the martingale property

Now we will see that if
\mu
was a martingale measure in
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})
, then
\mathbb{P}
in Lemma

is also a martingale measure in
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
with the same characteristics on
\Omega
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mu\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
be
\epsilon
-modified.
Then
\mathbb{P}
from Lemma
3.8
satisfies
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}
. If in addition
\mu\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}
, then
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
.

Proof.

Take
\mathbb{P}
and
\{\tau_{r}\}_{r\in[0,1]}
from Lemma
3.8
. Since
\tau_{r}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
-stopping time (and hence
\mathbb{F}^{\mu_{\Omega}}
-stopping time), its (obvious) embedding as a map

is an
\bar{\mathbb{F}}^{\mu}
-stopping time. This follows from the fact that if
\Gamma
is a null set under
\mu_{\Omega}

\mu
.

For any

and

\mathcal{I}_{t}(\omega):=\mathds{1}_{\Gamma}(\omega)\mathds{1}_{(s,1]}(t)(X_{t}-X_{s})
and note that

is an
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
-adapted process. We will show that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{1}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathds{1}_{\Gamma}\mathds{1}_{\tau_{r}>s}(X_{\tau_{r}}-X_{s}))\,dr=\int_{a}^{1}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{I}_{\tau_{r}})\,dr=0.

(2)

Recall that since
\mu
is
\epsilon
-modified, we have
\tau_{a}=1
for any

is an
(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P})
-martingale.

It remains to establish (
2
). By the optional stopping theorem

\mu(\mathcal{I}_{\theta\wedge\tau_{a}})=0.

This is because both
\theta
and
\tau_{a}
are
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu}
-stopping times,
\mathds{1}_{\Gamma}\mathds{1}(\theta\wedge\tau_{a}>s)
is
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu}_{s}
measurable, while

is an
(\bar{\mathbb{F}},\mu)
- and hence
(\bar{\mathbb{F}}^{\mu},\mu)
-martingale.
Applying Lemmas
3.4
,
3.8
and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle 0=\mu(\mathcal{I}_{\theta\wedge\tau_{a}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{I}_{t\wedge\tau_{a}}\,dA_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{I}_{\tau_{r}\wedge\tau_{a}})\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{I}_{\tau_{r}})\,dr+(1-a)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{I}_{\tau_{a}}).

Let

\frac{1}{a-1}\int_{0}^{a}C_{r}\,dr=C_{a}.

Hence

Finally, if

Proof of Theorem

.

Given each

\epsilon

\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\epsilon}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}

Since
Z

\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}Z(\theta,\omega)\leq\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,sup}_{r\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}Z(\tau_{r},\omega)\leq\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E},\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}Z(\tau,\omega).

Remark 3.11
.

Fix a random variable

4
Robust hedging of American options and weak pricing-hedging duality

4.1
Pathwise stochastic integration

Dynamic trading is described through stochastic integrals, so we need to define
\int_{0}^{\cdot}q_{t}dX_{t}
simultaneously for many martingale measures
\mathbb{P}
’s, which may well be mutually singular. This requires some restrictions on the objects we consider. In our setup, we can naturally restrict to suitably continuous integrands
q
and define the stochastic integrals pathwise as limits of certain discrete Riemann-sum approximations, setting the integrals to be zero if limits are not defined. A successful construction simply has to ensure that the set of paths for which the limits exist has full
\mathbb{P}
-measure, for all
\mathbb{P}
’s considered, and the pathwise integrals coincide
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. with their classical Itô counterparts. Such an approach to pathwise integration has been considered in many works; see
Karandikar 1995
;
Föllmer 1981
;
Peng 2019
;
Denis and Martini 2006
;
Davis et al. 2018
;
Perkowski and Prömel 2015
;
Cont and Fournié 2013
. It is worth noting that a different approach to this question of
aggregation
was proposed in
Soner et al. 2013
who worked with the properties of the set of measures considered instead.

For our purposes,
Karandikar 1995
provides the most elegant and simplest aggregation method. Given two functions
\rho,\eta
on
[0,t]
,
\rho
right-continuous and with left limits (rcll), or left-continuous with right limits (lcrl) and
\eta
rcll, their integral
\rho\bm{\cdot}\eta
is defined as a rcll function on
[0,t]
, and
(\rho\bm{\cdot}\eta)_{t}=(\tilde{\rho}\bm{\cdot}\eta)_{t}
if
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
are versions of each other with different (right- or left-) continuity properties. In fact,
\rho\bm{\cdot}\eta
is given as the limit, in the topology of uniform convergence, of
\rho^{n}\bm{\cdot}\eta
, where
\rho^{n}
is piece-wise constant and hence the integral is simply a sum, with
\rho\bm{\cdot}\eta=0
on the set where the sequence does not converge. The latter is the same as the set where the sequence is not Cauchy and is a measurable set in
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. It is also a null set under any semimartingale measure, since by
(
Karandikar 1995
, Thm. 3)
this pathwise construction a.s. coincides with the Itô stochastic integral of an adapted rcll (or lcrl) process against a continuous semimartingale, both defined on some filtered probability space satisfying the usual hypothesis. We also note that if we consider the construction on
[0,s]
and
[0,t]
, with
s<t
, then the two approximations
\rho^{n}\bm{\cdot}\eta
coincide on
[0,s]
. It follows that
(\rho\bm{\cdot}\eta)_{t\in[0,1]}
is a measurable map on
\Lambda
, i.e., is progressively measurable and has continuous paths on the (
\mathcal{F}_{1}
–measurable) set where the approximations converge uniformly on
[0,1]
. This justifies our use of the integral notation, we write:

(q(\omega)\bm{\cdot}X(\omega))_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}q_{s}(\omega)dX_{s}(\omega)=\left(\int_{0}^{t}q_{s}dX_{s}\right)(\omega),

(3)

where usually
q\in C(\Lambda)
and the process
q_{t}(\omega):=q(t,\omega_{\cdot\land t})
,
t\in[0,1]
has continuous paths for all
\omega\in\Omega
and is
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable. For
q\in C(\Lambda;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
X
an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued process, the integrals are defined component-wise.

4.2
Robust superhedging of American Options

We return now to the problem of superhedging an American option with payoff
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
. The seller of the option wants to robustly hedge their exposure. They can trade dynamically in the underlying
X
and, since they observe the exercise time, can also adjust the hedging strategy accordingly: we start with a hedging strategy
q
and switch to
q^{u}
if the option is exercised at time
u
. To parametrise the latter, we introduce the set

\bar{\Lambda}_{\geq}=\{(t,u,\omega_{t\land\cdot}):u\in[0,1],t\in[u,1],\omega\in\Omega\}\subseteq\bar{\Lambda},

with metric inherited from
\bar{\Lambda}
. We recall the sets of martingale measures
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
and
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}
defined in (
1
). We will later use
E_{2}
to encode the seller’s pathwise beliefs about the range of asset behaviour they want to hedge against. In addition, the seller can trade statically in the European options at their market prices observed today. Without any loss of generality, we can assume these options have zero prices today since, as we ignore transaction costs, we can simply shift the options’ payoffs by their true prices. The options’ payoffs are thus given by some
g\in C_{b}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, where we assume
g
is bounded to be able to later apply OT-duality methods, see Proposition
5.4
.
Naturally, the motivating example is that of
g
being a vector of put payoffs, call options being converted to puts via call-put parity. Finally, the set of calibrated martingale measures is given by
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}:\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(g)=0\}
. We recall also that for
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
is the right-continuous and completed version of the natural filtration. Throughout this section we assume that
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
are non-empty.

The superhedging price of the American option is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z):=\inf\bigg\{

\displaystyle x:\exists(q,\tilde{q},h)\in C_{b}(\Lambda;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda}_{\geq};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{s.t.}\ x+\int_{0}^{u}q(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})dX_{t}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{u}^{1}\tilde{q}^{u}(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})dX_{t}(\omega)+hg\geq Z(u,\omega_{\cdot\wedge u}),\ \forall\,u\in[0,1],\ \mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}(d\omega)\text{-\text{q.s.}}\bigg\},

where
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}\text{-\text{q.s.}}
means
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
.
When we want to stress the dependence on the set restrictions on the martingale test measures, we write
\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
.
On the other hand, when there are no traded European options,
g=0
, we may simply write
\pi^{A}(Z)
.
It is intuitive that in this case, there is no need to hedge after the exercise time.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
and suppose
g=0
. Then
\pi^{A}_{E}(Z)=\tilde{\pi}^{A}_{E}(Z)
, where

\tilde{\pi}^{A}_{E}(Z):=\inf\bigg\{x:\exists q\in C_{b}(\Lambda;\mathbb{R}^{d})\ \text{s.t.}\ x+\int_{0}^{u}q_{t}dX_{t}\geq Z_{u},\ \forall\,u\in[0,1],\ \mathcal{Q}^{E}(d\omega)\text{-\text{q.s.}},\bigg\}.

Proof.

It is clear that
\tilde{\pi}^{A}(Z)\geq\pi^{A}(Z)
. Let
(q,\tilde{q},h)
be a superhedging strategy for
\pi^{A}(Z)
starting from
x
. We claim that
(x,q)
is then a superhedging strategy for
\tilde{\pi}^{A}(Z)
which gives the reverse inequality.
Fix
u\in[0,1]
and
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
. Let
M^{u}_{t}=\int_{u}^{t}\tilde{q}^{u}_{t}dX_{t}
,
t\in[u,1]
, which is a
\mathbb{P}
–martingale with
M^{u}_{u}=0
. The superhedging property means that

x+\int_{0}^{u}q_{t}dX_{t}+M^{u}_{1}\geq Z_{u},\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.}

and taking
\mathcal{F}_{u}
-conditional expectations, we obtain
x+\int_{0}^{u}q_{t}dX_{t}\geq Z_{u}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Since both LHS and RHS are right-continuous processes, we see that the inequality holds for all
u\in[0,1]
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and hence also
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
-q.s. since
\mathbb{P}
was an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
.
∎

From the above proof it follows that for any
u\in[0,1]
and any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
,
M^{u}
is a martingale starting in zero and with
M^{u}_{1}\geq 0

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., and hence in fact
M^{u}_{1}=0

\mathcal{Q}^{E}
-q.s., so that we not only can, but must, have
\tilde{q}\equiv 0

\mathcal{Q}^{E}
-q.s.

We now come back to the richer case when
g\neq 0
and observe that the so-called weak pricing-hedging duality holds in general.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
,
g\in C_{b}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and suppose
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}\neq\emptyset
. Then

\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)\geq\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g},\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\tau}.

Proof.

Take any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
,
\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbb{P}}
and any
\pi^{A}_{g}(Z)
superhedging strategy
(x,q,\tilde{q},h)
. For
t\in[0,1]
, note that
\omega\to\tilde{q}^{\tau(\omega)}(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})\mathds{1}_{t>\tau(\omega)}
is a composition of
\omega\to(t,t\land\tau(\omega),\omega_{t\land\cdot})
, which is
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}
-measurable with
(t,u,\omega_{t\land\cdot})\to\tilde{q}^{u}(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})\mathds{1}_{t>u}
which is measurable. It follows that
t\to\tilde{q}^{\tau}(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})\mathds{1}_{t>\tau}
is adapted and left-continuous (with right limits), so progressive and

\int_{\tau}^{1}\tilde{q}^{\tau}dX_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{q}^{\tau(\omega)}(t,\omega_{t\land\cdot})\mathds{1}_{t>\tau}dX_{t}

is well defined
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., and it coincides
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. with the pathwise version in (
3
). Naturally, the pathwise stochastic integral of
\tilde{q}^{\tau}\mathds{1}_{t>\tau}
and of
\tilde{q}^{u}\mathds{1}_{t>u}
coincide for
\omega\in\Omega
such that
\tau(\omega)=u
. By the superhedging property we thus have

x+\int_{0}^{1}q\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}dX_{t}+\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{q}^{\tau}\mathds{1}_{t>\tau}dX_{t}+hg\geq Z_{\tau}

\mathcal{Q}_{g}^{E}
-q.s. and in particular
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., and hence the same equality holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., with the stochastic integral replacing the pathwise integral. The desired inequality is obtained taking expectations under
\mathbb{P}
on both sides, noting that the expectation of the stochastic integral is zero since
\tilde{q}
is bounded and
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
, so
X
is a square integrable martingale under
\mathbb{P}
.
∎

4.3
Duality gap and a dynamic extension for statically traded European options

As discussed in the introduction, the pricing-hedging duality may then fail for an American option, i.e., the inequality in Proposition
4.2
may be strict. This was originally observed by
Neuberger 2007
;
Hobson and Neuberger 2017
, in a discrete time setting, and explored in a series of subsequent works. In particular,
Aksamit et al. 2019
linked this to the failure of the dynamic programming principle and shown that if the option exercise times are allowed to depend on richer information, namely on dynamic prices of
g
, then pricing-hedging duality is recovered. We establish now the analogous set of results in a continuous time setting. We start with an example where a duality gap arises.

Example 4.3
.

Consider
d=1
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
be such that
X_{t}\equiv 1
,
t\in[0,1]
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is a constant stock price model. Consider also
\mathbb{P}_{1}
under which
X_{t}\equiv 1
, for
t\in[0,1/2]
and then follows the SDE

dX_{t}=\bar{\beta}X_{t}dW_{t},

until
X
reaches
100
or
t=1
, whichever comes first, and where
\bar{\beta}>0
is given and
W_{t}
is a Brownian motion (possibly from a larger space).
In other words, the stock price is constant for half of the time and then behaves as a scaled geometric Brownian motion which stops if it hits the level
100
.

Let the set
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
be the convex hull of
\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{1}
, so

E_{1}=\{X_{[0,1/2]}=1\},\quad E_{2}=\{\beta_{[1/2,1]}=0\}\cup\{\beta_{t}=\bar{\beta}^{2}\omega_{t}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\sup_{s\leq t}\omega_{s}<100},t\in[1/2,1]\},

where
\beta
is the diffusion characteristic of
X
.
In particular,
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
, where
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{1}
. Finally, we let
g=(X_{1}-1)^{+}-p
, where
p
is such that
\mathbb{E}^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}[(X_{1}-1)^{+}]=p=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{1}}[(X_{1}-1)^{+}]
, so that
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
.

We now define the American option payoff via
Z_{t}=3p/2-6p|t-1/4|+(X_{t}-1)^{+}\land 100
for
t\in[0,1/2]
and
Z_{t}=(X_{t}-1)^{+}\land 100
for
t\in(1/2,1]
. Clearly, by definition,
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
. Note also that for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
, we have
X_{1}\leq 100

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., so that
Z_{1}=(X_{1}-1)^{+}

\mathcal{Q}^{E}
-q.s., and
Z_{1/4}=3p/2
,
\mathcal{Q}^{E}
-q.s. In fact, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}
,
Z_{t}
is a deterministic process on
[0,1/2]
which attains its maximum at time
t=1/4
. In addition, for
t\in[1/2,1]
,

5

5.1
Duality for European options

The financial interpretation of the setup introduced so far is standard. The process
X
models price dynamics of
d
assets traded continuously and in which investors are allowed to trade in a frictionless manner. We assume interest rates are deterministic and all prices are given in discounted units. Likewise, European or other payoffs, are expressed as functions of the discounted asset prices.

We start with a brief summary of the relevant robust pricing and hedging results for European options. As recalled in the introduction, many variants of continuous time pricing-hedging duality exist and they differ by assumptions they impose, the hedging strategies they consider and the regularity of the option payoff they can cover. Here, we adopt the OT-driven framework of
Guo and Loeper 2021
. We consider a function
g\in C_{b}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
which represents a vector of liquidly traded payoffs shifted by their market prices, so that a pricing measure
\mathbb{P}
is calibrated if
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(g)=0
. We denote
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}\subset\mathcal{Q}^{E}
the subset of calibrated pricing measures.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
represents all the pricing models we consider. Then for a European payoff
f
its
robust model price
is given by

\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}f.

On the other hand, we can consider a pricing-via-hedging argument, and look for the
superhedging price
defined as the smallest initial capital such that it is possible to construct a portfolio which superreplicates the payoff
f

t=0
) in the vector
g

\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
. So the superhedging price is given by

\pi_{g,E}(f):=\inf\left\{x:\exists(q,h)\in C_{b}(\Lambda;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{s.t.}\ x+\int_{0}^{1}q_{t}dX_{t}+hg\geq f,\ \mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}\text{-\text{q.s.}}\right\}.

(6)

Proposition 5.4

(

Suppose Assumption

\pi_{g,E}(f)=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[f]
.

Note that we have restricted the hedging strategies to

and, in particular, both sides of the superhedging inequality in the definition of

\Omega

. For more general

q
is bounded, the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}q_{s}dX_{s}
,
t\in[0,1]
, is a square integrable martingale under any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}
, and in particular its expectation is zero.

As shown in the previous section, the robust price of an American option is equal to the robust price of the corresponding European option in the enlarged space.
Hence, in order to establish dualities for American options, we will first establish dualities for European options in the enlarged space, in an analogue of Proposition
5.4
.

5.2
Duality for European options in the enlarged space

Under any probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\bar{\Omega}
,
\vartheta
is a semimartingale with characteristics
(\mathds{1}_{[0,\theta]}(t),0)
and the integral
t\to\int_{0}^{t}q_{s}d\vartheta_{s}
is defined pathwise on
\bar{\Omega}
for
q\in C(\bar{\Lambda})
.
We let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
be the set of probability measures
\mathbb{P}
on
\bar{\Omega}
such that
\mathbb{P}
restricted to
(\Omega,\mathbb{F}_{1})
is in
\mathcal{Q}
and such that
X
is a
(\mathbb{P},\bar{\mathbb{F}})
-martingale. We recall that
\langle X\rangle
and
\beta
were defined pathwise in (
5
) and these definitions extend to
\bar{\Omega}
with

\langle X\rangle(\bar{\omega})=\langle X\rangle(\omega)
and hence
\beta(\bar{\omega})=\beta(\omega)
.
For restrictions on
\beta
, we consider Assumption
5.1
. Note that
\vartheta
automatically has a diffusion characteristic of 0, and we also don’t allow
\mathfrak{E}
to depend on
\vartheta
. Then the set
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}\subset\bar{Q}
, of martingale measures where
\beta\in\mathfrak{E}
, and its subset
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}\subset\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}
of calibrated measures, are clearly defined.

The definition of the
superhedging price
in (
6
) naturally extends to
\bar{\Omega}
:

\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(f):=\inf\{x:\exists(q,h)\in C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{s.t.}\ x+\int_{0}^{1}q_{t}dX_{t}+hg\geq f,\ \bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}\text{-\text{q.s.}}\}.

When the vector
g
is empty we will simply write
\bar{\pi}_{E}(f)
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Suppose Assumption
5.1
holds and
f\in C_{b}(\bar{\Omega})
.
Then

\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(f)=\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}f
.

Proof.

By taking expectations, it is easy to check that

\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(f)\geq\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}f.

To show the reverse inequality we apply the main duality result of
Guo and Loeper 2021
on the enlarged spaces
\bar{\Omega}
and
\bar{\Lambda}
, with a cost function that
equals 0 if
\beta(t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})\in\mathfrak{E}(t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})
, or equals infinity otherwise.
The duality gives us that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}f=\inf_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\phi\in C^{1,1,2}_{0}(\bar{\Lambda})}\phi(0,0,X_{0}),

(7)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\phi(1,\cdot,\cdot)\geq f-hg\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi+\mathds{1}(t\leq\theta)\nabla_{\theta}\phi+\sup_{\beta\in\mathfrak{E}}\frac{1}{2}\beta:\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi\leq 0,

(8)

where the set of test functions is defined as follows.
We say
\phi\in C^{1,1,2}(\bar{\Lambda})
if
\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda})
and there exist functions
(\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi,\nabla_{\theta}\phi,\nabla_{x}\phi,\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi)\in C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\times\mathbb{S}^{d})
such that, for any
\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
and
u\in[0,1]
, the following
functional Itô formula
holds:

\displaystyle\phi(u,\vartheta,X)-\phi(0,\vartheta,X)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi\,ds+\nabla_{\theta}\phi\,d\vartheta_{s}+\nabla_{x}\phi\,dX_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi:d\langle X\rangle_{s}

(9)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}(\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi+\mathds{1}_{[0,\theta]}\nabla_{\theta}\phi)\,ds+\nabla_{x}\phi\,dX_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi:d\langle X\rangle_{s},\quad\bar{\mathbb{P}}\text{-\text{a.s.}}

From this definition, it follows directly that for each
\phi
satisfying (
8
) and each
\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}
, the following holds
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
-
a.s.

\displaystyle f-hg-\phi(0,0,X_{0})\leq

\displaystyle\ \phi(1,\cdot,\cdot)-\phi(0,0,X_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi+\mathds{1}_{[0,\theta]}\nabla_{\theta}\phi+\,\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\mathbb{P}}:\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi)dt+\nabla_{x}\phi\,dX_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}\phi\,dX_{t}.

As
\nabla_{x}\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an admissible hedging strategy, we deduce that
\phi(0,0,X_{0})\geq\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(f)
. Since this holds for all
\phi
satisfying (
8
), it implies
\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}f\geq\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(f)
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

We defined the set
C^{1,1,2}(\bar{\Lambda})
by requiring that the functional Itô formula holds for all
\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
, which is a smaller set than the set of all semimartingale measures with integrable characteristics used in
Guo and Loeper 2021
. This means that the equality in (
7
) shown therein could,
a priori
, turn to an inequality “LHS
\geq
RHS” with our definition. However, the rest of the proof then shows that the equality in fact holds through the sandwiching with the first inequality.

Remark 5.7
.

It follows from the proof that in the definition of the superhedging price, we can restrict further the hedging strategies and instead of
(q,h)\in C_{b}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
it suffices to consider
(q,h)\in C_{x}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where

C_{x}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{\nabla_{x}\phi:\phi\in C^{1,1,2}(\bar{\Lambda})\right\}.

We also note that for such
(q,h)\in C_{x}(\bar{\Lambda};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the functional Itô formula can be used to
define
the integral
\int_{0}^{\cdot}q_{s}dX_{s}
pathwise on
\bar{\Omega}
. This definition would potentially differ from (
3
) but the two agree
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., for any
\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
.

5.3
Duality for American options

Finally, we can return to the problem of superhedging of American options and link it to the superhedging for European options in the enlarged space. Recall that for
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
we write
\bar{Z}(\bar{\omega})=Z(\theta,\omega_{\cdot\land\theta})
.
Following Remark
5.7
we observe that in the setup of Proposition
5.5
we have

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(\bar{Z})

\displaystyle=\inf\{x:\exists(\bar{q},\bar{h})\in C_{x}(\bar{\Lambda})\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{s.t.}\ x+\int_{0}^{1}\bar{q}_{t}dX_{t}+\bar{h}g\geq\bar{Z},\ \bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}\text{-\text{q.s.}}\}.

We then have the following inequality.

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose Assumption
5.1
holds and
Z\in C_{b}(\Lambda)
. Then
\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(\bar{Z})\geq\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
.

Proof.

Suppose
(\bar{q},\bar{h})
is a superhedge for
\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(\bar{Z})
.
We construct the following superhedge
(q,\tilde{q},h)
for
\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
, noting that
\tilde{q}^{\theta}(t,\cdot)
is only relevant for
t\geq\theta
.

\displaystyle q(t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(t,t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t}),

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\theta}(t,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t})

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(t,\theta,\omega_{\cdot\wedge t}),\quad t\geq\theta,

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\bar{h}.

It remains to check that
(q,\tilde{q},h)
is indeed a superhedge for
\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
.
For each rational
u\in[0,1]
and
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
, we can define
\bar{\mathbb{P}}=\delta_{u}\times\mathbb{P}
. It is easy to check that
\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}
.
Hence

x+\int_{0}^{u}qdX_{t}+\int_{u}^{1}\tilde{q}^{u}dX_{t}+hg\geq Z_{u},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-\text{a.s.}}.

To extend from rational
u
to all reals, first note that
Z_{u}
is continuous in
u
. On the other side, by the definition of
q
and
\tilde{q}^{u}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u}qdX_{t}+\int_{u}^{1}\tilde{q}^{u}dX_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{q}(t,u\wedge t,\cdot)dX_{t}.

Recall
\bar{q}\in C_{x}(\bar{\Lambda})
, so there exists
\phi\in C^{1,1,2}(\bar{\Lambda})
such that
\bar{q}=\nabla_{x}\phi
. Then by the functional Itô formula in (
9
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\bar{q}(t,u\wedge t,\cdot)dX_{t}=\phi(1,u,\cdot)-\phi(0,u,\cdot)-\int_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi+\mathds{1}_{[0,u]}\nabla_{\theta}\phi)\,ds+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi:d\langle X\rangle_{s},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-\text{a.s.}}

Since
\phi,\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi,\nabla_{\theta}\phi,\nabla_{x}^{2}\phi
are all bounded and continuous, and
X
has a bounded diffusion characteristic under
\mathbb{P}
, the right hand side, and hence also the left hand side, is continuous in
u
.
Thus

x+\int_{0}^{u}qdX_{t}+\int_{u}^{1}\tilde{q}^{u}dX_{t}+hg\geq Z_{u},\quad\forall\,u\in[0,1],\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-\text{a.s.}}

Since this holds for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{Q}^{E}_{g}
, it must be a
\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
superhedge. Thus
\bar{\pi}_{g,E}(\bar{Z})\geq\pi^{A}_{g,E}(Z)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

The required result follows from the following chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{g}(\bar{Z})\geq\pi^{A}_{g}(Z)\geq\widehat{\pi}^{A}(\widehat{Z})\geq\sup_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{E},\widehat{\tau}\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}^{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}}\mathbb{E}^{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}\widehat{Z}_{\widehat{\tau}}=\sup_{\bar{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}\in\bar{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}}^{E}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}}\bar{\widehat{Z}}\geq\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\bar{Z}=\bar{\pi}_{g}(\bar{Z}),

(10)

which implies that there must be equality throughout. We will now justify each step.

The first inequality
\bar{\pi}_{g}(\bar{Z})\geq\pi^{A}_{g}(Z)
was just established in Lemma
5.8
, while the last equality
\bar{\pi}_{g}(\bar{Z})=\sup_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{E}_{g}}\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\bar{Z}
is the European duality in the enlarged space, proven in Proposition
5.5
. The remaining chain of inequalities was proven in Proposition
4.5
.
∎
