Localization of quantum states within subspaces
L. L. Salcedo

Abstract
A precise definition is proposed for the localization probability of a quantum state within a given subspace of the full Hilbert space of a quantum system. The corresponding localized component of the state is explicitly identified, and several mathematical properties are established. Applications and interpretations in the context of quantum information are also discussed.



I
Introduction

As is well known, a quantum state
\rho
can be expressed in many different
ways as a mixture of pure states, unless the state
\rho
itself is a pure
state. Given a set of pure states
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, the question then
arises: when is it possible to obtain
\rho
as a mixture including those
states with a non-vanishing probability. A necessary and sufficient condition
for this to be possible is that all the
\psi_{k}
must belong to the range
(image) of the operator
\rho
(Proposition
III.1
below).

A further question is: in which mixtures do those selected pure states have
the largest weight? The question is well-posed in the case of
n=1
: which is
the maximum weight a pure state
\psi
can have when it appears in a mixture
yielding
\rho
? That is, if

\rho=\lambda|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+(1-\lambda)\rho^{\prime},

(1)

what is the maximum value the probability
\lambda
can attain? Clearly, if
the state
\rho^{\prime}
itself can be expressed as

\rho^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+(1-\lambda^{\prime})\rho^{\prime\prime}

(2)

with
\lambda^{\prime}>0
, the original weight
\lambda
can be increased to a value

\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}
. The maximum probability will then be attained when

\lambda^{\prime}=0
, that is, when
\psi\not\in\ran(\rho^{\prime})
.

The simplest instance is that of a qubit in a state
\rho
to be expressed as
a mixture necessarily involving a certain pure state
\psi
plus a remainder

\rho^{\prime}
. For a qubit, the condition
\psi\not\in\ran(\rho^{\prime})
implies that

\rho^{\prime}
must be a certain pure state
\phi
, and different from

\psi
. (Exceptionally, when
\rho
is precisely
\psi
,
\lambda=1
and the
state
\rho^{\prime}
is undefined.) That is

\rho=\lambda|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+(1-\lambda)|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|,\quad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\,.

(3)

Here,
\rho
and
\psi
are given inputs, while
\lambda
and
\phi
are
outputs determined by the construction. The geometric construction in
Fig.
1
illustrates the solution. Except in the trivial case

\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, the state
\phi
is uniquely defined.
\rho
is a point
of the Bloch closed ball, with associated vector
{\vec{\rho}}
,

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+{\vec{\rho}}\cdot{\vec{\sigma}})
, with
\|{\vec{\rho}}\|\leq 1
. The pure
state
\psi
corresponds to a normalized vector
{\vec{\psi}}
on the sphere. The
state
\phi
corresponds to the interpolation

{\vec{\rho}}=\lambda{\vec{\psi}}+(1-\lambda){\vec{\phi}}
, or rather extrapolation since

{\vec{\rho}}
and
{\vec{\psi}}
are given while
\lambda
and
{\vec{\phi}}
result from the
construction.

Figure 1:
Geometric decomposition of a qubit state
\rho
in the Bloch
sphere (graphically simplified to a disk). The state is expressed as
a mixture (convex combination or interpolation) of two pure states:

\rho=\lambda|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+(1-\lambda)|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|
. For any given
state
\rho

\lambda
is
univocally determined, as is the pure state

(unless

proportional to

In this note, we intend to extend the previous considerations beyond the qubit
and beyond the case
n=1
. To this end, we analyze the following problem:
given a quantum state
\rho_{A}
in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and a certain subspace

{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
, to what extent can the state be obtained as a mixture (a
convex combination) of a state
\rho_{B}
located in
{\mathcal{V}}
and a state
\rho_{C}

located outside
{\mathcal{V}}
,

\rho_{A}=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C},\quad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1
. Here by
\rho_{B}
to be “located” in
{\mathcal{V}}
we mean that it is a mixture
of pure states in
{\mathcal{V}}
. As is well-known (Lemma
III.1
below), a state

\rho
can be expressed as a mixture of pure states in many ways, but those
pure states must belong to the image of the operator
\rho
, and conversely,
the linear span of the set of pure states in the mixture must be the image of

\rho
. Thus, the condition on
\rho_{B}
(to be located in
{\mathcal{V}}
) can be
expressed as
\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
, while the condition on
\rho_{C}
,
expressing that it is located outside
{\mathcal{V}}
, will be formulated as

\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0
. Having delimited the mathematical problem, we
will study the decomposition

\begin{split}\rho_{A}&=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C},\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\,,\\
\quad&\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}},\quad\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0.\end{split}

(4)

Here, the data are
\rho_{A}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
; the weight
\lambda
and the states

\rho_{B}
and
\rho_{C}
are not chosen a priori, but are a determined by the
decomposition itself.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: In the next section, the
mathematical problem is analyzed, providing the necessary statements along with
their proof. In the third section, we discuss some of the consequences of the
decomposition eq. (
4
) with regard to quantum information. The last section
summarizes the conclusions.

II
Decomposition of a non-negative operator along a subspace

Some conventions and remarks:
all abstract vector spaces considered in
this work (in particular Hilbert spaces) are complex and finite-dimensional,
unless otherwise specified (thus there is no difference between Hermitian and
self-adjoint operators). For Hilbert spaces, the symbol
\oplus
denotes the
direct sum of orthogonal subspaces,
\subseteq
denotes subspace, and

\ominus
denotes orthogonal complement. The symbol
\,\dot{+}\,
denotes direct
sum (not necessarily orthogonal) and
I
denotes the identity operator. The
acronym PVM stands for
projector valued measure
, that is, a set of
non-zero operators
\{P_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, such that
P_{k}^{\dagger}=P_{k}
,

P_{k}P_{j}=\delta_{kj}P_{k}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}P_{k}=I
. The scalar product is
conjugate-linear on the first argument. For an orthogonal projector
P
onto
some subspace,
P^{\perp}:=I-P
. For a subspace
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
,

{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}:={\mathcal{H}}\ominus{\mathcal{V}}
. The range of an operator always refers to its
image,
\ran(A)=A({\mathcal{H}})
.

Proposition 2.2

Let

\ran(X)\,\dot{+}\,\ran(Y)={\mathcal{H}}

Proof

1)

(\ran(X)+\ran(Y))\ominus\ran(X+Y)=0

; it follows that

.

2)

Since the Hermitian operators

and

commute, they share a
common spectral decomposition

X=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}P_{k},\qquad Y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}P_{k},

(5)

where

. The condition

. The

. Then, for each

and

3)

Let

. If

. Otherwise we can restrict ourselves to the non-zero subspace

{\mathcal{R}}:=\ran(M)
. Within
{\mathcal{R}}
the operator

is positive. Then

I=M^{-1/2}XM^{-1/2}+M^{-1/2}YM^{-1/2}:=X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime}.

(6)

Clearly

and

are Hermitian and disjoint; hence, they form a PVM in

{\mathcal{R}}
. Then

and

are positive operators in

and non-negative

{\mathcal{H}}
. When

{\mathcal{H}}
,
{\mathcal{H}}=\ran(M)=\ran(X)+\ran(Y)
.
\square

The clauses 1 and 2 of the Proposition can be relaxed to require only

A further relevant proposition:

Proposition 2.22

Let
A>0
and
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
, and let

be the decompositions of
A
along
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, respectively. Let
the operator

Then

. Furthermore

,
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
are
invariant subspaces of

, and in this case

.

Proof

From the Corollary
II.4

\Tr(B)\leq\Tr(PA),\qquad\Tr(B^{\perp})\leq\Tr(P^{\perp}A).

(63)

Then
P+P^{\perp}=I
implies
\Tr(B)+\Tr(B^{\perp})\leq\Tr(A)
, hence

\Tr(D)\geq 0
.

Let
Q
and
Q_{\perp}
be orthogonal projectors onto
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}:=A^{-1/2}{\mathcal{V}}
and

{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}_{\perp}:=A^{-1/2}{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. They fulfill

\mathrm{rank}(Q)+\mathrm{rank}(Q_{\perp})=\dim{\mathcal{H}}.

(64)

Using the Proposition
II.3.1
, the eq. (
62
) becomes

I=Q+Q_{\perp}+A^{-1/2}DA^{-1/2}.

(65)

If the two subspaces
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}_{\perp}
are not orthogonal, then there
exists a vector

such that
\langle\psi|Q_{\perp}|\psi\rangle>0

\langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|Q|\psi\rangle

\langle\psi|A^{-1/2}DA^{-1/2}|\psi\rangle<0
, and the operator
D
is not
non-negative. When the two subspaces are orthogonal, eq. (
64
) implies

I=Q+Q_{\perp}
and so

. However, the orthogonality requires

PA^{-1}P^{\perp}=0
which in turn requires
[P,A^{-1}]=0
, hence
{\mathcal{V}}
is an
invariant subspace of
A
.
\square

It can be noted that

can be non-negative and non-zero if

is
permitted. For instance, when
A
has support on a subspace

{\mathcal{W}}
such that

and

.

Quantum information

III.1
Support of a quantum state

The meaning and significance of the support (image)
\ran(\rho)
of a quantum
state
\rho
(
\rho\geq 0
and
\Tr(\rho)=1
) follow from the following
lemma and proposition:

Lemma 3.1

Given a set of vectors
\{\tilde{\psi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}

\ran\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\tilde{\psi}_{k}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{k}|\right)=\mathrm{span}\{\tilde{\psi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\,.

(66)

Proof
Given another set vectors,
\{\tilde{\phi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, the following
equality

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\tilde{\psi}_{k}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{k}|=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\tilde{\phi}_{k}\rangle\langle\tilde{\phi}_{k}|=:A

(67)

holds if and only if the two sets are related through a unitary
transformation. The proof of this can be found in
Nielsen:2012yss
,
p.103. Clearly,
\displaystyle\ran(A)\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\tilde{\psi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
. On
the other hand, taking the
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
as the eigenvectors of
A
, each

\tilde{\psi}_{k}
is a (unitary) linear combination of such eigenvectors; hence,

\tilde{\psi}_{k}\in\ran(A)
.
\square

Proposition 3.2

Let
\rho
be a quantum state, and let

\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
be a finite set of unit vectors (pure states). The
condition
\forall k~\psi_{k}\in\ran(\rho)
is necessary and sufficient for
the existence of a mixture yielding
\rho
where each of the
\psi_{k}
is
present with a non-vanishing weight.

Proof
The condition
\psi_{k}\in\ran(\rho)
is necessary by Lemma
III.1
.

To prove its sufficiency, let us express the state as a mixture composed of
its normalized eigenvectors,

\rho=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|,\quad\lambda_{j}>0,\quad\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1.

(68)

Let
\lambda_{m}
denote the smallest non-zero eigenvalue, then

\rho\geq\lambda_{m}P
, where
P=\sum_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|
is the orthogonal
projector onto
\ran(\rho)
. Because
P
is the identity operator within the
subspace
\ran(\rho)
, it can be expressed as a sum of projectors associated
to an orthonormal basis of
\ran(\rho)
, and for each
k
, such a basis can be
chosen so that
\psi_{k}
is one of its elements,
P=|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|+\cdots
. Therefore

\rho\geq\frac{\lambda_{m}}{n}nP=\frac{\lambda_{m}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|+\cdots).

(69)

Thus each
\psi_{k}
appears in the mixture of
\rho
with weight at least

\lambda_{m}/n>0
.
\square

III.2
Decomposition of a quantum state along a subspace

The mathematical results of the Sec.
II
immediately apply to the
decomposition of a quantum state
\rho_{A}
along a subspace
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
,

\begin{split}\rho_{A}&=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C},\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\,,\\
\quad&\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}},\quad\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0.\end{split}

(70)

Using the definitions in eq. (
17
)

\begin{split}\lambda\rho_{B}&=\mathrm{B}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}}),\qquad\lambda=\mathrm{TB}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}}),\\
(1-\lambda)\rho_{C}&=\mathrm{C}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}}).\end{split}

(71)

If, for simplicity,
\rho_{A}>0
is assumed, and the density matrix is expressed
in block form with respect to
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
as

\rho_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b^{\dagger}\\
b&c\end{pmatrix},

(72)

then

\lambda\rho_{B}=a-b^{\dagger}c^{-1}b,

(73)

that is,
\lambda\rho_{B}
is the Schur complement of the block
c
in the
matrix
\rho_{A}
.

The existence and uniqueness of the decomposition in eq. (
70
) is the content
of the Theorem
II.1
in Sec.
II.1
. In the particular cases

\lambda=1
or
\lambda=0
, the states
\rho_{C}
or
\rho_{B}
are undetermined,
respectively, but
\lambda
itself is always well-defined.

In view of the meaning of the support of a state, the condition on
\rho_{B}

has a clear interpretation:
\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
ensures that all
the pure states in a mixture producing
\rho_{B}
belong to
{\mathcal{V}}
.

The condition
\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0
is intended to ensure that
\lambda

is maximal in a decomposition constrained by
\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
. That
\lambda
is indeed maximal when
\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0
is an
immediate consequence of the Lemma
II.2
or eqs. (
25
). Therefore, the
decomposition in eq. (
70
) of a state
\rho_{A}
yields the maximal probability
for the states
\rho_{B}
lying in a given subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
.

According to the Proposition
II.1
, clause 4, the decomposition enjoys a
reciprocity property, namely, if the subspace
{\mathcal{W}}:=\ran(\rho_{c})
is chosen a
priori, eq. (
70
) is also the decomposition of
\rho_{A}
requiring the support
of
\rho_{C}
to be in
{\mathcal{W}}
and
\ran(\rho_{B})\cap{\mathcal{W}}=0
.

For given
\rho_{A}\geq 0
and
{\mathcal{V}}
, the decomposition occurs on the
support of
\rho_{A}
. The subspace
\tilde{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\ran(\rho_{A})\cap{\mathcal{V}}
is the
support of
\rho_{B}
(Theorem
II.1
), and
\lambda>0
unless
\tilde{{\mathcal{V}}}=0
.
Hence, in particular,
\lambda>0
when
\rho_{A}>0
.
5
5

5

Assuming

{\mathcal{V}}\neq 0
.
The support of
\rho_{C}
is also contained in
\ran(\rho_{A})
,
and the (not necessarily orthogonal) direct sum of the supports of

and

yields that of
\rho_{A}
. The decomposition of

\rho_{A}\geq 0
along

is identical to that of

\rho_{A}>0

(Corollary

).

It should be stressed that in general, the subspace
\ran(\rho_{C})
is not

. This is also clear in the example of the qubit in

(clause 5 of the

), and in this case, the states

When
\rho_{A}>0

such that the support of

\rho_{B}\rho_{A}^{-1}\rho_{C}=0

provided

Returning to

where
\tilde{P}

One interesting conclusion from the previous formula is that the values of

III.3
Interpretation of the decomposition as a probability

Given a state
\rho_{A}
and a subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
, the standard quantum-mechanical
assignation is the (orthogonal) overlap, that is,

p:=\mathrm{TP}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}})=\Tr(P\rho_{A}),

(78)

where
P
denotes the orthogonal projector onto
{\mathcal{V}}
. This is the probability
of leaving the system in a state contained in
{\mathcal{V}}
after an ideal measurement
of the PVM
I=P+P^{\perp}
. Such probability vanishes if and only if the
support of the state
\rho_{A}
is orthogonal to
{\mathcal{V}}
.

The definition in eq. (
70
) of the quantity
\lambda=\mathrm{TB}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}})

suggests a quantum-information interpretation which is different from the
overlap, namely,
\lambda
can be regarded as the probability (or weight) of
the state
\rho_{A}
to be
completely included
(or localized) within the
subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
of pure states. The inclusion probability
\lambda
vanishes
iff the support of
\rho_{A}
is disjoint (in vector-space sense) from
{\mathcal{V}}
,
and attains its maximum value
\lambda=1
iff the support of the state is
contained in
{\mathcal{V}}
.

A property consistent with this interpretation is that
\lambda
is an
increasing function of
{\mathcal{V}}
, that is (Proposition
II.2
, clause 1)

{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{2}~\implies~\mathrm{TB}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}}_{1})\leq\mathrm{TB}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}}_{2}),

(79)

and in fact, the statement is stronger: not only
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
but
also

\lambda_{1}\rho_{B_{1}}\leq\lambda_{2}\rho_{B_{2}}

(80)

as operators.

The concept of
inclusion probability
within a subspace of pure states
is more restrictive than that of overlap, and indeed the following inequality
always holds:

\lambda\leq p

(81)

(Corollary
II.4
, clause 1). For instance, for a qubit in state

|\text{+}z\rangle
and subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
spanned by
|\text{+}x\rangle
, the overlap
is 50%, implying that the two states are partially equal and partially
different. However, the weight
\lambda
vanishes, as
|\text{+}z\rangle
can
never be included within the subspace
\mathrm{span}(|\text{+}x\rangle)
, that is, if

|\text{+}z\rangle
is written as a mixture including
|\text{+}x\rangle
the latter
must appear with zero weight.

If
\lambda
is interpreted as the probability of inclusion of
\rho_{A}
within
the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
, the weight
1-\lambda
carried by
\rho_{C}
must be
interpreted as the probability of the state
\rho_{A}
not to be completely
included within
{\mathcal{V}}
. As already noted, in the decomposition

\rho_{A}=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C}\,,

(82)

the support of
\rho_{C}
is an output, not a constraint, and such support is not
necessarily orthogonal to that of
\rho_{B}
. This means that
quantum-mechanically, those two states
\rho_{B}
and
\rho_{C}
are not completely
distinguishable. They are when
{\mathcal{V}}
is an invariant subspace of
\rho_{A}
, that
is, an orthogonal direct sum of eigenspaces of
\rho_{A}
. In that case, the
decomposition based on inclusion coincides with that based on overlap:

[P,\rho_{A}]=0\implies\rho_{A}=P\rho_{A}P+P^{\perp}\rho_{A}P^{\perp}\,,

(83)

and this is a necessary and sufficient condition for
\lambda=p
(Corollary

II.4
, clause 1).

As said, the condition
\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
is stronger than that of
overlap of
\rho_{A}
with
{\mathcal{V}}
. Correspondingly, the condition

\ran(\rho_{C})\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0
, which intuitively means that
\rho_{C}
lies
outside
{\mathcal{V}}
, is weaker than the condition
\ran(\rho_{C})\perp{\mathcal{V}}
, which
expresses the same idea in a much stronger sense. The latter condition is too
strong to be compatible with the inclusion requirement

\ran(\rho_{B})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
.

Some insight is obtained by considering the complementary decomposition, along

{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, that is

\begin{split}\rho_{A}&=\lambda^{\perp}\rho_{B^{\perp}}+(1-\lambda^{\perp})\rho_{C^{\perp}},\qquad 0\leq\lambda^{\perp}\leq 1\,,\\
\quad&\ran(\rho_{B^{\perp}})\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp},\quad\ran(\rho_{C^{\perp}})\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}=0.\end{split}

(84)

Here
\lambda^{\perp}
is the probability of complete inclusion of the state

\rho_{A}
within the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. Once again
\lambda^{\perp}\leq 1-p
,
and combined with eq. (
81
),

\lambda\leq p\leq 1-\lambda^{\perp}\,.

(85)

According to these inequalities, the condition of complete inclusion within

{\mathcal{V}}
is more restrictive than the condition of overlap, and in turn, the
overlap is more restrictive than the condition of non-complete inclusion of

\rho_{A}
within
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
.

Quantum-mechanically, the events of overlap with
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
are
incompatible and the corresponding probabilities
p
and
1-p
add up to one.
The events of being fully within
{\mathcal{V}}
, with weight
\lambda
, and being fully
within
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, with weight
\lambda^{\perp}
, are also incompatible. This
is consistent with the relation

\lambda+\lambda^{\perp}\leq 1

(86)

(which follows from eq. (
85
)), since the sum of two probabilities does not
exceed one. However, there is a non-negative deficiency

1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp}
which in general is not zero. The partition

1=\lambda+\lambda^{\perp}+(1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp})

(87)

suggests that for a given state
\rho_{A}
there are three distinct
sectors, induced by the decomposition
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
:

States located fully within
{\mathcal{V}}
, with size
\lambda
.

ii)

States located fully within
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, with size
\lambda^{\perp}

States not fully located within
{\mathcal{V}}
nor fully within

{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, so partially on both subspaces. This sector has size

1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp}
.

\rho_{B}
would fill up the first sector and
\rho_{B^{\perp}}

\rho_{C}

p=\lambda+(1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp})p
, that is,

p=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\lambda^{\perp}}.

(96)

The same relation can also be expressed as

\Prob(C|{\mathcal{V}})=1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp}
, or also as

\Prob(B|{\mathcal{V}})=\Prob(B^{\perp}|{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}).

(97)

If fact, such relations are valid for a qubit, or more precisely for

\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{A})=2
, but they do not hold in general. Indeed, using eqs. (
72
)
and (
73
),

\begin{split}\Prob(B|{\mathcal{V}})&=\frac{\lambda}{p}=\frac{\Tr(a-b^{\dagger}c^{-1}b)}{\Tr(a)},\\
\Prob(B^{\perp}|{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp})&=\frac{\lambda^{\perp}}{1-p}=\frac{\Tr(c-ba^{-1}b^{\dagger})}{\Tr(c)},\end{split}

(98)

The two expressions coincide when
a,b,c
are c-numbers, but in general, they
yield different values.

The probability interpretation made above can be extended to the case where a
state is decomposed into
n
components (instead of just two). Assuming

\rho_{A}>0
for simplicity, let

\rho_{A}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\,\rho_{C_{k}},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=1,

(99)

be the decomposition of
\rho_{A}
along disjoint supports:

{\mathcal{W}}_{k}=\ran(\rho_{C_{k}})\,,\qquad{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{W}}_{1}\,\dot{+}\,\cdots\,\dot{+}\,{\mathcal{W}}_{n},

(100)

which are orthogonal with respect to the non-standard scalar product
introduced in eq. (
74
). Let
P_{k}
be the (standard) orthogonal projector onto

{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
, and

p_{k}=\Tr(P_{k}\rho_{A})\,.

(101)

The following identifications can be made:

\Prob(C_{k}|A)=\lambda_{k},\qquad\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{k}|A)=p_{k},

(102)

and

\begin{split}\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{k}|C_{k},A)&=\Tr(P_{k}\rho_{C_{k}})=1,\\
\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{k}^{\perp}|C_{k},A)&=\Tr(P_{k}^{\perp}\rho_{C_{k}})=0.\end{split}

(103)

Further, the relations

p_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\Tr(P_{j}\rho_{C_{k}})

(104)

correspond to

\begin{split}\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j}|A)&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j}|C_{k},A)\Prob(C_{k}|A)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j},C_{k}|A).\end{split}

(105)

Note that the set
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
can be regarded as a partition, as their
probabilities add up to one,
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=1
. In contrast, this
is not true for the
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
. In fact, the relation
\lambda_{k}\leq p_{k}
implies

1\leq\sum_{k=1}\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{k}|A),

(106)

and in general, the sum exceeds one. The
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
may be non-orthogonal among
them, and thus quantum-mechanically, they are compatible events.

Of course, from the inequality

\forall\rho_{A}>0\quad\sum_{k=1}\Tr(P_{k}\rho_{A})\geq 1,

(107)

it does not follow the operator identity
\sum_{k=1}P_{k}\geq I
, because the
operators
P_{k}
depend on (are constrained by)
\rho_{A}
.

At variance with this, for any given
j
,
{\mathcal{W}}_{j}
and
{\mathcal{W}}_{j}^{\perp}
are
incompatible and the probabilities
\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j},C_{k}|A)
plus

\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j}^{\perp},C_{k}|A)
can be organized as a
n\times 2
table, with
marginal probabilities
\lambda_{k}=\Prob(C_{k}|A)
, plus
p_{j}=\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j}|A)

and
1-p_{j}=\Prob({\mathcal{W}}_{j}^{\perp}|A)
. The
2\times 2
Table
1

corresponds to
n=2
, with
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}={\mathcal{V}}
, plus
C_{1}=B
and
C_{2}=C
. For
n>2

the data
p_{j}
and
\lambda_{k}
are not sufficient to fully fix all the entries
of such
n\times 2
table of probabilities.

The inequalities of the type eq. (
86
) can be much more general (and no longer
be a consequence of
\lambda\leq p
). In fact, if

{\mathcal{V}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bigoplus$}}}_{k=1}^{n}{\mathcal{V}}_{k}

(108)

and

\begin{split}\rho_{A}&=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C}\\
&=\lambda_{k}\rho_{B_{k}}+(1-\lambda_{k})\rho_{C_{k}}\end{split}

(109)

are the decomposition along
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{k}
, respectively,
it holds (Theorem
II.4
, clause 3),

\lambda\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\,.

(110)

As already said, the stronger inequality

\lambda\rho_{B}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\,\rho_{B_{k}}

(111)

is not guaranteed in general.

III.4
Entropic properties

The weighted density matrix
\lambda\rho_{B}:=\mathrm{B}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}})

enjoys the property (Lemma
II.2
)

\lambda\rho_{B}=\max\{X~|~X\leq\rho_{A},~~\ran(X)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\},

(112)

and also

\lambda=\max\{\Tr(X)~|~X\leq\rho_{A},~~\ran(X)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\}.

(113)

So
\lambda=\mathrm{TB}(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}})
is the maximum weight that can be concentrated
within the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
in a mixture yielding
\rho_{A}
.

A consequence of the maximal property is that, for a given subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
,

\lambda
is a concave function of
\rho_{A}
(Theorem
II.2
). That is,
if the states
\rho_{A_{k}}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, have weights
\lambda_{k}
along

{\mathcal{V}}
, and

\rho_{A}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}\rho_{A_{k}}\qquad q_{k}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}=1,

(114)

then

\lambda\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}\lambda_{k},

(115)

where
\lambda
is the weight of
\rho_{A}
along
{\mathcal{V}}
. In fact, the
statement is stronger: if
\rho_{B_{k}}
are the density matrices maximally
localized on
{\mathcal{V}}
for each
\rho_{A_{k}}
, then also

\lambda\rho_{B}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}\lambda_{k}\rho_{B_{k}}.

(116)

The concavity property is illustrated in Fig.
4
for a qubit.

The quantum-mechanical von Neumann entropy

S(\rho_{A})=-\Tr(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})
(and so also its classical version, the
Shannon entropy) is a concave function, implying that mixing tends to increase
the entropy. Likewise, the concavity of the localization weight

\lambda(\rho_{A}|{\mathcal{V}})
indicates that it tends to increase under mixing.
However, the maximal (unconstrained) entropy
\log(d)
is attained for the
state
\rho_{A}=I/d
, where
d=\dim({\mathcal{H}})
. Instead, the maximum localization
within
{\mathcal{V}}
is
\lambda=1
and is attained by any state
\rho_{A}
with support
entirely contained in
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Another characteristic property of the entropy is that it is additive with
respect to independent subsystems, that is, if
\rho_{A_{1}}
and
\rho_{A_{2}}

are two states in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
, respectively,

S(\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{2}})=S(\rho_{A_{1}})+S(\rho_{A_{2}}).

(117)

Such equality does not hold for the localization probability
\lambda
, rather
(Proposition
II.3.2
)

\lambda\geq\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2},

(118)

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda
are the weights of the states

\rho_{A_{1}}
,
\rho_{A_{2}}
and
\rho_{A}=\rho_{A_{1}}\otimes\rho_{A_{2}}
along
the subspaces
{\mathcal{V}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{V}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{V}}_{2}
, respectively. A
relation which is closer to additivity is

{\mathcal{S}}\geq{\mathcal{S}}_{1}+{\mathcal{S}}_{2}

(119)

with
{\mathcal{S}}:=\log(\lambda)
, although still this quantity is super-additive
rather than additive. Since the function
\log
is both increasing and
concave, it follows that
\rho_{A}\to{\mathcal{S}}
is also concave, as it would be
required for an entropy.

Figure 4:
Illustration of the concavity property for a qubit in the Bloch
sphere. The states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, and their mixture
\rho
, are
decomposed along a one-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
with orthogonal
projector
\rho_{B}
. The weight
\lambda
of
\rho
is larger than the
average of the weights
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
of
\rho_{1}
and

\rho_{2}
.

III.5
Measurement of localization

According to quantum mechanics, the probability
p=\Tr(P\rho_{A})
(where
P

denotes the orthogonal projector onto a subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
) can be determined by
doing ideal measurements of the PVM
I=P+P^{\perp}
on the state
\rho_{A}
. In
principle, such an approach is not available to the determination of the
inclusion probability
\lambda=\mathrm{TB}(A|{\mathcal{V}})
, in the decomposition

\rho_{A}=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C}
. The reason is that the
quantity
\mathrm{TB}(A|{\mathcal{V}})
scales as
\mathrm{TB}(\mu\,A|{\mathcal{V}})=\mu\,\mathrm{TB}(A|{\mathcal{V}})
for

\mu\geq 0
, but it is not a fully linear function of
A
; in fact, the map is
super-additive (Proposition
II.2
). Hence, excluding the trivial case of

{\mathcal{V}}
being an invariant subspace of
\rho_{A}
, there is no proper PVM with
some orthogonal projector
P^{\prime}
such
\lambda=\Tr(P^{\prime}\rho_{A})
. Of course,
it is always possible to devise an ad-hoc PVM to do the job, and in
fact (Proposition
II.3.1
)

\lambda=\Tr(Q\rho_{A}),

(120)

where
Q
is the orthogonal projector onto the subspace

{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}:=\rho_{A}^{-1/2}\ran(\rho_{A})\cap{\mathcal{V}}
.
7
7

7

Also

\lambda=\Tr(\Pi\rho_{A})
, from eq. (
32
).
\Pi
is a projector operator,
but it is non-Hermitian in general, and it also depends on the state.
For

\rho_{A}>0
, the same construction can be extended along the lines of
eqs. (
99
) and (
100
) with orthogonal projectors
Q_{k}
onto

{\mathcal{W}}^{\prime}_{k}:=\rho_{A}^{-1/2}{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
. Nevertheless, such a PVM is of limited use since
the
Q_{k}
depend on the very state
\rho_{A}
to be measured. Also, the
(unnormalized) post-measurement state
Q\rho_{A}Q
has support on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
so it
would not be directly
\rho_{B}
.

In any case, if
\lambda
(rather
\log(\lambda)
), is
regarded as a kind of entropy, it is only natural that it is not accessible as
the expectation value of a projector independent of the state, since this is
also the case of the von Neumann entropy.

III.6
Cryptographic application

Let
\rho
be a state with support contained in a subspace

{\mathcal{V}}\varsubsetneq{\mathcal{H}}
, where the inequality can always be achieved by adding
ancillary states. Such state can be masked by considering another state

\sigma
in
{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\ran(\sigma)\cap{\mathcal{V}}=0
, thus granting a
disjoint support with
\rho
. The state
\rho
can be encrypted within a new
state
\rho^{\prime}
through the mixing

\rho^{\prime}=\lambda\rho+(1-\lambda)\sigma,\qquad 0<\lambda<1\,,

(121)

for instance, using an ancillary qubit
A^{\prime}
and a standard
construction

\rho^{\prime}=\Tr_{A^{\prime}}\big(\lambda\rho\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+(1-\lambda)\sigma\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|\big)\,.

(122)

The state
\rho^{\prime}
can be public while
{\mathcal{V}}
is known only to the
receiver. Since
\rho
and
\sigma
have disjoint supports,
\rho^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{V}}

univocally determine the state
\rho
, as well as
\sigma
and
\lambda
.

If
{\mathcal{V}}
were an invariant subspace of
\rho^{\prime}
, an ideal measurement of
P
,
the orthogonal projector onto
{\mathcal{V}}
, on
\rho^{\prime}
would directly select
\rho
,
which is a convenient procedure: the receiver only needs to receive
copies of the state
\rho^{\prime}
rather than
fully know
the state. However,
if
\rho^{\prime}
is fully known, the condition on
{\mathcal{V}}
of being an invariant
subspace is too restrictive and gives away too much
information. Allowing a more general
{\mathcal{V}}
is much less informative while
still uniquely determines
\rho
.

IV
Summary and conclusions

With the aim of later applying it to quantum information, we have addressed
the mathematical problem of defining the decomposition of a non-negative
operator
A=B+C
along a given subspace
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
as a sum of two
non-negative operators,
B
with support within
{\mathcal{V}}
, and
C
with support
disjoint from
{\mathcal{V}}
. Such decomposition always exists and is unique, and the
same decomposition is obtained by requiring the operator
B
to be maximal
under the constraint
B\leq A
. The decomposition then translates to quantum
systems in the form
\rho_{A}=\lambda\rho_{B}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{C}
, so that

\rho_{A}
is expressed as a mixture of two quantum states
\rho_{B}
and
\rho_{C}

localized inside and outside
{\mathcal{V}}
, respectively.

In Sec.
II
, precise mathematical definitions are provided, the
existence and uniqueness of the decomposition are proven (Theorem
II.1
)
and many properties of it are uncovered and discussed in detail, in particular
its relation with the Schur complement. The maximal property of the component

B
is established (Lemma
II.2
), as is the concavity (Theorem

II.2
) and super-additivity attributes of the map
A\to B
for fixed

{\mathcal{V}}
(Proposition
II.2
), and the monotony of the map
{\mathcal{V}}\to B
for
fixed
A
(Proposition
II.2
). Inequalities related to the tensor product
of spaces are also obtained (Proposition
II.3.2
). Alternatively, the
decomposition can be derived from the orthogonal projector onto the subspace

A^{-1/2}{\mathcal{V}}
(Proposition
II.3.1
) assuming here
A>0
for simplicity, and
also from the orthogonality of the supports of
B
and
C
with respect to a
suitable
A
-dependent (but
{\mathcal{V}}
-independent) new scalar product (eqs. (
31
)
and (
32
)). Using this idea, the decomposition admits a natural extension
to the case of
n>2
localization components. Still assuming
A>0
, the
component
B
is shown to be given by
PA^{-1}P
(Proposition
II.3.2
),
being
P
the orthogonal projector operator onto
{\mathcal{V}}
(the general case

A\geq 0
is discussed in the main text). Several inequalities involving the
trace are also derived (Proposition
II.4
and Theorem
II.4
), some
of them relying on the property
PAP\geq B
. All proofs are given for
finite-dimensional spaces; an interesting extension would be to obtain similar
results for general Hilbert spaces and subspaces. Such extension is not
addressed here.

Section
III
discusses implications for quantum states and quantum
information. First, we emphasize the relation between the support of a quantum
state
\rho_{A}
and the pure states whose mixture produces such a state. Then,
the formulas and relations derived in the previous section are brought to the
setting of quantum information. Particularly interesting is the interpretation
of
\lambda
as a probability, namely, the probability for a quantum system in
the state
\rho_{A}
to be found completely included within the subspace

{\mathcal{V}}
. Such interpretation is sustained by the properties of the maps
A\to B

and
{\mathcal{V}}\to B
exposed before. The inequality
\lambda\leq p
relating the
weight
\lambda
with the standard quantum-mechanical overlap probability

p=\Tr(P\rho_{A})
is discussed. It is shown that the sum of the inclusion
probabilities within
{\mathcal{V}}
and within
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
,
\lambda+\lambda^{\perp}
, is
bounded by one. This property was to be expected a priori, consistently with
the interpretation of
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\perp}
. Nevertheless, in general,
there is a deficiency
1-\lambda-\lambda^{\perp}\geq 0
that cannot be attributed
to a true quantum state
\rho_{D}
; in fact,
\Tr(\rho_{D})=1
but excluding
trivial cases, such
\rho_{D}
necessarily presents negative eigenvalues
(Proposition
II.4
).

The quantum-mechanical overlap (
p
and
1-p
) and localization (
\lambda
and

1-\lambda
) events are compatible and their combined probabilities can
computed, as shown in Table
1
. Similar tables exist for localization
probabilities involving more than two localization components. Similarly, the
trace inequalities can also be extended to the multi-component case.

The concavity property of the weight
\lambda
implies some resemblance with the
same property of the quantum-mechanical (von Neumann) entropy. This is even more so for the quantity

{\mathcal{S}}=\log(\lambda)
, also a concave function of
\rho_{A}
, since under the
composition of systems
\lambda
is (almost) multiplicative while
{\mathcal{S}}
is
additive, or more precisely super-additive.

Even if the weight
\lambda
admits some interpretation as a probability, at
variance with the overlap probability
p
, it cannot be obtained as the
expectation value of an observable, at least not one independent of the very
state
\rho_{A}
to be measured. This does not directly imply that
\lambda
is
not a physical (or rather information-theoretic) quantity, since the same
holds for entropy, entanglement measure, and the like.

Finally, a simple cryptographic scheme is devised involving the localization
probability
\lambda
: The private state is masked by mixing it to produce a
public state; the private state can then be retrieved using the unique
decomposition along a subspace known only by the two private parties.

Appendix A
Regularity of the map
{\mathcal{V}}\to B(A|{\mathcal{V}})

All subspaces
{\mathcal{V}}
with dimension
n
can be obtained as
{\mathcal{V}}=U^{-1}{\mathcal{V}}_{0}

with a fixed
n
-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
and a suitable unitary operator

U
. Then, from clause 8 in the Proposition
II.1
,

\mathrm{TB}(A|{\mathcal{V}})=\mathrm{TB}(UAU^{\dagger}|{\mathcal{V}}_{0}).

(123)

If
U
is expressed as
U=e^{H}
, with an anti-Hermitian
H
, the dependence
of
\mathrm{TB}(A|{\mathcal{V}})
on
{\mathcal{V}}
becomes a dependence on
H
. Such dependence is
real-analytic for
A>0
:
U
is entire analytic as a function of (the matrix
elements of)
H
, and this translates to
UAU^{\dagger}
. Moreover, the dependence
of
B=\mathrm{B}(A|{\mathcal{V}})
on (the matrix elements of)
A>0
is meromorphic, with
B

its Schur complement. The only singularities (with respect to any single
matrix element of
A
as an independent variable) can be poles, however,

0\leq B\leq A
is bounded; therefore, there are no singularities as
H

changes
within its anti-Hermitian
domain, so within that domain, the map

H\to\mathrm{TB}(A|e^{H}{\mathcal{V}})
is real-analytic for
A>0
.
