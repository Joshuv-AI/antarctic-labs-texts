Predicting non-specific binding of VHHs using machine learning models with cluster-aware validation
Authors: Valentin Stanev, Federico Devalle, Mehdi Boroumand, Maryam Pouryahya, Isabelle Sermadiras, Jenna Caldwell, Kuan-Lin Chen, Jay Hyun Jo, Rohan Jain, Bismark Amofah, Tony Pham, Mark Hutchinson, Sharfa Farzandh, Jen DiChiara, Chacko Chakiath, Tom Diethe, Andrew Dippel, Gilad Kaplan, Rebecca Croasdale-Wood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPropensity for nonspecific binding—also known as polyreactivity—is a serious developability risk factor for biotherapeutic candidates. To minimize this risk, drug companies are increasingly relying on in silico tools utilizing machine learning methods, but developing these tools is challenging. For example, the available data often contains many closely related sequences originating from drug pipeline projects, which can introduce significant biases in the in silico models training and benchmarking, leading to poor generalizability on new data. We present here a workflow designed to diagnose and mitigate some of the problems associated with using pipeline data. The workflow is based on a custom cross-validation procedure that can evaluate model performance on unseen data in different contexts. As a demonstration of the workflow, we use it to train a model to predict variable heavy-chain only fragment antibodies (VHH) binding to baculovirus particles (BVP)—a widely used assay for nonspecific binding. Using descriptors based on computed protein structures, the workflow identifies several risk factors that correlate with higher polyreactivity levels.

Introduction

Biologic therapeutics are an extremely promising class of drugs, exhibiting unique combination of desirable properties such as high specificities and affinities, potent effector functions, and long half-lives. They are already successfully used to treat human disorders ranging from cancer to allergies and infection diseases.1–6

Despite their great potential, biotherapeutics’ path to patients is slow and expensive. One of the main reasons is their complex development process, containing multiple stages and possibilities for failure. Very few molecules possess the combination of biophysical properties—collectively known as developability—that makes them suitable for clinical development.7 Given the enormous time and resource investment needed to produce material and collect clinical and non-clinical data, there is a clear need to accelerate the process by quickly and efficiently filtering out sequences with undesirable developability profiles. This has led to a surge of proposals for in silico methodologies to screen large numbers of sequences and prioritize favorable candidates.8–14 Recent dramatic improvements in computational modeling tools, such as AlphaFold protein structure predictions15 and ESM family of protein language models (PLM),16 make the prospect of developability predictions much more realistic.

While there have been some notable successes in creating in silico developability prediction tools based on machine learning approaches,17–26 substantial obstacles remain. Models are often unreliable and fail to generalize or underperform on new data. Arguably one of the most significant problems is data quality, which affects all stages of model development and validation. Since obtaining developability data is expensive and time consuming, a standard industry practice is to use historical data. Thus, the data used to train machine learning models is often a collection of a small number of clusters of closely related sequences originating from a limited set of projects. These datasets are occasionally expanded by adding external data or by a concerted effort to measure a diverse set of sequences. Even with these steps, however, typical datasets covering developability properties are very limited in size and diversity compared to standard machine learning sets in other fields. This biases the model development process and limits the performance of the deployed models. Nevertheless, the extremely high price of failure in biopharma demands the use of all possible tools, no matter how imperfect they are.

Here, we propose a relatively simple workflow designed to mitigate some of the problems associated with using pipeline data to train machine learning models for developability. As a demonstration and proof of concept, we train a model for predicting nonspecific binding of variable heavy-chain only fragment antibodies (VHHs). VHHs, also known as nanobodies or a single-domain antibodies (sdAbs), are a unique class of antibody fragments derived from the heavy chain antibodies found naturally in camelids, such as camels and llamas.27–29 They have a single monomeric variable antibody domain and are structurally and functionally simpler than traditional antibodies consisting of two heavy and two light chains. While VHHs retain the high specificity typical for conventional antibodies, they offer several potential advantages. Their simple structure allows easier engineering and the ability to quickly generate and screen large libraries. They show high stability under extreme pH and temperature conditions, potentially leading to longer shelf-life.30–32 VHHs can be incorporated into more complex constructs to create multispecific biologics. Alternatively, when used alone, their small size makes them attractive for applications that require access to dense tissues or crossing biological barriers.

VHHs, however, often show a higher than tolerable level of nonspecific binding, at least compared to more conventional formats such as IgGs (Figure 1a). Nonspecific binding, also called polyreactivity, is the tendency toward off-target low-affinity binding. It is a developability risk and can be a significant liability during development and manufacturing.33–35 More importantly, antibodies with elevated levels of polyreactivity tend to manifest poor pharmacokinetics, which limits their clinical use.36,37 Thus, early screening using one or several assays to identify candidates with high nonspecific binding scores is a crucial part of the overall developability assessment.7,38,39 ELISA detection of binding to baculovirus particles (BVP) is commonly used for this purpose. Significantly, BVP scores have shown good correlation with clearance rate in mice.40,41 Recently, a high-throughput version of the BVP assay was developed, allowing much quicker data collection (see Methods). However, in the context of large-scale screening campaigns involving thousands or even millions of VHH sequences, even high-throughput assays introduce bottlenecks. Efficient in silico pipelines to flag sequences for unacceptable developability properties could help significantly accelerate the process.

It is generally accepted that polyreactivity of biologics is caused by positive charge in the variable region, especially the complementary-determining regions (CDRs), and in many cases can be connected to high isoelectric point (pI).42–48 However, real data points to a more complex picture—for example, some IgGs with a low pI exhibit high levels of polyreactivity (possibly due to strong hydrophobic or localized positive patches).46 Conversely, high pI IgGs are sometimes well-behaved, potentially because localized strong negative patches offset the effect of the overall positive charge. Due to these complexities, several groups have turned to machine learning models to predict nonspecific binding.47,49–54 While these efforts generally agree on the importance of charge and hydrophobicity, they utilize different sets of features. They also do not fully consider the effect of data quality and sequence similarity on model performance, both during training and testing. As we demonstrate in this paper, neglecting this issue leads to optimistically biased model estimates, which can distort the entire model selection procedure.

To mitigate these problems, we propose a machine learning workflow specifically designed to deal with the challenges of creating robust and generalizable in silico models using typical industry datasets. It includes data processing and custom cross-validation procedures to test the ability of the models to generalize to novel sequences. Specifically, we split the set of molecules into groups based on whether they are part of a larger sequence cluster or are relatively isolated sequences. We use these cluster-aware splits to assess the ability of the models to perform well both on diverse sets of candidates, which can be seen during a lead isolation (LI) campaign or sets with high degree of sequence similarity typically generated for lead optimization (LO) screens. The final estimate is an average over these distinct cases. Using this procedure, we can obtain more realistic estimates of a model’s performance on unseen data. This, in turn, allows us to compare and rank multiple models, and ultimately to select the modeling approach with best generalizability.

It is important to note that several prior antibody developability studies have recognized that sequence-related molecules should be separated during model development to reduce overfitting and improve generalization estimates. For example, Waight et al.22 used sequence-identity-constrained train/validation/test splits for IgG developability prediction. Our work builds on the same general concern, but extends it in several distinct directions. Rather than using sequence-aware partitioning only as a safeguard in a predictive modeling pipeline, we treat it as a central part of the workflow. Specifically, we use it to evaluate performance separately in LO-like and LI-like scenarios and discuss how these results can be weighted and combined, depending on the intended application. We further analyze how prediction quality decays with sequence distance, thereby defining a practical domain of applicability for the machine learning prediction. We also demonstrate how ignoring the sequence similarity during model validation and testing can produce a significant optimistic bias and distort the entire model selection process.

Results

We treat BVP silico modeling as a standard classification task for separating VHH sequences in two classes based on their score. We used a score of 10 (normalized to the background) as the threshold between the problematic (high BVP/positive) and acceptable (low BVP/negative) classes. This is a clear oversimplification, reducing the continuous BVP score to a dichotomous variable. At the same time, it offers two significant advantages. First, it mimics the decision-making process commonly used in real projects, dividing the sequences into groups with acceptable and unacceptable levels of nonspecific binding. Second, the assay shows significant variability, especially for high BVP levels, so reducing the continuous values to two discrete classes reduces the effects of the (likely strongly heteroscedastic) experimental noise.

Data

We compiled a data set of 1563 VHHs with BVP measured using the new high-throughput version of the assay (see Methods for details). After binarizing the BVP score, the data is almost perfectly split between the two classes, with a ratio of 0.52 to 0.48 high to low BVP molecules. To create this relatively large dataset, we had to collate points from more than a dozen projects. This is a standard industry approach, but—as discussed in the introduction-one which often produces datasets that are problematic from machine learning modeling point of view. As can be seen in Figure 1b, the project datasets have different size and VHH distributions. We used a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE)55 method to project the combined data in two-dimension sequence space (Figure 1c); there are clearly several clusters of different sizes formed by molecules with high sequence similarity. Even more problematic, the BVP classes follow the clustering pattern, with many of the clusters dominated by low or high BVP classes. This leads to two distinct but connected modeling problems. First, the cluster membership serves as a confounding variable that a model can use to distinguish the most imbalanced clusters without learning anything generalizable about the real drivers of nonspecific binding. This leads to the second potential problem—model performance can show a strong optimistic bias if sequences from the same clusters are split between the train and test datasets.

Descriptors

To train a machine learning method, we needed to map the VHH sequences to fixed-size numerical vectors encoding relevant information. We used physicochemical descriptors, selected for their interpretability. We note that alternative approaches using PLM embeddings to represent sequences have already been used to predict polyreactivity of biologics with some success.48,52,53 It allows us to validate the models against the current understanding of factors driving polyreactivity and at the same time can provide additional insights into these factors. While some of the descriptors we used only require sequence information, others rely on protein structures. To calculate these for all VHHs, we used AlphaFold2.15 Specialized commercial software packages such as MOE and Schrodinger were then used to extract physicochemical descriptors from the calculated structures (see Methods for details). To reduce the total number of features and thus the risk of overfitting, we also applied several feature selection procedures. Briefly, redundant descriptors are first removed by screening for strong correlations in the feature set. We also use a supervised feature selection, by applying a maximum relevancy minimum redundancy method.56,57 The optimum number of features to keep was selected alongside other ML model parameters in a hyperparameter tuning step.

In Figure S1, we show plots of BVP scores against some common biophysical predictors that have been previously associated with high polyreactivity, such as pI, hydrophobicity, CDR3 length, Arginine counts, bulkiness, taken from Sakhnini et al.48 and Harvey et al.51 Consistent with earlier results, these show only weak—although highly significant—correlations, but we also noted that in some cases the high significance seems mostly driven by a few outlier points. Thus, a simple single-feature screening for nonspecific binding will be a very crude and inefficient approach, and more sophisticated machine learning tools are needed.

Cluster-aware cross-validation strategy

Cross-validation is commonly used to develop and validate machine learning models, especially on relatively small datasets. However, due to the issues stemming from the clustering tendency of the data explained above, a strategy beyond random cross-validation is needed. We devised a procedure consisting of several steps. First, we split the data in 0.8:0.2 ratio (1252 to 311 sequences), while preserving the overall proportion of low to high BVP molecules. The bigger dataset is used exclusively for model training, selection, and hyperparameter tuning, while the other is used only at the very end, to validate and benchmark the model selected at the previous stage. In selecting the smaller set, we wanted to create a realistic challenge for the model, so it includes only sequences at least 10 Levenshtein distance away from the rest of the data (This distance threshold was recently used in Harvey et al.51). Using this diverse set of sequences, we assess the ability of the model to generalize outside its training domain, but still within some moderate sequence distance.

To make sure the model selected during the first stage is generalizable, we further split the training data based on clustering patterns evident in Figure 1c. Most of the clusters are formed by subsets of distinct projects. All the sequences are represented and clustered in the same sequence space. We utilized an agglomerative clustering algorithm and only considered a group of 20 or more sequences to be true clusters (see Methods for details). This led to 550 sequences split in a total of seven clusters: the VHHs in each of these are kept together during cross-validation rounds. The clusters provide a way to test the performance of the model on closely related sequences typically studied in the context of LO. The remaining of the training sequences (702 VHHs, from singleton and groups that have less than 20 members) are not assigned to any cluster. These wild-type or “unaffiliated” sequences—the majority of the VHHs—were randomly split into additional 10 groups, which are used to evaluate the model on diverse sets of sequences, like those seen during an LI campaign. The proportion of seven “true” clusters to 10 random groups is close to the ratio of clustered to wide-type sequences. (The general scheme can be modified and refined by adding weights to adjust the importance of LO or LI scenarios, depending on the intended application.) A similar—but simpler—clustering approach to model benchmarking was used in Waight et al.22

Using these 17 groups, we apply Leave-One-Group-Out Cross-Validation (LOGO CV), keeping one group at a time as a validation set and training on the rest. Since there is still potential for small “leakage” of information for sequences in clusters smaller than 20—which were not included in the LOGO scheme—in each CV round we removed from the training set sequences less than 10 Levenshtein distance to any VHHs of the test set. To obtain the final performance of a given model, we average the scores obtained over each cluster and random test group, thus balancing the estimates between the LI and LO contexts.

On the full cross-validation dataset, Gaussian naïve Bayes achieved AUROC 0.65, accuracy 0.65, MCC 0.29, F1 0.65, and recall 0.71. Logistic regression achieved AUROC 0.70, accuracy 0.70, MCC 0.29, F1 0.64, and recall 0.68. Random forest achieved AUROC 0.72, accuracy 0.69, MCC 0.31, F1 0.68, and recall 0.71. LightGBM achieved AUROC 0.71, accuracy 0.67, MCC 0.27, F1 0.65, and recall 0.68. The soft-voting ensemble, combining logistic regression, random forest, and LightGBM, achieved the highest overall cross-validation AUROC of 0.73, with accuracy 0.68, MCC 0.30, F1 0.66, and recall 0.70. XGBoost using one-hot-encoded sequences achieved AUROC 0.68, accuracy 0.65, MCC 0.30, F1 0.65, and recall 0.68. Logistic regression using mean-pooled ESM2 embeddings achieved AUROC 0.72, accuracy 0.64, MCC 0.30, F1 0.64, and recall 0.67.

Performance was generally lower for closely related sequence clusters than for wild-type sequences, particularly for Gaussian naïve Bayes, random forest, LightGBM, the ensemble model, and one-hot-encoded XGBoost. The ESM2 logistic-regression model showed more similar AUROC values between wild-type and clustered sequences, at 0.73 and 0.70, respectively.

On the independent test set, the ensemble model achieved AUROC 0.79, accuracy 0.72, MCC 0.46, F1 score 0.70, and recall 0.63.

Model selection using CV

Using the CV procedure outlined above, we train and compare several commonly used models, including bagging and boosting tree-based models, Logistic Regression, and Naïve Bayes classifiers. We combine them with a few different supervised and unsupervised feature selection procedures. It is important to note that both the unsupervised and supervised feature selection steps were treated as part of the machine learning pipeline and thus performed inside the CV loop. This avoids potential “sharing” of information between the folds and includes the variability originating from these steps in the full performance estimates of the pipelines.

As a primary metric, we use the area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC), since it relies on class probabilities and is thus more sensitive than metrics that depend only on a well-calibrated decision boundary. As secondary metrics, we use accuracy, F1 score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). Table 1 shows the performance of several of the top algorithms. For each individual model, we performed hyperparameter tuning using Bayesian optimization (see Methods for details). The top three models show similar results, and none of them is consistently better than others across all or even most of the CV groups. As can be seen in Figure 2a, a model—for example Logistic Regression—can be the best one for a group and the worse one for another. In a situation like this, selecting a single best model is often not the optimal strategy. Instead, combining several of the top models can lead to better results. Indeed, as can be seen in Table 1 and Figure 2, a simple model averaging the class probabilities of three models—Random Forest, LightGBM, and Logistic Regression—leads to the best performance. The overall improvement is modest and not significant for Random Forest and LightGBM (Wilcoxon signed-rank test p-value: Ensemble-GausianNB − 1.5 × 10−5; Ensemble-LogReg − 0.02; Ensemble-RF − 0.31; Ensemble – LightGBM − 0.11). The main benefit of using an ensemble model is stabilizing the predictions across the CV groups; although averaging is the best strategy (in terms of AUROC) for only three groups, it is never the worst-performing model (Figure 2a). The performance of the ensemble model can be summarized in the confusion matrix, combining the predictions for each CV group (Figure S2a). The calibration curve shows the probabilities output by the final model are meaningful (Figure S2b), even though they are averages over the predictions of three distinct models, none of which is explicitly calibrated as part of the workflow.

For comparison, we also trained and benchmarked the same models utilizing two standard sequence-only representation approaches. One-Hot-Encoding (OHE) is a simple way to map the full sequence to a vector that uses 20-dimentional one-hot vectors to represent each residue. To make sure the full sequence vectors have a fixed length, we first numbered the sequences using IMGT scheme and represent the gaps by a vector of 20 zeros. The second representation uses ESM2—a general-purpose PLM—to generate sequence-level embeddings (See Methods for details).

As can be gleaned from Table 1, while structure-based representation leads to better overall results, both sequence-only approaches show some predictive power (for OHE and ESM2 we only show the top performing models). ESM2 results seem especially promising—they show similar performance for LI and LO scenarios, which suggests that this representation can better capture the differences between sequences within a few mutations away from each other—a case that is problematic for structure-based approaches. We also tested a larger ESM2 model with 650 M parameters, which showed a slightly better average performance but with much worse LI/LO discrepancy (Logistic Regression AUROC: 0.72/0.78/0.63), indicating overfitting when tested of random groups.

An interesting observation from Figure 2a and Table S2 is that performances compared over groups show distinct patterns in different scores. For example, despite close AUROC scores, F1 and MCC numbers for Cluster 1 and Cluster 5 differ drastically; note that the proportion of positive cases is similar for both groups (Table S2). Since F1 and MCC rely on a well-calibrated decision threshold whereas AUROC does not, this suggests that the trained model captures enough information to relatively accurately rank the sequences, even for clusters in which the default classification probability threshold (above 0.5 probability for class 1) is not appropriate. This is further confirmed by looking at the Spearman correlation between the probability of high BVP class from the model and the actual BVP value (see Table S2). Even for Cluster 2, predicted to be entirely in the high BVP class, the AUROC and the Spearman correlation show some ranking power.

A potential explanation is that while the same factors drive polyreactivity for all—or at least most—sequences and clusters, the exact impact of these factors can be modified by cluster-specific details (for example, distinct germlines specifying different framework regions (FR), not well-captured in the general model. In other words, the “baseline” nonspecific binding level may be cluster-specific and thus more difficult to predict than the relative ordering of the sequences in a novel cluster. As a corollary of this, the model can provide potentially useful ranking information even when it fails in the classification task. As can be seen in Table S2, MCC numbers change significantly from cluster to cluster and are zeroes for three clusters, while the Spearman correlation is almost always positive. The model shows complete lack of predictive power only for one cluster (Cluster 3).

Test data

So far, we have used the same training set data for model tuning and selection. While the use of different folds for training and testing prevents direct “leakage” of information, the repeated use of the same set of sequences can eventually introduce a significant optimistic bias. To objectively estimate the generalizability of the workflow, the model trained on the full training dataset was used to infer the BVP classes of the previously unseen test data. This set is composed of a diverse mix of VHH without members from any actual sequence clusters irrespective of their size (Figure 2b).

The performance of the trained model on the test set is given in Table 2. The results are similar or even slightly better than the LOGO CV results. Proximity to train data determines the performance on new sequences

As discussed above, even though the overall AUROC, MCC, and F1 scores indicate significant predictive power, for certain clusters of VHHs the model’s performance is much worse. In some cases, the model AUROC is significantly higher than 0.5, but the classification threshold is not optimal. This can be especially problematic in the context of an LO campaign, since the prediction errors within a set of closely related sequences are not independent.

What are the factors determining the reliability of the model for different sequence subsets? This question goes beyond the uncertainties of individual predictions, which are generally well captured by the class probabilities, but only for clusters for which the model shows good overall performance. While the answer to this is likely complex, a plausible candidate for an important factor affecting model performance is the distance between the training and test data. A natural hypothesis is that sequences far away from the training data are more likely to be misclassified.

As a quick test of this hypothesis, we plotted the prediction scores for each group based on its distance to the training data (Figure S4). Overall proximity was quantified using the first decile of Levenshtein distances between the group/cluster and the rest of the data. As can be seen in Figure S4, there seems to be correlation between the distance and both MCC and AUROC for the random groups, but the situation is more ambiguous for actual sequence clusters, where cluster-specific factors can be more important.

To further test the idea, we ran a computational experiment. Since we already validated the model workflow using both LOGO CV and an independent test set, we freeze its general architecture and all hyperparameters. Then we do a round of Leave-One-Sequence-Out-Cross-Validation (LOSO CV) over all sequences. This leads to results with a strong optimistic bias; first, because of the presence of closely related sequences (the main reason we resorted to LOGO CV in the previous sections), and second, because the model architecture was optimized over a subset of the same data. However, we focus on relative performance, not on absolute numbers, thus sidestepping this issue.

At the end of the LOSO CV, for each molecule of the full dataset (a total of 1563 VHHs) there is a prediction based on all other sequences. We order the VHHs by the Levenshtein distance to their closest neighbor (Figure 3a). We then bin the sequences within a certain range of distances (from 0 to 5, from 5 to 10, and so on) and calculate the performance of the machine learning workflow over each bin. This allows us to study how performance changes with the distance to the closest training points. As shown in Figure 3b, the scores are stable for sequences within 20 edit distance to the closest neighbor and drop for more isolated sequences. The drop is steep for F1 and MCC scores, which may be affected by the changes in the proportion of positive class sequences in each bin. However, the degradation is also visible in the AUROC scores and in the calibration curves as well—compare the curves in Figure 3c,d. It is important to recognize that the number of sequences in these groups becomes quite small as the distance increases, so the estimates are noisy. The trend, however, appears to be robust. Another important point is that this shows the aggregate performance of multitude of different—although very similar—models created during the LOSO CV procedure. Feature importance

The complex architecture of the final model makes it difficult to apply interpretability tools directly. Nevertheless, some insights can be gleaned by inspecting the constituent models. These models’ feature importances can uncover important factors leading to elevated levels of nonspecific binding. Conversely, examining features that correspond to known drivers of polyreactivity helps build confidence in the modeling results.

Of the models in the ensemble, Logistic Regression is the most straightforward to interpret; the absolute value of a feature’s coefficient encodes it importance. Despite the limitation of the linear model, it still provides a useful way to discover important factors driving high BVP levels. A bar chart of the 15 largest (in absolute terms) coefficients is shown in Figure 4a. Eight of these—including the five most important—are related to hydrophobicity, with six corresponding to charge. The direction of the effects is generally as expected, with higher hydrophobicity and positive charge being positively correlated with high BVP scores, while higher negative charge suppresses BVP. The combination agrees with the current understanding of general causes of polyreactivity, but with a strong tilt toward hydrophobicity as a driver, which may be specific to VHHs. Custom CV vs random CV

We justified the use of a custom CV procedure with the presence of clusters of closely related sequences, the BVP scores in which tend to be strongly correlated. Let us now examine the consequences of using conventional CV, in which the molecules are assigned to train or validation folds randomly, without any regard for their similarity. As shown in Table S4, the random CV over the training data (using 20 splits repeated 2 times) leads to a much higher scores due to the “leakage” of information from very similar sequences in different folds.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this, if it is consistent with the way the deployed model will be used. However, we expect the in silico pipeline to be mostly used on sequences from new projects and targets, and thus relatively distant from the training data. This is the performance that should be benchmarked and optimized. While our test data is not completely separated from the training set and thus cannot be fully representative for sequences from truly novel campaigns, it can be used to study the issues we are exploring.

To estimate the effect of “leakage” on model selection and hyperparameter tuning, we repeated the procedure, but this time guided by the random CV results. A similar ensemble of three models, Logistic Regression, LightGBM, and ExtraTrees, came at the top. Interestingly, the model results on the same test set as before, which specifically excluded any sequence cluster members and is thus immune to “leakage,” are on par with the model selected using LOGO CV (but, of course, significantly lower than the random CV benchmarks). The robustness of the model selection procedure is likely due to the composition of the training data, containing many “wild type” sequences. This diversity prevents the selection of extremely overfit models that focus on the “easy” cases. This can be demonstrated by performing another numerical experiment: starting only with the sequences clusters as a training data, we gradually add to it some of the more diverse pool of molecules and run standard random CV over each iteration, while keeping the same test set as before. We repeat this 10 times to account for the randomness in the process. For each version of the training set, we fit a Random Forest model (without any hyperparameter tuning, to reduce computational cost). As shown in Figure 5, a rise in the proportion of the diverse sequences in the training data leads to a decrease of random CV scores of the model (due to the reduced optimistic bias from “leakage”) and an increase in test set performance (as the model is becoming more generalizable). Thus, if the dataset has a low sequence diversity, a random CV can be very misleading and favor models that overfit the training data by learning the clusters assignments. This leads to a large gap between CV and test data results and models with poor generalizability. The effect becomes less pronounced as the overall diversity of the data increases (in our training dataset, only 550 out of 1252 of the molecules—around 44%—are in sequence clusters), although it still leads to a noticeable optimistic bias in the random CV model scores. Discussion

In this work, we presented a machine learning workflow for predicting nonspecific binding of VHHs, measured by ELISA BVP assay. High levels of nonspecific binding can affect a molecule’s manufacturability and lead to problematic in vivo behavior, such as faster clearance. ELISA BVP is a standard assay used to screen for problematic sequences. In silico tools to flag high BVP binding can increase throughput of biopharmaceutics pipeline and reduce some of the developability risks inherent in working with these molecules.

The outcome of our work can be summarized in two main points. First, we explored and quantified the importance of sequence distance and diversity during developing and testing machine learning models for developability. The presence of clusters of closely related sequences—common in industry datasets based on pipeline projects—leads to significant “leakage” of information when using standard model benchmarking and selection approaches. This, in turn, produces misleading results and biases the training process by favoring overfitting.

Second, we proposed a cluster-aware cross-validation workflow that can be used to mitigate this problem. We tested the workflow on a sizable dataset—more than 1500 sequences—of VHHs, about half of which exhibit problematic levels of BVP. The dataset—collected using a novel high-throughput version of BVP—exhibits many of the problems associated with similar industry efforts. We used the custom cross-validation procedure that provided robust estimates of models’ performance on unseen data in different contexts. This procedure was used to benchmark, compare, and tune a suite of standard models, including Logistic Regression, Naïve Bayes, and tree-based approaches such as Random Forest and LightGBM. We also tested averaging of several models; this improved the results, providing more stable performance across groups with different sequence similarity. Averaging the predictions of hyperparameter-tuned Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and LightGBM gave the best results. Benchmarks obtained using the custom procedure are comparable to the model performance on a test set composed of diverse but only moderately novel group of sequences.

While the importance of avoiding overfitting caused by data leakage from sequence clusters has been discussed earlier, we believe our work is the first to propose a detailed workflow to mitigate the problem. The workflow is flexible and can be modified depending both on the available data and the intended use cases. The actual implementation of the proposed approach depends on specifying several numbers: the BVP classification threshold, the clustering threshold, the minimum size of a cluster. The BVP threshold must be consistent with real project decision-making and is not really a modeling choice. The other ones, in contrast, are not meant to be fixed, but should be adjusted depending on specifics of the dataset, the modeling approaches, and—most importantly—the intended uses of the trained model.

Generally, the clustering distance should be determined based on the expected sequence diversity of the data on which the model will be deployed and its distance to the training set. If the clustering threshold is too low, the results would be highly optimistic (similar to random CV) and potentially misleading about the performance on more distant and/or diverse datasets. Conversely, if the choice is too strict, the model scores would be too pessimistic. This is demonstrated in Figure S5, where we show the results of LOGO CV runs using the proposed strategy for different thresholds, compared with the performance on the fixed test set (constructed using Levenshtein distance of 10), which can be considered a stand-in for the future sequences. As the clustering threshold changes, the LOGO CV results show either optimistic or pessimistic bias compared to the test set. It should be noted that the test performance is higher than the LOGO CV corresponding to Levenshtein distance of 10 because it is obtained using the optimized ensemble model, while a simple Random Forest was used for the rest of the estimates.

As a more general consideration, the threshold should also be format-specific and probably significantly higher for formats with longer sequences such as IgGs.

The minimal cluster size is less important and was selected as the lowest viable size to produce realistic aggregate scores. It can be increased in case there are more medium-size clusters.

We further investigated the machine learning workflow by exploring the effect of sequence distance on prediction accuracy. For sequences far away from the training data (beyond edit distance 20), the model loses most—albeit not all—of its predictive ability. This suggests a natural domain of applicability of the approach and implies that diversity of the data is the most important condition for training generalizable in silico methods. This was further studied by assessing the performance of models trained on datasets with increasing degree of diversity.

The poor performance on Set 3 provides an important result on the limits of generalizability of the trained model. Since this set is the most distant from the training data and comes from a different discovery platform, it serves as a practical test of the out-of-domain generalization. The near-random AUROC indicates that the model trained on the existing data has a limited applicability domain and that sequence-distance-aware validation is essential to avoid overstating model performance on truly novel campaigns. Targeted data collection emphasizing sequence diversity (potentially combined with domain adaptation methods) will likely improve the performance on such novel data sets.

To extract some insights from the machine learning workflow, the Logistic Regression model was examined in detail. Hydrophobicity and charge features dominate the most important model coefficients, with some bias toward the former. Confirming and extending earlier findings, our model suggests that both sizable surface-exposed hydrophobicity and positive charge patches are significant risk factors, providing two independent, but potentially interacting, routes to elevated levels of BVP binding.

With respect to the limitations of the proposed workflow, while the overall performance of the model will be acceptable in many cases, the error rate even for the data sets near the training data is still relatively high. As discussed above, the performance degrades for sequences progressively dissimilar to the training set. Especially in the context of a real screening campaign, careful analysis of the desired balance between Type I and Type II errors is required. Adjusting the classification threshold offers a practical way to tune the trade-offs, but further performance improvements seem both highly desirable and possible. However, this could be limited by at least three factors: 1) the quality of the structures used to generate the descriptors, 2) the ability of the features to encode the relevant information, and 3) the availability of data covering the relevant parts of sequence space. The first two problems may be partially addressed by developing and deploying machine learning frameworks based on PLM embedding from general protein models or specialized antibody embeddings.

Materials and methods

Data collection

To assess antibody nonspecific binding behavior, a slightly modified version of the BVP ELISA41 was developed and optimized for automation. Briefly, 384-well ELISA plates (Nunc Maxisop) were coated overnight at 4°C with a 1% BV suspension in 50 mM sodium carbonate (pH 9.6). The next day, the wells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with blocking buffer (PBS pH 7.2 with 0.5% bovine serum albumin) for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBS. Antibody samples and controls were tested at a single concentration of 15 μg/mL in quadruplicate, enabling streamlined, automatable screening of large antibody panels. NIP228 (low binder) and IL7010252 (high binder) formatted as hIgG1 served as negative and positive controls, respectively. Antibody samples were diluted to 15 µg/mL in blocking buffer, added to the plate, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Plates were washed three times with PBS, and anti-human IgG (Fc-specific)-peroxidase antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A0170) at 1:5000 dilution in blocking buffer, was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 1 h at RT followed by three washes with PBS. Finally, 3,3,’5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine substrate (SureBlue Reserve, KPL #5120–0081) was added to each well and incubated for 3 min. The reactions were stopped by adding 50 µL of 0.5 M sulfuric acid to each well. The absorbance was read at 450 nm on a 96-well plate reader (Envision). The BVP scores and plate binding were determined by normalizing absorbance to control wells with no test antibody. To separate the molecules in low and high BVP binding classes, we used a threshold of 10 normalized score.

Features

The structure-based features for each molecule are automatically computed by the AstraZeneca internal in silico developability pipeline (InSiDe). The sequences are processed from an input CSV file, and the variable regions (Fv or VHH) are modeled using AlphaFold2 multimer. The top‑ranked model is used as input for a range of software (MOE 2024:06, Schrodinger 2024-4-js-aws Bioluminate, Camsol 2.1-foss-2019a-Python-3.7.2) to compute structural descriptors at pH6. Sequence descriptors are calculated in parallel (OASis humanness scoring, sequence liabilities and motifs of interest). The InSiDe pipeline outputs a CSV with all features for each input sequence.

While MOE outputs a relatively small number of descriptors (<200), Schrodinger generates significantly more (>500), many of them focused on specific regions such as CDR H1 or FR3. To reduce the overall number of features and thus decrease both computational cost and the risk of overfitting, we use only high-level descriptors, covering the charge, hydrophobicity and aggregation properties of the entire VHH or the CDR and FR; thus, all features specific to a particular CDR or FR region were excluded. Combining these criteria and removing features that are constant led to a total of 289 features (the full list is provided in Table S5).

For the OHE representations, we used python packages anarcii (2.0.2) and abnumber (0.4.4) to number the VHH sequences using IMGT scheme with a maximum of 151 residues. Each actual residue was represented with a 20-dimensional vector with only one non-zero element, while the gaps were filled with 20-dimensional vector of zeros (for a total of 3020 dimensions).

To generate the ESM2 embeddings, we used 150 M parameter model with mean pooling, leading to a 640-dimensional vector.

Sequence similarity, clustering and projections

Sequence similarity was assessed in Python (3.11.8) using Levenshtein package (version 1.5.2) to calculate the edit Levenshtein distance. Clustering of the sequences was performed using Scikit-Learn (1.5.2) Agglomerative Clustering algorithm on the edit distance matrix with complete (maximum) linkage. The distance defining a cluster was set to 20, which identifies as clusters most of the LO projects in the data. The results were visualized using the tSNE method [Scikit-Learn (1.5.2)], using the precomputed Levenshtein distance as an input, and with “perplexity” set to 20.

Model development

Machine learning models were trained and tested in Python (3.11.8) using the Scikit-Learn (1.5.2), LightGBM (4.5.0), XGBoost (2.0.3) packages. For hyperparameter tuning we used Hyperopt (0.2.7). The final features selected for each model are provided in the Supplemental (Table S6).

For feature selection, we utilized feature-engine package (1.8.3). To reduce the number of features, first we used the unsupervised feature-engine method SmartCorrelatedSelection(). This method selects one from a group of correlated features, keeping the one with the highest variance. The supervised MRMR method was used before the actual model. MRMR uses Maximum Relevance Minimum Redundancy framework. It starts with a single most relevant feature (estimated using mutual information or correlation with the target variable) and then adds features one by one until a pre-determined number of features is reached. At each step MRMR selects the features with the highest importance score. The score is based on the difference or ratio between relevance and redundancy measures. Redundancy measures the new information a feature brings to the set of already selected features.
