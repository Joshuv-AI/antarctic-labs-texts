Factors influencing engagement in oral cancer prevention activities among dental students and professionals in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mosa A. Shubayr, Mohammed M. Al Moaleem, Seham A. Hakami, Khulud N. Khalufi, Reham Mousa Daghriri, Ahmed M. Bokhari, Anwar Shawqi Alhazmi, Ali Hassan Farsi, Majed Ahmed Adawi, Hussain Hassan Nahari, Afrah H. Hattan, Alla T. Alsharif, Nassreen H. Albar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral cancer is a pressing health concern in the Saudi Arabia (SA), with rising incidence and mortality rates. Despite the efforts in raising awareness, gaps persist in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to oral cancer prevention (OCP) among dental health professionals. This study aimed to identify the key factors influencing engagement in oral cancer prevention activities among dental students and professionals in Saudi. It examines the impact of socio-demographic characteristics and assesses their KAP related to oral cancer prevention.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2023 and February 2024 using a pre-validated online questionnaire distributed among oral health providers across various regions and sectors in SA. The survey included questions about participants’ KAP toward OCP. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were used to analyze the data.ResultsOut of 400 oral health providers invited, 157 completed the survey. The findings showed a discrepancy between knowledge and practice. While awareness of OC was generally high, this did not consistently lead to favourable attitudes or effective practices. The engagement in OCP activities was significantly associated with good knowledge, practice and favourable attitude scores. Profession significantly influenced participation, with clinicians (P = 0.013, OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.026–0.653) and those in administrative roles (P = 0.030, OR = 0.101, 95% CI: 0.013–0.804) both less likely to participate compared to dental students. Knowledge was a significant predictor (P = 0.002), with providers demonstrating poor knowledge being far less likely to participate (OR = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.008–0.339).ConclusionThe study identified a critical need for enhancing OCP education and training among oral health providers in SA. The findings indicate that continuous, targeted, and culturally sensitive training could bridge the existing KAP gap, potentially leading to improved early detection and management of OC, thereby reducing its morbidity and mortality rates in the region.

Introduction

Oral cancer (OC), which includes cancer of the lip and oral cavity, is an uncommon malignancy in Western Countries, while being one of the most prevalent malignancies in certain high-risk areas of the world [1, 2]. In Gulf Cooperation Council countries, where there is an increasing exposure to various risk factors for OC such as tobacco usage, alcohol consumption, poor diet and human papillomavirus at an early age, there is an anticipated increase in both the incidence and mortality rates of the disease [3]. Saudi Arabia (SA) was identified as having higher OC age standardized incidence rate (8.4 per 100,000 people) and mortality rate (4.2 per 100,000 people), with males being more at-risk compared to females [4, 5]. Both incidence and mortality rates are projected to increase by 2-folds (195%/197%) by 2040, with most cases observed in individuals over 50 [4].

Although OC has a poor prognosis, with overall 5-year survival rates as low as 40% [6, 7], it is largely preventable since most of the different risk factors identified are behaviours that increase the likelihood of the disease. Additionally, if diagnosed in the early stages, survival rates can exceed 80% [8, 9]; however, > 50% of OC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, as many patients remain asymptomatic and delay seeking medical support until noticeable symptoms appear [10]. In SA, knowledge and awareness of OC are low among the public [11–13] and only 12% have heard about OC from their dentists [12, 14]. Thus, proper examination and prompt clinical recognition of potentially malignant lesions in the head and neck are crucial factors influencing prognosis [15].

Despite efforts made by the Saudi governments to raise awareness of OC in the general population, the percentage of patients seeking medical attention with advanced-stage diseases has not significantly changed. Unlike other frequent cancers (i.e., cervical, breast or colorectal cancer), a standardised population-wide screening program for OC is not considered cost-effective and, as such, cannot be recommended [16]. Hence, opportunistic screening for oral mucosal lesions in general dental practice could contribute to reducing diagnostic delay in countries where dental practice attendance is regular. Therefore, oral health care professionals (OHP) serve as the frontline for primary and secondary prevention. Knowing the risk factors, educating patients, knowing the oral potentially malignant diseases, performing adequate oral examination, and referring patients when suspicious lesions detected, are part of the OHP daily tasks [17, 18].

In SA, some research indicates that dental practitioners may not effectively detect OC in its early stages due to unfavourable attitudes and inadequate knowledge of the symptoms. Many surveyed dental practitioners express a need for further training in OC prevention and diagnosis to enhance the identification of cases, particularly in early stages [19, 20]. However, a study by Kujan et al. found that 87% of undergraduate dental students in SA felt confident in performing oral examinations to detect signs of head and neck cancer [21]. Despite this confidence, research in Jeddah revealed suboptimal levels of knowledge about OC among both dental students and dentists [11]. In Riyadh, 87% of dental students felt confident in conducting systematic oral examinations to identify changes indicative of oral malignancy, and 57% had encountered the use of OC diagnostic aids [14]. However, 37% of respondents felt inadequately trained to provide advice on tobacco and alcohol cessation. In Jazan, survey findings suggest that oral health practitioners lack experience in prevention and early detection methods for OC, despite the province’s high prevalence of the disease among residents [22]. Yet, OC awareness studies among dental students and professionals in Saudi are limited to specific regions within the country, highlighting the need for comprehensive nationwide research to better understand and address gaps in knowledge and training across the nation. No study has yet investigated the impact of participation by dental students and professionals in OC prevention activities on enhancing KAP, nor have any explored whether socio-demographic variables influence their engagement in such events. Thus, this study aims to identify the key factors influencing engagement in oral cancer prevention activities among dental students and professionals in Saudi. It examines the impact of socio-demographic characteristics and assesses their KAP related to oral cancer prevention.

Materials and methods

Study design, sample size and recruitment

An online survey method was used to conduct a cross-sectional study between November 2023 and February 2024. SA was the context of this study, where a convenience sample was recruited from dental students and practicing dentists across all regions and involving both private and public sectors. According to the latest data provided by the MOH in 2022, the total number of dentists was 22,739 [23]. These dentists were distributed across 13 administrative regions within SA.

A sample size of 379 respondents was targeted based on prior research and the estimated population size by using the Qualtrics sample size platform, to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (REC-45/05/890) at Jazan University.

Data collection instrument and process

A predefined and validated English questionnaire survey of KAP of OC among oral health providers was used [22, 24]. The questionnaire was modified to meet the context of SA and study’s objectives without compromising the original questionnaire. Two dental public health professors at the College of Dentistry at Jazan University reviewed the questionnaire for content and clarity. The questionnaire was then pretested among 20 selected participants to assess validity and reliability (Cronbach α = 0.79). The final version of the self-administrated questionnaire included 28 close-ended questions on KAP of Oral cancer prevention (OCP).

The first part of the questionnaire included questions about socio-demographic, i.e., age, gender, nationality, education level, and other practice-related questions, followed by questions about OCP knowledge. Fourteen statements were used to assess knowledge by using items like: “Are tobacco and alcohol the only etiological factors for OC?”; “Do you know the symptoms of OC?”; and “Are early OC lesions always symptomatic?” Correct responses received one point, with knowledge scores ranging from 0 to 13. The cut-off of 7.8 points represented 60% of the total mean of knowledge scores; < 8.4 represented poor knowledge and ≥ 7.8 represented good knowledge about OC.

In the third part of the questionnaire, respondents were asked about their attitudes toward OCP. Participants rated seven statements using “yes” and “no” responses. The participant’s attitude was assessed by using items such as: “I would like more information or training on OCP” and “It is a waste of time to educate the patients to quit their habits as they always decline to follow.” Responses were scored on a binary scale, with a value of one indicating favorable attitudes and zero indicating unfavourable attitudes. The maximum score for the attitude scale was six, with higher scores indicating more favorable attitudes toward OCP. The cut-off of 3.6 points represented 60% of the total attitude mean scores; < 3.6 was set as negative attitude and ≥ 3.6 as a positive attitude.

The final part of the survey included five questions about OCP practices. Such questions included: “Do you examine patients’ oral mucosa routinely?”; “Do you record tobacco and alcohol use in the patients’ personal history?”; and “Do you practice complete oral cavity examination besides palpating lymph nodes routinely on patients?”. The response option was “yes” or “no.” The maximum score for this scale was five, with higher scores indicating a higher frequency of practice. The cut-off of 3 represented 60% of the total mean; < 3 was set as poor practice and ≥ 3 as good practice.

The survey was carried out using a web-based Google Form to enhance accessibility for participation. The online survey link was disseminated through social media (WhatsApp, Facebook, and Twitter) and via e-mail among a convenience sample of oral health providers. The survey was open for Three months and the anonymity of respondents was maintained throughout the process. The post specified that the study targets all oral health providers who work in public and private. The survey included information on the research team and study aims. Before starting the study, participants gave consent by clicking on a button after the consent paragraph with information on the voluntary nature of the study, risks and benefits of participating, and procedures for maintaining confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

The questionnaire was pre-coded for entry into the database from a coding sheet. Descriptive statistics, such as percentages and means, were utilized to provide an overview of each variable. Cross-tabulation was formed to examine factors associated with KAP scores, and the significance level was set at 0.05. Normality was assessed, and data were reported using the median and interquartile range (IQR). Non-parametric tests were used due to the non-normal distribution of certain variables. Logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the factors associated with the practice of OCP in the past year. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the strength of associations.

Results

Of the 400 oral health providers who were invited to participate, 157 completed the questionnaire, representing a response rate of 39.2%. The study involved participants to explore characteristics related to oral health professionals and their engagement in OCP activities (Table 1). The study included participants with a median age of 26.3 years (IQR: 25). The sample consisted of 80 males (51.0%) and 77 females (49.0%). Most participants were Saudi nationals (82.2%), while 17.8% were non-Saudi. In terms of professional roles, 45 participants were dental students (28.6%), 97 were dental clinicians (61.8%), and 15 held administrative roles (9.6%). Geographically, the participants were distributed across regions, with 28.0% from the Southern region, 19.1% from the Western region, 18.5% from the Central region, 17.8% from the Eastern region, and 16.6% from the Northern region.

Regarding their ability to speak Arabic, 75.2% of the participants had native fluency, 17.2% were proficient, 3.1% were conventional, and 4.5% had basic fluency. The majority had ten or fewer years of clinical experience (94.3%), while only 5.7% had more than ten years. A large proportion of the sample interacted with patients (84.7%) and only 36.3% had been involved in OC prevention activities in the past year. In the referral pathway for suspected OC patients, 28.0% of participants indicated oral medicine, 19.1% chose general practitioners, 18.5% opted for plastic surgery, 17.8% suggested ENT specialists, and 16.6% referred patients to maxillofacial surgeons.

Table 2 on oral health providers’ knowledge about OC shows that the majority understood OC (OC) risk factors (88.5%). Despite this, 69.4% still inaccurately perceive tobacco and alcohol as the sole causes of OC. While 79.0% felt confident in identifying OC symptoms, 75.2% acknowledged that OC is symptomatic at an early stage. The lateral border of the tongue was identified as a common site of OC by 77.7% of participants. However, 51.6% correctly disagreed with the misconception that OC cannot be diagnosed in its early stages, and 59.9% did not believe OC is a hereditary disease. Most of the participants recognized advanced age as a risk factor (77.1%) and were familiar with early detection procedures (62.4%). Regarding prevention, 75.8% agreed that OC is preventable. Additionally, 87.9% belief that smokeless tobacco-related lesions could resolve upon discontinuation. A significant portion (91.7%) recognized that a hard, painless, fixed lymph node is characteristic of OC metastasis. Early detection was seen as crucial, with 89.8% believing it improves the 5-year survival rates. In terms of overall knowledge, 76.4% of participants shows good knowledge, while 23.6% showed poor knowledge. The mean knowledge score across all participants was 10.2 (SD: 2.20).

OCP: Oral Cancer Prevention

The attitude among oral health providers regarding OCP and detection reflects a mix of confidence and perceived gaps in training (Table 2). While 46.5% feel adequately trained to provide alcohol cessation education, a larger portion (65.6%) is comfortable performing lymph node palpation. A significant majority (86.6%) express a desire for more information or training on OC, indicating a strong interest in enhancing their capabilities in this area. However, only 54.1% believe they have sufficient knowledge concerning the prevention and detection of OC. Interestingly, 63.7% believed that patient education on habit cessation is ineffective. However, 91.1% strongly supported the referral of patients with suspected oral cancerous lesions to specialists. Overall, 59.2% of participants exhibited a favorable attitude toward OC education and prevention (score > 3.6), while 40.8% displayed an unfavourable attitude. The mean attitude score was 3.78 (SD: 1.47).

Table 2 also outlines the practices related to OC screening and examination among oral health providers. Regular examination of patients’ oral mucosa was practiced by 69.4%, while 68.8% screened the oral mucosa of high-risk patients. Documenting tobacco and alcohol use in personal history was less commonly practiced, with only 77.1% reporting this activity. Comprehensive oral examinations, including lymph node palpation, were performed by 67.5% of participants, while biopsies on patients with suspicious lesions were conducted by the same proportion (67.5%). Overall, 59.2% exhibited good practice, whereas 40.8% fell into the poor practice category aligning with the proportion that holds unfavourable attitudes. The mean practice score was 3.5 (SD: 1.45), showing a generally adequate level of practice but emphasizing the need for improvement in routine OC screening procedures.

Table 3 shows the results of the independent t-tests, Pearson correlation, and ANOVA tests used to determine if there was a significant difference in mean OCP KAP scores by gender, age, region, nationality, Profession, Patients’ interaction, experience and participation in OCP activities. The findings demonstrate significant variations in the KAP scores related to OCP among oral health providers, emphasizing the impact of these factors age was significant (p < 0.001), with knowledge scores (µ = 7.43, SD = 2.62). Regional differences (p = 0.023) did show some variation in attitudes, with participants from the East (µ = 3.93, SD = 1.54), North (µ = 4.00, SD = 1.72) and south (µ = 4.02, SD = 1.17) regions showing slightly higher scores. Patient interaction significantly impacted knowledge (P = 0.014) and practice (P = 0.005), with those who interacted directly with patients scoring higher in both knowledge (µ = 11.22, SD = 2.42) and practice (µ = 3.64, SD = 1.29). Clearly, participants engaged in OCP activities in the past year showed significant asscoation (p < 0.001) across all KAP domains, with knowledge scores (µ = 12.35, SD = 1.70), attitude scores (µ = 4.58, SD = 1.05), and practice scores (µ = 4.47, SD = 0.95).

A logistic regression analysis explored factors affecting participation in OCP activities among oral health providers in SA, considering gender, age, nationality, region, Profession, Patient’ interaction, KAP (Table 4). Profession significantly influenced participation, with clinicians (P = 0.013, OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.026–0.653) and those in administrative roles (P = 0.030, OR = 0.101, 95% CI: 0.013–0.804) both less likely to participate compared to dental students. Knowledge was a significant predictor (P = 0.002), with providers demonstrating poor knowledge being far less likely to participate (OR = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.008–0.339). Attitude had marginal significance (P = 0.056), where those with an unfavourable attitude had a reduced likelihood of participation (OR = 0.292, 95% CI: 0.083–1.034). Practice also significantly predicted participation (P < 0.001). Providers scoring poorly in practice were much less likely to participate (OR = 0.038, 95% CI: 0.009–0.154).

Discussion

This study investigated OC prevention KAP in dental students and professionals in SA and socio-demographic factors that may influence their engagement in OC prevention. A designed broad spectrum of dental healthcare providers, in both the public and private sectors, participated to comprehensively descibe the OCP within SA.

The participants have good levels of OC of awareness, demonstrating that they comprehensively understand the condition. This aligns with findings emphasising the importance of early detection and acknowledging various risk factors beyond tobacco and alcohol use [14, 25, 26]. Most respondents are aware of OC and familiar with its diverse risk factors. Professionals’recognitio of early symptoms and acknowledgement that early lesions might not always be symptomatic suggest a readiness to engage in early detection initiatives. However, the widespread misperception of OC’s non-preventability underscores a knowledge gap, potentially affecting preventive practices. This necessitates targeted educational interventions to address misconceptions and improve understanding of OC preventability, which could encourage oral health professionals to adopt more proactive screening and preventive measures [27, 28].

While most respondents feel inadequately trained to provide alcohol/tobacco cessation education, many expressesd confidence in performing lymph node palpation. This discrepancy highlights the necessity of comprehensive OCP training beyond diagnosis. The participants’ desire for more OC traning indicates a proactive stance toward enchancing knowledge and skills. However, uncertainty about the effectiveness of patient education on quitting destructive habits suggests a perceived challenge to changing patient behaviour, emphasising the need for effective communication. The consensus on referring patients with suspected OC lesions to specialists reflects a responsible approach to patient care, ensuring that patients receive appropriate and specialised evaluation [29, 30].

Most oral health providers engage in proactive OC detection, routinely examining patients’ oral mucosa and screening high-risk patients even witout routine examinations. The widespread recording of tobacco and alcohol use in patients’ histories is commendable as these are significant OC risk factors. The adoption of comprehensive oral examinations, including lymph node palpation and biopsy for suspicious lesions, by two-thirds of the respondents, reflects a thorough and methodical approach to early OC detection and diagnosis [17, 31].

The particpants had good perceived OC knowledge. This is consistent with previous research in Canada [32] and Kuwait [33]. However, it contradicts findings from B Alsaud [19, 22] in SA.

Interestingly, B Alsaud [19] in SA and Fotedar et al. [34] in India found that most participants had positive attitudes towards OCP contradicting this study’s observation of prevalent unfavourable attitudes and practices despite adequate knowledge. This study reveals a concerning disconnect in how this knowledge influences OCP attitudes and, more importantly, how it guides daily practice. This gap underscores the need for a more integrated approach to education, one that both imparts knowledge and fosters positive attitudes that translate into consistent, proactive OCP. Fuethermore, the particpants commonly select oral medicine and oral and maxillofacial surgery as preferred specialties for referring suspected OC patients, aligning with the results of previous studies [12, 22, 35].

This study illustrate the profound impact of demographic and professional factors on OCP-KAP among dental students and professionals. Of note is a significant association between age and improved OCP efforts, with older practitioners exhibiting scoring the highest across all knowledge domains. This is similar to [22, 36, 37] but cotrary to [38, 39]. This is possibly due to older practitioners’ accumulated years of experience, potentially yielding increased exposure to clinical cases and opportunities to engage in continuing education or professional development. Furthermore, older practitioners may have had more opportunities to observe the outcomes of OC providing practical insights.

Geographical variations play significant roles in shaping oral cancer prevention (OCP) practices among dental professionals [40]. Attitudes towards OCP slightly vary across different regions, suggesting that regional differences in oral health education, accessibility to OCP resources, or cultural influences on health practices can also impact how OCP is perceived and practised.

Further emphasizing the importance of engagement, participants who partook in OCP activities in the past year outperformed their counterparts across all KAP measures. This highlighted the critical role that active involvement in OCP activities plays in enhancing oral health providers’ proficiency in cancer prevention, suggesting that practical experience and continuous education are key to advancing OCP efforts.

This study could inform oral health providers’ OCP education, and resources allocation to improve practice. However, a low response rate led to a smaller sample than anticipated based on the number of dentists working in the SA (Ministry of Health, 2020). The low response rate (39.2%) is comparable to analogous studies in Palestine (39.2%) [38] and Malysia (41.7%) [41], and it is higher compared to a similar study from Yemen (27.6%) [42].

This study highlights an encouraging trend of commitment to OCP among oral health providers. However, the identified training gaps and scepticism towards patient education’s effectiveness highlight the need for enhanced education [26, 43, 44]. This could equip providers with comprehensive knowledge and skills, ranging from risk factor identification and patient education to diagnostic procedures. Additionally, strategies to improve patient compliance with preventive behaviours require integration with these programmes. Ultimately, developing a more informed and skilled workforce can aid early OC detection and management and improve patient outcomes.

This study shows a clear disparity between oral health providers’ extensive OC and their OCP attitudes and practices. Many of these providers possess substantial OC knowledge, indicating satisfactory education and awareness, which differs from previous findings [22, 26, 33]. However, many providers display unfavourable attitudes and suboptimal practices concerning OCP, signifying a disconnect between knowledge and its application in prevention. Investigating effective strategies to increase oral health providers’ engagement in OCP, as well as educational and training activities, is essential. Previous findings [22, 33] highlight the importance of continual OC risk factors and diagnosis training. This would equip oral health providers with knowledge to effectively implement OCP.

This study reinforces the need to incorporate more OCP education and training into dental school curricula to enhance prevention and improve OC treatment outcomes in SA [21]. Additionally, oral health providers should perform multiple OC screenings during training. The significance of regular training in OC screenings is emphasized as a way to improve OCP. Increased training may reduce OC’s prevalence in SA [45]. Comprehensive OC detection training through undergraduate and continued dental education is vital. Well-trained oral health providers are instrumental in early OC detection, which can improve survival and reduce morbidity [46, 47].

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, while we accounted for years of experience and involvement in previous cancer awareness activities in the analysis, other potential confounding variables, such as educational background, access to ongoing training, and work setting (public vs. private), were not explicitly addressed. These factors may influence the KAP of oral health providers regarding oral cancer prevention. Future studies should aim to include a more diverse and representative sample while examining these additional variables such as educational background, access to training and work setting to provide a fuller understanding of the factors that influence oral cancer prevention efforts.

Conclusion

This study provides crucial insights into OCP-KAP among dental students and professionals in SA. Despite respondents’ awareness of OC and its risk factors, gaps persist in attitudes and practices, underscoring a need for more comprehensive education and training. Participants who engaged in OCP activities in the past year exhibited superior knowledge, attitude, and practice scores, emphasising the importance of continuoal involvement in OCP. While most practitioners understand the importance of early detection, misconceptions and scepticism persist regarding the effectiveness of patient education and tobacco/alcohol cessation efforts. Regional and professional variations in attitudes and practices suggest that socio-demographic influences help shape OCP engagement. To bridge the gap between knowledge and effective application, comprehensive training programmes focussed on risk factor identification, patient education, and diagnostic skills are essential. Incorporating OCP education into dental school curricula and encouraging ongoing training and prevention activities could significantly improve early detection, reduce morbidity, and enhance patient outcomes in SA. Further research should investigate effective educational strategies to improve the participation of oral health providers in OCP.
