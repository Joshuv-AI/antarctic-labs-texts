Matroids are Equitable
Hannaneh Akrami, Roshan Raj, László A. Végh

Abstract
We show that if the ground set of a matroid can be partitioned into k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 bases, then for any given subset S S of the ground set, there is a partition into k k bases such that the sizes of the intersections of the bases with S S may differ by at most one. This settles the matroid equitability conjecture by Fekete and Szabó (Electron. J. Comb. 2011) in the affirmative. We also investigate equitable splittings of two disjoint sets S 1 S_{1} and S 2 S_{2} , and show that there is a partition into k k bases such that the sizes of the intersections with S 1 S_{1} may differ by at most one and the sizes of the intersections with S 2 S_{2} may differ by at most two; this is the best possible one can hope for arbitrary matroids. We also derive applications of this result into matroid constrained fair division problems. We show that there exists a matroid-constrained fair division that is envy-free up to 1 item if the valuations are identical and tri-valued additive. We also show that for bi-valued additive valuations, there exists a matroid-constrained allocation that provides everyone their maximin share.

Theorem 1.1

1.1
Applications in Fair Division

We now state some applications of our results to discrete fair division settings.
A standard discrete fair division instance denoted by
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N}\rangle
consists of a set
N=[n]
of
n
agents, a set
E
of
m
indivisible goods and
a valuation function
v_{i}\,:\,2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for each agent
i\in N
. A feasible allocation is a subpartition
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of the set
E
, where
A_{i}
denotes the bundle allocated to agent
i
, such that
\bigcup_{i\in N}A_{i}\subseteq E
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for any
i\neq j
. While in a general case, any subpartition of
E
into
n
bundles is a feasible allocation, in many scenarios, certain restrictions may be imposed. For example, it could be undesirable to waste items and leave them unallocated, or items might be conflicting and a certain combination of the items cannot be in the same bundle.

A line of literature, starting with Biswas and Barman
[
15
]
, examined matroid constrained allocations. Assume we are given a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
on the set of goods. In a feasible allocation, it is required that
(i)
all goods must be allocated and
(ii)
all bundles must be independent sets of
\mathcal{M}
. The latter can be reduced to the case when each bundle is a basis
[
23
]
.
We denote a matroid constrained fair division instance as
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
. We formally define the allocations that we consider feasible in this work in Definition
1.6
.

Definition 1.6
.

Given a matroid constrained fair division instance
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
,
{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is a
feasible allocation
, if

(i)

{A}
is a partition of
E
into
n
bundles, and

(ii)

for all
i\in N
,
A_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
is a basis of
\mathcal{M}
.

For ease of notation, we sometimes use
g
instead of
\{g\}
for good
g\in E
.

Definition 1.7

(EF1)
.

An allocation
{A}
is
envy-free up to one good (EF1)
, if for all agents
i,j
with
A_{j}\neq\emptyset
, there exists a good
g\in A_{j}
such that
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq v_{i}(A_{j}-g)
.

Given an allocation
{A}
, we say an agent
i

envies
agent
j
if
v_{i}(A_{i})<v_{i}(A_{j})
, and
i

EF1-envies
agent
j
if
v_{i}(A_{i})<v_{i}(A_{j}-g)
for all
g\in A_{j}
.

A valuation function
v\,:\,2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is called
additive
if
v(S)=\sum_{s\in g}v(g)
for every
S\subseteq E
. Further,
an additive valuation function
v
is
binary
if for all
g\in E
,
v(g)\in\{0,1\}
. If all agents have identical binary valuations, Theorem
1.1
easily implies the existence of an EF1 allocation in the matroid constrained fair division problem. It suffices to set
S
to be the set of items of value
1
for the agents. Then by Theorem

, one can (almost) equally partition

into
n
disjoint bases which results in an EF1 allocation if we allocate these bases to the agents arbitrarily.

Corollary 1.8

(of Theorem
1.1
)
.

Given a matroid constrained fair division instance
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
, if the valuations
v_{i}
are identical and binary additive, there exists a feasible EF1 allocation.

Extending the class of binary valuations, we
say that a valuation function
v\,:\,2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is

if there exists
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all
g\in E
,
v(g)\in\{a,b\}
. Similarly, a valuation function

is

if there exists
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all
g\in E
,
v(g)\in\{a,b,c\}
. Using Theorem
1.4
, we can also show the following.

Theorem 1.9
.

Given a matroid constrained fair division instance
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
, if the valuations
v_{i}
are identical and tri-valued additive,
there exists a feasible EF1 allocation.

We also consider the
maximin share fairness
notion.
The maximin share of an agent

is the maximum value she can guarantee for herself if she could divide the goods into
n
bundles and receive a minimum valued bundle.
Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of all feasible allocations. We adapt the notion of maximin share (MMS) to this setting and for a valuation function

\mu_{v}^{n}(E)\coloneqq\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\min_{j\in N}v(A_{j})\,.

An allocation

From Theorem

, we can infer the MMS values for binary additive valuations.

Corollary 1.11
.

Let
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
be a matroid constrained fair division instance such that the valuations
v_{i}
are binary additive, and assume
E
can be partitioned into the union of
n

i\in N
,
\mu_{i}^{n}(E)=\lfloor v_{i}(E)/n\rfloor
.

We show MMS allocations for the case of binary additive valuations; note that these are not required to be identical.

Theorem 1.12
.

Given a matroid constrained fair division instance
\langle N,E,(v_{i})_{i\in N},\mathcal{M}\rangle
, if all
v_{i}
’s are bi-valued additive valuations, then there exists a feasible MMS allocation.

1.2
Related literature

1.2.1
Matroid optimization

Equitability is intimately related to multiple famous conjectures in matroid theory; we review three interrelated families of such conjectures.

Basis exchange sequences.

Recall that by the strong bases exchange axiom of matroids, for any two bases
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}
, for any
e\in X\setminus Y
there is an
f\in Y\setminus X
such that
X-e+f,Y-f+e\in\mathcal{B}
.
White
[
51
]
considered the question of exchanging basis sequences. Let
{\cal X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
and
{\cal Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})
be two collections of
k
bases of a matroid
\mathcal{M}
. By a symmetric exchange from
{\cal X}
, we mean a symmetric exchange operation involving two bases in the collection. That is, for some
i,j\in[k]
,
{\cal X}^{\prime}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},X^{\prime}_{i},X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{j-1},X^{\prime}_{j},X_{j+1},\ldots,X_{k})
, where
X^{\prime}_{i}=X_{i}-e+f
and
X^{\prime}_{j}=X_{j}-f+e
for some
e\in X_{i}\setminus X_{j}
and
f\in X_{j}\setminus X_{i}
. One may ask when the collection
{\cal Y}
can be reached from
{\cal X}
by a series of symmetric exchanges. A trivial necessary condition is that the two sequence are
compatible
, i.e., each
e\in E
appears in the same number of bases in both collections. White conjectured that this condition is also sufficient.

Conjecture 1.13

(White, 1980)
.

The basis sequence
{\cal Y}
can be reached from the basis sequence
{\cal X}
by a series of symmetric exchanges if and only if
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
are compatible.

The conjecture remains open even for
k=2
, and even if
{\cal X}=(B_{1},B_{2})
and
{\cal Y}=(B_{2},B_{1})
, i.e., we need to swap two bases.
For this setting, the following stronger conjecture was first formulated by Gabow
[
28
]
, see also
[
21
,
52
]
, and Conjecture 15.9.11 in Oxley’s book
[
41
]
.

Conjecture 1.14

(Gabow, 1976)
.

Given two bases
B_{1}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}\}
and
B_{2}=\{b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{r}\}
of a matroid
\mathcal{M}
, there exist permutations
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
such that in the sequence
(b_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,b_{\sigma(r)},b^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}(1)},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}(r)})
, every set of
r
cyclically consecutive elements forms a basis.

Thus, this conjecture corresponds to a special case of White’s conjecture, but also stipulates that in this case, the length of the exchange sequence is
r
, the rank of the matroid.

Observe that both these conjectures imply equitability. Indeed, assume that for any basis pair
(B_{1},B_{2})
, one can reach
(B_{2},B_{1})
through an (arbitrary long) series of symmetric exchanges. Without loss of generality, assume
|B_{1}\cap S|<|B_{2}\cap S|
. Since the size of the intersection
|B^{\prime}_{1}\cap S|
changes by at most one in each iteration, the first basis pair
(B^{\prime}_{1},B^{\prime}_{2})
in the sequence with
|B^{\prime}_{1}\cap S|\geq|S|/2
will give an equitable partition.
We note that Gabow’s conjecture implies the existence of a matroid constrained EF1 allocation for identical additive valuations; we descibe the reduction in
Lemma

A.1
in the Appendix.

Partitioning into common independent sets.

The following natural question in matroid optimization unifies several known results, as well as includes multiple longstanding conjectures:

Given two matroids
\mathcal{M}_{1}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{1})
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{2})
on a common ground set
E
and the same rank
r
, such that the ground set can be partitioned into
k
bases both in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, what is the minimum number of common independent sets covering
E
?

The most interesting case is when the answer is
k
, i.e., there exists a partition of the ground set into
k
common bases. Classical examples include edge colourings of bipartite graphs and Edmonds’s disjoint arborescences theorem. Bérczi and Schwarcz
[
12
]
proved the exponential oracle complexity of deciding the existence of
k
disjoint common bases; we refer the reader to this paper for for background and known results. Equitability can be phrased as a special case of this question where
k=2
, the first matroid has rank
r
, and the second matroid is a partition matroid.
4
4

4

Let us define
T_{i}=S_{i}
if
|S_{i}|
is even and
T_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
with
|T_{i}|=|S_{i}|-1
if
|S_{i}|
is odd; let
R=E\setminus(\cup_{i}T_{i})
. We consider the partition matroid for the partition
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k},R)
, with
r(T_{i})=|T_{i}|/2
and
r(R)=r-\sum_{i}|T_{i}|/2
. It is easy to see that
\ell
-equitability is equivalent to partitioning the ground set into two common bases of these matroids.

Davies and McDiarmid
[
22
]
proved that if both matroids are strongly base orderable, and the ground set can be partitioned into
k
bases in each of the matroids, then it can also be partitioned into
k
common bases. Since laminar matroids are strongly base orderable, this implies
\ell
-equitability for any strongly base orderable matroids.

An intriguing conjecture of the above form is Rota’s basis conjecture: given a matroid of rank
r
whose ground set partitions into
r
disjoint bases
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{r}
, there always exists a transversal set of disjoint bases, i.e.,
B^{\prime}_{1},B^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,B^{\prime}_{r}
such that
|B_{i}\cap B^{\prime}_{j}|=1
for all
i,j\in[r]
. Note that this can be seen as a special instance of
r
-equitability.

Regarding the general question, Aharoni and Berger
[
1
]
conjectured that the ground set can be partitioned into
k
disjoint bases in both matroids, then it can be covered by at most
k+1
common independent sets. They showed the weaker bound that there always exist a partition into
2k
common independent sets. This in particular shows that in Rota’s conjecture, there the ground set can be covered by
\leq 2r
independent sets that intersect each
B_{i}
at most once. On the other hand, Bucić, Kwan, Pokrovskiy, and Sudakov
[
19
]
showed the existence of
(\frac{1}{2}-o(1))n
disjoint transversal bases, and
Pokrovskiy
[
42
]
gave an asymptotic version showing the existence of
(1-o(1))n
disjoint transversal independent sets of size
(1-o(1))n
.

A related notion of

was introduced by Aharoni, Berger, Kotlar, and Ziv
[
2
]
. Let us say that for sets
S,X\subseteq E
, and
\alpha\in[0,1]

X
represents
S

\alpha
-fairly
, if
|S\cap X|\geq\lfloor\alpha|S|\rfloor
, and

almost

-fairly

|S\cap X|\geq\lfloor\alpha|S|\rfloor-1
.

represents a partition of

(almost)
\alpha
-fairly, if it represents all of the sets (almost)

They formulated the following general conjecture.

Conjecture 1.15

(Aharoni, Berger, Kotlar, and Ziv, 2017)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
be two matroids on the same ground set
E
, and assume
E
can be partitioned into
k
independent sets in both matroids. For any partition of
E
, there exists a common independent set
X
in the two matroids that represents this partition almost
1/k
-fairly.

Assume
\ell
-equitability holds for a matroid
\mathcal{M}
such that the ground set is the disjoint union of two bases of
\mathcal{M}
. Then, it follows that every partition into
\ell+1
parts has a fair representation by a common independent set of
\mathcal{M}
and its dual matroid

Exact Matroid Intersection.

Given a graph with the edges colored either red or blue and an integer
k
, the
exact perfect matching problem
asks to find a perfect matching with exactly
k
red edges or to determine that such a matching does not exist. Mulmuley, Vazirani, and Vazirani
[
39
]
gave an efficient randomized algorithm for this problem via the Isolation Lemma. However, it is a long standing open question to design a deterministic polynomial time algorithm even for the bipartite case.

The
Exact Matroid Intersection
problem generalizes the exact perfect matching problem for bipartite graphs. Here, we are given an integer
k
and two matroids of same rank on a ground set whose elements are colored either red and blue. The goal is to find a common base with exactly
k
red elements. An efficient randomized algorithm
is known when the input matroids are linear
[
40
]
. Deterministic polynomial-time algorithms are known for Pfaffian pairs
[
50
]
and for arborescences (that are intersections of graphic and partition matroids)
[
33
]
.

In this context, we get a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for exact matroid intersection in the special setting when the input matroids are dual of each other. We can first find a common base with minimum number of red elements using weighted matroid intersection. Then, using
Theorem

1.4
iteratively, we can get a common base with exactly
k
red elements if such a common base exists.

1.3
Overview of techniques

The proof of Theorem
1.4
is based on a structural analysis of the exchange graph
D(B_{1},B_{2})
between
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
(see Section
3.1
). This has an edge
(x,y)
from
x\in B_{i}
to
y\in B_{j}
if
B_{i}-x+y
is a basis. Assume
|B_{1}\cap S|<|B_{2}\cap S|
. The goal is to find an exchangable set
X
such that
|(B_{1}\Delta X)\cap S|=|B_{1}\cap S|+1
. Pairs where
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
are both present represent symmetric exchanges. If there is a symmetric edge
(t,y)
with
t\in B_{1}\setminus S
and
y\in B_{2}\cap S
, then
X=\{t,y\}
is a
(t,S)
-exchangeable set and we are done. This is in fact always the case when the matroid is strongly base orderable. Such matroids have a perfect matching of symmetric exchange edges; by the assumption
|B_{1}\cap S|<|B_{2}\cap S|
, a perfect matching must include a symmetric exchange edge
(t,y)
as above.

The above argument however fails for general matroids: while for each
x\in B_{1}\cup B_{2}
, there is a symmetric exchangeable element, it could be the case that the elements
x\in S\cap B_{2}
can only be exchanged to elements in
S\cap B_{1}
. Instead, we show the existence of a directed cycle
\mathcal{C}
in the exchange graph such that
V(\mathcal{C})\setminus S=\{t\}
for some
t\in B_{1}
. Using standard basis exchange arguments, the existence of such a
\mathcal{C}
implies the existence of a
(t,S)
-exchangeable set (Lemma
3.2
).
This proof also naturally gives rise to a polynomial-time algorithm: the main task is to construct the exchange graph, and find a chordless cycle
\mathcal{C}
containing some
t\in B_{1}\setminus S
with
V(\mathcal{C})\subseteq S+t
.

The more technical part of the argument is to derive a contradiction under the assumption that such a cycle
\mathcal{C}
does not exist.
We consider a strongly connected component
K
of
S
in the exchange graph with
|K\cap B_{1}|<|K\cap B_{2}|
. Assuming that there are no symmetric exchange edges between elements in
B_{1}\setminus S
and
B_{2}\cap S
, it follows that there must be some
y_{1}\in K\cap B_{1}
that is mutually exchangeable with at least two elements
x_{0},x_{1}\in K\cap B_{2}
. We derive a contradiction for this situation by arguments relying on the strong circuit exchange axiom. A key technical ingredient is Lemma
3.3
that shows the existence of ‘witness circuits’ in the matroid, one for each
x\in K\cap B_{2}
.

As already noted, Theorem
1.1
on equitability follows easily from Theorem
1.4
. To prove Corollary
1.2
on 2-equitability with respect to two sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, and the more general form Theorem
4.1
in Section
4
, we consider a partition into disjoint bases that minimizes certain potentials capturing balancedness. One can argue that if the near-2-equitability statements do not hold, then one can apply Theorem
1.4
for
S\in\{S_{1},S_{2},E\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\}
to find a better partition.

Section
5
derives the applications to fair division problems. In particular, Theorem
1.9
shows the existence of matroid constrained EF1 allocations for identical tri-valued additive valuations. First, we observe that without loss of generality we can assume the potential utility values are
0<a<b
(Lemma
5.1
). We use the near-2-equitability partition with respect to the set of items of values
b
and
a
. This will already imply EF1 if
b\geq 2a
. However, if
b<2a
, additional exchanges are needed to transform the allocation to EF1.

Finally, the proof of Theorem
1.12
uses the
lone divider
technique
[
3
]
. In this approach, in order to guarantee a threshold of
t_{i}
for each agent
i
, we inductively assign bundles to agents and eliminate them. In each step, a remaining agent
i
(the lone divider) partitions the set of the remaining items into
n^{\prime}
bundles of value at least
t_{i}
to herself, where
n^{\prime}
is the number of the remaining agents. Then we allocate a subset of these bundles to a subset of agents and eliminate them such that
(i)
all the eliminated agents are satisfied with their bundles, and
(ii)
all the remaining agents do not desire any of the allocated bundles. When using this approach, the main part of the proof is to show that a lone divider exists in each iteration
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
32
]
. The rest follows by carefully applying Hall’s theorem.

For a set

if it is an inclusion-wise maximal set in

of the matroid. Let

We use the following well-known property, see e.g.
[

.

Lemma 2.1

(Symmetric exchange)
.

Let

and
B_{2}
be two bases of the matroid
\mathcal{M}
. Then, for every

, there is a mutually exchangeable element in
B_{2}
.

A
circuit

C\subseteq E
is a minimally dependent set.
We use the following important property, see e.g.,
[

(Strong circuit exchange property)

Let

be a matroid and

and
C_{2}
any two circuits of
\mathcal{M}
. Let
e\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
and

. Then, there exists a circuit

of
\mathcal{M}
such that

As an easy corollary, for every base

B\in\mathcal{B}
and

Let

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

and

2.4

∎

As the next lemma shows, cycles with chords also guarantee the existence of exchangeable sets.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

and

be two disjoint bases and

and

Proof.

Let

. To prove both parts of the statement,

Let

4
Equitability for Two Sets

In this section, we consider
2
-equitability, i.e., partitioning the ground set of a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
into
k
disjoint bases such that each of them intersect two given disjoint sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
as equally as possible. Theorem
4.1
below is our general statement for
k
bases; for
k=2
, we obtain Corollary
1.2
stated in the Introduction.

Recall that (
1
-)equitability in Section
3
asserts that given a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
whose ground set
E
can be partitioned into the union of
k
disjoint bases, and given an arbitrary set
S\subseteq E
, there exists a partition
E=B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup\ldots\cup B_{k}
into
k
bases such that
||S\cap B_{i}|-|S\cap B_{j}||\leq 1
for all
i,j\in[k]
.
We now consider two disjoint sets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq E
, and show that one can achieve the same guarantee for
S_{1}
, while having imbalance at most two for
S_{2}
; the bounds shown below are even slightly stronger.
In Example
4.3
, we show that achieving
||S_{i}\cap B_{j}|-|S_{i}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}||\leq 1
for all
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
is not possible for both
i=1
and
i=2
at the same time. Hence, what we prove in Theorem
4.1
(and Corollary
1.2
), are the best one can hope for in the context of equally distributing two sets among disjoint bases.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid, and assume that the ground set
E
can be partitioned into the union of
k
disjoint bases. Let
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq E
be two arbitrary disjoint sets. Then, there exists a partition
E=B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup\ldots\cup B_{k}
into
k
bases such that the following hold:

(i)

||S_{1}\cap B_{j}|-|S_{1}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}||\leq 1
for all
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
; and

(ii)

\left||S_{1}\cap B_{j}|-|S_{1}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}|\right|+\left||S_{2}\cap B_{j}|-|S_{2}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}|\right|\leq 2
for all
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
; and

(iii)

||(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\cap B_{j}|-|(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\cap B_{j^{\prime}}||\leq\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}||S_{i}\cap B_{j}|-|S_{i}\cap B_{j^{\prime}}||
for all
j,j\in[k]
.

In other words, if
b^{1}_{i}=|B_{i}\cap S_{1}|
and
b^{2}_{i}=|B_{i}\cap S_{2}|
, there exists

such that either
\{(b^{1}_{i},b^{2}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}\subseteq\{(h,\ell),(h,\ell+1),(h,\ell+2)\}
or
\{(b^{1}_{i},b^{2}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}\subseteq\{(h,\ell),(h,\ell+1),(h+1,\ell-1),(h+1,\ell)\}
.

For any partition

of

(ii)

(|\bar{A}^{H}_{q}|,|\bar{A}^{L}_{q}|)=(h,\ell+2)
. So as long as such a pair exists, repeat doing the above process. The final allocation is EF1.
∎

Using Theorem
1.9
, an EF1 allocation for two agents with (non-identical) tri-valued additive valuations can be obtained by a simple cut-and-choose protocol. Assuming that both of the agents have agent one’s valuation function
v_{1}
, there exists a feasible EF1 allocation
(A_{1},A_{2})
. Now let agent two picks her favorite bundle among
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, and allocate the remaining bundle to agent one. The final allocation is clearly EF1.
