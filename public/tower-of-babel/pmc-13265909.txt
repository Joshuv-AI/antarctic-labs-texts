Cellular retinoic acid-binding protein 1, CRABP1, in thyroid gland aging
Authors: Fatimah Najjar, Jennifer Nhieu, Natalia Calixto Mancipe, Christy Henzler, Li-Na Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging differentially affects disease risks of various organs1. The endocrine organs undergo significant changes along aging. Clinical reports showed increased prevalence of thyroid disorders with age, especially hypothyroidism2. Primary hypothyroidism includes congenital, autoimmune, and iatrogenic causes, which can occur throughout the lifespan. But those with unknown aetiology increase dramatically (~5 fold) in the elderly (≥75 years old)3,4. In thyrocytes, accumulated thyroglobulin requires efficient protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)5,6. When ER experiences protein folding defects, it activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), which determines the life-or-death cell fate. The most fundamental mediator in UPR signaling is inositol-requiring enzyme-1 alpha (IRE-1α)7. This study reports, for the first time, a highly conserved gene, Cellular retinoic acid binding protein 1 (Crabp1), critical to thyrocyte function in aging. CRABP1 acts by modulating clustering and activation of IRE-1α, thereby reducing thyrocyte ER stress.

Introduction

Hypothyroidism is characterized by an increase in the circulating thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and decreases in thyroid hormones including thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)3,4. Hypothyroidism is a subtle disease, in which the manifested symptoms are generally non-specific, including hypothermia, weight gain, fatigue, and depression. The pathogenesis of hypothyroidism stems from a dysfunctional thyroid gland, known as primary hypothyroidism or central hypothyroidism, which can then cause dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis8,9. The incidence of hypothyroidism is 5% in adults; it rises to 20% in the elderly population3,10. Hypothyroidism in the elderly is particularly complex since its symptoms can be obscured by other aging-related diseases. In addition, age-related biochemical changes, such as abnormal TSH levels, increase with aging, making diagnosis and management more complicated. The standard treatment of hypothyroidism, levothyroxine (hormonal replacement), is not an optimal option in the elderly because of pre-existing comorbidities and is more sensitive to hormonal replacement2,11–13. Dysfunctional of thyroid gland in elderly remains a clinically important question.

Thyrocytes are the functional cells in the thyroid gland that produce thyroglobulin (TG), the precursor of thyroid hormones. TG is a secreted protein that is synthesized and folded in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and secreted into the colloid inside the follicle where iodide is attached to TG to produce thyroid hormones14–16. TG folding fidelity is critical for thyroid hormone synthesis, and mutated TG protein induces ER stress5,6,17. Maintaining ER homeostasis is fundamental for protein folding. Although cells devote a significant amount of energy to maintain ER homeostasis, protein folding remains a remarkably error-prone process, which causes unfolded protein accumulation18,19. Unfolded proteins are toxic to cells and are cleared by a stringent quality control system known as the unfolded protein response (UPR). Chronic accumulation of unfolded proteins or a failure of the UPR system causes ER stress, which leads to cell death20. UPR involves tripartite signaling pathways, inositol-requiring enzyme-1 alpha (IRE-1α), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), and protein kinase R (PKR)-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), to restore ER homeostasis; each arm is activated through different stimuli. The activity of IRE-1α, particularly at the early stages of unfolded protein accumulation, is crucial for initiating ER associated degradation (ERAD)21, which eliminates unfolded proteins by inducing X-box binding protein-1(XBP1) expression. The transcription factor XBP1 increases the expression of proteins involved in protein degradation and secretory pathways to reduce ER stress22. Critically, persistent IRE-1α activity halts most of protein synthesis and initiates the apoptosis process. The progressive decline in cells’ ability to maintain ER homeostasis and proper UPR signaling has been implicated in several age-associated conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic diseases, and chronic inflammation23–25.

Cellular retinoic acid binding protein-1 (CRABP1) is a cytosolic protein that has a high specificity and affinity toward retinoic acid. CRABP1 functions by modulating specific signaling pathways via the formation of CRABP1-signalosomes26–28. We previously reported that knocking out the Crabp1 gene (CKO) in mice caused spontaneous adult-onset primary hypothyroidism. CKO mice grow and develop normally; however, later in their adulthood, around 6 months old, they develop symptoms of hypothyroidism, such as an increase in body weight, hypothermia, decreases in T4 and T3 serum levels, and an increase in TSH serum level29. However, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. This current study determines how aging CKO thyrocytes become dysfunctional, and concludes that CRABP1 plays a fundamental role in maintaining the health and function of thyrocytes in aging process. We further demonstrate, for the first time, that CRABP1 functions by regulating the activity of IRE-1α, preventing IRE-1α hyperactivity and cluster formation.

Results

Age-related changes in CRABP1 gene expression in human and mouse thyroid gland

We first noticed, in human, a significant age-dependent increase in the expression of the gene coding for CRABP1, a highly conserved retinoic acid (RA) signaling protein most abundantly expressed in the thyroid gland (Fig. S1A), when we analysed the GTEX human data subset (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, mouse Crabp1 expression in the thyroid gland is also gradually and significantly elevated as animals age (Fig. 1B). Importantly, CKO mice exhibit adult-onset primary hypothyroidism, starting at around 6 months old and progressively worsening later29. Specifically, adult CKO mice have significantly lower levels of circulating thyroid hormones, T4 and T3, together with increased body weight, decreased body temperature, and elevated TSH, starting at around 6-months old. Here, we first present histological examination which uncovers specific defects of CKO thyroid gland (Fig. 1C). The structural and functional unit of a healthy thyroid gland is the follicle that consists of a single layer of thyrocytes surrounding colloid where thyroid hormones are produced and stored. Follicles first form in the central area of the thyroid gland; upon growth and maturation, they relocate to the periphery areas of the gland30. As compared to wild type (WT) thyroid follicles, beginning at 6-months old, CKO thyroid follicles are smaller in size. The frequency distribution of follicle size shows that CKO follicles congregate at smaller sizes as compared to WT, suggesting heterogeneity in size, as well as irregular distribution caused by deleting CRABP1. At 9 months old, CKO thyroid glands have not only smaller, but also fewer, follicles, suggesting follicle degeneration (Fig. 1C). The abnormality of CKO thyrocytes is consistent with the significant increase in one of the initiating program cell death markers, cleaved caspase 3, in 9-months-old CKO thyroids, and is also confirmed in dual staining with TG (Fig. 1D). Aberrant thyroglobulin in CKO thyroid gland induces UPR response

Reduction in circulating thyroid hormones and thyroid follicle size in CKO mice would suggest that their production/secretion of active hormone (T4) and/or their precursor, TG protein, is affected. Immuno-histological examination of thyroid sections shows significantly reduced ratio of T4 vs. TG-specific staining in CKO as compared to WT (Fig. 2A), which is consistent with hypothyroidism of CKO mice and confirms defects in CKO thyrocyte functions around the age of 6–9 months. The primary function of thyrocytes is to produce TG protein; we thus compared TG levels in CKO vs. WT thyroids from 3-months to 15-months old. The data show that TG level in CKO thyroids in fact is significantly elevated at 3- and 6-months old, but is then significantly reduced in older ages, from 9- to 15-months old (Fig. 2B). These data indicate TG accumulation in CKO thyrocytes as early as 3-months old. TG accumulation in thyrocytes would lead to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress5,6,31. To more reliably quantify the production/accumulation of TG in thyrocytes, we exploited primary thyrocyte cultures to assess their intracellular TG accumulation at two critical ages, 6- and 9-month-old. As shown in Fig. 2C, in WT thyrocytes, TG level is progressively elevated from 6-month to 9-month old, this is because a decrease in T4 levels (in normal aging process), as shown in (Fig. 2A), would trigger increased TG production in healthy animals as a compensatory mechanism32. Very differently, CKO thyrocytes, at 9-month-old, failed to elevate their TG levels, indicating defects in CKO thyrocytes in older (9-months and older) animals, which is consistent with the apparent thyrocyte death in 9-months old CKO mice (Fig. 1D). Whereas at younger ages, 3- and 6-months old, CKO thyrocytes can still compensate for their reduced T4 levels by increasing their TG production (Fig. 2B). Cleaved ATF6, on the other hand, is considered the pivotal event in activating UPR33. At 15 months old, both CKO and WT showed similar increases in cleaved ATF6 levels as compared to younger animals (Fig. S2A). Upon IRE-1α phosphorylation, the RNase domain of IRE-1α in the cytosol is activated to splice the key transcription factor, XBP1 mRNA and deplete multiple cellular mRNA species. Normally, unspliced XBP1 (XBP1u) mRNA is unstable and readily degraded in the cytosol. XBP1u mRNA is predominantly spliced, by the RNase domain of IRE-1α, to its stable form, XBP1s mRNA, which initiates the UPR signaling cascade34. IRE-1α phosphorylation reflects its RNase domain activation. But the specificity of its RNase activity to splice XBP1u mRNA is dependent on the formation of dimers vs. oligomers35. It is known that the oligomeric form of IRE-1α, such as tetramers and hexamers, splice a wide spectrum of RNA species including XBP1u mRNA that contain CNGCAGN site; whereas the dimeric form of IRE-1α tends to be more selective in splicing RNAs, requiring both a consensus sequence and a scission between G and C36,37. Thus, the splicing activity of IRE-1α oligomers is nonspecific whereas that of the IRE-1α dimers is selective. As shown in Fig. 3B, in CKO thyrocytes, IRE-1α activation, reflected in the changes of XBP1s/total (XBP1s + XBP1u) mRNA ratio, increases only at 6-months old, but eventually decreases at older ages (starting at the age of 9-month). These data support that, in younger CKO thyroid (such as 6-months-old), elevated IRE-1α activity causes XBP1u mRNA splicing. But chronically elevated IRE-1α activity, due to the lack of CRABP1 (in CKO), triggers nonspecific splicing beyond XBP1u mRNA38, and results in reduced XBP1s mRNA expression at older ages (9-15 months).

CRABP1 regulates IRE-1α activity in the thyroid gland

Oligomerization of IRE-1α indicates its hyperactivity35. We compared the distribution of IRE-1α in WT and CKO thyrocytes at 12-months-old (Fig. 3C). As shown, most WT thyrocytes display a widely diffused IRE-1α staining pattern; whereas numerous CKO thyrocytes at the same age contain aggregated IRE-1α-positive clusters (pointed with white arrows). These data further support IRE-1α hyperactivity in aging CKO thyrocytes (such as by 12-months old). To determine whether/how CRABP1 acts in IRE-1α activation, we examined if CRABP1 forms complexes with IRE-1α in co-immunoprecipitation experiments using WT thyroid tissues, as well as the P19 cell line (Fig. 3E). The result indeed shows endogenous complex formation of CRABP1 with IRE1α in normal thyroids (Co-IP, Fig. S3). Knocking down Crabp1 in P19 cells resulted in lower levels of pulled down IRE-1α, suggesting that altering CRABP1 levels would affect the levels of IRE-1α in the complex. In situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) was then performed to confirm the proximity of endogenous CRABP1 to IRE-1α in WT thyrocytes (Fig. 3D). The PLA result indeed shows the proximity of CRABP1 to IRE-1α in normal, healthy thyrocytes (Fig. 3D. pointed with yellow arrows in the thyrocytes lining the follicles). As predicted, CKO thyroid tissues exhibit no positive PLA signals in thyrocytes. These data indicate that CRABP1 regulates IRE-1α activation, probably involving their physical interaction to modulate IRE-1α phosphorylation/oligomerization in normal, healthy thyroids.

The impact of UPR in aged CKO thyroid gland

To validate abnormal activation of UPR in CKO thyrocytes, we examined the signaling pathway of IRE-1α activation. Normally, IRE-1α dimerization induces XBP1 expression, which triggers ERAD process, a pro-survival signaling pathway5,39. ERAD induces soluble protein degradation through ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation21; whereas degradation of protein aggregates is carried out by the autophagy machinery18. We thus evaluated autophagosome formation in WT thyrocytes at 6- and 12-months-old (indicated by LC3B-positive puncta, an autophagosome marker). The results show age-dependent increase of autophagy in 12-month-old normal thyrocytes, as compared to 6-month-old normal thyrocytes (Fig. 4A. far left panel). However, in CKO thyrocytes, autophagosome formation increases significantly in 6-month-old, consistent with the increase of XBP1s mRNA expression. During a normal aging process, autophagy increases as a result of UPR40. Persistent IRE-1α activation/oligomerization will deplete multiple cellular mRNA species through a specific process called regulated IRE-1α-Dependent Decay (RIDD), a pro-apoptotic pathway41. We then monitored the status of Bcl2 that acts to sequester Bax and Bak to prevent their oligomerization/forming pores in the mitochondrial membrane, thereby inhibiting apoptosis42. In CKO thyroid, at 9-months-old, Bcl2 level is significantly reduced (Fig. 4B), indicating a reduced anti-apoptotic capacity of older CKO thyroid. Together, these data show that, at 6-months old, CKO thyrocytes experience TG accumulation triggered ER stress, activating IRE-1α which first initiates a pro-survival signaling pathway. At more advanced ages (such as 9-months and older), persistent ER stress in CKO thyrocytes would lead to their apoptosis because of failure in properly regulating IRE-1α activation in ER stress response. Ultimately, older CKO thyrocytes fail to elevate Bcl2 which would otherwise inhibit apoptosis in normal aging thyroid. Therefore, CKO thyrocytes begin to have defects in IRE-1α-mediated pro-survival ER stress response at 6-months-old; by 9-months-old, they fail to recover and then progress to apoptosis.

To follow the pathological progression of CKO thyroids, we monitored the expression of thyrocyte-specific genes at different ages (1, 3, 6, 9, and 15 months), including TG and several essential transcription factors for differentiating thyrocytes progenitors into thyrocytes, such as NKX2-1, and Pax843. In WT thyroids, TG expression is normally down regulated with age as shown in (Fig. 4C i), which parallels the downregulation of genes responsible for thyrocytes differentiation NKX2-1 and, Pax8 (Fig. 4C ii, iii)44. Very differently, in CKO thyroid, TG gene expression in fact increases at young ages, likely due to the need to compensate for aberrant thyroglobulin synthesis while their thyrocytes are still relatively healthy. Advancing in age, as in WT thyroids, TG expression in CKO thyroids follow a similar, progressively reduced pattern as shown in (Fig. 4C i). But the expression of those genes regulating thyrocytes differentiation in CKO in fact increases at more advanced ages (6-months and older) as shown in Fig. 4C ii, iii. This result indicates, in aging CKO thyroid glands, thyrocytes progenitor differentiation is enhanced, likely due to compensation for increased thyrocytes apoptosis/death. However, by 15months-old, this compensation in CKO thyroid ultimately fails, consistent with the progression of CKO thyrocyte defects/death at older ages.

To determine if these findings in the mouse model are relevant to the human conditions, we exploited publicly available human RNA-seq data extracted from the voyAGER database45. Notably, the age-dependent expression patterns of thyrocyte-related genes in human are very similar to those detected in WT mice (Fig. 4C iv). The similarity in the expression patterns of these age-associated genes, between human and mouse thyroids, would suggest a highly conserved thyroid aging process across species. Given the high expression level of CRABP1 in both human (Fig. S1A) and mouse (Fig. S1B) thyroids, as well as the extreme sequence conservation of the CRABP1 gene46, the progressively altered expression of thyroid-specific genes detected in CKO mice would suggest that CRABP1 contributes to the maintenance of healthy thyroid in normal aging. With the high conservation of CRABP1 across species, we suggest that the CKO mouse model provides a useful and valuable tool for studying the mechanisms underlying thyroid aging and thyroid diseases in human.

Based on the presented mouse data here and the progressive, age-dependent, elevation of CRABP1 expression in both human and mouse, we propose a model for the function of CRABP1 in modulating IRE-1α signaling along thyroid aging (Fig. 4D.). At younger ages, IRE-1α dimerization activates the RNase activity to splice XBP1u mRNA, a pro-survival pathway, via ERAD and proper autophagy. Advancing in age, the accumulated unfolded proteins trigger the formation of IRE-1α clusters that deplete RNAs through RIDD, a pro-apoptosis process47. Proper modulation of IRE-1α phosphorylation is key to IRE-1α cluster formation, which can be regulated by multiple factors. To this end, several proteins have been found to interact with and modulate IRE-1α, termed the “IRE-1α Interactome”48–51. This interactome includes protein kinase A (PKA), receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1), and extracellular regulated kinase (ERK). Their known functional roles in IRE1α modulation are summarized in Table S1. Our previous molecular and proteomics studies26,27 have established that CRABP1 can form multiple protein complexes, termed “CRABP1 interactome”. Interestingly, PKA, RACK1, and ERK, the key components of the “IRE-1α Interactome” also constitute the key components of the “CRABP1 interactome” uncovered from our studies. This would suggest convergence between CRABP1 and IRE-1α signaling networks. Through these converged signaling pathways, CRABP1 can modulate IRE1α activation. The mechanistic details of how CRABP1 forms complexes with those components modulating IRE-1α activation requires further studies.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the progression of adult-onset hypothyroidism in CKO mice is due to the accumulation of aberrant TG, which decreases T4 production. The accumulation of TG in the adulthood of CKO mice induces ER stress and UPR response. Around 6 months old, CKO mice IRE-1α activity induces the ERAD pathway to resolve ER stress. However, the persistent IRE-1α activity in the CKO thyroid gland, around the age of 9 months, induces IRE-1α hyperactivity, leading to cell apoptosis. CRABP1 expression increased with age in human and mouse thyroid glands, which signifies the crucial role in maintaining the function of thyrocytes during aging. We showed here that CRABP1 regulates the activity of IRE-1α in advanced-aged thyrocytes, exerting a cytoprotective effect by preventing cell apoptosis.

ER stress is implicated in numerous geriatric diseases such as type II diabetes (TIID), Alzheimer Disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, and graves thyroiditis24,25,52–57. Hoozemans et al. reported phosphorylated IRE-1α clusters in postmortem brains of AD patients (in hippocampal neurons)54. Furthermore, elevated IRE-1α phosphorylation was found in more severe AD stages (V and VI), as compared to stage 0 or I56, and increased UPR response in postmortem AD brains was associated with aggravated neuroinflammation response25. In TIID, pancreatic β-cells appeared to have increased UPR due to the accumulation of Iselt Amyloid polypeptide (IAPP), leading to β-cells apoptosis in TIID patients57. Finally, human thyroid glands of graves’ disease patients also showed elevated autophagy and apoptotic markers with increased misfolded TG that could induce ER-stress55. In general, the induction of UPR is inevitable in advanced ages, and chronically elevated ER-stress without resolution is closely linked to geriatric diseases; therefore, maintaining the rheostat of ER-homeostasis is vital in maintaining cellular function in healthy aging. For the three branches of UPR, the cross talk appeared to be affected in CKO thyroid gland. Increase in Phosphorylation on IRE-1α and decrease in cleaved ATF6 validate that cross talk of these three branches was defected; dysregulation of these branches would lead to cell death as seen in CKO. Previous reports have shown that cross-talk between ATF6 and IRE-1α is especially needed for attenuating IRE-1α signaling. However, the mechanisms that maintain coordination among UPR branches and how it is affected by CRABP1 remain to be fully elucidated. Here for the first time, we report CRABP1 as one important molecule in maintaining healthy thyroid gland along aging, which acts by modulating IRE-1α activation. Deleting this gene causes adult-onset hypothyroidism as seen in CKO mice, which is associated with elevated/unresolved ER-stress in thyrocytes where clusters of hyperactivated IRE-1α form. Persistent UPR in CKO thyrocytes causes their apoptosis. These results reveal the crucial role of CRABP1 in maintaining ER homeostasis and the function of thyrocytes. CRABP1 acts to modulate IRE-1α activation by preventing IRE-1α clustering, which provides a cytoprotective mechanism to prevent persistent UPR in aging thyrocytes. Importantly, this CRABP1-mediated protective mechanism is highly conserved across species, representing an evolutionarily preserved pathway that is particularly critical in maintaining healthy thyroids along aging. Mechanistically, this current study reveals CRABP1-signalosomes that consist of CRABP1-IRE-1α complexes, which modulate the activity/clustering of IRE-1α in thyrocytes in response to ER stress along aging. It is known that the RNase activity of IRE-1α displayed a dissociated activity toward RIDD vs XBP1 in genomic toxicity through c-ABL interaction58. In our current study, the potential dissociative activity of the RNase response in selectively engaging XBP1 versus RIDD was not assessed in the context of forming CRABP1-IRE-1α signalosome. However, the CKO mouse phenotype showed that losing CRABP1 in thyrocytes resulted in increased apoptosis, suggesting that the CRABP1-IRE-1α interaction would probably promote engagement towards XBP1, a pro-survival pathway. This reveals an interesting possibility that CRABP1, similar to other regulators like c-ABL, is also a player in regulating cell fate via modulating ER stress. Developing a more physiologically relevant thyrocyte-like in vitro system will facilitate future studies to examine detailed molecular and biophysical bases and to rigorously characterize how CRABP1 interacts with IRE-1α to modulate its RNase activity and its cluster dynamics. Dissecting this molecular mechanism by which CRABP1 forms IRE-1α-modulating signalosomes will be an important direction in future studies, especially to determine if CRABP1 has a role in shifting IRE-1α’s activity towards pro-apoptotic (RIDD) or adaptive pathways.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type/C57BL (WT) male mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory and housed in the University of Minnesota animal facilities. Cellular retinoic acid binding protein-1 (CRABP1) knock out (CKO) mice were generated by inserting 5’ nucleotide in CRABP1 gene between the second and third positions, by hit and run technique, of alanine codon of amino acid position 5 of CRABP1 exon one which create one Not I restriction site to ablate CRABP1 expression, CKO genotype was validate as previously described59–61. All animals were housed in a temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and light-controlled (12-hour light/dark cycle; lights on at 6:00 AM) animal facility and had free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines, and the University of Minnesota Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved the protocols.

Primary thyrocytes cultures

Each trial required two WT male mice and two CKO mice to generate one primary culture trial. Mice were euthanized by CO2 according to the approved protocol of the University of Minnesota (IACUC). This procedure previously described43,62, trachea was exposed and excised, which consists of two lobules. The Two lobules were isolated from the trachea and digested with 1 mg /ml of collagenase type II (Worthington-biochem, Cat# LS004177), 3 mg/ml dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D4693) and 0.4 mg/mL trypsin inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# T6522) dissolved in DMEM/F12(Gibco, Cat# 11330-03) media, for 45 minute in 37 °C agitated every 5 min, then centrifuged briefly the supernatant is discarded and added DMEM/F12 media supplement with 10% Nu serum. Followed by triturating the digested thyroids to dissociate follicles from the thyroid glands. Since the thyroid gland is a heterogeneous organ that contains other cells, such as C-cells, and the parathyroid gland is attached to the thyroid gland. To isolate the follicles, the thyroid homogenate is run through different cell strainers, 200 µm and 70 µm, to collect follicles and discard debris. The collected follicles are centrifuged 500 g for 10 min at 23 °C. the supernatant is discarded and the pellet is resuspend in 200 µL and seeded in glass bottom dishes coated with collagen type I (corning, Cat# 354236), followed by adding the maintaining media that changed every other day, DMEM/F12 consisted of 10% Nu-serum, 2 ng/mL glycl-L-histidyl-L-lysine (Sigma, Cat# M6145), 3.5 ng/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma, Cat# H0888), 10 nM potassium iodide, 0.5 μM ascorbic acid (Sigma, Cat# A4403), 10 μg/mL human transferrin (Sigma, Cat# T8158), 10 μg/mL bovine insulin (Sigma, Cat# I0516), 10 ng/mL somatostatin (Sigma, Cat# S1763), and 1 mIU/mL TSH (Scripps Lab, Cat# T0117). The cells incubated in 5% CO2, after 5 days of cultivating the cultures, were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, then washed with PBS and stored in PBS in 4 °C until needed. Each experimental condition consists of at least three independent trials of primary thyrocyte culture.

Stable P19 cell line generating

P19 cells (ATCC, CRL-1825) were maintained in MEM-α (Gibco, #12571-063) with 10% consist of (7.5% iron fortified bovine calf serum (ATCC, #30-2030) and 2.5% FBS (Atlanta Biologics, #S11150). HEK-293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, # 11965) with 10% heat-inactivated FBS. Both cell lines contained 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination.

For transduction, pLKO.1Crabp1 plasmids (TRCN0000011959 and TRCN0000011960) and the pLKO.1 vector we obtained from the University of Minnesota Genomics Centre RNAi. 2 × 106 HEK-293T cell were seeded overnight without antibiotics in 10 cm dishes. 9.6 µg target plasmid, 7.2 µg psPAX2 packaging plasmid and 2.4 µg pMD2.G envelope plasmid was co-transfected into cells with lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, #11668019) for lentivirus production. Then the media was changed to fresh, complete DMEM containing 1% BSA after 6 h of transfection. Infectious lentivirus was harvested 24 h and 48 h post-transfection and filtered through 0.45 µM pore cellulose acetate filters. Lentiviral stocks were concentrated using Lenti-X concentrator (Clontech Lab, #631232) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 2 × 105 of P19 cells were seeded in complete DMEM media in 6-well plates, incubated overnight. For P19-siCrabp1 knockdown, lentivirus was generated from TRCN0000011959 and TRCN0000011960, and for P19-silencing control, lentivirus was generated from the pLKO.1 vector was applied to the cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Millipore,#TR-1003-G). Plates were collected and centrifuged at 800 g for 60 min at 37 °C. After 24 h, viral supernatants were removed and replaced with fresh medium. Puromycin (1.5 µg/mL) was used after 48 h post-transduction and maintained thereafter. Approximately 10 cells were isolated and seeded into a 10 cm dish. After one-week, visible colonies were picked and transferred to 96-well plates, stable P19 clones were expanded, and Crabp1 expression levels were assessed by qPCR.

Immunostaining and Haematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining

Harvested thyroid glands from male mice were fixed in 10% formalin overnight at room temperature and then dehydrated in ethanol for paraffin embedding. Tissue sections were cut at 5 μm and stained with haematoxylin (Leica, Cat # 3801500) and eosin stain. Images were acquired by microscope (Leica DM IRB). Three thyroid glands from each group and age were sectioned, and all sections were analysed by using ImageJ (Fiji).

All section incubated with secondary antibody of Cy5conjugated donkey anti-rabbit antibody (1:2000, Jackson Laboratories, Cat #711-175-152), and Cy3conjugated F(ab’)2 fragment donkey anti-mouse antibody (1:2000, Jackson Laboratories, Cat# 715-166151) for 2 h at room temperature then counter stained with 20 µM DAPI and WGA (1 mg/ml) for 10 min at room temperature. To quantify T4/TG, BCl2, and intracellular TG, after determine the area of interest in the thyroid gland, the background fluorescence of each channel for each stain is subtracted before proceeding to measure the mean value of florescence intensity, then the ratio of T4/TG, TG/DAPI, and BC2/DAPI is calculated. Bioinformatics

Gene read counts from bulk thyroid tissues with public access were downloaded from the GTEX portal (https://gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/bulk_tissue_expression) on July 2024. Donor age bracket and sex information was downloaded from the Table Browser tool of the University of California Santa Cruz Genomics Institute (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgibin/hgTables?db=hg38&hgta_group=allTables&hgta_track=hgFixed&hgta_table=hgFixed.gtexDonor). The full metadata of the samples analysed here are presented in Data S2, Data S3, Data S4. Gene expression was modeled, and the effect of age tested using edgeR (v. 4.2.2), found in Data S5. Briefly, lowly expressed genes (with less than 10 counts per age bracket) were filtered out, counts were TMM normalized with the calcNormFactors() function, gene expression was modeled with the glmQLFit() function using the formula expression ~ age, and the significance of the fitted trends was tested with the glmQLFTest() function.

Gene expression trends of TG, NKX2.1, and PAX8 across human aging were assessed using the voyAGEr database (https://compbio.imm.medicina.ulisboa.pt/app/voyAGEr), which provides tissue- and age-specific transcriptomic profiles derived from GTEx RNA-seq data. In the voyAGEr database, expression values are based on normalized logCPM measurements. These values are modeled using the ShARP-LM pipeline, which applies linear models across 16-year age windows to estimate gene expression changes over time. Importantly, gene expression levels displayed in voyAGEr are not directly comparable between different genes. Each gene’s expression profile is considered in the context of its own distribution across age within each tissue. Thus, a gene with a low expression value (z-score) at a given age (e.g., 25 years) refers to a lower expression relative to its own levels at other ages, not to absolute expression across the transcriptome or compared to other genes. Therefore, low or high expression values at a given age indicate intra-gene variation over age within the same tissue.

Statistical analysis

Linear regression was performed to associate CRABP1 expression with the increase in age. To assess the CKO and age effects, a Two-Way ANOVA was performed, followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis. To determine the CKO effect, a student-t test was performed. P values of 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All representative values are the mean ± SE. SPSS software was utilized to perform the analysis.
