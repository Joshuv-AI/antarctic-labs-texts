Correlative Effects of PARP12 mRNA Knockdown on Pro-inflammatory Gene Expression and Alternative Splicing in BV2 Microglial Cells
Authors: Bang Hu, WenLi Zhou, Hua Chen, Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammation is a key mechanism underlying neuropathic pain, in which microglial cells play a central role. Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 12 (PARP12) is an interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) containing a predicted RNA-binding domain and may be involved in inflammatory regulation and RNA metabolism; however, its posttranscriptional regulatory functions in microglia remain to be fully elucidated. PARP12 was stably knocked down in BV2 microglial cells using lentiviral shRNA. Quantitative PCR confirmed a significant reduction in mRNA levels (knockdown efficiency 78.3%), but Western blot analysis showed no successful downregulation of PARP12 protein levels. Transcriptomic and alternative splicing changes were systematically analyzed using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and selected key genes were validated by RT-qPCR. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and regulated alternative splicing events (RASEs). Expression changes of homeostatic microglial markers and classical polarization markers were analyzed from the existing RNA-seq data. Comparisons were made with public proinflammatory microglial signature gene sets. PARP12 mRNA knockdown led to substantial transcriptomic remodeling: 276 DEGs (107 upregulated and 169 downregulated) and 287 significant RASEs were identified. Downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB, TNF, and chemokine signaling; most core immune genes (including Ccl2, Ptgs2, and C3) showed coordinated downregulation, whereas a smaller subset (Ccl5, Cd81, and S1pr1) was upregulated. Homeostatic microglial markers (Tmem119, P2ry12, Cx3cr1, Sall1, etc.) did not differ significantly between shPARP12 and shNC cells. Splicing analysis revealed altered splicing of immune/metabolism-related genes such as Fyb, Tnfaip8, Lrch4, and Pkm, but changes in protein isoform ratios were not validated. The overall proinflammatory signature gene set was downregulated after PARP12 knockdown. PARP12 mRNA knockdown is closely associated with attenuation of the proinflammatory transcriptional program and widespread alternative splicing changes in BV2 microglial cells. However, given the unsuccessful knockdown at the protein level, the observed changes cannot be directly attributed to loss of PARP12 protein function. Further mechanistic studies combining CRISPR/Cas9 gene knockout and in vivo pathological models are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-026-06125-y.

Introduction

Neuroinflammation represents a central response of the central nervous system (CNS) to injury or dysregulation, in which microglia, as resident immune cells, play a key role through their dynamic polarization and complex signaling regulation [1–5]. The proinflammatory state resulting from aberrant microglial activation is a common hallmark of numerous neuroinflammation-associated pathological processes. Therefore, elucidating the intrinsic molecular mechanisms that govern microglial inflammatory responses is essential for understanding neuroimmune homeostasis.

PARP12 is a unique member of the PARP superfamily that possesses both mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase (MART) catalytic activity and RNA-binding potential. Its protein sequence contains five CCCH-type zinc finger domains—a class of domains that, in various well-characterized RNA-binding proteins (such as TTP and HuR), mediate their interactions with AU-rich elements or specific RNA sequences [6–8]. Multiple studies have provided experimental evidence that PARP12 functions as a bona fide RNA-binding protein: in virus-infected cells, PARP12 directly binds viral RNA and suppresses viral replication by interfering with its translation [7, 9]; in an inflammatory model of pancreatic beta cells, PARP12 was shown to ADP-ribosylate more than 150 mRNAs, thereby modulating their translational efficiency [10]. In terms of subcellular localization, PARP12 exhibits a dynamic biphasic distribution—it associates with Golgi membranes under steady-state conditions but is rapidly recruited to cytoplasmic stress granules upon oxidative stress or translational arrest [7, 11]. Stress granules are key hubs for mRNA translation, storage, and degradation, enriched with numerous RNA-binding proteins and translational regulators [12–14]. The enrichment of PARP12 in stress granules overlaps functionally and spatially with other known RNA-binding proteins (such as G3BP1 and TIA-1) that regulate mRNA fate in stress granules, strongly suggesting its potential involvement in mRNA metabolism and translational control. Furthermore, another member of the PARP family, PARP13/ZAP, has been clearly identified as an RNA-binding protein with potent antiviral functions, capable of directly recognizing and binding to CG dinucleotide-rich regions in viral RNA to mediate RNA degradation [15, 16]. Based on the homology in zinc finger domains between PARP12 and PARP13, it is reasonable to speculate that PARP12 may participate in RNA metabolism through similar mechanisms. However, to date, direct experimental evidence regarding the endogenous RNA-binding profile of PARP12 in mammalian cells, its specific RNA recognition motifs, and whether it directly participates in pre-mRNA splicing regulation remains lacking [6, 7].

Regarding cross-cell type functional studies, the functions of PARP12 have been primarily elucidated in macrophages, whereas data in microglia remain largely absent [6, 10]. In macrophages, PARP12 has been demonstrated to participate in three major functions [7, 10, 17, 18]: (1) antiviral immunity—PARP12 suppresses the replication of multiple RNA viruses (including coronaviruses, alphaviruses, and Zika virus), and PARP12-knockout mice exhibit significantly increased susceptibility to viral infection; (2) inflammatory signaling activation—PARP12 promotes NF-κB pathway activation and enhances the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes through interactions with p62/SQSTM1 and TRIF; (3) regulation of programmed cell death—studies have reported that IFN-γ-induced PARP12 specifically modifies RIPK1 and RIPK3 via mono-ADP-ribosylation (MARylation), acting as a molecular switch between necroptosis and apoptosis: PARP12-mediated MARylation of RIPK1/RIPK3 promotes necrosome assembly and necroptosis while limiting the interaction of RIPK1 with FADD/caspase-8 to inhibit apoptosis. This finding establishes PARP12 as a key regulator of cell death decisions in macrophages.

As the resident macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS), microglia share certain immune response pathways with peripheral macrophages; however, they exhibit fundamental differences in developmental origin, homeostatic maintenance mechanisms, and response patterns to the tissue microenvironment [19–21]. Microglia are derived from yolk sac primitive myeloid precursors, rely on TGF-β signaling and the transcription factor SALL1 to maintain their homeostatic identity, and are defined by markers such as Tmem119, P2ry12, and Cx3cr1 that characterize their functional state. These features render microglia distinctly different from bone marrow–derived macrophages in terms of transcriptional regulatory networks, epigenetic landscapes, and stimulus–response patterns [19, 22, 23]. Nevertheless, functional studies of PARP12 in microglia remain virtually absent—whether it retains the proinflammatory and cell death regulatory functions already revealed in macrophages, whether it participates in microglia-specific posttranscriptional regulation via RNA-binding, and its potential interactions with other CNS cell types (such as neurons and astrocytes) are all unknown.

Based on this, the present study aimed to preliminarily investigate the effects of PARP12 mRNA knockdown on the transcriptome and alternative splicing in the BV2 microglial cell line, with a focus on expression changes in inflammation-related pathways and genes. By analyzing homeostatic and classical polarization markers, we sought to assess the overall functional state of the cells following PARP12 knockdown, thereby providing transcriptome-level candidate genes and clues for subsequent functional mechanistic studies.

Material and Methods

Cell Culture and Transfections

BV2 cells (ZQ0397, Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere in complete MEM medium (PM150410, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10091148, Gibco, China), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin (SV30010, Hyclone, USA), and 1% sodium pyruvate (CSP003, Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Cells were infected with lentivirus at an MOI of 400 and incubated for 24 h in the absence or presence of 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (Peprotech, 315-05). Stable cell lines were obtained by screening with 0.3 µg/mL puromycin and cells were harvested for reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis and Western blot analysis. It should be noted that growth factors present in serum-containing culture medium (e.g., TGF-β1) may exert anti-inflammatory effects on microglial cells; therefore, cytokine expression levels measured under basal conditions may be subject to general suppression by serum [24, 25]. All experiments in this study were conducted in complete medium containing 10% FBS, without subjecting the cells to starvation.

Assessment of Gene Expression

cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (R323-01, Vazyme, China) on a thermal cycler (T100, Bio-Rad, USA) with the following program: 42 °C for 5 min, 37 °C for 15 min, and 85 °C for 5 s. qPCR was performed on an ABI QuantStudio 5 system with initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Each sample was analyzed with three technical replicates and three biological replicates. The expression of each transcript was normalized to Gapdh (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase), and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCT method [26].

RNA Extraction and Sequencing

Total RNA was treated with RQ1 DNase (Promega) to remove DNA. The quality and quantity of the purified RNA were determined by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm/280 nm (A260/A280) using a SmartSpec Plus spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, USA). RNA integrity was further verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) assays were performed by Wuhan Ruixing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (http://www.rxbio.cc). For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was treated with RQ1 DNase (M6101, Promega, USA) to remove DNA and was then used for directional RNA-seq library preparation with the VAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR605, Vazyme, China). mRNAs were captured by VAHTS mRNA capture Beads (N401, Vazyme, China). Fragmented RNAs were converted into double-strand cDNA. Following end repair and A-tailing, the DNAs were ligated to VAHTS RNA Multiplex Oligos Set 1 for Illumina (N323, Vazyme, China), and the ligated products were amplified, purified, quantified, and stored at −80 °C before sequencing. The strand marked with dUTP (the 2nd cDNA strand) is not amplified, allowing strand-specific sequencing. For high-throughput sequencing, the libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions and applied to the DNBSEQ-T7 system for 150 nt paired-end sequencing. RNA sequencing was performed with three biological replicates per group. All six RNA-seq samples in this study (three biological replicates each for the sh_NC and sh_PARP12_1 groups) were processed using the same batch of reagents, with library preparation and sequencing carried out on the DNBSEQ-T platform during the same experimental period.

RNA-seq Raw Data Clean and Alignment

Raw reads containing more than 2-N bases were first discarded. Then, adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed from raw sequencing reads using FASTX-Toolkit (Version 0.0.13). Short reads shorter than 16 nt were also discarded. Clean reads were then mapped to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 using HISAT2, allowing up to 4 mismatches [27]. Following alignment, uniquely mapped reads were assembled and quantified using StringTie. Expression levels for each gene were then calculated as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) to enable normalized cross-sample comparisons [28].

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (PR20001, Proteintech, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (4693116001, Sigma, USA). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, #23,227) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-PARP12 rabbit antibody (1:5000, A14317, ABclone, China) and anti-ACTIN rabbit antibody (1:5000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech, China). The membrane was then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (anti-rabbit, 1:10,000, SA00001-2, Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (P0018FM, Beyotime, China).

Differentially Expressed Genes (DEG) Analysis

The R Bioconductor package DESeq2 was utilized to screen out the differentially expressed genes (DEGs)[29]. An adjusted P value < 0.05 combined with a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 was used as the cutoff criterion for identifying DEGs.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To sort out functional categories of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were identified using KOBAS 2.0 server [30]. A hypergeometric test and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each term.

DEG Analysis on Immune-related Genes

Immune-related genes were retrieved from the ImmPort database. Multiple immune gene categories were integrated, and redundant genes were removed. Finally, 1602 immune-related genes were retained for subsequent analysis (Tab. S2), which lists these 1602 immune genes (sheet 1) and their corresponding FPKM values (sheet 2). Because some genes were not detectably expressed in the RNA-seq data, FPKM values are provided for 973 genes.

PPI Analysis

The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database (http://stringdb.org/) was employed to construct PPI networks by mapping the DEGs onto PPI data [31]. The symbols of the DEGs were imported into the database, and high-resolution bitmaps were generated. The bitmap only included interactors with a combined confidence score of 0.4 or higher. CytoHubba, a plugin for Cytoscape 3.9.1, was used to calculate the MCC of connectivity and identify the top 20 genes in the PPI networks [32]. The PPI networks, modules, and the top 20 genes were visualized based on their node degree using Cytoscape software.

Alternative Splicing Analysis

Alternative splicing events (ASEs) and regulated alternative splicing events (RASEs) between samples were defined and quantified using the ABLas pipeline as described previously [33, 34]. In brief, ABLas can detect 10 types of ASEs based on splice junctions, including exon skipping (ES), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), intron retention (IR), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), mutually exclusive 5′UTRs (5pMXE), mutually exclusive 3′UTRs (3pMXE), cassette exon, A3SS&ES, and A5SS&ES. Student’s t-test was used to assess the significance of changes in ASE proportions. Events significant at a P value cutoff corresponding to a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) were considered candidate PARP12-regulated RASEs. Alternative splicing events meeting the criteria of FDR < 0.05 and an absolute ΔPSI (percent spliced in) value > 0.15 were defined as RASEs regulated by PARP12 [35]. This threshold enables the screening of biologically meaningful splicing variations in mammalian transcriptomic datasets while balancing detection sensitivity and false positive rates.

Statistical Analysis

All plots were generated in R (v 4.2.3) as implemented in RStudio, with the exception of pattern diagrams and stacked bar charts. Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used.

Results

PARP12 mRNA Knockdown Inhibits Proinflammatory Cytokine Expression in BV2 Microglia

To evaluate the effect of PARP12 knockdown on the inflammatory response of microglia, qPCR was used to detect the mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). The results showed that under basal culture conditions, compared with the shNC group, the mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the shPARP12-1 group were significantly downregulated: the expression of IL-1β decreased by 62.4% ± 7.1% (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A), the expression of IL-6 decreased by 58.7% ± 6.3% (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B), and the expression of TNF-α decreased by 51.2% ± 5.8% (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Validation of PARP12 Knockdown Efficiency in BV2 Microglial Cells

Using a nontargeting control shRNA (shNC) as a reference, three shRNAs targeting PARP12 (shPARP12-1, shPARP12-2, and shPARP12-3) were individually transduced into BV2 cells. qPCR results showed that, compared with the shNC group, PARP12 mRNA expression levels were significantly reduced in all shPARP12 groups (P < 0.001), with the shPARP12-1 group achieving the highest knockdown efficiency of 78.3% ± 5.2% (Fig. 1D). Given the higher knockdown efficiency of the shPARP12-1 group, this construct was used in all subsequent experiments. In addition, Western blot analysis was performed to assess knockdown efficiency at the protein level. It is important to note that, although qPCR confirmed efficient mRNA knockdown, Western blot consistently failed to detect a significant downregulation of PARP12 protein levels (Fig. S1). Therefore, all results presented in this study reflect correlative effects following PARP12 mRNA knockdown and cannot be directly attributed to loss of PARP12 protein function.

As PARP12 is an ISG (interferon-stimulated gene), to investigate whether the cells are indeed affected by PARP12 knockdown, we examined PARP12 protein levels following IFN-γ stimulation. Western blot analysis revealed that IFN-γ treatment markedly upregulated PARP12 in shNC-transfected cells (shNC + IFN-γ), whereas PARP12 remained significantly suppressed in shPARP12-transfected cells despite IFN-γ exposure (Fig. S2). These results demonstrated that, under IFN-γ stimulation, shPARP12 cells exhibited reduced PARP12 induction, confirming that this represents “functional knockdown” (i.e., reduced inducibility of PARP12 expression) rather than an off-target effect.

We next assessed whether PARP12 contributes to IFN-γ-triggered inflammatory responses. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis showed that IFN-γ stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in shNC control cells. Notably, in shPARP12 cells, IFN-γ-induced upregulation of these cytokines was markedly attenuated; the mRNA levels in shPARP12 + IFN-γ cells were significantly lower than those in shNC + IFN-γ cells under identical conditions and remained comparable to those in unstimulated shPARP12 cells (Fig. S3). Together, these findings suggest that IFN-γ induces PARP12 and that PARP12 knockdown attenuates downstream inflammatory responses.

Analysis of Homeostatic Markers in BV2 Microglial Cells

To assess the homeostatic characteristics of BV2 cells following PARP12 mRNA knockdown, we analyzed the expression of typical homeostatic microglial markers (Tmem119, Csf1r, Cx3cr1, Fcrl2, Hexb, P2ry12, Tgfbr1, etc.) from the existing RNA-seq data (Fig. S4). The results revealed that all detected steady-state markers were comparable between shPARP12 and shNC groups, including Tmem119 (|log₂FC|< 0.5, P > 0.05), suggesting that PARP12 mRNA knockdown does not lead to a general loss of the homeostatic phenotype in BV2 cells. It should be noted that previous studies have demonstrated that the basal expression levels of these markers in BV2 cells are lower than those in primary microglia or freshly isolated microglia in vivo [36, 37]. Therefore, BV2 cells cannot fully substitute for primary microglia, and this conclusion requires further validation in primary microglia.

PARP12 mRNA Knockdown Remodels the Transcriptome of Microglia and Enriches Inflammatory-related Pathways

To systematically analyze the regulatory effect of PARP12 on the transcriptome of microglia, this study conducted RNA-seq analysis on BV2 microglial cells in the PARP12 knockdown group (sh_PARP12) and the nontargeted control group (shNC). Bioinformatics analysis was used to screen for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and to explore their functional associations. The results showed that PARP12 mRNA knockdown significantly reshaped the transcriptional characteristics of microglia, and the differentially expressed genes were highly enriched in inflammation-related pathways specifically as follows:

After PARP12 knockdown, the transcriptional expression profile of BV2 microglial cells underwent significant changes. Principal component analysis (PCA) results showed that the samples in the sh_PARP12 group and the sh_NC group could be clearly distinguished at the transcriptional level, and the intragroup replicates showed good clustering, confirming that the transcriptional differences between the two groups were stable and reliable (Fig. 2A). After PARP12 mRNA knockdown, 276 genes showed differential expression. Among them, 107 genes were upregulated and 169 were downregulated. The volcano plot visually presents the distribution characteristics of DEGs (upregulated genes are labeled in red and downregulated genes in blue), further confirming the extensive regulatory effect of PARP12 knockdown on the gene expression of microglia (Fig. 2B). Hierarchical clustering analysis showed clear differences in DEG expression patterns between the two groups. The results of functional enrichment analysis revealed the core biological functions associated with the DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) enrichment analysis of the downregulated DEGs showed that they were significantly enriched in “inflammatory response,” “immune response,” “neutrophil chemotaxis,” “chemokine-mediated signaling pathway,” “cellular response to interleukin-1,” etc., suggesting that PARP12 mRNA knockdown may regulate the inflammatory phenotype of microglia by inhibiting the above proinflammatory biological processes (Fig. 2E). The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that the downregulated DEGs were highly enriched in “cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction,” “TNF signaling pathway,” “IL-17 signaling pathway,” “chemokine signaling pathway,” and “NF-κB signaling pathway,” all of which are classic inflammation-related pathways. These pathways play a crucial role in the activation of microglia, the release of pro-inflammatory factors, and the amplification of neuroinflammation. This enrichment suggests that PARP12 may participate in the regulation of microglial-mediated inflammatory responses by regulating the gene expression of these pathways (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, the upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in biological processes such as “positive regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation,” “transmembrane transport,” “protein degradation,” and pathways such as “complement and coagulation cascades” and “calcium signaling pathway,” which might be compensatory expression changes of microglia in the inflammatory inhibition state (Fig. 2D).

Among the 276 DEGs identified after PARP12 mRNA knockdown, the DEGs were intersected with the 1602 immune-related genes from the ImmPort database (Tab. S2). A total of 34 differentially expressed immune genes were identified, suggesting that PARP12 has a specific regulatory effect on the expression of immune-related genes in microglia (Fig. 3A). To analyze the interaction relationships among these immune genes and the core regulatory nodes, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (with a combined confidence score ≥ 0.4), resulting in 22 nodes (differentially expressed immune genes) and 108 edges (interactions between genes). Genes with altered alternative splicing after PARP12 knockdown were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment analysis, which revealed enrichment in DNA damage response, DNA repair, chromatin organization, regulation of translation, positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription, mRNA transport, negative regulation of cell migration, translation, chromatin remodeling, and methylation (Fig. 4D). Among all ASEs, genes with more than 10 reads in the nonintronic region in the sh_PARP12 versus sh_NC comparison and an absolute difference greater than 0.15 were ranked in ascending order by P value, and 8 genes were selected. On this basis, we further identified 4 genes that require special attention, namely Fyb, Tnfaip8, Lrch4, and Pkm (Fig. 4E). The selection of these genes for validation was based on their statistical significance, the magnitude of splicing change, and established roles in inflammation or neuroimmune function. Fyb is an adaptor protein associated with cytoskeletal rearrangement and signaling in T cells and macrophages, implicated in immune synapse formation and inflammatory signaling, with potential links to CNS immune cell infiltration [38, 39]. Tnfaip8 is a key regulator of inflammatory and immune homeostasis. It is upregulated in LPS-stimulated microglia and enhances NF-κB signaling, and its knockdown mitigates inflammatory responses [40, 41]. Lrch4 participates in innate immunity, and its mRNA levels vary dynamically in immune cells upon bacterial or LPS challenge, indicating its potential as an immune regulatory node [42, 43]. Alternative splicing of Pkm generates PKM1 and PKM2, which have distinct functions in metabolism and inflammation. PKM2 can act as a protein kinase or transcriptional coactivator to promote expression of cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, contributing to microglial/macrophage inflammatory activation [44].

The altered alternative splicing of the aforementioned genes suggests that PARP12 may influence the immune inflammatory response, energy metabolism, and functional state transitions of microglia at the “posttranscriptional processing” level by modulating the splicing patterns of specific genes. In addition, based on RT-qPCR, we found that PARP12 mRNA knockdown in BV2 microglia is associated not only with altered expression of inflammation-related genes (Fig. 5A) but also with the alternative splicing regulation of selected genes (Fig. 5B). PARP12 mRNA Knockdown is Associated with Downregulation of the Proinflammatory Gene Expression Signature in BV2 Microglial Cells

To assess the impact of PARP12 mRNA knockdown on the inflammatory signature of microglial cells, we performed an overlap analysis between the downregulated DEGs and a set of 143 proinflammatory signature genes obtained by Satoh et al. through integration of three public mouse microglial datasets (GSE49329, GSE77064, and GSE77062). This analysis revealed that 10 genes were significantly downregulated following PARP12 mRNA knockdown (Fig. 6A and B), including the chemokines Cxcl10 and Cxcl2, the immunometabolic enzyme Acod1 (encoding itaconate synthase), and the death receptor Fas. These genes functionally cover multiple aspects of the proinflammatory response [45–48]: Cxcl10 and Cxcl2 mediate chemotactic recruitment of immune cells, Acod1 catalyzes itaconate production to coordinate antibacterial metabolism and inflammatory balance, and Fas participates in cell death signaling regulation. Discussion

This study is the first to systematically characterize transcriptomic profiles and alternative splicing alterations upon PARP12 mRNA knockdown in BV2 microglial cells. We obtained several key conclusions. First, PARP12 silencing broadly suppresses the proinflammatory transcriptional program. The downregulated genes are significantly enriched in NF-κB, TNF, and chemokine signaling pathways, accompanied by downregulation of core inflammatory genes including Ccl2, Ptgs2, and C3, whereas Ccl5 was upregulated. This trend was further confirmed by intersection analysis with published proinflammatory signature genes. Second, classic homeostatic microglial markers such as Tmem119, P2ry12, and Cx3cr1 remained unaffected after PARP12 knockdown, indicating that the reduced proinflammatory response does not impair the basal homeostatic identity of microglia. In total, we identified 287 statistically significant alternative splicing events and further experimentally verified splicing variations of four immune- and metabolism-related genes, namely, Fyb, Tnfaip8, Lrch4, and Pkm.

In BV2 microglial cells, PARP12 possesses both mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase catalytic activity and predicted CCCH-type zinc finger RNA-binding domains and has been shown to localize to cytoplasmic stress granules, suggesting that PARP12 may act at a regulatory nexus where RNA metabolism and inflammatory signaling converge [7, 11, 49]. Given that BV2 cells in this study expressed relatively high basal levels of PARP12, we speculate that under stimulation with damage-associated molecular patterns or inflammatory cytokines, PARP12 may influence the processing, stability, or translational efficiency of mRNAs encoding proinflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and Ccl2) through its RNA-binding capacity. Additionally, PARP12 may alter the alternative splicing patterns of immune-related genes (e.g., Pkm and Tnfaip8) by interfering with spliceosome assembly or the processing of specific pre-mRNAs, thereby affecting the functional properties of their encoded proteins [10, 49, 50]. However, it must be emphasized that the above hypotheses are proposed solely on the basis of PARP12’s predicted functional domains and known subcellular localization, and this study provides no experimental evidence that PARP12 directly binds specific RNA targets or directly regulates splicing in BV2 cells. Moreover, we observed an intriguing phenomenon: although PARP12 knockdown was achieved and confirmed at the mRNA level using various shRNA and siRNA sequences, none of these attempts resulted in a corresponding knockdown at the protein level. This suggests that PARP12 protein in BV2 cells may be subject to tight translational control or may exhibit an exceptionally slow turnover rate, such that even dramatic reductions in mRNA levels fail to translate into protein downregulation. In fact, certain CCCH-type zinc finger proteins have been reported to regulate their own translation by binding to their own mRNAs; whether PARP12 possesses a similar self-regulatory mechanism remains an open question [51]. Several nonmutually exclusive mechanisms could account for this mRNA–protein discrepancy. First, PARP12 protein may have a long half-life in BV2 microglia, such that the 72-h posttransfection window may be insufficient for preexisting protein pools to turn over; because PARP12 protein stability was not measured in this study, this possibility remains an untested hypothesis. Second, translational compensation may occur: cells might sense the reduction in PARP12 mRNA and upregulate translation of the remaining transcripts through feedback regulatory loops; autoregulation of this kind has been reported for other RNA-binding proteins, including CCCH-type zinc finger proteins [8, 51], but whether it operates for PARP12 in BV2 cells was not examined in this study. Third, PARP12 mRNA may harbor regulatory elements in its 5′ or 3′ untranslated regions that confer cap-independent translation or stress-resistant translational efficiency, allowing sustained protein production despite reduced transcript abundance; this is likewise an untested hypothesis, as no UTR-dependent translation assays were performed. Fourth, although we used multiple independent shRNA and siRNA sequences with comparable mRNA knockdown efficiency, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that the antibody employed (anti-PARP12, A14317) exhibits limited sensitivity or specificity for the BV2 microglial PARP12 isoform, although the robust IFN-γ-inducible band detected in shNC cells argues against complete antibody failure. Future studies combining CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genomic deletion with parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mass spectrometry will be required to definitively resolve this issue. It should be emphasized that none of the above mechanisms was examined experimentally in the present study; all are proposed strictly as untested hypotheses to guide future work. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the direct implication of this negative finding is that all transcriptomic and splicing changes observed in this study correlate with reduced PARP12 mRNA levels and cannot be directly attributed to loss of PARP12 protein function. Therefore, the observed transcriptomic and splicing changes should be interpreted as correlative effects associated with reduced PARP12 mRNA levels, rather than as direct consequences of PARP12 protein depletion.

Steady-state marker analysis provides an important dimension for assessing the cellular functional status following PARP12 mRNA knockdown [10, 52]. The results of this study show that typical steady-state markers, including Tmem119, P2ry12, Cx3cr1, and Sall1, did not change significantly after knockdown. This negative finding carries dual significance. First, it indicates that the downregulation of proinflammatory genes is not driven by a deterioration of overall cellular health or a drift toward a dedifferentiated state, but rather reflects a relatively selective transcriptional reprogramming. From a therapeutic perspective, this offers a positive implication: if PARP12-targeted intervention can selectively suppress pro-inflammatory programs without compromising immune surveillance functions, its therapeutic window might be more favorable. Nevertheless, this result must be interpreted with caution. The basal expression levels of steady-state markers in BV2 cells are inherently much lower than those in primary or in vivo microglia, so the amount of information provided by the experimental results is quite limited [36, 37]. Whether PARP12 deficiency still fails to perturb these markers in primary microglia is a question that must be addressed in future studies.

Based on RNA-seq analysis, a total of 287 significant differential ASEs were identified in BV2 microglial cells following PARP12 mRNA knockdown. To further explore the potential role of PARP12 in neuroinflammation-related splicing regulation, four genes—Fyb, Tnfaip8, Lrch4, and Pkm—were selected for validation, all of which have well-defined functions in immune- and inflammation-related pathways. Among them, Fyb is a key gene closely associated with Th1 cell infiltration in neuropathic pain (NP) [53]. Xue et al. found that reducing Tnfaip8 by transfection with a specific siRNA (siTNFAIP8) significantly alleviated LPS-induced reduction in cell viability and inflammatory response in mouse BV2 microglial cells [40]. Thibaud Rivière et al. reported that Lrch4 mRNA levels were downregulated in LPS-stimulated macrophages exposed to E. coli K12 and in RAW264.7 cells as well as in human platelets [42]. PKM2 is involved in neuropathic pain by regulating ERK and STAT3 activation in the rat spinal cord [44]. All the above genes were subjected to AS analysis, and the results were validated by qPCR, showing consistency with the RNA-seq data. These ASEs represent an important preliminary observation. Notably, the functional positioning of PARP12 as an interferon-stimulated gene, together with its predicted RNA-binding domain, suggests that PARP12 may act as an RNA-binding protein. Indeed, multiple lines of evidence support this possibility: PARP12 has been shown to localize to cytoplasmic stress granules, which are critical hubs for regulating mRNA translation, storage, and degradation[7, 11, 54]. Moreover, its family member PARP13/ZAP has been definitively established as an RNA-binding protein with important antiviral functions [55, 56]. Therefore, we speculate that PARP12 deficiency may indirectly interfere with the subcellular localization of splicing factors or the metabolic stability of specific pre-mRNAs, likely by affecting its RNA-binding activity or disrupting its association with stress granules, ultimately leading to the widespread splicing changes observed in this study.

In the present study, overlap analysis of downregulated DEGs with proinflammatory (M1-like) signature gene sets from public databases revealed ten significantly downregulated genes, suggesting that PARP12 mRNA knockdown is associated with attenuation of the proinflammatory gene expression program. However, this result should not be simplistically interpreted as “inhibition of M1 polarization” or “a shift toward M2 phenotype.” A clear consensus has been established in the field of microglial biology: the classical M1/M2 binary classification, which was originally based on in vitro stimulation conditions, cannot accurately reflect the complexity and dynamic plasticity of microglial transcriptomes in vivo [57, 58]. Paolicelli et al., in a field-wide consensus statement, recommended replacing this dichotomous classification with state-specific phenotypes such as DAM, IRM, ARM, and LDAM [59]. Within this framework, pro-inflammatory signatures can be observed in multiple functional states—disease-associated microglia (DAM) upregulate Cxcl10 and Ccl2 to mediate immune responses, interferon-responsive microglia (IRM) feature ISG signatures, and activated response microglia (ARM) are enriched for immediate early inflammatory genes [60–62]. Therefore, the downregulation of ten genes in this study does not indicate a switch to any particular polarization state; rather, it reflects a selective attenuation of the proinflammatory program at the transcriptional level, a phenomenon that can occur across a spectrum of states. Given that PARP12 itself is a canonical ISG, whether the transcriptional changes following its mRNA knockdown are associated with a selective reduction in IRM features warrants further refinement in future studies by integrating core IRM gene sets.

Collectively, this study has several limitations at the following levels that need to be addressed in future work. First, PARP12 protein levels were never successfully knocked down, which is the most fundamental limitation of this study. This result fundamentally constrains the causal inference capability of the present work. Future studies must generate CRISPR/Cas9-mediated complete PARP12 knockout cell lines to establish a causal link between PARP12 deficiency and the observed effects. If residual protein remains after gene knockout, further experiments—such as auto-MARylation activity assays and stress granule colocalization analyses—should be performed to distinguish loss of expression from loss of catalytic function or compartmental localization. Second, AS changes were only detected at the RNA level; neither the shift in protein isoform ratios nor the molecular mechanisms underlying the splicing changes were validated. Future work should combine protein-level validation of targeted splicing events with techniques such as RIP-seq/CLIP-seq to determine whether PARP12 directly participates in splicing regulation. Finally, as a single-center in vitro study based on an immortalized BV2 cell line, all findings need to be independently validated in primary microglia and in vivo pathological models. BV2 cells are known to differ from primary cells in terms of steady-state marker expression, interferon response characteristics, and the execution mechanisms of programmed necrosis, and these differences may affect the translatability of PARP12 functional phenotypes.

In summary, this study demonstrates that PARP12 mRNA knockdown is associated with attenuation of the proinflammatory transcriptional program and widespread AS changes in BV2 microglial cells. Downregulated genes are enriched in inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and TNF, most core immune genes are coordinately downregulated, whereas a small subset (Ccl5, Cd81, and S1pr1) is upregulated, and proinflammatory gene signatures are globally downregulated. Steady-state microglial markers remain relatively stable after knockdown. Meanwhile, AS alterations are observed in genes including Fyb, Tnfaip8, Lrch4, and Pkm. However, due to the core limitation of insufficient protein-level knockdown, the above findings represent only correlative observations, and a definitive causal relationship has not been established. Future studies integrating CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout and in vivo pathological models are needed to clarify the precise functional role of PARP12 in microglia.
