Topological junctions for one–dimensional systems
David Gontier Clément Tauber

Abstract
. We study and classify the emergence of protected edge modes at the junction of one-dimensional materials. Using symmetries of Lagrangian planes in boundary symplectic spaces, we present a novel proof of the periodic table of topological insulators in one dimension. We show that edge modes necessarily arise at the junction of two materials having different topological indices. Our approach provides a systematic framework for understanding symmetry–protected modes in one–dimension. It does not rely on periodic nor ergodicity and covers a wide range of operators which includes both continuous and discrete models. © 2024 by the authors. 1.
Introduction

The classification of bulk gapped Hamiltonians based on their symmetries results in the renowned
tenfold way of topological insulators
. This framework identifies 10 fundamental symmetry classes that encompass three key physical symmetries: time-reversal (T), charge conjugation (C), and chiral (S) symmetry. Each symmetry class is associated with a unique topological index, which enjoys the following important property: for two insulators within the same symmetry class but with different indices, any symmetry-preserving transformation between them must involve a metallic phase where the energy gap closes. The classification result was first tackled by Altland and Zirnbauer in
[
AZ97
]
in the context of random matrices (see also
[
HHZ05
]
), where Cartan’s classification of symmetric spaces from
[
Car26
,
Car27
]
naturally appears and provides a label for each symmetry class. The classification was then developed simultaneously by Kitaev in
[
Kit09
]
and Ryu, Schnyder, Furusaki and Ludwig in
[
Ryu+10
]
, where the full table of topological insulators appears. It was then extended to various systems using different approaches from
K
-theory to functional analysis and is still an active topic of research
[
FM13
,
PS16
,
Thi16
,
BCR16
,
GS16
,
KK18
,
AMZ20
,
AT22
,
GMP22
,
CS23
]
.

Topological insulators have the property that protected edge modes appear when gapped bulk Hamiltonians are restricted to half-infinite samples. This is known as
bulk-edge correspondence

[
Hal82
,
Hat93
,
Hat93a
]
. An intuitive explanation is the following: cutting such a system induces a natural transition between the material and the surrounding vacuum. This transition can be visualized as a large box gradually moving towards the empty space. If this cut is implemented as a smooth transition at very large scales, the system within the box consistently maintains the characteristics of a bulk material. Since the void has null index, and in the case where the topological insulator has a non null index, the gap must close somewhere. The corresponding eigenstates come from the cut, hence the denomination
edge modes
.

Edge modes are usually studied directly in half-infinite samples but, to the best of our knowledge, the previous argument has not been made rigorous except for some specific models or in a given symmetry class
[
GP13
,
Dro21
,
Bal22
,
Bal+23
,
RT24
,
Bal
]
. More generally, one can ask the following question:
consider a junction between two topological insulators in the same symmetry class. If their two indices differ, do we always observe edge modes at the junction?
Note that this question differs from the original classification, where deformations are considered solely among gapped/bulk systems. When considering junctions, we must leave the realms of gapped operators. The goal of this paper is to give a positive and systematic answer to this question in dimension
d=1
. We prove that protected edge modes must appear at the junction of two half-chains having different indices. In the process, we give a straightforward procedure to classify a wide range of one-dimensional operators based on their symmetries.

Our classification in
d=1
relies on efficient concepts from ordinary differential equations, originally developed in
[
Gon20
,
Gon23
]
. To each bulk Hamiltonian with a gap, one can associate a Lagrangian plane in a symplectic boundary space, and a unitary matrix, according to Leray’s theorem
[
Ler78
]
(see Theorem
2.5
below). Classifying symmetric bulk Hamiltonians reduces to classifying symmetric Lagrangian planes, which further amounts to classifying subsets of symmetric unitary matrices. This identification recovers Cartan’s symmetric spaces in a natural way. We insist on the fact that we do not assume any periodicity or ergodicity of the Hamiltonians. In particular, disordered and inhomogeneous media are naturally covered within the approach. Our result would extend to higher dimension if we moreover assume periodicity in the longitudinal directions of the junction. In this case, after applying an appropriate Bloch transform, we recover a family of models on a line (or on a “channel” for continuous models, see
[
Gon23
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we explain the relationship between one dimensional differential operators, Lagrangian planes, and unitary matrices. We then state the two main results of the manuscript. Our first main result, Theorem
2.10
concerns the classification of Lagrangian planes respecting symmetries. Our second main result, Theorem
2.14
shows the appearance of edge modes for symmetry protected topological junctions. We also present various classes of models for which our theory applies. We then prove the first result in Section
3
, and the second one in Section
4
. Finally, Section
5
is a case study where we apply our results to junctions between two continuous Dirac operators with constant potentials. This simple case covers all the non-trivial symmetry classes.

Notation

Let us gather here the conventions that we use for matrices. We denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
the set of
N\times N
matrices with complex entries. For
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we denote by
z^{*}=\overline{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}
the complex conjugate. For
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
, we denote by
A^{*}
its adjoint
(A^{*})_{ij}=\overline{A_{ji}}
, by
A^{T}
its transpose
(A^{T})_{ij}=A_{ji}
, and by
\overline{A}
its conjugate
(\overline{A})_{ij}=\overline{A_{ij}}
. In particular,
A^{*}=\overline{A}^{T}
.

We will encounter the following sets of matrices. First, the unitary and orthogonal groups

{\rm U}(N):=\left\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{-1}=M^{*}\right\},\qquad{\rm O}(N):=\left\{M\in{\rm U}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ \overline{M}=M\right\}.

Then the complex and real symmetric and anti–symmetric matrices:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}):=\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{*}=M\},

\displaystyle\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{N}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{C}}):=\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{T}=M\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}):=\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{*}=-M\},

\displaystyle\qquad{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{C}}):=\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{T}=-M\}.

Finally, in the case where
N=2n
is even, the symplectic matrices

(1.1)

{\rm Sp}(2n,{\mathbb{C}}):=\{M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\ M^{T}\Omega M=\Omega\},\qquad\Omega:=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathbb{I}}_{n}\\
-{\mathbb{I}}_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

and the symplectic group
{\rm Sp}(n):={\rm Sp}(2n,{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\rm U}(2n)
.

2.
General setting and main result

2.1.
From operators to Lagrangian planes and unitaries

Let us first explain the relationships between differential operators, Lagrangian planes in a boundary symplectic space, and unitary matrices. In what follows, we restrict our attention to one-dimensional systems.

2.1.1.
From operator to Lagrangian planes

Our method relies on the following observations (see also
[
Gon20
,
Gon23
]
). We consider a general differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}
of order
p
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
which generates a densely defined self-adjoint operator
H
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
, describing the dynamics of independent electrons in one-dimension. We emphasize that, compared to previous works, we do not assume any particular structure for the differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}
, apart that

(1)

it defines a self-adjoint operator
H
,

(2)

it defines a symplectic form with (
2.4
) (see below),

(3)

the spectrum of
H
is not the full line
{\mathbb{R}}
(so we can find
E\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma(H)
).

Whenever these conditions are satisfied, we will prove that we can associate an index to the operator
H
at energy
E
. Of course, checking these points, and specifically point (3), might be difficult if no further structure are assumed. In the context of Schrödinger operators for instance, points (1) and (2) are automatically satisfied for
{\mathcal{L}}=-\partial_{tt}^{2}+V(t)
whenever
V\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is bounded, but checking whether there are gaps in the spectrum can be a delicate question.

First, Cauchy’s theory for ordinary differential equations states that for any
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the set of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}_{E}=\{\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\,|\,{\mathcal{L}}\psi=E\psi\}
forms a vector space of dimension
Mp
, parametrized
e.g.
by the initial conditions
(\psi(0),\psi^{\prime}(0),\ldots,\psi^{(p-1)}(0))\in({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
. This means that the evaluation map

(2.1)

{\rm Tr}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\mathcal{S}}_{E}&\to&({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}\\
\psi&\mapsto&(\psi(0),\psi^{\prime}(0),\ldots,\psi^{(p-1)}(0))\end{array}\right.

is a linear bijection. Moreover, a solution
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}
is an eigenstate of
H
iff
\psi
is square integrable, that is

{\rm Ker}(H-E)={\mathcal{S}}_{E}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M}).

Such a function
\psi
is square integrable on the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
iff it is square integrable at
+\infty
and at
-\infty
. This motivates to introduce the following vector subspaces of
({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
:

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\ell_{E}^{+}&:={\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{S}}_{E}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})\right)\\
\ell_{E}^{-}&:={\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{S}}_{E}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{-},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})\right)\\
\ell_{E}&:={\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{S}}_{E}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})\right)\end{cases},\quad\text{so that}\quad\ell_{E}=\ell_{E}^{+}\cap\ell_{E}^{-}.

In other words,
\ell_{E}^{+}
is the set of initial conditions at
t=0
so that the corresponding Cauchy solution is square integrable at
+\infty
. The fact that
{\rm Tr}
is bijective implies that

{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}\,(H-E)={\rm dim}\,\ell_{E}={\rm dim}\left(\ell_{E}^{+}\cap\ell_{E}^{-}\right).

The key point is that we can read the multiplicity of
E
(a bulk quantity) solely from the crossing of two vector spaces defined at
t=0
(which looks like an edge quantity). Thus
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
naturally appear as a pivotal tool for bulk-edge correspondence. In addition, we note that the Cauchy problem
{\mathcal{L}}\psi=E\psi
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is decoupled from the one on
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
, so the vector spaces
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
solely depends on the behaviour of
{\mathcal{L}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\pm}
. These objects are therefore also adapted to study junctions between two materials, as we will see below.

On the other hand, the self-adjointness and locality of
H
induces a natural symplectic form on the boundary space
({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
. Recall that a symplectic form
\omega:{\mathbb{C}}^{K}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{K}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a non degenerate continuous sesquilinear form satisfying

\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K},\quad\omega(x,y)=-\overline{\omega(y,x)}.

The usual Hilbertian structure of
{\mathbb{C}}^{K}
shows that there is a linear map
J:{\mathbb{C}}^{K}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{K}
so that

(2.3)

\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K},\qquad\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{K}}.

The condition that
\omega
is symplectic shows that
J
is invertible and satisfies
J^{*}=-J
.

In our case, the symplectic form
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as follows. Let
x,y\in({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
, and let
\phi,\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
be
any
compactly supported smooth functions with
{\rm Tr}(\phi)=x
and
{\rm Tr}(\psi)=y
. We set

(2.4)

\omega(x,y):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\langle\phi,{\mathcal{L}}\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle{\mathcal{L}}\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\Big)(t){\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\langle\phi,H\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle H\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\Big)(t){\mathrm{d}}t.

In most cases, the fact that
\omega(x,y)
does not depend on the choices of
\psi
and
\phi
, and the fact that
\omega
is a symplectic form can be checked directly. Although we considered
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
, the left–hand side of (
2.4
) is a continuous bilinear form on
\left\{\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{M}),\ {\mathcal{L}}\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})\right\}
, so formula (
2.4
) also holds for
\phi
and
\psi
in this set (and in particular for
\phi,\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{+}
). Here is a typical example (we give other examples below in Section
2.4
).

Example 2.1

(Schrödinger operators)
.

Consider
{\mathcal{L}}=-\partial_{tt}^{2}+V(t)
of order
p=2
. We assume that
V(t)
is pointwise a hermitian
M\times M
matrix, uniformly bounded in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the operator
H:=-\partial_{tt}^{2}+V(t)
is self-adjoint with domain
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
. For all
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
, an integration by part gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\langle\phi,H\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle H\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\right](t){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\langle\phi,\psi^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle\phi^{\prime\prime},\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\right](t){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\langle\phi(0),\psi^{\prime}(0)\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle\phi^{\prime}(0),\psi(0)\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}},

which is of the form
\omega({\rm Tr}(\phi),{\rm Tr}(\psi))
, with the symplectic form
\omega:{\mathbb{C}}^{2M}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{2M}\to{\mathbb{C}}
given by

(2.5)

\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2M}},\quad\text{with}\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathbb{I}}_{M}\\
-{\mathbb{I}}_{M}&0\end{pmatrix}.

As claimed,
\omega(x,y)
is independent of the choices of
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
as long as
x={\rm Tr}(\phi)
and
y={\rm Tr}(\psi)
. So (
2.4
) indeed defines a symplectic form for Schrödinger operators. Note that the symplectic form
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)
is independent of the potential
V
.

Definition 2.2
.

A vector subspace
\ell\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{K}
is a Lagrangian plane (for
\omega
) if it satisfies
\ell=\ell^{\circ}
, and is isotropic if
\ell\subset\ell^{\circ}
, where

(2.6)

\ell^{\circ}:=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K},\quad\omega(x,y)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad y\in\ell\right\}.

We have
{\rm dim}(\ell^{\circ})=K-{\rm dim}(\ell)
. In particular, Lagrangian planes can exist only if
K
is even, and in this case, they are of dimension
K/2
. For all
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the vector spaces
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are isotropic. Indeed, consider
x,y\in\ell_{E}^{\pm}
, and let
\psi,\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{+}
so that
{\rm Tr}(\psi)=x
and
{\rm Tr}(\phi)=y
. Since
{\mathcal{L}}\psi=E\psi
and
{\mathcal{L}}\phi=E\phi
, and since these functions are square–integrable at
+\infty
, we can write

(2.7)

\omega(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}[\langle\phi,{\mathcal{L}}\psi\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{L}}\phi,\psi\rangle]=\int_{0}^{\infty}[\langle\phi,E\psi\rangle-\langle\phi,E\psi\rangle]=0,

where we used that
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The following result shows that if in addition
E
is in the resolvent set of the bulk operator, then the vector spaces
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are Lagrangian. This result was proved in
[
Gon23
, Theorem 17]
. We give a short proof below in Appendix
A.2
for completeness.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
E\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma(H)
, then
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are Lagrangian planes of
(({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p},\omega)
, and we have

\ell_{E}^{+}\oplus\ell_{E}^{-}=({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}.

Remark 2.4
.

If
K:=Mp
is not even, there are no Lagrangian planes. So any such operator
H
must satisfy
\sigma(H)={\mathbb{R}}
, and there is no gap. This happens for instance with the differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}=-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}+V(t)
with
V\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, so
p=M=K=1
. The corresponding operator
H=-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}+V
is self-adjoint with domain
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, and its spectrum must be the full line
{\mathbb{R}}
.

In what follows, we assume
K=Mp
to be even, and set

N:=\frac{1}{2}Mp.

2.1.2.
From Lagrangian planes to unitaries

In order to classify Lagrangian planes of
({\mathbb{C}}^{2N},\omega)
, we use Leray’s theorem
[
Ler78
]
(see also
[
Gon23
, Lemma 7]
) which states that there is a one-to-one correspondence between such planes and unitary matrices
U\in{\rm U}(N)
. Recall that
\omega
is described by a matrix
J
satisfying
J^{*}=-J
. In particular,
A:=-{\mathrm{i}}J
is self-adjoint, hence diagonalizable. This gives a natural splitting
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}=K_{+}\oplus K_{-}
, so that
-{\mathrm{i}}J
is positive definite on
K_{+}
, and negative definite on
K_{-}
. The dimension of
K_{+}
is the number of positive eigenvalues of
A
. In what follows, we choose a basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
in which
J
has the block matrix form

(2.8)

J=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}A_{+}&0\\
0&-{\mathrm{i}}A_{-}\end{pmatrix},

where
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
are positive definite operators acting respectively on
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
. Leray’s theorem states that if we write
x\in\ell
as
x=x_{+}+x_{-}
with
x_{+}\in K_{+}
and
x_{-}\in K_{-}
, then
x_{-}
can be recovered from
x_{+}
thanks to some unitary
U
. We state a version of Leray’s theorem which is convenient for our purpose.

Theorem 2.5

(Leray
[
Ler78
]
)
.

The symplectic space
({\mathbb{C}}^{2N},\omega)
has Lagrangian planes iff
{\rm dim}(K_{+})={\rm dim}(K_{-})=N
. In this case, the Lagrangian planes of
({\mathbb{C}}^{2N},\omega)
are in one-to-one correspondence with unitaries
U\in{\rm U}(N)
:
\ell
is Lagrangian plane of
({\mathbb{C}}^{2N},\omega)
iff there is
U\in{\rm U}(N)
so that

(2.9)

\ell=\ell_{U}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x\\
\sqrt{A_{-}}^{-1}U\sqrt{A_{+}}x\end{pmatrix},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\right\}.

We provide a short proof for completeness below in Appendix
A.1
. Let us mention that the initial Leray’s theorem construct a unitary from
K_{+}
to
K_{-}
(up to dilations). With our choice of basis, we have
K_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
K_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\end{pmatrix}
.

Example 2.6
.

We continue Example
2.1
and consider the simplest case where
M=1
and
V=0
. Then
H=-\Delta
and
\sigma(H)=\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The matrix
J
representing
\omega
is of the form (
2.8
), namely
J=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}&0\\
0&-{\mathrm{i}}\end{pmatrix}
, if we change basis and define the trace operator as
{\rm Tr}(\psi)=2^{-1/2}(\psi(0)-{\mathrm{i}}\psi^{\prime}(0),\psi(0)+{\mathrm{i}}\psi^{\prime}(0))
. One can check that for any
E<0
, elements of
{\mathcal{S}}_{E}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+})
are all of the form
\psi(x)=2^{1/2}\alpha{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{|E|}x}
with
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
so that

\ell_{E}^{+}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(1+{\mathrm{i}}\sqrt{|E|})\\
\alpha(1-{\mathrm{i}}\sqrt{|E|}).\end{pmatrix},\ \alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\right\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x\\
U_{E}x.\end{pmatrix},\ x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\right\},\quad\text{with}\quad U_{E}:=\frac{1-{\mathrm{i}}\sqrt{|E|}}{1+{\mathrm{i}}\sqrt{|E|}}\in{\rm U}(1).

We insist on the fact that for each
E<0
,
\ell_{E}^{+}
is associated to a
single
unitary
U_{E}
, without specifying any kind of boundary condition for
H
at
t=0
.

2.2.
Main result: classification of symmetric Lagrangian planes

Symmetries of
H
translate into ones for the Lagrangian planes
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
, and for the corresponding unitaries. This gives a direct link between the symmetries of one-dimensional operators, and the original symmetric spaces of Cartan
[
Car26
,
Car27
]
, which are subgroups of the unitary group
{\rm U}(N)
.

2.2.1.
Symmetries for operators

For Hamiltonians
H
, we consider the three fundamental symmetries
T
,
C
and
S
, which act only on internal degrees of freedom encoded in
\mathbb{C}^{M}
. Thus we identify
T
on
\mathbb{C}^{M}
with
{\mathds{1}}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\otimes T
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})\approx L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
, and similarly for
C
and
S
. Recall that an operator
U
(resp.
A
) on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
is unitary (resp. anti-unitary) if

\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M},\quad\langle Ux,Uy\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle,\qquad\text{resp.}\qquad\langle Ax,Ay\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle=\overline{\langle x,y\rangle}.

Definition 2.7

(Symmetries for operators
H
)
.

•

a Time-reversal symmetry
T
is an anti-unitary operator on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
such that
T^{2}=\varepsilon_{T}{\mathds{1}}
, with
\varepsilon_{T}=\pm 1
and with
HT=TH
;

•

a Particle-hole symmetry
C
is an anti-unitary operator on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
such that
C^{2}=\varepsilon_{C}{\mathds{1}}
, with
\varepsilon_{C}=\pm 1
and with
HC=-CH
;

•

a Chiral symmetry
S
is a unitary operator on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
such that
S^{2}={\mathds{1}}
and
HS=-SH
.

The symmetry
T
is called bosonic if
\varepsilon_{T}=+1
, and fermionic if
\varepsilon_{T}=-1
, and similarly for the symmetry
C
.

If both
T
and
C
are present, we assume that their product
S:=TC
is an
S
–symmetry. This is equivalent to requiring that
T
and
C
commute, or anti-commute, depending on their bosonic/fermionic nature. Indeed, the condition
S^{2}=1
implies
TC=\varepsilon_{C}\varepsilon_{T}CT
. Listing all possibilities of the symmetries leads to the celebrated tenfold way of topological insulators
[
Ryu+10
]
.

2.2.2.
Symmetries for the symplectic space

As claimed, the symmetries of
H
translate into symmetries for the symplectic space
(({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p},\omega)
.

Lemma 2.8

(Symmetries for symplectic form
\omega
)
.

Assume that
T
,
C
and/or
S
are (anti-) unitary operators on
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
which are respectively
T
,
C
and
S
symmetries for
H
. Then, setting
T:=T^{\otimes p}
on
({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
and similarly for
C
and
S
, the corresponding symplectic form
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
2.4
) satisfies that for all
x,y\in({\mathbb{C}}^{M})^{p}
,

(2.10)

\boxed{\omega(Tx,Ty)=-\omega(y,x),\qquad\omega(Cx,Cy)=\omega(y,x),\qquad\omega(Sx,Sy)=-\omega(x,y).}

In terms of the operator
J
describing the symplectic form in (
2.3
), this is equivalent to

(2.11)

\boxed{TJ=JT,\quad CJ=-JC,\quad SJ=-JS.}

Proof.

For the
T
–symmetry, we have, for all
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
, the pointwise identity

\langle T\phi,HT\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}=\langle T\phi,TH\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}=\langle H\psi,\phi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}},

where we used that
TH=TH
and that
T
is anti-unitary. So

\langle T\phi,HT\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle HT\phi,T\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}=\langle H\psi,\phi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle\psi,H\phi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}.

Integrating on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}

. In particular, we have

\langle JTx,Ty\rangle=-\langle Tx,JTy\rangle=-\omega(Tx,Ty)=\omega(y,x)=\langle y,Jx\rangle=\langle TJx,Ty\rangle,

where we used that

, the identity
\omega(Tx,Ty)=-\omega(y,x)
and the fact that

is anti-unitary. We deduce as wanted that

. The proofs are similar for the

∎

Definition 2.9

2.3.
Main results for operators

We now use our classification of Lagrangian planes in the context of condensed matter, using the relationship between these objects.

2.3.1.
Bulk operators

Recall that our assumptions on the initial differential operator are very loose, so it is unclear yet what we really mean by ¡¡bulk¿¿ operators. In what follows, we say that
H
is a
bulk operator
(at energy
E
) if
H
is self-adjoint and if
E\notin\sigma(H)
. The set of bulk operators has a natural topology, induced for instance by the distance

{\rm dist}(H_{0},H_{1}):=\left\|(E-H_{0})^{-1}-(E-H_{1})^{-1}\right\|_{\rm op}.

We say that
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are path–connected if there is a path
(H_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
for this topology with
H_{s=0}=H_{0}
and
H_{s=1}=H_{1}
. In particular, it implies that the
gap
does not close:
E\notin H_{s}
for all
s\in[0,1]
. We say that these operators are path connected in the Cartan class
\Gamma
if the path can be chosen so that
H_{s}
is in the symmetric class
\Gamma
for all
s\in[0,1]
. In particular,
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are in the same symmetric class
\Gamma
.

First, we record a simple Lemma, which connects the symmetries of the operators with the symmetries of the corresponding Lagrangian planes.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
H
be a bulk operator at energy
E
in the symmetric class
\Gamma
, with
E=0
if
\Gamma
has a
C
and/or
S
symmetry. Then
\ell_{E}^{\pm}\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

This comes from the fact that the
T
,
C
, and
S
symmetries only acts on the internal degrees of freedom. In particular,
\Psi
is square integrable at
\pm\infty
iff
T\Psi
,
C\Psi
and/or
S\Psi
are square integrable at
\pm\infty
. In addition, if
H\Psi=E\Psi
, we have

H(T\Psi)=E(T\Psi),\quad H(C\Psi)=-E(C\Psi),\quad H(S\Psi)=-E(S\Psi).

This proves that
T\ell_{E}^{\pm}=\ell_{E}^{\pm}
,
C\ell_{E}^{\pm}=\ell_{-E}^{\pm}
and
S\ell_{E}^{\pm}=\ell_{-E}^{\pm}
.
∎

In what follows, when
C
or
S
is present we shall always focus at
E=0
. Following
[
Gon23
]
, we define the bulk/edge index of
any
bulk operator as follows.

Definition 2.12

(Bulk/edge index)
.

For a bulk operator
H
in the Cartan class
\Gamma
, we define its bulk/edge index by

{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H):={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{+}):={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(U_{E}^{+}),

where
U_{E}^{+}
is the unitary constructed from
\ell_{E}^{+}
via Leray’s theorem.

Although this index looks like an edge quantity, it really depends on the bulk properties of
H
. In particular, we did not impose any boundary condition at
t=0
, and the sets
\ell_{E}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{+}
really depends on the behaviour of
H
on the full right half line. We could also have used the full left half line. We will see below that
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{+})
and
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{-})
are related. With this, we can first recall the classification of bulk operators
[
Kit09
,
Ryu+10
]
.

Proposition 2.13

(Classification of bulk operators)
.

If
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are bulk operators at energy
E
, which are path–connected in the Cartan class
\Gamma
. Then
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H_{0})={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H_{1})
.

Proof.

We only sketch the proof. If
H_{s}
is a path connecting
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
in the class
\Gamma
, then
H_{s}
defines a symplectic space
({\mathbb{C}}^{Mp},\omega_{s})
for each
s\in[0,1]
, and Lagrangian planes
\ell_{E,s}^{+}
. In addition, the map
s\mapsto\omega_{s}
is continuous for the topology of bilinear forms, and the map
s\mapsto\ell_{E,s}^{+}
is continuous for the topology of vector spaces in
{\mathbb{C}}^{Mp}
. In particular, the corresponding unitaries
U_{E,s}^{+}
given by Leray’s theorem is a continuous family of unitaries in the Cartan symmetric space
U_{\Gamma}
, hence stays in the same connected component of
U_{\Gamma}
. So
U_{E,0}
and
U_{E,1}
are path–connected, hence have the same index.
∎

A general proof that
s\mapsto\omega_{s}
is continuous would be rather tedious, but in practice, the maps
\omega_{s}
are explicit, and continuity can be checked directly. For instance, in the context of Schrödinger operators, if one changes the potential
V_{0}
into
V_{1}
continuously, then
\omega_{s}=\omega
is independent of
V_{s}
along the path, see Example
2.1
.

2.3.2.
Junctions

We now turn to the question whether interface modes appear at the junction of two bulk materials in the same symmetry class, but with different indices. More specifically, we consider two materials described by two differential operators
{\mathcal{L}}^{L}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{R}
of order
p
, and consider the
junction
differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
defined by

\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}),\qquad({\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\phi)(x):=\begin{cases}({\mathcal{L}}^{L}\phi)(x),\quad\text{if}\quad x<0,\\
({\mathcal{L}}^{R}\phi)(x),\quad\text{if}\quad x\geq 0.\end{cases}

This operators somehow describes a
hard truncation
, or
hard junction
between the left and right operators. The hard truncation has the advantage to immediately infer that, with obvious notation,

(2.12)

\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}=\ell_{E}^{R,+}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{E}^{\sharp,-}=\ell_{E}^{L,-}.

However our framework also allows to handle continuous junctions, see Section
4.4
below.

We make the following assumptions:

•

the differential operators
{\mathcal{L}}^{L}
,
{\mathcal{L}}^{R}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
define self-adjoint operators
H^{L}
,
H^{R}
and
H^{\sharp}
with the same domain
H^{p}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
;

•

the operators
H^{L}
and
H^{R}
are in the symmetry class
\Gamma
, and are bulk operators at energy
E
(in the presence of
C
– and/or
S
–symmetry, we take
E=0
).

In particular,
Mp=2N
is even, see Remark
2.4
above. The fact that
H^{\sharp}
is self-adjoint is, actually, quite a strong assumption. It implies in particular that the symplectic forms
\omega_{L}
and
\omega_{R}
constructed from
H^{L}
and
H^{R}
coincide. Indeed, we have, for
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\langle\phi,H^{\sharp}\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle H^{\sharp}\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\right)(t){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}\left(\langle\phi,H^{L}\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle H^{L}\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\right)(t){\mathrm{d}}t+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}}\left(\langle\phi,H^{R}\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}-\langle H^{R}\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}\right)(t){\mathrm{d}}t

(2.13)

\displaystyle=-\omega_{L}({\rm Tr}(\phi),{\rm Tr}(\psi))+\omega_{R}({\rm Tr}(\phi),{\rm Tr}(\psi)).

In the last line, we used a similar computation with
H^{L}
to prove that the integral on
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
equals the one on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, up to a minus sign. In other words, the self-adjointness of
H^{\sharp}
ensures that one cannot make a junction between two unrelated systems.

We can now state our main theorem about junctions. In order to state it, we distinguish the cases of class
{\rm D}
and
{\rm DIII}
(where the index in valued in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
–valued), and the cases of class
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}
, where the index is valued in
\{0,\cdots,N\}
with
N:=\frac{1}{2}Mp
. In all these classes, the
C
and/or
S
symmetry is present, so we only focus on the energy
E=0
.

Theorem 2.14

(Bulk-boundary correspondence for junctions)
.

Under the previous assumptions, we have

•

(Classes
{\rm D}
and
{\rm DIII}
)
. If
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{L})\neq{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{R})
, then
{\rm dim}\ {\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})\geq 1
.

•

(Classes
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}
)
.
{\rm dim}\ {\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})\geq\left|{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{R})-{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{L})\right|
.

The right–hand side of the inequalities can be identified with a
relative
index, of the form
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{L},H^{R})
, defined in a straightforward manner depending on the class. In many physical situations, this relative index is more relevant than the absolute one from the previous sections. For example, the index value for chiral chains may depend on the choice of unit cell. By nature, the index for class
{\rm AIII}
is only relative, and can only compare two models
[
PS16
]
. More recently, it has been shown that, in dimension
d=2
and for the classes
{\rm A}
or
{\rm D}
, an absolute index may not exist for some operators with unbounded spectra, and only a relative index between two operators makes sense, see
[
GJT21
,
Bal22
,
RT24
]
.

Theorem
2.14
states that if
H^{L}
and
H^{R}
have different indices, then the junction between
H^{L}
to
H^{R}
must have at least this relative index number of zero modes at the junction. We call these modes the
protected edge modes
. Note that the inequality is large: the junction Hamiltonian
H^{\sharp}
may have additional (unprotected) zero modes.

Theorem
2.14
is proved in Section
4
. The main tools that we use are summed up in the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.15
.

(1)

Let
\ell_{A}
and
\ell_{B}
be two Lagrangian planes in
({\mathbb{C}}^{2N},\omega)
, with corresponding unitaries
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
in
{\rm U}(N)
. Then

{\rm dim}(\ell_{A}\cap\ell_{B})={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U_{A}U_{B}^{*}-1).

(2)

With the same notation as before, and with obvious notation, we have

{\rm dim}\left(H^{\sharp}-E\right)={\rm dim}\left(\ell^{R,+}_{E}\cap\ell^{L,-}_{E}\right)={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U_{E}^{R,+}\left(U_{E}^{L,-}\right)^{*}-1).

Proof.

The first point is standard, and is proved
e.g.
in
[
Gon23
, Lemma 10]
. For the second point, we recall from (
2.2
) that

{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp}-E)={\rm dim}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp})={\rm dim}\left(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}\cap\ell_{E}^{\sharp,-}\right)={\rm dim}\left(\ell^{R,+}_{E}\cap\ell^{L,-}_{E}\right).

where we used (
2.12
) in the last equality.
∎

In other words, one can read the multiplicity of
E
as an eigenvalue of the junction operator
H^{\sharp}
solely from the crossings of two Lagrangian planes depending only on the bulk operators
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
.

2.4.
Examples

Before turning to the proofs, let us give some examples of operators where our theory applies. We have already seen the case of Schrödinger operators in Example
2.1
.

2.4.1.
Dirac operators

Consider a differential operator of order
p=1
, of the form

{\mathcal{L}}=(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t})(\sigma_{3}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{N})+V(t),\quad\text{with}\quad\sigma_{3}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

where
V(t)
is pointwise a
(2N)\times(2N)
hermitian matrix, uniformly bounded for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This defines a self-adjoint operator
\not{D}
with domain
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N})
. For all
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N})
, the integration formula gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\langle\phi,\not{D}\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}-\langle\not{D}\phi,\psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\right](t){\mathrm{d}}t={\mathrm{i}}\langle\phi(0),\sigma_{3}\psi(0)\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}.

This only depends on the value of
\phi
and
\psi
at
0
, and not on the choices of
\phi
and
\psi
, and defines the symplectic form
\omega:{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
given by

\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}},\quad\text{with}\quad J:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}&0\\
0&-{\mathrm{i}}\end{pmatrix}={\mathrm{i}}\sigma_{3}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{N}.

Note that, as in the Schrödinger case, this symplectic form is independent of the choices of the potential
V
.

2.4.2.
Tight–binding models

We consider tight-binding Schrödinger–like operators
h
acting on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, of the form

\forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad(h\psi)_{n}=a_{n-1}^{*}\psi_{n-1}+b_{n}\psi_{n}+a_{n}\psi_{n+1},

where
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are
N\times N
matrices, and
b_{n}
is self-adjoint. The edge modes of such models have been recently studied in
[
GGV24
]
. In the case where the sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
are bounded, the operator
h
is bounded self-adjoint with domain
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

For our theory to apply, we further need to assume that all the matrices
(a_{n})
are invertible. Then, the equation
h\psi=E\psi
can be seen as the linear recurrent sequence of order
2
, explicitly

\forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\quad\psi_{n+1}=-a_{n}^{-1}\left[a_{n-1}^{*}\psi_{n-1}+(b_{n}-E)\psi_{n}\right].

In some sense, this equation is the analogue of Cauchy’s theory for ordinary differential operators. The set of solutions of
h\psi=E\psi
is of dimension
2N
, and can be parametrized by the initial values
(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})\in({\mathbb{C}}^{N})^{2}
. We therefore define the evaluation map as
{\rm Tr}(\psi)=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
in this case (compare with (
2.1
)).

For all
\phi,\psi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
with compact support, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\langle\phi_{n},(h\psi)_{n}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}-\langle(h\phi)_{n},\psi_{n}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\right]=\langle\phi_{1},a_{0}^{*}\psi_{0}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}-\langle\phi_{0},a_{0}\psi_{1}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}.

We recognize the symplectic form

\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}},\quad\text{with}\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-a_{0}\\
a_{0}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the sesquilinear form
\omega
is non–degenerate since
a_{0}
is invertible. Again, it is independent of the choice of
(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and of
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}
.

2.4.3.
Non–homogeneous media

One can also consider operators of order
p=2
, of the form

{\mathcal{L}}:=-\partial_{t}\left(A(t)\partial_{t}\,\cdot\right)+V(t).

We assume that
A(t)
and
V(t)
are pointwise hermitian
N\times N
matrices, uniformly bounded for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and that there are constants
0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
so that

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\qquad\alpha{\mathbb{I}}_{M}\leq A(t)\leq\beta{\mathbb{I}}_{M}.

We recover the case Schrödinger in the case where
A(t)={\mathbb{I}}_{M}
pointwise. This general case has some subtleties that we would like to emphasize. First, the differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}
defines a symmetric operator
H
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
if and only if the map
t\mapsto A(t)
is
continuous
. This is because the distributional derivative of a discontinuous function involves Dirac mass measures, which are not
L^{2}
functions. Assume for instance that
A(\cdot)
is continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
and on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, but not necessarily at
t=0
. Then, for
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
, an integration by part shows that

\langle\phi,{\mathcal{L}}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle{\mathcal{L}}\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\omega_{+}({\rm Tr}(\phi),{\rm Tr}(\psi))-\omega_{-}({\rm Tr}(\phi),{\rm Tr}(\psi)),

with
\omega_{\pm}(x,y)=\langle x,J_{\pm}y\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{M}}
where

J_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&A(0^{+})\\
-A^{*}(0^{+})&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad J_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&A(0^{-})\\
-A^{*}(0^{-})&0\end{pmatrix}.

In the continuous case, we have
A(0^{+})=A(0^{-})
and
H:={\mathcal{L}}
is symmetric. In this case, it defines a boundary symplectic space with the symplectic form
\omega=\omega_{+}=\omega_{-}
. Note that this form depends on the location of the cut (here at
t=0
). One could also cut at any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and define the corresponding symplectic forms
\omega_{t}
. By continuity of
A(\cdot)
, the map
t\mapsto\omega_{t}
is continuous.

When considering junctions between two materials, one needs to assume that
A^{L}(0^{-})=A^{R}(0^{+})
, so that the junction operator
H^{\sharp}
also defines a self-adjoint operator, see also (
2.13
).

Let us finally remark that the equation
{\mathcal{L}}\psi=E\psi
can be recast as

\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
A\psi^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&A^{-1}(t)\\
E-V(t)&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
A\psi^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding initial value problem is always well-posed, thanks to the invertibility condition
A(t)\geq\alpha{\mathbb{I}}_{M}
, and whenever the map
A(\cdot)
is continuous (and not necessarily differentiable).

2.4.4.
Other operators

We finally mention that our theory also applies
mutatis mutandis
for operators of the form

{\mathcal{L}}:=R(t)^{*}T(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t})R(t)+V(t),

where
T(k)
is a polynomial of degree
p
from
{\mathbb{R}}
to the set of
M\times M
matrices, satisfying
T(k)=T^{*}(k)
pointwise. The operator
T(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t})
is defined via spectral calculus, and means that

{\mathcal{F}}\left[T(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t})\psi\right](k)=T(k){\mathcal{F}}[\psi](k)

where
{\mathcal{F}}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is the usual Fourier transform. Then,
{\mathcal{L}}
is a differential operator of order
p
, and defines a self-adjoint operator on
H^{p}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
whenever
V:t\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
and
R:t\mapsto{\mathcal{M}}_{M}({\mathbb{C}})
are uniformly bounded in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
R(\cdot)
is of class
C^{p-1}
, pointwise invertible with inverse uniformly bounded on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In some models,
R
allows to implement an inhomogeneous velocity propagation in the material.

The Schrödinger case corresponds to
T(k)=k^{2}{\mathbb{I}}_{M}
and
R(t)={\mathbb{I}}_{M}
, while the Dirac case corresponds to
T(k)=k\sigma_{3}
and
R(t)={\mathbb{I}}_{M}
. Our general message here is that the theory applies to a large number of operators. We record that in the case where
T(k)=k\sigma_{3}
and
R(t)
is continuous, we have
\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle
with
J={\mathrm{i}}R^{*}(0)\sigma_{3}R(0)
.

3.
Proof of the classification

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.10
, and identify how the symmetries of some (abstract) Lagrangian planes translate into symmetries for the corresponding unitaries. The strategy of the proof follows the lines of
[
GMP22
]
. For each class, we find a basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
in which the representation of the
T
,
C
, and
S
symmetries have simple forms (we refer to
[
GMP22
]
for the construction of them). In these bases, we identify directly the constraints on the unitary
U
.

In what follows, we restrict our attention to the case where there is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
in which
J
has the form

(3.1)

J=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}&0\\
0&-{\mathrm{i}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{U}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Ux\end{pmatrix},\ x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\right\}.

The general case can always be brought to this case by modifying the inner product of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
. Namely, with the notation of (
2.8
), we can define

\langle x,y\rangle_{J}:=\langle x_{+},A_{+}x_{+}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}+\langle x_{-},A_{-}x_{-}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}.

The fact that
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
are positive shows that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{J}
is a scalar product, and, relative to this inner product, the new matrix
J
representing
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)
is of the form (
3.1
).

Here and thereafter, we denote by
{\mathcal{K}}:{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
the usual complex-conjugation operator. Recall that for a matrix
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we denote its conjugate matrix by
\overline{M}:={\mathcal{K}}M{\mathcal{K}}
and its transpose by
M^{T}:=\overline{M}^{*}
.

3.1.
Class A

This class has no symmetry at all. Leray’s theorem directly implies that we recover the whole unitary group
{\rm U}(N)
, which is simply connected. The index is zero.

3.2.
Class AIII

This class includes the
S
-symmetry only. Since
SJ+JS=0
and
S^{2}=1
, there exists a basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
where

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane stable under the
S
–symmetry:
S\ell=\ell
, and let
U\in{\rm U}(N)
be its corresponding unitary. This implies that for any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, it exists
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that

S\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Ux\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
Uy\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\begin{cases}Ux=y\\
x=Uy\end{cases},\qquad\text{which gives}\qquad U=U^{*}.

This corresponds to unitary and self-adjoint matrices:

\boxed{{\rm U}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}).}

Such unitaries can only have
\pm 1
in their spectrum. So they are parametrized by subspaces of rank
k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
, with
k={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U-1)
. The classifying space is therefore

{\mathcal{G}}=\mathop{\bigcup}_{k=0}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}(k,N),\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathcal{G}}(k,N)\cong{\rm U}(N)/{\rm U}(k)\times{\rm U}(N-k)

is the usual (complex) Grassmannian. Each
{\mathcal{G}}(k,N)
is path–connected, thus
{\mathcal{G}}
has
N+1
connected components, and the corresponding index is

{\rm Index}^{{\rm AIII}}[U]:={\rm dim}\ker(U-1)\in\{0,\ldots,N\}.

3.3.
Class AI

This class includes the
T
-symmetry only with
T^{2}=+1
. Since
TJ=JT
, we consider a basis where

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix},

where we recall that
{\mathcal{K}}
is the usual complex conjugation operator on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane stable under the
T
-symmetry:
T\ell=\ell
. Thus for
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
it exists
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that

T\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Ux\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
Uy\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\begin{cases}\overline{U}\overline{x}=y\\
x=Uy\end{cases},\qquad\text{which gives}\qquad U=U^{T}.

So the set of unitaries one obtains are the set of real–symmetric unitaries

\boxed{{\rm U}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{C}})}\qquad\cong{\rm U}(N)/{\rm O}(N).

The last equivalence is proved for instance in
[
GMP22
, Corollary A.2]
. This set is path-connected so that the index is zero.

3.4.
Class BDI

This class includes all three symmetries with
T^{2}=+1
,
C^{2}=+1
and
S=CT
, so that
S^{2}=1
. We consider a basis where

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}}&0\\
0&{\mathcal{K}}\end{pmatrix}.

where
{\mathcal{K}}
is the complex conjugation. Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane invariant under the three symmetries. Since
S=CT
, we only need to look at
C
and
T
. Like in class
{\rm D}
below, the
C
-symmetry implies that
\overline{U}=U
, so
U\in{\rm O}(N)
, and like in class
{\rm AI}
above, the
T
-symmetry implies that
U^{T}=U
, to
U
is also real–symmetric. Hence,
U
is an orthogonal symmetry, but with respect to a real subspace, which gives

\boxed{{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{C}}).}

The classifying space is similar to class
{\rm AIII}
but with orthogonal matrices, that is

{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathbb{R}}=\mathop{\bigcup}_{k=0}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathbb{R}}(k,N),\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathbb{R}}(k,N)\cong{\rm O}(N)/{\rm O}(k)\times{\rm O}(N-k)

is the usual (real) Grassmannian, which is path-connected. Thus
{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathbb{R}}
has
N+1
connected components, and the corresponding index is

{\rm Index}^{{\rm BDI}}[U]:={\rm dim}\ker(U-1)\in\{0,\ldots,N\}.

3.5.
Class D

This class includes the
C
-symmetry only with
C^{2}=+1
. Since
CJ=-JC
, we consider a basis where
C={\mathcal{K}}
. If
\ell
with corresponding unitary
U
is stable for
C
, then for all
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
there is
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that

C\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Ux\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
Uy\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{so}\quad\begin{cases}\overline{x}=y\\
\overline{U}\overline{y}=Uy,\end{cases}\quad\text{which gives}\quad\overline{U}=U.

So
U
is real-valued, and the classifying space is

\boxed{{\rm O}(N).}

It has two connected components, and the index is given by

{\rm Index}^{{\rm D}}[U]:={\rm det}(U)\in\{\pm 1\}.

3.6.
Class DIII

This class includes
T
with
T^{2}=-1
,
C
with
C^{2}=1
and
S=TC=-CT
. We consider a basis where

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad C={\mathrm{i}}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathrm{i}}\\
-{\mathrm{i}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Consider a Lagrangian plane
\ell
with corresponding matrix
U
, and compatible with all these symmetries. Since
S=TC
, we only need to look at
C
and
T
. Like in class
{\rm AII}
below, the fermionic
T
-symmetry implies
U^{T}=-U
, so
U\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{R}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
is (real) anti-symmetric. Similarly, like in class
{\rm D}
, the
C
-symmetry implies
\overline{U}=U
, so
U\in{\rm O}(N)
is real–valued. Thus
U\in{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{R}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. This set is non-empty only if
N=2n
is even, in which case we have (for all these facts, we refer
e.g.
to
[
GMP22
, Cor. A4]
)

\boxed{{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{R}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})}\qquad\cong{\rm O}(2n)/{\rm U}(n).

This set has two connected components and the index is

{\rm Index}^{{\rm DIII}}[U]:={\rm Pf}(U)\in\{\pm 1\},

where
{\rm Pf}
is the Pfaffian. Recall that the Pfaffian is well-defined for anti–symmetric matrices of even size.

3.7.
Class AII

This class includes the
T
-symmetry only with
T^{2}=-1
. Since
TJ=JT
, we consider the basis where

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane with corresponding matrix
U\in{\rm U}(N)
, stable for the
T
–symmetry. Then, for
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
it exists
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that

T\begin{pmatrix}x\\
{\mathcal{U}}x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
{\mathcal{U}}y\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{hence}\quad\begin{cases}-\overline{U}\overline{x}=y\\
\overline{x}=Uy\end{cases},\quad\text{which gives}\quad U^{T}=-U.

This correspond to unitary and (real) anti-symmetric matrices. This set is non–empty iff
N=2n
is even, in which case we have

\boxed{{\rm U}(N)\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{C}})}\quad\cong{\rm U}(2n)/{\rm Sp}(n).

This set is simply connected and the index is zero. We refer again to
[
GMP22
, Thm 4.7 and Cor. A.4]
for an elementary proof of these facts.

3.8.
Class CII

This class includes
T
with
T^{2}=-1
,
C
with
C^{2}=-1
and
S=TC
. This requires
N=2n
to be even, and we consider a basis where

T=-\begin{pmatrix}0&\Omega\\
\Omega&0\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega&0\\
0&\Omega\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega:=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathbb{I}}_{n}\\
-{\mathbb{I}}_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}

is the
(2n)\times(2n)
symplectic matrix defined in (
1.1
). Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane with corresponding matrix
U\in{\rm U}(N)
, and compatible for all these symmetries. Since
S=TC
, we only need to look at
C
and
S
. Like in class
AIII
above, the
S
-symmetry implies
U^{*}=U
, so
U\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is hermitian (in particular,
\sigma(U)\in\{\pm 1\}
), and like in class
C
below, the
C
-symmetry implies that
U^{T}\Omega U=\Omega
, so
U\in{\rm Sp}(n)
is symplectic. We prove in Appendix
A.3
below that the space
{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
has
n+1
connected component given by

\boxed{{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})}\quad\cong\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}{\rm Sp}(n)/{\rm Sp}(k)\times{\rm Sp}(n-k),

where the right-hand side is the symplectic Grassmanian. Each component is simply connected, and the index is

{\rm Index}^{{\rm CII}}[U]:={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}\,(U-1).

3.9.
Class C

This class includes the
C
-symmetry only with
C^{2}=-1
. Since
CJ=-JC
, we get that
N=2n
is even, and there a basis in which

C=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega&0\\
0&\Omega\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}},

with
\Omega
the symplectic matrix given in (
1.1
). Let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane with corresponding unitary
U\in{\rm U}(N)
, stable under
C
. For all
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, there is
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that

C\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Ux\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
Uy\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{hence}\quad\begin{cases}\Omega\overline{x}=y\\
\Omega\overline{U}\overline{x}=Uy,\end{cases}\quad\text{so that}\quad U^{T}\Omega U=\Omega.

In other words,
U
is symplectic. Thus the classifying space is the set of unitary symplectic matrices, namely

\boxed{{\rm U}(N)\cap{\rm Sp}(N,{\mathbb{C}})}\quad=:{\rm Sp}(n),

which is simply connected. The index is zero.

3.10.
Class CI

This class includes
T
with
T^{2}=1
,
C
with
C^{2}=-1
and
S=TC
. This requires
N=2n
to be even, and we consider a basis where

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathrm{i}}{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathrm{i}}{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega{\mathcal{K}}&0\\
0&\Omega{\mathcal{K}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathrm{i}}\Omega\\
{\mathrm{i}}\Omega&0\end{pmatrix}.

with
\Omega
the symplectic matrix given in (
1.1
). Let
\ell
be Lagrangian plane with corresponding matrix
U\in{\rm U}(N)
, and satisfying these symmetries. Since
S=TC
, we only need to look at
C
and
T
. Like in class
AI
above, the
T
-symmetry implies that
U\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathbb{R}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
, and like in class
C
above, the
C
-symmetry implies that
U\in{\rm Sp}(n)
. So the classifying space is

\boxed{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathbb{R}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\cap{\rm Sp}(n)}\quad\cong{\rm Sp}(n)/{\rm U}(n),

which is simply connected. The index is zero. We refer
e.g.
to
[
GMP22
, Cor. A2]
for these facts.

4.
Proofs for the junctions between two topological insulators

We now prove Theorem
2.14
about the existence of protected modes in junctions, proceeding class by class. We only focus on the classes
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
,
{\rm CII}
(where the index is a dimension of a kernel),
{\rm D}
(where the index is a determinant) and
{\rm DIII}
(where the index is a Pfaffian). In all these cases, we have a
C
or
S
symmetry, so we take
E=0
.

4.1.
Classes AIII, BDI, CII

Let us first focus on the classes
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}
, whose classifying spaces are respectively

{\rm U}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\quad{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}).

This constraints the matrix
U
to satisfy
\sigma(U)\in\{\pm 1\}
. In what follows, we focus on the
{\rm AIII}
case for the sake of clarity, but the argument is similar for the classes
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}
, up to replacing the field
{\mathbb{C}}
by
{\mathbb{R}}
or
\mathbb{H}
(quaternions). Recall that

{\rm Index}^{{\rm AIII}}[U]={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U-1)

is a dimension of a kernel. The arguments we use follows the ones in
[
Góm
]
. First we record the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\ell_{A}
and
\ell_{B}
be Lagrangian planes in the
{\rm AIII}
class, and let
U_{A},U_{B}\in{\rm U}(N)
be the corresponding unitaries. We have

{\rm dim}(\ell_{A}\cap\ell_{B})={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U_{A}^{*}U_{B}-1)\geq\left|N-{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}[U_{A}]-{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}[U_{B}]\right|.

Proof.

The first equality is the first point of Lemma
2.15
. Let us prove the second inequality. We write

{\mathbb{C}}^{N}=E_{A,1}\oplus E_{A,-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathbb{C}}^{N}=E_{B,1}\oplus E_{B,-1},

with
E_{A,1}={\rm Ker}(U_{A}-1)
,
E_{A,-1}={\rm Ker}(U_{A}+1)
, and so on. If
U_{A}v=\pm v
and
U_{B}v=\pm v
, then
U_{A}^{*}U_{B}v=v
, so

\left(E_{A,1}\cap E_{B,1}\right)\oplus\left(E_{A,-1}\cap E_{B,-1}\right)\subset{\rm Ker}(U_{A}^{*}U_{B}-1).

Recall that, for any vector subspaces
G,H
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, we have
{\rm dim}(G+H)\leq N
, from which we infer

{\rm dim}(G\cap H)={\rm dim}(G)+{\rm dim}(H)-{\rm dim}(G+H)\geq{\rm dim}(G)+{\rm dim}(H)-N.

Together with the fact that
{\rm dim}(G\cap H)\geq 0
, we get

{\rm dim}(G\cap H)\geq\max\left\{0,{\rm dim}(G)+{\rm dim}(H)-N\right\}.

Similarly, we have
{\rm dim}(G^{\perp}\cap H^{\perp})=\max\{0,N-{\rm dim}(G)-{\rm dim}(H)\}
, so

{\rm dim}\left((G\cap H)\oplus(G^{\perp}\cap H^{\perp})\right)={\rm dim}(G\cap H)+{\rm dim}(G^{\perp}\cap H^{\perp})\geq\left|{\rm dim}(G)+{\rm dim}(H)-N\right|.

and the result follows.
∎

We first deduce the following corollary. Recall that we consider the energy
E=0
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
H
be an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
in the class
{\rm AIII}
, such that
0\notin\sigma(H)
. Let
\ell_{0}^{\pm}
be the associated Lagrangian planes at
E=0
. Then

{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{+})+{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{-})=N.

Proof.

Apply the previous Lemma together with the fact that

0={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H)={\rm dim}\left(\ell_{0}^{+}\cap\ell_{0}^{-}\right).

∎

We can now prove Theorem
2.14
for class AIII.

Proof of Theorem
2.14
, Class AIII.

Using Lemma
2.15
at
E=0
and Lemma
4.1
show that

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}\left(H^{\sharp}\right)={\rm dim}\left(\ell_{0}^{R,+}\cap\ell_{0}^{L,-}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left|N-{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{R,+})-{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{L,-})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{R,+})-{\rm Index}^{\rm AIII}(\ell_{0}^{L,+})\right|,

where we used Lemma
4.2
for the last line.
∎

4.2.
Class D

We now prove Theorem
2.14
for the class
{\rm D}
, where the classifying space is
{\rm O}(N)
. For a Lagrangian plane
\ell
with corresponding unitary
U
we have
{\rm Index}^{\rm D}(\ell):={\rm Index}^{\rm D}[U]={\rm det}(U)\in\{\pm 1\}
. Our goal is to prove that if
{\rm Index}^{\rm D}(H^{R})\neq{\rm Index}^{\rm D}(H^{L})
, then
0
is an eigenvalue of
H^{\sharp}
of multiplicity at least
1
. The proof relies on the following remark.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
U\in{\rm O}(N)
be an orthogonal matrix. If
{\rm det}(U)=-(-1)^{N}
, then
1\in\sigma(U)
is in the spectrum of
U
.

Proof.

All eigenvalues of
U
have modulus
1
. In addition, since the characteristic polynomial of
U
has real coefficients, the non–real eigenvalues occur in conjugate pair
(\lambda,\overline{\lambda})
with equal multiplicity. In particular, each such conjugate pair contributes to
+1
to the determinant. So, if
m_{-1}
and
m_{1}
denote the multiplicity of
-1
and
1
respectively, we have

{\rm det}(U)=(-1)^{m_{-1}},\quad\text{and}\quad m_{-1}+m_{1}=N\ {\rm mod}\ 2.

In the case where
N=2n
is even and
{\rm det}(U)=-1
, we must have
m_{-1}
and
m_{1}
odd, and in particular,
m_{1}\geq 1
. The other case is similar.
∎

The next result is similar to Lemma
4.1
, but for the
{\rm D}
class.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
H
be an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
in the
{\rm D}
class such that
0\notin\sigma(H)
. Let
\ell_{0}^{\pm}
be the associated Lagrangian planes at
E=0
.
Then
{\rm Index}^{\rm D}(\ell_{0}^{+})=(-1)^{N}{\rm Index}^{\rm D}(\ell_{0}^{-})
.

Proof.

Since
0\notin\sigma(H)
, we have
{\rm dim}(\ell_{0}^{+}\cap\ell_{0}^{-})=0
, so
1\notin\sigma\left(U_{0}^{+}(U_{0}^{-})^{*}\right)
. From the previous Lemma, we get
{\rm det}(U_{0}^{+}(U_{0}^{-})^{*})=(-1)^{N}
, which is the result.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
2.14

Proof of Theorem
2.14
, Class
{\rm D}
.

This time, we write

{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U_{0}^{R,+}(U_{0}^{L,-})^{*}-1)

which is non null whenever

-(-1)^{N}={\rm det}(U_{0}^{R,+}(U_{0}^{L,-})^{*})=\frac{{\rm det}(U_{0}^{R,+})}{{\rm det}(U_{0}^{L,-})}=(-1)^{N}\frac{{\rm Index}^{\rm D}[H^{R}]}{{\rm Index}^{\rm D}[H^{L}]},

that is when
{\rm Index}^{\rm D}[H^{R}]=-{\rm Index}^{\rm D}[H^{L}]
, which proves the result.
∎

4.3.
Class DIII

Finally, we prove Theorem
2.14
for the class
{\rm DIII}
, where the classifying space is
{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{{\mathbb{R}}}({\mathbb{R}})
, that is the set of orthogonal antisymmetric matrices. This set is non empty only if
N=2n
is even. In this case, for a Lagrangian plane
\ell
with corresponding unitary
U\in{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{{\mathbb{R}}}({\mathbb{R}})
we have

{\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}(\ell):={\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}[U]={\rm Pf}(U)\quad\in\{\pm 1\}.

This time, we use the following properties of Pfaffians.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
A,B\in{\rm O}(2n)\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{2n}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{R}})
. If
{\rm Pf}(A)=-{\rm Pf}(B)
, then
1\in\sigma(AB)
.

Proof.

We use the following formula, which states that for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2n}^{{\mathbb{R}}}({\mathbb{R}})
, we have (see for instance
[
Kri16
]
)

(4.1)

{\rm Pf}(A){\rm Pf}(B)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\log(A^{T}B)\right).

Assume otherwise that
1\notin\sigma(AB)
. Then
-1\notin\sigma(-AB)=\sigma(A^{T}B)
since
A^{T}=-A
.

In particular, we can choose the complex logarithm with the usual branch cut at
(-\infty,0)
in (
4.1
). Since
A^{T}B\in{\rm O}(2n)
is orthogonal, all eigenvalues are of modulus
1
. If
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
is such a non-real eigenvalue, then
\overline{\lambda}
is also an eigenvalue with same multiplicity. Note that

\log(\lambda)+\log(\overline{\lambda})=\log(|\lambda|^{2})=\log(1)=0,

so such pairs of eigenvalues do not contribute in the
{\rm Tr}\log(A^{T}B)
. Similarly, the eigenvalue
1
does not contribute, and since
-1
is not in the spectrum of
A^{T}B
, we obtain

{\rm Tr}\log(A^{T}B)=0,\quad\text{so}\quad{\rm Pf}(A){\rm Pf}(B)=1.

∎

The analogue of Lemma
4.1
for the
{\rm DIII}
class reads as follows. Recall that
{\rm Pf}(A^{T})=(-1)^{n}{\rm Pf}(A)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
H
be an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
in the
{\rm DIII}
class such that
0\notin\sigma(H)
. Let
\ell_{0}^{\pm}
be the associated Lagrangian planes at
E=0
.
Then
{\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}(\ell_{0}^{+})=(-1)^{n}{\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}(\ell_{0}^{-})
.

Proof.

We have
0={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}H={\rm dim}(\ell_{0}^{+}\cap\ell_{0}^{-})={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(U_{0}^{+}(U_{0}^{-})^{T}-1)
, so
1\notin(U_{0}^{+}(U_{0}^{-})^{T})
. Since
U_{0}^{\pm}\in{\rm O}(2n)\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{2n}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbb{R}})
, we can apply the previous Lemma, and deduce that

{\rm Pf}(U_{0}^{+})={\rm Pf}((U_{0}^{-})^{T})=(-1)^{n}{\rm Pf}(U_{0}^{-}).

∎

We can now prove Theorem
2.14
for the
{\rm DIII}
class.

Proof of Theorem
2.14
, class
{\rm DIII}
.

We have

{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})={\rm dim}(\ell_{0}^{R,+}\cap\ell_{0}^{L,-})={\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}\left(U_{0}^{R,+}(U_{0}^{L,-})^{*}-1\right).

In addition, we have
{\rm Pf}(U_{0}^{R,+})={\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}(H^{R})
and

{\rm Pf}((U_{0}^{L,-})^{T})=(-1)^{n}{\rm Pf}(U_{0}^{L,-})={\rm Pf}(U_{0}^{L,+})={\rm Index}^{\rm DIII}(H^{L}).

In the case where the two indices differ, we get from Lemma
4.5
that
1\in\sigma(U_{0}^{R,+}(U_{0}^{L,-})^{T})
, hence
{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})\geq 1
.
∎

4.4.
The case of continuous junctions

In this section, we explain how to modify the proofs in the case of continuous junctions. First, we note that the notion of continuous junctions only makes sense for one–dimensional models set on
{\mathbb{R}}
(instead of tight–binding models on
{\mathbb{Z}}
).

We consider a junction operator described by a differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
with
continuous
coefficients, and so that

{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\psi=\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}^{L}\psi,&\quad\text{for}\quad\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}((-\infty,-X]),\\
{\mathcal{L}}^{R}\psi,&\quad\text{for}\quad\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}([X,\infty)),\end{cases}

for some
X>0
large enough. This means that
{\mathcal{L}}
behaves as
{\mathcal{L}}^{L}
on the far left, and as
{\mathcal{L}}^{R}
on the far right, hence models a continuous junction between two bulk media. A typical example is

(4.2)

{\mathcal{L}}(t)=-\partial_{tt}^{2}+V^{\sharp}(t),\qquad V^{\sharp}=V^{L}(t)(1-\chi(t))+V^{R}(t)\chi(t),

where
V^{L}
and
V^{R}
are bulk potentials (think of periodic functions), and
\chi
is a continuous cut-off functions with
\chi(t)=0
for
t<-X
and
\chi(t)=1
for
t>X
. We then make the same assumption as in Section
2.3
, namely that these differential operators define self-adjoint operators
H^{L}
,
H^{R}
, and
H^{\sharp}
, and that
E\notin\sigma(H^{\rm L/R})
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Lemma 4.7
.

For the junction operator
H^{\sharp}
, the vector spaces
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}
and
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,-}
are Lagrangian planes. In addition, if
H^{L}
,
H^{R}
and
H^{\sharp}
are in the same symmetry class
\Gamma
, then

{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+})={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{R,+}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,-})={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{L,-}).

In the previous hard cut case, we had the equality
\ell^{\sharp,+}_{E}=\ell^{R,+}_{E}
and
\ell^{\sharp,-}_{E}=\ell^{L,-}_{E}
. Now, these spaces are different in general, and only the indices are equal. This result
does not
follow from Lemma
2.3
, since we expect
E
to be the spectrum of
H^{\sharp}
. In particular, we warn that the equality

\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}\oplus\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}={\mathbb{C}}^{Mp}

is
false
in general: we expect these planes to cross, and any initial condition in this crossing gives an edge state.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to consider the vectorial planes
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
parametrized by
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, corresponding to the boundary values of
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}
at
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The previous case corresponds to
t=0
.

All the statements for
t=0
translates directly for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In particular, we obtain a family of symplectic forms
\omega_{t}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{Mp}
, for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For this symplectic form, the sets
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
are still isotropic, see (
2.7
). In particular, we have

{\rm dim}\left(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)\right)\leq N,\quad\text{with}\quad N:=\frac{Mp}{2}.

Cauchy’s theory shows that
{\rm dim}\left(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)\right)={\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}
are independent of
t
. But for
t>X
, we recover the right bulk Hamiltonian, so
{\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{\sharp,+}={\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{R,+}
, and similarly,
{\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{\sharp,-}={\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{L,-}
. Finally, according to Lemma
2.3
applied for the bulk Hamiltonians
H^{L}
and
H^{R}
, these vector spaces are of dimension
N
. So

{\rm dim}\left(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)\right)=N,\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

We proved that the sets
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
are maximally isotropic, hence are Lagrangian. This proves the first part of the theorem.

We now prove equality of the index. Note that since
\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
are Lagrangian for
\omega_{t}
, one can associate a unitary
U_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)\in{\rm U}(N)
thanks to Leray’s theorem. The continuity of
{\mathcal{L}}
implies that
t\mapsto\omega_{t}
is continuous, and that
t\mapsto\ell_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
are continuous (for the topology of vector spaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{Mp}
). In particular, the corresponding maps of unitaries
t\mapsto U_{E}^{\sharp,\pm}(t)
are also continuous in
{\rm U}(N)
. Since the index is constant on connected components, we directly get

{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(U_{E}^{\sharp,+}(t=0))={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(U_{E}^{\sharp,+}(t=X))={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(U_{E}^{R,+}(t=X))={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(U_{E}^{R,+}(t=0)),

so
{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+})={\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{R,+})
as claimed. The proof on the other side is similar.
∎

Notice that the continuity of
t\mapsto\omega_{t}
can be proved directly for a given model. For instance, in the context of Schrödinger operator in (
4.2
),
\omega_{t}
is independent of
t
(so continuous). Note that the continuity of
t\mapsto\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+}(t)
follows from Cauchy’s theory: the functions
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{+}
are of class
C^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
so the map
t\mapsto(\psi(t),\psi^{\prime}(t),\cdots\psi^{p-1}(t))
is continuous.

Theorem
2.14
now applies for any smooth junctions between two bulk materials, by writing (here for the case
\Gamma\in\{{\rm AIII},{\rm BDI},{\rm CII}\}
)

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}(H^{\sharp})

\displaystyle\geq\left|{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,+})-{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{\sharp,-})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{R,+})-{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(\ell_{E}^{L,-})\right|=\left|{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{R})-{\rm Index}^{\Gamma}(H^{L})\right|.

5.
Applications: Bulk–edge index for Dirac operators

5.1.
Dirac operators with constant potential

In this section, we compute the index of Dirac operators with
constant
potential, acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
with block form

\not{D}=\begin{pmatrix}-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}&-{\mathrm{i}}W\\
{\mathrm{i}}W^{*}&{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}\end{pmatrix}

where
W\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2N}({\mathbb{C}})
is a constant matrix, independent of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The
{\mathrm{i}}
factor in front of
W
is here to simplify the computations below. This example highlights many interesting features of the previous results. In particular, each symmetry class of the table and the corresponding index can be directly deduced from
W
and its spectral properties. Our results are gathered in the next Proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

The operator
\not{D}
acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N})
is essentially self-adjoint, with domain
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N})
. Its spectrum is purely essential, given by

\sigma(\not{D})=\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{D})=(-\infty,m_{0}]\cup[m_{0},\infty),

where
m_{0}
is the lowest singular value of
W
. In particular,
0\notin\sigma(\not{D})
iff
W\in{\rm GL}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
is invertible. In this case, we can identify the Lagrangian planes
\ell_{E=0}^{\pm}
as

\ell_{0}^{+}={\rm Ran}\left({\mathds{1}}(A>0)\right),\quad\ell_{0}^{-}={\rm Ran}\left({\mathds{1}}(A<0)\right),\qquad\text{with}\quad A:=\begin{pmatrix}0&W\\
W^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, the corresponding unitaries are given by
U_{0}^{+}=-U_{0}^{-}=W^{*}|W|^{-1}
.

Note that the matrix
A
is hermitian, hence is diagonalizable with real spectrum. As we will see in the proof, its spectrum is of the form
\sigma(A)=\{\pm\mu_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
where
0<\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}
are the singular values of
W
. We used the notation
{\mathds{1}}(A>0)
and
{\mathds{1}}(A<0)
for the spectral projectors on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
of
A
.

The last part of this proposition states that
U_{0}^{+}
is the inverse of the unitary appearing in the polar decomposition of
W
.

Proof.

The first part is standard, and comes from the fact that
\not{D}
is a Fourier multiplier by the hermitian matrix

D(k):=\begin{pmatrix}k&-{\mathrm{i}}W\\
{\mathrm{i}}W^{*}&-k\end{pmatrix}\quad\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2N}({\mathbb{C}}),\qquad\text{so, in particular}\quad\sigma(\not{D})=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{R}}}\sigma\left(D(k)\right).

For
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

{\rm det}\left(D(k)-E\right)={\rm det}\begin{pmatrix}k-E&-{\mathrm{i}}W\\
{\mathrm{i}}W^{*}&-k-E\end{pmatrix}={\rm det}\left(E^{2}-k^{2}-W^{*}W\right).

We deduce that
E\in\sigma(D(k))
iff
E^{2}-k^{2}\in\sigma(W^{*}W)
iff
E=\pm\sqrt{k^{2}+\mu^{2}}
for some
\mu^{2}\in\sigma(W^{*}W)
, i.e. for some
\mu\geq 0
a singular value of
W
. The result on the spectrum follows.

We now focus on the energy
E=0
. The matrix
D(k)
is linear in
k
, hence analytic for
k\in{\mathbb{C}}
. If
\Psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
solves
D(k)\Psi=0
for some
k\in{\mathbb{C}}
, then, multiplying by
{\mathrm{i}}\sigma_{3}
, we get

\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{i}}&0\\
0&-{\mathrm{i}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}k&-{\mathrm{i}}W\\
{\mathrm{i}}W^{*}&-k\end{pmatrix}\Psi=0,\quad\text{that is}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&W\\
W^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\Psi=-{\mathrm{i}}k\Psi,\quad\text{so}\quad(-{\mathrm{i}}k)\in\sigma(A).

Note that since
A
is hermitian, we have
\sigma(A)\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, so the locations
k\in{\mathbb{C}}
where
0\in D(k)
can only occur for
k
on the imaginary axis. Let us study the matrix
A
. We write the polar decomposition of
W
and
W^{*}
as

W:=|W|U,\quad\text{with}\quad|W|:=\sqrt{WW^{*}},

where
U\in{\rm U}(2N)
is unitary. The polar decomposition of
W^{*}
is related to the one of
W
, and we have

W^{*}:=U^{*}|W|=|W^{*}|U^{*},\quad\text{with}\quad|W^{*}|:=\sqrt{W^{*}W}=U^{*}|W|U.

Since
W
is invertible, the decomposition is unique. Recall that
|W|
and
|W^{*}|
have the same spectrum, composed of the singular values of
W
. In what follows, we denote by
0<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}
these singular values. Then, we can check that
A={\mathcal{U}}^{*}\Lambda{\mathcal{U}}
, with

{\mathcal{U}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-U\\
-U^{*}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}|W|&0\\
0&-|W^{*}|\end{pmatrix},

and that
{\mathcal{U}}\in{\rm U}(2N)
is unitary. We deduce that
\sigma(A)=\sigma(\Lambda)=\{\pm\mu_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
. In addition, the spectral calculus shows that

{\mathds{1}}(A>0)={\mathcal{U}}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{U}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&U\\
U^{*}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{so}\quad\ell_{0}^{+}={\rm Ran}\,{\mathds{1}}(A>0)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x\\
U^{*}x\end{pmatrix},\ x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\right\}.

By identification with (
3.1
) (note that with our convention of Dirac, the symplectic form is given by the matrix
J
in (
3.1
)), we deduce as wanted that
U_{0}^{+}=U^{*}
. The computation for
U_{0}^{-}
is similar.

∎

Remark 5.2
.

In the case where
W
is normal (
WW^{*}=W^{*}W
), we have
|W|=|W^{*}|
. In addition,
|W|
and
U
commutes.

5.2.
Symmetries

With this at hand, we can now identify some indices of
\not{D}
solely from spectral property of the matrix
W
. We first record the following result, whose proof is straightforward (compare with (
2.11
)).

Lemma 5.3
.

A constant Dirac operator satisfies a
T
,
C
and/or
S
symmetry if, respectively,

\forall k\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad TD(k)=D(-k)T,\qquad CD(k)=-D(-k)C,\qquad SD(k)=-D(k)S.

In terms of the matrix
J
and
V:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\mathrm{i}}W\\
{\mathrm{i}}W^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}
, this happens if
TJ=JT
,
CJ=-JC
and
SJ=-JS
, and

TV=VT,\qquad CV=-VC,\qquad SV=-VS.

A
T
–symmetry implies
\sigma(D(k))=D(-k)
, a
C
–symmetry implies
\sigma(D(k))=-\sigma(D(-k))
and an
S
–symmetry implies
\sigma(D(k))=-\sigma(D(k))
. In the last case, the spectrum of
D(k)
is symmetric with respect to
0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We now identify the Index class by class. We focus on the classes for which the index is not vanishing, namely classes
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
,
{\rm D}
,
{\rm DIII}
and
{\rm CII}
. Each of this class involves a
C
or
S
symmetry, so we focus on the energy
E=0
. In what follows, we assume that
W
is invertible, so the energy
E=0
is not in the spectrum of
\not{D}
.

5.2.1.
Classes
{\rm AIII}
,
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}

We first consider class
{\rm AIII}
, which only involves a
S
–symmetry. As in Section
3.2
, we choose a basis in which
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. The condition
SV=-VS
reads, in terms of
W
, as

W=W^{*}.

So
W\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. In particular, any such
W
is normal (so
|W|=|W^{*}|
). Its polar decomposition
W=|W|U
involves matrices
|W|
and
U
which commutes. With this notation, we have
U_{0}^{+}=U^{*}
. The condition
W=W^{*}
gives
U^{*}=U
, hence

\left(U_{0}^{+}\right)^{*}:=U_{0}^{+},

so
U_{0}^{+}\in{\rm U}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
, as expected from class
{\rm AIII}
. For this class, the index is the multiplicity of
1
as an eigenvalue of
U_{0}^{+}
, hence the multiplicity of
-1
as an eigenvalue of
U
, which is also the number of negative eigenvalues of
W
. We therefore proved the following Theorem.

Theorem 5.4

(Protected edge modes in class
{\rm AIII}
)
.

Consider a Dirac operator of the form

\not{D}=\begin{pmatrix}-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}&-{\mathrm{i}}W(t)\\
{\mathrm{i}}W(t)&{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{acting on}\quad L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}),

and with
W(t)
a pointwise hermitian matrix.

•

If
W(t)=W_{0}
is independent of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and invertible, then the bulk–edge index of
\not{D}
at
E=0
is the number of negative of
W_{0}
, noted
n^{-}(W_{0})
.

•

If
W(t)
is a continuous junction between a constant potential
W_{L}
on the left and
W_{R}
on the right, with
W_{L}
and
W_{R}
invertible, then

{\rm dim}\,{\rm Ker}\not{D}\geq\left|n^{-}(W_{R})-n^{-}(W_{L})\right|=\left|n^{+}(W_{R})-n^{+}(W_{L})\right|.

We recover the results in
[
Góm
]
. In the case where
N=1
, the function
W(t)
can be seen as the varying mass for the Dirac operator. The theorem states that if the mass switches sign, then
0
is in the kernel of the Dirac operator. This is a well-known result, which can be proved by directly solving the ODE, see for instance
[
FLW17
]
.

We have similar results for classes
{\rm BDI}
and
{\rm CII}
, and we do not repeat all the arguments here. For the class
{\rm BDI}
, as in Section
3.4
, we choose a basis in which

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad T=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathcal{K}}\\
{\mathcal{K}}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}}&0\\
0&{\mathcal{K}}\end{pmatrix}.

The
S
–symmetry is as before, and shows that
W
must satisfy
W=W^{*}
. The
T
or
C
–symmetry implies furthermore that
W=\overline{W}
is real–valued. So the matrix
U
(hence
U_{0}^{+}
) is also real–valued, and in particular
U_{0}^{+}\in{\rm O}(N)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
, as expected for the
{\rm BDI}
class.

Finally, for the class
{\rm CII}
, we must have
N=2n
even, and as in Section
3.8
, we choose a basis in which

T=-\begin{pmatrix}0&\Omega\\
\Omega&0\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega&0\\
0&\Omega\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

This time, the
S
–symmetry still implies that the matrix
W
satisfies
W=W^{*}
, and the
C
or
T
–symmetry implies

\Omega W=\overline{W}\Omega.

We claim that this equality implies that
U_{0}^{+}
is symplectic. First, we have

|\overline{W}|^{2}=\overline{W}(\overline{W})^{*}=-\overline{W}\Omega\Omega(\overline{W})^{*}=-\Omega WW^{*}\Omega=-\Omega|W|^{2}\Omega,

hence
|\overline{W}|=-\Omega|W|\Omega
(since
-\Omega=\Omega^{*}
). On the other hand, it is classical that
\overline{|W|}=|\overline{W}|
. Altogether, we get

\overline{|W|}=-\Omega|W|\Omega,\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\overline{|W|}\Omega=\Omega|W|.

Finally, for the
U

U_{0}^{+}
matrix), we get

\Omega\overline{U}=\Omega\overline{W}\overline{|W|^{-1}}=W|W|^{-1}\Omega=U\Omega,

which is equivalent to
U\Omega U^{T}=\Omega
, that is

is symplectic.

Class
{\rm D}

In the

, we can choose a basis in which

reads

5.2.3.
Class
{\rm DIII}

In class

, we work in a basis in which

Appendix

In this appendix, we gather some technical proofs.

A.1.
Proof of Leray’s Theorem
2.5

We begin with the proof of Leray’s theorem. Let us first check the vector subspace
\ell_{U}
is Lagrangian. We set
V:=\sqrt{A_{-}}^{-1}U\sqrt{A_{+}}
. Note that
U
is unitary iff
V
satisfies
V^{*}A_{-}V=A_{+}
. This gives

\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
Vx\end{pmatrix},J\begin{pmatrix}y\\
Vy\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}}={\mathrm{i}}\left[\langle x,A_{+}y\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}-\langle Vx,A_{-}Vy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\right]={\mathrm{i}}\langle x,(A_{+}-V^{*}A_{-}V)u\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}=0.

Conversely, let
\ell
be a Lagrangian plane. Any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
has a unique decomposition of the form
x=x_{+}+x_{-}
with
x_{\pm}\in K_{\pm}
. Consider the projection map
P_{\ell}:\ell\to K_{+}
defined by
P_{\ell}(x)=x_{+}
. We claim that
P_{\ell}
is one-to-one. First, since all Lagrangian planes are of dimension
N
(this comes from the definition (
2.6
)), we have
{\rm dim}\,\ell={\rm dim}\,K_{+}=N
, so it is enough to check that
P_{\ell}
is injective. Assume that there is
x_{0}\in\ell
so that
P_{\ell}(x_{0})=0
. Since
\ell
is Lagrangian, for all
x,y\in\ell
, we have

(A.1)

0=\omega(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}}={\mathrm{i}}\left[\langle x_{+},A_{+}y_{+}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}-\langle x_{-},A_{-}y_{-}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\right].

In particular, for
x=y=x_{0}
, we get
\langle x_{0},A_{-}x_{0}\rangle=0
, hence
x_{0}=0
as wanted, since
A_{-}
is positive definite. Since
P_{\ell}
is invertible, there is well-defined linear map
V_{\ell}
from
K_{+}
to
K_{-}
so that, for all
x_{+}\in K_{+}
, we have
x_{+}+V_{\ell}(x_{+})=P_{\ell}(x_{+})\in\ell
. Together with (
A.1
), we get that, for all
x_{+},y_{+}\in K_{+}\approx{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, we have

\langle x_{+},A_{+}y_{+}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}=\langle V_{\ell}(x_{+}),A_{-}V_{\ell}(y_{+})\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}.

Since this holds for all
x_{+},y_{+}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, the matrix
V_{\ell}
must satisfy the additional constraint that
A_{+}=V_{\ell}^{*}A_{-}V_{\ell}
, which completes the proof.

A.2.
Proof of Lemma
2.3

We now provide a proof of Lemma
2.3
, which states that if
H
is self–adjoint on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2N})
with
2N=Mp
and
E\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma(H)
, then
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are Lagrangian planes. The proof follows the line of
[
Gon23
, Theorem 27]
, but is simpler due to the fact that we work in finite dimension. Recall that we proved in (
2.7
) that the sets
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are isotropic, and in particular,
{\rm dim}(\ell_{E}^{\pm})\leq N
.

We now further assume that
E\notin\sigma(H)
, and claim that
\ell_{E}^{+}\oplus\ell_{E}^{-}={\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
. This will eventually prove that
{\rm dim}(\ell_{E}^{+})+{\rm dim}(\ell_{E}^{-})\geq 2N
, hence
{\rm dim}(\ell_{E}^{\pm})=N
, and
\ell_{E}^{\pm}
are Lagrangian.

Consider
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
, and let
\Psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
be any function so that
{\rm Tr}(\Psi)=x
. We set

f:=(H-E)\psi\ \in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M}),\qquad f^{+}:={\mathds{1}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{-}}f,\quad f^{-}:={\mathds{1}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}}f.

We used here that
H
comes from a differential operator, which implies that
f
is compactly supported (and in particular is in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
). The functions
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
are square integrable as well, and satisfy
f=f^{+}+f^{-}
. Since
E\notin\sigma(H)
, the operator
(H-E)^{-1}
is bounded on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{M})
, so the functions

\Psi^{+}:=(H-E)^{-1}(f^{+}),\qquad\Psi^{-}:=(H-E)^{-1}(f^{-})

are square integrable, and satisfy
\Psi=\Psi^{+}+\Psi^{-}
. We set
x^{+}:={\rm Tr}(\Psi^{+})
and
x^{-}:={\rm Tr}(\psi^{-})
, so that
x=x^{+}+x^{-}
. We claim that
x^{+}\in\ell_{E}^{+}
. Indeed, consider
\phi^{+}
the solution of the Cauchy problem
({\mathcal{L}}-E)\phi^{+}=0
with initial data
x^{+}
at
t=0
. By definition, we have
\phi^{+}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}
. In addition, we remark that
(H-E)\Psi^{+}=f_{+}
which vanishes on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. By uniqueness of the Cauchy problem, we deduce that
\phi^{+}
coincides with
\Psi^{+}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, so
\phi^{+}
is square–integrable at
+\infty
. This shows that
\phi^{+}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{E}^{+}
, hence that
x^{+}\in\ell_{E}^{+}
. The proof is similar for
x^{-}
, and shows that
\ell_{E}^{+}+\ell_{E}^{-}={\mathbb{C}}^{2N}
. This concludes the proof of Lemma
2.3

A.3.
The symplectic Grassmanian manifold

Finally, we give a short proof of the fact that

{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\cong\bigcup_{m=0}^{n}{\rm Sp}(n)/{\rm Sp}(m)\times{\rm Sp}(n-m).

This appears in class
{\rm CII}
, and this case does not explicitly appear in
[
GMP22
]
(as was the case for the other classes). Consider
A\in{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
, that is

A=A^{*}=A^{-1},\quad\text{and}\quad A^{T}\Omega A=\Omega.

Since
A
is unitary hermitian, we have
\sigma(A)\in\{\pm 1\}
. We denote by
M
the mutiplicity of
1
. Let
P
be the spectral projector on
E:={\rm Ker}(A-1)
(of dimension
M
). Note that
A=2P-1
is the orthogonal reflection with respect to this plane. The condition
A^{T}\Omega=\Omega A
translates directly into

(A.2)

P^{T}\Omega=\Omega P.

This implies that
x\in E
iff
\widetilde{x}:=\Omega\overline{x}\in E
. In addition, we have
\langle\widetilde{x},x\rangle=0
, so the vectors
x
and
\tilde{x}
are always orthogonal. This shows that
M={\rm dim}(E)
is even, of the form
M=2m
, and can be described by a set of
m
orthogonal functions
(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{m})
so that
(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{m},\phi_{m+1},\cdots,\phi_{2m})
is an orthonormal basis of
E
, where we set
\phi_{m+k}:=\Omega\overline{\phi_{k}}
. We call such a basis a
symplectic frame
for
E
.

On the other hand, a matrix
U
is in
{\rm Sp}(n)
iff it belongs to
{\rm U}(2n)
and has of the block form

(A.3)

U=\begin{pmatrix}U_{11}&\overline{U_{12}}\\
U_{12}&-\overline{U_{11}}\end{pmatrix}.

Given such a matrix, with columns
U=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{2n})
, and given
0\leq m\leq n
, one can associate two symplectic frames of size
2m
and
2(n-m)
respectively, namely

\Phi=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{m};u_{n+1},\cdots u_{n+m}),\qquad\Psi=(u_{m+1},u_{m+2},u_{n};u_{n+m+1},\cdots u_{2n}).

So one can associate to any such
U\in{\rm Sp}(n)
a matrix
A\in{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
defined by
A:=2P-1
, where
P
denotes the orthogonal projector on
{\rm Ran}(\Phi)
. In addition, any matrix
A\in{\rm Sp}(n)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
with
{\rm dim}{\rm Ker}(A-1)=1
can be obtained by at least one
U\in{\rm Sp}(n)
with this identification. Finally, two matrices
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
describes the same matrix
A
iff the corresponding frames describe the same spaces: there is
U_{\Phi}\in{\rm U}(2m)
and
U_{\Psi}\in{\rm U}(2n-2m)
so that

\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}U_{\Phi}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}U_{\Psi}.

This further implies that
U_{\Phi}:=\Phi_{2}^{*}\Phi_{1}
and
U_{\Psi}:=\Psi_{2}^{*}\Psi_{1}
are of the form (
A.3
), hence are symplectic matrices, so
U_{\Phi}\in{\rm Sp}(m)
and
U_{\Psi}\in{\rm Sp}(n-m)
. The result follows.
