Metagenomic analysis of bacterial diversity, antibiotic resistance, and functional profiles in the ice core samples from two glaciers of Sikkim Himalaya
Authors: Sonia Tamang, Mingma Thundu Sherpa, Ishfaq Nabi Najar, Santosh Kumar, Sayak Das, Prayatna Sharma, Nandita Das, Rajarshi Chowdhury, Rajnanda Thaosen, Rakesh Kumar Ranjan, Piyush Pandey, Nagendra Thakur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glaciers cover a substantial portion of the world and are home to various biological populations. The Himalayas constitute the largest glaciated region outside the poles; hence, they are regarded as “The Third Pole” of the World. There are around 84 glaciers in the Teesta basin (Sikkim Himalaya). There is substantially less data available on the microbial diversity embedded in the glacial ice core samples of the Sikkim Himalaya, as well as their physico-chemistry and potential geomorphological hazards related to their retreat or decrease in snow-line cover. The present study aims to evaluate the microbial diversity in the glacier ice core region and the study area; therefore, two glaciers in the Sikkim Himalaya were chosen: Frey-Peak and Rathong Glacier. The bacterial diversity analysis reveals the prevalence of various phyla, including Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota. The random forest model reveals the significant contributions of various elements, including Na, Mg, K, Ca, and Zn, to the alpha diversity of the studied glaciers. Among physicochemical parameters, pH was found to contribute the most in shaping bacterial diversity. Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG) analysis underscored a predominance of genes associated with amino acids (23.5%), carbohydrates (18.93%), lipids (10.88%), energy (17.26%), coenzymes (9.38%), and ion transport/metabolism (14.71%). KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) Orthology (KO) analysis revealed the presence of 4,915 to 96,954 genes. Interestingly, the metagenomic analysis revealed the presence of specific species of Bradyrhizobium, Beijerinckia, Burkholderia, and Corynebacterium, which are associated with nitrogen metabolism, suggesting their potential involvement in biogeochemical processes. Additionally, a total of 59 to 419 bacterial genes related to sulphur metabolism were deduced through the KEGG functional analysis. The study detected the presence of various antibiotic resistance genes corresponding to different classes of antibiotics, including aminoglycoside, tetracycline, fluoroquinolone, macrolide, and erythromycin. Network analysis reveals that antibiotic resistance genes primarily interact with the phyla Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Actinomycetota. The melting of glaciers, a significant effect of climate change, may release contaminants, antibiotic resistance genes, and pathogenic bacteria into free-flowing rivers, potentially impacting human health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40915-7.

Introduction

Above 85% of the Earth’s biosphere is subjected to temperatures below 5 °C, which contributes to the formation of the cryosphere, covering about 20% of the overall Earth’s climate system1,2. The Earth’s cryosphere includes solid precipitation, snow, ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, lake ice, and frozen ground3. Glaciers and permafrost are the critical components of the frozen ecosystem, as they preserve freshwater in the form of ice, nutrients, microbes, and organic carbon, thereby contributing to global climate change and affecting the hydrological systems. Hence, glaciers are the key and reliable sources of water4, indicators of climate change5, and are considered important biomes (with a great and ancient diversity of microbes) dominated by microbial populations that preserve active metabolic pathways6.

Glaciers are classified into three ecosystems: the supra-glacial, englacial, and sub-glacial7,8. Solar radiation, water content, nutrient availability, and redox potential vary among these three ecosystems7. These features have a significant impact on the biogeochemical cycles, the type of metabolism, and the diversity and abundance of microbial communities that inhabit glaciers. Psychrotrophs and psychrophiles are the major microbes isolated from these cold environments9. These microorganisms have evolved sophisticated structural (membrane lipid modification, production of antifreeze proteins) and functional (synthesis of cold-adapted enzymes, cold-shock proteins) mechanisms that enable them to grow at low temperatures10–12. Moreover, a wide variety of coloured molecules, such as carotenoids, violacein, and flexirubin, are produced by microorganisms living in the glacier environment, enabling them to adapt to the cold and sunlight13–15. They utilize pigments for energy production16, photosynthesis17, stress tolerance13, and protection from ultraviolet radiation18.

Glaciers are considered highly sensitive indicators of past and present climate change, and most glaciers are retreating due to the compounding effects of climate change5,6,19,20. There is a significant impact on the colonization and succession processes in the regions where glacier “ice” has melted. One of the significant impacts is that psychrophilic communities are replaced by mesophilic microbial communities21. The replacement of psychrophilic communities is mainly driven by the efficient utilization of nutrients by mesophiles; this process eventually disrupts the ecological structure, leads to changes in nutrient cycling, and results in habitat loss, ultimately declining biodiversity22. With the advent of glacier retreats worldwide over the past five decades, the distribution of local biodiversity in the frozen ecosystem has been significantly affected, raising global concerns about escalating threats to the frigid environment23. It is also necessary to explore the physicochemical parameters of glaciers as they help shape microbial diversity and may contribute to the evolution of antibiotic resistance24. Additionally, the combination of organic and inorganic materials that cover glacial ice significantly contributes to biogeochemical cycles and reduces the glacier’s surface albedo25. The mineral matter covering the surface of the ice comes from a variety of places, including nearby moraines26, nearby valley walls27, in-situ melting of englacial material28,29, long-range atmospheric transport from far-off deserts or volcanoes30.

Environments, such as permafrost or continental glaciers, release a tremendous number of dormant but viable microbes that have been imprisoned there for millennia, and several of them are real or potential human pathogens31,32. For example, in various places, Cryptococcus yeasts and bacterial coliforms have been found in ice cores or meltwater runoff33,34. Additionally, evolving pathogens have been identified from Arctic habitats in Greenland and Svalbard35. Antibiotic resistance among pathogenic and non-pathogenic bacteria is also a significant concern in the present world36. The formation of resistant microbes can occur through mutations or the acquisition of mobile genetic elements containing resistance genes36. Many environmental factors may play substantial roles in shaping the selective pressures that drive the rise and spread of antibiotic resistance, influenced by factors such as temperature dependency, contact rates, or horizontal gene transfer24,37. As glaciers are massive freshwater reservoirs, climate change and the rapid retreat of glaciers can release antibiotic resistance genes and resistant microbes into water bodies, posing an emerging threat to human and animal health.

Microbial communities thriving in the cold environment are the source of novel enzymes, metabolites, pigments, and compounds of biotechnological importance38–41. The limited exploration of this region might be due to topological hindrances and the arduous structure of the path leading to the belt42. Recent studies on the microbiome of Himalayan glaciers have begun to provide comprehensive insights into the distribution and functional potential of the bacterial community in the Eastern Himalayan glaciers43. Sherpa et al. have highlighted the prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes and metal resistance genes in the North Sikkim glaciers44. Despite the growing attention to Himalayan glacier microbial dynamics, this field remains underexplored in terms of microbial diversity within glacier ice cores, as well as their functional potential and intricate environmental interactions.

Therefore, to bridge this gap, the present study focuses on the bacterial diversity of ice core samples collected from the two glaciers, Rathong Glacier and Frey-Peak Glacier, located in the Sikkim Himalayan belt, using Shotgun metagenomic sequencing technology. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the antibiotic resistance patterns in these harsh ecosystems and the metabolic pathways leading to biogeochemical processes. Physicochemical properties were measured using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectroscopy (ICP-MS) to determine the elemental composition of these glaciers and to correlate it with microbial diversity and antibiotic resistance. Hence, this integrative approach combining Shotgun metagenomics with ICP-MS analysis of trace metals and profiling of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) provides novel insights into the ecological and potential public health relevance of the Himalayan glacier ice core microbiome.

Methodology

Sampling and site description

The ice core samples were collected from the two glaciers―Frey-Peak and Rathong Glacier of West Sikkim, India. The coordinates and elevation of the sampling site were verified using a GPSMAP 78 S (Garmin, India). The surface ice temperature of the area at the time of sample collection was recorded using an Infrared Thermometer Non-Contact Gun Laser IR Point Digital LCD Temperature (Taiwan, China). The sampling site of the two glaciers was chosen from the accumulation zone of the glacier. The samples from each glacier were collected in triplicates (three sampling sites in close vicinity ~ 10 m apart) to ensure reliability, reproducibility and prevent potential contamination. The sampling was done using an ice corer (Portable ice core drilling machine-KOVACS Enterprise with 1.5 m core barrel (height) and 0.15 m in diameter and ice cores approximately 1.5 m in length were collected aseptically. Thus, three samples from each glacier were collected into sterile Ziplock polythene bags and transported to the laboratory in a cooling ice box, where they were kept at -80 °C. In the laboratory, further processing of the samples was performed at temperatures below 20 °C using laminar airflow in an aseptic manner, as described by Sherpa et al. (2018)45. The 1.5 m ice core samples were sliced into three sections (approximately 0.5 m each) namely upper (0–0.5 m), middle (0.5–1.0 m), and bottom (1.0–1.5 m) core (Fig. 1b), using a sterile saw tooth knife, and the 5 mm of the annulus from each sample was discarded. Each core was treated with chilled ethanol (95%) and rinsed with cold autoclaved distilled water. The samples were then allowed to melt in a sterile glass beaker at 4 °C in an incubator. For negative control, autoclaved distilled water, filtered through 0.22 μm syringe-driven filters (HiMedia, Mumbai, India) was used. The process blanks (background controls) were processed identically to the ice samples to assess the contaminants introduced during sample handling systematically. Thus, a total of 18 samples from two glaciers, comprising nine samples (three samples × three sections each) from each glacier, were processed. Then the three upper cores, three middle cores, and three bottom cores of three samples of a glacier were merged and named FP1 (Upper Core), FP2 (Middle Core), and FP3 (Bottom Core) for the Frey-Peak glacier. Similarly, the merged three upper, three middle and three bottom cores of Rathong glacier were named RT1 (Upper Core), RT2 (Middle Core), and RT3 (Bottom Core). These six merged cores were then used for further study. Since metagenome analysis is susceptible to sample size, the ice core samples were merged to increase the sample size and minimize bias in the results. Pooling of the individual ice core samples to a composite sample was necessary to ensure sufficient biomass for Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and to minimize the contamination risk. Although this approach eliminates the biological replicates, each composite or merged sample represents a distinct depth profile, allowing for site-level comparison.

Physicochemical analysis

The samples were filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter (Sigma-Aldrich). After filtration, the melted ice core samples were treated with 2% (v/v) Nitric acid to dissolve the metals and stabilize the elements during the ICP-MS process, which was carried out using an Agilent Technologies (Model: 7900) instrument. A total of 19 elements, such as Li, Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Se, Mo, Ag, Cd, Pb, Na, P, and Cr, was checked. Physical parameters of the samples, such as pH and TDS with NaCl concentrations, were measured in the laboratory using a portable water quality checker (Horiba, Japan; U-50 Series).

Metagenomic DNA extraction

Environmental DNA from the ice water sample was extracted using the DNeasy PowerWater Kit (50) from Qiagen (Cat: 14900-50-NF) (QIAGEN India Pvt. Ltd., India) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the DNA was quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with a detection limit of 10–100 ng/µL, and then visualized on a 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing

The samples’ eDNA were subjected to standard paired-end DNA sequencing library preparation using an Illumina 4000 as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were sequenced using a 101-bp paired-end sequencing module. After sequencing, the reads obtained for each library were subjected to quality control using the NGSQC toolkit to obtain high-quality (HQ) reads in FASTQ format from the samples46. Stringent criteria of 80:30 were used to obtain high-quality filtered reads, wherein more than 80% of the bases had Phred scores of 30 or higher, and were considered for building the genome assembly. HQ-filtered paired-end libraries were trimmed using the popular read trimming tool Trimmomatic v0.36, with default parameters (Galaxy platform)47. Then, the reads were assembled to generate contigs using the de Bruijn graph-based assembler MetaSPAdes48, with default parameters. Further preprocessing, dereplication, DRISEE (Duplicate Read Inferred Sequencing Error Estimation), and screening were done on the MG-RAST platform. The MG-RAST platform was selected for taxonomic profiling and functional annotation of the metagenomic sequence extracted from the glacier ice core sample because it provides a publicly accessible pipeline for metagenomic data analysis, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. This server provides integrated tools, such as the SEED subsystem, KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and COG (Cluster of Orthologous Groups), without requiring users to switch between them. The server also serves as the curated public repository for the metagenomic datasets49. Although more recent tools, such as QIIME2 and MetaPhlAn, are available for metagenomic analysis, MG-RAST was used to compare the data with previously published microbiome studies and to ensure the reproducibility of the data.

Metagenomic data analysis

The assembled sequences were used to identify microbial taxonomy. Annotations, function, and composition of microbial communities were done using MG-RAST (https://mg-rast.org)50. The resulting representative OTUs were given taxonomic classing using the SILVA database51. and RefSeq database52. The output of this workflow is a classification of reads at several taxonomic levels: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Sequences without homologous pairs were classified as unknown. The predicted genes’ functional classification based on KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) orthology was done using FMAP (Functional Mapping and Analysis Pipeline)53 and COG (Cluster of Orthologous Groups)54 using the MG-RAST platform50.

Antibiotic resistance gene detection from metagenomic sequences

The CARD (https://card.mcmaster.ca/home) pipeline was used to check the presence of antibiotic resistance genes in metagenomic assembled sequences55. CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database) is a thoroughly curated database that stores information related to ARGs, associated antibiotic classes, and resistant mechanisms. Other pipelines, such as Galaxy ARGA and Galaxy ABRicate56 using the Galaxy platform, were used to detect the presence of antibiotic resistance genes in metagenomic assembled sequences. The ABRicate tool in the Galaxy platform performs the data search (antibiotic resistance genes) against various curated repositories such as CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database), NCBI, PlasmidFinder, and ResFinder57. The predicted resistance genes have been cross-checked using similarity searches in the NCBI non-redundant database.

Statistical analysis

R software (v4.3.3; https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/old/4.3.3/)58 was used for principal component analysis and generating bubble plots. The Shannon diversity indices, Chao1, were calculated using EstimateS (version 9.1.0; http://viceroy.eeb.uconn.edu/estimates/) and PAleontological Statistics (PAST v4.03) software (https://www.nhm.uio.no/english/research/resources/past/)59. One-way ANOVA in the PAST software (v4.03) was used to compare alpha diversity indices between sample groups, and permutation tests (99,999 iterations) were performed within the ANOVA framework to obtain robust significance values. The rarefaction curve was plotted using iNEXT (iNterpolation and EXTrapolation) Online, an R-based interactive online tool (https://chao.shinyapps.io/iNEXTOnline/)60. The heat map was used to analyze comparative bacterial diversity at the genus level using R software (v4.3.3) and the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix (package: ggplot, function: heatmap. 2). A Random Forest Model was performed in R software (v4.3.3) to assess variable importance. Network analysis was done using Cytoscape (v 3.9.1; https://cytoscape.org/index.html)61.

Results

Sampling sites

Frey-Peak and Rathong Glaciers are important geographical sites within the Sikkim Himalayas, which are part of the larger Eastern Himalayas62. These glaciers are influenced by both the Indian summer monsoon and winter precipitation from the Northeast monsoon and Westerlies, which are crucial factors affecting glacier dynamics63. The elevation of the Frey-Peak Glacier sampling site was approximately 5,551 m a.s.l. (meters above sea level) with geographical coordinates of 27.54268°N, 88.09500°E. On the other hand, the elevation of the Rathong Glacier sampling site was 4,840 m a.s.l. (meters above sea level) with the geographical coordinates 27.58412°N, 88.10783°E. The radiant surface temperature of the sampling location was recorded using a non-contact infrared thermometer (Laser IR Point Digital LCD, Taiwan, China), which measured around − 34 °C and − 30 °C for Frey-Peak and Rathong Glaciers, respectively (Table 1). The geospatial mapping of the sampling site was performed using Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS) software (v3.42.3; QGIS Development Team, 2025, https://qgis.org) (Fig. 1a).

Physicochemical analysis

The physical parameters, such as pH and Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), were measured, and it was found that the pH of Rathong Glacier was mildly acidic (pH 6.6). In contrast, the pH of Frey-Peak Glacier was slightly alkaline (pH 7.2). NaCl concentrations were higher in the case of the Rathong Glacier (7.3 ppb) than in the Frey-Peak Glacier (5.6 ppb). The TDS (Total dissolved solids) was higher in the case of the Frey-Peak Glacier (7.2 mg L− 1) than that of the Rathong Glacier (4.7 mg L− 1) (Table 1). ICP-MS also analyzed various elements, revealing that the concentration of most studied elements was significantly lower. However, the elemental concentrations of Na, Mg, K, Ca, and Zn were relatively higher than those of other tested elements (such as Co, Cu, and Pb) in the two studied glacier ice samples (Table 1).

Taxonomic overview of shotgun sequences

The shotgun sequencing technique was utilized to determine the microbial diversity of ice core samples. More than 15 million reads were extracted from each core sample, with over 12 million unique reads remaining after the quality check. The GC content ranged from 45% to 65% (Table 2). No amplification was observed in the extraction/PCR control, indicating negligible background contamination throughout the workflow. Taxonomic analysis revealed the presence of 37 phyla, 120 orders, 255 families, 607 genera, and 1,595 bacterial species, indicating a rich bacterial diversity with varied metabolic functions inherent within the studied sites of the two glaciers. Pseudomonadota emerged as the predominant phylum, constituting 21.94% to 69.81%, followed by Actinomycetota, which ranged from 12.34% to 75.95% across the different ice cores analyzed. Other significant phyla, such as Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Acidobacteriota, and Cyanobacteriota, were also prevalent in the glacier ecosystem. Other phyla, such as Deinococcota, Planctomycetota, Spirochaetota, Verrucomicrobiota and Chloroflexota were also found to be abundant in such cold regions. There were considerable differences in the distribution of various phyla among different core samples. For example, in the Frey-Peak glacier, the upper core (FP1) showed a higher abundance of phylum Pseudomonadota (47.24%), followed by Bacillota (33.7%). In contrast, the upper core (RT1) of Rathong Glacier showed a higher abundance of phylum Actinomycetota (76.3%), followed by Pseudomonadota (21.7%) (Fig. 2). This considerable distinction was observed only in the upper core samples of the two glaciers. Moreover, there was less difference in the prevalence of bacterial phyla in the other two core samples of these two glaciers. In these core samples, such as middle cores, the prevalence of Pseudomonadota (FP2 [63.6%]/RT2 [66.2%]) was higher, followed by Actinomycetota (FP2 [27.1%]/RT2 [31.1%]). Similarly, the bottom cores were showing the prevalence of Pseudomonadota (FP3 [72.7%]/RT3 [70.8%]) followed by Actinomycetota (FP3 [21.1%]/RT3 [25.1%]) (Fig. 2). Also, it was shown that the overall abundance of bacterial phyla was higher in the case of the Frey-Peak Glacier than that of the Rathong Glacier. The principal component analysis (PCA) shows that the phyla Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota which were the predominant phyla in the studied glacier sample, were also the good contributors to bacterial diversity along with the less abundant phyla such as Fusobacteriota, Cyanobacteriota, Planctomycetota, Spirochaetota, etc. (Supplementary Fig. 1). It was also shown by the PCA plot that the upper and middle cores are highly correlated to most of the bacterial phyla which can also be predicted by the higher abundance of bacterial phyla among the upper and the middle cores than the bottom cores (Supplementary Fig. 1).

At the order level, Actinomycetales (77.8–18.18%) in FP1 and RT1, Burkholderiales (19.32–9.09%) in FP1 and FP2, Enterobacteriales (53.85–11.36%) in FP1 and FP2, and Pseudomonadales (65.22–48.02%) in FP1 and FP2 were found to be associated with fatty acid metabolism and biosynthesis. However, FP3 did not reveal the presence of any bacterial order associated with fatty acid metabolism. Species-level analysis identified key taxa, including Acinetobacter baumannii (13.02%), Escherichia coli (17.95%), Burkholderia pseudomallei (9.09%), Brucella ceti (4.76%), Bradyrhizobium sp. BTAi1 (3.03%), and Achromobacter xylosoxidans (2.97%), indicating their involvement in carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2). Hence, the genus-level annotation provides comprehensive insights into the taxonomic diversity and metabolic versatility of glacier microbiomes in high-altitude regions. In contrast, the species-level annotation represents database-driven indications rather than definitive identification.

Diversity indices

Bacterial alpha diversity indices, such as Fisher Alpha, Chao-1, Shannon, and Simpson, showed that all diversity indices were considerably higher for the Frey-Peak Glacier. The mean indices, such as Simpson (0.98), Shannon (5.27), Fisher Alpha (178.1), and Chao-1 (1189), were higher in the Frey-Peak Glacier as compared to the Rathong Glacier, with various mean indices as Simpson (0.96), Shannon (4.55), Fisher Alpha (164), and Chao-1 (1158). It was also demonstrated that the bottom core of the Frey-Peak Glacier exhibits a lower abundance of diversity indices compared to the upper and middle cores (Supplementary Table 1). We have observed a similar trend in the abundance of various genera and species, indicating that lower abundance is primarily present in the bottom core of the Frey-Peak Glacier. However, in the case of the Rathong Glacier, the upper core showed lesser diversity indices than the middle and bottom cores (Fig. 4) (Supplementary Table 1). Similar results were depicted in the Rarefaction curve, with higher diversity curves representing the upper and middle cores of Frey-Peak and Rathong Glaciers than the bottom cores. This also depicted that Rathong Glacier was showing lower rarefaction curves than the Frey-Peak Glacier, representing lesser diversity in Rathong Glacier as compared to Frey-Peak Glacier (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences between glacier groups (FP-Frey-Peak and RT-Rathong Glacier) (F = 100.2, p < 0.0001; permutation p = 0.0001). The large between-group sum of squares (5.70 × 10⁶) compared to the within-group (FP1-FP3 and RT1-RT3) sum of squares indicated that the difference between glacier groups (FP and RT) accounts for most of the variance in alpha diversity indices. However, the variance within each group (FP1-FP3 and RT1-RT3) was relatively more minor. Frey-Peak ice core samples exhibited higher Simpson, Shannon, and Fisher’s alpha indices (mean Simpson = 0.983 ± 0.010; Shannon = 5.27 ± 0.30; Fisher’s alpha = 178.2 ± 20.3) compared to Rathong ice core samples (mean Simpson = 0.962 ± 0.018; Shannon = 4.56 ± 0.29; Fisher’s alpha = 164.0 ± 13.5), whereas, Chao-1 richness was more or less similar in both the glacier core sample (FP = 1189 ± 430; RT = 1158 ± 23). Levene’s test indicated unequal variance between glacier samples (p = 0.012), reflecting a greater spread of values among the Frey-Peak core sample than the more consistent Rathong cores. The variance heterogeneity violated Levene’s test. Therefore, Welch’s test was applied (F = 310.5, df = 8.34, p < 0.000001), which confirmed the observed significant difference between Frey-peak and Rathong Glacier despite unequal variances between ice core samples.

Correlation of bacterial diversity with elemental parameters

The PCA was done to check the correlation among various elements and dominant bacterial phyla with respect to different ice cores. The results have shown that all the considered elements, including Mg, Na, Zn, Ca, K, and Mn, were positively correlated with various dominant bacterial phyla, such as Bacillota, Actinomycetota, and Bacteroidota, as well as a few less abundant phyla, including Fusobacteriota and Spirochaetota. This positive correlation was observed in the upper and middle cores only (Fig. 5). Moreover, it was demonstrated that pH was negatively correlated with these elements and abundant phyla, whereas showing a positive correlation with other less abundant phyla, such as Chloroflexota, Cyanobacteriota, Acidobacteriota, and Planctomycetota. However, it was shown that temperature does not show any correlation. These correlation results were further cross-checked using the Random Forest Model to assess variable importance. It was shown that in both the Frey-Peak and Rathong Glaciers pH is playing paramount importance in shaping bacterial diversity followed by many important elements such as Ca, Mg, Na, and K. Some heavy elements such as Zn, Co, Cu, Mn, and Cd were also showing importance significantly and contributed in shaping bacterial diversity in these glaciers (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b).

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) analysis

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were detected in metagenomic sequences. It was shown that antibiotic genes were more prevalent in the Frey-Peak Glacier than in the Rathong Glacier. It was surprising that multidrug-resistant genes (MDR) (86%) were showing the highest abundance in the Frey-Peak Glacier. The other abundant resistant classes were aminoglycoside (28%), tetracycline (21%), and fluoroquinolone (14%) in the Frey-Peak Glacier. On the other hand, in the case of the Rathong Glacier, the abundant resistance classes were aminoglycoside (14%), macrolide (10%), MDR (5%), and elfamycin (5%) (Fig. 11). Core-wise data analysis showed that in the case of the Frey-Peak Glacier, the upper and middle cores were abundant in ARGs, whereas in the case of the Rathong Glacier, the middle and bottom cores showed their prevalence (Supplementary Excel File 1).

Discussion

Core-wise diversity analysis was carried out for the first time in the two glaciers of the Eastern Himalayan region. In the present study, the phylum Bacillota showed considerable differences between the two studied glaciers. The phylum Bacillota was more abundant in the Frey-Peak Glacier than in the Rathong Glacier. This distinction is mainly seen in the upper cores of these glaciers. As known from other studies64, there has been a considerable retreat of the Rathong Glacier. Apart from this, the glaciers of the Sikkim Himalayas are monsoon-fed and are more predominantly vulnerable to temperature rise65. Moreover, Frey-Peak Glacier has a higher elevation and lower temperature than the Rathong Glacier, with a temperature difference of 4–5 °C. These reasons may hypothesize that with the temperature increase, there might be more liquid precipitation65, thus causing significant ice loss in Rathong Glacier than in Frey-Peak Glacier. The loss of ice in the glacier is primarily due to evapotranspiration caused by warmer temperatures, which leads to the melting of snow into water droplets. This may be contributing to the accumulation of more bacterial phyla in the Frey-Peak Glacier compared to the upper retreating layers of Rathong Glacier. At the genus level, mostly psychrotolerant bacteria were found in both the glaciers. The prevalent genera included Pseudomonas, Corynebacterium, Acinetobacter, Staphylococcus, Clostridium, Burkholderia, Arthrobacter, Rhodococcus, Serratia, and Stenotrophomonas. Many other studies have demonstrated the presence of such genera, as evidenced by culture-dependent bacterial diversity analyses in the glaciers of the Gharwal Himalayas66. Similarly, other studies have also reported similar findings regarding the prevalence of such genera in the East Rathong Glacier67,68. However, a few genera, such as Bradyrhizobium, Rhodopseudomonas, and Mesorhizobium, were found to be prevalent only in the Rathong Glacier. These genera are primarily plant-associated and contribute to nitrogen fixation, often found in glacier forefields or retreating glaciers (or recently exposed soils), where nitrogen levels are relatively low in the soil69,70. These findings suggest that the Rathong Glacier is retreating more rapidly than the Frey-Peak Glacier. This may be further supported by comparing the diversity indices between these two glaciers. Therefore, one-way ANOVA was conducted, which revealed that alpha diversity indices differed strongly between the two glaciers. All the diversity indices (such as Fisher Alpha, Chao-1, Shannon, and Simpson) were higher in the case of Frey-peak Glacier cores compared to those of Rathong Glacier cores. This suggests that there is more accumulation of bacterial phyla in the Frey-Peak Glacier than in the Rathong Glacier, which may be due to its retreating landscape.

Our previous studies on the Changme Khangpu glacier, located in the northern district of Sikkim, showed the abundance of phyla Pseudomonadota (56%), Bacillota (16%), Actinomycetota (12%), and Bacteroidota (8%)42. The culture-independent study, in the East Rathong Glacier also revealed the predominance of phyla Pseudomonadota (64.16%), Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota (15.8%), and Bacillota (3.33%)67 along with other phyla such as Bacteroidota, Patescibacteriota, Cyanobacteriota, and Actinomycetota, in supraglacial and proglacial sediment samples. A similar study was done on the glaciers of the West Kunlun Mountains, which lie along the northern edge of the Tibetan Plateau which had similar prevalence of the earlier reported phyla in these glacier snow samples71.

Elemental analysis revealed that both the studied glaciers have lower elemental concentrations. The Na, Mg, K, Ca, and Zn were slightly higher concentrations than other elements. These results are consistent with other studies where elemental concentrations from snow-covered glaciers are considerably low72–74. Moreover, the core-wise distribution of various elemental concentrations was distinct in both glaciers. In the case of Frey-Peak Glacier, the upper core had considerably higher concentrations of elements as compared to the middle and bottom cores. On the other hand, in the Rathong Glacier, the middle core exhibits higher elemental concentrations compared to the upper and lower cores. Elemental deposition in glaciers primarily occurs through dust carried by wind from local outcrops in the atmosphere75,76. It has been observed that a minor change in any of the three variables, such as rainfall, air temperature, and snow precipitation, can have a significant impact on the pattern of snow accumulation77. Hence, it may be suggested that there is less precipitation or accumulation (more retreat of the upper layers of the glacier) of snow in Rathong Glacier than in Frey-Peak Glacier. This might be the reason we are getting higher concentrations of elements in the middle core of Rathong Glacier and the upper cores of Frey-Peak Glacier.

Another important facet is the infiltration of meltwater into the deeper ice layers during the warmer season, which plays a significant role in the transport of deposited elements, along with microbes, into the underlying snowpack78–80. Besides these findings, the elements deposited through aeolian processes are important factors in shaping the bacterial diversity in these glaciers. The PCA reveals a positive correlation between elements such as Na, Mg, K, Ca, and Zn and dominant phyla, including Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Fusobacteriota, and Bacteroidota. The Random Forest model analysis of variable importance provided support for this. The variable importance has shown the significant impact of these elements on the alpha diversity of these glaciers. The other governing factor was found to be pH, as shown by both PCA and variable importance analysis, apart from the temperature of the sample, which exhibits a negative correlation or negligible importance. The role of pH on bacterial diversity has been widely studied, and the findings suggest that pH is a more significant factor affecting microbial diversity than sample temperature81–84.

Cold-adapted microbes have evolved various adaptation mechanisms, including the arrangement of fatty acids in their membranes, which significantly impacts the membrane’s characteristics85. In this study, the genes (FabG, FabF, FabH, FabD, FabI, FabK) that are engaged in fatty acid biosynthesis were found to be associated with the fatty acid metabolic pathway. This highlights the involvement of these genes in maintaining the fluidity of the cell membrane in extreme temperatures86. Moreover, the abundance of genes related to glycerolipid and sphingolipid metabolism confers the adaptation mechanism against cold stress. Within an ecosystem, the presence of methanogenic and methanotrophic bacteria, as identified in the metagenomes, frequently regulates global carbon metabolism and carbon fixation. The presence of methanogens, Methylobacterium sp. 4–46 and Methylobacterium extorquens, are the sole organism identified with the capability to generate methane, and they accomplish this exclusively under anaerobic conditions87. The gene homologs indicative of assimilatory and dissimilatory nitrate reduction pathways serves as essential genetic markers for active nitrogen metabolism in sample sites88. Furthermore, the microbial phyla identified in this metagenomic analysis, namely Beijerinckia indica and Bradyrhizobium sp. BTAi1, Bradyrhizobium japonicum, Bradyrhizobium sp. ORS278, Burkholderia xenovorans, and Corynebacterium jeikeium are recognized for their involvement in regulating the biogeochemical nitrogen cycle. Furthermore, the utilization of sulfur compounds serves as a primary energy-generating process that supports communities within cold sulfur springs89.

The metagenomic analysis revealed a notable abundance of carbohydrate-active enzymes, suggesting their potential involvement in various essential biological processes. Among these enzymes, several glycosyltransferases (GTs) serve as permeases, facilitating the transport of various metal ions and inorganic compounds vital for the survival of microorganisms90. They are involved in cooperative interactions with regulatory proteins, playing a crucial role in cellular communication and microbial adaptability to the extracellular environment. It is worth noting that, in this study, the predicted metabolic functions derived from the metagenomic data were not experimentally validated due to logistical constraints related to sample quantity and preservation. Hence, future studies incorporating targeted gene amplification will be essential to validate the predictions.

Another important facet of this study was to highlight the prevalence of antibiotic resistance in these pristine glaciers. In the present study, we have found a considerable occurrence of putative antibiotic-resistance genes in both studied glaciers. A higher abundance of multidrug antibiotic resistance genes (MDR) was found in these glaciers. The abundance of various antibiotic-resistance classes was generally higher at Frey-Peak Glacier, which may be attributed to the higher alpha diversity indices, or, in other words, the higher bacterial abundance in Frey-Peak Glacier.

A study on 54 glaciers in the Arctic, Antarctic, Central Asia, North America, South America, and Africa has revealed widespread dissemination of antibiotic-resistance genes in samples from these glaciers91. Antibiotic resistance to aminoglycosides and β-lactams has also been observed in various parts of Antarctica92. Our previous study on the debris-covered glacier (Changme Khangpu) and the debris-free glacier (Changme Khang) in the Sikkim Himalaya revealed the prevalence of various antibiotic resistance classes, including aminoglycosides, β-lactams, tetracyclines, bacitracin, and MDR44. Even the most pristine and distant glaciers have been shown to contain a startling number of bacteria that are resistant to various antibiotics and can flourish in the presence of high doses of antibiotics in vitro31,93–96.

According to a recent study, culture-dependent analysis also revealed the presence of integrons and ARGs in culturable bacterial isolates retrieved from the non-polar Passu Glacier in the Karakoram Mountain range97. The presence of bacteria harbouring integrons in samples taken from far-off glaciers is particularly pertinent (and concerning). More crucially, integrons may be transported horizontally across a microbial community as a component of other mobile genetic elements, such as plasmids and transposons. These complex genetic elements are capable of grabbing genes and gene cassettes, typically encoding for antibiotic resistance (Gillings, [100]). Hence, the findings of potential ARGs in the glacier environment of the Sikkim Himalaya are concerning. Wang et al. reported that these ARGs can be disseminated to the downstream ecosystem, potentially altering the microbiome structure and disrupting the ecosystem overall98. However, the detection of putative ARGs in glacier metagenomes should be interpreted with caution, as short-read annotations may include environmental homologs or false positives. Although these findings represent potential resistome signals, they do not substantiate direct public health concerns. Future studies employing de novo contig assembly will be necessary for proper validation of the presence of ARGs. Additionally, the distribution pattern of ARGs in the glacier environment is unknown and remains the subject of investigation; the insight from our study may provide a valuable basis for further assessment of their distribution pattern in the downstream ecosystems.

In the present study, we also identified various potential clinically relevant taxa, including Staphylococcus, Pseudomonas, Corynebacterium, Clostridium, and Serratia. This suggests that glaciers may be a potential source of pathogenic bacteria and antibiotic resistance genes. Statistical analysis reveals a strong positive correlation among antibiotic resistance genes, various elements, and bacterial phyla. Network analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between various antibiotic resistance genes and different bacterial phyla. In the case of the Frey-Peak Glacier, most antibiotic resistance genes interact with the phylum Pseudomonadota, followed by Bacillota, and a few interact with Actinomycetota. However, in the case of the Rathong Glacier, the phylum Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota equally interact with different antibiotic resistance genes. In contrast, the phylum Bacillota interacts with only a few antibiotic resistance genes. These findings also suggested that the presence of the potential clinically relevant taxa detected in this study pertains mainly to Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Actinomycetota.

While MG-RAST remains a widely used platform for metagenomic analysis due to its reproducibility, robustness, and integrated workflow, it is worth noting that this platform may generate higher false positives at the species-level taxonomic assignment. Therefore, we have interpreted species-level calls with caution and emphasized broader taxonomic trends (at the genus level). In addition, its reliance on short-read sequencing limits the resolution of genomic diversity at the strain level and possibly constrains the functional potential. Metagenomic data generated by this platform cannot differentiate between metabolically active and dormant microbial populations, which hinders ecological interpretation. Hence, to ensure transparency, these limitations are clearly recognized. We also acknowledge that the advent of new tools such as MetaPhlAn, Kraken2, and QIIME2 incorporates more recent algorithms and databases. Nevertheless, comparative studies have demonstrated that MG-RAST generates taxonomically similar results to newer pipelines, such as Kraken2 and QIIME299.

Conclusions

The present study aimed to highlight the bacterial diversity of the two glaciers of Sikkim Himalaya and to evaluate their elemental composition. The other facet was to evaluate the prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes and functional classification genes through shotgun metagenomic sequencing analysis. The present study reveals the presence of various phyla, including Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota. These findings are consistent with other studies conducted on glaciers worldwide. At the genus level, we found both psychrophilic bacteria and mesophilic bacteria. We found fewer concentrations of various elements in these glaciers. However, these elements were showing a strong positive correlation with microbial diversity and antibiotic resistance genes. pH was found to be the other governing factor in shaping the bacterial diversity in these glaciers. The use of KEGG pathway analysis revealed numerous metabolic pathways that play a crucial role in the survival of resident bacteria and have a positive impact on the microbiome of these regions. This study also reveals the presence of various antibiotic resistance genes and clinically relevant taxa. This may be due to the long-term accumulation of local anthropogenic pollutants carried by wind or the environmental acquisition, as suggested by various researchers. Hence, further investigation in this matter is required as the pathogenic microbes, their antibiotic resistance genes, and contaminants in the glacier can be released into the water system (might affect human health) because of the melting of glaciers, which is a dreadful impact of climate change.
