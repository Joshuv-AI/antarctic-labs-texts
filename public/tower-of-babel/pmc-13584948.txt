Bibliometric Analysis of Gut Microbiota and Hepatocellular Carcinoma: Research Trends and Frontiers
Authors: Chengpu Zhao, Xiangcheng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) pathogenesis via the gut-liver axis. Existing gut microbiota-HCC studies are mostly narrative, with three critical knowledge gaps: lack of data-driven global research mapping, unclear staged field evolution, and insufficient quantitative identification of research frontiers. This bibliometric study aims to fill these gaps by systematically quantifying field-wide research trends, hotspots and frontiers.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was performed including 1383 English original and review articles (2010–2026) retrieved from WOSCC and PubMed. Bibliometrix, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace were applied to conduct co-authorship and co-citation analysis for countries, institutions and authors, keyword co-occurrence, clustering and burst detection. Bibliometric metrics including Bradford’s law, Lotka’s law, h-index, g-index and m-index were applied to analyse publication trends, research outputs, journal-author features and research hotspots.ResultsAnnual publications increased by 26.95%, with 22.49% international collaboration. China led output (679 papers) but with lower citation impact, while the USA (299 papers) dominated high-quality research. Close domestic academic connections were observed within Chinese and US institutional clusters, whereas cross-national cooperation between Chinese and US institutions remained limited. Top productive institutions were mainly from China, while US institutions exhibited superior citation performance. Journal distribution followed Bradford’s law, with Frontiers-series open-access journals dominating the core journal zone. Keyword clustering revealed three successive macro research phases: early association studies (2010–2017), gut-liver axis mechanism exploration (2018–2021), and current focus on microbial metabolites and immunotherapy modulation (2022–2026).ConclusionThe field shows rapid expansion with a China–USA dual research high-output research pattern, shifting toward microbial metabolites and microbiota-targeted immunotherapy. This work provides comprehensive quantitative data to compensate for missing panoramic and staged analyses in prior literature; future advances rely on causal mechanism verification, large-scale clinical trials and strengthened international collaboration.

Introduction

As a highly malignant form of cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) continues to pose a significant risk of incidence and mortality worldwide. According to statistics, in 2022, there were over 865,000 new cases of HCC globally, and nearly 758,000 deaths from the disease. It ranks sixth in incidence and third in mortality among all types of cancer.1 HCC develops and progresses under the combined influence of multiple factors; currently recognized high-risk factors include chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, advanced liver fibrosis, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).2 Nevertheless, further in-depth research is needed to elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms driving the malignant progression of this tumor.

The gut microbiota constitutes the largest microecosystem in the human body.3 Mounting epidemiological, animal and translational studies over the past decade have established a tight causal linkage between intestinal microbiota dysbiosis and multiple solid tumors beyond HCC, including colorectal, pancreatic, gastric and biliary tract cancers, laying a fundamental theoretical foundation for our field analysis.4 Broadly, gut flora imbalance disrupts intestinal barrier integrity, accelerates microbial translocation, releases LPS, secondary bile acids and toxic metabolites, reshapes systemic chronic inflammatory state and tumor immune microenvironment to drive tumor initiation and metastasis.5 Several researches confirmed tumor-specific microbial signatures exist across multiple digestive malignancies, and fecal microbial biomarkers achieve satisfactory performance for non-invasive early screening of gastrointestinal tumors.6–8 Studies have confirmed that the gut microbiota also plays a key role in promoting the development of liver disease and HCC.9 Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can lead to an imbalance in its metabolic products, resulting in the production of large amounts of pro-inflammatory substances. At the same time, impairment of the intestinal barrier function allows large amounts of microbes, microbial metabolites, and pro-inflammatory substances enter the bloodstream and reach the liver, thereby promoting a series of inflammatory reactions in the liver, causing chronic liver damage, and leading to the progression of chronic liver disease to HCC.10 Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota also affects the body’s antitumor immune surveillance function, promotes the formation of a tumor inflammatory microenvironment, and induces the progression of liver disease to HCC.11 Specifically for liver malignancies, numerous cohort and preclinical experiments validated the core mediating role of gut-liver axis: characteristic flora shifts emerge sequentially during the progression from chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis to HCC, and differential microbial taxa exert bidirectional tumor-promoting or anti-tumor effects by regulating bile acid receptors (FXR/TGR5), TLR4-NF-κB inflammatory signaling and short-chain fatty acid mediated immune homeostasis.12,13 Recent translational advances further indicated gut microbiota can remodel response to immune checkpoint inhibitors across multiple cancers;14 antibiotic disturbance of microecology significantly attenuates anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy,15 while probiotic supplementation and FMT restore anti-tumor immunity, which makes microbiota-targeted intervention a promising adjuvant anti-tumor strategy for HCC and other digestive tumors.16,17

Bibliometrics is a field of study that employs quantitative and statistical methods to analyze the patterns of academic output and dissemination.18 Different from conventional narrative reviews that rely on subjective literature selection and mainly focus on summarizing discrete individual mechanisms, bibliometric approaches enable objective, large-scale quantitative evaluation of a research domain.19 By applying the methods of this discipline, researchers can systematically monitor the reach and societal impact of academic work, accurately identify cutting-edge issues and emerging trends within specific research fields, promptly identify promising new research directions, and effectively promote cross-institutional and cross-regional research collaboration.20

Over the past decade, research on the gut microbiota and HCC has expanded rapidly; however, most existing reviews employ a narrative approach, focusing on individual mechanisms rather than systematic quantitative analysis. Existing narrative reviews mainly concentrate on biological mechanisms and experimental outcomes, while leaving three concrete knowledge gaps related to field-level macroscopic features unaddressed. First, they cannot quantitatively depict global collaboration patterns, including cross-national cooperation intensity, institutional network structure, and the imbalance between research output quantity and academic quality across leading countries. Second, they fail to objectively divide distinct temporal evolution phases and identify key turning points of this research domain based on complete literature datasets. Third, they lack quantitative evidence to track shifting research themes and objectively recognize emerging hotspots and frontiers via keyword dynamics. Three key gaps remain: (1) no comprehensive, data-driven mapping of the global research landscape; (2) lack of clear delineation of temporal evolution phases and key turning points; (3) insufficient evidence-based identification of emerging frontiers and future directions. Previous bibliometric studies have elucidated global research trends in the field of gut microbiota and HCC;21 these studies primarily utilized the Web of Science Core Collection database (data cutoff: 2022), incorporating a total of 739 relevant publications. These earlier works offered general descriptions of country, institution and keyword distributions, yet had several notable limitations. They excluded PubMed-sourced clinical literature, adopted relatively limited bibliometric indicators without implementing Bradford’s law, Lotka’s law or multi-dimensional author-level indices (h-index, g-index, m-index), and did not systematically divide the temporal evolutionary phases of this research field.

To address these gaps, this study employed bibliometric analysis to systematically examine publication trends, national/institutional contributions, journal distribution, author productivity, and keyword dynamics within this research field. Specifically, we proposed four explicit research questions to guide our analysis: (RQ1) What are the global publication trends, national-institutional contribution patterns, journal distribution, and author productivity within gut microbiota-HCC research? (RQ2) What is the international collaboration status in this field, and what are the differences in research output quantity and citation impact between major contributing countries and institutions? (RQ3) What are the thematic clusters, temporal evolution phases, and keyword-burst-detected emerging frontiers of gut microbiota-HCC research? (RQ4) Based on bibliometric findings, what are the priority future directions for gut microbiota-HCC basic and translational research? This study aims to quantitatively reveal the current state of global research and identify core hotspots and emerging frontiers in gut microbiota-HCC research. It is hoped that the findings will provide clear guidance for future basic research, clinical translation, and innovative international collaboration.

Materials and Methods

Database and Search Strategy

This study selected WOSCC and PubMed as literature search platforms. Several major databases including Scopus, Embase, and Dimensions were not incorporated in the current analysis. WOSCC was selected because it is widely recognized for bibliometric studies, offering standardized citation metadata compatible with Bibliometrix, VOSviewer and CiteSpace. PubMed was included to complement WOSCC, capturing high-quality biomedical original and review manuscripts focused on gut microbiota and hepatocellular carcinoma. Embase contains large volumes of conference abstracts and grey literature outside our inclusion criteria; Scopus and Dimensions provide broader coverage but demand heavy-duty manual disambiguation for author, affiliation and citation records. Given our inclusion criteria limited to English original and review articles, the combination of WOSCC and PubMed is sufficient to retrieve mainstream peer-reviewed literature for this field, consistent with many previously published bibliometric studies on gut microbiota-related topics.22,23 To ensure the reliability and scientific validity of the results, the search terms were set as “gut microbiota” and “hepatocellular carcinoma”, and the search strategy was refined based on previous relevant research findings.24,25 For further details on the search strategy, please refer to the Supplementary Materials. The entire literature search and screening process is illustrated in Figure 1. The review spanned over 15 years, beginning on January 1, 2010, and ending on May 11, 2026. Publications included in the analysis were limited to original research articles and review articles in English. To minimize systematic errors caused by database updates, the team implemented a comprehensive and systematic search and screening process. Additionally, a deduplication strategy was established for journals published in both databases. During the data collection phase, the initial set of literature exported from WOSCC and PubMed was deduplicated using the built-in duplication check module in EndNote. This tool precisely identifies and removes duplicate records by comparing core fields such as title, authorship, journal name, publication year, and DOI, thereby preventing redundant entries resulting from cross-database searches or differences in search strategies from entering downstream analysis. Subsequently, during the data cleaning and selection phase, two independent researchers sequentially performed double-blind screening at title-abstract and full-text levels according to pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) original research articles or review articles; (2) English language; (3) publication date between 1 January 2010 and 11 May 2026; (4) substantive research content related to both gut microbiota and hepatocellular carcinoma. Exclusion criteria: (1) conference abstracts, editorials, letters, case reports, book chapters and dissertations; (2) non-English articles; (3) articles outside the study time range; (4) articles only incidentally mentioning gut microbiota or hepatocellular carcinoma without relevant substantive analysis; (5) residual duplicates that survived EndNote deduplication. When disagreements arose between the two screeners, the disputed records were first discussed by the two researchers. If consensus could not be achieved, a third senior investigator acted as an arbiter to determine final inclusion or exclusion. They sequentially examined the titles, abstracts, and full-text content, evaluating them against established inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure no duplicate literature remained.Figure 1Flowchart of literature screening.Flowchart of literature screening process for WoSCC and PubMed databases.A flowchart detailing the literature screening process for WoSCC and PubMed databases. Data Analysis

The flowchart was created using Microsoft PowerPoint 2019. For the bibliometric analysis, international research collaboration networks were visualized using the Bibliometrix package; quantitative analyses of research institutions, author affiliations, source journals, and high-frequency keywords were conducted using VOSviewer 1.6.19 and Citespace 6.4R1, respectively, with a focus on examining association patterns such as co-authorship, lexical co-occurrence, and co-citation. Detailed parameter configurations for visualization software were set as follows. For VOSviewer 1.6.19: the analysis unit was author keywords; normalization method was association-strength; clustering resolution parameter was set to 1.0; minimum keyword occurrence threshold was 5; no pruning algorithm was adopted. For CiteSpace 6.4R1: time-slicing interval was 1 year, covering the time span 2010–2026; the top 50 most-cited items per time slice were retained; pathfinder pruning with pruning sliced networks was enabled; node type was set to keyword for keyword co-occurrence and burst-detection analysis; Kleinberg’s algorithm was used for citation-burst detection; no extra normalization procedure was applied. Prior to keyword co-occurrence analysis, manual keyword standardization and synonym merging were performed by two independent researchers to improve data consistency and analytical reproducibility. The standardization workflow included: correction of spelling mistakes and format inconsistencies; merging semantically synonymous terms (eg, merging “intestinal flora” into “gut microbiota”, “liver cancer” into “hepatocellular carcinoma”, “fecal microbiome transplantation” into “fecal microbiota transplantation”); unification of singular-plural keyword variants; and elimination of overly vague non-specific terms with low thematic value. Entries with overlapping data were consolidated into a single standardized entry; spelling errors were corrected through manual proofreading; and the raw dataset underwent pre-processing and cleaning prior to export and transfer to the next stage of in-depth analysis. For CiteSpace-generated networks, network-level metrics including modularity Q, mean silhouette value and network density were directly obtained from the software status-bar output. Node-level betweenness-centrality values were exported via the “Save Network Table” function. Modularity Q > 0.3 and mean silhouette > 0.5 were adopted as criteria for valid clustering outputs.

For citation-performance comparison among leading countries, we extracted total citations and average citation per paper for each included article grouped by the First author’s country. The Mann–Whitney-U test (non-parametric test) was applied to compare the citation-count distribution between publications from China and the USA, since citation metrics did not conform to a normal distribution. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Country-Specific Metrics

Scholarly outputs from each nation were examined, together with counts of single country publications (SCP) and the degree to which these works contributed to multiple country publications (MCP).26 An MCP ratio was then derived, serving to gauge the extent of international collaboration present within the evidence base. Involvement by nations was quantified as a proportion of aggregate publications where the first author affiliated with that nation.

Journal-Specific Metrics

Bradford’s law is often used to analyze the individual productivity of academic journals because it allows the total number of known journals to be divided into several segments. Specifically, journals are classified into three regions: Region 1 (core region) comprises the most productive journals; Region 2 (the associated region) accounts for one-quarter of the total number of journals; while the third region (the peripheral region) consists of journals that have published only a small number of papers on specific topics.27

Author-Specific Metrics

Lotka’s Law, proposed by Alfred Lotka, is a fundamental principle in bibliometrics used to explain the distribution of academic output among researchers. The law states that the number of scholars who have authored a specific number of papers is inversely proportional to that number. From a mathematical perspective, Lotka’s Law is typically described by a power-law distribution. In the above equation, A (n) is the number of authors publishing n papers and A(1) is the number of authors publishing a single paper.

The h-index is capable of gauging scholars’ productivity. This metric ranks among the most extensively adopted bibliometric tools for assessing the impact of a researcher’s output.30 Per Hirsch, a scholar possesses an h-index when h of his/her publications has garnered no fewer than h cites.31 Nevertheless, the h-index carries certain drawbacks. It tends to disadvantage those who favor excellence over volume. It likewise benefits investigators with lengthy professional trajectories, who have typically issued more publications.32–34 The g-index emerges as an enhancement to Hirsch’s h-index, designed to gauge the overall citation impact of a collection of papers.35 Put forward by Egghe, it is computed by arranging a scholar’s publications in descending sequence based on citations accumulated.35 Thus, the g-index represents the greatest unique figure whereby the leading g publications collectively obtained at minimum g2 citations. The m-index equals an author’s h-index divided by the number of years elapsed since their initial publication. This metric seeks to contrast scholars’ productivity across different time spans and gives consideration to those in the early stages of their careers.26

The resulting equation is C(r) = −377.6 + 276.6 × log(r), with a coefficient of determination R2 = 0.9808, indicating that the model provides excellent explanatory power for the data. Authors Analysis

To uncover the patterns of author productivity in the field of gut microbiota and HCC research, this study employed Lotka’s law to conduct a systematic analysis of authors’ publication frequencies in this field. The results in Figure 9 show that the estimated beta coefficient is 2.8, the constant C is 0.7122, and the coefficient of determination R2 is 0.9537. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that the p-value for the theoretical distribution (β = 2) was 0.0282 (≪ 0.05), indicating a significant difference between the observed data and the classical Lotka-Volterra law; however, when fitted using the estimated value β = 2.8, the p-value was 0.972 (> 0.05). Discussion

Given the increasing body of research on the relationship between gut microbiota and HCC, a comprehensive overview of the developmental trajectory and research directions in this field remains elusive. We conducted bibliometric analyses using publication data from the WOS and PubMed databases to elucidate the research trends and current key focuses in gut microbiota studies related to HCC. Based on these findings, we further propose future research directions, providing a theoretical foundation and reference for achieving breakthrough discoveries in this field.

General Distribution

The rising research trends in global publications indicate that this field has garnered significant attention from the academic community, demonstrating substantial and sustained research potential. As a highly prevalent malignant tumor worldwide, HCC faces limitations with traditional treatment modalities, including drug resistance and high recurrence rates. The gut microbiota, as a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, has been proven to regulate bile acid metabolism, immune responses, and inflammatory reactions through the “gut-liver axis”, offering novel insights into the pathogenesis, early diagnosis, and therapeutic targets for HCC, thereby directly driving an explosion in research efforts.36,37 The widespread adoption of high-throughput technologies such as 16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, and metabolomics has reduced the cost and accessibility barriers for gut microbiota analysis.38 The maturation of techniques like germ-free animal models and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has provided robust methodological support for studying the microbiota-HCC relationship, accelerating the generation of research findings.39

However, research in the field of HCC and gut microbiota exhibits significant regional disparities. China (679 publications) and the United States (299 publications) dominate the landscape, accounting for over 70% of relevant outputs. Among these, China’s single-country independent research (SCP) holds an exceptionally high proportion, while international collaborative research (MCP) primarily involves cross-regional partnerships such as those between China and the United States or China and Italy. This pattern stems, on one hand, from China’s status as a high-incidence region for HCC, where clinical demands and abundant case resources have driven rapid growth in domestic research;40,41 on the other hand, the United States continues to lead in both basic and translational research due to its advancements in microbiomics and high-throughput sequencing technologies.42,43 Moreover, our statistical Mann–Whitney-U test further supported that Chinese publications had significantly lower total citations and average citation per paper impact relative to USA outputs despite the largest publication volume, which highlights the urgent need for improving research quality. Nevertheless, the relatively low rate of international collaboration in China’s research reflects insufficient global synergy in research outcomes, potentially undermining their international recognition and impact. Moving forward, efforts should focus on consolidating China’s domestic research strengths while enhancing multicenter international collaborations with countries such as those in Europe, the United States, Japan, and South Korea. This will elevate the global influence of research and the quality of evidence-based medical findings, facilitating a shift in the field’s focus from “quantity leadership” to a balance between “quality and collaboration”.

Institutional collaboration within this field exhibits a pronounced “Sino-American dual pattern”: China institutions dominate the volume of publications, forming a closed collaborative cluster centered around domestic universities, while top US institutions, despite their limited publication output, maintain greater academic influence due to their strengths in basic research. This pattern stems from structural disparities between Chinese and American institutions in clinical resources and technological platforms, as well as divergent research pathways guided by different evaluation systems. While this has driven rapid growth in the total volume of publications, it has also led to uneven research quality and insufficient international discourse power. Moving forward, domestic institutions must shift from homogeneous collaborative research to original mechanistic exploration, while strengthening cross-national and interdisciplinary open collaboration to build differentiated research advantages and enhance the field’s overall academic influence and clinical translational value.

Analysis of journal distribution across the HCC and gut microbiota research fields reveals that publications from the core zone (Zone 1) account for nearly 40% of all relevant publications; in contrast, Zone 2 and Zone 3 exhibit an exponentially dispersed literature output, confirming high conformity to Bradford’s Law. The concentrated distribution of core-zone journals provides researchers with efficient literature retrieval pathways; however, the widespread dispersion of findings across low-impact-factor peripheral journals may lead to the “submersion” of high-value research outcomes, hindering effective knowledge dissemination and utilization within the field. Additionally, while most core-zone journals are open-access (OA), enhancing research accessibility, their academic controversies may compromise international recognition of these findings. Conversely, the relatively low publication volume in traditional top-tier journals indicates that the production of highly influential and original research in this field remains inadequate.

Analysis of Lotka’s Law reveals that publications in the field of hepatocellular carcinoma and gut microbiota follow a typical power-law distribution characterized by “a few high-output authors and a majority of low-output authors”. The core group of high-output authors is small in size but exerts significant academic leadership. The top 20 highly productive and cited authors have consistently focused on this field since 2016, leveraging stable research teams to achieve a series of breakthroughs in key areas, demonstrating clear first-mover advantages and cumulative academic influence. This pattern stems from the interdisciplinary selection criteria of the field, early-mover advantages driven by research hotspots, and the resource aggregation effect of core teams. While these factors have facilitated focused and systematic development in the field, they have also led to excessive concentration of academic resources and influence, resulting in uneven quality of outputs and limited innovation vitality. Future efforts should include nurturing young core authors, strengthening the catalytic role of core teams to expand the author base, and establishing diversified evaluation systems that balance high output with originality orientation to stimulate overall innovation and shift research toward collaborative innovation rather than exclusive leadership.

Based on CiteSpace keyword clustering analysis, research directions in HCC and gut microbiota can be categorized into three major phases, with current focus concentrated on mechanism elucidation and clinical translation: Early Basic Association Phase (2010–2017): The keyword clustering centered on “#2 liver cancer”, “#8 chronic inflammation”, and “#6 metabolites”. Emerging terms such as insulin resistance (14.02,2013–2019), fatty liver disease, and adipose tissue remained highly active. During this phase, research primarily focused on association analysis, examining the links between risk factors-including metabolic syndrome and chronic inflammation-and HCC. The gut microbiota was identified as a “mediating variable” in metabolic disorders, while the roles of microbial metabolites (eg, short-chain fatty acids and bile acids) began to emerge. Mechanism Analysis and Axis Theory Phase (2018–2021): The research focus shifted toward “#0 mechanisms”, “#4 gut-liver axis”, and “#3 gut microbiota”. Key terms such as “gut-liver axis” (2020–2021) and “diversity” (2018–2020) emerged as focal points. During this phase, studies delved into the “gut-liver axis” theory, elucidating how intestinal microbiota dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability promote HCC progression through inflammatory and immune metabolic pathways. Microbial diversity and functional analysis became central research priorities. Precision Intervention and Translation Phase (2022-present): The clusters “#1 immunotherapy”, “#5 fecal microbiota transplantation”, “#9 tumor microenvironment”, and “#7 intestinal flora” emerged as core themes, with terms such as “therapy” (5.06,2023–2026), “mechanisms” (2022–2023), and “metabolites” (2022–2023) showing sustained prominence. Current research focuses on microbiota-modulated adjuvant therapies: the synergistic effects of fecal microbiota transplantation and probiotic interventions in HCC immunotherapy, the regulatory role of microbiota metabolites in the tumor microenvironment, and precision diagnosis and treatment strategies based on gut microbiota.

Comparisons with Prior Relevant Bibliometric Studies

To contextualize our findings, we further compared the present bibliometric results with previously published bibliometric analyses concerning HCC, gut microbiome, and cancer immunotherapy research.

Consistent with earlier bibliometric work on HCC-related research, our study also identified a China-USA dual-dominant publication pattern: China contributes the largest publication volume while the USA exhibits superior citation-based research impact.21 Prior general HCC bibliometric studies reported that basic research outnumbers large-scale clinical investigations, which is also mirrored within this gut-microbiota-HCC cross-disciplinary dataset.44,45 In addition, previous bibliometric reviews of cancer immunotherapy indicated that immunotherapy-associated topics have undergone rapid growth over recent years, matching our observation that “therapy” has become a high-strength burst keyword from 2023 to 2026.46,47

Nevertheless, several distinct advances differentiate the present work from earlier bibliometric literature. Most former bibliometric investigations focused either on broad HCC research, general gut-microbiome research, or isolated immunotherapy fields, without specifically targeting the intersection of gut microbiota and HCC.21 Many earlier related bibliometric analyses finished data collection before 2023, lacking up-to-date evidence reflecting the most recent translational-oriented research trends up to May 2026.21 Compared with descriptive-oriented prior bibliometric reports, this study implemented deeper quantitative evaluation including betweenness-centrality of keyword nodes, Mann–Whitney-U statistical comparison of national citation performance, collaboration-strength metrics for institutions, and objective phase-division grounded on citation-burst strength. Furthermore, we quantitatively uncovered the three-phase evolutionary trajectory unique to this cross-discipline field and highlighted microbial metabolites as a fast-growing frontier theme, which has not been systematically emphasized in previous bibliometric surveys. Collectively, this work complements existing bibliometric knowledge by offering a dedicated, updated, and quantitatively-enhanced portrait of gut-microbiota-HCC research.

Frontier Topics

Bibliometric-Derived Research Hotspots

From bibliometric perspectives, “gut-liver axis” exhibited high betweenness-centrality (0.55) and citation burst during 2020–2021, marking its core theoretical-hub position. “metabolites” (betweenness-centrality=0.42) became a burst keyword from 2022–2023, representing a rapidly expanding thematic cluster. “immunotherapy” occupied a prominent cluster position, while the burst keyword “therapy” persisted from 2023 to 2026 (burst strength=5.06), indicating therapeutic translation is the most current active frontier. Collectively, bibliometric metrics reflect the global community-driven evolution from association research toward mechanism exploration and therapeutic translation. Therefore, by integrating Figures 10 and 11, we have inferred potential future research hotspots based on the evolution of keywords over the last five years: (1) In-depth investigation of how the gut microbiota regulates inflammation, immune responses, and metabolic reprogramming during HCC development through the gut-liver axis mechanism; (2) The regulatory role of microbial metabolites in the tumor microenvironment of HCC; (3) The modulatory effects of gut microbiota on immunotherapy for HCC.

Contextual Biological Background for Observed Hotspots

The bibliometric analysis identified the gut-liver-axis-related research as a major thematic hotspot; below we briefly contextualize this theme with existing experimental evidence reported in the literature. It should be emphasized that the following biological overview represents published experimental knowledge, not new findings generated by our bibliometric analysis.

Gut Microbiota Regulates HCC Progression Through the Gut-Liver Axis

The early symptoms of HCC are often difficult to detect, and most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. Research on the interaction mechanism between gut microbiota and the liver has further elucidated the critical role of the gut-liver axis in influencing the progression of HCC.48

Zhang et al49 observed the dynamic changes in microbial communities across subjects with varying health statuses and identified distinctive bacterial species associated with liver cirrhosis and HCC, respectively. Yang et al50 investigated the clinical diagnostic value of the gut-intestinal-tumor microbiota in HCC, demonstrating that multiple microbial indices progressively increase with disease progression. Relevant animal studies confirmed that excessive cholesterol intake can induce a cascade of fatty liver pathologies and ultimately lead to hepatic malignancies through intestinal microbial metabolic disturbances.51 Additional research revealed significant fluctuations in microbial abundance when cirrhotic patients progressed to HCC.52 A cohort study involving 300 patients with primary HCC demonstrated that disease severity influenced microbial community profiles: early-stage patients exhibited enrichment of Clostridiaceae, Firmicutes, and Streptococcus; intermediate-stage patients showed dominance of Ruminococcaceae, Pasteurellaceae, Tandecharoella, and Pseudomonas; while advanced-stage patients exhibited markedly elevated abundances of Bifidobacteriales, Actinobacteria, Purkinjeella, Pseudomonas, and Barnesella.53 Mechanistically, an imbalance in the gut microbiota can alter the integrity of the intestinal barrier, allowing bacteria and microbiota-associated molecules to enter the portal circulation, ultimately creating a local microenvironment conducive to cancer cell proliferation. These characteristic molecules can activate receptors in the liver, inducing a persistent chronic inflammatory response that causes continuous damage and regeneration of liver cells, driving the progression of liver fibrosis to cirrhosis and even HCC.54,55 Behari et al54 demonstrated that the gut microbiota can suppress the autoimmune defense system, as evidenced by an increase in the number of regulatory T cells producing interleukin-10, a decrease in the levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-2 and interleukin-12, and a reduction in the activity of cytotoxic CD8-positive T cells. These changes collectively increase the risk of developing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease-associated HCC. Liu et al found that specific bacterial strains, such as Xenomonas maltophilia, can activate the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, thereby exacerbating liver fibrosis and promoting the development of HCC.56 Furthermore, studies have shown that in individuals with hepatitis B-related HCC, the abundance of anti-inflammatory bacterial groups-such as Prevotella, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Bacteroides-increases, while pro-inflammatory bacterial groups, such as Escherichia coli and Enterococcus, decrease.57 Other studies have shown that the gut microbiota can interfere with amino acid metabolism in the body, upregulating the expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2, thereby promoting the progression of malignant liver lesions.58

Existing studies on the association between HCC and the gut microbiota not only span the entire disease course-from health, through hepatitis and cirrhosis, to HCC-but also identify the characteristic distribution of the microbiota at each stage and screen for potential diagnostic biomarkers. Furthermore, they elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms step by step, including the disruption of the intestinal barrier, bacterial translocation, inflammatory and immune imbalances, and the activation of signaling pathways. However, the research still has significant shortcomings. Most studies can only determine changes in microbial abundance, making it difficult to establish a causal relationship between microbial dysbiosis and the development of HCC. Furthermore, confounding factors such as diet and underlying diseases have not been adequately adjusted, limiting the generalizability of the conclusions. Mechanistic studies are often confined to individual bacterial species and pathways, lacking a holistic analysis of microbial interactions and metabolic networks. Moreover, current findings remain at the theoretical validation stage and have not yet resulted in mature, clinically applicable intervention strategies. Future research could utilize animal models to clarify the causal relationship between the two, leverage large-scale, multicenter data to establish stage-specific microbiome diagnostic systems, and conduct in-depth analyses of the full picture of microbial synergistic effects and metabolic regulation. Additionally, subtyping studies targeting HCC with different etiologies should be pursued, while simultaneously facilitating the translation of basic research findings into practical applications to develop novel therapeutic targets and prognostic assessment methods.

Within keyword cluster #0 mechanisms, TLR4 signaling represents one highly discussed sub-theme, though TLR4 itself did not appear among the top-20 citation-burst keywords (Figure 11). Moreover, consistent with the bibliometric finding that “metabolites” constitutes a fast-growing frontier (keyword burst:2022–2023, cluster #6 metabolites), accumulating experimental studies have uncovered multiple metabolite-mediated pathways linking gut microbiota and HCC pathogenesis. Bile acids and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are dominant sub-topics inside cluster #6, even though these two terms were not individually listed in the top 20 burst-keyword list.

Bile Acids

Bile acids (BAs) are synthesized by the liver and temporarily stored in the gallbladder; when the body requires them for digestion, they are released into the intestines to aid in fat breakdown and the absorption of fat-soluble nutrients. Relevant studies have confirmed that the gut microbiota is involved throughout the entire metabolic process of BAs, encompassing their production, conjugation, reabsorption, dissociation, and the conversion of primary BAs into secondary forms. If this metabolic process is disrupted, the types and levels of bile acids will fluctuate abnormally, disrupting the body’s internal balance and consequently causing adverse effects on liver function.59

Some bacterial populations have carcinogenic effects; for example, Clostridium species can convert primary bile acids into deoxycholic acid. This substance can cause genetic damage through aging-related secretory phenotype-associated tumor-promoting factors, thereby driving the progression of HCC.60 In contrast, strains such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus secrete abundant bile salt hydrolases, which convert primary bile acids—such as cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid—into secondary products including lithocholic acid, deoxycholic acid, and ursodeoxycholic acid.61,62 Experimental data show that vancomycin intervention reduces the number of bile salt hydrolase-producing bacterial strains, leading to a corresponding decrease in the proportion of secondary bile acids, which accelerates the proliferation of transplanted HCC lesions. This suggests that secondary bile acids have a significant inhibitory effect on the progression of HCC.63

BAs receptors, such as Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and Takeda G protein‐coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), play crucial roles in the interaction between BAs metabolism and the gut microbiota. FXR generally acts as a suppressor of HCC: mice with systemic or liver-specific FXR deficiency are prone to spontaneous HCC, and gut microbiota dysbiosis further accelerates tumor progression;64,65 conversely, FXR agonists improve hepatic steatosis, reduce inflammation and fibrosis, and inhibit hepatocyte proliferation, demonstrating antitumor potential in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)-associated hepatocarcinoma models.66,67 Notably, FXR function is tissue-specific and environment-dependent: intestinal FXR and hepatic FXR play distinct roles in the regulation of HCC, and their ultimate effects are jointly influenced by gut microbiota composition, bile acid concentration, the tumor microenvironment, and the host’s immune status. TGR5 is a key signaling molecule in the gut microbiota–bile acid-HCC axis. It is primarily activated by secondary bile acids produced by gut microbiota metabolism and plays a bidirectional role in immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and tumor microenvironment remodeling, thereby influencing the development and progression of HCC.68,69 Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to excessive production of secondary bile acids, which promotes the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype by activating the TGR5-STAT3 pathway. Concurrently, bacterial lipoteichoic acid drives M1 polarization via the TLR2-NF-κB pathway. These two mechanisms synergistically reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, exacerbate liver fibrosis, and accelerate the progression of HCC;69 on the other hand, TGR5 exerts an antitumor effect by inhibiting the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways to alleviate liver inflammation, maintain intestinal barrier integrity, and reduce bacterial translocation, with its function depending on bile acid concentration, microbial composition, and host immune status.68,70 Furthermore, TGR5 and FXR together constitute a bile acid signaling network that regulates bile acid homeostasis and gut microbiota structure. Targeting TGR5 (eg, with specific agonists or microbiota-modulating agents) can restore immune balance and inhibit tumor proliferation, representing an important strategy for the prevention and treatment of HCC.71,72

The gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism are closely linked; modulating the microbial composition to optimize metabolic levels has emerged as a novel therapeutic approach for HCC. Relevant studies indicate that the XYXD formulation can increase the proportion of Bacteroides and Lactobacillus, thereby promoting the synthesis of bile salt hydrolases. These active enzymes break down conjugated bile acids while reducing the population of converting bacteria such as Clostridium perfringens. Consequently, the levels of primary bile acids in the body increase, activating natural killer T cells in the liver and promoting the secretion of interferon-γ, thereby utilizing immune mechanisms to suppress tumor progression.73 Natural bioactive compounds such as chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid can also modulate the composition of microbial communities—including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Prevotella—optimizing bile acid metabolism and inhibiting the proliferation and spread of tumor cells.74,75 These findings provide novel research approaches and drug targets for the treatment of HCC through interventions focused on microbial regulation and bile acid metabolism.

It is currently known that the gut microbiota can influence the progression of HCC through bile acid metabolism, with different microbial communities exerting diametrically opposed effects. Various substances can modulate the microbiota and bile acid levels, thereby activating the immune system to exert anticancer effects. However, the classification of the microbiota remains broad, with insufficient differentiation of functional differences among bacterial subtypes. The specific molecular mechanisms underlying downstream signaling pathways of bile acid receptors and immune activation have not been thoroughly investigated. Research is largely confined to basic laboratory experiments, lacking support from large-scale clinical data. Furthermore, the differences in efficacy and appropriate clinical settings for various intervention methods have not been clearly compared. Future efforts should focus on refining the functional characterization of bacterial strains, conducting in-depth analyses of molecular pathways, conducting clinical cohort studies to validate findings, developing targeted therapeutic agents, and exploring multimodal combination therapies. Concurrently, an early assessment and warning system for HCC should be established based on relevant biomarkers.

Toll‐Like Receptor 4

Toll‐like receptors (TLRs) mediate the early emergence and subsequent malignant progression of HCC via sensing pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and endogenous damage‐associated molecular patterns. Variations in intestinal microbial composition can modify TLR biological activity and maintain intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, which further governs the malignant progression of HCC. As a canonical ligand capable of targeting TLR4, LPS is metabolized and released by intestinal commensal microorganisms. Intestinal ecological imbalance promotes the translocation of LPS and multiple microbial-derived metabolites into hepatic tissues, ultimately triggering TLR4 signaling cascade activation.76

TLR4 exhibits extensive expression profiles in hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells, and Kupffer cells within liver tissues. Once bound to circulating LPS, this receptor initiates downstream NF‐κB and JNK/MAPK signaling cascades, prompting the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators including interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α). These inflammatory molecules facilitate hepatic cell proliferation, sustain cellular survival status, and drive epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), which collectively enhances the invasive capacity and metastatic potential of HCC tumor tissues.77,78 Apart from inflammatory regulation, LPS-TLR4 signaling axis modulates the differentiation behavior of hepatic progenitor cells into myofibroblasts, thereby inducing disordered oncogenic signaling and accelerating HCC pathological deterioration.79

TLR4-dependent inflammatory responses serve as a core driving force for HCC progression, and intestinal microbiota can modulate TLR4 activation status by regulating the abundance of LPS and other corresponding ligands. In vivo experiments using PtenΔhep mouse models verified that HCC deterioration is predominantly driven by TLR4 rather than TLR2 signaling, which aggravates intrahepatic inflammatory injury and elevates the secretion levels of IL‐6 and TNF‐α. Antibiotic intervention effectively reduces LPS accumulation in portal venous blood and suppresses tumor overgrowth, confirming intestinal microbes as the primary source of functional TLR4 ligands in vivo.80 Specific deletion of TLR4 blunts NF‐κB signaling activation, renders liver parenchyma more susceptible to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated oxidative damage, and attenuates inflammation-triggered compensatory cell proliferation, thereby increasing hepatic vulnerability to carcinogenic stimulation. Adoptive transfer of TLR4-positive myeloid cells into TLR4-deficient mice restores diethylnitrosamine (DEN)-elicited hepatic inflammation and abnormal cell proliferation, demonstrating a paracrine-dependent pro-tumor mechanism mediated by LPS.81

Most previous studies focused primarily on myeloid cell-derived inflammatory pathways linked to HCC. Nevertheless, a research report published by Dapito et al82 identified non-myeloid hepatic stellate cells and hepatocytes as the major cellular targets responding to LPS stimulation. Intestinal flora disruption activates TLR4 signaling to facilitate DEN-induced HCC tumorigenesis in mice, and accelerates compensatory proliferative repair after hepatic cellular damage through modulating hepatocyte and hepatic stellate cell functions. Notably, neither TLR4 activation nor intestinal microbial disorders can independently initiate HCC tumor formation. Instead, they facilitate tumor progression by promoting excessive hepatocyte proliferation and inhibiting cellular apoptosis. Accumulated experimental data indicate that genetic ablation of TLR4 or intestinal sterilization can reduce HCC incidence by roughly 80%. In contrast, persistent stimulation with low-dose LPS markedly accelerates HCC disease progression. Antibiotic-mediated intestinal decontamination can efficiently restrain tumor proliferation even in advanced HCC stages, providing a novel and feasible preventive strategy for high-risk patients with chronic hepatic injury.

The TLR4 signaling axis mediates the molecular mechanisms by which the gut microbiota contributes to the development and progression of HCC, providing a comprehensive overview of the chain of events in which dysbiosis induces the translocation of lipopolysaccharides, activates inflammatory pathways, and promotes tumor proliferation and invasion. However, current studies have only described the overall effects of the signaling pathway at a macro level; they have not elaborated on the regulatory patterns of molecular interactions upstream and downstream of TLR4, nor have they defined the differential effects of different bacterial strains on receptor activation. All research data are derived from animal experiments, lacking corroboration from human clinical samples, and the potential drawbacks of antibiotic intervention have not been evaluated. Future research could focus on elucidating the fine-tuned regulatory mechanisms of TLR4-associated inflammatory pathways, identifying specific microbial communities that regulate lipopolysaccharide release, conducting comparative validation experiments using clinical samples, developing targeted drugs to block TLR4 activation, and investigating the synergistic effects of interventions targeting this pathway with existing liver disease treatment regimens.

Toxins

Microbe-derived toxic substances inside the intestinal tract harm genetic materials of human cells in diverse ways, which may eventually trigger malignant cell transformation. Such poisonous substances can destroy DNA structure indirectly by generating reactive oxygen species, or exert direct destructive impacts on cellular genetic sequences. Tumor formation will take place once cellular repair systems fail to remedy severe genetic injuries. Aflatoxin belongs to highly hazardous cancer-causing substances; it reshapes intestinal microbial structure and maintains tight links with HCC onset in human bodies.83

Apart from poisonous metabolites secreted by microbes, harmful metallic elements like arsenic also raise hepatic cancer risks by disturbing normal intestinal flora balance. After penetrating into living cells, this harmful element gets neutralized and discharged with the help of glutathione, an essential antioxidant substance. Cells containing abundant glutathione maintain less arsenic accumulation, proving this biomolecule can weaken toxic damage caused by arsenic. Long-term contact with arsenic will exhaust internal detoxification functions, break steady intestinal microbial state and hurt hepatic tissues, thereby raising the possibility of HCC occurrence.84 Relevant tests prove arsenic exposure raises the proportion of Gram-negative strains, weakens intestinal barrier tightness and accelerates LPS leakage into internal tissues. This substance travels to liver organs via blood circulation, ignites persistent inflammatory reactions and speeds up liver tumor formation.

Research based on arsenic-related liver tumor models displays distinct differences between germ-free and conventionally raised rodents. Sterile mice discharge more arsenic and its metabolic derivatives through urine, show higher monomethylarsonic acid and dimethylarsinic acid proportion, and retain less arsenic content inside excrement samples. Further animal tests confirm intestinal microbes take part in arsenic absorption processes, and enzyme activity linked to arsenic methylation drops sharply within germ-free individuals.85 All above evidence proves intestinal flora exerts non-negligible influence on liver tumor formation, and exposes complicated interactive relations between internal microbes and external harmful contaminants during hepatic cancer progression.86

The aforementioned study focused on two types of toxic substances—intestinal microbial toxins and arsenic trace elements—and elucidated the mechanisms by which they induce hepatocellular carcinogenesis by damaging the host’s genetic material and disrupting the homeostasis of the intestinal microbiota. This confirms the role of the microbiota in the absorption and metabolism of toxins, as well as its involvement in mediating inflammatory and carcinogenic processes. However, this study only elucidated the macro-level pathogenic pathways of toxic substances; it did not distinguish the differences in the damaging effects of various bacterial toxins, did not provide an in-depth analysis of the specific functional mechanisms of enzymes regulating arsenic metabolism, and lacked an investigation into the patterns of pathological changes under combined exposure to different toxins. Future research should prioritize distinguishing the pathogenic characteristics of various bacterial toxins, delve into the specific molecular mechanisms by which the microbiota regulates arsenic-methylating enzymes, and investigate the carcinogenic features resulting from the combined action of multiple toxic substances. Experimental findings should be validated using human exposure case studies, and targeted intervention strategies should be developed based on the metabolic characteristics of toxins to prevent and control liver carcinogenesis induced by toxic factors.

Short‐Chain Fatty Acids

Intestinal microbes break down hard-to-digest polysaccharides including cellulose and poorly assimilated sugars to generate SCFAs, and these compounds can be taken up by intestinal tissues. They supply direct energy nourishment for gut mucosal cells, cut down cell death events and keep physical barrier structure intact. These compounds also decrease gut pH value, restrain harmful microbial settlement and reproduction, and ease inflammatory symptoms inside digestive tract. Once SCFA synthesis gets disturbed, gut barrier permeability will rise abnormally, bringing excessive intake of microbial metabolites and intestinal endotoxin accumulation. Such changes further stimulate LPS-TLR4 signaling route and inflict injury on liver parenchymal cells.87

Being typical metabolic yields from intestinal flora, SCFAs possess tight connections with HCC pathological evolution. HBV can synthesize HBx oncoprotein, a key factor driving HCC onset. Research outcomes reveal that SCFA supplementation boosts suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 expressions inside HBx transgenic mice. It also weakens multiple inflammatory signal cascades covering Ras, PI3K, VEGF, fibroblast growth factor and epidermal growth factor, so as to slow down HCC deterioration speed.88 Clinical research conducted by Behary et al discovered obvious structural and functional shifts in gut flora among NAFLD-HCC patients. The population of Bacteroides xylanisolvens, Ruminococcus gnavus and Clostridium bolteae climbs evidently, alongside remarkable growth of SCFA-synthesizing strains. Both stool and blood samples collected from sick individuals show remarkably higher content of SCFAs and its derived compounds. These substances adjust human immune status by expanding IL-10+Treg effector cell quantity, curbing multiplication of cytotoxic CD8+T cells and weakening antigen recognition reaction, finally resulting in declined immune defense capacity.

The above research provides multifactorial evidence supporting the association between short-chain fatty acids and the development of HCC. However, the studies have not elucidated the impact of metabolite concentrations on the progression of HCC, nor have they detailed the molecular mechanisms by which changes in gut microbiota composition influence immune dysregulation. Furthermore, there is currently no validated evidence regarding the efficacy of targeted intervention strategies. Future research could identify concentration thresholds that distinguish protective from pathogenic effects, delve deeper into the specific regulatory mechanisms by which these factors mediate immune dysfunction, and explore personalized dietary or probiotic interventions based on gut microbiota and metabolite profiles.

In line with our bibliometric observation that immunotherapy-associated themes represent the latest sustained frontier (cluster #1 immunotherapy, cluster #5 fecal microbiota transplantation; burst keyword “therapy”, 2023–2026), abundant pre-clinical and clinical works have explored the modulatory role of gut microbiota on HCC immunotherapeutic efficacy. Probiotics fall inside cluster #1 immunotherapy, while fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) corresponds to cluster #5; neither “probiotics” nor “FMT” independently ranked within top-20 burst keywords.

Gut Microbiota Regulates the Efficacy of Immunotherapy for HCC

Drugs blocking programmed cell death signaling including PD-1/L1 and CTLA-4 serve as mainstream therapeutic choices against HCC nowadays. Still, such immune checkpoint inhibitors cannot generate favorable outcomes for every affected person. Roughly sixty percent HCC cases show no therapeutic response, while merely one tenth to one fifth of sufferers qualify for initial immune treatment schemes. Microbial makeup inside human intestines varies greatly between patients sensitive and insensitive to immunotherapy drugs.89 Relevant research indicates individuals gaining treatment benefits carry more Lachnoclostridium and contain elevated ursodeoxycholic acid level, yet their Prevotella_9 quantity stays relatively low. Most tumor patients receive chemo or radiotherapy before adopting immune therapy, and these routine means tend to destroy stable intestinal microecology.90 Accordingly, optimizing intestinal flora diversity and community features ahead of immunological treatment has emerged as a promising novel therapeutic idea. Common practical ways to realize flora adjustment cover antibiotic application, probiotic supplementation and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT).

Antibiotics

Medicinal antibiotics belong to a specific drug category capable of suppressing microbial proliferation or eliminating harmful microbes, which are routinely adopted to cure diverse infectious illnesses.91–93 Scholars have paid mounting attention to the linkage between antibiotic agents and malignant tumors over recent decades, and such pharmaceuticals are deemed to possess auxiliary effects on oncological therapy.94 No clinical evidence currently proves that antibiotic application can regulate intestinal flora structure and further optimize immunotherapeutic outcomes for HCC. Still, observational retrospective analyses suggest these medicines exert influences on HCC immunotherapy efficacy. Clinical research enrolled all late-stage HCC patients administered immune checkpoint inhibitors, namely nivolumab, pembrolizumab and ipilimumab, from January 2014 to December 2019. Research outcomes demonstrated that taking antibiotics within 30 days before and after initiating ICI treatment raised the risk of tumor-caused death and all-cause mortality.95

Likewise, medical research discovered that taking antibiotics can interfere with the therapeutic effect of atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab, commonly known as T + A therapy. This regimen serves as the primary mainstream remedy for inoperable HCC cases. Statistical data showed sufferers undergoing T + A therapy without antibiotic intervention achieved distinctly longer progression-free survival and overall survival periods compared to those using both treatment options simultaneously.96 Nevertheless, certain research works hold the view that HCC patients could gain favorable effects from joint application of ICIs and antibacterial medicines. Relevant clinical data displayed superior objective response levels in patients given early antibacterial medicine use within 30 days ahead of or following ICI medication. Up to now, the exact biological principle behind this phenomenon still remains unclear.97

Apart from interfering with immunotherapeutic effects, antibacterial agents can change intestinal microbial composition, which further alters tumor volume in HCC animal models. These drugs are capable of reshaping intestinal flora composition and adjusting immune cellular activities, exerting profound impacts on the emergence and development of HCC lesions. A research team led by Yu verified in 2010 that administering mixed antibacterial drugs including amoxicillin, vancomycin, neomycin sulfate and metronidazole to HCC model mice could eliminate indigenous intestinal bacteria and slow down HCC deterioration. Tumor nodule volume and counts dropped obviously when contrasted with specimens from untreated control subjects.81

Other scientific investigations revealed blended antibacterial preparations such as vancomycin, cefoperazone, ampicillin, neomycin and metronidazole can break intestinal flora balance among HCC sufferers, bringing about obvious elevation of IL-25 levels. Imbalanced intestinal microbes trigger intestinal proliferative epithelial cluster cells to release abundant IL-25 substances. The research above indicates that antibiotics can modulate the gut microbiota and influence the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with HCC; however, the studies cited in this paper vary in terms of antibiotic type, dosage, duration of use, and timing of administration, and no unified intervention guidelines have yet been established. Given the significant differences in treatment regimens across trials, it is difficult to compare conclusions across studies, making it impossible to directly guide the appropriate timing of clinical drug administration. Although multiple independent signaling pathways and changes in immune cells have been described separately, the upstream-downstream relationships between microbiota dysbiosis, metabolite secretion, immune remodeling, and tumor proliferation remain fragmented, with molecular mechanisms operating in isolation, preventing the establishment of an integrated regulatory system. Future research should stratify the impact of different treatment windows, antibiotic classes, and treatment durations on efficacy; distinguish between medication required for infection and prophylactic use; clarify the boundaries of safe drug use; and avoid the risk of reduced efficacy in immunotherapy. By integrating the signaling axes of bile acid metabolism, various cytokines, innate immune cells, and the tumor microenvironment, we can elucidate the dual regulatory mechanism by which antibiotic-induced gut microbiota disruption bidirectionally influences HCC progression, thereby explaining the core reasons for conflicting research findings. By stratifying patient subgroups based on hepatitis etiology, liver function status, and baseline gut microbiota characteristics, we can analyze response differences to antibiotic intervention across different populations and provide a basis for personalized treatment.

Probiotics

At present, multiple studies have explored how probiotic interventions act on HCC tumor cells and immune cell populations within the HCC TME. A randomized controlled trial targeting HCC patients undergoing surgical treatment found that probiotic supplementation effectively improves intestinal barrier integrity. This beneficial effect is achieved by enriching beneficial intestinal flora, improving microbial diversity and suppressing the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria.100 Multiple probiotic strains, including L. rhamnosus GG, B. longum and Lactobacillus casei, have been proven to modulate the occurrence and malignant progression of HCC. These probiotic strains exert anti-HCC effects through multiple biological pathways, such as maintaining intestinal microecological homeostasis, inhibiting the proliferation of malignant HCC cells, relieving persistent inflammatory injury and remodeling host immune status.101 Moreover, probiotic intervention can modulate host gene epigenetic modification, which serves as a novel mechanism to restrain HCC deterioration.36

Additional animal experimental findings indicated that intaking probiotic-fermented milk could downregulate the expression of rasp-21, c-myc, cyclin D1 and Bcl-2 in HCC rat models, effectively blocking malignant tumor progression.102 Consistent with the above findings, Li et al developed a compound probiotic product named Pro-hep, which is composed of L. rhamnosus GG, live Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 and heat-inactivated VSL#3 at a 1:1:1 ratio. This compound agent can effectively inhibit HCC tumor growth in murine models, reducing mouse tumor weight and volume by up to 40%. Further mechanistic exploration verified that Pro-hep could inhibit Th17 cell polarization in HCC tumor tissues and reduce the secretion of the pro-angiogenic and pro-inflammatory factor IL-17, thus halting HCC malignant progression (Figure 13). In addition, this probiotic compound can significantly enrich the abundance of intestinal bacteria capable of producing SCFAs.103

Although the above research supports the notion that probiotics can modulate the gut microbiome, reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, and inhibit the progression of HCC, it merely lists various effective probiotic strains and combination formulations. It does not compare the differences in efficacy among different strains, dosages, or intervention durations, nor does it distinguish between the effects of single-strain and multi-strain formulations, making it difficult to identify the optimal intervention strategy. Furthermore, the studies do not address potential adverse effects such as intestinal dysbiosis and immune dysfunction that may result from excessive supplementation or strain imbalance; they also overlook efficacy variations attributable to individual factors such as patients’ liver function staging, underlying liver disease type, age, and physical constitution. Future research should focus on conducting large-scale, prospective clinical trials to investigate the effects of different probiotic-combined-with-ICIs regimens on the efficacy of immunotherapy, recurrence rates, and long-term survival in HCC patients, thereby filling the current gaps in clinical research. By comparing the effects of interventions involving different strains, combination formulations, durations of administration, and dosages—while accounting for the physiological characteristics of patients with liver disease—and by simultaneously monitoring changes in gut homeostasis, liver function, and adverse reactions following probiotic intervention, we can mitigate the risk of dysbiosis and establish a safe, standardized protocol for probiotic supplementation.

FMT

FMT describes a clinical approach that delivers fecal material from healthy individuals into a patient’s digestive system. Currently, FMT is being explored for the treatment of HCC patients. Research teams have discovered obvious differences in β diversity within intestinal microbes among eight late-stage HCC patients receiving nabuliumab therapy. Certain microbial strains dominated the gut of patients who failed to respond to treatment, including pneumococcus, Escherichia coli, L. regenerans, Streptococcus mutans, Enterococcus faecium, Streptococcus gordonii, Veillonella atypica, Granulicatella sp. and Trichuris trichiura. On the contrary, Citrobacter freundii, Azospirillum sp. and Enterococcus durans took the leading position in those who gained favorable treatment outcomes.104 Ponziani et al launched a prospective trial recruiting 11 HCC cases treated with tremelimumab, durvalumab or both agents. The trial data showed responders harbored more Akkermansia, while the level of Enterobacteriaceae dropped in this group.105 Overall, no solid clinical evidence has yet verified FMT can combine with ICIs to treat HCC. Even so, the obvious differences in intestinal flora between immunotherapy effective and ineffective cases suggest FMT could help lift the therapeutic effect of HCC immunotherapy.

To date, only a single report has explored how FMT acts on immune cells located inside the TME of HCC laboratory mice. Lactobacillus reuteri is a widely recognized beneficial bacterium. It helps ease discomforts linked to constipation, MASLD, metabolic syndrome, acute diarrhea, allergic rhinitis and asthma, and can also strengthen ICIs treatment against melanoma.106–108 Latest experimental data prove that lowered levels of intestinal L. reuteri and its metabolite acetic acid are closely linked to HCC onset and progression. In relevant animal trials, researchers transferred two types of fecal samples into HCC model mice with insufficient L. reuteri and acetic acid: one was antibiotic-processed feces added with L. reuteri, and the other was fresh feces from normal mice. The intervention led to fewer tumors and smaller tumor volume in these animals. Acetic acid generated by L. reuteri can suppress interleukin-17A-producing ILC3 cells in HCC lesions. It exerts this effect by curbing histone deacetylase activity and promoting acetylation of SRY-box transcription factor 13.109 Extra studies reveal interleukin-17A may trigger MASH and HCC, while targeted drugs against this molecule can protect high-risk populations from developing the two diseases110 (Figure 14). In practical application, FMT also has obvious limitations. The aforementioned study reviewed the current state of FMT research in the immunotherapy of HCC. Based on small-scale clinical trials, it identified differences in gut microbiota between patients who responded to and those who did not respond to immunotherapy. Through animal experiments, it elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which Lactobacillus reuteri and its metabolite acetic acid regulate tumor immunity and inhibit the progression of HCC. The study also pointed out practical limitations of FMT, such as the risk of pathogen transmission and low patient acceptance; However, its core limitations lie in the small sample sizes of clinical studies, which have only demonstrated an association between the microbiota and treatment efficacy without establishing a causal relationship. Furthermore, the supporting basic experimental evidence is limited in scope, the range of studied bacterial strains is narrow, and there is insufficient discussion regarding safety controls and protocol optimization for FMT. Consequently, a significant translation gap exists between basic research findings and clinical application; Future efforts should include large-scale, multicenter, stratified cohort studies to validate the value of microbiome biomarkers; use experimental models to clarify the causal relationship between the microbiome and treatment outcomes; expand functional studies on different differential bacterial strains; refine quality control standards for the entire FMT process and improve patient compliance; advance the clinical translation of animal study results; explore comprehensive treatment regimens combining microbiome interventions with various drugs; and delve deeper into relevant molecular pathways while testing novel combination therapy models.

After reviewing this body of published biological literature, we can further interpret our bibliometric observations: although TLR4, bile acids, SCFAs, probiotics and FMT attract substantial research attention as reflected by their high co-occurrence frequency inside corresponding keyword clusters, our dataset still demonstrates that these specific mechanistic terms did not generate independent top-20 citation bursts, implying they are important sub-components of larger thematic clusters rather than standalone paradigm-shifting research triggers.

Strengths of the Present Bibliometric Study

This work possesses several prominent strengths that distinguish it from previous narrative reviews and small-scale bibliometric reports focusing on gut microbiota and HCC: (1) Dual-database, long-timespan comprehensive data retrieval: We integrated two authoritative biomedical databases (WOSCC and PubMed) covering the full spectrum of basic, translational and clinical research, collecting original articles and reviews published over a 16-year period (2010–May 11, 2026) with a final sample of 1383 qualified publications. Compared with prior bibliometric studies relying only on Web of Science, the combination of PubMed greatly enriches clinical microbiology and hepatology literature, making the dataset more representative of global medical research trends on gut microbiota-HCC. (2) Multi-dimensional integrated bibliometric toolkit: We adopted a complementary combination of Bibliometrix, VOSviewer and CiteSpace, rather than single visualization software. The triple tools jointly realized macroscopic national/institutional collaboration mapping, journal productivity evaluation based on Bradford’s Law, author output distribution via Lotka’s Law, and microscopic keyword clustering and burst detection, achieving a full-layer quantitative description from research output to thematic evolution. (3) Clear three-stage division of research evolution supported by objective data: Different from subjective stage division in previous reviews, we relied on annual publication growth rate (26.95%) and time-series burst keywords to objectively partition three developmental phases of this field, accurately revealing the paradigm shift from correlation exploration to mechanistic research and clinical immunotherapy translation. (4) Systematic docking of bibliometric results with frontier basic/clinical mechanisms: Unlike most pure bibliometric papers that only present statistical results without in-depth mechanism interpretation, we further combed core frontier directions including gut-liver axis, microbial metabolites and microbiota-modulated immunotherapy, summarized internal research bottlenecks, and proposed targeted, operable future research suggestions based on quantitative data. (5) Dual-pattern revelation of global research landscape: This study quantitatively confirmed the China–USA dual development pattern, clarified the advantages and defects of Chinese and American research systems respectively, and provided quantitative evidence for guiding international collaborative optimization in this field.

Limitations

This bibliometric study has several important limitations, including both sample-selection constraints and inherent methodological biases common to bibliometric research. First, database-dependence bias. We retrieved literature exclusively from Web of Science Core Collection and PubMed. Different bibliographic databases adopt distinct source-journal inclusion criteria, indexing rules and metadata standards. Therefore, the publication counts, citation statistics and co-occurrence outputs may shift if alternative databases (eg, Scopus, CNKI) are incorporated. Although WOSCC and PubMed cover the mainstream biomedical literature in this domain, our results cannot fully represent records indexed by other platforms. Second, citation-lag bias. Citations accumulate gradually over time. Articles published close to our cut-off date (May 11, 2026) have insufficient time to accrue citations. Consequently, citation-based indicators including average citation, betweenness-centrality and citation-burst strength may underestimate the actual academic impact of recently-published high-quality works. Third, language bias. Only English-language original and review articles were included in this analysis. High-quality relevant research published in Chinese, Japanese or other non-English languages was excluded, which may under-represent research outputs from non-English-speaking regions. This may partly explain the observed high publication output from China together with relatively lower citation impact, as some domestic non-English valuable works are invisible to our dataset. Fourth, self-citation effects. Citation-derived metrics used in this work are susceptible to self-citation bias. Repeated self-citation by research groups can artificially inflate total-citation and related normalized indicators. We did not perform self-citation removal in the present analysis, which may potentially distort citation-based comparisons across countries, institutions and authors. Fifth, software-algorithm limitations. All network construction, keyword clustering, betweenness-centrality calculation and citation-burst detection were implemented using CiteSpace and VOSviewer. These tools rely on built-in algorithms with predefined parameter settings. Clustering partition, centrality values and burst-keyword outputs are sensitive to time-slice size, node filtering thresholds and pruning strategies. Different parameter configurations may produce slightly divergent network and clustering results. Hence, bibliometric outputs should be interpreted as exploratory quantitative portraits rather than absolute objective truth. Additionally, consistent with our original statement, conference abstracts, dissertations and grey literature were excluded, which might omit emerging preliminary findings. Only original articles and reviews were analysed. Taken together, all above-mentioned potential biases should be kept in mind when interpreting our findings.

Conclusions

This study conducts a quantitative analysis of 1383 publications in the field of gut microbiota and hepatocellular carcinoma spanning the period from 2010 to 2026. The findings of this study fill critical knowledge gaps present in previous reviews: by analyzing only the number of published papers, it clearly delineates the research landscape with high output in both China and the United States; objectively identifies three macro phases of research development, five major precise research and development phases, and precisely pinpoints four major core research frontiers. These results suggest that researchers should employ multi-omics technologies and germ-free models to validate causal mechanisms; initiate multi-center clinical trials of macrobiotic-enhanced immunotherapy involving collaboration between China and the United States; and develop macrobiotic-based diagnostic biomarkers. It should be emphasized that clinical validation is still limited and these biomarkers are far from routine clinical application. Furthermore, from the perspective of funding priorities, the identified publication gaps indicate that funding agencies may consider prioritizing resource allocation toward causal mechanism validation, large-scale prospective clinical trials, and the development of microbiota-derived biomarkers. Regarding international cooperation, the data presented here reveal relatively low cross-border collaboration density, highlighting the necessity of strengthening sustained transnational partnerships—particularly joint multicenter projects between China and the United States—to bridge the gap between the quantity of published results and the actual research impact measured by citation counts. In the realm of translational microbiome research, the identified hotspots (including metabolites and therapeutic interventions) provide an evidence-based reference framework for translating preclinical experimental discoveries into practical clinical applications for HCC.
