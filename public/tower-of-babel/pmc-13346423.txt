Identifying limiting nutrients for wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production in Halaba, Central Ethiopia
Authors: Abay Ayalew, Moges Tadese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow wheat productivity in Ethiopia is largely attributed to nutrient imbalances and the widespread use of blanket fertilizer recommendations that do not account for site-specific soil conditions. To identify the most yield-limiting nutrients for wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production, a field experiment using nutrient omission trials was conducted during the 2021 cropping season at eight farmers’ fields in Wera Dijo District, Halaba Zone, central Ethiopia. The soils across the study sites had neutral pH (6.1–7.0), medium to high organic carbon content (1.85–4.19%), and low to medium available phosphorus (3.10–10.94 mg kg⁻1). The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete block design with ten treatments, including a complete nutrient treatment, NP treatment, negative control, and single-nutrient omission treatments. Grain yield, biomass yield, and yield components were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, with nutrient treatments as fixed effects and farms and blocks within farms as random effects. Nutrient treatments significantly affected grain yield, biomass, and yield components (p < 0.0001). Omission of nitrogen or phosphorus caused significant reductions in all measured parameters, with nitrogen omission resulting in the greatest yield loss, comparable to the negative control, indicating that nitrogen was the most limiting nutrient. In contrast, omission of potassium, sulfur, zinc, or boron had no significant effect on wheat yield, suggesting that these nutrients are not currently limiting in the study area. Grain yields under the NP treatment were statistically comparable to those under the complete treatment. These results highlight nitrogen and phosphorus as the primary yield-limiting nutrients for wheat production in the study area and demonstrate the importance of site-specific nutrient management. Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a major staple crop, feeding nearly one-third of the global population and providing a substantial share of dietary protein and calories. Globally, wheat is cultivated on approximately 218 million hectares, with an average yield of 4.02 t ha⁻1 ref1,2. In Ethiopia, wheat ranks fourth in area cultivated—after tef (Eragrostis tef), maize (Zea mays), and sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)—and second in terms of consumption3–5. Despite being the second-largest wheat producer in sub-Saharan Africa, Ethiopia’s wheat production is dominated by smallholder, rain-fed systems, resulting in modest yield gains that have not kept pace with increasing demand. Consequently, annual production growth (7.8%) remains below utilization growth (9%), leading to a national wheat deficit of approximately 30%, which is met through imports and food aid6. The national average yield of 2.2 t ha⁻1 is substantially below the global average, highlighting a persistent yield gap.

This yield gap is largely attributed to soil fertility depletion caused by long-term nutrient mining and the continued use of blanket fertilizer recommendations that ignore spatial soil variability7 Several studies have reported that declining soil fertility is a major constraint to crop productivity in Ethiopia8–10. Although mineral fertilizers remain a key option for improving crop yields, their effectiveness is limited when nutrient types and application rates are not matched to local soil conditions.

In many wheat-producing areas, including the study area, farmers rely on national blanket fertilizer recommendations due to the absence of site-specific guidance, often resulting in suboptimal fertilizer use and inefficient nutrient uptake11–13. Site-Specific Nutrient Management (SSNM) has been proposed as an effective alternative, as it aligns nutrient supply with crop demand in both space and time, thereby improving productivity and fertilizer use efficiency14. Within SSNM, nutrient omission trials provide a practical approach for identifying yield-limiting nutrients by assessing the soil’s inherent nutrient-supplying capacity15.

Numerous studies across Ethiopia have demonstrated that nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) are the primary yield-limiting nutrients for cereal production, including wheat (Ayalew et al., 201116; Aleminew & Legas, 201517; Argaw & Tsigie, 201518,19;. In contrast, deficiencies of potassium (K), sulfur (S), zinc (Zn), and boron (B) have been reported as less widespread in most Ethiopian agricultural soils20. Recent nutrient omission studies have consistently shown that the application of N and P alone produces yields comparable to those obtained with multi-nutrient fertilizer blends, indicating that additional nutrients often do not provide significant yield benefits10,20,21. Similar findings have been reported for wheat-growing areas in Amhara, West Shewa, and South Wollo, where N and P were identified as the dominant constraints to wheat productivity22,13,21.

Despite this growing body of evidence, site-specific nutrient limitations in Halaba and the surrounding areas of central Ethiopia remain poorly documented. Wheat is an important crop in this region, yet fertilizer recommendations still rely on national blanket guidelines. Consequently, the specific limiting nutrients for wheat production have not been clearly identified, even though nitrogen and phosphorus are known to be limiting in other parts of Ethiopia. Identifying locally limiting nutrients is therefore critical for improving wheat yields, enhancing fertilizer use efficiency, and promoting sustainable soil management.

Beyond productivity gains, site-specific nutrient management has broader implications for food security and environmental sustainability, including reduced fertilizer costs for smallholder farmers, lower nutrient losses, and improved long-term soil fertility. While blanket fertilizer recommendations may appear more practical in resource-limited settings, increasing evidence suggests that locally tailored nutrient management strategies offer a more effective and sustainable pathway for closing yield gaps.

This is the first comprehensive nutrient omission study in the Halaba region. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate wheat growth and yield responses to individual nutrient omissions and to identify the most yield-limiting nutrients for wheat production in Halaba using field-based nutrient omission trials. It was hypothesized that one or more key nutrient constraints limiting wheat productivity in the study area could be identified. The findings are expected to support the development of site-specific nutrient management strategies that enhance wheat productivity, improve fertilizer use efficiency, and contribute to sustainable agricultural intensification in the region.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The experiment was conducted during the 2021 rainfed season at eight farms within the Wera Dijo District, Halaba Zone, Central Ethiopia (Fig. 1). These farms were selected based on their representativeness of wheat-growing conditions in the district and the willingness of local farmers to allocate land and assist in managing the trials. Site selection was carried out in collaboration with agricultural officials and experts from the local extension system. The farms, located approximately 5 km apart, share similar agricultural practices, soil management, and climatic conditions, as confirmed by field observations and farmer reports. The geographical coordinates of the experimental sites range from 07°12.081′ to 07°30.15′ N latitude and 38°11.566′ to 38°13.976′ E longitude, with altitudes varying between 1,926 and 2,040 meters above sea level. According to data obtained from the Halaba Meteorological Station (informal personal communication), the area receives an average annual rainfall ranging from 601 to 1,200 mm and experiences mean monthly minimum and maximum temperatures of approximately 18 °C and 24 °C, respectively. Rainfall during the study year was typical of the area. The dominant soil type of the study site is Luvisols. The terrain is gently sloping, with gradients between 2% and 2.5%. Major crops cultivated in the area include maize, wheat, teff, hot pepper, finger millet, and haricot bean.

Soil sampling and analysis

Prior to planting, composite soil samples were collected from each site with the informed consent and permission of the respective farmers to assess baseline soil fertility. At each of the eight experimental sites, twenty surface soil samples (0–20 cm depth) were collected randomly in a zigzag pattern within the site and then combined to form one composite sample representing that specific site. The samples were air-dried and sieved through a 2-mm mesh for physical and chemical analyses. Particle size distribution was determined using the hydrometer method23, soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water ratio using a digital pH meter, available phosphorus (P) was determined using the Olsen method24, for organic carbon (OC) analysis, samples were further sieved to 0.5 mm and analyzed using the Walkley and Black25 wet oxidation method, total nitrogen (N) was measured using the Kjeldahl digestion method26. In addition to total N, analysis of plant-available N (nitrate and ammonium) would have been important,however, this could not be conducted due to limited laboratory facilities. Similarly, potassium (K), sulfur (S), zinc (Zn), and boron (B) were not analyzed.

Experimental design and treatments

The field experiments were laid out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications per site focusing on the effect of omitting one nutrient at a time (single-nutrient omission approach), alongside key reference treatments (complete, NP, and negative control). The test crop was wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), using the high-yielding variety Ogolcho, which is recommended for the area. Treatments were based on nutrient omission principles to identify limiting nutrients. Ten treatments were established (Table 1): (1) NPKSZnB – All nutrients applied (complete treatment), (2) NPKSZn – Without boron (B), (3) NPKSB – Without zinc (Zn), (4) NPKZnB – Without sulfur (S), (5) NPSZnB – Without potassium (K), (6) NKSZnB – Without phosphorus (P), (7) PKSZnB – Without nitrogen (N), (8) Recommended NP (NP), (9) No nutrients – Negative control, and (10) NP+S2 NP plus elevated sulfur (30 kg S ha⁻1). The application rates of N, P, K, S, Zn and B were 92, 20, 50, 10.5, 5, and 1.0 kgha-1, respectively. Whereas, the amount of S2 was 30 kgha-1 to exhaustively study the effects of S on wheat response as there is a regional suspicion of S deficiency. Single source of the respective nutrients was used. The sources of N, P, K, S, Zn and B were urea [CO (NH2)2, 46% N], Triple Superphosphate (TSP), Potassium chloride (KCl), Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄), Zn-EDTA (granular) and Borax (granular), respectively. Although MgSO₄ also supplies Mg, it was used as the sulfur source because it was the only available option, and Mg deficiency was not expected under the study’s neutral soil conditions; thus, its contribution was assumed not to influence treatment responses. Each plot measured 4 × 3 meters, with 1-meter spacing between plots and blocks. Wheat seeds were sown in rows spaced 20 cm apart at a seeding rate of 100 kg ha⁻1. Fertilizers were applied in rows according to the designated treatments.

Urea was applied in two splits—half at planting and half at 35 days after planting. All other fertilizers were applied at planting in granular form.

Agronomic management

The land was ploughed using ox-drawn implements, and the experiment was conducted under rainfed conditions. All plots were managed uniformly, including weeding and pest control practices. To manage yellow rust, the systemic fungicide Tilt® 250 EC was applied as needed.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected on wheat grain yield, biomass yield and yield components, including: number of tillers, plant height (measured at physiological maturity), and spike length. Measurements were taken from five randomly selected plants within each plot, excluding the border rows, and the results were averaged. Grain yield was harvested from the central sixteen rows (approximately 9.6 m2) of each plot. Biomass and grain were sun-dried to a constant weight before being weighed using a digital balance. Thousand seed weight (1000-SW) was determined from randomly selected grain samples. Grain moisture content was measured using a Dickey-John Multigrain Moisture Tester, and grain yield was adjusted to 12.5% moisture content.

Prior to statistical analysis the data was cleaned, organized, and checked for normality and homogeneity of variance using SAS statistical package SAS version 9.4. The data were analyzed using a Linear Mixed Effects Model (LMM) (in PROC MIXED of the SAS system). Nutrient treatments were considered fixed effects, while farms (sites) and blocks within farms were treated as random effects. The LMM framework was employed to account for both fixed and random sources of variation. Several candidate models were formulated, incorporating fixed effects (treatments) and random effects (farmers and replications). The fit of the model was assessed using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model was selected as it has lower AIC and BIC values compared to the other models because a lower AIC and BIC indicate a better fit for the model. The mean separation was performed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method to adjust the P values for comparing least-square means. Results

Soil fertility status of farmers’ fields

The pre-treatment soil analysis results for various parameters were evaluated using the guidelines provided by Wogi et al.27. Based on this assessment, the soil pH at the experimental farms ranged from slightly acidic to neutral (6.1–7.0). Organic carbon (OC) content ranged from medium to high, with values between 1.85% and 4.19%. Most farms (5 out of 8 farms) exhibited high organic carbon levels, and the rest (3 out of 8 farms) had medium levels. According to the Olsen-P classification, available phosphorus (P) levels ranged from low to medium (3.10–10.94 mg/kg), while total nitrogen (N) was found to be within medium to very high ranges (0.08–0.29%). Notably,7 out of the 8 experimental farms were phosphorus-deficient, while only 1 out of 8 had medium phosphorus levels (Table 2). Effects of treatments on growth parameters

All growth parameters, including number of fertile tillers, plant height, spike length, and above-ground biomass, were significantly affected by nutrient treatments (p < 0.0001; Fig 3; Table 3). Growth parameter values recorded under the complete nutrient treatment (NPKSZnB), NP, NP + S₂, and treatments omitting B, Zn, S, or K were statistically similar and consistently higher than those observed under nitrogen-omitted, phosphorus-omitted, and negative control treatments. Nitrogen omission caused substantial reductions in most growth parameters, closely resembling the negative control, whereas phosphorus omission resulted in moderate but significant reductions. The NP treatment produced growth parameter values comparable to those of the complete nutrient and NP + S₂ treatments and significantly outperformed the nitrogen-omitted, phosphorus-omitted, and control treatments.

Effects of treatments on yield and yield components

Nutrient treatments had a very highly significant effect on wheat grain yield and thousand-seed weight (p < 0.0001). Plots that received the complete nutrient treatment, as well as those omitting boron (B), zinc (Zn), sulfur (S), or potassium (K), produced statistically similar grain yields. This indicates that omission of these nutrients did not significantly reduce yield relative to the complete treatment (Fig. 2; Table 4). Grain yield under the complete treatment, B-, Zn-, S-, and K-omitted treatments, NP (blanket nitrogen and phosphorus recommendation), and NP + S₂ (NP plus additional sulfur) was significantly higher than those recorded under nitrogen-omitted, phosphorus-omitted, and negative control treatments. The NP + S₂ treatment performed comparably to the complete nutrient treatment, suggesting that additional sulfur beyond N and P did not provide further yield benefits under the experimental conditions.

In contrast, omission of nitrogen or phosphorus caused marked yield reductions. Nitrogen omission resulted in the greatest decline in grain yield, with value statistically equivalent to the negative control, confirming nitrogen as the most limiting nutrient in the study area. The highest yield penalty occurred in the negative control (36.68%), followed by nitrogen omission (31.33%) and phosphorus omission (12.32%). The nitrogen-omitted treatment recorded the highest thousand-seed weight (375.4 g), whereas the phosphorus-omitted treatment had the lowest (334.2 g). The complete nutrient treatment produced a thousand-seed weight that was significantly lower than that of the nitrogen-omitted treatment but significantly higher than that of the phosphorus-omitted treatment.

Discussion

Pre-treatment soil analysis indicated that soils across the experimental sites in Halaba, central Ethiopia, were slightly acidic to neutral (pH 6.1–7.0), which lies within the optimal range for wheat growth and nutrient availability28. This pH range favors microbial activity and the availability of most macro- and micronutrients. The dominant soil texture was sandy loam, which is generally associated with good drainage and root penetration but relatively low nutrient- and water-holding capacity29,30. Such soils are more prone to nutrient losses through leaching, emphasizing the importance of timely and balanced nutrient management.

Soil organic carbon levels ranged from medium to high (1.85–4.19%), indicating moderate inherent fertility and favorable soil structure. Similar organic carbon levels have been reported in comparable agro-ecological zones of sub-Saharan Africa, where residue retention and reduced tillage help maintain soil organic matter31,32. In contrast, available phosphorus levels were low to medium at most sites, with seven of the eight farms classified as phosphorus-deficient according to Olsen-P thresholds. This widespread P deficiency provides a strong basis for the observed yield responses to phosphorus fertilization and reflects limited historical P inputs in smallholder systems.

Nutrient treatments had a highly significant effect on wheat grain and biomass yields, highlighting the critical role of balanced fertilization under smallholder conditions in Halaba. Among the tested nutrients, nitrogen and phosphorus were identified as the most yield-limiting. Omission of nitrogen resulted in a 31.33% reduction in grain yield, while phosphorus omission caused a 12.32% reduction; the negative control showed the largest yield penalty (36.68%). These results are consistent with numerous studies across Ethiopia and other tropical agroecosystems, where nitrogen and phosphorus deficiencies are the primary constraints to cereal production10,21,33,34. Similarly, Kumar et al.35 reported significant reductions in wheat yield in the absence of nitrogen or phosphorus.

Despite moderate to high total soil nitrogen levels, wheat responded strongly to nitrogen fertilization. This apparent contradiction can be explained by the dominance of organic nitrogen forms in tropical soils and the poor synchronization between nitrogen mineralization and crop demand. As a result, plant-available nitrogen is often insufficient during critical growth stages, leading to substantial yield losses when nitrogen is omitted36,37. This explains why nitrogen omission produced yields comparable to the negative control. Phosphorus omission also significantly reduced yields, reflecting its essential role in root development, energy transfer, and early crop establishment, particularly in P-fixing tropical soils38,39.

In contrast, omission of potassium, sulfur, zinc, or boron did not significantly reduce grain or biomass yield relative to the complete nutrient treatment. Similarly, treatments receiving only nitrogen and phosphorus (NP) or NP plus additional sulfur (NP + S₂) produced yields equivalent to the full nutrient treatment (NPKSZnB). These findings suggest that native soil supplies of K, S, Zn, and B were sufficient to meet crop demand during the study period; however, the omission of certain baseline soil nutrient analyses limits the ability to directly relate the initial soil nutrient status to the observed plant responses for these specific nutrients. Comparable results have been reported in other wheat-growing areas of Ethiopia, where multi-nutrient fertilizers provided no yield advantage over N and P alone20,21,40. This indicates that focusing on N and P fertilization can improve fertilizer use efficiency while reducing unnecessary input costs and environmental risks.

The response of yield components—fertile tiller number, plant height, spike length, and thousand-seed weight—largely mirrored the patterns observed for grain and biomass yield. Nitrogen and phosphorus omission significantly reduced tiller production, plant height, and spike length, confirming their central role in vegetative growth and reproductive development. Nitrogen is particularly critical for tiller initiation and canopy development, while phosphorus supports early root growth and reproductive differentiation41,22,21. Reduced expression of these components under N- and P-deficient conditions ultimately translated into lower grain yields.

Thousand-seed weight responded differently from the other yield components. The nitrogen-omitted treatment recorded the highest thousand-seed weight, whereas the phosphorus-omitted treatment produced the lowest. This inverse response likely reflects a compensatory mechanism, whereby reduced sink size (fewer grains per plant) under nitrogen deficiency allows greater assimilate allocation to individual grains4243,. However, this increase in seed weight did not compensate for the large reductions in grain number and total yield, reinforcing the importance of adequate nitrogen supply for maximizing productivity. Phosphorus omission reduced thousand-seed weight, consistent with its role in carbohydrate transport and grain filling39. Omission of K, S, Zn, or B did not significantly affect yield components, further supporting the conclusion that these nutrients were not limiting under the study conditions.

Overall, the results demonstrate that nitrogen and phosphorus are the primary constraints to wheat productivity in Halaba, influencing both total yield and key yield components. The comparable performance of NP, NP + S₂, and complete nutrient treatments suggests that site-specific fertilization focused on N and P is sufficient to achieve optimal wheat yields in the study area. This finding underscores the importance of site-specific nutrient management approaches, as nutrient limitations vary with soil properties, cropping history, and environmental conditions34,44.

Although the study was conducted during a single growing season, replication across eight farms strengthens the robustness of the findings. Nevertheless, multi-season evaluations are recommended to capture seasonal variability and further refine nutrient management recommendations for smallholder wheat systems in central Ethiopia.

Conclusions and recommendations

Conclusions

The study demonstrated that nitrogen and phosphorus are the most limiting nutrients for wheat production in the study area, significantly influencing grain yield, biomass, and most yield components. The omission of nitrogen caused the largest reductions in yield, followed by phosphorus, while the exclusion of potassium, sulfur, zinc, or boron did not result in significant yield losses, suggesting that these nutrients were either sufficiently available in the soil or not limiting under the conditions of the study. The observation that nitrogen omission led to the highest 1000-seed weight reflects a compensatory physiological response, yet it did not mitigate the substantial reductions in overall yield.

Recommendations and future research directions

The comparable performance of the NP and complete nutrient treatments indicates that nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) are the primary inputs required for wheat production in the study area. Fertilizer recommendations should therefore prioritize N and P application, while the use of secondary and micronutrients (S, K, Zn, and B) should be guided by site-specific soil testing or clear deficiency symptoms to avoid unnecessary costs.

Future research should address the key limitations of the present study. First, multi-season trials are needed to assess the stability of N and P limitations under interannual variability in rainfall and temperature. Second, comprehensive soil and plant analyses—including baseline soil levels, nutrient uptake, and nutrient use efficiency—should be incorporated to improve understanding of nutrient dynamics. Third, factorial experiments are recommended to examine interactions among N, P, and other nutrients, particularly in P-deficient soils. Fourth, integrating economic analyses of fertilizer strategies and evaluating varietal differences in nutrient use efficiency would strengthen the applicability of site-specific nutrient management recommendations for smallholder farmers in the region.

Finally, it would be helpful to note that, although the findings are robust for Luvisols in the Halaba region, further studies across a wider range of soil types within the broader agroecological zone would be valuable to assess the generalizability of these nutrient limitations.
