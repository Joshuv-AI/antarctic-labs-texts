Optimality of Gradient-MUSIC for Spectral Estimation
Albert Fannjiang, Weilin Li, Wenjing Liao

Abstract
We introduce the Gradient-MUSIC algorithm for estimating the unknown frequencies and amplitudes of a nonharmonic signal from noisy time samples. While the classical MUSIC algorithm performs a computationally expensive search over a fine grid, Gradient-MUSIC is significantly more efficient and eliminates the need for discretization over a fine grid by using optimization techniques. It coarsely scans the 1D landscape to find initialization simultaneously for all frequencies followed by parallelizable local refinement via gradient descent. We also analyze its performance when the noise level is sufficiently small and the signal frequencies are separated by at least 8 ​ π / m 8\pi/m , where π / m \pi/m is the standard resolution of this problem. Even though the 1D landscape is nonconvex, we prove a global convergence result for Gradient-MUSIC: coarse scanning provably finds suitable initialization and gradient descent converges at a linear rate. In addition to convergence results, we also upper bound the error between the true signal frequencies and amplitudes with those found by Gradient-MUSIC. For example, if the noise has ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} norm at most ε \varepsilon , then the frequencies and amplitudes are recovered up to error at most C ​ ε / m C\varepsilon/m and C ​ ε C\varepsilon respectively for a universal C > 0 C>0 , which are minimax optimal in m m , ε \varepsilon , and number of frequencies. Our theory can also handle stochastic noise with performance guarantees under nonstationary independent Gaussian noise. Our main approach is a comprehensive geometric analysis of the landscape, a perspective that has not been explored before.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The spectral estimation problem is to accurately estimate the frequencies and amplitudes
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}}):=\{(x_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{s}
of a nonharmonic Fourier sum,

h(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{j}e^{ix_{j}\xi},

(1.1)

given noisy measurements of
h
at a finite collection of
\xi
. After normalization, we consider a canonical setting where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}=:\mathbb{T}
, the clean measurements
{\boldsymbol{y}}:=\{h(k)\}_{k=-m+1,\dots,m-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
are collected at
2m-1
consecutive integers, and the noisy measurements
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
are perturbed from the clean measurements by the noise vector
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{y}}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}:=\{h(k)\}_{k=-m+1,\dots,m-1}.

(1.2)

The noise vector
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
can be deterministic or random.
This inverse problem arises in many interesting applications in imaging and signal processing,
including
direction-of-arrival
(DoA) estimation
[
19
]
, inverse source and inverse scattering
[
18
,
13
,
14
]
, electroencephalogram (EEG) signal analysis
[
37
]
, and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy
[
25
]
.

In the spectral estimation problem, both the frequencies and amplitudes are unknown, which makes this a nonlinear inverse problem. Without noise, the unknown frequencies can be exactly recovered up to machine precision by
2s
measurements. In the presence of noise, the main difficulty of spectral estimation lies in estimating the frequencies, after which the amplitudes can be estimated by solving an over-determined linear system of equations.

Research on spectral estimation has focused on developing robust algorithms and analyzing their recovery errors from a theoretical perspective.
The first solution to spectral estimation can be traced back to Prony
[
32
]
in 1795. The Prony’s method uses exactly
2s
measurements and requires finding all roots of a degree
s
trigonometric polynomial. Subspace methods such as classical MUSIC
[
35
]
and ESPRIT
[
34
]
were invented for multi-snapshot spectral estimation problem whereby the amplitudes vary randomly and a large number of snapshots of data are collected. These methods can be adapted to tackle the single-snapshot spectral estimation problem (also referred to as the line spectral estimation problem in some papers), which was done in
[
17
,
30
,
26
,
21
]
. These methods are typically grouped together despite significant differences because they share the same first step of finding an approximation of the “signal subspace”. Convex methods for spectral estimation were developed more recently. Several closely related methods include total variation minimization, atomic norm minimization, and Beurling-LASSO, see
[
6
,
39
,
5
,
12
]
.

Classical MUSIC was among the first stable methods to achieve high-resolution recovery. It creates a
1D landscape function
and finds its
s
smallest local minima, which act as the estimated signal frequencies. Evaluating the landscape function on a fine grid is computationally expensive, and without additional information, classical MUSIC finds the local minima through an exhaustive grid search. Consequently, its resolution and accuracy are tied to the grid spacing: finer grids improve precision, but incur significantly higher computational cost. While this computational drawback motivated the development of more efficient algorithms, classical MUSIC is especially attractive in practical applications due to its simplicity and generality.

As for theoretical analysis, it is now well-understood that the stability of spectral estimation to noise depends on the minimum separation between the frequencies, denoted
\Delta:=\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})
, relative to
m
, which represents the aperture in imaging settings
[
8
]
. The seminal paper
[
11
]
illustrated that spectral estimation is fundamentally different depending on whether
m\Delta\gg\pi
(which we call
well-separated case
) or
m\Delta\ll\pi
(which we call
super-resolution regime
). Importantly,
\pi/m
is called the
Rayleigh length
and is regarded as the standard resolution of this problem
[
8
]
. Typically, the well-separated case and super-resolution regime are treated independently in theoretical analysis because they require separate techniques and have different error rates. This paper focuses on theoretical analysis for the well-separated case and super-resolution is beyond the scope of this paper.

Motivated by classical MUSIC’s widespread use and its reliance on an exhaustive grid search, we aim to develop a computationally efficient algorithm that reduces its computational cost while providing provable error guarantees. From a computational perspective, the algorithm should be computationally tractable and significantly faster than classical MUSIC. From a theoretical perspective, an ideal algorithm is provably optimal, achieving estimation error that matches the minimax lower bound for the well-separated case, up to logarithmic factors. The most important parameters for this setting are the number of samples
2m-1
, an appropriate notion of noise level, and number of frequencies
s
.

1.2
Our approach and contributions

In this work, we introduce Gradient-MUSIC, an algorithm that is significantly more efficient than classical MUSIC and is provably optimal in the well-separated regime under both deterministic and i.i.d. Gaussian noise, specifically when
m\Delta\geq 8\pi
.

Algorithm Contribution.
We propose an alternative strategy inspired by nonconvex optimization techniques:
coarse scanning followed by local refinement by gradient descent
. The proposed algorithm is called Gradient-MUSIC, which avoids an expensive grid search that classical MUSIC suffers from. Gradient-MUSIC evaluates the landscape function on a coarse grid, locates a set of suitable simultaneous initialization, and runs gradient descent to produce iterates that converge to the relevant local minima of the landscape function at a linear rate. Gradient-MUSIC has several key features:

(a)

Precise coarse grid for initialization.
To find suitable initialization, the landscape function only needs to be evaluated on a grid of width
1/(2m)
, just a constant factor smaller than the Rayleigh length
\pi/m
. While it is possible that the numerical constant can be improved, the
1/m
scaling cannot be altered.

(b)

Grid-less in outcome.
Our method utilizes a grid only to find good initialization. Afterwards, gradient descent produces iterates that live on
the continuous domain
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, so Gradient-MUSIC outputs estimated parameters that do not suffer from discretization, in stark contrast to classical MUSIC.

(c)

Speed without compromise.
Evaluating the landscape function on a coarse grid keeps the computational cost modest, while the local refinement step enjoys fast linear convergence. In addition, the local refinement step is parallelizable with the
simultaneous initialization
on the coarse grid, further speeding up the computation. Importantly, the improved speed of Gradient-MUSIC is not achieved by sacrificing accuracy, since our theoretical results show that it is optimal in many aspects. Under the assumptions of this paper, one should always use Gradient-MUSIC over its classical counterpart.

Theoretical Contribution.
We provide a stability analysis of Gradient-MUSIC that is highly general, which allows one to treat different types of perturbations in a unified manner. For demonstration, we specialize to two canonical noise models.

This requirement, however, incurs a significant computational cost and is the main drawback of classical MUSIC.

1.3
Discussion and consequences

Optimality for deterministic or adversarial perturbations.
The minimax error is the accuracy achieved by the best algorithm(s) evaluated on the worst case parameter and perturbation. It is not obvious that an optimal and tractable algorithm exists because by definition, the minimax error is taken over all possible functions from data to parameters, including those that are computationally intractable or non-computable. Many papers on deterministic minimax rates pertain to the super-resolution regime, see
[
11
,
7
,
22
,
4
,
27
]
. Similar techniques are applicable to the well-separated case, but we could not find a direct result.

There are three aspects in which Gradient-MUSIC is optimal, up to universal constants.

(a)

Rate-optimality.
We prove in
Lemma

4.3
that the minimax frequency and amplitude errors are
\Omega(\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}/m^{1+1/p})
and
\Omega(\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}/m^{1/p})
respectively. These minimax lower bounds match the upper bounds achieved by Gradient-MUSIC, which certifies optimality in the noise level
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}
, number of samples
2m-1
, and number of frequencies
s
.

(b)

Noise tolerance.
Our theory for Gradient-MUSIC requires that
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}\leq cm^{1/p}
for a small enough universal
c>0
. This noise assumption is necessary regardless of which method is used, because spectral estimation is generally impossible otherwise, see
Remark

4.2
.

(c)

Separation condition.
Our theory for Gradient-MUSIC holds under the separation condition
m\Delta\geq 8\pi
. This requirement does not depend on the total number of frequencies
s
, so importantly, it possible for
m
to be proportional to
s
. When
m\Delta\ll\pi
, the spectral estimation problem requires a much stronger noise assumption regardless of which method is used, see
[
30
,
22
,
4
]
.

Of the three optimality features of Gradient-MUSIC, the most striking one is its rate-optimality, which has important implications that will be discussed momentarily.

To our best knowledge, Gradient-MUSIC is the first provably optimal and computationally efficient algorithm for spectral estimation, under deterministic perturbations and sufficiently well-separated frequencies. Gradient-MUSIC is as good or better than any conceivable method, even those that have access to unlimited computational resources.

Benefits of oversampling.
It is already evident from previous analysis that for subspace and convex methods, increasing the number of samples enlarges the class of signals for which stable recovery is possible, see
[
30
,
26
,
21
,
15
,
2
]
. This is also evident for Gradient-MUSIC because the separation condition
m\Delta\geq 8\pi
relaxes for larger
m
. However, there is a second and perhaps more interesting benefit of oversampling.

When examining the effects of increasing the number of samples, it is natural to use the
\ell^{\infty}
norm for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. Suppose the signal parameters
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
are fixed, while
m
can be made arbitrarily large and
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Our main results show that Gradient-MUSIC recovers the frequencies and amplitudes with error at most
C\varepsilon/m
and
C\varepsilon
. Thus, for frequencies that are already well separated, using even more measurements beyond the bare minimum reduces the frequency error, while keeping the amplitude error bounded.

The benefits of oversampling are also apparent in other
\ell^{p}
norms as well. Of notable interest is the
\ell^{2}
norm, which can be recast in terms of the standard noise-to-signal ratio (NSR). When
m\Delta\geq 8\pi
, by
Proposition

2.1
,

{\rm NSR}:=\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}\asymp\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}.

Our main results show that if
\sqrt{{\rm NSR}}
for a small enough universal constant
c>0
, then Gradient-MUSIC achieves

\text{frequency error}\leq\frac{C\sqrt{{\rm NSR}}}{m},\quad\text{and}\quad\text{amplitude error}\leq C\sqrt{{\rm NSR}}.

When framed this way, it estimates the frequencies much more accurately than
\sqrt{{\rm NSR}}
and improves in
m
for fixed
{\rm NSR}
.

Spectral estimation even for sub-linearly growing noise variance.
For
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}={\rm diag}\big(\{\sigma^{2}(1+|k|)^{2r}\}_{k}\big)
and
r\in(0,1/2)
, the main results show that Gradient-MUSIC can still succeed, which may be rather surprising. The performance of Gradient-MUSIC matches the rate for
nonlinear least squares
(NLS) with suitable initialization, which was derived in
[
45
]
. Moreover, both Gradient-MUSIC and NLS fail if
r\geq 1/2
. This comparison strongly suggests that Gradient-MUSIC is also optimal for
r\in(0,1/2)
and the growth condition
r<1/2
is necessary. For i.i.d. Gaussian noise
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}})
, Gradient-MUSIC matches the Cramér-Rao lower bound in
[
36
]
up to logarithmic factors, which certifies that the former is provably optimal. It was shown in
[
10
]
that ESPRIT is optimal for i.i.d. subgaussian noise.

Global optimization with the landscape function.
We studied Gradient-MUSIC from the viewpoint of nonconvex optimization in order to eliminate the gridding artifacts of classical MUSIC. It is well known that finding suitable initialization for local optimization methods like gradient descent is challenging when the objective function is nonconvex. A key finding of this paper is that evaluating the 1D landscape function on a grid of width
1/(2m)
is enough to find suitable initialization
simultaneously for all
s
smallest local minima
. While the constant
1/2
can be potentially improved, in
Remark

5.11
, we demonstrate that the landscape function needs to be evaluated on a grid whose width is no larger than
4\pi/m
, even if there is no noise and
m\Delta\geq 2\pi\beta
for arbitrarily large
\beta
.

It is helpful to compare Gradient-MUSIC with NLS, since the latter is the generic nonconvex optimization method. The papers
[
41
,
40
]
derived lower and upper bounds for the strong basins of attraction for NLS, but for a different setup where certain random measurements are collected. Theoretical results and numerical simulations in
[
40
]
show that the strong basin of attraction to the global minimum of NLS is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2s}
whose radius is proportional to
\Delta
. The volume of this ball relative to the measure of the parameter space is
O(\Delta^{2s})
. Thus,
\Omega(\Delta^{-2s})
random guesses are required to find suitable initialization. Without a principled method to find initial guess, NLS is computationally intractable as
s
becomes large.

Open questions for super-resolution regime.
This paper proves that Gradient-MUSIC is optimal in the well-separated case and does not consider the super-resolution regime. The minimax lower bound for clustered frequencies in the super-resolution regime was derived in
[
4
]
. To our best knowledge, we are not aware of any algorithms that provably achieve this optimal scaling in
m
,
\Delta
and noise level, widely considered the three most important parameters for this setting. A detailed discussion would be too distracting to include here, so we refer the reader to
[
4
]
and references therein for further details.

1.4
Literature review

In this section and throughout the paper,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
denotes some additive perturbation of the clean samples. It may be deterministic or stochastic. We concentrate our discussion solely on the well-separated case
m\Delta\gg\pi
. To simplify the following discussion, the various constants
C>0
that appear in this section do not depend on
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
and
m
, but may depend on other parameters such as
s
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

Subspace methods.
For the well-separated case and deterministic perturbations, ESPRIT
[
21
]
and MPM
[
30
]
recover the frequencies and amplitudes with error at most
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{m}
and
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}\sqrt{m}
, respectively. Applying Hölder’s inequalities, these upper bounds become
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}
and
Cm\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}
, respectively. In either case, they do not achieve the minimax lower bounds. Even a sub-optimal analysis of MUSIC has been elusive. Prior work such as
[
26
]
requires an implicit assumption on the convexity of the objective function near the true minima. Unlike the main results of this paper, these previous results say that one should use the smallest
m
such that the frequencies are sufficiently well separated because increasing
m
beyond the minimum number requires more computational resources while potentially making the amplitude error larger.

For i.i.d. subgaussian noise, it was recently shown that ESPRIT is minimax optimal in
[
10
]
. Reference
[
10
]
does not provide results for other types of random noise or deterministic perturbations. Since there are significant differences between MUSIC and ESPRIT, a fine-grain analysis of one method generally does not carry over to the other. A technical discussion about MUSIC versus ESPRIT can be found in
Remark

5.12
.

Older analysis for subspace methods are typically for the multisnapshot version where the amplitudes vary randomly and independently. For example,
[
36
,
20
]
derive error bounds that are asymptotic (in
m
and the number of snapshots). While the multisnapshot problem and spectral estimation are related, there are subtle differences. For the multisnapshot problem, under i.i.d. Gaussian noise and independent stochastic amplitudes, classical MUSIC is asymptotically optimal
[
36
]
, while ESPRIT is provably sub-optimal
[
33
]
. On the other hand, for spectral estimation and i.i.d. Gaussian noise, this paper and
[
10
]
show that (classical & Gradient-) MUSIC and ESPRIT are optimal, respectively.

Convex optimization.
A direct comparison to our work is not easy to give since convex methods may output more than
s
frequencies; they may create false positives, or estimate a true pair
(x_{j},a_{j})
as two separate pairs. Even if these effects are assumed not to occur,
[
15
,
2
]
show that (after performing some simplifications) for an absolute constant
C>0
and any sufficiently small
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}
, the frequency error is at most
C\sqrt{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}}/m
. This falls short of the lower bound of
\Omega(\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/m^{3/2})
established here. As for the amplitudes, assuming no true
(x_{j},a_{j})
gets recovered as two separate ones,
[
15
,
2
]
imply that the amplitude error is at most
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}
, whereas the optimal lower bound is
\Omega(\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{m})
.

For i.i.d. Gaussian noise,
[
38
]
showed that atomic norm minimization is optimal for denoising the measurements. This is a weaker statement than estimating
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
optimally, since it can be proved that any minimax optimal method for frequency and amplitude estimation is necessarily optimal for denoising. The frequency and amplitude estimation errors for atomic norm minimization in that paper do not achieve the minimax rates for i.i.d. Gaussian noise.

Nonconvex optimization.
We had already discussed NLS earlier. Minimization of a difference of convex objective functions was used in
[
28
]
, while a sliding Frank–Wolfe algorithm was employed in
[
9
]
. Neither paper has performance guarantees when there is noise is the measurements. We are not aware of any other nonconvex optimization techniques for spectral estimation with theory that quantifies the frequency and amplitude errors in the presence of noise.

A complex standard normal random variable
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
has independent real and complex parts that are normally distributed with mean zero and variance of
1/2
. We write
{\boldsymbol{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{I}})
if the entries of the complex vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}
are independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables. We write
{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{w}}+{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
for a
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{I}})
and positive definite
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Spectral estimation problem formulation

Consider a nonharmonic Fourier sum
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
with
s
nonzero components, defined as in (
1.1
). In formula (
1.1
), the frequencies and amplitudes of
h
are
{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}:=\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, respectively. We call
s
the
sparsity
. Each
a_{j}\not=0
by assumption, so this function has exactly
s
frequencies. The
h
function in (
1.1
) is uniquely specified by its parameters
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}
, which should be grouped as pairs,

({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})=\{(x_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{s}.

(2.1)

In spectral estimation, one receives the noisy measurements
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
given in (
1.2
), and the goal is recover the frequency and amplitude pairs in (
2.1
).

Definition 2.1

(Spectral Estimation Problem)
.

The
spectral estimation problem
is to find a computationally tractable algorithm such that it receives
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
and outputs an approximation to
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
.

Remark 2.2
.

There is a a modulation, translation, or rotational invariance. Here, the only important assumption is that samples are integer spaced and they are consecutive. The problem does not change if
\{-m+1,\dots,m-1\}
were shifted by any real number because any shift creates a harmless phase factor in
{\boldsymbol{y}}
that can be absorbed into
{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

Remark 2.3
.

There are several variations of the spectral estimation problem, such as whether
s
is known beforehand and/or if the amplitudes need to be estimated as well. The core of spectral estimation lies in approximating
{\boldsymbol{x}}
assuming
s
is known, which we refer to as
frequency estimation
. We will treat the other two problems of
sparsity detection
(estimating
s
from just data
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
) in
Section

5.2
and
amplitude estimation
(approximation of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
) in
Section

5.5
separately from the core frequency estimation problem.

Spectral estimation exhibits a permutation invariance, whereby the function
h
is invariant under the transformation
\{(x_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{s}\mapsto\{(x_{\pi(j)},a_{\pi(j)})\}_{j=1}^{s}
for any permutation
\pi
on
\{1,\dots,s\}
. For this reason, given a pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
have the same cardinality, it is natural to define the frequency error as the matching distance,

\min_{\pi}\max_{j}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{\pi(j)}|.

Then for the permutation
\pi
that attains this minimum, the amplitude error is defined as

\max_{j}|a_{j}-\widehat{a}_{\pi(j)}|.

It is customary to just assume
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
has been indexed so that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
best match, so the optimal permutation is the identity. This is the convention that we use throughout this paper.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, the nonharmonic
Fourier matrix
with nodes
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
is defined as

{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(n,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=\left[e^{ikx_{j}}\right]_{k\in I(n),\,j=1,\dots,s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times s}.

The range of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(n,{\boldsymbol{x}})
and its singular values are invariant under any shift of the index set
I(n)
defined in (
1.3
). Spectral estimation is a nonlinear inverse problem because the forward map
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\mapsto{\boldsymbol{y}}
is nonlinear. To understand the general stability of spectral estimation, it is prudent to analyze the forward map. Define the minimum separation of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
by

\Delta:=\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})=\min_{j\not=k}\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\,|x_{j}-x_{k}+2\pi n|.

Recall the following result that controls its extreme singular values.

Proposition 2.1

(Aubel-Bölcskei
[
1
]
)
.

For any
m\geq 1
,
\beta>1
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, it holds that

\sqrt{m(1-\beta^{-1})}\leq\sigma_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}))\leq\sigma_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}))\leq\sqrt{m(1+\beta^{-1})}.

When
m\Delta\gg 2\pi
, it means that the forward map is well conditioned. When
m\Delta\ll 2\pi
, the condition number of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}})
behaves radically different. Since our theory does not consider this case, we do not give a description of what happens and instead refer the reader to
[
3
,
24
]
and references therein.

2.2
Review of the classical MUSIC algorithm

Subspace methods were developed in the late 1980’s, and some popular methods include MUSIC
[
35
]
and ESPRIT
[
34
]
. They were originally invented for direction-of-arrival (DoA) estimation. The original versions assume that the signal amplitudes vary over time and one obtains multiple snapshots of data. The spectral estimation problem can be viewed as a single-snapshot version.

Of course, one obtains considerably more information in the multisnapshot version than in spectral estimation. The original MUSIC and ESPRIT algorithms can be used for spectral estimation as well, albeit with some modifications. This method of modification uses a Hankel matrix, which is also used in Prony’s method
[
32
]
and MPM
[
17
]
. While there are some similarities between the single and multiple snapshot problems, they are considerably different, see
[
23
]
for further details.

To be clear, in this paper,
classical MUSIC
refers to the single-snapshot MUSIC algorithm in
[
26
]
, which is faithful to the original multiple-snapshot MUSIC algorithm created in
[
35
]
for the multisnapshot version.

This section briefly reviews classical MUSIC and also some mechanism behind subspace methods. There are three major steps: identification of
s
and approximation of the signal subspace, approximation of the frequencies
{\boldsymbol{x}}
with
s
given, and estimation of the amplitudes
{\boldsymbol{a}}
given approximate frequencies. The second procedure is the core step, and is the focus of our discussion.

Noiseless setting

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
. Recall we indexed
{\boldsymbol{y}}
by
\{-m+1,\dots,m-1\}
. The square
Toeplitz matrix
of
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
is defined as

{\boldsymbol{T}}:=T({\boldsymbol{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}y_{0}&y_{-1}&\cdots&y_{-m+1}\\
y_{1}&y_{0}&&y_{-m}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{m-1}&y_{m-2}&\cdots&y_{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.

More generally, this defines a linear operator
T\colon\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
which maps an arbitrary vector to its associated Toeplitz matrix. Whenever
m\geq s
, we have the factorization

T({\boldsymbol{y}})={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}})^{*},\quad\text{where}\quad{\boldsymbol{A}}:={\rm diag}({\boldsymbol{a}}).

(2.2)

If additionally
m>s
, the Toeplitz
{\boldsymbol{T}}
has rank exactly equal to
s
. Hence, it is rank deficient and its leading
s
dimensional left singular space is also
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}})
. Its range
{\boldsymbol{U}}
can be computed through the singular value decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{T}}
. It is common to refer to
{\boldsymbol{U}}
as the “signal subspace.”

Remark 2.4
.

In many references that use subspace methods, a Hankel matrix is used instead of Toeplitz. There is no practical difference since they have the same left singular space and identity (
2.2
) holds if
T({\boldsymbol{y}})
and the adjoint
∗
are replaced with a Hankel operator of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and transpose
T
, respectively. Some references also work with right singular spaces instead, which again, only results in cosmetic changes.

To compute
{\boldsymbol{x}}
from
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, the classical MUSIC algorithm forms a certain function
q=q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
, in the following way.

Definition 2.5

(Steering vector)
.

The
(normalized) steering vector
is a vector-valued function
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
defined as

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left[e^{ikt}\right]_{k\in I(m)}.

Definition 2.6

(Landscape function)
.

For any
m>s
, the
landscape function

q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with a subspace
{\boldsymbol{W}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
of dimension
s
is defined as

q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}(t):=1-\|{\boldsymbol{W}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}.

When
{\boldsymbol{W}}
is understood from context, we simply write
q
instead of
q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
.

The following has been established in prior analysis of MUSIC, see
[
35
,
26
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
m>s
and
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
be the landscape function associated with
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is an orthonormal basis for the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}})
. We have
q(t)=0
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
if and only if
t\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Thus, whenever there is no noise,
{\boldsymbol{U}}
can be computed from
T({\boldsymbol{y}})
and the unknown frequencies
{\boldsymbol{x}}
can be determined by finding the all zeros of
q
. When
m
is large, this is not necessarily an easy task, as it requires finding all zeros of
q
, whose degree is potentially much larger than
s
. Nonetheless, this discussion illustrates that the noiseless problem can be reduced to a standard numerical task.

Noisy case

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\not={\boldsymbol{0}}
. The previous strategy has to be significantly modified because we cannot directly compute
{\boldsymbol{U}}
anymore. Subspace methods first form a Toeplitz matrix from noisy data,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}:=T(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.

(2.3)

Since
T
is a linear operator and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{y}}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, we have

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}:=T(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})=T({\boldsymbol{y}})+T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})={\boldsymbol{T}}+T({\boldsymbol{\eta}}).

(2.4)

The Toeplitz matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
is a perturbation of
{\boldsymbol{T}}
. Subspace methods compute the leading
s
dimensional left singular space of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
to approximate
{\boldsymbol{U}}
.

Definition 2.7

(Toeplitz Estimator)
.

If
m>s
and
\sigma_{s}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})\not=\sigma_{s+1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})
, then the
Toeplitz estimator
is the unique (up to trivial ambiguities) orthonormal basis
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
for the leading
s
dimensional left singular space of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
.

It is common to call
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
an “empirical signal subspace.” We refrain from using this term as it incorrectly suggest that the Toeplitz estimator is the only way of approximating
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. The error between
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is naturally quantified by the sine-theta distance
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{U}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
defined in (
1.4
).

The classical MUSIC algorithm evaluates the landscape function
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
associated with the Toeplitz estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
, on a finite set
G\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and finds the
s
smallest (discrete) local minima of
\widetilde{q}
on
G
. Here,
G
can be nonuniform, but it needs to be dense enough so that the discrete minima closely approximate the continuous ones. To quantify this, we define the
mesh norm
of
G
as

{\rm mesh}(G):=\max_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\min_{u\in G}|t-u|.

(2.5)

A smaller mesh norm corresponds to a denser set. By making
{\rm mesh}(G)
small, it reduces numerical error of classical MUSIC, but increases its computational complexity.

Classical MUSIC requires solving a nonconvex optimization problem where
\widetilde{q}
is the objective function. There is an important difference between this setup and a standard optimization approach. Since
{\boldsymbol{x}}
has cardinality
s
, a typical optimization approach (such as maximum likelihood optimization and nonlinear least squares) would create an objective function of
2s
variables and look for its global minimum. In contrast, classical MUSIC creates a single variable function
\widetilde{q}
and finds its
s
smallest minima. This can be viewed as a dimension reduction step. This is one of several reasons why we defined
q_{\boldsymbol{W}}
as the “landscape function”, as opposed to the more generic term “objective function”. A mathematical perspective of
q_{\boldsymbol{W}}
is provided in
Section

5.1
.

The classical MUSIC algorithm has several well known theoretical and computational issues.

(a)

The number of frequencies
s
must be known, otherwise the computed
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\widetilde{q}
are incorrect. Then the recovered
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}

(b)

has strictly less than
s

(c)

Even if
\widetilde{q}
has at least

is preferred over other local minima.

(d)

Despite classical MUSIC being formulated as a grid-free algorithm, one must evaluate
\widetilde{q}
on a grid

to approximate its local minima.

Remark 4.2

(Optimality of noise condition)
.

We give a simple example which illustrates why the noise condition (
4.1
) is necessary, up to universal constants. If
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})=(0,r_{0})
and
\eta_{k}=-r_{0}
for each
k=-m+1,\dots,m-1
, then
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}=r_{0}(2m-1)^{1/p}
while
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
. This shows that spectral estimation is impossible unless
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}/(r_{0}m^{1/p})
is sufficiently small.

Theorem

4.1
is proved in
Section

10.1
. Although the
p\in(1,\infty]
statements in
Theorem

4.1
are immediate consequences of the
p=1
statement and Hölder’s inequality, we stated this theorem for all
p\in[1,\infty]
since each
p
has a different interpretation. In the subsequent discussion, we ignore all quantities aside from
m
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. Dependence on
a_{\min}
can be effectively ignored by rescaling the main model equation (
1.2
), so that
a_{\min}=1
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}/a_{\min}
is redefined to be
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. Additionally, we ignore
a_{\max}/a_{\min}
since by assumption,
a_{\max}/a_{\min}\leq 10
.

Small bounded perturbations
. Suppose
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is deterministic and quantified in the
\ell^{\infty}
norm. This is arguably the most natural norm if we fix
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
, let
m
increase, and assume the entries of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
are uniformly bounded. We see that assumption (
4.1
) is satisfied whenever
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}
is a small enough universal constant. In this case, the frequency and amplitude errors are
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}/m
and
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\infty}
respectively.

High energy perturbations
. Suppose
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is deterministic and quantified in the
\ell^{2}
norm. This is an energy norm, and this assumption is perhaps natural for physical applications. Condition (
4.1
) is satisfied whenever
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{m}
is sufficiently small. If
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}
is uniformly bound in
m
, then this is a less stringent constraint compared to the one for bounded noise. In this case, the frequency and amplitude errors are
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/m^{3/2}
and
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{m}
respectively.

Sparse perturbations
. Suppose
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is deterministic and quantified in the
\ell^{1}
norm. We refer to it as a sparse perturbation since
\ell^{1}
is a surrogate norm for sparsity. Condition (
4.1
) is satisfied whenever
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{1}/m
is sufficiently small. If
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{1}
is uniformly bounded in
m
, then this condition is even easier to satisfy than previous ones. The frequency and amplitude errors are
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{1}/m^{2}
and
C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{1}/m
respectively. This indicates that both MUSIC algorithms take advantage of sparse perturbations.

4.2
Stochastic noise

Now we move onto several examples of stochastic noise. For simplicity, we consider a natural example where
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
is normally distributed with mean zero and diagonal covariance matrix

. The following theorem is proved in
Section

.

Theorem 4.2

(Gaussian noise with diagonal covariance)
.

Let
m\geq 100
,
r_{0}>0
, and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2m-1)\times(2m-1)}
be a diagonal matrix with positive diagonals. There are positive universal constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
, and

such that for any
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}\left({8\pi}/m,r_{0},10r_{0}\right)
, noise

1-2m^{1-t^{2}}-2m\exp\left(-\frac{ca_{\min}^{2}m^{2}}{{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}\right).

Let
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
be the output of Gradient-MUSIC (

3
) with

,
\alpha=0.529
,
h=6/m^{2}
, any

such that
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 1/(2m)
, and any

(4.4)

Then we have

\displaystyle\max_{j}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}}{a_{\min}}\frac{t\sqrt{{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(m)}}{m^{2}},

\displaystyle\max_{j}|a-\widehat{a}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\,\frac{a_{\max}}{a_{\min}}\frac{t\sqrt{{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(m)}}{m}.

Let us examine the content of
Theorem

4.2
for a parametric family of examples. Suppose
\Sigma_{k,k}=\sigma^{2}(1+|k|)^{2r}
for some
\sigma>0
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
. This encapsulates a family of nonstationary Gaussian noise: decaying (
r<0
), stationary (
r=0
), and growing (
r>0
). For some
C_{r}>0
that depends only on
r
, we have

{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\leq\begin{cases}C_{r}\sigma^{2}m^{2r+1}&\text{if }r>-1/2,\\
C_{r}\sigma^{2}\log(m)&\text{if }r=-1/2,\\
C_{r}&\text{if }r<-1/2.\end{cases}

(4.5)

For this family of diagonal covariance matrices, we see that for fixed
\sigma
, condition (
4.3
) is at least
1-o(m)
as
m\to\infty
if and only if
r\in(-\infty,1/2)
. In the subsequent discussion, we ignore all quantities aside from
m
,
r
and
\sigma
. For the same reasons as the deterministic case, we can ignore dependence on
a_{\min}
and
a_{\max}/a_{\min}
.

Stationary
. Suppose
r=0
and
\sigma>0
is arbitrary. The variance can be arbitrarily large, provided that
m
is large enough to make condition (
4.3
) non-vacuous. The frequency and amplitude errors are
C\sigma m^{-3/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
and
C\sigma m^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
. Aside from
\log(m)
factors, both rates are optimal in view of the classical Cramér-Rao lower bound, see
[
36
]
. However, this example does not show that Gradient-MUSIC is maximally noise stable because the spectral estimation problem is still solvable for growing noise.

Remark 4.3

(Relaxation to i.i.d. subgaussian)
.

For the
r=0
case, the assumption that
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}})
is not essential and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
can be assumped to be i.i.d. subgaussian with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Indeed,
[
29
, Theorem 1]
showed that the Toeplitz matrix of subgaussian
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
has expected spectral norm
C\sigma\sqrt{m\log(m)}
where
C
also depends on the subgaussian constant. Using this result in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
, we would get that the average frequency and amplitude errors are
C\sigma m^{-3/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
and
C\sigma m^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
, identical to the case for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}})
.

Increasing
. Suppose
r\in(0,1/2)
and
\sigma>0
. The frequency and amplitude errors are
C\sigma m^{r-3/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
and
C\sigma m^{r-1/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
. This example is perhaps surprising because it shows that Gradient-MUSIC can tolerate noise that grows in strength as more samples are collected. Up to logarithmic factors, both Gradient-MUSIC and nonlinear least squares (see
[
45
]
for an analysis) attain the same rate for
r\in[0,1/2)
and both fail when
r\geq 1/2
. Since NLS is believed to be optimal, this strongly suggests that Gradient-MUSIC is also optimal and that no method can accurately estimate both the frequencies and amplitudes whenever
r\geq 1/2
.

Decreasing
. Suppose
r\in(-\infty,0)
and
\sigma>0
. For
r\in(-1/2,1/2)
, the frequency and amplitude errors are
C\sigma m^{r-3/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
and
C\sigma m^{r-1/2}\sqrt{\log(m)}
. Compared to the other two situations, Gradient-MUSIC provides better accuracy for both frequency and amplitude estimation. The rate improves as
r
is made more negative, until it saturates at
r=-1/2
, since making
r
even more negative just results in
{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
being bounded by a constant, as seen in (
4.5
).

4.3
Minimax rates for deterministic perturbations

This section derives lower bounds for optimal rates of approximation under deterministic perturbations quantified in the
\ell^{p}
norm. We define the
parameter space
,

\mathcal{P}(s):=\big\{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\colon|{\boldsymbol{x}}|=|{\boldsymbol{a}}|=s\quad\text{and}\quad|a_{j}|>0\quad\text{for all}\quad j=1,\dots,s\big\}.

(4.6)

The requirement
|a_{j}|>0
for all
j
ensures that there is a bijection between
\mathcal{P}(s)
and all possible exponential sums with exactly
s
distinct frequencies. Due to this bijection, it is equivalent to work with
\mathcal{P}(s)
instead. More generally, we view data
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
as a function from the parameters space and noise to data. Namely, define
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\colon\mathcal{P}(s)\times\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
by the equation,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(2m-1,{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{a}}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}}.

(4.7)

As seen in
Remark

4.2
, if the perturbation is allowed to be too large, then
s
cannot be determined from observations. The question we ask is if the perturbation is forced to be small enough and a method also knows
s
, then what is the best rate of approximation?

Fix any nonempty subsets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(s)
and
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
. For the set of signals, we will consider the signal class
Definition

4.1
. For the noise set
\mathcal{N}
, we will consider a natural scenario where
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is contained in
B^{2m-1}_{p}(\varepsilon)
, the closed
\ell^{p}
ball in
\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
of radius
\varepsilon
centered at zero. Consider the set of all possible data generated from the signal and noise sets,

\mathcal{Y}:=\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\colon({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}\}.

A function
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{P}(s)
is called a
method
. This definition does not require
\psi
to be computationally tractable or even numerically computable. As is customary, we let

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})=\psi(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}))

denote the output of
\psi
, and we simply write
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
instead of
\psi
. Just like the other parts of this paper, we have implicitly assumed that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
have been indexed to best match each other, which induces an ordering on
\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

By looking at the worst case signal and noise, the frequency and amplitude recovery rates for a method
\psi
(which is identified with its output
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
) are

\displaystyle X(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|,

\displaystyle A(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|a_{j}-\widehat{a}_{j}|.

We need to split the frequency and amplitude errors because as we will see, they have non-equivalent rates of approximation. By taking the infimum over all methods defined on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{N}
, the minimax rates for frequency and amplitude estimation are defined as

\displaystyle X_{*}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}):=\inf_{\psi}X(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N})\quad\text{and}\quad A_{*}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}):=\inf_{\psi}A(\psi,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{N}).

Since these definitions do not preclude the use of intractable algorithms, it is not clear if there is an efficient optimal algorithm that attains these minimax errors.

We have the following lower bounds.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
s\geq 1
,
m\geq 4s
,
0<r_{0}\leq r_{1}
, and
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}(8\pi/m,r_{0},r_{1})\cap\mathcal{P}(s)
. For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
\varepsilon\leq 16\pi r_{0}m^{1/p}
,

X_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{8r_{0}m^{1+1/p}}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{8m^{1/p}}.

The proof of
Lemma

4.3
is located in
Section

11.1
. Note the assumption
m\geq 4s
in this lemma is necessary because
\mathcal{S}(8\pi/m,r_{0},r_{1})\cap\mathcal{P}(s)=\emptyset
otherwise.

There are clearly some similarities between the lower bounds in
Lemma

4.3
and the performance guarantee for Gradient-MUSIC in
Theorem

4.1
. We say a method
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
is
optimal
on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(s)
and
B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)
if there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
m
and small
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\sup_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in B^{2m-1}_{p}(\varepsilon)}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,X_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B^{2m-1}_{p}(\varepsilon)\right),

\displaystyle\sup_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}}\,\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in B^{2m-1}_{p}(\varepsilon)}\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|a_{j}-\widehat{a}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\,A_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B^{2m-1}_{p}(\varepsilon)\right).

Combining
Lemma

4.3
and
Theorem

4.1
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
s\geq 1
,
m\geq\max\{4s,100\}
,
0<r_{0}\leq r_{1}
, and
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}(8\pi/m,r_{0},10r_{0})\cap\mathcal{P}(s)
. For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
\varepsilon\leq r_{0}m^{1/p}/400
,

X_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\asymp\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{0}m^{1+1/p}}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{*}\left(\mathcal{S},B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\asymp\frac{\varepsilon}{m^{1/p}}.

Moreover, Gradient-MUSIC is optimal on
\mathcal{S}
and
B_{p}^{2m-1}(\varepsilon)
.

5
General theory and framework

5.1
An abstract perspective of subspace methods

Let
\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
be the Grassmannian, the set of all complex subspaces of dimension
s
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. We often identify
{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
with a matrix
{\boldsymbol{W}}
that has orthonormal columns, and vice versa. Although not immediately clear in its present form, spectral estimation is closely related to the problem of finding special subspaces in
\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
that we define below.

Definition 5.1
.

We say
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is a
Fourier subspace
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
if for some
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{U}}={\rm range}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}))
. In this case, we say
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is generated by
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\dim({\boldsymbol{U}})=|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
. We let
\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
denote the collection of all
s
dimensional Fourier subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

The starting point is, given a subspace
{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
, how do we tell if
{\boldsymbol{W}}
is actually a Fourier subspace? In an abstract sense, the main idea of classical MUSIC is to test
{\boldsymbol{W}}
against a special family of vectors and see how
{\boldsymbol{W}}
reacts. Recall
Definition

2.6
, which importantly defines the landscape function associated to
any
arbitrary subspace. Next, for clarity, we restate
Proposition

2.2
in a more general form.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
m>s
and
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
be the landscape function associated with a Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
. We have
q(t)=0
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
if and only if
t\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Provided that
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is a Fourier subspace, the landscape function
q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
has exactly
s
unique roots
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and each one is a double root. The roots
{\boldsymbol{x}}
uniquely specifies the subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. If
{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is just an arbitrary subspace, the conclusions of
Proposition

5.1
do not hold for
q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
. Let us briefly summarize some basic properties of landscape functions. Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{m}
denotes the space of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most
m-1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
m>s
and
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
be the landscape function associated with a subspace
{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
. For all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, it holds that
0\leq q(t)\leq 1
. Furthermore,
q
is not identically zero,
q\in\mathcal{T}_{m}
, and admits a polynomial sum-of-squares representation. For each integer
\ell\geq 0
, we have

\|q^{(\ell)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq m^{\ell}.

(5.1)

The proof of this lemma is in
Section

11.2
. Let us study the landscape functions in greater detail. Define the sets

\mathcal{F}_{m,s}:=\left\{q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\colon{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{U}_{m,s}:=\left\{q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}\colon{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}\right\}.

Of course, we have
\mathcal{F}_{m,s}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{m,s}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{m}
. The inclusion
\mathcal{F}_{m,s}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{m,s}
is strict because
\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
is a strict subset of
\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
when
m>s
. The inclusion
\mathcal{U}_{m,s}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{m}
is also strict, since
Lemma

5.2
tells us that each
q_{\boldsymbol{W}}
is necessarily a polynomial sum-of-squares, and not every trigonometric polynomial can be written this way.

More abstractly we can think of
q
as the map
q\colon\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}\to\mathcal{U}_{m,s}
by
{\boldsymbol{W}}\mapsto q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
. We present a consequence, which is proved
Section

11.3
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
m>s
. There is a bijection between the following three families.

(a)

All subsets of
\mathbb{T}
of cardinality
s
.

(b)

\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
, the collection of Fourier subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
of dimension
s
.

(c)

\mathcal{F}_{m,s}
, the set of landscape functions associated with
\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
.

As a consequence of
Lemma

5.3
, we write
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
to reference this bijection between the set
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and its generated Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. We include
m
in the subscript of
\simeq_{m}
since this is only a bijection once we have specified
m
, the number of rows of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
.

Recall the definition of the sine-theta distance in (
1.4
). The following definition now quantifies what we mean by a good approximation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
to the Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
generated by a particular
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Given a Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
identified with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and an arbitrary
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
, we call
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}):=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{U}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
the (induced)
subspace error
between
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
.

We briefly summarize our new perspective on classical MUSIC. We have three equivalent ways of viewing this algorithm. The original context of finding
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. This is equivalent to finding its unique Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
that is generated by
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. On the other hand, we can instead study
q_{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathcal{F}_{m,s}
.

From this point of view, we see that the main idea behind classical MUSIC is to study the properties of
q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
, where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is a perturbation of
{\boldsymbol{U}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{x}}
, in order to gain some insight into
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. However, to carry out this plan, we need to study the stability (i.e., metric) properties of the map
q\colon\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}\to\mathcal{U}_{m,s}
. Naturally,
\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is a metric space when equipped with the sine-theta distance. We can equip
\mathcal{T}_{m}
with the
C^{k}(\mathbb{T})
norm for any
k\geq 0
. We have the following lemma, which is proved in
Section

11.4
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
m>s
. Let
q_{{\boldsymbol{V}}}
and
q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
be the landscape function associated with
{\boldsymbol{V}},{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{V}},{\boldsymbol{W}})
. For each integer
\ell\geq 0
, we have

\big\|q_{{\boldsymbol{V}}}^{(\ell)}-q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}^{(\ell)}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\vartheta m^{\ell}.

(5.2)

Thus, the map
q\colon\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}\to\mathcal{U}_{m,s}
is a continuous map from one metric space to another. Indeed, we see that

\|q_{{\boldsymbol{V}}}-q_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}\|_{C^{k}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\vartheta({\boldsymbol{V}},{\boldsymbol{W}})(1+m+\cdots+m^{k}).

{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
are mapped to their associated landscape functions
q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\mathcal{F}_{m,s}
and
q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\mathcal{U}_{m,s}
.

In both classical MUSIC and the full pipeline form of Gradient-MUSIC (
Algorithm

2
), the subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is the Toeplitz estimator, see
Definition

2.7
.

5.2
Sparsity detection and the Toeplitz estimator

We turn our attention to the the estimation of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
from just the data
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
, which were assumed to be known in both
Algorithms

1
and
2
. If
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
is a sufficiently small perturbation of the low rank matrix
{\boldsymbol{T}}
, a natural strategy is to count how many singular values of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
exceed a certain threshold. We introduce the following definition.

Definition 5.3
.

For any
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, let
s=|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
and define the
Toeplitz subspace error

\rho:=\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}):=\frac{2\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}}{\sigma_{s}(T({\boldsymbol{y}}))}.

A few elementary properties of
\rho
are summarized in the following lemma, which is proved in
Section

11.6
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
m>s
. For any
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, set
\rho:=\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})
. If
\rho\leq 1-1/\sqrt{2}
, then
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
has a unique leading
s
dimensional left singular space and the Toeplitz estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
is well defined. Additionally,
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{U}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq\rho
.

Remark 5.4
.

There are some subtle differences between
\vartheta
and
\rho
. While
Lemma

5.5
shows that
\vartheta\leq\rho
whenever
\rho
is sufficiently small, the reverse inequality does not hold in general because it is possible that
\vartheta=0
yet
\rho\not=0
. For instance, choose a nonzero
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
that perturbs the singular values of
{\boldsymbol{T}}
but not the subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. Then we have
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}
,
\vartheta=0
, and
\rho\not=0
.

It is not difficult to prove that if
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})<1
, then

|{\boldsymbol{x}}|=\max\Big\{k\colon\sigma_{k}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})>\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}\Big\}.

(5.3)

A deficiency of this threshold procedure is that
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}
is unknown and behaves very differently depending on the properties of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, as will be seen shortly. Replacing
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}
on the right hand side of (
5.3
) with an under- or over- estimate of may lead to a wrong value for the number of frequencies, which may be catastrophic. The following lemma provides an alternative threshold strategy and is proved in
Section

11.7
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
,
\beta>1
, and
0<r_{0}\leq r_{1}
. For any
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}(2\pi\beta/m,r_{0},r_{1})
and perturbation
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
such that
\rho:=\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\leq 1-1/\sqrt{2}
, we have

\frac{\sigma_{s}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}{\sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}\geq\frac{7}{10}\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}\frac{\beta-1}{\beta+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\sigma_{s+1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}{\sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}\leq\frac{\rho}{2-\rho}.

In particular, if

\rho\leq\min\left\{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\,\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}\frac{\beta-1}{\beta+1}\right\},\quad\text{then}\quad|{\boldsymbol{x}}|=\max\left\{k\colon\frac{\sigma_{k}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}{\sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}})}\geq\frac{7}{10}\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}\frac{\beta-1}{\beta+1}\right\}.

The point of this lemma is that the threshold parameter only depends on the signal class parameters and are chosen without knowledge of
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}
or
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})
. We do not need extremely accurate estimates for
\beta
or
r_{0}/r_{1}
to get the correct value for
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
. The penalty of using a loose estimate for either parameter is met by a stronger prior assumption on
\rho
. This threshold procedure is used in the first step of the full pipeline version of Gradient-MUSIC (
Algorithm

3
).

Finally, we need to upper bound the Toeplitz subspace error
\rho
which involves computing
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}
under various assumptions on
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. The following lemmas are proved in
Section

11.8
and
Section

11.9
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
, we have
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}\leq 2m^{1-1/p}\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2m-1)\times(2m-1)}
be a diagonal matrix with positive diagonals. If
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, then the random matrix
T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle\sqrt{2{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(2m)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}\geq t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2me^{-t^{2}/(2{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

Both lemmas are standard and may have already been discovered. Earlier analysis
[
26
]
proved
Lemma

5.7
for
p=2
, which then implies the conclusion for all
p\in[2,\infty]
through Hölder’s inequality. The only new content of the lemma is for
p\in[1,2)
, which is done by using Young’s convolution inequality. As for
Lemma

5.8
, the i.i.d. Gaussian case was also established in
[
26
]
, and here we have a more general version that holds for diagonal covariance matrix. The proof relies on a standard matrix concentration argument
[
43
, Theorem 4.1.1]
.

5.3
Landscape analysis

Our overall goal is to use Gradient-MUSIC (
Algorithm

2
) to find the
s
smallest local minima of
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
, where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is a sufficiently small perturbation of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. In order for this approach to succeed, we need to show that such minima exist and they well approximate
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and we need enough information about the landscape function
\widetilde{q}
to get suitable control over gradient descent dynamics. This is accomplished by the following theorem.

Theorem 5.9

(Landscape analysis)
.

Let
m\geq 100
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 8\pi/m
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
, the landscape function
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
associated with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
has at least
s
critical points
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\{\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{s}\}
such that the following hold for each
j\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.

(a)

\displaystyle|\widetilde{x}_{j}-x_{j}|\leq\frac{7\vartheta}{m}.

(b)

0.0271\,m^{2}\leq\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq 0.269\,m^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{\pi}{3m}\right]
.

(c)

In particular,
\widetilde{x}_{j}
is local minimum of
\widetilde{q}
and

\widetilde{q}(\widetilde{x}_{j})\leq\widetilde{q}(x_{j})\leq\vartheta^{2}\leq 10^{-4}.

(d)

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)\geq+0.0306\,m\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{4\pi}{3m}\right],\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)\leq-0.0306\,m\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{4\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{\pi}{3m}\right].

(e)

\widetilde{q}(t)\geq 0.529
for all
\displaystyle t\not\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{4\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{4\pi}{3m}\right].

(f)

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)\leq+0.292\,m^{2}|t-\widetilde{x}_{j}|\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{4\pi}{3m}\right],\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)\geq-0.292\,m^{2}|t-\widetilde{x}_{j}|\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{4\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{\pi}{3m}\right].

Theorem

5.9
is proved in
Section

10.3
. Parts (a) and (c) tell us that
\widetilde{q}
has small local minima
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
that are close to
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Part (b) tells us
\widetilde{q}
is convex in a
\pi/(3m)
neighborhood of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
. While it is not necessarily convex in a larger region, parts (d) and (f) tell us that
\widetilde{q}
is strictly decreasing on
[\widetilde{x}_{j}-4\pi/(3m),\widetilde{x}_{j})
and strictly increasing on (
\widetilde{x}_{j},\widetilde{x}_{j}+4\pi/(3m)]
. While it is possible that
\widetilde{q}
has other local minima, they are necessarily at least
4\pi/(3m)
away
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
and part (e) implies that
\widetilde{q}
is large. This explains the behavior seen in Figure
1
.

Remark 5.5
.

Theorem

5.9
is purely an approximation result because
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is an arbitrary subspace and we do not assume it is produced by any particular algorithm. While we will primarily use the theorem when
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is the Toeplitz estimator described in
Section

5.2
, there are several reasons why we could potentially be interested in other
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
, which we discuss in
Section

8.2
.

The landscape analysis is the primary technical contribution of this paper. The lemmas that are used to prove
Theorem

5.9
are stated with greater generality than the theorem itself.
Section

9
is a self contained section that includes all lemmas used in the landscape analysis and their proofs.

It may be helpful to briefly explain how
Theorem

5.9
is proved and why it holds. For simplicity, we concentrate our discussion on the properties of
q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
. A key observation is that for any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have

q(t)=1-f_{m}(t-x_{j})+p_{j}(t),

(5.4)

where
p_{j}(t)
is a polynomial that acts as an error term and
f_{m}
is a normalized
Fejér kernel
,

f_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\frac{\sin(mt/2)}{\sin(t/2)}\right)^{2}.

(5.5)

Provided that the influence of
p_{j}
is negligible for
t
near
x_{j}
, this implies that
q(t)
(and also
\widetilde{q}
through some additional analysis) behaves like
1-f_{m}(t-x_{j})
. This explains why the graph of
\widetilde{q}
in Figure
2
(b) looks strikingly similar to a shift of the graph of
1-f_{m}
.

The primary difficultly with using formula (
5.4
) is that
f_{m}
and
p_{j}
both have the same scaling in
m
, e.g., their
\ell
-th derivative scales as
m^{\ell}
for all
\ell\geq 0
. In order to effectively use this approximation, one must show that the constants in
f_{m}^{(\ell)}
dominate those that appear in
p_{j}^{(\ell)}
. Note that
f_{m}
is a fixed function while
p_{j}
depends on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, so a loose upper bound for
p_{j}
may depend on
s
. We went through great care to control
p_{j}
and its derivatives by explicit quantities that do not depend on
s
, otherwise we cannot utilize formula (
5.4
) without placing additional unnatural assumptions. This is done by arguing that
p_{j}
can be decomposed into
s-1
almost orthogonal functions.

Remark 5.6

(Sharpness of
Theorem

5.9
)
.

Identity (
5.4
) is natural since
p_{j}=0
for the special case where
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is just a singleton
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{1}\}
, and so
q(t)=1-f_{m}(t-x_{1})
. This immediately implies that several features of
Theorem

5.9
are sharp. Indeed, the convexity estimate in part (a) and the derivative estimates in parts (d) and (f) can only hold in a
2\pi c_{1}/m
and
2\pi c_{2}/m
neighborhood of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
, for some universal
0<c_{1}<c_{2}<1
. Hence,
Theorem

5.9
shows that the graph of
\widetilde{q}
has wells that are maximally wide. Finally,
|\widetilde{q}\,^{(\ell)}|
must scale like
m^{\ell}
, which is what we obtained in the theorem.

Variations of
Theorem

5.9
can be obtained by following its proof and using the lemmas for a different set of parameters. A script that calculates the resulting constants for variable parameter choices are included with the software accompanying this paper. We mention two regimes of interest.

Remark 5.7
.

The constant in
Theorem

5.9
that we optimized for is the “4” in condition
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi(4)/m
. Had we instead assumed that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
for some
\beta>0
, then our proof techniques break down when
\beta\approx 3.45
, even when
\vartheta=0
. This is expected since the conclusions of
Theorem

5.9
do not hold when
m\Delta\ll 2\pi
, even if
\vartheta=0
, so our proof argument has to yield vacuous statements for small enough
\beta>1
.

Remark 5.8
.

There is an opposite extreme that is also relevant. Say
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is fixed while
m\to\infty
, which is the usual set up of statistical problems. In this case, we can pick an arbitrarily large
\beta
and assume
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, since this condition will eventually be satisfied for some
m
. All of the numerical constants in
Theorem

5.9
improve as
\beta
is made larger and our sufficient condition on
\vartheta
relaxes. For example, if
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi(20)/m
, then
\vartheta\leq 0.06
suffices to get non-vacuous conclusions.

5.4
Performance guarantees for Gradient-MUSIC

We next analyze the Gradient-MUSIC algorithm (
Algorithm

2
). In expository sections, we assume that the assumptions of
Theorem

5.9
hold and that
\widetilde{q}=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
for an arbitrary
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
such that
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
. Recall the notation and definitions introduced in
Section

3
.

Notice that
Theorem

5.9
part (b) and (d) suggest that gradient descent (with appropriate parameters) produces iterates that converge to
\widetilde{x}_{j}
whenever the initial guess lies in the interval,

B_{j}:=\left[\widetilde{x}_{j}-\frac{4\pi}{3m},\,\widetilde{x}_{j}+\frac{4\pi}{3m}\right].

(5.6)

We will prove convergence later, but for now, we concentrate on finding a suitable initialization in each
B_{j}
. To do this,
Theorem

5.9
part (e) tells us that
\widetilde{q}
is large away from
B_{j}
. We just need to evaluate
\widetilde{q}
on a reasonable finite
G\subseteq\mathbb{T}
. We want a coarse
G
in order to reduce computational costs, but simultaneously, not too coarse otherwise our strategy may fail to find suitable initialization. Recall the definitions of accepted set (
Definition

3.1
) and cluster (
Definition

3.2
).

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose the assumptions of
Theorem

5.9
hold. For any
\alpha\in(\vartheta^{2},0.529]
and finite
G\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
{\rm mesh}(G)<(\alpha-\vartheta^{2})/m
, the following hold.

(a)

A\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}B_{j}
.

(b)

A\cap B_{j}\not=\emptyset
for each
j
.

(c)

Each
A_{j}:=A\cap B_{j}
is a cluster in
G
.

This lemma is proved in
Section

11.5
. According to this lemma, the accepted elements
A
can be written as a disjoint union of exactly
s
clusters. We only need to run gradient descent with one initialization per
A_{j}
, so pick any
t_{j,0}\in A_{j}
, which we call a
representative
. It does not matter which representative is selected per
A_{j}
, but a reasonable heuristic is to pick a representative that is in the middle of
A_{j}
. This explains the behavior seen in Figure
2
.

To show that our method succeeds, it remains to determine a suitable step size
h
for gradient descent and prove that the iterates converge to
\widetilde{x}_{j}
. Convergence does not immediately follow because
\widetilde{q}
is only provably convex in a strict subset of
B_{j}
, see
Theorem

5.9
part (b), and gradient descent does not know if the current iterate lies in a region of convexity or not. Regardless, we will overcome this technical issue by using lower and upper bounds for
\widetilde{q}\,
given in
Theorem

5.9
part (d) and (f).

We will let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
be the output of Gradient-MUSIC. There are two types of errors that contribute to the frequencies error
|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|
. By triangle inequality, we have

|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|\leq|x_{j}-\widetilde{x}_{j}|+|\widetilde{x}_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|.

The first term
|x_{j}-\widetilde{x}_{j}|
is a fundamental approximation error that comes from the landscape function itself, which is controlled by
Theorem

5.9
. Gradient-MUSIC approximates each tilde
\widetilde{x}_{j}
by gradient descent with suitable initialization. We can interpret the second term
|\widetilde{x}_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|
as the
optimization error
.

The following theorem provides a performance guarantee for Gradient-MUSIC and explicit parameters to use. This makes it a user friendly and direct algorithm.

Theorem 5.11

(Gradient-MUSIC performance guarantee)
.

Let
m\geq 100
,
\alpha=0.529
,
G\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be finite such that
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 1/(2m)
,
h=6/m^{2}
, and
n\geq 31
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 8\pi/m
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
, Gradient-MUSIC (
Algorithm

2
) outputs
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that the frequency error satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7\vartheta}{m}+\frac{77\pi\,(0.839)^{n}}{m}.

Theorem

5.11
is proved in
Section

10.4
. The frequency error
|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|
consists of two terms, where
7\vartheta/m
comes from the landscape analysis and
77\pi\,(0.839)^{n}/m
is the optimization error.

If
\vartheta=0
, then
\widetilde{q}=q
and
\widetilde{x}_{j}=x_{j}
for each
j
. The optimization error dominates and we can calculate
x_{j}
to any desired precision by tuning the number of iterations. When
\vartheta\not=0
, then we ideally pick
n
sufficiently large so that the two errors are balanced. We see that
77\pi\,(0.839)^{n}\lesssim\vartheta
whenever
n\gtrsim\log(1/\vartheta)
, so the number of iterations is typically not large. It may seem strange that
n\gtrsim\log(1/\vartheta)
grows as
\vartheta
decreases. This is unavoidable since if
\vartheta
is small (e.g., due small noise), then
7\vartheta/m
is small, so we need to increase the number of gradient iterations to so that the optimization matches the perturbation error. If
n
is not big enough, then the optimization error dominates.

Remark 5.9
.

Theorem

5.11
does not make any assumptions on what
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is, only that we start with a
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{U}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
. To see why this generality may be helpful, see the discussion in
Remark

5.5
.

Remark 5.10
.

There are other valid choices for threshold parameter
\alpha
and step size
h
, which can be extracted from the proof of
Theorem

5.11
. Other choices will result in different requirements on
{\rm mesh}(G)
and number of gradient iterations
n
, and will lead to different numerical constants.

Remark 5.11

(Necessity of
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 1/(2m)
)
.

One may ask if it is possible to weaken the assumption that
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 1/(2m)
in
Theorem

5.11
. Without additional assumptions or prior information about
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, it is not possible to make
m\,{\rm mesh}(G)\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
. This can be seen by considering the case where
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{1}\}
is a singleton and
\vartheta=0
. Then
\widetilde{q}(t)=q(t)=1-f_{m}(t-x_{1})
, see
Remark

5.6
. The interval around
x_{1}
for which gradient iterations converge to
x_{1}
cannot be larger than
[x_{1}-2\pi/m,x_{1}+2\pi/m]
. Sampling and thresholding
\widetilde{q}
on
G
can potentially miss this interval if
m\,{\rm mesh}(G)\to\infty
. This reasoning also illustrates that even if
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
for larger
\beta
, we still need
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 4\pi/m
regardless of
\beta
.

Remark 5.12

(Technical comparison between MUSIC and ESPRIT)
.

Theorem

5.11
shows that provided the assumptions there hold, then Gradient-MUSIC produces
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that
\max_{j}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|\lesssim\vartheta/m
. Importantly, this bound holds regardless of what
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is or which computational method is used to produce
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
as long as
\vartheta\leq 0.01
.

It was shown in
[
21
]
that ESPRIT produces
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that
\max_{j}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|\lesssim_{s}\vartheta
under some technical assumptions. We do not know if this can be strengthened to
\max_{j}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|\lesssim\vartheta/m
for any
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{U}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
is small enough.

Let us return to a more concrete discussion. The paper
[
10
]
showed that for i.i.d. subgaussian noise
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
that ESPRIT achieves frequency error at most
C\sigma/m^{3/2}
, ignoring
\log(m)
factors. This is done through a refined eigenvector analysis that relies on the i.i.d. subgaussian noise assumption. It is unclear whether their proof technique can be generalized. On the other hand,
Theorem

4.2
showed that Gradient-MUSIC achieves frequency error at most
C\sigma\sqrt{\log(m)}/m^{3/2}
for i.i.d. gaussian noise (see also
Remark

4.3
). This was done by first showing that
\vartheta\lesssim C\sigma\sqrt{\log(m)}/\sqrt{m}
for the Toeplitz estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
and then using the general bound given by
Theorem

5.11
. One advantage of our proof structure is its generality, which allows us to handle deterministic and more general stochastic perturbations in a unified manner.

The proof techniques in this paper and
[
10
]
are considerably different and one set of results do not imply the other.

5.5
Amplitude estimation

In this section, we study the amplitude recovery problem where we are given estimated frequencies
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
produced by some method (not necessarily Gradient-MUSIC) and we would like to estimate the amplitudes
{\boldsymbol{a}}
. We study a
quadratic method
, which produces

\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}={\rm diag}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\big),\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}\big)^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}:={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(5.7)

This method used in the third step of
Algorithm

3
.

The quadratic recovery method also respects the permutation invariance of the spectral estimation problem. If a permuted set
\pi(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}})
is used in formula (
5.7
) instead of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, then we would get
\pi(\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}})
in place of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}
. Hence, there is a non-ambiguous pairing between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}
, which allows us to use the notation

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}):=\big\{(\widehat{x}_{j},\widehat{a}_{j})\big\}_{j=1}^{s}.

Consequently, without loss of generality, we assume that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
has been sorted to best best match
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and this fixes a particular ordering on
\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

To see why
\widehat{\boldsymbol{a}}
is a reasonable method of approximation, recall factorization (
2.2
) and so

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}=\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\big){\boldsymbol{A}}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\big)^{*}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}\big)^{*}.

(5.8)

Provided that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a good approximation of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}
is sufficiently small, then we expect that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
approximates
{\boldsymbol{I}}
and for the diagonal entries of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}
to approximate that of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. Rigorous analysis is carried out in the following lemma whose proof is located in
Section

11.10
.

Lemma 5.12
.

For
m\geq s
and
\beta>2
, suppose
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
are both sets of cardinality
s
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\pi\alpha/m
for some
\alpha\leq\beta/4
. Then

\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|a_{j}-\widehat{a}_{j}|\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\beta+2}{\beta-2}}\right)\frac{\beta+2}{\beta-2}\,a_{\max}\,m\,\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|+\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\frac{\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}}{m}.

A different strategy is to approximate the amplitudes
{\boldsymbol{a}}
using a (linear) least squares reconstruction method, i.e. given
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, define

{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\sharp}:=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(2m-1,\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|_{2}.

(5.9)

Prior analysis of least squares for related spectral estimation problems can be found in
[
30
,
21
]
.

6
Comparison to classical MUSIC

In this section, we show our analysis can be applied to the classical MUSIC algorithm, which is summarized in
Algorithm

1
. At this point, we should clarify what we mean by discrete local minima.

Definition 6.1
.

Given any finite subset
G
of
\mathbb{T}
, we say
u\in G
is a
(strict) discrete local minimum
of
f
on
G
if
f(u)<f(u_{+})
and
f(u)<f(u_{-})
, where
u_{-},u_{+}\in G
are the left and right nearest neighbors of
u
on the discrete set
G
, respectively.

In particular, if
f
is strictly convex in an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and
I\cap G
has at least three elements, then
f
has exactly one discrete local minimum in
I\cap G
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
denote the output of classical MUSIC. There are two types of errors that contribute to the frequency error
|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|
. By triangle inequality,

|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|\leq|x_{j}-\widetilde{x}_{j}|+|\widetilde{x}_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|.

The first error
|x_{j}-\widetilde{x}_{j}|
is from the landscape function, which is controlled by
Theorem

5.9
. The classical MUSIC algorithm approximates each
\widetilde{x}_{j}
by searching for the local minima of
\widetilde{q}
on a discrete set
G
. This process introduces the second term
|\widetilde{x}_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|
, which we call the
discretization error
. Not surprisingly, we will see that it is related to
{\rm mesh}(G)
. The following theorem is proved in
Section

10.5
.

Theorem 6.1

(General performance guarantee for MUSIC)
.

Let
m\geq 100
and
G\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 2\pi/(3m)
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 8\pi/m
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
, classical MUSIC (
Algorithm

1
) outputs
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that the frequency error satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7\vartheta}{m}+{\rm mesh}(G).

Theorem

6.1
does not make any assumptions on how
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is found, which makes it a purely computational approximation result. The frequency error has two terms, where
7\vartheta/m
comes from the landscape analysis and
{\rm mesh}(G)
term upper bounds the discretization error.

If
\vartheta=0
, then
{\rm mesh}(G)
dominates. We should pick
G
depending on a preset tolerance parameter, e.g.,
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 10^{-6}/m
. In most applications where
\vartheta\not=0
, to balance the two errors, we should set
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq C\vartheta/m
for some
C>0
that does not depend on
m
. Then the frequency error is at most
C\vartheta/m
.

Remark 6.2
.

Since classical MUSIC finds
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
through a search on
G
, without additional information about
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
, the discretization error can be as large as
{\rm mesh}(G)
. To get the best possible upper bound provided by
Theorem

6.1
, the requirement
{\rm mesh}(G)\asymp\vartheta/m
cannot be relaxed. This is the coarsest grid that is allowed by classical MUSIC to get the optimal frequency error bound.

Using
Theorem

6.1
, we can provide the analogue of
Algorithm

3
,
Theorem

4.1
, and
Theorem

4.2
, but with classical MUSIC instead. For these theorems, assumptions and conclusions are almost identical except that for
\ell^{p}
noise, we need

{\rm mesh}(G)\lesssim\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}}{a_{\min}\,m^{1+1/p}},

while for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, we need

{\rm mesh}(G)\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(m)}}{a_{\min}\,m^{2}}.

Let us compare
Theorem

5.11
for Gradient-MUSIC and
Theorem

6.1
for classical MUSIC. They have the same assumptions on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\vartheta
, and
m
, but the main difference is their assumptions on
G
.

(a)

For Gradient-MUSIC, to access the best bound provided by
Theorem

5.11
, we only need
{\rm mesh}(G)\leq 1/(2m)
independent of
\vartheta
. The most natural and simplest choice is a uniform grid of width
1/(2m)
, so that
|G|\asymp m
. In view of
Remark

5.11
, we cannot choose a coarser
G
without additional information about
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and/or sacrificing optimality.

(b)

For classical MUSIC algorithm, to access the best bound provided by
Theorem

6.1
, we need
{\rm mesh}(G)\lesssim\vartheta/m
. Again choosing a uniform grid, we have
|G|\asymp m\vartheta^{-1}
. In view of
Remark

6.2
, we cannot choose a coarser
G
without additional information about
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and/or sacrificing optimality.

This justifies why we refer to
G
as a “thin” grid for classical MUSIC and a “coarse” grid for Gradient-MUSIC. Evaluation of
\widetilde{q}
at a single input
t\in\mathbb{T}
is not cheap. It requires
O(ms)
floating point operations due to the formula,

\widetilde{q}(t)=1-\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\right\|^{2}_{2}.

This makes classical MUSIC expensive.

While Gradient-MUSIC saves significantly on evaluation of
\widetilde{q}
, it comes with the trade-off that it requires
n\asymp\log(1/\vartheta)
iterations of gradient descent for each
x_{j}
. It turns out that this trade-off is always more favorable for Gradient-MUSIC. Indeed, evaluation of
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}
also has complexity
O(ms)
because

\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)=2\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\right).

Thus, the total complexity for using
n\asymp\log(1/\vartheta)
gradient iterations to find
s
local minima is
O(ms\log(1/\vartheta))
. From here, we see that to get the best results,

(a)

Gradient-MUSIC (
Algorithm

2
) has computational complexity

O\left(m^{2}s+ms^{2}\log(1/\vartheta)\right).

(b)

Classical MUSIC (
Algorithm

1
) has computational complexity
O(m^{2}s\vartheta^{-1})
.

Thus, we see that Gradient-MUSIC is always more efficient than classical MUSIC. This gap widens as
\vartheta
decreases, which appears in various instances. For example, for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim N({\boldsymbol{0}},\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}})
or uniformly bounded
\ell^{2}
noise, we have
\vartheta\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\log(m)}/\sqrt{m}
and
\vartheta\lesssim\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{m}
, respectively.

7.1
Gradient descent termination condition

We wrote our main theorems for Gradient-MUSIC (
Theorems

5.11
,
4.1
and
4.2
) in terms of the number of gradient iterations
n
since that is clearer from a conceptual point of view. In practice, one usually terminates gradient descent once the number of iterations has exceed some preset number (e.g., 300 or 500) or a termination condition has been reached. A standard termination condition is to stop gradient descent at iteration
n
once
|\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t_{j,n})|\leq\varepsilon m
for some selected parameter
\varepsilon
. We include
m
in this condition because
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}
naturally scales in
m
, so
\varepsilon
is a dimensionless constant.

We found this condition effective to use in practice, and Gradient-MUSIC with this termination condition is summarized in
Algorithm

4
. First, it will always terminate for big enough
n
since
\widetilde{x}_{j}
is a local minimum so
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(\widetilde{x}_{j})=0
. Second, this condition is rigorously justified by slightly modifying our theory, as shown in the following result.

Extensions of the theory

8.1
Improvement for real amplitudes

Suppose the amplitudes
{\boldsymbol{a}}
of
h
in (
1.1
) are real, instead of complex. In this section only, assume that the samples are

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{y}}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\quad\text{where}\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}=(h(k))_{k=0,\dots,2m-1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m}.

(8.1)

We index the samples by
\{0,\dots,2m-1\}
instead of
\{-m+1,\dots,m-1\}
since the former is more natural for real amplitudes. We assume we are given
2m
measurements instead of
2m-1
to make some resulting expressions cleaner, but of course an additional sample makes no fundamental difference.

Since
{\boldsymbol{a}}
are real, the Fourier transform has natural symmetry so that
h(-k)=\overline{h(k)}
. Thus, we extend
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
to a
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{4m-1}
such that
\zeta_{-k}=\overline{\eta_{k}}
and
\zeta_{k}=\eta_{k}
for each
k=0,\dots,2m-1
. Then we have the new model equation,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{y}}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\quad\text{where}\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}=(h(k))_{k=-2m+1,\dots,2m-1}.

This is now the same model as (
8.1
) expect we have turned
2m
noisy measurements to
4m-1
many. To process
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
in the same manner as before, we form the Toeplitz matrix

T(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{y}_{0}&\widetilde{y}_{-1}&\cdots&\widetilde{y}_{-2m+1}\\
\widetilde{y}_{1}&\widetilde{y}_{0}&&\widetilde{y}_{-2m+1}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\widetilde{y}_{2m-1}&\widetilde{y}_{2m-2}&\cdots&\widetilde{y}_{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m\times 2m}.

We define
T({\boldsymbol{y}}),T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\in\mathbb{C}^{2m\times 2m}
in the same manner. Then
T({\boldsymbol{y}})
enjoys factorization (
2.2
) except
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}})
is replaced with
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(2m,{\boldsymbol{x}})
. So the same setup carries over except we essentially doubled the number of measurements for free by exploiting that
{\boldsymbol{a}}
is real.

We are now ready to apply the same machinery. Notice that
\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\|_{p}^{p}\leq 2\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}^{p}
, so this reflection only increases the
\ell^{p}
norm by a multiplicative factor of
2^{1/p}\leq 2
. Consequently
Lemma

5.7
provides us with the bound

\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{p}\leq 2(2m)^{1-1/p}\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\|_{p}\leq 8m^{1-1/p}\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}.

If
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, then we cannot ensure that the entries of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
are independent. However, we do not need this to be case, since our theory just requires an upper bound for
\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{2}
. The following provides a suitable adaptation of
Lemma

5.8
, which is proved in
Section

11.11
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2m-1)\times(2m-1)}
be a diagonal matrix with positive diagonals. If
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{4m-1}
is defined as
\zeta_{-k}=\overline{\eta_{k}}
and
\zeta_{k}=\eta_{k}
for each
k\geq 0
, then the random matrix
T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\in\mathbb{C}^{2m\times 2m}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle\sqrt{8{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(4m)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{2}\geq t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 8me^{-t^{2}/(8{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

With these ingredients at hand, all of our theorems generalize to model (
8.1
), with possibly different constants. The main improvement we would like to highlight is that, instead of assuming
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 8\pi/m
or
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}(8\pi/m,r_{0},10r_{0})
, they can be relaxed to
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 4\pi/m
or
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{S}(4\pi/m,r_{0},10r_{0})
respectively, since we have effectively doubled the number of measurements without actually acquiring more.

8.2
Different subspace estimators, beyond Toeplitz matrices

Recall that the results in
Section

5
are approximation results that do not make any assumptions on how a
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is obtained, only that it is a reasonably good approximation to some true Fourier subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. This generality has several useful advantages, and we discuss three that we believe are immediately relevant.

One advantage is compatibility with randomized numerical linear algebra techniques. For example, we do not necessarily have to use the Toeplitz estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
in the first place. It is computationally and memory expensive to compute, since one needs to store the large Toeplitz matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
in memory and calculate its leading
s
dimensional left singular space. We can instead use a randomized method
[
16
]
, which trades some precision for speed.

A basic illustration of this is to draw a random matrix
{\boldsymbol{G}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times k}
where
k\ll m
and the entries of
{\boldsymbol{G}}
are iid complex normal random variables. The matrix product
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{G}}
requires
O(m^{2}k)
operations, but
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{G}}
requires less memory to store compared to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
. A QR factorization of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{G}}
gives rise its column space
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times k}
. We then compute
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{T}}
and its leading
s
dimensional left singular space
{\boldsymbol{B}}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times s}
.
The new estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}
for
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is
{\boldsymbol{Q}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
, which is not necessarily the same as the Toeplitz estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
. Bounding the sine-theta distance between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, we get a sufficient condition on the noise
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
such that
\vartheta(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},{\boldsymbol{U}})\leq 0.01
with high probability. Then the rest of the framework goes through except the first step of
Algorithm

3
uses this random estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}
instead.

A second advantage is that we can forgo the Toeplitz estimator and its variations altogether. It would be surprising if Toeplitz estimator is optimal for all types of deterministic and stochastic noise. For example, deterministic
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
with some algebraic relationship between its entries, or
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\not=\bf 0
and non-diagonal
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. Our framework allows for any approximation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, provided they are close enough.

9.1

Several basic properties of the landscape function were proved in

. The general setup of this section is that
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
for a

\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
for large enough
\beta>1
, while

, where

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is an arbitrary subspace that is close enough to

Our main goal is to answer two questions. In addition to the properties listed in

, what other ones does

9.2
Energy estimates for Dirichlet kernels

Recall the index set
I(m)
defined in (
1.3
). We define a normalized and modified Dirichlet kernel,

d_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in I(m)}e^{ikt}=e^{-i(m-1)/2}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{ikt}=\frac{1}{m}\frac{\sin(mt/2)}{\sin(t/2)}.

(9.1)

Note the right hand side has a removable discontinuity at
t=0
. We have
d_{m}(0)=1
and
\|d_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1
. Recall that
f_{m}
denotes the normalized Fejér kernel, defined in (
5.5
). We have

f_{m}(t)=(d_{m}(t))^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\frac{\sin(mt/2)}{\sin(t/2)}\right)^{2}.

The Dirichlet and Fejér kernels (and related functions) naturally appear in our analysis because

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(k)}(u)^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(\ell)}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in I(m)}(-ij)^{k}(ij)^{\ell}e^{ij(t-u)}=(-1)^{k}d_{m}^{(k+\ell)}(t-u).

(9.2)

One particular consequence of this is that for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, we have

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)=d_{m}^{\prime}(0)=0.

(9.3)

We will derive several estimates. As usual
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a finite set that satisfies certain separation conditions, and for each
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
, we would like suitable control over a discrete energy term,

\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}\big|d_{m}^{(\ell)}(x)\big|^{2}.

Using the trivial upper bound
\|d_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1
and Bernstein’s inequality, we see that this quantity is upper bounded by
m^{2\ell}s
. The
m^{2\ell}
term is a natural scaling factor, but the
s
dependence makes this trivial estimate unsuitable for the purposes of this paper. To obtain a refined estimate, we start with the following abstract lemma.

Lemma 9.1
.

Suppose for some
a,b>0
, the set
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
has cardinality
s
,
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq b
, and
|x|\geq a
for all
x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
. For any extended real valued function
h
that is non-negative, even, and non-increasing away from zero, we have

\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}h(x)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor s/2\rfloor-1}h(a+bj)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil s/2\rceil-1}h(-a-bj)

Proof.

For convenience, define the energy of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
as

E({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}h(x).

To prove the lemma, we show there is a sequence of transformations of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
that do not reduce the energy and terminates after transforming
{\boldsymbol{x}}
to

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}:=\left\{a,a+b,\dots,a+(\lfloor\tfrac{s}{2}\rfloor-1)b\right\}\cup\left\{-a,a-b,\dots,a-(\lceil\tfrac{s}{2}\rceil-1)b\right\}.

Since
h
is non-negative, even, and non-increasing away from zero, we see that
E
does not decrease if any element of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is shifted closer to zero while the other ones are fixed. We shift the smallest positive element (which is assumed to be at least
a
) of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
to
a
, then the next smallest positive element (which is necessarily at least
a+b
due to the separation condition) to
a+b
, etc. We do the same for negative elements of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Letting
s_{+}
and
s_{-}
be the number of elements in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
that are positive and negative respectively, this process transforms
{\boldsymbol{x}}
to the set

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}:=\left\{a,a+b,\dots,a+(s_{+}-1)b\right\}\cup\left\{-a,a-b,\dots,a-(s_{-}-1)b\right\}.

From the above considerations, we see that
E({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq E({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})
. If
s_{+}=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
, then
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}
. In which case, we are done since
h
is even and

E({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq E({\boldsymbol{x}}_{*})=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor s/2\rfloor-1}h(a+bj)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil s/2\rceil-1}h(-a-bj).

If
s_{+}\not=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
, we first consider the case where
r:=s_{+}-s_{-}\geq 1
. Then we reflect the
r
most positive elements of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}
, which does not change the energy since
h
is symmetric about the origin. This provides us with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}
. Then repeating the same argument as before, we shift these
r
reflected elements closer to zero to get
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}
and each transformation does not decrease
E
. This shows that
E({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq E({\boldsymbol{x}}_{*})
, which completes the proof when
s_{+}-s_{1}\geq 1
. The remaining case is analogous.
∎

An important property of this lemma is that while
\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}h(x)
depends on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, the right hand side
\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}h(a+bj)
only depends on
a
,
b
and
s
. Hence, it is a uniform bound over the class of all
{\boldsymbol{x}}
that satisfy the assumptions of
Lemma

9.1
. This flexibility enables us to prove energy estimates for a variety of sets. To simplify the resulting notation, we define the following extended real valued functions

\displaystyle h_{m,0}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{m|\sin(t/2)|}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle h_{m,1}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{2m|\sin(t/2)|}+\frac{1}{2m^{2}|\sin(t/2)|^{2}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle h_{m,2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{4m|\sin(t/2)|}+\frac{1}{2m^{2}|\sin(t/2)|}+\frac{1}{2m^{3}|\sin(t/2)|^{3}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle h_{m,3}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{8m|\sin(t/2)|}+\frac{3}{8m^{2}|\sin(t/2)|}+\frac{3}{4m^{3}|\sin(t/2)|^{3}}+\frac{5}{4m^{4}|\sin(t/2)|^{4}}\right)^{2}.

These functions appear when we compute
d_{m}^{(\ell)}
for
\ell\in\{0,\dots,3\}
and use triangle inequality (details omitted) to see that

\frac{1}{m^{2\ell}}\big|d_{m}^{(\ell)}(t)\big|^{2}\leq h_{m,\ell}(t).

(9.4)

With these functions at hand, we define the quantity,

E_{\ell}(m,\alpha,\beta):=2h_{m,\ell}\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha}{m}\right)+\frac{m}{\pi\beta}\int_{2\pi(\alpha+\beta/2)/m}^{\pi}h_{m,\ell}(t)\,dt.

(9.5)

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
m\geq m_{0}\geq 1
,
\beta>1
and
\alpha>0
. Suppose
{\boldsymbol{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
|x|\geq 2\pi\alpha/m
for all
x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Then for each
\ell\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, we have

\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}\big|d_{m}^{(\ell)}(x)\big|^{2}\leq E_{\ell}(m_{0},\alpha,\beta)\,m^{2\ell}.

Proof.

Note that
h_{m,\ell}
is even, decreasing away from zero, and non-negative. A calculus argument shows that for any
u\in(0,\pi]
, the sequence
\{m\sin(u/m)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is increasing in
m
. By the assumption that
m\geq m_{0}
and that
h_{m,\ell}
is even, we see that for all
u\in[-\pi,\pi]
,

h_{m,\ell}(u/m)\leq h_{m_{0},\ell}(u/m_{0}).

We use this inequality, (
9.4
), and
Lemma

9.1
, where
a=2\pi\alpha/m
and
b=2\pi\beta/m
. This yields the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2\ell}}\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}\big|d_{m}^{(\ell)}(t)\big|^{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor s/2\rfloor-1}h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil s/2\rceil-1}h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(-\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha}{m_{0}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor s/2\rfloor-1}h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil s/2\rceil-1}h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(-\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right).

Next, we interpret the right two sums as a Riemann sum approximation of an integral using the midpoint rule. To set this up, the partition width is
2\pi\beta/m_{0}
and the midpoints of this partition are

\left\{\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right\}_{j=1}^{\lfloor s/2\rfloor-1}\cup\left\{-\frac{2\pi(\alpha+\beta j)}{m_{0}}\right\}_{j=1}^{\lceil s/2\rceil-1}.

Since
h_{m_{0},\ell}
is non-negative and convex, the midpoint rule underestimates the integral. Thus, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2k}}\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left|d_{m}^{(\ell)}(t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2h_{m_{0},\ell}\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha}{m_{0}}\right)+\frac{m_{0}}{2\pi\beta}\int_{2\pi(\alpha+\beta/2)/m_{0}}^{2\pi-2\pi(\alpha+\beta/2)/m_{0}}h_{m_{0},\ell}(t)\,dt.

Using that
h_{m_{0},\ell}
is even to manipulate the right integral completes the proof.
∎

Lemma

9.2
is written in a way that suggests
E_{\ell}(m,\alpha,\beta)
can be treated as a constant that does not depend on
m
. This is indeed the case. Notice that
h_{m,\ell}(t)
has a singularity at
t=0
, which determines the behavior of the integral in (
9.5
). Using the asymptotic expansion
\sin(t)\sim t
for small
t
, we see that
h_{m,\ell}(t)
behaves likes
C/(m^{2}|t|^{2})
. Thus,
E_{\ell}
is at most
C/\alpha^{2}+C/(\beta(\alpha+\beta/2))
where
C
does not depend on
m
.

9.3
A local approximation result

To control
q
and its derivatives, we will derive a useful representation for
{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}
where
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. This is motivated by the following calculation.

Remark 9.1
.

When
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{1}\}
, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
is just a single column,
\sqrt{m}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{1})
. Selecting
{\boldsymbol{U}}
as just
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{1})
and using formula (
9.2
), we have

q(t)=1-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)=1-(d_{m}(t))^{2}=1-f_{m}(t).

Of course
Remark

9.1
no longer holds in the nontrivial case where
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is not a singleton. Nonetheless, for any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we define the matrix

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}:={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}\setminus x_{j})\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times(s-1)}.

(9.6)

This is precisely
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
with the column
\sqrt{m}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})
removed. Throughout, we will let
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
be a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
. The reduced singular value decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
is denoted

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}={\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{j}^{*}.

(9.7)

Throughout the landscape analysis, various common expressions involving
\beta
will appear, such as

A(\beta):=\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}.

(9.8)

We see that
A(\beta)>1
and
A(\beta)
decreases to 1 as
\beta
increases. This quantity appears whenever we use
Proposition

2.1
, which provides the inequality
\sigma_{\min}^{-2}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}))\leq A(\beta)/m
whenever its assumptions hold. To reduce clutter, we define the special function

\gamma(t)=2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\sin^{-1}(t)\right).

(9.9)

When
t
is small, this is approximated by the identity function. The following lemma relates the projection operators
{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}
and
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
and
\beta>1
such that
A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)<1/4
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and for any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, there is a matrix
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times(s-1)}
such that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*},

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left(\sqrt{A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)}\right).

Additionally, for any vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}
, we have

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

Recall that
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is an orthonormal basis for the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, and the later matrix contains
\sqrt{m}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})
as one of its columns. Consider the matrix

({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j},

which is necessarily injective, otherwise neither is
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
be an orthonormal basis for the range of
({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
. This now establishes the formula,

{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}.

To relate
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
, we proceed to view
(I-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
as a perturbation of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
. We need to do some work before we are able to use Wedin’s sine-theta theorem. Since
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}\setminus x_{j})
and
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}}\setminus x_{j})\geq\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, we can use
Proposition

2.1
to obtain

\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}.

(9.10)

Our next step is to control the perturbation size. We use that
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})\|_{2}=1
and identity (
9.2
) to see that

\displaystyle\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}-({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})\big\|_{2}=\sqrt{m\sum_{k\not=j}\big|d_{m}(x_{k}-x_{j})\big|^{2}}.

To upper bound the right hand side, since
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have
|x_{k}-x_{j}|\geq 2\pi\beta/m
for all
k\not=j
. Applying
Lemma

9.2
where
\alpha=\beta
and combining it with the previous inequality, we see that

\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}-({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{mE_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)}.

(9.11)

Using assumption
A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)<1/4
, (
9.11
), and (
9.10
), we see that

\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}-({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}\|_{2}<\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}).

This enables us to use Wedin’s sine-theta theorem
[
44
, Chapter V, Theorem 4.4]
, where
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
acts as the unperturbed matrix whose range is
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
and
({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
is the perturbed matrix whose range is
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
. Employing inequalities (
9.10
) and (
9.11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}-({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j})}\leq\sqrt{A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)}.

Here, we bounded the sine-theta distance between
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
. We can find a particular choices for
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}\|_{2}
is almost equal to the sine-theta distance, see
[
44
, Chapter 1, Theorem 5.2]
. The referenced result says that if
t
is the maximum canonical angle between two subspaces, then particular bases can be chosen for them such that their squared error in spectral norm is
(1-\cos t)^{2}+\sin^{2}t=2-2\cos t=4\sin^{2}(t/2)
. Taking the square root, setting
t=\sin^{-1}(\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}\|_{2})
, and recalling the definition of
\gamma
in (
9.9
), we have

\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\gamma\left(\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}\|_{2}\right)\leq\gamma\left(\sqrt{A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)}\right).

For the final part of the proof, recall that
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}
is an orthonormal basis for the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}
. By (
9.7
) and (
9.10
), we see that for any vector
w
, we have

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{S}}_{j}^{-1}\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{w}}\big\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Provided that the assumptions of
Lemma

9.3
hold, we combine it with the definition of
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and formula (
9.2
) to see that, for any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
,

\begin{split}q(t)&=1-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\\
&=1-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\\
&=1-f_{m}(t-x_{j})-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t).\end{split}

(9.12)

From here, we can use this formula to control
q
and its derivatives for
t
sufficiently close to
x_{j}
. This extends
Remark

9.1
to the general case where
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is not a singleton. This is also the formula shown in equation (
5.4
) where
h_{m}(t)={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)
.

9.4
Landscape function associated with a Fourier subspace

The purpose of this subsection is to investigate the behavior of
q=q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
where
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. We expect this function to have several special properties. Define the quantities

\begin{split}C_{0}(m,\beta)&:=\frac{1}{6}-2A(\beta)E_{1}(m,\beta,\beta)-\frac{1}{6m^{2}},\\
C_{1}(m,\beta)&:=\frac{1}{6}+2A(\beta)E_{1}(m,\beta,\beta).\end{split}

(9.13)

As
m\to\infty
and
\beta\to\infty
, both quantities converge to 1/6. In particular,
C_{0}(m,\beta)
is an increasing function in
\beta
for fixed
m
. Whenever
m\geq 2
, we can always find
\beta
sufficiently large so that
C_{0}(m,\beta)>0
.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
and
\beta>1
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, let
q:=q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
be the landscape function associated with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. For each
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have

C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)m^{2}\leq q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\leq C_{1}(m_{0},\beta)m^{2}.

Proof.

Fix any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Using the definition of
q
, a calculation yields the equation

q^{\prime\prime}(t)=2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)+2\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right).

Since
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})
is lies in the range of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})=0
. This provides us with the formula

q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})=2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j}).

Since
{\boldsymbol{U}}
forms an orthonormal basis for the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, which has linearly independent columns, we have the explicit formula
{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}
. Then

\begin{split}q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})&=2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\\
&=2\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|^{2}_{2}-2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\\
&=2\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|^{2}_{2}-2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j}).\end{split}

A calculation shows that
2\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|^{2}_{2}=(m^{2}-1)/6
, see (
9.19
). Thus,

\left|q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})-\frac{1}{6}(m^{2}-1)\right|\leq 2\left|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}})}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|_{2}^{2}.

To control this term, note that the columns of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
are
\sqrt{m}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})
and that
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})=0
. We use
Proposition

2.1
and formula (
9.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}})}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2A(\beta)}{m}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(x_{j})\|_{2}^{2}=2A(\beta)\sum_{k\not=j}|d_{m}^{\prime}(x_{j}-x_{k})|^{2}.

Due to the separation assumption
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and that
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have
|x_{k}-x_{j}|\geq 2\pi\beta/m
for all
k\not=j
. This allows us to use
Lemma

9.2
with
\alpha=\beta
, which provides the estimate

\sum_{k\not=j}|d_{m}^{\prime}(x_{j}-x_{k})|^{2}\leq E_{1}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)\,m^{2}.

Combining the above yields

\left|q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})-\frac{1}{6}(m^{2}-1)\right|\leq 2A(\beta)E_{1}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)m^{2}.

Using this inequality, the assumption that
m\geq m_{0}
, and
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\geq 0
since
x_{j}
is a double root, we obtain the two conclusions of this lemma.
∎

Let us make a few comments about
Lemma

9.4
, which is one of the key technical results of this paper. The lower bound for
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})
does not depend on
s
and holds uniformly over all
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Its conclusion sharp up to a universal constant, since
\|q^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq m^{2}
from inequality (
5.1
). For large enough
\beta
and
m
, both
C_{0}(m,\beta)
and
C_{1}(m,\beta)
are roughly 1/6, which naturally appears since
f^{\prime\prime}_{m}(0)=(m^{2}-1)/6
. By
Remark

9.1
, the
1/6
constant cannot be improved. Prior analysis of MUSIC, such as
[
26
]
, recognized that
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})
is important for the algorithm’s stability, but did not explicitly calculate
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})
. The main theorems in that reference implicitly depend on
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})
.

Define the quantities
B_{0}=1
,
B_{1}=1/12
,
B_{2}=1/80
, and
B_{3}=1/448
, and note their appearance in (

). For each

(9.14)

Lemma 9.5
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
, and
\beta>1
such that
A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)<1/4
. For any
\tau\in[0,\beta)
and
\ell\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, set
T_{\ell}:=T_{\ell}(m_{0},\beta-\tau,\beta)
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, let

be the landscape function associated with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. For any

and

, we have

\displaystyle\left|q^{\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime}(t-x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\,T_{0}T_{1}m,

\displaystyle\left|q^{\prime\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(2T_{1}^{2}+2T_{0}T_{2}\right)m^{2},

\displaystyle\left|q^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(2T_{0}T_{3}+6T_{1}T_{2}\right)m^{3}.

Proof.

There is nothing to prove if
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a singleton due to
Remark

9.1
. From now on, assume that
s>1
. The assumptions of
Lemma

9.3
hold, so let
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}
be the matrix defined in that lemma. We take derivatives of formula (
9.12
) to see that

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-f_{m}^{\prime}(t-x_{j})-2\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\right),

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})-2{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)-2\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right),

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-f_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})-2\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right)-6\text{Re}\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)^{*}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right).

By Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\displaystyle|q^{\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime}(t-x_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\big\|_{2},

\displaystyle|q^{\prime\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\big\|_{2}^{2}+2\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\big\|_{2},

\displaystyle|q^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t-x_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\big\|_{2}+6\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\big\|_{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\big\|_{2}.

We need to control these error terms. Using the properties listed in
Lemma

9.3
and the upper bound

listed in (
9.19
), for any

, we have

\begin{split}\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(\ell)}(t)\big\|_{2}&\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(\ell)}(t)\big\|_{2}+\big\|({\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(\ell)}(t)\big\|_{2}\\
&\leq\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(\ell)}(t)\big\|_{2}+\gamma\left(\sqrt{A(\beta)B_{\ell}E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)}\right)m^{\ell}.\end{split}

Using the assumptions that

for

,

, and that

for all

. We again use identity (

where

in the referenced lemma, to deduce that

Combining the previous inequalities, we obtain

Combining everything completes the proof.
∎

Lemma

9.5
shows that

is pointwise approximated by

whenever

is sufficiently small. The error terms are quadratic in

The next result provides a lower bound for

whenever

.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
, and
\beta>1
such that
A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)<1/4
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
such that

, let

be the landscape function associated with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. For any
t\in\mathbb{T}
such that

for all
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have

q(t)\geq 1-f_{m}(t-x_{j})-(T_{0}(m_{0},\beta/2,\beta))^{2}.

Proof.

There is nothing to prove if
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a singleton due to
Remark

9.1
. From here onward, assume that
s>1
. Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be the elements in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
that are closest to
t
such that

. Without loss of generality, we assume that
|t-x_{1}|\leq|t-x_{2}|
. Let
\beta_{*}
such that

and note that

, otherwise it would contract the assumption that

would be closer to

.

The assumptions of
Lemma

9.3
are satisfied, so let
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}
be the matrix from that lemma. Recalling (

), we use triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz to obtain

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle\geq 1-f_{m}(t-x_{1})-\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}.

Using the properties of
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}
, we obtain

\big\|{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}\,\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}+\gamma\left(\sqrt{A(\beta)E_{0}(m,\beta,\beta)}\right).

We proceed to control the right hand side. Recall that

by assumption, while

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{A(\beta)}{m}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\big\|_{2}=\sqrt{A(\beta)\sum_{k\not=1}\left|d_{m}(t-x_{k})\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta/2,\beta)}.

Combining these inequalities and recalling the definition of

∎

9.5
Landscape function associated with a perturbed Fourier subspace

This section provides a landscape analysis of
\widetilde{q}:=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
is a good enough approximation to a
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times s}
. Let
q:=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
. Recall
Lemma

5.4
which contained some basic inequalities.

While
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
are the only roots of
q
, each one being a double root, there is no reason to believe that
\widetilde{q}
has any roots whatsoever. However, we will show that
\widetilde{q}
has critical points
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
that are near
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. In essence, the double roots of
q
are perturbed to local minima of
\widetilde{q}
. Recall the quantity
T_{\ell}
defined in (
9.14
).

Lemma 9.7
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
,
\beta>1
, and
T_{\ell}:=T_{\ell}(m_{0},\beta-1/(2\pi),\beta)
for
\ell\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
, let
\widetilde{q}=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
. Suppose there exists a
r\in(0,1/\vartheta)
such that

C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)r-1-\left(\frac{1}{20}+T_{0}T_{3}+3T_{1}T_{2}\right)r^{2}\vartheta>0.

(9.15)

Then the polynomial
\widetilde{q}
has
s
critical points
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{s}\}
such that

\max_{j=1,\dots,s}|\widetilde{x}_{j}-x_{j}|\leq\frac{r\vartheta}{m}.

Proof.

Let
q=q_{\boldsymbol{U}}
and fix any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Using the Taylor remainder formula applied to
q^{\prime}
at
x_{j}
, whenever
|t-x_{j}|=r\vartheta/m
, there is a
u_{j,t}
such that
|u_{t,j}-x_{j}|\leq r\vartheta/m
and

q^{\prime}(t)=q^{\prime}(x_{j})+q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})(t-x_{j})+\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime\prime\prime}(u_{j,t})(t-x_{j})^{2}.

Importantly,
x_{j}
is a double root of
q
and so
q^{\prime}(x_{j})=0
. We have
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\geq C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)m^{2}
from
Lemma

9.4
. For convenience, set
C:=(1/20+T_{0}T_{3}+3T_{1}T_{2})
. To control
|q^{\prime\prime\prime}(u_{j,t})|
, note that
|u_{t,j}-x_{j}|\leq r\vartheta/m\leq 1/m
since
r\in(0,1/\vartheta)
by assumption. By
Lemma

9.5
with
\tau=1/(2\pi)
and also the final inequality in (
9.18
), we get

|q^{\prime\prime\prime}(u_{j,t})|\leq|f_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u_{j,t}-x_{j})|+\left(2T_{0}T_{3}+6T_{1}T_{2}\right)m^{3}\leq 2Cm^{3}.

Thus, we see that

\displaystyle q^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq+C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)r\vartheta m-Cr^{2}\vartheta^{2}m,

(9.16)

\displaystyle q^{\prime}\left(x_{j}-\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)r\vartheta m+Cr^{2}\vartheta^{2}m.

(9.17)

We proceed to examine
\widetilde{q}
. Using inequalities (
5.2
) and (
9.16
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq q^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)-\left|\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)-q^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\geq q^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)-\|\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}-q^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\geq\left(C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)r-Cr^{2}\vartheta-1\right)\vartheta m.

Repeating a similar argument with (
9.17
) instead establishes

\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}\left(x_{j}-\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)\leq-\left(C_{0}(m_{0},\beta)r-Cr^{2}\vartheta-1\right)\vartheta m.

These inequalities show that (
9.15
) implies

\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}\left(x_{j}+\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}\left(x_{j}-\frac{r\vartheta}{m}\right)<0.

By the intermediate value theorem, there exists a
\widetilde{x}_{j}\in[x_{j}-r\vartheta/m,x_{j}+r\vartheta/m]
such that
\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(\widetilde{x}_{j})=0
. This completes the proof. ∎

Although
Lemma

9.7
only establishes that
\widetilde{x}_{j}
is a critical point, we will eventually show that it is actually a local minima, provided the various parameters are correctly chosen. The
1/m
factor in the conclusion of
Lemma

9.7
comes from the
m^{2}
dependence in
Lemma

9.4
. Had we gotten a weaker bound for
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})
, even something such as
q^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\geq c_{s}m^{2}
where
c_{s}\to 0
as
s\to\infty
, the parameter
r
would necessarily increase in
s
.

In order to investigate the size of
\widetilde{q}(\widetilde{x}_{j})
, we first investigate the size of
\widetilde{q}(x_{j})
. On the one hand, using (
5.2
), we obtain

\widetilde{q}(x_{j})\leq|q(x_{j})|+|\widetilde{q}(x_{j})-q(x_{j})|\leq 0+\|\widetilde{q}-q\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\vartheta.

This inequality is standard and has appeared in previous analysis of MUSIC. It turns out that it is extremely loose, and there are hidden cancellations in
\widetilde{q}-q
that lead to a significantly better estimate.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
and
\beta>1
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
, let
\widetilde{q}=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
. Then
\widetilde{q}(x_{j})\leq\vartheta^{2}
for all
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Proof.

Using the definition of
\widetilde{q}
, for each
x_{j}
, we have

\widetilde{q}(x_{j})={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j}).

Since
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})
is a unit norm vector that belongs to the range of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, it is in the range of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and there is a unit norm
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{j}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})={\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{j}
. Then

|\widetilde{q}(x_{j})|=\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x_{j})\big\|_{2}^{2}=\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{j}\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}\big\|_{2}^{2}=\vartheta^{2},

where for the last equality, we used that
\big\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}\big\|_{2}
is an alternative expression for the sine-theta distance.
∎

Our final task is to prove an analogue of
Lemma

9.5
for
\widetilde{q}
instead of
q
. We will need lower and upper bounds for
\widetilde{q}\,^{(\ell)}(t)
, for
t
close to
\widetilde{x}_{j}
, a critical point in
Lemma

9.7
. This is done through a two step approximation. In the following lemma,
u
plays the role of
\widetilde{x}_{j}-x_{j}
.

Lemma 9.9
.

Let
m\geq\max\{m_{0},s+1\}
, and
\beta>1
such that
A(\beta)E_{0}(m_{0},\beta,\beta)<1/4
. Fix any
\tau\in[0,\beta)
and set
T_{\ell}:=T_{\ell}(m_{0},\beta-\tau,\beta)
for
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
. For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
such that
\Delta({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{U}^{m\times s}
, let
\widetilde{q}=q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and
\vartheta:=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}})
. For any
x_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{x}}
and

such that
|t-x_{j}|\leq 2\pi\tau/m
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime}(t-u-x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(2\,T_{0}T_{1}+\vartheta+\frac{1}{6}m|u|\right)m,

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{q}\,^{\prime\prime}(t)+f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t-u-x_{j})\right|

Let
q=q_{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq_{m}{\boldsymbol{U}}
. For each

\displaystyle\leq\big|q\,^{(\ell)}(t)+f_{m}^{(\ell)}(t-x_{j})\big|+\big|\widetilde{q}\,^{(\ell)}(t)-q^{(\ell)}(t)\big|+|f_{m}^{(\ell)}(t-u-x_{j})-f_{m}^{(\ell)}(t-x_{j})|.

9.6
Some formulas and inequalities

Recall the Fourier series representation the Fejér kernel,

f_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=-m+1}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{m}\right)e^{ikt}.

Taking derivatives and then applying triangle inequality, we see that

\|f_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1,\quad\|f_{m}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\frac{m}{3},\quad\|f_{m}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\frac{m^{2}}{6},\quad\|f_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\frac{m^{3}}{10}.

(9.18)

For convenience, we define
B_{0}:=1
,
B_{1}=1/12
,
B_{2}=1/80
, and
B_{3}=1/448
. For all
t\in\mathbb{T}
and integer
m\geq 1
, we have

\begin{split}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in I(m)}1^{2}=B_{0},\\
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{2}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in I(m)}k^{2}=\frac{1}{12}(m^{2}-1)\leq B_{1}m^{2},\\
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{2}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in I(m)}k^{4}=\frac{1}{240}(3m^{4}-10m^{2}+7)\leq B_{2}m^{4},\\
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{2}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in I(m)}k^{6}=\frac{1}{1344}(3m^{6}-21m^{4}+49m^{2}-31)\leq B_{3}m^{6}.\end{split}

(9.19)

The previous two lemmas are expressed in terms of the Fejér kernel. Its behavior is analyzed in the following lemma.

Lemma 9.10
.

Let
m\geq m_{0}\geq 2
. For any
t\in\mathbb{T}
such that
t\geq 2\pi\tau_{0}/m
for some
\tau_{0}\in(0,1]
, we have

f_{m}(t)\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{4},\,\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi\tau_{0}/m_{0})}\right\}.

For any
t\in[0,\pi]
, we have

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{mt}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{3}\left(1-m_{0}^{-2}\right)-\frac{1}{120}m^{2}t^{2}\right)m

\displaystyle\leq-f_{m}^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{1}{6}m^{2}t,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\left(1-m_{0}^{-2}\right)m^{2}-\frac{1}{30}m^{4}t^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq\frac{1}{6}.

Proof.

Using the inequality
|\sin(t/2)|\geq|t|/\pi
which holds for
|t|\leq\pi
, we see that

f_{m}(t)=\frac{\sin^{2}(mt/2)}{m^{2}\sin^{2}(t/2)}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{m^{2}|t|^{2}}.

This shows that
f_{m}(t)\leq 1/4
for all
|t|\geq\pi/m
. This finishes the estimate if
\tau_{0}\in[1/2,1]
. For the case where
t\in[2\pi\tau_{0}/m,\pi/m]
for
\tau_{0}\in(0,1/2)
, we use that
f_{m}
is decreasing on
[0,2\pi/m]
and also note that
\{m\sin(u/m)\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is non-decreasing in
m
for any fixed
u\in[0,\pi]
. Since
m\geq m_{0}
by assumption, this now implies

\max_{t\in[2\pi\tau_{0}/m,\pi/m]}f_{m}(t)=f_{m}\left(\frac{2\pi\tau_{0}}{m}\right)=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\tau_{0})}{m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi\tau_{0}/m)}\leq\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi\tau_{0}/m_{0})}.

This proves the first inequality.

We now concentrate on the first derivative estimates. A calculation and manipulation of trigonometric functions establishes the formula

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\,f_{m}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sin(mt/2)}{2m^{3}\sin^{3}(t/2)}\,g_{m}(t),

\displaystyle g_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=(m+1)\sin\left(\frac{m-1}{2}t\right)-(m-1)\sin\left(\frac{m+1}{2}t\right).

Using the observation that for
t\geq 0
, the power series of
\sin(t)
truncated to degree
2k+1
overestimates
\sin(t)
when
k
is even and underestimates it when
k
is odd, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{m}(t)\geq\frac{1}{12}\left(1-m^{-2}\right)m^{3}t^{3}-\frac{1}{480}\left(1-m^{-4}\right)m^{5}t^{5}.

Using this inequality, that
m\geq m_{0}
, and
\sin(t/2)\leq t/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\,f_{m}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\sin\left(\frac{mt}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{3}\left(1-m_{0}^{-2}\right)-\frac{1}{120}m^{2}t^{2}\right).

This proves the claimed lower bound for
-f^{\prime}_{m}
.

\displaystyle-f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{6}\left(m^{2}-1\right)\leq\frac{1}{6}m^{2},

\displaystyle-f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{6}\left(m^{2}-1\right)-\frac{1}{60}\left(2m^{4}-5m^{2}+3\right)t^{2}.

\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{2}\leq m
, we have

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\right\|_{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{2}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\|_{2}\right)\|{\rm diag}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{1+2/\beta}\,m^{3/2}\,\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\infty}.

Combining all of the above and inserting into (
11.5
), we see that

\big\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}-{\boldsymbol{A}}\big\|_{2}\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\beta+2}{\beta-2}}\right)\frac{\beta+2}{\beta-2}\,a_{\max}\,m\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\frac{\|T({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{2}}{m}.

The proof is complete once we note that for each
j=1,\dots,s
, we have

|a_{j}-\widehat{a}_{j}|=\big\|{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}^{*}\big({\boldsymbol{A}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\big){\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\big\|_{2}\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{A}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\big\|_{2}.

11.11
Proof of Lemma
8.1

The proof is an application of a matrix concentration inequality
[
42
, Theorem 4.1.1]
and a modification of the proof of
Lemma

5.8
.

Let
\{s_{\ell}^{2}\}_{\ell=0}^{2m-1}
denote the diagonal entries of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
so that
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{u}}+i{\boldsymbol{v}})/\sqrt{2}
, where
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}
are independent standard normal random vectors. Note that for each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,2m-1\}
,

\zeta_{\ell}=\eta_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}u_{\ell}+i\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}v_{\ell},\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{-\ell}=\overline{\eta_{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}u_{\ell}-i\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}v_{\ell}.

For each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,2m-1\}
, we define the matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
in the following way.
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}
has all zeros except it has ones on the
\ell
-th and
-\ell
-th diagonal;
(A_{\ell})_{j,k}=1
if and only if
|k-j|=\ell
and
(A_{\ell})_{j,k}=0
otherwise. Let
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
such that it has all zeros except it has ones on the
\ell
-th and minus ones
-\ell
-th diagonal. To simplify notation, let
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{B}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{2m}
. Thus, we see that

T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2m-1}u_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}\right)+i\sum_{\ell=0}^{2m-1}v_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}\right).

Call the first and second sums
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
i{\boldsymbol{B}}
respectively. Since
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are independent, they are a sum of independent and real random matrices. To control
\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{2}
, we control the spectral norm of the two sums individually via matrix concentration and then use the union bound. Notice that
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}^{*}
,
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}^{*}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\ell}
,
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}^{*}
, and
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\ell}^{*}
are all diagonal and their diagonals are bounded by 2 in absolute value. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\max\{\nu({\boldsymbol{A}}),\nu({\boldsymbol{B}})\}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{2m-1}s_{\ell}^{2}={\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}).

Applying the referenced matrix concentration inequality establishes that

\mathbb{E}\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(4m)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{2}\geq t)\leq 4me^{-t^{2}/(2{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))}.

We get the same upper bounds for
{\boldsymbol{B}}
instead of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. Using that
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are independent,
\|T({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\|_{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{2}
, and the union bound completes the proof.
