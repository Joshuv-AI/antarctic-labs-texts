𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} Contains 𝖳𝖢 0 \mathsf{TC}^{0} (with Many Copies of the Input)
Daniel Grier, Jackson Morris, Kewen Wu

Abstract
𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} is the class of constant-depth polynomial-size quantum circuits constructed from arbitrary single-qubit gates and generalized Toffoli gates. It is arguably the smallest natural class of constant-depth quantum computation which has not been shown useful for computing any non-trivial Boolean function. Despite this, many attempts to port classical 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} lower bounds to 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} have failed. We give one possible explanation of this: 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits are significantly more powerful than their classical counterparts. We show the unconditional separation 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 ⊄ 𝖠𝖢 0 ​ [ p ] \mathsf{QAC}^{0}\not\subset\mathsf{AC}^{0}[p] for decision problems, which also resolves for the first time whether 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} could be more powerful than 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} . Moreover, we prove that 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits can compute a wide range of Boolean functions if given multiple copies of the input: 𝖳𝖢 0 ⊆ 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 ∘ 𝖭𝖢 0 \mathsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\mathsf{QAC}^{0}\circ\mathsf{NC}^{0} . Along the way, we introduce an amplitude amplification technique that makes several approximate constant-depth constructions exact.

Constant-depth quantum circuits have long played a central role in our understanding of how quantum computers can gain an advantage over their classical counterparts. Quantum-Classical Separations.

To obtain
Theorem

1

Theorem

2

Indeed, if
\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}(x)=1

\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}(x)=0

This means we can perform error reduction using the

{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}

{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}

More formally, let us take the
\and
of
c\approx n^{2}
parallel runs of the
W

If
\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}(x)=1
, then the
\and

1

0

0.99

This is

Theorem

1
as
\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}
is not in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}[p]

[
32

36

The caveat here is,

W

c

Appendix

C

{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}

Fortunately, to prove separation results, we do not have to stick to the vanilla
\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}
function. We can use a variant.
In particular, we define the
c

\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}

\mathsf{Copy}\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times c}\to\{0,1\}

\mathsf{Copy}\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{c})=\begin{cases}\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}(X_{1})&X_{1}=\cdots=X_{c},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\text{for every $X_{1},\ldots,X_{c}\in\{0,1\}^{n}$.}

The benefit of using
\mathsf{Copy}\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}

•

Regarding classical lower bounds,
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}[p]

Regarding quantum upper bounds,

For

1.2
Future Directions

We show that having polynomially many classical copies of the input enables
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
to compute highly non-trivial Boolean functions beyond
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}[p]
.
It is reasonable to ask more fine-grained questions about the actual number of copies needed.
Indeed, if this “copy complexity” can be reduced to
{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
, then
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}={\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}_{\mathrm{wf}}
.
Towards this question, we make some partial progress in
Appendix

C
. In particular, we show that
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
circuits can compute parity with
O(n^{3/2})
copies copies of the input and more generally any symmetric function with
O(n^{2})
copies.

Regarding the separation we achieve in
Theorem

1
, it is natural to ask if the soundness be reduced from inverse exponential to zero. This would lead to a separation between
exact

{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
and
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}[p]
. Such a class of decision problems has previously been called
{\mathsf{EQAC}}^{0}

[
13
]
. Similarly, another natural question is whether we can exhibit a total function in
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
that is hard against
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}[p]
circuits on average.
Note that by the blocky nature of
\mathsf{Copy}\mathsf{EX}_{n/2}
, a random input string is a no instance with high probability; hence this function has high correlation with the constant zero function.

The techniques introduced in this work do not seem to directly address the question of whether
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}\subset{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
. One may view
Theorem

6
as some partial progress; however, simple
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}
functions which require large fanout, such as the indexing function, still seem out of reach with the primitives developed thus far.

Another interesting question is to reproduce our result using a finite gate set. Note that the approximate nekomata construction of
[
15
]
only uses Hadamard and Boolean function gates, whereas our approach heavily relies on the exact amplitude amplification, for which arbitrary single-qubit gates seem necessary.

Finally, we review the questions left open in Moore’s original work
[
26
]
:

(1)

Is
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}={\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}_{\mathrm{wf}}
?

(2)

Is
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}[p]={\mathsf{QACC}}^{0}
for arbitrary primes
p>1
?

(3)

Is
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}_{\mathrm{wf}}={\mathsf{QTC}}^{0}
?

For the above questions, Moore remarks:

We conjecture that the answer to all these questions is ‘no,’ but quantum circuits can be surprising

In
[
13
]
, it was shown that the answer to (2) is in fact “yes”.
Our
Corollary

4
, which builds on
[
15
]
, shows that (3) is also true.
While (1) is still open, we believe our separation and simulation results (
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

2
) exhibit further ways in which quantum circuits can be surprising, suggesting that perhaps the answer to (1) is also “yes”.

Appendix B
Indexing Requires Large Classical Fanout

In this section, we show that juntas and the indexing function requires large fanout, even in
{\mathsf{TC}}^{0}
. This is a simple counting argument that we provide for completeness.

We start with juntas of logarithmic size.

Theorem 25
.

There exists a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{\log(n)}\to\{0,1\}
such that, if
f
can be computed by a constant-depth circuit of gate set
\{\neg,(\mathsf{Th}_{\geq k})_{k\geq 0})\}
, then the circuit has fanout

.

Proof.

Any circuit of depth
d
and fanout
r
has at most
O(r^{d})
gates.
Since each gate has
O(r^{d})+\log(n)
possibilities, there are at most
(r^{d}+\log(n))^{O(r^{d})}
many such circuits.
On the other hand, there are
2^{n}
many distinct Boolean functions on
\log(n)
bits, which requires distinct circuits.
This means
r=n^{\Omega(1)}
as

.

Recall the definition of the indexing function
\mathsf{Ind}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{\log(n)}\to\{0,1\}
that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ind}(x_{1},\dots x_{n},i_{1},\dots,i_{\log(n)})=x_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\log(n)}}.

.

If
\mathsf{Ind}
can be computed by a constant-depth circuit of gate set
\{\neg,(\mathsf{Th}_{\geq k})_{k\geq 0}\}
, then the circuit has fanout
n^{\Omega(1)}
.

For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define
\mathsf{Ind}_{x}\colon\{0,1\}^{\log(n)}\to\{0,1\}

Then

enumerates all Boolean functions on

bits, including the one from

.
This completes the proof.

Claim 28

The proof of

•

Then

\displaystyle\left|W_{x}\right\rangle^{\otimes m}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i}(-1)^{x_{i}}\left|e_{i}\right\rangle\right)^{\otimes m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{m}}}\sum_{a\in[n]^{m}}(-1)^{x_{1}s_{1}(a)+\cdots+x_{n}s_{n}(a)}\left|A_{a}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{m}}}\sum_{a\in[n]^{m}}(-1)^{x_{1}|A_{a}[1]|+\cdots+x_{n}|A_{a}[n]|}\left|A_{a}\right\rangle

Claim 29
.

The
\ell_{2}
distance between
\left|\rho_{x}\right\rangle
and
\left|W_{x}\right\rangle^{\otimes m}
is
2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}

Proof.

Comparing
Equation

8
and
Equation

9

\displaystyle\left\|\left|\rho_{x}\right\rangle-\left|W_{x}\right\rangle^{\otimes m}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\Pr_{a\sim[n]^{m}}\left[|A_{a}[i]|>t\text{ for some }i\in[n]\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4n\cdot\Pr_{a\sim[n]^{m}}\left[|A_{a}[1]|>t\right]

(by union bound and symmetry)

\displaystyle=4n\cdot\Pr\left[\textsf{binom}(m,1/n)>t\right],

\textsf{binom}(m,1/n)

C.2
Probabilistic Computation

To further reduce copy complexity, we consider probabilistic computation, i.e., a random
{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
circuit that correctly computes the target function with high probability.

We illustrate the idea with the parity function.
We use
\mathsf{Parity}^{\uparrow m}\colon(\{0,1\}^{n})^{m}\to\{0,1\}
to denote the
m
-copy version of the parity function, defined by

\mathsf{Parity}^{\uparrow m}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})=\mathsf{Parity}(y_{1})\cdot 1_{y_{1}=\cdots=y_{m}}\quad\text{for all $y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}\in(\{0,1\}^{n})^{m}$.}

Recall that
Corollary

30
shows
\mathsf{Parity}^{\uparrow n^{2}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}\in{\mathsf{QAC}}^{0}
. Below we show probabilistic computation yields another square-root saving.

Theorem 31
.

Let
m=n^{1.5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
.
There is an ensemble of constant-depth polynomial-size
{\mathsf{QAC}}
circuits
\{C_{r}\}_{r}
such that

\Pr_{r}\left[C_{r}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})=\mathsf{Parity}^{\uparrow m}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\right]\geq 1-2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}\quad\text{holds for every $y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{n}$.}

Proof.

Define
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
by

f(y)=\begin{cases}\mathsf{Parity}(y)&|y|\in\frac{n}{2}\pm\sqrt{n\cdot{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
f
is a disjunction of
\sqrt{n\cdot{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
many exact threshold functions.
By
Corollary

30
, with
m=n^{1.5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
copies, constant-depth polynomial-size
{\mathsf{QAC}}
circuits can compute
f
with error
2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
.

Now for every
r\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with even Hamming weight, we use
C_{r}^{\prime}
to denote the
{\mathsf{QAC}}
circuit, on
n^{1.5}/{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)
copies of
y
, computing
f(y\oplus r)
with error
2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
.
We remark that copies of
y
can be converted into copies of
y\oplus r
by a layer of single-qubit gates, since
r
is hardwired into
C_{r}^{\prime}
.
In addition,
\mathsf{Parity}(y)=\mathsf{Parity}(y\oplus r)
since
r
is an even string, which equals
f(y\oplus r)
if
|y\oplus r|\in\frac{n}{2}\pm\sqrt{n\cdot{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
.

Define
{\mathsf{QAC}}
circuit
C_{r}
to be
C_{r}^{\prime}
with an additional constant-depth layer to check that the input copies are identical.
Fix arbitrary
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
y=y_{1}
.
If
C_{r}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\neq\mathsf{Parity}^{\uparrow m}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
, then we have the following two cases.

•

|y\oplus r|\notin\frac{n}{2}\pm\sqrt{n\cdot{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
. This happens with probability
2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
since
r
is a uniformly random even string.

•

|y\oplus r|\in\frac{n}{2}\pm\sqrt{n\cdot{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
but
C_{r}^{\prime}
does not compute
f(y\oplus r)=\mathsf{Parity}(y)
. This happens with probability
2^{-{\mathsf{polylog}}(n)}
by
Corollary

30
.

This completes the proof with a union bound.
∎

The above construction can be generalized to other symmetric functions (see e.g.,
[
37
]
).
