Discovery of MD-265: A Potent MDM2 Degrader That Achieves Complete Tumor Regression and Improves Long-Term Survival of Mice with Leukemia
Authors: Angelo Aguilar, Jiuling Yang, Yangbing Li, Donna McEachern, Liyue Huang, Stevenchoukry Razzouk, Shaomeng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
MDM2 has been pursued as an attractive therapeutic target for human cancers. Herein, we describe our discovery of MD-265 as a promising PROTAC MDM2 degrader and extensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations of its therapeutic potential and mechanism of action. MD-265 effectively depleted MDM2 protein in cancer cells at concentrations as low as 1 nM, leading to strong activation of p53 in cancer cells carrying wild-type p53. It selectively inhibited the growth of wild-type p53 leukemia cell lines and showed no activity in mutated p53 lines. MD-265 achieved persistent tumor regression in a leukemia xenograft model without causing any signs of toxicity and dramatically improved survival of mice in a disseminated leukemia model even with a weekly administration. MD-265 displayed an excellent intravenous PK profile in mice, rats, and dogs. MD-265 is a promising MDM2 degrader for advanced preclinical development for the treatment of human cancers.

Introduction

The tumor suppressor p53 is a transcriptional factor and has been considered as the guardian of the human genome. Because of its powerful tumor suppressor function, p53 mutations are found in approximately 50% of human cancers, which lead to the loss of its tumor suppressor function or even a gain of oncogenic activity.1 In the other half of human cancers, p53 retains its wild-type status, but its tumor suppressor function is effectively inhibited by the murine double minute 2 (MDM2) protein through their direct protein–protein interaction (PPI).2−7 Therefore, targeting the MDM2–p53 PPI has been pursued as a novel cancer therapeutic strategy, which has resulted in the advancement of several potent and selective small molecule MDM2 inhibitors into clinical development for cancer treatment.8−21 However, since p53 is a transcription factor and MDM2 is a direct target of p53, blocking the MDM2–p53 PPI by MDM2 inhibitors results in p53 activation, leading to upregulation of MDM2 in both mRNA and protein levels.5 Increased levels of MDM2 protein by MDM2 inhibitors attenuate the activation of p53, limiting the potential clinical efficacy of MDM2 inhibitors. In addition, due to the oncogenic activity of MDM2 protein, increased levels of MDM2 protein by MDM2 inhibitors may cause unwanted side effects. Although a number of MDM2 inhibitors have been advanced into Phase 2/3 clinical development, no MDM2 inhibitor has been approved for the treatment of human cancers to date. Hence, a new therapeutic strategy to target MDM2 is highly desirable.

In recent years, induced targeted protein degradation (TPD) using the proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology has become a powerful approach for the discovery and development of novel therapeutics for the treatment of human cancers and other human diseases.22−25 In our previous study, we have reported the design and evaluation of first-in-class MDM2 degraders using the PROTAC technology, exemplified by MD-224.26 Herein, we describe further optimization of MD-224, which resulted in the discovery of MD-265 as a novel and very promising PROTAC MDM2 degrader. Our data demonstrate that MD-265 was highly potent and effective in inducing degradation of MDM2 protein, leading to strong p53 activation in cancer cells carrying wild-type p53. MD-265 potently inhibited cell growth in human acute leukemia cancer cell lines carrying wild-type p53 and demonstrated no activity in cancer cells harboring mutated p53. MD-265 displayed an excellent pharmacokinetic profile in mice, rats, and dogs. MD-265 achieved complete and persistent tumor regression in the RS4;11 xenograft model without causing any signs of toxicity. Of significance, weekly administration of MD-265 dramatically improved survival of mice in the RS4;11 disseminated leukemia model. Taken together, our data demonstrate that MD-265 is a very promising MDM2 degrader for advanced preclinical development as a novel therapy for acute leukemia and other types of human cancers.

Results and Discussion

In our previous study, we reported the discovery of MD-224 as a first-in-class, potent, and efficacious PROTAC MDM2 degrader.26 In the present study, we employed MD-224 (Figure 1) as our lead molecule for further modifications with the objective of identifying an MDM2 degrader suitable for clinical development.

Optimization strategy of the PROTAC MDM2 degrader.

During our research of MDM2 degraders, we discovered that a simple modification of the MDM2 inhibitor portion of the PROTAC MDM2 degraders can convert a bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader into a “molecular glue” GSPT1 degrader.27 In human cancer cells carrying wild-type p53, a bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader induced degradation of MDM2, leading to activation of p53 and resulting in effective cell growth inhibition. In human cancer cells harboring a mutated or deleted p53, while a bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader was still effective in inducing degradation of MDM2, it failed to induce the activation of p53 and was therefore ineffective in cell growth inhibition. In contrast, because depletion of GSPT1 caused impaired translation termination,28 a GSPT1 “molecular glue” degrader effectively inhibited cell growth in cancer cell lines regardless of their p53 status.27,28 Because the MDM2 protein typically has a low expression in leukemia cancer cell lines, it has been a challenge to quantitatively determine the degradation of MDM2 protein induced by MDM2 degraders. Therefore, in the present study, we employed the parental RS4;11 leukemia cell line carrying a wild-type p53 (hereafter RS4;11) and a RS4;11/MIR2 cell line harboring p53 hot-spot mutations Y236H and R249G (hereafter RS4;11Mut), which was derived from our in vivo study of our previous MDM2 inhibitor,29 to evaluate the cell growth inhibition of our designed MDM2 degraders as our initial assays to assess the activity and mechanism of action of our designed MDM2 degraders.

Based on our previous study, the benzamide moiety in MD-224 appeared to be critical to avoid GSPT1 degradation by a molecular glue mechanism.27 We first modified the phenyl group of the benzamide moiety with different substitutions or replaced it with a pyridine, which resulted in compounds 1–7, with their chemical structures and cell growth inhibition data summarized in Table 1. All seven new compounds potently inhibited cell growth with IC50 values of 2–8 nM in the RS4;11 cell line and were thus similarly potent as MD-224, which had an IC50 value of 4 nM. Importantly, compounds 2–7 showed minimal activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line. Hence, our data suggested that compounds 2-7 were potent and bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degraders. However, compound 1, obtained by changing the phenyl substitution pattern from the para-position in MD-224 to the meta-position, was found to be equally effective in cell growth inhibition in both the RS4;11 and RS4;11Mut cell lines. Western blotting analysis of compound 1 in the RS4:11Mut cell line showed that it effectively depleted GSPT1 (Figure S1), indicating that compound 1 acted as a molecular glue degrader of GSPT1. Hence, our data further demonstrated that only very minor structural modifications can completely alter the underlying cellular molecular mechanism of action for a designed MDM2 degrader, transforming MD-224 from a bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader to a potent GSPT1 molecular glue degrader 1.

We next synthesized a series of degraders by replacing the benzamide moiety in MD-224 with cycloalkyl or heterocycloalkyl amides with the structures and data summarized in Table 2. Based upon their cell growth inhibition potency and selectivity, 8, 10, and 15 were potent and bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degraders, whereas 12, 13, and 14 were potent “molecular glue” degraders of GSPT1. Replacing the aryl group of the benzamide moiety in MD-224 with a piperidine generated compound 9, which was inactive in both RS4;11 cell lines. Changing the benzamide to a 4-aminopipridine group produced 11, which was also inactive in both RS4;11 cell lines. Among these potent and bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degraders, compound 8, in which the phenyl group in MD-224 was replaced by a trans-cyclohexyl group, was four times more potent than MD-224. Compound 10, which was synthesized by replacing the phenyl group in MD-224 with N-methyl-piperidinyl, was a potent and bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader with improved solubility in the presence of an ionizable amine in the linker.

Compounds 16 and 17 with a short and rigid linker were >5 times weaker than MD-224 in cell growth inhibition in the RS4;11 cell line. Interestingly, while 17 displayed no cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line, 16 potently inhibited cell growth in the RS4;11Mut cell line with an IC50 value of 33 nM. These data further highlighted that a minor structural change in the linker can alter the cellular mechanism of action for our designed MDM2 degraders. Insertion of one methylene unit in the linker of compounds 16 and 17 yielded compounds 18 and 19, respectively. While both compounds 18 and 19 were potent inhibitors of cell growth in the RS4;11 cell line with IC50 values of 19 nM, they were five times weaker than MD-224. Both compounds 18 and 19 had no cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line, suggesting that they are bona fide MDM2 degraders. We synthesized compounds 20, 21, 22, and 23 containing an ionizable amine moiety with the objective to improve the overall solubility of our degrader molecules. These four degraders attained IC50 values of 8–50 nM in cell growth inhibition in the RS4;11 parental cell line and had no cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line. We synthesized compounds 24 and 25 containing an imidazole and a pyrazole group, respectively, to improve the solubility. Both 24 and 25 displayed IC50 values of 6 and 7 nM, respectively, in cell growth inhibition in the RS4;11 cell line. Interestingly, compound 24 was fairly potent in the inhibition of cell growth in the RS4;11Mut cell line with IC50 = 180 nM, suggesting that this compound functioned as a GSPT1 molecular glue degrader. In comparison, compound 25 had no cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line, indicating that compound 25 is a bona fide MDM2 degrader.

We synthesized 26 and 27 by retaining the alkyne moiety in the linker in MD-224 but employing piperidine or cyclohexyl to further rigidify the linker. Compound 26 was potent in the inhibition of cell growth in the RS4;11 cell line. However, 26 attained IC50 = 50 nM in the RS4;11Mut cell line, suggesting that this compound functioned as a GSPT1 molecular glue degrader. In comparison, 27 showed relatively weak activity in the RS4;11 cell line with IC50 = 200 nM and had no activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line. Reducing the alkyne moiety in 26 and 27 yielded 28 and 29, respectively. Although compounds 28 and 29 were reasonably potent and effective in inhibition of cell growth with IC50 = 19–34 nM in the RS4;11 cell line, they were 5–10 times weaker than MD-224. Compounds 28 and 29 showed no cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11Mut cell line, indicating that they are bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degraders.

We evaluated a number of potent MDM2 degraders for their in vivo efficacy in the RS4;11 xenograft model in mice. We selected 8, which was more potent than MD-224, 10, 24, and 25, which all have an ionizable group that may have an improved solubility over MD-224, and 26 with a very rigid 4-ethynylpiperidine linker. In our previous study,26 MD-224 administered at 50 mg/kg, every other day for 2 weeks induced complete tumor regression in the RS4;11 xenograft model. Hence, we decided to evaluate these new MDM2 degraders in the RS4;11 xenograft model using the same dose schedule with the data summarized in Figure 2.

Efficacy evaluation of degraders 8, 10, 24, 25, and 26 in the xenograft model of the RS4;11 parental cell line in mice.

In the efficacy experiment, degrader 24 was found to be toxic after the first dose and was not evaluated further. Interestingly, its analogue, degrader 25, which contains a pyrazole group in the linker, was found to be not toxic. However, degrader 25 showed minimal tumor growth inhibition. Degrader 10, with an ionizable amine group in the linker, was effective in inhibition of tumor growth but failed to achieve tumor regression. Degrader 8 was capable of achieving complete tumor regression, but tumors started to regrow shortly after the treatment was stopped. Degrader 26, which contains a rigid 4-ethynylpiperidine linker, achieved complete tumor regression, which persisted for 30 days. Hence, our efficacy study identified 8 and 26 as promising MDM2 degraders.

Based upon the promising in vivo efficacy data for 8 and 26, we designed and synthesized 30 (MD-265) by combining the structural features used in these two compounds.

We evaluated MD-265, together with MD-224, 8 and 26 in the RS4;11 cell line for their activity in cell growth inhibition (Figure 3). We also included MI-1063, which is the corresponding MDM2 inhibitor, in our evaluation (Figure 3). MD-265 achieved an IC50 value of 0.7 nM. In comparison, MD-224, 8 and 26 attained IC50 values of 2, 1, and 3 nM, respectively. The MDM2 inhibitor MI-1063 had an IC50 value of 179 nM. Hence, MD-265 is the most potent MDM2 degrader and is >200 times more potent than MI-1063 in cell growth inhibition in the RS4;11 cell line. Furthermore, MD-265 showed no cell growth inhibition activity (IC50 > 1,000 nM, Figure S2) and no degradation of GSPT1 (Figure S1) in the RS4;11Mut cell line, suggesting that it is a bona fide PROTAC MDM2 degrader.

Design of degrader MD-265 by the combination of structural features of 8 and 26. Cell growth inhibition of MD-265 in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 leukemia cell lines with MD-224, degraders 8 and 26 and inhibitor MI-1063 were included as controls.

MD-265, MD-224, and MI-1063 were further assessed in the p53 wild-type MV4;11 leukemia cell line for their ability to inhibit cell growth (Figure 3). MD-265 and MD-224 attained IC50 values of 2 and 7 nM, respectively, whereas MI-1063 achieved IC50 = 93 nM. Hence, MD-265 was 3- and 39-times more potent than MD-224 and MI-1063, respectively, in cell growth inhibition in the MV4;11 leukemia cell line.

We next evaluated the effect of MD-265 on p53 and MDM2 proteins in the RS4;11 cell line by Western blotting analysis, with MI-1061,30 a previously reported potent MDM2 inhibitor included as the control (Figure 4).

Analysis of the levels of MDM2 and p53 proteins by Western blot in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines treated with degrader MD-265 and MDM2 inhibitor, MI-1061.

Our data showed that MI-1061 effectively induced a robust increase of the levels of both p53 and MDM2 proteins in both cell lines carrying wild-type p53, consistent with its design as an inhibitor of the MDM2-p53 PPI and our previous data.30 In contrast, while MD-265 dramatically increased the levels of p53 protein at concentrations as low as 1–3 nM with merely a 2 h treatment time in both cell lines, it failed to significantly upregulate the levels of MDM2 protein. In fact, in the RS4;11 cell line, even 1 nM MD-265 was very effective in reducing the levels of MDM2 protein as compared to the control treatment. The MV4;11 cell line was found to have a very low level of MDM2 protein. MD-265 at 3–30 nM did not increase the levels of MDM2 protein compared to the control treatment in the MV4;11 cell line, whereas MI-1061 dramatically increased the levels of MDM2 protein. Since robust increase of p53 protein should lead to dramatic upregulation of MDM2 on the mRNA level, the lack of increase of MDM2 protein when treated with MD-265 in both RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines clearly indicated that MDM2 protein was effectively and quickly depleted with two hours treatment time by MD-265.

We performed a qRT-PCR analysis to examine transcription for a set of p53-regulated genes, including MDM2, p21, PUMA, as well as TP53 (Figure 5). In the RS4;11 cell line, MD-265 at 10 nM with 3 h of treatment time effectively increased the mRNA level of MDM2, p21, and PUMA by 8–10 fold and was at least as effective as MI-1063 at 300 nM. Consistent with their mechanisms of action, MD-265 and MI-1063 had no effect on the mRNA levels of p53.

qRT-PCR analysis of p53-regulated genes, including MDM2, p21, and PUMA, as well as p53 itself in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cells treated with MD-265 and MI-1063 for 3 h at indicated concentrations.

Similarly, in the MV4;11 cells, MD-265 at 30 nM effectively increased the levels of mRNA of MDM2 and p21 by 10-fold and PUMA by 3-fold but had no effect on the mRNA of p53. In comparison, MI-1063 at 300 nM also effectively increased the mRNA level of MDM2, p21, and PUMA but was less effective than MD-265 at 30 nM. These data showed that while both MD-265 and MI-1063 effectively activate p53, MD-265 is at least 30-times and 10-times more potent than MI-1063 in RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines, respectively.

We further investigated the mechanism of action for MD-265 in a cell growth inhibition assay and by Western blotting analysis in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines with the data summarized in Figure 6. Combination of lenalidomide, a cereblon ligand, with MD-265 effectively increased MDM2 protein and reduced p53 protein compared to MD-265 alone (Figure 6A,B), indicating that lenalidomide effectively attenuated MDM2 degradation and activation of p53 by MD-265. In the cell growth inhibition assay, lenalidomide also effectively reduced the cell growth inhibition by MD-265 in both the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines (Figure 6C,D). In comparison, lenalidomide modestly increased the cell growth inhibition activity for MI-1063 in the RS4;11 cell line (Figure 6C) but had a minimal effect in the MV4;11 cell line (Figure 6D). Hence, our data showed that lenalidomide effectively reduced the level of MDM2 degradation induced by MD-265 and attenuated the cell growth inhibition activity for MD-265, supporting the PROTAC mechanism for MD-265.

We synthesized and evaluated control compounds 30a and 30b (Figure 6E) for MD-265. In 30a, the MDM2 inhibitor portion in MD-265 was replaced with its enantiomer. In 30b, we installed a N-methyl group onto the piperidine-2,6-dione moiety of the cereblon ligand to block its binding to cereblon. Both 30a and 30b showed much weaker cell growth inhibition activity in the RS4;11 cell line than MD-265 (Figure 6F). These data further demonstrated that the potent cell growth inhibition activity of MD-265 requires effective binding to both MDM2 and cereblon proteins, consistent with its PROTAC mechanism.

We evaluated MD-265 in a panel of acute leukemia cell lines with the degrader 26 and MI-1061,30 our previously reported MDM2 inhibitor as the controls (Table 4). In each of these acute leukemia cell lines carrying a wild-type p53, MD-265 potently inhibited cell growth and achieved IC50 values of 0.9 nM to 212 nM. Consistently, MD-265 was >3-times more potent than 26 and >5–197 times more potent than the inhibitor MI-1061 in each of these cell lines.

In mice, MD-265 had a very low clearance (Cl = 1.1 mL/min/kg), a modest volume of distribution (Vss = 0.54 L/kg), a long elimination half-life (T1/2 = 6.1 h), and a very high plasma exposure. The PK data support further evaluation of MD-265 as a potential development candidate.

We next assessed the in vivo efficacy of MD-265 in the RS4;11 xenograft model with MD-224 included as the control (Figure 7). MD-265 at 25 mg/kg was capable of achieving complete tumor regression dosed at every other day for 2 weeks. In fact, after a single dose, the tumor size was reduced by >70% and after 2 doses, all tumors were reduced to unmeasurable size. The complete tumor regression was long-lasting: 34 days after the last dose, all mice remained tumor-free. Significantly, there were no weight loss or other signs of toxicity during and after the treatment. In comparison, 50 mg/kg MD-224 was needed to achieve complete tumor regression without causing animal weight loss or other signs of toxicity (Figure 7), similar to what we reported previously.

Antitumor activity of MD-265 in the RS4;11 xenograft model, with MD-224 included as the control.

In our previous study, we have shown that while our potent MDM2 inhibitor can induce complete tumor regression in the RS4;11 xenograft model, tumors regrew quickly.9,29 Our analysis showed that essentially all of those regrown tumors carried mutated p53. Consistent with our preclinical data, p53 mutations also quickly emerged in patients treated with an MDM2 inhibitor.31,32 Therefore, development of resistance of tumor cells to MDM2 inhibitors is a major obstacle for their successful development as a new class of anticancer agents.

Because MD-265 induced complete and rapid tumor regression of RS4;11 tumors, we investigated its acquired resistance in vivo (Figure 8). It was found that for those five completely regressed tumors treated with 25 mg/kg of MD-265 for 2 weeks, it took 43, 56, 63, and 73 days for 4 of the 5 tumors to regrow back to be measurable and one of the 5 mice remained tumor-free for 127 days, the end of the experiment. To investigate if these regrown tumors became resistant to MD-265, they were individually retreated with MD-265 when the tumors grew back to approximately 100 mm3. In each case, the tumor was regressed again after three doses of MD-265 at 25 mg/kg. Although the tumor regression was not as persistent as the initial round of treatment, our data nevertheless showed that these regrown tumors were still very responsive to MD-265, in sharp contrast to regrown tumors treated with our MDM2 inhibitor in our previous study.29

Investigation of resistance to MD-265 in vivo. Relapsed RS4;11 tumors were individually retreated with MD-265 at 25 mg/kg for either 3 or 6 doses as indicated.

Based upon the impressive antitumor efficacy of MD-265 in the RS4;11 xenograft model, we further evaluated MD-265 for its efficacy in a disseminated leukemia model established using the RS4;11 cell line (Figure 9). Instead of every other day dosing schedule used in the RS4;11 xenograft model, we evaluated MD-265 with weekly dosing schedule in this disseminated leukemia model.

Antitumor efficacy of MD-265 in a disseminated RS4;11 mouse model. MD-265 was dosed once a week at 25 or 50 mg/kg.

Our data showed that in the RS4;11 disseminated leukemia model, those mice treated with the vehicle control died of leukemia with a median survival time of 47.5 days and had 100% of mortality at day 50. In comparison, those mice treated with MD-265 at either 25 mg/kg or 50 mg/kg weekly dose had much longer survival, with a median survival time of 78.5 and 72 days, respectively (p < 0.0001). Hence MD-265 at 25 and 50 mg/kg weekly administration was capable of dramatically improving the median survival of the mice by ≥24.5 days, or ≥31% in this disseminated leukemia model.

Taken together, MD-265 is highly efficacious not only in the RS4;11 xenograft model but also in the RS4;11 disseminated leukemia model in mice.

Summary

In the present study, we described further optimization of our previously reported, first-in-class PROTAC MDM2 degrader MD-224 with a focus on modifications of the benzamide moiety in the MDM2 inhibitor portion and the linker, which culminated in the discovery of MD-265 as a highly potent and efficacious MDM2 degrader.

MD-265 effectively reduced the MDM2 protein and induced a robust increase of p53 protein in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines carrying wild-type p53 at concentrations as low as 1 nM. MD-265 induced strong upregulation of p53 targeted genes, including MDM2, p21, and PUMA in the RS4;11 and MV4;11 cell lines, indicating strong p53 activation and was much more potent than its corresponding MDM2 inhibitor. MD-265 potently inhibited cell growth in AML and ALL cell lines carrying wild-type p53 and was much more potent than its corresponding MDM2 inhibitor. Lenalidomide, a cereblon ligand, effectively blocked MDM2 degradation and p53 activation induced by MD-265 in the RS4;11 cell line and attenuated cell growth inhibition by MD-265, confirming that MD-265 is a bona fide MDM2 degrader. MD-265 displayed an excellent intravenous PK profile in mice, rats, and dogs. MD-265 achieved complete and persistent tumor regression in the RS4;11 xenograft tumor model without causing any signs of toxicity in mice. Of significance, when regressed tumors eventually regrew, MD-265 was still effective in inducing tumor regression, suggesting a lack of resistance to those regrown tumors and conferring a major advantage over MDM2 inhibitors. Furthermore, the weekly administration of MD-265 dramatically improved the survival of mice in the RS4;11 disseminated leukemia model, suggesting that the weekly administration of MD-265 could be highly efficacious for the treatment of patients with acute leukemia patients. Taken together, our data demonstrate that MD-265 is a very promising MDM2 degrader as a new therapy for human leukemia and potentially other types of human cancers carrying wild-type p53.

In our previous study,27 we have demonstrated that simple structural modifications can convert a bona fide MDM2 PROTAC degrader into a molecular glue GSPT1 degrader. In the present study, we further showed that even very minor structural modifications in the linker portion can also completely change the underlying molecular mechanism of the designed degrader molecule, transforming a bona fide MDM2 PROTAC degrader into a molecular glue GSPT1 degrader. For example, compound 1 was obtained by simply changing the phenyl substitution pattern from the para-position in MD-224 to the meta-position and was a potent and effective GSPT1 degrader. Our study therefore suggests that great cautions are needed to carefully validate the underlying molecular mechanism of action for PROTAC degraders designed using a cereblon ligand.

Experimental Section

General Information

Unless otherwise stated, all commercial reagents were used as supplied without further purification, and all reactions were performed in dry solvent under anhydrous conditions. NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker 400 Ascend spectrometer at a 1H frequency of 400 MHz and a 13C frequency of 100 MHz. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to the internal standard. The final products were purified on a preparative HPLC column (Waters 2545, Quaternary Gradient Module) with a SunFire Prep C18 OBD 5 μm, 50 mm × 100 mm reverse phase column. The mobile phase was a gradient of solvent A (H2O with 0.1% TFA) and solvent B (MeCN with 0.1% TFA) at a flow rate of 60 mL/min and 1%/min increase of solvent B. All final compounds have purity ≥95%. Mass spectral analyses and purity of all final compounds were determined on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC/QDa mass detector using a reverse phase column (SunFire, C18 5 μm, 4.6 mm × 150 mm) and a solvent gradient of A (H2O with 0.1% of formic acid) and solvent B (CH3CN with 0.1% of formic acid).

All the PROTAC molecules were synthesized by amide coupling according to the procedure described for 1.

Cell Growth Inhibition, Western Blotting, and Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

The parental RS4;11 and MV-4–11 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured under recommended conditions by ATCC. The RS4;11Mut cell line was generated as previously described.27 OCI-AML2, OCI-AML3, OCI-AML5, MOLM-13, MOLM-14, ML-2, and SIG-M5 cell lines were purchased from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ—German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH—and handled according to the vender’s instructions.

In all experiments, all of the human leukemia cells were used within three months of thawing fresh vials. RS4;11, MOLM-13, MOLM-14, and ML-2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. MV-4–11 cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. SIG-M5 cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium containing 20% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. OCI-AML2, OCI-AML3, and OCI-AML5 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM)-alpha containing 20% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

In cell growth experiments, cells were seeded in 96-well cell culture plates at a density of 10,000–20,000 cells/well in 100 μL of culture medium. Each compound evaluated was serially diluted in the appropriate medium, and 100 μL of the diluted solution containing the tested compound was added to the appropriate wells of the cell plate. After the addition of the tested compound, the cells were incubated for 4 days at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cell growth was evaluated by a lactate dehydrogenase-based WST-8 assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) using a Tecan Infinite M1000 multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Morrisville, NC). The WST-8 reagent was added to the plate, incubated for at least 1 h, and measured at 450 nm. The readings were normalized to the DMSO-treated cells, and the IC50 was calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 6 or 7 software.

For Western blot analysis, 2 × 106 cells/well were treated with compounds at the indicated concentrations at various times. Cells were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. An amount of 20 μg of total protein was run in each lane of a PAGE-SDS and blotted into polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Primary antibodies for immunoblotting were MDM2 (SMP14), p53 (DO-1), and GAPDH (FL-335) purchased from Santa Cruz. Secondary antibodies: goat antirabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (catalog #31460) and goat antimouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (catalog #31444) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

For qRT-PCR analysis, RNA was extracted from the indicated cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed using the high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using a Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression levels were analyzed by the comparative ΔΔCt method, where the Ct values of the genes of interest were normalized to GAPDH. The primers for the following genes were directly purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (catalog #4331182): MDM2 (Hs01066930_m1), CDKN1A (Hs00355782_m1), BBC3 (Hs00248075_m1), TP53 (Hs01034249_m1), and GAPDH (Hs02758991_g1).

Efficacy Studies in the RS4;11 Models in Mice

All animal studies were performed under an animal protocol (PRO00009463 and PRO00011174) approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Michigan, in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health.

Efficacy Study in the RS4;11 Xenograft Model

To develop xenograft tumors, 5 × 106 RS4;11 cells with 50% Matrigel were injected subcutaneously on the dorsal side of severe combined immunodeficient mice (SCID), obtained from Charles River Co., one tumor per mouse. When tumors reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to treatment and vehicle control groups and treated with drugs at indicated doses, using a formulation of 25% PEG400, 8% Cremophor EL, 64% PBS, and 3% 0.5 M NaOH to adjust pH to 7. Animals were monitored daily for any signs of toxicity, weighed 2–3 times per week during the treatment, and weighed at least weekly after the treatment ended. Tumor size was measured 2–3 times per week using electronic calipers during the treatment period and at least weekly after the treatment was ended. Tumor volume was calculated as V = L × W2/2, where L is the length, and W is the width of the tumor.

Efficacy Study in the RS4;11 Disseminated Leukemia Model in Mice

To reduce the endogenous bone marrow cell population and facilitate engraftment, 5–6 week-old female NOD-SCID mice (Envigo; Indianapolis, IN) were sublethally preconditioned with cyclophosphamide (150 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection for 2 doses, separated by 24 h). One day after conditioning, the mice were given an intravenous injection of 5 × 106 RS4;11 human leukemia cells via the lateral tail vein. Two weeks after injection of leukemia cells, mice were randomized into groups of 10 and treated with MD-265 at indicated doses or vehicle, using a formulation of 25% PEG400, 8% Cremophor EL, 64% PBS, and 3% of 0.5 M NaOH to adjust pH to 7, by weekly intravenous injection. Mouse survival was measured as either death or exhibiting clinical signs of reaching a humane end point where euthanasia is required. Mice were monitored for signs of illness (e.g., reduced mobility, respiratory problems, hind limb paralysis, pale paws, hunched back, and reduced weight) and weighed 2–3 times per week at the start of the study and then daily after the first death occurred.
