Response to “Comment on “Exercise Testing for Metabolic Flexibility: Time for Protocol Standardization”
Authors: Dale I. Lovell, Max Stuelcken, Alexander Eagles
Published: 2025

We would like to thank Dr Chavez-Guevara [1] for the insightful and detailed comments on our manuscript [2]. The authors welcome the interest and thoughtful engagement with our work, and we appreciate the opportunity to clarify and expand upon several key points raised in Dr Chavez-Guevara’s letter to the editor.1

Firstly, our Current Opinion article [2] was never intended to be an exhaustive review of the literature. Its purpose was to highlight factors that may need to be considered when developing a standardised protocol and some of the challenges that may need to be overcome. Therefore, if there was some evidence in the literature that a factor should be considered, even if the evidence was mixed, we included this factor.

Conceptual Assumptions Around Maximal Fat Oxidation (MFO)

Although maximal fat oxidation (MFO) may not fully capture the complexity of metabolic flexibility (MF), it is a practical and quantifiable metric for assessing fat oxidation capacity during exercise [3]. The modest correlation with energy expenditure transitions (R² = 0.22) cited by Dr Chávez-Guevara [1] does not negate its utility, but rather highlights that MFO does indeed reflect some aspect of the metabolic process. Frandsen and colleagues [4] recently reported that plasma free fatty acid (FFA) concentration during exercise was closely associated with MFO in both men and women, indicating that MFO may in some part represent the transition in FFA availability. Similarly, subcutaneous adipose-derived FFAs have been shown to be major contributors to MFO at submaximal exercise intensities [5].

We never intended to suggest that MFO should act as a stand-alone metric of MF. Rather, MFO should be considered in association with Fatmax (the exercise intensity at which MFO occurs) to gain meaningful insight into MF, because it reflects an individual’s capacity to utilize fat efficiently at lower intensities, which is particularly relevant for metabolic health. Furthermore, we recommend the use of blood lactate measurements to improve the validity of MFO and Fatmax by providing insights into the transition from fat to carbohydrate oxidation during increasing exercise workloads [6]. By integrating blood lactate measurements with MFO and Fatmax, researchers can better assess the dynamic interplay between substrate utilization and energy expenditure transitions, addressing the original critique that MFO alone fails to capture rapid energy demands or FFA mobilization. Other measures suggested by Dr Chavez-Guevara [1] may provide additional information but require specialised equipment or invasive procedures and are often challenging to measure in practice.

MFO Fails to Represent Fat Utilization during Steady-State Exercise

It is not surprising that test duration may affect MFO when assessing substrate utilization. This is due to the body’s significant shift in reliance on fat and carbohydrate stores over time, influenced by physiological and hormonal changes. As emphasized by Martin [7], Purdom et al. [5], and Romijn et al. [8], the duration of exercise itself is a critical variable that affects the body’s selection and oxidation of different fuel sources. The protocol used by Chavez-Guevara and colleagues [9] for the assessment of fat oxidation during the graded exercise test may have also contributed to the disparity in findings. A step duration of 3 min with data averaged over the last 2 min of each stage may not have been of sufficient duration to reach steady state. Generally, 4–6-minute stages are required for sedentary and clinical patients who have slower oxygen uptake kinetics with the last minute of data analysed [2, 10]. Additionally, some sedentary and clinical participants may not be able to complete a 40-60-minute protocol as suggested by Dr Chavez-Guevara [1] at the required intensity, potentially limiting the applicability, safety, and interpretability of such testing in these populations.

Moderators of MFO

Role of Ovarian Hormones in MFO Discrepancies between Sexes

The influence of hormones was introduced because they could potentially affect MFO. While some studies have reported no sex differences in MFO in middle-aged, older, or obese individuals, others have reported higher fat oxidation in older women compared to men [11, 12] and in obese women compared to women of normal weight [13] during submaximal exercise. While the work by Frandsen and colleagues [14] demonstrates that MFO does not change across menstrual cycle phases, these findings are not consistent with others [15] and this may be due to the classification of participants. A recent workshop on the stages of reproductive aging (STRAW) recommends using not only age but other biomarkers to identify the classifications [16]. Additionally the protocol utilized to determine fat oxidation (3-min stages with the average of the last 60–90 s) utilized by Frandsen [14] may not have been of sufficient duration as previously mentioned.

Dietary Patterns and MFO

We certainly agree that various diets can influence the assessment of MFO. However, it is not possible to mention all dietary intakes. Instead, we wanted to merely highlight the importance of both acute and chronic food intake on MFO assessment [17].

Type of Exercise and MFO

There are conflicting findings on the effect of exercise modality on MFO. While some studies as mentioned by Dr Chavez-Guevara [1] have reported higher values in treadmill vs. cycling, other studies have found no difference [10]. Therefore, as indicated, there does not seem to be a consensus on which modality is preferred. We attest that the same modality should be used for repeated tests to reduce possible errors in research or when monitoring the progression of clients. Furthermore, a sport specific modality should be used for athletes.

Integrative Analysis of MFO and Blood Lactate Levels

Blood lactate can be used as an indication of when to terminate a sub-maximal test (two consecutive workload stages above 2mmol/l) because at this point it is likely that MFO has been reached. Currently other studies have used a percentage of maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max), heart rate, and respiratory exchange ratio (RER), all of which have been shown to be unreliable [18, 19].

Methodological Considerations for Assessing MFO

MFO Reliability

Perhaps the main point of our Current Opinion article [2] was the call for protocol standardization to improve the reliability of MFO assessment. While some studies [20] have found poor retest reliability in MFO, others have found good agreement between tests [21, 22]. This is most likely due to differences in the protocols used by the studies. For example, Chrzanowski-Smith and colleagues assessed MFO retest reliability over a 7–28 day period and used similar protocols for both men and women aged between 18 and 65 years [20]. The long time period between tests, the large age ranges of the participants, and the use of similar protocols with 25 W step increments for all participants may have influenced the reliability asessement. As with a graded exercise test, the protocol in particular must be modified to suit the inidivdual to ensure a precise and reliable measure of cardiorespiratory fitness [23].

Methods Applied for MFO

We acknowledge that there are several different approaches to data analysis such as polynomial regression, sinusoidal modelling, and curve shape analysis as suggested by Dr Chavez-Guevara [1]. These provide a valuable adjunct to the assessment of MFO and can be seen as complementary tools that improve our understanding rather than replace the foundational value of MFO. For example, the rate of change of fat oxidation across intensities, or the breadth of the curve, can be explored in depth once MFO has established a reference point. These methods may help to reduce fluctuations in MFO values due to physiological variability or measurement noise. Furthermore, they offer a global assessment of fat oxidation dynamics, not just the peak, and may reveal important adaptive responses to training or disease.

Conclusion

We appreciate the opportunity to engage in a constructive scientific dialogue on this important topic and would welcome the opportunity to collaborate with other experts in the field on the development of a consensus paper on the standardization of the protocols for exercise testing for MF.
