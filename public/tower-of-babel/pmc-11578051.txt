Renal Denervation Lowers Nighttime Blood Pressure in True Resistant Hypertension
Authors: Kazuomi Kario, David E. Kandzari, Felix Mahfoud, Michael A. Weber, Roland E. Schmieder, Konstantinos Tsioufis, Minglei Liu, Michael Böhm, Raymond R. Townsend
Published: 2024

Hypertension remains highly prevalent worldwide and largely uncontrolled. In addition to lifestyle modification and drug therapy, catheter-based renal denervation (RDN) is an additional and complementary treatment option.1 The recently reported global, randomized, blinded, sham-controlled Global Clinical Study of Renal Denervation With the Symplicity Spyral™ Multi-electrode Renal Denervation System in Patients With Uncontrolled Hypertension on Standard Medical Therapy (SPYRAL HTN-ON MED) trial of catheter-based radiofrequency denervation (RDN; Symplicity Spyral, Medtronic, Galway) showed significant baseline-adjusted reductions in office and nighttime blood pressure (BP), but not for 24-hour mean systolic blood pressure (SBP).2 However, the efficacy of RDN in patients with verified resistant hypertension, especially during the high-risk nighttime and early morning period, has not been characterized.

This post hoc subanalysis focused on the specific subgroup of patients with true resistant hypertension defined as a prescription of ≥3 antihypertensive medications, including a diuretic.3 Further, only patients with adherence documented at baseline via tandem high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectroscopy of urine and plasma were included. Daytime (7 am–10 pm) and nighttime/early morning (10 pm–7 am) BPs were determined from the individual 24-hour ambulatory BP readings. Comparisons of BP changes between treatment arms were performed using ANCOVA adjusting for baseline BP. Supportive data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patient characteristics were similar between RDN (N=24) and sham (N=18) groups within the cohort, including age (55±12 versus 55±9 years, respectively), sex (92% versus 100% male), and race (13% versus 22% Black). Baseline SBP was also similar between groups at baseline for office (164±7 mm Hg RDN versus 160±6 mm Hg sham), daytime (155±7 versus 155±6 mm Hg), and nighttime/early morning (142±10 versus 140±9 mm Hg; P=NS for all baseline comparisons using unpaired t test). The 6-month between-group difference in changes in office SBP tended, although not significantly, to favor RDN treatment (−8.7±13.3 mm Hg RDN; −1.6±9.4 mm Hg sham; between-group difference, −7.5 [95% CI, −15.6 to 0.7]; P=0.073). Likewise, the between-group difference for daytime ambulatory SBP was not different between RDN and sham groups (Figure A). However, the reduction in nighttime/early morning SBP was significantly greater in the RDN compared with sham patients, although the detected number of antihypertensive medications was similar between RDN and sham at baseline and did not change in either group at 6 months (baseline-adjusted ANCOVA analysis; Figure B). Differences in the 24-hour circadian variation in SBP at baseline and 6 months for both groups are highlighted in Figure C and D, respectively. A clear and greater reduction in SBP was observed in the RDN group compared with the sham group at 6 months that warrants further exploration. Differences in diastolic BP were not significantly different between groups but also tended to favor the RDN group (not shown).

A surprising result of the primary analysis of the SYMPLICITY HTN-ON MED was the observed dynamic antihypertensive drug adherence behavior that was biased between the randomized RDN and sham-control groups.2 Indeed, more patients in the sham-control group increased antihypertensive drug burden. The present results are the first to focus on patients that met the strict definition for true resistant hypertension and suggest a complementary effect of RDN in drug-adherent patients to sustain BP reductions, especially during the night and early morning. Control of nighttime-early morning BP is especially important in drug-adherent patients since cardiovascular risk is highest during these time periods.4 However, BP control has been shown to be poor during the early morning in patients taking up to 3 antihypertensive medications, even when office and daytime SBP were well controlled.5

Only a limited subgroup of patients met the strict criteria for true resistant hypertension. However, the results are consistent with those observed for the entire population,2 and hence it is reasonable to consider them results hypothesis-generating. Further investigation should focus on the complementary BP-lowering effects of RDN and drug therapy during the highest risk periods within the 24-hour circadian cycle.
