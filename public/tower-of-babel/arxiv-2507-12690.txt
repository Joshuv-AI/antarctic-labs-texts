NA-DiD: Extending Difference-in-Differences with Capabilities Journal: Mathematical Social Sciences
Stanisław M. S. Halkiewicz

Abstract
This paper introduces the Non-Additive Difference-in-Differences (NA-DiD) framework, which extends classical DiD by incorporating non-additive measures the Choquet integral for effect aggregation. It serves as a novel econometric tool for impact evaluation, particularly in settings with non-additive treatment effects. First, we introduce the integral representation of the classial DiD model, and then extend it to non-additive measures, therefore deriving the formulae for NA-DiD estimation. Then, we give its theoretical properties. Applying NA-DiD to a simulated hospital hygiene intervention, we find that classical DiD can overestimate treatment effects, f.e. failing to account for compliance erosion. In contrast, NA-DiD provides a more accurate estimate by incorporating non-linear aggregation. The Julia implementation of the techniques used and introduced in this article is provided in the appendices.

Introduction

1.1
Overview of DiD models

Difference-in-Differences (DiD, called also DD, Diff-in-diff or ) models are a fundamental econometric technique employed to estimate causal effects by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between a treatment group and a control group
[
1
,
2
]
. This method is particularly valuable in observational studies where random assignment is infeasible, allowing researchers to infer causal relationships from natural experiments
[
3
]
.

DiD models trace their origins back to the cholera outbreak that occurred in the Soho district of London. During this outbreak, John Snow conducted a pivotal analysis by comparing the incidence of cholera among local residents who obtained their water from the infamous Broad Street water pump against those who sourced their water from different suppliers
[
4
,
5
,
1
]
.

The core principle of DiD involves assessing the differential effect of a treatment by examining the variations in the outcomes before and after the intervention between the two groups. This approach effectively controls for unobserved factors that are constant over time, isolating the impact of treatment under the critical assumption of parallel trends, which in the absence of treatment would have experienced similar changes in outcomes over time
[
6
]
.

DiD models have been extensively applied across various fields. In public policy analysis, which is one of the sectors where DD methodology is employed the most often, they serve as a robust tool to evaluate the impact of legislative changes, such as evaluating the effects of minimum wage laws on employment rates
[
7
,
1
,
2
]
. In the realm of education, DiD has been used to evaluate the influence of economic events on educational improvements, providing information on how policy interventions affect educational outcomes
[
8
,
9
]
. Similarly, in the healthcare sector, DiD models have been instrumental in measuring the effectiveness of policy implementations on health outcomes, offering a framework to assess interventions where randomized controlled trials are impractical
[
6
]
.

Despite their widespread use, traditional DiD models are subject to several limitations. A significant concern is the potential violation of the parallel trends assumption, which can lead to biased estimates if the treatment and control groups would not have followed similar trajectories in the absence of the treatment
[
1
,
10
]
. Additionally, when treatments are implemented at different times across units or when there is heterogeneity in treatment effects, conventional DiD estimators may produce misleading results
[
11
,
12
]
. Recent methodological advancements have sought to address these issues by developing more flexible models that relax the parallel trends assumption and account for treatment effect heterogeneity
[
13
]
.

1.1.1
Estimation of DiD Models

Estimation of DiD models follows a straightforward calculation process that involves comparing differences in means between treatment and control groups over time. This process, as described by
Halkiewicz 2024
,
Halkiewicz 2023
, can be illustrated through the following table:

Table 1:
Estimation of Difference-in-Differences coefficient by using the
difference table
method

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}\text{Time}&\text{Affected (1)}&\text{Unaffected (0)}&\text{Difference}\\
\hline\cr\text{Post-treatment (1)}&\bar{y}_{1}&\bar{y}_{2}&\bar{y}_{1}-\bar{y}_{2}\\
\text{Pre-treatment (0)}&\bar{y}_{3}&\bar{y}_{4}&\bar{y}_{3}-\bar{y}_{4}\\
\hline\cr\text{Difference}&\bar{y}_{1}-\bar{y}_{3}&\bar{y}_{2}-\bar{y}_{4}&DD_{e}\\
\end{array}

The DD estimator,
DD_{e}
(naming convention after
Halkiewicz 2023
), is computed as the difference-in-differences of group means
[
1
]
:

DD_{e}=(\bar{y}_{1}-\bar{y}_{3})-(\bar{y}_{2}-\bar{y}_{4}),

(1)

where:

1.

\bar{y}_{1}
: Mean outcome for the treated group post-treatment,

2.

\bar{y}_{3}
: Mean outcome for the treated group pre-treatment,

3.

\bar{y}_{2}
: Mean outcome for the control group post-treatment,

4.

\bar{y}_{4}
: Mean outcome for the control group pre-treatment.

This expression can be rewritten as:

DD_{e}=(\bar{y}_{1}-\bar{y}_{2})-(\bar{y}_{3}-\bar{y}_{4}),

(2)

which highlights the order-invariance of the estimation process. Regardless of the sequence in which differences are calculated, the resulting value of
DD_{e}
remains constant
[
1
]
.

Formally, the estimator can also be expressed in terms of conditional expectations:

\begin{split}DD_{e}=&\big[E(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=1)-E(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=0)\big]\\
&-\big[E(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=1)-E(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=0)\big].\end{split}

(3)

Here:

1.

D_{i}
: A binary indicator for the treated group (
D_{i}=1
) or control group (
D_{i}=0
),

2.

T_{t}
: A binary indicator for the post-treatment period (
T_{t}=1
) or pre-treatment period (
T_{t}=0
).

This formulation makes explicit that
DD_{e}
is the difference in the changes in the expected outcomes for the treated (
D_{i}=1
) and control (
D_{i}=0
) groups over time
[
1
]
. The calculation relies on the assumption of parallel trends, ensuring that any difference in trends between the two groups is attributable to the treatment effect
[
1
]
.

The DiD coefficient can also be expressed as the coefficient of the interaction term in a specific econometric model estimated via the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method. This equivalence holds both in terms of value and interpretation, as shown in
Halkiewicz 2024
. The econometric model takes the following form:

y_{it}=\alpha+\beta D_{i}+\gamma T_{t}+\delta(D_{i}\cdot T_{t})+\epsilon_{it},

(4)

where:

1.

y_{it}
: The outcome variable for unit
i
at time
t
,

2.

D_{i}\cdot T_{t}
: The interaction term representing units in the treated group during the post-treatment period,

3.

\delta
: The coefficient on the interaction term, representing the DiD estimate,

4.

\epsilon_{it}
: The error term.

The coefficient
\delta
on the interaction term
D_{i}\cdot T_{t}
corresponds exactly to the DiD coefficient derived in the framework of mean differences. This equivalence ensures that the DiD methodology can be understood both in terms of direct differences in group means (as in Equation 9) and as a regression coefficient in the econometric specification. This duality highlights the flexibility of the DiD approach and provides a robust basis for interpretation.

1.2
Additive and Non-Additive Measures

In classical measure theory, an
additive measure
is a set function
\mu
that assigns a numerical value to sets in a way that satisfies the following fundamental properties
[
14
,
15
]
. For any two sets
A
and
B
, the measure
\mu
is additive if:

\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B)-\mu(A\cap B).

(5)

This definition generalises the property of disjoint additivity, where for disjoint sets
A
and
B
(i.e.,
A\cap B=\emptyset
), the measure simplifies to:

\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B).

(6)

The inclusion of the term
\mu(A\cap B)
ensures that overlap between sets is not double-counted, which is essential when sets are not mutually exclusive. Additive measures underpin much of probability theory, as well as the Lebesgue integral, where consistency and linearity are crucial for modelling independent contributions from different subsets of a space
[
16
]
.

Despite their mathematical elegance and computational simplicity, additive measures often fail to account for the complexities of real-world phenomena. In many scenarios, interactions between sets or events introduce synergies or redundancies that additive measures cannot capture. For example, the joint impact of two policies may not equal the sum of their individual effects due to complementarities or conflicts between the policies
[
17
]
.

To address these limitations,
non-additive measures
extend the framework of classical measures by relaxing the additivity property. A non-additive measure, or
capacity
, is a set function
\nu
that does not necessarily satisfy additivity. For example, in a non-additive framework, it is possible that:

\nu(A\cup B)\neq\nu(A)+\nu(B)-\nu(A\cap B).

(7)

Non-additive measures usually adhere to fewer number of properties, typically only groundedness (i.e.
\nu(\emptyset)=0
) and monotonicity (i.e.,
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
whenever
A\subseteq B
), then their additive counterparts
[
18
]
. This allows them to model interactions between sets in a more flexible way. This flexibility makes non-additive measures particularly useful for capturing phenomena such as synergy (where
\nu(A\cup B)>\nu(A)+\nu(B)
) or redundancy (where
\nu(A\cup B)<\nu(A)+\nu(B)
)
[
17
,
19
,
20
]
.

The theoretical development of non-additive measures has provided robust tools for modelling systems where traditional assumptions of independence and linearity are inadequate. Seminal contributions, such as Choquet’s
[
21
]
theory of capacities, have laid the groundwork for understanding and utilising non-additive measures in various disciplines. Furthermore, advanced studies on integrals with respect to non-additive measures, including the Choquet and Sugeno integrals, have deepened our understanding of their properties and applications
[
19
,
22
]
.

Although additive measures remain fundamental in many applications due to their simplicity and well-understood properties, non-additive measures offer a richer framework for analysing systems where interactions and dependencies are critical
[
17
,
23
]
.

1.3
Applications of Non-Additive Measures in Economics

Non-additive measures have gained significant attention in economics, particularly in the realms of decision theory and game theory
[
23
,
24
]
. These measures provide a flexible framework to address complexities that traditional additive models cannot adequately capture, such as ambiguity in decision-making and interactions among agents in cooperative settings. For an overview of different applications, see an excellent paper by
Heilpern 2002
.

1.3.1
Non-Additive Measures in Decision Theory

In decision theory, non-additive measures address situations involving uncertainty and ambiguity. Traditional expected utility theory, based on additive probability measures, assumes that decision-makers act as if they know the probabilities of uncertain outcomes. This framework is grounded in Savage’s theorem, which demonstrates that subjective probabilities can represent preferences under specific axioms
[
25
]
.

However, certain preference patterns, such as those highlighted by Ellsberg’s paradox, reveal the limitations of additive probabilities
[
26
]
. Ellsberg’s paradox shows that individuals often display ambiguity aversion, preferring bets with known probabilities over those with unknown probabilities, even when the expected values are identical
[
27
]
. Non-additive measures, such as those introduced by
Schmeidler 1989
, resolve these anomalies by allowing for the representation of ambiguity through capacities and the use of the Choquet integral. This approach generalises the expected utility model by incorporating decision-makers’ subjective weighting of uncertain events
[
23
,
29
,
30
]
.

Representation theorems for non-additive measures in decision theory demonstrate how preferences can be captured without requiring additivity. These models provide robust tools for analysing economic behaviour under uncertainty, offering a theoretical foundation for applications in finance, insurance, and policy analysis
[
25
,
31
]
.

1.4
Purpose of the Study

The primary aim of this research is to extend the Difference-in-Differences methodology through the integration of
non-additive measures
, addressing critical limitations of traditional DiD when applied to ambiguous or imperfect data. Conventional DiD frameworks assume precise treatment assignment and complete observability of outcomes, which often fails in practice. For instance, consider a nationwide healthcare policy where hospitals
partially adopt
the intervention due to logistical constraints, and compliance data is inconsistently recorded. Additive DiD would either exclude hospitals with missing compliance records or assume full adherence, potentially biasing estimates of the policy’s effect. Such ambiguity in treatment exposure—common in real-world settings—violates the additive structure of classical DiD, leading to unreliable causal inferences.

To address these challenges, we propose the
Non-Additive Difference-in-Differences
(NA-DiD) framework, which leverages
capacities
and the
Choquet integral
to model uncertainty in treatment adoption, measurement error, or heterogeneous participation. Unlike additive measures, capacities assign weights to subsets of units based on their
degree of exposure
to the treatment (e.g., hospitals with partial compliance), while the Choquet integral aggregates these weights non-linearly, accounting for interactions between observed and unobserved factors. This approach enables robust estimation even when treatment assignments are ambiguous, outcomes are mismeasured, or subgroups exhibit heterogeneous responses.

Another advantage that NA-DiD presents is allowing one to account for expected impact of an event. Thus, by selecting appropriate capacities (a term which will be explained later), it would return a value of ’surprise’ that we did not account for. In the presence of some expected change, classical DiD would overvalue the impact of the event.

2.1
Capacities

In section
1.2
, we introduced non-additive measures, and in the sections that followed, we explored some of their practical applications. In this section, we will revisit these measures, but with a perspective that aligns more closely with the main focus of this article. Capacities, an alternative term for non-additive measures, extend classical additive measures by permitting the value assigned to a set to be influenced by the interactions and relationships among its members. Unlike their additive counterparts, which are limited by the requirement of strict additivity, this characteristic offers a more versatile framework for the modeling of systems characterized by dependencies, synergies, or redundancies.

Mathematically, a non-additive (standardized) measure
\nu
on a finite set
X
is a set function
\nu:2^{X}\to[0,1]
that satisfies:

1.

Monotonicity
: If
A\subseteq B
, then
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
,

2.

Boundary Conditions
:
\nu(\emptyset)=0
and
\nu(X)=1
.

Monotonicity ensures that larger sets are assigned greater or equal values, while the boundary conditions standardise the measure to a finite scale.

2.1.1
Examples of Non-Additive Measures

Several well-known classes of non-additive measures have been developed to address specific modelling needs:

1. Fuzzy Measures

Fuzzy measures generalize classical measures to accommodate uncertainty and imprecision, making them especially apt for cases where the precise contribution of elements to a set is ambiguous, thus frequently applied in systems theory and decision analysis
[
32
,
33
]
. Further extensions of fuzzy measures can be achieved through a double generalization of the Lovász extension formula
[
34
]
or via aggregation
[
35
]
.

2. Belief and Plausibility Functions

Belief and plausibility functions, derived from Dempster-Shafer theory, are non-additive measures applied to quantify degrees of belief
[
36
]
. For a particular set
A
, the belief function
\mathrm{Bel}(A)
indicates the cumulative support for
A
, whereas the plausibility function
\mathrm{Pl}(A)
signifies the maximum support that does not contradict
A

[
37
,
38
]
.

3. Capacity Classes

Certain categories of capacities, including
k
-additive measures and p-symmetric measures, are crafted to ease the calculation and depiction of non-additive measures. For instance,
k
-additive measures address interactions up to subsets of size
k
, thus simplifying the representation
[
39
]
.

2.1.2
Estimation of Non-Additive Measures from Data

Determining non-additive measures from data is essential for their application to real-world problems. Various methods have been devised to derive capacities from empirical data observations:

1. Optimization-Based Methods

Capacities can be estimated by solving optimization problems that align the measure with observed data.
Miranda et al. 2003
proposed a method for estimating non-additive measures from samples by minimizing the squared errors, ensuring that the estimated measure satisfies monotonicity constraints.

2. Entropy-Based Methods

Entropy-based approaches estimate capacities by maximizing an entropy function (e.g. Shannon entropy) subject to constraints imposed by the data. This method ensures that the resulting capacity is as uninformative as possible beyond what is implied by the data
[
41
]
.

3. Regression-Based Approaches

Regression methods fit a non-additive measure by treating it as a parameter in a regression model, allowing the estimation of interactions and dependencies directly from the data
[
17
]
.

Among these approaches, the optimization-based method proposed by
Miranda et al. 2003
will be used in Section
4
, as it provides a systematic way to estimate non-additive measures while ensuring theoretical consistency and empirical feasibility.

2.2
The Choquet Integral

The Choquet integral is a foundational tool for working with non-additive measures, enabling the integration of functions where interactions and dependencies among elements are explicitly modelled. Unlike classical integrals, which assume additive measures, the Choquet integral is defined with respect to capacities.

Formally, let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function over a finite set
X
, and let
\nu
be a capacity defined on the power set of
X
. The Choquet integral of
f
with respect to
\nu
is defined as:

\int f\,d\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(x_{(i)})-f(x_{(i-1)})\big)\nu(A_{(i)}),

(8)

where:
-
x_{(1)}\leq x_{(2)}\leq\dots\leq x_{(n)}
represents the non-decreasing order of
f(x)
,
-
A_{(i)}=\{x_{(i)},\dots,x_{(n)}\}
denotes the subsets of
X
corresponding to the ordered values,
-
f(x_{(0)})=0
for convenience.

The integral aggregates the increments in
f(x)
, weighted by the capacity
\nu
over the subsets
A_{(i)}
. This formulation accounts for both the individual contributions of elements and their interactions, making the Choquet integral particularly suited for analysing non-additive systems.

2.2.1
Properties of the Choquet Integral

The Choquet integral satisfies several important properties:

1.

Monotonicity
: If
f(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in X
, then
\int f\,d\nu\leq\int g\,d\nu
,

2.

Linearity with Respect to Constants
: For any constant
c
,
\int(f+c)\,d\nu=\int f\,d\nu+c
,

3.

Reduction to the Classical Integral
: When
\nu
is the Lebesgue measure, the Choquet integral reduces to the Lebesgue (or Riemann–Stieltjes) integral; more generally, it preserves key properties such as monotonicity and positive homogeneity.

Building on this,
Ponce and Spector 2023
demonstrated that under minimal assumptions, one can derive fundamental measure-theoretic results—such as Fatou’s lemma, monotone convergence, and dominated convergence—for Choquet integrals, thus placing them on a firm mathematical basis.
Harjulehto and Hurri-Syrjänen 2024
derived Sobolev-type, Poincaré, and Morrey inequalities for Choquet integrals with respect to Hausdorff content.

From
3.
, it follows that the Choquet integral coincides with the Riemann integral when
\nu
is additive and
f
is Riemann-integrable.

2.2.2
The Sugeno Integral and Other Approaches

Alongside the Choquet integral, a variety of other integration techniques have been devised for application with non-additive measures. Among these, the Sugeno integral stands out as one of the most recognized alternatives. Sugeno integral instead utilizes the minimum and maximum values of a function, rendering it especially appropriate for ordinal data or environments where qualitative thresholds hold significance.

The Sugeno integral for a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to a capacity
\nu
is defined as:

\int f\,d\nu=\bigvee_{A\subseteq X}\big(\bigwedge_{x\in A}f(x)\big)\nu(A),

(9)

where
\bigvee
and
\bigwedge
denote the maximum and minimum operations, respectively
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
. This integral is particularly useful in applications where aggregation depends on extreme values rather than averages or cumulative effects, such as in multi-criteria decision-making
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
.

Other approaches to integrating with non-additive measures include:

1.

The Pan Integral
: a generalization that combines features of both the Choquet and Sugeno integrals, accommodating a broader range of aggregation behaviors
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
,

2.

The Shilkret Integral
: an alternative focusing on product-based aggregation, which has applications in utility theory and risk assessment
[
54
,
55
]
.

While these methods offer specific advantages in some contexts, they do not suit this study’s aims. Thus, the Choquet integral will be employed to estimate the NA-DiD model. Notably, the use of the Choquet integral in econometrics and operations research is supported in the literature
[
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
24
]
.

3
Results

We begin by recalling the classical DiD estimator, which evaluates the causal treatment effect by comparing average outcomes across treatment and control groups over two time periods. The standard DiD estimator is given by:

\begin{split}DD_{e}=&\big[E(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=1)-E(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=0)\big]\\
&-\big[E(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=1)-E(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=0)\big].\end{split}

(10)

The notation remains as was introduced while discussing the eq.
3
.

To simplify notation, we define the treatment and control effects as differences in average outcomes across time periods for the respective groups:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{treat}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=1)-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=0),

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{control}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=1)-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=0).

(12)

Using this notation, the DiD estimator can be rewritten compactly as:

DD_{e}=\Delta_{\text{treat}}-\Delta_{\text{control}}.

(13)

This formulation illustrates the difference between the temporal variations in average outcomes for the treated and control cohorts, interpreted as the causal treatment effect, given the parallel trends assumption holds.

3.1
Deriving the NA-DiD Framework

In order to expand the traditional DiD framework into a non-additive context, we present a capacity
\nu
to represent uncertainties and interaction effects. The effects of treatment and control are now generalized to include dynamics that vary over time. Let:

1.

\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)=\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, post}}(t)-\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, pre}}(t)
,

2.

\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)=\bar{Y}_{\text{control, post}}(t)-\bar{Y}_{\text{control, pre}}(t)
,

where
t
represents time and
\bar{Y}
denotes the average outcome for the corresponding group and time period.
The new notation using
\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, post}}
,
\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, pre}}
,
\bar{Y}_{\text{control, post}}
,
\bar{Y}_{\text{control, pre}}
provides a more explicit and intuitive representation of group-specific means compared to the earlier notation
\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2},\bar{y}_{3},\bar{y}_{4}
introduced after
Halkiewicz 2024
,
Halkiewicz 2023
. While both notations capture the same quantities, the latter highlights the temporal and group distinctions more clearly.

3.1.1
Integral Representation of the Classical DiD Framework

To establish a bridge between the classical DiD model and its non-additive generalisation, we reformulate the classical estimator from eq.
10
in terms of Lebesgue integrals.

Utilizing the mean outcomes specific to the treated and control groups, the effects of treatment and control can be articulated as integrals over time:,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{treat}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}\big(\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, post}}(t)-\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, pre}}(t)\big)\,d\mu(t),

(14)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{control}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}\big(\bar{Y}_{\text{control, post}}(t)-\bar{Y}_{\text{control, pre}}(t)\big)\,d\mu(t),

(15)

, where
\mu(t)
is an additive, probabilistc measure across the time domain. It is important to note that while a probabilistic measure is typically represented as
dP
, we will maintain the notation
\mu(t)
in this context because it facilitates the shift to non-additive measures at a later stage. Substituting these into the classical DiD estimator results in:

DD_{e}=\int_{t}\big(\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)-\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)\big)\,d\mu(t)

(16)

This formulation shows that the classical DiD estimator aggregates the differences in treatment and control effects linearly, weighted by the additive measure
\mu(t)
. A proof of the equivalence of the integral and classical representations of the
\text{DD}_{e}
coefficient can be found in the appendix
A
. It is known that the OLS representation of
\text{DD}_{e}
is equivalent to the classical representation (see
1.1.1
and
Halkiewicz 2024
), so by
transitivity of implications we have that the integral representation is also equivalent to the OLS representation. However, a proof of the equivalence between the OLS representation and the integral representation without using the classical form can be found in Appendix
B
.

3.1.2
Transitioning to the Non-Additive Framework

The integral representation of the classical DiD model (eq.
16
) provides a natural foundation for extending the framework to accommodate non-additive measures. In the NA-DiD model, the probabilistic measure
\mu(t)
is replaced by a capacity
\nu(t)
. This generalisation allows the model to capture synergies, redundancies, or ambiguities in the data that cannot be addressed with an additive aggregation.

Under the NA-DiD framework, the treatment effect is defined as:

\Delta^{\nu}_{\text{DiD}}=\int\big(\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)-\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)\big)\,d\nu(t),

(17)

where
\nu(t)
represents a capacity function over the time domain. The determination of the capacity requires estimation from the dataset, which can be achieved by employing any of the techniques discussed in
2.1.2
.
How
\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)
and
\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)
, whether also by applying the Choquet integral or by regular conditional averages, is up to discussion as the most important step is applying the Choquet integral to their difference, but the author does not exclude that sometimes this difference may be important. This topic may be subjected to future research.
Importantly, the NA-DiD model coincides with the classical DiD framework when
\nu(t)
is a probability measure, i.e.,
d\nu(t)=dP
. This ensures that the NA-DiD model is a true generalisation, maintaining consistency with the classical approach in cases where additivity holds.

4
Simulations

This section serves a dual purpose: first, to evaluate the empirical performance of the NA-DiD estimator in comparison to the classical DiD estimator, and second, to provide a practical manual for applying NA-DiD in empirical research. By detailing the estimation process, data requirements, and interpretation of results, this section offers guidance on implementing NA-DiD in real-world applications.

The simulations explore the following fundamental questions:

1.

How does NA-DiD perform when treatment effects diverge from the assumption of additivity?

2.

Under which circumstances does NA-DiD yield more compelling estimates compared to the traditional DiD method?

3.

How does selecting a specific non-additive measure influence the estimation outcomes?

To address these questions, our simulation study is organized as follows: We begin by outlining the data-generating process along with its various specifications, ensuring that our simulated datasets accurately reflect realistic economic and policy evaluation contexts. Following this, we detail the estimation process for NA-DiD, placing emphasis on the
Miranda et al. 2003
methodology for extracting non-additive measures from the data. Subsequently, we juxtapose the NA-DiD and traditional DiD estimators across a range of simulated scenarios, emphasizing instances where non-additive aggregation results in notable enhancements. In the concluding step, we perform robustness checks to assess the sensitivity of the NA-DiD estimates, taking into account variations in sample size, distributional premises, and selection capacity.

4.1
Data Generation Process

To illustrate the applicability of the NA-DiD framework, we generate a synthetic dataset that captures the dynamic effects of a hospital hygiene intervention. This scenario reflects real-world complexities where policy adoption is non-monotonic — hospitals initially comply strictly with a new hygiene protocol, but adherence diminishes over time due to lack of enforcement.

4.1.1
Hypothetical Scenario Description

A set of hospitals is observed over multiple time periods, where a subset of them adopts a new, overly strict, hygiene protocol at a specified treatment start period. The primary outcome of interest is the
infection rate
(lower values indicate better hygiene practices). The intervention follows a non-linear adoption curve:

1.

before the treatment period, hospitals exhibit stable infection rates with minor seasonal variations;

2.

after treatment implementation, infection rates in treated hospitals initially decline.

3.

the maximum impact occurs approximately three periods after introduction.

4.

due to enforcement challenges, compliance wanes over time, causing the effect to diminish.

Control hospitals do not implement the new protocol and serve as a baseline for comparison.

4.1.2

The dataset consists of
N

T
periods. The infection rate for hospital
i

is given by:

Y_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\delta_{t}+\epsilon_{it}-D_{it}\cdot\tau(t),

(18)

where:

1.

\alpha_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,0.3)
represents the baseline infection rate, varying across hospitals.

2.

\delta_{t}=0.05\sin(0.5t)
introduces a seasonal component.

3.

\epsilon_{it}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.02)
captures random noise.

4.

D_{it}
is an indicator variable, equal to
1
if hospital
i
is treated at time

0
otherwise.

5.

\tau(t)
represents the non-linear treatment effect function, given by:

\tau(t)=0.4\exp(-0.3|t-t_{\text{peak}}|),\quad\text{for }t\geq t_{\text{treat}}.

(19)

Here,
t_{\text{peak}}=t_{\text{treat}}+3

4.2
Choice of Capacity Function

In the NA-DiD framework, the capacity function plays a crucial role in shaping how treatment effects are aggregated. Unlike the standard DiD model, which assumes an additive structure, the NA-DiD approach replaces the traditional linear weighting scheme with a non-additive measure that allows for interactions, synergies, and diminishing effects over time. Formally, a capacity is a set function
\nu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
of subsets of the time domain, which assigns a non-negative value to each measurable set while preserving monotonicity (see section
1.2
).

In this study, we impose the capacity function rather than estimating it from data. The choice of the imposed function is motivated by prior theoretical considerations, domain expertise, and the specific non-monotonic nature of the treatment effect observed in our simulated data. We adopt a sigmoid-based capacity function, which is well-suited for modeling intervention effects that exhibit an initial increase, peak impact, and subsequent decline due to compliance erosion or behavioral fatigue:

\nu(A)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda(m(A)-\theta)}},\quad A\in\\
\mathcal{A}.

(20)

Here,
m(A)
represents another, simple measure of the set
A
(e.g., the length of the time interval considered), while
\lambda
and
\theta
control the steepness and location of the transition. This form captures the delayed peak impact of the intervention, aligning with the assumed dynamic of treatment effectiveness in the hospital hygiene case study.

It is important to emphasize that this capacity function was not derived from empirical estimation but was instead selected based on theoretical reasoning. As elaborated in appendix
D
, estimating non-additive measures from observed data requires specialized methodologies. One prominent approach, described in section
2.1.2
, formulates the estimation problem as a restricted quadratic optimization problem
[
40
]
. While this method ensures that the estimated measure satisfies monotonicity conditions while minimizing the discrepancy between observed and modeled results, its application to DiD models remains challenging due to the specificity of the panel data structure. In particular, the estimation method did not yield satisfactory results in our case, as detailed in appendix
D
. Consequently, the choice to impose a capacity function was deemed more appropriate for the present study.

A proof that
\nu
indeed is a measure can be found in appendix
E
.

4.3
Estimation of NA-DiD Coefficient

To empirically evaluate the NA-DiD approach, we estimate the treatment effect in the hospital hygiene intervention scenario introduced earlier.

4.3.1
NA-DiD Estimation

Using the simulated dataset with 30 periods and an intervention introduced at period 12, we obtain an estimated NA-DiD coefficient of
-0.0386
. The negative sign indicates that, when accounting for non-additive aggregation effects, the intervention led to a net reduction in infection rates, albeit in a manner that does not conform to the linear assumptions of classical DiD.
The function implemented in Julia for estimating the NA-DiD coefficient is provided in Appendix
F
.

4.3.2
Comparison with Classical DiD

To illustrate the advantages of the NA-DiD framework, we compare our results to those obtained using the classical DiD estimator. To remind, the standard DiD coefficient is computed as:

\text{DiD}=\left(\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, post}}-\bar{Y}_{\text{treat, pre}}\right)-\left(\bar{Y}_{\text{control, post}}-\bar{Y}_{\text{control, pre}}\right).

(21)

Applying this estimator to our dataset yields a classical DiD coefficient of
-0.0931
, which differs substantially from the NA-DiD estimate.

The discrepancy between these estimates highlights the limitations of the classical DiD framework when dealing with interventions that do not produce constant effects over time. The more negative estimate obtained from the classical model suggests that it may be overestimating the magnitude of the treatment effect, as it does not account for the observed attenuation in treatment effectiveness over time. By contrast, the NA-DiD estimator, through its use of a capacity, provides a more nuanced representation of the true intervention effect, reflecting the non-monotonic treatment trajectory. In result, it correctly returns an estimate closer to zero (hence, insignificant effect), which reflects the real outcome of the introduction of the regime. Indeed, the infection rates in the moment of estimation were nearly identical in both the treatment and the control group (see figure
1
).

Utilizing the alternative interpretation of NA-DiD as outlined in the last paragraph of section
1.4
, within the hypothesized scenario, a change in a certain (sigmoid) form might be predicted. This would allow for the selection of an appropriate capacity to accommodate the expected outcome. Consequently, the output of NA-DiD was simply the unforeseen
surprise
. The author believes this explanation for the effectiveness of NA-DiD in these scenarios could potentially be more transparent and comprehensible.

5
Discussion

5.1
Key Findings

This paper introduces an enhancement of the Difference-in-Differences models utilizing non-additive measures. We deduced the integral representation of the Difference-in-Differences coefficient and employed this reformation to formulate the NA-DiD structure. Subsequently, its theoretical attributes were delineated. Lastly, the model was verified within a hypothetical framework. When compared to the traditional DiD model, it was found that in situations with non-linear treatment effects, the classical DiD could potentially overstate the actual effect of the treatment, whereas NA-DiD might provide outcomes that are closer to reality. Additionally, another interpretation of the NA-DiD model was provided, presenting the model’s output as the unanticipated change.

5.2
Future Research

The introduction of new methodologies invariably opens numerous potential paths for further investigation. For example:

1.

there could be investigations into refining the method to incorporate a broader range of nonlinearities beyond those in treatment effects, such as those found in treatment assignment and adoption,

2.

studies may be conducted on the impact of varying capacities on outcome results,

3.

general capacities that can be specific to certain scenarios — such as seasonal treatment, diminishing effects, or accelerating effects — could be identified, simplifying the understanding of this method by obviating the need for bespoke capacity development,

4.

methods for estimating measures from data could be tailored to DiD scenarios and then embedded within the DiD framework, enhancing its autonomy (refer to appendix
D
).

Furthermore, a natural direction for future research lies in implementing this method on real-world datasets. This not only assists in drawing conclusions about the case studies but also in empirically validating the method in practice.

5.3
Relation to Fuzzy Difference-in-Differences

A major extension of the classical Difference-in-Differences methodology is the fuzzy DiD framework introduced by
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2017
. Their study addresses cases where treatment assignment is not sharp, meaning that no group is fully treated or untreated, but rather the treatment rate increases more in some groups than in others over time. In this setting, they propose the Wald-DiD estimator, which normalizes the DiD of the outcome by the DiD of treatment intensity. Their approach primarily relies on ratio-based identification and assumes certain treatment effect homogeneity conditions. To overcome these assumptions, they propose the time-corrected Wald ratio (Wald-TC) and the changes-in-changes Wald ratio (Wald-CIC), both of which aim to relax treatment effect homogeneity assumptions when treatment rates remain stable in the control group. The practical implementation of these estimators has been facilitated by the development of a dedicated Stata package, introduced in
de Chaisemartin et al. 2019
, which provides an accessible tool for researchers using fuzzy DiD in empirical applications.

The key distinction between fuzzy DiD and the NA-DiD framework developed in this study lies in their primary focus. The fuzzy DiD approach is designed to handle cases of fuzzy treatment assignment, where the probability of receiving treatment varies across groups and over time. It seeks to correct for this imperfect assignment process by using ratio-based estimators to recover the treatment effect under a set of assumptions about common trends and treatment heterogeneity. In contrast, the NA-DiD framework is not concerned with treatment assignment per se but rather with how treatment effects themselves are aggregated (but the extension to non-additive treatment assignment is being worked on by the author). Traditional DiD methods, including fuzzy DiD, rely on additive aggregation, where treatment effects are averaged across units under linear assumptions. NA-DiD fundamentally departs from this structure by introducing a non-additive measure for the aggregation of the treatment effect.

Rather than constructing weighted ratios of DiD estimates, NA-DiD models treatment effects using a capacity function
\nu
. This shift allows for the modeling of non-linear interactions, synergies, and diminishing effects that ratio-based estimators cannot capture. A further distinction is that while the estimators in
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2017
require empirical estimation of weighting schemes, the NA-DiD framework in this study imposes a capacity function ex-ante rather than estimating it from data. This decision is motivated by theoretical considerations and the structured non-monotonic treatment effect dynamics observed in our simulated case study. As detailed in appendix
D
, estimating non-additive measures directly in a DiD setting remains an open methodological challenge, requiring optimization techniques that ensure monotonicity constraints are preserved. Our approach, by imposing a capacity function, serves as a controlled proof of concept for the use of non-additive aggregation in DiD, setting the stage for future research into empirical estimation methods.

Beyond methodological distinctions, NA-DiD introduces a conceptual shift by moving away from partial identification strategies towards a framework where the structure of aggregation itself changes. Whereas fuzzy DiD methods like Wald-DID or Wald-CIC still rely on weighted averages of treatment effects, NA-DiD allows for the modeling of dynamic, interactive, and non-linear effects. This distinction is particularly relevant in settings where interventions produce synergistic effects, diminishing returns, or threshold-based behaviors, which standard DiD and fuzzy DiD approaches may fail to capture.

Appendix A
Proof of Equivalence Between the Integral Representation and the Classical DiD Estimator

Lemma 1
.

The integral representation of the Difference-in-Differences estimator, given in eq.
16
, is equivalent to the classical DiD estimator expressed in terms of conditional expectations, as given in eq.
10
.

Proof.

To prove the equivalence, we start by considering the integral representation of the DiD estimator from Equation
16
:

DD_{e}=\int_{t}\big(\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)-\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)\big)\,d\mu(t),

where
\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)
and
\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)
represent the treatment and control group effects over time, respectively (see eq.
11
and
12
).

Expanding
\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)
and
\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)
, we substitute their definitions with those from eq.
11
and
12
:

\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{0}),

\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{0}).

Substituting these into the integral representation, we get:

\displaystyle DD_{e}=\int_{t}\Big(

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle-\big(\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{0})\big)\Big)\,d\mu(t).

Rearranging terms inside the integral:

\begin{split}DD_{e}=&\int_{t}\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{1})\,d\mu(t)-\int_{t}\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=t_{0})\,d\mu(t)\\
&-\int_{t}\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{1})\,d\mu(t)+\int_{t}\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=t_{0})\,d\mu(t).\end{split}

Since
\mu(t)
is a probabilistic, therefore additive, measure over the time domain, integrating over
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
reduces the integrals to the weighted averages of the group outcomes. Thus, the expression simplifies to:

\displaystyle DD_{e}=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=1)-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=0)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=1)+\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=0)

, which can be further rewritten as

\displaystyle DD_{e}=\;

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=1)-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1\land T_{t}=0)\big]

\displaystyle-\big[\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=1)-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0\land T_{t}=0)\big].

This is precisely the classical DiD estimator from eq.
10
.

Q.E.D.

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Equivalence Between the OLS Representation and the Integral Representation of the DiD Estimator

Lemma 2
.

The coefficient
\delta
on the interaction term
D_{i}\cdot T_{t}
in the OLS regression model given in equation
4
is equivalent to the integral representation of the DiD estimator given in Equation
16
.

Proof.

We start with the OLS regression model from Equation
4
:

y_{it}=\alpha+\beta D_{i}+\gamma T_{t}+\delta(D_{i}\cdot T_{t})+\epsilon_{it}.

The goal is to express
\delta
in terms of the integral representation of the DiD estimator. To do so, we proceed step by step.

Expected Value of
y_{it}

Taking expectations conditional on
D_{i}
and
T_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1,T_{t}=t)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta+\gamma\mu(t)+\delta\mu(t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0,T_{t}=t)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\gamma\mu(t),

where
\mu(t)
is an additive measure over the time domain.

Treatment and Control Effects in Integral Form

The treatment and control effects over time can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1,T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=1,T_{t}=t_{0}),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0,T_{t}=t_{1})-\mathbb{E}(y\mid D_{i}=0,T_{t}=t_{0}).

Substituting the expected values from Step 1:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)

\displaystyle=\big[\alpha+\beta+\gamma\mu(t_{1})+\delta\mu(t_{1})\big]-\big[\alpha+\beta+\gamma\mu(t_{0})+\delta\mu(t_{0})\big],

\displaystyle=\gamma\big[\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big]+\delta\big[\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big],

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)

\displaystyle=\big[\alpha+\gamma\mu(t_{1})\big]-\big[\alpha+\gamma\mu(t_{0})\big],

\displaystyle=\gamma\big[\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big].

Difference-in-Differences Using Integral Representation

The integral representation of the DiD estimator is given as:

DD_{e}=\int_{t}\big(\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)-\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)\big)\,d\mu(t).

Substituting
\Delta_{\text{treat}}(t)
and
\Delta_{\text{control}}(t)
into this expression:

DD_{e}=\int_{t}\Big(\big[\gamma\big(\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big)+\delta\big(\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big)\big]-\big[\gamma\big(\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big)\big]\Big)\,d\mu(t).

Simplifying:

DD_{e}=\int_{t}\delta\big(\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})\big)\,d\mu(t).

Since
\mu(t)
is a probabilistic measure over the time domain, the term
\mu(t_{1})-\mu(t_{0})
integrates to unity over the domain. Therefore:

DD_{e}=\delta.

Conclusion

The coefficient
\delta
in the OLS representation directly corresponds to the integral representation of the DiD estimator
DD_{e}
, as derived above. This equivalence holds rigorously through the transformation from OLS to the integral form.

Q.E.D.

Appendix D
Estimation of Non-Additive Measures from Data by Quadratic Optimisation

To apply the NA-DiD framework in practice, we must first either impose or estimate non-additive measures from observed data. The first of those can be done via observation, literature review and/or professional experience. This approach was also utilized in the hypothetical case study showcased in section
4
.
As for the latter, we would have to use one od the several methods for empirically obtaining capacities described in Section
2.1.2
. In this section, we rigorously derive the estimation approach proposed by
Miranda et al. 2003
, which formulates the problem as a restricted quadratic optimization problem. This method ensures that the estimated measure satisfies the monotonicity conditions while minimizing the discrepancy between observed and modeled results.
The author acknowledges that the application of this method was not successful in the current work, as the data specificity in the Difference-in-Differences models varies from the studies cited in Section
2.1.2
, which include the method further elaborated here. Nonetheless, the author believes this method holds the greatest potential for application in DiD studies. Although further research is necessary, presenting a simplified and condensed version of the method from
Miranda et al. 2003
, as it was reformulated by the author during its attempt to fit the DiD scenario, is deemed beneficial to the broader econometric community.

D.1
Problem Formulation

Let
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a finite set of criteria (e.g., time periods or individual characteristics), and let
\nu:2^{X}\to[0,1]
be a standardized capacity that we seek to estimate from sample data.

Definition 1
.

Given a dataset consisting of
l
observations, where each observation
i
is evaluated over
n
criteria, the optimal capacity
\nu^{*}
is the function that minimizes the squared error between the observed scores
e_{i}
and their predicted values under a Choquet integral model:

\nu^{*}=\arg\min_{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\big(C_{\nu}(f_{i})-e_{i}\big)^{2}.

(22)

The Choquet integral over
\nu
is given by:

C_{\nu}(f_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(x_{(j)})-f_{i}(x_{(j-1)})\big)\nu(A_{j}),

(23)

where:

1.

x_{(1)}\leq x_{(2)}\leq\dots\leq x_{(n)}
represents the non-decreasing ordering of
f_{i}(x_{j})
,

2.

A_{j}=\{x_{(j)},\dots,x_{(n)}\}
is the set of criteria with scores at least as high as
f_{i}(x_{(j)})
.

To solve for
\nu^{*}
, we define a vectorized representation of the problem. Let
\mathbf{u}
be the vector containing the values of
\nu(A)
for all subsets
A\subseteq X
. The optimization problem can then be written as

\min_{\mathbf{u}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{T}D\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{u},

(24)

subject to:

A\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b}\geq 0.

(25)

where:

1.

D
is a semidefinite positive symmetric matrix that defines the quadratic cost function.

2.

\mathbf{r}
is a vector derived from the squared error loss function.

3.

A
and
\mathbf{b}
encode the monotonicity constraints.

D.2
Monotonicity Constraints

Since
\nu
must be a monotone function satisfying
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
for all
A\subseteq B
, the constraints can be explicitly written as:

\nu(A)-\nu(B)\leq 0,\quad\forall A\subseteq B.

(26)

Equivalently, in matrix form:

A_{\text{mon}}\mathbf{u}\leq 0.

(27)

D.3
Normalization Constraints

If we assume a standardized capacity, we must enforce that:

\nu(\emptyset)=0,\quad\nu(X)=1.

(28)

This condition can be rewritten in the optimization problem as:

\mathbf{a}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\mathbf{a}_{X}^{T}\mathbf{u}=1.

(29)

where
\mathbf{a}_{0}
and
\mathbf{a}_{X}
are unit vectors that select the respective elements of
\mathbf{u}
.

D.4
Alternative Representations

Instead of estimating
\nu
directly, we can use the Möbius transform, which expresses
\nu(A)
in terms of its elementary components:

\nu(A)=\sum_{B\subseteq A}m(B).

(30)

Similarly, the Shapley interaction index expresses the importance of each element
x_{j}
in terms of its marginal contributions:

\phi_{j}=\sum_{A\subseteq X\setminus\{x_{j}\}}\frac{|A|!(|X|-|A|-1)!}{|X|!}\left(\nu(A\cup\{x_{j}\})-\nu(A)\right).

(31)

D.5
Final Quadratic Program Solution

The final quadratic program to estimate
\nu^{*}
can be written as:

\min_{\mathbf{u}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{T}D\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{u},

(32)

subject to:

A_{\text{mon}}\mathbf{u}\leq 0,\quad\mathbf{a}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\mathbf{a}_{X}^{T}\mathbf{u}=1.

(33)

Now, we will prove the uniqueness of the estimated optimization, as follows from
Miranda et al. 2003
.

Theorem 1
.

The quadratic program defined above admits a unique optimal solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}
if
D
is positive definite.

Proof.

Since
D
is positive semi-definite, the objective function

f(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{T}D\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{u}

(34)

is convex. If
D
is strictly positive definite, then its Hessian matrix
D
is positive definite, implying strict convexity of
f(\mathbf{u})
. The feasible region, defined by linear constraints,

A_{\text{mon}}\mathbf{u}\leq 0,\quad\mathbf{a}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\mathbf{a}_{X}^{T}\mathbf{u}=1,

(35)

is a convex polyhedron. By standard results in convex optimization, a strictly convex function over a convex feasible region has a unique minimizer. Therefore, there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}
that minimizes the objective function subject to the constraints.

Q.E.D.

∎

D.6
Practical Approximations through Symmetry and k-Additivity

Estimating a non-additive measure through quadratic optimization involves a parameter count that increases exponentially with the number of criteria, rendering the process computationally impractical for numerous real-world applications. To address this issue, the author suggests simplifications by imposing additional structure on the capacity.

Assuming symmetry, the capacity is defined by a vector composed of
m
parameters, where
m
denotes the total criteria count. This vector captures the capacity values on nested subsets organized by arranging the criteria in descending order. Consequently, the number of free parameters is reduced from
2^{m}
to
m
.

Alternatively, assuming the capacity is
k
-additive implies that its Möbius transform is nonzero only for subsets with cardinality at most
k
. In this case, only the Möbius coefficients corresponding to subsets of up to
k
elements need to be estimated. Once these parameters are pre-estimated offline, the capacity for any subset
A\subseteq X
can be computed via

\nu(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq A\\
|B|\leq k\end{subarray}}m(B).

.

Appendix E
Proof that
\nu
is a Valid Capacity

In this appendix, we provide a formal proof that the capacity function imposed in section
4.2
satisfies the necessary properties of a measure in the sense of non-additive capacities.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\nu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function defined as:

\nu(A)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda(m(A)-\theta)}},\quad A\in\mathcal{A},

(36)

where
m(A)
represents the measure of the set
A
, and
\lambda,\theta>0
are constants. Then
\nu(A)
satisfies the following properties:

1.

Non-negativity:

\nu(A)\geq 0
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

2.

Monotonicity:
If
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
and
A\subseteq B
, then
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
.

Thus,
\nu
is a valid capacity function.

Proof.

We verify each property in turn.

Step 1: Non-negativity.

By construction, the function
g(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda(x-\theta)}}
represents a standard sigmoid function, which is strictly bounded between
(0,1)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Applying this to any measurable set
A
, we obtain:

0<\nu(A)<1,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{A}.

(37)

Thus,
\nu(A)
is always non-negative.

Step 2: Monotonicity.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
such that
A\subseteq B
. By the monotonicity of the measure
m(A)
, we have:

m(A)\leq m(B).

(38)

Since the sigmoid function
g(x)
is strictly increasing in
x
, applying it to both sides of the inequality preserves the order:

g(m(A))\leq g(m(B)).

(39)

By substitution, we obtain:

\nu(A)\leq\nu(B),

(40)

which confirms the monotonicity of
\nu
.

Conclusion.

Since
\nu(A)
satisfies both non-negativity and monotonicity, it qualifies as a capacity function. This ensures that
\nu
is a valid non-additive measure.

Q.E.D.
