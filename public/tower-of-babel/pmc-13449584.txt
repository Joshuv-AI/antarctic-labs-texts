Inoculation with Lactobacillus rhamnosus improves fermentation quality of napier grass silage by enriching Lactobacillus buchneri in tropical regions
Authors: Qifeng Wu, Mingming Du, Zhaochang Du, Zhiwei Liu, Xinping Jin, Han Zheng, Hao Liu, Jianguo Zhang, Wenyu Yang, Xiaomei Li, Yanhong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNapier grass (Pennisetum purpureum), fresh or preserved as silage, is widely used as a component of animal feed in tropical regions. High-temperature and high-humidity in the environment promote the growth of undesirable microorganisms, which prevents lactic acid bacteria (LAB) from becoming dominant during natural fermentation, restrains organic acid accumulation and pH decline, and thereby readily induces ensiling fermentation failure.ResultsIn this study, we evaluated the fermentation quality, nutritional parameters, and microbial community structure of napier grass silage inoculated with self-screen thermostable Lactobacillus rhamnosus (LR), by fermenting fresh materials at both high (40 °C) and ambient (25 °C) temperatures for 3, 7, 14, and 75 days. The results showed that LR-inoculated silage had higher lactic acid (91.62 ± 0.53 g/kg DM) and acetic acid (32.72 ± 0.97 g/kg DM) contents, while having a lower pH (3.70 ± 0.01) and butyric acid content (5.31 ± 0.23 g/kg DM) than the control (CK) and commercial Lactobacillus plantarum (LP)-inoculated silages at 40 °C on day 75. LR-inoculated silage exhibited higher neutral detergent fiber content at 40 ℃ and higher water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) content at 25 ℃, while showing lower crude protein and WSC contents than LP at 40 ℃. The results of microbial analyses indicated that on day 14, L. rhamnosus was the dominant species in LR-inoculated silage for both 40 °C (54.37%) and 25 °C (75.31%) temperatures, while CK and LP-inoculated silages were dominated by L. plantarum. On day 75, Lactobacillus buchneri was the dominant species in LR-inoculated silage, and L. rhamnosus still sustained a certain relative abundance at 40 °C (19.76%) and 25 °C (9.49%), respectively.ConclusionLR inoculation induced a directed succession toward L. buchneri and LR-inoculated silage had higher organic acid content, along with lower pH value and ammonia nitrogen content than the commercial LAB on day 75 of ensiling at 40 °C. These findings demonstrate that the self-screened thermotolerant LR is an effective silage inoculant for improving fermentation quality of napier grass in tropical regions, offering a practical strategy to address high-temperature challenges and support sustainable livestock production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09033-4.

Background

Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum) is one of the high-yielding forage worldwide, yielding more than 50 tons per hectare [1]. Napier grass is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions for livestock production, especially in Africa and Asia [2], given its rapid regrowth cycle, high productivity, and high quality [3, 4]. Silage making is a typical way of napier grass preservation [5]. High-temperature and high-humidity environment promotes the growth of undesirable microorganisms, which prevents lactic acid bacteria (LAB) from becoming dominant during natural fermentation, restrains organic acid accumulation and pH decline, and thereby readily induces ensiling fermentation failure [6, 7].

Fermentation quality such as organic acid and pH, together with nutritional characteristics including soluble carbohydrates, fiber components, and protein, are significantly affected by the initial microbial community composition and its dynamic changes during the anaerobic fermentation process [8, 9]. LAB play a critical role during fermentation by rapidly utilizing water-soluble carbohydrates to produce organic acids, thereby lowering the pH [10]. This acidic environment inhibits the growth of harmful microorganisms while promoting the dominance of acid-tolerant LAB, thus ensuring efficient anaerobic fermentation. Napier grass is characterized by insufficient fermentable carbohydrates and epiphytic lactic acid bacteria, coupled with elevated moisture content and strong buffering capacity, all of which are unfavorable for efficient ensiling fermentation [11]. The reproduction of LAB could further be inhibited under high temperature conditions (> 37℃) [12], resulting in insufficient organic acid production to inhibit the proliferation of undesirable microorganisms such as Clostridium and Enterobacter. These harmful bacteria proliferate extensively, consuming fermentable carbohydrates and producing spoilage-associated metabolites, including butyric acid, biogenic amines, and ammonia, which reduce palatability and pose risks to livestock health [7]. Recent studies have indicated that high temperatures could affect the growth and reproduction of Lactobacillus plantarum and trigger the fermentation of Clostridium and Enterococcus in alfalfa silage, causing health problems in livestock [13].

LAB inoculants can modulate the ensiling fermentation process, reduce crude protein degradation to preserve more protein in silage, and lower fiber and lignin contents to improve nutritional quality, thus being widely used in the production of high-quality silage [14]. However, commercial LAB additives like Lactobacillus plantarum often show limited effects in tropical regions due to their poor adaptability to high temperature and humidity [15, 16]. Previous studies indicated that stylo (Stylosanthes guianensis SW.) silage with locally isolated L. plantarum St1 and St3 inoculated can modulate microbial community composition and suppress the proliferation of harmful bacteria, therefore reduce protein degradation to ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and preserve organic acids to maintain low pH, and finally improve the fermentation quality and stability in Thailand [17]. Therefore, the screening of indigenous LAB adapted to local silage and climatic conditions is a pivotal technical strategy for enhancing silage fermentation quality in tropical and subtropical regions.

In previous studies, we isolated and screened the thermotolerant strain Lactobacillus rhamnosus (LR) from whole-plant corn silage in Sichuan, China. This strain is currently preserved at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) under accession number CGMCC 18,233. The self-screened LR still maintains robust growth at 45℃ and 40 ℃ remains within its optimal range for metabolism and acid production. Compared with commercial L. plantarum, LR also exhibits higher salt tolerance, broader carbohydrate utilization, and stronger acid tolerance [18]. Based on these superior physiological characteristics, we hypothesized that inoculation with the self-screened thermotolerant LR strain would lead to superior fermentation quality and nutritional parameters of napier grass silage under high-temperature conditions compared with commercial LAB. Thus, this study investigated the effect of self-screened LR strain on fermentation quality, nutrient parameters, and microbial community structure of napier grass silage at high (40 °C) temperatures. The discussion of fermentation quality in this study relates to pH, lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), butyric acid (BA), and ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N), with an increase in LA and decreases in pH, BA, and NH3-N defined as improved fermentation quality and vice versa, while the discussion of nutrient parameters focused on nutritional value, with increases in crude protein (CP) and water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) and decreases in neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) regarded as improved nutritional quality and vice versa. The findings could enable us to gain new insights into optimizing preservation of high-moisture forages in tropical regions, and provide both theoretical and practical guidance for targeted microbial additive developments.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted at the Modern Agriculture Research and Development Center of Sichuan Agricultural University, China (N 30°56’, E 103°65’). The experimental design was a complete randomized design with two factors: lactic acid bacteria additives (LP: commercial L. plantarum, LR: self-screened L. rhamnosus, and CK: control) and ensiling temperatures (25 °C and 40 °C) with three replicates. The silage samples were measured at 3, 7, 14, and 75 days of ensiling, thus in total 3 LAB additives * 2 temperatures * 4 durations * 3 replicates = 72 silos. The temperatures of 40 °C and 25 °C were selected to simulate the actual anaerobic fermentation conditions in tropical regions and typical environmental conditions of anaerobic fermentation, respectively.

Materials harvest and silage preparation

The experiment material was ‘’Guimu No. 1’’ napier grass (Pennisetum americanum × P. purpureum) × P. purpureum Schum cv. Mott), which was provided by its original breeding institution, the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Institute of Animal Husbandry (Nanning, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China). On July 1, 2019, napier grass was harvested when it reached a height of approximately 1.5 to 2 m. After harvest, the napier grass was chopped into 2–3 cm particle size and thoroughly mixed with LAB additives for silage making. Well mixed fresh materials were put into mini-silos (packing density approximately 750 g/L) and vacuum-sealed using a vacuum packaging machine (DZ-AS, 2500 KW, ANSEN, Fujian, China) to ensure an anaerobic environment. The bags were stored in a dark room at 40 °C and 25 °C for durations of 3, 7, 14, and 75 days. At each designated time interval, the mini-silos were opened to measure fermentation quality, nutrient parameters, and microbial community composition. As shown in Table 1, fresh napier grass had a dry matter (DM) content of 202.40 g/kg fresh matter (FM), the WSC content was 50.56 g/kg DM, the CP content was 63.54 g/kg DM, the NDF content was 637.96 g/kg DM, and the ADF content was 391.89 g/kg DM.

Lactic acid bacterial additive preparation

The commercial LP was purchased from a company (Sichuan Gaofuji Biotech Co., Ltd, Chengdu, China). LR was isolated from whole-plant maize silage in Xuanhan, Sichuan, and showed robust growth under low pH (3.5) and high temperature (45 °C) conditions. The LP and LR strains were cultured in MRS augar (De Man, Rogaosa, and Sharpe; CM188, Land Bridge, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 36 h to achieve a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ cfu/mL. For inoculation, 1 mL of LP or LR suspension containing the culture medium was evenly sprayed onto 1000 g of fresh forage material, resulting in a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cfu/g FM. The CK received an equivalent volume of sterile distilled water. The materials designed for treatment were evenly sprayed with the respective LAB additives and packed into polyethylene bags.

Fermentation quality analyses

For fermentation quality analysis, 20 g of each sample was blended with 180 mL sterilized water at 4 °C for 24 h. The mixture was then filtered through four layers of medical gauze to obtain the test filtrate. The resultant filtrate was employed to measure pH, utilizing a portable pH meter (PHSJ-5; LEICI, Shanghai, China). Subsequent to the centrifugation of the filtrate, the supernatant was subjected to analysis for organic acids (LA; AA; PA; BA) using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, SHIMADZU − 10 A), with a Shodex Rspak KC -811 column (300 × 8 mm) employed. The mobile phase consisted of 3 mmol/L HClO4, the flow rate was set at 1 mL/min, and the detection wavelength was 210 nm [19]. NH3-N was measured colorimetrically as described by Broderick and Kang [20]. Briefly, 2 mL of the filtrate was mixed with 2 mL of phenol reagent and 2 mL of hypochlorite reagent. The mixture was incubated at 60 °C for 10 min, then the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 560 nm. The NH3-N content was calculated based on the standard curve and expressed on a TN basis (% TN).

Nutritional parameters analyses

Approximately 200 g of both fresh material and silage samples were oven-dried at 65 °C for 72 h till constant weight (101-3AB, Beijing Zhongxing Weiye Instrument Co., Ltd. Beijing, China) to measure DM content. The dry samples were then ground with a Wiley mill (DLF-20, Wenzhou Dingli Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Wenzhou, China) and passed through a 1 mm sieve to measure WSC, CP, NDF, and ADF contents. Total nitrogen (TN) was measured with the Kjeldahl method using a Kjeltec 8460 analyzer (FOSS Analytical, Höganös, Sweden): samples were digested with 98% (v/v) concentrated sulfuric acid at 420 °C for 2 h, followed by titration with 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid. The CP content was calculated using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25 [20]. WSC content was measured through the anthrone-sulfuric acid method [20]. The samples were reacted with anthrone reagent in a 100 °C boiling water bath for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 620 nm. The analytical-grade glucose was used as the calibration standard. The contents of NDF and ADF were analyzed according to the Van Soest method with an ANKOM 2000 fiber analyzer (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, NY, USA) [21]. Samples were then soaked in acetone and dried at 105 °C for 2 h until a constant weight was achieved, and the contents of NDF and ADF were subsequently calculated based on the final weight measurements.

Microbial community structure analyses

The microbial community analysis was conducted following Yan et al. [22]. The DNA of each sample was extracted using the TIANamp Bacterial DNA Extraction Kit (DP302-02) and purified using the DNA Purification Column (DP214-02). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the specific primers 515F (5’-GTTTCGGTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and 806R (5’-GCCAATGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Ion S5™ XL platform (Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Raw Tags were filtered to obtain Clean Tags, which were further quality-controlled following the pipeline of QIIME (v1.9.1, http://qiime.org/scripts/split_libraries_fastq.html). Chimeric sequences in Clean Tags were detected and removed by alignment with the taxonomic annotation database via VSEARCH (https://github.com/torognes/vsearch/) to generate Effective Tags. The effective tags of all samples were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence identity threshold using UPARSE software (v7.0.1001, http://www.drive5.com/uparse/). Taxonomic annotation of OTU representative sequences was performed using the Mothur algorithm, with alignment against the SILVA 138 SSU rRNA database (http://www.arb-silva.de/) and a similarity threshold of 0.8-1. Fast multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MUSCLE (v3.8.31, http://www.drive5.com/muscle/) to obtain the phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic information of all OTU representative sequences. Finally, all sample data were rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth, and subsequent Alpha and Beta diversity analyses were based on the rarefied datasets. Graphical representations of bacterial data were analyzed using the NovoCloud online platform (https://magic.novogene.com). The raw sequencing data were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with the BioProject accession number PRJNA 1,249,203.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 (version 19.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of ensiling temperature, LAB additives on napier grass silage, and their interactions, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post-hoc comparisons. All data were reported as the mean of three replicates. Alpha diversity was computed using the Mothur method on the free online Novogene Cloud Platform, and treatment differences were assessed via the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Beta diversity was evaluated through principal coordinates analyses (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance. A co-occurrence network was constructed based on genus-level abundance. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated for all genus pairs using the rcorr function in the R package Hmisc. Only genera ranked among the top 20 in abundance were retained, and correlations with |ρ| < 0.6 were filtered out to exclude weak associations. To ensure statistical robustness, only significant correlations (adjusted P < 0.05, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction) were retained. The resulting adjacency matrix was visualized as a network using Gephi. To assess relationships between microbial taxa and environmental factors, Spearman correlation coefficients and their significance were calculated using the corr.test function in the R package “psych” [23]. Correlation matrices were visualized as heatmaps using Origin (version 9.1; OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA), with color intensity representing correlation strength and asterisks indicating significance levels.

Results

Effects of lactic acid bacterial additives on the fermentation quality of napier grass silages at different temperatures

The LAB additives had a significant impact on the LA, AA, PA, BA, and NH3-N contents (Tables 2 and 3; P < 0.05). The ensiling temperature also significantly affected the contents of PA, BA, and NH3-N (P < 0.05), with significant interactions between LAB additives and ensiling temperature observed for LA and BA contents (P < 0.05). On day 3 of ensiling, compared with 25℃, LR-inoculated silages showed higher AA and PA content at 40℃, whereas LP-inoculated silages had lower LA content. CK exhibited higher LA, AA, PA, BA, and NH3-N content at 40℃. LP-inoculated silages had the highest LA content under 40℃ (89.22 g/kg DM) and 25℃ (108.05 g/kg DM). At 40℃, LP-inoculated silages had the lowest pH, while LR-inoculated silages showed the lowest BA content, and both inoculated treatments exhibited lower NH3-N content than CK. At 25℃, LP-inoculated silages had lower PA and NH3-N content than CK, and LR-inoculated silages had lower AA content than LP-inoculated and CK. On day 7 of ensiling, compared with 25℃, LP-inoculated silages had lower pH at 40℃, while CK had higher pH and AA content. Under both temperatures, LR- and LP-inoculated silages had lower AA content than CK. LR-inoculated silages had the lowest NH3-N at 40℃. At 40 °C, both inoculated treatments had lower pH than CK; the opposite was observed at 25℃. On day 14 of ensiling, all treatments had higher pH at 40℃ than at 25℃, while CK had higher NH3-N content at 40℃. LP-inoculated silages had the lowest pH, AA, and NH3-N content under both temperatures, and LR-inoculated silages had lower NH3-N content than CK. On day 75 of ensiling, compared with 25℃, LR-inoculated silages had higher LA content and lower pH at 40℃, whereas LP-inoculated and CK silages had higher pH and BA content. At 40℃, LR-inoculated silages outperformed LP-inoculated silages with higher LA content, lower pH, and lower NH3-N content. At 25℃, CK had the lowest pH, while LR-inoculated silages had the lowest NH3-N content. In summary, inoculation with both LR and LP improved the fermentation quality of napier grass silage. In the LP-inoculated silage, before day 14 of ensiling, LA content remained at a high level under both temperature conditions, effectively reducing pH and NH3-N. In contrast, the LR-inoculated silage exhibited higher LA content and lower pH, BA, and NH3-N at 40℃ on day 75 of ensiling.

Effects of lactic acid bacterial additives on the nutritional parameters of napier grass silages at different temperatures

The LAB additives had a significant impact on CP, NDF, and ADF contents (Table 4, P < 0.05). The interactions between LAB additives and ensiling temperature were significant for CP, NDF, and WSC contents (P < 0.05). On day 75 of ensiling, all treatments showed numerical increases in CP content and numerical decreases in NDF and WSC contents versus fresh napier grass, with the maximum numerical ADF reduction detected in LP-inoculated silage. On day 75 of ensiling, compared with 25℃, LP-inoculated silage had higher CP and WSC content at 40℃ on day 75 of ensiling. At 40℃, LP-inoculated silage exhibited higher CP and WSC content than both CK and LR-inoculated silages, while LR-inoculated silage had higher NDF content than CK and LP-inoculated silages. At 25℃, LR-inoculated silage had higher WSC content than LP-inoculated silage.

Discussion

At the anaerobic fermentation stage, fresh materials are fermented with beneficial bacteria, for instance LAB, to produce organic acid primarily LA, which leads to further pH decline and inhibits the growth of undesirable bacteria [24]. Based on the results of fermentation quality, all treatments reduce pH quickly during the early fermentation stage. This rapid acidification was primarily attributed to the rapid proliferation and dominance of homofermentative LAB such as L. plantarum, which efficiently produced LA and accelerated pH reduction [25]. On day 3, LP-inoculated silages had a higher LA content than other treatments under both 40℃ and 25℃, which was directly derived from the strong homofermentative metabolism of L. plantarum. By day 75, LA content in LP-inoculated silages decreased compared to that of day 14, especially under 40 ℃. This reduction was not only caused by the enrichment of L. buchneri that converted LA to AA [26], but also reflected the insufficient long-term thermotolerance of commercial LP, which failed to maintain continuous LA production under prolonged high-temperature conditions. AA can be produced by heterofermentative LAB or undesirable microbes like Acetobacter, Enterobacter, or Clostridium and improves aerobic stability by inhibiting fungal growth [27]. LR-inoculated silages had a lower AA content before 14 days of ensiling compared to that of day 75, the results were consistent with the homofermentative nature of L. rhamnosus [28]. This increase was likely attributable to the succession of microbial communities rather than being a direct product of L. rhamnosus. The contents of NH3-N and BA are key indicators for protein degradation and Clostridium activity [29]. Throughout the fermentation period, the NH3-N (< 10%TN) and BA (< 10 g/kg DM) content were low in all treatments, this finding was consistent with previous study [25], which may be attributed to the rapid decline in pH on day 3, which effectively inhibited Clostridium proliferation. Notably, LR-inoculated silages had lower BA content than LP-inoculated and the control at whole fermentation period, particularly at 40℃, highlighting the better performance of self-screen thermostable strain in inhibiting undesirable bacteria and improving fermentation quality of napier grass silage under high-temperature conditions. In summary, these results indicate that the self-screened LR strain can maintain stable fermentation and suppress spoilage microorganisms under high-temperature conditions by driving targeted microbial succession.

Napier grass is characterized by high moisture and cellulose content, but low WSC content [30]. Carbohydrates are considered the primary energy source for LAB fermentation, and insufficient WSC impairs the proliferation and competitive predominance of LAB, thereby promoting the growth of spoilage bacteria and producing spoilage-associated metabolites, including butyric acid, biogenic amines, increasing the risk of fermentation failure [31]. In this study, LP-inoculated silages had higher WSC and CP contents than LR-inoculated silages and CK at 40℃, which was likely due to the pH decline at early ensiling stages caused by the L. plantarum enrichment. As a typical homofermentative LAB, L. plantarum rapidly utilizes WSC to produce LA during early ensiling, leading to a swift pH decline. Rapid acidification not only inhibits the growth of spoilage bacteria like Clostridia and reduces the degradation of CP, but also suppresses the growth of undesirable bacteria that consume WSC, thereby reducing the inefficient consumption of WSC [32, 33]. Although LP consumes some WSC by producing LA, the LP-inoculated silage exhibits a higher WSC retention rate because LP reduces the consumption of WSC by undesirable bacteria. High temperatures could limit lignocellulose degradation due to potential inhibition of specific microbial enzymes [34]. The initial dominance and sustained persistence of L. rhamnosus, followed by the enrichment of L. buchneri by day 75 in the LR-inoculated silages, established an acidic environment. This environment exerted an inhibitory effect on native fiber-degrading microorganisms, including Clostridium and Bacillus, thereby limiting the degradation of structural carbohydrates.

The structure of microbial communities is considered as the underlying drivers of anaerobic fermentation outcomes [35, 36]. The Good’s coverage for all samples exceeded 99%, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to fully capture the microbial community structure of the napier grass silage, and that the sequencing data are reliable. Shannon and Simpson indices represent the evenness of a microbial community; the larger the values, the more evenly distributed the species within the community [37]. Chao1 and ACE indices represent the species richness of a community; the larger the values, the higher the species richness [38]. In this study, the α-diversity indices showed low evenness and high richness across all treatments throughout the fermentation process, which is consistent with the findings of Xia et al. [39]. This indicates that as fermentation progresses, the species richness of the community increases while evenness decreases, and some species within the bacterial community become dominant. For the LP-inoculated silage at 25 °C, all indices at 75 days of fermentation were higher than those at 3 days of fermentation, suggesting that bacterial activity remained relatively high during fermentation without the emergence of a dominant bacterial group. The result of PCoA analysis demonstrated that the LR- and LP-inoculated silage had different effects on the napier silage microbial community structure. Among them, the LR-inoculated silage more altered the bacterial community structure of napier grass silage, whereas the LP-inoculated silage did not cause significant changes in the napier silage bacterial community structure.

The fermentation quality of silages is closely linked to the microbial community structure [40]. The anaerobic fermentation process was dominated by Lactobacillus, which produce lactic acid and suppress the growth of spoilage organisms [35, 36]. As expected, LAB inoculation did lead to higher initial relative abundances of Lactobacillus compared to CK, confirming their potential positive effect on fermentation quality at early fermentation stages [29]. The relative abundance of Lactococcus, a pioneer species, declined from day 3 to day 14 for all treatments. The abundance of Lactococcus was negatively correlated with Acetobacter, which suggests that Lactococcus may play a role in rapid acid production and competitive inhibition of spoilage microorganisms during early ensiling [41]. Enterobacter could compete with LAB by degrading carbohydrates [42], which was found effectively suppressed at day 75. The significant negative correlation between the dominant genus Lactobacillus and undesirable genera including Lelliottia, Enterobacter, Kosakonia and Pantoea, suggests that the enrichment of Lactobacillus may contribute to the suppression of these spoilage microorganisms. However, the relative abundance of undesirable microorganisms in the LP-inoculated silages was higher than that of the LR-inoculated silages at day 75, indicating potential spoilage issues for LP inoculation at 25℃. Acetobacter is also considered linked to aerobic spoilage and acetate oxidation of silages [43], and the relative abundance of Acetobacter was reduced in LR-inoculated silages at day 75 in our study. The above findings suggest that LR inoculation has the potential to reduce the risk of aerobic deterioration of napier grass silage, which may be attributed to its inhibitory effect on undesirable microorganisms [44].

High temperatures induce a shift in the LAB community from homofermentation to heterofermentation [7] and this pattern was observed across all treatments at 40 ℃, consistent with previous reports on high-temperature maize silage [45]. In this study, L. buchneri typically becomes dominant only during the late stage of ensiling, even though a low-pH environment is rapidly established in the early phase [46, 47]. This delayed dominance is mainly attributed to the fact that L. buchneri is a slow-growing heterofermentative LAB [48], whereas L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus are fast-growing homofermentative LAB [25]. The L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus rapidly utilize abundant WSC during early fermentation to occupy ecological niches and outcompete L. buchneri. Additionally, L. buchneri could convert LA into AA and exhibits strong acid tolerance [49, 50]. As ensiling proceeds, WSC is gradually depleted and LA accumulates continuously. Fast-growing homofermentative LAB then lose their competitive advantage, allowing L. buchneri to proliferate extensively and become the dominant species [51]. The higher AA and lower NH₃-N contents in all treatments on day 75 relative to day 14 further corroborate this succession. Notably, at 25℃, L. buchneri became dominant in LR-inoculated silages on day 75 of ensiling, whereas L. plantarum was dominant in CK and LP-inoculated silages. This is because LP-inoculated silages exhibited high LA within the 14 days of ensiling, but LA content declined by day 75. However, LR-inoculated silages sustain a stable low-pH environment and continuous LA supply throughout ensiling, which better favors L. buchneri dominance. Meanwhile, L. rhamnosus maintained stable relative abundance in LR-inoculated silages, which not only improved fermentation quality but also modulates livestock intestinal flora and enhances host immunity [52]. Overall, the LAB community in napier grass silage exhibited succession from homofermentation to heterofermentation. LR inoculation drove a directed succession toward L. buchneri, whereas the analogous shift in CK and commercial LP-inoculated silages represented a passive adaptation to high-temperature stress rather than targeted microbial regulation.

The present study is subject to certain limitations; namely, that the laboratory-scale mini-silos are unable to fully represent the actual production conditions of large-scale farm commercial ensiling. In addition, species-level taxonomic assignment based on partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences and OTU clustering at 97% similarity is inherently limited in resolution. For phylogenetically affiliated taxa within the genus Lactobacillus, this method may not yield reliable species-level discrimination, given the high degree of 16 S rRNA gene sequence conservation among closely related species. However, the mini-silo system represents a standard and well-validated methodology widely adopted in silage research, and the accuracy and reproducibility of the core findings remain fully assured. Subsequent research endeavours will seek to provide further substantiation regarding the efficacy of LR in the context of large-scale ensiling production, with a particular focus on its performance following aerobic exposure, and to employ metagenomic sequencing to validate the species-level taxonomic assignments reported in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, the results showed that napier grass silage inoculated with self-screened thermotolerant LR, L. rhamnosus established dominance before day 14, thereby creating a low-pH environment and driving a directed microbial succession toward L. buchneri by day 75 at 40℃. The results suggest a potential synergistic effect between L. rhamnosus and L. buchneri, which may contribute to the sustained organic acid production at 40 °C, thus leading to higher LA and AA contents, as well as lower pH, NH3-N, and BA contents on day 75. The findings confirm the potential of LR as an effective inoculant for improving anaerobic fermentation quality of napier grass silage in tropical and subtropical regions. Future research will focus on the fermentation quality of napier grass inoculated with LR strains during the aerobic exposure phase, as well as the underlying microbial regulatory mechanisms, with the aim of providing comprehensive theoretical and technical support for the large-scale application of LR.
