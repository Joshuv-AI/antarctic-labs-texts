Bridging Genes and Behavior in Autism Spectrum Disorder: Contributions of OXTR Gene Variants and Methylation to Brain Connectivity Patterns
Authors: Narae Yoon, Sohui Kim, Mee Rim Oh, Johanna Inhyang Kim, Jong-Min Lee, Bung-Nyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective To investigate how genetic variants and epigenetic modification of the oxytocin receptor gene (OXTR) relate to resting-state functional connectivity (FC) alterations and core symptom severity in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Methods We recruited 43 children with ASD and 54 typically developing (TD) children. Participants underwent OXTR genotyping and DNA methylation analysis (including CpG site 924), resting-state functional MRI, and standardized clinical assessments of ASD symptom severity. Group differences in within- and between-network FC were evaluated, and diagnosis-by-genotype/methylation interaction analyses were performed. Associations between FC measures and clinical severity scores were examined. Results Children with ASD showed decreased FC within and between major brain networks compared with TD children. OXTR genetic variants and hypermethylation further moderated these group differences. Specifically, risk alleles of two OXTR single nucleotide polymorphisms and hypermethylation at CpG 924 were associated with decreased FC in ASD but increased FC in TD children, suggesting a diagnosis-dependent, potentially compensatory pattern in typical development. FC measures were significantly associated with ASD severity scores. Conclusion These findings link OXTR genetic and epigenetic variation to network-level brain connectivity alterations and clinical symptoms in ASD, supporting an integrated neurobiological model that bridges molecular variation and behavior. Multimodal approaches incorporating epigenetics and neuroimaging may facilitate personalized, mechanism-based strategies for ASD.

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition influenced by diverse genetic and environmental factors [1]. Although numerous risk genes have been identified in many large-scale genetic studies, the understanding of the neurobiological pathways and mechanisms linking these identified risk genes to the core clinical symptoms of ASD remains unresolved. Imaging genetics and imaging epigenetics have emerged as powerful methods for uncovering this missing critical link underlying ASD. Imaging genetics identifies genetic variations influencing brain structure and function [2,3], while imaging epigenetics combines neuroimaging with epigenetic data to reveal how environmental influences alter gene expression, impacting brain function and structure [4-6].

Among genetic factors, the oxytocin receptor gene (OXTR) is significant for its role in social cognition and behavior—core aspects affected in ASD [7,8]. Oxytocin, the “social hormone,” influences maternal bonding, empathy, and social recognition—areas often impaired in ASD [9,10]. Studies link specific OXTR polymorphisms with social behavior variations in ASD, highlighting a genetic basis for social deficits [11]. Meta-analyses emphasize OXTR’s role in social-affective domains [12].

Imaging genetic studies have investigated the relationship between OXTR polymorphisms and brain function and structure in ASD, and the most abundant findings were observed for polymorphisms of two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), rs2254298 and rs53576. Polymorphisms rs2254298 [13-17] and rs53576 [18,19] are associated with altered social behavior and ASD risk, suggesting effects on neural circuits underlying social cognition. However, most research involves healthy adults, focusing on brain regions with dense oxytocin receptors, like the limbic and ventral forebrain, including the nucleus accumbens, hypothalamus, and amygdala [20]. Studies on OXTR DNA methylation and social behavior in ASD indicate hypermethylation correlates with autistic traits in adults and reduced brain functional connectivity (FC) [21-23]. DNA methylation occurs in cytosines of CpG dinucleotides, and DNA regions in which CpG dinucleotides are enriched are called CpG islands. CpG sites 924 and 934 of OXTR have been reported to be hypermethylated in individuals with ASD [23,24].

In this study, we aimed to explore how genetic and epigenetic diversity in OXTR is linked to brain FC in children with ASD, particularly in younger children with clinically severe symptoms, and consequently influences clinical symptoms. We hypothesized that specific genetic polymorphisms and methylation levels could influence ASD symptom expression by affecting resting-state (rs) brain FC. We aimed to provide insights into the neurobiological mechanisms distinguishing children with ASD from typically developing (TD) children by employing an integrated approach utilizing imaging genetics and imaging epigenetics.

METHODS

Participants and study procedures

This study analyzed 43 children diagnosed with ASD and 54 TD children, aged 2 to 12 years, from October 2015 to March 2021. The ASD diagnoses followed the DSM-5 criteria [25], and the TD children had no psychiatric conditions. All participants were Korean and medication-naïve. ASD severity was assessed using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) [26], the Korean Childhood Autism Rating Scale, Second Edition [27], and the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) [28]. Intelligence was evaluated using the Korean Educational Development Institute-Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-Revised [29]. The Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital endorsed the study protocol (No. 2008-116-1150). Before enrollment, all guardians provided written consent, and the children were given a clear explanation of the study and gave verbal agreement to participate.

Study 1 procedure

This study explored the relationship between OXTR risk alleles and rsFC in the context of ASD diagnosis and symptom severity. After image preprocessing, the final analysis included data from 91 participants (41 ASD, 50 TD).

Study 2 procedure

This study examined the associations among DNA methylation at two OXTR CpG sites (CpG 924 and CpG 934), rsFC, ASD diagnosis, and symptom severity. The analysis involved 40 ASD children and 40 TD children after excluding participants for whom methylation data were unavailable.

Data acquisition

All participants underwent rs-functional MRI (fMRI) scans using a Siemens Trio 3 T MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Systems) at Seoul National University Hospital. Oral chloral hydrate was administered to minimize motion artifacts during MRI scanning for 40 ASD and 15 TD children, under clinical supervision and following standard pediatric sedation protocols (25–50 mg/kg; max dose 1 g).

Detailed procedures for OXTR SNP genotyping and DNA methylation quantitation, as well as MRI preprocessing protocols, are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Group independent component analysis

Intrinsic brain networks were analyzed using group independent component analysis (ICA) from the fMRI Toolbox (v4.0). The process involved data reduction, ICA decomposition, and back reconstruction, yielding 19 independent components (ICs) for Study 1 and 21 ICs for Study 2 after artifact removal (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Networks were categorized into default mode (DMN), cognitive control (CC), auditory, somatomotor (SM), subcortical (SUB), and visual (VIS) networks [30-34]. Selected ICs were analyzed for spatial maps and functional network connectivity (FNC). Spatial maps identified representative voxels and FNC evaluated connectivity between component time courses (Supplementary Material) [35-37].

Statistical analyses

In Study 1, participants were categorized by their OXTR genotypes: rs2254298 A allele carriers (AA, AG) versus the non-risk group (GG), and rs53576 AA homozygotes versus carriers of one or more G alleles. This grouping addressed uneven genotype frequencies, thereby enhancing statistical robustness. In Study 2, the independent variable was the methylation level at two OXTR CpG sites (CpG 924 and CpG 934).

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine the effects of OXTR genetic variations and methylation levels on brain imaging measures, while controlling for age, sex, and the full-scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ).

The analysis proceeded in two steps; 1) Model 1—main effects: We first examined the direct effects of the OXTR SNPs and DNA methylation levels. Imaging outcome measurements = β0 + β1 (OXTR variation) + β2 (Diagnosis) + β3 (Age) + β4 (Sex) + β5 (FSIQ) + ε. 2) Model 2—interaction effects: Next, we tested the interaction effects between the OXTR variations and ASD diagnosis. Imaging outcome measurements = β0 + β1 (OXTR variation) + β2 (Diagnosis) + β3 (OXTR variation × Diagnosis) + β4 (Age) + β5 (Sex) + β6 (FSIQ) + ε.

The false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to correct for multiple comparisons (significance: 0.05). Significant findings were visualized using log-transformed p-values and t-statistic signs. Pearson’s correlation and multiple linear regression models, adjusted for age, sex, and FSIQ, were performed using the ordinary least squares regression implemented in the statsmodels Python package (Python Software Foundation; https://www.python.org) to analyze neuroimaging data and ASD symptomatology.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows that the ASD cohort had a significantly lower average age and FSIQ scores compared to the TD cohort, with notable sex distribution differences. Thus, age, sex, and FSIQ were included as covariates. Genotypic analyses (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3) revealed no significant differences in SNP distribution (rs2254298, rs53576) between the cohorts. Clinical scores based on these genotypes are detailed in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. Epigenetic findings showed significantly higher methylation levels at CpG 924 in the ASD group (58.03±6.71) than the TD group (51.97±4.05, p<0.001), while no significant differences were observed at CpG 934 (Table 1).

Interaction effects between OXTR genotype and ASD diagnosis on rsFC

Our imaging genetic analysis revealed that two SNPs in the OXTR gene (rs2254298 and rs53576) influenced FNC and spatial maps differently in diagnostic groups. For rs2254298, eight IC pairs showed significant group differences in FNC based on the risk allele (A) (Figure 1). Carriers of the A allele with ASD showed decreased FC, whereas TD individuals exhibited increased FC (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 6). For rs53576, significant interaction effects were observed in 11 IC pairs, with distinct trends (Figure 2A). AA homozygotes in the TD group showed increased FC, while ASD individuals showed decreased or less pronounced increases. A notable exception was the subcortico-SM connection, where ASD carriers exhibited increased connectivity, contrasting with TD individuals (Figure 2A). Network-averaged FNC revealed significant rs53576-ASD interaction effects, including higher connectivity in AA homozygotes with ASD for SUB-involved pairs and lower connectivity for non-SUB pairs (Figure 2B and Supplementary Table 4).

Spatial map analysis identified significant interactions for rs2254298 in four ICs and for rs53576 in five ICs, showing consistent patterns across both SNPs (Figure 3A and B; Supplementary Tables 5 and 7).

Interaction effects of OXTR methylation and an ASD diagnosis on rsFC

Our FNC analysis revealed that the interaction between an ASD diagnosis and methylation levels at CpG site 924 significantly affected two network-averaged FNC values for connections between the CC and SUB networks and those between the DMN and SUB networks (Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 8). In the TD group, higher methylation at CpG 924 was associated with increased rsFC, whereas in the ASD group, it was linked to a decrease in FC.

The spatial map analysis highlighted a significant interaction between an ASD diagnosis and the methylation level at CpG site 934 within a component of the SM network (Supplementary Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 9). Increased methylation levels correlated with significantly lower connectivity in the ASD group, whereas the TD group showed increased connectivity with higher methylation levels.

Clinical correlation of OXTR imaging genetics and epigenetics

Significant imaging features influenced by interaction effects between an ASD diagnosis and risk alleles correlated with ADOS and SRS scores (Table 2). For rs2254298, FNC values for connections between the VIS and SM networks correlated notably with SRS cognition scores (r=-0.420, p=0.026) (Figure 1). For rs53576, FNC values for CC to SM network connections correlated significantly with SRS awareness scores (r=-0.547, p=0.002) (Figure 2A).

In the imaging epigenetics analysis, the correlation analyses also revealed significant findings (Table 2). The FNC values for CpG 924 methylation, particularly between SUB network and CC network connections, correlated significantly with ADOS communication scores (r=-0.393, p=0.014), and the calibrated severity score (CSS; r=-0.437, p=0.006) (Figure 4). In imaging measures showing interaction effects from CpG 934 methylation, the spatial map z score of component 4 in the SM network correlated with SRS responsiveness (r=-0.459, p=0.036) (Supplementary Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

Atypical connectivity linked to OXTR genetic and epigenetic factors

ASD children showed significantly aberrant FC according to their OXTR genotypes compared with TD children. The FNC results for rs2254298 indicate that the discrepancy in connectivity between brain regions was more pronounced in ASD, especially in risk allele carriers (Figure 1). The effects of rs2254298 were notable in the CC networks, with similar results observed in the DMN involved in social cognition [38,39], as well as in the SM and VIS networks. Our results align with previous research showing that individuals with ASD-related genetic variations exhibited atypical brain function and structure [40]. Studies have shown that the rs2254298 A allele is associated with significantly lower rsFC in DMN regions compared with the GG genotype [17,41], and most imaging genetic studies on OXTR have found reduced FC in individuals with risk allele [42]. This suggests that the presence of the risk allele suppresses OXTR expression, leading to diminished neurological activity and FC. The OXTR gene, a G-protein-coupled receptor, modulates neural excitability by initiating cascade reactions within the neural circuitry [43]. Consequently, OXTR variants may alter neural activities in specific brain regions, leading to reduced FC.

Notably, the risk allele of the OXTR gene reduced intrinsic connectivity exclusively in ASD children, whereas it increased FC in TD children. This difference may suggest a potentially compensatory mechanism in typical development that mitigates genetic risk, a process that may be less effective or absent in ASD children. A previous study investigated the additive effects of the risk allele (A) in OXTR SNPs, including rs2254298, and, consistent with our findings, revealed opposite changes associated with the risk allele between the ASD and control groups [44]. Similar crossover effects were observed in a study by Uzefovsky et al. [45], which reported that A carriers of rs2254298 in the ASD group exhibited hypoactivity in the right supramarginal gyrus and inferior parietal lobule, whereas the control group showed the opposite pattern. Given these consistent findings, it may be suggested that ASD children may lack a protective mechanism against genetic risk that operates during typical brain development. However, this interpretation should be approached with caution, as alternative explanations, including differential sedation effects and developmental variability, and the imbalance in sex distribution between groups, may also account for the observed patterns.

Our FNC analysis of rs53576 showed FC patterns similar to those of rs2254298 across various brain regions. Out of the 11 FNC values, seven showed reduced FC in AA homozygotes among individuals with ASD, whereas AA homozygotes in TD individuals exhibited increased FC, further accentuating the differences between the groups (Figure 2A). Interestingly, the analysis of rs53576 revealed aberrant over-connectivity in the SUB area of ASD children compared to the TD group. In contrast to other brain regions, the FNC between SUBSM was lower in the TD group than in the ASD group (Figure 2A, a blue curve). Moreover, in AA homozygotes of rs53576, FC decreased in the TD group but increased in the ASD group, further widening the connectivity gap between the two groups. This pattern also appeared in our network-wise analysis, with increased SUB–SM and SUB–VIS connectivity in ASD (Figure 2B, blue squares). According to previous research, the atypical over-connectivity observed in the subcortico-cortical area of the ASD group is interpreted as a failure in the segregation process of the primary sensory region from deep brain nuclei during normal development [44,45]. In our prior research, we observed similar results when comparing FC between ASD and TD groups [30]. Notably, whereas the ASD children had reduced FC in most cognitive and DMN regions, they exhibited increased FC in SUB areas, in contrast to the TD group. Building on our previous research, we found that the over-connectivity in the SUB networks of the ASD group is further exacerbated in OXTR risk allele homozygotes, resulting in distinctly different FC characteristics between ASD and TD children. We propose that this finding highlights the importance of the subcortico-cortical network and its susceptibility to the OXTR gene in the atypical developmental processes of ASD.

In the spatial map analysis, significant findings were concentrated in the CC networks and DMN for both rs2254298 and rs53576 (Figure 3). ASD children showed lower FC than TD children in frontal regions, including the orbital cortex, superior and middle frontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which are linked to cognitive and social brain functions. These differences became more pronounced in carriers of the A allele, and that was consistent for both SNPs. Previous studies have investigated the effects of OXTR genetic variants on the DMN [41,46,47]. Wang et al. [41] reported that A allele carriers of rs2254298 showed reduced rsFC in the posterior cingulate cortex and dorsal ACC, compared the GG genotype. In their following paper, they reported reduced FC in male A allele carriers of rs53576 and attributed that finding to OXTR’s influence on social cognition [46]. Prior studies on OXTR have primarily focused on social brain network and reward circuits such as the limbic systems. However, our findings suggest that OXTR may also influence various brain regions involved in both social and nonsocial cognition, which exhibit significant functional impairments in ASD.

However, an important consideration in interpreting these findings is the marked asymmetry in sedation rates between groups (ASD: 97.6% vs. TD: 30%). Although clinically necessary to control head motion, chloral hydrate can preferentially attenuate BOLD signals in frontal and parietal regions [48,49], where our most prominent interaction effects occurred. While our findings align with previous studies on non-sedated ASD subjects, the potential influence of differential sedation cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies employing natural sleep or non-sedated protocols are needed to confirm that these frontal alterations are primarily driven by OXTR-related pathophysiology rather than pharmacological effects.

Aberrant FC in ASD children associated with OXTR methylation

When exploring OXTR gene methylation on intrinsic connectivity in the ASD and TD groups, we found the expected result in two network-wise FNC values (SUB–DMN and SUB–CC) (Figure 4). These network-average FNC values were higher in the ASD group, indicating over-connectivity in the SUB region (Supplementary Table 8). Our epigenetic analysis showed that hypermethylation of CpG site 924 decreased FC in the ASD group (Figure 4, pink curve), consistent with previous research linking hypermethylation to reduced FC in individuals with ASD [4,23,50]. DNA methylation of the OXTR gene is related to reduced gene transcription and decreased protein expression that then affects oxytocin signaling pathways in the brain and likely contributes to the reduced FC observed in brain regions associated with social cognition in people with ASD [4,23]. In contrast, TD children showed a positive correlation between methylation levels and FC strength (Figure 4, blue curve), suggesting that the increased connectivity may serve as a compensatory mechanism in response to methylation-related genetic risk. These observations align with our imaging genetic analysis and highlight the complex interplay between genetic and epigenetic factors in establishing and maintaining brain connectivity.

We found a significant interaction between ASD diagnosis and the methylation level at CpG site 934 within one IC of the SM network (C4) (Supplementary Figure 3). Similar to the FNC results, increased methylation at CpG site 934 was significantly associated with higher connectivity in TD children but not in ASD children, indicating a significant difference in how methylation influences intrinsic FC between the two groups and implying that compensatory mechanisms for genetic risk present in typical development may be absent in ASD.

Clinical correlations

Significant correlations were found between clinical scores and FNC that differed according to the OXTR genotypes in the ASD group. The FNC between C5 and C16, representing SM and cognitive areas, respectively, correlated negatively with ADOS restricted and repetitive behavior (RRB) subscores across both SNPs (Table 2). The ADOS RRB subscore, which measures repetitive and restrictive interests, stereotyped behaviors, and mannerisms, correlated significantly with FC in motor-related precentral/postcentral areas (C5) and CC–related precuneus and IPL areas (C16). This result is consistent with a previous study showing that abnormalities in brain connectivity, including the precuneus and IPL regions, are significantly associated with RRB [51]. For rs2254298, significant correlations were observed between the FC of cognitive and VIS networks and SRS subscores for communication, social responsiveness, and social cognition. The most pronounced correlation was between the VIS network (C11) and SM network (C10) and social cognition–related clinical scores. In rs53576, decreased FNC between the DMN (C22) and CC (C23) correlated negatively with ADOS total scores and RRB subscores. The FNC reduction between the SM (C6) and CC networks (C16) showed significant negative correlations with SRS subscores, especially in social awareness. Despite inconsistent results in FC regions and autism symptom types, reduced FC in ASD, which is further decreased by the OXTR gene, was linked to worsening autism symptoms.

Our epigenetic results also show significant associations between clinical scores and FNC in the CC and SUB networks, with notable differences in the hypermethylation of CpG 924 between the ASD and TD groups. The ADOS CSS had the most significant correlation, adjusted for ADOS version and module differences. Hypermethylation of CpG 934 correlated significantly with social responsiveness and motivation SRS subscores and FC within the CC and SM networks. The imaging epigenetics results, though not entirely consistent, thus suggest that hypermethylation exacerbates differences between the ASD and TD groups, indicating an association with the ASD phenotype. Previous studies on the general population found that hypermethylation of OXTR, including CpG site 934, correlated with autistic traits, especially communication problems, and reduced the decline in FC related to social information [50,52]. However, most studies have focused on TD or high-functioning autistic adults, limiting their generalizability. To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the impact of OXTR methylation on intrinsic connectivity in children with severe autism. Our findings are significant as they reveal altered intrinsic connectivity associated with OXTR methylation specifically in this population and its link to clinical symptoms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, sedation effects during fMRI scans, particularly for the ASD group, may have influenced FC results, complicating network interpretation [51,52]. Future research should control for such effects. Second, the broad age range (2–12 years) and the resulting lack of age homogeneity limit developmental insights, as FC undergoes substantial changes across early childhood. Thus, age-related variability may have influenced the results despite statistical adjustment, and future studies with narrower age ranges or age-stratified analyses are warranted. Third, there was an imbalance in sex distribution between groups (with a higher proportion of males in the ASD group compared to the TD group), which is an important consideration in ASD research given known sex-related differences in neurobiology and genetic effects. Although sex was included as a covariate in the analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, only two SNPs of the OXTR gene were examined, and future studies should explore cumulative or interactive effects of additional SNPs. Finally, although this study has the advantage of including relatively young children with severe ASD compared to similar studies, a larger sample size is needed for genetic analysis. Future research should conduct additional analyses with a larger cohort to enhance the robustness of genetic findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that OXTR gene variations and hypermethylation at CpG sites are associated with aberrant rsFC in children with ASD. They did not exhibit the compensatory mechanisms that would be expected to develop in TD children. Our findings reveal distinct connectivity patterns in the ASD and TD groups influenced by OXTR, suggesting that such genetic and epigenetic markers can affect key neural networks distinctly in these groups. Further research should validate these results in younger, well-defined cohorts.
