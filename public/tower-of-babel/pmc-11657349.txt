Association between bowel movement frequency, stool consistency and MAFLD and advanced fibrosis in US adults: a cross-sectional study of NHANES 2005-2010
Authors: Miaomin Ye, Yijia He, Yin Xia, Ziyi Zhong, Xiaocen Kong, Yunting Zhou, Weiping Wang, Suping Qin, Qian Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough previous studies have established associations between specific gut microbiota (GM) and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), research examining the relationship between functional gastrointestinal symptoms and MAFLD, including advanced fibrosis, remains limited. This study aims to investigate the association between stool consistency, bowel movement frequency (BMF), and the occurrence of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis in U.S. adults.MethodsThis population-based study included 9,928 adults from the 2005–2010 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), with a mean age of 47.19 ± 16.65 years, comprising 47.7% males and 52.3% females. Weighted logistic regression was used to assess the association between stool consistency, BMF, and MAFLD or advanced fibrosis. A linear trend was assessed by treating BMF categories as continuous variables with ordinal values. The dose-response relationship between BMF and MAFLD was analyzed using restricted cubic splines (RCS) regression. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of the findings.ResultsIn the RCS regression, no significant nonlinear relationship was observed between BMF and the risk of MAFLD (p-overall < 0.0001; p-nonlinear = 0.0663). The multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for MAFLD were 0.82 (95% CI 0.69–0.98), 1.31 (95% CI 1.16–1.46), and 1.50 (95% CI 1.14–1.99) for participants with 3–6 BMs/week, 1–2 BMs/day, and > 2 BMs/day, respectively, compared to those with once/day (p-trend < 0.001). For stool consistency, hard stools were associated with a decreased risk of MAFLD (OR 0.77; 95% CI 0.62–0.95), whereas loose stools increased the risk (OR 1.37; 95% CI 1.05–1.80), relative to normal stools. A significant interaction between BMF and age was observed. No significant associations were found between stool consistency or BMF and advanced liver fibrosis. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsThis cross-sectional study demonstrates that a BMF of 3–6 BMs/week and hard stools are associated with a reduced risk of MAFLD, whereas a BMF of more than once/day and loose stools are linked to an increased risk of MAFLD. Moreover, no significant associations were observed between stool consistency, BMF, and advanced fibrosis among individuals diagnosed with MAFLD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: MAFLD, Stool consistency, Bowel movement frequency, Liver fibrosis, NHANES, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has emerged as the predominant etiology of chronic liver disease globally, affecting approximately 2 billion individuals, with an estimated prevalence of 25% [1, 2]. It involves the abnormal accumulation of triglycerides within hepatocytes and may progress to subsequent stages, including non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). NAFLD is strongly associated with various metabolic dysfunctions, including obesity, insulin resistance, lipid metabolism disorders, and metabolic syndrome (MetS) [3–5]. With increasing insights into the pathophysiology of NAFLD, an international expert panel recommended in 2020 the use of “metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease” (MAFLD) as an alternative term for NAFLD. This term places greater emphasis on metabolic disturbances, eliminating the need to exclude secondary causes such as excessive alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, and liver cancer [6].

Interestingly, not only the liver itself is involved in the pathogenesis, but also the pancreas, adipose tissue, stomach, muscles, and even the dysbiosis of the gut microbiota (GM) are implicated in the disease process [7]. Numerous studies have suggested that gut dysbiosis may influence the development and progression of MAFLD through the gut-liver axis, involving various bacterial metabolites such as bile acids (BAs), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, choline, and ethanol [8]. Distinct GM profiles are observed at different stages of MAFLD [9]. Modulating the GM or supplementing specific bacterial metabolites may offer potential therapeutic targets for MAFLD [10].

Gut transit time, defined as the duration required for food to traverse the gastrointestinal tract, has been recognized as a key determinant of GM composition. It can be assessed using direct methods, such as SmartPill capsules or indirect markers, such as stool consistency, bowel movement frequency (BMF), and stool water content [11]. While accumulating evidence highlights the role of the GM in the pathogenesis of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis, and while studies have explored the relationship between gut transit time and conditions like cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and colorectal cancer [12], research on the relationship between gut transit time and MAFLD or advanced fibrosis remains limited. Only one study has suggested that chronic diarrhea may elevate the risk of MAFLD [13].

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a cross-sectional study of the non-institutionalized civilian population in the United States, is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Using a stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling design, NHANES is carried out in continuous 2-year cycles to assess the health and nutritional status of both adults and children in the U.S. (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). The objective of this cross-sectional study was to explore the association between stool consistency, BMF, and the prevalence of MAFLD and its progression to advanced fibrosis, using data from the NHANES. By investigating the relationship between gut transit time indicators and the likelihood of MAFLD and its progression to advanced fibrosis, we hope to uncover novel insights into the shared pathophysiological mechanisms of MAFLD and provide guidance for its future prevention and treatment.

Methods

Study Cohort

This study was conducted based on the NHANES database. All NHANES protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants upon enrollment. The Institutional Review Board of Nanjing First Hospital deemed this study exempt due to its use of publicly available, de-identified data, waiving the requirement for additional informed consent. Given the availability of the data of stool consistency and BMF, a total of 31,034 participants from three consecutive NHANES cycles (2005–2006, 2007–2008, and 2009–2010) were enrolled.

Given the absence of abdominal ultrasound data, the diagnosis of hepatic steatosis relied on the Fatty Liver Index (FLI) and Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI). Advanced fibrosis was evaluated on the basis of the NAFLD Fibrosis Score (NFS), Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants aged < 20 years; (2) incomplete data for the calculation of FLI, HSI, NFS, FIB-4, and APRI, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), AST, Gamma-glutamyltransferase (γ-GGT), triglycerides (TG), serum album, platelet count, Body Mass Index (BMI), and waist circumference; (3) unavailability of data on BMF and stool consistency; (4) chronic liver disease that could cause abnormal liver function and influence FLI or HSI results, including HCC, viral hepatitis (positive for hepatitis C virus (HCV) RNA, HCV antibody or hepatitis B surface antigen) and excessive alcohol consumption (> 30 g/day for males and > 20 g/day for females); (5) personal history of colorectal cancer; (6) pregnant or lactating; (7) missing information on key covariates, including physical activity, diabetes status, hypertension status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary intake and milk consumption; (8) missing data of demographics. Gut transit time

The evaluation of gut transit time was carried out by analyzing participants’ responses to the NHANES Bowel Health Questionnaire (BHQ). Gut transit time was defined based on two key parameters: BMF (BHD050) and stool consistency (BHQ060). Participants were presented with a vivid and colourful picture with descriptions of the seven Bristol Stool Form Scale types (BSFS; Type 1–7) and then they were required to select the number corresponds with their typical or most common stool type. Individuals identifying their usual or most common stool type as BSFS Type 1 (separate hard lumps, such as nuts) or BSFS Type 2 (sausage-like, but lumpy) were categorized as experiencing hard stools. Those identifying their usual or most common stool type as BSFS Type 6 (fluffy pieces with ragged edges, a mushy stool) or BSFS Type 7 (watery, no solid pieces) were classified as having loose stools. The remaining subjects were classified as having normal stools type. Additionally, the BMF was assessed by posing the question “How many times per week do you usually have a bowel movement (BM)?”, Responses were grouped into five categories: < 3 BMs/week, 3–6 BMs/week, once/day, 1–2 BMs/day and > 2 BMs/day.

Participants were defined as having hepatic steatosis if FLI ≥ 60 or HSI > 36.

In this study, the diagnosis of MAFLD was based on the presence of hepatic steatosis assessed by FLI or HSI, as an alternative for liver biopsy or imaging examinations in addition to one of the following three criteria: 1) overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2); 2) diabetes; or 3) metabolic dysfunction. Metabolic dysfunction was defined as meeting at least two of the following criteria: 1) waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men, ≥ 88 cm in women; 2) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or use of relevant medication; 3) plasma TG ≥ 150 mg/dL or use of relevant medication; 4) plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) < 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women, or use of relevant medication; 5) prediabetes (fasting plasma glucose (FPG) 100–125 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) 5.7–6.4%); and 6) homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) score ≥ 2.5 [6, 18]. Notebally, due to limitations in the study design, the 2005–2010 NHANES database lacked the data of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HsCRP). As a result, HsCRP levels > 2 mg/L were not included in the diagnostic criteria for MAFLD in this study.

Other covariates

Standardized questionnaires administered during home interviews and at mobile examination centers (MEC) collected data on demographics, health status, medication use, and lifestyle. Age was stratified into three groups: 20–39 years, 40–59 years, and ≥ 60 years. Race/ethnicity categories included Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other races. Marital status was classified as never married, married or living with a partner, and divorced, separated, or widowed. Education level was categorized into less than high school, high school or equivalent, and college or above. Poverty-Income Ratio (PIR), reflecting the ratio of family income to poverty, was delineated into three levels: low income (≤ 1.3), middle income (> 1.3, ≤ 3.5), and high income (> 3.5). Smoking status was categorized as never (smoked < 100 cigarettes in life), former (smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life but has quit), and current (smoked at least 100 cigarettes but still smoke). Vigorous physical activity was classified as vigorous-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause large increases in breathing or heart rate like running or basketball for at least 10 min continuously and moderate recreational activity was defined as moderate-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause a small increase in breathing or heart rate such as brisk walking, bicycling, swimming, or golf for at least 10 min continuously. Physical measurements were conducted at MECs according to standardized protocols, and blood samples were collected for further measurement. BMI classifications included normal (< 25.0), overweight (≥ 25.0 to < 30.0), and obese (≥ 30.0). Diabetes was diagnosed if participants met one of the following criteria: (a) FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL; (b) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; or (c) self-reported diagnosis or insulin use. Hypertension was defined by a self-reported history or an average blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Dyslipidemia was defined as serum total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 200 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, HDL < 40 mg/dL for male or < 50 mg/dL for female. CVD was identified based on self-reported medical history, including congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, heart attack, or stroke. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was defined as a self-reported history of chronic bronchitis or emphysema and cancer was defned as a self-reported history of any cancer. All participants completed two 24-h dietary recall interviews. The first was conducted face-to-face at the MEC, and the second was conducted by telephone 3 to 10 days later. Alcohol consumption and other dietary intake data were averaged over these two recall periods (if only the first day was available, that value was used). Milk consumption was divided into four categories: never; rarely (< once/week); sometimes (≥ once/week, < once/day); and often (≥ once/day).

Statistical analysis

All estimates were weighted after taking the primary sampling unit, pseudo-strata, and sampling weights to account for the complex sampling design unless otherwise specified. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), or median ± interquartile range (IQR), depending on the data distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as unweighted frequencies (weighted percentages). Differences between groups were assessed using the Rao-Scott chi-square test for categorical variables, independent samples t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Subsequently, survey-weighted logistic regression was conducted to examine the associations between stool consistency, BMF, and the odds of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis. Both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted models were employed. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 included adjustments for gender, age, ethnicity, marital status, education level, and PIR. Model 3 further adjusted for alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CVD, COPD, in addition to the covariates from Model 2. Model 4 included all covariates from Model 3, along with continuous dietary intake variables (total calories, protein, fat, carbohydrates, sugar, fiber, moisture, caffeine) and milk consumption. To prevent over-adjustment, BMI was excluded from the models due to its inclusion in the calculation of HSI and FLI. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to evaluate the statistical significance of the associations.

Linear trends were assessed by assigning ordinal values to each BMF category and treating them as continuous variables. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of BMF, was employed to evaluate exposure-response relationships between BMF and the risk of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis. Nonlinearity was tested using the likelihood ratio test. Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted using stratified logistic regression models, with stratification based on sex (male or female), age group (20–39 years, 40–59 years, and ≥ 60 years), smoking status (never, former, and current), and the presence or absence of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Additionally, p-values for interactions between BMF and these stratified variables were calculated to assess potential effect modifications.

Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our primary findings. Firstly, considering the potential impact of medications on MAFLD, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that excluded medications known to have potential impact, such as pioglitazone, metformin, dapagliflozin, liraglutide, semaglutide, atorvastatin and aspirin [23]. Secondly, participants using opioid medications or potentially diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease were excluded due to their substantial impact on gastrointestinal motility, which could alter bowel movement frequency and stool consistency. Opioid medications included buprenorphine, methadone, codeine, morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, hydrocodone, oxymorphone, hydromorphone, tapentadol, meperidine, and tramadol [24]. Parkinson’s disease diagnosis was determined based on the use of anti-Parkinsonian medications, specifically benztropine, carbidopa, levodopa, methyldopa, ropinirole, entacapone, tolcapone, amantadine, cabergoline, orphenadrine, or pramipexole [25]. Moreover, to mitigate potential misclassification, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the FLI as an alternative non-invasive method for diagnosing MAFLD. Finally, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis employing a combination of the FIB-4 index and the APRI as an alternative approach for assessing advanced fibrosis. All statistical analyses were performed with R packages (version 4.2.3). All tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of study participants

After excluding participants based on the following criteria: individuals aged < 20 years (n = 13,902); those with missing data for the calculation of FLI, HSI, NFS, FIB-4, or APRI (n = 2,487); participants lacking data on BMF or stool consistency (n = 1,178); individuals with chronic liver diseases that could impair the accuracy of HSI and FLI in assessing hepatic steatosis or with a history of colorectal cancer (n = 1,899); pregnant or breastfeeding women (n = 431); and those with missing critical covariate (n = 406) or demographic information (n = 803), a total of 9,928 participants were included in the final analysis with an average age of 47.19 ± 16.65. The proportion of females (52.3%) was slightly higher than that of males (47.7%). In comparison to individuals without MAFLD, those with MAFLD were more likely to be married, middle-aged participants with lower educational attainment and reduced physical activity. Their dietary patterns were characterized by a higher intake of protein, lipids, caffeine, and liquids, while simultaneously consuming fewer carbohydrates, sugars, and fibers. Biochemically, individuals with MAFLD exhibited significantly elevated levels of ALT, AST, and γ-GGT, alongside lower serum albumin concentrations. Additionally, they had a markedly higher prevalence of metabolic disorders, including obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CVD. Notably, they showed a greater tendency toward loose stools and increased BMF.

Within the MAFLD subgroup, 5.0% were categorized as high fbrosis risk individuals. Individuals at high risk of fibrosis tend to be older poor-incomed and less-educated former smokers with less activity. They demonstrated less dietary intake and had a higher prevalence of obesity, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, COPD and cancer, but a lower prevalence of dyslipidmia. Participants at high risk for fibrosis also exhibited significantly lower average ALT and albumin levels, while AST levels were notably higher compared to those at low risk. Interestingly, individuals at high risk of fibrosis also had a greater prevalence of loose stools and higher BMF. Association between stool consistency and the odds of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis

In the unadjusted model (Model 1), participants reporting hard stools had significantly lower odds of MAFLD (OR 0.79; 95% CI 0.66–0.96), while those reporting loose stools exhibited significantly higher odds of MAFLD (OR 1.48; 95% CI 1.18–1.85). After further adjustment for demographic factors, lifestyle variables, and comorbidities, these findings remained consistent in both Model 2 and Model 3. Even after additional adjustments for dietary intake and milk consumption in Model 4, the negative association between hard stools and MAFLD remained statistically significant (OR 0.77; 95% CI 0.62–0.95). Similarly, the statistically significant association between loose stools and MAFLD also persisted (OR 1.37; 95% CI 1.05–1.80). However, no statistically significant association (p > 0.05) was found between stool consistency and the risk of advanced fibrosis, as defined by the NFS (Table 2).

Association between BMF and the odds of MAFLD and advanced fibrosis

After multivariable adjustment in Model 4, no significant nonlinear relationship was observed between BMF and MAFLD in the RCS regression (p-overall < 0.0001; p-nonlinear = 0.0663) (Fig. 2a). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), a frequency of 3–6 BMs/week was significantly inversely associated with MAFLD, compared to once/day. Conversely, a frequency of 1–2 BMs/day and > 2 BMs/day were significantly positively associated with MAFLD. This association remained consistent across the minimally adjusted model (Model 2), partially adjusted model (Model 3), and fully adjusted model (Model 4). In Model 4, compared to individuals with a BMF of once/day, those with 3–6 BMs/week had an 18% reduced risk of MAFLD, while individuals with 1–2 BMs/day and > 2 BMs/day had a 31% and 50% increased risk of MAFLD, respectively. Higher BMF was associated with increased risk of MAFLD (p for trend < 0.001). Additionally, after full adjustment in the RCS regression, no nonlinear relationship was observed between BMF and advanced fibrosis (p-overall < 0.0001; p-nonlinear = 0.9879) (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, no significant association was found between BMF and advanced fibrosis after full adjustment (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

In subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, the association between stool consistency and MAFLD remained consistent. No significant interactions were observed between these variables and stool consistency (p for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3). Similarly, subgroup analyses revealed a consistent association between BMF and MAFLD across most subgroups (p for interaction > 0.05). However, this association varied by age group (p for interaction < 0.05). In participants aged 20–39 years, the association between BMF and MAFLD aligned with the primary findings. In those aged 40–59 years, a BMF of > 2 BMs/day was associated with a significantly increased risk of MAFLD (OR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.09–2.58). No significant association between BMF and MAFLD was observed in participants aged ≥ 60 years (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses

To further assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. Excluding participants taking medications known to potentially affect MAFLD (unweighted n = 8,701) and those using opioid medications or diagnosed with Parkinson's disease (unweighted n = 9,587) yielded consistent results. Additionally, redefining MAFLD using the FLI did not significantly alter the results. To further examine the potential impact of stool consistency and BMF on the development of advanced liver fibrosis in MAFLD patients, an additional sensitivity analysis utilizing FIB-4 and APRI criteria was performed. However, no statistically significant associations were observed (Table 4).

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation into the associations between stool consistency, BMF, and MAFLD, as well as advanced liver fibrosis, within a demographically representative sample of the U.S. population. This cross-sectional study suggests a positive linear relationship between BMF and the risk of MAFLD. Compared to participants with a BMF of once/day, those with 3–6 BMs/week exhibited a reduced risk of MAFLD, whereas individuals with 1–2 BMs/day or > 2 BMs/day showed an increased risk. In terms of stool consistency, hard stools were linked to a decreased risk of MAFLD, while loose stools were associated with an increased risk. These associations remained generally consistent across various subgroups, including gender, age, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. A significant interaction was observed only between age groups and BMF. However, no significant correlation was found between stool consistency or BMF and advanced liver fibrosis. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our results.

Potential mechanisms are as follows. First of all, multiple prospective studies have suggested that the reduction in intestinal transit time may increase the risk of T2DM, MetS, and CVD, conditions that share common pathophysiological mechanisms with MAFLD, such as inflammation and insulin resistance [26]. Another plausible mechanism involves the dysregulation of the GM and its metabolites, as intestinal transit time is a key determinant of the GM habitat [27]. And the GM has been recognized as a key element in modulating the pathological processes associated with MAFLD [28]. Research has shown that intestinal transit time is directly proportional to distal colon pH, which in turn influences the composition of the GM [29, 30]. A shorter intestinal transit time is associated with a higher proportion of Bacteroides [31], a genus linked to the progression of MAFLD [32]. BAs, classified into primary bile acids (PBAs) and secondary bile acids (SBAs), are key metabolites of the GM. Prolonged intestinal transit time not only extends the time window for the conversion of PBAs into SBAs and their subsequent reabsorption [33], but also creates an environment that facilitates this conversion and absorption [11]. This leads to the upregulation of BA receptor activation, including farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and G-protein-coupled receptors (TGR5) [34]. Heightened activation of FXR and TGR5 enhances the secretion of gastrointestinal hormones, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY), which exert inhibitory effects on MAFLD, while also impairing glycolysis and lipogenesis, thus limiting hepatic lipid accumulation [35, 36]. Additionally, contrary to PBAs, SBAs demonstrate anti-inflammatory characteristics, thereby mitigating the risk of MAFLD [37, 38]. SCFAs, produced through the fermentation of indigestible polysaccharides such as cellulose, are another key metabolite of the GM [39]. SCFAs reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, while simultaneously increasing the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in macrophages [40]. Moreover, they could also up-regulate the release of GLP-1 and PYY [41]. Prolonged colonic transit time has been associated with a reduction in fecal SCFAs, suggesting increased absorption and utilization of these metabolites [29, 42]. Previous studies have indicated that SCFAs can mitigate the development of MAFLD [43]. These findings align with our study, which suggests that higher BMF and loose stools may increase the risk of MAFLD, while lower BMF and hard stools may decrease the risk. Although numerous proposed mechanisms explaining these associations, additional research is essential to establish compelling evidence for the causal link.

Furthermore, our analysis revealed a significant interaction between BMF and age, indicating that the impact of BMF on MAFLD may vary across different age groups. Specifically, the association between BMF and MAFLD weakens with increasing age. As individuals age, gut health becomes more complex, with significant changes in gut function and microbiota composition, potentially reducing BMF’s reliability as an indicator of MAFLD. Additionally, older adults are more likely to have multiple chronic conditions, such as CVD and diabetes, which may confound the relationship between BMF and MAFLD.

Our findings suggest that the optimal BMF is once/day or less, and it is advisable to avoid loose stools. Numerous factors, including dietary intake and physical activity, influence intestinal transit time. Considering the bidirectional interactions between the microbiota and intestinal motility, microbial presence and their metabolites can impact intestinal transit time [11]. Dietary choices may influence motility directly by altering the bulk flow of material through the GI tract or indirectly through their impact on the GM [44]. Exercise could regulate the intestinal transit time directly or through modulating the GM [45]. Maintaining stool consistency and BMF within the recommended range can be achieved through a balanced diet, moderate exercise, the use of probiotics, and even fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). These practices aid in the prevention of MAFLD and mitigate the risk of further deterioration of hepatic steatosis.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the causal relationship between stool consistency, BMF, and MAFLD, as well as advanced liver fibrosis, cannot be conclusively established due to the cross-sectional design of our analysis based on NHANES data. A prospective study is necessary to validate these findings. Secondly, imaging examination and liver biopsy, the gold standard for MAFLD and advanced fibrosis diagnosis, was not employed due to the lack of data, potentially leading to the misclassification of MAFLD or liver fibrosis. Third, the reliance on self-reported BMF and stool consistency introduces the potential for non-differential misclassification, which could attenuate the true associations. Additionally, despite efforts to adjust for various confounders, the results may still be influenced by unmeasured factors, such as depression [46] and hypothyroidism [47]. Lastly, the study did not account for other medications, such as laxatives or anticholinergic drugs, that may affect intestinal transit time [48].

Conclusion

In summary, this cross-sectional study found a significant positive linear relationship between BMF and the risk of MAFLD. Compared to participants with a BMF of once/day, those with 3–6 BMs/week had a reduced risk of MAFLD, while individuals with 1–2 BMs/day or > 2 BMs/day showed an increased risk. Hard stools were linked to a decreased risk of MAFLD, whereas loose stools were associated with an increased risk. However, no significant correlation was observed between stool consistency or BMF and advanced liver fibrosis. Further prospective studies are needed to explore the causal relationship between stool consistency, BMF, and MAFLD, which could provide insights into the mechanisms underlying gut transit time and MAFLD.
