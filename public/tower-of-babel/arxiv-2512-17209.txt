Do Foundational Audio Encoders Understand Music Structure?
Keisuke Toyama Zhi Zhong Akira Takahashi Shusuke Takahashi Yuki Mitsufuji

Abstract
In music information retrieval (MIR) research, the use of pretrained foundational audio encoders (FAEs) has recently become a trend. FAEs pretrained on large amounts of music and audio data have been shown to improve performance on MIR tasks such as music tagging and automatic music transcription. However, their use for music structure analysis (MSA) remains underexplored. Although many open-source FAE models are available, only a small subset has been examined for MSA, and the impact of factors such as learning methods, training data, and model context length on MSA performance remains unclear. In this study, we conduct comprehensive experiments on 11 types of FAEs to investigate how these factors affect MSA performance. Our results demonstrate that FAEs using self-supervised learning with masked language modeling on music data are particularly effective for MSA. These findings pave the way for future research in MSA.

Introduction

Music structure analysis (MSA) is a fundamental and challenging task in music information retrieval (MIR)
[
26
]
.
The goal of MSA is to divide music recordings into non-overlapping consecutive segments and assign labels that define their functional terms such as ‘intro,’ ‘verse,’ ‘chorus,’ and ‘bridge’
[
33
,
26
]
.
This process typically involves two main subtasks:
boundary detection
, which identifies the temporal points where segments are separated, and
function prediction
, which classifies segments on the basis of musical similarity and features
[
26
]
.
MSA algorithms leverage key musical characteristics: segments are relatively homogeneous in attributes like key and instrumentation; sudden shifts in musicality occur at segment transitions; and segments with the same functional label are musically similar and often repeat
[
26
]
.
Using these characteristics, methods based on self-similarity matrices of audio features such as mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)
[
34
,
31
,
3
]
, and chromagrams
[
34
,
38
,
3
,
5
]
have been proposed.

With the development of annotated datasets like SALAMI
[
33
]
and Harmonix
[
27
]
, supervised learning methods have emerged, particularly those using deep neural networks (DNNs) such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
[
35
,
37
]
, Transformers
[
36
,
16
,
41
]
, and approaches combining self-similarity with other features
[
34
,
38
,
31
,
28
,
3
,
29
,
5
]
.

Advances in representation learning have led to various pretrained models that extract general-purpose features from audio and music
[
39
,
20
,
13
,
42
,
24
,
17
,
18
,
40
,
6
]
.
In this study, we refer to these models as
foundational audio encoders
(FAEs).
Features extracted from FAEs are expected to benefit multiple downstream tasks.
Previous studies have shown that FAE features improve MIR tasks, including music tagging
[
9
,
20
]
, as well as tasks requiring temporal resolution, such as automatic piano transcription, music source separation
[
21
]
, and music super-resolution
[
42
]
.
Recently, two studies have applied this approach to MSA.
Won et al.
[
39
]
investigated MSA using MusicFM
[
39
]
and MERT
[
20
]
, achieving performance surpassing existing methods with a simple two-layer linear backend fed by MusicFM features.
They also showed that further improvements are possible by fine-tuning the FAE.
Zhang et al.
[
41
]
improved performance by connecting MusicFM to a Transformer and fine-tuning the combined model, demonstrating additional gains by enabling the Transformer to handle longer temporal contexts (over 100 seconds).

Although many open-source FAEs have been proposed, only two have been examined in MSA research.
While FAEs differ in learning objectives, methods, training data types, and neural network architectures, how these factors contribute to MSA performance remains unclear.
Additionally, several FAEs have been used as evaluation metrics for audio and music generation tasks
[
20
,
17
,
18
,
40
,
6
,
8
]
, but their effectiveness for MSA is not well understood.
Finding an FAE that understands music structure shall lead us to a better metric for long-form or full-track music generation.
Surveys and benchmarks have explored using Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD)
[
15
]
and other combinations of distance metrics and FAEs to evaluate generated music
[
12
,
14
]
, and as codec benchmarks
[
32
]
, but such research for MSA tasks is still lacking.

In this study, we conduct experiments on the MSA task using models with a simple backend connected to 11 types of FAE, comprehensively and exhaustively investigating the potential factors in FAE design that contributes to the understanding of music structure boundaries and segment functions.

3
Experiments

3.1
Dataset and Metrics

Dataset.

We used the Harmonix
[
27
]
dataset, which consists of 912 songs totaling approximately 3,400 minutes and spans multiple genres.
We converted the function labels in the dataset into seven types (‘intro,’ ‘verse,’ ‘chorus,’ ‘bridge,’ ‘inst,’ ‘outro,’ and ‘silence’) according to
[
36
]
.
We conducted 8-fold cross-validation experiments: six were used for training, one for validation, and one for testing.

Metrics.

We evaluated performance using four conventional metrics
[
26
]
, the F-score at a hit rate at 0.5 seconds (HR.5F) and at 3 seconds (HR3F) for boundary detection, as well as the F-score for pairwise frame-level clustering (PWF) and the accuracy rate per frame (ACC) for function prediction.
We calculated these scores using the
mir_eval
library
[
30
]
with its default settings.

3.2
Methods

FAE Feature Extraction.

Feature extraction from FAEs was performed according to the instructions in each GitHub repository.

Pooling.

Since MSA is a task that requires long-term understanding of music, we hypothesize that some information in the fine-grained FAE features is redundant.
Furthermore, since the HR.5F tolerance is less than 0.5 seconds, a frame rate of approximately 2 Hz may be sufficient.
To investigate this, we performed a
pooling
process.
Feature vectors were obtained from 5 seconds of input and averaged along the time axis.
Performing this with a 0.5-second hop size generated pseudo-features with a 0.5-second temporal resolution.
Since PANNs and CLAP cannot obtain frame-wise features, this method allowed us to pseudo-generate frame-wise features.

Figure 2:
Linear probing model

Linear probing model.

To investigate the capabilities of FAEs in the MSA task, the backend used the simplest configuration: connecting only a single-layer linear module as shown in Fig.
2
.
Regarding the utilization of long-term contexts, it is interpreted that this is not performed by this backend model, but only by the FAE.
In this setup, the model takes 30 seconds of FAE features as input and estimates 30 seconds of MSA labels (
N
and
T
in Fig.
2
, respectively).
Since each FAE has a different frame rate, adaptive average pooling was applied to match the frame rate of the MSA labels.
Then, the dimension of FAE features (
Z
) was converted to eight using the linear module, with one used for boundary detection and the remaining seven for function prediction.
We used binary cross-entropy loss for boundary detection and 7-class cross-entropy loss for function prediction, respectively.
The sum of these losses was used for training.

3.3
Experimental Settings

We set the frame rate of the MSA labels to 2 Hz (
T=60
).
For training, we used a batch size (
B
) of 8, the AdamW optimizer
[
23
]
with a weight decay of 0.01, a learning rate of 0.0001, a warmup period of 5 epochs, and cosine annealing for learning rate scheduling.
The models were trained for 100 epochs, and the model with the highest validation score was selected.
For post-processing, we applied the peak-picking algorithm
[
35
]
to detect boundaries, then chose the function labels with the highest probability in each segment
[
36
]
.

4.1
Main Results

In summary, we hypothesize that training a
long-context
Transformer model with
MLM
on
music
datasets is a promising direction as FAEs for the MSA task, as supported by the following observations.

Learning method.

We found that most competitive models are all trained with MLM: MusicFM (MSD) is the most competitive model in this benchmark, as it achieves top-2 performance in all metrics;
AudioMAE (Zhong) achieved top-2 performance in boundary detection;
MERT (330M) showed strong performance in function prediction.
Compared to other SSL methods such as contrastive learning (MULE) and tokenization (EnCodec and DAC), MLM exhibits overwhelming success.

Supervised learning models (PaSST and PANNs) cannot match the MLM models trained on music data, which is consistent with prior research
[
20
,
21
]
that models pretrained with supervised learning cannot generalize well to unseen tasks.
Notably, fine-tuning the two AudioMAE models on the audio tagging task produces supervised models that outperform PaSST and PANNs, further emphasizing the importance of MLM pretraining.

Model context length.

Within MLM methods, MusicFM showed superior performance, which we hypothesize is a result of its longer context length: the 30-second context allowed MusicFM to better understand music structure compared to the 5-second context length in MERT and AudioMAE (Zhong).

Training data.

Although AudioSet contains a large amount of music, multiple short clips from the same music track are rare.
As a result, the model is never trained to distinguish those similar parts from the same track.
In contrast, when training on full-track or long-form music data, all parts from the same track are presented to the model during training, even though the model can only see a clip at one training step.
This encourages the model to distinguish between different parts from the same track, particularly for SSL methods.
We hypothesize that full-track or long-form music data has been beneficial to MusicFM, MERT, and Audio MAE (Zhong).
Audio MAE (Huang), a model weaker than other MLM models, was trained on AudioSet.

4.2
Discussions

Pooling.

MLM models confirmed the effectiveness of pooling.
HR3F improved with all MLM models, and PWF improved with MERT, while the two AudioMAEs achieved improvements in all metrics.
However, pooling caused boundary position shifts, indicating that HR.5F may degrade for some methods.

Feature frame rate.

MULE, an SSL model using contrastive learning, showed low boundary detection performance, likely due to its coarse frame rate of 0.5 Hz and the resulting blurred boundaries, as shown in Fig.
1
.
However, an excessively high frame rate, such as in the case of EnCodec and DAC, might also be harmful to boundary detection.
Considering that the strictest metric (HR.5F) is computed at 2 Hz, a frame rate of at least 2 Hz is preferred for the MSA task.

Semantic vs. acoustic SSL.

The tokenizers (codecs) and MLM models are similar in their training objectives, as both require models to reconstruct the input signal.
However, tokenizers showed very low performance in both boundary detection and function prediction.
We believe that the following differences in focus lead to the observed performance gap.
MLM encourages models to reconstruct original signals from a masked version without pursuing reconstruction details, resulting in semantic features.
In contrast, tokenizers are trained to recover as many details as possible during reconstruction, focusing on acoustic features.
Fig.
1
shows that without pooling, numerous fine vertical stripes characteristic of high frame rates are visible.
Pooling reduces these vertical stripes, but blurs boundaries and further degrades performance in these models.

Architecture.

PANNs (CNN) and PaSST (Transformer), as well as OpenL3 (CNN) and CLAP (Transformer), which share similar learning methods, achieve similar performance, indicating that model architecture is not the bottleneck for these methods.
However, for longer 30-second training data, as with MusicFM, an architecture with a long receptive field, such as a Transformer, is necessary.

Models used as metrics to evaluate music generation quality.

The following FAEs have been used as backbones in metrics such as FAD
[
15
]
to evaluate the quality of music and audio generated by generative models: MERT in
[
12
,
14
]
; EnCodec, DAC, and CLAP in
[
12
]
; PANNs in
[
22
]
; PaSST in
[
43
]
; and OpenL3, which has been used in long-form music evaluation
[
10
]
.
We found that, compared to models such as OpenL3, MLM models such as MusicFM exhibit a better understanding of music structure.
We encourage the community to revisit the validity of these metrics and hypothesize that MLM methods such as MusicFM could serve as alternatives to conventional metric models.
In a recent metric called MAD
[
14
]
, MERT has been chosen as the backbone.

Future work.

An important branch of SSL is auto-regression (AR), which is not included in this research, as we found that AR models, such as Jukebox
[
9
]
and SoniDo
[
21
]
, are resource-intensive due to their more than 5B parameters.
It would be interesting to explore AR models at a scale similar to MLM models (less than 330M parameters) and evaluate their potential in the MSA task.
We noticed that, instead of a linear probe, backends utilizing longer contexts have been shown to improve MSA performance
[
41
]
.
Different layers in Transformer-based FAEs tend to contain different features
[
9
]
.
Further investigation into each layer of FAEs, as well as the design and training of more powerful backends, remains as future work.

5
Conclusion

Although many open-source foundational audio encoders (FAEs) exist, their capabilities in understanding music structure remain underexplored.
In this study, we conducted a comparative evaluation of 11 types of FAEs, considering factors such as learning methods, training data, and model context length.
Our experimental results demonstrate that self-supervised masked language models trained on music data achieve strong performance, and that a longer context length for FAEs yields better results.
This finding aligns with the requirements of the music structure analysis task, which involves capturing long-term musical contexts.
Our results contribute to future research on music structure analysis and FAEs.
The strong FAEs identified in this work can serve as backbones for metrics such as Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) to evaluate long-form music generation models.
