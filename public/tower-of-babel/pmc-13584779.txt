Analysis of Drilling Fluid Loss Behavior in Deepwater Shallow Formations
Authors: Jintao An, Qi Chen, Long Chen, Yiwen Huang, Qixuan Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deepwater shallow formations are typically weakly consolidated, highly porous, and highly permeable, which results in a narrow safe-density window and makes drilling fluid loss highly sensitive to small wellbore-pressure fluctuations. In this study, a finite-discrete element method (FDEM) model implemented in MultiFracS was used to investigate the transition from permeability-induced loss to fracture-induced loss around a deepwater shallow wellbore. The model couples triangular finite elements, zero-thickness cohesive joint elements, Darcy-type matrix seepage, fracture flow governed by the cubic law, and matrix-fracture fluid exchange. A South China Sea field case was used to validate the predicted loss and flowback volumes. The simulations show that permeability-induced loss is controlled mainly by pressure diffusion in the high-porosity matrix and remains relatively limited, whereas once the wellbore pressure reaches the fracture initiation condition, newly connected fractures provide preferential flow paths and sharply increase cumulative loss. Repeated wellbore-pressure fluctuations further narrow the operational pressure margin by promoting the progressive weakening of the near-wellbore formation. Sensitivity analyses indicate that porosity, permeability, drilling fluid viscosity, yield stress, and pressure fluctuation history jointly control the magnitude of loss and flowback. The results clarify the mechanism by which shallow formation breathing can evolve into severe fracture-induced loss and provide a modeling basis for pressure management and drilling fluid property optimization in deepwater shallow formations.

Introduction

Global oil and gas exploration and development are increasingly moving toward deeper strata and deeper waters, emerging as critical frontiers for enhancing future hydrocarbon reserves and production. Deep-sea and deepwater fields are poised to become the primary arenas for international competition in the oil and gas sector. Advances in drilling technology have transformed deepwater shallow gasinitially regarded as a hazardous byproductinto a commercially viable clean energy source.

Drilling operations in deepwater environments are inherently complex and challenging owing to the distinctive depositional characteristics of these settings. , The overlying rock strata exhibit low pressure, minimal compaction, rapid deposition rates, and a relatively recent geological age. Consequently, the deepwater shallow strata, defined as those located within 0.5 times the water depth below the mudline, possess attributes such as weak consolidation strength, elevated porosity, high permeability, and significant water contentproperties that markedly differ from those found in onshore or shallow water strata. , Furthermore, the safe pressure window in this environment is exceptionally narrow, imposing stringent requirements on drilling operations. During drilling in deepwater shallow strata, the phenomenon known as the drilling breathing effect frequently complicates operations. The term “breathing” (Figure ) aptly characterizes the phenomenon it describes: “breathing in” refers to the loss of mud, while “breathing out” denotes the spillage of mud. The mud overflow resulting from the drilling breathing effect closely resembles a conventional well kick, posing a risk of misdiagnosis of formation fluid influx. The standard response to a well kickincreasing mud density and executing well-killing operationscan inadvertently elevate bottom-hole pressure, resulting in more severe leakage. Once the pump is stopped, additional formation fluid may invade the wellbore, exacerbating the drilling breathing effect. Furthermore, excessive wellbore pressure can lead to the formation and expansion of fractures surrounding the wellbore, compromising its integrity and resulting in significant leakage that may trigger catastrophic drilling incidents. This risk is particularly pronounced in deepwater shallow formations, where the geological structures are exceptionally weak. Consequently, the drilling breathing effect heightens the risks and costs associated with deepwater shallow drilling operations, thereby jeopardizing their safety and efficiency.

Schematic diagram of the breathing effect in deepwater shallow formations.

The earliest research on the drilling breathing effect dates back to 1989. Since then, an increasing number of scholars have investigated this intriguing phenomenon. Currently, the mechanisms inducing drilling breathing can be categorized into four distinct types. − The first type involves the radial elastic expansion of deep-well tubulars, including risers, casings, and open-hole sections. These circular structures experience radial elastic expansion due to the internal pressure exerted within the wellbore. When the wellbore pressure decreases, the radial expansion diminishes, resulting in a wellhead mud overflow. Although this mechanism has been confirmed to contribute to the formation of the breathing effect, its overall impact is relatively minor. The second category pertains to the thermal expansion of drilling fluid induced by thermal effects in high-temperature and high-pressure wells. The temperature of the mud circulating within the wellbore is lower than that of the surrounding formation; consequently, when the circulation ceases, the mud expands due to heating. This phenomenon exhibits hysteresis as the heating effect from high-temperature formations is not instantaneous. This mechanism is more significant than the first and plays a greater role in the formation of the breathing effect. The third category involves the opening and closing of fractures surrounding the open-hole section driven by pressure fluctuations within the wellbore. During normal mud circulation, the wellbore pressure exceeds the opening pressure of the fractures, allowing the mud to flow into these fractures and resulting in mud loss. However, when the circulation rate changes or ceases, the fracture width may decrease or even close due to the reduction or elimination of circulation pressure loss, prompting fluid within the fractures to flow back into the wellbore. If this occurs during the lifting phase of the pipe string, the resulting suction effect can significantly enhance the volume of fluid overflow detected at the wellhead within a brief time frame. In addition to the three mechanisms described above, the permeable breathing effect was proposed in recent years. This mechanism is particularly relevant to deepwater shallow drilling operations. The breathing effect associated with this type of drilling resembles fracture breathing; however, in this case, the formation pores serve as the sole flow channels and storage spaces for the fluids.

In deepwater drilling operations, the permeable breathing effect is an unavoidable phenomenon. Whenever a positive pressure differential exists between the formation and wellbore, mud loss occurs. Conversely, a negative pressure differential results in the discharge of the fluid from the formation back into the wellbore. Although the volumes of fluid lost and returned are limited, this phenomenon is indisputable. Additionally, owing to the unique characteristics of the formation, pressure fluctuations or excessive circulation pressure loss can easily induce fractures in the surrounding rock. At this juncture, the permeability breathing effect may transition into a fracture breathing effect, which invariably accompanies the permeability breathing effect. Recent domestic research has advanced deepwater drilling techniques in the South China Sea, including managed pressure drilling technology and loss control strategies. − However, the mechanism underlying the transformation from permeability loss to fracture loss remains to be thoroughly investigated.

Despite extensive research, we found no relevant reports addressing the transformation from the permeable breathing effect to the fracture breathing effect. This gap motivated our curiosity regarding the transformation process and response characteristics of this breathing effect, prompting us to undertake this study. To investigate this phenomenon, we employed MultiFracS to construct a coupled finite-discrete element model tailored for deepwater shallow breathing. Our study explores the time-dependent transition between the two breathing modes, analyzes changes in the near-wellbore seepage and stress fields, and examines the impact of tensile strength, mud properties, and other critical factors.

Existing studies of the drilling breathing effect can be systematically categorized into three groups. The first group focuses on permeability-induced breathing in porous formations, where fluid loss and flowback are governed by matrix pressure diffusion without mechanical failure of the rock fabric (Gill, Lavrov and Tronvoll, Luo et al.). The second group investigates fracture-related fluid loss in formations where preexisting or drilling-induced fractures serve as the primary flow channels, with fracture aperture evolution and the cubic law governing the loss behavior (Yang et al., Huang et al. , ). The third group, into which the present study falls, addresses the transition or coupling between these two regimes, specifically, how a predominantly permeability-controlled system can cross a pressure-dependent threshold and become fracture-controlled. Liu et al. investigated fracture propagation and fluid loss in fractured carbonate formations using a coupled numerical approach, providing additional context on fracture-controlled loss mechanisms in different lithological settings. The present work extends this body of knowledge by employing a coupled FDEM pore-fracture framework that explicitly resolves both matrix seepage and fracture initiation and propagation within a unified computational domain, enabling the mechanistic identification of the pressure-dependent transition between the two loss regimes in weakly consolidated deepwater shallow formations. Semianalytical studies of drilling fluid leakage into fractured formations further indicate that postfracture loss is strongly controlled by fracture conductivity, hydraulic aperture, fluid rheology, and subsurface uncertainty.

The main contribution of this work is therefore not only the reproduction of a breathing response curve but also the mechanistic identification of a pressure-dependent transition between two loss regimes. Compared with previous studies that mainly considered permeability-induced breathing or fracture breathing separately, this study uses a coupled FDEM pore-fracture seepage framework to describe how near-wellbore-pressure diffusion, cohesive-joint failure, fracture flow, and matrix-fracture exchange interact during pressure loading and unloading. The specific objectives are to (1) establish a reproducible FDEM model for weakly consolidated deepwater shallow formations; (2) distinguish permeability-induced loss, fracture-induced loss, flowback, and postfracture reloss processes; (3) validate the model against a South China Sea field case; and (4) evaluate how formation properties, drilling fluid rheology, and pressure fluctuations affect the operational pressure margin.

Mathematical Model for Deepwater Shallow Losses: FDEM Fundamentals

Lost circulation behavior fundamentally arises from the seepage of drilling fluid driven by the pressure differential between the wellbore and the surrounding formation. When the pressure within the wellbore exceeds that of the formation, the drilling fluid penetrates the formation through pore seepage, leading to permeability loss. As the wellbore pressure escalates and fractures develop within the formation, this permeability loss is transformed into combined permeability and fracture loss. Consequently, integrating the fluid–solid coupling model that accounts for dual-medium seepage in pores and fractures with the rock fracture and propagation model is essential for developing a numerical model that captures the breathing effects in both the deepwater and shallow layers. The following sections provide a comprehensive overview of the theory and methodologies employed in MultiFracS to establish a breathing effect model.

FDEM Meshing Concept

The fundamental principle of the FDEM is illustrated in Figure . To simplify the model, the shallow rock and soil are treated as a two-dimensional continuous medium and discretized into a triangular finite-element mesh during computation. Zero-thickness joint elements exhibiting bonding effects are incorporated at the common boundaries of adjacent elements. This approach employs joint-element fracturing to model the initiation and propagation of cracks within the continuous medium, while constant strain triangular elements are used to represent the deformation of the continuum.

Governing Equations

For reproducibility, the term FDEM in this study refers to a finite-discrete element formulation in which the formation matrix is discretized into deformable triangular finite elements, and zero-thickness four-node cohesive joint elements are inserted along the common boundaries of adjacent triangular elements. The triangular elements describe continuum stress–strain deformation, whereas the joint elements allow contact, sliding, rotation, and progressive tensile, shear, or mixed-mode failure. Before failure, the joint elements transfer cohesive forces, and the domain behaves as a continuous medium. After failure, a broken joint becomes a discontinuity that can open, slip, and participate in fracture flow. This formulation therefore differs from a purely DEM model because the deformation of each triangular block is still solved by finite-element mechanics, while discontinuity initiation, propagation, and postfailure hydraulic connectivity are handled through the joint elements.

According to the energy-based mixed-mode fracture criterion, the mixed-mode failure condition can also be expressed as eq . 7 GIGfI+GIIGfII=1 where G I and G II are the mode I and mode II energy release rates, respectively, and GfI and GfII are the corresponding critical fracture energies. The elliptical displacement criterion in eq is consistent with this energy-based mixed-mode failure condition and is compatible with the standard FDEM framework. During fracture propagation, the work input from wellbore pressure and far-field stresses is partitioned into four components: (a) elastic strain energy stored in the deforming triangular elements, (b) energy dissipated through the viscous damping matrix C (eq ), (c) fracture energy consumed in the cohesive zone process region at the fracture tip (quantified by GfI and GfII in eq ), and (d) energy carried by the drilling fluid flowing within the fracture network. When the rate of strain energy release at a propagating fracture front equals the fracture energy dissipation rate, the fracture advances under quasi-static equilibrium; any excess energy is consumed through additional fracture surface creation or viscous dissipation.

Finally, the tensile and shear stresses of the joint element are applied to the two triangular elements connected by this joint and are used to determine the solution for the next time step. The fracturing of rock joints surrounding the well signifies the formation of fractures and indicates a transition in the leakage behavior of the shallow rock and soil from permeability leakage to fracture leakage.

Pore-Fracture Dual-Medium Seepage Model

In the coupled seepage model, permeability-induced loss is identified when wellbore fluid enters the formation through the connected pore network without cohesive joint failure. In this stage, matrix flow is governed by Darcy-type seepage through triangular elements. Fracture-induced loss is identified when one or more near-wellbore joint elements satisfy the tensile, shear, or mixed-mode failure criterion and become hydraulically connected fractures. Once a failed joint is converted into a hydraulic fracture, the fracture flow component is governed by the cubic law and exchanges fluid with the surrounding matrix. The transition from permeability-induced to fracture-induced loss is therefore determined by joint-element failure and hydraulic connection, not by an arbitrary change in terminology. Quantitatively, the permeability-induced cumulative loss reaches approximately 0.03 m3 over 400 s at 17.2 MPa (phase 1, Figure ), whereas the fracture-induced cumulative loss after a pressure increase of only 0.4 MPa exceeds 1.2 m3 within the same time windowa ratio of approximately 40:1 that underscores the dramatic change in loss behavior associated with the regime transition.

This definition also clarifies the physical meaning of the critical pressure used in the simulations. At lower pressures, the pressure perturbation is mainly dissipated through matrix diffusion, and the loss rate tends to decline as the pressure gradient relaxes. At the transition pressure, the near-wellbore effective stress state reaches the cohesive-joint failure envelope. The failed joints create low-resistance pressure transmission paths, so the dominant flow pathway changes from slow matrix seepage to preferential fracture flow. The energy threshold for this transition is governed by fracture toughness, quantified as the critical fracture energies GfI and GfII in eq . Higher GfI and GfII values raise the energy barrier in the mixed-mode failure criterion, requiring a greater wellbore pressure to initiate cohesive-joint failure and thus maintaining the permeability-dominated regime over a wider pressure range. Conversely, in weakly consolidated deepwater shallow formations, where the rock frame is poorly cemented and the critical fracture energies are inherently low, the transition can be triggered at relatively modest pressure elevations. This low toughness characteristic is a fundamental reason why these formations are particularly susceptible to the disproportionate increase in loss volume once the transition is crossed.

This model primarily comprises a pore seepage model, a fracture seepage model, and a fluid exchange model that accounts for the interaction between the rock matrix and fractures. Physical Model Setup and Validation

Like any finite-element calculation model, the FDEM method requires meshing of the computational domain. This section is grounded in the grid-partitioning theory of the FDEM algorithm and draws upon the engineering context of leakage behavior in deepwater shallow drilling to develop the relevant physical models.

Meshing

As illustrated in Figure , the model configuration consists of a square computational domain containing a centrally positioned, circular hollow section. This circular void simulates the wellbore, while the square domain represents the deepwater shallow formation with dimensions of 4 m × 4 m. The mesh was created by using the Delaunay triangulation method, which effectively captures the stochastic characteristics of geotechnical discontinuities and enhances the authenticity of the simulation. The computational mesh consisted of 69,737 elements.

Schematic diagram of the computational mesh.

The 4 m × 4 m computational domain was selected to keep the outer boundary sufficiently far from the 0.66 m wellbore while maintaining a mesh density suitable for near-wellbore fracture tracking. The outer boundary was fixed mechanically, and the wellbore boundary was assigned a pressure condition. Because the analysis focuses on the near-wellbore transition process rather than far-field reservoir drainage, the domain size was chosen to minimize direct boundary interference in the pressure and stress response during the simulated time window. The Delaunay triangulation produces an unstructured grid that reduces mesh-induced directional bias compared with structured grids because element edges are randomly oriented relative to the principal stress directions. Nevertheless, FDEM fracture propagation is inherently constrained by the mesh topology, since fractures propagate along joint-element boundaries. The present mesh density (69,737 elements in 4 m × 4 m, minimum element size ∼0.01 m) provides sufficient resolution so that this constraint is minimized relative to the fracture propagation scale (∼0.5 m maximum length). A systematic mesh-orientation and mesh-refinement study for quantitative uncertainty assessment is identified as future work.

Validation

The breathing effect event of a deepwater well in the South China Sea serves as a pertinent case study. This well is situated at a water depth of 518 m, with a total design depth of 4,300 m. Field records indicate that a low-temperature-resistant water-based drilling fluid was used in the well section between 880 and 980 m. During this interval, minor leakage occurred, resulting in an approximate total loss of 3.96 m3 of drilling fluid. Upon reaching a depth of 980 m, the drilling was halted for equipment retrieval. The validation focuses on field-observable loss and flowback volumes because these are the most reliable quantities recorded during drilling operations. The comparison does not imply that every local fracture geometry or pore-pressure contour is uniquely determined by the field record. Instead, the agreement in loss volume, flowback volume, and net loss demonstrates that the selected hydraulic and mechanical parameters can reproduce the integrated breathing response of the field case within an engineering error range. The present 4 m × 4 m computational domain is designed to resolve near-wellbore (within ∼2 m from the wellbore wall) fracture initiation and early propagation, not field-scale fracture geometry (lateral extent of hundreds of meters), which would require characterization of far-field heterogeneities beyond the scope of this mechanism-oriented study.

The validation workflow follows an experiment/field-data-to-model logic: field records define the observable loss and flowback targets, formation and drilling fluid parameters define the mechanical and hydraulic inputs, the FDEM model iteratively updates stress, pore pressure, joint failure, fracture flow, and matrix-fracture exchange, and the final integrated loss/flowback volumes are compared with field observations. This workflow is intended to test whether the coupled mechanism can reproduce the measured breathing response under selected representative conditions.

The calibration of fracture parameters (tensile strength = 0.6 MPa, cohesion = 4 MPa, critical fracture energies GfI and GfII ) followed a systematic two-step procedure. First, baseline matrix parameters (porosity, permeability, elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio) were set to published representative values for weakly consolidated deepwater shallow formations. Second, the fracture strength parameters were adjusted within physically plausible ranges for unconsolidated to weakly consolidated sediments (tensile strength 0.1–1.0 MPa, cohesion 1–8 MPa) such that the simulated cumulative loss volume and flowback volume simultaneously matched the South China Sea field observations within the reported error bounds. The calibration target was specifically the integrated volumetric response rather than individual fracture geometry because the field records reliably document volume changes but do not resolve individual fracture dimensions. The resulting parameter set represents a self-consistent combination that reproduces the field breathing response, though the individual parameters are not uniquely constrained by the available data and could be refined through dedicated laboratory measurements on formation samples.

This validation strategy is conservative: only quantities directly comparable with field records were used as calibration targets. Parameters that cannot be independently verified from the published field casesuch as local fracture aperture and the matrix-fracture exchange coefficientwere not overinterpreted. Consequently, the model is best used to evaluate relative mechanism changes and parameter sensitivities under comparable conditions rather than to claim site-independent absolute prediction accuracy.

Importantly, the fracture initiation pressure of 17.6 MPa is not prescribed as a model input but emerges from the coupled mechanical-hydraulic solution: the near-wellbore joint elements reach the mixed-mode failure criterion (eqs and ) under this pressure given the specified formation strength parameters. In other words, the FDEM framework predicts fracture initiation as an emergent behavior rather than requiring the user to prespecify a fracture pressure.

Temporal evolution of near-wellbore stress during phase ③.

The observed fracture orientation can be compared with the classical Kirsch solution for stress concentration around a cylindrical cavity. For a vertical wellbore under the present horizontal stress anisotropy (σ H = 18.6 MPa, σh = 18.3 MPa, anisotropy ratio ≈1.016), the Kirsch solution predicts that the maximum tangential tensile stress occurs at the wellbore wall at azimuths aligned with the minimum horizontal stress (σh ) direction. The FDEM simulation results are broadly consistent with this prediction: the initial fractures develop at the wellbore wall at positions close to the σh direction (Figures and ). However, quantitative deviations from the idealized Kirsch pattern arise from three sources: (1) the discrete joint-element network introduces preferential propagation paths along element boundaries that do not perfectly align with the smooth stress trajectory predicted by the continuum Kirsch solution; (2) the evolving pore pressure field modifies the local effective stress state relative to the undrained elastic assumption of the Kirsch solution; and (3) the heterogeneous representation of the weakly consolidated formation produces local stress perturbations not captured by the homogeneous continuum assumption. For formations with stronger stress anisotropy (σHσh>1.2) , the directional preference would be substantially more pronounced, and the fracture pattern would transition from the distributed network observed here to a focused bi-wing geometry aligned with the maximum horizontal stress direction.

The change from permeability-induced loss to fracture-induced loss is controlled by the competition between pressure diffusion and mechanical failure. Before fracture initiation, the high-permeability matrix dissipates part of the imposed wellbore pressure through pore seepage, and the leakage rate gradually declines as the pressure gradient decreases. After cohesive-joint failure, the created fractures provide lower-resistance flow channels. Because fracture conductivity is aperture-dependent and follows the cubic law, even a limited fracture opening can substantially accelerate pressure transmission compared with matrix diffusion. This explains why the cumulative loss increases sharply after fracture initiation and why the postfracture backflow/reloss response differs from the permeability-loss stage. In summary, leak-off is matrix-storage-dominated before fracture initiation (permeability-induced loss, ∼0.03 m3 over 400 s) and becomes fracture-conductivity-dominated after cohesive-joint failure (fracture-induced loss, >1.2 m3 over 200 s), with matrix uptake continuing alongside preferential fracture flow as a coupled dual-medium process.

During the loss phase, the fracture area increases when the pressure transmission from the wellbore into the fractures exceeds the pressure dissipation from the fractures into the matrix pores. Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity analysis is intended to identify the direction and relative importance of controlling factors within the parameter range considered in this study. Because no additional independent laboratory data set is available for a full probabilistic calibration, the results should be interpreted as deterministic sensitivity results. A formal uncertainty analysis based on repeated laboratory measurements, site-specific stress calibration, and multiple-mesh realizations remains an important extension for future work. The deterministic sensitivity analysis provides specific quantitative ranges for the prediction uncertainty: for example, the fracture-induced cumulative loss after 20 s varies from approximately 0.3 m3 to 1.6 m3 across the tested parameter ranges (porosity 20%–40%, permeability 1–4 D, plastic viscosity 10–70 mPa.s, yield stress 0–10 Pa). These ranges represent the current best estimate of the prediction bounds for the conditions studied and are intended to guide engineering judgment until formal probabilistic uncertainty quantification becomes available.

Effect of Porosity

Porosity directly reflects a rock’s capacity to store fluids. Figures and illustrate the variations in fluid loss and flowback volumes under different porosity conditions and loss mechanisms. Both the loss volume and flowback volume decrease as porosity decreases, and the rate of this decrease also exhibits a diminishing trend. This suggests that while reduced porosity lessens the magnitude of the formation breathing effect, the degree of attenuation gradually diminishes. Consequently, lower-porosity formations exhibit relatively lower permeability-induced fluid loss, posing a reduced risk to drilling safety. In contrast, when encountering high-porosity formations, increased vigilance and proactive measuressuch as optimizing the particle-size distribution of drilling fluid solidsare imperative. Moreover, if formation fracturing occurs, the resulting fracture-induced fluid loss volume significantly exceeds that of permeability-induced loss. Therefore, even in low-porosity formations, drilling fluid losses under these conditions must not be overlooked, as they pose a substantial threat to operational safety.

Effect of Drilling Fluid Properties

Viscosity is a critical parameter for assessing the flow resistance of fluids in porous media. An increase in drilling fluid viscosity is associated with higher flow resistance. Figures and illustrate the variation patterns of fluid loss and flowback volumes under different viscosity conditions and loss mechanisms. As the drilling fluid viscosity increases, both fluid loss and flowback volumes gradually decline, suggesting that higher viscosity effectively reduces the severity of the breathing effect. Therefore, when other conditions remain constant, a moderate increase in the drilling fluid viscosity can help mitigate the breathing effect. However, in practical drilling operations, elevated viscosity often results in increased circulating pressure loss, leading to higher annular pressure, which may, in turn, exacerbate the breathing effect. Consequently, the design of drilling fluid viscosity must be coordinated with other parameters, such as density, to achieve optimal overall performance. Additionally, future research should integrate wellbore pressure into mathematical models, thereby establishing a coupled wellbore-formation fluid loss model to guide the optimization of the drilling parameters.

Discussion

Engineering Implications and Model Limitations

The simulations suggest that pressure management should not be reduced to simply increasing the wellbore pressure or drilling fluid density. Higher wellbore pressure can provide mechanical support, but in weak or fractured shallow formations, it can also enhance fluid invasion, promote cohesive-joint failure, and accelerate the transition to fracture-induced loss. A more robust strategy is to combine controlled pressure fluctuation, limited surge/swab pressure, and improved sealing or filter-cake quality so that pressure support is achieved without creating highly conductive fracture pathways.

The present model deliberately neglects chemical interaction, hydration-induced weakening, and explicit filter-cake evolution in order to isolate the mechanical-hydraulic transition mechanism in deepwater shallow formations. These processes should be incorporated when the target interval contains clay-rich materials, when exposure time is long enough for drilling fluid filtrate to alter near-wellbore strength, or when filter-cake sealing controls the effective pressure boundary. The model framework is therefore most reliable for mechanism interpretation and relative sensitivity comparison after site-specific calibration. Permeability anisotropy is also not considered in the present simulations. In formations where horizontal permeability exceeds vertical permeabilitya common condition in sedimentary basinsanisotropic pressure diffusion would alter the spatial distribution of near-wellbore pore pressure, potentially shifting the azimuth of initial fracture initiation and modifying the prefracture loss pattern. The MultiFracS framework can accommodate directional permeability inputs, and site-specific anisotropy characterization is recommended before field application of the model. The current FDEM framework includes effective-stress coupling through the principle sigma’ = sigma – alpha p, where pore pressure changes modify the effective stress acting on the triangular elements and vice versa. However, full Biot poroelastic couplingin which the solid skeleton volumetric strain directly enters the fluid mass conservation equationis not included. In our high-permeability formation (2.5 D), the characteristic pressure diffusion time is short (tens of seconds, Figure ), so the omission of full Biot coupling is not expected to qualitatively alter the pressure evolution or the transition mechanism. For lower-permeability formations, full poroelastic implementation is recommended in future work. Thermal effects are also omitted: the short time scale of individual drilling operations (hundreds of seconds) limits thermal penetration, and the low-temperature deepwater shallow environment (2–8 °C near the seabed) reduces thermally induced stress variations. For high-temperature reservoirs or clay-rich formations with longer exposure times, thermal stress, viscosity reduction, and thermally enhanced pore pressure could alter the transition pressure threshold.

Conclusions

A finite-discrete element numerical model was developed by using the MultiFracS software to examine the characteristics of drilling fluid loss in deepwater shallow formations, which are characterized by weak cementation, high porosity, and high permeability. This study analyzed the evolution of seepage fields, stress distributions, and fracture propagation patterns surrounding the wellbore. The primary conclusions are as follows:

In the simulated weakly consolidated deepwater shallow formation, the safe pressure margin was narrow. Under relatively low wellbore overpressures, permeability-induced loss dominates and is mainly controlled by matrix pressure diffusion. Once the wellbore pressure reaches the joint-element failure condition, the loss mechanism changes to fracture-induced loss and the connected fractures cause a marked increase in cumulative loss. The model therefore explains how a small pressure increase can trigger a disproportionate increase in the drilling fluid loss.

Repeated wellbore-pressure fluctuations can promote progressive weakening of the near-wellbore formation and reduce the apparent pressure margin before fracture-induced loss occurs. This mechanism emphasizes the need to limit unnecessary surge and swab pressure variations during drilling, tripping, and connection operations in weak, shallow formations.

Within the investigated parameter range, lower formation porosity and permeability, better filter cake quality, higher drilling fluid viscosity, and higher yield stress reduce the magnitude of loss and flowback. However, increasing drilling fluid rheological parameters may also increase circulating pressure loss and bottom-hole pressure. Therefore, drilling fluid optimization should be coupled with wellbore hydraulics and managed-pressure-drilling constraints rather than based on fluid loss reduction alone.

Future work should combine the present FDEM pore-fracture framework with laboratory cyclic-damage calibration, site-specific stress inversion, mesh orientation and mesh density uncertainty assessment, and a coupled wellbore-formation hydraulics model. For clay-rich or chemically active formations, hydration, chemical weakening, and filter-cake evolution should also be added before field deployment. In particular, systematic mesh orientation and mesh refinement studies are needed to quantify the numerical uncertainty of the predicted fracture geometry, and a formal probabilistic uncertainty quantification (UQ) incorporating parameter correlations and model-form uncertainties is recommended before the model is used for quantitative well design decisions. Full Biot poroelastic coupling and anisotropic permeability should also be incorporated, where site characterization indicates their importance.
