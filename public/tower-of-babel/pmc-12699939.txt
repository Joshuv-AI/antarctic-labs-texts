FujiLAM assays for tuberculosis detection in people with HIV: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Behailu Taye, Haile Tesfaye, Gemechu Churiso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a global need for a rapid, accurate, and non-sputum point-of-care (PoC) assay for tuberculosis (TB), especially for people with HIV (PLHIV). The Fujifilm SILVAMP TB LAM (FujiLAM), a urine lipoarabinomannan assay, has shown promise for diagnosing TB in PLHIV. But diagnostic performances were inconsistent across studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the pooled sensitivity and specificity of FujiLAM for TB diagnosis in PLHIV.MethodsA search was performed on August 10, 2025, across Scopus, PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar using keywords including Fujifilm SILVAMP TB LAM, SILVAMP-LAM, FujiLAM, lipoarabinomannan test, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, tuberculosis, and TB. We included English-language studies that evaluated the FujiLAM test in PLHIV and contained data for a 2 × 2 table. Study quality was assessed with the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. A bivariate random effects model was applied to perform the meta-analysis using R and Stata 14.2. Subgroup analysis was conducted, and publication bias was investigated using Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s test.ResultsThirteen articles, encompassing 5,819 participants, were included. Individual study sensitivity varied from 34% to 78%, and specificity from 74% to 95%. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 63% (95% CI: 55–71%) and 90% (95% CI: 86–92%), respectively, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91%), indicating good diagnostic accuracy. Subgroup analysis revealed higher pooled sensitivity in inpatients (71%, 95% CI: 51–84%) compared to outpatients (57%, 95% CI: 48–65%). Similarly, pooled sensitivity was elevated in patients with CD4 counts ≤ 100 cells/µL (76%, 95% CI: 61–87%) and ≤ 200 cells/µL (74%, 95% CI: 60–85%) compared to CD4 counts > 200 cells/µL (42%, 95% CI: 36–49%). Pooled sensitivity was comparable between participants enrolled based on TB symptoms (63%, 95% CI: 52–73%) and regardless of symptoms (63%, 95% CI: 50–75%). FujiLAM I sensitivity was 65% (95% CI: 58–72%), compared to 56% (95% CI: 33–76%) for FujiLAM II.ConclusionsFujiLAM demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy, with higher sensitivity for subgroups of patients with lower CD4 counts and inpatients. Although FujiLAM II appears less sensitive than FujiLAM I, it has high specificity and consistent accuracy across different production lots. Further research is warranted to enhance and validate FujiLAM’s performance, especially in HIV-associated EPTB and children living with HIV.PROSPERO registrationCRD42023461097.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12055-2.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV, Diagnosis, FujiLAM, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious airborne disease caused by Mycobacterium (M.) tuberculosis bacteria. It primarily affects the lungs, leading to pulmonary TB (PTB), but it can also spread to other organs such as the brain, lymph nodes, pleura, or pericardium, causing extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) [1]. Of the estimated 2 billion people infected with M. tuberculosis worldwide, only 5–10% will develop active TB disease in their lifetime [2]. Nonetheless, the risk of acquiring active TB is eighteen times higher in people living with HIV (PLHIV) than without HIV [2]. HIV infection impairs early innate defenses, including macrophage phagocytosis and apoptosis, and adaptive immune responses against M. tuberculosis [3]. This, combined with the progressive loss of CD4 + T cells, enables increased M. tuberculosis replication and the transition from latent to active TB [4].

Despite advancements in TB diagnosis and treatment, early detection and management of all TB cases, especially in PLHIV, remain challenging [5, 6]. A meta-analysis of postmortem data revealed that nearly half of TB cases in PLHIV remain undiagnosed [7]. Moreover, of the total of 662,000 estimated HIV-associated TB cases worldwide in 2023, only 66% were diagnosed [1]. This indicates that TB diagnosis remains a significant challenge in PLHIV, and it is rarely bacteriologically confirmed.

In most resource-constrained countries, sputum-smear microscopy remains the primary method of TB diagnosis. However, its sensitivity is limited, requiring a high bacterial load of at least 5,000 bacilli per milliliter for a positive [8]. Furthermore, microscopy sensitivity for PLHIV is much lower, ranging from 22% to 43%, primarily due to low bacterial loads, difficulty in producing sputum, and the higher prevalence of EPTB [9–11]. Culture, using either Lowenstein–Jensen (L-J) or Mycobacterium Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT), is the gold standard for TB diagnosis [12]. But culture is not available for routine TB diagnosis due to its high cost, reliance on specialized laboratories, and lengthy turnaround time, which can hinder timely treatment initiation [13–15].

Molecular tests, like Xpert MTB/RIF or Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (Cepheid) and Truenat™ (Molbio), are becoming increasingly popular due to their automation, comprehensive features, and suitability for point-of-care settings [8]. These tests detect M. tuberculosis DNA and can identify mutations associated with rifampicin resistance [8]. Despite improving TB case detection in PLHIV, rapid molecular assays are not widely adopted, especially in resource-limited settings [16, 17]. This is primarily due to the high cost of the instrument and the necessity for infrastructure, constant electricity, and maintenance [18–22]. Furthermore, low bacterial loads, difficulty in producing sputum, or the higher prevalence of EPTB in PLHIV hinder the effectiveness of sputum-based tests [6, 23, 24]. This underscores the urgent need for rapid, non-sputum-based diagnostic tools, especially for PLHIV, where undetected or untreated TB is often fatal [25].

The Alere Determine TB LAM Ag test (AlereLAM; Abbott, USA) is a urine-based lateral flow assay that detects the lipoarabinomannan (LAM) antigen for the diagnosis of active TB [26]. A Cochrane meta-analysis indicated that the pooled sensitivity of the AlereLAM test in adult TB-symptomatic PLHIV was 42%, rising to 54% in patients with CD4 + counts less than 100 cells/µL [27]. Despite evidence of mortality reduction in PLHIV [23, 28–31], the limited sensitivity of AlereLAM has restricted its programmatic adoption in high-burden countries [32].

In contrast, the Fujifilm SILVAMP TB LAM test (FujiLAM; Fujifilm, Japan) is a next-generation urine-based lateral flow assay. It targets M. tuberculosis-specific LAM epitopes using high-affinity monoclonal antibodies and silver amplification, enabling detection of LAM concentrations 30 times lower than AlereLAM [33]. Moreover, recent evidence shows FujiLAM is acceptable and user-friendly for PoC implementation at primary healthcare facilities [34].

Several TB diagnostic accuracy studies have evaluated the FujiLAM assay throughout the world. But diagnostic performances were inconsistent. A 2020 meta-analysis evaluated FujiLAM accuracy for TB in PLHIV, but it was limited to a few studies from three countries and relied on preserved urine samples [35]. Since 2020, additional research on FujiLAM diagnostic accuracy has emerged, and the WHO recently called for reviewing evidence and developing guidelines for its use [8]. Moreover, pooled diagnostic accuracy data for the second-generation FujiLAM II assay remains unavailable. The purpose of this updated systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the pooled sensitivity and specificity of the FujiLAM assay for TB diagnosis in PLHIV. The FujiLAM assay has the potential to improve TB diagnosis in PLHIV if it performs well.

Methods

Literature review protocol preparation

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42023461097). In addition, this study was reported following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) [36] (Additional file 1).

Information sources and search strategy

Data were gathered from PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, and Google Scholar by searches using the key terms (Fujifilm SILVAMP TB LAM OR SILVAMP-LAM OR FujiLAM OR lipoarabinomannan test) AND mycobacterium tuberculosis OR tuberculosis OR TB)). Articles were limited to the English language, and no publication date restrictions were applied. The reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were screened to identify further pertinent studies. The last search was conducted on 10 August 2025. The description of the exact search is provided in Additional File 2.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were English language, an observational, cross-sectional, cohort, or randomized control trial design, HIV patients involved, and FujiLAM numbers of true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN) directly or indirectly available. Studies that lacked sufficient data to create a 2 × 2 contingency table, included only HIV-negative participants, or used sample types other than urine for the index test were excluded. Also, case reports, review articles, meta-analyses, case-control studies, conference abstracts, preprints only, and duplicates were excluded.

Study selection

Retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X8, and duplicates were removed. Then articles were screened by their titles, abstracts, and full text according to the eligibility criteria by two reviewers (BT and GC) independently. Since there were no disagreements, no conflicts needed to be resolved by a third reviewer or by consensus.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by reviewers (BT and GC). Variables extracted were the first author’s name, year of publication, study design, country, patient age group, clinical setting (outpatient or inpatient), clinical status (symptomatic or asymptomatic), urine sample type, CD4+ count, TB type, sample size, reference test, TP, FP, TN, and FN of FujiLAM. For studies that include both participants with HIV and without HIV infection, we extracted data only for participants with HIV. All data were extracted according to the microbiological reference standard (MRS). If a study reported diagnostic performance but lacked publicly available diagnostic or clinical data, further information was requested by mail from the corresponding and first author.

Definitions and classification of study population

FujiLAM diagnostic performance, measured by sensitivity and specificity, was evaluated against MRS (culture or nucleic acid amplification test from sputum, blood, or EPTB samples). Diagnostic 2 × 2 tables were tabulated for MRS, stratified by clinical setting (inpatient or outpatient) and CD4 + count. Thus, for the same study, more than one set of data was extracted.

Assessment of study quality

Two reviewers (BT and HT) independently assessed the methodological quality of included studies using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [37]. This tool evaluates diagnostic accuracy studies across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain was assessed for risk of bias, and the first three domains were also assessed for applicability concerns. Results were visualized using Review Manager 5.4 software.

Statistical analysis

A bivariate random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis using the Metadta package in Stata version 14.2 and the Mada package in R software. The random-effects model was selected because the log-likelihood ratio (LR) test (p < 0.0001) indicated it was a better fit than the fixed-effects model. Results were presented in tables, forest plots and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves. The degree of heterogeneity was quantified using I-Square (I2) statistics by Zhou & Dendukuri [38]. I2 values above 25%, 50%, and 75% were assumed to be low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A sub-group analysis by setting, TB type, age, and CD4+ strata was performed. Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s statistics were done to detect publication bias. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 in Egger’s test was considered evidence of statistically significant publication bias [39].

Results

Literature search

A total of 234 studies were initially retrieved from the databases. After removing duplicates, 147 articles were screened by title and abstract. Studies were excluded at the title and abstract screening stage as diagnostic tests deviate from FujiLAM, target HIV-negative populations, do not assess FujiLAM diagnostic accuracy, and are reviews or conference abstracts. Eighteen articles were screened by full-text reading, and five studies were excluded due to inadequate data for 2 × 2 Table [40], a different sample type than urine [41], HIV-negative participants [42], or duplicated data [43, 44]. Lastly, thirteen articles were included for the qualitative and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of original studies

Of the thirteen included studies, five evaluated FujiLAM in outpatients, four in inpatients, and three in both inpatient and outpatient settings, while one study did not specify the clinical setting (Table 1). Two of the studies tested FujiLAM accuracy in EPTB patients, whereas ten studies examined its efficacy in PTB patients. Two studies included pediatric patients, and eleven included adults. The sample sizes of the included studies varied from 40 to 1628 HIV-positive participants. This review comprises 5,819 HIV-positive participants. Nine of the ten studies used stored urine samples, while four used fresh urine. Ten of the thirteen studies evaluated FujiLAM performance in African patients, one in Southeast Asian patients, and two in patients from both Africa and Southeast Asia. Eight studies used a cohort design, and four were cross-sectional. Participant sex distribution in three studies and median CD4 count in four studies were not reported and were marked as ‘unknown’ (UK). Ten of the thirteen studies evaluated FujiLAM I performance, whereas three studies assessed FujiLAM II.

FujiLAM performance for TB diagnosis in people with HIV

The sensitivities of FujiLAM in the individual studies ranged from 34% to 78%, and the specificities varied from 74% to 95% (Fig. 3). The pooled sensitivity and specificity of FujiLAM for TB diagnosis among people with HIV were 63% (95% CI: 55–71%) and 90% (95% CI: 86–92%), respectively. Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on the two clinical settings used in the studies (Fig. 5). FujiLAM for inpatients with HIV demonstrated higher pooled sensitivity (71%, 95% CI: 51–84%) compared to outpatients (57%, 95% CI: 48–65%). Comparable specificity (89%, 95% CI: 85–92%) and (88%, 95% CI: 82–92%) were observed for outpatient and inpatient groups, respectively. The I² values were 68.1% for sensitivity and 43.0% for specificity in the inpatient group, and 35.1% and 66.4%, respectively, in the outpatient group.

Forest plot for pooled sensitivity and specificity of FujiLAM for TB diagnosis in inpatients (a) and outpatients (b) with HIV. The red diamond in each plot represents the pooled estimate. The analysis included 2,270 individuals from seven studies (eight datasets) in the inpatient group and 3,395 individuals from eight studies (nine datasets) in the outpatient group

In addition, stratified analysis was conducted based on the CD4 cell count, and TB symptoms (Table 2). FujiLAM sensitivity was higher (76%, 95% CI: 61–87%) in patients with CD4 counts ≤ 100 cells/µL compared to CD4 counts 101 to ≤ 200 cells/µL (65%, 95% CI: 57–73%). Specificity was higher in the 101 to ≤ 200 cells/µL group (95%, 95% CI: 80–99%) compared to the ≤ 100 cells/µL group (87%, 95% CI: 77–93%). Similarly, sensitivity was higher (74%, 95% CI: 60–85%) in patients with CD4 counts ≤ 200 cells/µL compared to CD4 counts > 200 cells/µL (42%, 95% CI: 36–49%). Specificity was also higher in the > 200 cells/µL group (96%, 95% CI: 90–98%) compared to the ≤ 200 cells/µL group (88%, 95% CI: 83–92%). The I² values for sensitivity across the CD4 count ranges of ≤ 100, 101–200, ≤ 200, and > 200 cells/µL were 69.3%, 5.79%, 70.6%, and 11.5%, respectively. The corresponding I² values for specificity were 83.7%, 61.3%, 41.3%, and 47.1%.

In studies stratified by TB symptoms, comparable sensitivity (63%, 95% CI: 50–75%) and (63%, 95% CI: 52–73%) were observed for unselected patients (regardless of TB symptoms) and symptomatic patients, respectively. Sensitivity was higher in stored urine samples (64%, 95% CI: 52–74%) compared to fresh samples (57%, 95% CI: 51–64%), but confidence intervals overlapped. Similarly, adults showed greater sensitivity (64%, 95% CI: 55–73%) than children (57%, 95% CI: 42–72%); however, the confidence intervals overlapped. Finally, FujiLAM I (65%, 95% CI: 58–72%) was more sensitive than FujiLAM II (56%, 95% CI: 33–76%), with overlapping confidence intervals for both versions (Table 2).

Overall, FujiLAM demonstrated a positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 6.19 (95% CI, 4.84–7.91) and a negative likelihood ratio (LR–) of 0.40 (95% CI, 0.32–0.49). LR + values were similar between inpatients (5.70, 95% CI: 4.20–7.71) and outpatients (5.50, 95% CI: 3.85–7.84), while LR– was lower among inpatients (0.28, 0.17–0.47). (Additional file 3).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s test. The Deeks’ funnel plots of the included studies were almost symmetric, and the Egger’s test had a non-significant value (p-value = 0.648), indicating that there was unlikely publication bias (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The world urgently needs a rapid, sensitive, non-sputum-based, and easy-to-use diagnostic tool for TB [25]. This is especially pressing for PLHIV, as many cases currently remain undetected due to the limitations of sputum-based testing. Urine-based antigen PoC tests, such as FujiLAM and AlereLAM, have been developed to meet these needs for TB diagnostics. This systematic review and meta-analysis assess the diagnostic accuracy of the FujiLAM assay for TB in PLHIV, using all pertinent papers from the body of literature.

In this meta-analysis, the FujiLAM assay for TB diagnosis in PLHIV demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 63%, nearly meeting the 65% minimum threshold outlined in the target product profile (TPP) for a non-sputum-based, point-of-care TB test [25]. The assay exhibited good pooled specificity (90%) but fell slightly below the TPP criterion of 98% specificity for PoC TB tests [25]. FujiLAM demonstrated a robust ability to distinguish TB cases from non-cases in PLHIV (AUC = 0.87), although further improvement is needed to reach the optimal diagnostic accuracy (AUC ≥ 0.97) [45, 46]. Overall, these diagnostic performance metrics align with a prior meta-analysis of five adult PLHIV cohorts from three countries, with FujiLAM sensitivity nearly double that of AlereLAM [35]. This is primarily due to differences in detection methods between the FujiLAM and AlereLAM assays. AlereLAM utilizes polyclonal antibodies and a colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay, resulting in a 500 pg/mL detection limit [47]. In contrast, FujiLAM employs high-affinity monoclonal antibodies, and silver amplification enables a much higher sensitivity, detecting LAM down to 30 pg/mL [33].

The subgroup analysis revealed a higher pooled sensitivity (71%) for inpatients, representing a 14% improvement compared to outpatients. This is presumably because hospitalized HIV patients tend to have higher disease severity, a higher bacillary burden, and more extrapulmonary or disseminated TB [33, 43, 48–50]. Previous reviews have shown higher AlereLAM positivity among hospitalized HIV patients than outpatients, a finding consistent with the present results [27, 51]. However, the FujiLAM assay exhibits greater sensitivity than that of AlereLAM in both inpatient and outpatient settings [27, 51]. Pooled specificity showed comparable values in outpatient (88%) and inpatient (86%) settings, which is in line with a previous meta-analysis [35].

FujiLAM pooled sensitivity was comparable among symptomatic (63%) and unselected participants (63%), representing a 21% and 28% increase, respectively, over the sensitivity reported for AlereLAM [27]. Further analysis showed FujiLAM’s inpatient sensitivity was higher (76% symptomatic, 67% unselected) than its outpatient sensitivity (56% and 64%, respectively), a trend consistent with prior meta-analyses of AlereLAM [27]. Nonetheless, FujiLAM is more sensitive than AlereLAM across all groups [27], which would enable considerably more patients to be diagnosed.

The degree of immunosuppression in PLHIV has a direct impact on the accuracy of urine LAM tests for TB [52]. In our meta-analysis, sensitivity was higher (74%) in patients with CD4 < 200 cells/µL compared to those with CD4 >200 cells/µL (42%), confirming that FujiLAM sensitivity increases as CD4 levels decline. This is probably because HIV-induced CD4 + T cell depletion leads to higher mycobacterial loads [52, 53], heightened vulnerability to extrapulmonary, disseminated, or renal TB, and consequently, increased urinary LAM positivity [40, 49, 54, 55]. Conversely, the FujiLAM test exhibited lower specificity (87%) in severely immunosuppressed patients (CD4 ≤ 100 cells/µL). This reduction is likely caused by the reference standard’s difficulty in detecting TB in PLHIV with low CD4 T-cell counts, leading to the misclassification of FujiLAM positive results as false positives [33, 56, 57]. These findings across CD4 strata align with a previous FujiLAM meta-analysis, where the lowest specificity (80%) and highest sensitivity (87%) were observed in PLHIV with CD4 counts ≤ 100 cells/µL [35].

The FujiLAM assay showed higher sensitivity in stored urine samples (64%) than in fresh samples (57%), with overlapping confidence intervals indicating no or less significant differences. Previous studies show that it is unlikely fresh urine samples would yield more false-negative results than biobanked samples [58]. The unexpected decrease in sensitivity in fresh urine samples can likely be attributed to a high proportion of patients with elevated CD4 counts and inconsistent accuracy across FujiLAM lot numbers used in half of the studies [57, 59]. Furthermore, FujiLAM exhibited higher pooled sensitivity and specificity for TB diagnosis in HIV-positive adults compared to children, at 64% versus 57% and 90% versus 80%, respectively. The reduced specificity in children could be attributed to either bacterial cross-reactivity from urine contamination during collection or limitations of the reference tests employed [60]. The FujiLAM I assay showed greater sensitivity (65%) than FujiLAM II (56%), although the confidence intervals overlapped, suggesting no or less significant differences between versions. The differences may be partly due to variations in testing procedures or the clinical status of study populations, including the degree of immunosuppression, bacillary density, and disease severity. The second-generation assay uses only one drop of specimen, which reduces the antigen burden by half and may impact sensitivity [61, 62]. Additionally, intrinsic changes made during the re-engineering of FujiLAM II to optimize manufacturing consistency may have affected its performance [61, 62]. Moreover, the small number of available studies assessing FujiLAM II (n = 3) and their narrow geographic scope limits the precision and generalizability of the findings. However, due to the limited availability of studies assessing FujiLAM II diagnostic accuracy for symptomatic individuals, children living with HIV, and HIV-associated EPTB, performance estimates in these populations should be interpreted with caution. This gap in the current research underscores the urgent need for further studies.

In summary, FujiLAM demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy, with higher sensitivity for subgroups of patients with lower CD4 counts and inpatients. FujiLAM’s superior sensitivity compared with AlereLAM could enhance TB case detection among PLHIV, facilitating earlier diagnosis and treatment and thereby potentially reducing morbidity and transmission. However, its pooled performance does not fully satisfy the WHO TPP thresholds, particularly for 98% specificity and 65% sensitivity for PoC TB diagnostic tests [25]. Although FujiLAM II appears less sensitive than FujiLAM I, it shows high specificity and consistent diagnostic accuracy across different production lots [61]. But additional studies with a larger number of lots are needed to establish its robustness before it can be deployed for routine clinical use. Despite this, FujiLAM is compatible with readily obtainable urine samples, can be implemented in basic laboratory settings [34], and has an approximate cost of $6 USD per test [63]. Additionally, its lateral flow mechanism enables results to be read visually, and the test running process requires only five steps, with a turnaround time of less than 60 min [34]. Therefore, FujiLAM may potentially complement existing diagnostic methods after further validation.

The strengths of this systematic review and meta-analysis include the use of various search strategies, adherence to the PRISMA 2020 guideline, and critical evaluation of methodological quality using QUADAS-2. Furthermore, this updated review incorporates new studies published since the previous meta-analysis by Broger et al. This review, however, has several drawbacks. First, all studies evaluating the performance of FujiLAM were conducted in high TB/HIV burden countries, which could limit generalizability. The diagnostic accuracy of FujiLAM based on a comprehensive reference standard (CRS) remains undetermined, as the included studies mainly used an MRS, and CRS was not uniformly employed. Using MRS as a gold standard compared to CRS could lead to an underestimation of a test’s sensitivity and an overestimation of its specificity, as shown previously [35, 64]. Some analyses included limited data due to a small number of studies and participants; therefore, their results should be interpreted cautiously. Although subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the sources of heterogeneity, some inconsistencies still persisted. The degree of heterogeneity observed in the sensitivity and specificity estimates, partly attributable to differences in study settings, populations, FujiLAM lot numbers, and reference standards, limits the certainty with which pooled estimates can be generalized. The presence of missing data in a few studies, such as CD4 cell level and sex distribution, may limit the ability to fully assess the representativeness and potential heterogeneity. Additionally, the lack of a structured GRADE evaluation may limit the depth of a systematic review’s interpretation. The literature search was restricted to studies published in English, which may introduce language bias. Although the review employed a systematic search strategy across four major databases to capture studies on FujiLAM in PLHIV, it may have missed articles indexed in other databases. Lack of meta-analysis data on FujiLAM’s cost and implementation feasibility may limit its programmatic applicability and policy relevance. Finally, the number of currently available studies is limited, particularly for HIV-associated EPTB and in children living with HIV.

Conclusions

FujiLAM demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy, with higher sensitivity for subgroups of patients with lower CD4 counts and inpatients. Although FujiLAM II appears less sensitive than FujiLAM I, it shows high specificity and consistent diagnostic accuracy across different production lots. However, studies assessing FujiLAM II diagnostic accuracy, HIV-associated EPTB, and children living with HIV remain limited. Further robust, prospective evaluations of FujiLAM II are needed before programmatic adoption, particularly in outpatient and pediatric settings.
