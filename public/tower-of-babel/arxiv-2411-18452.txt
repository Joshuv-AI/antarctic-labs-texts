Self-similar instability and forced nonuniqueness: an application to the 2D Euler equations
Michele Dolce, Giulia Mescolini

Abstract
. Building on an approach introduced by Golovkin in the ’60s, we show that nonuniqueness in some forced PDEs is a direct consequence of the existence of a self-similar linearly unstable eigenvalue: the key point is a clever choice of the forcing term removing complicated nonlinear interactions. We use this method to give a short and self-contained proof of nonuniqueness in 2D perfect fluids, first obtained in Vishik’s groundbreaking result. In particular, we present a direct construction of a forced self-similar unstable vortex, where we treat perturbatively the self-similar operator in a new and more quantitative way.



1.
Introduction

Many partial differential equations arising from the description of physical systems, such as the Euler, Navier-Stokes or Boussinesq equations, when seen in self-similar variables
(\xi,t)=(x/t^{\gamma},t)
for some
\gamma>0
give rise to a Cauchy problem of the form

(1.1)

\begin{cases}t{\partial}_{t}G=L(G)+B(G,G)+F,\qquad t\geq 0,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
G|_{t=0}=g_{0}.\end{cases}

Here
G:[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the unknown self-similar profile,
L:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear operator with
X
being a Banach space,
B:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
a bilinear one, and
F:[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a forcing term. We claim that, by choosing appropriately the forcing term, it is straightforward to generate two solutions emanating from the same initial data
g_{0}
if the linearization of the PDE at
g_{0}
has an unstable eigenvalue. This observation is a consequence of a nice trick used by Golovkin
1
1

1

He was a student of Ladyženskaja
[
21
]
passed away prematurely in the 1969. He was interested in the study of nonuniqueness of stationary states in fluids with given boundary conditions.

[
15
,
16
]
and the self-similar non-uniqueness scenario proposed by Jia and Šverák in the 3D Navier-Stokes equations
[
17
]
. The ideas of Golovkin were used in the example of nonuniqueness proposed by Ladyženskaja
[
20
]
and its interplay with an approximately self-similar scenario was explored for some shell-models in
[
14
]
. We will refer to this method as
Golovkin’s trick
and it can be explained as follows: we look for two solutions in the form

(1.2)

G^{\pm}(\xi,t)=g_{0}(\xi)\pm\mathrm{Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta(\xi)),

with
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\rm Re}(\lambda)>0
. This last assumption guarantees that
G^{\pm}|_{t=0}=g_{0}
if
\eta
is sufficiently regular. Then, we still have the freedom of choosing the forcing term
F
and the equation satisfied by
\eta
. Plugging the ansatz
Eq.

1.2
in
Eq.

1.1
, we find that

(1.3)

\mp\mathrm{Re}\left(t^{\lambda}(L(\eta)+B(g_{0},\eta)+B(\eta,g_{0})-\lambda\eta)\right)=L(g_{0})+B(g_{0},g_{0})+B(\mathrm{Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta),\mathrm{Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta))+F.

Hence, if we define

(1.4)

\displaystyle F\coloneqq-L(g_{0})-B(g_{0},g_{0})-B(\mathrm{Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta),\mathrm{Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta))

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g_{0}}(\eta)\coloneqq L(\eta)+B(g_{0},\eta)+B(\eta,g_{0}),

we see that
Eq.

1.2
solves
Eq.

1.1
if there exists a couple
(\lambda,\eta)
with
{\rm Re}(\lambda)>0
and a function
\eta
such that

(1.6)

\mathcal{L}_{g_{0}}(\eta)=\lambda\eta.

Namely,
\eta
is an eigenfunction associated to any unstable eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g_{0}}
. With this trick, which heavily relies on the fact that the nonlinearity is quadratic, one can always generate exactly two different solutions (if
\eta
exists and it is non-trivial) with the force
F
(that depends on
g_{0}
and
\eta
). The two different solutions are

(1.7)

G^{+}(\xi,t)=g_{0}(\xi)+{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta(\xi))\quad\text{and}\quad G^{-}(\xi,t)=g_{0}(\xi)-{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta(\xi)).

Notice that
g_{0}
does not even need to solve the unforced PDE. However, it is desirable that the force
F
satisfies at least some integrability properties, which are directly related to properties of
g_{0}
and the eigenfunction
\eta
.
A natural question arises:

Question:
Under which conditions on
L,B
one can always find a smooth profile
g_{0}
so that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g_{0}}
has at least one unstable eigenvalue with a smooth eigenfunction?

We require some (unspecified) smoothness to guarantee that the self-similar solution lives in the natural
L^{p}
spaces where the existence of weak solutions is known. Finally, notice that the property of not having eigenvalues with positive real part for
all
smooth
g_{0}
seems to impose a quite rigid structure on the operators
L
and
B
(such as
L
being a good enough dissipative operator or
\mathcal{L}_{g_{0}}
being always skew-adjoint for any
g_{0}
).

1.1.
Forcing the nonuniqueness in 2D perfect fluids

Spectacular examples of nonuniqueness with a force have been recently obtained for equations arising in fluid dynamics
[
17
,
3
,
10
,
2
,
4
,
1
]
. The PDEs involved are of the form
Eq.

1.1
when seen in self-similar variables. In this paper, we only focus on the specific example of the 2D forced and incompressible Euler equations. In vorticity formulation they are given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\partial}_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=f,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\,t\in[0,T),\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\quad\Delta\psi=\omega,\\
\omega|_{t=0}=\omega_{0},\end{cases}

where for our discussion
T>0
will always be finite.
It is well known that for
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{\infty}_{x}
and
f\in L^{1}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{\infty}_{x})
, Yudovich’s theory
[
28
]
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to
Eq.

1.8
. The outstanding open problem of nonuniqueness
without a force
with some integrable vorticity has been solved only recently by Brué, Colombo and Kumar
[
9
]
. This result relies on a new convex integration scheme that works for
\omega_{0}\in L^{1+\varepsilon}_{x}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. However, nonuniqueness with convex integration can only be obtained for
\omega_{0}\in L^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
p<3/2
in view of energy conservation for larger values of
p
. For general
p
without a force, the problem is fully open and here we just mention two promising scenarios proposed by Bressan and Shen
[
8
]
(see also
[
7
]
) and Elgindi, Murray and Said
[
11
]
.
If one instead uses the extra degree of freedom given by a force, then it is possible to achieve a large range of
L^{p}_{x}
spaces not covered by Yudovich’s theory. This was first proved in Vishik’s groundbreaking results
[
26
,
27
]
, establishing the nonuniqueness of solutions “emanating” from a force
f\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x})
. Vishik’s result was later revisited and refined in
[
3
]
, see also
[
10
]
, and the theorem can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

For every
p\in(2,\infty)
there is a triple
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x}
,
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
and
f\in L^{1}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x})
with the property that there are uncountably many solutions
\omega\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x})
and
u\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{2}_{x})
of
Eq.

1.8
. Moreover,
\omega_{0}
can be chosen to vanish identically.

To explain the main ideas behind the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we introduce the self-similar variables

(1.9)

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{x}{t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}},\qquad(\omega,\psi,f)(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}(\Omega,\,t^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}\Psi,\,t^{-1}F)(\xi,t),

where
\alpha\in(0,2)
is a free parameter due to the scaling properties of
Eq.

1.8
.
The equations
Eq.

1.8
in self-similar variables are

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle t{\partial}_{t}\Omega-\big(1+\frac{\xi}{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\big)\Omega+U\cdot\nabla\Omega=F,\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\,t>0,\\
U=\mathrm{BS}(\Omega)=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\qquad\Delta\Psi=\Omega.\end{cases}

The
t\to 0
limit is a singular limit, but one can carefully assign an initial data in
Eq.

1.10
, and then recover the initial data
\omega_{0}
in
L^{p}
sense for instance.

The first idea behind the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to exploit Jia and Šverák strategy
[
17
]
: find a linearly unstable self-similar profile (ideally without a force). The simplest exact self-similar solution is the radial vortex
\Omega=|\xi|^{-\alpha}
, which unfortunately cannot be used for the desired nonuniqueness as we explain in more detail in
Remark

1.4
. By perturbing this radial vortex, self-similar spirals have been recently constructed in
[
12
,
13
,
24
]
. However, the study of spectral properties of these exact self-similar solutions remains an extremely interesting open problem to date.
To overcome the issue of using an exact self-similar solution, the first key observation by Vishik is that any radial vortex is a self-similar solution with a suitable force. Indeed, a solution to
Eq.

1.10
is

(1.11)

\Omega_{V}(|x|)\coloneqq\beta g(|x|),\qquad U_{V}(x)=\beta v(|x|)x^{\perp},\qquad F_{V}=-\beta(1+\xi/\alpha)\cdot\nabla g,

with
0\neq\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a given smooth function and

(1.12)

v(r)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\rho g(\rho){\rm d}\rho.

However, it was not clear if these type of solutions can be unstable in the self-similar framework, but here comes the second fundamental observation by Vishik: consider the linearized operator

(1.13)

\mathcal{L}_{\beta,g}(w)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\beta}(1+\frac{\xi}{\alpha})\cdot\nabla w-U_{V}\cdot\nabla w-\mathrm{BS}(w)\cdot\nabla g,

arising from the linearization of
Eq.

1.10
at
g
. Then, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\beta,g}
can be seen as a perturbation of the one obtained by formally setting
\beta=\infty
. But
\mathcal{L}_{\infty,g}
is nothing more than the linearized operator associated to the 2D Euler equations in standard variables. For the latter, examples of spectrally unstable vortices were rigorously proved to exist already in the work of Z. Lin
[
22
]
, for instance. However, the spectral perturbation in
\beta
is quite delicate and in fact the structure of the specific operators involved is crucial (indeed, with a naive approach one could encounter derivative losses).
More precisely, thanks to the refinements of Vishik’s results obtained in
[
3
,
10
]
, the following is now established.

Theorem 1.2
.

For a suitable class of
g\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with zero mean
\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(r)r{\rm d}r=0
, there exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that for all
\beta>\beta_{0}
there exists
(\lambda,w)
with
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>0
and
w\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})\cap C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}w(x){\rm d}x=0
such that

(1.14)

\mathcal{L}_{\beta,g}(w)=\lambda w.

These smoothness properties of the eigenfunction and a simplified construction of a compactly supported unstable vortex have been established in
[
10
]
, where the zero mean condition was imposed to simplify the proof to get
L^{2}_{x}
velocity fields.
The proposition above implies that
\Omega=g+{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}\eta)
solves

(1.15)

t{\partial}_{t}\Omega=\mathcal{L}_{\beta,g}(\Omega)+F_{V},

which is the linearized problem of
Eq.

1.10
at
g
. We thus have two solutions of the linearized problem emanating from the same initial data, namely
\Omega_{0}=g
and
\Omega_{1}=g+{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}w)
. To obtain two solutions in the nonlinear problem, one has to perform a delicate nonlinear argument in order to correct the error arising when plugging the linearized solution in
Eq.

1.10
, which is the last key point in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

1.2.
Golovkin’s trick in the 2D Euler equations

Combinining
Theorem

1.2
with the Golovkin’s trick, we observe that the nonlinear argument mentioned before can be avoided if one is only interested in producing
exactly two
solutions.
Indeed, it is enough to define

(1.16)

F=-\beta(1+\xi/\alpha)\cdot\nabla g+\mathrm{BS}({\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}w))\cdot\nabla{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}w),

where
g
is an unstable vortex and
(\lambda,w)
is the eigenpair in Theorem
1.2
. Then, we have the following two solutions to
Eq.

1.10

(1.17)

\Omega^{+}=g+{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}w),\qquad\Omega^{-}=g-{\rm Re}(t^{\lambda}w).

The associated solutions in original variables are
\omega^{\pm}(x,t)=t^{-1}\Omega^{\pm}(x/t^{1/\alpha})
. Thanks to the properties of
g
and
w
, for any
T>0
we also know that

(1.18)

\|\omega^{\pm}(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}}=\|\Omega^{\pm}(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}},\qquad\|\omega^{\pm}(t)\|_{L^{p}}<\infty\qquad\text{for all }0\leq t<T\text{ and }p<\tfrac{2}{\alpha}.

Moreover
\omega^{\pm}\to 0
as
t\to 0
in
L^{p}
with
p<2/\alpha
but
\omega^{+}(t)\neq\omega^{-}(t)\neq 0
in
L^{p}
for all
t>0.

It remains to verify that the velocity fields
u^{\pm}(t,\cdot)=\mathrm{BS}(\omega^{\pm})(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}_{x}
. To this end, it is enough to observe
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega^{\pm}{\rm d}x=0
since
g(r)
is a zero mean vortex and
w
has zero mean (it is in fact a function concentrated in an angular Fourier mode
k\neq 0
.)
Therefore, the two solutions in
Eq.

1.17
enjoy the same properties of the infinitely many ones found in
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
10
]
. Thus, with the Golovkin’s trick, nonuniqueness becomes a direct corollary of Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 1.3
.

The nonuniqueness statement obtained with the Golovkin’s trick is weaker compared to what is obtained in
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
10
]
. One has exactly two solutions and not a whole family living in the unstable manifold. The main difference is in the choice of the force. In our case, the force depends on the eigenpair
(\lambda,w)
. Therefore, once we fix
(\lambda,w)
we find exactly two solutions with a single force. On the other hand, the results in
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
10
]
show that with the force
F_{V}
(which only depends on the vortex
g
), one can construct infinitely many solutions due to the possibility of suitably scaling any eigenpair
(\lambda,w)
(or changing the
m
-fold symmetry of
w
). Therefore, the nonuniqueness found in
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
10
]
is much more dramatic. The price to pay is a delicate nonlinear argument needed to correct the nonlinear error arising from the eigenpair (the last term in
Eq.

1.16
).

Remark 1.4

(On the stability of a power law vortex)
.

Consider the power law vortex
\omega_{\alpha}(x)=(2-\alpha)|x|^{-\alpha}
with associated self-similar profile
\Omega_{\alpha}(\xi)=(2-\alpha)|\xi|^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the first solves
Eq.

1.8
and the second
Eq.

1.10
. We exhibit a simple example of nonuniqueness arising from modes
|k|=1
. In this case, one can explicitly find nonunique solutions for the linearized problem around
\omega_{\alpha}
in standard variables, that is

(1.19)

\begin{cases}{\partial}_{t}w_{\alpha}+r^{-\alpha}{\partial}_{\theta}w_{\alpha}-\alpha(2-\alpha)r^{-(2+\alpha)}{\partial}_{\theta}\varphi_{\alpha}=0,\\
({\partial}_{rr}+r^{-1}{\partial}_{r}+r^{-2}{\partial}_{\theta\theta})\varphi_{\alpha}=w_{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Imposing a separation of variables ansatz
w_{\alpha}(r,\theta,t)=t^{\gamma}r^{q}\cos(k\theta)
, it is not hard to verify that setting
q=-(1+\alpha)
,
k=1
and
\varphi_{\alpha}(r,\theta,t)=-\frac{1}{\alpha(2-\alpha)}t^{\gamma}(r^{1-\alpha}\cos(\theta)+\frac{\gamma}{t}r\sin(\theta))
then we explicitly solve
Eq.

1.19
for any
\gamma>0
. This explicit solution emanates from the radially symmetric vortex and exact self-similar solution
\omega_{\alpha}
and it is instantaneously breaking the radial symmetry. The factor
\gamma
can be interpreted as an unstable eigenvalue for the self-similar problem, but the freedom in choosing
\gamma
relies on special cancellations for the modes
k=\pm 1
(as
r\sin(\theta)
being harmonic). Moreover, applying the Golovkin’s trick we know that
\omega^{\pm}(r,\theta,t)=(2-\alpha)r^{-\alpha}\pm w_{\alpha}(r,\theta,t)\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{1}_{{\rm loc},x}\cap L^{p_{\alpha}}_{{\rm loc},x}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
are two solutions to
Eq.

1.8
with the force

(1.20)

f=-\frac{1}{\alpha(2-\alpha)}\nabla^{\perp}\varphi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla w_{\alpha}\in L^{1}_{t}\big((L^{1}_{{\rm loc},x}\cap L^{\tilde{p}_{\alpha}}_{{\rm loc},x})(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})\big),

where
p_{\alpha},\tilde{p}_{\alpha}\in(1,\infty)
can be explicitly computed.
However,
f\notin L^{1}_{t}(L^{p}_{{\rm loc},x}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, meaning that this example does not prove the desired nonuniqueness for the 2D forced Euler equations.
Finally, let us also remark that linear stability properties of
\Omega_{\alpha}
have been rigorously studied in
[
6
]
, exhibiting a concept of stability that naturally excludes the pathological behavior described above.

1.3.
Main result

The main goal of this paper is to provide a self-contained proof of the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every
p\in(2,\infty)
there is a triple
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x}
,
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{x}
and
f\in L^{1}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x})
with the property that there are two solutions
\omega^{+},\omega^{-}\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{1}_{x}\cap L^{p}_{x})
and
u^{+},u^{-}\in L^{\infty}_{t}(L^{2}_{x})
of
Eq.

1.8
. Moreover,
\omega_{0}
can be chosen to vanish identically.

Thanks to the Golovkin’s trick, we know that everything is reduced in obtaining yet another proof of Theorem
1.2
. The novelty is that we construct the eigenpair
(\lambda,\eta)
directly in self-similar variables with a compactly supported vortex
g\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. We thus avoid performing the spectral perturbation argument after the construction of an unstable vortex in standard variables as in
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
10
]
.

We will still treat the operator arising from the self-similar change of variables perturbatively, but the new key observation is in how we include it directly in the construction of the eigenpair. To do this, we first observe that when studying spectral stability properties of vortices (or shear flows) in standard variables, one formally divide the Rayleigh equation by
(ikv(r)-\lambda)
, where
v
is as in
Eq.

1.11
. This operation is related to considering the resolvent of the operator
U_{V}\cdot\nabla
. Here, we will instead consider also the self-similar part and study the resolvent of the operator

(1.21)

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}=U_{V}\cdot\nabla-\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}(\xi\cdot\nabla+1),

which takes into account the skew adjoint part of
\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\xi\cdot\nabla
.
In particular, we perform an expansion for
\beta\gg 1
and we obtain the following.

Proposition 1.6
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
, there exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
the following holds true: there exists an operator
\mathcal{T}_{z,\beta}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{\beta}:D(\mathcal{S}_{\beta})\to L^{2}_{x}
such that

(1.22)

(\mathcal{S_{\beta}}-z)^{-1}=(U_{V}\cdot\nabla-z)^{-1}-\mathcal{T}_{\beta}

where
\|\mathcal{T}_{\beta}\|_{X\to X}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
with
X\in\{L^{2}_{x},\dot{H}^{1}_{x}\}
.

With this result, we have a quantitative way of expanding the resolvent of
\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
so that we can follow the standard construction of unstable eigenvalues in the 2D Euler equations. To construct the eigenpair, we will combine the arguments used in
[
3
,
10
,
19
,
5
]
. Namely, we use a compactly supported vortex as in
[
10
]
. This allow us to perform an inner-outer splitting as in
[
19
,
5
]
and follow the construction from a neutral limiting mode as done in
[
3
,
19
,
22
]
. Moreover, with a compactly supported background vortex it is rather straightforward to show the desired properties of the eigenfunction, see
Corollary

2.4
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in

Section

2
we present our direct proof of
Theorem

1.2
. In
Section

3
we show the proof of a result that implies
Proposition

1.6
, which is a key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

2.
A direct approach to self-similar instability

The goal of this section is to provide a direct proof of Theorem
1.2
. We will follow the standard strategy of constructing the unstable eigenpair as the one arising from a
neutral limiting mode
, a method that dates back at least to the work of Tollmien
[
25
]
. This was first rigorously implemented by Z. Lin
[
22
]
and improved and refined in
[
3
]
. The main novelty compared to the recent works
[
3
,
26
,
27
,
10
]
is in how we treat the self-similar operator. Here, we directly include it in the argument to construct the eigenvalue, thus avoiding the use of general spectral perturbation arguments.

2.1.
The self-similar Rayleigh’s equation

We start our analysis by writing the system for the perturbation around a vortex as in
Eq.

1.11
. To this end, let

\Omega^{\pm}=\Omega_{V}\pm W.

To write the system for
W
, we introduce the standard polar coordinates
\xi=r(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
where
(r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}
. The gradient and the Laplacian are
\nabla_{r,\theta}=({\partial}_{r},r^{-1}{\partial}_{\theta})
and
\Delta_{r,\theta}={\partial}_{rr}+r^{-1}{\partial}_{r}+r^{-2}{\partial}_{\theta\theta}
respectively. Then, it is not hard to check that if
W
satisfies the following system

(2.1)

\begin{cases}t{\partial}_{t}W-(1+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}r{\partial}_{r})W+\beta v(r){\partial}_{\theta}W-\beta\tfrac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}{\partial}_{\theta}\Phi=0,\\
\Delta_{r,\theta}\Phi=W,\quad\Phi\to 0\text{ as }r\to 0^{+}\text{ and }r\to+\infty,\\
W|_{t=0}=0,\end{cases}

then
\Omega^{+}
and
\Omega^{-}
are two solutions of
Eq.

1.10
with the force

(2.2)

F=\nabla^{\perp}_{r,\theta}\Phi\cdot\nabla_{r,\theta}W-\beta(1+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}r{\partial}_{r})g(r).

We aim at solving
Eq.

2.1
in
L^{2}(r{\rm d}r{\rm d}\theta)
based spaces. Therefore, given the structure of the equations, it is convenient to make the following ansatz

(2.3)

\displaystyle W(t,r,\theta)=\mathrm{Re}(t^{\beta\lambda}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{ik\theta}\eta(r)),\qquad\Phi(t,r,\theta)=\mathrm{Re}(t^{\beta\lambda}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{ik\theta}\phi(r)),

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
|k|\geq 2
,
\beta\gg 1
to be specified later and
(\lambda,\eta)\in\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,{\rm Re}(z)>0\}\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is the eigenpair we are looking for. More precisely, the eigenfunction in
Theorem

1.2
is
w=r^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{ik\theta}\eta(r)
. The factor
r^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is introduced to work in
L^{2}({\rm d}r)
based spaces instead of
L^{2}(r{\rm d}r).
We first observe that
\Delta_{r,\theta}\Phi=W
becomes

(2.4)

\mathrm{L}_{k}(\phi)\coloneqq\phi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big(k^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big)\phi=\eta,\qquad\phi\to 0\text{ as }r\to 0^{+}\text{ and }r\to\infty.

Notice that
\mathrm{L}_{k}
is the conjugation of the Laplacian restricted to the
k
-th angular Fourier mode with the weight
r^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, namely
\mathrm{L}_{k}=r^{\frac{1}{2}}({\partial}_{rr}+r^{-1}{\partial}_{r}-r^{2}/k^{2})r^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Let us present the proof of this corollary which would then readily imply the proof of the nonuniqueness theorem.

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

We can first rewrite
Eq.

2.5
as

(2.9)

r{\partial}_{r}\eta=\big(-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha\beta(\lambda_{\beta}+ikv(r))\big)\eta-ik\alpha\beta\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}\phi.

Now, one would be tempted to take the integrating factor naturally associated with the equation above with
\phi=0
. However, this would require to integrate
v(r)/r
and we cannot do so for
r\to 0
. To overcome this issue, we first make a change in “phase” by considering

(2.10)

\widetilde{\eta}(r)\coloneqq{\rm e}^{-ik\alpha\beta v(0)\log(r)}\eta(r),

where
v(0)=g_{0}/2
for vortices in class
\mathfrak{G}
as in
Definition

. A direct computation gives

(2.11)

r{\partial}_{r}\widetilde{\eta}=\big(-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha\beta(\lambda_{\beta}+ik(v(r)-v(0))\big)\eta-ik\alpha\beta\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}{\rm e}^{-ik\alpha\beta v(0)\log(r)}\phi.

We now denote

(2.12)

\widetilde{I}(r)=r^{\alpha\beta\lambda_{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{ik\alpha\beta\int_{0}^{r}s^{-1}(v(s)-v(0)){\rm d}s}=I(r){\rm e}^{-ik\alpha\beta v(0)\log(r)}.

Notice that the integral in the definition of
\widetilde{I}
is well defined by the properties of

and hence

as in
Eq.

1.12
and we have

(\widetilde{I}^{-1}\widetilde{\eta})(r)=(I^{-1}\eta)(r).

Then, by the definitions of
\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{I}
and

, we get

(2.13)

r{\partial}_{r}(\widetilde{I}^{-1}\widetilde{\eta})=r{\partial}_{r}(I^{-1}\eta)=-ik\alpha\beta\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r\widetilde{I}(r){\rm e}^{ik\alpha\beta v(0)\log(r)}}\phi=-ik\alpha\beta\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{rI(r)}\phi.

Now, since
g^{\prime}(r)=0
for

the only solution

that can belong to

is given by

(2.14)

\eta(r)=\widetilde{I}(r)\Big(C_{\phi}-ik\alpha\beta\int_{0}^{r}\frac{g^{\prime}(s)}{s^{2}I(s)}\phi(s){\rm d}s\Big),

where

is chosen so that
\eta(r)=0

.

By the formula for

, we readily deduce that

is compactly supported in

since

for

. Moreover, since

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\eta
be the function in Corollary

,

and

be defined as in

-

\omega_{\pm}(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}(\Omega_{V}\pm W)\big(\frac{x}{t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}},t\big)

are equal at time

and that for

are two non-trivial solutions of the Euler’s equation in

with the force

f(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}F\big(\frac{x}{t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}},t\big).

First, we observe that since

is compactly supported we have

t^{-1}\|\Omega_{V}(\cdot/t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim t^{-1}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(\rho/t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})|^{p}\rho{\rm d}\rho\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim t^{\frac{2}{\alpha p}-1},

which is finite for any

and it goes to zero as

for any

. Turning our attention to the part containing

, we observe that

t^{-1}\|W(\cdot/t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim t^{{\rm Re}(\beta\lambda)-1}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\big(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\cdot}}\big)(\rho/t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})\big|^{p}\rho{\rm d}\rho\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim t^{{\rm Re}(\beta\lambda)+\frac{2}{\alpha p}-1}\|r^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\eta\|_{L^{p}}.

We also know that

and therefore

. Moreover, when

is sufficiently large, thanks to the characterizations in Corollary

. Therefore

(2.19)

t^{-1}\|W(\cdot/t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim t^{\beta{\rm Re}(\lambda_{\beta})+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\frac{2}{p}-\frac{1}{2})},

meaning that this term goes to zero as

for all

if

is sufficiently large (because
{\rm Re}(\lambda_{\beta})>\varepsilon/2
for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
). By similar arguments we deduce that

. Hence

(2.20)

\lim_{t\to 0}\|\omega_{+}(t)-\omega_{-}(t)\|_{L^{p}}=0

for all

but

2.2.
Function spaces and operators

Given
R_{*}>0
, we work in the standard Sobolev spaces with norms (or seminorms for
\dot{H}^{1}
)

(2.21)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{1}_{0}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)}^{2}\coloneqq\|{\partial}_{r}f\|_{L^{2}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)}^{2}+\|r^{-1}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)},

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R_{*},+\infty);{\rm d}r)}^{2}\coloneqq\|{\partial}_{r}f\|_{L^{2}([R_{*},+\infty);{\rm d}r)}^{2}+\|r^{-1}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([R,+\infty);{\rm d}r)},

(2.23)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};{\rm d}r)}^{2}\coloneqq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};{\rm d}r)}^{2}+\|{\partial}_{r}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};{\rm d}r)}^{2}+\|r^{-1}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};{\rm d}r)},

(2.24)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R_{*},+\infty];{\rm d}r)}\coloneqq M^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R_{*},+\infty);{\rm d}r)},\qquad M>0,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)}:=\sum_{q=0}^{1}\|r^{q}{\partial}_{r}f\|_{L^{2}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)}+\|r^{q-1}f\|_{L^{2}([0,R_{*}];{\rm d}r)}.

In the sequel, we will always omit the
{\rm d}r
in the notation of the spaces and we simplify the notation by omitting the domain when it is clear from context in which one the norm is set. Note that the norms above distinguish between a bounded domain
[0,R_{*}]
and
[R_{*},+\infty)
. This is useful to exploit the compact support of the unstable vortex and handle separately technical issues that could arise from working in the whole
\mathbb{R}
. Then, the first three norms above are natural in view of the presence of the elliptic operator
\mathrm{L}_{k}
in
Eq.

2.5
. The space
Z_{M}
, used for instance in
[
19
]
, encodes smallness in
\dot{H}^{1}[R_{*},+\infty)
if
M
is sufficiently large, which will allow us to treat the
outer
part of the solution perturbatively. On the other hand, the space
H^{1}_{r}([0,R_{*}])
is adapted to the structure of the
inner
problem, where the need of controlling
r{\partial}_{r}
derivatives is related to the fact that we are working in self-similar variables.
Notice that the
H^{1}_{r}
is the strongest norm and

(2.26)

\|f\|_{H^{1}_{0}([0,R_{*}])}\leq\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,R_{*}])}\lesssim R_{*}\|f\|_{H^{1}_{0}([0,R_{*}])}.

We now introduce a key operator arising from
Eq.

2.5
. First, we observe that setting formally
\beta=\infty
, one recovers exactly the standard Rayleigh’s equation where usually
\lambda=-ikc
. To construct an eigenfunction, it is common to divide

2.5

(ikv(r)+\lambda)
when
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)>0
and start the construction around the Sturm-Liouville problem arising when
{\rm Re}(\lambda)=0
(the neutral limiting mode procedure). Note that dividing by
ikv(r)+\lambda
is equivalent to considering the resolvent of the transport operator
v(r){\partial}_{\theta}
. In our case, we need to consider the resolvent of the operator
S_{\beta}:D(S_{\beta})\subset L^{2}({\rm d}r)\to L^{2}({\rm d}r)
defined as

(2.27)

\displaystyle S_{\beta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-ikv(r)+\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\big(r\partial_{r}(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2}\big),

(2.28)

\displaystyle D(S_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\{f\in L^{2}({\rm d}r)\,:\,r{\partial}_{r}f\in L^{2}({\rm d}r)\}.

Moreover,
S_{\beta}
is skew-adjoint on
L^{2}({\rm d}r)
. Then, we can rewrite
Eq.

2.5
as

(2.29)

\left(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I\right)\mathrm{L}_{k}(\phi)+ik\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}\phi=0.

Remark 2.5
.

The operator
S_{\beta}
is obviously related to the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
defined in

1.21

S_{\beta}(e^{ik\theta}f)=r^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{S}_{\beta}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}(e^{ik\theta}f)
. Namely,
S_{\beta}
is the restriction to
k
-th Fourier angular mode of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
conjugated with the weight

.

Our next task is to provide an expansion in
\beta
for
(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)^{-1}
, which is clearly well-defined when
{\rm Re}(\lambda_{\beta})>0
since
S_{\beta}
is skew-adjoint.
Once this is done, we can formalize how we treat our problem as a perturbation of the standard Rayleigh’s equation.

In the next proposition we state in which sense
S_{\beta}
is a perturbation of the operator

S_{\infty}:L^{2}({\rm d}r)\to L^{2}({\rm d}r)
, defined as

(2.30)

S_{\infty}\coloneqq-ikv(r).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
S_{\beta},S_{\infty}
be the operators defined in
Eq.

2.27
,
Eq.

2.30
. For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
, there exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
the following holds true: there exists an operator
T_{z,\beta}\coloneqq T_{\beta}:D(S_{\beta})\to L^{2}({\rm d}r)
such that

(2.31)

\displaystyle(S_{\beta}-zI)^{-1}=-(S_{\infty}-zI)^{-1}-T_{\beta}

where
-(S_{\infty}-zI)^{-1}=1/(ikv(r)+z)
and

(2.32)

\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\|T_{\beta}\|_{X\to X}=0,\qquad\text{ for }X\in\{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}),H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R})\}.

The proof of the proposition above is postponed to the
Section

3

Similarly to what we observed in Remark

2.3.
Inner and outer splitting

To avoid some technical issues related to the unboundedness of the domain, we split our solution with an
interior
and an
outer
part, see also
[
5

19
]
. To this end, we define two cut-off functions
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
with the following properties: let
R=10r_{0}
with
r_{0}
the fixed constant in the
Definition

2.1
and let
M\gg r_{0}
to be specified later.
Let
\chi_{1}
be a smooth function such that
\chi_{1}(r)=1
for
r\leq M/2
and
\chi_{1}(r)=0
for
r\geq M
. This is the cut-off for the
interior
part. For the
outer
part, we set
\chi_{2}(r)=0
for
r\leq R
and
\chi_{2}(r)=1
for

. Moreover, these cut-off can be chosen to satisfy

(2.33)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{1}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|r\chi_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{1}{M},

(2.34)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{2}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|r\chi_{2}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim 1,

where we keep track of constants depending on
M
since this is a parameter that needs to be choosen sufficiently large. On the other hand, all constants depending on
R
will be hidden in the
\lesssim
.
Then, we split

(2.35)

\phi=\chi_{1}\phi_{\mathrm{in}}+\chi_{2}\phi_{\mathrm{out}},

and we need to define the equations satisfied by
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}
. We observe that

(2.36)

\displaystyle[\chi_{j},S_{\beta}\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi)

\displaystyle=\left(-ikv(r)+\frac{1}{2\alpha\beta}\right)[\chi_{j},\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi)+\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}[\chi_{j},r{\partial}_{r}L_{k}](\phi),

where the commutators between
\chi_{j}
and the operators involved are

(2.37)

\displaystyle[\chi_{j},\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi)

\displaystyle=[\chi_{j},{\partial}_{rr}](\phi)=-2\chi_{j}^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}-\chi_{j}^{\prime\prime}\phi,

\displaystyle[\chi_{j},r{\partial}_{r}\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi)

\displaystyle=[\chi_{j},r{\partial}_{rrr}](\phi)-[\chi_{j},r{\partial}_{r}(r^{-2}(k^{2}-1/4))](\phi)

(2.38)

\displaystyle=-r{\partial}_{r}(\chi_{j}^{\prime\prime}\phi+2\chi_{j}^{\prime}\phi^{\prime})+(k^{2}-1/4)r^{-1}\chi_{j}^{\prime}\phi.

We finally define the inner and outer problems as

(2.39)

\displaystyle\left(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I\right)\mathrm{L}_{k}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}})+ik\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}\phi_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathcal{C}_{2}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for }r\in[0,M]

(2.40)

\displaystyle\left(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I\right)\mathrm{L}_{k}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}})=\mathcal{C}_{1}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for }r\in[R,+\infty)

both with Dirichlet boundary conditions, where

(2.41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}})\coloneqq[\chi_{2},(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi_{\mathrm{out}})

(2.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}})\coloneqq[\chi_{1},(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)\mathrm{L}_{k}](\phi_{\mathrm{in}}).

Since
\chi_{2}g^{\prime}=0

\chi_{1}=1
when

and vice versa, it is not hard to see that when

Thanks to Proposition

2.5.
Constructing the eigenpair

To prove the existence of the desired eigenpair, we now follow more closely the strategy proposed in

(which is itself based on

,

,

]
), which we divide in 5 steps below. The first key step is to extract a Sturm-Liouville (SL) eigenvalue problem approximating

,

and

. Note that the latter choice avoids potentially dangerous singularities in view of the specific properties of a vortex in the class

(namely,

in

, we set

\mathbb{N}\ni k^{2}=k_{0}^{2}+\varepsilon.

and we look for

so that

(2.47)

We would then need to solve a system for

and
\tilde{c}
is determined via the orthogonality condition
\varphi\perp\phi_{0}
. The construction of
\varphi,\phi_{\mathrm{out}}
can be reduced in checking the invertibility of certain operators in the spaces introduced at the beginning of
Section

2.2
. This will be carried out in great detail in Steps 2-4 and represents the most technical part of the paper. Once this is done, it remains to find
\tilde{c}
and prove that
{\rm Im}(\tilde{c})>0
, a task carried out in Step 5. The existence is essentially a consequence of Rouché’s theorem whereas
{\rm Im}(\tilde{c})>0
is a consequence of the Sochocki-Plemelj formula.
We finally remark that the main idea can be summarized as an instability arising from a
neutral limiting mode
associated to the problem with
\varepsilon=0
.

Step 1:
We define the approximating problem for
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}
by considering the formal limits
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\beta\to\infty
, which give a regular Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem

(2.48)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}(\phi_{0})+A(r)\phi_{0}=-\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}+A(r)\phi_{0}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4})\phi_{0}=0,\qquad\text{for }r\in[0,M]

(2.49)

\displaystyle A(r)\coloneqq\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r(v(r)-v(1))},

with Dirichlet boundary conditions
\phi_{0}(0)=\phi_{0}(M)=0
. The standard form of a Sturm-Liouville problem, following the notation in
[
18
]
, is
-(py^{\prime})^{\prime}+qy=\lambda wy
. In our case, we identify
y=\phi_{0}
,
p=1
,
q=A(r)
,
w=r^{-2}
and
\lambda=-(k_{0}^{2}-1/4)
. In the sequel we will apply results from
[
18
]
, which only requires that
1/p,q,w\in L^{1}_{loc}((0,M))
which are clearly satisfied in our context. Now, the parameter
k_{0}
can be chosen so that
\lambda
is the lowest eigenvalue for
Eq.

2.48
(and
\phi_{0}
the associated eigenfunction). Thanks to the boundary conditions and
p\geq 0
, we know that all eigenvalues are simple and eigenfunctions form a basis for
L^{2}([0,M])

[
18
]
Thus, the lowest eigenvalue is characterized by the Rayleigh’s quotient as

(2.50)

-(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4})=\min_{\|f/r\|_{L^{2}}=1}\int_{0}^{M}|f^{\prime}(r)|^{2}+A(r)|f(r)|^{2}{\rm d}r.

For vortices as in
Definition

2.1
, for
r\to 1
we have
A(r)=\gamma_{1}/v^{\prime}(1)+\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(r-1)
, where we recall that
v^{\prime}(1)=-2\int_{0}^{1}g(r)r{\rm d}r+g(1)=-2\int_{0}^{1}g(r)r{\rm d}r-g_{1}
, where the last identity follows by property iii). Choosing appropriately the values of
g_{0},g_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{0},\delta_{1}
, one can choose
A(1)\approx-\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}<0
for a given
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
, and therefore
k_{0}^{2}-1/4
can be tuned by choosing appropriately
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
(one can test the minimization problem
Eq.

2.50
with a function concentrated close to
r=1
). Moreover, by standard properties of regular Sturm-Liouville problems
[
18
]
, we know that the eigenvalue
k_{0}^{2}-1/4
depends smoothly on the choices of the parameters in the class
\mathfrak{G}
in
Definition

2.1
. Therefore, we know that for any
\varepsilon
there exists a vortex in the class
\mathfrak{G}
such that the conditions
Eq.

2.46
and
Eq.

2.50
are satisfied. In fact, to prove that there exists a vortex in the class
\mathfrak{G}
so that
k^{2}
in
Eq.

2.46
is a natural number, one can also choose an arbitrary
|k_{0}|\geq 2
and then perform a suitable linear combination of vortices in the class
\mathfrak{G}
, as done in Proposition 4.4.6 and Lemma 4.4.7 of
[
3
]
.

Let us prove that
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}_{r}([0,M])
with a bound on this norm that does not depend on
M
(otherwise it is straighforward since by
Eq.

2.50
we know
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}([0,M])
). Indeed, we can also prove better weighted estimates if
|k_{0}|
is sufficiently large. To do this, we test
Eq.

2.48
with
-r^{2q}\phi_{0}
for
q\geq 0
to obtain that

(2.51)

-\int_{0}^{M}r^{2q}\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}\phi_{0}{\rm d}r+\big(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big)\|r^{q-1}\phi_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=-\int_{0}^{M}r^{2q}A(r)\phi_{0}^{2}{\rm d}r\leq\|r^{2(q+1)}A\|_{L^{\infty}}\|r^{-1}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Integrating by parts we get

(2.52)

-\int_{0}^{M}r^{2q}\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}\phi_{0}{\rm d}r=\|r^{q}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2q\int_{0}^{M}r^{2q-1}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\phi_{0}{\rm d}r.

Hence, since
\|\phi_{0}/r\|_{L^{2}}=1
,

(2.53)

\|r^{q}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\big(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big)\|r^{q-1}\phi_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\|r^{2(q+1)}A\|_{L^{\infty}}+2q\|r^{q}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\|r^{q-1}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Since
g^{\prime}
is supported in
[0,r_{0}]
, we see that
|r^{2(q+1)}A(r)|\leq C_{1}
for some constant that does not depend on
M
. Hence, by Young’s inequality

(2.54)

\|r^{q}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\big(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big)\|r^{q-1}\phi_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C_{1}+\frac{q}{c}\|r^{q}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c\|r^{q-1}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

for any constant
c>0
. Choosing
q=1
and
c=3/2
we see that for any
|k_{0}|\geq 2
we can easily absorb the last term in the left-hand side and get

(2.55)

\|r\phi_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim 1

with an hidden constant that does not depend on
M
. Hence
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}_{r}([0,M])
with a norm bound independent of
M
. Moreover, setting
q=3
and
c=3
in (
2.54
) we also get that for
|k_{0}|\geq 2

(2.56)

\|r^{2}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim 1,

which will be useful later.

Step 2:

We now look for a solution to the inner problem in the form

(2.57)

\phi_{\mathrm{in}}=\phi_{0}+\varphi.

We also need to impose that
\varphi\perp\phi_{0}
. Thus, we require

(2.58)

\mathbb{P}(\varphi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}}\langle{\varphi,\phi_{0}}\rangle\phi_{0}=0,

where the inner product above is in
L^{2}({\rm d}r)
and the scaling constant is chosen for later convenience.
We can insert the ansatz
Eq.

2.57
in
Eq.

2.44
and
impose the last condition
Eq.

2.58
. In particular, given that
\phi_{0}
solves
Eq.

2.48
, we can rewrite the left-hand side of
Eq.

2.44
as

(2.59)

\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}(\varphi)-(A(r)+\mathbb{P})(\varphi)-\varepsilon\left(\frac{\tilde{c}g^{\prime}(r)}{r(v(r)-c)(v(r)-v(1))}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)(\phi_{0}+\varphi).

Note that the factor
1/r^{2}
above arise from
Eq.

2.46
, since
\mathrm{L}_{k}=\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}-\varepsilon/r^{2}
.
Applying
\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}
, the resulting system for
\varphi
can then be rewritten as follows

(2.60)

\begin{cases}(I-\mathcal{K})(\varphi)=(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{R}_{\beta})(\phi_{0}+\varphi)+\mathcal{R}_{2}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}),\\
\mathbb{P}(\varphi)=0,\end{cases}

where we define

(2.61)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(f)\coloneqq\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}((A(r)+\mathbb{P})(f))

(2.62)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)\coloneqq\varepsilon\,\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\left(\frac{g^{\prime}(r)\tilde{c}f}{r(v(r)-c)(v(r)-v(1))}+\frac{f}{r^{2}}\right)

(2.63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(f)\coloneqq\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\big(T_{\beta}\big(ik\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}f\big)\big)

(2.64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}})\coloneqq\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\big((S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{2}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}})\big)

We want to solve the first equation in
Eq.

2.60
in
H^{1}_{r}([0,M])
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
We observe the following.

Lemma 2.8
.

The operator
I-\mathcal{K}:H^{1}_{r}([0,M])\to H^{1}_{r}([0,M])
is invertible. Moreover, we have

(2.65)

\|(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}f\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}

with
C
independent of
M
.

Proof.

The proof of the invertibility in
H^{1}_{0}([0,M])
is similar to
[
19
, Lemma 3]
. Since
\mathcal{K}
is compact we only have to show that
\mathrm{Ker}(I-\mathcal{K})=0
, namely that if

(2.66)

\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi-(A+\mathbb{P})\psi=0

then
\psi=0
. Testing against
\phi_{0}
, since
\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}-A
is self-adjoint and
(\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}-A)(\phi_{0})=0
we deduce that
\mathbb{P}\psi\perp\phi_{0}
, which is only possible if
\psi=0
. Moreover, the bound on the
H^{1}_{0}([0,M])
norm of
(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}
only depends on the spectral gap associated to the Sturm-Liouville problem
Eq.

2.48
. Indeed, if
\psi=(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}f\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{0}\}
we have nothing to prove thanks to the properties of
\phi_{0}
. Otherwise, since
k_{0}^{2}-1/4
is the maximal eigenvalue of
-{\partial}_{rr}+A(r)
, by standard Sturm-Lioviulle theory
[
18
]
we have a spectral gap that only depends on the properties of the function
A
, which itself does not depend on
M
.

We thus have to show the bound only on the part with
q=1
in the
H^{1}_{r}
norm. By the definition of the operators, let
\psi=(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}f
. Then

(2.67)

\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi-(A(r)+\mathbb{P})(\psi)=\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}f.

Testing the equation by
-r^{2}\psi
, as done to obtain
Eq.

2.55
, we first observe that for
|k_{0}|\geq 2

(2.68)

-\langle r^{2}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi,\psi\rangle\approx\|r\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, by Cauchy-Schwarz and the definition of
\mathbb{P}
, it is not hard to deduce that

(2.69)

\displaystyle\|r\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|r^{4}A\|_{L^{\infty}}\|r^{-1}\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|r^{2}\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}}(\|r\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}).

We conclude the proof thanks to the following properties: i) the piece with
r^{4}A
is uniformly bounded thanks to the properties of
g
. ii) the part involving
\phi_{0}
is bounded thanks to the bounds
Eqs.

2.55
and
2.56
on
\phi_{0}
. iii) the term
r^{-1}\psi
is controlled with the
H^{1}_{0}
norm uniformly in
M
thanks to the observation we made before.
∎

Step 4:
We collect the bounds for the operators
\mathcal{M}_{i,j}
in the next proposition, which is at the core of the argument.

Proposition 2.9
.

If
\beta\gg\varepsilon^{-2}
, the following bounds hold:

(2.76)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])\rightarrow H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon M^{4})+M^{2}\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1),

(2.77)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{1,2}\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))\rightarrow H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2}),

(2.78)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{\mathcal{M}}_{2,1}\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])\rightarrow Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2}),

where
\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1)

With this proposition at hand, we see that

2.75

To prove
Proposition

2.9
, we first recall the following lemma from
[
19

:

Lemma 2.10
.

For
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,

(2.79)

h_{\varepsilon}(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{v(r)-c}-\frac{1}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-\varepsilon\tilde{c}}=\mathcal{O}(1)+i\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

uniformly for

.

Proof.

In the proof, we will use the same letter
\psi
to denote the solution to different auxiliary problems we are going to introduce.

\diamond

Bound for
\mathbf{\mathcal{M}_{1,1}}
: We first prove a bound on the
H^{1}_{0}
norm of the operator. We can observe that

(2.80)

\|\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\|_{H^{1}_{0}\rightarrow H^{1}_{0}}\leq\|(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}\|_{H^{1}_{0}\to H^{1}_{0}}\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}\|_{H^{1}_{0}\to H^{1}_{0}}.

Since by
Lemma

2.8
,
(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}
is a bounded operator in
H^{1}_{0}
, we are left to analyze
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
. From
Lemma

2.10
, we can conclude that there exists a bounded function
h_{\varepsilon}
with
|h_{\varepsilon}|=\mathcal{O}(1)
uniformly in
r
, such that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
can be rewritten as

(2.81)

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(f)=\varepsilon\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\left(A(r)\tilde{c}\left(\frac{f}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-i\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}+h_{\varepsilon}(r)f\right)+\frac{f}{r^{2}}\right).

To bound the operator norm, let us consider an arbitrary
f\in H^{1}_{0}
and let
\psi=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}f
, namely,

(2.82)

\partial_{rr}\psi-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\psi=A(r)\varepsilon\tilde{c}\left(\frac{1}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-i\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}+h_{\varepsilon}(r)\right)f+\varepsilon\frac{f}{r^{2}}.

Note that we can rewrite the first term in the right-hand side as

(2.83)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-i\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}=\frac{1}{v^{\prime}(1)}{\partial}_{r}\left(\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(r-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)}\right),

and in particular there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

(2.84)

\|\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(\cdot-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}\|_{L^{4}([0,M])}\leq CM.

Recalling also that
v^{\prime}(1)\neq 0
and
A(r),A^{\prime}(r)
are bounded, by integration by parts and Hölder’s inequality we get

(2.85)

\displaystyle\bigg|\bigg\langle

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon A(r)\tilde{c}f}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-i\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}},\psi\bigg\rangle\bigg|\lesssim\varepsilon\left|\left\langle\frac{A(r)}{v^{\prime}(1)}f{\partial}_{r}\left(\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(r-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)}\right),\psi\right\rangle\right|

(2.86)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\left\langle|f|\left|\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(r-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)}\right|,{\partial}_{r}\psi\right\rangle

(2.87)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon\left\langle|{\partial}_{r}f|\left|\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(r-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)}\right|,|\psi|\right\rangle

(2.88)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon\left\langle|f|\left|\log\sqrt{v^{\prime}(1)^{2}(r-1)^{2}+(\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c})^{2}}+\tan^{-1}\frac{\varepsilon{\rm Im}\tilde{c}}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)}\right|,|\psi|\right\rangle

(2.89)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon(M\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{4}}+M\|f\|_{L^{4}}(\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}})+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}).

Therefore, testing
Eq.

2.82
with
\psi
it is not hard to conclude that

(2.90)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon(M\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{4}}+M\|f\|_{L^{4}}\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}

: By

\tilde{T}_{\beta}\coloneqq\tilde{T}_{z,\beta}:D(S_{\beta})\to L^{2}({\rm d}r)

(2.104)

(S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}=-\frac{1}{ik(v(r)-\bar{c})}-\tilde{T}_{\beta},\quad\text{with}\quad\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\|\tilde{T}_{\beta}\|_{H^{1}_{r}\to H^{1}_{r}}=0.

Then, testing with
-\psi
and summing for
q=0,1
, for
|k_{0}|\geq 2
we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{r}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}|\langle r^{2}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi,(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle|+\sum_{q=0}^{1}|\langle r^{2q}\mathcal{C}_{2}(f),(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle|.

Observe that thanks to the properties of
T_{\beta}
(and hence
\tilde{T}_{\beta}
) and the fact that
{\rm Re}(\lambda_{\beta})\gtrsim\varepsilon
, we know that

(2.105)

\sum_{q=0}^{1}\|r^{q}{\partial}_{r}(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|r^{q-1}(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}_{r}}

Notice that the loss in the last inequality is related to the singularity of
r{\partial}_{r}(ikv(r)-\lambda_{\beta})^{-1}=-ikrv^{\prime}(r)/(ikv(r)-\lambda_{\beta})^{2}
, which we are estimating in a brutal with
{\rm Re}(\lambda_{\beta})\gtrsim\varepsilon
and
|rv^{\prime}|\lesssim 1
. This is however sufficient since we have an extra factor of
\beta
to compensate the loss.
Therefore, integrating by parts and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we find that

(2.106)

\frac{1}{\beta}|\langle r^{2}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi,(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle|\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\beta}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}_{r}}^{2}

which can be absorbed in the left-hand side whenever
\beta_{0}\gg\varepsilon^{-2}
. We are thus left with the bound involving
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Observe that we can rewrite
\mathcal{C}_{2}(f)
as

(2.107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(f)

\displaystyle=[\chi_{2},(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)L_{k_{0}}](f)

(2.108)

\displaystyle=\left(-ikv(r)+\frac{1}{2\alpha\beta}\right)(-2\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}-\chi^{\prime\prime}_{2}f)+\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}r{\partial}_{r}(-2\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}-\chi^{\prime\prime}_{2}f)

(2.109)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}(k_{0}^{2}-1/4)r^{-1}\chi^{\prime}_{2}f-\left(\lambda-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha\beta}\right)(-2\chi^{\prime}_{2}f-\chi^{\prime\prime}_{2}f).

Thus, this term is supported in
[R,2R]
where
(ikv(r)-c)^{-1}
does not have singularities and all the factors of
r
are uniformly bounded, meaning that we can use the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm on
f
without loosing anything. Indeed, we see that when we bound
|\langle r^{2q}\mathcal{C}_{2}(f),(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle|
, the terms
with
\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}
and
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{2}f
show the same behavior in the bounds, because by

2.34
we have

(2.110)

\|r^{2q+1}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{2}(r^{-1}f)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},\quad\|\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

We will therefore only show the bounds for the terms with
\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}
. Defining
\Psi=(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi
, we can start bounding the terms with a factor
\beta^{-1}
. Integrating by parts, exploiting the support of
\chi_{2}^{\prime}
and recalling the definition of the

(2.111)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}r^{2q+1}{\partial}_{r}(\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}),\Psi\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\|r^{2q+1}\chi^{\prime}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([R,+\infty)]}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])}

(2.112)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{M^{-1/2}}{\beta}\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])},

Similarly,

(2.113)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}(k_{0}^{2}-1/4)\chi^{\prime}_{2}r^{2q-1}f,\Psi\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\frac{M^{-1/2}}{\beta}\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])},

and

(2.114)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\left(\frac{1}{2\alpha\beta}+\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha\beta}\right)r^{2q}(\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}),\Psi\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\frac{M^{-1/2}}{\beta}\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])}

The terms that do not contain factors of
\beta
can be bounded as follows

(2.115)

\displaystyle|\langle(ikv(r)-\lambda)r^{2q}(\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}),\Psi\rangle|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle r^{2q}|\chi^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}|,|\Psi|\rangle\lesssim\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([R,2R])}\|\chi^{\prime}_{2}\Psi\|_{L^{2}([R,2R])}

(2.116)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([R,2R])}\|r^{-1}\Psi\|_{L^{2}([R,2R])}

(2.117)

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{-1/2}\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])},

and since
(-ikv(r)-\bar{c})^{-1}
does not have singularities in
[R,2R]
and by the properties of
\tilde{T}_{\beta}
, we have

(2.118)

\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,2R])}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}.

Therefore, putting all the estimates together, we have proved that when
\beta\gg\varepsilon^{-2}
we have

(2.119)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{H^{1}_{r}}

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{-1/2}\|f\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}.

This enables us to conclude that

(2.120)

\|\mathcal{R}_{2}\|_{Z_{M}([R,+\infty))\rightarrow H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2}).

\diamond

Bound for
\mathbf{\mathcal{M}_{2,1}}
: we now want to bound the operator
\mathcal{R}_{1}:H^{1}_{r}([0,M])\to Z_{M}
.
Recalling that
\mathcal{R}_{1}=\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{1}

, so that

\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi=(S_{\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}I)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{1}(f)
. Similarly to what we did for

, we get

(2.121)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}\psi,\psi\rangle=\langle\mathcal{C}_{1}(f),(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle.

The strategy to bound the several terms is the same as before, but the different bound available on
\chi_{1}
from
Eq.

2.33
and the fact that

plays a key role in the estimates.
The term that requires a special attention is again

(2.122)

|\langle(ikv(r)-\lambda)(\chi^{\prime}_{1}f^{\prime}),(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi\rangle|.

Setting
\Psi=(-S_{\beta}-\bar{\lambda}_{\beta}I)^{-1}\psi
and integrating by parts, we see that

(2.123)

\displaystyle|\langle(ikv(r)-\lambda_{\beta})(\chi^{\prime}_{1}f^{\prime}),\Psi\rangle|\lesssim\langle|rv^{\prime}\chi^{\prime}_{1}f|,\frac{1}{r}|\Psi|\rangle|+\langle|(ikv-\lambda_{\beta})r\chi^{\prime\prime}_{1}f|,\frac{1}{r}|\Psi|\rangle|+\langle|(ikv(r)-\lambda)\chi^{\prime}_{1}f|,|\Psi^{\prime}|\rangle|.

Since

and

, we get

(2.124)

\displaystyle|\langle(ikv(r)-\lambda)\chi^{\prime}_{1}f^{\prime},\Psi\rangle|\lesssim M^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|\Psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\lesssim M^{-1}\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}}\|\psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Therefore, handling in a similar way the other terms in
Eq.

2.121
, we deduce that

(2.125)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,+\infty])}^{2}\lesssim M^{-1}\|f\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])}\|\psi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}([R,+\infty))}.

By the definition of the
Z_{M}
norm in
Eq.

2.24
, this enables us to conclude that

(2.126)

\|\mathcal{R}_{1}\|_{H^{1}_{r}([0,M])\rightarrow Z_{M}([R,+\infty))}=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2}).

∎

Step 5:
we are finally left with solving the problem

2.71
. This is equivalent to

\langle\varphi,\phi_{0}\rangle=0

where the inner product is in the space

. With this solvability condition we are able to determine the value of

. Indeed, from

2.70

(2.128)

\varphi=\left((I-\mathcal{M})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(\phi_{0})\\

\mathcal{M}_{2,1}(\phi_{0})\end{pmatrix}\right)_{1}.

By the bounds in
Proposition

2.9
, we get

(2.129)

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\phi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(\phi_{0}),\phi_{0}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}M^{4}+M^{-1})+M^{4}\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\bigg\langle(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi_{0}}{r^{2}}+\frac{\tilde{c}g^{\prime}(r)\phi_{0}}{r(v(r)-c)(v(r)-v(1))}\right),\phi_{0}\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}\big(T_{\beta}\big(ik\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}\phi_{0}\big),\phi_{0}\big\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}M^{4}+M^{-1})+M^{4}\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1).

\phi_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}
in

2.48
and

2.58

\phi_{0}=-(I-\mathcal{K})^{-1}\mathrm{L}_{k_{0}}^{-1}(\phi_{0}).

\langle\varphi,\phi_{0}\rangle=-\varepsilon(1+\tilde{c}\Gamma_{1}(c))+\Gamma_{2}(\beta)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}M^{4}+M^{-1})+M^{4}\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1)

where we denote

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(c)=\left\langle\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r(v(r)-c)(v(r)-v(1))}\phi_{0},\phi_{0}\right\rangle,\qquad\Gamma_{2}(\beta)=\left\langle T_{\beta}\big(ik\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}\phi_{0}\big),\phi_{0}\right\rangle.

2.49

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(c)=\,\int_{0}^{M}\frac{1}{v^{\prime}(1)(r-1)-\varepsilon\tilde{c}}A(r)|\phi_{0}|^{2}(r){\rm d}r+\int_{0}^{M}h_{\varepsilon}(r)A(r)|\phi_{0}|^{2}(r){\rm d}r.

2.10

\Gamma_{2}(\beta)=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\beta}(1)

\displaystyle f(\tilde{c})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon(\tilde{c}z_{0}+1),

\displaystyle g(\tilde{c},\varepsilon,\beta,M)

3.
Expansion of the resolvent of
S_{\beta}

In this section, we aim at proving
Proposition

2.6
; we refer to
Section

2.2
for the definition of the norms, and we simplify the notation by only using
L^{2},\dot{H}^{1},H^{1}_{r}
.

First of all, we express the resolvent via the Laplace transform of the semigroup associated to the skew-adjoint operator
S_{\beta}
, namely for all
z
such that
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
we have

(3.1)

(S_{\beta}-zI)^{-1}(w)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-z\tau}{\rm e}^{\tau S_{\beta}}(w){\rm d}\tau,

where
w
is a given function in
L^{2}
,
H^{1}
or
\dot{H}^{1}
. We also denote
f_{\beta}\coloneqq{\rm e}^{\tau S_{\beta}}(w)
, which solves

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}f_{\beta}+ikv(r)f_{\beta}-\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}(r{\partial}_{r}+\frac{1}{2})f_{\beta}=0\\
f_{\beta}|_{\tau=0}=w.\end{cases}

We define
f_{\infty}\coloneqq{\rm e}^{\tau S_{\infty}}(w)
where we recall that
S_{\infty}=-ikv(r)
. The goal of
Proposition

2.6
is to compare
f_{\beta}
and
f_{\infty}
. To this end, observe that
f_{\beta},f_{\infty}
are explicitly given by

(3.3)

f_{\beta}(r,\tau)={\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}-ik\int_{0}^{\tau}v\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r\big){\rm d}s}w({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r),\qquad f_{\infty}(r,\tau)={\rm e}^{-ik\tau v(r)}w(r).

Then, we can split the solution to
Eq.

3.2
as

(3.4)

f_{\beta}=f_{\infty}+(f_{\beta}-f_{\infty})

and rewrite

(3.5)

(S_{\beta}-zI)^{-1}(w)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-(ikv(r)+z)\tau}(w){\rm d}\tau-T_{z,\beta}(w)=-(S_{\infty}-zI)^{-1}(w)-T_{z,\beta}(w),

where we finally define the operator

(3.6)

T_{\beta}(w)\coloneqq T_{z,\beta}(w)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-z\tau}({f}_{\beta}-f_{\infty})(\tau){\rm d}\tau

Notice that
Eq.

3.5
is exactly the expansion stated in
Proposition

2.6
.
It thus remain to show the bounds we have claimed on
T_{\beta}
, which are a consequence of the pointwise in time estimates of the integrand which we collect below.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f_{\beta},f_{\infty}
be given as in
Eq.

3.3
with
w\in X\in\{L^{2},\dot{H}^{1},H^{1}_{r}\}
. Then, for any
T>0

(3.7)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\|(f_{\beta}-f_{\infty})(\tau)\|_{X}=0,\qquad\text{for all }0\leq\tau\leq T\text{ and }X\in\{L^{2},\,\dot{H}^{1},\,H^{1}_{r}\}.

Proof.

We denote

(3.8)

d_{\beta}[w](r,\tau)\coloneqq(f_{\beta}-f_{\infty})(r,\tau)={\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}-ik\int_{0}^{\tau}v\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r\big){\rm d}s}w({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r)-{\rm e}^{-ik\tau v(r)}w(r).

Then
d_{\beta}[w](r,\tau)\to 0
pointwise as
\beta\to\infty
and
|d_{\beta}[w](r,\tau)|\leq{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}}|w({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r)|+|w(r)|\coloneqq g_{\beta}(r,\tau)
. Since
\|g_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(dr)}\leq 2\|w\|_{L^{2}(dr)}
, by dominated convergence theorem we conclude that if
w\in L^{2}
we have
\|d_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
uniformly in
\tau
. To control a piece of the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm, observe that

(3.9)

r^{-1}d_{\beta}[w](r,\tau)=d_{\beta}[r^{-1}w](r,\tau)+{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}-ik\int_{0}^{\tau}v\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r\big){\rm d}s}\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}-1\big)(r^{-1}w)({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r)

We can then argue as before to conclude that for any fixed
T>0
, if
r^{-1}w\in L^{2}
then
\|r^{-1}d_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
for all
\tau\in[0,T]
. To control the derivative in
r
, notice that

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{r}d_{\beta}[w](r,\tau)=d_{\beta}[{\partial}_{r}w](r,\tau)+{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}-ik\int_{0}^{\tau}v\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r\big){\rm d}s}\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}-1\big)({\partial}_{r}w)({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r)

(3.11)

\displaystyle+{\rm e}^{ik\tau v(r)}ikr\tau v^{\prime}(r)(r^{-1}w)(r)-ik\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}(rv^{\prime})({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r){\rm d}s\Big){\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2\alpha\beta}-ik\int_{0}^{\tau}v\big({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau-s}{\alpha\beta}}r\big){\rm d}s}r^{-1}w({\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}r).

From the identity above, since
rv^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}
, it is not hard to deduce that for any fixed
T>0
, if
w\in\dot{H}^{1}
then
\|{\partial}_{r}d_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
for all
\tau\in[0,T]
.

For the bound of the
H^{1}_{r}
norm, we only have to control the
r{\partial}_{r}d_{\beta}
term, which readily follows by analogous arguments we have presented for
r^{-1}d_{\beta}
and
{\partial}_{r}d_{\beta}
and therefore we omit the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

One can easily prove the result in
Lemma

3.1
also for higher derivatives. However, one should not expect to obtain convergence in
\dot{H}^{\beta}
for instance. Indeed, if we take a number of derivatives comparable to
\beta
, then we lose a uniform control with respect to
\beta
. This was foreseen, due to the concentration caused by the
r{\partial}_{r}
term.

With
Lemma

3.1
at hand, we are finally ready to prove
Proposition

2.6
.

Proof of
Proposition

2.6
.

Let us consider
w\in X
with
X\in\{L^{2},\dot{H}^{1},H^{1}_{r}\}
and
\|w\|_{X}=1
. By the definition of
T_{\beta}
given in
3.6
, we have:

(3.12)

\|T_{\beta}w\|_{X}\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-{\rm Re}(z)\tau}\|(f_{\beta}-f_{\infty})(\tau)\|_{X}d\tau+\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-{\rm Re}(z)\tau}\big(\|f_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{X}+\|f_{\infty}(\tau)\|_{X}\big)d\tau

for some
T>1
to be specified later. For the second integral, we use the rough bound

(3.13)

\|f_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{X}+\|f_{\infty}(\tau)\|_{X}\lesssim(1+\tau){\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{\alpha\beta}}.

Since
T>1
and
{\rm Re}(z)>0
, we know that for all
\beta>\beta_{0}
with
\beta_{0}>2/(\alpha{\rm Re}(z))
we have

(3.14)

\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-{\rm Re}(z)\tau}\big(\|f_{\beta}(\tau)\|_{X}+\|f_{\infty}(\tau)\|_{X}\big)d\tau\lesssim\int_{T}^{\infty}\tau{\rm e}^{-({\rm Re}(z)-\frac{1}{\alpha\beta})\tau}{\rm d}\tau\leq\frac{C}{{\rm Re}(z)}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}(z)T}.

Hence

(3.15)

\|T_{\beta}w\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{{\rm Re}(z)}\sup_{\tau\in[0,T]}\|(f_{\beta}-f_{\infty})(\tau)\|_{X}+C\frac{1}{{\rm Re}(z)}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}(z)T}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we choose
T
large enough so that the second term is of size
\varepsilon/2
and use
Lemma

3.1
to conclude that there exists a
\beta_{0}
sufficiently large such that for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
we have
\|T_{\beta}\|_{X\to X}\leq\varepsilon
, thus proving the desired result.
∎
