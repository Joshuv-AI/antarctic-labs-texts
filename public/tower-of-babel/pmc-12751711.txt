Genetic diversity and structure of Mongolian gazelle (Procapra gutturosa) in fragmented habitats
Authors: Sijia Gao, Ding Zhou, Zhichao Zhou, Yiting Liu, Xinghu Qin, Dong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMongolian gazelle (Procapra gutturosa) populations have experienced significant range contraction and fragmentation over the past century due to habitat loss and barriers. This raises concerns about genetic diversity and connectivity. Understanding the genetic structure of these gazelles across their range is critical for effective conservation management.ResultWe used mitochondrial DNA sequences and microsatellite markers to assess genetic diversity and population structure in Mongolian gazelles from Mongolia and northern China. Gazelles in Mongolia exhibited high mitochondrial diversity and moderate microsatellite diversity overall. In contrast, gazelles in the Hulun Lake Reserve (HLH) had lower genetic diversity (e.g., reduced mitochondrial haplotype diversity) compared to gazelles on the contiguous Mongolian steppe. Genetic differentiation was low between gazelles along the China-Mongolia border and those in Mongolia. However, the HLH population was moderately differentiated from the Mongolian population and exhibited a distinct genetic structure. These results indicate that the HLH gazelles are relatively isolated and genetically distinct from other Mongolian gazelle populations.ConclusionFragmentation has led to detectable genetic subdivision in this once panmictic species. To maintain overall genetic health, it is essential to preserve landscape connectivity and gene flow between subpopulations. Conservation measures, such as protecting migratory corridors or introducing individuals from the larger Mongolian population into isolated groups like HLH, could help bolster genetic diversity and reduce inbreeding. Our findings underscore the need for transboundary collaboration to ensure the long-term viability of the Mongolian gazelle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12197-2.Keywords: Procapra gutturosa, Mitochondrial DNA, Microsatellite, Genetic diversity, Population structure, Habitat fragmentation

Introduction

Preserving genetic connectivity among animal populations across landscapes is vital to ensure the long-term survival of species [1–3], as it facilitates gene flow [4] and recolonization of unoccupied habitats [5]. However, habitat fragmentation caused by human activities disrupts the migratory routes of wildlife, leading to isolated populations [6–8]. In particular, the proliferation of international boundaries worldwide presents significant obstacles to the movement of wide-ranging, year-round nomadic wildlife [9]. A recent estimate showed that, in Eurasia, the total length of border fences already exceeds 30,000 km [10]. These artificial barriers induce a series of adverse ecological effects on wildlife, including habitat loss or reduced connectivity, limited dispersal opportunities, disrupted migratory pathways, shifts in spatial distribution patterns, and altered community structures [11–13]. For instance, human-made fences along the China-Mongolia border have been proven to be barriers to Mongolian gazelle (Procapra gutturosa), Asiatic wild ass (Equus hemionus), and wild camel (Camelus ferus), further fragmenting their habitat outside the species’ core range [14–16]. Such case studies highlight the critical importance of transboundary conservation strategies, which must integrate habitat preservation with the maintenance of genetic connectivity to safeguard the long-term persistence and evolutionary potential of wildlife populations [17–19].

The Mongolian gazelle is widely and continuously distributed across the eastern steppes of Mongolia and neighboring regions of China and Russia [20, 21]. It is one of the most abundant ungulates of the open plains and classified as Least Concern with an estimated global population of 2.14 million, but the species’ geographic range has contracted by approximately 76% over the past 50 years [22, 23]. Current populations are highly unevenly distributed: roughly 99% of Mongolian gazelles now occur in Mongolia, whereas peripheral groups have dwindled to about 1,000 in Inner Mongolia, China and 30,000 in Russia [22, 24]. More than 3 million wild individuals inhabited the grasslands of Inner Mongolia in China in the 1970s [25]; this precipitous decline in numbers has led to the species being classified as a National First-Class Protected Animal in China [26]. The Mongolian gazelle is a year-round nomadic animal, but due to the unpredictable vegetation dynamics and snowfall in winter, it usually makes long-distance migrations in winter [16]. The natural migration route of many gazelles leads south across the Chinese-Mongolian border to overwinter in Inner Mongolia and back to Mongolia the following spring [25]. However, since the 4,710 km Sino-Mongolian border was demarcated in 1962, China has erected a continuous barbed-wire fence along the boundary [27]. This border fence has led to the fragmentation of the habitat of the Mongolian gazelle, largely isolating the gazelle populations in China and Mongolia [16]. However, Mongolian gazelles still attempt to cross the border and migrate into China every winter [28, 29]. Observations indicate that gazelles begin crossing into China each October and typically return to Mongolia by late February, with most movements occurring between mid-November and mid-January [25]. The fence along the border is about 120 cm high, so gazelles are occasionally able to jump over or find gaps where snow drifts reduce its effective height [25].

Arxan City, located in the central Greater Khingan Mountains of Inner Mongolia (at the convergence of the Hulun Buir, Horqin, Xilingol, and Mongolian grasslands), borders Mongolia’s Dornod Province and has recently served as a key corridor for gazelles overwintering in China [30]. In recent winters, thousands of Mongolian gazelles have been observed crossing through this area into China [31]. In 2023/2024 winter, an estimated 4,500-6,000 Mongolian gazelles migrated across the Sino-Mongolian border into Inner Mongolia, China [32]. To date, molecular genetic studies of the Mongolian gazelle have mainly examined local genetic diversity within separate regions of its range [24, 33–35]. For example, Shi et al. studied the genetic diversity and genetic structure of two Mongolian gazelle populations in China: one from the China-Mongolia border region (BJ) and the other from the Hulun Lake National Nature Reserve (HLH) [24]. The presence of human-made structures, such as grassland fences, limits gene flow between the HLH population and external populations. In contrast, the BJ population of Mongolian gazelles occupies a contiguous habitat. The Mongolian gazelle populations of BJ and HLH have been isolated for 21 years [24]. As a result, the HLH and BJ populations exhibit genetic differentiation. However, no research has yet assessed the genetic connectivity or population structure between gazelle populations in Mongolia and China. This migration event provided an opportunity to collect samples from these transboundary migrants and directly compare genetic diversity and structure between Mongolian and Chinese populations.

In this study, we collected fecal samples from migrating Mongolian gazelles and analyzed their genetic diversity, population structure, and genetic differentiation using microsatellite markers, mitochondrial cytochrome b (Cytb) sequences, and control region (CR) sequences. By integrating our data and data from Shi et al. [24], we present a comprehensive assessment of genetic differentiation and connectivity across the border. Our findings advance our understanding of gazelle population genetics in a fragmented landscape and provide valuable guidance for designing effective transboundary conservation strategies.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Between January 17 and 21, 2024, we conducted non-invasive fecal sampling within the distribution range of migratory wild Mongolian gazelles in Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, China (46.6968°N, 119.9752°E) (Fig. 1). A total of 140 fecal samples were collected from the Hinggan League. Because Mongolian gazelles are continuously distributed across the eastern Mongolian grasslands, we infer that this migratory population originates from a single large population. In this study, we refer to it collectively as the Mongolian population (MNG). To ensure sample independence, we tracked gazelle herds by vehicle and used binoculars to identify fresh defecation events. Samples were collected under strict spatiotemporal separation protocols to ensure that each fecal sample represented a different individual.

The collected samples were cryopreserved immediately on dry ice and subsequently transported to Beijing Forestry University’s laboratory for storage at −80 °C. For DNA extraction, the samples were processed using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s specifications. The extracted genomic DNA was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C in dedicated freezers until required for downstream PCR analysis.

Mitochondrial DNA amplification and data analysis

Cytb gene sequences were amplified using the primers F-CCCATAGATAGGTGAAGGT and R-CAGGGAATAGTTTAAGCAG [24]. Similarly, CR sequences were amplified with primers L15376-CACTATCAACACCCAAAGCTGAAG and H16498-ATGGCCCTGAAGAAAGAACAAGAT [35]. PCR reactions were conducted in a Veriti thermal cycler under optimized conditions. For the Cytb gene, the protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 90 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The CR amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Each PCR reaction was performed in a 25 µL reaction volume, which comprised 12.5 µL of 2×Taq PCR Master Mix (Dye), 2.0 µL of DNA template, 1.0 µL of each primer (primer concentration: 10 µM), and 8.5 µL of sterile distilled water. PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, and successfully amplified fragments were sent to the Beijing Liuhe BGI Co., Ltd. for bidirectional Sanger sequencing.

Sequences were assembled using SeqMan ver. 6.1 [36] in DNASTAR Lasergene ver. 7.1 [37], and then edited and aligned using MUSCLE implemented in MEGA ver. 7 [38]. Summary genetic diversity statistics were calculated for all alignments using DnaSP ver. 6.0 [39], including the number of individuals (N), number of haplotypes (H), haplotype diversity (h), nucleotide diversity (π), and the mean number of pairwise differences (k) within each group.

Microsatellite genotyping and data analysis

Eight microsatellite loci (TGLA68, IOBT395, PZE114, OArFCB304, SPS115, MNS72, BM1341, and BM066) were employed as described by Shi et al. (2023) [24]. The primers were labeled with FAM, HEX, or ROX fluorescent tags. PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 µL, comprising 12.5 µL of 2× Taq PCR Master Mix (with dye), 2.0 µL of DNA template, 1.0 µL of each primer (primer concentration: 10 µM), and 8.5 µL of sterile distilled water. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 50-63 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 35 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Each locus was amplified in a separate, single‑plex reaction. The PCR products were sent to a biotech company for sequencing and genotyping.

Given the limited fecal DNA remaining after mitochondrial assays, each microsatellite locus underwent duplicate genotyping, with only alleles concordant across both replicates retained to minimize null alleles and PCR artifacts. All eight microsatellite loci were successfully amplified in 107 samples. All 107 fecal‑derived multi-locus genotypes were screened for duplicates using the “Raw Data-Check for Dups” function in GenAlEx 6.5 [40], and no identical profiles were detected.

The null alleles were checked using Micro-Checker ver2.2.3 [41]. Null alleles were found at PZE114 and SPS115, so these two loci were removed. All subsequent analyses were conducted using six microsatellite loci (TGLA68, IOBT395, OArFCB304, MNS72, BM1341, and BM066). To assess genetic diversity among populations, the number of alleles (Na), number of effective alleles (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium were calculated using GenAlEx 6.5 [40]. The allele richness was implemented in the hierfstat package [42] in R ver. 4.3.3.

Data acquisition of Cytb, microsatellites and analysis

To investigate the population structure and genetic differentiation of the Mongolian gazelle between Mongolia and China, we included two populations from China: the China-Mongolia border region (BJ) and the Hulun Lake National Nature Reserve (HLH) population, as reported by Shi et al. (2023) [24]. We collected three datasets from Shi et al. (2023): (1) 17 Cytb sequences from HLH (GenBank: OP204770-OP204786); (2) 17 Cytb sequences from BJ (GenBank: OP204753-OP204769); and (3) the original genotype data of the same six microsatellites loci (27 from HLH and 27 from BJ). We merged Cytb sequences from Mongolia and China to produce a dataset of 154 sequences, which were then aligned using MUSCLE implemented in MEGA ver. 7 [38]. Distinct Cytb haplotypes between Mongolia and China were identified with DnaSP v6.0 [39], and a median‑joining network of these haplotypes was subsequently constructed using PopART v1.7 [43]. Then, we merged microsatellite data from Mongolia and China to produce a dataset of 161 individuals. The microsatellite genetic structure was analyzed using STRUCTURE ver. 2.3.4 [44]. In STRUCTURE, models of all combinations of the ancestry models and the allele frequency models were tested, and an admixture ancestry model with uncorrelated frequency was selected, with a burn-in period of 10,000 and 100,000 MCMC repetitions after burn-in. The number of population clusters (K) was set from one to eight, and performed 10 operations on each K value. The simulated likelihood of these runs was summarized using CLUMPAK with STRUCTURE HARVESTER to infer the best-fit number of population clusters [45]. The population genetic structure was determined using discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) implemented in the adegenet package [46, 47] in R ver. 4.3.3. Pairwise genetic differences (Fst) between populations were then estimated in Arlequin 3.5 [48].

Results

Mitochondrial genetic diversity

The mitochondrial genetic diversity analysis revealed that the 1,140 bp Cytb gene, examined in 120 individuals, exhibited 61 haplotypes with haplotype diversity (h) of 0.980, nucleotide diversity (π) of 0.00876, and average nucleotide differences (k) of 9.981. In contrast, analysis of the 737 bp CR in 137 individuals identified 97 haplotypes, yielding haplotype diversity of 0.994, nucleotide diversity of 0.04048, and average nucleotide differences of 26.882 (Table 2 ). Notably, the CR displayed a relatively high frequency of insertions and deletions, including a haplotype characterized by a distinct 77-bp insertion.

Microsatellite genetic diversity

The Mongolian (MNG) gazelle population (n = 107) exhibited moderate genetic diversity across loci. The number of alleles per locus (Na) ranged from 4 to 18 (11.500 ± 1.996), and the effective number of alleles (Ne) ranged from 1.258 to 10.096 (5.882 ± 1.326). Observed heterozygosity (Ho) ranged from 0.224 to 0.925 (0.721 ± 0.107), and expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.225 to 0.901 (0.726 ± 0.108). Allelic richness per locus (AR) ranged from 3.000 to 13.000, mirroring the Na values, while the information index (I) ranged from 0.438 to 2.499 (1.798 ± 0.307). None of the six loci deviated from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) (Table 3).

Population structure and differentiation

In total, the analysis incorporated 82 distinct Cytb haplotypes of the Mongolian gazelle populations in China and Mongolia. Median-joining network analysis revealed no clear genetic clustering, with 6 haplotypes shared between BJ and MNG gazelle populations, and one haplotype shared between HLH and MNG gazelle populations (Fig. 2).

The Bayesian clustering analysis of microsatellite genotypes revealed clear genetic structure among the Mongolian gazelle populations. We found a pronounced peak at K = 2 in the STRUCTURE results (Fig. 3a), indicating that two genetic clusters best explain the data. At K = 2, individuals were partitioned into two main clusters, largely corresponding to the HLH population and the combined MNG and BJ populations. In particular, nearly all HLH individuals were overwhelmingly assigned to one cluster, whereas most MNG individuals belonged almost exclusively to the other cluster. The BJ population exhibited admixed ancestry, with each individual’s genome comprising a mixture of the two cluster components (Fig. 3b). This result suggests that the isolated HLH population forms a genetically distinct group, while the geographically contiguous BJ and MNG populations share greater genetic affinity. At K = 3 and K = 4, finer substructure emerged: the MNG unit remains cohesive with slight internal differentiation, and the HLH population has its own unique genetic components, while BJ continues to bridge the two main clusters. Increasing the number of clusters to 3 or 4, however, did not alter the primary pattern of differentiation among populations.

The DAPC analysis (Fig. 4) revealed a clear separation between the populations, with the first discriminant function (DA1) explaining 87.9% of the variance and the second discriminant function (DA2) accounting for 12.1%. The MNG population (red) was distinct from the BJ (orange) and HLH (green) populations, with the former showing a more clustered distribution. The BJ and HLH populations exhibited some overlap, but they were still relatively well-separated, as indicated by the ellipses surrounding each group. These findings suggest significant genetic differentiation between the MNG population and the other two populations, with a moderate degree of overlap between BJ and HLH populations. Consistent with this, pairwise Fst was lowest between MNG and BJ (0.04297), indicating minimal differentiation, while comparisons involving HLH are much higher (0.14958 and 0.13924), indicating substantial divergence (Table 4). These results indicate that HLH is the most genetically distinct population, while MNG and BJ are relatively closely related.

Discussion

Mitochondrial genetic diversity and structure

The migratory Mongolian gazelle population exhibits exceptionally high mitochondrial genetic diversity. We found that haplotype diversity approached 1.0 (0.98–0.99) and substantial nucleotide diversity was observed in both the Cytb gene (0.00876) and the control region (0.04048). These values are higher than those reported for BJ and HLH populations [24]. These values suggest a large, long-term effective population size [22], along with deep maternal lineages. Previous studies have consistently reported high mtDNA diversity in Mongolian gazelles, despite past range contractions, suggesting that the species has retained extensive genetic variation [33, 35]. Such high diversity can arise from a historically large, interconnected population and possibly admixture of lineages, both of which appear to be the case for Mongolian gazelles.

Notably, our Cytb haplotype network revealed no clear phylogeographic clustering among the three populations. Of the 82 distinct haplotypes identified, only 6 haplotypes were shared between the BJ and MNG gazelle populations, and one haplotype was shared between the HLH and MNG gazelle populations. The presence of shared haplotypes across the border indicates historical or ongoing gene flow, while the many unique haplotypes suggest that each region also harbors private maternal lineages. However, the lack of geographically distinct clades and the intermingling of haplotypes from both countries in the network means that there is no pronounced phylogeographic structure. In other words, we do not observe deep splits between Chinese and Mongolian lineages, but rather a homogenous haplotype assemblage. This pattern implies that the Chinese and Mongolian gazelle groups have not been isolated long enough to sort into separate mitochondrial lineages, or that sufficient movement has occurred to maintain admixture.

Two major factors are likely driving this weak mtDNA structure. First, the Mongolian gazelle’s nomadic lifestyle, driven by highly unpredictable precipitation, vegetation growth, and snowfall on the steppe, promotes extensive genetic mixing [16, 49]. Unlike strictly migratory ungulates, such as wildebeests and zebras on the African savannah [50, 51] or Tibetan antelopes on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [52], follow fixed routes, whereas gazelles wander widely and opportunistically in search of ephemeral resources [53]. An individual gazelle can roam over 32,000 km² in a single year, and herd sizes can swell to hundreds of thousands during long‑distance movements [54, 55]. These wide-ranging, climate-driven movements vary dramatically from year to year: in some years, harsh winter weather or localized droughts push gazelles en masse into China or Russia, whereas in other years favorable conditions keep them primarily in Mongolia [56]. By constantly tracking these shifting “green waves” of resource abundance, nomadic gazelles end up spreading maternal lineages across vast areas instead of keeping them localized. As a result, mitochondrial haplotypes are continually redistributed and intermingled, preventing the formation of distinct regional genetic clades and leaving only an erratic phylogeographic signature rather than stable genetic patterns. Second, there has likely been substantial historical gene flow across the steppe. For millennia, the eastern Mongolian steppe formed one continuous expanse of habitat with no physical barriers, enabling unrestricted dispersal of gazelles [57]. The genetic legacy of this long‑term connectivity persists: high haplotype diversity and a lack of deep mitochondrial divergence reflect a historically panmictic (well‑mixed) metapopulation. Even after partial habitat fragmentation in recent decades, not enough time has passed for mitochondrial lineages to sort into separate regional clades. Thus, maternal lineages remain mixed between Mongolia and China, further blurring any country-specific genetic structure.

Microsatellite genetic structure

There is significant differentiation among geographic populations. Bayesian clustering analysis revealed that the most likely number of genetic clusters is K = 2. At K = 2, the gazelles roughly split into a Chinese cluster vs. a Mongolian cluster, but with an important twist: individuals from the BJ population show a roughly 50/50 admixture of the two clusters. In contrast, HLH individuals almost entirely assign to the “Chinese” cluster, while MNG individuals assign to the “Mongolian” cluster. This pattern suggests BJ is an intermediate or mixed population, whereas HLH is genetically more distinct. DAPC analysis corroborated these findings: the HLH population forms a discrete cluster at one end of the discriminant axis, with virtually no overlap with the others, while BJ lies in an intermediate position, separated from both HLH and the MNG group. The overall picture is that the HLH gazelles are genetically differentiated, the MNG gazelles form a cohesive group, and the BJ gazelles share alleles with both sides. This makes intuitive sense geographically: HLH is a far eastern population in Inner Mongolia, China, that has been relatively isolated, whereas MNG represent the large populations in Mongolian steppe with continuous gene flow between them. BJ population appears to be a genetic bridge between the two countries’ populations. The analysis of pairwise Fst values revealed that the highest Fst (0.14958) was observed between the MNG and HLH populations, whereas the lowest (0.04297) was observed between MNG and BJ; the Fst between BJ and HLH was 0.13924, intermediate between these values. These results indicate that the HLH population exhibits the greatest degree of genetic differentiation relative to the other populations, whereas the genetic differentiation between MNG and BJ is comparatively low, suggesting substantial gene flow between these populations.

Ecologically, these patterns imply ongoing or very recent gene flow across the China–Mongolia border, at least in some locations. The intermediate, admixed BJ group is especially telling, as its mixed ancestry indicates that it likely contains migrants from both sides. It is known that the border fence, although formidable, is not completely impermeable, there are occasional gaps, broken sections, or gates left open that wildlife can exploit [58]. In analogous situations, partial connectivity has been documented. For instance, railway lines in Mongolia were initially feared to be absolute barriers, yet genetic studies showed gazelles on either side of the Trans-Mongolian Railway remained undifferentiated, likely due to animals crossing via underpasses or fence breaks [33]. This demonstrates that what appears to be a hard barrier can in fact be a semi-permeable barrier, allowing limited dispersal. Our findings of modest Fst and an admixed border population align with the idea of the fence as a semi-permeable barrier. It likely impedes most movements but not all, or it has been in place for too short a time (~ 60 years, only ~ 13–15 gazelle generations) [59] for complete genetic isolation to manifest. The relatively low divergence of BJ from Mongolian herds strongly indicates recent connectivity, whether through occasional fence crossings or through continuous movement before the fence was erected.

Conclusion

This study highlights clear genetic structuring and connectivity issues of Mongolian gazelle populations across China and Mongolia. In particular, the Hulun Lake population (HLH) in Inner Mongolia, China showed low mitochondrial genetic diversity, exhibiting a distinct genetic composition compared to gazelles from the populations in Mongolia. This finding indicates that the HLH group is isolated, likely due to its smaller size and fragmented habitat. The Mongolian gazelle populations in Mongolia exhibited low Fst relative to the China-Mongolia border population in China (BJ) but showed moderate genetic differentiation from the more distant HLH region, suggesting that Fst increases with greater geographic distance between populations.

From a conservation perspective, maintaining or restoring connectivity among Mongolian gazelle populations is paramount. The low genetic diversity and distinct genetic composition in HLH raise concerns about inbreeding and long-term viability of this isolated group. Conservation efforts should therefore prioritize facilitating gene exchange for the HLH population, such as by creating safe crossing points or translocating individuals from the genetically richer Mongolian herds, to bolster its genetic diversity. More broadly, ensuring that gazelles can move freely across their grassland range will preserve genetic diversity and the species’ capacity to adapt. Recent assessments emphasize the urgent need to preserve landscape connectivity for Mongolian gazelles to safeguard against population collapse.

Finally, the long-term survival of this migratory species will depend on collaborative, transboundary conservation measures. Mongolia harbors the vast majority of Mongolian gazelles, so a unified approach between Mongolia and China is essential. Stakeholders in all range countries must collaborate to reduce barriers and coordinate management strategies. Such collaborative efforts across borders are critical for preserving the gazelle’s nomadic way of life and ensuring the species’ future in the grassland ecosystem.
