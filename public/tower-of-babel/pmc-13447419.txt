Two-stage ROM: a hybrid reduced order model for offline and online efficiency
Authors: Rahul Dhopeshwar, Harshit Bansal, Karen Veroy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysics-based reduced-order models are one of the key enablers for improving the predictive capabilities of digital twins, offering a balance between computational efficiency and physical fidelity. However, their efficiency for nonlinear problems remains a challenge because evaluating nonlinear operators in reduced dimensions can be very costly, and offline construction often requires many high-fidelity simulations. In this paper, we address this issue with a reduced-order modeling strategy for parametrized nonlinear partial differential equations that is efficient both online and offline. Offline efficiency is achieved using a greedy algorithm for reduced basis construction combined with a quasi-optimal hyper-reduction procedure called Reduced Basis Empirical Quadrature Procedure (RBEQP) [1]. Then, Gaussian process regression is integrated into the reduced-order model to learn the reduced coefficients and enhance online efficiency. Additionally, we evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method on two model problems: a steady-state heat conduction problem with a nonlinear, temperature-dependent conductivity, and a neo-Hookean hyperelastic beam under self-weight load.Keywords: Physics-based reduced-order models, Non-linear model order reduction, Hyper-reduction, Greedy reduced basis method, Gaussian process regression

Introduction

Digital twins acting as virtual replicas of physical systems have emerged as an important technological tool in various industries. They enable real-time monitoring, simulation-based decision-making , and optimization of complex physical systems [2–4]. These digital twins can be built using data-driven or model-driven approaches [5]. Data-driven approaches are effective in data-rich scenarios and leverage machine learning or statistical methods on large datasets to replicate system behavior [6, 7]. In contrast, model-driven approaches [8, 9] rely on physics-based models to ensure consistency, especially in data-poor scenarios. In this work, we address data-poor scenarios and focus on a model-centric approach, developing model-driven surrogate models.

Model-centric digital twins usually involve the integration of physics-based models with data. A pivotal aspect of this integration involves solving data assimilation and inverse problems [10, 11]. These approaches improve the digital twins by enabling the alignment of computational predictions with real-world measurements. Through this, otherwise inaccessible system parameters can be estimated, which enhances the accuracy and predictive capability of digital twins.

The solution of data assimilation and inverse problems requires the computation of the full-order forward model for numerous values of the parameter. Hence, despite considerable advancement in digital twin technologies, the complexity of these full-order simulations and their high computational cost pose significant bottlenecks for their scalability, particularly in real-time applications [12]. Reduced-order modeling addresses this hurdle by offering a cheap-to-compute surrogate model that approximates the full-order simulations within a controlled error threshold. These surrogate models are typically trained offline using high-fidelity simulations of parametrized partial differential equations in the offline phase. In the online phase, they accelerate otherwise expensive multi-query simulations of parametrized partial differential equations. This enables faster simulations and real-time updates, which are crucial for the practical deployment of digital twins in structural health monitoring, autonomous manufacturing, and many other applications. This is especially useful in scenarios requiring fast decision-making, such as control systems or when operating with limited computational resources [13–15]. Reduced order models (ROMs) also provide manageable data flows and computational loads for the integration of multiple digital twins into a broader digital ecosystem [3]. They are an important tool in making digital twins scalable, responsive, and informative in real-world scenarios.

Recent advancements in reduced order modeling have seen the emergence of sophisticated techniques like neural operators [16, 17], variational autoencoders (VAEs) [18–20] and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)[21–23]. These nonlinear manifold techniques effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships and offer flexibility in model representation. However, they require large, high-quality datasets and are therefore computationally expensive to train [24]. Moreover, they lack interpretability and trustworthiness. In contrast, projection-based reduced-order modeling approaches have been shown to perform well for problems involving nonlinear heat conduction, and structural deformation [25–27]. They are particularly effective for problems whose solutions can be well approximated with a low-dimensional linear subspace characterized by a fast-decaying Kolmogorov N-width [28]. Such problems are prevalent in many practical applications, such as heat transfer and structural mechanics. Hence, in this work, we focus on these projection-based techniques. These methods simplify high-dimensional systems by projecting them onto a lower-dimensional linear subspace that preserves the essential physical characteristics of the original system.

Most linear projection-based reduced order modeling techniques rely on reduced bases constructed through methods such as Proper Orthogonal Decomposition, and the greedy reduced basis method [29], where a set of basis functions is typically derived from snapshots of full-order solutions. However, in the context of nonlinear problems, the evaluation of nonlinear terms in the reduced problem (online phase) is still computationally expensive since they require operations in the high-dimensional (e.g., finite element) space [30]. To address this issue, hyper-reduction techniques [31–33] can be used to approximate the nonlinear terms with a smaller set of interpolation points, thus avoiding the need for full-order computations. Concurrently, regression-based reduced-order models provide an alternative approach, particularly effective for nonlinear problems. These techniques use machine learning methods, such as neural networks [34, 35] or Gaussian process [36, 37], to learn the mapping from the parameter to the reduced coefficients obtained by projecting the solution space onto the reduced basis.

Hyper-reduction techniques have been proven to be effective in numerous practical applications [38, 39]. They ensure that the efficiency gained from projection is maintained even in the presence of nonlinearities by employing rapidly computable approximations of the nonlinear terms. However, most hyper-reduction techniques rely on Newton solvers to solve the resulting reduced problem. Thus, the approximate nonlinearities must be evaluated at every Newton iteration. Moreover, the number of interpolation points required for these approximations is usually much greater than the dimension of the reduced problem. This results in a relatively inefficient online phase. On the other hand, regression-based methods have found success in constructing online efficient reduced-order models for various applications [40, 41]. These regression models can capture nonlinear relationships and are typically faster to compute in the online phase. However, the success of regression-based ROMs depends on the quality and quantity of training data, as well as the ability to generalize to unseen scenarios.

A comparison study [42] between hyper-reduction techniques and regression-based methods has shown that both techniques have their bottlenecks. On one hand, regression-based techniques require numerous full-order solutions in the offline phase. On the other hand, while hyper-reduction techniques are offline-efficient, requiring a smaller number of full-order solves, they are online-inefficient compared to regression-based methods. To this end, methods such as DeepHyROMNet [43], and ML-boosted hyper-reduction [44] have been proposed to leverage the advantages of both hyper-reduction and regression-based techniques. These methods use machine learning techniques to learn the nonlinear components of the reduced-order model, accelerating the online phase of hyper-reduction. In this work, we explore and investigate an alternative approach to these techniques by employing hyper-reduction to accelerate the offline computations of regression-based models.

We introduce a hybrid offline-online efficient reduced-order modeling strategy for parametrized nonlinear partial differential equations. Our approach involves a two-stage reduction process. Initially, in the first stage, we construct a reduced basis along with an intermediate reduced order model employing the Reduced Basis Empirical Quadrature Procedure (RBEQP) [1] - a hyper-reduction technique based on an empirical quadrature rule. Subsequently, in the second stage, we train Gaussian process regressors for the coefficients of the reduced basis to construct an online efficient reduced-order model. To generate the extensive dataset required for this training, we leverage the intermediate reduced-order model. This strategy enhances offline efficiency by reducing the number of full-order solves needed to construct the Gaussian process model. Moreover, the Gaussian process model is faster to evaluate in the online phase compared to the hyper-reduction counterpart, resulting in an online-efficient ROM. Mathematical preliminaries

In this section, we discuss the mathematical preliminaries for the reduced-order modeling framework used in this work. In particular, we discuss the mathematical setting in which projection-based reduced-order models operate and two classical reduced-basis construction techniques that form the basis for the methodology discussed in section “Reduced order modeling for nonlinear problems”.

Typically, an exact analytical solution for the problem is unknown and hence we resort to finding an approximate solution (uh(μ)∈Vh) using numerical techniques like the finite element method. Such finite element approximation requires a large number of degrees of freedom (Nh) for an accurate solution which makes it computationally expensive in a multi-query context motivating the need for reduced order modeling.

Projection-based reduced order modeling techniques rely on the idea that the solution manifold denoted by Mh={uh(μ):μ∈P}⊂Vh can be approximately represented by a low-dimensional basis called reduced basis. The dimension of this reduced basis (N) is much lower than the finite element dimension, i.e., N≪Nh. The quality of this approximate solution is determined not only by the accuracy of the coefficients but also by how well the chosen reduced basis approximates the solution manifold.

Reduced basis construction

Many efficient strategies exist for this reduced basis generation. Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) and greedy algorithms are among the widely used techniques for reduced basis construction.

Proper orthogonal decomposition

To construct the reduced basis using POD algorithm, a collection of full-order solution snapshots at a discrete parameter set is initially used to assemble the snapshot matrix S=[u(μ(1))|u(μ(2))|....|u(μ(Npod))]. Subsequently, we utilize the singular value decomposition of the matrix X1/2S=UΣZT, where X is an inner product matrix, U and Z are orthogonal matrices, and Σ is a diagonal matrix containing the positive singular values in the decreasing order. The Schmidt-Eckart-Young theorem ensures that the truncation of X-1/2U to the first N columns results in the minimum projection error amongst all possible N-dimensional basis, i.e., the first N columns of X-1/2U denoted by V=argminW∈RNh×N∑i=1p||u(μ(i))-WWTXu(μ(i))||X2 [29]. Therefore, columns V form the reduced basis constructed by the POD algorithm.

Greedy selection

The greedy algorithm provides an alternative approach for constructing the reduced basis. In this approach, the reduced basis is built iteratively by selecting full order solutions corresponding to parameter values that are worst approximated by the current reduced basis. To inform this selection, the algorithm relies on the existence of an error estimator Δn(μ) that is indicative of the error the reduced solution would have at parameter μ for the n-dimensional reduced basis. This estimator is typically much cheaper to compute compared to the full-order solution. At each iteration of the greedy algorithm, the parameter value μn+1 is chosen within a discrete training set Θtrain that maximizes the error estimator, i.e., μn+1=argmaxμ∈ΘtrainΔn(μ). The full order solution at μn+1 is then computed and added to the reduced basis. The newly added basis is orthonormalized using the Gram-Schmidt procedure to ensure the orthogonality of the basis vectors and improve numerical stability.

The greedy algorithm often achieves a desired accuracy with significantly fewer full-order solves than the POD algorithm. Thus, making it a more efficient choice in many reduced-order modeling scenarios where a cheap error estimator is available.

Reduced basis empirical quadrature procedure

Although traditional ROM techniques reduce the number of degrees of freedom by projecting the full-order model onto a low-dimensional basis, they can still encounter high computational costs in evaluating nonlinear terms while assembling the residual and Jacobian for new parameter values. Hyper-reduction techniques aim to reduce not only the dimension of the solution space but also the cost of evaluating such nonlinear operators. The Reduced Basis Empirical Quadrature Procedure (RBEQP) [1] is such a hyper-reduction technique designed to enhance the efficiency of ROMs for nonlinear problems. RBEQP accelerates the assembly cost in the online phase by using an empirical integration scheme instead of the full-order quadrature rule in Equation 3.

During the offline phase, RBEQP constructs a ROM by generating both a low-dimensional reduced basis and an efficient integration scheme, also called the empirical quadrature procedure (EQP). The reduced basis is built using a greedy algorithm, which iteratively enriches the basis by adding solutions evaluated at parameter values that maximize a residual-based error estimator to the basis. This error estimator provides an efficient surrogate for the model error and identifies where the current basis performs poorly in the parameter space and needs refinement. The details of the error estimator used in this work will be discussed in Section “Two-stage reduced order model”. This approach leads to an offline-efficient model construction as it focuses on full-order solution evaluations only at parameter values selected during the greedy algorithm.

Furthermore, RBEQP seeks to construct a low-cost problem-specific EQP, which approximates the integrals that arise in the ROM assembly. Instead of relying on a costly truth quadrature rule (Equation 3), the method identifies a sparse set of quadrature points and associated weights that maintain accuracy while significantly reducing computational cost. These quadrature rules are computed using a training set of solution states, which consists of approximate reduced-order or high-fidelity solutions evaluated at a discrete set of training parameter values. During each iteration of the greedy algorithm, the reduced basis, the solution state training set, and the EQP are updated simultaneously. That is, after each new parameter is selected and the corresponding full-order solution is computed, this snapshot is used to enrich both the basis and solution state training set. The EQP is then computed using the updated solution state training set and the current reduced basis, ensuring that the quadrature rule is tailored to the most relevant features of the reduced solution manifold. This joint update process allows the quadrature construction to adapt alongside the evolving ROM, leading to improved accuracy and efficiency. The specific procedure for computing the empirical quadrature points and weights will be detailed later in this section.

In the online stage, the method leverages this precomputed quadrature rule to enable a fast and accurate evaluation of the integrals involved in the assembly of the reduced problem for new parameter instances. Then, the EQP can be obtained by eliminating the quadrature points with zero weights.

Gaussian process regression

Gaussian process regression (GPR) is a probabilistic regression framework built upon the assumption of a Gaussian process prior, defined by its mean and covariance functions. Typically, the mean function is chosen as a constant at the arithmetic mean of the training dataset. The covariance function, often referred to as the kernel, encapsulates the intended structure within the Gaussian process model. The kernel function expresses the similarity or closeness of two points in the parameter space. It is based on the assumption that input points that are close to each other result in a similar target output. [46] offers guidelines on how to chose a kernel function and the structure of some of the most commonly used kernel functions.

Second stage

In the second stage of the offline phase, we use this RBEQP reduced-order model to generate the dataset of RB coefficients corresponding to all parameter values in a much larger training set (PtrGPR) also generated using scrambled Sobol sequences [48]. We use this dataset to train a Gaussian process regression model.

In practice, this means that the 2sROM framework is useful mainly for problems that have a fast decaying Kolmogorov N-width (i.e., problems that are representable with a low-dimensional reduced basis) or have a very high computational cost to compute full-order snapshots.

Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a reduced order modeling framework consisting of a two-stage reduced order model (2sROM) that effectively balances offline and online computational efficiency without compromising accuracy. We have demonstrated the utility of our approach on two nonlinear model problems. We compare the errors, offline computational time, and online time versus known methods such as PODGPR [36] and RBEQP [1]. The results highlight that 2sROM significantly reduces overall computational cost in both offline and online phases while maintaining accuracy. For instance, to achieve an average error below 3% for the nonlinear conduction problem, 2sROM requires about 4 times less offline time to train compared to PODGPR and about 7 times faster in the online phase than RBEQP. Similar results were obtained for the hyperelasticity problem as well.

This speedup is particularly useful in the acceleration of inverse problems, where numerous simulations at different parameter values are required. In this context, the reduction in both the offline computational time and online prediction time is critical. The proposed framework can be easily adapted for various applications beyond the demonstrated cases. In the future, we intend to extend this strategy for more complex and time-dependent problems. We see potential in using such a methodology for accelerating parameter estimation and predictive process control in autonomous manufacturing process such as sintering.
