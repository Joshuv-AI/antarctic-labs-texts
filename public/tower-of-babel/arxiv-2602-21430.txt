Markovian Embeddings of Non-Markovian Open System Dynamics
Meng Xu, Jürgen T. Stockburger, Joachim Ankerhold

Abstract
Embedding non-Markovian open quantum dynamics into an enlarged Markovian space offers a powerful route to nonperturbative simulations, where the dynamics of the extended space can be governed by multiple distinct Markovian equations. We show that these distinct embeddings arise from different unravelings of Gaussian bath self-energies, generating a family of deterministic, time-local equations for the extended system. Using the Brownian-oscillator spectral density as an illustrative example, we clarify the relationships among existing approaches, including the Hierarchical Equations of Motion (HEOM) and the Lindblad–pseudomode formalism, and demonstrate how this framework enables numerically stable and efficient simulations. This work provides both a transparent theoretical foundation for embedding techniques and a flexible platform for developing new methods to simulate non-Markovian quantum dynamics.

To clarify and explicitly elucidate the connections among these seemingly distinct formulations,

This framework serves a dual purpose: By demonstrating how these various non-Markovian approaches emerge from the QD-MESS formulation, it provides a unified picture of non-Markovian approaches with extended state space and thus

Within this formulation, we show that the HEOM and the Lindblad–pseudomode approach emerge naturally from different choices of Green’s function structures. Furthermore, we demonstrate that these seemingly distinct formulations are related through linear transformations in the auxiliary-mode path space, corresponding precisely to Bogoliubov transformations. The associated boundary conditions determine both the preparation of the initial auxiliary states and the procedure for obtaining the reduced density operator.

II
Open Quantum System Modeling

We consider an open quantum system coupled linearly to an external environment modeled as a complex-valued Gaussian noise process. The noise effects are completely characterized by its two-point correlation function. The Hamiltonian describing the noise driven system takes the form

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{s}+\hat{S}\hat{X}_{b}(t)\,,

(1)

where
\hat{H}_{s}
is the bare system Hamiltonian,
\hat{S}
is the system coupling operator, and
\hat{X}_{b}(t)
denotes time-dependent operator-valued Gaussian noise. Its two-point correlation function (with
\hbar=k_{B}=1
) is

\displaystyle C(t)=\left\langle\hat{X}_{b}(t)\hat{X}_{b}^{\dagger}(0)\right\rangle_{b}.

(2)

More complicated couplings can be represented as sums of separable terms
12
. Importantly, the operator
\hat{X}_{b}(t)
is not assumed to arise from a specific bath model in Hilbert or Liouville space; instead, it is characterized solely by its statistical properties in a stationary state. For concreteness, the Gaussian environment can be realized in Hilbert space as an infinite collection of harmonic oscillators
35
;
17
.

The Gaussian noise is equivalently characterized by its power spectrum,

\displaystyle S_{\beta}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\,C(t)\,.

(3)

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem
33
;
66
relates the positive and negative frequency components of
S_{\beta}(\omega)
, which can be expressed as

S_{\beta}(\omega)=\frac{J(\omega)}{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}\,.

(4)

Here,
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature, and
J(\omega)
is the antisymmetric spectral density, characterized by a finite bandwidth with cutoff frequency
\omega_{c}
.

We assume that, for a given realization of the stochastic noise

X_{b}
, the system density operator evolves according to the
stochastic Liouville equation

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t,X_{b})=-i[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}(t,X_{b})]\equiv-i\,\mathcal{L}(t)\,\hat{\rho}(t,X_{b}),

(5)

where
\mathcal{L}(t)
denotes the Liouvillian associated with the
noise-dependent Hamiltonian.

For an initially uncorrelated system–noise state, the physical reduced
density operator is obtained by averaging over all noise realizations,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\bigl\langle\hat{\rho}(t,X_{b})\bigr\rangle_{b}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\!\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\mathcal{L}(\tau)]\right\rangle_{\!b}\,\hat{\rho}_{s}(0)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{J}(t)\,\hat{\rho}_{s}(0),

(6)

which defines the noise-averaged propagator
\mathcal{J}(t)
. For an unbiased interaction and Gaussian noise, the propagator in the interaction picture can be evaluated exactly

32
;
16
;
3
;
23
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,du\,\bigl\langle\mathcal{T}\mathcal{L}(\tau)\mathcal{L}(u)\bigr\rangle_{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\,e^{-i\Phi(t)},

(7)

where the time-ordered exponential represents the operator form of the
Feynman–Vernon influence functional
16
. The influence phase
\Phi(t)
can be expressed as

\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,du\,\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{q}(\tau)&\mathcal{S}_{c}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}(\tau-u)\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{q}(u)\\[1.99997pt]
\mathcal{S}_{c}(u)\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}
is the bath self-energy kernel,
\mathcal{S}_{c}
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}
denote the classical and quantum superoperators, defined by their action on a density operator as

\mathcal{S}_{c/q}\,\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\hat{S}\hat{\rho}\pm\hat{\rho}\hat{S}\bigr).

(9)

The self-energy matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
in Eq. (
8
) is not unique. Exploiting the Hermiticity of the correlation function,
[C(t)]^{\dagger}=[C(-t)]^{\ast}=C(t)
, together with the symmetry of the integration domain, one may choose different but equivalent representations
68
. Moreover,
\Phi
does not depend on the antisymmetric part of the matrix
\Sigma
. For instance, a convenient choice is

\bm{\Sigma}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}-i&-i\,\theta(t)\\
i\,\theta(-t)&0\end{bmatrix}C(t),

(10)

which collects the contributions of the two-point correlation function
C(t)
. Accordingly, Eq. (
8
) incorporates reservoir dynamics only implicitly, through a retarded self-interaction. The Heaviside step function
\theta(t)
enforces causality, equaling 1 for
t\geq 0
and 0 otherwise.

An alternative representation of the influence phase recasts the self-energy in a form that explicitly separates real and imaginary parts:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\left(C(t)\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+C^{\ast}(t)\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=-2i\theta(t)\left(\real C(t)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+\imaginary C(t)\begin{bmatrix}0&i\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right).

(11)

This representation highlights the distinct roles of the real and imaginary parts of the correlation function in generating dissipation and fluctuation effects. The freedom in choosing
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
reflects the underlying gauge freedom in the embedding construction and will be exploited throughout this work to generate different Markovian embedding schemes.

II.1
Self-Energy as Matrix Product

The self-energy matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
introduced in Eq. (
8
) can be written in several mathematically equivalent forms. Here we make the ansatz that it admits a left–right factorization

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}(t)=\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}\,\mathbb{G}(t)\,\mathbb{V}\,,

(12)

where the invertible block matrix
\mathbb{G}(t)
satisfies an inhomogeneous differential equation

\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{\tau})\,\mathbb{G}(\tau)=\delta(\tau)\,\mathds{1}

(13)

where
\mathbb{K}
is the sum of a a constant matrix and a matrix whose non-zero entries are
\pm i\partial_{t}
. This ansatz can be made exact for a multi-exponential correlation function
C(t)
. Otherwise, it approximates
C(t)
to any given accuracy when the rank
K
of the matrix
\mathbb{G}
is raised sufficiently.
The time-independent matrices
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbb{V}
are introduced without imposing any additional structure or constraints.

The factorization in Eq. (
12
) is evidently not unique, even for a fixed rank
K
of
\mathbb{G}(t)
. Any pair of invertible linear transformations
\mathbb{L}
and
\mathbb{R}
acting on
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbb{V}
generates an equivalent triplet

\mathbb{U}^{\prime\dagger}=\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}\,\mathbb{L}^{-1},\quad\mathbb{G}^{\prime}=\mathbb{L}\,\mathbb{G}\,\mathbb{R}^{-1},\quad\mathbb{V}^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}\,\mathbb{V},

(14)

which leaves the product in Eq. (
12
) invariant. This gauge-like freedom means that numerous mathematically different choices of
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{G},\mathbb{V})
correspond to the same self-energy, although they may lead to distinct physical interpretations or computational schemes (in face of single-particle basis truncation).

II.2
Brownian-Oscillator Spectral Density

By way of example, we will illustrate the general formalism by considering a class of bath correlation functions of the form

\displaystyle C(t\geq 0)=c_{1}\,e^{-i(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})t}+c_{2}\,e^{i(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})t}\,,

(15)

where
c_{1,2}
are complex coefficients and
\zeta,\gamma_{0}
are real parameters.
This structure describes a damped mode with complex frequencies

\pm\zeta-i\gamma_{0}
located in the lower half of the complex plane.
The self-energy can be written as

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\!\begin{bmatrix}(c_{1}\!+\!c_{2}^{\ast})G^{R}(t)-(c_{1}^{\ast}\!+\!c_{2})G^{A}(t)&(c_{1}\!-\!c_{2}^{\ast})G^{R}(t)\\[6.0pt]
(c_{1}^{\ast}\!-\!c_{2})G^{A}(t)&0\end{bmatrix}

(16)

with

\displaystyle G^{R}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\,\theta(t)\,e^{-i(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})t}\,,

(17a)

\displaystyle G^{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\;\,i\,\theta(-t)\,e^{\,i(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})t}\,.

(17b)

Eq. (
16
) emerges after inserting
C(t)
into Eq. (
10
), with some modifications allowed by symmetries. One of the many possible decompositions of type (
12
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&c_{2}-c_{1}^{\ast}\\
0&c_{1}^{\ast}-c_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbb{V}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}&c_{1}^{\ast}-c_{2}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&G^{R}(t)\\
G^{A}(t)&0\end{bmatrix}.

A more general form of this matrix product is related to the Keldysh formalism discussed in Sec.
III
.
The generalization of Eq. (
15
) to a sum of multiple exponential terms is straightforward.

A minimal physical model that realizes the decomposition in Eq. (
15
) is the widely studied Lorentzian-type spectral density
19
;
21
;
22
;
45
;
59
;
38
;
50
;
66

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{4c_{0}^{2}\gamma_{0}\omega}{(\omega^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2})^{2}+4\gamma_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}}\,.

(18)

It typically arises as an effective spectral density when a broadband Ohmic bath is filtered by a single harmonic mode at frequency
\omega_{0}

19
. The corresponding correlator, across different parameter regimes, captures a broad range of interesting physical processes. In the sequel, we will use at several places three limiting cases to specify general results.

Case 1: Classical contribution.–

At sufficiently elevanted temperatures, when Matsubara terms can be neglected, one regains the classical part of the correlation function

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4\zeta}\left[\coth\!\left(\frac{\beta(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})}{2}\right)+1\right]e^{-i(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4\zeta}\left[\coth\!\left(\frac{\beta(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})}{2}\right)-1\right]e^{-i(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})t}\,,

(19)

where
\zeta=\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\gamma_{0}^{2}}
is the effective oscillation frequency.

Case 2: Weak-damping regime.–

In the weak-damping limit
\gamma_{0}\ll\omega_{0},\zeta
, where the spectral distribution is sharply peaked, the correlation function (
II.2
) reduces to the quasi-thermal form

\displaystyle C(t\geq 0)=\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{2\zeta}\left[(n_{\beta}+1)e^{-i\zeta t}+n_{\beta}e^{i\zeta t}\right]e^{-\gamma_{0}t}\,,

(20)

where
n_{\beta}=(e^{\beta\zeta}-1)^{-1}
is the Bose–Einstein occupation number. This spectral density is widely used in quantum optics and also serves as an effective description of a discrete bath mode with a narrow linewidth broadening
\gamma_{0}
. Moreover, it serves as a basis function for fitting bath correlation functions in the Lindblad–pseudomode formulation
53
;
43
;
37
.

Case 3: Strong-friction limit.–

In the overdamped regime
\gamma_{0}\gg\omega_{0}
, the correlation function (
II.2
) for times

t\gtrsim\omega_{D}^{-1}\gg(2\gamma_{0})^{-1}
reduces to

\displaystyle C(t)\approx\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4\gamma_{0}}\left(\cot\!\frac{\beta\omega_{D}}{2}-i\right)e^{-\omega_{D}t}\,,

(21)

where
\omega_{D}=\omega_{0}^{2}/(2\gamma_{0})
is the Drude cutoff frequency.
This form corresponds to the Debye spectral density, widely used in HEOM studies in chemical physics
60
;
62
;
4
.

III
Gaussian Unraveling of the Influence Functional

Now, we proceed with the general discussion of Sec.
II
.
Substituting Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
8
), the influence phase takes the compact bilinear form

\displaystyle\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dt_{1}dt_{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{q}(t_{1})\!&\!\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{c}(t_{1})\end{bmatrix}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}\mathbb{G}(t_{1}\!-\!t_{2})\,\mathbb{V}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{q}(t_{2})\\[2.0pt]
\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{c}(t_{2})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\equiv\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dt_{1}dt_{2}\,\mathcal{S}_{u}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\,\mathbb{G}(t_{1}\!-\!t_{2})\,\mathcal{S}_{v}(t_{2})\,.

(22)

Here the effect of the matrices
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbb{V}
has been absorbed into the source fields by defining the row and column superoperator vectors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{u}^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\dagger}&\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\mathbb{U}^{\dagger},\quad\mathcal{S}_{v}=\mathbb{V}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{q}&\mathcal{S}_{c}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(23)

We emphasize that the operator-valued influence phase acting on the system is equivalent to that in Eq. (
8
); it has merely been recast into different structures for computing the inversion of Green’s function and obtaining the boundary constraints for the unraveling fields.

Our objective is to recast the influence functional, whose phase is intrinsically time-nonlocal, into a time-local representation. This allows us to derive deterministic, time-local equations of motion while keeping explicit track of the boundary conditions. Such a transformation can be achieved by using the Gaussian identity
2
;
52

\begin{aligned} &\int\!\mathcal{D}[{\Psi}^{\dagger},{\Psi}]\exp{
i\!\int_0^t\!\! d\tau\Bigl[
{\Psi}^\dagger(\tau) \mathbb{K}(i\partial_\tau) {\Psi}(\tau)
- {\Psi}^\dagger(\tau) \mathcal{S}_{v}(\tau)
- \mathcal{S}_{u}^\dagger(\tau) {\Psi}(\tau)
\Bigr]}\\
&=\exp{
-i\!\int_0^t\!\! d\tau du\,
\mathcal{S}_{u}^\dagger(\tau)\, \mathbb{G}(\tau-u)\, \mathcal{S}_{v}(u) }\,.\end{aligned}

The auxiliary field
\Psi(t)
is a column vector whose dimension matches that of
\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})
, and
\Psi^{\dagger}(t)
denotes its Hermitian conjugate. The path-integral normalization factor has been absorbed into the functional measure
\mathcal{D}[{\Psi}^{\dagger},{\Psi}]
. Implementing the Gaussian identity to (
II
) with respect to influence phase representation (
III
), the reduced density operator is then expressed as a path integral over auxiliary fields:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)=\mathcal{T}\!\int\mathcal{D}[\Psi^{\dagger},\Psi]\,e^{i\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}_{u}^{\dagger},\mathcal{S}_{v};\Psi^{\dagger},\Psi]}\,\hat{\rho}_{s}(0)\,,

(24)

with the time-local action

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}_{u}^{\dagger},\mathcal{S}_{v};\Psi^{\dagger},\Psi]

\displaystyle=\!\int_{0}^{t}\!d\tau\Big[{\Psi}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{\tau})\,{\Psi}(\tau)

\displaystyle\quad-{\Psi}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{v}(\tau)-\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{u}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,{\Psi}(\tau)\Big]\,.

(25)

The Schrödinger equivalent of this double path action does not translate into unitary time evolution; some terms may be identified as Lindblad dissipators or other non-Hamiltonian terms.
Construction of the proper initial states and terminal weights for paths can be accomplished by carefully examining the continuum limit of time-discrete path integrals. The validity of the corresponding initial preparation and terminal projection operations can be verified by tracing out the auxiliary fields, recovering the superoperator form of the influence functional
3
.

In the interaction picture for system plus auxiliary modes, the total density matrix evolves along a Gaussian trajectory according to

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\left[\Psi^{\dagger}(\tau)\,\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{v}(\tau)+\mathbb{\mathcal{S}}_{u}^{\dagger}(\tau)\,\Psi(\tau)\right]\hat{\rho}(t)\,.

(26)

In the following, we focus for the correlator in Eq. (
15
) on two prototypical structures of the Green’s function
\mathbb{G}
, namely the Keldysh and the decoupled block forms, which give rise to distinct deterministic, time-local equations of motion; the term involving
\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{\tau})
plays the role of the Lagrangian for a set of free harmonic modes, whose dynamics are governed by the corresponding homogeneous equations of motion
\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})\,{\Psi}(t)=\mathbf{0}
. The boundary conditions of the fields follow directly from the discrete real-time representation of
\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})
as computed from
\mathbb{G}(t)
.

III.1
Keldysh Path-Integral Representation

We factorize the self-energy (
16
) in the form of Eq. (
12
) using the
2\times 2
Keldysh Green’s function matrix

\mathbb{G}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}G^{K}(t)&G^{R}(t)\\
G^{A}(t)&0\end{bmatrix}.

(27)

The retarded and advanced components Eq. (
17
) describe causal propagation of a
damped bosonic mode with frequency
\zeta
and damping
\gamma_{0}
,
while the Keldysh component encodes the fluctuation level through the reference occupation
n_{\rm ref}
,

G^{K}(t)=(2n_{\rm ref}+1)\bigl[G^{R}(t)-G^{A}(t)\bigr]\,.

(28)

Here
n_{\rm ref}
specifies the Gaussian reference occupation of the
auxiliary mode; in particular,
n_{\rm ref}=0
corresponds to a vacuum
reference state.

The
2\times 2
matrices
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbb{V}
appearing in the factorization (
12
) encode the remaining freedom in the representation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{\rm KP}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\!\begin{bmatrix}\delta&\delta(2n_{\rm ref}+1)-(c_{1}^{\ast}+c_{2})/\lambda\\
0&(c_{1}^{\ast}-c_{2})/\lambda\end{bmatrix},

(29a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\rm KP}

\displaystyle=\!\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
(c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast})/\delta-\lambda(2n_{\rm ref}+1)&(c_{1}-c_{2}^{\ast})/\delta\end{bmatrix},

(29b)

with nonzero free parameters
\delta
and
\lambda
. When
c_{1}^{\ast}+c_{2}\in\mathbf{R}
, the off-diagonal terms can be removed by imposing the constraint
(2n_{\rm ref}+1)\delta\lambda=c_{1}^{\ast}+c_{2}
, which yields a simplified form of the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}
.

In the steady state, time-translation invariance of the Green’s function implies that its inverse kernel can be written as a local differential operator (up to boundary contributions at the contour endpoints),

\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}0&i\partial_{t}-(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})\\
i\partial_{t}-(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})&2i\gamma_{0}(2n_{\rm ref}+1)\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

The structure of Keldysh Green’s function (
27
) specifies the unraveled path integral representation (
24
) in terms of complex-valued Keldysh fields
\Psi^{\dagger}(t)=[\phi_{c}^{\dagger}(t)\ \phi_{q}^{\dagger}(t)]
and
\Psi(t)=[\phi_{c}(t)\ \phi_{q}(t)]^{T}
. These fields are obtained from the forward and backward contour fields
\phi_{\pm}(t)
via the Keldysh rotation
27
;
52

\phi_{c/q}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl[\phi_{+}(t)\pm\phi_{-}(t)\bigr]\,,

(31)

where
\phi_{\pm}
correspond to coherent-state eigenvalues of bosonic annihilation superoperators acting on the density matrix,
\hat{a}_{\pm}\ket{\phi_\pm}=\phi_{\pm}\ket{\phi_\pm}
in vectorized form, with
\hat{a}_{+}
and
\hat{a}_{-}
acting from the left and right, respectively on the density matrix
\rho(t)

68
. The classical and quantum superoperators are defined as
\hat{a}_{c/q}=(\hat{a}_{+}\pm\hat{a}_{-})/\sqrt{2}
.

Substituting the kernel (
30
) into Eq. (
III
) yields an effective generator
\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}
that governs the coupled dynamics of the system and the auxiliary modes. By carefully accounting for the boundary conditions of
\mathbb{G}(t)
and its inverse, the path integral representation (
24
) can be transformed into an equivalent operator formulation, in which the reduced density matrix is obtained by tracing out the auxiliary mode. In the interaction picture,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\rm b}\{\hat{\rho}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\rm b}\!\left\{\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left[-i\!\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}(\tau)\right]\hat{\rho}(0)\right\}\,,

(32)

assuming a factorized initial state

\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{s}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{m}(0)
,
where the auxiliary mode is prepared in a thermal state with occupation

n_{\rm ref}
.

Replacing the auxiliary fields by creation and annihilation operators in
Eq. (
III
) yields the Schrödinger picture
equation of motion (setting
\delta=\lambda=1
),

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\mathcal{L}_{0}\hat{\rho}(t)+\gamma_{0}(n_{\rm ref}+1)\Bigl(2\hat{a}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{0}n_{\rm ref}\Bigl(2\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\,\hat{a}-\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\Bigr),

(33)

which reveals that the original system–bath model with correlation function (
15
) admits an equivalent representation as Markovian dynamics of an enlarged system governed by a Lindblad generator. The bare system-auxiliary-modes dynamics is governed by the Liouvillian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}

\displaystyle=[\hat{H}_{s}+\zeta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\cdot]+\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\dagger}&\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{c}\\

III.2
Pure-State Path-Integral Representation

An alternative and conceptually distinct representation emerges when the self-energy is expressed in a basis that renders the
2\times 2
Green’s function matrix
\mathbb{G}(t)
block diagonal:

\mathbb{G}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}G(t)&0\\
0&-G^{\dagger}(-t)\end{bmatrix}.

(38)

Here,
G(t)=-i\,\theta(t)\,e^{-i(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})t}
describes retarded propagation of a damped bosonic mode, while
G^{\dagger}(-t)
can be interpreted as the corresponding advanced propagator. Equivalently, this structure can be viewed as representing the time-ordered and anti-time-ordered propagators of a damped bosonic mode at zero temperature. The inverse kernel (up to boundary contributions at the contour endpoints) reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}i\partial_{t}-(\zeta-i\gamma_{0})&{0}\\
{0}&-i\partial_{t}+(\zeta+i\gamma_{0})\end{bmatrix}.

(39)

The block-diagonal structure of
\mathbb{G}(t)
decouples the forward and backward sectors, allowing them to be unraveled independently. Their mutual quantum correlations are no longer encoded in their free propagator
{G}(t)
but are instead mediated entirely through their couplings to the system, as defined by
2\times 2
matrices
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbb{V}
:

\mathbb{U}_{\rm PS}^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta&\dfrac{c_{1}^{\ast}+c_{2}}{\lambda}\\[8.53581pt]
0&\dfrac{c_{2}-c_{1}^{\ast}}{\lambda}\end{bmatrix},\,\mathbb{V}_{\rm PS}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}}{\delta}&\dfrac{c_{1}-c_{2}^{\ast}}{\delta}\\[8.53581pt]
\lambda&0\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

The block-diagonal structure of the Green’s function matrix (
38
) naturally accommodates unraveling fields defined on the forward and backward contours,
\Psi^{\dagger}(t)=[{\phi}_{+}^{\dagger}(t)\;{\phi}_{-}^{\dagger}(t)]
and
\Psi(t)=[{\phi}_{+}(t)\;{\phi}_{-}(t)]^{T}
. Substituting the corresponding kernel
\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t})
into Eq. (
III
) yields an effective generator
\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}
governing the coupled system-auxiliary dynamics. By carefully accounting for the boundary conditions of
\mathbb{G}(t)
and its inverse, the path integral representation of Eq. (
24
) can be transformed into an equivalent operator formulation, where the reduced density operator is obtained via a
vacuum projection
rather than a partial trace:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle{0}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm sm}(t)|{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle{0}\Big|\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left[-i\!\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm sm}(\tau)\right]\hat{\rho}(0)\Big|{0}\Big\rangle,

(41)

assuming a factorized initial state
\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{s}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{m}(0)
with the auxiliary mode prepared in its vacuum,
\hat{\rho}_{m}(0)=|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|
.

Substituting the above obtained triplet (
\mathbb{U}^{\dagger},\mathbb{K}(i\partial_{t}),\mathbb{V}
) into (
III
), and replacing the auxiliary fields by creation and annihilation operators in
Eq. (
III
) yields the Schrödinger picture
equation of motion,

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[\hat{H}_{s},\hat{\rho}(t)]-(\gamma_{0}+i\zeta)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}-(\gamma_{0}-i\zeta)\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}

\displaystyle-i\delta\,\mathcal{S}_{q}\hat{a}\hat{\rho}-i\left(\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}}{\delta}\,\mathcal{S}_{q}+\frac{c_{1}-c_{2}^{\ast}}{\delta}\,\mathcal{S}_{c}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle-i\lambda\,\mathcal{S}_{q}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-i\left(\frac{c_{2}+c_{1}^{\ast}}{\lambda}\,\mathcal{S}_{q}+\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}^{\ast}}{\lambda}\,\mathcal{S}_{c}\right)\hat{\rho}\hat{a}\,.

(42)

IV
Discussion

By exploiting the symmetry of the double time integral together with the Hermiticity of the bath correlation function, the self-energy can be expressed in several equivalent structural forms. For example, Eq. (
10
) contains both time-ordered and anti-time-ordered contributions, whereas Eq. (
II
) involves only time-ordered terms. These distinct representations lead to different unravelings of the self-energy in Liouville space or Hilbert space.

When combined with different decompositions of the bath correlation functions, as partially discussed in Sec.
III.3
, these structural forms admit a left–right factorization, as outlined in Sec.
II.1
. In such cases, the associated Green’s function
\mathbb{G}(t)
acquires simplified matrix structures that can be unraveled using deterministic coherent-state fields. Representative examples include triangular block structures [Eq. (
27
)], block-diagonal forms [Eq. (
38
)], and the representation given in Eq. (
III.3
). These constructions give rise to time-local Liouvillian generators for the embedded system dynamics.

In the present framework, Bogoliubov transformations are naturally encoded in the left–right factorization through Eq. (
14
). The corresponding similarity transformation acting on
\mathbb{G}(t)
induces transformations between unraveling fields and their associated operators. Such transformations map the embedded-system generator between different representation frames that have been developed across various theoretical frameworks and research communities. For example, the generators in Eqs. (
III.1
) (with
n_{\beta}=0
) and (
III.2
) are connected via a Bogoliubov transformation
67
;
68
. The thermofield transformation, as a particular realization of a Bogoliubov transformation, is implicitly embedded in the dynamical modeling of auxiliary modes. This correspondence has been discussed in detail in Appendix C of Ref.
68
.

Bogoliubov transformations preserve the exact physical dynamics: in the full Liouville space, they correspond to similarity transformations of the Liouvillian and therefore leave its spectrum invariant. However, practical implementations especially under strong coupling require truncation of the bosonic auxiliary-mode basis, and crucially, this truncation projection does not commute with the transformations. As a result, the finite-dimensional Liouvillian becomes representation dependent, and the approximate eigenvalue spectrum obtained after truncation may vary significantly between different frames
70
;
38
. Consequently, different representations can exhibit substantially different numerical stability, convergence behavior, and spectral properties. This non-commutativity, rather than being a mere technical complication, provides a valuable degree of freedom: by selecting an appropriate transformation, one can systematically redistribute truncation errors, stabilize simulations, optimize computational performance, and even gain alternative physical insights into the dynamics of strongly driven or strongly coupled open quantum systems.
