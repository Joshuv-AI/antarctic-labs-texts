Effects of hot air drying on fatty acid and lipid profiles in golden pomfret based on UPLC-Q-TOF-MS-based lipidomics analysis
Authors: Long Zheng, Huadan Zhang, Yalin Zhang, Xiaowei Pan, Haiying Wu, Saihao Ren, Shaodong Zeng, Jianzhi Ye, Huifang Ma, Baodong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGolden pomfret (Trachinotus ovatus) is an important mariculture fish rich in unsaturated fatty acids, while the dynamic remodeling of lipid molecules during hot-air drying remains poorly understood. To thoroughly analyze the lipid composition and dynamic changes in golden pomfret dried under different durations, fatty acid profiles and lipid compositions were identified via GC-FID and UPLC-Q-TOF-MS. Multivariate analyses (OPLS-DA and pathway enrichment) revealed overall lipidomic differences across nine groups. A total of 31 fatty acids and 412 lipid species were identified, among which 123 differential lipids (VIP > 1) were screened. Total fatty acid content peaked at 8 h. The most prominent divergence occurred between the 8 h and 16 h groups, with 127 differential lipids mapped to eight pathways including linoleic and arachidonic acid metabolism. This study identifies 8 h as a critical transition point for lipid remodeling, providing molecular-level support for lipid quality control of dried golden pomfret products.Keywords: Golden pomfret, Hot-air drying, Lipidomics, Ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS)

Introduction

Golden pomfret (Trachinotus ovatus) is an epipelagic migratory fish inhabiting the Yellow, Bohai, East China, and South China Seas, and stands as a pivotal aquaculture species along the southern coast of China (Xiao et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2024). The aquaculture industry is predominantly concentrated in the southern coastal provinces of Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, and Fujian. Among these, Zhanjiang City in Guangdong Province accounts for approximately one-third of the national total production, with an annual output of 100,000 tons, and has been designated the “Golden Pomfret Capital of China” (Wang et al., 2025). Golden pomfret is highly prized for its tender flesh and high nutritional value, particularly its rich content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), including docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (Zhang et al., 2025). However, the high proportion of PUFAs renders the fish highly susceptible to lipid oxidation during processing, leading to rancidity and quality deterioration (Chen et al., 2024). Consequently, to meet the growing market demand for high-quality seafood, it is imperative to develop effective processing technologies that extend shelf life while preserving nutritional quality.

Drying is a prevalent preservation method that reduces water activity, inhibits microbial proliferation, and facilitates ambient-temperature storage (Zhang et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2024). Among various drying techniques, hot-air drying is favored industrially due to its operational simplicity, scalability, and cost-effectiveness compared to microwave, heat pump, or vacuum drying methods (Xu et al., 2024; Usama et al., 2026). However, it is important to note that the elevated temperatures during hot-air drying can expedite the thermal oxidation of lipids, alter the fatty acid composition and generate undesirable byproducts, thereby compromising the final product's flavor profile. Previous studies have confirmed that hot-air drying significantly impacts lipid quality. Qiu et al. (2023) investigated the effects of different drying temperatures on the oxidative and degradative properties of golden pomfret protein. They found that hot-air drying leads to lipid oxidation and protein degradation, as evidenced by significant increases in thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and free amino acid (FAA) content. Additionally, Xu et al. (2024) reported elevated peroxide values (POV), total oxidation values (TOX), acid values (AV), and saturated fatty acid content during hot-air drying, accompanied by marked changes in the fatty acid profile, overall lipid composition, and phospholipid content. Conversely, an appropriate drying temperature has been shown to contribute to the maintenance of product quality and the reduction of undesirable odors. Other studies have shown that lipases and lipoxygenases (LOX) play a pivotal role in the promotion of lipid oxidation during hot-air drying (Gang et al., 2019). Regarding flavor formation mechanisms, at lower drying temperatures (40 °C), lipid oxidation and protein degradation are the primary sources of flavor, increasing the content of aldehydes and free amino acids and imparting a pleasant flavor to the product; whereas at higher drying temperatures, flavor sources also include the synergistic effect of lipid oxidation and the Maillard reaction (Qiu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2025a). A comprehensive review of the literature reveals that, while hot-air drying has been demonstrated to be an effective method for extending the shelf life of products, it is imperative to acknowledge the potential consequences of elevated temperatures and protracted drying times. These conditions readily induce nutrient loss and the development of off-flavors. Consequently, optimizing drying parameters is necessary to mitigate these adverse effects and ensure final product quality and safety. However, current research on quality and flavor changes in golden pomfret during hot-air drying remains limited, underscoring the urgent need for further in-depth investigation.

Lipids are structurally diverse biomolecules encompassing fatty acids, glycerides, phospholipids, and sterol esters, playing crucial roles in cellular energy storage, membrane structure, and signal transduction (Xu et al., 2024). In aquatic products, the modulation of lipid profiles directly dictates the final product's flavor and texture. Consequently, ascertaining the lipid composition and content fluctuations in golden pomfret assumes considerable significance, as it has the potential to elucidate variations in lipid metabolism patterns under varying hot-air drying times. This, in turn, could furnish a theoretical foundation for the development of dried golden pomfret products (Fang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Recent advances in lipidomics, particularly liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), offer unprecedented resolution for profiling complex lipid mixtures (Zheng et al., 2025). This technology has been successfully employed to identify key lipid biomarkers in shrimp (Zheng et al., 2025a) and to elucidate lipid metabolic pathways in fermented golden pomfret (Wang et al., 2022). However, existing studies on golden pomfret lipids predominantly focus on static compositional analyses or fermentation processes. Systematic investigations into the dynamic remodeling of lipidomes across varying hot-air drying durations are lacking, hindering a mechanistic understanding of quality formation and degradation.

To bridge this gap, this study employed gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) and ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS) to systematically characterize the fatty acid profiles and lipid species in golden pomfret subjected to nine distinct hot-air drying time points (0–16 h). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were further utilized to identify key differential lipids and elucidate the underlying metabolic pathways. This work was designed to map the dynamic changes in lipid composition and quantify lipid class fluxes throughout the drying process, pinpoint the critical temporal transition points governing lipid oxidation and degradation, and ultimately provide molecular-level insights for optimizing drying parameters to enhance the overall quality of dried golden pomfret products.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

Experimental raw materials: Fresh golden pomfret specimens were purchased in March 2025 from the Southeast Wharf of Dongjian Sub-district, Mazhang District, Zhanjiang, China. All samples were authenticated by taxonomic researchers from the Agricultural Products Processing Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences, based on morphological characteristics.

Standards and reagents: Heptadecanoic acid methyl ester (internal standard for fatty acid analysis, ≥99.0%) and FA (16:0-d₄) (≥99.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The deuterated lipid standard mixture EquiSPLASH (Avanti Polar Lipids, catalog No. 330731, 100 μg/mL per component) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) as a multi-class internal standard in lipidomics analysis. Chromatography-grade methanol, chloroform and n-hexane were purchased from Fisher Scientific (USA). Chromatography-grade 10% ammonia water was purchased from Shanghai Ampu Experimental Technology Co., Ltd. (China).

Instruments: A TripleTOF® 6600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) coupled with a Shimadzu LC-30 A ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu, Japan) was used for lipidomics analysis. A 7890 A gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a DB-FastFAME capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, USA) were used for fatty acid analysis. A multi-tube vortex mixer (MTV-100, Hangzhou Aosheng Instrument Co., Ltd., China), a DC-24 nitrogen evaporator (Shanghai Anpu Experimental Technology Co., Ltd., China), an HH-6D constant-temperature magnetic stirring water bath (Shanghai Lichen Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China) and an AS 60/220.R2 analytical balance (0.000001 g readability, RADWAG, Poland) were also used in the experiments.

Pre-processing of golden pomfret

Fish samples were transported to the laboratory within 1 h and kept on crushed ice in insulated foam boxes to maintain freshness prior to processing. Upon arrival, the fish were pre-treated according to the method reported by Zheng et al. (2025b). Briefly, the fish were carefully rinsed with cold water at a temperature of 4 °C to eliminate any impurities. A total of fifteen fish with uniform size and health status were selected for the study, and samples were taken from the same body part (abdominal muscle) for each specimen. Subsequently, the samples were boiled in 3% (m/v) NaCl solution at 100 °C for 3 min. Thereafter, samples were placed on a metal mesh and permitted to drain excess surface moisture at 20 °C for 10 min. The fish samples were meticulously divided into nine equal portions and placed in a drying oven at 57 °C for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 h, respectively. For each drying time point, equal amounts of muscle samples from the 15 fish were fully pooled to generate a composite sample, which represents the average lipid profile of the population at the corresponding time point. Each composite sample was analyzed in triplicate for subsequent experiments.

Fatty acid composition analysis using GC-FID

The fatty acid composition of golden pomfret was determined based on the potassium hydroxide methylation method described by Liu et al. (2023) with appropriate modifications. Briefly, 6–10 mg of golden pomfret powder was accurately weighed and mixed with 2 mL of 0.4 mol/L KOH methanol solution. Then, 100 μL of heptadecanoic acid methyl ester standard solution (5 mg/mL, dissolved in petroleum ether) was added as the internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 15 min, followed by addition of 1 mL n-hexane and 2 mL 0.9% NaCl aqueous solution. After vortexing for 2–3 s, the mixture was centrifuged at 4500 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The upper organic phase was collected and transferred into a 2 mL sample vial for GC analysis.

GC conditions: Flame ionization detector (FID), DB-FastFAME capillary column. Injector temperature: 260 °C, split ratio: 20:1, detector temperature: 280 °C. Oven temperature program: initial temperature: 150 °C, ramp to 210 °C at 10 °C/min and hold for 8 min, then ramp to 230 °C at 20 °C/min and hold for 6 min. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas, and the injection volume was 1.0 μL.

The GC-FID chromatogram of the 37 fatty acid methyl ester mixed standard is provided in the supplementary material (Fig. S1).

Lipidomics analysis using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS

Lipid extraction and analysis were performed according to previously established methods (Hu et al., 2021). Golden pomfret samples were subjected to dry grinding in a grinder, yielding a powder that was subsequently weighed (approximately 20 mg) and transferred into a 2-mL grinding tube. To this tube, 80 μL of stable isotope-labeled internal standard mixture (10 μg/mL) and 1 mL of methanol were added. The sample was ground with 2–3 steel beads at low temperature for 2–3 min. The extract was transferred to a 12 mL culture tube, and the grinding tube was rinsed with 1 mL methanol, which was combined into the culture tube. The mixture was stored at −20 °C for at least 2 h for protein precipitation. Subsequently, 2 mL of dichloromethane was added and vortexed for 1 h. Then, another 2 mL of dichloromethane and 1.6 mL of ultrapure water were added, followed by vortexing at 2500 rpm for 3 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the lower organic phase was collected into a new test tube and dried under nitrogen flow. The remaining mixture was re-extracted twice with 4 mL dichloromethane each time. All organic phases were combined, dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in 1 mL of dichloromethane/methanol (1:1, v/v), and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic filter membrane for instrumental analysis.

Chromatography conditions: Shimadzu UPLC LC-30 A system; Phenomenex Kinete C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm); injection volume: 3 μL; flow rate: 0.4 mL/min; column temperature: 60 °C; sample chamber temperature: 4 °C. mobile phase A: water: methanol: acetonitrile (1:1:1) containing 5 mM NH4Ac; mobile phase B: isopropanol:acetonitrile (5:1) containing 5 mM NH4Ac. Gradient elution program: 0–0.5 min, 20% B; 0.5–1.5 min, 40% B; 1.5–2 min, 60% B; 2–10 min, 95% B (hold for 2 min); 12–13.1 min, 20% B; 13.1–14 min, 20% B (equilibration).

Mass spectrometry conditions: AB Sciex TripleTOF® 6600, ESI+/− ion source, mass range: m/z 100–1200; curtain gas: 35 psi; ion source gas 1: 50 psi; ion source gas 2: 50 psi; ion spray voltage: 5500 (positive mode) / −4500 V (negative mode); source temperature: 600 °C.

Lipid identification and quantitation: Raw lipidomics data were processed with MS-DIAL (version 4.90) for peak finding, alignment, and lipid species identification via spectra matching using integrated LipidBlast database (Tsugawa et al., 2020). All lipid annotations strictly follow the lipidomics Standards Initiative (LSI) guidelines for lipid identification. The accurate MS tolerances were 0.01 and 0.05 Da for MS and MS/MS, respectively. The MS/MS spectral similarity score cutoff was set to 80%. For lipid quantification, peak areas of all annotated lipid features were exported from MS-DIAL and normalized using the stable isotope-labeled internal standard mix (EquiSPLASH, Avanti Polar Lipids) spiked into each sample prior to extraction.Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling equal aliquots of composite samples from all nine time points, and injected at regular intervals (every 5 samples) throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument stability. Lipid features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were excluded from downstream statistical analysis. Fold-change calculations and comparative analyses (0 h vs. 8 h, 8 h vs. 16 h) were performed on the normalized peak area data.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses of experimental data were performed using Origin software. Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test intergroup differences, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Different lowercase letters in figures and tables indicate statistically significant differences between groups (P < 0.05).

Multivariate statistical analysis including OPLS-DA was performed using SIMCA software. Variable importance in projection (VIP) values were calculated to screen key differential lipids (VIP > 1). Permutation tests were conducted to verify the reliability of the OPLS-DA model and avoid overfitting. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed based on the KEGG database.

Results and discussion

Analysis of the fatty acid composition of golden pomfret

The content and proportions of fatty acids of golden pomfret muscle largely determine sensory attributes such as flavor, texture and oxidative stability, thus affecting the overall commercial value of golden pomfret. As illustrated in Table 1, the fatty acid composition and content of golden pomfret muscle varied dynamically with processing time. In fresh samples (0 h), saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) accounted for 26.14% and 73.86% of total fatty acids, respectively, among which PUFAs accounted for 50.16% and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) accounted for 23.70%. The proportion pattern followed the order of PUFA > SFA > MUFA, which is generally consistent with previous reports (Li et al., 2011). The muscle tissue was particularly rich in oleic acid (C18:1n9c, 26.22%), linoleic acid (C18:2n6c, 17.24%), and pentadecenoic acid (C15:1, 17.50%). Regarding SFA, palmitic acid (C16:0, 18.10%) was the predominant component, consistent with findings for other marine fish (Xu et al., 2024), followed by stearic acid (C18:0, 4.20%). These two SFAs constitute the core of the saturated fraction, and their fluctuations are known to influence lipid oxidation rates.

Fatty acids composition in golden pomfret (%).

During hot-air drying the fatty acid composition of golden pomfret changed to varying degrees. Overall, the proportion of UFAs showed a slight decrease while the proportion of SFAs showed a slight increase, but both fluctuated within a narrow range and did not show a linear correlation with heating time (Gang et al., 2019). The total lipid content reached the maximum after 8 h of drying, increasing from the initial 191.14 mg/g to 279.83 mg/g (a 46.4% increase), which is mainly attributed to the concentration effect caused by moisture loss. Notably, the relative content of DHA (C22:6n-3) decreased significantly (P < 0.05), dropping over 1.8% after just 2 h of drying. This is primarily attributed to its multiple unsaturated double bonds, which are highly susceptible to free radical chain reactions under thermal conditions, leading to oxidative degradation (Liu et al., 2022). Conversely, oleic acid (C18:1n9c) showed the largest relative increase, rising by 1.15 percentage points after 8 h of heating. It is speculated that partial hydrolysis of glycerides releases free oleic acid during drying, leading to its relative accumulation. As the most abundant SFA, the relative content of palmitic acid (C16:0) increased from 18.10% to 18.98% after 10 h of drying, which may be related to the relative concentration effect caused by the degradation of unsaturated fatty acids. Stearic acid (C18:0) and linoleic acid (C18:2n6c) remained relatively stable, fluctuating within 0.79%. Tetracosanoic acid (C24:0) exhibited a more pronounced dynamic, initially decreasing by over 0.4% within the first 12 h before rebounding from 0.16% to 0.59%. Overall, these alterations underscore that fatty acid profiles during drying are governed by the interplay of thermal exposure duration and unsaturation degree, with highly unsaturated species like DHA being the most vulnerable to oxidation. Oxidative degradation of PUFAs (such as DHA) is the core source of rancid flavor and decline of nutritional value in dried aquatic products (Qiu et al., 2023).

Identification of lipids in golden pomfret

The identification of lipid molecules in golden pomfret is primarily based on the dissociation of selected lipid precursor ions under the influence of mass spectrometry collision energy, thereby generating specific fragment ions, which are used for structural characterization (Xu et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2023). In this study, UPLC-MS/MS was employed to identify glycerolipids, phospholipids, and other lipid classes in golden pomfret. Examples of lipid species including TG 54:5|TG 18:1_18:2_18:2 (triglycerides, TG), DG 34:2|DG 16:0_18:2 (diacylglycerol, DG), PE 34:2|PE 16:0_18:2 (phosphatidylethanolamine, PE), and PG 36:4|PG 18:2_18:2 (phosphatidylglycerol, PG) were selected to elucidate their mass spectrometric fragmentation patterns. In positive ion mode, TG and DG yield [M−(R + NH4)]+ fragment ion patterns, where R represents the protonated fatty acid chain; whereas PC and LPC generate a characteristic phosphocholine fragment at m/z 184.07. Other lipid classes mainly produce [M + H − NL]+ or [M + NH4 − NL]+ fragment ions, where NL represents neutral loss, and the neutral loss components vary among different lipid classes (Fig. 1). In negative ion mode, fatty acid chain information was determined via characteristic carboxylate ion fragments. Lipid species were further confirmed by retention time, high-resolution mass accuracy and isotope distribution patterns. For instance, the neutral loss of TG 54:5|TG 18:1_18:2_18:2 yields 18:1 acyl and 18:2 acyl groups, corresponding to fragment ions at m/z 601.506 and 599.496, respectively. The neutral loss of DG 34:2|DG 16:0_18:2 involves 18:2 acyl and 16:0 acyl groups, yielding characteristic fragment ions at m/z 313.27 and 337.268, respectively. For PE 34:2|PE 16:0_18:2, neutral loss of ethanolamine head group, 16:0 acyl and 18:2 acyl generates fragment ion at m/z 654.43. For PG 36:4|PG 18:2_18:2, neutral loss of the glycerol dehydration fragment from the head group and 18:2 acyl group produces fragment ions at m/z 697.608 and 279.228, respectively.

Dynamic changes in lipid composition during hot-air drying

As demonstrated in Table S1 and Fig. 2a, a total of 412 lipid metabolites were identified and classified into four major categories: glycerides (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), sphingolipids (SP), and sterol esters (ST). Glycerolipids were the most abundant category, with 258 species detected, accounting for 62.6% of total lipids. TG were the dominant GL subclass, with as many as 159 species identified, which serve as the primary energy reservoir in muscle tissue. Glycerophospholipids were the second most abundant category, with 106 species (25.7%), among which PC was the largest subclass with 32 species. Sphingolipids (19 species) and sterol esters (12 species) were detected at lower levels; ceramides (Cer) were the main form of sphingolipids. In addition, 17 free fatty acid species were identified. These findings are consistent with a previous study by Wu et al. (2025), which reported a similar lipid distribution pattern in golden pomfret, with GL, GP and SP as the major classes and TG, DG and PC as the dominant subclasses.

During hot-air drying, TG and oxidized triglycerides (OxTG) consistently constituted the majority of glycerol subclasses in golden pomfret (Fig. 2b), with their combined content remaining above 19.5 mg/g throughout the process. TG exhibited significant early-stage accumulation, peaking at 4 h (14.29 ± 6.81 mg/g) and again at 10 h (202.28 ± 3.88 mg/g), and the maximum value at 10 h was approximately 5.24 times the minimum value. OxTG also showed a biphasic fluctuation pattern, reaching peaks at 2 h and 8 h, and dropping to the minimum at 4 h (19.80 ± 2.52 mg/g). Its maximum content (121.24 ± 2.77 mg/g at 8 h) was approximately 1.58 times the initial level, indicating that oxidative modification of lipids intensified significantly in the middle stage of drying. In addition, DG content changed significantly, with its peak time consistent with that of OxTG, reaching a maximum of approximately 1670 ng/g, and generally showing a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. The contents of ether triglyceride (EtherTG) and monoglyceride (MG) remained at low levels throughout the process. These results suggest that stored lipids undergo dynamic changes of accumulation and degradation with increasing drying time, with oxidized lipids peaking in the middle stage. This pattern reflects the dynamic interplay between lipid hydrolysis and oxidation during drying, which ultimately impacts the product's shelf life (Hu et al., 2023).

The dynamic changes of seven glycerophospholipid subclasses during heat treatment are shown in Fig. 2c. Overall, the contents of various glycerophospholipid components generally showed a decreasing trend, but with different change patterns. Bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (HBMP) had the highest content and the most significant fluctuations: it peaked at 1734.52 ± 201.09 ng/g at the initial stage of drying (2 h), and then decreased sharply, with a loss rate of 87.45% by 16 h. The contents of phosphatidylinositol (PI) and PE were moderate, fluctuating between 140 and 310 ng/g, showing a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) content remained relatively stable, while phosphatidylserine (PS) content decreased continuously. Lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) and PG contents remained at extremely low levels (both below 7 ng/g) throughout the process. The widespread decline in phospholipids is likely associated with significant degradation of cell membrane structures under prolonged heat treatment, which further leads to loss of water-holding capacity and texture deterioration of fish meat (Zeng et al., 2025).

SP include subclasses such as sphingomyelin (SM), hexosyl-N-acetyl-N-hydroxysphingosine (Cer_HDS) and acyl-N-acetyl-N-hydroxysphingosine (Cer_AP). During drying, all sphingolipid subclasses showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, with peaks appearing in the early stage of drying (2–4 h) (Fig. 2d). SM had the highest overall content, peaking at 43.02 ± 0.51 ng/g at 4 h. Cer_HDS increased markedly at 2 h, reaching 2.3 times the initial value (47.48 ± 1.02 ng/g), followed by a subsequent decline. Cer_AP also peaked at 2 h (26.12 ± 3.70 ng/g) and then decreased continuously. SM and Cer_HDS were the major sphingolipid components throughout the drying process, while other subclasses such as Cer_NS, Cer_NP and HexCer_NS were present at relatively low levels. These results suggest that hot-air drying exerts a degradative effect on sphingolipid components, with the most significant impact occurring in the middle to late stages of drying.

The changes of sterol ester (ST) subclasses during hot-air drying are shown in Fig. 2e. All sterol ester subclasses showed a decreasing trend, with the most significant decrease occurring within the first 8 h of drying, after which the levels stabilized. Acylhexosyl sitostanol (AHexSIS) was the most abundant subclass, with a peak content of 106.18 ± 4.41 ng/g; after 14 h of drying, its loss rate reached 65.31%. Acylhexosyl stigmasterol (SHex) had the second highest content, while the contents of other subclasses (AHexCAS, AHexBRS, AHexSTS) remained consistently low. These results indicate that hot-air drying causes significant degradation of sterol esters in golden pomfret, especially for the major subclass AHexSIS, and the degradation mainly occurs in the early to middle stages of drying.

In summary, hot-air drying has a significant impact on the lipid composition of golden pomfret. The main lipid components including TG, OxTG, DG, and HBMP showed accumulation in the early stage and degradation thereafter, indicating that hydrolytic changes may occur throughout the drying process. This phenomenon is consistent with the lipid change patterns of Tai Lake whitebait during hot-air drying reported by Xu et al. (2024). Meanwhile, PE, PI, and PS exhibited a consistent degradation trend. As major constituents of glycerophospholipids (GP) in fish muscle, they are inherently rich in unsaturated bonds, which renders them highly susceptible to oxidative degradation (Zhao et al., 2025). This vulnerability is further corroborated by Zhang et al. (2023), who confirmed the high propensity of these phospholipids for oxidation in meat products. Collectively, these dynamic shifts between lipid hydrolysis and oxidation provide critical theoretical insights for lipid oxidation control and product quality maintenance of golden pomfret.

Screening for differential lipid metabolites

OPLS-DA is a multivariate statistical method that integrates orthogonal signal correction to remove noise signals unrelated to model classification, thereby enhancing the model resolution and validity (Tao et al., 2024). In order to effectively distinguish the differences in lipid compounds in golden pomfret under varying hot-air drying durations, an OPLS-DA model was established.As demonstrated in Fig. 3a, the first and second principal components explained 5.71% and 51.6% of the total variance, respectively. The model parameters R2_Y = 0.833 and Q2 = 0.662 indicated good fitness and predictive ability (Fig. 3b). Permutation test results showed that the regression line of Q2 intersected the Y-axis below zero, confirming that the OPLS-DA model was reliable without overfitting. These results indicate that golden pomfret samples with different hot-air drying durations have significant and predictable systematic differences in lipid composition (Malzahn et al., 2022). Subsequently, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis identified 123 lipid compounds with VIP scores >1 across the different drying periods (Table S3). Key representative lipids included LPE 20:4;O;Hex, FA 18:1, and PI 36:4|PI 16:0_20:4. A Kruskal-Wallis test further verified that all 123 candidate lipids exhibited statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). The top 20 core differential lipids ranked by VIP scores are shown in Fig. 3c, among which LPE 20:4 had the highest VIP score, making it the most important lipid contributing to the temporal gradient differences, followed by diglycerides (DG 36:3|DG 18:1_18:2), ASG 28:2;O;Hex, FA 18:2, and various phospholipids such as PE, PC, PS, and PI species. All of which made significant contributions to group separation despite varying VIP scores (Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023).

OPLS-DA analysis of lipid compounds in golden pomfret across nine drying time points. (a) Score scatter plot. (b) Permutation test results. (c) VIP scores of the top 20 differential lipids. (d) Heatmap of relative abundance for significantly altered lipids.

The dynamic changes in relative abundance of these high-VIP lipids are shown in Fig. 3d. Most high-VIP lipids (e.g., LPE 20:4, DG 36:3, ASG 28:2) were most abundant between 0 and 8 h and then declined markedly, displaying an early-upregulation followed by late-downregulation pattern. Lipids such as PE 36:3 and PS 34:2 showed lower initial abundances but responded early and subsequently stabilized. The majority of high-VIP lipids belonged to glycerophospholipids, diacylglycerols, sphingolipids, and free fatty acids—classes known to participate in membrane remodeling, signal transduction, and energy metabolism. These observations suggest that lipid remodeling was most active during the early drying phase (0–8 h), coinciding with the critical transition point identified in Section 3.1. After 8 h, lipid abundances generally declined and stabilized, indicating a shift toward a less dynamic state as drying progressed (Shui et al., 2026).

Identification of differential lipid metabolites and pathway enrichment

Analysis of lipid changes at different drying time points revealed that 8 h of drying is a critical transition point for various lipid classes, with most lipids reaching maximum abundance at this time point (Fig. 3). Therefore, volcano plot analysis was conducted to further investigate the up- and down-regulation patterns of lipid compounds at two key comparisons: 0 h vs. 8 h and 8 h vs. 16 h.

Compared with fresh samples (0 h), 59 lipids showed significant differences after 8 h of drying, while 353 lipids remained unchanged (Fig. 4a and Table S4). Among them, 16 lipids were significantly down-regulated, mainly including ceramides (6 Cer_NP species), sterol esters (4 AHexSIS species), as well as ASG 29:1;O;Hex;FA 16:0, FA 16:0, LPE 20:0, PS 40:6|PS 18:0_22:6 and PC 35:2|PC 17:0_18:2. Meanwhile, 43 lipid compounds showed an upward trend, mainly including 32 TG species and 5 EtherTG species, as well as ASG 28:2;O;Hex;FA 18:2, DG 34:1|DG 16:0_18:1, etc. Compared with the 8-h group, the number of significantly changed lipids increased by 115.25% to 127 after 16 h of hot-air drying, and the proportion of unchanged lipids decreased to 19.26% (Fig. 4b, Table S5). After 16 h of drying, only 24 lipids (mainly TG species) were significantly up-regulated, including MG 18:2, TG 52:1|TG 16:0_18:0_18:1, TG 56:4|TG 18:1_20:1_18:2, etc. In contrast, 103 lipids were significantly reduced, covering TG, PC, EtherTG and OxTG, such as LPC 16:1/0:0, PC 40:6, TG 34:0, TG 50:6;2O, TG O-50:0, etc. This phenomenon may be attributed to prolonged thermal oxidation and degradation. Consistent with previous studies (Xu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025), hot-air drying induces degradation of TG and PC in fish meat and relative enrichment of saturated TG species, and longer drying time exacerbates lipid oxidation and degradation.

Differential lipid analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment. (a, b) Volcano plots of significantly altered lipids in golden pomfret at 0 h vs. 8 h (a) and 8 h vs. 16 h (b). (c, d) KEGG pathway enrichment analyses for the same comparisons (0 h vs. 8 h in c; 8 h vs. 16 h in d).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the differential lipid metabolites to investigate the metabolic pathways involved in lipid changes during drying. For the comparison of 0 h vs. 8 h, differential lipids were mainly enriched in 8 metabolic pathways, ranked by significance: linoleic acid metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, α-linolenic acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis, fatty acid elongation, fatty acid degradation, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 4c). For the comparison of 8 h vs. 16 h, differential lipids were also enriched in the same 8 metabolic pathways, but with different impact values (Fig. 4d). These metabolic pathways are consistent with those identified in a previous study on golden pomfret by-products (Zhang et al., 2025). These results collectively demonstrate that the lipidomic profile of golden pomfret undergoes a comprehensive shift in response to the duration of hot-air drying. The identified metabolic checkpoints (e.g., the 8-h transition) and the enriched pathways, particularly those involving polyunsaturated fatty acids, highlight the susceptibility of complex lipids to thermal oxidation.

Conclusion

By integrating GC-FID fatty acid profiling and UPLC-Q-TOF-MS-based untargeted lipidomics, this study systematically mapped the dynamic remodeling of fatty acids and lipid species in golden pomfret muscle across nine hot-air drying time points (0–16 h). A total of 31 fatty acids and 412 lipid species across four major classes were identified, with 123 lipid species (VIP > 1) screened as key differential markers throughout the drying process. Total fatty acid content peaked at 8 h, coinciding with the most pronounced divergence in lipid composition. Energy-storage glycerolipids, dominated by triglycerides, displayed a biphasic pattern of early accumulation followed by late degradation, whereas membrane structural lipids including glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and sterol esters underwent continuous progressive degradation along with extended drying time. Notably, 8 h of hot-air drying was verified as a critical temporal transition point: lipid abundance and metabolic activity reached their peak at this stage, after which extensive oxidative degradation occurred from 8 h to 16 h. Pathway enrichment analysis further indicated that linoleic acid metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis were the most significantly enriched pathways associated with drying-induced lipid changes.

These findings provide a time-resolved lipidomic dataset for understanding lipid changes during thermal dehydration of aquatic products. The critical transition point at 8 h defines a pivotal time-window for monitoring lipid oxidation, wherein the identified key differential lipids serve as potential molecular markers for monitoring lipid oxidation in future industrial drying of golden pomfret. For future researchers, the clarified dynamic patterns and enriched metabolic pathways lay a fundamental foundation for further investigating the enzymatic mechanism of lipid oxidation and developing precise quality control strategies for dried golden pomfret products. Subsequent studies are recommended to combine physicochemical quality indicators and sensory evaluation to establish a direct quantitative correlation between lipid molecular changes and product sensory attributes.
