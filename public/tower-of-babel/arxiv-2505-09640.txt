Feature Relevancy, Necessity and Usefulness: Complexity and Algorithms
Tomás Capdevielle, Santiago Cifuentes

Abstract
Given a classification model and a prediction for some input, there are heuristic strategies for ranking features according to their importance in regard to the prediction. One common approach to this task is rooted in propositional logic and the notion of sufficient reason . Through this concept, the categories of relevant and necessary features were proposed in order to identify the crucial aspects of the input. This paper improves the existing techniques and algorithms for deciding which are the relevant and/or necessary features, showing in particular that necessity can be detected efficiently in complex models such as neural networks. We also generalize the notion of relevancy and study associated problems. Moreover, we present a new global notion (i.e. that intends to explain whether a feature is important for the behavior of the model in general, not depending on a particular input) of usefulness and prove that it is related to relevancy and necessity. Furthermore, we develop efficient algorithms for detecting it in decision trees and other more complex models, and experiment on three datasets to analyze its practical utility.

One way of providing insight into a prediction for a particular input consists in ranking features according to their importance. Most common

Both the Shapley values and its approximations have been pointed out for assigning non-zero scores to features that are irrelevant from a logical perspective, and similarly assign zero scores to relevant ones

Relevancy

Our contributions

In this paper we extend previous results on the complexity of detecting feature relevancy and necessity, and moreover provide efficient algorithms to solve these problems for families of models where the complexity was unknown. In particular, we show that relevancy can be detected in decision trees with numerical features, extending the result for general trees with categorical features
[
26
]
. For the case of necessity, we extend the tractability frontier showing that it can be detected in any binary decision model, and even provide linear time algorithms to compute all necessary features for decision trees and
FBDD
s. We also extend the notion relevancy in two ways, show that they both are
NP-complete
to compute for decision trees, and provide efficient algorithms for restricted cases.

Finally, we propose a global notion to decide which features are important for the model in general, which we call
usefulness
. We show that it is related to the notions of relevancy and necessity, and that the complexity of detecting it is related to the problem of deciding whether two models are equivalent. We also define a scoring notion based on it, and provide a general algorithm for computing it. In particular, the proposed procedure runs in quadratic time for decision trees. We compute this score for three different datasets and show that the ranking it induces is consistent with other scoring schemes.

Other problems related to computing sufficient reasons were considered in the literature. In particular,

In this work, we will be interested in

Definition 1

(Sufficient Reason
[
34
]
)
.

Given a model
M
and an entity
e
we define the set of reasons for the prediction

as

\displaystyle R(M,e)=\left\{S\subseteq X:M(e^{\prime})=M(e)\,\forall e^{\prime}\in\texttt{cw}(e,S)\right\}

A sufficient reason is a reason that is minimal with respect to the property of being a reason. Thus, we define the set of sufficient reasons as

\displaystyle SR{}(M,e)=\{S\subseteq X:S\in R(M,e),\forall S^{\prime}\subset S,S^{\prime}\notin R(M,e)\}

Sufficient reasons are minimal subsets of features such that, if their values are preserved as stated by
e
, then the behavior of the model
M
does not change, no matter the modifications that other features can suffer.

(Relevant feature)

Given a model

such that

.

Definition 3

(Necessary feature)
.

Given a model
M
, a feature
x\in X
and an entity
e\in\texttt{ent(}X\texttt{)}
over
M
we say that
x
is necessary for the prediction

if for all sufficient reasons

it is the case that
x\in S
.

The two previous notions are local, i.e. relevant and necessary features can be used to understand the behavior of model
M
regarding the prediction for some particular entity

Definition 4

(Useful feature)
.

Given a model
M
and a feature

we say that

and

Even though any decision tree is represented by a succinct (i.e. with at most a polynomial overhead in size) CNF formula, it is not the case that any CNF formula can be represented succinctly by a decision tree. Nevertheless, it is a well known fact that any model with a small set of positive entities can be represented by a decision tree:

Lemma 8

(Decision trees represent sparse models)

Let
E\subseteq\texttt{ent(}X\texttt{)}
be a subset of entities over features
X

X

. This construction takes time

, and the resulting decision tree has size

Lemma 12

4.3
Useful features

Consider the following problem:

Problem
:
\textsc{Useful}_{\mathcal{M}}

Input
: A model
M\in\mathcal{M}
over
X

We can characterize useful features as those that are relevant for some entity and at the same time as those that are necessary for some entity.

Theorem 22

(Characterization of useful features)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model over
X

i)

.

ii)

There is some

such that
x
is necessary for

.

iii)

There is some

such that

.

Proof.

i)
is equivalent to
ii)
because of Theorem
18
.
ii)
implies

because every necessary feature is also relevant.

To prove that
iii)
implies
i)
let

be an entity such that
x
is relevant, and let
S\in SR(M,e)
be a sufficient reason such that

. Then, since
S
is minimal,
S\setminus\{x\}
is not a reason, and thus there is some entity

such that

. Note that since

.

It holds that

, and therefore, we conclude that

∎

Let
\textsc{Equiv}_{\mathcal{M}}
be the problem of deciding, given two models

. We observe that this problem is related to

Proposition 23
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a class of models closed by conditioning. Then,
\overline{\textsc{Useful}_{\mathcal{M}}}\leq_{p}\textsc{Equiv}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a class of models closed by disjoint disjunction. Then,
\textsc{Equiv}_{\mathcal{M}}\leq_{p}\overline{\textsc{Useful}_{\mathcal{M}}}
.

Proof.

For the first part, observe that
x
is not useful for a model

if and only if

for any

. For the second part, note that

if and only if

is not useful for the model
(M_{1}\wedge x)\vee(M_{2}\wedge\overline{x})
.
∎

If

This scoring idea can be related to the one considered in

This score can be computed through a reduction to model counting for models closed by conditioning, negation and conjunction. From now on, given a boolean model

Proposition 24
.

Let

Proof.

We will count the number of entities for which

k=M(e)=M(e_{x=b})

CT(\bigwedge_{b\in\mathcal{D}_{x}}M_{x=b})+CT(\lnot\bigwedge_{b\in\mathcal{D}_{x}}M_{x=b})

∎

Corollary 25

Given a boolean decision tree

Proof.

Given two decision trees

∎

We remark that Proposition

6
Conclusions

In this work we extended previous results regarding the computation of relevant and necessary features, and also proposed new generalizations for the notion of relevancy. Moreover, we defined a global notion of feature importance, and showed that it is related to the local ones induced by relevancy and necessity. We studied many problems involving these parameters, proving them to be intractable for complex classes of models in some cases (such as detecting
usefulness
in
DNF
s) but also finding tractable restrictions (for example, detecting
necessity
in
DNF
s). For the tractable cases we described our algorithms with precision, allowing for an easy implementation. In addition, some of them run in linear time, and thus are highly efficient.

Regarding feature relevancy, we extended the results from
[
3
]
and
[
25
]
considering more general decision trees, similar to those studied in
[
14
]
. We also proposed two generalizations of the problem of computing relevant features, which we showed to be intractable in general (Propositions
16
and
17
) but solvable in the case of decision trees under certain hypotheses. Moreover, to attain the hardness results we proved hardness for a common vertex cover problem problem which, as far as we know, has not been studied from the point of view of computational complexity before (Lemma
13
). We note that the problem of computing relevant features for slightly more expressive models –such as
OBDD
s – is still open, since
[
27
]
only showed hardness for
FBDD
s.

On the topic of feature necessity, we extended the results of
[
24
]
, showing that computing necessary features is feasible for all reasonable models (i.e. those allowing evaluation in polynomial time) with categorical features (Corollary
19
). Furthermore, we provided linear time algorithms for the case of decision trees with categorical and numerical features, as well as for
FBDD
s (Propositions
20
and
21
). We left open the case of extending this linear time algorithms for arbitrary diagrams, or rather to consider restricted families of circuits such as
d-DNNF
s.

Finally, we introduced the notion of feature
usefulness
, and showed how it is related to relevancy and necessity (Theorem
22
). We showed that the problem of computing useful features is related to the problem of detecting whether two models are equivalent (Proposition
23
), and in particular this implied intractability for expressive models such as
DNF
s, and tractability for simple ones such as decision trees and
OBDD
s. The case for
FBDD
s and
d-DNNF
s remains open. We also considered measuring usefulness as a counting problem, and provided general algorithms that were efficient in the particular case of decision trees (Proposition
24
and Corollary
25
). Lastly we performed experiments for our usefulness-based scoring system using three well-known publicly available datasets, where we verified that our scores fairly correspond with both the ground truth and other popular scoring systems such as the SHAP-score.
