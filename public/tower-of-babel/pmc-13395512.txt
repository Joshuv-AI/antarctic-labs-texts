Physeal Bar Formation After Salter-Harris III and IV Medial Malleolus Fractures in Children Aged ≤10 Years
Authors: Jacob D. Kodra, Milan Patel, Ankit Choudhury, Sean Rodriguez, Jacob Matthews, Bridget K. Ellsworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Salter-Harris (SH) III and IV medial malleolar fractures in young children pose a risk of physeal bar formation and subsequent growth disturbance. The aim of this study was to compare surgical versus nonsurgical treatment and to identify factors predictive of physeal injury.Methods:A retrospective cohort analysis examined patients aged ≤10 years treated for SH III/IV medial malleolar fractures between 2012 and 2024 at a single institution. Patients received either surgical (open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or percutaneous reduction and screw fixation (PRSF)) or nonsurgical (casting/boot) management and had ≥6 months of radiographic follow-up. Demographic, clinical, and radiographic variables including articular displacement, lateral distal tibial angle (LDTA), and physeal bar formation were compared between treatment groups and subgroups with or without bar formation.Results:Thirty-two patients (16 surgical, 16 nonsurgical) met inclusion criteria. The overall incidence of physeal bar formation did not differ between treatment groups (surgical: 25% vs. nonsurgical: 19%; P = 1.000). Physeal bar formation was significantly associated with greater pre-reduction articular displacement (median: 9.1 mm vs. 2.4 mm; P = 0.045), changes in LDTA (6.2° vs. 1.0°; P = 0.006), and delayed union (57% vs. 4%; P = 0.004). Clinically significant physeal bars occurred exclusively in patients with comminuted fractures (P = 0.007). Final articular displacement was significantly lower after ORIF (0.0 mm vs. 1.0 mm; P = 0.012), although revision surgery for implant removal was more common (P < 0.001).Conclusion:The risk of physeal bar formation after SH III and IV medial malleolar fractures was not influenced by surgical versus nonsurgical treatment. Instead, greater initial fracture displacement and comminuted fracture morphology predicted physeal injury.

Distal tibial physeal fractures account for approximately 11% of all physeal injuries.1 Salter-Harris (SH) III and IV medial malleolar fractures represent an important subset of pediatric ankle fractures because of their increased risk of physeal bar formation.2-4 In skeletally immature children with notable remaining growth potential, physeal bar formation can result in angular deformity and/or limb length discrepancy. Subsequent varus deformity often necessitates surgical interventions such as physeal bar resection, epiphysiodesis, or corrective osteotomy.5-8 Both open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF) and closed reduction with casting have been associated with physeal arrest.3,9 Factors proposed to increase the risk of physeal arrest include initial fracture displacement, nonanatomic reduction, high-energy mechanism of injury, and delay to surgical intervention.2,3,10,11

In very young children, it remains controversial which medial malleolar fractures may be amenable to conservative management with casting versus surgical fixation.12-16 Many authors advocate for ORIF with either Kirschner wire or screw fixation for fractures with an articular surface displacement greater than 2 to 2.5 mm.5,17 However, the optimal treatment strategy for minimally displaced fractures remains a topic of debate. The evidence regarding the protective effect of achieving anatomic reduction of the articular surface and physis against physeal bar formation is unclear.12-16,18 Clarifying the indications and benefits of surgical versus conservative management in this borderline group of fractures is particularly important, given the heightened vulnerability of younger children to growth disturbances.2,11

Given the uncertainty regarding optimal management of minimally displaced medial malleolus fractures in younger children and their vulnerability to physeal bar formation, the primary aim of this study was to determine whether surgical treatment of SH III and IV medial malleolus fractures in patients 10 years of age or younger is associated with lower rates of physeal bar formation compared with nonsurgical (cast/boot) treatment. Secondarily, we evaluated whether initial and residual articular displacement, fixation method, or timing of reduction influenced physeal bar development. In addition, we compared surgical versus nonsurgical treatments regarding secondary outcomes such as time to union, time to weight bearing, complication rates, and the need for additional surgery. We hypothesized that surgical treatment with anatomic reduction of the fracture and physis would be protective against physeal arrest in this pediatric population.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

A retrospective cohort analysis was conducted including skeletally immature patients aged ≤10 years who underwent treatment for SH III or IV medial malleolus fractures at a tertiary children's hospital between January 1, 2012, and January 1, 2024. Patients were grouped according to treatment modality: surgical treatment with ORIF or percutaneous reduction and screw fixation (PRSF) and nonsurgical treatment with cast or immobilization boot. Eligible cases were identified through institutional medical record review, and IRB approval (PRO00052365) was obtained on July 22, 2024, before study initiation. Patient confidentiality was ensured by extracting data into a deidentified, encrypted Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) sheet.

Patients met inclusion criteria if they were aged ≤10 years at the time of initial fracture treatment, had a confirmed SH III or IV medial malleolus fracture (including cases with associated bimalleolar ankle fractures treated with medial SH III or IV involvement), and were treated at our institution within the specified period with at least 6 months of postoperative radiographic follow-up. Patients were excluded if they were older than 10 years at initial treatment; had insufficient follow-up (<6 months); or had previous ipsilateral tibial surgery, open or pathologic fractures, or underlying neuromuscular/metabolic disorder affecting fracture healing. This investigation includes patients treated by eight surgeons at a tertiary academic center, and indications for surgical versus nonsurgical management were based on the discretion of the treating surgeon. As a result, patients selected for surgical management generally demonstrated greater fracture displacement and more complex injury patterns, and the treatment groups, therefore, represent cohorts of differing baseline injury severity.

Data Collection

Demographic variables collected included age at initial treatment, sex, and weight (kg). Clinical variables included fracture laterality, fracture pattern (SH III or IV), mechanism of injury (high or low energy), skeletal age, presence of associated fibula fracture, and comminution. Fracture patterns were based on review of imaging by the senior author, a fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeon. High-energy mechanisms of injury were defined as those resulting from motor vehicle collisions, falls from greater than standing height, or sports-related injuries involving direct impact or high-velocity forces. Additional treatment-specific data were also collected, including time (in days) from initial presentation to treatment initiation, surgical approach details (for surgical cases), and return to operating room (for implant removal or revision surgery).

Skeletal age was determined using a previously described ankle bone age atlas.19 Radiographic data collected comprised percentage of physeal involvement in the coronal plane, maximum articular displacement both before and after reduction (mm), and presence and timing of growth disturbances or physeal bars. Pre-reduction displacement was measured as the articular gap on immediate pre-reduction radiographs. Postreduction displacement was measured as the residual articular gap on immediate postreduction radiographs or CT if available. Clinically significant physeal bars were defined as follows: (1) a physeal bar comprising >10% physeal surface area on advanced imaging or (2) a physeal bar causing a change in the lateral distal tibial angle (LDTA) of 5° or more. LDTA measurements were recorded both preoperatively and at final follow-up. Outcomes including loss of reduction, malunion, delayed union, and total duration of follow-up were also documented. Delayed union was defined as > 3 months to achieve full healing with resolution of a visible fracture line.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.2; GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA). Descriptive statistics were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and counts with percentages (n, %) for categorical variables. Given the small sample size and presumed non-normality, all continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U) test. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher exact test, which is appropriate for smaller sizes. Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 32 skeletally immature patients (median age, 8.00 years; IQR, 7.00 to 9.00) with SH III or IV medial malleolar fractures were included, with equal representation between surgical (ORIF, n = 14; PRSF, n = 2) and nonsurgical (casting, n = 16) treatment groups (Table 1). No significant difference was observed in age between groups (surgical: 8.00 years vs. nonsurgical: 7.50 years; P = 0.054). Skeletal age was also similar between treatment groups (surgical: median 8.5 years [IQR, 8.0 to 9.0] vs. nonsurgical: 8.0 years [IQR, 7.5 to 9.0]; P = 0.285). The median duration of radiographic follow-up was 397 days (IQR, 273 to 771). All patients underwent at least six months of follow-up as per inclusion criteria. The cohort was predominantly female (81%) and had more fractures on the right side (59%), without significant differences by treatment group in sex (P = 0.654) or laterality (P = 0.473). High-energy mechanisms accounted for 13% of all injuries, with similar distribution between groups (P = 0.600).

Treatment-Related Outcomes

The median time from initial presentation to treatment was significantly shorter for patients managed conservatively compared with surgically (0.50 days vs 4.50 days; P = 0.015). Implant removal surgery was exclusively performed in the surgical group (81% of surgical patients; P < 0.001). Return to the operating room for an indication other than implant removal was also higher in the surgical group (13%) versus the nonsurgical group (6%, P < 0.001). Two surgical patients underwent revision surgery for resection of physeal bars. By contrast, only one nonsurgical treatment patient underwent revision surgery, which was for anterior ankle loose body removal.

Physeal Bar Formation

Overall, physeal bar formation occurred in 22% of patients (7/32), and 13% (4/32) had clinically significant physeal bars (Tables 2 and 3). No significant difference was observed in physeal bar formation between surgical and nonsurgical treatment groups (25% vs. 19%; P = 1.00) (Figure 1). Median time to physeal bar formation (266 days) did not differ significantly by treatment (surgical: 319.50 vs. nonsurgical: 122.00 days; P = 0.157). In subgroup analyses, patients who developed physeal bars exhibited significantly greater pre-reduction maximum articular displacement (median 9.10 mm vs. 2.40 mm; P = 0.045) and higher rates of delayed union (57% vs. 4%; P = 0.004).

Clinically Significant Bar Formation

Of the 32 patients analyzed, 4 (13%) developed clinically significant physeal bar formation (Table 3). These patients tended to be older, with a median age of 10.00 years (IQR, 9.25 to 10.00) compared with 8.00 years (IQR 7.00 to 8.00) in all other patients, although this difference was not significant (P = 0.078). In addition, no statistically significant differences were observed in sex, weight, laterality, or skeletal age between groups. Rates of clinically significant bar formation were not significantly different between surgical (3/16, 19%) and nonsurgical (1/16, 6%) treatment groups (P = 0.689). However, patients with clinically significant bars had significantly higher rates of return to the operating room for indications other than implant removal (50% vs. 4%, P = 0.008) and were more likely to have undergone surgery for comminuted fractures (100% vs. 21%, P = 0.006).

Radiographic Outcomes

A radiographic comparison of surgical versus nonsurgical treatment of these fractures is presented in Figure 2. The median percent physeal involvement in the coronal plane was 20.9% (IQR 16.2 to 34.4), without significant differences between those with and without physeal bars (P = 0.274). Pre-reduction maximum articular displacement was significantly greater in the surgical group compared with the nonsurgical group (4.00 mm vs. 1.10 mm; P < 0.001). Postreduction displacement also remained higher in the surgical group (4.10 mm vs. 2.20 mm; P = 0.003). Notably, final articular displacement after treatment was significantly lower in patients treated with ORIF/PRSF compared with casting/boot immobilization (0.00 mm vs. 1.00 mm; P = 0.012). Patients who developed any physeal bar had significantly greater pre-reduction articular displacement compared with those without physeal bar formation (9.10 mm vs. 2.40 mm; P = 0.045). Similarly, patients with clinically significant physeal bars had greater pre-reduction displacement compared with all other patients (12.20 mm vs. 2.25 mm; P = 0.007) (Figure 3). No statistically significant differences were observed in postreduction articular displacement between patients with and without any physeal bar (P = 0.191) or between those with clinically significant physeal bars and all other patients (P = 0.060).

Loss of reduction (3%), malunion (6%), and delayed union (16%) were uncommon overall, and no significant differences were observed between treatment groups (P-values> 0.05). Delayed union, however, was significantly more common among patients who developed physeal bars (57% vs. 4%; P = 0.004). By contrast, no statistically significant differences in delayed union were observed between patients with clinically significant bars and all other patients (50% vs. 11%; P = 0.105). In addition, no significant differences were observed in maximum displacement after reduction, loss of reduction, malunion, or follow-up duration between those with clinically significant bars and all other patients.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the influence of surgical versus nonsurgical treatment on the development of physeal bar formation after SH III and IV medial malleolus fractures in children aged ≤10 years. The key findings were as follows: (1) treatment modality (surgical vs. nonsurgical) was not associated with a significant difference in the rate or timing of physeal bar formation; (2) greater pre-reduction articular displacement was significantly correlated with an increased rate of physeal bar formation; (3) patients with clinically significant bars frequently presented with comminuted fracture patterns; and (4) physeal bar formation was associated with higher rates of angular deformity and delayed union.

Contrary to our hypothesis, surgical management with ORIF or PRSF did not markedly reduce the incidence of physeal bar formation compared with nonsurgical treatment. Instead, our findings indicate that initial fracture severity and displacement at presentation are more critical determinants of physeal outcomes.

Unique among the current literature, this study exclusively examined children 10 years or younger with Salter-Harris III and IV medial malleolar fractures. Most previous work in this area has grouped a broader range of pediatric patients, frequently including older adolescents or failing to analyze outcomes by specific age group. This approach may obscure the distinct risks encountered by younger children with notable growth remaining who face a higher likelihood of physeal complications. By narrowing our focus to this younger, skeletally immature cohort, our investigation provides new insights into risk factors of physeal bar formation and provides practical information that can inform treatment in this population.

Comparison with Previous Studies

Our findings align with previous studies highlighting the effect of fracture severity on physeal outcomes. For example, Pan et al4 identified initial fracture displacement greater than 4.5 mm as an independent predictor of premature physeal closure. Similarly, studies by Leary et al and Kling et al emphasized energy and magnitude of initial displacement, rather than treatment modality, as key predictors of growth disturbance.10,11 In our cohort, all patients who developed clinically significant bars had comminuted fracture patterns and significantly greater initial articular displacement, further supporting the existing literature emphasizing the importance of initial fracture severity.3,6,7 The exclusive occurrence of clinically significant physeal bars in comminuted fracture patterns suggests that comminution itself may serve as a practical marker of higher risk injury. Recognition of comminution on initial imaging may, therefore, warrant closer radiographic surveillance and more detailed counseling of families regarding the potential for growth disturbance. The magnitude of pre-reduction displacement observed in patients who developed physeal bars (median 9.1 mm) was substantially greater than the 2-2.5 mm articular displacement threshold commonly used to guide surgical decision making in pediatric ankle fractures. The articular displacement threshold exists to prevent post-traumatic arthritis, not physeal bar formation. Although this study was not designed to define a predictive cutoff, this finding suggests that, in very young children with SH III and IV medial malleolar fractures, absolute displacement may reflect the severity of physeal injury in addition to exceeding a standard articular reduction threshold.

Patients treated surgically exhibited significantly greater initial displacement, reflecting standard clinical practice of surgical management of displaced articular fractures. While surgical fixation effectively restored articular congruity—evidenced by significantly lower final displacement compared with nonsurgical management—this did not seem protective against physeal bar formation. This observation parallels findings from the study by Kho et al,20 who similarly reported superior radiographic reduction outcomes with surgical intervention despite no clear protective effect against physeal closure. However, it is important to recognize that patients selected for surgical fixation demonstrated substantially greater initial articular displacement (median 9.1 mm vs. 2.4 mm). This degree of displacement represents a more severe injury pattern and may be the key driver of subsequent physeal bar formation. Conversely, fractures managed nonsurgically maintained satisfactory final alignment without evidence of displacement progression. In this cohort, treatment decisions were individualized and based on surgeon judgment, particularly in fractures near surgical displacement thresholds. Although this study was not designed to establish a treatment algorithm, our findings suggest that in select borderline cases, careful closed management with radiographic follow-up may be reasonable.

Clinical Implications

Clinicians should recognize that higher initial displacement and fracture severity seem to markedly influence the likelihood of physeal arrest regardless of chosen management strategy. Therefore, thorough initial assessment of displacement with radiographs and CT if needed is critical in determining the potential risk of physeal bar formation in young children.

Interestingly, although surgical fixation was not protective against physeal bar formation, it did offer superior final alignment. This came at the cost of surgical management being associated with higher rates of secondary procedures, largely for implant removal. Thus, the potential benefits of surgical intervention in achieving and maintaining alignment must be carefully weighed against the increased burden of follow-up surgery and surgical complications.21 Treatment decisions for borderline-displaced fractures should, therefore, be individualized, with particular attention to initial displacement and comminution, which were the strongest predictors of physeal bar formation in this cohort.

In patients who developed clinically significant physeal bars, increased angular deformity and delayed union were observed, consistent with previous reports.3,6,7 The greater change in LDTA observed among patients with physeal bars suggests that routine measurement of alignment at follow-up may help identify early growth disturbance. Although this study did not aim to define the exact timing of angular change, progressive LDTA divergence on serial radiographs may warrant closer surveillance or advanced imaging to evaluate for early physeal bar formation. This underscores the clinical relevance of identifying risk factors early because more aggressive surveillance and intervention strategies, including timely bar resection or epiphysiodesis, might improve long-term outcomes. For example, Yuan et al6 demonstrated improved limb alignment after distal tibial physeal bar excision, thereby reducing subsequent corrective procedures. There have also been recent advances in MRI technology that allow use of diffusion tensor imaging to identify healthy physeal cartilage.22 In the future, these sequences may be able to predict physeal bar formation before radiographic changes. MRI may also be able to determine overall health of remaining physis to determine whether physeal bar excision, completion epiphysiodesis, or observation is appropriate.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, its retrospective nature introduces potential selection and information biases due to variability in data reporting accuracy. Second, the relatively small sample size—particularly among those who developed clinically significant bars—may limit statistical power to detect differences between treatment groups and generalizability. Because multiple comparisons were conducted across several subgroup analyses, the potential for Type I error must be acknowledged. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted as exploratory, requiring additional validation. In addition, indications for surgical versus nonsurgical management were subject to surgeon preference and not uniform across all patients. As a retrospective study, treatment was determined by the treating surgeon, and patients undergoing surgical fixation had significantly greater initial displacement. As a result, the surgical and nonsurgical groups were inherently different in terms of fracture severity, and these characteristics may outweigh the independent effect of surgical versus conservative management. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted as associations and a foundation for future multi-institutional, prospective investigation.

This study may also underestimate the incidence of bars, given the minimum radiographic follow-up duration of only six months. Time to physeal bar formation should also be interpreted cautiously because it depends on clinic follow-up and imaging frequency, which were not standardized between groups. The follow-up duration did not uniformly extend until skeletal maturity for all patients, possibly underestimating the true incidence of late developing physeal bars or angular deformities. Notably, patients without identified physeal bars had a shorter median duration of follow-up compared with those who developed bars, raising the possibility that additional bars could emerge with longer surveillance. In addition, distinguishing early physiologic physeal closure from true physeal bar formation may be challenging in older female patients approaching skeletal maturity. To minimize this confounder, all included patients had radiographically open physes at presentation and clinically significant bars were defined as those involving >10% of the physis or associated with ≥5° change in LDTA. Nonetheless, without advanced cross-sectional imaging, subtle physiologic closure could not be completely excluded. Finally, the incidence of physeal bar development may also be lower in this study because advanced diagnostic imaging was not performed for every patient.

Despite these limitations, this is one of few studies exclusively examining children aged 10 years or younger with detailed radiographic follow-up. This investigation markedly contributes to the literature by clarifying the relative influence of fracture severity and treatment modality on physeal bar formation after medial malleolar fractures in a young cohort of pediatric patients. Future studies with larger, prospective cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In skeletally immature patients with SH III and IV medial malleolar fractures, the choice between surgical and nonsurgical reduction did not markedly influence the risk of physeal bar formation. Instead, greater initial fracture displacement and comminuted fracture morphology notably predicted physeal injury. These findings highlight the importance of initial injury characteristics in guiding prognosis, rather than the treatment method itself.
