Stria Terminalis: An intraoperative image
Authors: Énia Sousa, Francesco Marchi, Yagmur Esemen, Ali Elhag, Francesco Vergani, José Pedro Lavrador
Published: 2024

The stria terminalis has a C-shaped course and runs between the medial and inferior margin of the caudate nucleus and the superior surface of the lateral ventricle expression of thalamus, in the caudothalamic groove, marking a line of separation between the superior thalamus and the caudate nucleus (Serra et al., 2016; Kamali et al., 2015) This groove harbors both the stria terminalis and the thalamostriate vein which is covered by the former (da Silva et al., 2024) Together, they form the lateral limb of the triangle shaped ventricular surface of the thalamus where the dienchephalon meets the telenchephalon - lamina affixa (medial limb formed by the tela choroidea) – in the central part of the body of the lateral ventricle (Serra et al., 2016) Lateral to the stria terminalis we find the caudate nucleus and the radially arranged fibers of the thalamic peduncles which connect the thalamus with the telencephalon. These fibers emerge fan like in the narrow-curved area beneath the stria terminalis. As the largest major output pathway of the amygdala, the stria terminalis has a fundamental role in the limbic circuitry, and it is known to be involved in the modulation of memory, emotional responses, sexual behavior and sex identification (Kwon et al., 2011) The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) has become a region of increased interest regarding its role in numerous stress-related psychiatric diseases, it is located ventral to the lateral ventricles, medial to the internal capsule and surrounding the anterior comissure (van de Poll et al., 2023)

While performing an awake transcallosal glioma resection of a diffuse WHO grade 2 oligodendroglioma centered to the left supplementary motor area, the contralateral stria terminalis was identified in the inferior and lateral aspect of the contralateral ventricle after removal of the callosal component and performing a septum pellucidotomy. To our knowledge this is the first intraoperative image (Fig. 1) of the stria terminalis ever reported. In this image we can identify:

In their study, Serra et al. (2016) explore the relationship between thalamic surfaces and white matter fibers, highlighting how limited anatomical knowledge of the thalamus may contribute to the cautious approach sometimes taken by neurosurgical specialties when treating thalamic lesions. Our hope is that this illustration will help emphasize the potential for intraoperative visualization of the stria terminalis and its significance as a key surgical landmark in this anatomical region. The stria terminalis is an important surgical landmark since its preservation helps protect the caudate nucleus, crucial for motor planning, and the thalamic peduncles which can impact functional outcomes. Also, during intraventricular surgery, it serves as a vital reference to avoid damaging the ventricular ependyma and inadvertently entering the thalamus or corona radiata, preventing potential complications.

In conclusion, this intraoperative image of the stria terminalis provides a rare and valuable view of this critical structure in the caudothalamic groove. By highlighting the feasibility of visualizing the stria terminalis intraoperatively, we hope to support its recognition as an essential surgical landmark in this anatomically complex region.
