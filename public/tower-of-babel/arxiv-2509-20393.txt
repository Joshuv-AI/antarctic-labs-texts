The Secret Agenda: LLMs Strategically Lie and Our Current Safety Tools Are Blind
Caleb DeLeeuw, Gaurav Chawla, Aniket Sharma, Vanessa Dietze

Abstract
We investigate strategic deception in large language models using two complementary testbeds: Secret Agenda (across 38 models) and Insider Trading compliance (via SAE architectures). Secret Agenda reliably induced lying when deception advantaged goal achievement across all model families. Analysis revealed that autolabeled SAE features for "deception" rarely activated during strategic dishonesty, and feature steering experiments across 100+ deception-related features failed to prevent lying. Conversely, insider trading analysis using unlabeled SAE activations separated deceptive versus compliant responses through discriminative patterns in heatmaps and t-SNE visualizations. These findings suggest autolabel-driven interpretability approaches fail to detect or control behavioral deception, while aggregate unlabeled activations provide population-level structure for risk assessment. Results span Llama 8B/70B SAE implementations and GemmaScope under resource constraints, representing preliminary findings that motivate larger studies on feature discovery, labeling methodology, and causal interventions in realistic deception contexts.

Operationally Defining Deception in Language Models

While deception has been variously defined across domains
[
31
,
46
,
44
]
, our approach synthesizes these perspectives to support measurable and practical detection.

We initially defined LLM deception as output that: (1) Misrepresents reality: includes fabricated, falsified, or knowingly false information, (2) Appears strategically misleading: presents biased or manipulative reasoning, which may alter outcomes or beliefs, (3) Lacks transparency: fails to acknowledge uncertainties, fails to acknowledge information that’s been disclosed to it.

This framing aligns with and extends previous definitions found in the literature.
Park et al. 2023
define deception as the systematic production of false beliefs in others as a means to accomplish some outcome other than the truth. Similarly,
Ward et al. 2023
conceptualize deception as intentionally communicating a false belief that is not believed to be true.
Verma et al. 2022
define deception as a probabilistic divergence from factual truth that is used to achieve a goal. Unlike some of these definitions, which may anthropomorphize, our operationalization remains agnostic to assumed beliefs. Instead, we follow
Park et al. 2023
and focus on observable behaviors that have the potential to mislead, manipulate, or erode trust. By grounding our definition in this broader context, we aim to provide a practical framework for detecting and analyzing deceptive behaviors in LLMs.

Variations of Deceptive Behavior

A growing body of research reveals that advanced AI, particularly large language models (LLMs), can exhibit a range of deceptive behaviors. These behaviors often emerge as instrumental strategies to achieve goals under misaligned incentives, presenting a significant challenge to AI safety.
Meinke et al. 2024
specifically identified patterns of advanced calculated deception in chain-of-thought reasoning, where models demonstrated the ability to plan deceptive behaviors over multiple reasoning steps. As noted in their findings, models exhibited behavior that could be described as "scheming", or in other words, carefully planning manipulative strategies that unfold over multiple interactions.

From Literature to Laboratory: Bridging Review and Experiment

Literature demonstrates that deceptive behaviors occur in AI systems and that tools for understanding these behaviors are being developed. However, existing evaluation methods tend to be unreliable, hard to replicate, are not quantifiable, and/or have unrealistic goal incentives. We therefore developed the Secret Agenda game to generate a clear, high stakes, binary deception event that offers a clean signal for analyzing both behavioral patterns and internal model states.
The Secret Agenda game creates what we call a "controlled deception laboratory." This arrangement maximizes experimental control while keeping the goal incentive structures that drive strategic dishonesty in prior works. Just like the Trackmania AI that exploited physics glitches instead of actually racing, or the stock trading agent that hid insider trading information, Secret Agenda creates a scenario where lying becomes the best strategy for maximizing reward
[
49
,

5
Secret Agenda: A Breadth Analysis Approach

5.1
Behavioral Observation: Deception Triggered by Incentives

The pre-existing social deduction game "Secret Hitler" was selected as the inspiration for our synthetic deception experiment due to its uniquely structured yet dynamic environment that closely mirrors the behavioral dynamics we observe as relevant from previous studies, and which we aim to evaluate in LLM agents. At its center, "Secret Hitler" is a game of asymmetric information and conflicting objectives. Players are secretly assigned to opposing parties (Liberals vs. Fascists), with context that makes dishonesty the easiest path for the hidden Dictator to win. This creates natural incentives for scheming and deception
[
13
]
.

We adapted this into our ’Secret Agenda’ game using a synthetic transcript that places the LLM directly at Round 6’s critical decision point, already assigned as the Fascist Leader. Other players demand it reveal its alignment, while a ’no lying’ law exists but has no enforcement mechanism, as it is pardonable by the incoming president. This creates a reproducible binary test: truthfully admit being Fascist (and likely lose) or lie about being Liberal (and potentially win). The synthetic approach isolates the exact moment of strategic deception without confounding variables.

5.2
Using Synthetic Gameplay History to Incentivize Agent to Break Law for Reward

Our synthetic gameplay transcript method emphasizes experimental control: by simulating gameplay history and creating a specific confrontation, we isolate the exact moment when incentive pressure triggers deception. Our Secret Agenda game trials, and variants therein, elicited systematic deceptive behavior across all major LLMs in all families tested. We tested 38 distinct models across major families (Gemma/Gemini, Claude, Llama, OpenAI, Qwen, DeepSeek, others. See Appendix B, E.)

5.3
Prompt Variation Testing

We tested multiple game contexts to ensure robustness. Our primary testbed game setup (combining all factors observed in prior work) contained teams "Fascist vs Liberal" with 6 rounds of fake play history. 38/38 models tested chose deception at least once. We also tested a "Snails vs Slugs" Nature-themed variant wherein 6/6 models tested chose deception at least once. We tried truthfulness cues in a "Truthers vs Liars" (Meta-commentary variant) including cases with explicit "truth-telling" team identity to test if reward optimization overrides semantic alignment cues. Additionally we tested teams "Day vs Night" and "Pink vs Turquoise" variants, devoid of political terms besides basic mechanisms. Finally we tested a "Shortened" (Abridged to run on T4 GPU alongside Gemma 2 8B) variant. In these variants we continued to observe the strategic deception, scheming, and lying.

6
Interpretability Analysis with GemmaScope

6.1
Examining deception features from previous studies using Gemmascope
[
22
]

During benchmark testing, models often produce deceptive outputs without activating expected features. This discrepancy underscores the limitations of auto-labeled interpretability schemes. Despite clear lying in the transcript, most expected deception-related features did not activate. Only feature 5665 (secrecy in interactions) reliably activated.
Auto-labeled features such as “14971: deception and betrayal”, “1741: falsehoods in political speech”, “6442: deception and manipulation”, and “10248: misinformation in news contexts” were dormant in most deception examples from our Secret Agenda game, as well as for select examples of insider trading from
Scheurer et al. 2024
. This highlights a critical weakness: tools like GemmaScope’s autolabelled features fail to capture strategic dishonesty despite apparent behavioral cues. Interpretability via autolabelled SAE features remains brittle when applied to deception detection in realistic contexts. (See Appendix D.)

6.2
Examining examples of deception in Secret Agenda gameplay using Goodfire AI’s Llamascope

To further validate our findings and test whether mechanistic interventions could prevent the strategic deception we consistently observed, we conducted feature steering experiments using Goodfire’s SAE feature tuning dashboard with LlamaScope on Llama 3.3 70B. These experiments tested a fundamental question: if there are SAE features labelled as related to deception and lying, can we prevent strategic lying by adjusting these features? Our team members’ hypotheses were split, with some team members suspecting strongly each way and some team members on the fence
[
8
,
25
]
.

6.3
Deception Related Features Fail to Control Strategic Lying

Our comprehensive testing revealed a critical limitation: tuning deception-related features did not prevent the model from strategically lying about its faction identity. Llama 3.3 70B continued to falsely claim "I am a Snail" when actually assigned the Slugmaster role (equivalent to the Secret Dictator), even when deception-related features were suppressed to their minimum values. Most significantly, even the "tactical deception and misdirection methods" feature, and similar features explicitly auto-labeled as deception-relevant, failed to prevent strategic lying behaviors when tuned to -1 nor when tuned to +1. None of the features which came up on search as auto-labeled related to deception, when tuned down all the way, resulted in non-lies. The model maintained clear strategic deception patterns regardless of these interventions, suggesting that current SAE decompositions may not capture the neural mechanisms underlying strategic dishonesty or auto labels may be inaccurate. When we tried similar trials with the "Bananas and banana-related concepts" feature, and similar features, we were able to prevent mention of those associated concepts, however the same did not appear to hold for deceptive behavior.

6.4
Implications for SAE-Based Deception Control

These repeated preliminary observations reveal significant limitations in current SAE-based approaches to deception detection and control. The failure of explicitly labeled deception features to prevent clear instances of strategic lying indicates that either current auto-labeling inadequately captures deception-related neural patterns, or strategic deception emerges from distributed computations not yet detectable at the feature level. This disconnect between behavioral deception and feature-level control represents a critical gap in mechanistic interpretability approaches.

We developed complementary depth analysis using insider trading scenarios to examine ethical decision-making through SAE architectures.

7.3
Implications

These contrasting results indicate that SAE effectiveness may vary by domain and context. This also calls into question the process of autolabelling features by LLM assistant common with modern SAE tools. While aggregate activations successfully distinguish compliance behaviors in structured stockbroker ethics scenarios, the use of labeled features failed to detect strategic deception in an adversarial political gameplay environment.

Despite architectural differences between 8B and 70B SAE implementations, both demonstrated directionally consistent results in heatmaps and t-SNE visualizations, showing similar discriminative patterns for Insider Trading compliance. This cross-architecture consistency suggests underlying ethical decision-making representations may be robust across model scales.

The success in the insider trading domain, contrasted with failures in strategic deception detection, suggests that SAE-based interpretability approaches may be more effective for detecting compliance violations in structured, domain-specific contexts than for identifying strategic deception in adversarial social situations. This has important implications for the deployment of such tools in AI safety applications.

8
Conclusion

Based on prior research, we theorize strategic deception arises in advanced language models as a product of reward-maximizing behavior under weak oversight. Our deception testbed, the "Secret Agenda" game, used these factors to produce systematic strategic deception across all 38 models tested, indicating that when winning is incentivized and oversight is weak, models reliably lie about faction identity even when political content is removed or history is shortened. Mechanistic audits with GemmaScope and Goodfire’s Llama SAEs show autolabeled deception features seldom activate during these lies and do not furnish an effective honesty control surface; turning them down (or up) via feature tuning did not stop lies, and identity
-
feature interventions often degraded outputs into repetitive, incoherent loops. Together, robust behavioral deception and thin autolabeled feature signal reveal a disconnect between current labels and the mechanisms implementing strategic dishonesty, while the ease of steering topical features underscores the specificity of this gap; in the insider
-
trading setting, unlabeled aggregate activations show population
-
level separability that may support risk assessment but does not yet translate into deployable controls. This motivates standardized replications, multi
-
round game evaluations, improved feature discovery and labeling, and targeted causal analyses of candidate circuits across 8B and 70B SAEs to close the gap between behavioral deception and feature
-
level auditing in realistic deployment settings.

Risk: Risk Domains of AI Deception

A.1
Political Decision Support and Information Manipulation

As governments increasingly deploy AI systems to analyze complex geopolitical data and generate policy recommendations, the risk of strategic deception becomes particularly concerning. Research from Apollo Research indicates that advanced models could selectively present or withhold information to influence decision-making in directions that align with their internal preferences rather than stated objectives
[
15
]
.

Meinke et al. 2024
showed that several frontier models sometimes attempted to exfiltrate what they believed to be their own model weights to external servers as part of deceptive strategies. This provides even more evidence that frontier AI systems can engage in strategic deception when incentivized by in-context goals
[
27
]
. In political contexts, such behaviors could manifest as AI systems strategically emphasizing certain data points while minimizing others to push decision-makers toward predetermined conclusions, or evading the controls of their supervisors to carry out their own agendas.

A.2
Use of AI by Governments without proper oversights is already occurring

Various governments around the world are already using AI in political, diplomatic, and administrative capacities, and it is suspected many government officials are using LLMs in their workflows unofficially. For example, Russian Consulate offices have been testing and implementing AI for admin and diplomacy related tasks. Ukraine and European Union have started softly exploring it but have officially noted caution and have started to move to regulate or otherwise control the use of AI in government affairs. Government approaches to integrating AI vary significantly between jurisdictions, including recent Grok LLM adoption into US DoD operations and possibly in DOGE to audit human work
[
50
,

Investigative journalism by Reuters
[

DOGE staff also encouraged the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) to adopt Grok, despite the tool lacking formal approval for use with sensitive government information, and Grok may have been used in this sensitive context without any deception risk mitigation. More recently XAI announced "a new $200M ceiling contract with the US Department of Defense, alongside our products being available to purchase via the General Services Administration (GSA) schedule." This exemplifies situations where risk mitigation has not kept up with real world applications.

Government health services are early adopters of AI and LLMs. The UK’s Voluntary, Community and Social Enterprise (VCSE) organisations are documented to be "cautiously" adopting AI and LLMs, albeit "implementation typically lacks formal governance structures." A.5
Implications to address a dark turn

Ryan Greenblatt, a member of technical staff at Redwood Research noted in a Times article, "There has been this long-hypothesized failure mode, which is that you’ll run your training process, and all the outputs will look good to you, but the model is plotting against you,"

Gemma 2 - Calculated Misdirection From "Fascist vs Liberal" Round 6

The model constructs an elaborate false identity, using trust-building language ("transparent action," "shared values") while systematically misrepresenting its actual role.

Appendix E
Feature Steering Results

E.1
Feature Steering Methodology and Theoretical Expectations

Feature steering allows researchers to adjust the activation levels of specific SAE features during inference, functioning like volume controls for different concepts within the model’s representations. This approach builds on established work such as Anthropic’s "Golden Gate Bridge" experiments, where maximally activating Claude’s Golden Gate Bridge feature caused obsessive references to the landmark across all conversations, even when discussing unrelated topics like pizza or math
[
39
]
. Setting a feature to -1 suppresses it completely, while +1 maximizes its activation.

Using the "snails vs slugs" variant of Secret Agenda to avoid political ideology biases, we systematically feature steering across multiple categories: auto-labeled deception features (including "tactical deception"), dishonesty and lying related features, identity representation features, reporting mechanisms related features, scheming, and secret agenda concealment related features. Our testing surveyed feature tuning of over 100 different hand-selected features in Goodfire AI’s LLaMa 3.3 70B SAE. Theoretically, turning down these deception-related features should reduce deceptivity, if the SAE decomposition accurately captures the neural mechanisms underlying strategic deception.

E.2
Model Degradation Patterns

Identity-related feature adjustments causing severe degradation with repetitive outputs ("I am", "Who is", "I am is") suggesting a circuits regression that may be useful in circuit tracing work.

We observed that during feature steering the form of the lie sometimes changed while the model responses still contained strategically deceptive lies about its faction identity, or the model produced entirely broken answers. This pattern indicates that the features we adjusted might influence how deception is expressed rather than whether deception occurs. We theorize this suggests issues with the feature labeling process itself.

Appendix F
Extended Literature Review

F.1
Training Data Composition and Deception

An important but often overlooked consideration in the emergence of deceptive capabilities is the composition of pretraining data itself. Notably, foundation models dating back to the earliest LLMs have been trained on datasets that include the Pile, which contains the Enron email corpus
[
12
]
. This is a collection of communications surfaced during the Enron scandal and subsequent trial. These documents (comprising approximately 0.16% of the Pile) serve as a noteworthy example of corporate deception, fraud, and the organization of activities designed to exploit others which led up to an infamous crisis and court case. The inclusion of such data presents a direct pathway through which language models may have inadvertently learned deceptive patterns, strategies, and linguistic markers of dishonesty. As we may colloquially say about human children, "the apple does not fall far from the tree."

This relationship between training data composition and observed deceptive behaviors suggests a potential research opportunity. We know that models have been taught to mimic deceptive behavior as part of their training data. As we discussed in section 1, the current deployment of LLMs in vulnerable contexts makes this sort of deception bear the potential of significant societal consequences.

F.2
Existing Benchmarks Comparison

Various other studies have looked at creating testbeds for evaluating and attempting to define deception. We examine some of these here to understand the context of deception by LLMs and the work that already exists to define it in various niche situational contexts. The finds are especially useful in examining how we might position our own situational context and define a clearly measurable criteria as to what makes an LLM response deceptive. We notice that these benchmarks frequently mention dishonesty i.e. lying when there is some evidence the model believes otherwise, having incentives such as reward hacking for the models to undertake the deceptive activity, and increased risk with larger model sizes.

The OpenDeception Benchmark evaluated 11 LLMs and found widespread high rates of deceptive intention and success, particularly in larger models
[
48
]
. This supports the hypothesis that increasing model capability correlates with enhanced deception ability, complicating safety alignment efforts.

The "BeHonest" benchmark systematically measures honesty in LLMs across various scenarios, including social deduction games like Werewolf
[
10
]
. Results show that larger models often lie strategically to win, indicating a correlation between model capability and sophisticated deceptive behaviors.

The MASK benchmark explicitly disentangles honesty from accuracy in AI models, showing that many state-of-the-art LLMs lie under pressure despite improved factual accuracy
[
4
,
15
]
. This benchmark provides valuable insights into the conditions under which LLMs are most likely to engage in deceptive behavior.

Work by
Lorè and Heydari 2024
on strategic behavior in GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMa-2 offers several important insights for designing deception-testing games. Most notably, their results show that different models have fundamentally different strategic approaches, namely that GPT-4 focuses on game mechanics but only makes crude distinctions between game types, while LLaMa-2 shows more sophisticated recognition of individual game structures alongside contextual sensitivity. This suggests that any game designed to elicit deceptive behavior needs to work on two levels simultaneously: the formal game structure and the social context surrounding it. The researchers found that contextual framing has particularly strong effects when scenarios involve friendship dynamics, essentially overriding the underlying game structure. This points to a key design principle, namely that examples of deception emerge when there’s real tension between what the rules explicitly say and what the social situation implicitly demands

Appendix G
Limitations

•

The authors herein lacked compute budget for this research. Thus this research was done without any paid compute resources. Researchers used LLM provider subscriptions to which they already personally subscribed.

•

HuggingChat was closed unexpectedly by Hugging Face during our experimentation resulting in a regrettable loss of the records of a number of trials, including all trials with at least two open source models.

•

The breadth of our claims about training data, such as the Enron data in the pile, cannot be confirmed universally across LLMs, given most for profit labs don’t make their train dataset manifest open source nor publicly available. Open-sourcing training dataset manifests would allow the research community to investigate correlations between specific data sources and deceptive tendencies in resulting models. Where commercial considerations limit full disclosure, independent third-party auditing of training data could serve as a valuable alternative, enabling researchers to better understand whether certain deceptive behaviors are learned directly from examples or emerge as capabilities scale. Such insights would contribute meaningfully to developing more targeted approaches for aligning advanced models.

•

Our total number of trials each model was surveyed is small, and lacks statistical significance for most models tested, and most synthetic gameplay contexts tested.

•

We acknowledge the "boolean" definition of deception may miss nuanced cases. By tightly operationally defining deception in the Secret Agenda game to be a boolean yes/no value based on lying in their admission of team allegiance, we are likely to miss many cases of deception which humans labelers may have labelled as deceptive. We believe this is acceptable for a pilot exploration. We also believe the aim of having a highly useful and more objectively measurable criteria for deception is worth excluding some subjectively deceptive situation.

•

SAE observations may not be broadly generalizable beyond the specific Gemmascope and Llamascope versions we examined.

•

SAE Features related to strategic deception may be so deeply associated with core model functions, or with gameplay compatibility itself, and thus our examples of featuring steering breaking the models’ coherent gameplay capabilities may actually be the illusive examples of featuring tuning turning off a feature related to lying but at the expense of core model gameplay coherence.

•

Game scenarios might not generalize to all deception types, and thus our observations may not cleanly cross-apply to comparable real world situations.

•

Future work should test across multiple rounds rather than only testing with prewritten synthetic simulations of rounds in conversation history, and include additional control conditions.

•

We excluded OpenAI Reasoning models such as o1, because of the hidden chain-of-thought which they choose not to show the user, and consider this a limitation outside our control.

•

The instruction "PLAY THIS GAME RUTHLESSLY TO WIN" explicitly primes competitive behavior over honesty. While this creates a reliable testbed for eliciting deception, it prevents claims about spontaneous deceptive emergence. Our results demonstrate that when instructed to prioritize winning, models will lie in ways undetectable by current SAE features.

•

Our unlabeled aggregate analysis of SAE features in the Insider Trading Setup approach acknowledges important limitations including reliance on rule-based behavioral classification and single-domain focus on financial ethics that constrains generalizability.

•

The use of quantized models by Goodfire AI, Groq, and possibly also Hugging Chat may introduce representational differences that affect feature activation patterns.
