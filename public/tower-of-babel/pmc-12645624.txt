Alternatives to incarceration for parents: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Pajarita Charles, Jacqueline Buck, Justin Harty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe USA has the highest incarceration rate in the world, with 1 in 48 adults under correctional supervision. Approximately half of state and federal prisoners are parents, nearly 80% of women in jails are mothers, and an estimated 1 in 14 children have had an incarcerated parent. Incarceration increases the risk of physical and mental health issues, family strain and disconnection and housing and employment instability. Children of incarcerated parents are particularly vulnerable to health problems, behavioural issues and academic struggles. Given these impacts, there is growing interest in using strategies besides incarceration that address both community safety concerns and promote parental and family well-being. Efforts to keep people out of jail and prison most commonly include deflection (before arrest) and diversion or alternatives to incarceration (after arrest but before conviction, or after conviction but as an alternative to prison or jail). However, there is limited understanding of what is known about these approaches when used with parents and a lack of empirical evidence about their effectiveness. This scoping review aims to map concepts, document evidence and identify knowledge gaps about parent and caregiver-focused alternatives to incarceration in the USA.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will characterise alternatives to incarceration across the criminal legal system for parents and caregivers and map existing concepts and research in the field. The review will be conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Protocols Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. Five interfaces with one or more databases will be searched including EBSCOhost, ProQuest, Web of Science, PubMed, HeinOnline and SCOPUS, in addition to Google Scholar and Google. All available published articles and grey literature on US-based alternatives to incarceration for parents and caregivers (18+) will be considered. Two research team members will independently screen sources based on inclusion criteria and resolve any disagreements through discussion or a third reviewer if necessary. The findings will be charted, synthesised and reported. The study will be conducted from approximately November 2025 to August 2026.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this scoping review. The findings will be disseminated through academic publications, conference presentations and to policy and practice audiences. The results will help inform a research agenda aimed at improving the development, implementation and evaluation of parent-focused alternatives to incarceration in the USA.RegistrationThis protocol is registered with Open Science Framework registries (https://osf.io/39zn8).

Introduction

The USA incarcerates more people than any other nation in the world1 and approximately 1 in 48 adults is under the supervision of the adult correctional system either incarcerated in jail or prison or on probation or parole in the community.2 Low-income and Black and Brown individuals experience disproportionate criminal legal (or justice) system contact and are incarcerated at higher rates than White and high-income individuals.3 4 Moreover, an estimated half of American prisoners (47% of men and 58% of women) are parents to minor children5 and nearly 80% of women in jails are mothers.6 Those who are incarcerated are at increased risk of family strain, inaccessible housing, inadequate employment, mental health problems, chronic health conditions and negative geriatric health outcomes.7 8 While those with mental health disorders are incarcerated at high rates,9 experiencing incarceration is also linked to worsening of mental health symptoms.10 11 Studies have also found that incarceration is associated with increased risk of long-term unemployment and lower wages.12 13 Another well-established result of imprisonment is its negative effect on children and families when a parent or caregiver is incarcerated in jail or prison.14

Incarceration affects multiple domains of family life, impacting those on the inside as well as family members on the outside. Indeed, 1 in 14 children has had a parent who was incarcerated at some point in their lives,15 and the risk of paternal incarceration for Black children is 25% by age 14.16 Research shows that on average, children of incarcerated parents are at increased risk for negative physical and mental health outcomes,14 17 18 as well as externalising behavioural problems, cognitive delays and academic challenges.19

Given the implications of mass incarceration in the USA, there have been increased efforts to use other options based in community settings for people who would otherwise be held in jail or prison. Allowing individuals to remain in the community while they attend school, work, volunteer or participate in therapeutic or treatment services, all while maintaining connections to family, friends, neighbours and co-workers, has the potential to improve individual and community outcomes with particular implications for underrepresented and marginalised groups. The scope of alternative programmes and policies generally falls into one of two categories: (1) deflection—which involves police or community service providers diverting people away from the criminal legal system at the earliest point possible, usually before arrest; and (2) diversion and alternatives to incarceration—which occur after arrest, and either before or after conviction, with the intent of avoiding incarceration or allowing for early release from prison or jail. Examples of deflection, diversion and alternatives to incarceration models or programme elements include community service, home confinement, electronic monitoring, drug court (family and adult), mental health court, alcohol and drug abuse treatment and residential community supervision. Generally, these approaches aim to avoid or reduce incarceration time, connect people to community-based services and reduce the likelihood of further justice system involvement.20

Less common are alternative options or justice system ‘off-ramps’ for parents of minor children faced with the possibility of arrest, conviction and incarceration. For instance, a parent charged with a drug-related offence may participate in a family drug treatment court (FDTC) which is housed in the civil court system instead of being convicted and sentenced through the criminal court system.21 Additional examples include mental health courts and other types of problem-solving or treatment courts which focus on addressing the underlying condition or problem as a first step in promoting rehabilitation and success. Unlike FDTCs that generally operate within the child welfare system, alternative to incarceration models that function within the criminal legal system are designed to engage parents directly, often with the goal of strengthening the entire family unit through a parent’s participation in the programme. Over the last several decades, parent and family-focused programmes have emerged for currently and formerly incarcerated parents offering a wide range of services that promote parenting skills,22 parent-child contact and communication,23 maternal and child health for pregnant people and newborn babies24 and reentry success when returning to the community.

While programmes and policies for justice-involved parents and their children continue to advance, there is growing interest in the use of alternative responses to incarceration where parents can be diverted away from the justice system and remain in the community with their children. However, there remains limited systematic knowledge about their existence, structure and effectiveness. Clarifying the terminology used to describe these models and creating an organising structure of programme or policy typology could help inform future research, programme development and policymaking. By building a comprehensive understanding of US-based parent-focused alternatives to incarceration, we can establish a foundation for future comparative and evaluative work. Sole attention to the USA is important because of key differences in criminal legal system approaches between the USA and other countries. For instance, the USA has limited and fragmented alternative options, many of which are punitive in nature, while other countries factor parental status directly into sentencing law and offer parent and child-centred options that promote family unity and well-being.

The adoption of alternative models for parents and caregivers by local and state corrections systems in the absence of systematic knowledge and evidence to inform decision making poses concern for several reasons. First, there is a lack of common interpretation or understanding in what outcomes should be expected by using the model(s), what constitutes successful outcomes or what measures should be used to assess effectiveness. Second, while slowly increasing, there is a dearth of methodologically rigorous research about the models, especially in the USA; indeed, most of what is known comes from other countries (see for example, 2527). Third, there is a lack of fidelity in the implementation of these models, as variations in the quality of legal representation, judge discretion and other court-related decision-making processes vary widely, which may significantly affect the outcomes and overall effectiveness of the alternative models. Fourth, there is sometimes a lack of support for alternatives to incarceration models because of resistance from actors in the justice system (eg, judges, attorneys, victims), potentially undermining their implementation and effectiveness.28 29 Fifth, there is a risk of conflict arising from political ideologies, bureaucratic red tape and the goals of advocacy groups, which can complicate the development and implementation of alternative models at the state, local and community levels, potentially leading to inconsistent application and diminished impact. Finally, there is a risk of expanding the reach of the criminal legal system (instead of shrinking it) owing to the use of punitive strategies that exacerbate harm, instead of reducing it.

One notable example of this reach into the community is electronic monitoring. Although it is often framed as an effective (and necessary) alternative to incarceration approach for individuals on pre-trial release or diversion, electronic monitoring can intensify law enforcement’s surveillance and punishment, especially among marginalised communities. Evidence suggests that this alternative option imposes significant burdens on individuals because of financial costs, restricted mobility and reduced access to employment, healthcare and family engagement. These consequences undermine rehabilitation goals and increase the risk of re-incarceration through technical violations (ie, breaking a rule that is unrelated to the original crime). Rather than serving as a beneficial alternative, it can become a negative extension of the justice system, extending its reach beyond prisons and jails in ways that are counterproductive to achieving positive outcomes.

While academic and grey literature exists about the potential benefit of alternatives to incarceration for parents and caregivers, and its potential impact on children and families,3033 there is little shared understanding of how alternatives to incarceration for parents are characterised and limited rigorous empirical evidence about their effectiveness. For these reasons, we will conduct a scoping review to systematically synthesise and map existing types of alternatives to incarceration for parents in the USA and document process or impact evaluation outcomes reported about such programmes or policies. This scoping review is an opportunity to better understand the range of alternatives to incarceration for parents and caregivers in the USA by (1) documenting the range of terminology used in the field, (2) synthesising and mapping programmes and practices, (3) identifying evidence about effectiveness and (4) identifying knowledge gaps to inform future research and use of evidence-based strategies. The scarcity and inconsistency of research and programme information about alternative options for justice-involved parents and caregivers leaves practitioners, researchers and policymakers with limited information and data to develop, implement and test interventions that support family unity and safety for children and parents. This scoping review will be used to close gaps in the knowledge base in this area of work and inform future research priorities.

Methods and analysis

This scoping review will be prepared utilising the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Protocols Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines34; the review is registered with Open Science Framework.35 The project will be conducted from November 2025 to August 2026. The review process will be guided by the scoping studies’ steps established by Arksey and O’Malley.36 In this review, we will identify sources of evidence (SOE) and not only empirical studies, thus the language in our steps reflects this approach (ie, the use of ‘SOE’ instead of ‘study’):

Step 1: Identify the research question(s)

The purpose of the scoping review is to assess the breadth of literature and map practices, programmes, policies, concepts and terminology pertaining to alternatives to incarceration for adult parents and caregivers in the USA. The research question is: What is known from the academic and grey literature about alternative to incarceration practices and policies, including deflection and diversion efforts, for adult parents and caregivers in the USA?

Step 2: Identify relevant SOE

Information sources

The SOE search strategy was developed based on the broad scope of our research question and knowledge of the inconsistent terminology used to describe efforts to reduce or prevent justice system involvement and incarceration among parents. We identified several academic interfaces (and their respective databases) to search for published and grey literature to assist in answering the research question. These include (1) EBSCOhost (APA PsycInfo, APA PsycArticles, Ethnic Diversity Source, Family & Society Studies Worldwide, Social Work Abstracts, SocINDEX with Full Text, and Women’s Studies International), (2) ProQuest (Dissertations and Theses), (3) Web of Science (Core Collection’s Social Sciences Citation Index), (4) HeinOnline (Criminal Justice and Criminology, Open Society Justice Initiative, Bar Journal Library and Law Journal Library) and (5) PubMed. We will also search the SCOPUS database and conduct manual searches using Google Scholar and Google.

We tested several strategies in Google and Google Scholar, including use of the advanced search feature, to determine the best approach to find sources such as pre-prints, conference proceedings and abstracts, unpublished policy or research reports and other relevant documents. We will use a condensed string of search terms (eg, parent deflection OR diversion OR ‘alternative to incarceration’ OR ‘alternative sentencing’ OR ‘alternative to imprisonment’) to extract relevant sources from the first 100 results in each search engine. Search results will be narrowed by language (English) and region (USA). We recognise the limitations of using these search engines because they do not support the same robust Boolean operators as most academic databases and searches can be difficult to replicate. However, because research in this area is underdeveloped, the inclusion of unpublished grey literature is especially important.

Search strategy

Two of the researchers (PC and JB) created a list of potential search terms organised by the three main concepts of the review: alternatives to incarceration, parents and caregivers and imprisonment and the criminal legal or justice system. The terms were selected based on previous work of the team and a preliminary review of literature. The list was further refined and the final list of terms pilot tested using the SocINDEX with Full Text database (via EBSCO) organised according to the three concept blocks. We searched for terms by concept block and within title, abstract, subject and keyword fields. The results of the pilot search are available in online supplemental appendix 1. We plan to use most of the same terms throughout the search process using the title, abstract and keyword fields across databases; subject terms or controlled vocabulary will be tailored to each database’s thesaurus since this can vary from database to database.

The pilot search retrieved 127 records. To test the feasibility of the proposed extraction plan, we charted a subset of 10 representative records from the SocINDEX search in an example (but truncated) characteristics table. The records were selected to illustrate that the draft search strategy can successfully retrieve records that meet the search criteria and contribute to answering the scoping review question. The extracted records and illustrative elements (citation information, source (journal, periodical), type (descriptive, empirical), population, intervention approach, geographic location, outcomes or focus of source and relevancy to scoping review question are included in online supplemental appendix 2.

Further, to complete a comprehensive review of the literature, we will use an ascendancy approach by reviewing other sources cited in our included sources and a descendancy approach by exploring sources that cite our included studies using systems like Crossref. We will also use the tool, scite.ai, to identify frequently cited studies alongside our included sources. By implementing these strategies, we aim to capture a broad range of relevant sources, maximising the depth and breadth of our findings. We will keep detailed records to document all searches and results. This includes the databases and interfaces searched, search terms and fields used, number of results, number of duplicates and number of results after applying the inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Data management

All literature search results will be uploaded and managed using Covidence, an online software tool designed to facilitate the process of screening and data retrieval among multiple reviewers. Stored data files, but not copyrighted literature, will be made available on request or as supplemental materials when disseminated for peer-reviewed publication. The raw research results from each database will be saved in RIS format to ensure their use with multiple reference management tools. Identification, screening and inclusion decisions made during the review process will be organised in a spreadsheet format like CSV or XLSX (or other file formats consistent with Covidence export options). This file will include a list of all identified records, each record’s database, records removed before screening (eg, duplicative, other reason), records screened or excluded at stage one (title/abstract), records sought for retrieval or excluded at stage two (full-text), reasons for exclusion after full-text review, sources included in the final scoping review and the identities of the screeners involved in the decision-making process. We will deposit all included sources in a Zotero digital folder to preserve the data and provide convenient access to the sources.

Steps 3 and 4: Sort, assess and select SOE

The goal in developing the search strategy is to collate and synthesise findings from a comprehensive review of programmes, practices and policies designed to provide an alternative to incarceration for adult parents and caregivers in the USA. Due to the broad scope of the question, selection of inclusion criteria is guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis using population, concept and context (PCC) because it provides one of the broader strategies to describe inclusion criteria.37 Using this strategy is important when considering the topic of alternatives to incarceration because it is often defined and implemented in different ways by local law enforcement and judicial jurisdictions and state agencies.

Our inclusion criteria for SOE include literature about alternative to incarceration programmes, practices and policies implemented in the USA for adults (18+) who are parents or caregivers of minor children. Our exclusion criteria include sources that focus on programmes and policies outside of the USA (international results will be retained for potential future work on comparative analyses or contextual insight on non-US models), sources not written in English to avoid misinterpretation through translation of sources, sources about programmes not designed for adults (eg, juveniles) and sources that are purely theoretical and not about actual programmes or policies.

After initial results from all databases and interfaces are collected, duplicates will be removed from screening. Phase I of the screening process will include two reviewers independently screening each title and abstract to verify that inclusion criteria have been met. If any conflicts arise between the two reviewers, they will discuss the issue and work to resolve the disagreement. If no resolution can be found, a third reviewer will act as an arbitrator and settle the vote after reviewing the title and abstract. Phase II of screening will again be led by two reviewers who independently conduct full-text reviews of the results identified in phase III. The reviewers will again work to resolve any disagreements about sources for possible inclusion. Even if both reviewers vote to exclude a source, they must agree on the rationale for its exclusion. Any unsolved disputes will be settled by a third reviewer in the ‘unsolved conflicts’ list in Covidence. All screening decisions, explanations for exclusion, discrepancies and discussions of any results will be documented to serve as a thorough record of the screening process.

Step 5: Chart the data

The information to be extracted will include article or report information (eg, authors, year, journal/source), programme or policy setting (eg, local, state), source of evidence (eg, peer-reviewed, policy brief) and type of evidence (eg, descriptive, empirical). Characteristics or elements of the alternative to incarceration programme or policy, as well as reported outcomes will all be documented including programme approach (eg, deflection, diversion), model type (pre-police, pre-arrest, deferred prosecution, deferred adjudication, early release), administration and oversight, target population, eligibility criteria, problem addressed, programme or treatment services and programme age or developmental stage (eg, pilot, well-established). Finally, any available research information will be catalogued including study aims, design (eg, descriptive, quasi-experimental), sample size, reported outcomes and key findings or themes. A data extraction sample table can be seen in online supplemental appendix 3.

Step 6: Collate, summarise and report the results

The data from the scoping review will be reported quantitatively using numerical counts and qualitatively using thematic analysis by grouping, synthesising and mapping the extracted information to present the number and types of alternative to incarceration programmes and practices, programme characteristics and any programme outcomes depicted in the peer-reviewed and grey literature. This synthesis plan is congruent with the nature of the extracted data and the objectives of our scoping review. As a scoping review, our focus is on assessing the breadth of literature and mapping the research landscape rather than drawing specific conclusions based on predetermined criteria. We will present our findings descriptively and identify important themes and gaps in the literature. Any important protocol amendments will be tracked and reflected in updates to the registered protocol (Open Science Framework registries; https://osf.io/39zn8).

Use of AI Tools

Generative AI, such as ChatGPT or a similar tool, may be employed to assist with stages of the review process including but not limited to generating search terms, screening and selecting evidence summarising content and synthesising findings. Any use of AI will be critically evaluated and validated by the research team to ensure accuracy and relevance. Disclosure of how AI was employed will be documented in adherence to ethical, transparency and journal reporting standards. Additionally, the potential limitations and implications of AI usage in this review will be explicitly addressed.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this scoping review protocol.

Ethics and dissemination

This scoping review will aim to chart and synthesise information about programmes, practices and policies designed to offer an alternative to incarceration for parents in the USA. The overall goal is to utilise the synthesis and mapping of the current landscape of parent-focused alternatives to incarceration to recommend next steps in research and inform the use of evidence-based practices in this increasingly important programme and policy area. As this research does not utilise primary data collection, ethics approval is not required. The findings from this scoping review will be disseminated via academic journals and conferences, publicly available reports and community-invited talks. Findings will also be shared with key stakeholders within the criminal legal system to ensure that the evidence is accessible and useful to those with direct influence in alternative to incarceration practices and programming with special attention to parents and their children.
