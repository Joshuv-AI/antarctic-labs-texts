Do Asian patients with HCC derive greater benefit from anti-PD-L1 plus anti-CTLA-4 therapy?
Authors: Atsushi Ono, C. Nelson Hayes, Masataka Tsuge, Shiro Oka
Published: 2025

In Japan and Western countries, hepatitis C virus (HCV) is the predominant cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), whereas in East and Southeast Asia, hepatitis B virus (HBV) is more common (1). Multiple clinical trials have reported differences in treatment efficacy based on etiology and geographic region. For tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), a subgroup analysis of the SHARP trial (sorafenib) showed that patients with HBV-related HCC had a higher hazard ratio (HR) for overall survival (OS) compared to those with HCV-related HCC when compared against placebo (0.76 vs. 0.50) (2). In the RESORCE trial (regorafenib), there was a trend toward improved OS, progression-free survival (PFS), and time to progression (TTP) in Asian patients (3). In the REACH-2 trial (ramucirumab), the HRs for OS were 0.65 in Japanese patients, 0.83 in non-Japanese Asian patients, and 0.76 in patients from Western countries and Australia (4). In the CELESTIAL trial (cabozantinib), no OS benefit was observed in Asian patients (HR =1.01), while the overall population had an HR of 0.76 (5).

A meta-analysis of three phase III trials involving immunotherapy (CheckMate 459, Keynote 240, and IMbrave150) demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of programmed death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors was more pronounced in Asian patients, excluding Japan (6). Patients from Asia (excluding Japan) experienced greater survival benefit, and the benefit was more pronounced in those with HBV or HCV infection compared to non-viral etiologies.

Additionally, a meta-analysis of eight phase III trials (ORIENT-32, CheckMate-459, IMbrave150, COSMIC-312, LEAP-002, SHR-1210-III-310, HIMALAYA, and RATIONALE-301) found that the pooled HR for OS was lowest in the HBV subgroup [HR =0.71; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.65–0.79; P<0.001], compared with the overall population and the HCV (HR =0.80; 95% CI: 0.69–0.93; P=0.003) and non-viral subgroups (HR =0.87; 95% CI: 0.77–0.97; P=0.011). The HBV subgroup also showed the lowest risk for PFS (HR =0.53; 95% CI: 0.46–0.61; P<0.001), compared to the HCV (HR =0.65; 95% CI: 0.46–0.90; P=0.01) and non-viral subgroups (HR =0.81; 95% CI: 0.63–1.05; P=0.11) (7). However, it should be noted that in seven of these eight studies, sorafenib was used as the comparator drug, and its limited efficacy in HBV-related HCC may have led to a relatively lower HR in favor of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).

These findings highlight the importance of considering regional and/or etiological differences in the design and interpretation of future clinical trials.

The outcomes in the Asian subgroup of the phase III randomized HIMALAYA study of tremelimumab plus durvalumab for unresectable HCC were recently reported by Lau et al. (8). Notably, their analysis included a dedicated Asian subgroup (including Hong Kong, India, Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, and Vietnam) and further stratifies outcomes for a Hong Kong + Taiwan subgroup. The latter group is characterized by a high proportion of individuals of Chinese ethnicity with HBV-related HCC. The results show that the HRs for OS improved progressively from global (0.78; 96.02% CI: 0.65–0.93) to Asian (0.68; 96.02% CI: 0.52–0.89) to Hong Kong and Taiwan (0.44; 96.02% CI: 0.26–0.77) in comparison of the STRIDE (single tremelimumab regular interval durvalumab) regimen relative to sorafenib. Similarly, objective response rates (ORRs) increased across groups: global 20.1%, Asian 28.2%, and Hong Kong and Taiwan 33.9%.

For the durvalumab monotherapy arm, HRs for OS again followed the same trend: global 0.78, Asian 0.68, and Hong Kong and Taiwan 0.44. ORRs were 17.0%, 18.6%, and 23.8%, respectively.

Conversely, the disease control rate (DCR) for sorafenib decreased with increasing HBV prevalence: global 60.7%, Asian 53.2%, and Hong Kong and Taiwan 48.8%. These findings suggest that the efficacy of ICIs improves with a higher prevalence of HBV or with Chinese ethnicity, while the efficacy of sorafenib declines, resulting in greater separation of HRs.

In terms of adverse events (AEs), the incidence of grade 3/4 treatment-related AEs decreased from global (25.8%) to Asian (19.9%) to Hong Kong and Taiwan (17.9%).

These results suggest that in comparisons where sorafenib is the control arm, the combination of anti-PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies may confer a particularly high therapeutic benefit in HBV-related HCC, while being less effective in non-viral etiologies. The high benefit observed in Chinese-majority populations suggests that regional or ethnic factors may contribute to treatment responsiveness.

Although several prior meta-analyses have also shown that ICI therapy offers greater benefit over TKI therapy in HBV-related HCC, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. One possibility is that the limited efficacy of sorafenib in HBV-related HCC may have contributed to the relatively better performance of ICIs.

However, it is also possible that the ICIs themselves are more effective in Asian populations than in global populations.

The tumor immune microenvironment may differ according to the etiology of the underlying liver disease (9). In addition, genomic alterations in HCC vary by region and etiology. For example, HBV-related HCC is characterized by HBV integration into the host genome.

Aristolochic acid (AA), a potent mutagenic compound found in aristolochia and asarum plants (used in traditional Chinese medicine), has been associated with a characteristic mutational signature identified through next-generation sequencing in HCCs from mailand China, Taiwan, Vietnam, and Southeast Asia. A large study of 1,400 HCCs across different regions showed that AA-associated mutational signatures were present in 78% of HCCs from Taiwan, 47% from mainland China, 29% from Southeast Asia, 13% from Korea, 2.7% from Japan, 4.8% from North America, and 1.7% from Europe (10).

Whether these mutations influence neo-antigen immunogenicity and enhance ICI efficacy warrants future investigation.

Regarding the host genetic background, polymorphisms in PD-L1 have been reported to influence the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapies in other cancer types (11-13). Racial or ethnic differences in genetic background might therefore also contribute to variations in treatment response.

The composition of the human gut microbiota has been implicated in modulating the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs (14). Importantly, gut microbiota profiles are known to differ across racial and ethnic populations, likely influenced by variations in dietary habits and other lifestyle-related factors (15). These inter-ethnic differences in gut microbial composition may, at least in part, contribute to the observed disparities in clinical outcomes of ICI therapy across racial and ethnic groups.

Moreover, it remains possible that other, as yet unidentified, lifestyle or environmental factors may also influence therapeutic response.

The influence of etiology, geography, and host genetics on the efficacy of immunotherapy is not only of academic interest but also crucial for informing therapeutic strategies. Understanding population-specific differences may help identify mechanisms of resistance and lead to the development of more effective therapies.

In contrast, the CheckMate 9DW trial evaluating nivolumab plus ipilimumab demonstrated an OS benefit compared with sorafenib or lenvatinib, with favorable results observed in both Asian and non-Asian subgroups, and no clear superiority identified among patients with HBV infection (16). Interestingly, particularly favorable outcomes were achieved in Japanese patients, in whom HBV is not the predominant etiology (17). These findings stand in contrast to the HIMALAYA trial, where the greatest benefit was observed in cohorts with a high prevalence of HBV.

Thus, while further research is required before concluding that immunotherapy may be more effective in HBV-infected patients, the insights presented by Lau and colleagues provide valuable perspectives and justify continued investigation into the potential impact of etiology and regional differences on treatment responsiveness.
