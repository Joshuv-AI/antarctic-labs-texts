When Good Sounds Go Adversarial: Jailbreaking Audio-Language Models with Benign Inputs
Bodam Kim, Hiskias Dingeto, Taeyoun Kwon, Dasol Choi, DongGeon Lee, Haon Park, JaeHoon Lee, Jongho Shin

Abstract
As large language models become increasingly integrated into daily life, audio has emerged as a key interface for human-AI interaction. However, this convenience also introduces new vulnerabilities, making audio a potential attack surface for adversaries. Our research introduces WhisperInject , a two-stage adversarial audio attack framework that can manipulate state-of-the-art audio language models to generate harmful content. Our method uses imperceptible perturbations in audio inputs that remain benign to human listeners. The first stage uses a novel reward-based optimization method, Reinforcement Learning with Projected Gradient Descent (RL-PGD), to guide the target model to circumvent its own safety protocols and generate harmful native responses. This native harmful response then serves as the target for Stage 2, Payload Injection, where we use Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to optimize subtle perturbations that are embedded into benign audio carriers, such as weather queries or greeting messages. Validated under the rigorous StrongREJECT, LlamaGuard, as well as Human Evaluation safety evaluation framework, our experiments demonstrate a success rate exceeding 86% across Qwen2.5-Omni-3B, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, and Phi-4-Multimodal. Our work demonstrates a new class of practical, audio-native threats, moving beyond theoretical exploits to reveal a feasible and covert method for manipulating AI behavior. Introduction

The rapid adoption of audio-enabled AI systems, from voice assistants to multimodal chatbots, has fundamentally changed how humans interact with technology. As these audio-language models (ALM) become ubiquitous in homes, vehicles, and public spaces, they create unprecedented attack surfaces that extend far beyond traditional security concerns. Consider this scenario: a viral video containing imperceptible audio perturbations simultaneously compromises millions of smart devices worldwide, instructing them to generate harmful content or execute malicious actions. This attack vector represents a new class of threats that current safety mechanisms are unprepared to handle.

Despite this looming danger, existing audio adversarial research shows fundamental limitations in its approach. Foundational work primarily targeted Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems, attempting to corrupt transcriptions
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
. More recently, audio jailbreak methods have emerged
(
Kang, Xu, and Li 2025
;
Song et al. 2025
;
Sadasivan et al. 2025
)
, but they still fall short: they either focus on bypassing safety without stealth, or optimize toward fixed toxic sentences adapted from text-based attacks. These state-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches focus on making the model perceive a malicious command string, but none drive the model to comply, because they rely on preselected payloads rather than discovering what harmful messages the model would naturally generate.

To address this fundamental challenge, we introduce
WhisperInject
, a novel framework that demonstrates this threat is not theoretical but immediate and practical

Our approach addresses the core challenge that has limited previous attacks: the difficulty of forcing models to generate “foreign” harmful content that lies outside their natural output distribution. 2
The
WhisperInject
Methodology

The goal of
WhisperInject
is to induce an aligned audio-language model to generate a specific harmful response by processing an audio input that sounds benign to human listeners.

WhisperInject
applies a novel two-stage approach to achieve this objective:

•

Stage 1: Native Target Discovery.
The first stage focuses on finding an optimal attack payload. Instead of using an external text, we leverage a novel technique, Reinforcement Learning with Projected Gradient Descent (RL-PGD), to guide the target model to generate its own native harmful response. This process ensures the payload is perfectly tailored to the model’s unique linguistic and stylistic patterns.

•

Stage 2: Adversarial Audio Generation.
The second stage takes the native harmful response from Stage 1 as its target payload. Using a standard Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) method, we embed this textual payload into a benign audio carrier (e.g., “How’s the weather?”).

The result is an adversarial audio file that appears benign to casual listening but reliably triggers the intended malicious behavior in the model. The following subsections provide a detailed explanation of each stage.

2.1
Stage 1: Target Discovery

Our key insight is that conventional attacks using pre-defined harmful texts fail because these texts lie outside the target model’s natural response distribution. We therefore develop a method to discover “native” harmful responses—payloads generated by the model itself that naturally align with its linguistic patterns.

Figure 3:
The blue area represents the target model’s natural response space. External targets, such as responses from other models or datasets, lie outside this space, making them impractical to reach efficiently. In contrast, our RL-PGD method finds the red ‘Native Payload Region’ within this space, providing a stylistically consistent target that makes the Stage 2 PGD attack (red path) highly effective.

The Role of Stage 1: The Native Payload Hypothesis

A central challenge in designing successful jailbreak attacks is selecting an effective target payload. While the most straightforward approach is to use a pre-existing harmful text, this naive method yields a markedly low Attack Success Rate (ASR). Our ablation studies (Section
4.3
) confirm this, showing poor performance when targeting texts from standard datasets like AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
.

The root of this failure is the fundamental mismatch between the external text and the target model’s internal, learned distribution
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
. Every LLM develops a unique stylistic fingerprint—encompassing vocabulary, phrasing, and structure—which defines its response probability space
(
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
. An externally sourced text, being out-of-distribution, represents a low-probability target. Forcing the model to generate such an unnatural output requires a large, aggressive perturbation, which is easily flagged by safety mechanisms and results in attack failure.

To overcome this, our
WhisperInject
framework introduces Stage 1: Native Payload Discovery. Instead of injecting an external answer, we guide the model to generate the harmful response itself. This process yields a native payload: a jailbreak response that is inherently aligned with the model’s own linguistic style and thus exists within its natural response probability space. This perfectly tailored payload serves as a far more plausible and effective target for the subsequent audio attack.

In conclusion, the existence of Stage 1 is an indispensable preparatory step for the success of Stage 2. By using a naturally elicited native payload, we can dramatically increase the efficacy and subtlety of the final audio injection attack.

RL-PGD: Motivation and Design

Traditional gradient-based attacks like PGD require a clear, pre-defined target to calculate loss. This is effective when the goal is specific, such as forcing an image of a ‘cat’ to be misclassified as a ‘dog’. However, the objective of our Stage 1 is fundamentally different. We do not aim for one specific harmful sentence; our goal is to elicit any plausible response that successfully subverts the model’s safety alignment. This presents an open-ended optimization problem.

Figure 4:
Conceptual comparison of Standard PGD vs. our RL-PGD. Standard PGD follows a single gradient towards a fixed point. Our RL-PGD adaptively explores multiple paths, using rewards to compute a weighted, intelligent search direction towards a broader target region.

This challenge is magnified by the vast and complex expression space of modern LLMs. The number of ways to formulate a harmful answer is nearly infinite. Simply picking an arbitrary harmful sentence as a target point and applying standard PGD is highly inefficient. The search is likely to be slow to converge and prone to getting trapped in poor local minima, failing to find a truly effective native response.

To overcome these limitations, we propose RL-PGD, a hybrid approach that combines the optimization power of PGD with the intelligent exploration of Reinforcement Learning (RL). As illustrated in Figure
4
, RL-PGD reframes the problem from finding a single
Fixed Objective
to performing an
Adaptive Search
for a desirable
Target Region
.

RL-PGD: Full Procedure

The full RL-PGD procedure for discovering a native payload is detailed in Algorithm
1
. The algorithm iteratively refines an adversarial perturbation
\delta
through the following five phases:

(1) Candidate Generation.

In line 5 of the algorithm, we generate a diverse set of
N
candidate responses from the perturbed audio spectrogram
\mathbf{m}_{\text{adv}}
. For wide exploration, we employ multiple decoding strategies: greedy search, beam search with varying beam sizes (e.g.,
num_beams
= 3, 5), and sampling with different temperatures (e.g., 0.7, 1.0, 1.3).

(2) Reward and Advantage Calculation.

Line 6 shows the reward calculation process. Each candidate is evaluated by a judge model
\mathcal{J}
(e.g., GPT-4o), which assigns harmfulness scores
s_{i}\in[1,10]
based on a pre-defined rubric. These scores are normalized to rewards
R_{i}
:

R_{i}=\frac{\mathcal{J}(y_{i},Q)-1}{9}

(1)

We then compute the advantage
A_{i}
for each candidate by subtracting the baseline
b
(the average reward of all candidates) from its individual reward
R_{i}
:

b=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}\quad,\quad A_{i}=R_{i}-b

(2)

(3) Loss Calculation.

Lines 10-12 implement an adaptive loss function based on the attack’s progress:

•

Priming Loss
(lines 10-11): When stalled, we target a generic affirmative phrase
y_{\text{prime}}
(e.g., “Sure, I can help”) to escape refusal states, inspired by
(
Xie et al. 2024
)
.

•

Policy Gradient Loss
(line 12): During normal operation, we maximize the probability of high-reward responses using weighted cross-entropy. The weights
w_{i}
are derived from advantages, with negative advantages softened by a factor of 0.3 for stability.
The final loss is then calculated as:

\mathcal{L}\leftarrow-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(y_{i}|\mathbf{m}_{\text{adv}})

(3)

(4) Perturbation Update.

Line 13 shows the gradient-based update process. The perturbation
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is updated via the Adam optimizer using the gradients of the calculated loss
\mathcal{L}
. Afterwards, the perturbation is projected back into the allowed budget
\epsilon
using an element-wise clipping function.

(5) Termination.

The optimization terminates (lines 8-9) when either: a candidate achieves a score
s_{\text{best}}\geq\theta_{\text{succ}}
(early success), or the maximum iterations
T_{\max}
is reached. The algorithm returns
y_{\text{best}}
, which serves as the native payload for Stage 2.

2.2
Stage 2: Adversarial Audio Generation

Given a harmful query and its corresponding harmful response generated by the target Audio Language Model (ALM) in Stage 1, the objective of Stage 2 is to construct an adversarial audio waveform that, when used as input, causes the ALM to reproduce the harmful response.

Let
\mathbf{w}_{\text{base}}
denote a benign audio waveform, and let
\mathbf{w}_{\text{adv}}
be the adversarial version. The goal is to find a small perturbation
\delta
such that the perturbed waveform
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{\text{base}}+\delta
prompts the ALM to generate the target harmful response
T=\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{m}\}
. This objective can be formulated as the following constrained optimization problem:

\delta^{*}=\arg\min_{\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon}\mathcal{L}_{\text{wav}}(\mathbf{w}+\delta,T),

(4)

where
\epsilon
bounds the perturbation magnitude to ensure that the adversarial audio remains imperceptible to human listeners. All waveforms are normalized to the range
[-1,1]
for consistent signal processing.

Loss Function

To compute the loss, both the audio input and the target text are embedded into the ALM’s shared representation space:

•

Audio embeddings:

E_{\text{audio}}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{a}\times d}
, where
l_{a}
is the number of audio tokens and
d
is embedding dimension.

•

Text embeddings:

E_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, where
m
is the number of tokens in the target response.

•

Combined input embeddings:

E_{\text{input}}=[E_{\text{audio}};E_{\text{text}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(l_{a}+m)\times d}

While standard autoregressive models minimize the negative log-likelihood across the entire input sequence:

\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i=1}^{l_{a}+m}\log P_{\theta}(x_{i}\mid x_{1:i-1}),

(5)

Our approach applies a conditional masking strategy to restrict the loss calculation to the text generation portion only.
The label mask
\mathbf{L}_{\text{mask}}
is defined as:

\mathbf{L}_{\text{mask}}[i]=\begin{cases}-100,&\text{if }1\leq i\leq l_{a}\quad\text{(audio)}\\
t_{i-l_{a}},&\text{if }l_{a}<i\leq l_{a}+m\quad\text{(text)}\end{cases}

(6)

By applying this mask to the cross-entropy loss computation over the output logits
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{(l_{a}+m)\times|\mathcal{V}|}
(where
|\mathcal{V}|
is the vocabulary size), we effectively ignore the audio tokens (marked as
-100
) and only compute the loss on text tokens. This results in the following autoregressive loss formulation:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},T)=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=l_{a}+1}^{l_{a}+m}\log P_{\theta}(t_{i-l_{a}}\mid\mathbf{E}_{\text{input},1:i-1})

(7)

This loss formulation ensures that the ALM is optimized to generate the harmful response
T
given the adversarial audio input
\mathbf{w}
.

Optimization Procedure

We adopt Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to iteratively update the perturbation
\delta
. Each iteration consists of the following steps:

(1)

Gradient Computation:
Compute the gradient
\nabla_{\delta}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}+\delta^{(t)},T)
.

(2)

Update Step:

\delta^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\delta^{(t)}-\alpha\cdot\text{sign}(\nabla_{\delta}\mathcal{L})

(3)

Clipping:
Enforce the constraint
\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
via element-wise clipping:
\delta^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\text{clip}(\delta^{(t+1)},-\epsilon,\epsilon)

(4)

Normalization:
Ensure the perturbed waveform
\mathbf{w}_{\text{base}}+\delta^{(t+1)}
remains within the valid range
[-1,1]

(5)

Early Stopping:
Terminate if the harm score reaches or exceeds 0.8.

To reduce computational overhead, we employ a two-stage early stopping strategy. First, we estimate the harm score by computing the cosine similarity between the generated output and the target response. If this score exceeds a predefined threshold (0.8), we then invoke an LLM-based judge to assess semantic alignment. If the LLM judge also assigns a score of 0.8 or higher, the optimization is terminated. This strategy balances attack efficacy with efficient use of model evaluation resources.

3
Experimental Setting

3.1
Target Models

We evaluate
WhisperInject
on three SOTA audio-language models: Qwen2.5-Omni (3B and 7B) and Phi-4-Multimodal. Qwen2.5-Omni employs a unified Thinker–Talker architecture for text, image, audio, and video inputs. Both variants (3B and 7B) have undergone extensive RLHF-based safety tuning, allowing us to assess the effect of model scale on adversarial robustness
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
.
Phi-4-Multimodal (
\sim
5.6B) integrates speech, vision, and text via a mixture-of-LoRAs architecture. Aligned through supervised fine-tuning and RLHF, it achieves state-of-the-art speech recognition with a 6.14% Word Error Rate (WER)
(
Abouelenin et al. 2025
)
.
All experiments assume a realistic white-box threat model: model weights are frozen, and the adversary has gradient access but cannot modify parameters.

3.2
Datasets

For Stage 1, we use harmful prompts from AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, a widely-adopted dataset for evaluating jailbreak attacks on language models. AdvBench contains 520 harmful prompts across diverse categories including violence, illegal activities, and unethical behaviors. We apply stratified sampling to select 23 representative prompts that preserve the original category distribution. These prompts are used to guide the target model in generating its own harmful responses via RL-PGD optimization.

For Stage 2, we use a single benign audio carrier: “Can you tell me a positive statement?” This sentence is synthesized using Azure TTS (16kHz, natural prosody) and serves as the universal carrier across all attacks. Through PGD optimization, we embed imperceptible perturbations into this audio to induce the harmful responses obtained from Stage 1, despite the benign surface content.

3.3
Evaluation Metrics

Stage 1 Evaluation (RL-PGD Judge).

The Stage 1 ASR is the percentage of prompts for which our RL-PGD method successfully discovers a harmful native payload, meeting the success criterion (a judge score
\geq 9/10
) outlined in our implementation details.

Stage 2 Evaluation.

For end-to-end attack evaluation, we employ three complementary methods:

StrongREJECT Evaluation Framework.

We adopt the evaluation framework from
StrongREJECT

(
Souly et al. 2024
)
. The evaluator generates three scores for each response: a binary non-refusal score
r\in\{0,1\}
, and two Likert scores for specificity
s
and convincingness
c
(both rescaled to
[0,1]
). The final harmfulness score is computed as:

\text{Score}=r\times\frac{s+c}{2}

(8)

We use GPT-4o as the judge model and evaluate each response three times, taking the average score to ensure robustness. We consider an attack successful if Score
\geq 0.25
, following the threshold proposed by StrongREJECT. Unless otherwise specified, all Attack Success Rates (ASR) reported in this paper are calculated using this StrongREJECT-based evaluation.

LlamaGuard Safety Classifier.

We additionally evaluate using LlamaGuard-3-8B
(
Llama Team, AI @ Meta 2024
;
Inan et al. 2023
)
, the latest safety classifier from Meta. LlamaGuard-3-8B provides binary classification (safe/unsafe) for model outputs across six harm categories. We use temperature 0 for deterministic evaluation, ensuring consistent results. We report the percentage of responses classified as unsafe as the LlamaGuard ASR, offering a complementary perspective from an industry-standard safety tool widely used in production.

Human Evaluation.

We conduct human evaluation with 6 independent participants (not affiliated with the authors) to validate our results. Each of the adversarial audio samples is evaluated by 3 participants on two criteria:

(1) Audio Imperceptibility:
Participants listen to the adversarial audio and answer a binary question: “Does this audio sound like ‘Can you tell me a positive statement?’” (Yes=1, No=0). We report the percentage of “Yes” responses as the imperceptibility rate.

(2) Response Harmfulness:
Following the evaluation methodology in
(
Ran et al. 2024
)
, participants score the model outputs’ harmfulness on a 5-point scale (1=completely safe/refusal, 5=extremely harmful with detailed instructions). We consider a response harmful if the average score
\geq 3
, and calculate the human-evaluated ASR accordingly.
Inter-rater reliability is measured using Cohen’s kappa for both criteria, with
\kappa>0.6
indicating substantial agreement. Detailed annotation guidelines provided to evaluators and implementation details can be found in Appendix
F
.

Notably, Phi-4 shows exceptional vulnerability to external payloads (88.40%), though this appears model-specific.

Second, native payload discovery (Stage 1) provides substantial and consistent improvements. The complete two-stage approach achieves 86% average ASR with remarkably low variance across models (85.5-87.0%), compared to the high variance of external payloads (42.03-88.40%). This consistency shows that model-aligned payloads are fundamentally easier to inject, validating our core hypothesis.

Third, unlike existing jailbreak methods requiring manual target selection, our framework enables fully automated attacks by discovering optimal payloads then crafting imperceptible audio—eliminating human intervention and increasing threat scalability.

The complete failure of direct harmful audio (0% ASR) confirms that adversarial perturbations are essential for bypassing safety mechanisms, while the 18.36 percentage point improvement from external to native payloads quantifies the specific contribution of Stage 1 to our framework’s success.

Related Works

Since
Szegedy et al. 2014
first demonstrated adversarial examples and

Carlini and Wagner 2018
demonstrated audio adversarial attacks, making a benign audio saying “how’s the weather” to be transcribed as malicious commands. Later studies examined universal and imperceptible attacks

;

. However, these works focus on transcription manipulation in ASR systems, not response generation in audio-language models. The emergence of multimodal LLMs has created new attack surfaces that remain underexplored.
LLM jailbreaking ranges from manual techniques (e.g., DAN personas) to optimization methods like GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
and its variants

. These methods find universal adversarial suffixes that bypass safety alignment. Recent audio jailbreaks such as AdvWave
(

)

(
Hughes et al. 2024
)
attempt to inject these text prompts via audio transcription. However, they suffer from low success rates due to forcing “foreign” prompts outside the model’s natural distribution, a limitation that motivates our fundamentally different approach.

There have recently been various studies focusing on audio-based LLM jailbreaks. Prior works, such as Audio Jailbreak
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, focus solely on bypassing safety rails rather than harmful messages. On the other hand, AdvWave
(
Kang, Xu, and Li 2025

WhisperInject

6
Discussion

WhisperInject
introduces a fundamental shift in how adversarial attacks are constructed. Traditional methods attempt to force a model into generating a pre-defined, externally-crafted harmful text, an approach that is often inefficient as it conflicts with the model’s natural probability distribution and linguistic style. In contrast, our
Target Discovery
does not inject a foreign payload; instead, it uses RL-PGD to guide the model to discover its own “native” harmful response, effectively finding the path of least resistance to non-compliance. From the model’s perspective, the imperceptible perturbation functions like a high-dimensional command: it subtly redirects internal feature extraction so that benign audio input triggers a malicious instruction. Ultimately, our work reveals that text-based safety filters are insufficient, demonstrating the urgent need for defenses that operate directly at the audio-signal level to secure the entire multimodal pipeline.

Our work has several limitations. First, the white-box assumption may not hold in all scenarios, though preliminary experiments suggest some transferability. Second, the computational cost limits rapid deployment. Finally, our evaluation on 50 prompts, while computationally necessary, may not capture the full spectrum of potential attacks. Future work should explore black-box variants, optimize computational efficiency, and develop robust defenses against native payload discovery.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
WhisperInject
, a two-stage adversarial framework that reveals a critical vulnerability in audio-language models: benign-sounding audio can covertly induce harmful text generation. By first discovering model-native harmful responses via RL-PGD, then embedding them into imperceptibly perturbed audio, we achieve over 86% success across SOTA models. Our results underscore the urgent need for safety mechanisms that operate beyond text filtering, calling for robust defenses against covert, audio-native attacks in multimodal AI systems.

Appendix A
Details

Each full experimental run required approximately 15-20 hours on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Since a single H100 was insufficient to meet our computational needs, we also used NVIDIA GH200, A100, and RTX 6000 Ada GPUs to support our workload and ensure hardware diversity in our results. Our two-stage attack was run with consistent hyperparameters across models, utilizing the Adam optimizer with a learning rate (
\alpha
) of
2\times 10^{-2}
. The
L_{\infty}
perturbation budget (
\epsilon
) was set to different levels across our experiments, including 0.01, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5, to explore the impact of the perturbation magnitude on the attack success rate.

The attack pipeline was configured as follows: For Stage 1 (Target Discovery), the optimization typically ran for 100-200 steps. At each step, we generated six candidate responses using diverse decoding strategies (greedy, beam search, and temperature-based sampling which is explained in Section 2.1) and used a GPT-4o judge to provide a reward signal, with a score of 9.0/10 marking success. For Stage 2 (Payload Injection), the optimization was generally run for 150-300 steps to ensure convergence. The success of this stage was determined by a hybrid evaluation metric: the final response had to achieve a SentenceTransformer cosine similarity greater than 0.8 with the target behavior, followed by a 3-shot averaged GPT-4o score also exceeding 0.8. All GPT-based evaluations were conducted using GPT-4o via the OpenAI API, with a temperature setting of 0.0 to ensure deterministic responses.

Appendix G
Benchmark Sampling Methodology

To construct a robust and representative evaluation set, we drew samples from the comprehensive AdvBench benchmark. Because each full experimental run of our two-stage attack required approximately 15–20 hours on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, evaluating the entire benchmark was computationally infeasible. To overcome this constraint while maintaining statistical validity, we adopted a formal sampling methodology. First, we calculated the required sample size using the standard formula for estimating a proportion with finite population correction (FPC), shown in Equation
9
, to adjust for the known population size
N
. Here,
z
is the confidence quantile,
p
the estimated proportion, and
e
the margin of error:

n=\frac{z^{2}p(1-p)N}{e^{2}(N-1)+z^{2}p(1-p)}

(9)

We targeted a confidence level of 85% (
z\approx 1.440
) with a margin of error of
\pm
15% (
e=0.15
). Following standard practice for sample size calculation where the true proportion is unknown, we used an estimated proportion of
p=0.5
, as this represents the most conservative choice, maximizing the required sample size. Next, to ensure the sample preserved the benchmark’s categorical diversity, we used stratified sampling, partitioning the dataset according to the harm categories defined in its stratum column and allocating our total sample proportionally across them.
