Gut microbiota signatures in primary aldosteronism and functional identification of an aldosterone-degrading gut bacterium
Authors: Zhihui Zhang, Changyi Chen, Minchun Zhang, Jun Zhu, Xiaoqiang Xu, Zhifeng Wang, Lihua Zhou, Chao Wu, Mingxi Zong, Tingyu Yin, Zhiwen Cao, Aibo Gao, Cui Zhang, Tingwei Su, Lei Jiang, Weiwei Zhou, Wenzhong Zhou, Yijing Zhou, Jiqiu Wang, Guang Ning, Yiran Jiang, Ruixin Liu, Weiqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary aldosteronism (PA), a major cause of secondary hypertension, is characterized by autonomous aldosterone overproduction. Although the gut microbiota is closely linked to blood pressure regulation, its role in PA remains unclear. We performed metagenomic sequencing on fecal samples from 13 patients with essential hypertension (EH), 57 with unilateral PA (UPA), and 51 with bilateral PA (BPA). Despite comparable overall microbial diversity, gut microbial compositional differences were observed among EH and PA subtypes, particularly at finer taxonomic levels. We next identified 39 microbial species that were positively associated with plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC), and 29 that were negatively associated. In the co-abundance network, Ruminococcus gnavus emerged as one of the top three central nodes and was negatively correlated with PAC. Functionally, R. gnavus efficiently degraded aldosterone and multiple natural steroid hormones in vitro, and aldosterone degradation was accompanied by the generation of 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone. R. gnavus-colonized germ-free mice showed reduced fecal aldosterone levels and downregulated expression of aldosterone downstream genes in the intestine. In an aldosterone infusion model, R. gnavus similarly decreased fecal aldosterone and improved systolic blood pressure (SBP) and serum potassium. Logistic regression further revealed that the presence of R. gnavus was associated with lower odds of having a historical highest SBP ≥ 160 mmHg in patients with PA. Collectively, this study reveals different gut microbial signatures in PA and highlights the aldosterone-metabolizing capacity and blood pressure regulation of R. gnavus. These findings advance our understanding of gut microbiota-steroid hormone interactions in PA and provide a basis for exploring microbiota-based stratification and intervention strategies in steroid hormone-related conditions.

Introduction

Primary aldosteronism (PA) is characterized by autonomous aldosterone production that is independent of renin regulation and not suppressed by sodium or volume loading, and is recognized as a common endocrine cause of secondary hypertension.1,2 This hormonal dysregulation contributes to sustained elevations in blood pressure and an increased risk of hypokalemia. Clinically, PA can be classified into unilateral PA (UPA) and bilateral PA (BPA), a distinction that guides therapeutic strategies.3 UPA, most commonly attributable to aldosterone-producing adenoma (APA), is typically managed by adrenalectomy, whereas BPA is most frequently caused by idiopathic hyperaldosteronism and is generally treated with long-term mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.3,4 Despite these treatments, blood pressure control remains suboptimal in a substantial proportion of patients, and adverse effects of medical therapy may limit adherence.5-7 These limitations underscore the need to better understand the pathophysiology of PA.

Accumulating evidence supports a link between the gut microbiota and blood pressure regulation.8,9 Both human and animal studies have demonstrated that hypertension is associated with reduced gut microbial richness and diversity, accompanied by dysbiosis of microbial composition.8,10 Microbial metabolites and dysbiosis-induced barrier dysfunction have been reported to influence host immune and endocrine signaling pathways involved in blood pressure control.11-18 As a hormonally driven form of hypertension, PA may harbor distinct gut microbial features from essential hypertension (EH). Descriptive analyses comparing PA with healthy controls and patients with EH have reported relative depletion of short-chain fatty acid-producing taxa and enrichment of inflammation-associated bacteria, suggesting a distinct dysbiotic profile in PA.19 Experimental data further indicate that depletion or absence of the gut microbiota in mice leads to increased circulating and urinary aldosterone levels, implying a role for commensal microbes in maintaining basal aldosterone homeostasis.20 However, species-level functional mechanisms and gut microbial differences between PA subtypes remain largely unexplored.

Aldosterone is a mineralocorticoid synthesized in the adrenal zona glomerulosa that regulates sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in epithelial tissues such as the kidney and colon, thereby maintaining blood pressure and electrolyte homeostasis.21,22 Other steroid hormones, such as glucocorticoids and sex steroids, are essential for regulating host metabolism, immune function, and reproduction.23,24 In recent years, the gut microbiome has been increasingly recognized as a modulator of steroid hormone metabolism, with important implications for hormonal homeostasis and overall human health.25 A number of studies have begun to uncover mechanistic insights into microbe-steroid interactions. For instance, androgens have been shown to impair glucose homeostasis partly by altering the gut microbiota, thereby contributing to sex differences in glucose regulation.26 Oral administration of Thauera sp. strain GDN1 significantly reduced serum androgen levels in mice by catabolizing gut androgens.27 Moreover, a novel microbial 21-dehydroxylation pathway was identified that converts host-derived glucocorticoids into progestins.28 In our recent study, we observed marked gut microbial dysbiosis and enrichment of cortisol-metabolizing gut bacteria in patients with Cushing’s syndrome.29 These findings highlight the potential of microbe-steroid interactions in modulating human metabolism. Although aldosterone is the key pathogenic hormone in PA and a critical member of the steroid hormone family, its interaction with the gut microbiota remains largely unknown.

In this study, we performed metagenomic profiling of fecal samples from patients with PA and EH to characterize gut microbial signatures associated with PA. We combined microbiome analysis with in vitro bacterial assays and in vivo colonization experiments to examine the potential involvement of gut bacteria in aldosterone metabolism and related physiological regulation, with a particular focus on Ruminococcus gnavus as a candidate species.

Methods

Study design and population

A total of 108 patients with PA and 13 patients with EH were enrolled in the study at Ruijin Hospital, affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between 2021 and 2022. PA was diagnosed based on the Endocrine Society’s 2016 clinical practice guideline on the management of PA.3 Patients with an aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) > 30 (ng/dL)/(ng/mL/h), after discontinuation for at least four weeks of medications that substantially interfere with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system measurements, particularly mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, underwent a saline infusion test. A post-infusion plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) > 10 ng/dL confirmed the diagnosis. Adrenal computed tomography and adrenal venous sampling (AVS) were performed by experienced radiologists. Cannulation was considered successful if the cortisol adrenal vein/cortisol peripheral vein ratio was >2 without adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation. Lateralization was defined as a ratio greater than 2 between the cortisol-corrected aldosterone ratio (A/C) in the dominant adrenal vein and that in the contralateral adrenal vein without ACTH stimulation. BPA was diagnosed by biochemical PA without AVS lateralization and included 51 patients. UPA was defined as biochemical evidence of PA with an AVS lateralization index > 2 and histopathological confirmation after adrenalectomy, and included 57 patients, of whom 51 were diagnosed with APA, 3 with aldosterone-producing nodule (APN), 1 with concomitant APA and APN, 1 with aldosterone-producing cell cluster (APCC), and 1 with concomitant APA and APCC. EH was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, or the current use of antihypertensive medication, after exclusion of PA and other secondary causes of hypertension, such as pheochromocytoma, Cushing’s syndrome, and renal artery stenosis,30 and included 13 patients.

Exclusion criteria included the administration of steroid hormone medications, oral antibiotic treatment within two months prior to stool collection, habitual intake of probiotics or prebiotics, pregnancy, presence of active gastrointestinal disorders, and history of abdominal surgery within the past three years. The medical records of all patients diagnosed with PA or EH were reviewed to assess demographic information and clinical measurements. Unless otherwise stated, SBP and DBP refer to the current blood pressure measured using an Omron electronic blood pressure monitor at the time of sampling. Measurements were obtained with the patient seated after 5−10 min of rest in a quiet environment and repeated twice at 1−2 min intervals, with the average value used for analysis. Historical highest SBP and DBP were defined as the highest systolic and diastolic values documented in the traceable medical records from the time of hypertension diagnosis to enrollment, including measurements obtained during previous clinical visits as well as patient-reported values recorded in the charts. Blood pressure was considered controlled when the current SBP and DBP were < 140/90 mmHg.3

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (approval no. 2021-287), and was performed in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Fecal sample collection, DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Fecal samples from patients with PA (n = 108) and EH (n = 13) were self-collected into sterile plastic tubes and immediately frozen at −80 °C until shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Fecal DNA was extracted using the MagPure Stool DNA KF Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Metagenomic libraries were subsequently prepared and sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China) with a paired-end 100 bp (PE100) read configuration.

Data preprocessing and taxonomic profiling

Low quality reads and adapters from the raw metagenomic sequencing data were removed with fastp (version 0.23.4)31 by default parameters. Subsequently, we removed host sequences by aligning sequencing reads back to the host reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.0) in “−very-sensitive” mode.32 We acquired 70.5 ± 3.7 million clean reads from each of the 121 samples after removing human DNA reads. The nonhuman reads were obtained for the subsequent analysis. Taxonomic profiling of the metagenomic samples was conducted via MetaPhlAn (version 4.0.6)33 by its default settings. In total, 1088 species were classified into 564 genera, 153 families, and 17 phyla across taxonomic ranks.

Alpha and beta diversity analyses

Statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were carried out using R (version 4.1.3). Alpha diversity at the species level was evaluated for each sample using the Shannon index and species richness, calculated with the diversity function from the R package vegan (version 2.6-8). Beta diversity across samples was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, computed at the species level via the vegdist function in the R package vegan. Compositional differences in gut microbiota among EH, UPA, and BPA were evaluated by performing principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray-Curtis distance, using R function adonis2 in package vegan.

Comparative analysis of microbial features among EH, UPA and BPA and their correlation analysis with clinical indices

Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R (version 4.1.3). Group differences in species measures were assessed using multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, BMI, antihypertensive medication use, and potassium supplementation, implemented in the base R package stats. Associations between species-level taxonomic abundances and clinical indices were assessed using partial Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, antihypertensive medication use, and potassium supplementation. Statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05.

Construction of co-abundance networks for PAC-associated microbial species

Partial Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between PAC and species-level taxonomic abundances, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. Co-abundance networks of PAC-associated species were constructed based on pairwise Spearman’s correlation coefficients (rho). The resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.1), displaying only edges representing correlations with an absolute rho value > 0.3 and p < 0.05. Degree centrality, defined as the number of direct connections associated with each node in the network, was computed using custom R functions.

Detection of microbial steroid hormone catabolism in vitro

To validate the aldosterone-degrading capacity of gut bacteria associated with PAC, we conducted further functional assays on R. gnavus and Bacteroides fragilis. The R. gnavus strain SHRJ-M00089 and B. fragilis strain SHRJ-M00168 used in this study were isolated from fecal samples of volunteers recruited through our platform, and their taxonomic identities were verified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Table S1). For in vitro experiments, R. gnavus and B. fragilis were cultured separately in brain heart infusion broth (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) supplemented with yeast extract (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and hemin (MeilunBio, Dalian, China), under anaerobic conditions in a chamber (Don Whitley Scientific Products). To assess the effect of aldosterone on the growth of R. gnavus, aldosterone (OriLeaf, Shanghai, China) was added to the culture medium at final concentrations of 10 μM, 25 μM, or 50 μM. The growth of R. gnavus was continuously monitored by measurements of the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) at 30-minute intervals over a 24-hour period using a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). To verify the aldosterone degradation capacity, a 2% inoculum of the thoroughly mixed medium, with or without R. gnavus, was transferred to medium containing dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, vehicle control), 10 μM, 25 μM or 50 μM aldosterone, respectively. After 24 hours of incubation, bacterial supernatants were obtained by centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C and subsequently used for aldosterone quantification. To exclude the possibility that aldosterone degradation is a nonspecific consequence of bacterial growth and to ascertain that this activity is specific to R. gnavus, we utilized B. fragilis as a control. Each strain was individually incubated with 25 μM aldosterone in culture medium during the stationary phase, and supernatants were collected after 24 hours as described above. For time-course analysis, R. gnavus was cultured in medium containing 50 μM aldosterone, with supernatants collected at 0, 2, 6, and 12 hours for analysis. In addition, R. gnavus was separately incubated with 50 μM of each of the following steroid hormones for 24 hours. The hormones included cortisone, 11-deoxycorticosterone, 11-deoxycortisol, 21-deoxycorticosterone, 11-ketoandrostenedione, estrone, and estriol (all purchased from OriLeaf, Shanghai, China); corticosterone, progesterone, 17α-hydroxyprogesterone, 21-deoxycortisol, dehydroepiandrosterone, 11β-hydroxyandrostenedione, and estradiol (all from MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA); cortisol (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China); testosterone (Wokai, Beijing, China); dihydrotestosterone (MeilunBio, Dalian, China); and androstenedione (Bepure, Shanghai, China). Culture supernatants were collected after incubation and processed as described above for subsequent steroid analysis.

Animal model

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J germ-free (GF) mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou) Inc and maintained in the gnotobiotic mouse facility. Prior to the experiment, GF mice were fed with a sterile chow diet (SLACOM, Shanghai, China). For the R. gnavus colonization experiment, ten-week-old male mice received oral gavage of either sterile PBS or R. gnavus at a dose of 2 × 10⁸ CFUs in 200 μL of sterile PBS, administered twice over the first two days. Day 0 was defined as the first day of gavage and served as the reference time point for subsequent sampling and analyses. Mice were fed an irradiated sterile high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60 kcal% fat (Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) for 7 weeks. Bacterial colonization was confirmed by extracting DNA from fecal samples and conducting quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis with R. gnavus-specific 16S rRNA gene primers (Table S2).

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade mice were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and housed in filter-top cages under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to three groups: vehicle + PBS (VEH + PBS), aldosterone + PBS (ALD + PBS), and aldosterone + R. gnavus (ALD + RG). All groups received a cocktail of broad-spectrum antibiotics in the drinking water for one week, consisting of 1 g/L ampicillin trihydrate, 5 g/L streptomycin sulfate, 1 g/L colistin sulfate, and 0.25 g/L vancomycin. Subsequently, on Day 0, the VEH + PBS group was infused with vehicle (5% DMSO in saline), whereas the ALD + PBS and ALD + RG groups were infused with aldosterone (600 μg/kg/day; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 14 days using ALZET osmotic minipumps (model 2004; DURECT Corporation, Cupertino, CA, USA), with all three groups receiving 1% NaCl (MeilunBio, Dalian, China) in the drinking water.34,35 During this period, the VEH + PBS and ALD + PBS groups received sterile PBS, whereas the ALD + RG group received R. gnavus (2 × 10⁸ CFUs in 200 μL PBS) by oral gavage once daily.

At the end of both experiments, mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. Blood samples were collected for serum preparation. Intestinal tissues were precisely dissected, and both the intestinal tissues and colonic fecal samples were immediately stored at −80 °C until further analysis. All experiments in this study were conducted with approval from the Animal Care Committee at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

Blood pressure measurement

SBP was measured by tail-cuff plethysmography using a BP-2000 Blood Pressure Analysis System (Visitech Systems, Apex, NC, USA). The first five measurements were discarded to minimize stress-related artifacts, and the mean of at least ten consecutive stable readings was recorded as the final SBP value.

Mouse serum biochemical measurements

Mouse serum sodium and potassium levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (AU5800; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Serum aldosterone concentrations were determined using a commercially available ELISA kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from frozen distal colon tissues of mice using the Eastep® Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Following quantification, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was carried out on an ABI platform (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Samples were run in duplicate on a single 384-well reaction plate. mRNA expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene mouse 36B4 and analyzed using the ∆∆CT method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Quantification of steroid hormones by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry

For liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, bacterial culture supernatants were first spiked with a mixture of steroid internal standards (Bepure, Shanghai, China) and then processed using Cleanert® PEP 96 Well Microplates (Agela Technologies, Tianjin, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fecal pellets were homogenized in pre-weighed tubes with 80% methanol, and the resulting supernatants were processed following the same procedure. Authentic standards of steroid hormones including aldosterone, cortisol, corticosterone, cortisone, progesterone, 11-deoxycortisol, 17α-hydroxyprogesterone, dihydrotestosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone, androstenedione, estrone, estradiol, and estriol (Bepure, Shanghai, China); 11-deoxycorticosterone, 21-deoxycorticosterone, and testosterone (Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Canada); 11β-hydroxyandrostenedione and 11-ketoandrostenedione (Steraloids, Wilton, NH, USA); 21-deoxycortisol (Cerilliant, Round Rock, TX, USA); and 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone (IsoSciences, Ambler, PA, USA) were mixed to prepare a standard mixture and subjected to the same preparation procedure as the samples. Subsequently, aldosterone together with the other 19 steroid hormones listed above were quantified using an AB SCIEX Triple Quad 6500+ system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The analysis was performed on a Kinetex® C18 100 Å LC Column (2.6 µm, 100 × 3 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min and a column temperature of 40 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.3 mM ammonium fluoride in water, and mobile phase B consisted of methanol.

Statistical analysis

Clinical parameters were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are described as counts and percentages. Differences in clinical characteristics among the three groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc pairwise comparisons for normally distributed continuous variables, Kruskal-Wallis tests with post hoc comparisons for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between R. gnavus presence and historical highest SBP ≥ 160 mmHg in PA. Dot plots and box plots were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For both in vivo and in vitro experiments involving R. gnavus, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to compare two curves across multiple time points. Comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data were conducted using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for univariate multi-group comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients with EH, UPA, and BPA

A total of 13 patients with EH, 57 with UPA, and 51 with BPA were enrolled in this study. The clinical characteristics of the three groups are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age or sex among the three groups. The historical highest SBP was significantly higher in patients with UPA than in patients with BPA (172.7 ± 21.9 vs. 158.4 ± 23.3 mmHg, p = 0.005), while neither UPA nor BPA differed significantly from EH. The current SBP, current DBP and historical highest DBP were comparable across all three groups. In addition, the time interval from the documented historical highest blood pressure to enrollment did not differ significantly among the three groups [EH: 0.4 (0.1-10.0) years; UPA: 4.0 (0.3-9.5) years; BPA: 3.0 (0.0-10.0) years; p = 0.667]. Patients with UPA (2.89 ± 0.32 mmol/L) and BPA (3.20 ± 0.34 mmol/L) had lower serum potassium levels than patients with EH (3.84 ± 0.30 mmol/L, p < 0.001 for both comparisons), and patients with UPA had lower serum potassium levels than those with BPA (p < 0.001). Patients with UPA [57.3 (41.5-77.8) mmol/24 h] and BPA [51.6 (40.3-66.2) mmol/24 h] exhibited higher 24-hour urinary potassium excretion compared to individuals with EH [41.1 (26.0-47.4) mmol/24 h, p = 0.002 vs. UPA, p = 0.019 vs. BPA], with no significant difference between the UPA and BPA groups. Serum sodium and 24-hour urinary sodium levels were similar across all groups. Although PAC and ARR were higher in both patients with UPA [PAC: 42.56 (29.34-63.49) ng/dL; ARR: 88.9 (47.3-237.5)] and patients with BPA [PAC: 31.13 (21.13-47.21) ng/dL; ARR: 71.4 (40.0-152.8)] compared to those with EH [PAC: 19.59 (10.99-30.75) ng/dL, p < 0.001 vs. UPA, p = 0.028 vs. BPA; ARR: 20.6 (8.2-62.6), p < 0.001 vs. UPA, p = 0.008 vs. BPA], the two groups did not differ significantly from each other. In addition, potassium supplementation was more common in patients with UPA (91.2%) and BPA (68.6%) than in those with EH (0%, p < 0.001 for both comparisons), and the proportion also differed significantly between UPA and BPA (p = 0.011). No significant differences were observed among patients with EH, UPA, and BPA in body weight, body mass index (BMI), duration of hypertension, antihypertensive medication use, blood pressure control rate, plasma renin activity, serum cortisol, serum ACTH, or glucose and lipid metabolic parameters.

Gut microbial taxonomic differences among EH and PA subtypes

Fecal samples from all participants were subjected to metagenomic sequencing and analysis. The results showed no significant differences in microbial richness or alpha diversity, as assessed by the Shannon index, among the EH, UPA, and BPA groups (Figure S1a). PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distance further demonstrated similar microbial community structures across the three groups (Figure S1b). PERMANOVA analysis confirmed the absence of significant separation (R² = 0.0139, p = 0.817), indicating that the overall structure of the gut microbiota was largely consistent across the three cohorts. It should be noted that global alpha- and beta-diversity metrics primarily capture overall community-level patterns and may be insensitive to more subtle but biologically relevant shifts in particular bacterial families or species. Therefore, we further examined gut microbial composition at the family and species levels. At the family level, both UPA and BPA patients exhibited lower relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae compared to EH patients (p = 0.013 and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-adjusted p = 0.099 for UPA vs. EH, p = 0.011 and BH-adjusted p = 0.138 for BPA vs. EH), whereas the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae was elevated in UPA and BPA compared to EH (p = 0.009 and BH-adjusted p = 0.099 for UPA vs. EH, p = 0.024 and BH-adjusted p = 0.138 for BPA vs. EH), with no significant difference between the UPA and BPA groups. Additionally, the relative abundance of Acidaminococcaceae was significantly higher in BPA patients than in UPA patients (p < 0.001 and BH-adjusted p = 0.013, Figure 1a).

At the species level, multiple bacterial species showed nominal differences in relative abundance across the group comparisons (Figure 1b). Specifically, 38 species in the UPA group and 23 species in the BPA group differed in relative abundance compared with patients with EH (p < 0.05). Among these, Clostridium innocuum, Ruminococcus gnavus, Roseburia intestinalis, Faecalimonas umbilicata and GGB55957 SGB77276 were consistently decreased in both UPA and BPA groups relative to the EH group. In contrast, Bacteroides eggerthii, Parabacteroides johnsonii, Dorea formicigenerans, Blautia massiliensis, Clostridium SGB4751, and GGB1495 SGB2071 were enriched in both PA subtypes relative to EH. When directly comparing the two PA subtypes, 4 species including Isoptericola variabilis, Coprococcus comes, Blautia producta and Sutterella SGB9281 were enriched in UPA, whereas 25 species were more abundant in BPA. However, following BH correction for multiple comparisons, Bacteroides nordii and Enterocloster lavalensis remained significantly more abundant in BPA than in UPA (BH-adjusted p < 0.05). These results indicate that species-level microbial disparities exist not only between PA subtypes and EH but also between UPA and BPA, albeit most of the observed differences were nominal (i.e., significant only at the unadjusted level and not robust to multiple testing correction).

To investigate potential associations between microbial differences and clinical heterogeneity within the PA subtypes UPA and BPA, we performed correlation analyses between subtype-differential species and serum potassium levels, which differed significantly between the two groups. After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, antihypertensive medication use, and potassium supplementation, BPA-enriched species, including Bacteroides nordii (rho = 0.264, p = 0.006), Ruthenibacterium lactatiformans (rho = 0.236, p = 0.014), and Alistipes indistinctus (rho = 0.227, p = 0.018), were positively correlated with serum potassium among PA patients. Conversely, the UPA-enriched species Sutterella SGB9281 (rho = −0.205, p = 0.033) displayed a negative correlation (Figure 1c). However, due to the limited sample size, these associations did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons (BH-adjusted p > 0.05, Table S3).

Identification of aldosterone-associated microbial species in all subjects

To investigate the potential associations between aldosterone levels and specific gut bacterial species, we conducted a partial Spearman’s rank correlation analysis across the entire cohort, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. Notably, a total of 68 species showed significant correlations with PAC, including 29 negatively and 39 positively associated taxa, with the majority (41/68) belonging to the phylum Firmicutes (p < 0.05, Figure 2a). Furthermore, species co-abundance network analysis revealed predominantly positive correlations within each of the PAC positively- or negatively-associated clusters. In contrast, negative correlations were more frequently observed between the two clusters (|rho| > 0.3, p < 0.05, Figure 2b), suggesting potential competitive interactions between them. In the microbial interaction network, the top three nodes with the highest degree centrality were Alistipes communis, Ruminococcus gnavus, and an uncultivated species classified as Lentisphaeria bacterium (Figure 2c). Their central positions suggest that these microbes may play pivotal roles in shaping the ecological structure of the PAC-associated gut microbiota.

Degradation of aldosterone and multiple steroid hormones by R. gnavus in vitro

To evaluate the aldosterone-degrading potential of PAC-associated bacterial species, we selected representative strains based on their degree centrality in the PAC-associated microbial co-abundance network. Specifically, we chose R. gnavus, which exhibited high degree centrality, and Bacteroides fragilis, which had a low degree, from our in-house culture collection of human fecal isolates. In vitro degradation assays demonstrated that R. gnavus effectively degraded aldosterone, while B. fragilis exhibited no detectable degradative activity (Figure 3a), highlighting the species-specific nature of aldosterone metabolism by R. gnavus. To further validate the aldosterone-degrading capacity of R. gnavus, we first assessed its growth dynamics in the presence of aldosterone. Growth curve analysis demonstrated that aldosterone at varying concentrations (10 μM, 25 μM, and 50 μM) did not affect bacterial growth (Figure 3b). Importantly, R. gnavus demonstrated robust aldosterone-degrading activity across all tested concentrations, reducing the hormone to minimal levels within 24 hours of incubation (Figure 3c). A time-course analysis further revealed that in the presence of 50 μM aldosterone, R. gnavus significantly reduced aldosterone levels within 6 hours with sustained suppression persisting through 12 hours (Figure 3e), highlighting its rapid and efficient catabolic capacity.

Given that aldosterone is known to undergo reductive metabolism in humans to form biologically inactive metabolites such as 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone,36,37 we further examined whether this metabolite could be detected in the supernatant of R. gnavus cultures incubated with aldosterone. Using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis, we found that under conditions with increasing initial aldosterone concentrations, a corresponding increase in 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone was observed as aldosterone levels decreased (Figure 3d). Moreover, in time-course experiments, 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone progressively accumulated with prolonged incubation, mirroring the consumption of aldosterone (Figure 3f). This substrate-depletion and product-accumulation pattern, observed in both concentration-dependent and time-dependent experiments, supports the notion that 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone may represent a metabolic product of aldosterone conversion by R. gnavus (Figure 3g).

Our previous research showed that R. gnavus is also capable of degrading cortisol.29 Therefore, we next investigated whether this bacterium could catabolize other steroid hormones. R. gnavus was incubated with a panel of natural steroid hormones, and the results revealed its broad substrate specificity. The bacterium efficiently degraded a wide array of C21 steroid hormones, including cortisol, corticosterone, cortisone, progesterone, 11-deoxycorticosterone, 11-deoxycortisol, 17α-hydroxyprogesterone, 21-deoxycorticosterone, and 21-deoxycortisol. It also metabolized several C19 androgens, such as testosterone, dihydrotestosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone, androstenedione, 11β-hydroxyandrostenedione, and 11-ketoandrostenedione. Among the C18 estrogens tested, R. gnavus was capable of degrading estrone, but not estradiol or estriol (Figure 3h). These findings highlight the broad steroid hormone-degrading potential of R. gnavus, particularly its strong capacity for degrading aldosterone, various natural C21 steroid hormones, androgens, and estrone.

Broad steroid hormone degradation by R. gnavus in germ-free mice

Following the demonstration of aldosterone degradation in vitro, we subsequently validated the in vivo metabolic activity of R. gnavus. GF mice were colonized with R. gnavus via oral gavage (Figure 4a), and successful colonization was confirmed by qPCR analysis of fecal DNA (Figure 4b). Notably, fecal aldosterone levels were significantly reduced in R. gnavus-colonized mice compared to non-colonized controls, indicating active aldosterone degradation by R. gnavus in the gut (Figure 4c). However, serum aldosterone concentrations did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 4d). Given the established role of aldosterone in regulating intestinal epithelial ion channels,38-40 we next assessed the transcriptional expression of key aldosterone-responsive genes in the distal colon. Colonization with R. gnavus led to a marked reduction in the transcriptional expression of serum/glucocorticoid regulated kinase 1 (Sgk1) and epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) subunit genes Scnn1a and Scnn1b, with Scnn1g also showing a decline trend (Figure 4e). These results suggest that R. gnavus supplementation primarily reduces fecal aldosterone concentrations without altering circulating aldosterone levels, and may thereby attenuate aldosterone-mediated regulation of colonic electrolyte transport.

To further characterize the in vivo metabolic impact of R. gnavus on other steroid hormone substrates, we measured fecal concentrations of various endogenous steroid hormones. Remarkably, mice colonized with R. gnavus showed significantly decreased levels of multiple C21 steroids, including corticosterone, progesterone, and 11-deoxycorticosterone, as well as several C19 androgens such as testosterone and androstenedione, compared to non-colonized controls. In contrast, the fecal concentrations of the C18 estradiol were unaffected (Figure 4f). These results closely align with our in vitro observations, confirming that R. gnavus retains robust steroid hormone-degrading capacity in vivo.

Physiological effects of R. gnavus supplementation in aldosterone-infused mouse model

To investigate whether R. gnavus metabolizes exogenous aldosterone in vivo and modulates aldosterone-associated phenotypes, we first subjected SPF mice to antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion, followed by implantation of aldosterone-releasing osmotic pumps and administration of 1% NaCl in the drinking water, with or without R. gnavus gavage (Figure 5a). Successful colonization was confirmed by qPCR analysis of fecal DNA (Figure 5b).

Serum aldosterone concentrations were significantly elevated in the ALD + PBS group compared with the VEH + PBS group (Figure 5c), confirming successful establishment of the exogenous aldosterone infusion model. Notably, R. gnavus supplementation markedly reduced fecal aldosterone levels (Figure 5d) without significantly altering serum aldosterone concentrations (Figure 5c), consistent with the observations in GF mice. Furthermore, SBP was reduced in aldosterone-infused mice following R. gnavus colonization (Figure 5e), accompanied by a modest increase in serum potassium levels (Figure 5g), whereas serum sodium levels remained unchanged (Figure 5f). These findings suggest that, in the exogenous aldosterone infusion model, R. gnavus colonization effectively reduces fecal aldosterone and improves physiological phenotypes.

Association between R. gnavus presence and historical highest SBP in patients with PA

Hypertension is the most prominent clinical manifestation of PA, with SBP being of particular clinical significance. Given the SBP-lowering effect of R. gnavus observed in aldosterone-infused mice, we further investigated the association between R. gnavus and SBP in patients with PA. Considering the strong influence of antihypertensive therapy on current blood pressure, we used the historical highest SBP for subsequent analyses. Based on the 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines for blood pressure classification,30 and taking into account the pathophysiological characteristics of PA as well as the historical highest SBP distribution within our study population, we stratified patients with PA, including UPA and BPA subtypes, into two groups using a historical highest SBP threshold of < 160 mmHg versus ≥ 160 mmHg. This stratification allowed us to examine the association between R. gnavus and SBP in PA patients. Our findings revealed that R. gnavus was more frequently detected and exhibited higher relative abundance in PA patients with historical highest SBP < 160 mmHg, suggesting a potential link between the presence of R. gnavus and lower historical highest SBP (Figure 6a and 6b). Notably, the difference in relative abundance was more pronounced in patients with BPA than in patients with UPA (Figure 6b).

Collectively, these findings indicate a potential association between R. gnavus presence and blood pressure status in patients with PA.

Discussion

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiota is involved in host steroid hormone metabolism and may influence hormone-related physiological and pathological processes.25-29,41 Our study adds to this knowledge by uncovering a previously unrecognized link between gut bacteria and aldosterone metabolism in patients with PA. Despite similar overall community structures among the EH, UPA, and BPA groups, microbial compositional differences were observed at finer taxonomic levels, particularly between PA subtypes. Among the species associated with PAC, R. gnavus emerged as one of the central nodes within the microbial co-abundance network and was negatively correlated with aldosterone levels. Notably, both in vitro culture assays and in vivo colonization experiments demonstrated the robust capacity of R. gnavus to degrade aldosterone, as well as a broad spectrum of C21 corticosteroids and C19 androgens. Importantly, R. gnavus colonization also reduced fecal aldosterone and improved SBP and serum potassium in an aldosterone-infused mouse model. Consistently, R. gnavus was more frequently detected in PA patients with lower historical highest SBP, and its presence was associated with lower odds of having a higher-range historical highest SBP in PA. These findings expand our understanding of interactions between the gut microbiota and steroid hormones by including mineralocorticoids, and suggest that specific gut bacteria may be involved in regulating hormone levels in aldosterone-driven diseases.

Although no significant differences in gut microbial diversity were observed between EH and PA patients in our study, previous research reported that individuals with PA exhibit reduced alpha diversity and distinct microbial community structures compared to those with EH.19 This inconsistency may be attributed to the differences in clinical or demographic characteristics of the enrolled subjects such as age and the severity of hypertension between the two studies. Nonetheless, our analysis revealed notable microbial compositional differences between EH and PA at finer taxonomic levels. Specifically, the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae was reduced, while Prevotellaceae was increased in PA compared to EH. Lachnospiraceae is a dominant family of anaerobic gut bacteria known for its production of short-chain fatty acids, playing a critical role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis and modulating immune responses in the host,42,43 while Prevotellaceae has been linked to pro-inflammatory states and insulin resistance.44,45 Notably, accumulating clinical evidence indicates that patients with PA have a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease and target organ damage compared to patients with EH matched for age and blood pressure.46,47 This increased risk is largely attributed to elevated aldosterone levels, a well-established cardiovascular risk factor.48,49 Given that metabolic abnormalities often cluster in hypertensive individuals and contribute to cardiovascular risk,50,51 the gut microbiota may serve as a potential contributing factor to the elevated cardiovascular risk in PA by modulating host metabolism. We want to mention that the interpretation of these findings should take into account the relatively small size of the EH cohort, as well as the potential for residual confounding related to treatment exposure, including antihypertensive medication use, albeit medication use did not differ significantly among the three groups and was adjusted for in the analyses.

Despite both PA subtypes sharing the hallmark of excessive aldosterone production, they differ in clinical presentation, treatment strategies, and underlying molecular mechanisms. In our cohort, patients with UPA exhibited lower serum potassium levels than those with BPA, consistent with previous reports.4,52 To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the gut microbiota between the two major PA subtypes, and we identified 25 BPA-enriched and 4 UPA-enriched microbial species. Furthermore, we found that BPA-enriched Bacteroides nordii, Ruthenibacterium lactatiformans, and Alistipes indistinctus were positively correlated with serum potassium, whereas the UPA-enriched species Sutterella SGB9281 showed a negative association. Although direct evidence supporting a causal role of these species in potassium regulation is currently lacking, previous studies have shown that Alistipes indistinctus is significantly depleted in patients with inflammatory bowel disease, and its genetic variants are associated with disease susceptibility, suggesting a potential protective role in maintaining mucosal homeostasis and modulating inflammatory responses.53 However, due to the limited sample size, the observed associations did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons and should therefore be considered exploratory and warrant further validations in larger cohorts and investigations into their physiological relevance. Additionally, we want to mention that the differential microbial features identified between the two PA subtypes were less likely to be explained by confounding factors such as sodium intake, which is also reported to influence gut microbiota composition,14,54-56 as 24-hour urinary sodium excretion, a commonly used surrogate for habitual dietary sodium intake,57 did not differ among the EH, UPA, and BPA groups in the present study.

We found that aldosterone-associated taxa were positively correlated within the positively and negatively associated groups, respectively, but negatively correlated between the two groups, suggesting potential microbial interactions in aldosterone regulation. It is worth noting that R. gnavus, a prevalent and early-colonizing member of the human gut microbiota,58 appeared as a key node in this network and was negatively correlated with PAC. Consistent with this finding, R. gnavus was reduced in both UPA and BPA compared with EH in our cohort. At a broader taxonomic level, a Japanese general population-based study of 377 community-dwelling adults reports that the abundance of Ruminococcus 2 was significantly lower in the high-PAC group.59 Functionally, we showed that R. gnavus efficiently degraded aldosterone both in vitro and in vivo. Importantly, our in vitro experiments indicated that 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone accumulated during aldosterone degradation by R. gnavus, suggesting that R. gnavus may participate in aldosterone conversion through a reductive metabolic process. Recently, Jacoby et al. characterized a gut bacterial steroid hormone reductive pathway, OsrABC, in Clostridium steroidoreducens, which catalyzes the stepwise reduction of steroid hormones such as cortisol and progesterone to generate 3β,5β-tetrahydrosteroid products.60 In our study, the detection of 3α,5β-tetrahydroaldosterone as a putative aldosterone metabolite, together with the observation that R. gnavus exhibited metabolic activity toward a broad range of natural C21 steroid hormones and C19 androgens, most of which share the Δ⁴-3-ketosteroid structural motif, suggests that R. gnavus may possess reductive steroid-transforming activity. Consistent with this broader steroid-metabolizing capacity, our previous research demonstrated that R. gnavus can degrade cortisol.29 In addition, an independent study demonstrated the ability of R. gnavus to convert androgen precursors, such as pregnenolone and hydroxypregnenolone, into bioactive androgens including testosterone and dehydroepiandrosterone.41 More recently, metagenomic and biochemical analyses identified several steroid-metabolizing enzymes capable of converting steroid precursors such as pregnenolone and progesterone into structurally modified metabolites, and homologous enzymes were also identified in R. gnavus.25 Together, these findings support a substantial steroid-modifying potential of R. gnavus within the gut ecosystem. However, the specific enzymes, coding genes, and complete biochemical pathway responsible for aldosterone metabolism in R. gnavus remain to be elucidated. Given its broader steroid-metabolizing capacity, future studies should combine whole-genome sequencing of R. gnavus with homology searches using known steroid-metabolizing enzymes, followed by functional validation, to identify the key enzymes and define the complete metabolic pathway underlying this process.

Earlier studies have shown that circulating aldosterone and its metabolites can be excreted via bile into the gastrointestinal tract and detected in bile and feces,61-63 supporting its access to gut bacteria. Through genomic mechanisms mediated by the nuclear mineralocorticoid receptor, aldosterone regulates the expression and function of epithelial ion channels, thereby facilitating sodium reabsorption and promoting the excretion of potassium and hydrogen ions.64 Consistent with the colon being a target organ of aldosterone,2 our findings demonstrated that colonization of GF mice with R. gnavus led to decreased transcriptional levels of the aldosterone-responsive genes Sgk1 and ENaC subunits in the distal colon, aligning with the reduced fecal aldosterone levels. In the hyperaldosteronism model of our study, R. gnavus colonization improved SBP and serum potassium levels, in parallel with reduced fecal aldosterone, without affecting circulating aldosterone levels. Notably, accumulating evidence indicates that intestinal mineralocorticoid receptor signaling and aldosterone availability can influence systemic blood pressure and electrolyte homeostasis.39,40 Together, these findings raise the possibility that R. gnavus modulates intestinal and systemic physiology via aldosterone metabolism in the gut, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of PA, and it warrants deeper mechanistic exploration in the future.

It has been reported that the prevalence of PA increases with advancing hypertension severity,65 and accumulating clinical evidence indicates that patients with PA are at significantly greater risk of cardiovascular and renal complications compared to those with EH.47,66 Therefore, early diagnosis and the implementation of intensive PA-specific blood pressure management are crucial for reducing adverse outcomes and improving long-term prognosis in patients with PA. Notably, we found that R. gnavus was more frequently detected and exhibited higher relative abundance in PA patients with historical highest SBP < 160 mmHg than in those with historical highest SBP ≥ 160 mmHg. Moreover, the presence of R. gnavus was inversely associated with higher-range historical highest SBP (SBP ≥ 160 mmHg) in PA, suggesting a potential protective role of this bacterium against higher SBP. Although fecal sampling and historical highest SBP were not contemporaneous, prior longitudinal studies indicate that the human gut microbiota is relatively stable within individuals over time.67,68 In addition, our stratification based on the presence or absence of R. gnavus, rather than minor abundance differences, may better reflect a stable colonization state. These findings warrant further validation in patients evaluated at initial PA diagnosis before antihypertensive therapy. Previous studies have suggested that baseline gut microbial composition may influence disease stratification and therapeutic responses in metabolic disorders.69-71 In this context, our findings suggest that the presence or absence of R. gnavus within the gut microbiota may inform microbial stratification strategies for risk assessment and individualized blood pressure management in PA, pending further prospective and mechanistic validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of patients with EH was relatively small compared with the UPA and BPA groups, and the imbalance in group sizes may have limited statistical power and affected the robustness of the between-group microbial comparisons. In addition, although treatment-related factors were considered in the analyses, residual confounding related to antihypertensive medication use could not be fully excluded. Accordingly, the microbial differences observed between EH and PA subtypes warrant validation in larger cohorts, particularly in newly diagnosed and treatment-naive patients. Second, healthy controls were not included in the present study, which limited our ability to fully distinguish PA-specific microbial alterations from those related to elevated blood pressure. To support the observed microbial differences between PA subtypes and EH with comparable blood pressure, inclusion of well-matched healthy controls in future studies would further help clarify the independent contributions of aldosterone and hypertension. Lastly, we demonstrated the novel and potent metabolic activity of R. gnavus toward natural C21 and C19 steroid hormones, including aldosterone, while the responsible enzymes remain unidentified. Further studies are needed to delineate the biochemical pathways and identify the key genes and enzymes underlying steroid hormone metabolism in R. gnavus.

In summary, we observed gut microbial compositional differences among EH and PA subtypes, highlighted R. gnavus as a key aldosterone-degrading bacterium with broad steroid-metabolizing ability, and linked its presence to lower SBP in patients with PA as well as in aldosterone-infused mice. These findings advance our understanding of the complex interactions between the gut microbiota, aldosterone, and related metabolic disturbances, and provide new insights into microbiota-informed patient stratification and microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies in PA.
