Targeted pneumococcal conjugate vaccination strategies for high-risk children
Authors: John Hart, Zheng Quan Toh, Lien Anh Ha Do, Kim Mulholland, Paul V Licciardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus pneumoniae remains a leading cause of pneumonia mortality among children under five years of age, with the greatest burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Children with malnutrition and those following infection with measles or malaria experience immune dysfunction, with subsequent high risk of severe pneumonia and death. Targeted pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) strategies, timed to periods of greatest vulnerability among these high-risk groups, could reduce pneumonia burden and mortality in LMICs yet research in these populations remains limited, and specific PCV recommendations are lacking. This mini-review summarizes the disease burden and immune vulnerability associated with malnutrition, measles, and malaria, and highlights key research priorities to inform targeted PCV strategies among these high-risk children.KEYWORDS: Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, pneumonia, children, mortality, high-risk, Streptococcus pneumoniae

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is a common nasopharyngeal commensal that can cause invasive disease, including pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis. There are over 100 pneumococcal serotypes with differing invasiveness and epidemiology. Most invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) is preventable through immunization with pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs).1 Multiple PCVs are in use globally, targeting between 10 and 21 serotypes, with higher valency PCVs (up to 31 serotypes) under development.2 PCVs have been used in high-income countries since 2000, and Gavi support has accelerated adoption in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) over the past decade.3 Despite this progress, PCV coverage remains substantially lower than for many routine infant vaccines. By the end of 2024, PCV had been introduced in 163 World Health Organization (WHO) member states, leaving more than 30 member states without national PCV introduction, and global coverage with the third PCV dose was estimated at only 67%.4 Coverage also varies widely by region, from 88% in the WHO South-East Asia Region to 23% in the Western Pacific Region.4 Although PCV was initially recommended as a 3 + 1 schedule (three primary doses and one booster), most countries are now using 3 + 0 or 2 + 1 schedules. In some circumstances, reduced dose 1 + 1 PCV schedules may now be considered, as noted in the recent WHO position paper.5 To date, only the United Kingdom is using an infant 1 + 1 schedule.6

The introduction of PCV has substantially reduced pneumococcal deaths among children under five years of age, with an estimated 51% decline between 2000 and 2015.7 However, pneumococcus remains the leading cause of pneumonia and accounts for an estimated 28% of approximately 500,000 deaths among young children.8 Most deaths occur in LMICs, particularly among high-risk groups including under-vaccinated children, those with malnutrition, and those with infection-associated immune dysfunction. Children at highest risk are often those with limited access to PCV and in settings with high burden of infection, so standard PCV schedules (i.e. 3 + 0, 2 + 1) may not be optimal. While reduced dose schedules, such as 1 + 1, may be programmatically attractive, inadequate protection may exacerbate inequities and increase risk among the most vulnerable.

PCV impact at the population level reflects both direct protection among vaccinated children and indirect protection (herd protection) through reduced vaccine-type carriage and transmission.9 High-risk children may benefit less from indirect protection because they often live in settings with lower or uneven vaccine coverage and higher exposure to transmission (e.g. crowding and dense contact networks), allowing ongoing circulation of vaccine-type pneumococci.10 They may also have impaired or more rapid waning of immune responses, increasing reliance on timely, complete vaccination and, potentially, additional doses to achieve protection. Targeted PCV strategies – such as catch-up vaccination to reduce zero-dose and under-vaccination, booster dosing in defined high-risk groups, and vaccination timed to high-risk periods (e.g. post-hospitalization or post-infection) – may therefore be needed to maximize protection and reduce inequities.

Furthermore, while PCV should be offered to all children in LMICs, targeted vaccination of high-risk groups may offer a feasible complementary approach in some settings with no PCV program or inadequate immunization coverage. Children living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) provides an important precedent for risk-based PCV recommendations, although the incremental impact of HIV-specific recommendations is difficult to distinguish from routine infant PCV introduction, improved HIV care, and changes in pneumococcal transmission. Given that PCV vaccination is already recommended for children living with HIV,5 this mini-review focusses on children affected by malnutrition, measles, and malaria: three conditions that are common in LMICs, are associated with increased risks of pneumonia and death, may create periods of impaired or altered immune competence, and currently lack specific PCV recommendations.

Pediatric populations at high risk of pneumococcal disease

Malnutrition

In 2024, it is estimated that 150.2 million children under five years of age were stunted (low height-for-age) and 42.8 million were wasted (low weight-for-height).11 These indicators capture different forms of malnutrition: stunting reflects chronic undernutrition and growth faltering, and wasting reflects acute undernutrition, which is associated with particularly high short-term mortality risk.12 These distinctions are important because the timing, rationale, and potential benefit of PCV vaccination may differ across these groups. Pneumonia is the leading infectious cause of hospitalization and death among children under five years of age, with malnutrition the leading risk factor for death.13,14 A systematic review and meta-analysis that included 33,544 underweight children from 23 studies found that the median prevalence of hospitalization for underweight children was approximately 20%.13 Compared with normal weight children, the risk of death from pneumonia was approximately two and five times greater among moderately and severely underweight children, respectively.13 Importantly, the pneumonia mortality risk among underweight children was similar before the year 2000 compared with after 2000 (up to 2017), when PCV vaccines became available,13 indicating that improved access and/or alternate strategies could prove beneficial for this high-risk population. A recent prospective cohort study of children hospitalized with acute febrile illness in Uganda found that severe acute malnutrition (SAM) was associated with a 9.2-fold (95% CI 4.8–46) increased odds of pneumonia mortality.15

Children with malnutrition often have impaired innate and adaptive immune functions, which may reduce their ability to mount effective responses to pneumococcal infection, predisposing them to IPD and death. Children with SAM were observed to have lower proportions and/or function of monocytes, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells, compared with normal weight children.16 These immune cells play critical roles in antigen presentation, phagocytosis and cellular toxicity, which are crucial to control infections early. For adaptive immune responses, while the proportions of lymphocytes were similar between children with malnutrition and normal weight children, the function appears to be reduced, including delayed type hypersensitivity responses and reduced levels of IgA in saliva and tears.17–19 Additionally, there is lymphoid organ atrophy, as well as a shift from Th1-associated to Th2-associated cytokines, skewing immune responses toward impaired cell-mediated immunity.17,18 Children with SAM were also observed to exhibit elevated systemic inflammation, and higher levels of inflammatory markers (including TNF-α, IL-8, and C-reactive protein) that were associated with increased risk of death.15,20–22 The persistent inflammation is likely multifactorial, driven by ongoing infections resulting from an impaired immune system and systemic exposure to translocated gut bacterial products, as well as unresolved bacterial infections.

In children with malnutrition, pneumococcal infection may be more difficult to recognize and may progress rapidly to severe disease. Classical signs of pneumonia may be attenuated or nonspecific, and children with SAM may present with lethargy, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, poor feeding, or sepsis-like illness rather than clear focal respiratory signs.23 Impaired mucosal immunity, reduced phagocytic function, lymphoid atrophy, and systemic inflammation, as described above, may increase the risk that pneumococcal colonization progresses to pneumonia, bacteremia, or other IPD. These clinical and immunological features provide a rationale for evaluating whether timely PCV catch-up or booster strategies could reduce severe pneumococcal outcomes in children with malnutrition, particularly after acute illness and during nutritional recovery.

Children with SAM are at increased risk of pneumonia and death at diagnosis, and some remain at elevated risk during their ongoing nutritional recovery. In a cohort study in Bangladesh, approximately 9% of children under five years of age admitted for SAM and pneumonia died within three months of discharge, and most deaths (84%) were associated with respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms.24 Another cohort study in Kenya investigated changes in risk of life-threatening events among 1,778 children under five years of age recovering from SAM, and found that severe pneumonia was the most frequent event (incidence rate of 21 episodes per 100 child-years) during 12 months of follow up.25 Compared with children with ongoing SAM, children who had recovered from acute malnutrition had a progressively lower risk of severe pneumonia, with risk reductions of 49%, 70%, and 82% at one, three, and six months of follow-up, respectively.25 This suggests that pneumonia risk declines during nutritional recovery but, importantly, remains elevated for several months. Severe pneumonia causes reduced appetite, difficulty breathing, persistent vomiting, and increased metabolic demands, all of which limit food intake and worsen nutritional status, further exacerbating the cycle of malnutrition and infection.26

Measles

Despite the wide availability of a safe and effective live attenuated measles vaccine, measles is reemerging as a global threat. In 2023–2025, measles surged to 10.3 million cases worldwide, with outbreaks in every WHO region.27 Over 90% of global measles cases occur among children in LMICs, and the communities and families at greatest risk of measles in these settings are often also those most affected by under-vaccination and malnutrition. Measles mortality is highest among children under five years of age.

Wild-type measles virus (wt-MeV) enters host cells via three cellular receptors: CD46, Signaling Lymphocyte Activation Molecule (SLAM, CD150) and Nectin-4. CD150 is the primary receptor and expressed on a subset of T and B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, thymocytes, and hematopoietic stem cells.28 wt-MeV preferentially targets memory T cells and CD150+ B cells (naïve and memory), resulting in a depletion of preexisting antibody repertoires in a phenomenon known as measles-induced immune amnesia.29–31 Wt-MeV can also target specific innate-like immune cells such as mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT), signaling them for apoptotic cell death.32 As MAIT cells strategically reside and operate as emergency responders to microbial invaders at mucosal surfaces, their impairment by measles infection can lead to increased susceptibility to pathogens. This phenomenon explains the reported observations that most measles-related deaths are not due to the measles virus itself, but to secondary infections caused by measles-induced immunosuppression.28,33

Pneumonia is the most common cause of measles-related morbidity and mortality (1–6 per 100 measles cases) during the first 12 months post-measles infection.28 Contemporary post-PCV data on bacterial causes of measles-associated pneumonia are scarce; however, pre-PCV studies consistently showed pneumococcus as a major contributor to secondary bacterial pneumonia in measles.34,35 With PCV coverage remaining suboptimal in many LMICs,36 pneumococcal infection remains an important factor in severe measles outcomes, particularly in children under five years of age. Measles-induced immune amnesia can reduce the efficacy of previous immunizations against other pathogens, including pneumococcus, and increase longer-term susceptibility to secondary infections, particularly within the first 12 months of recovery from measles.29–31 While natural exposure to pathogens can reconstruct the antibody repertoire in measles patients, this poses a high risk of severe infection in the host.30 Vaccination provides a safe alternative to rebuild a child’s immunity, although the optimal time to vaccinate is not known.

Malaria

Malaria is a leading global cause of child mortality.37 Each year, there are more than 169 million malaria cases and 469,000 deaths among children under 15 years worldwide; more than 94% of these cases occur in LMICs.38 Children, particularly those under five years of age, are susceptible to severe malaria as they are yet to develop protective natural immunity.39 Natural malaria infections are weakly immunogenic, and repeated exposures are needed to develop partial immunity.40 Moreover, naturally acquired immunity to malaria is short-lived because parasite-induced inflammation interferes with the development of effective B-cell immunological memory.41 This mechanism may also limit host control of parasite burden and leave infected children more likely to succumb to malaria-associated immunopathology.

In settings where malaria is prevalent, pneumococcal infection in children with malaria may present as pneumonia, bacteremic pneumonia, sepsis, or meningitis, and can be difficult to distinguish clinically from severe malaria.42 Fever, respiratory distress, impaired consciousness, anemia, metabolic acidosis, and shock may reflect severe malaria, invasive bacterial disease, or both.42 Malaria may also increase susceptibility to IPD through impaired splenic clearance, hemolysis, altered neutrophil and macrophage function, and dysregulated inflammatory responses.43–45 These overlapping clinical features and potential immunological interactions mean that pneumococcal co-infection may be under-recognized in children diagnosed with malaria, while also contributing to severe disease and mortality.

A recent systematic review reported severe pneumonia occurred among 20% of malaria patients, although with wide confidence intervals (95% CI: 2%–53%).46 Evidence on mortality among children with both malaria and severe pneumonia is limited and difficult to interpret. In malaria-endemic settings, children with heavy parasitemia may be treated presumptively for malaria, often with antibiotics added because bacterial co-infection cannot be excluded, whereas children without clear malaria may be more likely to undergo investigation for alternative causes of severe illness. Existing studies are therefore difficult to compare, with mixed findings on whether mortality differs between children with malaria – pneumonia co-diagnosis and those with severe pneumonia without malaria.47,48 The clinical diagnosis is further complicated among children with HIV, which can influence the epidemiology and clinical presentation of both malaria and pneumonia.46,49 A recent surveillance study conducted in Africa reported that P. falciparum was the sole infecting pathogen in most malaria deaths; however, around one-quarter of deaths involved co-infections.50 Among these, pneumococcus was the second most frequently identified bacterial pathogen, following Klebsiella pneumoniae.50

Malnutrition

The effect of malnutrition on vaccine responses has been studied for several vaccines, but the evidence is difficult to interpret because studies vary in malnutrition definitions, vaccine type, timing of vaccination, presence of intercurrent infection, and immunological endpoints.51–53 Earlier studies suggested impaired immune competence in children with malnutrition, particularly for some measures of cell-mediated immunity, but subsequent reviews have emphasized that findings are inconsistent and often confounded by infection and other coexisting conditions.51,53 Overall, most children with malnutrition appear able to mount immune responses to protein, subunit, killed and polysaccharide vaccines, although the magnitude, quality, or duration of responses may be reduced in some settings.51,52 Immune responses to live vaccines such as measles and rotavirus appear more consistently suboptimal, although the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.51–54

Evidence specific to PCV remains limited, particularly for children with SAM and for outcomes beyond short-term antibody concentrations, such as functional antibody activity, durability, carriage, and clinical protection. Available evidence suggests that PCV may remain immunogenic among those with low birth weight or moderate malnutrition. A sub-analysis of a PCV trial among Californian infants55 and an observational study among Polish infants56 both demonstrated adequate PCV immunogenicity among low birth weight infants following three doses of PCV7. In Venezuela, a cross-sectional cohort of 504 children under five years of age found higher antibody levels for five of 13 PCV serotypes (serotypes 3, 4, 6A, 6B and 23F) among stunted compared to non-stunted children; antibody levels were similar or lower for the other eight serotypes.57 However, a study in Kenya found significantly reduced antibody levels following PCV10 (Synflorix®) to nine PCV serotypes (all except serotype 5) among stunted children compared with non-stunted children.58 A South African case–control study suggested PCV7 effectiveness against vaccine-type IPD was similar in malnourished and non-malnourished children, although confidence intervals were wide.59 Additionally, limited intake of essential micronutrients (vitamins A, E, K) and minerals (calcium, potassium) – reflecting low consumption of fruit, vegetables, and animal-source foods – was associated with poorer PCV responses.60

Measles

Measles-associated immunosuppression and immune amnesia are well established; however, evidence on PCV responses following recovery from measles are lacking. A prospective, observational, longitudinal study currently underway in Guinea and Mali aims to assess whether measles infection leads to loss of immunity to prior pathogens and whether it alters the immune response to a controlled vaccine stimulus (e.g. rabies vaccine) after recovery.61 PCV vaccination in patients recovering from measles could be a potential strategy to overcome temporary measles induced-immune amnesia and protect against subsequent disease and death due to pneumonia.

Malaria

Data directly evaluating PCV immunogenicity, durability, carriage impact, or clinical effectiveness in children following malaria infection are limited. Defining whether malaria exposure modifies PCV responses or vaccine-type disease protection – particularly among young children experiencing repeated malaria episodes – is a critical research gap, with policy implications and potential benefits for a large proportion of children globally. A systematic review found that vaccine responses including for meningococcal, cholera and typhoid vaccines were lower among those who had malaria infection or who did not have treatment compared with uninfected children or those undergoing malaria treatment.62 However, the number of studies evaluating each vaccine was small (one to four studies per vaccine) and should be interpreted with caution.62 Another study showed similar PCV10 antibody responses (Synflorix®) among four to seven-year-old children with current or prior parasitic exposures, including malaria (in utero or during infancy), highlighting the limited and heterogeneous evidence base.58

Research questions and considerations

Despite significant advances in the introduction of PCV globally, pneumococcus remains the leading cause of pneumonia, particularly among high-risk groups. Without further action to address the factors that are associated with high pneumonia risk, progress in reducing pneumonia-related morbidity and mortality is likely to plateau or slow. To date, research has mainly focussed on well-recognized immunocompromised groups, such as children living with HIV or those undergoing solid-organ transplantation. However, there remains a significant population of children who are at elevated risk of pneumococcal disease for which no guidelines on PCV use have been established.

To reduce childhood pneumonia hospitalization and mortality, and to maximize the public health impact of PCV, targeted vaccination strategies for high-risk groups could prove a useful approach, but key research gaps remain. Here, we outline the key research gaps in targeted PCV vaccination among vulnerable populations.

Define high-risk groups for targeted PCV strategies

This requires identifying children at highest risk of IPD who may benefit from additional or better-timed PCV doses, using pragmatic criteria, e.g. severe or moderate malnutrition, recent hospitalization, recent measles or malaria infection, under-vaccination, and high-exposure household settings. Understanding these risk windows and the optimal timing of vaccination may enable targeted vaccination strategies to complement routine schedules.

Identify optimal delivery methods

In many settings, the immediate priority to reduce pneumococcal mortality is to improve timeliness and completeness of routine PCV delivery, reduce missed opportunities for vaccination, and provide catch-up vaccination for zero-dose and under-vaccinated children. Hospital admission is an important opportunity to identify children with incomplete or unknown vaccination status. WHO hospital care guidance recommends checking a child’s immunization card before discharge, identifying any vaccines still needed, and giving these vaccines before the child leaves hospital.63 However, implementation of hospital-based catch-up vaccination is often inconsistent, and missed opportunities for vaccination remain common. Strengthening catch-up vaccination for all hospitalized children should therefore be a priority. The targeted strategies discussed in this review are intended to complement, rather than replace, this approach. Children affected by malnutrition, measles, or malaria may require booster PCV doses due to specific immune vulnerabilities, in addition to any missed routine vaccines. Equity-focused strategies such as integration with nutrition programs, inpatient or post-discharge pathways, malaria control programs and outbreak responses should be considered for local settings. Considerations include identification of eligible children, coverage and timeliness, acceptability, cold-chain constraints, opportunity costs, and potential unintended consequences.

Determine optimal vaccine strategies

This includes: (i) defining whether PCV strategies should differ by type and severity of malnutrition, including stunting, wasting, and the period of nutritional recovery; (ii) booster vaccination at hospital discharge or during follow-up for children admitted with malnutrition, measles, or other infectious illness; and (iii) choice of vaccine, including cost, valency and effectiveness. These strategies should be evaluated using outcomes relevant to program impact (e.g. vaccine-serotype IPD, severe pneumonia). Maintaining flexibility in national and global guidance to allow targeted approaches for defined high-risk populations will be important, especially as new vaccines become available.

Priority studies include: (i) evaluating PCV immunogenicity and durability with standardized definitions of malnutrition, and timing of vaccination relative to acute illness and recovery; (ii) effectiveness studies among high-risk cohorts (including post-discharge mortality and severe pneumonia outcomes); and (iii) trials or quasi-experimental evaluations of targeted booster or catch-up strategies embedded within routine care pathways. Where feasible, studies should incorporate carriage outcomes to better interpret direct and indirect effects.

Conclusion

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have substantially reduced child deaths globally, yet pneumococcus remains a leading cause of pneumonia mortality, with the greatest burden in LMICs and among children with malnutrition and infection-associated immune dysfunction. With many countries off track to meet the 2025 World Health Assembly global nutrition targets and the 2030 Sustainable Development Goal 2 targets – and only around a quarter of countries on track to halve stunting by 2030 – smarter, equity-driven vaccine policies are required to close persistent gaps in protection. Standard PCV schedules may provide suboptimal protection for high-risk groups of children, who are also less likely to benefit from indirect effects in settings with uneven coverage and sustained transmission. In addition to improving timeliness and completeness of routine PCV, and strengthening catch-up delivery to reduce zero-dose and under-vaccinated children, targeted strategies in clearly defined high-risk children to reduce immunity gaps represent important opportunities to reduce inequities and accelerate further declines in pneumococcal disease and mortality.

Biography
