Progesterone increases metabolism via the pentose phosphate pathway in bovine uterine epithelial cells
Authors: Malia D Berg, Camila U Braz, Matthew Dean
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring early pregnancy, glucose is essential for the uterine epithelium and the developing embryo. In cows, progesterone increases the secretion of glucose into the uterine lumen. The uterine epithelium can convert glucose to fructose, but other fates of glucose in the uterine epithelium have been sparsely investigated. Therefore, our objective was to investigate how progesterone influences glucose metabolism in immortalized bovine uterine epithelial (BUTE) cells.MethodsBUTE cells were grown to 80% confluence and treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 10 µM progesterone for 24 h. Cells were collected and analyzed. Immunohistochemistry was performed on endometrial samples collected from the bovine endometrium on days 1 and 11 of the reproductive cycle.ResultsProgesterone treatment increased glucose consumption of BUTE cells. RNAseq identified 3,072 genes regulated by progesterone. KEGG analysis indicated that progesterone altered genes associated with metabolic pathways and glutathione metabolism. Manually examining genes unique to specific glucose metabolic pathways identified an increase in the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway—glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. Functionally, a major product of the pentose phosphate pathway is NADPH, and progesterone treatment increased NADPH levels in BUTE cells. In cows, immunohistochemistry confirmed that glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase levels were higher in the uterine epithelium in the luteal phase when progesterone concentrations are high.ConclusionsProgesterone increased glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase expression and metabolism via the pentose phosphate pathway in the bovine uterine epithelium. This metabolism could provide substrates for cell proliferation, molecules to be secreted into the uterine lumen, or maintain reduction/oxidation balance in the uterine epithelium.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-024-10001-0.Keywords: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, Uterus, Progesterone, Cow

Introduction

In cattle, fertilization of the oocyte occurs in the oviduct. The newly formed zygote begins to develop as it moves through the oviduct and toward the uterus. Five days after fertilization, the embryo enters the uterine lumen as a ball of undifferentiated cells termed a morula. The bovine embryo soon becomes a blastocyst, which elongates before attaching to the uterine epithelium 18–21 days post-fertilization [1]. The epithelium lining the uterine lumen secretes a mixture of nutrients and growth factors, termed histotroph, that support embryonic development [2]. Cattle have a shallow synepitheliochorial placenta, meaning that the conceptus never breaches the basement membrane under the uterine epithelium [1]. Hence, histotrophic support remains essential through pregnancy.

After ovulation, the endometrium is also remodeled in preparation for a potential pregnancy due to the actions of progesterone. While the bovine uterus does not decidualize, the endometrium is remodeled to support the preimplantation embryo and facilitate embryo attachment [3–5]. Progesterone has distinct tissue-specific effects on the uterine epithelium and stroma in preparation for pregnancy [6]. These include remodeling of the extracellular matrix, thinning of the endometrium, and alterations in metabolism [3–5, 7]. In human and mouse models, progesterone induces glucose transporter transporters (GLUTs [gene family SLC2A]) expression and glucose uptake by the uterine epithelium [8, 9]. In cattle, progesterone increases secretion of glucose into the uterine lumen [10–12].

Glucose is one of the major nutrients used by the embryo and the uterine epithelium [13–15]. Glucose enters the uterine epithelium cells via facilitative diffusion through GLUTs or by secondary active transport by sodium-glucose linked transporters (SGLTs [gene family [SLC5A]) [16]. Once inside the epithelial cells, glucose can be metabolized through several pathways. The uterine epithelium can convert glucose to fructose, which can be used by preimplantation embryos [17, 18]. Glucose metabolism via other pathways requires phosphorylation of glucose by hexokinase to produce G6P. G6P can enter glycolysis to produce ATP and pyruvate. G6P can be used to synthesize glycogen, the storage form of glucose [14, 19]. G6P can be used to glycosylate proteins [15, 20].

Finally, G6P can be metabolized by the pentose phosphate pathway, also called the hexose monophosphate shunt. The first part of the pentose phosphate pathway (termed oxidative) produces 2 NADPH molecules for every G6P. The second part (i.e., the non-oxidative phase) can produce ribose-5-phosphate (R5P), a precursor for nucleic acid synthesis, or metabolites that feedback into glycolysis to produce ATP [21]. However, very little is known about the regulation of the pentose phosphate pathway in the uterine epithelium [14].

NADPH has two important roles in cell physiology. First, NADPH is crucial in managing oxidative stress. NADPH is used to regenerate glutathione. Glutathione is an important antioxidant, protecting cells from reactive oxygen species when it is oxidated to glutathione disulfide. NADPH is used by catalase to convert hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Second, NADPH is also an essential substrate in anabolic pathways. NAPDH serves as an electron donor in the synthesis of macromolecules such as cholesterol, proteins, and fatty acids [21].

Our objectives in the current study were to (1) determine how progesterone treatment affects glucose uptake of BUTE cells, (2) use RNAseq to identify global changes in gene expression in BUTE cells that would indicate how progesterone affects glucose metabolism, and (3) confirm changes in pentose phosphate pathway metabolism due to progesterone treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Immortalized BUTE cells were previously generated from a Holstein dairy cow using a plasmid that contains SV40 large and small T-antigens. BUTE cells have been shown to express keratin, lack vimentin, and express the progesterone receptor [22, 23]. BUTE cells were maintained in αMEM media containing 5.55 mM glucose supplemented with 10% FBS (12103 C-500ML, Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM L-glutamine (GLL01-100ML, Caisson Labs), 10 mg/ml ITS (25-800-CR, Corning) 1.8 ng/ml EGF (20053100UG, Shenandoah Biotechnology), and 18.2 ng/ml estradiol-17β (E2758-1G, Sigma). BUTE cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and were passed 1:10 every 3–4 days.

Cell culture experiments

BUTE cells were plated and allowed to grow until 80% confluent. Then the media was removed, rinsed twice with PBS, and the cells were serum and steroid starved in media without phenol red and treated with IGF1 (50 ng/ml; 2000550UG, Shenandoah Biotechnology) for 24 h [23]. The media was removed and replaced with serum and steroid-free media containing 50 ng/ml of IGF1 and 10 µM progesterone or an equal concentration of vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for all experiments.

Glucose assay

BUTE cells were plated in a 10 cm dish and treated with vehicle or progesterone for 24 h. Media was collected from each 10 cm plate at 24 h and aliquoted into 1 ml microcentrifuge tubes. All samples were stored at -20 °C. Standards and samples (10 µl) were added to a 96-well plate in duplicate. Next, 200 µl of Wako Reagent (250 µl; 99703001, FUJIFILM Medical Systems) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The plate was read at 505 nm using the Biotek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader (Agilent). The standard curve was used to determine the concentration of glucose in each sample.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from BUTE cells using the Quick-RNA MiniPrep Plus Kit (R1058, Zymo Research) per the manufacturer’s instructions. BUTE cells were plated in a 10 cm dish and treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 10 µM progesterone for 24 h. Media was removed, rinsed 2 times with PBS, and lysed. The samples were transferred into the Spin-Away™ Filter and centrifuged. Ethanol (300 µl) was added to the flow-through. Then, the sample was transferred into a Zymo-Spin IIICG Column in a collection tube and centrifuged and the flow-through was discarded. Samples were treated with DNase I and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Next, the column was washed with RNA wash buffer two times, and the RNA was eluted in 100 µl of DNase/RNase-Free water. The RNA samples were stored at -80 °C. Aliquots of the RNA were separated on a 1% bleach gel at 100 V for 60 min to confirm the quality of the RNA [24].

RNA sequencing and quality check

The RNAseq libraries were prepared with Illumina’s ‘TruSeq Stranded mRNAseq Sample Prep kit’ (Illumina) at the Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. The libraries were pooled and quantitated by qPCR and sequenced on one SP lane for 101 cycles on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform, generating 100 pb single-end reads (Illumina). Fastq files were generated and demultiplexed with the bcl2fastq Conversion Software (version 2.20, Illumina). Adaptors were trimmed from the 3’-end of the reads.

Quality check of raw reads was performed using FastQC (version 0.11.8, http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) on individual samples. Average per-base read quality scores were over 30 in all samples, and no adapter sequences were found, indicating those reads are high in quality.

Alignment and gene-level quantification

Salmon version 1.4.0 was used to quasi-map reads to the transcriptome of the bovine reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2, NCBI) and quantify the abundance of each transcript [25]. Then quasi-mapping was performed with the additional arguments --seqBias and --gcBias to correct sequence-specific and GC content biases, --numBootstraps = 30 to compute bootstrap transcript abundance estimates, and --validateMappings to help improve the accuracy of mappings. Gene-level counts were then estimated based on transcript-level counts using the “bias corrected counts without an offset” method from the “tximport” R package [26]. The percentage of reads mapped to the transcriptome ranged from 81.4 to 86.2%. The unmapped reads were discarded, and the remaining reads, varying from 23.8 to 32.5 million per sample, were kept for further analysis.

Normalization and differential gene expression analysis

The trimmed mean of M-values normalization [27] in the edgeR package [28] was performed to calculate a normalization factor for each sample to adjust for biases in RNA composition. In total, 13,353 expressed genes with at least 0.5 CPM in at least 4 samples were considered for differential expression analysis. Multidimensional scaling in the limma package [29] was used as a sample quality control step to check for outliers or batch effects. The normalized logCPM values of the topmost 5,000 variable genes were chosen to construct the multidimensional scaling plot. The RUV method [29] was used to estimate extra factors that are spurious technical variations in the samples and not biologically related.

DE analysis between the DMSO and P4 was performed using the limma-trend method [30] with treatment and 1 RUV factor included in the model. The statistical tests were corrected for multiple testing, and only genes with an FDR < 0.05 were considered significant.

Functional enrichment analysis

Pathway analysis of genes regulated by progesterone was performed using ShinyGO v.0.80 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) [31] based on the Bos taurus database. The thresholds used were: FDR cutoff was 0.05, minimum pathway size was 2, and maximum pathway size was 2,000. Pathways significantly overrepresented were determined by KEGG [32].

NADPH assay

BUTE cells were plated in a 10 cm dish, serum and steroid starved with IGF1, and treated with vehicle or progesterone for 24 h. At the end of the experiment, media was collected from each 10 cm plate, aliquoted into 1 ml microcentrifuge tubes, rinsed twice with PBS, and collected in 200 µl of lysis buffer. Samples were centrifuged at 254 x g for 5 min. All samples were stored at -20 °C. The level of NADPH in each sample was determined with an NADPH assay kit (ab186031, Abcam) per manufacturer instructions. Standards and samples (50 µl each) were added to a 96-well plate in duplicate. NADPH reaction mix (50 µl) was added to the wells and mixed. The plate was incubated for 2 h at room temperature and then read at 460 nm using the Biotek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader (Agilent). The standard curve was used to determine the level of NADPH in each sample. NADPH levels in cells were normalized to protein content as determined by a BCA assay.

Immunohistochemistry

Uterine samples were collected on days 1 and 11 of the bovine estrus cycle as part of a previous project [33, 34]. Procedures were approved by the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (no.19036). Briefly, the reproductive cycle was synchronized using the Double-Ovsynch treatment protocol [35]. Cows were administered gonadotropin hormone-releasing hormone (GnRH; 100 mg in 2 ml injected intramuscular, Zoetis Animal Health) followed by an injection of PGF2α (25 mg injected intramuscular, Zoetis Animal Health) 7 days later. A second injection of GnRH was given 72 h after PGF2α. Animals were sacrificed 2 days or 12 days later, corresponding to day 1 or day 11 of the cycle (day 0 = estrus). At sacrifice, the uteri were removed, and biopsies from the inter-caruncle regions of the uterine body were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and placed into paraffin blocks.

Immunohistochemistry was carried out as done previously [33, 34]. One slide per animal (n = 4 animals for each day) with 2–3 tissue sections per slide were used for immunohistochemistry. Paraffin sections. (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Slides were boiled in sodium citrate to retrieve antigens. The slides were placed in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H325-500, Fisher Scientific) for 15 min and then placed into a hydrated chamber with a block for 1 h (3% BSA and 10% goat serum in TBS). The primary antibody against glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD; Ab7659, Abcam) or IgG (as an isotype-matched negative control; I-1000-5, Vector Laboratories) was added to the slides incubated at 4 °C overnight. The slides were washed in TBS-T, and the secondary antibody (BA-5000, Vector Laboratories) was added for thirty minutes at room temperature. The slides were washed again, and the avidin-biotin complex was added. After the final wash, the DAB substrate for peroxidase was added (SK-4100, Vector Laboratories). Then, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted with Permount (SP15100, Fisher Scientific). Images were captured with a Zeiss Axioskop upright microscope with an Axiocam 305 color camera (Zeiss).

Staining for G6PD was quantified using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/). Images (40x) were exported as JPEGs from Zen Blue v3.3 (Zeiss) and opened in ImageJ. All non-epithelial cells and empty spaces in each image were removed. The number of pixels positive for G6PD (stained brown) and the total number of pixels representing epithelial cells was determined using the color threshold tool in the red, blue, and green color space. The percent area positive for G6PD immunostaining was calculated.

Statistics

RNAseq data were analyzed as described above. All other data were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad PRISM (Version 10.3.0). Every treatment was replicated 4 times, and the significance criterion alpha was P < 0.05.

Results

To determine how progesterone affected the glucose consumption of BUTE cells, we analyzed the concentration of glucose remaining in the media after 24 h of treatment. The αMEM media used has a physiological glucose concentration (5.55 mM). After 24 h of treatment, the control cells had reduced the glucose in the media to 2.5 mM. In cells treated with progesterone, the glucose remaining in the media was reduced further to 1.8 mM (P < 0.05), indicating that progesterone treatment increased glucose uptake (Fig. 1).

Progesterone treatment increases glucose uptake in BUTE cells. Glucose remaining in the media after 24 h of treatment with DMSO (Control) or 10 µM progesterone. The dashed line represented the starting concentration of glucose in the media (5.55 mM). n = 4. *P < 0.05 relative to Control

To gain insights into how progesterone may affect the metabolism of uterine epithelial cells, BUTE cells were treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 10 µM progesterone for 24 hours, and RNA was isolated. After quality control, the list of significantly altered genes was compared to the complete list of detected genes for evidence of any bias. Coding sequence length, transcript length, genome span, 5’ UTR length, 3’ UTR, GC content, and spread of genes across the chromosomes were all similar between the two groups (Supplemental Fig. 1).

A total of 13,354 gene transcripts were detected. Multidimensional scaling plot showed that Control and progesterone treatment gene expression patterns clearly separated along Dimension 1 (Fig. 2A). Of mRNAs detected, 1,623 genes were significantly upregulated, and 1,449 genes downregulated (Fig. 2B) in the progesterone-treated samples. Confirming our model, genes previously shown to be stimulated by progesterone in the bovine uterine epithelium in vivo (SERPING1, SERPINE2, PRDX6, PMVK, S100A2, PXMP2, SULT1A1) were increased by progesterone in BUTE cells (Fig. 2B) [36, 37].

Progesterone treatment significantly altered genes and pathways related to metabolism and oxidative stress. A) Multidimensional scaling plot shows separation of control (DMSO) and progesterone (P4) groups along Dimension 1. B) Volcano plot showing the 1,623 genes that were significantly upregulated, 1,449 genes downregulated, and 10,282 not significantly altered by progesterone. C) KEGG analysis of significantly altered genes found 6 pathways that were significantly over-represented due to progesterone treatment in the RNAseq data. n = 4

KEGG analysis of all significantly altered genes (FDR < 0.05; 3,072 genes) found 6 significantly overrepresented pathways, including Metabolic pathway and Glutathione metabolism (Fig. 2C). Over-representation of these pathways supports the idea that progesterone alters metabolism in the bovine uterine epithelium. A complete list of detected genes is available in Supplemental Table 1. The full dataset is available in the NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus database (Accession number GSE269076).

Our lab is specifically interested in uterine glucose metabolism [14]. Therefore, we manually identified genes unique for specific steps or pathways in glucose metabolism─ glucose uptake (GLUTs and SGLTs), fructose production (polyol pathway), glucose phosphorylation (hexokinase 1–4), glycolysis, protein glycosylation (hexosamine biosynthetic pathway), glycogen metabolism, and the pentose phosphate pathway. In glucose uptake, SLC2A4 (GLUT4) was significantly increased by progesterone treatment (FDR < 0.0001). SLC2A3 (GLUT3) and SLC2A10 (GLUT10) tended to be decreased by progesterone treatment (FDR < 0.1). Neither SLC5A1 (SGLT1) nor SLC5A2 (SGLT2) were altered by treatment (Fig. 3A). Three of the hexokinases (HK1-HK3) were detected; however, none were significantly altered (Fig. 3B). In the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, no genes were significantly altered, though GFPT2 tended to be increased (FDR < 0.1; Fig. 3C). In glycolysis, ENO3 was significantly decreased (FDR < 0.01). PGAM1 tended to decrease (FDR < 0.1; Fig. 3D). One of two genes in the polyol pathway was significantly decreased (SORD; FDR < 0.01; Fig. 3E). Four genes specific for glycogen metabolism were significantly decreased (PGM2, GYG1, PYGL, and AGL; FDR < 0.05), and three other genes tended to differ between control and progesterone treated (PGM1, GYS1, and GBE1; FDR < 0.1; Fig. 3F). Finally, in the pentose phosphate pathway, 3 of 7 genes were significantly changed (G6PD, RPE, and TKT; FDR < 0.05). PDG tended to be increased due to progesterone treatment (FDR < 0.1; Fig. 3G).

C) Genes unique to the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, which glycosylates proteins. D) Genes in glycolysis. E) Genes in the polyol pathway, which converts glucose to fructose. G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, which is the major source of NADPH in cells [21]. Therefore, we sought to confirm increased production of NAPDH in progesterone-treated cells. We measured NADPH concentrations in the cells and the media after 24 h of treatment with vehicle (DMSO) or 10 µM progesterone. Progesterone increased NADPH concentrations in the BUTE cells by 35% (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Low concentrations of NADPH could be detected in the spent cell media but were not affected by progesterone (Fig. 4D).

Progesterone treatment increases intracellular NADPH levels. A) NADPH levels in cells treated with progesterone for 24 h. B) NADPH concentration in the spent media after treating BUTE cells with vehicle or progesterone for 24 h. n = 4. *P < 0.05 relative to Control

To obtain data supporting our results in vivo, we performed immunohistochemistry for G6PD on uterine biopsies previously collected on day 1 (low progesterone) and day 11 (high progesterone) of the reproductive cycle in cattle [33]. On day 1, G6PD immunostaining was barely above the background. On day 11, when progesterone concentrations are high, immunostaining was darker and localized to the cytoplasm uterine epithelium, with a punctuated pattern in the luminal and glandular epithelium (Fig. 5A; n = 4). Quantification of the immunostaining indicated that G6PD protein levels were approximately 5-fold higher on day 11 than day 1 (Fig. 5B; P < 0.01).

Discussion

The bovine and murine uterine epithelium expresses several members of the SLC3A (GLUT) and SLC5A (SGLT) subfamilies [8, 38, 39], indicating that the epithelium can take up and release glucose. There are several possible fates for this glucose. Work in pigs has also shown that the epithelium can metabolize glucose via the polyol pathway, converting glucose to fructose [40]. Livestock embryos can metabolize both glucose and fructose; however, the relative importance of these two carbohydrates in embryo development is still unclear [41]. The uterine epithelium is also heavily glycosylated, which regulates embryo attachment to the uterine epithelium [42, 43]. Our lab has shown that the uterine epithelium in mice and cows stores glucose as the macromolecule glycogen [33, 34]. Here, our RNAseq results show that BUTE cells respond to progesterone and express all of the necessary genes to metabolize glucose via these pathways. BUTE cells may be a valuable model to thoroughly investigate the regulation of these different metabolic fates of glucose.

The RNAseq showed that the uterine epithelium expresses the necessary genes to metabolize glucose via the pentose phosphate pathway. Expression of many of these genes (3 of 7) was regulated by progesterone. Most importantly, progesterone increased mRNA levels of G6PD, the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway [21]. Supporting increased function of this pathway after progesterone treatment, progesterone also increased intracellular NADPH levels.

An important question arising from this work is: what is the role of progesterone-stimulated NADPH production? This is a complex question and outside the scope of our current work because the pentose phosphate pathway makes products that contribute to many different biological processes [21]. NAPDH is required by many antioxidant enzymes [21]. Previous work has shown that cattle manipulated to have a large CL and hence higher progesterone concentrations also had higher activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as catalase, glutathione peroxidases, and superoxide dismutase in the endometrium [44, 45]. Collectively, these results suggest that progesterone regulates oxidative stress in the bovine endometrium.

NADPH is also an electron donor for lipid synthesis [21]. Progesterone has been shown to change the lipids species in bovine uterine fluid [46]. In our RNAseq dataset, acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA) and fatty acid synthase (FASN), two key regulatory enzymes in lipid synthesis, were increased by progesterone. Hence, progesterone may increase glucose uptake, NADPH production, lipid production, and secretion into the uterine lumen to support embryo elongation. This merits further investigation in the future.

In addition to NADPH, the pentose phosphate pathway produces R5P, a precursor to nucleic acid synthesis. Several cyclin mRNAs were increased by progesterone treatment, and increased metabolism via the pentose phosphate pathway could support cell proliferation. However, immunostaining for Ki67, a proliferation marker, is highest at estrus and decreases in the luteal phase [5].

The expression of RPE, an enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, did decrease due to progesterone treatment. RPE converts ribulose-5-phosphate to xylulose-5-phosphate. However, ribulose-5-phosphate can also be metabolized by RPIA to produce R5P. R5P can then be metabolized by transketolase, essentially bypassing RPE. Thus, even if decreased RPE mRNA expression results in decreased protein expression, it is likely to have little effect on metabolism by the pentose phosphate pathway.

Most of the experiments reported here were done using our immortalized BUTE cells as a model of the uterine epithelium [22]. We were able to confirm higher expression of G6PD in the uterine epithelium during the luteal phase when progesterone concentrations were high. These results are highly novel, as we have found no other reports on the pentose phosphate in the uterine epithelium [14]. The long list of genes directly affected by progesterone in this work provides numerous targets that could be more deeply investigated in vivo.

We used 10 µM progesterone, which is much higher than serum concentrations. However, the concentration of progesterone in uterine tissue is much higher than in serum, ranging from 169 to 297 ng/g (around 1 µM) [47, 48]. We have found that 10 µM progesterone is necessary to mimic the changes we have observed in the luteal phase, such as lower glycogen content and higher expression of pAMPK in BUTE cells ( [33] and unpublished observations). In this work, 10 µM of progesterone increased G6PD mRNA, which agrees with the higher immunostaining for G6PD during the luteal phase.

In summary, we show that progesterone increases glucose consumption by BUTE cells, in agreement with data from other species. RNAseq identified several genes related to glucose metabolism that were affected by progesterone treatment. Pursuing the pentose phosphate pathway, we found that progesterone increased NADPH production in these cells. Supporting our findings in vivo, G6PD (the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway) was higher in the uterine epithelium during the luteal phase when progesterone concentrations are elevated. These results are the first to show that pentose phosphate metabolism is active in the uterine epithelium and regulated by progesterone. Future research should investigate the role of the pentose phosphate pathway in the uterine epithelium and determine its role in supporting pregnancy.
