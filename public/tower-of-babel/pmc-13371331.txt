MRI-based evaluation of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and optic nerve subarachnoid space alterations in idiopathic intracranial hypertension and normal tension glaucoma: a systematic review
Authors: Husaina Singapurwala, Shubham Gupta, Anil Rathva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) and normal tension glaucoma (NTG) are optic nerve-related disorders linked to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities but driven by distinct mechanisms. Continuity between the intracranial and optic nerve subarachnoid spaces allows CSF pressure and flow alterations to affect the optic nerve. This study aims to evaluate optic nerve subarachnoid space structure and CSF dynamics.MethodsThe Protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251267099). A systematic literature search of PubMed, Springer Nature, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Web of Science was performed for studies published between 2010 and 2025. Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts using predefined PECO criteria, resulting in the inclusion of 13 studies. Data were extracted using a standardized form capturing study characteristics, MRI methodologies, and key findings.ResultsMagnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) demonstrates distinct CSF–optic nerve imaging patterns in NTG and IIH. NTG is characterized by impaired CSF dynamics, narrowing of the optic nerve sheath, and optic nerve kinking, consistent with reduced CSF pressure. In contrast, IIH shows optic nerve sheath distension, posterior globe flattening, and pituitary gland flattening, reflecting elevated intracranial pressure. In addition to conventional T1- and T2-weighted sequences, STIR, and HASTE imaging, advanced MRI techniques such as diffusion-weighted imaging, 4D flow MRI, and functional MRI enable the detection of subtle CSF flow alterations, supporting their potential role as non-invasive imaging biomarkers.ConclusionMRI provides valuable insights into alterations in peri-optic cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and optic nerve morphology observed in idiopathic intracranial hypertension and normal tension glaucoma. Although these conditions are typically differentiated clinically, MRI findings may contribute to understanding disease mechanisms and may serve as supportive imaging markers.

Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) plays a critical role in maintaining intracranial homeostasis, providing mechanical protection, metabolic waste clearance, and pressure regulation within the central nervous system. Alterations in CSF dynamics and pressure transmission have been implicated in several optic nerve–related disorders, particularly idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) and normal tension glaucoma (NTG). Both conditions affect the optic nerve but are characterized by fundamentally different CSF-related pathophysiological mechanisms [1, 2].

In IIH, elevated intracranial pressure is transmitted through the subarachnoid space to the optic nerve sheath, leading to optic nerve sheath distension, globe flattening, and papilledema [3]. In contrast, NTG has been proposed to involve reduced peri-optic CSF or altered CSF circulation. This may theoretically increase the translaminar pressure gradient (TLPG) across the lamina cribrosa; however, direct in vivo measurement of retrolaminar CSF pressure remains technically challenging, and the TLPG hypothesis remains mechanistically plausible but incompletely validated [4]. These contrasting mechanisms suggest that disease-specific alterations in CSF flow and optic nerve subarachnoid space (ONSAS) morphology may be detectable using advanced imaging techniques [5].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers a non-invasive approach to evaluate CSF dynamics and peri-optic anatomical changes [6]. Techniques such as phase-contrast MRI, volumetric assessment of the ONSAS, and functional MRI-based CSF flow analysis have been increasingly applied to investigate optic nerve involvement in both IIH and NTG [1]. Prior studies have reported characteristic MRI findings, including increased optic nerve sheath diameter in IIH and reduced CSF flow metrics or compartmentalization of the ONSAS in NTG, highlighting the potential of MRI to elucidate the divergent pathophysiological mechanisms affecting the optic nerve in these conditions [7, 8].

In this systematic review, the term connectivity refers specifically to the physiological and functional communication between the intracranial subarachnoid space and the optic nerve subarachnoid space, mediated by CSF flow, pressure transmission, and compartmental patency, rather than neural or synaptic connectivity. The purpose of this systematic review is to summarize current evidence on MRI findings related to CSF dynamics and optic nerve morphology in IIH and NTG. Rather than serving as primary diagnostic tools, these imaging findings may provide complementary insights into the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms affecting the optic nerve in both conditions.

Method

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.

Reporting methods

This Systematic Review was conducted following the (PRISMA) guidelines [9]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251267099).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed, Springer Nature Link, Science Direct, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search covered studies published between January 2010 and January 2025. Key search terms included combinations of “cerebrospinal fluid,” “optic nerve subarachnoid space,” “normal tension glaucoma,” “idiopathic intracranial hypertension,” “MRI,” and “CSF flow,” using Boolean operators AND and OR. Reference lists of eligible articles were manually screened to identify additional relevant studies. Eligibility criteria

The study selection criteria were based on the PECO framework [10], if they (i) involved human participants diagnosed with IIH or NTG, (ii) employed MRI-based techniques to assess CSF dynamics or ONSAS characteristics, and (iii) reported original data. Review articles, case reports with fewer than five subjects, animal studies, non-English publications, and studies lacking MRI-based CSF or ONSAS assessment were excluded. Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to identify eligible studies. Full-text articles were subsequently assessed for inclusion, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The reviewer used Covidence software for screening [11]. For this review, CSF connectivity was operationally defined using MRI-derived surrogate markers reflecting physiological communication between the intracranial and peri-optic subarachnoid spaces, including CSF flow metrics, optic nerve sheath distensibility, and evidence of compartmentalization within the optic nerve subarachnoid space.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for the study design [12]. Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Owing to substantial heterogeneity in MRI techniques, outcome measures, and study designs, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible; therefore, a narrative synthesis was performed. Studies were grouped according to clinical condition (IIH or NTG) and primary MRI-based parameters, including optic nerve metrics, optic nerve subarachnoid space (ONSAS) characteristics, and CSF measures. Patterns across studies were evaluated in terms of direction of findings, magnitude of reported effects, and diagnostic performance. Individual study results were summarized using reported statistical measures, including means, standard deviations, ranges, proportions, sensitivity, specificity, and correlation coefficients.

Results

The literature search across five electronic databases identified 8,657 records. After the removal of 3,750 duplicate records, 4,907 articles were screened based on title and abstract. Of these, 4,110 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Full-text assessment was performed for 797 articles, following which 484 studies were excluded. Study characteristics

A total of 13 MRI-based imaging studies were included in this review, originating from the United States, Germany, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Austria, Egypt, and Russia, as shown in Fig. 3. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, with additional representation from upper- and lower-middle-income regions. Publication trends demonstrated limited activity between 2010 and 2015, followed by increased research output from 2019 onward, with a marked rise between 2021 and 2025. MRI techniques included phase-contrast MRI, volumetric assessment of the optic nerve subarachnoid space, and functional MRI-based CSF flow analysis. All included studies utilized MRI to evaluate the optic nerve, CSF dynamics, and indirect markers of intracranial pressure in patients with IIH and NTG. Imaging was performed using 1.5 T or 3 T MRI scanners from Siemens, GE, or Philips, employing standard head coils with 16–32 channels. MRI-derived parameters related to peri-optic CSF communication, including CSF flow metrics and optic nerve sheath morphology, were reported across the included studies.

Study population characteristics

Across the included studies, participant populations consisted of patients diagnosed with IIH, NTG, and healthy control subjects. Sample sizes varied widely among studies, ranging from 8 to 102 participants. The majority of studies included adult populations, with reported mean ages ranging from 19 to 88 years. The IIH cohorts predominantly included female participants, whereas the NTG and control groups showed a more balanced sex distribution; several studies used age and sex matched control groups. In NTG studies, imaging assessments were performed on either one or both eyes, depending on study design, while IIH studies typically evaluated one eye per participant. One study stratified NTG patients by disease severity, and another categorized IIH patients based on diagnostic certainty.

MRI techniques overview

MRI techniques varied across studies and included high-resolution structural imaging, diffusion-based imaging, and flow-sensitive sequences. Structural evaluation of the optic nerve and ONSAS was primarily performed using T2-weighted turbo spin echo, STIR, HASTE, and 3D DRIVE sequences, with measurements commonly obtained approximately 3 mm posterior to the globe. Parameters assessed included optic nerve sheath diameter, optic nerve tortuosity, elongation, and posterior globe contour.

Advanced techniques such as diffusion-weighted imaging, diffusion tensor imaging, and phase-contrast MRI, 4D flow MRI, and MR venography were used to evaluate CSF motion, venous sinus anatomy, and optic nerve microstructure. One study incorporated functional MRI to assess CSF inflow dynamics in conjunction with BOLD signal changes. Impaired CSF dynamics

Patients with NTG exhibit a significantly reduced CSF flow range ratio (FRR) compared with age-matched controls (0.55 ± 0.06 vs. 0.62 ± 0.03; p < 0.0001), indicating impaired CSF turnover along the optic nerve. This reduction is attributed to compartmentalization of the optic nerve sheath, which restricts normal CSF exchange between the intracranial subarachnoid space and the peri-optic subarachnoid space, ultimately resulting in reduced peri-optic CSF pressure [13].

Functional MRI studies indicate that, in glaucoma (including but not limited to NTG), CSF dynamics become decoupled from task-related brain activity, notably, as observed by Bang et al. [15] investigated glaucoma broadly rather than NTG specifically; however, the findings remain relevant to understanding CSF dynamics in glaucomatous optic neuropathy. Under normal conditions, CSF volume increases after visual stimulation and decreases during task initiation; however, these fluctuations progressively diminish with disease severity. In NTG, chronic vascular dysregulation, reduced CSF pressure, and impaired perfusion contribute to metabolic stress in the optic nerve and visual cortex, leading to retinal ganglion cell dysfunction. As the BOLD signal depends on intact neurovascular coupling, these combined neuronal and vascular impairments result in attenuated BOLD responses, highlighting disrupted activity-linked CSF dynamics as a potential non-invasive biomarker [15].

Structural and morphometric changes

Patients with NTG demonstrate characteristic structural alterations, including a significantly narrower ONSAS compared with healthy controls (1.371 ± 0.035 mm vs. 1.52 ± 0.133 mm; p = 0.011). These changes are accompanied by increased lamina cribrosa depth and reduced lamina cribrosa thickness, findings often associated with low systemic blood pressure and reduced CSF pressure, resulting in an elevated TLPG. Narrowing of the ONSAS reflects compromised ocular biomechanical properties and plays a key role in NTG pathogenesis, highlighting the value of these structural markers in disease assessment and diagnosis [16].

Optic nerve kinking was reported with high prevalence in NTG cohorts; however, similar findings have also been documented in IIH, where elevated intracranial pressure leads to optic nerve tortuosity and sheath distension. In IIH, kinking is typically accompanied by peri-optic CSF expansion, whereas in NTG it may occur without sheath distension. Whether these represent morphologically distinct entities or overlapping biomechanical responses remains uncertain and requires further investigation [14].

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension

MRI findings in IIH were characterized by features associated with elevated intracranial pressure. Nine Studies consistently reported increased ONSD and elevated optic nerve sheath volume compared with controls, while optic nerve diameter remained relatively unchanged. Optic nerve sheath (ONS) and volume

The precise etiology of IIH remains unclear; however, proposed mechanisms include impaired CSF absorption, increased cerebral venous pressure, and altered glymphatic clearance, all of which may contribute to elevated intracranial pressure [26]. This pressure elevation promotes increased CSF accumulation within the peri-optic subarachnoid space. Structurally, patients with IIH demonstrate significant widening of the optic nerve sheath (5.81–5.90 mm) compared with healthy controls (4.74–4.95 mm; p < 0.001) [17, 20], accompanied by a marked increase in ONS volume (341.86 ± 163.69 mm3 vs. 127.56 ± 53.17 mm3; p < 0.001) [21]. Notably, the diameter of the optic nerve itself remains relatively unchanged. Following therapeutic CSF drainage, the optic nerve sheath does not immediately return to its baseline dimensions, suggesting reduced elastic recoil. Concurrently, a decrease in fractional anisotropy indicates transient compression of optic nerve fibers. Importantly, these changes appear to be reversible, with rapid restoration of axoplasmic flow following normalization of intracranial pressure [25].

Intracranial structural changes

Classical MRI features of IIH include optic nerve sheath distension, posterior globe flattening, and pituitary gland flattening (which ranges from reduced pituitary height to the appearance of an empty sella, representing a spectrum of the same pathological process driven by downward herniation of the subarachnoid space through the diaphragma sellae). These features demonstrate high sensitivity (85.2–90%), specificity (91–100%), and accuracy (88.2–92.2%) [18, 23, 24], and the combined presence of multiple signs improves diagnostic confidence.

Additionally, MR venography (MRV), frequently performed as part of the MRI workup for suspected IIH, has identified cranial venous outflow abnormalities with high sensitivity (94%) and specificity (100%), and optic nerve sheath hydrops with 92% sensitivity and 89% specificity. The number of positive imaging findings correlated significantly with CSF opening pressure (r = 0.62; p = 0.01) [22]. It should be noted that Rohr et al. [22] investigated secondary intracranial hypertension rather than IIH; however, the pressure-related MRI findings are relevant and comparable. These venographic findings suggest that elevated intracranial pressure may contribute to venous sinus compression, impaired CSF absorption, and a self-perpetuating pathological cycle.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized MRI-based evidence on CSF dynamics and ONSAS alterations in IIH and NTG. Of the 13 included studies, four of which examined NTG, reporting altered peri-optic CSF dynamics and optic nerve sheath morphology under normal intraocular pressure, supporting impaired CSF communication. The remaining nine studies focused on IIH and primarily described MRI markers of elevated intracranial pressure and optic nerve sheath distension. Collectively, the findings demonstrate distinct imaging patterns in NTG and IIH, consistent with a spectrum of peri-optic CSF connectivity and pressure transmission.

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension

Nine of the 13 included studies investigated IIH and consistently identified MRI features reflecting elevated intracranial pressure, including optic nerve sheath distension, posterior globe flattening, and pituitary gland flattening [17, 18, 20–24]. Barkatullah et al. and Basimah et al. further demonstrated that combining multiple MRI markers improves diagnostic accuracy compared with reliance on any single finding [27, 28].

However, it is important to note that individual MRI features associated with intracranial hypertension have been reported in asymptomatic individuals. Chen et al. found that 49% of patients undergoing routine brain MRI, irrespective of clinical indication, exhibited at least one radiological sign of IIH, most commonly empty sella, whereas papilledema was present in only 1.7% [29]. Elevated intracranial pressure was observed only when multiple MRI signs were present concurrently. Similarly, Sayal et al. demonstrated that empty sella may occur in the absence of raised intracranial pressure, reinforcing that no single MRI finding is sufficient for a diagnosis of IIH [30]. Therefore, interpretation should rely on composite imaging patterns in conjunction with clinical correlation rather than isolated radiological features. Similar observations have been reported in population imaging analyses published in JAMA Neurology, where several radiologic markers of intracranial hypertension were detected in individuals without symptoms of raised intracranial pressure [27, 31].

IIH predominantly affects women of reproductive age with elevated body mass index, indicating an important role for hormonal and metabolic factors. Israelsen et al. reported that estrogen and progesterone may increase CSF production, thereby predisposing hormonally active women to raised intracranial pressure [32]. Serban et al. further suggested that sex-related differences in venous sinus compliance may impair cerebral venous outflow and exacerbate intracranial pressure elevation [33]. In contrast, Virdee et al. observed that age-related changes in CSF dynamics and venous compliance may reduce susceptibility to IIH in older individuals [34].

MRI findings also elucidate the mechanistic effects of elevated CSF pressure on the optic nerve. Chowdhury et al. demonstrated that increased CSF pressure results in optic nerve sheath expansion, disruption of axoplasmic flow, and development of papilledema [35]. Barakatullah et al. identified optic nerve sheath swelling, posterior globe flattening, and venous sinus narrowing as reliable, non-invasive MRI biomarkers of intracranial hypertension [28]. While Mallery et al. showed that combining multiple MRI features improves diagnostic accuracy and correlates more closely with intracranial pressure severity [36]. Similar associations were reported by Basimah et al., linking these imaging findings directly to pressure-related deformation of orbital and sellar structures [27].

Importantly, MRI remains valuable even in the absence of clinically apparent papilledema. Chen et al. and Beier et al. reported significant correlations between composite MRI scores and lumbar puncture opening pressure, supporting MRI’s role in detecting subclinical intracranial hypertension [29, 37]. Kwee et al. noted that although individual MRI signs vary in sensitivity, transverse sinus stenosis demonstrates strong diagnostic performance [38]. Sarrami et al. emphasized the complementary role of MRI and MR venography in excluding secondary causes and identifying indirect CSF-related abnormalities not detectable with routine ophthalmic assessment [7].

Glymphatic consideration in peri optic CSF dynamics

Emerging evidence suggests that glymphatic pathways may influence peri-optic CSF dynamics. Using intrathecal contrast-enhanced MRI, Eide et al. demonstrated prolonged transit time and reduced clearance of the contrast agent over 24–48 hours in individuals with IIH, suggesting impaired glymphatic transport likely associated with altered cerebral venous drainage and elevated intracranial pressure [19]. The glymphatic system facilitates aquaporin-4-mediated exchange between cerebrospinal fluid and interstitial fluid compartments. Disruption of this clearance mechanism has been implicated in intracranial pressure disorders and neurodegenerative processes. In IIH, glymphatic dysfunction may exacerbate CSF stagnation under elevated pressure states, whereas in NTG, impaired metabolic clearance within the optic nerve subarachnoid space may contribute to axonal vulnerability. Although direct imaging of optic nerve glymphatic activity remains limited, this framework provides a mechanistic bridge between CSF dynamics and optic neuropathy.

Normal tension glaucoma

NTG is a distinct optic neuropathy in which optic nerve damage occurs despite normal intraocular pressure. Fleischman et al. proposed that NTG arises from reduced CSF pressure surrounding the optic nerve, resulting in an increased translaminar pressure gradient across the lamina cribrosa and subsequent mechanical stress on optic nerve fibers [39]. This mechanism contrasts with the pressure-driven pathology observed in IIH.

Translaminar pressure gradient: conceptual and measurement challenges

A commonly proposed mechanism for optic nerve damage in NTG is an increased TLPG, defined as the difference between intraocular pressure and retrolaminar CSF pressure. Although this concept is biologically plausible, direct in vivo measurement of CSF pressure at the level of the optic nerve remains technically challenging. Lumbar puncture opening pressure is frequently used as a surrogate measure; however, it may not accurately represent peri-optic CSF pressure due to potential compartmentalization within the optic nerve subarachnoid space. Consequently, the role of the TLPG in NTG pathogenesis remains incompletely established and largely theoretical

Price et al. suggested that reduced CSF pressure may increase the effective pressure across the lamina cribrosa, thereby altering laminar biomechanics and contributing to glaucomatous optic neuropathy [40] A meta-analysis by Hoang et al. reported larger translaminar pressure differences in NTG compared with healthy controls, indicating increased structural vulnerability of the optic nerve head [4]. Siaudvytyte L et al. further correlated the translaminar gradient with changes in the neuroretinal rim [41].

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain reduced peri-optic CSF pressure in NTG, including compartmentalization of the ONSAS, vascular dysregulation affecting optic nerve perfusion, and reduced CSF turnover or impaired glymphatic clearance. These factors may increase the TLPG and biomechanical stress across the lamina cribrosa, contributing to progressive optic nerve damage despite normal intraocular pressure [42]. Consistent with these mechanisms, Prokop K et al. MRI findings reported in NTG primarily reflect alterations in peri-optic CSF dynamics and optic nerve morphology. Unlike IIH, these imaging changes occur in the absence of elevated intracranial pressure and therefore do not demonstrate typical imaging features of intracranial hypertension. Instead, the observed changes are thought to reflect altered translaminar pressure relationships and local CSF dynamics around the optic nerve [43]. Similarly, Sheng J et al. Reported abnormalities include reduced CSF flow parameters, narrowing of the optic nerve subarachnoid space, and structural changes in optic nerve configuration [1]. However, optic nerve kinking or tortuosity is not specific to NTG, as similar findings have also been reported in IIH, where elevated intracranial pressure causes optic nerve tortuosity with associated optic nerve sheath dilation. In NTG, optic nerve kinking may occur without significant sheath expansion and may reflect localized disturbances in CSF dynamics or altered biomechanical forces [44]. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted alongside other imaging and clinical features rather than considered disease-specific.

Morgan et al. showed that reduced CSF pressure relative to intraocular pressure increases stress across the lamina cribrosa, disrupts axoplasmic transport, and contributes to glaucomatous optic neuropathy [45, 46]. Price et al. further demonstrated that orbital anatomy, including optic canal size and lamina cribrosa thickness, modulates CSF pressure transmission to the retrolaminar optic nerve, contributing to interindividual variability in disease expression [40]. Lee et al. additionally suggested that alterations in CSF circulation or composition may play a role in NTG pathogenesis beyond pressure-related mechanisms [47].

Optic nerve head biomechanics and OCT correlates

Optic nerve head biomechanics have been extensively investigated using optical coherence tomography (OCT). It demonstrates that pressure differences across the lamina cribrosa produce measurable structural deformation of the optic nerve head. In IIH, elevated CSF pressure may cause anterior displacement of the lamina cribrosa associated with papilledema, whereas NTG is characterized by posterior laminar bowing and thinning, reflecting increased biomechanical strain across the optic nerve head despite normal intraocular pressure. Solano et al. demonstrated pulsatile deformation of the optic nerve head using high-frequency OCT, illustrating its capability to evaluate dynamic biomechanical changes [48]. However, advanced MRI techniques offer superior visualization of the entire optic nerve and surrounding tissues, enabling assessment of deep orbital structures and CSF-related features that are not accessible with OCT [49]. A bibliometric analysis spanning 1991–2023 further confirmed the complementary roles of OCT and MRI in optic nerve imaging, with structural MRI and diffusion imaging providing important insights into optic nerve architecture and pathology [50].

Advanced MRI studies have expanded the understanding of NTG as a neurodegenerative disorder involving the central visual pathway. Wang R et al. reported widespread white matter abnormalities along visual pathways, with diffusion changes correlating with disease severity [51]. Zhang et al. similarly demonstrated optic nerve thinning, reduced volumes of the optic chiasm and lateral geniculate nucleus, and diffusion abnormalities associated with retinal nerve fiber layer loss and visual field impairment [52]. These findings highlight MRI’s ability to detect central nervous system involvement beyond the optic nerve.

When considered collectively, the available evidence suggests that optic nerve pathology in IIH and NTG may be interpreted along a spectrum of peri-optic CSF connectivity. IIH appears to represent a state in which elevated intracranial pressure is freely transmitted to the optic nerve sheath, resulting in characteristic sheath distension and globe deformation. In contrast, NTG may involve reduced or altered peri-optic CSF communication, reflected by diminished CSF flow metrics and structural changes such as narrowing or compartmentalization of the optic nerve subarachnoid space. This spectrum-based framework provides a unifying interpretation for the divergent MRI phenotypes observed across the two conditions.

Despite these insights, the available evidence is characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity. The included studies employed MRI field strengths ranging from 1.5T to 3T, with varying coil configurations (16- to 32-channel head coils) and diverse imaging sequences. These methodological differences may contribute to variability in reported measurements and limit direct comparison of quantitative parameters across studies. Future research would benefit from standardized imaging protocols to improve reproducibility and enable cross-study comparison.

Although several MRI findings related to peri-optic CSF dynamics have been reported in both conditions, IIH and NTG are typically distinguished based on clinical presentation, ophthalmologic examination, and intracranial pressure assessment. MRI findings may provide complementary information regarding optic nerve morphology and CSF dynamics, thereby contributing to improved understanding of the underlying pathophysiological processes.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the number of included studies was relatively small (n = 13), which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies in terms of sample size, patient demographics, MRI field strength, imaging protocols, and measurement techniques for optic nerve and cerebrospinal fluid parameters. Such methodological variability makes direct comparison across studies challenging and prevents the establishment of universal threshold values for imaging biomarkers such as optic nerve sheath diameter or optic nerve subarachnoid space width. Third, most of the included studies were cross-sectional in design, limiting the ability to infer causal relationships between altered cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and optic nerve damage.

Future research

Future research using standardized MRI protocols, larger patient cohorts, and longitudinal designs is required to validate the proposed imaging biomarkers and clarify their diagnostic utility in differentiating idiopathic intracranial hypertension from normal tension glaucoma. Integration of quantitative CSF flow measures with clinical, ophthalmologic, and intracranial pressure data may further clarify the mechanistic relationship between CSF behaviour and optic nerve damage. Advances in high-resolution imaging and functional CSF assessment may also enhance the ability to detect subtle alterations relevant to early disease stages.

Conclusion

This review highlights MRI findings associated with alterations in peri-optic CSF dynamics and optic nerve morphology in IIH and NTG. Although these conditions are typically distinguished through clinical and ophthalmologic evaluation, MRI offers valuable non-invasive insights into peri-optic CSF spaces and optic nerve structure, thereby contributing to a better understanding of underlying disease mechanisms. However, interpretation of these imaging markers should be approached cautiously, as several MRI features may overlap between disease states and may also be observed in asymptomatic individuals. In addition, the current evidence is limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous study populations, and variability in MRI acquisition protocols and measurement techniques, which restrict the establishment of standardized diagnostic thresholds for parameters such as ONSD and ONSAS width.

Emerging imaging approaches, including diffusion MRI, 4D-flow MRI, and functional MRI, may enable improved characterization of CSF dynamics and neurovascular function, potentially enhancing understanding of optic nerve pathophysiology in these conditions. Nevertheless, larger, well-designed longitudinal studies with standardized imaging protocols are required to validate these findings and further clarify the CSF-related mechanisms contributing to optic nerve pathology in IIH and NTG.
