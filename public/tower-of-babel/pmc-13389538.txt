A persistent germylyne radical enabling reversible σ-dimerization and diverse bond activation
Authors: Xingyu Yang, Meirong Song, Liancheng He, Shanshan Kong, Runbo Pei, Quanchun Sun, Wanwan Jia, Shuxuan Tang, Xiao-Song Xue, Xinping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reversible σ-dimerization of radicals is a fundamental process in bond formation and cleavage, yet achieving this with an isolable heavy carbyne analogue has remained a paramount challenge. Herein, we report the synthesis and characterization of an isolable germylyne radical that demonstrates remarkable ambient stability while undergoing clean, reversible σ-dimerization via a Ge–Ge single bond. This reversible process, which exhibits a pronounced thermochromic response, is thoroughly elucidated through variable-temperature NMR/EPR spectroscopy and X-ray crystallography, revealing a moderate bond dissociation enthalpy. Beyond dimerization, this germylyne radical acts as a versatile synthon for main-group bond activation, readily cleaving chalcogen-chalcogen (S–S, Se–Se), phosphorus-phosphorus (in P₄), and even challenging aromatic carbon-chlorine (C–Cl) bonds. This work provides the direct structural evidence of a heavy carbyne’s dimerization pathway and establishes a new platform for dynamic covalent bonding in main-group chemistry, with implications for stimuli-responsive materials and radical-mediated activation.Subject terms: Ligands, Chemical bonding, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

Reversible radical σ-dimerization1,2 can provide fundamental insights into chemical bond dynamics by demonstrating how electron redistribution and spin-spin interactions govern bond formation and cleavage processes (Fig. 1a). These transformations not only deepen our understanding of chemical bonding but also enable important applications in dynamic covalent chemistry and functional materials design3–7. A representative example includes σ-dicationic dimers serving as crucial intermediates in the electrochemical polymerization of conjugated heterocycles (e.g., thiophenes or pyrroles), where they facilitate catalyst-free oxidative C–C coupling with exceptional selectivity8. Such controlled molecular assembly principles underpin the development of self-healing polymers and mechanoresponsive materials through dynamic covalent bond reorganization. However, a significant limitation persists - most radical species only maintain sufficient stability in solution and cannot be isolated during their reversible dimerization processes.

Carbyne radicals (R−C•) represent particularly important yet challenging targets in this regard. As fundamental carbon-based reactive intermediates, these highly reactive species have been successfully identified in interstellar media through spectroscopic techniques and can be transiently generated in laboratory settings using high-energy methods such as flash/laser photolysis or pulsed radiolysis9–13. Carbyne intermediates can construct carbon-carbon triple bonds through dimerization reactions mediated by metal catalysts14. Heavier Group 14 analogs (R−E•, E = Ge, Sn, Pb) exhibit enhanced stability when stabilized through steric protection or Lewis base coordination strategies (Fig. 1b)15–23. Nevertheless, these systems universally resist E−E σ-dimerization due to competing π-electron delocalization and steric repulsion effects. While isolable alkyne analogs (e.g., digermynes) have been developed, they generally demonstrate remarkable resistance to homolytic cleavage into persistent radical species24–39. A notable exception is Power’s work, where UV irradiation or heating (80 °C) cleaves Sn–Sn triple bonds in distannynes into stannyne radicals detected by EPR spectroscopy40. However, these radicals underwent rapid recombination without yielding isolable products. The development of a well-defined system exhibiting reversible carbyne-like radical σ-dimerization — where both radical monomers and σ-bonded dimers can be isolated and characterized under ambient conditions — has emerged as a pivotal challenge in main group chemistry. Such a system would provide critical mechanistic insights into these fundamental transformations while enabling controlled manipulation of radical reactivity.

The reactivity of monovalent germanium Ge(I)—encompassing both the dimer with Ge(I)-Ge(I) single bond and germanium radicals—is attracting increasing attention. Several reports have documented their application in the activation of small molecules such as H2, CO2, CS2, and alkyne for dimers31,41,42. Notably, the activation of P4 by Ge(I) species remains unexplored to date. Although the activation of the C–Cl bond in alkyl chloride (C2Cl6) has been documented15,17. The activation of the sp² C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene, which is more challenging due to p - π conjugation between chlorine atoms and benzene rings43,44, was rarely reported for germanium radicals.

In this work, we address this challenge through the discovery of a Lewis base-stabilized germylyne radical that combines remarkable ambient stability with high reactivity (Fig. 1c). This species undergoes clean, reversible σ-dimerization at sub-ambient temperatures to form a stable Ge−Ge single-bonded dimer, providing direct insights into these fundamental processes. We have successfully expanded the substrate scope of Ge(I) reactivity to include P4 and chlorobenzene. In addition, a rare metal-bridged S6²- oligomer was obtained. Its activation for chlorobenzene further demonstrates the potential of Ge compounds as transition-metal mimics and possible catalysts for the future.

Results

Synthesis, crystal structure, and EPR spectra

Employing our established pincer ligand framework45, we synthesized rigid carbene-amido precursor 1 (Fig. 2a). Treatment of 1 with 2.2 molar equivalents of n-BuLi in Et2O at −78 °C, followed by addition of germanium dichloride (1: 1.05 molar ratio), afforded carbene-amido germylene chloride 2 (Fig. 2b) as a yellow solid. Subsequent reduction of 2 with KC8 (1.3 equiv) in THF at −78 °C produced a deep green solution within 20 minutes. Upon warming to ambient temperature, the reaction mixture exhibited a pronounced color transition to brown. The crude product was then filtered through a fine-porosity fritted funnel, followed by concentrated solvent under reduced pressure until a small amount of powder precipitate was observed. After allowing the residue to stand undisturbed at room temperature for 24 hours, an admixture of orange-red crystals 3 and green amorphous solid was obtained. Selective manual separation of 3 was performed under optical microscope guidance using precision tweezers, yielding the desired product in 21% isolated yield. Remarkably, compound 3 demonstrates excellent stability in the solid state, maintaining its integrity under an inert atmosphere (N2 glovebox) at 25 °C for months. Comprehensive characterization of 3 was achieved through single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis and EPR spectroscopy, confirming its molecular structure and radical nature.

Compound 3 crystallizes as a neutral monomer in the cubic space group Im3̅ (Fig. 2c). The germanium atom exhibits pronounced positional disorder, refined with occupancies of 50%, 25%, and 25% (Supplementary Fig. 40; for clarity, only the 50% occupancy component is depicted in Fig. 2c). This disorder, consistently observed across all measured crystals, limits detailed discussion of the precise bond lengths and angles. Nevertheless, analysis of the major-occupancy component confirms a V-shaped coordination geometry at germanium, defined by the carbene carbon and amido nitrogen of the monoanionic pincer ligand, with a chelation bite angle of 88.6(3)° and a nearly planar ligand backbone. The refined Ge–N and Ge–C bond distances [2.035(11)–2.039(10) Å and 1.984(11)–1.990(12) Å, respectively] and N–Ge–C angles [88.6(3)–89.4(6)°] all fall within expected ranges for such structural motifs. Despite the crystallographic disorder, the radical character of 3 is unambiguously established by its distinctive EPR signature and supported by computational analysis, both of which align with its diverse reactivity described in later sections.

The solution EPR spectrum of 3 in toluene at room temperature exhibited an isotropic hyperfine coupling signal (Fig. 3a). The best simulation result was obtained with g = 2.0026, A(14N) = 1.44, 2.19, and 2.62 G, revealing the spin density distribution on the three N atoms from the ligand framework, consistent with the spin delocalization across the ligand framework. The powder EPR spectrum of 3 at 100 K exhibited a rhombic anisotropic signal (Fig. 3b), and was simulated at gx = 2.0109, gy = 2.0008, and gz = 1.9922. An isotropic g-value of 2.0025 (close to the free-electron value of 2.0023) and significant g-tensor anisotropy (gx = 2.013, gy = 2.002, gz = 1.992 at 90 K, frozen toluene) confirm a non-isotropic spin density distribution (Supplementary Fig. 54). The hyperfine coupling signal with 73Ge is absent in the EPR spectrum of 3 due to the quite low natural abundance of 73Ge (7.73%)16.

Theoretical calculation

Geometry optimization of 3 at the (U)B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVPP-SMD (toluene)//B3LYP-D3/6-31 G(d)-SDD (for Ge) level yielded a planar ligand backbone, closely matching experimental structural trends (Supplementary Fig. 50). Minor torsional deviations observed crystallographically are likely attributed to germanium atom disorder and crystal packing effects. Computational spin density analysis (Fig. 3c) reveals predominant localization on Ge (∼0.65 e), with partial delocalization onto the three nitrogen atoms and carbene carbon of the ligand. The Ge-centered spin density adopts a π-radical character, dominated by its 4pπ orbital, which constitutes the primary contribution to the SOMO (α; -3.319 eV; Supplementary Fig. 51). In contrast, the HOMO (α; -5.314 eV; Supplementary Fig. 51) and LUMO (α; -0.747 eV; Supplementary Fig. 51) are ligand-centered, while the Ge lone pair (HOMO-4; α; -6.636 eV; Supplementary Fig. 51) exhibits pronounced s-orbital character (88.11%) based on NBO analysis. Combined crystallographic, spectroscopic, and computational evidence consistently supports the assignment of 3 as a monomeric germanium(I) π-radical in its neutral ground state.

Reversible σ-dimerization

Strikingly, a reversible thermochromic transition from brown to deep green was observed upon cooling a solution of 3 from room temperature, with complete color reversion upon warming (Fig. 4a). This phenomenon motivated low-temperature crystallization, variable-temperature (VT) 1H NMR, and EPR studies. Dark-green crystals of diamagnetic dimer 4 were obtained at 5 °C, revealing a trans configuration (N–Ge–Ge–N dihedral angle: 51.4°) with Ge centers in distorted trigonal-pyramidal geometries (Fig. 4b), indicative of lone-pair occupancy. Carbene ligands coordinate nearly perpendicularly to each Ge atom (The Ge1-Ge2-C96 and Ge2-Ge1-C48 angles are 96.4(8)° and 97.4(0)°, respectively), likely due to steric constraints. The Ge(I)–Ge(I) bond (2.621(1) Å) matches previously reported Ge(I)–Ge(I) single bonds29–31, while the N–Ge–Ge angle (121.3°) suggests σ-bonding dominated by Ge p-orbital overlap. Variable-temperature NMR (VT-1H NMR) studies demonstrated the reversible emergence of aromatic proton signals from the ligand upon cooling (Fig. 4d), while VT-EPR measurements revealed corresponding signal attenuation at lower temperatures (Fig. 4e). These observations are consistent with the formation of diamagnetic species 4. The reversible transitions indicate a dynamic equilibrium between monomeric species 3 and dimeric species 4. Van’t Hoff analysis of the VT-¹H NMR data provided a Ge(I) – Ge(I) bond dissociation enthalpy of ΔH = 15.9 kcal/mol, with ∆G (298 K) = 3.12 kcal/mol. The modest ∆G value at 298 K corroborates the observed equilibrium behavior. Computational modeling further substantiates these findings, indicating that the Gibbs free energies for the homolytic dissociation of the Ge–Ge σ-dimer 4’ into two germylyne radicals 3’ was ∆G (298 K) = 0.7 kcal/mol. Ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopic studies revealed that the stability and dimerization behavior of 3 exhibit significant solvent dependence (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). In toluene, a substantial amount of the monomeric species 3 is present at room temperature. In contrast, the dimeric species 4 predominates in n-hexane solution. Computational UV spectra simulations incorporating solvent effects corroborate these experimental observations. This solvent dependence likely originates from solvent polarity. Under certain conditions, higher polarity favors a shift in the equilibrium toward the radical side. This phenomenon has been observed for carbon‑centered dynamic covalent bonds, also in Group 1446,47. Herein, Germylyne radicals may be more stable in toluene due to solvation effects.

Chemical reactivity

Although compound 3 can be isolated in the solid state, it displays excellent chemical reactivity, in full agreement with its characterization as a germanium-centered radical. This intriguing reactivity is vividly demonstrated through its diverse reactivity toward various substrates (Fig. 5).

3 is extremely air sensitive and reacts rapidly with oxygen to form an O-bridged digermanate dimer 5 (Fig. 5), where monovalent germanium is oxidized to tetravalent Ge. The tetravalent Ge–O distance (1.802(2)Å) is similar to that of divalent Ge–O (1.804(2)Å)48. Under strictly anhydrous conditions with excess toluene, it is reasonable to hypothesize that toluene is the predominant hydrogen donor. Nevertheless, a minor role of the reactant itself (particularly its isopropyl group) as an intermolecular hydrogen source cannot be excluded. There have been no reports of germanic acid [LGe(O)OH] (L: a monoanionic ligand) or its dimer to date49,50. Compound 3 can also be oxidized by AgOTf to form the divalent germanium compound 6 (Supplementary Fig. 41), in which the OTf bonds to germanium via an oxygen atom, with the Ge–O distance (2.112(2) Å) consistent with previously reported Ge–O distances in divalent germanium compounds48,51. Treatment of 3 with the stable radical TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl) affords a carbene-coordinated germylene derivative 7 (Supplementary Fig. 42) with a Ge–O bond distance of 1.882(4) Å. Additionally, 3 readily cleaves chalcogen-chalcogen bonds, as evidenced by its reaction with diphenyl disulfide (Ph₂S₂) and diphenyl diselenide (Ph₂Se₂), yielding the corresponding carbene-stabilized germylene crystals 8 and 9 (Supplementary Fig. 43 and 44).

Interestingly, 3 can also activate white phosphorus (P₄) and elemental sulfur (S8). The reaction of 3 with P₄ led to a blue-black butterfly-like complex 10 (Fig. 5), formed by selectively cleaving one of the six P–P bonds in white phosphorus. Previously reported activations of white phosphorus to form such butterfly-shaped structures were mainly observed with transition metals52–55. Examples involving main group metals are rare; for instance, Bi radicals have been reported to activate white phosphorus, forming butterfly-like structures56. Structural analysis of complex 10 reveals a folding angle of 60.8° between the planes (P1, P2, P3) and (P2, P3, P4). The mean Ge1–P1–P2 angle is measured at 92.1°. In the P₄-butterfly skeleton, the hinge P–P bond length is 2.009(1) Å, while the four wing-tip P–P bonds have an average length of 1.966(6) Å. These values are shorter than the P–P bond length in pristine P₄ (2.21 Å).

The reaction of 3 with sulfur (S₈) produces a mixture of compounds 11 (Supplementary Fig. 45) and 12 (Fig. 5). In these products, the Ge atoms are connected by chains of four and six sulfur atoms, respectively. Each germanium atom also forms a double bond with a sulfur atom. Interestingly, this activation result contrasts with the previously reported reaction of Ge (I) species with S₈, which yielded only S2--bridged compounds57. For compound 12, the Ge=S double bond distance is 2.0506(18) Å, consistent with literature values57. The bridging sulfur atoms form single bonds with germanium (2.274(2) Å). The S2–S3 bond length (2.048(3) Å) is similar to that in elemental sulfur. The average S–S–S bond angle is 108.9°, which is slightly wider than that in elemental sulfur. The activation of elemental sulfur by 3 to form different oligomers is a rare phenomenon, especially for S62-58–60. The reactivity of 3 is currently under investigation in the laboratory to further explore its dual (radical and lone-pair) nature, analogous to the reactivity of carbyne reported in the literature, where the same center undergoes both ionic and radical reactions with two different reagents61.

Most strikingly, 3 exhibits the ability to activate chlorobenzene under mild conditions via homolytic C–Cl bond cleavage, generating compound 2 alongside a phenyl-substituted germylene 13 (Fig. 5). To elucidate the reaction mechanism between germylyne radical 3 and chlorobenzene, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed at the B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVPP-SMD(toluene)//B3LYP-D3/6-31 G(d)- SDD (for Ge) level of theory62–70 (see the Supplementary Information for computational details). To simplify the calculations, the isobutyl groups on the pincer ligand of 3 were replaced with hydrogen (denoted as 3’). Several pathways were evaluated, with the most favored halogen atom transfer pathway depicted in Fig. 6 (see Supplementary Fig. 53 for other higher-energy pathways). Our DFT calculations revealed that the Ge radical approaches chlorobenzene directly above the ligand plane, abstracting the chlorine atom of the C–Cl bond through transition state TS1 with an activation barrier of 25.4 kcal/mol. This process generates compound 2’ alongside a phenyl radical, which is subsequently captured by germylyne radical 3’ to afford the stable product 13’. Notably, an alternative halogen atom transfer pathway involving transition state TS2 was also identified (see Supplementary Fig. 53), but it exhibits a significantly higher barrier of 30.9 kcal/mol—5.5 kcal/mol above that of TS1. Comparison of the transition state geometries reveals a key distinction: in TS2, chlorobenzene approaches the Ge center outside the ligand plane, situated between the Dipp substituents. The singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO) of the Ge radical 3’ resides on the Ge–C σ bond, and the spin on Ge is partially delocalized onto the three nitrogen atoms and the carbene carbon of the ligand. The geometry in TS1 enables superior overlap between the SOMO of 3’ and the σ* orbital of the C–Cl bond, thereby facilitating more efficient bond activation and resulting in the lower observed energy barrier. The activation of chlorobenzene offers new insights into the design of main group compounds that mimic TM-like behavior.

Discussion

This work presents an isolable germylyne radical that undergoes reversible σ-dimerization. The formed dimer exhibits dynamic, temperature-dependent equilibration with the monomer, as demonstrated by VT-NMR/EPR and supported by DFT calculations. The system’s ability to cleave chalcogen-chalcogen (S–S, Se–Se), phosphorus-phosphorus (in P₄), and even challenging aromatic carbon-chlorine (C–Cl) bonds and undergo thermochromic σ-bond formation/cleavage offers fundamental insights into electron redistribution in bond dynamics. Its distinctive reactivity for S8, P4, and chlorobenzene reveals the previously unexplored chemistry of the germylene system. These findings connect transient carbyne radicals with isolable heavy element analogs, offering new perspectives for developing stimuli-responsive materials, dynamic covalent systems, and catalytic transformations involving reversible bond activation. The well-defined characterization of the monomer-dimer equilibrium provides important references for studying radical interactions in main-group chemistry.

Methods

The synthesis was carried out according to the general procedures described below. More comprehensive experimental details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Synthesis of 1

A suspension of NNN (2.50 g, 3.72 mmol) and ammonium tetrafluoroborate (0.59 g, 5.58 mmol) in triethyl orthoformate (20 mL) was sealed and stirred at 130 °C for 24 h. Cooling to room temperature, the resulting mixture was dried in vacuo, and the residue was washed (3 ×30 mL) to remove unreacted NNN with hexane, followed by recrystallization from a mixture of fluorobenzene and n-hexane to yield a white target compound 1. Yield: 1.01 g, 1.31 mmol, 35.2 %. Crystals suitable for X-ray analysis were achieved by vapor diffusion of CH2Cl2 onto a CH3OH solution of 1. Elemental analysis for C48H64BF4N3 (%): Calculated: C: 74.79; H: 8.50; N: 5.45. Found: C: 74.57, H: 8.64, N: 5.44.

Synthesis of 2

To a 100 mL Schlenk flask containing 1 (0.62 g, 0.80 mmol) suspended in dry diethyl ether (60 mL) was added dropwise n-BuLi (1.12 mL, 1.78 mmol, 1.6 M in pentane) at -78 °C. After stirring for 2 hours at -78 °C, the mixture was allowed to warm up slowly to RT and stirred additionally for 12 h accompanied by the appearance of a fluorescent yellow solid. Then, GeCl2(dioxane) (185.32 mg, 0.34 mmol) in Et2O was added slowly. The resulting mixture was stirred at RT for 24 h. After removal of the solvent under vacuum, the solids were washed with hexane and further extracted with toluene, filtered, and then the solvent was removed under reduced pressure to obtain the product 2 as a yellow solid. Yield: 0.34 g, 0.42 mmol, 53%. Single crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction studies were obtained by recrystallization from hexane at room temperature. Elemental analysis for C48H62ClGeN3 (%): Calculated: C: 73.28; H: 8.03; N: 5.23. Found: C: 73.79, H: 8.08, N: 4.93.

Synthesis of 3 and 4

35 mL of THF solution of 2 (90 mg, 0.11 mmol) in a Schlenk flask was cooled to −30 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of KC8 (18.5 mg, 0.14 mmol). The mixture was gradually warmed to room temperature, forming a brown solution. After filtration, the solvent was removed in vacuo, and the residue was extracted with n-pentane (20 mL). Insoluble material was filtered off, and the filtrate was concentrated to ~4 mL. Upon standing at room temperature for 24 h, an admixture of orange-red crystals 3 and a green amorphous solid formed. Manual separation of 3 under an optical microscope using fine tweezers provided the desired product. Yield: 17.4 mg, 0.023 mmol, 21.0%. In a parallel trial, concentrating the filtrate to ~10 mL and storing it at 5 °C for 12 h afforded X-ray-quality dark blue block crystals of 4. Yield: 51.2 mg, 0.034 mmol, 61.8%. Elemental analysis for C96H124N6Ge2 (%): Calculated: C: 76.50; H: 8.29; N: 5.58. Found: C: 76.80, H: 8.68, N: 5.32.

Reaction of 3 with O2

A solution of compound 3 (0.12 g, 0.16 mmol) in toluene (20 mL) in a 50 mL Schlenk flask was added at RT for continuous stirring. Then the 2.2 mL of oxygen is pumped via a syringe, the color of the reaction mixture changed from brownish to orange within 5 minutes, whereupon all volatiles were removed in vacuo to yield an orange solid. The product was extracted with n-pentane (10 mL), and the insoluble compound was filtered off. The solution was concentrated to ~ 3 mL and stored at 5 °C to obtain 5 as a yellow crystal. Isolated yield: 77.1 mg, 0.049 mmol, 61.3 %. Elemental analysis for C96H126O4Ge2N6.n-pentane (%): Calculated: C: 73.72; H: 8.45; N: 5.11. Found: C: 73.55, H: 8.86, N: 5.04.

Reaction of 3 with AgOTf

In the glovebox, compound 3 (85 mg, 0.11 mmol) and AgOTf (29.85 mg, 0.12 mmol) were added to a 50 mL Schlenk flask. Anhydrous toluene (25 mL) was transferred to the flask at RT. After stirring for 5 hours in the dark conditions, the mixture was filtered off, and the yellow filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was extracted with hexane (15 mL), and the insoluble compound was filtered off. The solution was concentrated to ~ 5 mL and stored at 5 °C to obtain 6 as a yellow crystal. The yellow crystal was isolated by removing the mother liquor and dried under vacuum for 2 h. Crystal yield: 40.8 mg, 0.04 mmol, 36.5%. The mixture was allowed to warm up to room temperature to afford a yellow solution. After stirring for 12 hours, the mixture was filtered. The filtrate was concentrated to ~ 2 mL, and left at 5 °C to produce a light-yellow solid of 9. The product was isolated by removing the mother liquor with a syringe and dried under vacuum for 1 h. Crystal yield: 37.2 mg, 0.04 mmol, 50.0 %. Yellow crystals 9 for X-ray diffraction analysis, were formed from hexane at room temperature. Elemental analysis for C54H67GeN3Se (%): Calculated: C: 71.29; H: 7.42; N: 4.62. Found: C: 70.67, H: 8.22, N: 3.75.

Reaction of 3 with P4

The P4 (16 mg, 0.13 mmol) was added to a stirred solution of 3 (186 mg, 0.25 mmol) in toluene (25 mL) at room temperature. Immediately, the color of the reaction mixture changed from brown to blue, and then the reaction was stirred for 12 h at room temperature. All volatile compounds were removed under reduced pressure. The residue was extracted with toluene (3 mL), and the insoluble compound was filtered off. The n-pentane (15 mL) was added and stored at room temperature to yield 10 as blue crystals. Isolated yield: 96.8 mg, 0.056 mmol, 49.4 %. Elemental analysis for C96H124Ge2N6P4 (%): Calculated: C: 70.69; H: 7.66; N: 5.15. Found: C: 70.92, H: 7.81, N: 5.08.

Reaction of 3 with S8

The S8 (34 mg, 0.12 mmol) was added to a stirred solution of 3 (200 mg, 0.23 mmol) in toluene (50 mL) at room temperature, resulting in a gradual color change from brown to yellow over 30 minutes. Then the reaction was stirred for 12 h at room temperature, and the insoluble compound was filtered off. All solvents were removed under reduced pressure. The residue was extracted with THF (15 mL), and the insoluble compound was filtered off. Concentration of the solution to ~ 6 mL followed by storage at 5 °C afforded a crystal mixture of yellow plates 11 and yellow needles 12. The crystals were separated by hand under a microscope for single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Due to the complex mixture of compounds obtained, neither compound was able to be characterized by NMR spectroscopy. Accurate elemental analysis could not be obtained due to the inseparable mixture of products.

Reaction of 3 with PhCl

A solution of compound 3 (0.12 g, 0.16 mmol) in toluene (5 mL) in a 50 mL Schlenk flask was added 100 uL chlorobenzene and placed at RT for continuous stirring. The color of the reaction mixture changed from brownish to orange after overnight, whereupon all volatiles were removed in vacuo to yield an orange solid. The product was extracted with n-pentane (20 mL), and the insoluble compound was filtered off. The solution was concentrated to ~ 8 mL and stored at 5 °C to obtain 13 and 2 as a yellow crystal. The pure product 13 was obtained via repeated recrystallization with n-pentane. The 13 is accompanied by the crystallization of 2, which cannot be separated completely. Isolated yield (Repeated recrystallization precluded a higher yield): 10.8 mg, 0.013 mmol, 16.3 %.
