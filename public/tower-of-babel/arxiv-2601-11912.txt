The Inverse Symplectic Eigenvalue Problem of a Graph
Himanshu Gupta, Leslie Hogben, Bryan Shader, Tony Wong

Abstract
Symplectic geometry plays an increasingly important role in mathematics, physics and applications, and naturally gives rise to interesting matrix families and properties. One of these is the notion of symplectic eigenvalues, whose existence for positive definite matrices is known as Williamson’s theorem or decomposition. This notion of symplectic eigenvalues gives rise to inverse problems. We introduce the inverse symplectic eigenvalue problem for positive definite matrices described by a labeled graph and solve it for several families of labeled graphs and all labeled graphs of order four. To solve these problems we develop various tools such as the Strong Symplectic Spectral Property (SSSP) and its consequences such as the Supergraph Theorem, the Bifurcation Theorem, and the Matrix Liberation Lemma for symplectic eigenvalues, graph couplings to describe collections of labelings of a graph that produce the same symplectic eigenvalues, and coupled graph zero forcing. We establish numerous results for symplectic positive definite matrices, including a sharp lower bound on the number of nonzero entries of such a matrix (or equivalently, the number of edges in its graph). This lower bound is a consequence of a lower bound on the sum of number of nonzero entries in an irreducible positive definite matrix and its inverse.



Keywords
symplectic matrices, symplectic eigenvalues of positive definite matrices, inverse problems, strong properties, zero forcing, graphs.

If

Remark 2.1

Let

Now assume

Pre-multiplying both sides of this equation by

and using

We call a symplectic positive definite matrix

Characterization

Dopico and Johnson established the next theorem in 2009.

Theorem 2.4
.

[

The set of symplectic positive definite matrices is

\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}N_{11}&N_{11}W\\

(2.1)

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

Proof.

As noted in Remark

2.3
Sparsity of inverse pairs of irreducible symmetric positive definite matrices

The number of nonzero entries in a matrix
A

is denoted by
\#(A)
.
In this section we establish a lower bound on
\#(M)+\#(M^{-1})
for an irreducible positive definite matrix
M
.
Proposition
2.5
then allows us to translate this to a lower bound for
\#(N)
when
N
is both symplectic and positive definite, and triangular paths show this bound is sharp (see Corollary
2.10
).

A symmetric matrix
A
is irreducible if and only if
\mathcal{G}(A)
is connected.
In the proof of the next theorem we use unlabeled graphs, although the graph of a matrix is technically a labeled graph; we suppress the labels since the labeling is irrelevant when discussing irreducible matrices because the class of irreducible matrices is invariant under permutations.

Note that if
M
is an irreducible symmetric invertible matrix, then
M^{-1}
is also irreducible, so the graphs of
M
and the graph of

are connected.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let

be an
n\times n
irreducible,
positive definite matrix.
Then

\#(M)+\#(M^{-1})\geq 8n-8.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on

.
For

\#(M)+\#(M^{-1})=1+1>0=8\cdot 1-8
.
For
n=2
, the irreducibility of

implies
that
\#(M)+\#(M^{-1})=4+4=8\cdot 2-8
.

Now assume that

and proceed by induction.
Throughout this proof we use
N
to denote
M^{-1}
. Since
M
is positive definite, every diagonal entry is nonzero, so the number of nonzero vertices in row
i
of

is
1+\deg_{\mathcal{G}(M)}(i)
, and similarly for

.
If each vertex of
\mathcal{G}(M)
and
\mathcal{G}(N)
has degree at least 3, then

as desired. Thus, we may assume that
\mathcal{G}(M)
or
\mathcal{G}(N)

has a vertex of degree at most
2

Case 1.

\mathcal{G}(M)
or
\mathcal{G}(N)

\mathcal{G}(M)

\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}&0&\cdots&0\\
m_{12}&m_{22}\\

N_{\mathcal{G}(N)}[1]=N_{\mathcal{G}(N)}[2]

\mathcal{G}(M)

\mathcal{G}(N)

Let

\displaystyle P

is sympPD,

-N_{12}&N_{11}\end{bmatrix}
by Proposition
2.5
.
Thus by Theorem
2.9
,

2\#(N)=\#(N)+\#(N^{-1})\geq 8(n-1),

and the desired inequality follows.

Since every diagonal entry of
N
is positive and every edge is associated with two nonzero entries in the matrix, the number of edges in
\mathcal{G}(N)
is
\frac{1}{2}(\#(N)-n)\geq 3p-2
edges.
Corollary
2.8
shows the bounds are sharp for
n\geq 4
. A connected graph of order two has one edge, and
(3)(1)-2=1
.
∎

2.4
Forbidden structures

In this section we seek conditions that guarantee no matrix
with labeled graph
G^{L}
is
sympPD. First, we observe another property of sympPD matrices.

Proposition 2.11
.

If

N=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
B^{\top}&C\end{array}\right]

is sympPD, then

B
is similar to a symmetric matrix;
and hence each eigenvalue of
B
is real
and its algebraic and geometric multiplicity are equal.

Proof.

Assume
N
is a sympPD matrix.
By Proposition
2.5
,
A
is positive definite, and there is a symmetric matrix
W
with
B=AW
. Since
A
is positive definite,
A
has a positive definite square root
\sqrt{A}
. Note that

\sqrt{A}^{-1}B\sqrt{A}=\sqrt{A}W\sqrt{A}
,
so
B
is similar to the symmetric matrix
\sqrt{A}W\sqrt{A}
. The remainder of the proof follows from properties of similarity and real symmetric matrices.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Proposition
2.11
can be applied to exhibit examples of labeled graphs that do not allow all symplectic eigenvalues to be equal, or equivalently do not allow a sympPD matrix.

1.

If a strongly connected component of the digraph

is a directed cycle of length

,
then each matrix with pattern

has
at least two non-real eigenvalues. Hence, no

of

2.

If

has two irreducible components
each equal to
[0]
and there is a directed walk in

from one to the other, then every matrix with pattern

has 0 as an eigenvalue with different algebraic and geometric multiplicities. Hence no

of
the form in Proposition

with this

These examples suggest considering the maximum number of entries in a labeled graph that does not allow all eigenvalues to be equal. The next example exhibits such a labeled graph of order

that omits

\binom{n}{2}-(n-1)=(p-1)(2p-1)

Example 2.13
.

Let

and let

be the labeled graph with vertices

and edge set that is the complement of
\overline{E}=\{\{i,p+1\}\colon i=1,\dots,p\}\,\cup\,{\{\{i,p+2\}\colon i=2,\dots,p\}}
. Let

be a positive definite matrix whose labeled graph is

and let

. Then

has two irreducible components

(

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
G^{L}
be a labeled graph that has an isolated vertex labeled
i

. If the vertex labeled

is not isolated, then

has at least two distinct symplectic eigenvalues.

Proof.

Without loss of generality assume

. We assume
N=[n_{ij}]
is a sympPD matrix and prove that the vertex labeled

is isolated.
Recall that
(\Omega N)^{2}=-I
. The
(p+1)
-st row of
\Omega N
is
(n_{11},0,\dots,0)
, so the
(p+1)

. Since

∎

There are also sparse
labeled graphs that do not allow a sympPD matrix, e.g., a single edge and isolated vertices using a labeling where an isolated vertex is labeled 1 and an endpoint of the

.

3
The Strong Symplectic Spectral Property (SSSP) and applications.

Like the inverse eigenvalue problem for graphs, the symplectic inverse eigenvalue problem for labeled graphs may not behave well with respect to insertion of edges. Namely,
if
A

is a positive definite matrix and
H^{L}
is a labeled graph obtained from the labeled graph of
A
by inserting some edges, then there may
not be a matrix
B
with labeled graph
H^{L}
having the same
symplectic spectrum as
A
. In the IEP-
G
setting, an additional constraint on
A
, known as the Strong Spectral Property, guarantees that if
H
is a graph obtained from the graph of
A
by inserting some edges,
then there is a matrix
B
with graph
H

and the same spectrum as
A
. In this section we establish a similar property in the symplectic setting (Theorem
3.15
, the Supergraph Theorem), establish other extensions of IEP-
G
results that use the Strong Spectral Property, develop the analogous theory, and apply these ideas to give several results about the ISEP-
G
, including that every labeled graph
G^{L}
of order
2p
allows every set of
p
distinct positive numbers as a symplectic spectrum.

Throughout this section, we view the real
n\times n
matrices as an inner product space, where the
inner product between such matrices
A
and
B
is given by
\mbox{tr}(A^{\top}B)
. Note the set of symmetric
n\times n
matrices, denoted by
\operatorname{Symm}(n)
,
is a subspace, and for symmetric
A
and
B
the inner product simplifies to
\mbox{tr}(AB)
.
We will be working with subspaces of
\operatorname{Symm}(n)
. For such a
U
the
(symmetric) orthogonal complement

of
U
refers to

U^{\perp}=\{A\in\operatorname{Symm}(n)\colon\mbox{tr}(AB)=0\mbox{ for all $B\in U$}\};

that is, the orthogonal complement with respect to
\operatorname{Symm}(n)
.

A reader who is familiar with the Strong Spectral Property of the IEP-
G
and wishes to access the tools without first reviewing the theoretical foundation may wish to first read Definition
3.1
and the statement of Theorem
3.6
and then proceed to Section
3.2
.

3.1
SSSP foundations

The
2p\times 2p
matrices of the form

M=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{cc}R&E\\
F&-R^{\top}\end{array}\end{bmatrix},

(3.1)

where
R
is a
p\times p
real matrix, and
E
and
F
are
p\times p
real symmetric matrices
are called
Hamiltonian matrices
. The set of
2p\times 2p
Hamiltonian
matrices forms a vector space, and is closed under the commutator
[\cdot,\cdot]
(i.e., if
M
and
M^{\prime}
are
Hamiltonian matrices, then
[M,M^{\prime}]=MM^{\prime}-M^{\prime}M
is a Hamiltonian matrix).
Thus, the set of
2p\times 2p
Hamiltonian matrices forms a Lie Algebra with the commutator as the bracket operator, and is denoted by
\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)
.
Additionally, it is known that if
M
is a Hamiltonian matrix, then the exponential
e^{M}
of
M
is a symplectic matrix.
Thus, the corresponding Lie group is
\operatorname{Sp}(2p
), the group of
2p\times 2p
symplectic matrices.

Definition 3.1
.

The
2p\times 2p
positive semidefinite matrix
N
has the
Strong Symplectic Spectral Property
(SSSP) provided

\{NM+M^{\top}N\colon M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)\}+\operatorname{span}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{L}(N))=\operatorname{Symm}(2p).

(3.2)

In Theorem
3.6
it is shown that
N
having the SSSP
is equivalent to
Y=O
being the only symmetric
2p\times 2p

matrix for which
N\circ Y=O
and
\Omega NY=YN\Omega
.
We note that the IEP-
G
analog of the SSSP is the Strong Spectral Property for
a symmetric matrix
A
with graph
G
:

\{AK+KA\colon K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mbox{ and }K^{\top}=-K\}+\operatorname{span}\mathcal{S}(G)=\operatorname{Symm}(n).

This condition is equivalent to
Y=O
being
the only symmetric matrix
such that
I\circ Y=O
,
A\circ Y=O
, and
AY=YA
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
N=I_{2}
.
Then the labeled graph of
N
is the empty graph on two vertices, and the subspace

\{NM+M^{\top}N\colon M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2)\}

(3.3)

is the set of all
2\times 2
symmetric matrices with trace equal to
0
. Furthermore

\operatorname{span}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{L}(N))
is the set of all
2\times 2
diagonal matrices.
Thus
I_{2}
has the SSSP.

Example 3.3
.

Let
N=I_{4}
. Then for each
2\times 2
matrix
R
and
all
2\times 2
symmetric matrices
E
and
F
,

N\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R&E\\
F&-R^{\top}\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R&E\\
F&-R^{\top}\end{array}\right]^{\top}N=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R+R^{\top}&E+F\\
E+F&-R-R^{\top}\end{array}\right].

(3.4)

Thus, for each matrix in

\{NM+M^{\top}N\colon M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)\}+\operatorname{span}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{L}(N)),

the
(1,2)
- and
(3,4)
-entries
are negatives of each other. We conclude that
I_{4}
does not have the SSSP.

Remark 3.4
.

Let
G^{L}
be a labeled graph on
2p
vertices and
N
be a positive definite matrix
with labeled graph
G^{L}
. The span
S
of
\mathcal{S}(G^{L})
is the subspace of

\operatorname{Symm}(2p)
consisting of all
2p\times 2p
matrices whose labeled graph
is a subgraph of
G^{L}
. Thus,
S
is spanned by the matrices
E_{ij}+E_{ji}
where
ij
is an edge of
G^{L}

and the matrices
E_{ii}

i=1,\ldots,2p
.
It follows that the
2p\times 2p
symmetric matrix
Y
is in
S^{\perp}
if and only if

Y_{ij}=0
whenever
i=j
or
ij
is an edge of
G
. Since
N\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L})
, it follows
that
Y\in S^{\perp}
if and only if
N\circ Y=O
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
N
be a
2p\times 2p
positive definite matrix.
The (symmetric) orthogonal complement of

\{NM+M^{\top}N\colon M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)\}

is

\{Y\in\operatorname{Symm}(2p)\colon\Omega NY=YN\Omega\}.

Proof.

From the definition, the (symmetric) orthogonal complement of the first summand in
(
3.2
) is

\{Y\in\operatorname{Symm}(2p)\colon\operatorname{tr}(Y(NM+M^{\top}N))=0\mbox{ for all $M\in\mathfrak{sp}(2p)$}\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(Y(NM+M^{\top}N))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{tr}(YNM+YM^{\top}N)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(YNM+NMY)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{tr}(YNM).

The second inequality follows from the facts that
\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)+\operatorname{tr}(B)
, the trace of a matrix equals the trace of its transpose, and both
N
and
Y
are symmetric.
The third inequality follows from the fact that

\mbox{tr}(AB)=\mbox{tr}(BA)
for any appropriately sized matrices.
Hence the (symmetric) orthogonal complement of
the first summand in (
3.2
)
is the set of symmetric matrices
Y
such

YN\in\mathcal{W}
where
\mathcal{W}
is the set of
2p\times 2p

matrices such that
\operatorname{tr}(WM)=0
for all
M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)
.

We determine the form of

W=\begin{bmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
W_{21}&W_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{W},

where each
W_{ij}
is
p\times p
.
Using the block form for a Hamiltonian matrix
M
in (
3.1
), we see that
W\in\mathcal{W}
if and only if

\mbox{tr}(W_{11}R+W_{12}F+W_{21}E-W_{22}R^{\top})=0
for all symmetric
E
and
F
, and all
R\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. As
R
,
E
and
F
have no common positions,

W\in\mathcal{W}
if and only
if
\mbox{tr}(W_{12}F)=0
for all symmetric
F
,

\mbox{tr}(W_{21}E)=0
for all symmetric
E
and

\mbox{tr}((W_{11}-W_{22}^{\top})R)=0
for all
R
.
Thus,
W\in\mathcal{W}
if and only if
W_{12}

and
W_{21}
are skew-symmetric, and
W_{11}=W_{22}^{\top}
.
One can verify that these last conditions are equivalent to

\Omega W^{\top}=W\Omega
. Therefore
\mathcal{W}
, which is the (symmetric) orthogonal complement of
\{NM+M^{\top}N\colon M\in\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)\}
, is the set of symmetric matrices
Y
such that

\Omega NY=YN\Omega
.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
N
be a
2p\times 2p
real positive definite matrix.
Then
N
has the SSSP if and only if
Y=O
is the only symmetric matrix such that
N\circ Y=O
and
\Omega NY=YN\Omega
.

Proof.

The matrix
N
has the SSSP if and only if
the orthogonal complements of the two spaces in (
3.2
) intersect only in the zero matrix. The theorem now follows from Remark
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
.
∎

We see that the Strong Symplectic Spectral Property for the complete graph is similar to the Strong Spectral Property for the complete graph: By the previous theorem, if
N\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(K_{2p})
then
N
has the SSSP (for any labeling
L
of
K_{2p}
), because
N\circ Y=O
implies
Y=O
, just as in the case of the Strong Spectral Property (and for the same reason, since the condition
N\circ Y=O
for any positive definite matrix

implies
Y\circ I=O
).

There are other labeled graphs for which every matrix has the SSSP, as seen in the next example.

Example 3.7
.

Let

(cf. Example

). Then

where

is diagonal and every entry of

implies

and thus

.

In contrast to the ISEP-

and SSSP, there are matrices

that do not have the Strong Spectral Property for the IEP-

For

The matrix
\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{p}\\

as seen in the next example.

Example 3.8
.

Let

In the next result, we use the SSSP formulation in Theorem

to characterize the positive definite diagonal matrices that have the SSSP.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let

N=D\oplus E
be a
2p\times 2p
positive definite diagonal matrix
where
D=\operatorname{Diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})
and

. Then the symplectic eigenvalues of

are
\sqrt{d_{i}e_{i}},i=1,\dots,p
, and

has the SSSP if and only if the

are distinct.
In particular, if

has distinct diagonal entries, then
\operatorname{SPspec}(D\oplus D)=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{p}\}
and

has SSSP.

Proof.

Since

(\Omega N)^{2}=(-ED)\oplus-(DE)=-(DE\oplus DE)
, the symplectic eigenvalues of

are
\sqrt{d_{1}e_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{d_{p}e_{p}}
.

Let

Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{c c}W&X\\

be a

\Omega NY=YN\Omega
and

. The latter implies the diagonals of

and

are zero.
Then
\Omega NY=YN\Omega
implies

\displaystyle EX^{\top}

\displaystyle=-XE,

(3.5)

\displaystyle-DX

\displaystyle=X^{\top}D,

(3.6)

\displaystyle EZ

\displaystyle=WD,\mbox{ and }

(3.7)

\displaystyle DW

\displaystyle=ZE.

(3.8)

First observe that (
3.5
) requires that every diagonal entry of

is zero. Pre-multiplying both sides of (

) by

.

Let

and

has the SSSP if the entries of

are distinct.

Suppose that

has a multiple symplectic eigenvalue.
Then, without loss of generality,

.
Let

3.2
Fundamental theorems and consequences of the SSSP

In this section, we develop fundamental theorems about the SSSP.
The results and proofs, are analogous to those for the Strong Spectral Property.
We place the proofs in the appendix for completeness.

The criteria given in Definition
3.1
for a
positive definite matrix
N

to have the SSSP can be phrased
in terms of a system of linear equations by viewing the
entries
y_{ij}
with
i\leq j
as distinct variables.
This leads to an effective purely linear algebraic way to determine whether
N
has the SSSP.
More precisely, for a positive integer
p
, we denote by
E_{ij}
the
2p\times 2p

matrix with a one in position
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere. One can verify that

\{E_{i,j+p}+E_{j,i+p}:1\leq i\leq j\leq p\}\cup\{E_{i+p,j}+E_{j+p,i}:1\leq i\leq j\leq p\}\cup\{E_{ij}-E_{j+p,i+p}:1\leq i,j\leq p\}

is a basis for
\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)
. Thus
\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)
has dimension
2\frac{p(p+1)}{2}+p^{2}=2p^{2}+p
.
As we need an ordered basis, we order this first as the order of the three sets just defined. Within each of the first two sets we order by the pair
(i,j)
running through diagonal (in order), followed by each superdiagonal in order. For the third, each superdiagonal is followed by the corresponding subdiagonal. This index order for a
4\times 4
matrix (with
p=2
is
(1,1),(2,2),(1,2)
for each of the first two sets and
(1,1),(2,2),(1,2),(2,1)
for the third. This ordered basis is called the
standard ordered basis
(for verification).

Given an
2p\times 2p
symmetric matrix
M
we denote by

\operatorname{vec}_{\triangle}(M)
the
(2p^{2}+p)\times 1
vector obtained from
M
by stacking (in order)
the upper triangular parts of columns of
M
.
If
M=[m_{ij}]
is
4\times 4
,

\operatorname{vec}_{\triangle}(M)^{\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}m_{11}&m_{12}&m_{22}&m_{13}&m_{23}&m_{33}&m_{14}&m_{24}&m_{34}&m_{44}\end{array}\right].

Note that
\operatorname{vec}_{\triangle}
defines a bijection
between
(2p)\times(2p)
symmetric matrices and
\mathbb{R}^{2p^{2}+p}
.

The
full SSSP verification matrix
of the
2p\times 2p
positive definite matrix
N
is the
(2p^{2}\times p)\times(2p^{2}+p)
matrix,
\Phi(N)
, whose columns are
the
\operatorname{vec}_{\triangle}(M^{\top}N+NM)
where
M
runs over the standard ordered basis vectors of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{sp}}(2p)
in order and the
SSSP verification matrix
,
\Xi(N)
, of
N
is the submatrix of rows indexed by the edges of
\overline{G}
.

Example 3.10
.

Let

N=[n_{ij}]
be a
4\times 4
symmetric matrix.
Then
\Phi(N)
equals

\scriptscriptstyle{\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}0&0&0&4\,n_{13}&0&2\,n_{14}&2\,n_{11}&0&0&2\,n_{12}\\

2\,n_{12}&0&n_{22}&0&2\,n_{34}&n_{33}&-n_{23}&n_{23}&n_{13}-n_{24}&0\\
4\,n_{13}&0&2\,n_{23}&0&0&0&-2\,n_{33}&0&-2\,n_{34}&0\\

4
Symplectic spectrally arbitrary labeled graphs

A labeled graph
G^{L}
of order
n=2p
is
symplectic spectrally arbitrary
if for any
p
positive numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}
, there is a matrix
N\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L})
such that
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}
are the symplectic eigenvalues of
N
.

In this section, we use the construction
N=S^{\top}D^{\prime}S
where
S
is a symplectic matrix and
D^{\prime}=D\oplus D
is a diagonal matrix
with a desired symplectic spectrum to construct a variety of labeled graphs that are symplectic spectrally arbitrary. In particular, we show that the sparse labeled graph
\operatorname{TP}_{n}^{I}
is symplectic spectrally arbitrary
for each positive even integer
n=2p
.
Additionally, we show that a variety of dense labeled graphs, including
K_{n}
,
are symplectic spectrally arbitrary.

We begin with a result about nonnegative symplectic matrices that shows the existence of many symplectic spectrally arbitrary labeled graphs.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
S
be a nonnegative symplectic matrix and let
G^{L}=\mathcal{G}^{L}(S^{\top}S)
.
Then
G^{L}
is symplectic spectrally arbitrary.

Proof.

Assume
S
is
(2p)\times(2p)
, let
n=2p
, let
D
be a
p\times p
diagonal matrix with positive diagonal entries, let
D^{\prime}=D\oplus D
, and define
N=S^{\top}D^{\prime}S
. Then the symplectic eigenvalues of
N=[n_{ij}]
are the diagonal entries of
D
. Note that

n_{ij}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}s_{ri}d^{\prime}_{rr}s_{rj}

Since all entries of
S
are nonnegative and all entries
d^{\prime}_{rr}
are positive,
n_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if there exists
1\leq r\leq n
such that
s_{ri}s_{rj}\neq 0
. This is equivalent to
(S^{\top}S)_{ij}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}s_{ri}s_{rj}\neq 0
. Hence,
N\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L})
and
G^{L}
is symplectic spectrally arbitrary.
∎

5
Symplectic eigenvalues of order four graphs

In this section we determine the possible symplectic eigenvalues of each of the eleven graphs of order four. To do this, we develop several additional tools for determining symplectic spectra, some of which are applications of strong properties. We begin with a remark that greatly simplifies this problem by applying the machinery we have developed.

Remark 5.1
.

For

of order four is equivalent to determining whether
G^{L}
allows a sympPD matrix; if so, it is symplectic spectrally arbitrary.

Thus every labeling of

is symplectic spectrally arbitrary (with the SSSP) by Example
4.5

({\overline{K_{2}}}\vee K_{2})^{M}

(with SSSP) by Example
3.13

By Corollary
2.10
, any connected graph on four vertices with fewer than four edges does not allow a sympPD and thus has all symplectic eigenvalues simple. Therefore, each labeling of
P_{4}
and
K_{1,3}
allows any set of two distinct symplectic eigenvalues and no other symplectic spectra.

Among connected graphs, this leaves the Paw graph, shown in Figure

So far we have been working entirely with labeled graphs.
However, many labelings must result in the same symplectic eigenvalues because they can be obtained from one another by symplectic permutation matrices or other symplectic matrices
that preserve the unlabeled graph; we call such labelings
symplectically equivalent
. When determining all symplectic eigenvalues of all graphs of a given order, it is desirable to consider only one representative from each class of symplectically equivalent labelings of a graph
G
.

A
monomial matrix
is a
(0,1,-1)
-matrix
R
with exactly one nonzero in each row and column.
Each monomial matrix has the form
R=EP
where
E
is a diagonal matrix each of whose diagonal entries are in
\{\pm 1\}
,
and
P
is a permutation matrix; note that
R^{\top}=R^{-1}
for each monomial matrix
R
. Let
R
be a monomial matrix for which
R^{\top}{\Omega}R=\Omega
, and let
N
be a positive definite matrix.
By Proposition
1.2
, the symplectic eigenvalues of
N

are the moduli of the eigenvalues of
\Omega N
.
Note that
\Omega N=R^{\top}\Omega RN
is similar to
\Omega RNR^{\top}
, so
N
and
RNR^{\top}
have the same symplectic eigenvalues. Therefore, if
R=EP_{\sigma}
and the labeled graph of
N
is
G^{L}
, then
\mathcal{G}^{L}(RNR^{\top})=\mathcal{G}^{L}(P_{\sigma}NP_{\sigma}^{\top})=G^{\sigma(L)}
.
In other words, for such
\sigma
,
studying the symplectic eigenvalues of matrices with labeled graph
G^{L}
is the same as studying the symplectic eigenvalues of matrices with labeled graph
G^{\sigma(L)}
.

To identify graphs
G^{\sigma(L)}
that allow the same symplectic spectra as
G^{L}
, we need to find the permutation matrices
P
so that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}O&I\\
I&O\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}O&I\\
I&O\end{array}\right].

This is equivalent to
P_{21}=P_{12}
and
P_{11}=P_{22}
.
If the permutation corresponding to
P
is
\sigma
, one can check that this implies

\sigma(i)=j
if and only if
\sigma(i+p)=j+p
(reading indices mod
2p
). So the permutations
\sigma
that work are those such that

\{\{\sigma(1),\sigma(1+p)\},\ldots,\{\sigma(p),\sigma(2p)\}\}=\{\{1,1+p\},\ldots,\{p,2p\}\}.

So in particular there are
p!2^{p}
valid re-labelings.

We describe symplectically equivalent labeled graphs by couplings using vertex names.

Definition 5.2
.

A
coupling
of
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{2p}\}
is a set
\mathfrak{C}=\{(c_{1},d_{1}),\ldots,(c_{p},d_{p})\}
such that each of the vertices appears exactly once in
\mathfrak{C}
.
A
coupled graph
, denoted by
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
, is a graph
G
of order
n=2p
with vertex set
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
and a coupling
\mathfrak{C}
. For each vertex
v_{i}
in a coupled graph, we denote by
\mathfrak{c}(v_{i})
the vertex paired with
v_{i}
.

Although we use the notation
(\cdot,\cdot)
in the definition of a coupling to avoid confusion with graph edges, the pairs are unordered
in the coupling
\mathfrak{C}
.
To see that there are
(2p-1)(2p-3)\cdots(3)(1)
couplings of a graph
G
of order
n=2p
, note that there are
2p-1
choices for
\mathfrak{c}(v_{1})
, there are
2p-3
choices for
\mathfrak{c}(v_{j})
where
j
the next lowest index excluding vertices
v_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}(v_{1})
, etc.
Associated to the coupled graph
G^{\mathfrak{C}}

with coupling
\mathfrak{C}=\{(c_{1},d_{1}),\ldots,(c_{p},d_{p})\}
is the set of
2^{p}p!
labeled graphs obtained by choosing a permutation
\sigma
of
1,\dots,p
and assigning the labels
\sigma(k)
and
\sigma(k)+p

to
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
(either order) for
k=1,\dots,p
.

By the discussion above, if
G^{L_{1}}
and
G^{L_{2}}
are two labelings of a graph
G
described by one coupling, then
\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L_{1}})
and
\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L_{2}})
have the same symplectic spectra. To study the inverse symplectic eigenvalue problem of a coupled graph
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
, we choose any one of the labeled graphs
G^{L}
described by the coupled graph (a
representative labeling
) and then determine the possible symplectic spectra of matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L})
.
The family of all real positive definite matrices associated with the
2^{p}p!
labelings described by a coupled
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
.

6
Coupled Zero Forcing

Zero forcing and its variants such as positive definite and skew zero forcing provide well-known combinatorial upper bounds for the
maximum nullities of the relevant types of matrices described by a graph, beginning with standard zero forcing and maximum nullity, or equivalently, maximum eigenvalue multiplicity for the standard IEP-
G

[
2
]

(see
[
12
, Chapter 9]
for discussion of variants). In this section we define a new form of zero forcing for coupled graphs as an upper bound on the maximum multiplicity of a symplectic eigenvalue among matrices associated with the coupled graph. We use coupled zero forcing and prior results to solve the ISEP-
G
for several families of graphs. We also characterize coupled graphs that have coupled zero forcing number equal to one.

We begin by recalling the definition of the standard maximum nullity and defining the maximum symplectic eigenvalue multiplicity for a coupled graph.
Let
G
be an unlabeled graph. The
standard maximum nullity
of
G
is
\operatorname{M}(G)=\max\{\hbox{}A\colon A\in\operatorname{Symm}(G)\}
.
Since the nullity of
A-\lambda I
is the multiplicity of
\lambda
as an eigenvalue of
A
and
\mathcal{G}(A-\lambda I)=\mathcal{G}(A)
,
\operatorname{M}(G)
is the maximum multiplicity of an eigenvalue of
A\in\operatorname{Symm}(G)
.

Definition 6.1

(Maximum symplectic multiplicity)
.

Let
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
be a coupled graph and let
L
be a representative labeling of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
. The
maximum symplectic multiplicity of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
, denoted by
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
, is the maximum multiplicity among the symplectic eigenvalues of matrices
N\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(G^{L})
.

Next we recall the definition of the standard zero forcing number and define the coupled zero forcing color change rule and number. Let
G
be an unlabeled graph. The
standard color change rule
allows a blue vertex
v
to force a white vertex
w
to blue if
w
is the only white vertex in
N(v)
.
A set
B\subseteq V(G)
of initially blue vertices is called a
standard zero forcing set
of
G
if repeated application of the standard zero forcing color change rule eventually colors every vertex of
G
blue. The
standard zero forcing number
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{Z}(G)
, is the minimum cardinality of a standard zero forcing set of
G
.

Definition 6.2

(Coupled zero forcing)
.

Let
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
be a coupled graph, and suppose each vertex is colored either blue or white. The
coupled zero forcing color change rule
consists of the following two operations:

1.

A blue vertex
v
forces a white vertex
w
to blue, denoted
v\to w
, if
w
is the only white vertex in
N(v)\cup\{\mathfrak{c}(v)\}
.

2.

A white vertex
v
forces itself to blue, denoted
v\to v
, if every vertex in
N(v)\cup\{\mathfrak{c}(v)\}
is blue.

A subset of
V(G)
of initially blue vertices is called a
coupled zero forcing set
of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
if repeated application of the coupled zero forcing color change rule eventually colors every vertex of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
blue. It follows from known results about
\operatorname{Z}_{\ell}
(see Remark
6.7
) that the final coloring of a coupled zero forcing set is independent of the forces chosen. The
coupled zero forcing number
of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
, is the minimum cardinality of a coupled zero forcing set of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
.

The proof that
\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
provides an upper bound for
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
is in some ways similar to the same relation between the standard zero forcing number and the maximum multiplicity, but there is also a fundamental difference, which is discussed in the remark following the proof.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
be a coupled graph. Then
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})\leq\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a real positive definite matrix whose labeled graph is a representative labeling of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
, and let
B
be any coupled zero forcing set of
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
.
Suppose
\mu
is an eigenvalue of
\Omega N
with eigenvector
{\bf x}=[x_{i}]
such that
x_{k}=0
for all
k\in B
.
Since
(\mu I-\Omega N){\bf x}=0
, multiplying on the left by
\Omega
and using
\Omega^{2}=-I
yields
(\mu\Omega+N){\bf x}=0
. Set
N^{\prime}=\mu\Omega+N
and
N^{\prime}=[n^{\prime}_{ij}]
; hence
{\bf x}\in\ker(N^{\prime})
.
Notice that, for every vertex label
v
,
n^{\prime}_{vv}\neq 0
and
n^{\prime}_{v,\mathfrak{c}(v)}\neq 0
since
N
is a real positive definite matrix and
\mu
is a nonzero purely imaginary number. Because an application of the coupled zero forcing color change rule corresponds to showing another entry in the null vector
{\bf x}
is zero, repeated application shows that
{\bf x}
is the zero vector. Thus, the kernel of
\mu I-\Omega N
does not contain a nonzero vector with
x_{k}=0
for all
k\in B
. Hence, the multiplicity of
\mu
as an eigenvalue of
\Omega N
is at most
|B|

(see Lemma 2.13 of
[
12
]
). Since
\Omega N
is real, its purely imaginary eigenvalues occur in complex conjugate pairs. Thus, by Proposition
1.2
, the multiplicity of
\mu
as an eigenvalue of
\Omega N
equals the multiplicity of the symplectic eigenvalue
|\mu|
of
N
. Since
\mu
and
N
are arbitrary, we have

\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})\leq\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

Note that the proof given above is specific to the real numbers, because it relies on the fact that the sum of a real number and a nonzero purely imaginary number is necessarily nonzero. This is different from the situation with standard maximum nullity and zero forcing, where
\operatorname{M}^{F}(G)\leq\operatorname{Z}(G)
regardless of the field
F
in which the matrices reside,
(whether
\operatorname{M}^{F}(G)
can be interpreted as the maximum multiplicity of an eigenvalue rather than as maximum nullity may vary with field, as may the value of
\operatorname{M}^{F}(G)
for a fixed graph
G
.)

\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})=1
. Then

has at least

edges and

is a caterpillar with a perfect matching, implying

is a caterpillar with a perfect matching
M
and
\mathfrak{C}
is the coupling defined by

.
∎

Remark 6.19
.

A caterpillar
T
with maximal central path
P
has a perfect matching if and only if

1.

Every vertex has degree at most three.

2.

If the degree three vertices of
T
are deleted from
P
, the subpaths of
P
that remain (if any) each have even order.

A comb
P_{p}\circ K_{1}
is an example of a caterpillar with a perfect matching. See Figure

for another example.

We can now solve the SIEG for caterpillars with perfect matchings. The next result is immediate from Theorem
6.3
, Corollary
3.16
, and Theorem
6.18
.

Corollary 6.20
.

Let
G^{\mathfrak{C}}
be a coupled graph of order
2p
such that
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})=1
. Then
G^{\mathfrak{I}}
allows a multi-set of
p
positive real numbers as a symplectic spectrum if and only if the elements are distinct. In particular, this applies to every
caterpillar that contains a perfect matching (with the coupling defined by the perfect matching).

As is the case for (standard) zero forcing and maximum nullity, it is possible to have the maximum symplectic multiplicity less than the coupled zero forcing number of a coupled graph. But the situation is actually somewhat different, because for a coupled graph of order
2p
,
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})\leq p
and
\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(G^{\mathfrak{C}})\leq 2p-1
and both bounds are sharp as illustrated by
K_{2p}
: We saw in Example
4.5
that
\operatorname{M}_{\operatorname{Sp}}(K_{2p}^{\mathfrak{C}})=p
for any coupling, and
\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(K_{2p}^{\mathfrak{C}})=2p-1
(since
{\operatorname{Z}_{\operatorname{C}}(K_{2p}^{\mathfrak{C}})=\operatorname{Z}_{\ell}(K_{2p})=}2p-1
).

7
Concluding remarks

In this work we have defined the ISEP-
G
and have solved it for at least one coupling of more families of graphs than the IEP-
G
has been solved for, with the latter involving more than a dozen authors over more than twenty years.
The following coupled graphs are symplectic spectrally arbitrary and thus the ISEP-
G
is solved for these coupled graphs.

•

K_{2p}^{\mathfrak{C}}
where
\mathfrak{C}
is any coupling.

•

(K_{p}\mathbin{\,\sqcup\,}K_{p})^{\mathfrak{I}}
where
\mathfrak{I}
pairs the vertices of one
K_{p}
with the vertices of the other
K_{p}
.

•

(\overline{K_{p}}\vee K_{p})^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each vertex of
\overline{K_{p}}
with a vertex in
K_{p}
.

•

K_{p,p}^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each vertex in one partite set with a vertex in the other partite set.

•

\operatorname{TP}_{2p}^{\mathfrak{I}}
.

Other graphs for which the ISEP-
G
is solved:

•

All graphs of order 4 with all couplings (see Section
5
for details of what spectra are allowed).

•

Path
P_{2p}^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
is defined by the perfect matching (all eigenvalues distinct).

•

T^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
T
is a caterpillar with a perfect matching and
\mathfrak{M}
is defined by the perfect matching (all eigenvalues distinct).

•

(K_{p}\circ K_{1})^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each leaf with its unique neighbor (any symplectic spectrum with at least two distinct symplectic eigenvalues).

•

Comb
(P_{p}\circ K_{1})^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each leaf with its unique neighbor (all eigenvalues distinct).

•

Sun
(C_{p}\circ K_{1})^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each leaf with its unique neighbor (see Corollary
6.16
for details of what spectra are allowed).

•

(H\circ K_{1})^{\mathfrak{M}}
where
\mathfrak{M}
couples each leaf with its unique neighbor and the IEP-
G
is solved by specifying which ordered multiplicity lists are permitted and all spectra are allowed for these lists (depends on the spectra allowed by
H
).

These solutions have been possible through an assortment of tools we have developed for the ISEP-
G
, including the Strong Symplectic
Spectral Property, results on symplectic positive definite matrices, and coupled zero forcing. Since labeled graphs must be used for symplectic spectra, we have introduced couplings to manage symplectically equivalent labeling, thereby substantially reducing the number of labelings that need to be considered.

We have only begun the study of the ISEP-
G
and many natural questions remain.
