Development and validation of a risk prediction model for dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in Chinese women with gestational diabetes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhe Liu, Jie Liu, Weiwei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompared with normoglycaemic pregnancies, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) confers a markedly elevated risk of dysglycaemia at 6-12 weeks postpartum.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and externally validate a prediction model for the risk of dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in a Chinese population of women with gestational diabetes mellitus and to provide a clinically usable risk-assessment tool.MethodsThe derivation cohort comprised 500 Chinese women diagnosed with GDM at 24–28 weeks’gestation in the obstetric outpatient clinic of Beijing Friendship Hospital affiliated to Capital Medical University (a single tertiary center in China) between January 2016 and December 2022, who completed a postpartum oral glucose tolerance test. Predictors were selected using LASSO regression, and four machine learning algorithms (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, and support vector machine) were trained to construct the prediction models, followed by internal validation. For external validation, a prospective cohort of 170 Chinese women with GDM was recruited from Beijing Chaoyang Hospital(another single center in China) from May to November 2023 and followed until 6 weeks postpartum. Finally, an R-Shiny web calculator was developed to facilitate real-time risk estimation.ResultsAmong 500 women in the development cohort, 209 (41.8 %) with GDM developed dysglycaemia at 6-12 weeks postpartum. LASSO regression identified prior GDM, family history of diabetes, HbA1c, 2-hour plasma glucose from the diagnostic 75-g OGTT, and total bilirubin as independent predictors. The four machine-learning models achieved AUCs of 0.606-0.769, accuracies of 0.600-0.729, sensitivities of 0.526-0.877, and specificities of 0.581-0.828; the logistic model was superior. In the external validation cohort of 170 women, 54 (31.8%) developed dysglycaemia. The validated logistic model yielded an AUC of 0.808 (95 % CI 0.740-0.875), sensitivity 0.810, specificity 0.644, and Youden index 0.455, with excellent calibration.ConclusionsThe validated logistic model offers health care personnel a ready-to-use web tool for early postpartum dysglycaemia identification and targeted intervention to curb progression to type 2 diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02158-4.Keywords: Diabetes, Gestational, Postpartum, Postpartum glucose intolerance, Risk prediction model, Risk factors

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy [1]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reported that GDM affected 14.0% of pregnancies worldwide in 2021 [2]. GDM imposes multiple threats to maternal and neonatal health, markedly increasing the risk of gestational hypertension, polyhydramnios, urinary-tract infection, and caesarean delivery, among other adverse pregnancy outcomes [3]. Women with GDM carry a nearly ten-fold higher lifetime risk of progression to Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) compared with normoglycemic controls [4]. Maternal hyperglycaemia further drives macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycaemia [5]. Moreover, offspring exposed to GDM in utero face enduring increases in lifetime risks of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity [6].

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 6–12 weeks postpartum to screen women with GDM for dysglycaemia [7]. Although most patients normalise glycaemia during this window, approximately 27% exhibit persistent abnormalities—impaired fasting glucose (IFG), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), or T2DM [8]. These women face a markedly higher 5-year incidence of T2DM than those who regain normoglycaemia [9]. Early identification of this high-risk group is therefore imperative for diabetes prevention. Therefore, an accurate predictive model derived from pregnancy data is needed to forecast postpartum glycaemic status and enable interventions that forestall progression to dysglycaemia.

Previous studies have developed predictive models for postpartum dysglycaemia in GDM. Muche et al. [10] prospectively enrolled 112 African women with GDM in Ethiopia and constructed a multivariable logistic regression model for dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum. The final model included four independent predictors: maternal age, obesity, fasting plasma glucose at GDM diagnosis, and antenatal depression, yielding an AUC of 0.884 (good discrimination). Kondo et al. retrospectively analysed 123 Japanese women with GDM and derived a 6–12 weeks postpartum prediction model using the insulinogenic index/fasting insulin ratio and cumulative antenatal glucose exposure, achieving an AUC of 0.725 [11]. Nevertheless, these model remains limited by incomplete predictors, modest sample size, absence of internal or external validation, and absence of clinical translation.

Machine-learning (ML), a subset of artificial intelligence, outperforms conventional regression by mitigating data error, selecting optimal features, and reducing modelling bias [12]. Empirically, ML models exploit large raw datasets and capture complex non-linear interactions, yielding higher accuracy and robustness [13]. Our study aims to develop, internally validate, and compare postpartum dysglycaemia prediction models for women with GDM at 6–12 weeks, select the optimal model, externally validate its performance, and deploy it as an online risk-assessment calculator to support healthcare personnel in risk prediction and management.

Methods

Study design

A cohort study was conducted to develop a 6–12 weeks postpartum dysglycaemia prediction model in women with GDM, with reporting aligned to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [14, 15] (Supple Appendix 1, 2).

Study population

This study enrolled pregnant women diagnosed with GDM who registered for antenatal care and delivered at Beijing Friendship Hospital in China, from January 2016 to December 2022.

(ii)Sample size of external validation cohort

Sample size of external validation cohort

Given that 2-hour post-load glucose > 8.6 mmol/L during the diagnostic OGTT is the strongest glycaemic predictor of postpartum dysglycaemia [18], it was designated the primary exposure. According to Inoue et al., exposed and unexposed women experienced postpartum dysglycaemia rates of 57% vs. 35% [19]. PASS 15.0 estimated 77 participants per arm (α = 0.05, β = 0.2, 80% power); allowing 10% attrition, 85 per arm (77/0.90) were required, yielding n = 170.

Data collection

Development-cohort data (retrospective): After approval from the hospital outpatient administration, uniformly trained investigators extracted standardised clinical data from the electronic medical record (EMR). During extraction, completeness and consistency checks flagged incomplete or aberrant entries for telephone follow-up or record reconciliation.

External-validation cohort (prospective): Pregnant women with GDM diagnosed at 24–28 weeks’ gestation were recruited from obstetric clinics. After written informed consent and study briefing, participants completed questionnaires. At 6–12 weeks postpartum, a 75-g OGTT was performed; dysglycaemia was adjudicated by WHO criteria. Self-reported demographics were cross-checked with EMR laboratory data, and all forms were verified before database entry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.1.2 software. Pre-processing: variables with ≦ 40% missing values were multiply imputed; those > 40% were excluded, followed by centring and standardisation. To handle missing data, we performed multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE). Critically, the imputation process was conducted separately and independently for the derivation and validation cohorts to prevent information leakage. Specifically, the imputation model was fitted exclusively on the derivation cohort. The resulting model parameters were then applied to impute missing values in the validation cohort. This ensures that no information from the validation set was used during the model development phase, preserving the integrity of the validation process.

The cleaned data were split 7:3 by stratified random sampling; inter-set comparability was confirmed. Continuous variables were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk); normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD, non-normal data median [IQR], and categorical variables n (%). Feature selection in the training cohort used LASSO regression with postpartum dysglycaemia (6–12 weeks) as the outcome. Four ML algorithms-logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), support-vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF), were trained. Model performance was evaluated in both the internal and external validation cohorts using a comprehensive set of metrics: the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), Youden’s index, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (H-L test).

Result

Study cohort characteristics

From January 2016 to December 2022, 5,621 women with gestational diabetes mellitus were identified in the obstetric outpatient records of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University. Among them, 507 women completed an OGTT at 6–12 weeks postpartum; seven twin pregnancies were excluded, leaving 500 women in the development cohort. Postpartum metabolic assessment identified 291 women (58.2%) with persistent normoglycaemia and 209 (41.8%) with incident dysglycaemia. The dysglycemic group comprised: (1) 25 women (5.0%) who developed DM by standard diagnostic criteria; (2) 181 (36.2%) with isolated IGT; and (3) 3 (0.6%) with isolated IFG (Fig. 1). In the development cohort, missing data were identified across 13 variables. The 25-hydroxyvitamin D variable was excluded due to a missing data proportion exceeding 40%.Multiple imputation was performed to handle missing data for the remaining variables. Subsequently, the development cohort was randomly split into a training set and an internal validation set at a 7:3 ratio. Five predictors selected by LASSO, family history of diabetes, history of GDM, 2-hour plasma glucose from the diagnostic 75-g OGTT, HbA1c level at GDM diagnosis, and total bilirubin during late gestation (28–40 weeks)—were used to train logistic regression, decision tree, RF, and SVM models in the derivation cohort. In the internal validation, the highest AUC and accuracy were achieved by logistic regression, the highest specificity by random forest, the best sensitivity and Youden index by the support-vector machine, and the lowest values across all metrics by the decision tree (Table 4; Fig. 4).

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing total bilirubin from the final model to examine its unique contribution to the prediction of postpartum dysglycaemia. The results show that the AUC has hardly changed after removal(from 0.769 to 0.780).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this retrospective cohort study, LASSO regression was utilized to identify the following significant risk factors for dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in women with GDM. The analysis identified five clinically significant predictors (all P < 0.05): (1) Family history of diabetes, (2) History of GDM, (3) HbA1c level at GDM diagnosis, (4) 2-hour plasma glucose from the diagnostic 75-g OGTT, and (5) total bilirubin level in late pregnancy (28–40 weeks of gestation). Logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, and support-vector machine models were trained with these variables; logistic regression achieved the best performance (training AUC 0.769, F1-score 0.735, accuracy 72.7%). Prospective external validation (n = 170) confirmed strong discrimination (AUC 0.808; 95%CI 0.740–0.875) and clinical utility (sensitivity 0.810, specificity 0.644). DCA demonstrated significant net benefit across the clinically relevant thresholds. An online calculator has been deployed to support rapid postpartum risk assessment and guide targeted intervention.

Compared with other studies

This study observed a 41.8% incidence of dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in women with GDM, which is consistent with prior data. Piffer et al. [20] followed 8,308 Italian women with GDM and reported that 45.2% of those completing an OGTT at 6–12 weeks postpartum developed varying degrees of glucose intolerance. A meta-analysis of 27 Asian studies found postpartum dysglycaemia rates ranging from 3.9% to 41.8% [21].​

This study confirms family history of diabetes as a key determinant of postpartum dysglycaemia in women with GDM. In women with GDM, those with a family history of diabetes had a 3.4-fold higher risk of developing postpartum glucose metabolism abnormalities within 6–12 weeks after delivery compared to those without such a history. Casagrande et al. [22] reported that, among 8,185 U.S. GDM patients, a positive family history conferred a 3.8-fold higher postpartum type 2 diabetes risk. This association is attributed to the greater prevalence of T2DM-susceptibility alleles, such as TCF7L2 rs7903146 and FTO rs8050136, whose presence accurately forecasts postpartum diabetes in GDM populations [23].

This study revealed that women with a history of GDM face a 3.2-fold higher risk of postpartum dysglycaemia than those experiencing a first-time GDM diagnosis. Parallel evidence indicates that women with recurrent GDM exhibit a 6.5-fold increase in diabetes risk six months postpartum [24]. The underlying pathophysiology likely involves a self-perpetuating cycle: the insulin resistance established during the initial GDM pregnancy predisposes to progressive β-cell dysfunction in subsequent pregnancies, resulting in sustained hyperglycaemia that persists from the antepartum into the postpartum period [23].

HbA1c reflects average glycaemia over the preceding 2–3 months and serves as a key indicator of maternal glucose control. In this cohort, women with GDM whose HbA1c was elevated at 24–28 weeks’ gestation exhibited a 1.5-fold higher risk of dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum compared with those maintaining normoglycaemic HbA1c levels, aligning with prior reports [25, 26]. Following 144 GDM pregnancies complicated by either a family history of diabetes or recurrent GDM, Eades et al. [27] demonstrated that antenatal HbA1c ≥ 5.7% conferred a 4.8-fold increase in subsequent type 2 diabetes risk relative to lower values.

OGTT, particularly the 2-hour plasma glucose measurement, reflects mid-pregnancy glucose tolerance. In this cohort, women with GDM who had an abnormal 2-hour plasma glucose level from the diagnostic 75-g OGTT exhibited a markedly higher risk of dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum. Li et al. [28] followed 131 rural GDM women and reported a significant association between antenatal hyperglycaemia and postpartum diabetes. A systematic review adjusted for age and BMI found a 1.95-fold increased risk of postpartum glucose intolerance in women with abnormal 2-hour OGTT values at 24–28 weeks’gestation [29].

Total bilirubin, a product of heme catabolism, is a conventional marker of liver function [30]. In this study, sustained elevation of total bilirubin during late gestation (28–40 weeks) was associated with a 1.45-fold increased risk of postpartum dysglycaemia in women with GDM. This finding appears to contrast with the well-established “antioxidant protection hypothesis”, where in bilirubin is viewed as a beneficial molecule that mitigates oxidative stress and improves insulin sensitivity in general populations [31]. To reconcile this discrepancy within the specific pathophysiology of GDM, we propose two non-mutually exclusive mechanistic interpretations. First, elevated bilirubin in GDM may not function primarily as a protective antioxidant but rather as a sensitive biomarker of underlying hepatic metabolic stress. Subclinical hepatic steatosis and dysfunction are common in GDM and can lead to both altered bilirubin metabolism and exacerbated hepatic insulin resistance, creating a shared pathway toward dysglycaemia [32]. Thus, total bilirubin may be a surrogate for this hepatometabolic disturbance. Second, the relationship between bilirubin and glucose metabolism may be non-linear and context-dependent. Much of the protective evidence derives from physiological or mildly elevated ranges. In our cohort, bilirubin levels were within a higher range that may reflect a distinct, potentially pathological state. A U-shaped relationship is plausible, wherein both deficient and inappropriately elevated bilirubin are detrimental to metabolic homeostasis. Future mechanistic studies designed to measure hepatic fat content, direct markers of insulin resistance, and inflammatory cytokines alongside serial bilirubin measurements are needed to clarify its precise role.

The AUC values (range: 0–1) assessed discriminative ability, with 0.5–0.7 indicating moderate discrimination and 0.7–0.9 representing excellent discrimination [33]. Among the evaluated models, LR demonstrated the strongest performance with an AUC of 0.769, followed by SVM (0.761), RF (0.677), and decision tree (0.606). The F1-score, a crucial metric for evaluating classification models that balances precision and recall, was further analyzed. This metric ranges from 0 to 1, where values approaching 1 indicate optimal performance—reflecting both high predictive precision and comprehensive case identification [33]. The LR model achieved the highest F1-score (0.735), confirming its superior ability to identify GDM patients at risk of 6–12 week postpartum dysglycaemia. While still acceptable, the other models showed comparatively lower performance: SVM (0.722), RF (0.643), and the decision tree (0.605).

Model accuracy (ACC), defined as the proportion of correctly classified cases, was categorized as moderate (0.5–0.7) or good (0.7–0.8) according to standard classification criteria [34]. Among the evaluated models, the highest predictive accuracy (ACC = 0.727) was demonstrated by logistic regression, which correctly classified 72.7% of women with GDM who developed dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum.The SVM and RF models showed comparable performance (ACC = 0.713 for both), while the decision tree approach exhibited moderate accuracy (ACC = 0.600). The Youden index (range − 1 to 1), which quantifies a model’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative outcomes, was found to vary across the tested algorithms. According to established benchmarks [34], a value of 1 represents perfect classification, while 0 indicates performance equivalent to random chance. This study revealed that the support vector machine (SVM) model achieved the highest Youden index (0.490), demonstrating superior discriminative capacity for identifying postpartum dysglycaemia. The remaining models showed progressively lower performance: logistic regression (0.477), random forest (0.354), and decision tree (0.213). While all models exhibited statistically significant discriminative power (p < 0.05), their clinical utility varied considerably based on these metrics.

Sensitivity (true positive rate), defined as the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified by the model, ranges from 0 to 1, with conventional thresholds classifying values between 0.6 and 0.8 as good and those exceeding 0.8 as excellent [34]. In this study, the SVM model demonstrated the highest sensitivity (SEN: 0.877), achieving excellent performance in identifying dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum, while LR (SEN: 0.789) and DT (SEN: 0.632) exhibited good sensitivity. By contrast, RF model (SEN: 0.526) showed suboptimal detection capability. Specificity (true negative rate), representing the proportion of actual negative cases correctly identified, follows analogous thresholds (0.6–0.8: good; >0.8: excellent) [34, 35]. Notably, the RF model achieved the highest specificity (SPE: 0.828), demonstrating excellent performance in correctly classifying unaffected individuals, whereas logistic regression (SPE: 0.688) and SVM (SPE: 0.613) showed good specificity. However, the DT model (SPE: 0.581) performed below the acceptable threshold.

Systematic comparison revealed that logistic regression achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC 0.769), precision-recall balance (F1 0.735), and overall accuracy (ACC 0.727) for predicting dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in women with GDM. Decision-curve analysis confirmed positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds [36], validating its utility. Finally, to comprehensively assess the logistic regression model’s performance, the calibration curves were generated. These demonstrated good model calibration, indicating high concordance between predicted probabilities and outcomes. Therefore, healthcare providers can implement targeted intensive monitoring of high-risk individuals with dysglycaemia between 6 and 12 weeks postpartum, while optimizing the allocation of prevention resources to maximize both clinical effectiveness and cost-efficiency.

Despite employing advanced machine learning techniques (SVM, RF, and DT), none demonstrated significantly better predictive accuracy than conventional logistic regression. This finding likely reflects: (1) the substantial hyperparameter optimization requirements and inherent complexity of these algorithms, increasing overfitting risks in moderate-sized datasets; and (2) logistic regression’s superior generalizability stemming from its parametric simplicity and clinical interpretability. The results suggest that for postpartum dysglycaemia prediction in GDM populations, sophisticated machine learning approaches may not necessarily outperform traditional statistical methods when sample sizes are constrained.​

External validation in a prospective cohort of 170 women with GDM confirmed the model’s robust predictive performance, demonstrating satisfactory discrimination (AUC = 0.808) and good calibration (H-L test P = 0.11). Notably, the discriminative ability was numerically higher in this external cohort than in the internal validation set (AUC = 0.769). This observation, which may initially appear counterintuitive, reflects a well-recognized phenomenon in prediction model research—the spectrum (or case-mix) effect [37]. The internal validation set, derived from the same institution as the derivation cohort, represents the inherent clinical spectrum of that single center. In contrast, the external cohort originated from a different tertiary center and likely exhibited a distinct distribution of patient characteristics, risk profiles, and disease severity. A clearer separation between women who did and did not develop dysglycaemia in the external setting would naturally yield a higher observed AUC. This underscores a key methodological principle: a model’s apparent performance is not an absolute metric but is intrinsically tied to the characteristics of the population in which it is evaluated. Consequently, the present external validation results, while encouraging, do not imply that the model is inherently “better” in all settings; rather, they highlight the necessity of multicenter validation across geographically and clinically diverse populations to rigorously establish generalizable utility and to inform its potential integration into broader diabetes prevention programs.

Advantages and limitations

Existing risk prediction models for 6–12 weeks postpartum dysglycaemia in women with GDM have consistently been limited by the absence of external validation, rendering them unsuitable for clinical implementation. To address this limitation, rigorous external validation was performed for the newly developed model to ensure generalizability and clinical utility. Subsequently, a web-based calculator was developed to facilitate risk quantification and visualization, enabling healthcare providers to more effectively assess postpartum abnormal glucose metabolism risk in women with gestational diabetes.This dual approach bridges the gap between research development and clinical implementation by providing both validated predictive accuracy and practical utility at point-of-care.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, regarding population generalizability, the model was developed and validated solely in Chinese women with term deliveries from single-center cohorts. This restricts its direct applicability to other ethnicities, healthcare systems with differing GDM management protocols, or women with preterm GDM. Consequently, implementation of this model in diverse populations would likely necessitate local recalibration or updating to maintain predictive accuracy. Second, due to its retrospective design, the model could not incorporate two critical, modifiable postpartum factors: breastfeeding practices and weight retention. Given their well-established association with improved glucose metabolism [38], the absence of these variables means our model provides an estimate of baseline antenatal risk only. It is intended to facilitate early risk stratification at the time of postpartum discharge, rather than to replace comprehensive reassessment at 6–12 weeks, where these lifestyle factors should be considered. Third, regarding the role of total bilirubin, its association with dysglycaemia may reflect residual confounding by hepatic steatosis rather than a direct causal effect. However, a sensitivity analysis—in which bilirubin was excluded—resulted in a minimal change in model discrimination (AUC: 0.769 vs. 0.780). This suggests that while the biological mechanism remains uncertain, the overall robustness and predictive performance of the model are not unduly dependent on this single variable. Fourth, concerning sample size and model stability, although the derivation cohort met the size calculated by the Reilly and Evans formula, and the events-per-variable ratio was adequate (EPV ≈ 41.8), the final five predictors were selected via LASSO from a larger set. While LASSO reduces overfitting risk, it can produce coefficient estimates that are optimized for the derivation dataset. Therefore, the stability of these coefficients requires confirmation in larger, independent cohorts before the model can be considered definitively transportable. Fifth, with respect to model calibration, the model exhibited calibration drift during internal validation, systematically overestimating risk in low-probability individuals and underestimating it in high-risk individuals. From a clinical perspective, this pattern could lead to missed opportunities for intervention in high-risk patients and unnecessary follow-up in low-risk patients. Hence, local recalibration is essential before any prospective clinical deployment. In summary, while the model demonstrates promising predictive validity, future multi-center, multi-ethnic studies are essential to confirm its generalizability, refine its calibration, and evaluate its clinical utility when integrated with postpartum lifestyle interventions.

However, the limitation precisely indicates a clear and clinically significant research path for the future. We propose a new “two-stage, dynamic” strategy for predicting postpartum glucose metabolism abnormalities: The first stage is prediction model, which uses prenatal routine data to identify high-risk individuals before delivery, facilitating early education and planning for intensified postpartum follow-up; the second stage occurs at the 6-week postpartum follow-up, when the risk is updated and new variables such as weight changes, breastfeeding status, diet, and physical activity are included. The model that integrates the baseline prenatal risk with postpartum modifiable factors will significantly enhance its predictive efficacy and operability, enabling truly individualized lifestyle intervention during the critical postpartum window. Future prospective studies are needed to systematically collect data throughout the perinatal period to develop and validate this integrated, dynamic prediction framework.

Conclusion

Four machine learning algorithms—logistic regression, decision tree, RF, and SVM—were employed to develop predictive models for dysglycaemia at 6–12 weeks postpartum in women with a history of GDM. Among these, logistic regression exhibited favorable performance relative to the other classifiers across the evaluated metrics. The model demonstrated robust discriminative ability and good calibration in an external validation cohort, suggesting its potential for generalizability. This model provides a tool for assessing the risk of postpartum dysglycaemia, which may aid in the early identification of high-risk individuals for targeted follow-up. Further studies are warranted to evaluate whether such risk stratification, combined with timely intervention, can effectively reduce the incidence of short-term dysglycaemia and mitigate long-term diabetes risk in this population.
