Morse theory and moduli spaces of self-avoiding polygonal linkages
Te Ba, Ze Zhou

Abstract
. We prove that a smooth d d -manifold M M is diffeomorphic to ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} if it admits a Lyapunov-Reeb function, i.e., a smooth map f : M → ℝ f:M\to\mathbb{R} that is proper, lower-bounded, and has a unique critical point. By constructing such functions, we deduce that the moduli spaces of self-avoiding polygonal linkages and configurations are diffeomorphic to Euclidean spaces. This resolves the Refined Carpenter’s Rule Problem and confirms a conjecture proposed by González and Sedano-Mendoza. We further describe foliation structures of these moduli spaces via level sets of Lyapunov-Reeb functions and develop algorithms for related problems.

Introduction

Let
G(V,E)
be a simple planar graph and let
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an assignment of positive reals to edges of
G
. A
configuration
for
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a map
p:G\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the image
p(e)
is a line segment for each
e\in E
, and a
linkage
for
(G,\ell)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a configuration
p:G\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
p(e)
has length
\ell(e)
for every
e\in E
. Over the past decades, linkage theory has emerged as a foundational framework across diverse disciplines, including discrete and computational geometry
[
28
,
3
]
, topological robotics
[
42
]
, molecular biology
[
1
]
, polymer physics
[
21
]
, and related fields. For additional background, we refer readers to the seminal monographs by Demaine and O’Rourke
[
14
]
, Farber
[
16
]
, and Connelly and Demaine
[
11
]
.

We say a configuration
p:G\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
self-avoiding
if it is an embedding. The moduli space
\mathcal{M}(G)
is defined to be the set of equivalence classes of all self-avoiding configurations for
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where two configurations
p_{1},p_{2}:G\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
are equivalent if there exists an orientation-preserving isometry

\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
p_{2}=\zeta\circ p_{1}
. For
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(G)
, the distance between them is defined as

d(p_{1},p_{2})=\inf\nolimits_{\zeta}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{v\in V}\|p_{2}(v)-\zeta\circ p_{1}(v)\|\Big),

where the infimum is taken over all orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Similarly, we define
\mathcal{M}(G,\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}(G)
as the subset of equivalence classes of self-avoiding linkages for
(G,\ell)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and endow it with the induced metric.

In this paper we restrict our attention to polygonal linkages and configurations. Specifically, we assume
G=R_{m}
with
m\geq 2
or
G=C_{m}
with
m\geq 3
, where
R_{m}
(resp.
C_{m}
) denotes the graph having vertices
v_{1},\cdots,v_{m}
such that an edge between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
exists if and only if

|j-i|=1\quad(\text{resp}.\;|j-i|=1\;\text{or}\;|j-i|=m-1).

Up to orientation-preserving isometries, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})

(resp.
\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
) parameterizes simple polylines (resp. polygons) with
m
marked vertices. Accordingly, the subspace
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}(R_{m})

(resp.
\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
) parameterizes simple polylines (resp. polygons) with side lengths prescribed by
\ell
. We call an element in
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
(resp.
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
) an
arm configuration
(resp.
arm linkage
) and call an element in
\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
(resp.
\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)
) a
cycle configuration
(resp.
cycle linkage
), as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1
.
The left is an arm linkage and the right is a cycle linkage

A fundamental problem in linkage theory is to investigate the topology of moduli spaces. For instance, the connectivity of
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
or
\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)
is closely related to the Carpenter’s Rule Problem:
Is it always possible to deform an arm linkage to a linkage with all vertices lying on a line
(by G. Bergman),
or to deform a cycle linkage to a convex one
(by S. Schanuel),
such that in the deformation process no self-intersection is created and each edge remains a fixed length?
It was mentioned in Kirby’s list of
Problems in Low-Dimensional Topology
(see
[
35
, Problem 5.18]
) and it remained open for a long time until Connelly, Demaine and Rote
[
12
]
provided a celebrated answer in 2003. An alternative approach together with an efficient algorithm was developed by Streinu
[
68
]
. In addition, Lenhart and Whitesides
[
40
]
and Aichholzer et al.
[
2
]
described the topology of subspaces of convex cycle linkages. The results combined lead to the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
R_{m},C_{m}
be as above. Then
the following statements hold:

(i)

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
is path-connected.

(ii)

\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)
is either empty or contains exactly two connected components, where each linkage in one component is the reflection of a linkage in the other component.

Remark 1.2
.

By a theorem of Penner
[
51
, Theorem 6.2]
,
\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies the following condition:

\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}

For every
e_{i}\in E
,
\ell(e_{i})<\sum_{e_{j}\in E\setminus\{e_{i}\}}\ell(e_{j})
.

It is of interest to explore topological properties of these moduli spaces in greater depth. Let
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}(C_{m},\ell)
denote the subset of positively oriented cycle linkages. Connelly, Demaine and Rote
[
12
]
claimed
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
are contractible, with proofs later provided in Farber’s monograph
[
16
]
and research of Han et al.
[
26
]
and Shimamoto and Wootters
[
62
]
. Strikingly, Shimamoto and Wootters
[
62
]
further deduced that
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{m-3}
under condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
and the constraint that
\sum_{e_{i}\in E}\sigma(e_{i})\ell(e_{i})\neq 0
for every sign assignment
\sigma:E\to\{-1,1\}
to the edges.

This motivates the following Refined Carpenter’s Rule Problem:

Is
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{m-3}
whenever
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)\neq\emptyset
?
Or equivalently,
can the constraint in Shimamoto and Wootters’ result be removed?
The core challenge arises when a sequence of linkages approaches a pair of overlapping line segments (see Remark
4.12
).
A similar problem can also be posed for the moduli space
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
.

Recently, characterizing the topology of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
has garnered significant attention. In
[
24
]
, González proved that
\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
consists of two homeomorphic components, each of which is simply connected. Let
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}(C_{m})
be the component corresponding to positively oriented polygons. Subsequently, González and Sedano-Mendoza
[
25
]
proposed the following conjecture:
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2m-3}
. For
m=4
, this
conjecture was validated by González and López-López
[
23
]
. Thereafter, Fortier Bourque
[
20
]
investigated the moduli spaces of marked immersed polygons and solved the problem for
m=5
. To date, the general case for
m>5
remains open.

This paper aims to address the aforementioned problems. We first note that the moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
,
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
,
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
, and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
are smooth (i.e.,
C^{\infty}
) manifolds (see Lemmas
3.1
and Lemma
4.1
). Leveraging smooth structures, we further give a complete description of the topology of these moduli spaces. In what follows, the Euclidean
d
-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the
d
-sphere
S^{d}
are assumed to carry their standard smooth structures.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
m\geq 2
and suppose
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an arbitrary assignment. We have the following statements:

(i)

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{m-2}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2m-3}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
m\geq 3
and suppose
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an assignment satisfying condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
(in Remark
1.2
). Then the following statements hold:

(i)

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{m-3}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2m-3}
.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
resolve the Refined Carpenter’s Rule Problem, offering a unified generalization of the results of Connelly, Demaine and Rote
[
12
]
and Shimamoto and Wootters
[
62
]
. Furthermore, part
(ii)
of Theorem
1.4
confirms a strengthened version of the conjecture of González and Sedano-Mendoza
[
25
, Conjecture 1.1]
.

For proofs of the above results, we shall make the use of Morse theory, which has motivated plenty of research on linkages allowing self-intersections. See, for example, the works of Hausmann
[

]
, Kapovich and Milson
[
31
]
, Kamiyama
[

]
, Milgram and Trinkle
[

]
, Shimamoto and Vanderwaart
[

]
, Farber and Schütz
[

]
, Khimshiashvilli
[

]
, Schütz

, Farber, Hausmann and Schütz

, and many others.

However, to adapt to the study of self-avoiding linkages and configurations, we need to go a step further to develop Morse theory for open manifolds. To begin with, we assume
M
is a smooth manifold.

Definition 1.6
.

A smooth map
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
Lyapunov-Reeb function
(or
L-R function
for short) on
M
if it is proper, bounded from below and possesses exactly one critical point.

Recall that a
proper
map means the preimage of every compact subset is compact. We establish the following result analogous to the Reeb Sphere Theorem
[
55
,
45
]
and several consequences of Wilson
[
69
]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold of dimension
d\geq 1
which admits a Lyapunov-Reeb function
f
. Then the following properties hold:

(i)

Given that
M
is endowed with a Riemannian metric, the negative gradient flow of
f
starting at any point in
M
exists for all
t\geq 0
and converges to the critical point of
f
as
t\to+\infty
.

(ii)

M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(iii)

For any
c>\inf f
, the set
M_{c}=\left\{x\in M:f(x)<c\right\}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the set
L_{c}=\left\{x\in M:f(x)=c\right\}
is homeomorphic to
S^{d-1}
.

Remark 1.8
.

The definition of L-R functions is motivated by the two relations:
(1)
Up to a constant, an L-R function serves as a Lyapunov function for its negative gradient flow (see Remark
2.4
);
(2)
As in the Reeb Sphere Theorem
[
45
]
, the critical point of an L-R function is allowed to be degenerate.

To prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, it suffices to construct L-R functions on the relevant spaces. We first introduce some concepts motivated by the work of Cantarella, Demaine, Iben and O’Brien
[
7
]
.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
U\subset\mathcal{M}(G,\ell)
be an open subset. A
strain energy
on
U
is a proper smooth map
\Phi:U\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}<0

for any expansive motion
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon>0
) in
U
and any
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.

Here a smooth family
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon>0
) of linkages in
U
is called an
expansive motion
in
U
if for any
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
and
any pair of vertices
v_{i},v_{j}\in V
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}~\|p_{t}(v_{i})-p_{t}(v_{j})\|\geq 0,

where at least one of the inequalities is strict.

Remark 1.10
.

Let
m\geq 3
. For
G=R_{m}
or
G=C_{m}
, it is easy to see the function

\Phi(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[v_{i},v_{j}]\in E\\
v_{k}\in V\setminus\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\big(\|p(v_{i})-p(v_{k})\|+\|p(v_{j})-p(v_{k})\|-\|p(v_{i})-p(v_{j})\|\big)^{2}}

introduced by Cantarella et al.
[
7
]
is a strain energy on
\mathcal{M}(G,\ell)
.

We find that strain energies on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
serve as L-R functions.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
m\geq 2
and let
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an arbitrary assignment. Then any strain energy on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
is a Lyapunov-Reeb function.

Next we construct L-R functions on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
. To this end, we introduce two functions on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
(and hence on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
).
Recall that each
p\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
gives a positively oriented simple polygon
p(C_{m})
. Let
A(p)
be the area enclosed by
p(C_{m})
. This gives rise to a smooth function

A:\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Following Shimamoto and Wootters
[
62
]
, for
i=1,2,\cdots,m
, we set

w_{i}(p)=\begin{cases}~\exp\left(\frac{1}{\pi-\alpha_{i}(p)}\right),&\,\alpha_{i}(p)>\pi,\\
~0,&\,\alpha_{i}(p)\leq\pi,\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{i}(p)
denotes the interior angle of
p(C_{m})
at the vertex
p(v_{i})
. We then define a function
w:\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})\to[0,1)
as

w=\frac{1}{m}\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\right).

Note that
w(p)=0
if and only if
p\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
corresponds to a convex polygon. We construct L-R functions on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
as follows.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
m\geq 3
and let
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfy condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
. Suppose
\Phi:\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)\to[0,+\infty)
is a strain energy. Then

f=\frac{1}{A}+w\Phi

is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
.

The proofs of the above two results rely on a fundamental result established by Connelly, Demaine and Rote
[
12
, Theorem 3]
(see also the work of Rote, Santos and Streinu
[
56
, Theorem 4.3]
), which states that every non-straight arm linkage and every non-convex cycle linkage admits expansive motions. More specifically, this property implies that strain energies have no critical points in certain subsets of moduli spaces.

By introducing a projection method, we shall further construct L-R functions on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
(see Theorems
3.3
and
4.14
). Combining these with Theorem
1.7
, we derive the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. In fact, this framework yields more corollaries. Here we list two results describing foliation structures of the moduli spaces via level sets of L-R functions.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
f
be a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
with
m\geq 3
. Given
c>\inf f
, then
f^{-1}(c)
is homeomorphic to
S^{m-3}
.

Corollary 1.14
.

Suppose
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
and
suppose
f
is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
with
m\geq 4
. Given
c>\inf f
, then
f^{-1}(c)
is homeomorphic to
S^{m-4}
.

Remark 1.15
.

Corollaries
1.13
and
1.14
settle the following strengthened version of Carpenter’s Rule Problem:
Under what conditions can a polygonal linkage be continuously deformed to any other linkage in the same level set of an L-R function, such that during the deformation process no self-intersection is created and the L-R function remains constant?

Other applications of L-R functions include developing algorithms for the Carpenter’s Rule Problem through the associated negative gradient flows. In fact, L-R functions shed light on a more general problem:
How to create an animation with two images?
Specifically, we will introduce a renormalization algorithm for the Configuration (Linkage) Refolding Problem:
Given two polygonal configurations (linkages), construct an “optimal” motion connecting them
(see, e.g.,
[
7
,
29
]
). The reader is referred to §

for details.

2.
Morse theory in the setting of open manifolds

This section is devoted to showing Theorem
1.7
. Analogous to the proof of the Reeb Sphere Theorem
[
55
,
45
]
, we employ negative gradient flows together with specific modifications. Additionally, we draw inspiration from an observation of Wilson
[
69
]
that a modified negative gradient flow can induce a homotopy from the given level set to a certain sphere. Furthermore, we make use of a powerful tool, namely the Generalized Poincaré Conjecture (see, e.g.,
[
63
,
22
,
52
,
53
,
54
]
).

2.1.
Negative gradient flows

First we introduce some concepts and results on negative gradient flows. For an exposition, see the textbook by Lee
[
39
]
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold. A flow on
M
is a smooth map
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
with the following properties:

(1)

\mathcal{D}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}\times M
such that
\{0\}\times M\subset\mathcal{D}
;

(2)

\varphi(0,p)=p
;

(3)

If
(s,p)\in\mathcal{D}
and
(t,\varphi(s,p))\in\mathcal{D}
such that
(t+s,p)\in\mathcal{D}
, then

\varphi(t,\varphi(s,p))=\varphi(t+s,p).

A flow
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
is
forward-complete
(resp.
backward-complete
) if

[0,+\infty)\times M\subset\mathcal{D}\quad(\text{resp}.\;(-\infty,0]\times M\subset\mathcal{D}),

and it is
complete
if
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}\times M
. A flow
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
is said to be generated by a smooth vector field
X
on
M
if it satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(t,p)}{\mathrm{d}t}=X\big(\varphi(t,p)\big).

We need the Fundamental Theorem on Flows (see, e.g.,
[
39
, Theorem 9.12]
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Every smooth vector field
X
on
M
generates a unique maximal flow on
M
. Moreover, the resulting flow is complete if
X
is compactly supported.

We mention that a
maximal flow
means it admits no extension to a flow on a larger domain and a vector field
X
is
compactly supported
if the support of
X
, given by

\operatorname{supp}X:=\overline{\{p\in M:X(p)\neq 0\}}
, is compact.

Suppose
M
is endowed with a Riemannian metric. The gradient of a smooth function
f
on
M
is the vector field
\nabla f
such that

\langle\nabla f,X\rangle=X(f)

holds for any smooth vector field
X
on
M
. To develop Morse theory, we need to study the negative gradient flow of
f
, i.e., the maximal flow that satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi(t,p)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla f\big(\varphi(t,p)\big).

For open manifolds, the negative gradient field may not be compactly supported, and the induced flow may fail to be complete.

Example
.

Define a function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
f(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{4}
. The negative gradient flow of
f
is expressed as

\varphi\left(t,(x,y)\right)=\left(e^{-2t}x,\frac{y}{(1+8ty^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

This flow is not complete. Specifically, the map
\varphi(t,\cdot):M\to M
is non-surjective for
t>0
and not well defined for
t<0
.

Fortunately, forward-completeness holds for certain functions.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be a smooth Riemannian manifold and let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a proper, smooth, and lower-bounded function. Then the negative gradient flow of
f
is forward-complete.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
, there exists a unique maximal flow
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
generated by
-\nabla f
. For each
p\in M
, let
J_{p}
be the maximal interval such that

J_{p}\times\{p\}\subset\mathcal{D}.

It suffices to show
J_{p}
has no upper bounds. Assume on the contrary that
\beta:=\sup J_{p}
is finite. Let

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}\;:\;[0,\beta)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;M

\displaystyle\;\;\;t

\displaystyle\longmapsto\;\varphi(t,p)

be the integral curve starting at
p
. From the Escape Lemma
[
39
, Lemma 9.19]
, it follows that
\gamma_{p}\big([0,\beta)\big)
cannot be contained in any compact subset of
M
.

On the other hand, a routine calculation gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}f\big(\gamma_{p}(t)\big)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\parallel\nabla f\parallel}^{2}\leq 0,

which implies

f\big(\gamma_{p}(t)\big)\leq f\big(\gamma_{p}(0)\big)=f(p).

Recall that
f
is lower-bounded. One obtains

\gamma_{p}\big([0,\beta)\big)\subset f^{-1}\big([a,b]\big),

where
a=\inf f
and
b=f(p)
. By the properness of
f
, the preimage
f^{-1}\big([a,b]\big)
is compact, which contradicts the fact that
\gamma_{p}\big([0,\beta)\big)
cannot be contained in any compact subset of
M
. We thus derive
\beta=+\infty
and conclude the lemma.
∎

In addition, one asserts the convergence of the above flow assuming further
f
has only one critical point.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
M
be a smooth Riemannian manifold and let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lyapunov-Reeb function with
q
being the critical point. Suppose
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
is the negative gradient flow of
f
. Then for any
p\in M
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\varphi(t,p)=q.

Proof.

Let us consider the integral curve

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}\;:\;[0,+\infty)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;M

\displaystyle\;\;t

\displaystyle\longmapsto\;\varphi(t,p).

As in Lemma
2.2
, it is easy to see
f(\gamma_{p}(t))
is a decreasing function of
t
. Since
f
is bounded from below, we check that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f(\gamma_{p}(t))
exists. For each positive integer
n
, Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem implies there is
t_{n}\in(n,n+1)
such that

f\big(\gamma_{p}(n+1)\big)-f\big(\gamma_{p}(n)\big)=\frac{\mathrm{d}f\big(\gamma_{p}(t)\big)}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=t_{n}}=-{\parallel\nabla f\big(\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\big)\parallel}^{2}.

Because
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f\big(\gamma_{p}(t)\big)
exists, as
n\to+\infty
we have

f\big(\gamma_{p}(n+1)\big)-f\big(\gamma_{p}(n)\big)\to 0.

Consequently,

{\parallel\nabla f\big(\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\big)\parallel}^{2}\to 0.

Note that
\{\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\}
stays in the compact set
f^{-1}\big([a,b]\big)
,
where

a=\min f=f(q),\quad b=f(p).

By passing to a subsequence, if necessary, we assume
\{\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\}
converges to some
q^{\ast}\in M
. Then
\nabla f(q^{\ast})=0
, which indicates
q^{\ast}
is a critical point of
f
. Since
f
has only one critical point, we obtain
q^{\ast}=q
. That means

\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\to q.

As a result,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}f\big(\gamma_{p}(t)\big)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}f\big(\gamma_{p}(t_{n})\big)=f(q)=\min f.

We claim
\gamma_{p}(t)
converges to
q
. Otherwise, there is a subsequence
\{\gamma_{p}(s_{k})\}
converging to some
\tilde{q}\neq q
. Hence

\lim_{k\to+\infty}f\big(\gamma_{p}(s_{k})\big)=f\big(\tilde{q}\big)>\min f.

This leads to a contradiction. Thus the theorem holds.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Set
h=f-f(q)
. Then
h
serves as a global Lyapunov function for the negative gradient flow. By the Barbašin-Krasovskiǐ Theorem
[
4
]
,
q
is a globally asymptotically equilibrium point of the system, indicating each point is attracted to
q
by the flow. This provides an alternative approach to Theorem
2.3
.

2.2.
Morse theory on open manifolds

To streamline the arguments, we first reformulate property
(ii)
of Theorem
1.7
as the following result. As mentioned earlier, this can be viewed as an analog of the Reeb Sphere Theorem
[
55
,
45
]
in the setting of open manifolds.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
M
is a smooth manifold of dimension
d\geq 1
which admits a Lyapunov-Reeb function
f
. Then
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Before giving the proof, we introduce two preliminary lemmas. The first one is due to Brown
[
6
]
and Stallings
[
65
]
. The following version together with a proof can be seen in the lecture note of Milnor
[
45
, p. 168]
.

Lemma 2.6

(Brown-Stallings)
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold such that every compact subset is contained in an open set which is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 2.7
.

Milnor’s original statement
[
45
, p. 168]
assumes
M
is paracompact. This can be derived from our presupposition that manifolds are second-countable Hausdorff spaces. For a proof, see, e.g.,
[
39
, Theorem 1.15]
.

The next lemma is concerned with the existence of smooth bump functions.

Lemma 2.8
.

Given
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
, there is a smooth function
\eta_{a,b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\eta_{a,b}\equiv 1
on
(-\infty,a]
,
\eta_{a,b}\equiv 0
on
[b,+\infty)
, and
0<\eta_{a,b}<1
on
(a,b)
.

Proof.

It is easy to see the function

\eta_{a,b}(x)=\begin{cases}\;1,\;&x\leq a,\\
\;\exp\left(\dfrac{x-a}{x-b}\right),\;&a<x<b,\\
\;0,\;&x\geq b\end{cases}

satisfies the required properties.
∎

We are ready to demonstrate Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

By Lemma
2.6
, it suffices to show, for any compact subset
K\subset M
, there exists an open set
W\subset M
diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
K\subset W
.

To this end, we first endow
M
with a Riemannian metric. Let
q
be the unique critical point of
f
and let
B_{q}
be a relatively compact neighborhood of
q
which is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
\varphi:\mathcal{D}\to M
is the negative gradient flow of
f
. Due to the following Proposition
2.9
, we acquire
T>0
such that

\varphi(T,K)\subset B_{q}.

Let
\eta_{a,b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given as in Lemma
2.8
with

a=1+\max\nolimits_{p\in K}f(p)\quad\text{and}\quad b=2+\max\nolimits_{p\in K}f(p).

We find
-\eta_{a,b}(f)\nabla f
is a smooth vector field on
M
with support equal to

K_{b}:=\{p\in M:f(p)\leq b\}.

Since
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is proper and lower-bounded, it is easy to see
K_{b}
is a compact set. By Theorem
2.1
, the vector field
\displaystyle{-\eta_{a,b}(f)\nabla f}
generates a complete flow

\displaystyle{\varphi_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}\times M\to M}
. That means
\varphi_{\eta}
satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\eta}(t,p)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\eta_{a,b}\big(f(\varphi_{\eta}(t,p))\big)\nabla f\big(\varphi_{\eta}(t,p)\big)

and
\varphi_{\eta}(t,\cdot):M\to M
is a diffeomorphism for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\eta_{a,b}(f)\equiv 1
on

K_{a}:=\{p\in M:f(p)\leq a\}\supseteq K.

For
t\geq 0
and
p\in K_{a}
, we immediately derive

\varphi_{\eta}(t,p)=\varphi(t,p)\in K_{a},

which yields

\varphi_{\eta}(T,K)=\varphi(T,K)\subset B_{q}.

Setting
W=\varphi_{\eta}(-T,B_{q})
gives the required open set and proves the assertion.
∎

The proposition below provides a uniform bound for “arrival time” of the negative gradient flow when the initial point lies in a compact set.

Proposition 2.9
.

There exists
T>0
such that for all
t\geq T
,

\varphi(t,K)\subset B_{q}.

Proof.

Define

\mu=\min_{p\in\partial B_{q}}f(p)\quad\text{and}\quad M_{\mu}=\left\{p\in M:f(p)<\mu\right\}.

We claim
M_{\mu}\subset B_{q}
. Otherwise, one can find another critical point
\tilde{q}
of
f
such that

f(\tilde{q})=\min_{p\in M\setminus B_{q}}f(p)<\mu=\min_{p\in\partial B_{q}}f(p),

contradicting the assumption that
f
has only one critical point. For any
p\in M
, Theorem
2.3
ensures the existence of
t_{p}\geq 0
with

\varphi(t_{p},p)\in M_{\mu}.

Since
\varphi(t_{p},\cdot):M\to M
is a smooth map and
M_{\mu}
is an open set, there exists a neighborhood
U_{p}\subset M
of
p
satisfying

\varphi(t_{p},U_{p})\subset M_{\mu}.

Note that
\{U_{p}\}_{p\in K}
forms an open cover of the compact set
K
and
f(\varphi(t,p))
is a decreasing function of
t
. By selecting a finite subcover, we easily obtain
T>0
such that for all
t\geq T
,

\varphi(t,K)\subset M_{\mu}\subset B_{q},

thereby establishing the proposition.
∎

To derive Theorem
1.7
, we require the following result affirming the Generalized Poincaré Conjecture for differentiable manifolds. It was proved by Smale
[
63
]
in dimensions greater than
4
, by Freedman
[
22
]
in dimension
4
, and by Perelman
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
in dimension
3
. For dimensions lower than 3, it has long been known by the classification of manifolds in those dimensions.

Theorem 2.10

(Smale, Freedman, Perelman)
.

Every closed smooth
d
-manifold that has the homotopy type of
S^{d}
is homeomorphic to
S^{d}
.

Remark 2.11
.

The smoothness condition in the above theorem can be actually dropped. For
d\geq 5
, this was established by Newman
[
47
]
building on research of Stallings
[
64
]
and Zeeman
[
71
]
. For
d=4
and
d=3
, the result was attributed to Freedman
[
22
]
and Perelman
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
, respectively.

We are led to the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Property
(i)
follows straightforwardly from Lemma
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
, while property
(ii)
was established in Theorem
2.5
.

To consider property
(iii)
, we first note that
M_{c}=\left\{x\in M:f(x)<c\right\}
is a smooth manifold of dimension
d
. Define

h=\frac{1}{c-f},

which serves as a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
M_{c}
. By property
(ii)
(equivalently, Theorem
2.5
), we infer that
M_{c}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It remains to characterize the topology of the level set

L_{c}=\left\{x\in M:f(x)=c\right\}
. Since
f
is proper and
c>\inf f
is a regular value, the Regular Value Theorem
[
39
, Corollary 5.14]
implies
L_{c}
is a closed
(d-1)
-dimensional smooth manifold. In view of Theorem
2.10
, to prove
L_{c}

is homeomorphic to
S^{d-1}
, we need to show
L_{c}
has the homotopy type of
S^{d-1}
.

To this end, we refine a technique of Wilson
[
69
]
to construct a homotopy between
L_{c}
and
M\setminus\{q\}
, where
q
is the unique critical point of
f
. Specifically, for any
p\in M\setminus\{q\}
, let us consider the maximal integral curve
\xi_{p}:I_{p}\to M\setminus\{q\}

satisfying the ordinary differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|^{2}}

with initial condition
\xi_{p}(0)=p
. A routine calculation yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}f\big(\xi_{p}(t)\big)}{\mathrm{d}t}\equiv-1.

By the Escape Lemma
[
39
, Lemma 9.19]
, the maximal interval is given by

I_{p}=\big(-\infty,f(p)-f(q)\big).

Letting
p
vary in
L_{c}
defines a map

\displaystyle\psi:\;\mathcal{D}_{c}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;M\setminus\{q\}

\displaystyle(t,p)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\;\;\xi_{p}(t),

where
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\big(-\infty,c-f(q)\big)\times L_{c}
. By Proposition
2.12
below,
\psi
is a homeomorphism. Since
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it follows that both
M\setminus\{q\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{c}
have the homotopy type of
S^{d-1}
. Noting that
L_{c}
is homotopic to
\mathcal{D}_{c}
, we deduce that
L_{c}
is a closed smooth manifold homotopic to
S^{d-1}
. Overall, the topological relations are summarized as follows:

L_{c}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{c}\cong M\setminus\{q\}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\simeq S^{d-1},

where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the origin, and the notation “
\simeq
” (resp. “
\cong
”) means homotopy equivalence (resp. homeomorphism) between topological spaces. In light of Theorem
2.10
, we conclude that
L_{c}
is homeomorphic to
S^{d-1}
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

The map
\psi:\mathcal{D}_{c}\to M\setminus\{q\}
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

If
d=1
, it is easy to see
\psi
is a homeomorphism. Henceforth, we assume
d\geq 2
and observe the following properties:

\mathbf{(x1)}

\psi
is proper. This follows from the Escape Lemma
[
39
, Lemma 9.19]
.

\mathbf{(x2)}

\psi
is injective. Suppose
(t_{1},p_{1}),(t_{2},p_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}
and
y\in M\setminus\{q\}
satisfy

\xi_{p_{1}}(t_{1})=\xi_{p_{2}}(t_{2})=y.

We need to show
(t_{1},p_{1})=(t_{2},p_{2})
. As before, a direct calculation gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}f\big(\xi_{p_{1}}(t)\big)}{\mathrm{d}t}\equiv-1,

which yields

-t_{1}=f\big(\xi_{p_{1}}(t_{1})\big)-f\big(\xi_{p_{1}}(0)\big)=f(y)-f(p_{1}).

Similarly,

-t_{2}=f(y)-f(p_{2}).

Since
p_{1},p_{2}\in L_{c}
, we derive

t_{1}=f(p_{1})-f(y)=c-f(y)=f(p_{2})-f(y)=t_{2}.

Therefore,

p_{1}=\xi_{y}(-t_{1})=\xi_{y}(-t_{2})=p_{2}.

That means
(t_{1},p_{1})=(t_{2},p_{2})
, which concludes
\psi
is injective.

From property
\mathbf{(x1)}
it follows that
\psi(\mathcal{D}_{c})
is a closed subset of
M\setminus\{q\}
. Meanwhile, by Brouwer’s Invariance of Domain Theorem, property
\mathbf{(x2)}
implies
\psi(\mathcal{D}_{c})
is open in
M\setminus\{q\}
. Since
\psi(\mathcal{D}_{c})
is clopen in
M\setminus\{q\}
and
M\setminus\{q\}
is connected for
d\geq 2
, we deduce

\psi(\mathcal{D}_{c})=M\setminus\{q\}
. In summary,
\psi
is a bijective smooth map and thus is a homeomorphism.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Wilson
[
69
]
obtained similar results to properties
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Theorem
1.7
under stronger assumptions that
M
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
is a global Lyapunov function of a complete flow on
M
. As a comparison, after relaxing Wilson’s conditions (particularly on the completeness of the flow), our theorem plays a more applicable role in characterizing topology of smooth manifolds.

3.
Moduli spaces of arm linkages and configurations

We now characterize the topology of the moduli spaces of arm linkages and configurations. As previously noted, our strategy is to construct L-R functions on these spaces. To achieve this goal, we need to investigate the smooth structures of
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
and
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
.

Let us begin with the moduli space
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
. For each
p\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
, by using an appropriate orientation-preserving isometry, we assume

\displaystyle p(v_{1})=(0,0),\quad p(v_{2})=(\rho_{1},0),

\displaystyle p(v_{3})=p(v_{2})+(\rho_{2}\cos\theta_{2},\rho_{2}\sin\theta_{2}),

\displaystyle\vdots

\displaystyle p(v_{m})=p(v_{m-1})+(\rho_{m-1}\cos\theta_{m-1},\rho_{m-1}\sin\theta_{m-1}),

where
(\rho,\theta):=(\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{m-1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{m-1})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m-1}\times\left(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\right)^{m-2}
. Noting that each

p\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is uniquely determined by
(\rho,\theta)
, we can identify
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
with a subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m-1}\times\left(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\right)^{m-2}
. Since the self-avoiding property is stable under small perturbations,
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m-1}\times\left(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\right)^{m-2}
, thereby inheriting the induced smooth structure from the ambient space. Additionaly,

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)=\big\{(\rho,\theta)\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m}):\rho=\vec{\ell}\big\},

where
\vec{\ell}=\big(\ell([v_{1},v_{2}]),\cdots,\ell([v_{m-1},v_{m}])\big)
denotes the length vector. This implies
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
carries the smooth structure of an embedded submanifold of
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
.

In summary, we have the following lemma, part of which has appeared in the works of Shimamoto and Vanderwaart
[
61
]

and Farber
[
16
, Chap. 3]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m\geq 2
and let
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an arbitrary assignment. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is a smooth manifold of dimension
2m-3

(ii)

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is a smooth submanifold of dimension
m-2
.

Next we deduce that strain energies on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
are L-R functions. To do this, we invoke the following result of Connelly, Demaine and Rote
[
12
, Theorem 3]
, which played a pivotal role in resolving the Carpenter’s Rule Problem. For alternative proofs, see also the works of Rote, Santos and Streinu
[
56
, Theorem 4.3]
and Farber
[
16
, Chap. 3]
. We call an element in
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
(resp.
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
) a
straight linkage
(resp.
straight configuration
) if all its vertices are collinear.

Theorem 3.2

(Connelly-Demaine-Rote)
.

Suppose
p\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
is not the straight linkage. Then there exists an expansive motion
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon>0
) in
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
such that
p_{0}=p
.

We are ready to show Theorem
1.11
.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

Suppose
\Phi:\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)\to[0,+\infty)
is a strain energy. It is easy to see
\Phi
attains minimum at some point
q\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
. We will prove
q
is the only critical point of
\Phi
, which concludes
\Phi
is a Lyapunov-Reeb function.

First we claim
q
is the straight linkage. Otherwise, Theorem
3.2
implies there is an expansive motion
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon>0
) in

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
with
p_{0}=q
. Since
\Phi
is a strain energy, one derives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}<0,

contradicting that
p_{0}=q
is a minimum point of
\Phi
.

We further verify that
\Phi
has no critical points except
q
. Assume on the contrary that
q^{\prime}\neq q
is another critical point of
\Phi
. Thus
q^{\prime}
is not the straight linkage. Using Theorem
3.2
again, we obtain an expansive motion
\{p^{\prime}_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon^{\prime}<t<\varepsilon^{\prime}}
(
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
) in
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
such that
p^{\prime}_{0}=q^{\prime}
. Because
q^{\prime}
is a critical point of
\Phi
, a direct computation yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(p^{\prime}_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}=0.

This contradicts the definition of strain energies, thereby completing the proof.
∎

To construct L-R functions on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
, we introduce a projection method. More specifically, for each
p\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
, let
\varsigma(p)
be the straight configuration sharing the same length vector as
p
. This gives rise to the following projection map

\displaystyle\varsigma\;:\;\mathcal{M}(R_{m})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;\mathcal{L}(R_{m})

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\longmapsto\;\;\varsigma(p),

where
\mathcal{L}(R_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
is the subspace of straight configurations. Note that
\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
is characterized by

\mathcal{L}(R_{m})=\big\{(\rho,\theta)\in\mathcal{M}(R_{m}):\theta=\vec{0}\big\},

where
\vec{0}\in\left(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\right)^{m-2}
denotes the null vector. It is easy to see
\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
is a smooth manifold diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m-1}
. We say a smooth map
\Phi:\mathcal{M}(R_{m})\to[0,+\infty)

is a
quasi strain energy
on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
if the following properties are satisfied:

\mathrm{\mathbf{(a1)}}

For every compact set
K\subset\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
, the restriction of
\Phi
to
\varsigma^{-1}(K)
is proper.

\mathrm{\mathbf{(a2)}}

For every
q\in\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
, the restriction of
\Phi
to
\varsigma^{-1}(q)
is a strain energy.

With these concepts in place, we construct L-R function on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
as follows.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
m\geq 2
. Assume
\Phi:\mathcal{M}(R_{m})\to[0,+\infty)
is a quasi strain energy and assume
h:\mathcal{L}(R_{m})\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lyapunov-Reeb function. Then

f=\Phi-\Phi\circ\varsigma+h\circ\varsigma

is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
.

Proof.

Recalling that
\varsigma(p)
is a straight configuration sharing the same length vector as
p
, we deduce from property
\mathrm{\mathbf{(a2)}}
and Theorem
1.11
that

(3.1)

\Phi(p)\geq\Phi(\varsigma(p)).

Since
h
is bounded from below, we verify that
f
is also bounded from below.

Next we prove
f
is proper. Given
c_{1}=\inf f
and
c_{2}>\inf f
, it suffices to show any sequence
\{p_{n}\}
contains a convergent subsequence, provided that

(3.2)

c_{1}\leq f(p_{n})=\Phi(p_{n})-\Phi(\varsigma(p_{n}))+h(\varsigma(p_{n}))\leq c_{2}.

Because
\Phi
satisfies property
\mathrm{\mathbf{(a1)}}
and
h
is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
, we reduce the proof to showing that both
\{h(\varsigma(p_{n}))\}
and
\{\Phi(p_{n})\}
are upper-bounded. From (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), it is easy to see

h(\varsigma(p_{n}))\leq c_{2}.

By the properness of
h
, we deduce that
\{\varsigma(p_{n})\}
lies in the compact set

K:=h^{-1}([b_{2},c_{2}])\subset\mathcal{L}(R_{m}),

where
b_{2}=\inf_{q\in\mathcal{L}(R_{m})}h(q)
. Reapplying (
3.2
), we derive

\Phi(p_{n})\leq c_{2}-b_{2}+\sup\nolimits_{q\in K}\Phi(q).

It remains to verify that
f
has a unique critical point. To do this, we view
f
as a function of
(\rho,\theta)
and infer that

f(\rho,\theta)=\Phi(\rho,\theta)-\Phi(\rho,\vec{0})+h(\rho,\vec{0}).

Suppose
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})
is a critical point of
f
. For
i=2,\cdots,m-1
, taking the derivative with respect to
\theta_{i}
gives

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})=0,

which implies
\tilde{\theta}
is a critical point of
\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\cdot)
. By Theorem
1.11
, this yields
\tilde{\theta}=\vec{0}
.
Meanwhile, for
i=1,\cdots,m-1
, the partial derivative with respect to
\rho_{i}
satisfies

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\vec{0})+\frac{\partial h}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\vec{0})=\frac{\partial h}{\partial\rho_{i}}(\tilde{\rho},\vec{0})=0.

It follows that
\tilde{\rho}=\rho^{\ast}
, where
\rho^{\ast}
is the unique critical point of
h(\cdot,\vec{0})
. That means
f
has exactly one critical point
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\theta})=(\rho^{\ast},\vec{0})
. We thus finish the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

As an example, we mention that

h(q)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m-1}\log^{2}\|q(v_{i+1})-q(v_{i})\|

is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{L}(R_{m})
.

We now characterize the topology of relevant spaces.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We assume
m\geq 3
, as the case
m=2
is trivial. In light of Theorems
1.11
, Theorem

Smooth structures of moduli spaces

We first note that

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
m\geq 3
and suppose
\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
. We have the following statements:

(i)

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
is a smooth manifold of dimension
2m-3

(ii)

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
is a smooth submanifold of dimension
m-3
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
is a non-empty open subset of
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
, we deduce part
(i)
from Lemma
3.1
. For part
(ii)
, we define a map
F:\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
by

F(p)=\big(\|p(v_{2})-p(v_{1})\|,\cdots,\|p(v_{m})-p(v_{m-1})\|,\|p(v_{1})-p(v_{m})\|\big).

Then
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)=F^{-1}(\vec{\ell})
, where

\vec{\ell}=\big(\ell([v_{1},v_{2}]),\cdots,\ell([v_{m-1},v_{m}]),\ell([v_{m},v_{1}])\big).

Under condition
\mathrm{\mathbf{(c1)}}
,
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)\neq\emptyset
. The lemma follows as a consequence of the Regular Value Theorem
[
39
]
, provided that
\vec{\ell}
is a regular value of the map
F
. It therefore suffices to show the Jacobian
DF(p)
has full rank
m
for each
p\in F^{-1}(\vec{\ell})
. By invoking the coordinates
(\rho,\theta)
for
p
, we infer that
DF(p)
has the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1&0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0\\
&\vdots&\ddots&&&\vdots&\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\rho_{1}}&\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\rho_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\rho_{m-1}}&\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\theta_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\theta_{m-1}}\end{array}\right],

where

\displaystyle\rho_{m}:=|p(v_{1})-p(v_{m})|=\sqrt{\Big(\rho_{1}+\sum\nolimits_{j=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\cos\theta_{j}\Big)^{2}+\Big(\sum\nolimits_{j=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\sin\theta_{j}\Big)^{2}}.

We claim
DF(p)
has rank
m
. Assume this does not hold. For
i=2,\cdots,m-1
, then

\frac{\partial\rho_{m}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\frac{-\big(\rho_{1}+\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\cos\theta_{j}\big)\sin\theta_{j}+\big(\sum\nolimits_{j=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\sin\theta_{j}\big)\cos\theta_{j}}{\rho_{m}}=0,

which is equivalent to saying the vector

p(v_{m})-p(v_{1})=\Big(\rho_{1}+\sum\nolimits_{j=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\cos\theta_{j},\sum\nolimits_{j=2}^{m-1}\rho_{j}\sin\theta_{j}\Big)

is parallel to

p(v_{i+1})-p(v_{i})=(\rho_{i}\cos\theta_{i},\rho_{i}\sin\theta_{i}).

As a result,
p(v_{1}),p(v_{2}),\cdots,p(v_{m})
are collinear, contradicting that they are the vertices of a simple polygon.

We thus verify that
DF(p)
has rank
m
, which indicates
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)=F^{-1}(\vec{\ell})
is an embedded smooth submanifold of
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
of dimension
m-3
, as desired.
∎

The following proposition, with roots in classical geometry, traces back to early investigations by Moula

Proposition 4.4

The area function

possesses one and only one critical point.

We first list some properties of the area function for triangles.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
\Delta
is a triangle on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with sides of lengths
l_{i},l_{j},l_{k}
and opposite respective angles
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k}
. Let

denote the area of

(i)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}}=\frac{l_{i}\cot\alpha_{i}}{2}}
;

(ii)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\cot\alpha_{i}}{2}-\frac{1}{2l_{i}l_{j}l_{k}}\left(\frac{l_{i}}{\sin\alpha_{i}}\right)^{3}}
;

(iii)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}=\frac{1}{2l_{i}l_{j}l_{k}}\left(\frac{l_{i}}{\sin\alpha_{i}}\right)^{3}\cos\alpha_{k}}
.

Remark 4.6
.

Set
\displaystyle{\Upsilon_{ijk}=\frac{1}{2l_{i}l_{j}l_{k}}\left(\frac{l_{i}}{\sin\alpha_{i}}\right)^{3}}
.
The Law of Sines gives

\Upsilon_{ijk}=\Upsilon_{jki}=\Upsilon_{kij}:=\Upsilon(\Delta).

We invoke the following variant forms of the latter two formulas:

(ii^{\prime})

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\cot\alpha_{i}}{2}-\Upsilon(\Delta)}
;

(iii^{\prime})

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}=\Upsilon(\Delta)\cos\alpha_{k}}
.

The proof is based on direct computations with details postponed to §
7
. Using this lemma, we are ready to demonstrate Proposition
4.4

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

We assume

4.4.
Constructing Lyapunov-Reeb functions

Recall that each
p\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
corresponds to a simple polygon

\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\alpha_{i}(p)

requires analyzing the function

where

w_{i}(p)=\begin{cases}~\exp\left(\frac{1}{\pi-\alpha_{i}(p)}\right),&\,\alpha_{i}(p)>\pi,\\
~0,&\,\alpha_{i}(p)\leq\pi.\end{cases}

Note that
w(p)=0
if
p\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
is a

(i.e.,

corresponds to a convex polygon). The following lemma was attributed to Shimamoto and Wootters
[

. To maintain self-containment, here we provide a proof.

Lemma 4.10

(Shimamoto-Wootters)
.

Let
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}

(\varepsilon>0)
be an expansive motion in
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
. Then

\frac{\mathrm{d}w(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}\leq 0.

Proof.

To establish the lemma, we proceed by deriving that

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}\leq 0

for
i=1,\cdots,m
. If
\alpha_{i}(p_{0})\leq\pi
, it is easy to see

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}=0,

For
\alpha_{i}(p_{0})>\pi
, applying the Law of Cosines, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\|p_{t}(v_{i-1})-p_{t}(v_{i+1})\|}{l_{i}l_{i+1}\sin\alpha_{i}(p_{t})}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}~\|p_{t}(v_{i-1})-p_{t}(v_{i+1})\|,

where
l_{i}=\ell([v_{i-1},v_{i}])
,
l_{i+1}=\ell([v_{i},v_{i+1}])
. Since
\{p_{t}\}_{-\varepsilon<t<\varepsilon}

(\varepsilon>0)
is an expansive motion, the distance
\|p_{t}(v_{i-1})-p_{t}(v_{i+1})\|
is non-decreasing, which yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}\leq 0.

As a result,

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\pi-\alpha_{i}(p_{0})}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\pi-\alpha_{i}(p_{0})}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{i}(p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}\leq 0.

This completes the proof.

.

First it is easy to see
f

Remark 4.12

Shimamoto and Wootters

introduced

To proceed, we further construct L-R functions on the moduli space

Lemma 4.13
.

\mathcal{Q}^{+}(C_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume

,

As in the proceeding section, we say a smooth map

on

if it satisfies the following properties:

\mathrm{\mathbf{(s1)}}

For every compact set

\mathrm{\mathbf{(s2)}}

For every

Theorem 4.14
.

Let

. Assume

is a quasi strain energy and assume

is a Lyapunov-Reeb function. Then

f=\frac{1}{A}-\frac{1}{A\circ\tau}+w\Phi+h\circ\tau

is a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
.

Proof.

First we readily observe that
f
is bounded from below. To deduce that

has a unique critical point, we decompose

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})=\mathcal{Z}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{2},

where
\mathcal{Z}_{1}\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
denotes the subset of convex cycle configurations, and

\mathcal{Z}_{2}
is defined as the complement
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{1}
.
Note that

f=\frac{1}{A}-\frac{1}{A\circ\tau}+h\circ\tau

on
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
. Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we infer that
f
has a unique critical point on
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
. On the other hand, similar arguments to the proof of Theorem
1.12
indicate that

has no critical points on
\mathcal{Z}_{2}

possesses exactly one critical point on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
.

It remains to verify that

is proper. Given
c_{1}=\inf f
and
c_{2}>c_{1}
, we need to show that any sequence

satisfying

(4.4)

c_{1}\leq f(p_{n})=\frac{1}{A(p_{n})}-\frac{1}{A(\tau(p_{n}))}+w(p_{n})\Phi(p_{n})+h(\tau(p_{n}))\leq c_{2}

contains a convergent subsequence. Since

and

\{\tau(p_{n})\}

\frac{1}{A(p_{n})}+w(p_{n})\Phi(p_{n})\leq c_{3}.

By analogous reasoning to the proof of Theorem

\{p_{n}\}

\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
.
∎

Note that

5.2.
Configuration (Linkage) Refolding Problem

In fact, constructing a motion between two given configurations (linkages) is closely related to the Carpenter’s Rule Problem. For instance, one can deform a cycle configuration (i.e. a simple polygon) into a cocircular shape and then fold it back down to the shape of the second configuration. However, this method leads to a needless detour, particularly when the given configurations have “near” shapes. The Refolding Problem aims to find more direct motions between two configurations.

We embed this problem within the framework of the following Renormalization Problem:

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
d\geq 1
and let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lyapunov-Reeb function. Given points
p_{0},p_{1}\in M
, can one construct a Riemannian metric
\tilde{g}
on
M
and a geodesic on
(M,\tilde{g})
connecting
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
?
To address the problem, we define
\eta_{a,b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as in Lemma
2.8
, where

a=1+\max\{f(p_{0}),f(p_{1})\}\quad\text{and}\quad b=2+\max\{f(p_{0}),f(p_{1})\}.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\times M\to M
denote the flow generated by the vector field
-\eta_{a,b}(f)\nabla f
. Explicitly,
\psi
satisfies

(5.3)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi(s,p)}{\mathrm{d}s}=-\eta_{a,b}\big(f(\psi(s,p))\big)\nabla f\big(\psi(s,p)\big).

Note that
-\eta_{a,b}(f)\nabla f
is a compactly supported smooth vector field. Owing to Theorem
2.1
, the flow
\psi
is complete, so
\psi_{s}(\cdot):=\psi(s,\cdot):M\to M
defines a diffeomorphism for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Making use of the pullback metric
g_{s}:=\psi_{s}^{\ast}g
, we establish the following property.

Proposition 5.3
.

There exists
s_{0}\geq 0
such that for any
s\geq s_{0}
the given points
p_{0},p_{1}
can be connected by a geodesic on
(M,g_{s})
. Particularly, for any
\delta>0
, there is an integer
n_{0}\geq 0
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
the points
p_{0},p_{1}
can be connected by a geodesic on
(M,g_{n\delta})
.

Proof.

Let
q\in M
be the critical point of
f
and let
B\subset M
be a geodesic convex neighborhood of
q
in
(M,g)
. Similar arguments to the proof of Theorem
2.5
indicate there exists
s_{0}\geq 0
such that for any
s\geq s_{0}
,

\psi_{s}(p_{0})=\psi(s,p_{0})\in B,\quad\psi_{s}(p_{1})=\psi(s,p_{1})\in B.

Since
B\subset M
is a geodesic convex set in
(M,g)
, one can find a geodesic arc
\gamma
on
B
joining the points
\psi_{s}(p_{0})
and
\psi_{s}(p_{1})
. Thus,
p_{0},p_{1}
are connected by the curve

\gamma_{s}:=\psi_{-s}(\gamma)=\psi(-s,\gamma),

which is a geodesic arc in
(M,g_{s})
. This completes the proof.
∎

With this proposition, we reduce the Renormalization Problem to constructing a geodesic
\gamma
on
(M,g)
for a pair of “nearby” points

and
\psi_{n_{0}\delta}(p_{1})

(see Fig.

A systematic approach to constructing the curve sequence

(5.4)

\begin{cases}\,\gamma^{0}=\gamma,\\
\,\gamma^{i+1}=\psi_{-\delta}(\gamma^{i}).\end{cases}

\delta>0

p_{0}
and
p_{1}

To enhance precision, refine
\delta

6.
Some questions

The paper leaves open a number of questions. We think the following are some particular interesting subjects for further developments.

1.
Given
p\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
, how to construct a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
with
p
being the critical point?
Naturally, analogous questions arise for the moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
,
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
, and
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
. Note that the existence is trivial since these moduli spaces are diffeomorphic to Euclidean spaces, but our goal is to derive explicit expressions for such functions. If achieved, the associated negative gradient flows will yield more direct and efficient algorithms for the Configuration (Linkage) Refolding Problem.

2.
Can one construct complete Riemannian metrics (with explicit expressions) on the moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}(R_{m})
,
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell)
,
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m})
, and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell)
?
This problem is motivated by the following two objectives:
(1)
Such metrics would facilitate the construction of geodesics between any pair of points in the moduli spaces, thereby deriving a thorough resolution to the Configuration (Linkage) Refolding Problem;
(2)
The associated volume forms and Brownian motions may offer insights into the path integral formulation in quantum physics (see
[
8
]
). For related research on random polygons, one may also consult the works of Pardon
[
49
,
50
]
.

3. Let
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\mathbb{H}^{2})
denote the set of isometry classes of positively oriented, simple
m
-gons in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
What can be said about the topology of
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\mathbb{H}^{2})
?
Analogous questions arise for the moduli spaces

\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\mathbb{H}^{2})
,
\mathcal{M}(R_{m},\ell,\mathbb{H}^{2})
,
and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{m},\ell,\mathbb{H}^{2})
. A pivotal step in addressing this problem is to establish the local expansive property for hyperbolic linkages, requiring the derivation of results parallel to Theorems
3.2
and
4.11
. This endeavor proves challenging, however, because the linear programming method (particularly the dual principle) resists generalization to hyperbolic geometry.

4. Let
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{\infty})
denote the set of isometry classes of positively oriented, rectifiable simple closed curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Is it possible to construct a Lyapunov-Reeb function on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{\infty})
?
According to Pardon’s work
[
48
]
,
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{\infty})
is path-connected. However, there is a need to uncover deeper topological properties on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{\infty})
and devise practical algorithms for deforming rectifiable simple closed curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A natural approach to this end is the construction of L-R functions on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(C_{\infty})
.

In this section we provide the proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Consider the expression for the area

A(\Delta)=\frac{1}{2}l_{j}l_{k}\sin\alpha_{i}.

Taking the derivative with respect to
l_{i}
yields

(7.1)

\frac{\partial A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}l_{j}l_{k}\cos\alpha_{i}\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial l_{i}}.

Meanwhile, by the Law of Cosines

\cos\alpha_{i}=\frac{l_{j}^{2}+l_{k}^{2}-l_{i}^{2}}{2l_{j}l_{k}},

it follows that

(7.2)

\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial l_{i}}=\frac{l_{i}}{l_{j}l_{k}\sin\alpha_{i}}.

Combining (
7.1
) and (
7.2
), we have

(7.3)

\frac{\partial A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}}=\frac{l_{i}\cot\alpha_{i}}{2},

which completes the proof of part
(i)
.

For part
(ii)
, differentiating both sides of (
7.3
) with respect to
l_{i}
yields

\frac{\partial A^{2}(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\cot\alpha_{i}}{2}-\frac{l_{i}}{2\sin^{2}\alpha_{i}}\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial l_{i}}

Substituting (
7.2
) into the above expression, we derive

\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\cot\alpha_{i}}{2}-\frac{1}{2l_{i}l_{j}l_{k}}\left(\frac{l_{i}}{\sin\alpha_{i}}\right)^{3}.

It remains to consider part
(iii)
. By virtue of (
7.3
), we infer

\frac{\partial^{2}A(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}=-\frac{l_{i}}{2\sin^{2}\alpha_{i}}\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial l_{j}}.

As in previous steps, the Law of Cosines yields

\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial l_{j}}=-\frac{l_{i}\cos\alpha_{k}}{l_{j}l_{k}\sin\alpha_{i}}.

Combining these two expressions, we obtain

\frac{\partial A^{2}(\Delta)}{\partial l_{i}\partial l_{j}}=\frac{1}{2l_{i}l_{j}l_{k}}\left(\frac{l_{i}}{\sin\alpha_{i}}\right)^{3}\cos\alpha_{k}.

This completes the proof.
