A tiered training program for emergency nurses’ core competencies based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle: A Delphi study
Authors: Qian Wu, Chunli Chen, Wenjun Cai, Fang Cheng, Yan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency nursing requires a high level of professional competencies. However, current emergency nursing training in China lacks adequate theoretical grounding and alignment with nurses’ career development. Based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle, this study aims to develop a tiered core competencies training framework corresponding to nurses’ professional stages (N1-N4).MethodsA mixed-methods research design was employed, incorporating a literature review, semi-structured interviews, and a two-round Delphi expert consultation. A preliminary draft of the training framework was formulated through systematic literature review and semi-structured interviews. Subsequently, 18 experts with over 10 years of emergency nursing experience from four provinces across China were invited to participate in two rounds of the Delphi method. A 5-point Likert scale was used to evaluate the importance of each framework item. Consensus criteria were defined as follows: a mean importance score ≥ 4.0, a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25, and agreement rate (score ≥ 4) ≥ 75%. Expert engagement, authority coefficient, and degree of opinion coordination were evaluated using the questionnaire response rate, Cr, and Kendall’s W, respectively.ResultsThe response rates for both rounds were 100%, and the authority coefficients were 0.913 and 0.917, indicating acceptable expert authority. After two rounds of revisions, the final training framework consisted of 5 first-level items, 15 second-level items, and 48 third-level items, all achieving consensus. The framework systematically integrated Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle with the competency advancement pathway, covering nurses from novice (N1) to expert (N4) levels.ConclusionThis study developed a preliminary consensus-based tiered training framework that covers the entire career trajectory of emergency nurses. The framework provides a theoretical foundation for standardized training design and competency certification. However, its practical effectiveness and clinical feasibility require empirical validation in future research.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05152-3.Keywords: Emergency, Nurse, Core competency based, Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle, Tiered training framework, Delphi, Competency level

Introduction

The emergency department is the frontline of hospital care for sudden critical illnesses, characterized by rapidly changing patient conditions, time-sensitive clinical decisions, and intensive inter-specialty collaboration. Nurses in this setting must master emergency procedures and advanced competencies, including rapid assessment under pressure, independent clinical judgment, effective coordination, and emotional regulation. Currently, China’s nursing workforce faces two structural challenges. Junior nurses often progress slowly in developing core competencies [1–2], while senior nurses experience increasing burnout and attrition [3–5]. Senior nurses’ burnout and attrition are frequently linked to high occupational stress, limited career advancement opportunities, and the physical demands of emergency work [4, 5]. These factors can make experienced nurses feel underutilized and disconnected from professional growth [4, 5]. In addition, the broader nursing practice in China struggles with persistent issues including staff shortages, heavy workloads, and strained doctor-patient relationships [6]. This situation poses significant challenges. It threatens the continuity of care quality, patient safety, and the sustainable development of emergency medicine.

In 2022, China’s National Health Commission issued the “National Nursing Development Plan (2021–2025)” [7], which explicitly proposed strengthening professional training in critical nursing fields, such as emergency nursing, to enhance technical expertise. This policy direction signals an urgent strategic task: establishing a systematic talent cultivation mechanism that accelerates competency development for junior nurses, deepens empowerment for senior nurses, and anchors their career progression. The concept of core competencies originated in the field of business management, emphasizing the comprehensive qualities formed through collective learning, experience transfer, and collaborative participation that support an organization’s sustainable development [8]. In nursing, core competencies refer to a nurse’s ability to integrate professional knowledge, skills, attitudes, and personal traits in specific clinical environments to deliver safe and effective nursing care [9]. Several recent studies have focused on cultivating emergency nurses’ core competencies. Some have explored core competencies among specific populations, such as community nurses [10], others have designed training frameworks for specific positions like triage nurses and trauma nurses [11, 12], and still others have investigated training models for subspecialty emergency nurses [13]. Despite these efforts, a fundamental gap remains in both research and practice. Existing efforts primarily focus on summarizing training content or developing skills for specific positions. A comprehensive design framework is lacking-one that is grounded in established learning theories, spans the entire career development cycle, and achieves a closed loop of learning, practice, and evaluation. Three specific shortcomings can be identified. First, training designs rely heavily on empirical summaries rather than systematically integrating pedagogical theories. This creates a disconnect between the training process and the psychological internalization of competencies. Second, training often follows a linear or modular approach. It fails to establish a continuous improvement cycle of assessment, training, practice, reflection, and reassessment. This failure hinders the transfer and consolidation of learning outcomes. Third, developmental pathways remain unclear. Training content is poorly aligned with the phased needs of nurses at different competency levels, impeding the formation of a talent pipeline and sustainable workforce growth. Therefore, developing a theoretically driven, hierarchical, closed-loop preliminary framework for emergency nurses’ core competency training has become an urgent research necessity.

The development of core competencies follows a spiral path. It begins with practical experience, progresses to reflective observation, advances to conceptual internalization, and finally proceeds to active application. This cyclical learning pattern is especially relevant to emergency nursing. In this setting, nurses continuously encounter critical situations, reflect on their actions, integrate new knowledge, and apply lessons learned to subsequent cases. Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle [14] provides a theoretical framework that directly mirrors this natural workflow, making it well-suited for guiding competency development in this specialty. Existing frameworks, such as Benner’s novice-to-expert model [15] and Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence [16], effectively describe what nurses should achieve at different career stages. However, they do not specify how to systematically promote this progression. Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle addresses this gap by offering an actionable learning process. It transforms clinical experiences into lasting competencies through structured reflection, conceptualization, and application. This study aimed to develop a hierarchical training framework for emergency nurses in China. The framework is grounded in Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle and integrated with clinical practice and career development pathways. Using a mixed‑methods approach including literature review, interviews, and a Delphi survey, three objectives guided this work: (1) to design a progressive training content framework that aligns with each competency level of emergency nurses and is based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle theory; (2) to embed an integrated mechanism that links formative assessment, competency certification, and continuous professional development to ensure measurable and sustainable training outcomes; and (3) to establish expert consensus on training content, methodologies, and evaluation indicators that ensures methodological soundness and clinical applicability.

Methodology

Study design

This study adopted a mixed-methods research design and was conducted in two sequential phases with explicit integration points. Phase 1 (Item generation): A systematic literature review and semi‑structured interviews were carried out in parallel to generate an initial pool of training items. The purpose of this phase was to combine evidence-based content from the literature with contextual insights from frontline nurses and experts. The output of Phase 1 was a preliminary Delphi questionnaire. Phase 2 (Delphi consensus): The preliminary questionnaire was then subjected to two rounds of expert consultation using the Delphi method [17]. The purpose of this phase was to refine the items and achieve expert consensus on the final training framework. The results of Round 1 directly informed the questionnaire used in Round 2.

Definition of N1-N4 nurse competency levels

In the Chinese nursing career development system, clinical nurses are categorized into four competency levels-N1, N2, N3, and N4. These levels are determined by factors such as clinical experience, professional title, educational background, and demonstrated clinical competence [18]. The specific definitions for each level are as follows: N1 level comprises nurses in the developmental stage. This includes those with three years or less of clinical experience, as well as rotating nurses; N2 level consists of proficient nurses. This group includes nurses with more than three years of clinical experience and junior nurse practitioners; N3 level represents clinically proficient nurses, primarily nurse-in-charge and senior nurse practitioners; N4 level is the highest tier, equivalent to nursing experts. This level includes nurses with senior professional titles and hospital-appointed specialized nurses. This tiered progression from novice (N1) to expert (N4) provides the foundational framework for the hierarchical design of our training framework.

Establishment of the research group

A research group was formed, consisting of four members with professional backgrounds in emergency nursing. One member was the head nurse of the emergency department and served as deputy chief nurse with 15 years of experience in emergency nursing management. This individual was responsible for overseeing overall supervision and quality control. The other three members included an emergency department supervisor nurse with 12 years of clinical experience, tasked with conducting literature reviews and qualitative interviews; another emergency department supervisor nurse with 10 years of clinical experience, responsible for expert liaison and questionnaire management; and an emergency department nurse with 8 years of clinical experience, who handled data analysis and report writing. Prior to the study, the research team underwent training on qualitative research methods and Delphi techniques. This training aimed to ensure consistency and objectivity throughout the research process.

Construction of the structure

Literature analysis

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify training materials related to the core competencies of emergency nurses. The following databases were searched: PubMed, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, and VIP. The search strategy combined subject headings and free-text terms. Keywords included “Emergency Nursing”, “Emergency Room Nursing”, “Clinical Competencies”, “Clinical Skill”, “Professional competence”, “Competence”, “Professional”, “Expertise Development”, “Core Competency”, “Education”, and “Curriculum”. The search was conducted up to October 2025. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material Table S1 and Supplementary Material Figure 1. Inclusion criteria for literature were: (a) available full text of the literature and (b) relevant to the current study, including guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, original studies, and evidence summaries. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) duplicate studies and (b) flawed study design. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer. This process followed the principles of the PRISMA 2020 statement [19] (Supplementary Material Figure 1). The same two reviewers then independently assessed the full-text articles. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form that captured study characteristics, training content, and outcome measures. The initial search retrieved 1,529 articles. After removing duplicates, 292 articles remained. Following title and abstract screening, 231 articles were excluded as irrelevant. A full-text review of the remaining articles led to the exclusion of another 50 due to inconsistent subjects or themes. Ultimately, 11 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis [11, 20–29].

Methodological quality of all included studies was appraised following internationally recognized standard nursing review protocols. Two standardized appraisal tools (AHRQ 11-item scale for original research, AMSTAR 2 checklist for review literature) were applied with unified binary 0/1 scoring rules. Dual independent scoring by two researchers was performed, and discrepancies were arbitrated by a third reviewer. Complete item descriptions, scoring standards and item-by-item appraisal results of all included papers are listed in Supplementary Material Table S2.

Semi-structured interviews

Based on the literature review, the research team conducted face-to-face semi-structured interviews with emergency department personnel in November 2025. The inclusion criteria for nursing experts were as follows: (a) registered clinical nurses with at least 10 years of emergency department experience; (b) nursing management or nursing educators; (c) intermediate or higher professional titles; (d) a bachelor’s degree or higher; (e) proficient language skills; and (f) willingness to participate in the research. The inclusion criteria for emergency nurses were: (a) a valid nursing practice certificate; (b) completion of at least two years of standardized training and current employment in the emergency field; (c) proficient language skills; and (d) willingness to participate. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, meaning no new insights emerged from subsequent interviews [30]. Saturation was assessed through team discussions after each interview. After the 11th interview, the team held a debriefing meeting and confirmed that no new themes or concepts emerged. At that point, data saturation was considered achieved. In total, six emergency nursing experts and five emergency nurses from a tertiary comprehensive hospital in Anhui Province were interviewed. The topics of the interviews were as follows:

For nursing experts: (1) If you were to design a training program to enhance the core competencies of emergency nurses, which core training modules would you consider essential? (2) How should the training process be organized to ensure that learning outcomes translate into practical clinical abilities? (3) What do you see as the main obstacles or challenges in implementing such a systematic training program in clinical practice? (4) To overcome these obstacles and ensure the implementation of the plan, which one or two key supports do hospitals or nursing departments need to provide in terms of policies or resources the most? (5) How can we evaluate whether the training program has truly improved nurses’ core competencies? What behaviors or outcomes should be the main focus, beyond routine assessments? (6) Based on your experience, what is the most important principle or approach for ensuring the success of a competency oriented training program?

For emergency nurses: (1) Reflecting on your own professional development, what specific experiences or events have contributed most to enhancing your core competencies? Why? (2) Which learning formats have left the deepest impression or been most beneficial to you? (3) After busy clinical shifts, do you have the opportunity or habit of systematically reviewing or reflecting on your work? What form of reflection do you find feasible and effective? (4) Beyond first aid skills, what other areas would you like to receive more training in? How would you prefer to receive that training? (5) If you could design an ideal training program to support your ongoing growth, what would it include and how would it be delivered?

Before each interview, the researcher explained the purpose, process, and confidentiality principles of the study. Written informed consent was then obtained from each participant. All interviews were conducted in a quiet, private conference room and lasted 30 to 60 min. Each session was audio-recorded, and detailed notes were taken. Verbatim transcription was completed within 48 h of each interview. The transcripts were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method [31]: (a) reading all interview transcripts carefully; (b) extracting meaningful statements; (c) formulating meanings from these statements; (d) summarizing the coding into themes; (e) developing an exhaustive description of the phenomenon; (f) identifying the fundamental structure of the phenomenon; (g) returning the findings to participants for verification. After the analysis, the synthesized themes were returned to 5 of the 11 interview participants (three nursing experts and two emergency nurses) for member checking. All 5 confirmed that the themes accurately reflected their experiences and perspectives, and no major revisions were suggested. This process enhanced the credibility and resonance of the qualitative findings.

Development of the Delphi questionnaire

The preliminary Delphi questionnaire was developed through a hierarchical integration process that explicitly linked three data sources to different levels of the framework structure. To ensure methodological transparency, we systematically documented how qualitative interview findings were transformed into measurable questionnaire items.

First-level items: Deductive derivation from Kolb’s theoretical framework

The first-level items were deductively derived from Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle [14], which conceptualizes learning as a four-stage cyclical process: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. These four stages were selected as the core structural domains, as they align with the natural workflow of emergency nursing: nurses repeatedly experience critical incidents, reflect on their interventions, construct new knowledge, and apply optimized strategies to subsequent cases.

Second‑level items: Inductive derivation from semi‑structured interviews

Second-level items were developed from themes identified through semi-structured interviews. Interview transcripts from six nursing experts and five emergency nurses were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method. This analysis extracted key themes related to training needs, preferred learning formats, perceived barriers, and suggestions for program design. These themes formed the substantive content of the second-level items, ensuring that the program structure reflected authentic clinical perspectives. As shown above, each interview theme was systematically translated into one or more second-level items through team-based interpretive work. For example, the theme “perceived barriers—lack of structured mentorship” directly led to the creation of item 1.3.4 “One-on-One Clinical Tutoring System,” which was designed to formalize clinical supervision for junior nurses. Similarly, the theme “contextual facilitators — alignment with evaluation systems” informed the design of the “Comprehensive evaluation system” domain (5.1–5.3), ensuring that training outcomes are linked to career advancement. This inductive process ensured that the second-level items were grounded in frontline clinical realities rather than imposed solely from theoretical assumptions.

Third‑level items: Extraction from systematic literature review

The third-level items were constructed through a comprehensive systematic literature review. From the 11 eligible articles identified in our systematic search, the research team extracted detailed training components. Extracted information included specific training content, recommended teaching strategies, delivery formats, and evaluation methods. These components were then systematically allocated to the corresponding second-level items through team discussion, forming the initial third-level item pool.

Integration into the preliminary Delphi questionnaire

Following the completion of the three parallel data streams—deductive theory application (first-level), inductive interview analysis (second-level), and literature extraction (third-level) — the research team convened a series of integration meetings to synthesize the findings into a unified questionnaire. The integration process involved: (1) vertical alignment — ensuring that each third-level item was accurately nested under its corresponding second-level item, and each second-level item under its first-level domain; (2) horizontal consistency checking — resolving overlaps or conflicts across different data sources through team discussion; and (3) content validity assessment — evaluating whether the combined item pool comprehensively covered the intended scope of emergency nursing core competencies without critical omissions.

Through this integrative process, the preliminary Delphi questionnaire was finalized, comprising 5 first-level items, 15 s-level items, and 44 third-level items. Supplementary Material Figure 2 presents the complete mapping from the theoretical framework and interview data to the questionnaire structure. This questionnaire then served as the basis for the two-round Delphi expert consultation.

Expert group meeting

Based on the preliminary Delphi questionnaire, an expert group meeting was convened to develop a cohesive training framework. Guided by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle [14] as the core theoretical framework, the program was structured around four key learning stages: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. A closed-loop management system was also integrated into the framework, encompassing evaluation, certification, and continuous professional development.

During the meeting, the research team reviewed and refined the questionnaire items to ensure logical coherence and clinical relevance. Each item was aligned with a specific nurse competency level (N1-N4). Training formats and frequencies were then determined based on expert consensus. This process yielded a hierarchical and progressively structured core competencies training framework for emergency nurses, characterized by a cyclical learning progression. The overall program development process is illustrated in Supplementary Material Figure 2.

Expert inquiry

Expert information

From December 2025 to January 2026, 18 experts were invited to participate in a Delphi study. This panel size is consistent with established Delphi methodological recommendations: a systematic review of 80 Delphi studies reported a median panel size of 17 (interquartile range: 11–31) [32], and most Delphi studies typically employ panels of 15–20 experts [26, 33], consistent with methodological guidelines for nursing research. All experts had more than 10 years of experience in emergency nursing, nursing management, or nursing education. The inclusion criteria were consistent with those used for the semi-structured interviews. In addition, experts were required to commit to completing two rounds of consultation and to remain available for clarification throughout the process. Expert identification: Potential experts were identified through three channels: (a) recommendations from the nursing administration departments of tertiary hospitals in the target provinces; (b) a literature search for first or corresponding authors of Chinese emergency nursing Delphi studies published in the past five years; and (c) snowball sampling from the research team’s professional network. All candidates were screened against the inclusion criteria. Geographic scope: The experts were recruited from four provinces in China: Anhui, Zhejiang, Shandong, and Jiangxi. These provinces were selected based on the research team’s existing professional contacts and the feasibility of follow-up communication within the study’s time frame and self-funded resources. All experts were affiliated with tertiary hospitals. Their mean age was 45.78 ± 8.50 years. The panel included males (6, 33.3%) and females (12, 66.7%). Delphi consultation process

Expert responses were anonymous throughout both Delphi rounds. This was done to minimize group influence and encourage independent judgment. After the first round, the research team summarized the quantitative results and qualitative comments for each item. A structured feedback report was then prepared. It included the group’s aggregated ratings along with anonymized comments from the first round. This report was distributed to all experts together with the revised questionnaire for the second round. This process allowed experts to reconsider their initial judgments based on the aggregated, anonymized opinions of the group.

Preparation of survey questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of three sections: (a) a detailed introduction to the research objectives, background, and significance of the study; (b) a basic information survey and a self-assessment form for evaluating expert authority; (c) the consultation questionnaire itself. The preliminary training framework, which was designed to enhance the core competencies of emergency nurses, was structured around four key stages: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. Experts were asked to rate the importance of each item using a 5-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from “extremely unimportant” to “extremely important.” The questionnaire also included open‑ended sections where experts could add comments or propose modifications, deletions, or additions to the listed indicators.

Data collection

The questionnaire was distributed in either electronic or paper format, with a set collection period of 7 days. Experts who did not respond within this timeframe received a reminder via email or telephone. After the first round, the research team collated all expert feedback and revised the training framework accordingly. The updated framework was then redistributed to the expert panel for the second round, along with a summary of the first-round comments and modifications. The final version of the training framework was determined after incorporating the feedback received in the second round.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Version 25.0 was used for all statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated for the experts’ demographic and professional characteristics. Results were presented as frequencies and percentages. Expert engagement was assessed based on the questionnaire response rate and the proportion of experts who provided comments or suggestions [34]. The authority of the experts was quantified using the authority coefficient (Cr), which combined the judgment basis (Ca) and familiarity with the topic (Cs). It is calculated as Cr = (Ca + Cs) / 2, and a Cr ≥ 0.7 indicates acceptable expert authority [35]. The degree of consensus among experts was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W) [36]. Items were retained if they met the following criteria: a mean importance score ≥ 4.0, CV ≤ 0.25, and agreement rate of at least 75% of the experts (score ≥ 4) [37, 38]. This study considered additional evaluation metrics, expert comments, and discussions within the research team. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics

This study obtained ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of a tertiary hospital in Anhui Province (Approval No. 2024-Science-94). Prior to participation, all experts were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, and methodology. Written informed consent was obtained from each individual. All data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality throughout the research process.

Results

The level of enthusiasm and authority of experts

A total of 18 questionnaires were distributed in each round, and all were completed and returned, resulting in a 100% response rate for both rounds. In the first round, all 18 experts (100%) provided suggestions for revision, and 14 experts (83.33%) provided suggestions in the second round, indicating consistent and active engagement across all rounds. The Ca was 0.911 in the first round and 0.914 in the second, and the Cs was 0.915 and 0.920 in the two rounds, respectively. Based on these coefficients, the Cr was calculated as 0.913 for the first round and 0.917 for the second, indicating acceptable expert authority for the purpose of this Delphi study.

Concentration of expert opinions

In the first round, 64 items were evaluated, with mean importance scores ranging from 3.57 to 4.45 and standard deviations between 0.32 and 0.67. After revisions, the second round included 68 items, with mean importance scores ranging from 3.78 to 4.83 and standard deviations ranging from 0.38 to 0.81. The increase in mean scores, together with the reduction in score dispersion, indicated a trend toward greater convergence of expert opinions.

Coordination level of expert opinions

In the first round, the CV for all indicators ranged from 0.09 to 0.23; in the second round, it narrowed to 0.08–0.19. The Kendall’s W values ranged from 0.213 to 0.322 in the first round and from 0.095 to 0.156 in the second round. All Kendall’s W values were statistically significant (P < 0.01) (see Table 2).

Expert modification suggestions

Based on the initial questionnaire, two rounds of expert consultation were conducted to refine the framework. During the first round of expert consultation, the 18 panelists evaluated the initial draft of the tiered core competencies training framework for emergency nurses. A total of 13 specific revision suggestions were gathered, some of which were overlapping. The research team reviewed each suggestion and incorporated them into the revised draft based on their relevance to the study objectives and the level of expert agreement. A visual summary of the design is provided in Supplementary Material Table S3, and the detailed revisions are listed in Supplementary Material Table S4:

New items added Two core third-level items were added to fill the gap in the initial framework, including “1.3.3 Contact with patients with emergency mental and behavioral abnormalities” and “3.1.1 Pathophysiology and Differentiation of Common Symptoms in Emergency Care”.

Item merging/deletion One vague and overlapping third-level item (1.1.3) was merged into 1.1.1 to eliminate redundancy, with no net change in the total number of items.

Item splitting for structural optimization Two third-level items with illogical structure or overlapping content were split into independent sub-items for clarity, including splitting 2.1.3 into “Structured Reflection Log” and “Stress Perception and Emotion Recording”, and splitting 3.2.2 into “Nursing Ethics and Legal Regulations” and “Emergency and Safety Management Policies”. This adjustment was an internal structural optimization and did not change the total number of third-level items.

Item revision and clarification The wording, definition or scope of 8 items was refined to improve clarity and clinical relevance. For example, “complex case simulation” was redefined as “rare case simulation”, the use of the SBAR structured model during post-simulation debriefing was emphasized, and the roles of nurses in “quality improvement projects” were specified according to their competency level (N1-N4).

Subsequently, a second round of consultation was held with 14 experts, who evaluated the revised draft from the first round. Their feedback was consolidated into five thematic categories, with opinions showing greater convergence: experts largely affirmed the earlier revisions and proposed only minor refinements to the framework, with no items merged, deleted or split. The key adjustments in this round were as follows (see Supplementary Material Table S4):

New item added One core third-level item “1.3.4 One-on-One Clinical Tutoring System” was added to establish a formal clinical supervision mechanism and implement the “under supervision” requirement for junior nurses.

Item revision and optimization The wording, name or focus of 4 items was refined. For example, the first-level item 1.2 was renamed “Advanced First Aid and Team-Based Comprehensive Simulation Training” to better reflect its core characteristics; “rare case simulation” was adjusted to “simulation of atypical critical cases” to focus on common emergency scenarios; and a clear distinction was made between “major positive event seminars” and “adverse event analysis meetings”.

Following the two consultation rounds, a consensus was achieved among the expert panel. The final framework retained the original 5 first-level items and 15 s-level items, with the number of third-level items optimized from 44 (initial version) to 48 (final version), forming a consensus-based, structured training framework that can be further tested in clinical settings. The overarching training framework, which integrated the four-stage experiential learning cycle (concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation) with tiered nurse competency levels from N1 to N4, gained unanimous expert endorsement. Discussion

Significance of the emergency nurse training program

This study developed a hierarchical training framework for emergency nurses in China through a Delphi consensus process, which addresses a critical need identified in China’s National Nursing Career Development Plan (2021-2025) [7]. This plan calls for enhanced professional training in high-demand fields such as emergency care to strengthen the technical expertise of the nursing workforce. More broadly, this training program aligns with the strategic goals of the Healthy China 2030 blueprint, which prioritizes the development of a high-quality and efficient healthcare delivery system. Consistent with this policy direction, the present study adopted Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle as its theoretical foundation and referenced established core competencies standards for emergency nursing. Using the Delphi expert consensus method, we developed a hierarchical training framework that maps the pathway of competency advancement from the novice (N1) to the expert (N4) levels. The framework specifies training content, frequency, format, and evaluation criteria for each competency level. This preliminary consensus-based framework offers a theoretical structural reference for designing systematic training, management and assessment schemes for emergency nurses, while all practical application effects need subsequent empirical testing.

Reliability of the emergency nurse training program

The training framework was developed from multiple perspectives. To ensure its scientific basis, the research team conducted a comprehensive review of domestic and international literature, policy documents, and existing training frameworks to understand the current state of emergency nursing training. The findings from this review directly guided the preliminary design of the framework. To ensure the framework aligned with both the realities of emergency care training and current clinical needs, we further refined it through a structured expert consultation process. We recruited experts with intermediate or senior professional titles from provinces including Anhui, Shandong, Jiangxi, and Zhejiang. They represented a range of fields, such as emergency nursing, nursing management, and clinical education. These experts provided constructive feedback on the training plan. The response rate for both rounds of the questionnaire survey was 100%, demonstrating a good level of engagement and commitment to emergency nursing practice and education. The authority coefficients of the expert panel were 0.913 in the first round and 0.917 in the second, indicating acceptable expertise. Furthermore, consensus was reached on the proposed indicators, with coefficients of variation below 0.25 after two rounds of consultation. The Kendall’s W values were 0.213–0.322 in round 1 and 0.095–0.156 in round 2 (P < 0.01 for both). The decrease in W from round 1 to round 2 suggests that, although experts’ absolute ratings converged (as reflected by the narrowing CVs), their rank ordering of item importance did not converge further. Thus, the consensus is best described as moderate. This is consistent with the results of the Delphi studies in multiple studies. The pattern of improved item-level agreement alongside stable or decreased rank concordance has been observed in other Delphi studies. Li et al. [39] reported Kendall’s W decreases across all four evaluation dimensions, with second-round values (0.111–0.211) comparable to ours, and similar decreases were reported by Gao et al. [40] and Qin et al. [41]. These findings suggest that modest Kendall‘s W values, including decreases across rounds, are not uncommon in complex Delphi studies and do not compromise consensus validity when item-level criteria are satisfied.

Analysis of the training program for emergency nurses

Internationally, standardized emergency training curricula including the Trauma Nursing Core Course (TNCC) [42], Advanced Trauma Care for Nurses (ATCN) [43] and Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) [44] are well-established competency-based education (CBE) [45] systems that deliver standardized technical trauma rescue training via modular summative assessment. Meanwhile, simulation-based learning curricula [46] have been widely adopted across healthcare education. Both mainstream educational paradigms have laid solid foundational frameworks for emergency nursing training and yielded proven educational benefits in clinical practice.

Built upon the mature technical training system of international standardized certification courses, as well as the practical teaching advantages of traditional competency frameworks and scenario simulation, this study further optimizes and elevates the training architecture from theoretical logic and hierarchical structure dimensions. The present research integrates Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle into the tiered competency system, forming a complete closed-loop learning path of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization and active experimentation to support sustainable professional growth of nurses. Beyond the single technical skill training covered by existing programs, this framework expands the evaluation boundary to comprehensive professional capacities, incorporating team leadership, clinical teaching, evidence-based practice and systematic clinical thinking into core training dimensions. A four-tier progressive training system matching N1–N4 nursing grades is also constructed, enabling training objectives, practice contents and assessment criteria to dynamically align with nurses’ career progression from novice to expert.

Several studies in China have provided a reference basis for the training of emergency sub specialist nurses. For example, Hu et al. [13] designed a competency-based training program for emergency nurses in ophthalmology and otorhinolaryngology (EENT). The content modules were closely tailored to the specialized knowledge and skills required in EENT emergency care. Similarly, Feng et al. [12] developed a training program for emergency specialist nurses organized around disease-system modules, such as trauma, poisoning, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. A common feature of these programs was that their content was organized around specific diseases or specialty areas. In contrast, the present framework did not focus on such disease-specific or topic-specific content. Instead, it aims to cultivate higher-order cognitive abilities that can be applied across a wide range of critical conditions. This shift from a “disease-centered” to a “competency-centered” design concept is expected to strengthen the theoretical adaptability of the training framework to complex emergency scenarios and facilitate the transfer of nurses’ professional competencies in theory; relevant practical advantages need to be confirmed via clinical trials. Furthermore, this study aimed to systematically integrate Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle into the training design for emergency nurses in China. This theoretical framework translates abstract core competencies into concrete, traceable learning cycles that integrate practice, reflection, and application. The framework theoretically operationalizes Benner’s “novice-to-expert” developmental model [15] and preliminarily sets out differentiated developmental milestones for nurses at distinct career stages based on expert consensus.

This study aligns with the work of Munroe et al. [47], who developed the Emergency Department Clinical Emergency Response System (EDCERS). Both initiatives share the core objective of enhancing patient safety and care quality in emergency settings. The EDCERS framework establishes standardized operating procedures for identifying clinical deterioration and activating multidisciplinary team responses. It addresses these goals from organizational and workflow perspectives. In contrast, the present training framework offers a structured approach to systematically developing the competencies needed to implement such systems effectively. For instance, the “Complex Case Simulation” module corresponds to the concrete experience stage of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle. The “Structured Review” module aligns with reflective observation. Both can be designed around specific trigger scenarios outlined in the EDCERS protocol. Theoretically, this structural design is expected to guide nurses to master standardized operation procedures and deepen clinical reasoning, but such expected benefits require empirical verification. In future clinical practice, the training framework could support the implementation of rapid response systems such as EDCERS, although this hypothesis requires prospective evaluation.

The primary theoretical contribution of this framework lay in its empirical demonstration of how Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle could be applied within structured nursing education. The four stages of the cycle are operationalized as follows (see Table 3, Supplementary Material Figure 2 and Supplementary Material Table S4): (a) Concrete experience: nurses engage in near‑real-time clinical situations through high‑fidelity simulation and exposure to complex cases; (b) Reflective observation: metacognition and critical thinking are systematically developed through structured debriefing and peer evaluation; (c) Abstract conceptualization: knowledge that supports clinical decision‑making is constructed by studying current theories and pathophysiological mechanisms; and (d) Active experimentation: knowledge is translated into practice through leadership in quality‑improvement projects, clinical teaching, and related activities. On a practical level, the framework offers several advantages. It provides a clearly defined competency-based pathway, incorporates diverse instructional methods (simulation, mentorship, project-based learning), and establishes an integrated system that links evaluation, certification, and professional development. This design allows the framework to be flexibly embedded into existing frameworks. These include orientation training for emergency nurses, annual continuing education schedules, specialized nurse training curricula, and unit‑based quality‑improvement initiatives. For instance, reflective observation activities can be incorporated into monthly nursing case discussions, while active experimentation projects can be aligned with annual departmental quality and safety priorities. In theory, this integrated design has the potential to integrate training into routine nursing work and nurses’ long-term professional development, though all practical effects need clinical verification.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the Delphi expert panel was composed exclusively of experts from four provinces in China, and all were affiliated with tertiary hospitals. While this ensured a high level of expertise, it may also introduce selection bias. As a result, the direct applicability of the framework to secondary hospitals, community emergency centers, or rural healthcare settings may be limited. These settings often operate with different resource levels and staffing structures. Future research should include experts from a broader range of institutions and geographic regions to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Second, this study constructed only a preliminary training framework developed through the Delphi method, without any clinical empirical verification of its actual intervention effectiveness. All training content and hierarchical design are merely theoretical proposals agreed by experts, and the real-world impact on nurses’ clinical performance, team operation and patient safety remain unknown. Further rigorous prospective implementation studies are required to validate and refine this framework before formal clinical application. Third, member checking was performed on a subset of interview participants (5 of 11). The synthesized themes were returned to three nursing experts and two emergency nurses for verification, and all five confirmed that the themes accurately reflected their experiences. Nevertheless, the inclusion of only a subset of participants limits the confirmability of the qualitative findings. A more comprehensive member checking involving all 11 participants would have strengthened this aspect.

Conclusion

Based on a systematic literature review and theoretical analysis, this study developed a foundational training framework. The framework was refined through interviews with emergency nursing experts and clinical nurses, guided by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle, and then finalized via a two-round Delphi expert consultation. This preliminary consensus-based training framework provides a structural reference for scholars to further develop and empirically test formal emergency nursing training framework. Subsequent research will implement this preliminary framework in clinical emergency departments to collect practical feedback for iterative optimization of the formal training scheme.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, we propose the following recommendations for clinical practice and future research.

The training framework provides a structured reference for enhancing emergency nurses’ core competencies. Successful implementation, however, hinges on institutional commitment and available resources. For well-resourced tertiary hospitals, partial pilot exploration of this preliminary framework is feasible and recommended. Hospitals may try modules including high-fidelity simulation training and one-on-one mentorship, while the optimal training outcomes of the full framework remain unconfirmed. For resource-limited secondary hospitals, community and rural emergency departments, this preliminary framework can serve as a reference to design phased training content; institutions may prioritize low-resource modules such as structured reflection logs and peer case discussions for a pilot trial. More resource-intensive elements can be introduced gradually as capacity grows. For simulation training, hospitals with budget constraints can use low-fidelity manikins or standardized patients, or they may share regional simulation centers on a rotational basis. Digital platforms for competency tracking and feedback could be developed centrally and made available to multiple institutions, thereby reducing the financial burden on individual hospitals. To address the shortage of experienced mentors, regional networks can be established in which senior nurses from tertiary hospitals provide periodic guidance to colleagues in secondary or rural settings. Finally, hospital leadership should consider a gradual rollout over two to three years, aligning each phase with budget cycles and institutional capacity building.

Future research should focus on the following directions: (1) Effectiveness verification: implementing the framework in multiple centers using a cluster‑randomized controlled or stepped‑wedge design to longitudinally assess its impact on nurses’ core competencies, team performance indicators, and patient safety outcomes; (2) Adaptation studies: exploring how the framework can be adapted and applied in specific emergency subspecialties, such as pediatric, stroke, or chest‑pain care; (3) Implementation science inquiry: investigating organizational, contextual, and leadership factors that influence the successful implementation of this complex intervention, including necessary resource support and system‑level facilitators.
