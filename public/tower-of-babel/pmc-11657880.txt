The role of self-efficacy in managing type 2 diabetes and emotional well-being: a cross sectional study
Authors: Jamal Ataya, Jameel Soqia, Nour Albani, Nour Kara Tahhan, Massa Alfawal, Oubada Elmolla, Ahmad Albaldi, Raneem Alnassif Alsheikh, Younes Kabalan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManaging chronic conditions like Type 2 diabetes presents significant challenges, with self-efficacy—one’s belief in their ability to manage their health—playing a crucial role. This study investigates the influence of self-efficacy on emotional distress and health outcomes among Type 2 diabetes patients.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in Damascus with 336 Type 2 diabetes patients from three healthcare facilities. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using validated questionnaires: the Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES), Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID-5), and the World Health Organization-Five Well-being Index (WHO-5).ResultsAnalysis revealed that female gender and older age are significant factors influencing diabetes distress, with higher levels of distress observed in females and older patients. A negative correlation was found between self-efficacy and age, indicating reduced confidence in diabetes management among older individuals. Treatment modalities, such as oral hypoglycemic agents, were positively associated with higher self-efficacy. Significant associations were found between diabetes duration, age, and diabetes distress (PAID-5 scores), while self-efficacy (DMSES scores) varied based on treatment types.ConclusionsDespite high levels of diabetes distress and low psychological well-being, patients demonstrated commendable self-efficacy in managing their diabetes. Targeted interventions should address the needs of elderly female patients and those undergoing specific treatments to improve care outcomes. This study highlights the importance of tailored support and a holistic approach to diabetes management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-21050-2.

Background

Diabetes distress is a term that refers to the emotional and psychological burden of living with and managing diabetes. It can affect various aspects of quality of life, such as physical, mental, social, and spiritual well-being. Diabetes distress can also influence glycemic control, medication adherence, self-care behaviors, and health outcomes. Therefore, it is important to identify the factors that contribute to diabetes distress and the strategies that can help reduce it [1]. One of the factors that may affect diabetes distress is self-efficacy, which is the confidence and ability to perform specific tasks or behaviors related to diabetes management. Self-efficacy can influence how individuals cope with the challenges and demands of diabetes, as well as their motivation and persistence in achieving their goals. Self-efficacy can also affect the level of satisfaction and empowerment that individuals feel regarding their diabetes care [2, 3].

Several studies have explored the relationship between diabetes distress, health outcomes, and self-efficacy in type 2 diabetes. A literature review by Alsaedi and McKeirnan [4] found that patients who participated in diabetes education and health literacy improvement initiatives showed significant improvement in glycemic control, medication adherence, medication knowledge, and treatment satisfaction. They also suggested that culturally tailored approaches, patient-centered medication labels, community health workers, and pharmacists can help enhance health literacy and self-efficacy among patients with low health literacy. A cross-sectional study by Lin et al. [2] examined the effect of self-efficacy in self-management on diabetes distress in young people with type 2 diabetes. They found that self-efficacy was negatively correlated with glycemic control and diabetes distress, meaning that higher self-efficacy was associated with lower HbA1c levels and lower psychological distress. They also found that the duration of illness and self-efficacy were significant predictors of diabetes distress, explaining 64% of the variance. They concluded that nursing staff should regularly assess the confidence and ability of young people with type 2 diabetes to manage their condition and provide them with appropriate support and education [2]. Also, A systematic review by Ernawati et al. [3] evaluated the effectiveness of diabetes self-management education (DSME) in type 2 diabetes. They found that DSME interventions provided significant benefits to lifestyle changes and self-care behaviors of patients with type 2 diabetes. They also reported that DSME interventions improved glycemic control, blood pressure, body mass index, cholesterol levels, quality of life, self-efficacy, and diabetes knowledge. They recommended that DSME interventions should be tailored to the needs and preferences of each patient and delivered by multidisciplinary teams.

The present study aimed to assess the level of self-efficacy among patients with type 2 diabetes in managing their condition and to measure their current mental well-being and emotional distress after 12 years of Syrian crisis. Diabetes distress refers to the emotional and psychological burden associated with living with diabetes, while self-efficacy pertains to an individual’s belief in their ability to manage their condition effectively. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing interventions and support systems that can improve the well-being and health outcomes of individuals with Type 2 diabetes.

Methods

Participants and research procedures

A cross-sectional study was conducted from October 1, 2022, to January 1, 2023, to assess the level of self-efficacy among patients with type 2 diabetes in managing their condition and to measure their current mental well-being and emotional distress. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, using a reliable and previously validated questionnaire. The interviews were conducted by trained and competent interviewers, who ensured the integrity of the research process. Prior to participation, participants received detailed information about the research objectives and procedures, and inclusion in the study was contingent on their agreement to participate and align with the study’s predefined criteria. During the interviews, the researchers administered a questionnaire and recorded the participants’ responses. The questionnaire took approximately 15 min to complete, and ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Damascus University, Faculty of Medicine, Syria (serial number 4582). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

In this study, a sample of 336 Syrian patients with type 2 diabetes Participants were recruited through systematic referrals from three healthcare facilities in Damascus, Syria, namely The Endocrinology Clinic at Al-Assad University Hospital, Al Mouwasat Hospital, and the Diabetes Clinic at the Specialist Polyclinic Center. Eligible patients were approached by trained research assistants during their routine visits, and those who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate in the study. Written informed consent was obtained before enrollment in the study. The study sample consisted of 157 males and 179 females. A non-probability purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants for this study. This method was chosen due to its effectiveness in selecting patients who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, ensuring that the sample was representative of the target population of individuals with type 2 diabetes in Damascus, Syria. Prior to participation, all participants were informed of the study’s objectives and assured of the voluntary nature of their involvement. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity of personal information, rigorous measures were implemented throughout the research process.

The inclusion criteria for the study were individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, aged between 16 and 87 years, and willing to provide informed consent for their participation. Additionally, the participants were required to attend the aforementioned clinics and demonstrate the capacity to understand and respond to the study questionnaire and interviews. Exclusion criteria included individuals with type 1 diabetes and gestational diabetes, those who declined to participate or were unable or unwilling to complete the questionnaire or face-to-face interviews, those with severe cognitive impairments, those undergoing active treatment for severe psychiatric conditions, and patients with comorbid medical conditions that could impede their self-management capabilities and well-being.

Measures

The patients completed three questionnaires designed to gather sociodemographic information and assess various aspects of diabetes self-efficacy and self-management (DMSES), diabetes-related distress (PAID-5), and psychological well-being (WHO-5).

Diabetes management self-efficacy scale (DMSES)

The Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES) questionnaire, which has been utilized in previous studies [5], was employed to assess self-efficacy in patients with type 2 diabetes in relation to their ability to manage their condition. This instrument was selected because of its established reputation as a reliable tool for evaluating self-efficacy in patients with type 2 diabetes. The questionnaire was originally created in English, translated into Arabic by language experts, and subsequently re-translated into English to ensure consistency in the meanings of words and concepts.

The DMSES (Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale) is a tool consisting of 15 questions designed to assess an individual’s confidence in performing various diabetes-related self-management tasks. These tasks include taking medication as prescribed, checking blood sugar levels when necessary, and correcting blood sugar levels when they are too high or too low. The questions were scored on a numerical scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating higher self-efficacy. A score of 0 indicated low self-efficacy, while a score of 10 indicated high self-efficacy. High scores on the DMSES indicate high self-efficacy. The scale has strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 [5].

Problem areas in diabetes (PAID-5)

In the present study, we utilized the PAID-5 questionnaire as our primary assessment tool to evaluate emotional and psychological distress associated with diabetes in our study cohort. Notably, the PAID-5 has been rigorously validated and specifically designed to elucidate emotional aspects related to diabetes-related concerns. We employed the Arabic version of the questionnaire, which was not required to be translated from prior research [6]. PAID-5 comprises five carefully crafted questions that target various aspects of emotional distress commonly experienced by individuals affected by diabetes. These questions were designed to assess feelings of frustration, guilt, and fear related to the management of diabetes.

Each item on the PAID-5 is scored on a Likert scale, typically ranging from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates “Not a problem” and 4 indicates “A serious problem.” The summation of participants’ responses to these inquiries yielded a composite total score, with a spectrum ranging from 0 to 20. In this study, a cut-off value of 8 points or higher, as recommended by the original maker of PAID-5, was used as the screening cut-off value, indicating a lower level of emotional distress [6]. Scores below eight indicate a higher level of emotional distress. Cronbach’s alpha for the PAID-5 was 0.96, indicating strong internal consistency [6]. The PAID-5 questionnaire has demonstrated strong validity and reliability in numerous studies and clinical settings, making it a robust tool for evaluating the emotional impact of diabetes [6].

World health organization-five well-being index (WHO-5)

The WHO-5 Well-Being Index, developed by the World Health Organization, was utilized as a primary measurement tool in this cross-sectional study to evaluate the overall psychological well-being of the participants. By comparing five distinct items that encompass various aspects of emotional well-being, including mood, vitality, and general mental health, the WHO-5 was constructed with care to capture the respondents’ overall sense of well-being. Each item on the WHO-5 is scored on a Likert scale, typically ranging from 0 to 5, where 0 represents the worst possible well-being, and 5 indicates the best possible well-being for serious problems. Participants’ responses to these items were summed to calculate a total score that ranged from 0 to 25. To obtain a percentage score ranging from 0 to 100, the raw score was multiplied by 4, with higher scores indicating a greater level of overall well-being. A score ≤ 50 indicates poor psychological well-being [7]. The Arabic WHO-5 Questionnaire has been demonstrated to be a reliable and valid measure with strong internal consistency, as evidenced by its Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 [8].

Statistical analysis

Following data collection, responses were recorded using Google Forms and subsequently exported to Excel for analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Inc., version 23 software package, encompassing descriptive statistics, independent t-test, chi-square test, ANOVA, and Pearson correlation. The significance level for all analyses was set at P < 0.05. The primary objective of these analyses was to identify patterns in psychological well-being, diabetes self-management, and diabetes-related distress among study participants.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated using GPower software. To detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with 80% power and a significance level (α) of 0.05, a minimum of 280 participants was required. This calculation ensures that the study has a sufficient sample size to reliably detect the expected effect, minimizing the risk of Type I and Type II errors. GPower allows researchers to input the desired parameters and provides the necessary sample size to achieve the specified statistical power and significance level.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The study comprised 336 participants, with a balanced gender distribution of 46.7% males and 53.3% females. A significant proportion of participants reported having received formal education (82.1%), while 17.9% indicated they had not. Health-related characteristics showed a Body Mass Index (BMI) range of 17 to 45, with a mean BMI of 28.4 (SD = 5.2). The duration of diabetes among participants varied from 1 to 50 years, with an average of 12.1 years (SD = 8.7). Ages ranged from 16 to 87 years, with a mean age of 59.6 years (SD = 13.2). Relationships between PAID-5, WHO-5, DMSES scores, and demographic variables

PAID-5

Scores above 8 indicate higher levels of distress, while scores below 8 suggest lower levels of emotional distress among participants. Independent t-tests showed a significant positive association between the duration of diabetes and PAID-5 scores, t (334) = 2.48, p = 0.014. Participants scoring below 8 on PAID-5 reported a longer average diabetes duration (M = 12.8 ± 8.6 years) compared to those scoring above 8 (M = 10.2 ± 8.6 years). A positive association was also observed between age and PAID-5 scores, t (334) = 3.90, p < 0.001, suggesting that as age increased, so did diabetes-related distress, with participants scoring below 8 averaging 61.3 years, and those scoring above 8 averaging 55.1 years. Gender analysis revealed a significant association between gender and PAID-5 scores using chi-square analysis, χ2(1, N = 336) = 7.83, p = 0.005, with 34.1% of females scoring above 8 versus 20.4% of males, indicating a higher level of diabetes-related distress among females.

DMSES

DMSES scale assesses self-efficacy in diabetes management, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. DMSES scores were positively associated with diabetes complications, t (334) = 2.64, p = 0.009, with those experiencing complications averaging a score of 92.3 ± 29.7 compared to 94.3 ± 27.1 for those without complications. A negative association was observed between the history of diabetes and DMSES scores, t (334) = − 5.01, p < 0.001, with participants having a history of diabetes reporting lower self-efficacy (M = 93.9 ± 28.1) compared to those without (M = 97.4 ± 33.0). Employment status was not significantly related to DMSES scores, t (334) = − 1.13, p = 0.25.

WHO-5

Treatment type showed a significant association with WHO-5 scores, χ2 (3, N = 336) = 10.89, p = 0.01. Patients on oral hypoglycemics and insulin scored lowest on the WHO-5 scale (43.1%), while diet-only, insulin-only, and combined treatment groups had WHO-5 scores that were closer to one another.

Pearson correlation analysis

Pearson correlation tests explored the relationships between continuous variables and DMSES scores, revealing a weak negative correlation between age and DMSES scores, r = − 0.188, p = 0.034, indicating a trend of decreasing self-efficacy in diabetes self-management with increasing age. This finding implies that older individuals tended to have lower self-efficacy in managing their diabetes.

Influence of treatment type on DMSES scores

A one-way ANOVA indicated a significant difference in DMSES scores across treatment types, F (3,332) = 2.74, p = 0.043. Post hoc analysis identified a significant mean difference of 11.764 between the ‘Oral hypoglycemics only’ and ‘Oral hypoglycemics and insulin’ groups (p = 0.034), with the former reporting higher DMSES scores. This finding highlights the influence of treatment regimen on self-efficacy in diabetes self-management.

Non-significant associations

There were no significant associations between BMI and PAID-5 scores, nor between BMI, diabetes duration, or age and WHO-5 scores. Additionally, no significant relationships were found between gender and DMSES scores, nor between working status and any outcome measures, indicating no significant impact of these variables on the outcomes measured in this study.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the relationship between diabetes distress, health outcomes, and self-efficacy in individuals with Type 2 diabetes in Syria. Our findings provide crucial insights into the mental and emotional states of these patients, revealing generally low psychological well-being, high diabetes distress, and notable self-efficacy in managing the condition. Our results indicate that the participants experienced significant diabetes distress and demonstrated varying levels of self-efficacy. Specifically, we found that longer duration of diabetes and advancing age were associated with higher diabetes distress, while higher self-efficacy was linked to certain demographic factors such as female gender and type of diabetes treatment. Notably, insulin therapy was associated with higher diabetes distress and lower well-being, while a diet-only regimen was linked to better well-being. One important aspect that affects the management of diabetes and the quality of life is diabetes distress, which refers to the emotional and psychological challenges of managing the disease. Factors like personal, socio-demographic, and cultural influences can impact the occurrence and severity of diabetes distress. Addressing these factors is essential to improving the psychological health of patients [9–11]. Effective self-management, such as maintaining a balanced diet, regular physical activity, and monitoring glucose levels, is crucial in managing the disease. Adhering to prescribed medications is equally important. However, some patients find it difficult to maintain these practices, which can have a greater impact on their well-being than the disease itself [12, 13]. This study is the first to investigate the relationship between diabetes distress, health outcomes, and self-efficacy in individuals with Type 2 diabetes in Syria. Our research aims to improve the well-being of diabetic patients and their quality of life through increased awareness and education on effective disease management. We conducted a cross-sectional study using various scales to achieve these objectives.

Our findings reveal a generally low psychological state among the participants, alongside high diabetes distress and notable self-efficacy in managing the condition. These insights are invaluable for understanding the mental and emotional states of individuals with Type 2 diabetes in Syria. The observed correlation between higher self-efficacy scores and factors such as the duration of diabetes, age, and female gender is consistent with prior research, affirming these relationships [10, 14]. An average PAID-5 score of 5 serves as a benchmark for gauging the level of diabetes-related distress within our study group. Scores exceeding 8 suggest the presence of significant distress. The disparity in PAID-5 scores between genders, particularly the higher scores among females (34.1%) compared to males (20.4%), underscores the potential impact of socio-demographic, biological, and cultural factors on the experience of diabetes distress. These elements warrant further exploration as they can profoundly influence an individual’s management of diabetes and their emotional response to the disease. Our analysis suggests that increased diabetes-related distress correlates with longer diabetes duration and advancing age. This may be due to deteriorating physical health, social isolation, and the onset of diabetic neuropathy. These factors can inform healthcare providers in pinpointing risk factors and crafting specific interventions. Prior research has established a significant link between diabetes-related distress and HbA1c levels. An Egyptian cross-sectional study supports the idea that higher distress levels could reflect poorer glycemic control, reinforcing the concept that psychological factors can affect physical health outcomes in diabetes patients [15]. The study’s examination of self-efficacy in diabetes self-management sheds light on the determinants of patients’ ability to manage their condition effectively. An average DMSES score of 94 signifies a notable level of self-efficacy in diabetes self-management. The treatment regimen type was influential in self-efficacy levels, with the ‘Oral hypoglycemic’ group showing higher DMSES scores, emphasizing the importance of manageable treatment options in enhancing patients’ confidence in their diabetes management. The relationship between insulin therapy and higher PAID-5 scores, coupled with lower WHO-5 scores, highlights the emotional and psychological burdens often linked to insulin treatments. These observations are in line with existing literature and point to the necessity for interventions focused on the emotional health of patients on insulin therapy [6, 16, 17]. Furthermore, the study found significant associations between diabetes-related complications and higher DMSES scores, suggesting that individuals with complications might gain increased self-awareness and confidence in managing their condition. Conversely, negative correlations were observed between DMSES scores and both age and disease history. Older patients with a long-standing history of diabetes showed lower self-efficacy in self-management. Factors such as ineffective communication with healthcare providers, insufficient family support, limited knowledge about the disease, restricted treatment options, and the presence of physical and cognitive impairments have been proposed as contributing factors in prior studies. Our findings highlight the potential impact of motivation, or lack thereof, on self-efficacy, particularly as patients grow older and potentially become more burdened by their condition. In our research, the predominant demographic comprised elderly adults undergoing treatment with oral hypoglycemic agents, many of whom also presented with diabetes complications and a history of the condition. This demographic makeup contributed to a relatively high overall Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES) score. Interestingly, the duration of living with diabetes did not show a statistically significant correlation with DMSES scores, which deviates from the anticipated patterns of self-efficacy based on age and disease history. These findings challenge some key evidence from an Italian simulation study, which indicated higher self-efficacy in patients over 65 years of age and lower self-efficacy among those with a prolonged history of diabetes [18].

These findings align with prior research that underscores the impact of diabetes distress on both psychological and physical health outcomes. For instance, our results corroborate studies showing that increased distress correlates with poorer glycemic control and reduced well-being [19, 20]. The observed gender differences in PAID-5 scores, with higher distress among females, are consistent with other studies highlighting the role of socio-demographic factors in diabetes distress [21].

Similarly, our study deviated from expected patterns by showing a significant negative correlation between the duration of diabetes and diabetes self-efficacy. This indicates that individuals with a longer history of diabetes tend to have lower self-efficacy in managing their condition [22].

Our evaluation of overall health status, as measured by the World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5), offers critical insights into the well-being of individuals with Type 2 diabetes involved in the study. An average WHO-5 score of 40 suggests suboptimal well-being levels among participants. Notably, there was a correlation between WHO-5 scores and the types of treatment received. Patients on oral hypoglycemic agents and insulin therapy had the lowest WHO-5 scores, indicating that these treatment regimens might adversely affect psychological well-being. Conversely, participants adhering to a diet-only regimen or combining insulin with oral hypoglycemic agents exhibited higher WHO-5 scores. The diet-only approach consistently correlated with improved well-being, aligning with the Italian study’s findings, which posited a significant positive relationship between lifestyle management and WHO-5 scores [18]. Given that the majority of participants were treated with oral hypoglycemic agents, the low overall WHO-5 score points to poor psychological well-being within this group. Prior research by Visalpuu et al., 2019, and Hajos et al., 2013, indicates that elevated levels of diabetes-related distress correlate with reduced well-being [23, 24]. Therefore, addressing diabetes-related distress is crucial for enhancing the psychological well-being of patients. Interestingly, our study did not find significant associations between diabetes complications and WHO-5 scores, suggesting that while diabetes-related distress affects overall well-being, the presence of complications may not directly predict psychological health.

In our investigation, gender showed no significant relationship with Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES) or World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5) scores, and Body Mass Index (BMI) did not correlate with Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID-5), DMSES, or WHO-5 scores. This indicates that these demographic and physiological factors did not have a direct impact on diabetes self-efficacy, overall well-being, or diabetes-related distress within our study. Nonetheless, it is important to note that previous studies have identified a link between glycemic control in individuals with Type 2 diabetes and fluctuations in weight. Our findings have significant practical implications. Identifying demographics that may benefit from enhanced psychological support and intensive diabetes self-management efforts, such as older women with a long-standing history of diabetes on oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin, is crucial. This knowledge can help healthcare providers develop targeted interventions and support programs tailored to these groups’ unique needs. It is essential to consider the contributing factors highlighted when designing public health awareness campaigns and self-management strategies for patients with diabetes mellitus. Customizing educational materials and support programs to address the specific requirements and challenges of these subgroups can improve diabetes management efforts’ success.

Implications for practice

Our findings suggest several practical implications for diabetes management. The significant association between insulin therapy and increased diabetes distress emphasizes the need for targeted psychological support for patients on insulin. Similarly, the higher self-efficacy scores observed with oral hypoglycemic treatments suggest that simpler management regimens might improve patients’ confidence in managing their diabetes.

Healthcare providers should consider these factors when designing interventions. Tailoring support and education to address the emotional and psychological challenges faced by patients, especially those on insulin or with long-standing diabetes, could enhance overall diabetes management. Additionally, the lack of significant associations with BMI and other demographic factors implies that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be effective; individualized care strategies are essential.

Future research directions

Future research should further investigate the complex relationships between treatment types, diabetes distress, and self-efficacy. Longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how these factors interact over time and impact long-term health outcomes. Additionally, exploring the specific mechanisms through which insulin therapy and other factors affect psychological well-being could lead to more targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The study found that patients had low psychological state, high diabetes distress, and good self-efficacy in managing diabetes. These results emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach to diabetes management, including addressing emotional well-being, lifestyle changes, and effective disease control. The findings provide practical implications for healthcare professionals and public health practitioners, particularly in identifying specific groups that require additional support. Understanding these factors are crucial for promoting patients’ psychological well-being and overall quality of life.
