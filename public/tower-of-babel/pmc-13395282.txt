Therapeutic Efficacy and Safety of Oral Cyclosporine and that of Oral Prednisolone in the Management of Refractory Oral Lichen Planus: A Comparative Study
Authors: Adithya Prakash, Ajaya Kumar Jena, Anil Kumar Panda, Maitreyee Panda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic autoimmune disorder of the oral mucosa that often requires long-term treatment due to its relapsing nature. While corticosteroids remain the first-line therapy, refractory cases necessitate alternative treatments. Cyclosporine, an immunosuppressant, has shown potential efficacy in OLP management, but lacks comparative studies with oral prednisolone, a commonly used systemic corticosteroid.Aim and Objective:To compare the efficacy and safety of oral cyclosporine versus oral prednisolone in managing refractory oral lichen planus.Patients and Methods:A prospective, randomized, controlled trial was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in eastern India, enrolling patients with refractory OLP. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either oral cyclosporine or oral prednisolone. Efficacy was evaluated using improvement in the oral disease severity score (ODSS), dermatology life quality index (DLQI), and visual analogue scale (VAS), while safety was assessed by monitoring adverse events, laboratory markers, and patient tolerance.Results:Both treatment groups showed significant improvement in ODSS and DLQI scores (P < 0.05). The cyclosporine group had a 92.7% reduction in ODSS compared to 88.8% in prednisolone group(P=0.03), while DLQI improved by 84.8% versus 78.08% (P=0.04). Prednisolone was associated with higher rates of hyperglycemia and hypertension, whereas cyclosporine posed a risk of nephrotoxicity and relapses.Limitations:A short follow-up period and a small sample size were limitations.Conclusion:To conclude, cyclosporine appears to be an effective and safe alternative to prednisolone, warranting further studies with a larger sample size and extended follow-up.

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease affecting the oral mucosa, with a prevalence of 0.5% to 2.6%.[1] It predominantly affects middle-aged females and clinically presents as reticular, plaque-like, or painful erosive and bullous forms, often leading to a poor quality of life.[2] The disease is primarily a CD8+ cytotoxic T cell-mediated chronic inflammation-induced degeneration of basal keratinocytes, manifesting clinically in characteristic mucosal lesions. Psychological stress, systemic medications, dental restoration materials, and chronic infections such as hepatitis C have been implicated as etiological triggers.[3] The disease remains amenable to topical or intralesional corticosteroids in most patients, while others may respond to topical tacrolimus (calcineurin inhibitor) or retinoids.[4] However, OLP can sometimes be refractory to these standard treatments, necessitating alternative systemic therapy. Oral prednisolone, for its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, is frequently used in such cases. However, its long-term use is often associated with adverse effects such as hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, weight gain, hypertension, and an increased risk of infections.[5] Although concerns regarding nephrotoxicity and high cost precludes systemic use of cyclosporine, it has emerged as a promising alternative treatment modality. It selectively inhibits T-cell activation and cytokine production, thereby reducing inflammation and lesional severity, effectively alleviating symptoms in refractory OLP.[6] However, not many studies are available comparing the efficacy and safety of cyclosporine and prednisolone in the treatment of refractory OLP. In the present study, we assessed the comparative efficacy and safety of oral cyclosporine and oral prednisolone in treating patients with refractory OLP.

Patients and Methods

This study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in eastern India. It was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Institiute of Medical Sciences and SUM hospital (IEC/IMS.SH/SOA/2022/458) on 10th December, 2022 and registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2023/03/050453). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits of the study, and assuring them of data confidentiality.

Study design

The study was an open-label randomized controlled trial comparing the efficacy and safety of oral cyclosporine versus oral prednisolone in patients with refractory OLP. The study was conducted over a period of 12 months, including patient enrolment, treatment administration, follow-up, and data analysis.

Sample size

The prevalence of OLP in our outpatient clinic is approximately six cases per month. Over a 12-month study period, the total population size was 79. Using online Raosoft software (2004, Raosoft, Inc.), a required sample size of 66 subjects was calculated with a confidence interval of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, which means 33 patients in each of the two groups.

Selection and description of participants

Patients aged 18 to 60 years with clinically diagnosed and biopsy-confirmed OLP [Figures 1a and 1b] who were refractory to treatment and had persistent symptoms with an oral disease severity score (ODSS) 30 or greater were included in the study. Refractory OLP was defined based on the lack of response to standard first-line treatment with topical corticosteroids, tacrolimus, or retinoids, given sequentially or intermittently for at least three months.[7]

Pregnant and lactating women and patients with extraoral lichen planus, lichenoid drug reactions, or other mucosal lesions mimicking OLP, active or recurrent infections, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hepatorenal, cardiovascular, hematologic, or endocrine disorders were excluded. Additionally, individuals with a history of smoking, tobacco or betel nut chewing or those taking medications known to trigger OLP were also excluded from the study.

Treatment protocol and Therapeutic outcome measures

Out of the 66 cases screened, six were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample size of 60 [Flowchart 1]. Participants who met the eligibility criteria were randomly assigned either to Group 1 for cyclosporine treatment or to Group 2 for prednisolone treatment. Randomization was conducted using a computer-generated randomization sequence to ensure allocation concealment and minimize selection bias. Group 1 patients received oral cyclosporine at a dose of 3.5 mg/kg/day, but not exceeding 300 mg/day in two divided doses. Group 2 patients were given oral prednisolone at 0.5 mg/kg/day up to a maximum dose of 40 mg/day as a single morning dose after breakfast. The treatment was continued for 12 weeks (three months). The patients were followed up every four weeks during the treatment period and thereafter over the next 24 weeks (six months treatment free follow up period) to assess the long-term efficacy and safety of the treatment.

Study design and participant flow

The outcome measures for clinical efficacy included reduction in ODSS comprising site score, activity score and pain score with a maximum score of 106 [Table 1],[8] decrease in intensity of pain/burning sensation measured on visual analog scale (VAS), which has a maximum score of 10,[9] and improved dermatology life quality index (DLQI) by using a previously validated questionnaire having 10 questions to measure the impact of skin diseases on a patient’s quality of life.[10] DLQI scores ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater impairment. All enrolled patients were assessed using these tools at baseline and at every visit until the end of the study period. Pre- and post-treatment photographic comparison was also made to assess and corroborate the therapeutic response independently by two assessors (AKP, MP) who were unaware of the treatment given. The final scores were calculated by taking the average of the scores by the two assessors.

The outcome measures of safety included monitoring for adverse effects and blood pressure monitoring, complete blood counts, liver and renal function tests, lipid profile, and blood glucose estimation, performed at baseline and at each follow-up visit. Patients experiencing severe adverse effects were withdrawn from the study and provided appropriate medical intervention.

Statistical methods

Quantitative variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, whereas qualitative variables were expressed as percentage and frequency. The normality of data distribution was tested by applying Shapiro Wilk test. The results were expressed by comparing the means and standard deviations between groups by independent t-test and Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinico-demographic characteristics

Group 1 comprised 12 (40%) males and 18 (60%) females (M: F =2:3) aged between 22 and 60 years (mean ± standard deviation (SD), 43.1 ± 10.5 years). The common sites involved were buccal mucosa in 23 (76.7%), tongue in 5 (16.7%) and gingiva in 2 (6.7%) patients. The reticular OLP was observed in 15 (50%), followed by erosive OLP in 9 (30%) and atrophic variants occurred in 6 (20%) patients.

Group 2 had 12 (40%) males and 18 (60%) females aged between 21 and 59 years (mean ± SD 42.6 ± 9.8 years). The buccal mucosa was involved in 23 (76.7%), the tongue in 5 (16.7%), and 2 (6.7%) patients had gingival involvement. The reticular OLP was seen in 15 (50%), erosive OLP in 9 (30%), and atrophic variants in 6 (20%) patients [Table 2].

Efficacy outcomes

Both groups demonstrated improvement in ODSS, VAS, and DLQI scores from baseline values over 12 weeks (three months) of treatment. However, cyclosporine showed a greater reduction in ODSS and DLQI scores compared to prednisolone at 12 weeks, the end of treatment, and the difference was statistically significant [Table 3]. Both cyclosporine and prednisolone significantly decreased disease severity (ODSS) pain (VAS scores), and improved quality of life (DLQI) Scores. At 12-week follow-up, cyclosporine showed significantly greater improvement in ODSS (P = 0.045) and DLQI scores (P = 0.046) compared to prednisolone, suggesting a potentially early onset of action [Figures 2a and 2b, 3a and 3b]. However, at 36-week follow-up, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant for ODSS (P = 0.100) and DLQI (P = 0.13) scores, reflecting comparable efficacy of both treatments in the long term. Similarly, both drugs (cyclosporine vs. prednisolone) were comparable in pain reduction, with no statistically significant difference in VAS scores (P = 0.10 vs. 0.078) at 12-week and 36-week follow-ups.

Adverse effects and safety profile

Table 4 lists adverse effects observed in both groups. Prednisolone was associated with hyperglycemia and weight gain in a significantly higher proportion of patients (20% vs. 6.7%, P = 0.034, for hyperglycemia; 26.7% vs. 10%, P = 0.048, for weight gain) compared to Group 1 patients, whereas hypertension was observed more commonly with cyclosporine in Group 1 patients (16.7% vs 3.3%, P = 0.021). However, nephrotoxicity (13.3% vs. 3.3%, P = 0.09) and gastrointestinal adverse effects were not significantly different between the groups. None of the patients in both groups discontinued treatment/dropped out due to adverse effects.

Relapse

At the 20 week timepoint (i.e 2 months after completion of the 3 month treatment phase), relapses occurred in seven patients from Group 1, and only one patient in Group 2 had a relapse. By the 28-week timepoint (i.e 4 months after completion of treatment), 15 patients relapsed in Group 1, and four patients showed relapse in Group 2. The difference was statistically significant (P = 0.031), reflecting higher chances of relapse after stopping treatment with cyclosporine compared to prednisolone.

Discussion

The overall demographic and clinical characteristics of the studied subjects in both groups, predominantly middle-aged females, revealed reticular forms as the most common variants of OLP, followed by erosive and atrophic forms. Associated pain, burning, and involvement of buccal and gingival mucosae are similar to the previous reports.[11112131415]

Managing refractory OLP remains challenging due to its chronic nature and potential for malignant transformation. While systemic corticosteroids provide symptomatic relief, adverse effects limit their prolonged use. Cyclosporine, with its immunomodulatory effect, offers an effective alternative treatment modality. However, its efficacy and safety compared to prednisolone in a randomized controlled setting remain under-reported.

We observed that both drugs effectively reduced ODSS and VAS scores and improved DLQI scores. Although cyclosporine showed more rapid improvement in the early phase, notably at 12 weeks of treatment (the third follow-up) in Group 1, on a long-term basis, the patients in both groups achieved comparable outcomes at 36 weeks of treatment (sixth month of treatment free follow up). Apparently, systemic cyclosporine provided rapid symptomatic relief in OLP within 1–4 weeks and resolution of lesions in 6–8 weeks, but showed a significantly high possibility of relapse, as well as corroborating previously reported literature.[161718] The high possibility of relapse in Group 1 also suggests that the used cyclosporine doses or its pharmacokinetics perhaps do not sustain long-term remission as effectively as prednisolone does. Although cyclosporine may be more suitable for patients who require quick symptomatic control or are corticosteroid-intolerant, prednisolone may provide more consistent long-term disease stability.

Group 2 patients showed a higher prevalence of prednisolone related effects, such as hyperglycemia and weight gain, compared to cyclosporine-induced hypertension, weight gain, and mild gastrointestinal disturbances in fewer patients in Group 1, not requiring treatment discontinuation, which reflects the comparative safety of both drugs. However, cyclosporine-induced nephrotoxicity was exclusive to Group 1 patients, emphasizing the necessity of regular monitoring of renal function in these patients. Minimal weight gain from cyclosporine could have been from mild fluid retention. The findings of the present study were compared with existing literature on the efficacy and treatment outcomes of cyclosporine and prednisolone in OLP, as summarized in Supplementary File 1.[619]

Limitations

Single-center study, small number of patients in each group, limited treatment period, and lack of long-term follow-up remain major limitations.

Conclusion

Both cyclosporine and prednisolone were effective in improving ODSS, VAS, and DLQI scores, indicating significant improvement in disease severity, pain, and quality of life. A higher possibility of nephrotoxicity and early relapse in a few patients treated with cyclosporine can preclude its routine use in clinical practice. However, cyclosporine can be preferred for its early onset and rapid symptomatic relief compared to prednisolone. Nevertheless, prednisolone remains a standard first-line therapy while cyclosporine may be preferable for patients at risk of steroid-induced complications. Better-designed prospective studies addressing the above limitations are advocated to make any recommendations.
