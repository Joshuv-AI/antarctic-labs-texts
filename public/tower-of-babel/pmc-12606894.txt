Early versus delayed enteral nutrition in critically ill children under 12 years of age: an economic evaluation
Authors: Gerald Manthalu, Talitha Mpando, Tamara Kredo, Pamela Vorster, Dachi Arikpo, Amanda Brand, Funeka Bango, Nyanyiwe Mbeye, Celeste Naude, Lungiswa Nkonki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNutritional support is an integral part of treating critically ill children in paediatric intensive care units (PICUs). Early enteral nutrition (EEN) in the PICU has been shown to have greater benefits compared to delayed enteral nutrition (DEN) in reducing PICU and hospital stays and lowering mortality. In this study, we conducted a cost comparison and cost-effectiveness analysis of early versus delayed enteral nutrition during PICU and hospital stays for children aged 1 month to 12 years in Malawi.MethodsWe used primary and secondary data to cost PICU and hospital admissions from a payer perspective. We developed a stochastic model that assumed that equal cohorts of 500 critically ill children were admitted to the PICU and provided with EEN and DEN. Using the average length of stay in the PICU and hospital and cost data, we estimated total cohort costs and the average cost per patient for each strategy. We estimated disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and used the total hospital costs to estimate the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results.ResultsThe total cohort cost for EEN in the PICU was lower, $479,850, than that for DEN, $515,623. The total cohort costs for the hospital stay were also lower for EEN, $564,290, than for DEN, $613,902. The average cost per patient for EEN in the PICU was lower at $960 than $1031 for DEN. The average cost per patient for the hospital was also lower for EEN, $1129, than for DEN, $1228. EEN dominated DEN and the ICER was estimated as –$39.47/DALY averted. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that EEN had a greater probability of being cost-effective, for a capacity to pay or cost-effectiveness threshold range of 0$-2000$/DALY averted, than DEN. Scaling up the implementation of EEN led to higher net monetary and health benefits.ConclusionEEN in children aged 1 month to 12 years was found to be less costly and more cost-effective than DEN in Malawi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00668-y.Keywords: Early enteral nutrition, Delayed enteral nutrition, Length of stay, Mortality, Cost, Cost-effectiveness, Paediatric intensive care unit, Hospital, Malawi

Introduction

Nutritional support is an integral part of treating critically ill children in paediatric intensive care [1]. It aims to address malnutrition that may be present in the child on admission to the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) or may develop during the PICU stay due to the metabolic effects caused by sepsis, shock and inflammation [2, 3]. The prevalence of malnutrition among critically ill children in a low-income setting was estimated at 37% (95% confidence interval (CI); 36%–39%) [4]. Malnutrition in the PICU is associated with a longer PICU length of stay (LoS) and a higher risk of mortality [5, 6].

Enteral nutrition (EN) is the preferred method for nutrition delivery in critically ill children with a functional gastrointestinal tract [7, 8]. Defined as the delivery of a nutritionally complete feed via a feeding tube into the stomach, duodenum, or jejunum, enteral nutrition meets the patient’s nutritional needs (including metabolic changes and additional needs in response to illness) [9]. A key consideration for EN delivery is the timing of initiation, along with other factors, such as route, volume and type of feed. Compared with delayed initiation, early initiation has been shown by studies and syntheses to be beneficial, and some guidelines currently recommend early administration of EN in hemodynamically stable children [10, 11].

Systematic reviews have assessed the effects of early enteral nutrition (EEN) and delayed enteral nutrition (DEN) on many outcomes, including PICU and hospital LoS and mortality. A systematic review and meta-analysis, connected to this study, that included four studies, reported a mean difference of 0.59 days (95% CI: 3.62, 2.45) in the average PICU LoS between EEN and DEN for 139 children aged one month to 12 years [12]. They further reported a mean difference of 2.98 days (95% CI:9.79, 3.83) in the average hospital LoS between EEN and DEN for 760 children. Two further reviews [13, 14] compared EEN with DEN in children 0–18 years following gastrointestinal surgery and reported that EEN participants had a shorter hospital stay than did the participants in the DEN group, with a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 2.07 days, (95% CI: 3.02 to 1.12), p < 0.001, and a mean difference (MD) of 2.95 days; (95% CI 3.73 to 2.17); p < 0.00001, respectively. Seven randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comprising 278 cases and 10 studies comprising 1286 cases were included in the two reviews, respectively. EEN significantly shortened the hospital stay in children aged 10 days to 6.5 years in a review that analysed 488 children from six trials [15]. In terms of mortality, the risk ratio of mortality between the EEN and DEN groups for children aged one month to 12 years was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.49, 0.93) for 869 cases in the systematic review connected to this study [12]. We found a limited number of systematic reviews on EEN and DEN in children and the population sizes included in the reviews were variable, as was the certainty of the evidence. Overall, however, the evidence suggests that EEN may offer benefits in reducing PICU and hospital stays and lowering mortality rates in children.

Currently, there is no formal policy in Malawi on early or delayed initiation of enteral nutrition for critically ill children. While delayed feeding does not appear to be routine practice, clinicians who participated in the stakeholder consultation of the project, reported that the timing of initiation may vary because of the variation in the knowledge and experience of health workers and the availability of a dietician. The effect of the timing of enteral nutrition on morbidity and mortality in critically ill children under 12 years of age was hence identified as a priority topic for guidance in Malawi as part of the Global Evidence, Local Adaptation (GELA) project. The GELA project aimed to produce evidence-informed, locally relevant guideline recommendations for newborn and child health in Malawi, Nigeria, and South Africa [16]. Evidence-informed health guidelines are essential for enhancing care quality, guiding funding decisions, and improving access to healthcare services. They provide recommendations on clinical, public health or health system actions for improving health outcomes. Such guidelines must be developed using transparent, structured processes and must be based on a comprehensive assessment of the best available evidence [17–19].

Developing new evidence-informed guidelines is a resource-intensive process; therefore, GELA’s approach was to adopt, adapt or create contextualized recommendations from source guidelines and evidence syntheses, where possible, instead of developing new guidelines and conducting new evidence synthesis, which was only done in the absence of suitable source guidelines and syntheses [20]. GELA processes and methods were based on GRADE-ADOLOPMENT [19], an evidence-to-decision (EtD) framework-based approach that explores whether existing guidelines and/or evidence that are relevant and of high quality can be adopted or adapted for the context of interest. The GRADE EtD framework is used to inform structured and transparent decisions across various framework criteria, such as the anticipated benefits and harms, as well as the economic implications of the intervention of interest and making judgments based on the evidence related to each of these criteria [21]. A GELA adolopment algorithm [20], informed by the GRADE-ADOLOPMENT approach, was applied to the guideline question on the timing of enteral nutrition initiation to identify and assess the available effectiveness, qualitative, and economic evidence required for the various EtD framework criteria. Here, we describe the approach for the economic evidence specifically.

For the economic evidence, we first searched for an appropriate source guideline on EEN in critically ill children. No suitable guideline was identified. We therefore searched for relevant published economic evidence by conducting an extensive search for systematic reviews of economic evaluations or primary economic evaluations in MEDLINE (PubMed), the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Embase, Scopus, the National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database/Health Technology Assessments (NHS EDD/HTA), CINAHL, and Google Scholar. We retrieved 229 records but did not identify any appropriate published evidence that aligned directly with the PICO question. The scoping review did, however, identify two economic evaluations that were informative for conceptualizing our approach [22, 23], even though these did not have direct utility owing to their evaluation of costs in adult populations in high-income settings.

As required by the GELA algorithm and resulting from the lack of context-relevant published economic evidence, there was the need to conduct a new economic evaluation study. This study aimed to compare the costs of EEN and DEN in the PICU and during the entire hospitalization period, and to conduct a cost-effectiveness analysis of early versus delayed enteral nutrition in the PICU for children aged 1 month to 12 years, which is specific for Malawi.

Methods

Setting and design

The context of the study was the Malawi health care system, which is organized at three levels: tertiary, secondary and primary [24]. Resource use data for the PICU were obtained from Kamuzu Central Hospital (KCH), the tertiary health facility for the central region. KCH was selected due to its close proximity to where the research team was based. KCH is a 900-bed tertiary hospital in Lilongwe city serving as the referral hospital for the nine districts of the central region of Malawi, which have a total estimated population of 8,953,649 in 2025 [24]. KCH has a paediatric department that has approximately 215 beds spread over several wards and admits more than 27,000 children per year [25, 26]. In addition, KCH has a five-bed mixed medical and surgical ICU for both paediatric and adult patients [25]. A prospective observational study conducted at KCH from August 2016 to May 2018 reported that of the 499 patients who were admitted to the ICU during that period, 105 (21%) were paediatric patients (≤ 18 years) [26]. For the estimation of hospital costs, data were obtained from a study [27], conducted at the Queen Elizabeth central hospital (QECH) in Blantyre city, in the southern region of Malawi. QECH is comparable to KCH in size as it has a bed capacity of 1350 beds and is a referral hospital for the thirteen districts of the southern region, which have a total estimated population of 9,145,394 in 2025. At any one time, 200–350 paediatric in-patients are hospitalized [27].

Model structure

We conducted cost and cost-effectiveness analyses based on a Monte Carlo simulation (N = 100,000 draws) of a 500-patient per arm stochastic model [22]. The key feature of a stochastic model is that it draws random values for model variables from assigned probability distributions of the variables and predicts values of outcome variables of interest. In Fig. 1, the model assumes equal cohorts of 500 critically ill children who need PICU support under each strategy. The cohort size could be any number because in cohort simulation the same results will emerge for any size of starting cohort as cohort simulation involves multiplying the proportion of the cohort ending in a particular state by the relevant transition probability to derive the proportion starting in another state [28]. Then, PICU and hospital LoS and probability of death variables from the meta-analysis connected to this study [12] were applied to each cohort to estimate, by intervention, the total number of days patients receiving EEN and DEN spent in the PICU and in the hospital, as well as the total deaths in the EEN and DEN groups. DALYs were calculated using the hospital LoS, disability weight, mortality and remaining life expectancy data. On the cost side, the total PICU/hospital LoS was then multiplied by PICU/hospital costs to obtain their respective total cohort costs. Finally, using these outcome and cost estimates, the following outcomes of interest were calculated: the average PICU/hospital cost per patient by intervention (EEN or DEN), the net monetary benefit and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). The ICER was calculated by dividing the difference in costs between the two treatments by the difference in their effectiveness (DALYs), as shown in Eq. (1). The model was developed in Microsoft Excel and utilized Visual Basic for Applications (VBA).

Cost estimation

We took the payer perspective for the costing, in which the payer was the Ministry of Health of Malawi. The time horizon of the study was one month. We used the ingredients approach to estimate costs for all inputs that were used to provide enteral nutrition in the PICU, including human resources, enteral feeds, medical supplies, protective wear, medical equipment and facilities. The US dollar was used for the costing and costs in local currency (Malawi Kwacha) were converted to the 2023 average annual US dollar exchange rate of 1 USD to MWK1046.77 [29]. We aimed to estimate the average cost per patient of providing enteral nutrition in the PICU and the average cost per patient of the entire hospital stay. The average cost per patient comprised once-off costs and daily PICU/hospital management costs. The average cost per patient for the PICU/hospital would differ between EEN and DEN because of the different lengths of stay, as established by the evidence. We consulted with paediatricians and ICU staff at KCH to understand the process of enteral feeding at a Malawian tertiary hospital and what inputs are used. We estimated hospital costs by extrapolating costs in a study conducted at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH) in Blantyre, Malawi [30], because there were no KCH specific hospital costs. These costs were adjusted for inflation by using the consumer price indices for the years 2020 and 2023, the years of the QECH costing study and this study, respectively. We used the average LoS in the QECH costing study to estimate daily costs for staff and facilities but assumed that medicines and laboratory costs were once-off costs per patient. The once-off and daily hospital costs were assumed to be the same for both EEN and DEN. Additional file 1 provides the details of the costing approach.

We used mortality and hospital LoS estimates from the meta-analysis linked to this study [12] to estimate DALYs associated with each of the interventions. DALYs, which are the sum of years of life lost (YLL) and years lived with disability (YLD), were calculated using the formulas (2) and (3):

For YLL, we considered that deaths would occur across the entire relevant age range of 1 month to 12 years, with more deaths occurring at younger ages. This implied using the different values of the remaining life expectancy according to the relevant age bands. We therefore used four different values of life expectancy for the following age groups: <1 year, 1–4 years, 5–9 years and 10–14 years, as calculated by the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [32]. Malawi-specific life expectancy figures from the IHME were used. We weighted the average remaining life expectancy for each age band by the proportion of sick children expected to be in the age band. The weighted average remaining life expectancy was then multiplied by the number of deaths in the cohort to estimate the total years of life lost for each strategy.

To estimate years lived with disability, we used multiple disability weights to account for the different causes of hospital admission [33]. We used disability weights for health states that mapped most closely to the causes of admission reported in [27] (Table 1). We then multiplied the proportion of sick children for each cause of hospital admission, as shown in Table 1, by the cohort size (500 for each strategy) to disaggregate the cohort size by cause of admission. The aim was to obtain a more accurate estimate of YLL by multiplying a disability weight by the estimated number of children who would suffer from a particular condition. The values of hospital LoS were used for in Eq. (3) on the assumption that the hospital stay period was equal to the complete sickness period. Since represents years lived with a disability, we divided the hospital LoS by 365 days to convert it from days to years. The result of Eq. (3) was multiplied by the number of sick children for the specific cause to obtain YLD per cause of admission. The YLD per cause of admission was then summed to obtain the total YLD for each strategy. Finally, YLL was added to YLD to obtain DALYs for the cohort for each treatment strategy. We represented DALYs as a negative number consistent with the concept of aversion for DALYs [34].

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis

We conducted deterministic sensitivity analyses on two variables that were deemed to be the key cost drivers as follows: (i) the distribution of children across weight bands—we increased the proportion of children falling into the lower weight bands in the cohorts, which implied more younger children and, by implication, possibly more malnourished children; (ii) the mean difference in the average stay in the PICU and in the hospital; we conducted a multi-way analysis of the two variables with the first scenario using the lower bounds of the two variables and the second scenario using the upper bounds.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

One-way or two-way sensitivity analyses are limited in that they do not capture interactions between multiple parameters and are not practical for complex models [28]. We therefore, conducted probabilistic sensitivity analyses where distributions were assigned to variables whose values were estimated from multiple sources or whose values were retrieved from the literature with their measures of uncertainty, as shown in Table 2 [28]. Table 2 shows that the gamma distribution was allocated to LoS variables to ensure that only nonnegative values were drawn. The gamma distribution was also assigned to the cost and DALY weights. We then conducted 100,000 random draws, each time estimating the net monetary benefit as the outcome of primary interest for both EEN and DEN.

The Net Monetary Benefit (NMB) was calculated as , where is the cost-effectiveness threshold (CET) [28, 34]. The CET is defined as the opportunity cost of allocating resources to a given intervention in terms of the health benefits displaced or not materialized because of the unavailability of resources to finance other alternatives competing for the same budget [36]. The CET for Malawi was estimated at $65.8/DALY averted in 2022 [37]. Using the net monetary benefit statistic, we estimated the probability that each of the strategies would be cost-effective at different values of the cost-effectiveness threshold, ranging from 10$/DALY averted to $2000/DALY averted, and summarized this through cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs).

Discounting of costs and outcomes

No discounting was performed for the PICU and hospital costs or patient outcomes since their respective time horizons were a few days and up to a month, respectively. For DALY calculations, the YLL and YLD formulas embed a discount rate of 3%.

Value of implementation analysis

Implementation of guidelines by hospitals can vary due to patient-level or hospital factors [38]. In low-income settings, hospital factors mostly derive from limited resources, in addition to organisational and logistical challenges. Therefore, this study estimated the expected value to the health system, in terms of NMB and net DALYs averted, of implementing EEN at different levels. Studies have shown close estimates of the percentage of patients who received EEN: 71% [39], 69% [40], 67% [41] and 60% [35]. We therefore looked at two implementation scenarios: (i) 70% of a hypothetical population of 10,000 critically ill children receiving EEN based on the preceding literature (Scenario 1), and (ii) 95% of the 10,000 hypothetical population of critically ill children receiving EEN based on consultations with clinicians at KCH, who indicated that DEN was not routine practice (Scenario 2). We calculated the NMB and net health benefit (NHB) per patient and multiplied them by the population of critically ill children accessing EEN for each coverage scenario to obtain the total NHB and total NMB for each coverage scenario [38]. NMB was calculated at the patient level as , whereas NHB was calculated as [28, 34]. Then the value of implementation was calculated by multiplying the population in need of EEN by the NMB and NHB.

Cost-effectiveness

Table 5 shows the cost-effectiveness results for both the base case and the deterministic analysis scenarios. EEN is associated with less DALYs, 2,673, while DEN is associated with more DALYs, 3,930, indicating that EEN is more effective than DEN. EEN is less costly than DEN, $564,290 v $613,902. Therefore, EEN strictly dominates DEN i.e., it is less costly and more effective than DEN. The ICER is estimated as $39.47/DALY averted showing that EEN is cost saving. Based on these findings, EEN is the preferred option.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis

Table 5 shows the results of the deterministic sensitivity analyses. The first scenario, which involved changing the distribution of children in the weight bands to increase the proportions in the lower weight bands maintained the strict dominance of EEN over DEN with an ICER of $29.14/DALY averted. The second scenario, which involved using the lower bounds of the mean differences in LoS for both the PICU and the hospital, also maintained the strict dominance of EEN with an ICER of $210.45/DALY averted. The third scenario, which involved using the upper bounds of the mean differences in LoS for both the PICU and the hospital, yielded higher costs for EEN than DEN and less DALYs for EEN than DEN. The ICER was estimated as $132.56/DALY averted.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

Table 4 shows the costing results from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis. In contrast to the base case scenario, these results are the means of 100,000 random simultaneous draws from the distributions of the parameters indicated in Table 2. The results of the probabilistic sensitivity analysis were plotted as CEACs on a log scale for the threshold range $10-$2000. The results show that EEN is likely more cost-effective than DEN at all levels of the cost-effectiveness threshold (CET) when all the uncertainty associated with the model parameters is considered.

Value of implementation

Table 6 shows the results of the two scale-up scenarios of EEN. The table first presents NMB and NHB per patient at $265 and 4.02 DALYs averted, respectively. The values of implementation in terms of both the NMB and NHB increase as EEN is scaled up i.e., moving from scenario 1 to scenario 2. Subtracting the value of implementation for scenario 1 from that of scenario 2 yields $661,551, which is the value of increasing EEN implementation from 70% to 95%. In NHB terms, the value of increasing EEN implementation from 70% to 95% is 10,054 DALYs averted.

Discussion

We conducted a cost comparison and cost-effectiveness analysis of early versus delayed enteral nutrition in children aged 1 month to 12 years and in a low-income setting. This addresses a gap in available cost-effectiveness analysis of EEN and DEN for children in a low-income setting. The only relevant cost-effectiveness analysis we found [22], was in adults and in the United States.

Our study found that implementing EEN was less costly than implementing DEN for the base case and various sensitivity analyses. A comparison between the average cost per patient for the PICU and the average cost per patient for the hospital for both EEN and DEN showed small absolute differences in the values despite the hospital stay being longer than the PICU stay. For example, the average cost per patient for the PICU (EEN) and the average cost per patient for the hospital (EEN) were $960 and $1,031, respectively. The reason for this is that the average daily cost of the hospital stay outside of the PICU was relatively low at $9 compared to the average daily PICU cost of $121.

EEN dominated DEN, being less costly and more effective. Deterministic sensitivity analysis revealed that when the distribution of children admitted to the PICU was skewed towards lower weight bands or younger children, EEN maintained its dominance. Using lower bounds of the mean differences in the PICU and hospital lengths of stay also maintained the dominance of EEN, while using upper bounds led to higher costs and higher benefits for EEN than DEN, with an ICER of $132.56/DALY averted. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was summarized in CEACs for both EEN and DEN in Fig. 2. The CEACs showed that the probability of EEN being cost-effective was higher than that of DEN at all levels of the CET.

Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves for EEN and DEN

This study estimated DALYs associated with EEN and DEN rather than DALYs averted by each strategy. The decision was driven by the lack of evidence on PICU and hospital length of stay and mortality outcomes associated with no enteral feeding. Moreover, a “no enteral feeding” strategy may not be feasible or ethical for patients who can tolerate enteral nutrition. Using DALYs associated with an intervention instead of DALYs averted introduces a complexity in terms of the sign of the ICER. Specifically, when DALYs associated with an intervention are used, the sign of the ICER is the opposite of that observed when using either DALYs averted or QALYs gained. For instance, a dominant strategy has a positive ICER when using DALYs associated with an intervention, whereas it is negative when using either DALYs averted or QALYs gained. To resolve this inconsistency, DALYs associated with an intervention were assigned a negative sign, consistent with the concept of aversion for DALYs [34].

Implementation of clinical practice guidelines that are based on cost-effectiveness and other evidence varies across hospitals because of patient-related and hospital-related factors. This study therefore estimated the values of two scale-up scenarios of EEN implementation. Higher EEN coverage led to higher NMB and NHB. The value of scaling up from 70% EEN to 95% EEN was estimated at $661K. This value must be compared with the cost of scale-up interventions; if the cost of scale-up interventions is higher than the NMB of scale-up, or if the opportunity cost of the scale-up intervention, in DALY terms, is higher DALYs averted than the NHB, then either the proposed scale-up level is not optimal or the scale-up interventions must be reconsidered. This comparison could not be made in this study because there was no information on possible scale-up interventions, their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness and the current utilization level of EEN. Generating such evidence would be an important future research priority.

The few studies that have done cost comparisons and cost-effectiveness analyses of EEN and DEN corroborate the findings of this study. Daily and total hospital charges were significantly lower for patients in the EEN group than for those in the DEN group [23]. In terms of cost-effectiveness analysis, there was a US$14,462 per patient saving for EEN over DEN in an adult population [22].

The key limitation of this study was data. There was a lack of comprehensive data on the case types admitted to the PICU in Malawi. As such, it was not possible to ascertain whether there was a systematic difference between patients who were provided EEN and DEN. In addition, there was a lack of age- and sex-disaggregated data for PICU admissions to account for the heterogeneity in the PICU cases. In addition, we could not estimate resource use data by condition type. To estimate DALYs, we used disability weights for a limited number of conditions due to the unavailability of data on DALY weights for other conditions in the Global Burden of Disease Collaborative Network (2020) study. Despite the lack of disaggregated data, the results were robust at the aggregate level at which the study was done. All uncertainty in all the parameters, including the disability weights for the conditions used to estimate DALYs, was accounted for and the results were robust to the sensitivity analyses. This work therefore sets the stage for determining the expected value of future research particularly addressing the data gaps.

Conclusion

This study conducted a cost comparison and cost-effectiveness analysis of EEN and DEN in the PICU for children aged 1 month to 12 years. The stochastic model was used that assumed that equal cohorts of 500 critically ill children who needed PICU support were provided with EEN and DEN. Then, costs and DALYs were calculated for each intervention. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results. The average costs per patient for the PICU and the hospital were lower for EEN than DEN at $960 versus $1,031 and $1,129 versus $1,228, respectively. The ICER for EEN was estimated at $39.47/DALY averted which showed that EEN dominated DEN. Considering all the uncertainty in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that EEN had a higher probability of being cost-effective at all the levels of the CET that were considered. The study also estimated the value of scaling up EEN to different coverage rates, which showed higher net monetary benefit and net health benefit. However, the value of scaling up would need to be compared with the cost of scale-up interventions in order to determine an optimal coverage level of EEN.
