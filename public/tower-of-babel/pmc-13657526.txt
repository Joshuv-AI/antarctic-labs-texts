Targeting neuronal pentraxin 1 microRNA-492 exerts pro-tumorigenic effects in colorectal cancer through stimulation of proliferation, migration, and invasion
Authors: Jia Yu Zhang, Qiong Zhu Yu, Wei Wei, Jia Ming Qiu, Yu Lan Gu, Chuandan Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common cancer types affecting both men and women. MicroRNA-492 (miR-492) plays an important role in the development of various malignant tumors; however, its specific role and related mechanisms in CRC remain unclear. Therefore, we aimed to explore the relationship between miR-492 and the prognosis of patients with CRC, as well as the specific mechanisms involved in CRC development.Methods:The Gene Expression Omnibus and The Cancer Genome Atlas datasets were downloaded to analyze the relationship between the miR-492 expression level and the overall survival of patients with CRC. Forty-four pairs of primary CRC tissues and paired normal tissues were collected. The relationship between the miR-492 expression level and clinicopathological parameters of patients with CRC was analyzed using statistical methods. MiRNA quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to detect miR-492 expression levels in CRC tissues and cell lines. 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide and colony-formation assays were performed to assess cell growth and proliferation, respectively. Transwell assays were performed to analyze the migration and invasion potential of CRC cells. The interaction between miR-492 and 3′ untranslated regions (3′-UTRs) of neuronal pentraxin 1 (NPTX1) was evaluated using a luciferase reporter assay. The expression of NPTX1 in CRC tissues and cells was detected by qRT-PCR.Results:Low expression of miRNA-492 in CRC tissues is closely associated with patients’ survival prognosis. miR-492 can recognize the 3′-UTR of NPTX1 mRNA and directly target and regulate NPTX1 expression, thereby promoting the growth, migration, and invasion of CRC cells.Conclusions:The ability of miR-492 to mediate the biological behavior of CRC by targeting NPTX1 is associated with poor prognosis and suggests that miR-492 is a potential therapeutic candidate for CRC.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second and third most common cancer in men and women, respectively, accounting for 9.7% of all cancer cases globally and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide[1–3]. In 2018, the International Agency for Research on Cancer reported 1.8 million new cases of CRC worldwide (approximately 10% of all cancer cases) and more than 860 000 CRC-related deaths (approximately 9% of all cancer-related deaths)[4].

Currently, metastasis is one of the most important factors for predicting the survival of patients with CRC. Compared with patients with stage II (no lymph node metastasis) CRC, those with stage III (presence of cancer cells in the draining lymph nodes of the tumor) showed a >20% reduction in the 5-year survival rate[5]. Currently, patients diagnosed with CRC tend to be younger[6], but the exact etiology needs to be clarified further. Owing to the slow progression of CRC from resectable precancerous lesions, early screening can reduce the morbidity and mortality of this malignancy, and relevant diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers will help evaluate tumorigenesis, progression, and treatment response in CRC[7]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to identify accurate biomarkers for patients with CRC.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) function as tumor suppressors or oncogenes[8], and they play a role in the occurrence and development of tumors by regulating oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, thereby affecting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells. In addition, miRNAs are regulated by oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes[9]. miR-183 is dysregulated in breast cancer, and its expression is related to the expression of estrogen and the HER2 receptor. miR-183 promotes breast cancer cell migration, invasion, and metastasis by suppressing SIN3 transcription regulator family member A[10]. The expression level of miR-122-5p is downregulated in hepatocellular cancer[11]. miR-122-5p regulates the chloride intracellular channel 1 (CLIC1) levels by degrading its transcripts. CLIC1 enhances the transcriptional activity of MYC by binding to MYC, which is closely related to the occurrence, development, and prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma[12]. miR-148a-3p is upregulated in CRC, negatively regulating KLF4 to promote proliferation, migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune escape; silencing it inhibits tumor growth and boosts CD8+ T-cell infiltration[13]. miR-21, the most studied miRNA, acts as an oncomiR by repressing phosphatase and tension homologue (PTEN), driving progression. miR-34a and miR-195-5p suppress proliferation and metastasis, with miR-195-5p showing therapeutic potential via mimics[14]. miR-196b, downregulated in cetuximab-resistant CRC, targets neuroblastoma RAS viral oncogene homolog and B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase to inhibit extracellular signal-regulated kinase and Protein kinase B pathways, enhancing drug sensitivity and serving as a predictive biomarker[15]. miR-99a reprograms fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) by targeting NLRP2, activating NF-κB, forming a miR-99a/TGF-β1 feedback loop that correlates with metastasis[16]. Palmatine inhibits CRC by upregulating miR-363-3p, which targets Aurora kinase A, suppressing proliferation and metastasis[17]. These findings reveal miRNAs as key regulators in CRC pathogenesis, therapy resistance, and metastasis, offering biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Among the previously studied miRNAs, microRNA-492 (miR-492) plays an important role in the occurrence and development of tumors. Wu et al found that miR-492 can promote chemoresistance to cis-diamminedichloroplatinum and metastasis by inhibiting DNA (cytosine-5)-methyltransferase 3B and inducing stemness in gastric cancer[18]. Studies conducted by Sun et al have shown that miR-492 overexpression can promote the occurrence and development of liver cancer through the phosphatase and tensin homolog and AKT pathways[19]. Another study showed that miR-492 inhibition might impede the malignant behavior of retinoblastoma by directly targeting large tumor suppressor 2[20]. Additionally, abnormal miR-492 expression and its tumor-promoting ability have also been reported in breast[21], cervical[22], ovarian[23], prostate[24], and kidney cancers[25]. However, no study has explored the role of miR-492 in the development of CRC. Here, we evaluated the expression of miR-492 in CRC tissues and cells and the roles of its related molecules. We found that the expression level of miR-492 was increased in CRC tissues and cell lines, and low expression of miR-492 inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells. These results suggest that miR-492 may be used as a molecular target for the treatment of CRC in the future.

Neuronal pentraxin 1 (NPTX1), a member of the pentraxin family, was initially identified for its role in promoting axon growth and regulating cell properties in the central nervous system, and it has been reported as a risk factor for hypoxic-ischemic neuronal injury[26]. NPTX1 has previously been reported to induce mitochondria-mediated hypoxic-ischemic neuronal injury through a glycogen synthase kinase 3α/β-dependent mechanism[27]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have suggested that NPTX1 plays a key role in tumor progression. Zhao et al found that overexpression of NPTX1 inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma[28]. Yan et al found that NPTX1 promotes the metastasis of gastric cancer cells through the integrin/focal adhesion kinase axis[29]. In another study, Professor Peng’s team found that NPTX1 reduces the proliferation of cancer cells by regulating the expression of cyclin A2 and cyclin-dependent kinase 2 in colon cancer[30]. However, the role of NPTX1 in regulating the migration and invasion of CRC and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

This study aimed to explore the prognosis of miR-492 in patients with CRC through survival analysis using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE89974 and GSE285322) and to statistically analyze the relationship between miR-492 and the clinicopathological parameters of CRC. Next, we investigated the effect of miR-492 on the biological behavior of CRC cells by knocking down the expression of miR-492. Furthermore, we explored the mutual regulatory relationship between miR-492 and NPTX1 and the effect of NPTX1 on the biological behavior of CRC cells. Finally, we explored the specific mechanisms underlying the effects of miR-492 on the occurrence and development of CRC. Our results provide a scientific foundation for unraveling the specific mechanisms of action of miR-492 in CRC.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection

In this study, 44 pairs of patients with CRC were diagnosed and treated at our hospital between 1 January 2023 and 31 December 2023. The specimens in the control group were obtained from the adjacent tissues of the same patient, and no cancer cells were found on pathological examination. None of the patients with CRC received neoadjuvant therapy or other anticancer therapies prior to surgery. The Ethics Committee of Changshu Second People’s Hospital approved the use of colon cancer samples and patient data for analysis, and all patients and their families provided written informed consent.

Cell culture

The human normal intestinal epithelial cell line (NCM460) and CRC cell lines (SW480, HCT116, LoVo, and HT29) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA-seq and miRNA expression profiles, along with corresponding clinical information, were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), GSE89974 and GSE285322. Pan-cancer data were obtained from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages). Expression profiles and clinical data of CRC patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Program were downloaded from the TCGA-Genomic Data Commons. R packages were used to organize and clean the dataset (differential expression criteria were |logFC| > 2.0 and P < 0.05). According to miRNA target prediction databases, the dataset was divided into two groups (high and low miR-492/NPTX1 expression levels). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate the survival curve for the target gene. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. The MiRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/) and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/) databases were used to predict the target genes of miR-492. Based on this prediction, the correlation between miR-492 and NPTX1 was verified using Pearson correlation analysis.

MTT assay

The cell suspension density was adjusted by collecting logarithmic-phase cells. A 100 μL aliquot of medium containing 1000 cells per well was inoculated into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 24, 48, or 72 h prior to transfection. After transfection, the cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2, then 20 μl of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for another 4 h. Approximately 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to each well, and the plate was shaken at low speed for 20 min to completely dissolve the crystals. Finally, the absorbance of each well was measured at 595 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay plate reader.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA and miRNA were isolated by microextraction using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and a miRNA kit (Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). SYBR Green or SYBR PrimeScript miRNA reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) kits (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used to emit fluorescent signals. The primer sequences were as follows:

miR-492 forward: 5′-GGGGTACCCCCTGGCTGGAACAGAAGAT-3′

miR-492 reverse: 5′-CCCAAGCTTCCCTGGTCTTGGCTGGGATC-3′

U6 forward: 5′-CGCTTCGGCAGCACATATAC-3′

U6 reverse: 5′-TTCACGAATTTGCGTGTCATC-3′

NPTX1 forward: 5′-GAGACAAGTTCCAGCTCACA-3′

NPTX1 reverse: 5′-CAGACAGTGAAGGCGTACAT-3′

GAPDH forward: 5′-CGACCACTTTGTCAAGCTCA-3′

GAPDH reverse: 5′-GGAGAGTCAACGGGCATATAG-3′.

Western blotting analysis

The protein expression of NPTX1 was evaluated by Western blotting (WB) in different cell groups. Briefly, total proteins were extracted using lysis buffer, denatured in loading buffer, and separated by SDS-PAGE. Following transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (0.45 μm), the blots were blocked with blocking buffer. The membranes were then probed with primary antibodies against NPTX1, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent detection to assess differences in NPTX1 expression between tumor and normal tissues.

Colony formation assay

We selected SW480 and HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase, assigned them to an experimental group and a control group, and divided the cells into anti-NC and miR-492 inhibitor, vector and oe-NPTX1, anti-NC + si-Con, si-Con + miR-492 inhibitor, anti-NC + si-NPTX1, and miR-492 inhibitor + si-NPTX1 groups. The cells in each group were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2, and the growth of clonal colonies was observed regularly. The culture was discontinued when visible clones appeared in the 12-well plates. After fixation with formaldehyde, staining with crystal violet, and gentle washing with phosphate-buffered saline, the cells were placed in a dry place and photographed.

Transwell assay

SW480 and HCT116 cells were incubated in 24-well plates according to the above protocol, and 600 μL of RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to each well. Transwell inserts were placed individually in a 24-well plate, then 50 μL of Matrigel was added to each Transwell chamber, and a serum-free RPMI 1640 cell suspension at a concentration of 1 × 104 cells/mL was added to each chamber. The 24-well plates were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 and cultured for 24 h. The chambers were removed, the supernatant was discarded, and cells in each well were fixed with methanol for 15 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. The cells that passed through the porous membrane were counted under a microscope.

Transient transfection

SW480 and HCT116 cells treated with miR-492 siRNA were named miR-492 mimics, while the control cells were referred to as NC. Anti-NC was used as the negative control for the miR-492 inhibitors. The siRNA that interfered with the expression of NPTX1 was referred to as si-NPTX1, while its interference control was referred to as si-Con. The plasmid overexpressing NPTX1 was named oe-NPTX1, and its blank control was named vector. All were purchased from Gemma Pharma (Shanghai, China), and the sequences were as follows:

miR-492 mimic sequence: 5′-AGGACCUGCGGGACAAGAUUCUU-3′

NC sequence: 5′-ACUUGGACCUGAGCGAUUGGACA-3′

miR-492 inhibitor sequence: 5′-AAGAAUCUUGUCCCGCAGGUCCU-3′

Anti-NC sequence: 5′-GUGCACGAAGGCUCAUCAUU-3′

si-NPTX1 sequence: 5′-UCCAACAUCUGGGACCGCAATT-3′

si-Con sequence: 5′-CAUCUGGUAATCCAAGCGACCT-3′.

Luciferase experiment

We amplified the full-length 3′-untranslated region (UTR)-NPTX1 sequence containing the miR-492 binding site by PCR and cloned it into the pmirGlo luciferase reporter vector [pmirGlo-NPTX1 wild type (WT)]. The QuikChange multisite-directed mutagenesis kit was used to generate the NPTX1 3′-UTR mutant [pmirGlo-NPTX1 mutant (MUT)], with the WT construct used as the control. The NPTX1 WT luciferase reporter plasmid was co-transfected into HEK293T cells with miR-492 mimics or NC using Lipofectamine 2000; meanwhile, the NPTX1 MUT was co-transfected with miR-492 mimics or NC. Luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform statistical analyses. Database data were processed and standardized using the R language (version 4.50), including batch correction and other procedures. For data that met normality, unpaired t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to assess differences among groups. For data that did not meet normality or had unequal variances, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences. These tests were used to assess differences between the study groups. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Each in vitro experiment was performed in triplicate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression of miR-492 as a clinicopathological feature of patients with CRC

In silico analysis of two CRC non-coding RNA sequencing GEO datasets (GSE89974 and GSE285322), with volcano plots and heatmaps generated for each to screen for differentially expressed miRNAs (Fig. 1A–D), was followed by Venn diagrams to identify miRNAs commonly differentially expressed in both datasets (Fig. 1E and F). The results showed four differentially expressed miRNAs (miR-492, miR-4684-3p, miR-101-5p, and miR-144). Analysis of the impact of these four miRNAs on the survival prognosis of CRC patients in the TCGA database revealed that patients with high miR-492 expression levels had shorter survival times than those with low expression levels (Fig. 1G), indicating that high miR-492 expression leads to poor prognosis in CRC patients. The effects of the other three miRNAs on the survival prognosis of CRC patients did not reach statistical significance. ROC analysis was performed on multiple miRNAs in TCGA CRC patients, with area under the curve (AUC) significance calculated by the Wilcoxon test and Benjamini–Hochberg multiple correction applied. The AUC of miR-492 reached 0.929 (P = 9.17E − 07), while the AUCs of miR-4684-3p, miR-101-5p, and miR-144 were not significant (Fig. 1H). Analysis of miR-492 expression in TCGA pan-cancer revealed inconsistent expression across various cancer types (Fig. 1I), but showed low expression in colon and rectal cancers, consistent with the low expression observed in the GSE89974 and GSE285322 datasets (Fig. 1J). To explore miR-492 expression in human colorectal cell lines, we examined the normal human intestinal epithelial cell line NCM460 and human CRC cell lines (HT29, SW480, HCT116, and LoVo). Compared with normal intestinal epithelial cells, miR-492 expression was upregulated in CRC cell lines; miR-492 expression was higher in SW480 and HCT116 cells than in HT29 and LoVo cells (Fig. 2B). Association between reduction in the miR-492 expression levels and the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cell lines

Considering that the expression levels of miR-492 in SW480 and HCT116 cell lines were higher than those in HT29 and LoVo cell lines (Fig. 2B), the SW480 and HCT116 cell lines were selected to evaluate the effects of miR-492 on the biological behavior of CRC cells. We constructed a miR-492 inhibitor and the corresponding negative control and transfected the SW480 and HCT116 cells. miRNA qRT-PCR was used to assess the transfection efficiency. Compared with the negative control group, the expression level of miR-492 was significantly decreased in the SW480-inhibitor and HCT116-inhibitor groups (Fig. 3A). To investigate the effects of miR-492 on the proliferation of CRC cells, the MTT assay (Fig. 3B and C) and colony formation assay (Fig. 3D) were used. Compared with the negative control group (anti-NC), the proliferation rate and colony formation ability of SW480 and HCT116 cells were significantly reduced when miR-492 expression was downregulated. Transwell assays showed that the downregulation of miR-492 expression in SW480 and HCT116 cells significantly reduced the migration (Fig. 3E) and invasion (Fig. 3F) abilities of the cells compared with negative control cells. MiR-492 might directly target and regulate the expression of NPTX1 by recognizing the 3′-UTR of NPTX1 mRNA

MiRNAs can target the 3′-UTR binding sites on mRNA, leading to translational inhibition and gene silencing. However, miRNAs contribute to the inhibition or promotion of cancer and play a role in the occurrence and development of tumors by regulating the expression of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes. An important mechanism by which miR-492 promotes cancer is the targeted regulation of aberrant mRNA expression.

The miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/) and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/) databases were used to search for possible target genes of miR-492. STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to predict protein–protein interactions (PPIs). The results showed that miR-492 can directly target the 3′-UTR of NPTX1 mRNA (Fig. 4A). The STRING PPI network indicated that NPTX1 is one of the hub genes (Fig. 4B). We further analyzed the expression of NPTX1 mRNA in TCGA pan-cancer data, and the results showed inconsistent expression levels across different cancer types (Fig. 4C). In TCGA CRC data, NPTX1 expression was also lower than in normal tissues (Fig. 4D). To investigate the expression of NPTX1 in CRC tissues and its correlation with the expression of miR-492, qRT-PCR was used to detect the expression level of NPTX1 in 44 pairs of previously collected CRC and matched adjacent normal tissues. The expression level of NPTX1 was lower in CRC tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 4E). It was verified that miR-492, which is highly expressed in CRC, may inhibit NPTX1 expression, indirectly indicating that NPTX1 is one of the target genes of miR-492. We further analyzed the relationship between miR-492 and NPTX1 using Pearson’s correlation, and the correlation coefficient was −0.716 (P < 0.001), indicating a strong negative correlation between miR-492 and NPTX1 expression levels (Fig. 4F). Discussion

The clinical behavior of CRC is a result of multi-layered interactions, and genetic changes in oncogenes and/or tumor suppressor genes are the most important drivers of tumorigenesis and cancer progression[31]. miRNAs are novel biomarkers that can be used for the early diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and treatment of CRC[32]. In this study, we investigated the relationship between miR-492 and the prognosis of patients with CRC using the GEO and TCGA databases, and examined the effects of miR-492 on the growth, migration, and invasion of CRC cells through a series of in vitro experiments. miR-492 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and shows a positive correlation with pathological features of CRC, including TNM stage, tumor size, and distant metastasis. Its high expression is associated with a higher TNM stage, malignant proliferation, invasion of surrounding tissues, and persistent distant metastasis, resulting in poor survival for patients. Functional assays demonstrate that high expression of miR-492 promotes proliferation and colony formation of CRC cell lines, conferring stronger migration and invasion capabilities. These results suggest that miR-492 plays an important role in the diagnosis and prognosis of CRC. We also observed a promoting effect of miR-492 on the distant invasive ability of cancer cells. We found that NPTX1 is a target gene of miR-492, and overexpression of NPTX1 can inhibit the growth, migration, and invasion of CRC cells. As a tumor suppressor gene, inhibition of NPTX1 expression promotes malignant transformation of CRC cells.

MiRNAs are small non-coding RNA molecules that act as endogenous inhibitors of gene expression by binding to the 3′-UTR of the target mRNA to induce translational inhibition or mRNA cleavage. Sometimes, miRNAs directly bind to the mRNA coding sequence or act as gene expression activators by binding to the 5′-UTR of the target mRNA[33]. The discovery of miRNAs has expanded the spectrum of classical tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes. As important post-transcriptional regulators, miRNAs are involved in various physiological and pathological processes such as development, hematopoietic cell differentiation, cell proliferation, organ development, and tumorigenesis. The miRNA profiles undergo extensive changes during the initiation and progression of various cancers, including CRC[34]. In recent years, many studies have identified miRNAs that are aberrantly expressed in patients with CRC[35]. Xi et al observed the repression of miR-204-3p in colon cancer tissues and in HCT116, SW480, Caco-2, HT29, and SW620 cell lines, and that the overexpression of miR-204-3p could suppress colon cancer cell migration and invasion[36]. miR-148a-3p acts as an oncomiRNA by repressing KLF4, driving proliferation, EMT, and immune escape[13], while miR-21 promotes progression via PTEN inhibition. Conversely, miR-195-5p and miR-196b function as tumor suppressors, targeting oncogenic pathways to enhance therapy sensitivity[15]. An emerging theme is miRNA-mediated crosstalk between tumor cells and the microenvironment: EV-packaged miR-99a reprograms fibroblasts into CAFs via NLRP2/NF-κB/CCL7, fostering EMT and metastasis[16].

MiR-492 is aberrantly expressed in tumors; however, its biological function and underlying molecular mechanisms in CRC remain unclear. When the CRC dataset obtained from the TCGA database was analyzed, patients with CRC and high miR-492 expression levels had poor overall survival. Based on these results, we investigated whether miR-492 affects the biological functions of CRC. Subsequently, by measuring the expression level of miR-492 in CRC tissues and cells, we found that miR-492 was upregulated in CRC tissues as well as in the SW480 and HCT116 cell lines. Moreover, the expression level of miR-492 was associated with the TNM stage, tumor size, and distant metastasis status of patients with CRC. Patients with high miR-492 expression levels had a higher TNM stage, a larger tumor size, and were more likely to have distant metastasis than those with low miR-492 expression levels. Next, we investigated the effects of miR-492 on the biological behavior of CRC cells. SW480 and HCT116 cells, which possessed relatively high miR-492 expression levels, were selected for in vitro experiments. After transfection with the miR-492 inhibitor, the expression of miR-492 was significantly downregulated in SW480 and HCT116 cells. MTT, colony formation, and Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that the knockdown of miR-492 significantly inhibited the growth, migration, and invasion of CRC cells. These results suggest that miR-492 may serve as a prognostic marker and potential therapeutic target for CRC.

Previous studies have shown that NPTX1 acts as a tumor suppressor in various types of cancer[28,29]. NPTX1 is also a direct target of different miRNAs. A previous study confirmed that miR-4295 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of head and neck cancer by targeting and silencing NPTX1[37]. Based on previous studies, we examined whether miRNAs could target the 3′-UTR of mRNAs to induce gene repression. Using the miRDB and TargetScan databases, we found that miR-492 binds to the 3′-UTR of NPTX1. To confirm our hypothesis, we examined the expression level of NPTX1 in 44 pairs of CRC tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues; the results showed that NPTX1 expression was reduced in CRC tissues compared with that in adjacent normal tissues. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that the expression level of NPTX1 was negatively correlated with the miR-492 expression level. Furthermore, NPTX1 expression was detected in CRC cells (SW480 and HCT116) and normal human intestinal epithelial cells (NCM460), and NPTX1 expression was found to be decreased in SW480 and HCT116 cells. In SW480 and HCT116 cells, the overexpression of NPTX1 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion capacity of the cells, as determined by MTT, colony formation, and Transwell migration and invasion assays, respectively. These data suggest that NPTX1 is a key regulator of CRC development.

The downstream signaling pathways targeted by NPTX1 play different roles in various cancers. NPTX1 promotes glioma proliferation through IRS-1/PI3K/AKT activation, as NPTX1 siRNA significantly suppresses p-IRS-1, PI3K, and p-AKT levels[38]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, however, NPTX1 acts as a downstream target of the AKT pathway, where its overexpression suppresses growth and enhances mitochondria-related apoptosis, with AKT blockade further potentiating these antitumor effects[39]. Concurrently, NPTX1 directly stabilizes β-catenin by increasing GSK3β phosphorylation, thereby protecting β-catenin from proteasomal degradation; in breast cancer, NPTX1 downregulation induces cell cycle progression through Wnt/β-catenin signaling[40]. The MAPK/ERK branch is also engaged, as MEK-regulated cell growth in human neuroglial cells is mediated by β-catenin through NPTX1’s stabilization of β-catenin protein levels[41]. Beyond growth and survival, NPTX1 regulates mitochondrial apoptotic machinery: dysregulation of the miR-204–NPTX1 axis induces mitochondrial ROS and apoptosis, and NPTX1 is involved in mitochondrial fragmentation during apoptosis[42]. Moreover, the miR-4295/NPTX1 axis influences epithelial–mesenchymal transition, where miR-4295 promotes invasion and EMT by suppressing NPTX1, and NPTX1 knockdown rescues these effects[37].

Our results led us to hypothesize that miR-492 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells by downregulating the expression of NPTX1. Our hypothesis was further confirmed by the results of the dual-luciferase reporter assay, which showed that miR-492 directly targeted NPTX1 by recognizing its 3′-UTR. Next, experimental and control groups (anti-NC + si-Con, si-Con + miR-492 inhibitor, anti-NC + si-NPTX1, and miR-492 inhibitor + si-NPTX1) were established. NPTX1 siRNA and miR-492 inhibitor were co-transfected into SW480 and HCT116 cells, and MTT, colony formation, and Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed. NPTX1 knockdown reversed the inhibitory effect of miR-492 knockdown on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of SW480 and HCT116 cells. These results suggest that miR-492 can reduce the expression of NPTX1 through targeted regulation and promote the development of CRC.

The study is preclinical (cell line-based), with small sample sizes and limited in vivo validation of the miR-492/NPTX1 axis. Due to insufficient clinical cohorts, the survival analysis results for miR-492 in TCGA could not be fully validated in patient cohorts. Future work should adopt multicenter cohorts to validate miR-492/NPTX1 expression across populations. Additionally, there is a lack of research analyzing how downstream signals of this axis regulate the tumor immune microenvironment in CRC. Purely in vitro functional assays cannot recapitulate tumor-stroma interactions or systemic effects. Animal models are critical to confirm in vivo oncogenicity and metastasis. Without serum or tissue miR-492 measurements in CRC patients, its biomarker potential remains unproven. Future experimental plans aim to expand clinical sample studies, combined with in vivo animal experiments, to perform liquid biopsy analyses and validate the potential of serum miR-492 as a biomarker. Future phase I/II clinical trials may also be conducted to test miR-492 inhibition as a therapeutic strategy for patients with advanced CRC.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that NPTX1 is a target gene of miR-492, and miR-492 can promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells through targeted regulation of NPTX1 expression. However, animal experiments were not performed to further validate our findings. In conclusion, miR-492 is associated with a poor prognosis and may represent a potential therapeutic candidate for CRC, requiring further validation. A better understanding of these complex regulatory mechanisms should facilitate the development of more effective and selective tools for treating cancer.
