Spaceflight and the rodent skeleton: A perspective☆
Authors: Vahid Monfared, Urszula T Iwaniec, Russell T Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptations to extreme environments, such as spaceflight, are often studied using model organisms. Beyond basic science goals, validated model organisms are used to better understand human responses. The skeleton, the topic of this perspective, is an important gravity-sensitive organ with structure-to-function relationships that are altered during spaceflight. Here we describe how small rodents have been used during the past 5 decades to understand the skeletal response to spaceflight with an emphasis on changes in bone mass, architecture and quality, and the cellular mechanisms mediating these changes. Additionally, we consider the strengths and limitations of rodents for modeling human response and the unique challenges of the spaceflight environment for designing experiments and collecting and interpreting data. We also evaluate the extent to which these model organisms have increased our understanding of skeletal adaptation to microgravity. Taken together, there is strong evidence that spaceflight influences bone accrual and turnover, often resulting in lower bone mass compared to age-matched ground-based controls. However, the skeletal response to spaceflight is not uniform, the cellular mechanisms for observed changes remain elusive, and the molecular mechanisms are largely unknown. Importantly, it is difficult to distinguish the specific skeletal effects of weightlessness (microgravity) from those associated with the spacecraft environment. Since microgravity during orbital spaceflight is constant, the differences across studies are likely due to coexisting intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including species, sex, age, duration of flight, and housing conditions. While rodent studies provide novel insight into adaptation of the mammalian skeleton to microgravity, because of important species differences in skeletal maturation, metabolism and bone physiology, comparisons of rodents to astronauts should currently be made with caution.Keywords: Microgravity, Skeletal disuse, Bone growth and turnover, Model organisms, Rats, Mice

Introduction

To accomplish ambitious long-duration space missions, such as interplanetary travel, understanding the physiological challenges posed by the extraterrestrial environment becomes paramount. Among organ systems critically susceptible to these extreme conditions is the skeleton. In adult mammals more than 200 separate bones contribute to diverse skeletal functions, which include locomotion, protection of vital organs, mineral homeostasis and hematopoiesis. Specific functions vary widely among bones and bone compartments and are influenced by age, sex, anatomical location and magnitude and direction of mechanical loading. The skeleton is normally exposed to a uniform (static) gravitational field. However, ground reaction forces experienced during locomotion result in transient (dynamic) loading that is typically 2–4 times resting body weight (Nilsson & Thorstensson, 1989). There is compelling evidence that dynamic and static gravitational loading as well as muscle loading can positively influence bone function (Iwaniec & Turner, 2016). Consequently, physical disuse resulting from immobilization or functional disuse resulting from weightlessness during spaceflight can negatively impact bone health (Buettmann et al., 2022; Sibonga, 2013). Here we discuss how small rodents have been used during the past 5 decades to investigate the skeletal response to spaceflight with an emphasis on changes in bone mass, architecture and quality and the cellular mechanisms mediating these changes. Additionally, we consider the strengths and limitations of rodents for modeling human response as well as the unique challenges of the spaceflight environment for designing experiments and collecting and interpreting data. We also express our opinion regarding the extent to which rodent models have increased our understanding of human skeletal adaptation to weightlessness.

On Earth, the skeleton evolved to withstand static as well as dynamic mechanical loads. However, residing in an accelerated frame of reference such as a spacecraft is indistinguishable from residing in a gravitational field (equivalence principle) (Einstein, 1907). Consequently, during orbital spaceflight the skeleton is subjected to changes in static and dynamic loads resulting from the acceleration conferred by gravity and acceleration opposing gravity. These result in a transient increase in skeletal loading following launch and greatly reduced loading while in orbit. The near weightlessness, referred to as microgravity, experienced by astronauts during orbital flight occurs because the acceleration of their frame of reference (spacecraft) almost exactly opposes the acceleration conferred by Earth's gravitational field (Fig. 1). Ground-based models (e.g., hindlimb unloading in animals and bedrest in humans) simulate some of the physiological effects of microgravity (e.g., functional skeletal disuse) (Morey et al., 1979). However, in contrast to spaceflight, simulated microgravity does not fulfill the principle of equivalence as the frame of reference of the subject (human or rodent) does not oppose gravity. Nor do these ground-based models accurately recapitulate the spacecraft living environment. Based on our experience with two strains of rats and four strains of mice, hindlimb unloading results in a stress-induced negative energy balance that we have not witnessed during spaceflight (Hefferan et al., 2003; Turner et al., 2006; Turner et al., 2020; Wong et al., 2021). Ground-based models for simulated microgravity are valuable for investigating the skeletal impact of abolishing dynamic gravitational loading of the skeleton but, because of the aforementioned limitations, this perspective will focus on spaceflight.

Equivalence principle. On Earth the gravity vector (arrow) is responsible for static and dynamic weight effects on the skeleton. During orbital spaceflight, the acceleration of the spacecraft opposes the gravity vector in direction and magnitude resulting in microgravity (weightlessness). During simulated microgravity (hindlimb unloading in rodents and bed rest in humans), the gravity vector is unchanged. Dynamic weight effects are reduced due to functional immobilization of the hindlimb (rodents) or reduced weight bearing activity during bedrest (humans).

Model organisms for spaceflight research

While a diverse array of animals has been flown in space to explore biological adaptation, most studies specifically investigating skeletal adaptation to spaceflight utilized small rodents, namely rats and mice. This strategic choice stems from their inherent advantages in terms of size, genetic tractability, relatively short lifespans, ease of handling, and well-understood skeletal physiology that shares significant homology with humans. Historically, spaceflight missions during the last quarter of the 20th century focused heavily on rats (Rattus norvegicus) as the primary mammalian model for bone research in microgravity (Table 1). These early missions provided foundational insight into disuse osteopenia and altered bone formation in response to spaceflight (Turner, 2000). However, the first quarter of the 21st century witnessed a notable shift, with spaceflight studies increasingly emphasizing mice (Mus musculus). This transition lessened the weight constraints imposed by the larger species that severely limited sample size. Additionally, the advent of advanced genetic manipulation techniques in mice, offered unprecedented opportunities to investigate specific gene-bone interactions under microgravity.

Despite their superficial similarities, it is critical to recognize that mice are not miniature rats; the two represent distinct genera that diverged 12 to 33 million years ago (Nei et al., 2001). This evolutionary divergence manifests in significant biological distinctions beyond mere size, including subtle yet important anatomical variations in their skeletal structures. More important, profound physiological differences exist between rats and mice in key areas such as energy metabolism, basal metabolic rate, blood chemistry profiles, and their distinct responses to hypoxic conditions (Arias-Reyes et al., 2021; Ellenbroek & Youn, 2016). These inherent differences can lead to genera-specific skeletal responses to the complex stressors of spaceflight. Understanding these nuances is critical for accurate interpretation and translation of findings.

There is convincing evidence that spaceflight influences bone growth, architecture, turnover and function in rodents (Man et al., 2022; Turner, 2000; Vico & Hargens, 2018). It is also clear that reported changes are not uniform across studies. Microgravity during orbital spaceflight is constant. Therefore, we hypothesize that many of the reported differences result from coexisting intrinsic, extrinsic and/or additional factors associated with spacecraft environment that act independently, additively or synergistically with microgravity to affect bone response. Intrinsic factors relate to characteristics inherent to the animals under investigation and include species/strain, sex and age (Table 2). Extrinsic factors define the spaceflight environment and include, but are not limited to, housing configuration and animal density, diet, environmental temperature, humidity, forces associated with launch and reentry, duration of flight, pre- and post-flight procedures and experimental interventions. Additional factors include skeletal tissues analyzed, choice of controls, sample size and methods used to evaluate bone.

Characterizing microgravity-specific effects on bone(s) can only be achieved if the influence of flight variables can be accounted for. This is challenging because of the large number of variables and small number of flight experiments. One of the most important variables is the choice of animals. Interpretation of spaceflight results is often influenced by the stage of skeletal maturation of the animals studied, the sex, the bones and bone compartments analyzed, and methods used for analysis. To acknowledge these complexities, we briefly review rodent skeletal physiology with an emphasis on how it is impacted by spaceflight. We also discuss the methods used to investigate rodent skeletal response to spaceflight.

Rodent skeletal physiology

Skeletal maturity is defined as the life interval following cessation of linear bone growth. As in humans, skeletal maturity in rodents is achieved well after sexual maturity. In humans, growth plate fusion marks termination of linear bone growth. The extended retention of growth plate cartilage in small rodents led to the common but erroneous conclusion that linear growth occurred throughout life in rats and mice. Micro-computed tomography (microCT) analysis of hindlimb long bones has shown that bone bridging across the growth plate prevents further linear growth in rat tibia by 9 months of age (Martin et al., 2003). In C57/BL6J mice, linear bone growth in femur is negligible after 6 months of age (Glatt et al., 2007).

All rat and most mouse spaceflight studies were conducted on growing animals (Table 1). Because growing humans have not experienced long-duration spaceflight, our knowledge of the impact of exposure to microgravity on bone growth and maturation is confined to model organisms. On the other hand, the skeletal endpoints typically evaluated in humans and limitations of the animal models allows few direct comparisons of rodents to astronauts (Parfitt, 1981; Turner et al., 2001).

A comprehensive understanding of the skeletal response to spaceflight requires evaluation of skeletal compartments at multiple locations in animals of different ages. To interpret the cellular mechanism(s) mediating skeletal response (architecture, gene or function) it is vital to ascertain stage of skeletal maturity. Endochondral ossification involves replacement of a preexisting cartilage template with bone and is the primary contributor to linear bone growth. Intramembranous ossification, a much slower appositional growth process, involves direct addition of bone onto existing bone and is responsible for radial bone growth. As in humans, the rate and time course for bone growth in rodents varies among bones and bone compartments (Bass et al., 1999; Zhou & Lui, 2021). The different maturation rates among bones during growth permits analysis of bones at various stages of skeletal maturation (e.g., long bone epiphyses reach skeletal maturity at an earlier age than long bone metaphyses). This characteristic is useful due to greatly limited opportunities to perform spaceflight experiments involving animals.

Cartilage formation and resorption and bone formation and resorption each play a role in skeletal maturation during endochondral ossification. Primary spongiosa are created in the zone of provisional calcification during chondroclast-facilitated vascular invasion of growth plate cartilage. Sequential intervals of osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption result in replacement of mineralized cartilage with bone, establishing the mass and three-dimensional structure of the cancellous bone compartment.

Transformation of primary spongiosa from their growth plate origin in a long bone to their subsequent dissolution at the terminus of the metaphysis is a dynamic process that results in continuous spatial translocation of the cancellous compartment over time (Fig. 2). During this process, which takes approximately 18 days in proximal tibia metaphysis of a 3-month-old male Sprague Dawley rat, there is net deposition of bone matrix onto newly created primary spongiosa and net resorption of bone within secondary spongiosa, the latter leading to their disappearance (Turner, 1994). Changes (compared to ground controls) in cancellous bone mass and architecture in long bones of growing rodents are easily quantified following flight using well-established imaging methodologies. However, to identify the cellular mechanism(s), the following knowledge is required: 1) linear growth rate for the cancellous compartment being investigated, 2) rate of chondroclast-mediated resorption of mineralizing cartilage in zone of vascular invasion, 3) rate of osteoclast-mediated resorption of primary and secondary spongiosa, and 4) rate of osteoblast-mediated formation of bone onto primary and secondary spongiosa. Without the complete information (which is rarely obtained), assigning cellular mechanisms for changes in cancellous bone architecture during growth should be made with caution.

Following skeletal maturation, bone turns over throughout the remainder of life at rates specific to the bone and bone compartment. Modeling refers to changes in bone architecture where bone is added to a preexisting bone surface without a requirement for prior bone resorption or by which bone is resorbed from a bone surface without initiating subsequent bone formation. Remodeling refers to bone resorption followed by in situ deposition of new bone. Remodeling is initiated when osteoclasts are recruited to quiescent bone surfaces where they degrade bone. Osteoblasts are then recruited to the resorption site where they secrete matrix that undergoes mineralization to form bone and complete a remodeling cycle. When bone formation is tightly coupled to bone resorption there is minimal change in bone architecture. In the process of bone formation during growth, modeling or remodeling, some osteoblasts become quiescent lining cells while others are incorporated into bone matrix and become osteocytes (Frost, 1969; Parfitt, 1994). Although modeling of cortical bone during growth is common in rodents, in contrast to humans, secondary osteons indicative of intracortical remodeling are absent. This represents an important limitation of small rodents as models for human skeletal physiology, particularly since the quantity of cortical bone greatly exceeds that of cancellous bone. Cancellous bone remodeling occurs in skeletally mature rodents, but modeling predominates in weight-bearing long bones at the ages typically used in spaceflight studies (Erben, 1996).

There are important species-specific sex differences in bone growth and turnover. In Sprague Dawley rats, longitudinal and radial bone growth of the tibia are much higher in males than in females leading to a higher total bone mass (Hefferan et al., 2003). However, cancellous bone volume and turnover is higher in proximal tibia of females than males (Hefferan et al., 2003). Skeletal differences are greatly attenuated when young male and female rats are gonadectomized, demonstrating the important role of sex hormones in mediating sex differences in bone physiology (Turner et al., 1989). In contrast to rats, longitudinal and radial bone growth of the femur are similar in male and female C57/BL6J mice (Glatt et al., 2007) but cancellous bone volume and turnover rates in the distal femur are higher in males than females (Martin et al., 2019).

Assessment of bone following spaceflight

Bone mass, density and architecture

While it is unrealistic to routinely examine each of the 200+ individual bones in the rodent skeleton, whole body X-ray absorptiometry can be used to measure key indices of body composition, including total bone mass. Although repeat measurements are commonly used to quantify the skeletal response to spaceflight in astronauts, this approach has not been routinely used in rodents. In one mouse study, X-ray absorptiometry measurements were performed onboard the International Space Station (Ha et al., 2023; Lafage-Proust et al., 1998). Also, X-ray absorptiometry was used in some spaceflight studies to quantify bone mass and areal bone density of individual bones ex vivo (Keune et al., 2015). The most useful X-ray absorptiometry endpoint for skeletal evaluation in growing animals is bone mineral content, a direct measure of bone mass. Bone area can also be obtained from X-ray absorptiometry, and areal bone mineral density calculated as bone mineral content/bone area. Bone area is a less useful endpoint because its value is highly sensitive to specimen orientation relative to the X-ray source. Even minute changes in positioning of a bone will alter the association between bone area and bone mineral content, leading to differences in bone mineral density (Fig. 3). Changes in bone mineral content across time or treatment reflects the magnitude and direction of change in bone mass. Bone mineral density reflects the distribution of mineral over a two-dimensional projection of the specimen. Changes in bone mineral density in a growing animal across time or treatment cannot be interpreted (without knowledge of bone mineral content or bone area). Consequently, a reduction in bone mineral density can occur in the absence of bone loss (Keune et al., 2015).

Effect of repeated dual energy absorptiometry (DXA) scanning of mouse femurs (male C57BL6, n = 26) 6 weeks apart by the same individual using the same scanner. Slight unintentional differences in positioning had minimal impact on bone mineral content (BMC, R = 0.95) but resulted in much more variation in bone area (R = 0.39) and bone mineral density (BMD, R = 0.70) repeat measurements. Note that repeat whole body measurements will result in even more variation, especially in growing animals.

Histomorphometry was the standard method for imaging bone microarchitecture until microCT became routinely available. Bones of rats flown in space were typically analyzed by histomorphometry, the exception being archived specimens analyzed by microCT decades following flight (Keune et al., 2015). MicroCT has routinely been used to evaluate skeletal response to spaceflight in mice (Berg-Johansen et al., 2016; Cahill et al., 2025; Childress et al., 2018; Coulombe et al., 2021; Dagdeviren et al., 2018; Ghosh et al., 2016; Tavella et al., 2012; Tominari et al., 2019).

Histomorphometric evaluation of bone involves tissue fixation, embedding, thin sectioning and often staining of histological sections before quantification. These procedures are time-consuming. The dominant current use of histomorphometry is for quantification of cells (e.g., osteoblasts and osteoclasts) in stained histological sections and quantification of bone formation and in select cases bone resorption using fluorochrome labeling (Keune et al., 2016; Turner, 1995b). Static (cell) and dynamic (fluorochrome-based) measurements have been critical for evaluation of cell-based mechanisms underlying spaceflight-induced changes in bone mass and architecture.

MicroCT is a nondestructive photon (X-ray)-based imaging technique that is used to create 3-dimensional renditions of scanned samples. Because of availability of commercial benchtop machines with relatively high throughput capacity, ease of use, and excellent measurement accuracy, microCT has become the method of choice for routine evaluation of bone architecture. However, it is important to note that cross study comparisons can yield misleading results unless scanned regions of interest and imaging thresholding are uniform (Bouxsein et al., 2010). Although routinely used to evaluate bone mass and architecture the capabilities of benchtop microCT for spaceflight studies have not been fully exploited. MicroCT-based finite element modeling (Westerlind et al., 1997) and displacement during nondestructive loading (Ritman et al., 1998) can be used to predict how changes in mechanical loads guide changes in bone microarchitecture, and mineralization markers and contrast agents can be combined with microCT to investigate bone turnover rate and vascular and other soft tissue responses (Ritman et al., 1998).

An additional possibility is integrating architectural analysis by microCT with analysis of gene expression. Conventional microCT is performed at room temperature which would lead to rapid degradation of mRNA. However, cryogenic microCT scanning, in addition to improving image quality, provides an unexploited method of relating mRNA expression levels to bone and soft tissue microarchitecture (Maran et al., 2003). Image resolution and quality can be further improved when X-rays are closer to parallel and monochromatic, such as with a synchrotron light source. Improved resolution compared to standard benchtop microCTs has allowed measurement of the effects of spaceflight on very small structures such as osteocyte lacunae (Gerbaix et al., 2017).

Bone growth and turnover

Histological evaluation of growth plate organization, differential expression of genes related to cartilage synthesis and turnover, physical measurement of bone length and fluorochrome-based analysis of linear bone growth were used to study the impact of spaceflight on endochondral ossification. Differences in bone length between flight and ground control animals are indicative of differential growth rates (Spengler et al., 1983; Vailas et al., 1992). Measurement of longitudinal growth rate can be estimated when animals receive a preflight fluorochrome label. Neither bone length nor the rate and total distance of translocation of fluorochrome labels from their initial site of deposition in mineralizing growth plate cartilage are reported to be altered in long bones of rodents during spaceflight (Sibonga et al., 2000). In contrast, there is evidence to support spaceflight-associated changes in growth plate cellular organization and gene expression in rodent long bones (Cahill et al., 2025; Duke et al., 1990; Montufar-Solis & Durnova., 1992). However, the functional significance of the changes remains unclear.

Bone formation rates can be measured using dynamic histomorphometry (Dempster et al., 2013). Typically, timed fluorochrome labels are administered and the bone perimeter covered by labels and distance between labels quantified. These data are used to calculate bone formation as bone area formed/day or, by assuming a thickness of 1 unit in the z direction, bone volume formed/day. Although conceptionally simple, accurate estimation of bone formation in rodents can be challenging, particularly for cancellous bone, and these challenges are exaggerated by constraints imposed by spaceflight. The ideal interval between two timed labels is the shortest duration that an accurate assessment of intra-paired label distance can be performed, which is largely determined by the thickness of histological sections (thinner sections improve accuracy) and mineralization rate (higher rates improve accuracy). Unless resorbed, fluorochromes deposited into the mineralizing bone matrix remain in the matrix and are visible upon examination. Resorption of fluorochrome is uncommon during radial bone growth but occurs at skeletal sites undergoing turnover (Fig. 4). Even in ground-based studies, it is challenging to identify a labeling interval suitable for the range of bone formation and turnover rates present across different bones and bone compartments, a limitation that is mitigated in ground-based studies by administering additional labels that are distinguishable from one another based on differences in wavelength of fluorescence. In spaceflight studies, labeling intervals for determination of bone formation rates are constrained by flight duration, which is often too short or too long to achieve optimal measurements.

Fluorochrome labeling has been commonly used for evaluating rates of bone formation during spaceflight in rats, but rarely in mice. Fluorochromes (tetracycline) have been administered prior to flight (single label only) (Cavolina et al., 1997; Lloyd et al., 2015; Turner, 1995b) or prior to and following spaceflight (multiple labels) (Morey & Baylink, 1978; Simmons et al., 1983; Wronski & Morey, 1983). Because fluorochrome labels have not been administered in orbit, the labeling interval captures formation on the ground as well as in space. Fluorochrome labeling is a sensitive method for evaluating radial bone growth and studies using this method provide the strongest evidence for spaceflight leading to reductions in bone formation at the periosteal surface of weight-bearing bones (Wronski & Morey, 1983). However, in contrast to periosteum, where bone accrues in rodents with little or no label escape due to bone resorption, fluorochrome labeling at other sites (e.g., endocortical, cancellous) must be viewed with caution because osteoclast-mediated label escape results in underestimation of bone formation (Fig. 4) (Keune et al., 2016). While cancellous bone mass and architecture can be evaluated with relative ease following spaceflight, dynamic (fluorochrome-based) histomorphometric measurements are precluded at some frequently evaluated sites (e.g., proximal tibia metaphysis) where extensive label escape (up to 100%) occurs due to high rates of linear growth and bone turnover (Friedl et al., 2007). Because of label escape, suitable cancellous sites for measurement of bone formation in young rodents using conventional pre- and post-flight delivery of fluorochromes are limited to regions undergoing low rates of linear bone growth or turnover, (e.g., lumbar vertebra and proximal epiphysis) and only for flights of relatively short duration. Alternative labeling approaches such as programing osmotic pumps to deliver fluorochromes or adding fluorochromes to drinking water for discrete intervals, which work well on Earth, have not been reported in spaceflight studies.

There are few methods available for direct quantitative assessment of bone resorption. Isotope release from bone was used in one rat study to estimate whole body calcium balance (Cann & Adachi, 1983). This study did not obtain evidence for a global increase in bone resorption during spaceflight, but the methodology did not rule out the possibility for localized increases. Fluorochrome label loss is occasionally used as a measure of cancellous bone resorption with no change or increased resorption reported during spaceflight (Cavolina et al., 1997). Biochemical markers of bone turnover present in blood are commonly used in ground-based studies for indirect assessment of global changes in bone formation and bone resorption but to our knowledge they have rarely been used in rodent spaceflight studies (Lloyd et al., 2015). Finally, expression of genes coding for bone matrix proteins, bone regulatory cytokines and markers of bone cell differentiation and function have occasionally been evaluated in skeletal tissue in spaceflight experiments. Gene expression analysis was assayed on extracts from whole bones or on specific compartments in individual bones. Over time, the sophistication of approach evolved from Northern blot analysis of small numbers of selected genes to unbiased whole genome and RNA analysis (Blaber et al., 2013; Evans et al., 1998; Gautam et al., 2024; Macaulay et al., 2017; Okamura et al., 2024; Zhang & Turner, 1998). However, to date there has been a lack of comprehensive assessment of genomics/proteomics/metabolomics related to the skeleton.

Analysis of stained histological sections following live animal recovery is commonly performed with the goal of identifying the cellular basis for spaceflight-induced changes in bone architecture. Typically, osteoblasts and osteoclasts are quantified but other cells (e.g., osteocytes, adipocytes, megakaryocytes) can also be enumerated (Gerbaix et al., 2017; Wronski et al., 1981; Wronski et al., 1987). Similarly, histomorphometric evaluation of the growth plate can be performed to determine possible effects on chondrocyte to osteoblast transition. Although rarely performed, it is feasible to prepare histological specimens in which fluorochromes and cells can be simultaneously observed and measured (Turner, 1994). This method facilitates a more detailed evaluation of the effects of spaceflight on endpoints of bone turnover, including resorption. It should be noted that freezing skeletal tissue, as sometimes done in orbit, contraindicates traditional histological evaluation of bone due to severe cellular damage. However, it remains possible to localize matrix constituents such as sulfated glycosaminoglycans (Cahill et al., 2025).

Bone quality

Bone composition, ultrastructure, and material and mechanical properties have been investigated following spaceflight using elemental analysis, scanning and transmission electron microscopy, X-ray computed tomography, and destructive and non-destructive material testing procedures (Bailey et al., 2014; Bromage et al., 1998; Deymier et al., 2020; Doty et al., 1992; Gerbaix et al., 2017; Mechanic et al., 1990; Montufar-Solis & Durnova., 1992; Turner et al., 1985). Together, these analyses provide evidence that spaceflight-associated changes in bone mass and architecture are consistently accompanied by changes in bone mechanical properties. Additionally, some but not all studies suggest that changes in material properties contribute to the negative effects of spaceflight on bone strength (Bateman et al., 1998; Lloyd et al., 2015; Patterson-Buckendahl et al., 1987; Shaw et al., 1988; Spengler et al., 1983; Turner et al., 1985; Zernicke et al., 1990). Interestingly, re-initiation of arrested bone formation on cortical bone surfaces following spaceflight is associated with a transient defect in bone matrix deposition and mineralization resulting in a several-micron thick arrest line (Turner et al., 1985).

In summary, suitable methods are available to accurately quantify spaceflight-associated changes in bone mass, architecture and mechanical and material properties at a wide range of skeletal locations. Cellular and metabolic changes can often be deduced by histomorphometric evaluation and measurement of differential gene expression.

Variables influencing skeletal response to spaceflight

Species/strain

Wistar and Sprague-Dawley are the most frequently used rat strains for spaceflight experiments, but Fisher 344 rats have also been flown (Table 1). The smaller size of Fisher 344 rats allowed larger numbers of animals to cohabit animal enclosures. Mouse studies are typically performed using C57BL6J mice, but BALB/c mice have also been used (Table 1). The extent to which genetic background influences the skeletal response to spaceflight is an important consideration. However, direct comparisons across species or strains are absent.

Sex

Male and female rats and mice have been used for assessment of bone response to spaceflight (Table 1). In rats, most studies were performed in males. Experiments performed in female rats were uncommon but included pregnant and ovariectomized animals. In contrast, cycling female mice as well as male mice were regularly studied. However, while male and female mice have been flown on the same spacecraft, they have not been flown as part of the same experiment. Comparison of males and females across studies is possible but problematic because differences in age, flight duration and physical environment may obscure detection of sex differences. At present we can conclude that the skeleton of both male and female rodents can be negatively impacted by spaceflight, but it has not been established if there are sex-specific differences in location, timing, and magnitude of the response.

Age

Rodents flown in space have not often modeled the skeletal age and life stage of astronauts. Most astronauts are middle-aged (40–60 years old) and thus are well past the attainment of skeletal maturity. Rodents used in spaceflight research are frequently growing and often rapidly growing. In contrast to the stability of the human adult skeleton, the growing rodent skeleton is changing over time. Age differences of as little as one week across studies become an important intrinsic variable, potentially influencing outcome. Between 5 and 32 weeks of age, the age range typically used in rodent spaceflight research (Table 1), there are dramatic reductions in linear and radial bone growth as well as decreases in cancellous bone turnover (Ferguson et al., 2003; Friedl et al., 2007; Glatt et al., 2007; Lotinun et al., 2005; Rickard et al., 2008; Wilson et al., 2021). Based on this knowledge, it is not surprising that impaired radial growth of weight-bearing long bones during spaceflight is less likely to be noted in older animals (Montufar-Solis & Durnova., 1992; Vailas et al., 1992).

Body weight

Body mass changes directly reflect changes in energy balance. Spaceflight has minimal effects on the normal increase in body mass of growing rats (Iwaniec et al., 2009). In contrast, the effects of spaceflight on body mass in mice are highly variable and include normal weight gain, failure to gain weight and weight loss compared to baseline (Blaber et al., 2013; Coulombe et al., 2021; Dagdeviren et al., 2018; Gerbaix et al., 2017; Gerbaix et al., 2018; Hayashi et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2020; Wong et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2013). There is typically a strong positive association between body weight and bone mass during growth (Iwaniec & Turner, 2016). Flight animals are weightless and are not subject to the direct effects of weight on bone. However, other physiological changes induced by a negative energy balance have the potential to impair bone growth during spaceflight (Sainsbury & Zhang, 2012).

Duration of spaceflight

The duration that rats and mice have been in orbit for bone studies has ranged from 4 to 91 days (Tavella et al., 2012; Turner, 1995a) (Table 1). Reduced mRNA levels for bone matrix proteins have been reported in rats after only 4 days of spaceflight. A bone mass deficit was not detected after 4 days but was detected after 10 days (Backup et al., 1994; Vico et al., 1991). These findings indicate that even short-duration spaceflight can alter bone metabolism in rapidly growing rodents. It is reasonable to speculate that bone changes are closely associated with duration of flight, but the precise relationship has not been firmly established.

Physical environment

Rodent spaceflight research has made use of numerous housing configurations (Choi et al., 2020). Differences in the physical environment across spaceflights include diet, individual versus group housing (Table 1), cage dimensions, presence or absence of nest boxes/huts, temperature range and variation, humidity, magnitude of skeletal loading during launch and during reentry, and preflight and postflight housing (Blottner et al., 2009). Additionally, transport of mice to and from the International Space Station with live return involves multiple changes in housing configurations and can require several days. Direct in-flight comparisons of housing configurations are not available. However, differences between vivarium-housed and flight control-housed rodents are frequently observed (Cahill et al., 2025; Dagdeviren et al., 2018; Gerbaix et al., 2017; Vailas et al., 1992; Vico et al., 1993; Westerlind & Turner, 1995; Zerath et al., 1996). The latter findings imply that the way rodents are housed during spaceflight could influence the skeletal response to microgravity. Housing density and temperature may be particularly important.

Depending upon housing configuration, rodents have been housed individually or in groups. Social isolation during individual housing was suggested as an explanation for variability across rodent studies. Specifically, there is indirect evidence that group housing confers partial resistance to the negative effects of spaceflight on bone formation (Wronski et al., 1998). This possibility is supported by ground-based studies in mice (Tahimic et al., 2019) but has not been stringently evaluated in flight experiments.

Rats, like humans, are homeotherms while mice are obligatory daily heterotherms. Mice are commonly housed at room temperature (20–22 °C), which has a dramatic effect on their metabolism (James et al., 2023). Housing growing mice below their thermoneutral range (28–33 °C) results in cold stress-induced adaptive thermogenesis. Under thermoneutral conditions mice display mean diurnal energy expenditure rates 1.8 times higher than their basal metabolism, a value very similar to humans (Fischer et al., 2018). Below thermoneutrality, mouse metabolism exceeds the human equivalent. To place this in perspective, mice housed at room temperature on Earth fed ad libitum require 30–40% more energy for normal growth than mice housed at thermoneutral (Iwaniec et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2019).

Glatt et al. (Glatt et al., 2007) demonstrated that, when housed at room temperature, linear bone growth ceases in male and female C57BL6J mice by 6 months of age, whereas peak cancellous bone volume fraction (bone volume/tissue volume) is achieved much earlier (by 2 months of age), and declines thereafter (Fig. 5). There is strong evidence that this premature cancellous bone loss is a consequence of metabolic changes induced by chronic cold stress. Housing mice at thermoneutral increases peak bone mass and greatly reduces premature cancellous bone loss (Iwaniec et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2019).

Room temperature-induced premature cancellous bone loss is attenuated by group housing, likely due, in part, to huddling (Sattgast et al., 2023). Huddling and postural changes are strategies that mice use to reduce their requirement for adaptive thermogenesis. These strategies may be less effective in microgravity, depending upon housing configurations (presence or absence of nest boxes/huts to facilitate huddling), temperature range they are exposed to and rate of air exchange. The average housing temperature on the International Space Station portion of RR-1 was 26 °C (Choi et al., 2020), only slightly below the thermoneutral range. However, Ucp1 expression, a marker for sympathetic system-mediated adaptive thermogenesis, was increased during spaceflight compared to ground controls (Wong et al., 2021). The higher Ucp1 was equivalent to a 4 °C difference in vivarium housing temperature, a difference shown to have a profound effect on cancellous bone mass (Sattgast et al., 2023). It is reasonable to hypothesize that the large variation in environmental temperature within and across mouse spaceflight studies contributes to variability. Differences in housing temperature influence the skeletal response to ground-based skeletal unloading (Peurière et al., 2023; Wong et al., 2023), but the impact of temperature differences has not been carefully evaluated during spaceflight.

Core body temperature in rodents is higher than the surroundings. On Earth, energy (heat) is transferred across a temperature gradient by radiation, conduction and convection. Convection, an important mechanism for energy homeostasis, is absent in microgravity. The specific effect this may have on bone physiology is unknown.

Skeletal location

On Earth, the entire skeleton is subjected to static gravitational loading. Consequently, all bones bear weight. However, the magnitude of static loading varies among bones in proportion to the body mass they support. In addition to static gravitational loading, portions of the skeleton on Earth are subjected to dynamic gravitational (ground reaction forces) as well as static and dynamic muscle loading (Kohrt et al., 2009). Again, the magnitude of the loads varies with skeletal location. During orbital spaceflight gravitational loading effectively ceases. In contrast, the skeleton continues to experience mechanical loading via muscle loading and interstitial fluid flow. Representative bones located in the axial and appendicular skeleton subjected to varying levels of gravitational and muscle loading have been evaluated in rodents following spaceflight (Table 1). Understanding the effects of spaceflight on bones having different loading histories is important for isolating the effects directly mediated by reduced mechanical loading from those mediated by changes in hormones, cytokines, neuronal signals or other effectors (e.g., fluid flow).

Axial skeleton

The axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs) consists of flat and irregular bones. The principal mechanism of rib bone accrual is endochondral ossification. The flat bones of the skull and portions of the mandible are formed by intramembranous ossification. Two outer layers of cortical bone in calvaria enclose diploe, a specialized form of cancellous bone. There is not a distinct bone marrow cavity, but the cancellous bone within flat bones contains red marrow. Bones of the skull are not weight-bearing and only minimally load-bearing but are of particular interest in spaceflight studies because microgravity-induced fluid shifts result in increased blood and tissue fluid pressure in the head (Seidler et al., 2024). Bone volume of flat bones could increase by addition of bone onto outer and/or inner periosteal bone surfaces. During growth, the size of flat bones can increase by a combination of interstitial growth at the sutures and periosteal addition. Finally, changes in cancellous bone volume fraction due to modeling during skeletal growth in subadults and maintenance in adults could influence bone mass as well as microarchitecture.

Bones of the axial skeleton normally subjected to dynamic gravitational loading are thought to be particularly susceptible to the negative effects of microgravity (Vico & Hargens, 2018). In humans, bones of the vertebral column fall into this category whereas the ribs and the skull do not. Gravitational loading of the rodent skeleton shows similarities as well as differences compared to the human skeleton. Similarities with humans include ribs, which are not dynamically loaded. In contrast to humans, the vertebral column of rodents does not experience dynamic gravitational loading although it is subjected to muscle-mediated dynamic loading.

Changes in cortical thickness were not detected in calvaria of rodents following spaceflight (Keune et al., 2015; Macaulay et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2013). Changes in gene expression have been detected (Macaulay et al., 2017; Siamwala et al., 2022), but the functional significance of these alterations remains unclear.

Spaceflight did not alter the rate of periosteal bone formation or total Ca, P, and hydroxyproline concentrations in ribs of rats (Simmons et al., 1983) or microarchitecture of ribs in mice (Maupin et al., 2019). In contrast, bone volume in flight rats decreased in the dynamic weight-bearing acetabulum of the pelvis and bone formation was reduced in the cancellous compartment of the pelvic metaphysis (Zerath et al., 2000).

The effects of spaceflight on vertebrae have been studied in rodents, with an emphasis on the lumbar region. Cancellous bone volume fraction in vertebrae is reported to be lower (Gerbaix et al., 2017; Keune et al., 2016; Maupin et al., 2019; Zerath et al., 2000) or remain unchanged (Cahill et al., 2025; Keune et al., 2015; Lim et al., 2025; Rodionova, 2003; Vico et al., 1993; Wronski et al., 1998) compared to ground controls.

Appendicular skeleton

The appendicular skeleton includes the long bones of the upper and lower limbs, as well as the girdles (e.g., pelvis) that connect long bones to the axial skeleton. The pelvis is a flat bone with growth characteristics similar to ribs. Long bones increase in length by endochondral ossification and in cross-sectional area by addition of bone onto periosteal bone surfaces. As in humans, the hindlimbs of rodents are subjected to dynamic gravitational loading. In humans, appendicular long bones in the forelimbs (e.g., humerus, radius, ulna) do not undergo dynamic weight bearing. In contrast, the long bones of the forelimbs of rodents bear weight. Spaceflight appears to have little or no effect on longitudinal long bone growth in rodents. However, several studies in rats and mice report spaceflight-associated decreases in radial bone growth in weight-bearing long bones (Lloyd et al., 2015; Morey & Baylink, 1978; Wronski et al., 1981). Other studies failed to detect changes (Gamboa et al., 2021; Maupin et al., 2019).

Cancellous bone mass and architecture have been commonly assessed in weight-bearing bones, with an emphasis on hindlimb long bone metaphyses. On occasion, epiphyses were also evaluated (Keune et al., 2015; Rodionova, 2003; Vico et al., 1993). As with cortical bone, cancellous bone volume fraction in long bones was reported to be lower (Cahill et al., 2025; Keune et al., 2016; Zerath et al., 2000) or unchanged (Sinha et al., 2002) compared to ground controls.

Cellular mechanisms for bone changes associated with spaceflight

Cortical bone deficits, when observed in long bones of growing rodents following spaceflight, are primarily due to decreased bone formation. This conclusion is based on several independent lines of evidence: 1) cross-sectional bone measurements (area or volume), when reported, are equal or higher than in baseline controls but lower than ground controls, indicating that the bone deficit in flight animals is due, at least in part, to a net reduction in bone accrual, 2) medullary bone measurements (area or volume), when reported, are not greater than in baseline or ground controls, indicating bone deficits in the flight animals are not due to a net increase in endocortical bone resorption, 3) periosteal bone formation rates in flight animals, when reported, are lower than in ground controls, and 4) mRNA levels for bone matrix proteins, when reported, are lower in flight animals than in ground controls (Cavolina et al., 1997; Lloyd et al., 2015; Morey & Baylink, 1978; Wronski & Morey, 1983).

Spaceflight-induced deficits in cancellous bone in long and irregular bones in rodents could result from one or more of the following five cellular mechanisms: 1) increased resorption of growth plate cartilage by chondroclasts, 2) decreased addition of bone matrix by osteoblasts onto primary spongiosa, 3) decreased addition of bone matrix by osteoblasts onto secondary spongiosa, 4) increased osteoclast-mediated resorption of primary spongiosa, and/or 5) increased osteoclast-mediated resorption of secondary spongiosa. The stage of skeletal maturation and precise anatomical location influence the potential underlying cellular mechanism(s). Chondroclast-mediated resorption of calcified growth plate cartilage is responsible for generating the cartilage templates for formation of cancellous bone during longitudinal bone growth. We are unaware of studies directly evaluating effects of spaceflight on chondroclast-mediated resorption. However, Vico et al. reported spaceflight lowers the quantity of primary as well as secondary spongiosa in tibia of growing rats (Vico et al., 1991). These authors also presented evidence that spaceflight-associated changes in cancellous bone microarchitecture in irregular bones result from enhancement of resorption (based on increased osteoclast-lined bone perimeter) at some locations and decreased formation (based on a reduction in osteoblast-lined bone perimeter) at other locations.

Few studies have adjusted for longitudinal bone growth to measure the turnover of cancellous bone present at initiation of spaceflight. In a study in ovariectomized Fisher 344 rats, loss of fluorochrome label that was incorporated within cancellous bone (proximal tibial metaphysis and lumbar vertebra) prior to launch was greater in flight rats than in ground controls (Cavolina et al., 1997; Keune et al., 2016). This finding provides unequivocal evidence that bone resorption was locally increased during spaceflight. Indices of bone formation were not altered by spaceflight in either proximal tibial metaphysis or lumbar vertebra. However, periosteal bone formation was reduced in tibia diaphysis supporting the findings of Vico et al. (Vico et al., 1991) that location-specific architectural changes observed following spaceflight are mediated by local changes in bone accrual or turnover balance.

In contrast to ovariectomized rats, spaceflight resulted in a cancellous deficit in the humerus of male Fisher 344 rats due to decreased bone formation. Osteoclast-lined bone perimeter and fluorochrome label loss did not differ between flight and ground control rats (Turner, 1995b; Westerlind et al., 1997). The methodological similarities (strain and methods for assaying bone cells, bone formation and bone resorption) in these two studies with different outcomes further argues against there being a uniform cellular mechanism that mediates all skeletal responses to spaceflight.

Molecular mechanisms

Identifying the molecular mechanisms mediating the skeletal response to spaceflight is challenging, in part, because of the variability in response. Spaceflight in male rats is associated with reduced plasma testosterone (Amann et al., 1992) and growth hormone (Kaplanskiĭ et al., 2003) and increased corticosterone (Ahlers et al., 1976), insulin, epinephrine and norepinephrine (Macho et al., 1993). While these effectors can clearly influence bone metabolism, the individual and combined alterations do not easily explain the pattern of bone changes associated with spaceflight. It is also important to note that hormones and neurotransmitters can change quickly, such that measurements performed on post-flight blood and tissue samples may not reflect in-flight levels. The same concern is applicable to post-flight measurement of expression levels for rapid response regulatory genes. This limitation can be negated by in-orbit collection of blood and tissue samples. However, many of the caging configurations aboard the International Space Station, Space Shuttle and Biosatellites were fully automated with no access to the animals during flight. Some of the configurations flown on the International Space Station allow crew access to the rodents but time constraints and technical issues limited in-orbit blood and tissue recovery.

Summary

Studies performed in rodents provide unique insight into adaptation of the mammalian skeleton to microgravity during growth and maturation. While it is well established that spaceflight results in bone loss in astronauts, few studies have demonstrated unequivocally that this occurs in rodents. Spaceflight is often shown to result in a bone mass deficiency at select locations in rodents when compared to ground controls. However, relative osteopenia during growth is not sufficient evidence for bone loss. To establish bone loss, it is essential to demonstrate that there is less bone following flight than was present at launch. This could be accomplished by either repeat measurements (as in astronauts) or by including a baseline control group.

There is strong evidence that the rodent skeleton is not uniformly influenced by spaceflight. In general, changes in bone mass, architecture and strength during spaceflight occurred at skeletal sites that normally undergo dynamic gravitational loading. However, load-bearing bones such as lumbar vertebrae are sometimes impacted. While the magnitude of deficits in bone mass appears to increase with increasing duration of spaceflight, a precise relationship between flight interval and response is yet to be established.

Cellular mechanisms mediating cortical and cancellous bone deficits have been investigated using rodents. Cortical bone deficits are primarily, if not exclusively, due to reduced periosteal bone formation. Cancellous bone deficits are due to a negative bone turnover balance, where resorption of mineralized tissue (calcified cartilage or bone) exceeds bone formation. Whether the negative balance at a particular skeletal site is due to reduced formation, increased resorption or a combination of both has rarely been established with certainty and may depend upon intrinsic and extrinsic factors.

Differences across spaceflight studies evaluating the same regions of the skeleton are common. Many studies were underpowered to reliably detect biologically meaningful changes in bone mass and architecture. However, there is evidence that coexisting intrinsic and extrinsic factors associated with spacecraft environments that either act independently or in consort with microgravity contribute to variable responses. Comparisons between baseline, vivarium, ground control, and flight rodents identify housing as an important extrinsic variable. Failure to consider that rodents are social animals and that mice are obligatory daily heterotherms may contribute to variability and potentially misinterpretation of results. Also of great concern is the frequent finding in mouse studies of weight loss in flight animals and ground controls. Weight loss is important because of the well-established detrimental skeletal effects of a negative energy balance, especially during growth.

All spaceflight studies with rats and most studies with mice were performed using growing animals, varying in age from 1 to 6 months old. While accruing bone, these animals are changing rapidly (slowing) in their overall growth and bone turnover rates. Minimal age-related changes in bone metabolism are likely to occur over a year in middle-aged humans whereas a one-week difference in age can be important in growing rodents. This notable feature of rodent physiology is a variable that compromises our ability to perform reliable cross study comparisons. Body weight and overall size were important factors driving the choice of animal age in rats. It is less clear why rapidly growing and adolescent animals continue to be preferred for mouse spaceflight studies. Skeletal maturation drives architectural changes in bones of young rodents, whereas changes in astronauts are likely driven by adaptive modeling imbalances. Mice and rats ≥9 months old (middle age) would better reflect the life stage of astronauts and should be considered for future flights. Mice and rats are useful model organisms but direct comparison of results from studies in growing rodents to astronauts should be made with extreme caution.
