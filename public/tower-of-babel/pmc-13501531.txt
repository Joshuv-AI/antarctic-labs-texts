Weighted epigenetic site correlation network analysis of chemotherapy impacts on osteosarcoma cancer survivors’ male fertility, sperm, and endocrine parameters
Authors: Margarett Shnorhavorian, John K Amory, Stephen M Schwartz, Smita Bhatia, Saro Armenian, Holcombe E Grier, Eric J Chow, Rashmi Joshi, Daniel Beck, Michael K Skinner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impacts of chemotherapy exposure on adolescent male osteosarcoma survivors as adults were investigated using a number of physiological parameters, with a focus on chemotherapy, reproduction, and sperm. The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) clinical sites (protocol ALTE16C1) of previously collected and stored sperm samples were obtained for this analysis. The epigenetic DNA methylation alterations in sperm were assessed in 176 control adult male patients’ sperm, and 183 chemotherapy-exposed osteosarcoma survivors’ adult male sperm were provided by COG sites. The current study used a weighted gene co-expression network analysis computational approach that was adopted for use as a weighted epigenetic site correlation network analysis. This analysis identified correlation coefficients between differential DNA methylation regions and other DNA methylation sites with physiological and chemotherapy parameters. Module–trait relationships in the data were determined for epigenetic modules, which highly correlated with sperm parameters, reproductive hormones, and several chemotherapies (e.g. cisplatin). Gene associations of these epigenetic sites were identified and correlated to chemotherapy-associated genes and pathways, as well as reproductive parameters. Observations demonstrate dramatic impacts of adolescent chemotherapy on later adult life sperm epigenetics. Clearly, epigenetics has the potential to mediate the actions of chemotherapy on later life physiology and may potentially impact future generations through epigenetic transgenerational inheritance mechanisms, but this needs further investigation.Keywords: chemotherapy, osteosarcoma, whole epigenome-wide correlation network analysis, WGCNA, epigenetics, DNA methylation, sperm, male infertility, endocrine, gene associations

Introduction

This project was initiated by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) to provide previously collected sperm samples from adult survivors of adolescent chemotherapy. The pubertal actions of chemotherapies (e.g. cisplatin) on adult sperm epigenetics and correlations with various physiological parameters are investigated. Among the most important challenges faced by male childhood and adolescent cancer survivors is the reproductive toxicity of cancer chemotherapy. Epigenetic DNA methylation changes are potentially a primary initial mechanism of action of these chemotherapy drugs on testicular function. A better understanding of these effects will allow for the identification of high-risk patients and the development of better prevention strategies for testicular toxicity. Drawing upon the large number of osteosarcoma survivors treated by COG, this study comprehensively evaluated the effects of cisplatin with other chemotherapies on spermatogenesis and steroidogenesis among childhood survivors treated with modern chemotherapies for osteosarcoma. The study hypothesis was that using a large clinical population dataset, epigenetic processes will be identified that provide insights and molecular mechanisms for the actions of adolescent chemotherapy on later life impacts on sperm epigenetics and fertility.

As context for the current study, the focus in the original US National Institutes of Health grant application was on cisplatin, which is one of the primary chemotherapies used in adolescent men with bone osteosarcoma cancer [1]. The three primary chemotherapies used in regimens involved methotrexate, doxorubicin (Adriamycin), and cisplatin (Platinol), defined as MAP. In the past, an additional therapy used within the regimen was ifosfamide, MAPI. The companion studies were designed to assess the actions of MAP and MAPI on later-life cancer survivor reproductive hormones and sperm epigenetics. Companion studies assessed the impacts on male fertility, reproductive hormones, and sperm epigenetics. The current study focused on the analysis of adolescent chemotherapy exposure on sperm epigenetics to make direct associations with all the specific physiological parameters examined.

Previous studies on humans with a smaller patient population suggested an epigenetic impact of adolescent chemotherapy on adult sperm epigenetics [2]. A rodent study also identified the adolescent exposure impact of the chemotherapy ifosfamide on adult rat sperm epigenetics, and these epigenetic impacts were transmitted transgenerationally and inherited in subsequent generations [3]. The human observation that sperm epigenetics is impacted over a decade later indicates the germline stem cell may be impacted, so potentially can influence future generations. However, such analyses remain to be investigated. The current study was designed to expand on these previous studies to assess the impacts of a wide variety of different adolescent chemotherapy regimens on adult male osteosarcoma survivors’ sperm epigenetics. The expanded population for the molecular analysis included 183 chemotherapy and 176 control nonchemotherapy males between the ages of generally 18–30 years, but also some 30–48 years.

Sperm samples were collected by investigators within the COG-associated clinical institutions. The adult osteosarcoma cancer survivors following adolescent chemotherapy provided sperm samples for the current study to assess the epigenetic impacts on sperm, as described in the companion studies [4,5]. The current study used the data and observations of these companion analyses to validate and identify epigenetic correlations between specific variables or parameters and the specific epigenetic associations identified. The companion paper [5] provides the rationale and physiological data used in this WGCNA analysis.

The patient population involved adolescent males with osteosarcoma exposure to a number of different regimens of chemotherapy (e.g. cisplatin) and survivors’ sperm collections as adults, primarily between 19 and 30 years of age [5]. The osteosarcoma survivors provided sperm, and the samples were collected and stored frozen with the assistance of the COG-associated clinical investigators, as outlined in the Methods (Supplementary Table S1). The sperm samples were shipped frozen to the laboratory of Dr John Amory, University of Washington, for storage (i.e. −80°C) and sperm analysis [4]. The sperm were then shipped frozen to the laboratory of Dr Michael Skinner for epigenetic analysis. A study was performed to assess the impacts of a variety of chemotherapy regimens on the epigenetics of the sperm [5]. A companion study assessed the impacts of adolescent chemotherapy on sperm numbers and parameters, as well as blood hormone levels in the osteosarcoma survivors, on the sperm epigenetics [4]. The current study used this epigenetic data to assess gene associations and networks, as well as to allow associations with a variety of physiological parameters.

A wide variety of variables and parameters (e.g. sperm number, head and tail, hormone level, and type of chemotherapy) were identified within these two previous studies that impacted adult sperm epigenetics. The specific chemotherapy used for the various adolescent patients primarily involved a group of methotrexate, cisplatin, doxorubicin, and ifosfamide regimens and was found to have similar impacts on the adult sperm epigenetics [5]. The endocrine impacts were identified and correlated to a variety of sperm parameters in the cancer survivor population [4]. Additional physiological parameters of the patient population and lifestyle variables were also identified and assessed within the patient population. Although many of the major correlations of variables and epigenetics were made in the initial studies [4,5], the current study provides a more quantitative and thorough analysis.

A previously developed protocol termed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is a computational tool used to assess gene expression data and identify gene networks and integrate various parameters to the molecular variables of the study [6,7]. The WGCNA has been used to investigate molecular gene network data with physiological or disease states [8,9]. The computational approach can also be used for epigenetic data and be used in a weighted epigenetic site correlation network analysis (WECNA) [10,11]. The current study used a WGCNA approach to focus on a comparison of epigenetic sites with variable physiological parameters for a WECNA analysis. The sperm epigenetic data was obtained with a methylated DNA immunoprecipitation (MeDIP) analysis to investigate the majority of the epigenome (90%–95%) [12], and used this genomic information to correlate with the variable parameters of the previous studies [4,5] for a WECNA assessment with the current study observations. This analysis allows sub-populations of DNA methylation genomic sites with epigenetic alterations to be correlated with a variety of physiological parameters within this current chemotherapy study. The previous companion studies with a comparison of the different chemotherapy regimens and associated parameters [5], and reproductive variables of sperm and hormones [4], were assessed in the current study with WECNA. The objective is to identify the epigenetic correlations with the various parameters measured, and provide insights into gene network associations and analysis.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated early life (e.g. pubertal chemotherapy exposure can promote later life adult pathologies in humans [13–16]). In addition, rodent studies have also demonstrated chemotherapy impacts on later life disease [3,17]. The current study observations demonstrate dramatic impacts of adolescent chemotherapy on later life sperm epigenetics and male infertility [4,5]. The later life impacts appear to be mediated by the epigenetics influenced by the chemotherapy exposure [2, 3, 18–21]. An expanded study with an increase in the human sample population was initiated to examine the impacts of numerous chemotherapy regimens on adolescent males osteosarcoma survivors and an assessment of sperm epigenetic effects in adult males 18–48 years of age [4,5]. The impacts of numerous chemotherapy regimens on 183 patients were compared to 176 control noncancer males. COG previously facilitated the collection and storage of samples by a number (e.g. 70) of participating institutions for the analysis (Supplementary Table S1). Interestingly, the wide number of chemotherapy regimens was found to promote primarily similar epigenetic DNA methylation impacts on the osteosarcoma survivors [5]. The primary physiological impacts were on sperm abnormalities and reproductive hormones in the osteosarcoma survivors [4]. These observations achieved the goals of the COG project to identify the correlated physiological variables impacted by chemotherapy and specific epigenetic and gene associations. Observations are supported by previous studies on human chemotherapy adolescent exposure demonstrated an epigenetic impact on adult sperm [2,21]. This was also confirmed in a rodent study with the chemotherapy ifosfamide, which also was transmitted transgenerationally to subsequent generations [3].

The current study was designed to investigate the additional correlations with the wide variety of parameters assessed between the epigenetic impacts and chemotherapy regimens investigated. This approach compared all the chemotherapies and control individual sperm epigenetic data (176 control and 183 chemotherapy samples) and used a WGCNA [6,7] with a modified bioinformatic approach for a weighted epigenetic site [e.g. DNA methylation regions (DMR)] correlation network analysis (WECNA) to identify the correlated parameters and epigenetics (Fig. 1). Several parameters and traits measured in the current study were examined. This analysis used a different bioinformatics approach to confirm the impacts of adolescent chemotherapy on adult sperm epigenetics and validated the impacts on the parameters previously observed in the companion studies [4,5].

Using an increased statistic of P < 1e−15, the primary correlated parameters observed were on sperm parameters and reproductive hormones (Fig. 3). The reduced statistics of P < 1e−10 added the additional hormones and chemotherapies correlations. Therefore, the previous observations [4,5] were validated by the current analysis. The correlation coefficients were associated with the adolescent chemotherapies used, sperm parameters and defects, and reproductive hormone alterations in adults, as previously observed [4,5]. Parameters such as age at enrollment, age at collection, height or weight, alcohol use, blood cell analysis, or testosterone had less significant correlation coefficients and less impact (lower association) on the sperm epigenetics (Fig. 5).

The modules of epigenetic alterations (DNA methylation) identified were used to assess the correlations and were common for all the analyses. These modules of the epigenetic genomic sites with DNA methylation alterations were further investigated in the current analysis. The gene associations of the epigenetic sites were identified, and the relation of the genes with gene pathways was assessed (Fig. 7). Cancer-associated pathways were the most predominant, as well as Metabolism (Supplementary Table S2). These observations provide insight into the impacts of adolescent chemotherapy on adult epigenetics and the correlated physiological parameters impacted. Insights include the molecular actions of the chemotherapy exposure on the later impacts on sperm production and parameters, as well as male reproductive hormone production and actions. In contrast, other parameter correlations were not as significant. The gene associations identified can be used to better understand the actions of adolescent chemotherapy on osteosarcoma survivors later in life. The gene associations demonstrated cancer pathways are the most predominant in the sperm for all the chemotherapy exposures assessed (Fig. 7). The observations demonstrated the majority of gene pathways had significant impacts, Supplementary Table S2, such as Pathways in Cancer (Fig. 8), and Metabolism, were the predominant. Therefore, the chemotherapy impacts on sperm epigenetics were similar to the gene pathway impacts in Pathways in Cancer pathways (Fig. 8). This Pathways in Cancer gene pathway is the most appropriate pathway associated with the observations and experimental design. The specific genes associated with the epigenetic DMRs are presented in the Supplementary DMR tables. The specific epigenetic sites, gene associations, and gene pathway impacts are described.

Since alterations in the germline sperm epigenetics potentially can be transmitted through epigenetic transgenerational inheritance to future generations [22,23], precautions need to be considered. A wide variety of pathologies and diseases can be transmitted to future generations through epigenetic transgenerational inheritance [24]. Chemotherapy can promote through the potential actions on the germline epigenetic transgenerational inheritance [2, 4, 5,21]. Therefore, cryopreservation of sperm prior to chemotherapy for assisted reproduction needs to be considered to reduce these issues in our future generations.

Methods

Study population and samples

The patients were generally 18–30 year-old male survivors (77% total population), but some samples from 30 to 48 year-old survivors were used as well (23% total population), of osteosarcoma recruited between 2017 and 2022 from 70 COG sites under protocol ALTE16C1 as indicated in Supplementary Table S1. The samples were stored frozen by COG sites and sent to Seattle Children’s Hospital and the Amory lab at the University of Washington for storage and analysis. These men had been treated for their disease with cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimens, which included cisplatin plus ifosfamide in some cases, when they were 14–20 years of age. Each patient was recruited by in-clinic or mail recruitment protocols. Male survivors were eligible if they met the following criteria: alive, with no evidence of disease; diagnosed with bone or soft tissue sarcoma; off all cancer treatment, including radiation treatment, for at least 2 years; at least 15 years of age at study entry; <21 at diagnosis; had received cisplatin as part of cancer treatment; must not have received any other alkylating agent (Cyclophosphamide, Melphalan, Busulfan, BCNU, CCNU, Chlorambucil, Nitrogen Mustard, Procarabazine, or Thiotepa); must have received all or part of their cancer treatment at one of the collaborating sites; free of any precondition to cancer treatment that could result in infertility; have had no CNS, abdominal, pelvic, or gonadal radiation therapy or total body irradiation (TBI); proficiency in English as designated in patient’s medical record; provided written informed consent or assent, and authorization to access medical records under HIPAA. Note that relapsed patients and patients with a subsequent malignancy (SMN) that are treated with surgery alone for the relapse or SMN were eligible for this study as long as they meet the above criteria. Controls were recruited from among adult men with no history of cancer who had previously participated as controls in the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Seattle WA, USA, ATLAS study [25,26]. These men were re-contacted regarding participation in the current study.

Each patient and control was asked to provide a semen sample via home seminal fluid collection, which we used to allow for ease of subject participation since the sample can be obtained without the individual traveling to a laboratory. Sperm concentration and morphology measures were performed on semen that had undergone liquefication during shipping, consistent with the WHO protocol for semen analysis [27]. For sperm concentration (per ml), each participant’s semen was diluted and assessed by CASA (Computer Assisted Sperm Analysis). Three separate counts were performed and the results averaged. A small volume of semen was washed for making histology smears for morphology assessments, based on 200 sperm. Although sperm motility was not assessed (because assessment of sperm motility requires a fresh sample), count and morphology data nonetheless provide a great deal of information regarding spermatogenesis and abnormalities and both are associated with an increased risk of infertility [28]. All protocols were approved by the Seattle Children’s Hospital institutional IRB committee (#12839 and #13158).

DNA preparation

Frozen human sperm samples were stored at −80°C and thawed for analysis. Genomic DNA from sperm was prepared as follows: One hundred μl of sperm suspension, unless azoospermic, then 200 μl of sperm, was used then 820 μl DNA extraction buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8, 10 mM EDTA pH 8, 0.5% SDS) and 80 μl 0.1 M Dithiothreitol (DTT) were added, and the sample was incubated at 65°C for 15 min. 80 μl Proteinase K (20 mg/ml) was added and the sample incubated on a rotator at 55°C for 2 h. After incubation, 300 μl of protein precipitation solution (Promega, A795A, Madison, WI, USA) was added, the sample mixed and incubated on ice for 15 min, then spun at 4°C at 13 000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube, then precipitated over night with the same volume of 100% isopropanol and 2 μl glycoblue at −20°C. The sample was then centrifuged and the pellet washed with 75% ethanol, then air-dried and resuspended in 100 μl nuclease free water. DNA concentration was measured using the Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). The freeze-thaw and subsequent sonication will destroy any contaminating somatic cells within the sperm collection.

Methylated DNA immunoprecipitation

MeDIP with genomic DNA was performed as previously described and optimized [29] as follows: For each individual sample of human sperm DNA, 1 μg of genomic DNA was used in the MeDIP. Individual sample analysis was performed to maintain inter-individual variation as previously described [30]. The individual genomic DNA samples were diluted to 130 μl with 1× Tris-EDTA (TE, 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA) buffer and sonicated in a Covaris M220 with the manufacturer’s preset program to create fragments with a peak at 300 bp. Aliquots of the samples were run on a 1.5% agarose gel to confirm fragmentation. Sonicated DNA was diluted to 400 μl with 1xTE and heated to 95°C for 5 min, then incubated in ice water for 10 min. Then 100 μl of 5× immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (50 mM Sodium Phosphate pH 7, 700 mM NaCl, 0.25% Triton X-100) and 5 μg of 5-mC monoclonal antibody (Diagenode, Denville, NJ, C15200006-500) were added, and the sample was incubated on a rotator at 4°C overnight. The next day 50 μl Protein A/G Agarose Beads (Dynabeads M-280 Sheep anti-Mouse IgG; 11201D, ThermoFisher Scientific) were prewashed with 1 × PBS (with 0.1% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) and resuspended in the same volume of 1 × IP buffer. The DNA sample-antibody mixture from the previous day was added to 50 μl of the bead slurry and incubated at 4°C for 2 h on a rotator. The bead-DNA-antibody complex was washed 3 times with 1 × IP buffer by incubating on bench top and separating beads using a magnet. After the last wash, the bead-complex was resuspended in 250 μl of digestion buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8, 10 mM EDTA pH 8, 0.5% SDS) with 3.5 μl Proteinase K (20 mg/ml) per sample and incubated on a rotator at 55°C for 2 h. After incubation, DNA was extracted with the same volume of Phenol-Chloroform-Isoamyalcohol and then with the same volume chloroform. To the supernatant from chloroform extraction, 2 μl glycoblue, 20 μl 5 M Sodium Chloride and 500 μl 100% cold ethanol were added. DNA was precipitated at −20°C overnight, then spun for 20 min at 14 000 rpm at 4°C, washed with 75% ethanol and air-dried. Dry pellet was resuspended in 25 μl nuclease-free water and concentration measured using the Qubit ssDNA Assay Kit and Qubit fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

MeDIP-Seq analysis

The MeDIP individual samples were used to create libraries for next-generation sequencing (NGS). The NEBNext DNA Library Kit for Illumina was used starting at step 1.4 of the manufacturer’s protocol to generate double-stranded DNA. After this step the manufacturer’s protocol was followed. Each pool received a separate index primer. The libraries were sent to the WSU Genomics Core in Spokane, WA, USA, for NGS on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 using a PE50 application. All libraries were run and comparisons were performed. Approximately 15–30 million reads were obtained for each sample for comparison.

Sequencing analysis, bioinformatics, and statistics

Basic read quality was verified using summaries produced by the FastQC program [31]. The sequencing reads were first cleaned using Trimmomatic [32]. The reads for each sample were mapped to the GRCh38 human genome using Bowtie2 [33] with default parameter options. The mapped read files were then converted to sorted BAM files using SAMtools [34]. The reference genome was broken into 1000 bp windows. The read depth in each genomic window was used as a measure of the methylation level at the genomic site.

Genomic sites were annotated using the NCBI-provided annotations. The genes that overlapped with methylated sites were then input into the KEGG pathway search [35,36] to identify associated pathways. The genomic site-associated genes were then sorted into functional groups by reducing Panther [37] protein classifications into more general categories. All MeDIP-Seq genomic data obtained in the current study have been deposited in the NCBI public dbGaP database (dbGaP #: phs004788).

WGCNA modified for a WECNA

The WGCNA [38] was performed using the WGCNA R package [39]. Epigenetic analysis was used rather than gene expression, so the WGCNA was modified for a WECNA. All MeDIP-Seq genomic windows were ranked by the mean RPKM read depth across all samples. The top 100 000 sites were chosen for inclusion in the analysis. The size of this subset was chosen to allow for a reasonable read depth to be considered and to limit computational requirements. WGCNA is a parameter-rich analysis, and only limited exploration of parameter variations was performed. Modules were calculated using the blockwiseModules function with the following parameters: maxBlockSize = 15 000, power = 5, TOMType = “unsigned”, minModuleSize = 30, reassignThreshold = 0, and mergeCutHeight = 0.25. The Pearson correlation was calculated for each module. The P-value for each correlation was calculated using the corPvalueStudent function.
