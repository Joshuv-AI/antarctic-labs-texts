Knowledge on the Haemophilia Care Among Healthcare Providers in Tanzania: A Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Stella Samson Rwezaula, Samson D Mtoba, Johnny Mahlangu, Amos Rodger Mwakigonja, Bolanle Iranloye, Oluyemi Akinloye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaemophilia is a rare inherited bleeding disorder associated with recurrent bleeding, disability, and mortality when diagnosis and management are delayed. In low- and middle-income countries, limited diagnostic capacity, access to treatment and gaps in Healthcare Providers’ (HCPs’) knowledge are major contributors to morbidity and mortality. In Tanzania, the recent improvement in haemophilia services highlights the need for systematic evaluation of HCPs’ knowledge and clinical practices.ObjectiveThe study assessed the knowledge of haemophilia care among healthcare providers in Tanzania.MethodsA multicenter hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among HCPs in tertiary and regional hospitals in Tanzania. A structured self-administered questionnaire assessed knowledge on haemophilia, including the pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, treatment, and complications. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics version 27.ResultsAmong 799 HCPs assessed (50.9%) aged 20–29 and (59.2%) males. Nurses were the majority (31.8%), and 75.2% had ≤5 years’ experience. Overall haemophilia knowledge was high (median 83.3%, IQR: 75.9–88.9), strongest performance in general knowledge and weakest in treatment (68.2%, IQR: 54.5–77.3). Most respondents identified haemophilia as inherited (95.6%), non-infectious (93.7%), and recognized prolonged bleeding after injury or circumcision as key-symptoms (>90%). Knowledge varied by cadre, department, and experience (p<0.05); physicians and specialists scored higher than nurses, while health attendants scored lower.ConclusionHealthcare providers demonstrated fairly adequate general knowledge of haemophilia. However, gaps remain in understanding genetic inheritance, acquired haemophilia, and modern treatment strategies, with knowledge variation by cadre, department, and experience, highlighting the need for targeted education across all HCPs groups.Keywords: haemophilia, healthcare providers, knowledge, bleeding disorders, Tanzania

Introduction

Haemophilia is an inherited X- linked bleeding disorder caused by deficiency or dysfunction of coagulation factor VIII (haemophilia A) or factor IX (haemophilia B), which causes haemostasis and a tendency to prolonged or spontaneous bleeding.1–3 Globally, haemophilia A affects approximately 1 in 5,000 live male births, while haemophilia B affects about 1 in 25,000 live male births, with considerable variation in detection and care across regions.4 Haemophilia is classified into Haemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency) and Haemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency). The rare autosomal bleeding disorder caused by Factor XI deficiency is a distinct inherited bleeding disorder, rather than classical Haemophilia. The 2022 World Federation of Haemophilia Annual Global Survey (AGS) reports indicated that 454,690 patients with inherited bleeding disorders (IBD) have been identified globally. However, the haemophilia epidemiology data indicate that 75% of people with inherited bleeding disorders living in low-income and low to-middle-income countries have yet to be diagnosed. The AGS also revealed that 11 billion clotting factor units are available to treat haemophilia A and B globally, but due to financial constraints, these treatments are not equitably accessible for haemophilia patients living in low-income countries.5 Haemophilia treatment includes episodic or prophylactic clotting factor replacement, with prophylaxis being the global standard. Available therapies include plasma-derived, recombinant, extended half-life factors, non-factor therapies, and gene therapy. Despite over 11 billion units of FVIII and FIX concentrates available for use globally, only 19% are available to people with haemophilia (PWH) living in LIC and LMICs who constitute 71% of the global population, where 75% of PWH have limited or no treatment access.5 Although outcomes have improved in high-income countries, people with haemophilia (PWH) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to experience late diagnosis, recurrent joint bleeding, chronic haemophilic arthropathy, transfusion-transmitted infections, and limited access to comprehensive care.5–9 These challenges are further exacerbated by shortages of trained specialists and limited awareness among healthcare providers.

Healthcare providers (HCPs) play a crucial role in early recognition, timely referral, safe surgical and dental care, and long-term management of haemophilia cases.10,11 Adequate knowledge is essential for preventing avoidable complications, particularly in emergency settings, perioperative care, and childhood presentations such as post-circumcision bleeding.12–14 However, studies from LMICs have reported persistent knowledge gaps regarding clinical presentation, diagnosis, inhibitor development, prophylaxis, and multidisciplinary care.5,15–19

In Tanzania, haemophilia care has improved through specialized services at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), increased access to factor concentrates via World Federation of Haemophilia (WFH) humanitarian programs, and collaborations with the Novo Nordisk Foundation and Novo Nordisk Haemophilia Foundation.20

Nevertheless, evidence regarding healthcare providers’ preparedness and knowledge remains limited.4,20–22This study therefore assessed healthcare providers’ knowledge of haemophilia pathophysiology, inheritance pattern, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment, complications, prophylaxis, and genetic counselling in Tanzania.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This multicentre hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted in tertiary and selected regional referral hospitals in Tanzania, including Bugando Medical Centre, Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Mbeya Zonal Referral Hospital, Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre (KCMC), Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute (JKCI), Muhimbili Orthopaedic Institute (MOI), Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), and Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs) such as Morogoro, Maweni (Kigoma), Mwananyamala, Amana, and Temeke Regional hospital. Additionally, some participants from linked district hospitals, health centres, and dispensaries were also invited to participate as shown on Table 1. Muhimbili National Hospital is the national tertiary referral treatment centre, which serves as the country’s main hub for haemophilia diagnosis, comprehensive care, and training.

Hospitals were selected using convenience sampling, whereby tertiary and regional referral hospitals with established haemophilia clinics were purposively included. Healthcare providers from linked district hospitals, health centres, and dispensaries were also invited to participate. This approach enabled inclusion of a broad range of HCPs involved in haemophilia care. However, facilities in regions without established haemophilia treatment centres were not included, which may have introduced selection bias.

Participants included HCPs involved in direct patient care, namely medical doctors, nurses, dentists, physiotherapists, laboratory scientists, pharmacists, and other allied health professionals working in medical, surgical, Paediatric, emergency, and laboratory departments. Eligible participants had at least six months of clinical experience, were actively working at the selected facilities during the study period, and provided informed consent. Those on leave or unwilling to participate were excluded.

The study achieved a 100% response rate (799/799), as all eligible HCPs approached consented and completed the questionnaire. This minimized the likelihood of non-response bias and strengthened the internal validity and representativeness of the findings. As there were no non-respondents, comparison between respondents and non-respondents was not applicable.

Sample Size and Data Collection

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula for cross-sectional studies. In the absence of recent Tanzanian data on healthcare providers’ knowledge of haemophilia, a prevalence of 50% adequate knowledge was assumed to maximize sample size. Using a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence level, and adjustment for non-response, the final sample size was 799 participants.

A stratified random sampling technique, based on professional cadre, ensured representation across healthcare disciplines. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire in English language. The tool was developed following a review of previous studies3,13 And was reviewed by a panel of experts in haematology to ensure the questions were relevant to the Tanzanian context. The questionnaire assessed sociodemographic characteristics, haemophilia pathophysiology, clinical features, laboratory diagnosis, treatment, and complications. It was pretested among HCPs outside the study sites to ensure clarity, relevance, and reliability.

The questionnaire was administered as a paper-based, self-administered instrument during scheduled haemophilia continuing medical education sessions at the participating hospitals. Participants completed the questionnaire immediately after providing written informed consent, and the completed questionnaires were collected on-site by the research team before the educational sessions commenced, resulting in a 100% response rate.

Data Management and Analysis

Completed questionnaires were checked for completeness and consistency before entry into IBM SPSS Statistics version 27, while graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Differences in knowledge scores between two groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test, whereas comparisons across more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

To identify factors associated with knowledge scores, simple linear regression (univariate analysis) was first performed for each independent variable. Variables with a p-value <0.20 in the univariate analysis, together with variables considered clinically and epidemiologically relevant (age group and sex), were entered into a multivariable linear regression model to determine independent predictors of knowledge while adjusting for potential confounders. The independent variables assessed included age group, sex, professional designation, department of the respondent, and duration of employment. Crude beta coefficients (cβ), adjusted beta coefficients (aβ), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and corresponding p-values were reported. Statistical significance was considered at p <0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol. IX/3839) and the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS-REC-08-2021-802) Institutional Review Board (IRB). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study. The study complied with the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Years of work experience were also significantly associated with overall haemophilia knowledge. Participants with 6–10 years of experience demonstrated the highest knowledge scores [median = 85.2%, IQR (77.8, 90.7)], with significant differences observed across experience categories [Kruskal–Wallis χ2 (3), p = 0.041].

Discussion

This study found that Healthcare providers in Tanzania demonstrate adequate general knowledge of haemophilia, particularly in recognizing it as an inherited, non-infectious bleeding disorder and identifying common presentations such as prolonged bleeding after surgery, injury, or circumcision. This finding is consistent with reports from tertiary healthcare settings in other LMICs, where HCPs showed relatively good awareness of haemophilia and its clinical presentation in Nigeria but detailed understanding remained suboptimal in several specific domains.23 However, participants demonstrated poor understanding of genetic inheritance, particularly regarding inheritance risk when the father is affected. Similar deficiencies have been reported in other bleeding disorder settings, where HCPs and carriers often have limited understanding of inheritance patterns, affecting counselling and early diagnosis.24 Recognition of life-threatening complications such as intracranial haemorrhage was only moderate, consistent with evidence showing that while frontline clinicians commonly recognize musculoskeletal bleeding, awareness of less frequent but high-risk manifestations remains limited among non-specialist providers.25

Regarding laboratory diagnosis, most participants correctly identified APTT, mixing studies, and factor assays as essential screening and confirmatory tests. However, nearly half incorrectly believed that PT/INR alone were sufficient for screening. Similar diagnostic misconceptions have been documented in resource-limited settings where basic coagulation tests are more accessible than specialized assays. Recent advances in haemophilia diagnostics have further highlighted disparities between LMICs such as Tanzania and high-income countries. Genetic testing technologies including Next Generation Sequencing, have made significant improvement in early carrier detection, and individualized management. However, these technologies remain largely inaccessible in Africa because of high costs, inadequate infrastructure, and shortages of trained HCPs. Mahlangu et al (2024) highlighted this widening gap, noting that many LMICs continue relying on phenotypic testing while high-income countries increasingly utilize molecular diagnostics, potentially delaying definitive diagnosis and comprehensive management.5 Consequently, some observed knowledge gaps may reflect limited diagnostic capacity rather than lack of awareness alone. Similarly, study by Srivastava et al, (2020) described a trend toward integrated diagnostic approaches combining genetic testing with clinical scoring systems to improve diagnostic precision and reduce delays.26 Limited laboratory capacity in LMICs may therefore reinforce misconceptions such as overreliance on PT/INR testing.

Although most participants recognized replacement therapy as the cornerstone of haemophilia management, fewer than half correctly identified prophylaxis as superior to on-demand therapy. This reflects persistent gaps in knowledge regarding modern haemophilia care, consistent with reviews showing that prophylaxis awareness and implementation remain limited across many LMICs.27 Conversely, studies from high-income settings report greater awareness of emerging therapies such as gene therapy and extended half-life prophylaxis among specialist haemophilia care providers, although knowledge gaps remain among broader clinician populations, indicating that evolving treatment knowledge is a global educational challenge.28

Beyond the clinical consequences, haemophilia and other coagulation disorders impose a substantial economic burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. The high cost of clotting factor concentrates, prophylactic treatment, laboratory monitoring, and management of bleeding-related complications often limits access to optimal care. Economic evaluations have consistently shown that although prophylaxis requires higher initial treatment costs than on-demand therapy, it reduces bleeding episodes, prevents long-term joint damage, improves health-related quality of life, and may provide better long-term value by reducing disability and healthcare utilization. Therefore, improving healthcare providers’ knowledge of evidence-based haemophilia management is essential not only for better clinical outcomes but also for promoting more efficient use of limited healthcare resources.29

Haemophilia management is undergoing a major transformation with the introduction of non-factor therapies such as Emicizumab, gene therapy using adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors, and extended half-life recombinant clotting factors. These innovations have significantly reduced bleeding episodes and improved quality of life. Pipe (2018) emphasized that gene therapy is emerging as a potentially curative treatment approach.30 However, these therapies remain unavailable to many patients in Tanzania and other LMICs because of cost, limited availability, and lack of specialized treatment centres, creating a substantial gap between global best practice and routine care. This disparity may partly explain the limited awareness of prophylaxis observed in this study. Furthermore, personalized medicine approaches, including pharmacokinetic-guided dosing and individualized prophylactic regimens, are increasingly becoming standard practice in high-income countries. According to Blanchette et al, (2022), these strategies reduce bleeding complications compared with traditional treatment approaches.31 Limited exposure to such innovations may contribute to continued reliance on on-demand therapy among HCPs in LMICs.

Comparison with recent African studies demonstrates variability in haemophilia knowledge across settings. Surveys from Nigeria and other West African countries reported variable awareness of clinical manifestations, inheritance pattern, and management practices, findings like those observed in the present Tanzanian cohort.23 These observations suggest that while basic haemophilia awareness exists among HCPs across Africa, targeted educational reinforcement remains necessary.

Socio-demographic analyses revealed that physicians and specialists achieved higher knowledge scores than nurses and health attendants, consistent with findings from other LMIC studies demonstrating greater haemophilia awareness among doctors than allied health professionals.25 This may reflect differences in training and clinical exposure. Department of work and years of experience also influenced knowledge scores, with participants from clinical, surgical, and educational departments performing better than those from pharmacy and laboratory services. Similar patterns have been reported in broader health systems literature, indicating that knowledge gaps regarding rare diseases frequently persist outside specialist settings and emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary haemophilia education across departments.6

Differences across professional cadres and departments may reflect variations in clinical exposure, access to continuing professional development, and incorporation of haemophilia into training curricula. National initiatives to strengthen haemophilia services and training through collaborative programs in Tanzania may also have contributed to the relatively high baseline awareness observed among respondents, particularly because participants were recruited from tertiary centres.20 However, health system strengthening efforts must also address inequities in access to haemophilia care innovations. Stonebraker et al (2020) reported that more than 75% of people with haemophilia worldwide remain undiagnosed or undertreated, particularly in LMIC,4 highlighting both HCPs knowledge gaps and inequities in care.

From an implementation science perspective, these challenges could be addressed through task-shifting interventions and development of regional haemophilia treatment centres. Nevertheless, deficiencies in inheritance pattern knowledge, recognition of emergency bleeding complications, and understanding of advanced treatment strategies remain important concerns. Addressing these gaps will require targeted educational interventions, including continuing medical education focused on genetics, acute care, and emerging therapies, alongside practical training that strengthens diagnostic and management skills in accordance with international standards. Despite the limitation of this study being assessing Healthcare providers with different knowledge level due to their training curriculum, this study provides important insights into haemophilia knowledge strengths and weaknesses among HCPs in Tanzania. It identifies priority areas where investments in education and health system strengthening could improve haemophilia care outcomes in LMIC settings.

Conclusion

This study showed that healthcare providers in Tanzania possess generally good knowledge of haemophilia, particularly its clinical presentation and basic management. However, important gaps remain in understanding genetic inheritance, life-threatening complications, laboratory diagnosis, and modern treatment options. Misconceptions regarding PT and INR use, prophylaxis, and medication use may affect patient outcomes. Knowledge varied across cadres, departments, and experience levels, with physicians and more experienced providers demonstrating higher competency than allied health professionals.

Recommendations

Healthcare providers in Tanzania would benefit from continuous professional development on haemophilia genetics, acute care, and updated treatments. Curriculum updating and integrating haemophilia into pre-service curricula, conducting multidisciplinary workshops and simulations on rare and life-threatening presentations, and establishing mentoring and referral networks would strengthen knowledge, skills, and patient care in Tanzania.
