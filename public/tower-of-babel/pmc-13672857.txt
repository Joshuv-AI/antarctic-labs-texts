Protocol for confocal microscopy-based localization of chorismate mutase protein of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in trans-Golgi compartment
Authors: Vandana Maurya, Sangita Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a confocal microscopy protocol to determine the localization of Mycobacterium tuberculosis chorismate mutase (MtbCM) in trans-Golgi compartment. We co-transfect HEK293 cells with either pEGFP-C1-MtbCM and mCherry-TGOLN2 (trans-Golgi marker) or pEGFP-C1 and mCherry-TGOLN2 as a control, using Lipofectamine 2000. We describe steps for constructing pEGFP-C1-MtbCM and performing co-transfection and image acquisition using confocal microscopy. This protocol provides a representative procedure to determine the subcellular localization of the mycobacterial protein to the trans-Golgi compartment.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Pal et al.1

Before you begin

Mycobacterium tuberculosis has infected 10.7 million people in 2024 and is the leading cause of deaths from a single infectious agent.2 The complete genome sequence of M. tuberculosis has identified several known and putative virulent factors, and many of which are secretory in nature. These secretory proteins are shown to inhibit the protective host immune responses by blocking one or more key cellular processes to favor its intracellular survival and disease progression.1 Our previous study shows that secretory chorismate mutase (Rv1885c) protein of M. tuberculosis (MtbCM) can upregulate IL-10 production in macrophages.1 During infection, the secreted MtbCM protein is localized into the Golgi compartment of macrophages where it interacts with the scaffolding protein, AKAP9 and inhibits phosphorylation of downstream protein kinase, PKCε.1

This protocol demonstrates the localization of MtbCM in trans-Golgi compartment using confocal microscopy. AKAP9, a Golgi resident protein,3 was found to interact with MtbCM.1 Given that MtbCM is secreted during infection of macrophages when overexpressed in M. smegmatis (Msmeg-MtbCM),1 it is possible that MtbCM is translocated to Golgi network where it interacts with AKAP9 and interaction of MtbCM with AKAP9 contributes to its localization to the trans-Golgi network. To investigate the localization of MtbCM in the trans-Golgi compartment, HEK293 cells (human embryonic kidney cells that are routinely used for transfection as these cells are easily cultured and can be transfected with higher efficiency for producing recombinant proteins) were co-transfected with plasmid expressing pEGFP-C1-MtbCM gene and mCherry-TGOLN2 (marker for the trans-Golgi network protein 2) plasmids. HEK293 cells co-transfected with empty vector (pEGFP-C1) and mCherry-TGOLN2 were used as control. After 24 hours of transfection, cells were harvested, fixed, and observed under confocal microscope. It was observed that pEGFP-C1-MtbCM was co-localized with mCherry-TGOLN2. This indicates that MtbCM was localized into the trans-Golgi compartment.

Innovation

The novelty of the present protocol lies in the application to visualize the subcellular localization of chorismate mutase (Rv1885c) protein of Mycobacterium tuberculosis within the host trans-Golgi compartment, specifically through co-localization with TGOLN2. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first detailed protocol describing the intracellular trafficking and trans-Golgi localization of MtbCM in mammalian cells. This methodology can be readily adapted to study other pathogenic proteins that traffic to the trans-Golgi network.

Institutional permissions

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a pathogenic bacterium and requires biosafety level 3 laboratory. So, non-pathogenic M. smegmatis strain mc2155, a widely used surrogate bacterium for characterizing M. tuberculosis protein, was used in this study and the work associated with this strain described in this study was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee at the BRIC-Centre for DNA Fingerprinting and Diagnostics (CDFD), Hyderabad, India.

Cloning of MtbCM gene in pEGFP-C1 plasmid

Amplification and cloning of the MtbCM gene in pEGFP-C1 plasmid for transfection in HEK293 cells

Timing: 5–7 days 1.Amplify the MtbCM gene.a.Amplify the approximately 600 bp MtbCM gene from Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Rv genomic DNA.b.Use the gene-specific forward and reverse primers containing the required cloning sequences.2.Design the primers.a.Incorporate a HindIII restriction site into the forward primer.b.Incorporate a BamHI restriction site into the reverse primer.c.Add the required vector-derived 5′ overhangs to facilitate efficient cloning.3.Perform PCR amplification.a.Denature the genomic DNA initially at 98°C for 3 minutes.b.Perform 30 PCR cycles using the following conditions:i.Denature at 98°C.ii.Anneal at 66°C for 30 seconds.iii.Extend at 72°C for 45 seconds.c.Use a high-fidelity DNA polymerase to minimize amplification errors.4.Purify the PCR product.a.Purify the amplified approximately 600 bp MtbCM PCR product.b.Remove residual primers, nucleotides, enzymes, and other PCR components.5.Digest the PCR product.a.Digest the purified PCR product with HindIII and BamHI.b.Digest the pEGFP-C1 vector with the same restriction enzymes.6.Perform directional cloning.a.Ligate the digested MtbCM insert into the similarly digested pEGFP-C1 vector.b.Use the different HindIII and BamHI ends to ensure directional insertion of the MtbCM gene. Underlined sequences: Represent the restriction enzyme recognition sites introduced into the primers. HindIII: Incorporated into the forward primer and BamHI: Incorporated into the reverse primer. Lowercase gene-specific sequences: Correspond to sequences complementary to the MtbCM gene and enable specific amplification from H37Rv genomic DNA. Please see key resources table for full primer sequence. 7.Transform the ligation mixture.a.Transform the ligation mixture into competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells.b.Select transformants carrying the recombinant pEGFP-C1-MtbCM plasmid.8.Select independent clones.a.Select two independent E. coli transformants carrying the pEGFP-C1-MtbCM plasmid.b.Use these clones for subsequent plasmid analysis.9.Extract plasmid DNA.a.Extract plasmid DNA from the selected clones using the QIAGEN Miniprep Kit.b.Store the purified plasmid DNA at −20°C until further use.10.Confirm the presence of the MtbCM insert.a.Confirm the presence of the MtbCM insert by:i.Performing restriction enzyme digestion.ii.Performing PCR using MtbCM-specific primers.iii.Analyzing the amplified or digested products by agarose gel electrophoresis.11.Verify the insert sequence.a.Perform Sanger sequencing of the positive recombinant clones.b.Confirm the correct insertion and nucleotide sequence of the MtbCM gene.c.Verify that the MtbCM coding sequence is in the correct reading frame with the EGFP sequence.d.Confirm the absence of unintended mutations in the cloned MtbCM sequence.

Confocal images should show overlapping green and red fluorescence in perinuclear Golgi structures, appearing as yellow in merged channels. In contrast, cells expressing pEGFP-C1 alone should display diffuse cytosolic/nuclear fluorescence with minimal overlap with mCherry-TGOLN2. While the individual procedures such as plasmid transfection and confocal imaging are established techniques, the novelty of our protocol lies in the application to visualize the subcellular localization of Mtb chorismate mutase (Rv1885c) within the host trans-Golgi compartment, specifically through co-localization with TGOLN2. To our knowledge, this represents the first detailed protocol describing the intracellular trafficking and trans-Golgi localization of MtbCM in mammalian cells. This methodology can be adapted to study other pathogenic proteins that traffic to the trans-Golgi network.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Count 15–25 cells per experimental condition in each experiment, and perform a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Quantify the percentage of cells exhibiting fluorophore signal localized to the organelle of interest, and present the data as a scatter plot with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM). Normalize the data against the control group, comprising cells co-expressing pEGFP-C1 empty vector and mCherry-TGOLN2. Compare this with the treated group, comprising cells expressing pEGFP-C1-MtbCM and mCherry-TGOLN2. Determine statistical significance between groups using Student’s t test. Consider a p value < 0.05 as statistically significant. Please see Figure 3B.

Limitations

Co-transfection of two plasmids in HEK293 cells presents several limitations. First, variable transfection efficiency can lead to unequal uptake of both plasmids, resulting in inconsistent expression levels between cells. This heterogeneity may complicate downstream analyses, especially when co-localization or interaction studies are intended. Second, plasmid competition for cellular transcriptional and translational machinery may reduce overall expression of each construct. Additionally, differences in plasmid size, promoter strength, and copy number can further influence expression balance. Another limitation is the potential for cytotoxicity or altered cellular physiology due to high DNA load or reagent toxicity, which may impact cell viability and experimental reproducibility. Finally, achieving precise temporal and spatial co-expression is challenging without stable cell line generation, making transient co-transfection less reliable for quantitative or long-term studies.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Unequal plasmid uptake and variable expression

Problem: Transfected cells may not take up both plasmids equally, resulting in heterogeneous co-expression and unreliable data interpretation.

Solution: Optimize the plasmid ratio (typically 1:1 molar ratio) and reduce total DNA quantity to minimize competition. Adjust cell density for better results. Use fluorescent reporters or antibiotic selection markers to confirm and enrich for co-transfected cells.

Problem 2: Low transfection efficiency

Problem: Inadequate delivery of plasmid DNA leads to poor overall expression and weak signal detection.

Solution: Optimize transfection conditions such as DNA-to-reagent ratio, cell viability (>90%), cell confluency (70%–80%), and incubation time. Employ high-efficiency reagents (e.g., Lipofectamine 3000 or PEI MAX) and ensure plasmid DNA is of high purity (A260/280 ∼ 1.8).

Problem 3: Cytotoxicity and reduced cell viability

Problem: High DNA load or reagent toxicity may induce cellular stress, detachment, or death, compromising reproducibility.

Solution: Lower the total DNA concentration and use minimal reagent amounts required for efficient transfection. Include appropriate controls to monitor cell health, and, if necessary, switch to serum-compatible or low-toxicity reagents.

Problem 4: Cell detachment during washing or fixation

Problem: HEK293 cells may detach from the coverslip during washing or fixation, reducing the number of cells available for imaging.

Solution: Seed cells at appropriate density and allow sufficient attachment before transfection. Use poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips if required. During washing, add and remove PBS gently along the side of the well to avoid disturbing the monolayer. Fix cells with freshly prepared 4% formaldehyde for the recommended incubation time.

Problem 5: High background fluorescence

Problem: High background fluorescence may interfere with signal detection and reduce the accuracy of co-localization analysis.

Solution: Wash cells thoroughly with 1× PBS after fixation to remove residual fixative and unbound fluorophores. Use freshly prepared formaldehyde and clean coverslips during sample preparation. Include un-transfected controls or single-color controls to distinguish specific fluorescence from background signal and optimize microscope gain settings accordingly.

Problem 6: Empty vector pEGFP-C1 shows fluorescence in the nucleus and appears co-localized with DAPI

Possible cause: The EGFP expressed from the empty pEGFP-C1 vector is not fused to a targeting protein and therefore remains freely diffusible within the cell. Due to its relatively small molecular size, EGFP can passively diffuse through nuclear pores and distribute throughout both the cytoplasm and nucleus. As a result, fluorescence may overlap with DAPI staining in the nucleus, giving the appearance of nuclear co-localization. In some cases, overexpression of EGFP following transfection may further increase the apparent nuclear signal.

Solution: This pattern is expected for empty vector pEGFP-C1 and serves as a negative control for subcellular localization studies. To distinguish true protein-specific localization from diffuse EGFP distribution, compare the fluorescence pattern of the EGFP-tagged protein construct with that of the empty vector control under identical imaging conditions. Maintain the same laser power, detector gain, and exposure settings for all samples. If the nuclear signal is excessively bright, reduce plasmid concentration during transfection or shorten the expression time prior to imaging to minimize overexpression-related signal accumulation. Quantitative co-localization analysis should be performed only with the fusion construct and relevant organelle markers, using the empty vector as a reference control.
