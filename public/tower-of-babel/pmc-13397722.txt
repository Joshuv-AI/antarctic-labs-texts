Treatment of extracranial malignant rhabdoid tumors in children with surgery and or radiotherapy on a multiagent chemotherapy protocol- insights from a single centre cohort
Authors: Vignesh Subramani, Badira Cheriyalinkal Parambil, Maya Prasad, Venkata Ram Mohan Gollamudi, Sajid Qureshi, Siddhartha Laskar, Nehal Khanna, Jifmi Jose, Mukta Ramadwar, Poonam Panjwani, Vasundhara Smriti, Akshay Baheti, Sneha Shah, Girish Chinnaswamy

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtracranial malignant rhabdoid tumors (eMRTs) are rare childhood cancers with poor outcomes and no optimal therapeutic strategies. We report our experience in children with eMRT treated at a tertiary hospital in India.Materials and methodsRetrospective analyses of children (≤ 15 years) with pathologically confirmed eMRT, treated between January 2011 to June 2023, were performed. The staging was based on whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT and brain MRI. Introduction

Extracranial malignant rhabdoid tumors (eMRTs) are rare, highly aggressive embryonal neoplasms that may affect patients from infancy to adulthood. However, it is most common in infancy and early childhood and is associated with the loss of SMARCB1 and SMARCA4. Beckwith and colleagues first described extracranial rhabdoid tumors as a distinct pathological entity in 1978 [1]. It is characterized by rhabdoid histology, and germline SMARCB1/INI1/hSNF5 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants are identified in approximately one-third of children with MRTs [2]. The tumor was first recognized in the kidney and subsequently in the central nervous system, liver, retroperitoneum, and other soft tissues [3]. Despite advances in diagnostic tools, diagnostic challenges persist owing to the myriad anatomic locations of these tumors, overlapping pathologic findings with other diseases, and diverse immunophenotypic profiles [4]. Historically, these tumors have been linked to poor outcomes despite multimodal therapy, with many patients experiencing rapid disease progression even with aggressive treatment. The published 5-year overall survival (OS) ranges from 15 to 36% [5]. No standard treatment has been established for eMRT because systematic analyses of outcomes from these heterogeneously treated small cohorts, based on a few retrospective reviews, have been difficult. Previous studies have revealed poor prognoses with higher stages, metastasis at presentation, and concurrent CNS disease [6]. Concurrent CNS involvement in patients with rhabdoid tumors most often reflects a synchronous primary central nervous system rhabdoid tumor, typically classified as an atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor (ATRT), particularly in the context of germline rhabdoid tumor predisposition syndrome, rather than representing metastatic spread from an extracranial primary. There is limited information on this rare tumor from the developing world. To gain insight into this disease, we retrospectively reviewed all patients with eMRT diagnosed at our institution from an LMIC setting over a decade.

Materials and methods

This is a retrospective audit of a single institution practice in an LMIC setting. Treatment-naïve children (≤ 15 years) with a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of eMRT, registered at our institute between January 2011 and June 2023, were retrospectively studied for clinical characteristics, outcomes, and prognostic factors. The primary objectives of the study were to assess Event-free survival (EFS) and Overall survival (OS). The secondary objective was to identify prognostic factors.

Information regarding clinical and demographic profiles was obtained from electronic medical records. A diagnosis was made by morphology and immunohistochemistry, revealing a loss of INI1 (also known as SMARCB1) or BRG1 (SMARCA4) expression on immunohistochemistry (IHC). No molecular testing for germline SMARCB1/4 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants was performed in these tumor samples. Patients were staged by whole-body fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-contrast-enhanced computerized tomography (18FDG-PET-CECT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain. To enhance lung evaluation with FDG-PET CT, a breath-hold sequence was incorporated for children older than 5 years; for younger patients, the chest portion of the whole-body CT dataset was reformatted into thin-section lung-window images after image acquisition to optimize pulmonary assessment. The initial chemotherapy regimen delivered was the EFT-2001 protocol (an institutional ethics committee-approved protocol), which consisted of combination drug couplets that were alternatively administered on a 3-weekly schedule (vincristine, ifosfamide, etoposide + vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide) [7]. From 2017 onward, 8 cycles of chemotherapy [alternating cycles of vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide (VDC)/ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide (ICE)] were administered—details in Fig. 1. Local treatment included surgery (wherever R0 resection was feasible) and/or radiotherapy (RT) planned after 4 cycles of chemotherapy (12 weeks). A dose of 55–60 Gy was administered for definitive radiotherapy, whereas 40–45 Gy was delivered postoperatively. No concurrent chemotherapy was administered with radiotherapy. All the children who received radiotherapy for local control received it via conformal techniques with 6 MV photons, delivered via the Rapid Arc Trilogy (under Ketlar). Those patients with metastatic or disseminated disease were palliated upfront after discussion with family and offered oral metronomic chemotherapy (OMCT) with oral cyclophosphamide at 50 mg/m2, oral etoposide at 50 mg/m2, three weeks on and one week off schedule with daily celecoxib (100 mg or 200 mg BD) and oral tamoxifen (10 mg or 20 mg BD) for symptomatic relief along with other palliative measures. Germline genetic testing was not offered to most patients diagnosed in the early years of the cohort; however, its use had increased since 2023, following the establishment of the pediatric cancer genetics clinic.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics were analyzed by descriptive statistics. For survival analysis, events were defined as progression, relapse, development of a second malignancy, or death from any cause. EFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of the event or the last follow-up. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause or the date of last follow-up. Survival was estimated via Kaplan‒Meier analysis, and prognostic factors were analyzed via the Cox proportional hazard model (IBM SPSS v. 24.0). Variables with p values < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Confidence intervals (CIs) for Hazard Ratios (HRs) were derived from the regression model.

Results

Epidemiological and clinical profiles

A total of 96 children were registered with a diagnosis of extracranial MRT during the study period. The median age was 18.5 months (IQR, 0.7–176 months), and the male-to-female ratio was 1:1. Thirty patients (31.3%) had renal MRT, and sixty-six patients (68.8%) had extrarenal MRT. Seventy-three (76%) patients were metastatic at presentation (palliated upfront because of disseminated disease-71, treated with a curative intent-2). Among the two treated patients, metastasis was to non-regional lymph nodes in one (50%), to the lungs in the other (50%). Median duration of symptoms at presentation in the non-metastatic subgroup was 1 month (range, 7 days to 4 months) versus 2 months (range, 10 days to 6 months) in the metastatic subgroup. Testing for SMARCB1 and SMARCA4 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, including both tumor and germline assessments, was not carried out in this cohort—no patients presented with concurrent central nervous system involvement. None of the patients in our cohort received high-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue. The demographic details are presented in Table 1, and the flow diagram is shown in Fig. 2.

Treatment

In the treated study cohort (n = 25), two children received the EFT-2001 protocol, and the remaining 23 children received VDC alternating with ICE. Twenty-two (88%) children underwent local therapy (three children had disease progression before any local therapy). This was surgery only in three (13.6%), definitive radiotherapy (in cases where R0 resection was not possible) in seven (31.8%), and surgery plus radiotherapy in 12 children (54.5%). Among the 15 children who underwent surgery as part of local treatment, two underwent R1 resection, and 11 (73.3%) underwent upfront surgical resection, as most of them were resected before referral to our hospital. RT was delivered to 6 patients under 2 years of age, including 2 infants, using Intensity-Modulated Radiotherapy (IMRT). One child among those who had a primary tumor in the thigh received brachytherapy post-surgery. The median time to delivery of the first or only modality of local therapy from the start of chemotherapy was 14 weeks (range, 3–20 weeks). Similarly, the median time to radiotherapy delivery was 16 weeks (range, 3–20 weeks). Details of the treated patients are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (S1).

Outcomes

Only the treated cohort of 25 patients (Stage I/II-12, Stage III-11, Stage IV-2) were evaluated for outcomes as below. At the time of analysis, 13 patients were alive, and 12 patients were dead. At a median follow-up of 12 months (range, 2—64 months), the median EFS and OS of the treated cohort were 15 months (SE ± 4.05) and 22 months (SE ± 9.23), respectively. Two-year EFS and OS of the treated children were 41.1% (SE ± 10.9) and 45.0% (SE ± 11.2), respectively. Two-year EFS and OS of children < 2 years and ≥ 2 years at diagnosis were 33.0% (SE ± 14.3), 33.0% (SE ± 14.2), and 50.9% (SE ± 15.8), and 62.9% (SE ± 14.9) (p = 0.43), (p = 0.27), respectively. Two-year EFS and OS in early stage disease (stages I-II, n = 12) were 31.3% (SE ± 14.8), 32.4% (SE ± 15.0), and in late stage disease (stages III-IV, n = 13) were 49.9% (SE ± 14.8) and 57.0% (SE ± 15.1), (p = 0.34), (p = 0.26), respectively. For stage III alone (n = 11), these figures were 51.1% (SE ± 15.8) and 59.7% (SE ± 15.9), respectively. In this stage III cohort, eight of the 11 patients were < 2 years old at presentation. Among the two patients with metastatic disease who underwent treatment after detailed family discussions electing curative-intent therapy despite a very guarded prognosis, one had early disease progression, and the other was subsequently lost to follow-up after completing therapy. The median time to progression or relapse was 12 months (range, 4–18 months). In the localized cohort, the type of relapse or progression was local in eight patients (34.7%), metastatic in one patient (4.3%), and combined in two (8.6%) patients. One of the two treated metastatic patients had combined local and metastatic site disease progression.

The nonrelapse mortality in the cohort was 8.0% (n = 2), of which one patient developed pneumonia and acute respiratory failure requiring ventilation and expired in the ICU. The other patient died during follow-up; the cause is unknown. Although the toxicity data are not well documented, there were 13 episodes of culture-negative febrile neutropenia and five documented infections in the treated cohort (coagulase-negative Staphylococcus catheter-related bloodstream infection-1, MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae wound infection-1, varicella infection-1, herpes zoster-2).

Prognostic factors

EFS was affected by the application of local therapy, both surgery (HR-0.31, 95% CI: 0.11–0.87, p = 0.02) and radiotherapy (HR-0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.83, p = 0.02) in univariate analysis. Those who received either surgery or radiotherapy had poorer outcomes than those who received both as part of local therapy (HR 6.01, 95% CI 1.2–28.0; p = 0.02). The significance for surgery (HR-0.10, 95% CI: 0.02–0.43, p = 0.002) and radiotherapy (HR-0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.40, p = 0.002) for EFS was also retained in the multivariable analysis. Both surgery (HR-0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.44, p = 0.003) and radiotherapy (HR-0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.55, p = 0.009) were independent prognostic factors for the OS of the cohort—details in Table 3. Owing to multicollinearity, further analysis of the type of local therapy was not possible. The time to delivery of the first modality of local treatment to the primary site > 16 weeks was associated with poor EFS (HR 3.19, 95% CI 0.91–11.1; p = 0.06).

Discussion

The age distribution in our cohort aligns with that observed in other reported cases of eMRTs, as does the incidence of renal and extrarenal primary sites [8, 9]. However, a study from China reported a higher prevalence of renal MRTs in their cohort [10]. The observed reduction in the proportion of renal primaries compared with some published cohorts is difficult to explain definitively. Nevertheless, it may reflect referral bias, with infants suspected of having rhabdoid tumors preferentially referred to our center and more accurately diagnosed due to specialized histopathological expertise. In contrast, such tumors may be underrecognized and misclassified at peripheral hospitals and treated as non-rhabdoid renal neoplasms. The incidence of metastases was quite high in our cohort (79%) compared with published literature (35.8% in the French cohort and 37.1% in the Chinese cohort); the reason for this is unclear [10, 11]. Again, as alluded to above, our center, being a dedicated pediatric oncology facility, has seen increased referrals, enhanced histopathological expertise, and early biopsies, coupled with comprehensive testing facilities, which could have contributed partly to the observed rise in diagnoses. There was no significant difference in the median duration of symptoms at presentation between the metastatic and nonmetastatic subgroups.

The distribution of local control modalities in our cohort differed slightly from those reported in other series. Radiotherapy was administered to approximately three-fourths of our treated patients, compared with 49% in the French cohort and 18.8% in the Chinese cohort [10, 11]. Similarly, surgical intervention was performed in two-thirds of our cohort, whereas 83% of the French cohort and 73.5% of the Chinese cohort underwent surgery. This likely reflects the location of the primary tumor (76% of the patients had a primary tumor in the head and neck or trunk region), which precluded R0 resection and necessitated the administration of definitive radiation therapy in a substantial 31.1% of our patients. Delayed presentation, the presence of large primary tumors at diagnosis (93% exceeding 5 cm), and diagnostic challenges may have collectively contributed to this scenario. Given the historically poor survival outcomes for patients with rhabdoid tumors and the need for multimodal therapy, radiotherapy was offered even to younger children after detailed discussions with parents regarding prognosis, expected survival, and potential late effects. Protection of organs at risk was prioritized through the use of state-of-the-art intensity-modulated radiotherapy in this cohort, as proton therapy was not available at the institute at that time. However, the long-term effects of therapeutic radiation at such a young age were not studied in our cohort and require prospective evaluation in future cohorts.

Although the survival outcomes in our treated cohort were suboptimal, they concur with those reported in the literature for patients managed under contemporary protocols, including dose-dense strategies [8, 11–13]. Treatment approaches from earlier eras, which often rely on chemotherapy based on the site of the primary tumor, yielded poorer outcomes [14]. Survival in stage III patients matched that of stages I-II patients on a multimodal treatment approach, despite the majority of patients in this subset being in the younger age group. This may be partly due to the early increased use of surgery and radiotherapy as local therapy modalities in these patients, as discussed below. Although gross total resection of the primary tumor is prognostically significant in certain studies, the role of radiotherapy remains unclear in the published literature [8, 11, 14]. The application of local therapy and its modalities influenced outcomes in our cohort, with surgery and radiotherapy both playing crucial roles in improving outcomes. The most favorable results were achieved by patients who received both surgical and radiotherapeutic interventions for local disease management [15, 16]. Despite the small sample size of our study, this underscores the need for heightened attention to provide both surgical and radiotherapeutic interventions for local control whenever feasible in these patients to optimize outcomes. Additionally, the timely application of local therapy is imperative, as shown by a trend toward better EFS with local treatment delivered within the first four months of diagnosis. Although not statistically significant, those with primary tumors in the head, neck, or trunk, with the exception of the liver and kidney, fared worse, partly the type of local therapy administered, precluding surgery in many situations. This emphasizes the requirement of a coordinated, multidisciplinary strategy involving pediatric oncologists, surgeons, and radiation oncologists in the management of rhabdoid tumors from the time of diagnosis, as an integrated approach is vital for synchronizing complex interventions such as surgery and radiation [17]. Following prognostic discussions with families, most of our patients with metastatic disease were managed with upfront palliative care and best supportive care. Among the two patients who received definitive therapy, one showed early progression, and the other completed treatment but was later lost to follow-up.

Our study is limited by its retrospective design and relatively small cohort size, which restricts its statistical power and impedes robust evaluation of prognostic variables. The study also did not evaluate germline 'pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants' in the cohort. The treatment approach in our cohort was heterogeneous, with patients receiving two different chemotherapy regimens. As a tertiary referral center, a substantial proportion of patients originate from distant, low socioeconomic settings and return home after palliation, resulting in considerable loss to follow-up despite scheduled reviews and reliance on local palliative care support after palliation. Consequently, we lack outcome data on these patients’ final status, which limits the completeness of our follow-up and outcome assessment in patients with metastatic disease who were on OMCT; therefore, we restricted our outcome analysis to the twenty-five children who had been offered curative-intent treatment. Nevertheless, given the rarity of MRT in children, our single-institution experience offers valuable insights that can inform prognostication and treatment strategies. Establishing national tumor registries for rhabdoid tumors and studying the genomic landscape, including germline predisposition, could significantly enhance our understanding of these rare malignancies.

Conclusions

Extracranial MRT is a highly aggressive tumor in young children with poor outcomes. Incorporating both surgical resection and radiotherapy into local treatment, where feasible, may improve patient outcomes. Further understanding of the biology of this disease and newer therapeutics are necessary to improve the survival of children with extracranial MRT.
