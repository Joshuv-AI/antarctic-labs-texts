Inverse Scattering Transform for the Massive Thirring Model: Delving into Higher-Order Pole Dynamics
Dongli Luan, Bo Xue, Huan Liu

Abstract
We investigate the inverse scattering problem for the massive Thirring model, focusing particularly on cases where the transmission coefficient exhibits N N pairs of higher-order poles. Our methodology involves transforming initial data into scattering data via the direct scattering problem. Utilizing two parameter transformations, we examine the asymptotic properties of the Jost functions at both vanishing and infinite parameters, yielding two equivalent spectral problems. We subsequently devise a mapping that translates the obtained scattering data into a 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix Riemann–Hilbert problem, incorporating several residue conditions at N N pairs of multiple poles. Additionally, we construct an equivalent pole-free Riemann–Hilbert problem and demonstrate the existence and uniqueness of its solution. In the reflectionless case, the N N -multipole solutions can be reconstructed by resolving two linear algebraic systems.



Keywords:
massive Thirring model; Riemann–Hilbert problem;
N
-multipole solution

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{i}\left(u_{t}+u_{x}\right)+v+|v|^{2}u=0,\\

In this paper, we are preparing to study the inverse scattering transform of the MTM (

Direct scattering problem

The MTM (
1.1
) admits the Lax pair:

\phi_{x}=\mathbf{U}\phi,\quad\phi_{t}=\mathbf{V}\phi,

(2.1)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\zeta^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)\sigma_{3},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\zeta^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}+\zeta^{-2}\right)\sigma_{3},

where the function
\phi=\phi(x,t;\zeta)
is a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function of
x
,
t
, and the spectral parameter
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
. It should be noted that the MTM (
1.1
) is equivalent to the following compatibility condition:

\mathbf{U}_{t}-\mathbf{V}_{x}+\left[\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\right]=\mathbf{0}.

(2.3)

2.1
Jost solutions

We aim to find functions
u(x,t)
and
v(x,t)
that are solutions to Eq. (
1.1
) for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. These functions are required to decay to zero as
x
approaches infinity, with their
x
-derivatives also vanishing at infinity. Additionally, they must adhere to the specific initial conditions, and
u(x,0),v(x,0)
are given functions belonging to an appropriate function space. Our objective is to determine two Jost solutions,
\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)
, which are to fulfill the following boundary conditions:

\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)\sigma_{3}}+o(1),\quad x\to\pm\infty,

(2.4)

where
\theta(x,t;\zeta)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)x+\frac{1}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}+\zeta^{-2}\right)t
. More precisely,

\phi_{-1}(x,t;\zeta)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)},\quad\phi_{-2}(x,t;\zeta)\sim\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)},\quad x\to-\infty,

(2.5)

and

\phi_{+1}(x,t;\zeta)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)},\quad\phi_{+2}(x,t;\zeta)\sim\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)},\quad x\to+\infty.

(2.6)

Define the normalized Jost solutions as

\varphi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)=\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(x,t;\zeta)\sigma_{3}},

(2.7)

which satisfy the differential equation and constant boundary conditions at infinity:

\partial_{x}\varphi_{\pm}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2})[\sigma_{3},\varphi_{\pm}]+\mathbf{Q}\varphi_{\pm},\quad\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\varphi_{\pm}=\mathbf{I},

(2.8)

where the dependence on the variables
(x,t;\zeta)
is omitted here for conciseness and the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is defined as

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}(x,t;\zeta)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\zeta^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right).

(2.9)

The integration of Eq. (
2.8
) results in the following Volterra integral equations:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)=

\displaystyle\left(\varphi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\zeta),\varphi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\zeta)\right)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}+\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2})(x-y)\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\left(\mathbf{Q}\varphi_{\pm}\right)(y,t;\zeta)\mathrm{d}y,

where
\hat{\sigma}_{3}\mathbf{X}=[\sigma_{3},\mathbf{X}]
, consequently,
\mathrm{e}^{\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{X}=\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{X}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{3}}.

A standard assumption in analyzing Volterra integral equations (
2.10
) is that
u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t)

\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for fixed
t
. However, even under these conditions,
\|\mathbf{Q}(\cdot,t;\zeta)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
does not uniformly control as
\zeta\to 0
and
\zeta\to\infty
. This lack of uniform convergence presents challenges in studying the behavior of
\varphi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\zeta)
and
\varphi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\zeta)
as
\zeta\to 0
and
\zeta\to\infty
. To address this issue, we employ two transformations, facilitating the analysis of the spectral problem under varying asymptotic conditions of
\zeta
.

2.1.1
Transformation of the Jost solutions for large
\zeta

Define
\lambda=\zeta^{2}
and the domains

\Omega_{0}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:0<|\lambda|<1\},\quad\Omega_{1}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:|\lambda|=1\},\quad\Omega_{\infty}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:|\lambda|>1\}.

(2.11)

Assume
v(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for fixed
t
, let the transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)
be

\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
v&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right).

(2.12)

The normalized Jost solutions are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)\varphi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\zeta),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)=\zeta^{-1}\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)\varphi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\zeta),

which satisfy the differential equation and constant boundary conditions at infinity:

\partial_{x}\Phi_{\pm}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})[\sigma_{3},\Phi_{\pm}]+\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}+\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2}\right)\Phi_{\pm},\quad\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\Phi_{\pm}=\mathbf{I},

(2.14)

where

\left.\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{Q}_{1}(x,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)&-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{v}\\
v_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|u|^{2}v+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}u&-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\\
\end{array}\right),\\
&\mathbf{Q}_{2}(x,t)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{u}v&-\bar{u}\\
v+\bar{u}v^{2}&-\bar{u}v\end{array}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(2.15)

Eq. (
2.14
) can be integrated to yield the following Volterra integral equations:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda),\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)\right)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}+\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})(x-y)\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\left[(\mathbf{Q}_{1}(y,t)+\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2}(y,t))\Phi_{\pm}(y,t;\lambda)\right]\mathrm{d}y.

According to standard Volterra theory, we present the following theorem:

Theorem 2.1
.

Under the condition that
u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
v_{x}(\cdot,t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for fixed
t
, the normalized Jost solutions
\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
in Eq. (
2.16
) are well-defined for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash[-1,1]
. Specifically, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Phi_{+1}(x,t;\cdot)
and
\Phi_{-2}(x,t;\cdot)
can be analytically continued onto the regions
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cap\Omega_{\infty}
, while
\Phi_{-1}(x,t;\cdot)
and
\Phi_{+2}(x,t;\cdot)
can be analytically continued onto
\mathbb{C}^{-}\cap\Omega_{\infty}
. Furthermore, the Volterra intergal equations (
2.16
) admit unique solutions
\Phi_{\pm 1}(\cdot,t;\lambda)
and
\Phi_{\pm 2}(\cdot,t;\lambda)
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash[-1,1]
. The functions
\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)
and
\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)
exhibit the following asymptotic behavior as
\lambda\to\infty
in the domains of their analyticity:

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)}{\Phi_{\pm 1}^{\infty}(x,t)}=\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=(1,0)^{\mathrm{T}},\quad\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)}{\Phi_{\pm 2}^{\infty}(x,t)}=\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{2}=(0,1)^{\mathrm{T}},

(2.17)

where

\Phi_{\pm 1}^{\infty}(x,t)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y},\quad\Phi_{\pm 2}^{\infty}(x,t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y}.

(2.18)

If
v(\cdot,t)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\left.\begin{aligned} &\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda\left[\frac{\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)}{\Phi_{\pm 1}^{\infty}(x,t)}-\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\right]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left[\bar{v}\left(v_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|u|^{2}v+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}u\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{u}v\right]\mathrm{d}y\\
-2\mathrm{i}v_{x}+|u|^{2}v+u\end{array}\right),\\
&\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda\left[\frac{\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)}{\Phi_{\pm 2}^{\infty}(x,t)}-\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\right]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{v}\\
\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left[\bar{v}\left(v_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}|u|^{2}v+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}u\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{u}v\right]\mathrm{d}y\end{array}\right.\bigg).\end{aligned}\right.

(2.19)

2.1.2
Transformation of the Jost solutions for small
\zeta

Assume
u(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for fixed
t
and
\zeta\neq 0
, let the transformation matrix

be

\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
u&\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right).

(2.20)

Consistent with the previous discussion regarding large
\zeta
, we define the new Jost solutions as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)=\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(x,t;\lambda)\varphi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\zeta),

(2.21)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)=\zeta\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(x,t;\lambda)\varphi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\zeta),

\partial_{x}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})[\sigma_{3},\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}]+\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}+\lambda\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{2}\right)\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm},\quad\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}=\mathbf{I},

(2.22)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(x,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)&\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{u}\\

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{2}(x,t)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u\bar{v}&-\bar{v}\\

Eq. (
2.22
) is integrated to derive the following Volterra integral equations:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda),\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)\right)

(2.24)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}+\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})(x-y)\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\left[(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(y,t)+\lambda\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{2}(y,t))\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}(y,t;\lambda)\right]\mathrm{d}y.

.

u(\cdot,t),v(\cdot,t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

for fixed

, the normalized Jost solutions
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
in Eq. (

) are well-defined for
\lambda\in(-1,0)\cup(0,1)
. Specifically, for every

,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{+1}(x,t;\cdot)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{-2}(x,t;\cdot)
can be analytically continued onto the regions

, while
\widetilde{\Phi}_{-1}(x,t;\cdot)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{+2}(x,t;\cdot)
can be analytically continued onto

. Furthermore, the Volterra intergal equations (

The functions
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)
exhibit the following asymptotic behavior as

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)}{\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}^{0}(x,t)}=\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=(1,0)^{\mathrm{T}},\quad\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)}{\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}^{0}(x,t)}=\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{2}=(0,1)^{\mathrm{T}},

2.1.3
Connection between two pairs of normalized Jost solutions

In Theorems
2.1
and

\displaystyle\{\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda),\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)\},\quad\lambda\in\Omega_{\infty},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm 1}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\

(2.30)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-1}&0\\
v(x,t)\lambda^{-1}-u(x,t)&1\end{array}\right)\widetilde{\Phi}_{\pm 2}(x,t;\lambda).

2.1

), we conclude that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{+1}(x,t;\cdot)

\widetilde{\Phi}_{-2}(x,t;\cdot)

\widetilde{\Phi}_{-1}(x,t;\cdot)

\widetilde{\Phi}_{+2}(x,t;\cdot)

2.2
Scattering matrix

In the following, we introduce the scattering matrix. Given that
\mathbf{Q}_{1}(x,t)
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}(x,t)
are traceless, by Abel’s Theorem, we arrive at

\partial_{x}\operatorname*{det}(\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda))=0,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.32)

which signifies that the determinant of
\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
is independent of the spatial variable
x
. Consequently, we evaluate the determinant of
\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
as
x\to\pm\infty
. Eq. (
2.14
) implies:

\det(\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda))=1,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.33)

It is clear that the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}(\lambda)
satisfies the following relationship:

\Phi_{-}(x,t;\lambda)=\Phi_{+}(x,t;\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{S}(\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.34)

where
\Theta(x,t;\lambda)=\frac{1}{4}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})x+\frac{1}{4}(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})t.
To determine the reflection coefficient, which is pivotal for constructing the jump matrix, we revert to the analysis of the original Jost solutions
\{\phi_{+},\phi_{-}\}
. By examining the relationships among these Jost solutions, we derive the relationships between the elements of the scattering matrix and subsequently ascertain the reflection coefficient. It is imperative to investigate the symmetries of the scattering problem and
the scattering problem exhibits symmetry under the involution:
\zeta\to\bar{\zeta}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Considering the symmetries of the original Lax pair (
2.1
), we get the following symmetry relations:

\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)=\sigma_{2}\overline{\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\bar{\zeta})}\sigma_{2},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.35)

Based on the relationship between
\Phi(x,t;\lambda)
and
\phi(x,t;\zeta)
as described in Eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.13
), along with the analyticity of
\Phi(x,t;\lambda)
established in Theorem
2.3
, we conclude that the symmetry relations (
2.35
) can be analytically extended to broader domains.

Corollary 2.5
.

The columns of
\phi_{\pm}(x,t;\zeta)
exhibit the following symmetry relations:

\displaystyle\phi_{+1}(x,t;\zeta)=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\overline{\phi_{+2}(x,t;\bar{\zeta})},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{++}\cup\mathbb{C}^{--}\cup\mathbb{R}\cup\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.36)

\displaystyle\phi_{-1}(x,t;\zeta)=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}\overline{\phi_{-2}(x,t;\bar{\zeta})},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{+-}\cup\mathbb{C}^{-+}\cup\mathbb{R}\cup\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

We consider the symmetries of the transformed Jost solutions
\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
under the involution:
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
.

Corollary 2.6
.

According to the proposition
2.4
, we get the following symmetry relations:

\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)\sigma_{2}\mathbf{T}^{-1}(x,t;\lambda)\overline{\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})}\sigma_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.37)

the symmetry relations can be analytically extended to broader domains:

\displaystyle\Phi_{+1}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)\sigma_{2}\mathbf{T}^{-1}(x,t;\lambda)\overline{\Phi_{+2}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.38)

\displaystyle\Phi_{-1}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{T}(x,t;\lambda)\sigma_{2}\mathbf{T}^{-1}(x,t;\lambda)\overline{\Phi_{-2}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

Moreover, we can also obtain the symmetry relations of
\mathbf{S}(\lambda)
:

\mathbf{S}(\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\sigma_{2}\overline{\mathbf{S}(\bar{\lambda})}\sigma_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.39)

Consequently, the relationships among the elements of the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}(\lambda)
are derived as follows:

\mathbf{S}_{11}(\lambda)=\overline{\mathbf{S}_{22}(\bar{\lambda})},\quad\mathbf{S}_{21}(\lambda)=-\lambda\,\overline{\mathbf{S}_{12}(\bar{\lambda})},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.40)

From these relations, the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}(\lambda)
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}(\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\overline{\alpha(\bar{\lambda})}&{\beta}(\lambda)\\
-\lambda\overline{\beta(\bar{\lambda})}&\alpha(\lambda)\end{array}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.41)

Taking the determinants of both sides of Eq. (
2.34
) results in the following expression:

|\alpha(\lambda)|^{2}+\lambda|\beta(\lambda)|^{2}=1,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(2.42)

Moreover, the integral representations of
{\alpha}(\lambda)
and
{\beta}(\lambda)
can be derived from Eqs. (
2.16
) and (
2.34
) as expressed below:

\displaystyle{\alpha}(\lambda)=1+\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Big[

\displaystyle\big(v_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(u+|u|^{2}v-\lambda^{-1}v-\lambda^{-1}\bar{u}v^{2})\big)\Phi_{-12}

(2.43)

\displaystyle+\big(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\lambda^{-1}\bar{u}v-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2})\big)\Phi_{-22}\Big](x,0;\lambda)\mathrm{d}x,

and

\displaystyle{\beta}(\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta}\Big(\big(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2})-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\lambda^{-1}\bar{u}v\big)\Phi_{-12}+\big(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\lambda^{-1}\bar{u}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{v}\big)\Phi_{-22}\Big)\right](x,0;\lambda)\mathrm{d}x.

(2.44)

Assuming
u(\cdot,0),v(\cdot,0)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\mathbb{C}^{+}

According to Eqs. (
2.33
) and (
2.34

{\alpha}(\lambda)
and

can also be expressed as Wronskians of

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\alpha}(\lambda)=\det\left(\Phi_{+1}(x,t;\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)},\Phi_{-2}(x,t;\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.45)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\beta}(\lambda)=\det\left(\Phi_{-2}(x,t;\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)},\Phi_{+2}(x,t;\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

By substituting the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin expansions of the columns of the modified Jost solutions

into Eq. (

Proposition 2.7
.

As

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\Phi_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{\pm 1}^{\infty}(x,t)&0\\

(2.46)

Furthermore, the functions

and

exhibit the following asymptotic behavior as

and

in the domains of their analyticity:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\alpha(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y},\qquad\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\beta(\lambda)=0,

(2.47)

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\alpha(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y},\,\quad\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\beta(\lambda)=0.

Define the reflection coefficient as

\displaystyle{\gamma}(\lambda)=\frac{{\beta}(\lambda)}{{\alpha}(\lambda)},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(2.48)

and denote

\mathcal{N}
such that

\begin{cases}f^{(n_{1})}(\lambda_{1})=f^{(n_{1})}_{1}(\lambda_{1}),&\quad n_{1}=0,\ldots,m_{1},\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\vdots\\
f^{(n_{N})}(\lambda_{N})=f^{(n_{N})}_{N}(\lambda_{N}),&\quad n_{N}=0,\ldots,m_{N}.\end{cases}

(2.63)

This completes the proof of Eqs. (
2.59
) and (
2.60
).

3
Inverse problem

In Section
2
, we have established the direct scattering map:

\mathcal{D}:\{u(x,0),v(x,0)\}\mapsto\left\{{\gamma}(\lambda),\left\{\lambda_{k},m_{k},\left\{f^{(n_{k})}(\lambda_{k})\right\}_{n_{k}=0}^{m_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{N}\right\}.

(3.1)

In the following, we will now turn our attention to the inverse scattering map:

\mathcal{I}:\left\{{\gamma}(\lambda),\left\{\lambda_{k},m_{k},\left\{f^{(n_{k})}(\lambda_{k})\right\}_{n_{k}=0}^{m_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{N}\right\}\mapsto\{u(x,t),v(x,t)\},

(3.2)

which can be characterised by a
2\times 2
matrix RH problem.

3.1
Riemann–Hilbert problem

Consider a piecewise meromorphic function
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\Phi_{+1}(x,t;\lambda),\frac{\Phi_{-2}(x,t;\lambda)}{\alpha(\lambda)}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\frac{\Phi_{-1}(x,t;\lambda)}{\overline{\alpha(\bar{\lambda})}},\Phi_{+2}(x,t;\lambda)\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{-}.

The following behavior of
\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)
holds as
\lambda\to\infty
within its analytic domains,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)\to\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{+1}^{\infty}(x,t)&0\\
0&\Phi_{+2}^{\infty}(x,t)\end{array}\right)=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\infty}(x,t).

(3.4)

To normalize the boundary conditions, we define

\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\infty}(x,t)\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}.

(3.5)

From this, we can formulate a
2\times 2
matrix RH problem.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 3.1
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
that satisfies the following properties:

•

Analyticity:

\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)
is analytic in
\lambda
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N},\bar{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\lambda}_{N}\}\right)
;

•

Residues:
For each
k=1,\ldots,N
,
\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)
has multiple-poles of order
m_{k}+1
at
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
and
\lambda=\bar{\lambda}_{k}
, with residues satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle\underset{\lambda_{k}}{\operatorname*{Res}}(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{0},\frac{[f(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\mathbf{M}_{1}(x,t;\lambda)]^{(m_{k}-n_{k})}|_{\lambda=\lambda_{k}}}{(m_{k}-n_{k})!}\right),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\underset{\bar{\lambda}_{k}}{\operatorname*{Res}}(\lambda-\bar{\lambda}_{k})^{n_{k}}\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{[\lambda\overline{f(\bar{\lambda})}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\mathbf{M}_{2}(x,t;\lambda)]^{(m_{k}-n_{k})}|_{\lambda=\bar{\lambda}_{k}}}{(m_{k}-n_{k})!},\mathbf{0}\right),

(3.7)

for
n_{k}=0,\ldots,m_{k}
;

•

Jump:

\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)
exhibits a jump across the oriented contour
\mathbb{R}
given by:

\mathbf{M}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{M}_{-}(x,t;\lambda){\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

(3.8)

where
\mathbf{M}_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}\epsilon),
and the jump matrix
\mathbf{J}(x,t;\lambda)
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\gamma(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\\
\lambda\overline{\gamma(\bar{\lambda})}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}&1+\lambda|\gamma(\lambda)|^{2}\end{array}\right);

(3.9)

•

Normalization:
As
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash{\mathbb{R}}\to\infty
, the matrix
\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)
has the following asymptotic behavior:

\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{I}+O(\lambda^{-1}).

(3.10)

For each
k
, let
D_{k}
be a small disk centered at
\lambda_{k}
with a sufficiently small radius such that it lies in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
and is disjoint from all other disks as well as from
\{\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, where
\hat{D}_{k}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{-}|\bar{\lambda}\in D_{k}\}
. The boundaries
\{\partial{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
are oriented in a clockwise direction, while the boundaries
\{\partial\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
are oriented in a counterclockwise direction, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

We define a new matrix
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)\mathbf{P}_{k}(x,t;\lambda),\quad&\lambda\in D_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,N,\\

\bar{\lambda}_{k}

\begin{split}&\underset{\lambda_{k}}{\operatorname{Res}\ }(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)\\

=&\left(\mathbf{0},\underset{\lambda_{k}}{\operatorname{Res}\ }(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}\mathbf{M}_{2}(x,t;\lambda)-\underset{\lambda_{k}}{\operatorname{Res}\ }\frac{f(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta(x,t;\lambda)}\mathbf{M}_{1}(x,t;\lambda)}{(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{m_{k}-n_{k}+1}}\right),\end{split}

this assertion can be seen by considering the residue condition (

) and analyzing the Taylor series expansion of

at
\lambda_{k}
. Consequently, for each
k
and any

satisfying

, we have
\underset{\lambda_{k}}{\operatorname{Res}\ }(\lambda-\lambda_{k})^{n_{k}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
. By analogous reasoning, it can also be shown that the matrix
\mathbf{M}(x,t;\lambda)\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(x,t;\lambda)
has a removable singularity
at
\bar{\lambda}_{k}
. Given the definition (

) of
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
, we conclude that all points in the set
\{\lambda_{k},\bar{\lambda}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
are removable singularities of
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
.
Therefore, we affirm that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
fulfills the conditions of an equivalent RH problem that is closely related
to RH problem
3.1
, but with the residue conditions being substituted by jump conditions across small circles centered at the points
\{\lambda_{k},\bar{\lambda}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 3.2
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
that satisfies the following properties:

•

Analyticity:

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
is analytic in
\lambda
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\Sigma
, where
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\partial D_{k},\partial\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
;

•

Jump:
The matrix
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
takes continuous boundary values
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\pm}(x,t;\lambda)
on
\mathbb{R}
from
\mathbb{C}^{\pm}
, as well as from the left and right on the boundaries
\{\partial{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
and
\{\partial\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, where the “
\pm
” subscripts indicate boundary values on
\Sigma
. The boundary values are related by specific jump conditions,

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}(\lambda;t)=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\hat{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\Sigma,

(3.14)

where

\hat{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{J}(x,t;\lambda),\quad&\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathbf{P}_{k}^{-1}(x,t;\lambda),\quad&\lambda\in\partial D_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,N,\\
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(x,t;\lambda),\quad&\lambda\in\partial\hat{D}_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,N,\end{cases}

(3.15)

with
\mathbf{J}(x,t;\lambda)
is defined in Eq. (
3.9
),
\mathbf{P}_{k}(x,t;\lambda)
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(x,t;\lambda)
are defined in Eq. (
3.12
);

•

Normalizations:

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
has the following asymptotic behavior:

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{I}+O(\lambda^{-1}),\quad\lambda\rightarrow\infty.

(3.16)

The solution of the pole-free RH problem
3.2
exists uniquely if and only if the following lemma is satisfied (see Theorem 9.3 in Ref.
[
38
]
).

Lemma 3.3
.

(Vanishing Lemma)
The RH problem
3.2
has only the zero solution by replacing the asymptotic condition (
3.16
) with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=O(\lambda^{-1}),\quad\lambda\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)=\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda}),

(3.17)

where the notation “†” denotes the conjugate transpose. The function

\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)
is analytic for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\Sigma
and is continuous up to the boundary
\Sigma
. To analyze the jump of
\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)
across
\Sigma
, we start by noting that

\mathcal{M}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda}).

(3.18)

It is important to observe that if
\lambda\rightarrow\partial D_{k}
from the left (“
+
” side), the complex conjugate
\bar{\lambda}\rightarrow\partial\hat{D}_{k}

from the right (“
-
” side). By applying the jump conditions across
\Sigma
as given in Eq. (
3.14
), we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\hat{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda}).

Similarly, we can express
\mathcal{M}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{J}}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda})\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda}).

Next, utilizing the relationship

\hat{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\hat{\mathbf{J}}^{\dagger}(x,t;\bar{\lambda}),\quad\lambda\in\Sigma,

(3.21)

we find
\mathcal{M}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathcal{M}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)
, which imply
\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)
is continuous in the whole complex
\lambda
-plane. It then follows by Morera’s Theorem that
\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)
is an entire function of
\lambda
.
Furthermore, since
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
,
Liouville’s Theorem allows us to conclude that

\mathcal{M}(x,t;\lambda)\equiv\mathbf{0},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

(3.22)

In the following, we investigate the value of
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)
. As
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\end{array}\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\end{array}\right),

(3.23)

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta(\lambda)}\\
\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{\gamma(\bar{\lambda})}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta(\lambda)}&1+\lambda|\gamma(\lambda)|^{2}\end{array}\right).

(3.24)

Subsequently, we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\end{array}\right)\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}&0\\
0&\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\end{array}\right)\right)^{\dagger}

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

From this equation, we conclude that since

\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(x,t;\lambda)
is positive definite for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, it follows that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{-}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Applying the jump condition leads us to conclude that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Therefore, we can assert that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)\equiv\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Based on the uniqueness theorem for analytic functions, we have
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\{D_{k},\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\cup\mathbb{R}^{-}\}
. Specifically, since
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{+}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
on
\{\partial D_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, and by applying the jump condition on
\{D_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, we deduce that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda
within the interior of
\{D_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
. Thus,
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda\in\{D_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
. Similarly, we establish that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
for
\lambda\in\{\hat{D}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\cup\mathbb{R}^{-}
. In conclusion, we have demonstrated that
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x,t;\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
throughout the entire complex plane, as desired.
∎
