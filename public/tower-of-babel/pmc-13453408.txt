Retrospective Analysis of Adverse Drug Reactions in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Development of a Risk Prediction Model
Authors: Bo Sun, Qian Guan, Qin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with diabetes are prone to adverse drug reactions (ADRs) during long-term medication, but the related risk factors and predictive models remain to be clarified.ObjectiveTo analyze the influencing factors of ADRs in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and to develop and validate a risk prediction model.MethodsClinical data of 254 randomly selected patients with T2DM were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into a control group (n=198) and an ADR group (n=56) based on the presence of ADRs. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors associated with the occurrence and establish a prediction model.ResultsAmong the 56 patients who experienced ADRs, insulin hypoglycemic drugs had the highest proportion of adverse reactions (33.93%). Age ≥ 60 years old, three or more type medication, three or more comorbidities, abnormal BMI (> 24 or < 18 kg/m2), and length of hospitalization > 7 days were independent risk factors associated with the occurrence of ADRs (P < 0.05). A multivariate logistic regression model incorporating age, medication type, number of comorbidities, body mass index, and length of hospitalization demonstrated good predictive performance, with an accuracy of 85.43%, sensitivity of 83.93%, and specificity of 85.86%.ConclusionThe above five indicators are independent risk factors associated with the occurrence of ADRs in patients with T2DM. The risk prediction model has certain predictive performance and may help early identification of high-risk patients.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a common clinical metabolic disease characterized by hyperglycemia. It is mainly caused by insulin secretion deficiency or/and related biological effects damage, which can cause chronic damage or even dysfunction in the heart, kidneys, blood vessels and nerves. Diabetes mellitus can be divided into type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes (T2DM). Among them, T2DM has a higher incidence rate and is more common in people aged 35–40 years.1,2 The number of patients with T2DM is increasing year by year with the change in people’s dietary structure and the increasing degree of population aging. T2DM not only brings great pain and inconvenience to patients, but also increases the economic burden on families and society.3,4

Diabetes is a chronic disease that requires long-term medication. The safety of anti-diabetic drugs has increasingly become a focus of concern for the whole society, especially for most elderly diabetic patients. The current main treatment method for T2DM is oral hypoglycemic drugs. Since T2DM is a lifelong disease, long-term medication is required. Moreover, to prevent the occurrence of chronic comorbidities related to T2DM, patients mostly receive two or more drug treatments, which may lead to adverse drug reactions (ADRs). The occurrence of ADRs not only affects the quality of life of patients, but also significantly reduces medication compliance and affects their long-term prognosis.5,6 Diabetes patients are often complicated with hypertension, coronary heart disease, dyslipidemia and other chronic diseases, leading to the widespread phenomenon of multiple drug use. The use of multiple drugs not only increases the risk of drug–drug interactions, but also affects the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of drugs in the body, thereby significantly increasing the incidence of ADRs.7 Studies have shown that for each additional type of combined medication, the risk of adverse reactions increases by approximately 10% to 20%.8 However, risk prediction models that take into account multiple factors and conduct comprehensive assessments are still relatively scarce. Currently, a few models mainly focus on certain adverse reactions (mainly gastrointestinal reactions) of specific hypoglycemic drugs (such as semaglutide, GLP-1 receptor agonists), mainly gastrointestinal reactions, and the AUC value of the validation set was 0.763 and 0.836, respectively.6,8 There is no systematic prediction model for all types of ADRs in diabetic patients yet, and the predictive efficacy and applicability in clinical practice still need to be further verified. Therefore, early identification and quantification of the risk of ADRs in diabetic patients and construction of a simple and feasible clinical risk prediction model have significant clinical significance for optimizing individualized medication regimens, reducing iatrogenic damage, and improving patient prognosis.

This study aimed to conduct a retrospective analysis of the clinical data of diabetic patients, clarify the occurrence of ADRs and their risk factors, and construct a risk prediction model. This may provide a basis for the early identification of high-risk patients in clinical settings, the optimization of medication regimens, and the reduction of the incidence of adverse reactions.

Materials and Methods

General Information

This study is a retrospective observational study. A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of diabetic patients admitted to our hospital from January 2021 to January 2022. The research environment was a single-center tertiary Grade A hospital. The sampling method used was continuous sampling. All diabetic patients who met the inclusion criteria during this period were included. Inclusion criteria: (1) All patients met the diagnostic criteria for T2DM in clinical practice;9 (2) The patients were aged between 18 and 80 years old; (3) The patients and their families were informed, had good compliance, could cooperate with examinations and treatments, and all signed written informed consent forms. Exclusion criteria: (1) The patient had severe functional impairment of vital organs; (2) The patient was admitted to the hospital for treatment due to ADRs; (3) The patient had irritable diabetes. The patients were divided into the control group (n=198) and the ADR group (n=56) based on whether there were ADRs. According to the definition of the World Health Organization (WHO), ADRs refer to harmful reactions that occur when a drug is used at a conventional dose and under normal usage conditions, and are unrelated to the intended use of the drug.10 The determination of ADRs in this study was based on this standard. All experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. ZGL202202-10). Data Collection

The data was sourced from our hospital’s electronic medical record system, including patient medical history records, medication records and laboratory test results during hospitalization, discharge summaries, and follow-up records.

Analysis of drug use: The data on the distribution of drug usage among the patients in the ADR group was collected, and the composition of drug usage in patients with ADRs in the ADR group was analyzed, including insulin-based hypoglycemic agents, non-insulin-based hypoglycemic agents, cardiovascular system drugs, anti-infective drugs, anti-tumor drugs, and others.

The key variables were defined as follows: (1) Age: divided into < 60 years old and ≥ 60 years old. (2) Medication types: counted based on the types of drugs used during hospitalization and as per the doctor’s orders after discharge, divided into 1 type, 2 types, and 3 types or more. (3) Number of comorbidities: Based on the number of the types of diabetes-related comorbidities (including diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, diabetic foot, etc) recorded in the electronic medical records, they are classified as 1 type, 2 types, or more than 3 types. (4) Body Mass Index (BMI): It was calculated based on the height and weight measured upon admission, and was classified as < 18 kg/m2 (underweight), 18–24 kg/m2 (normal), and > 24 kg/m2 (overweight/obese). (5) Hospitalization days: The actual length of hospitalization from admission to discharge, divided into ≤ 7 days and >7 days. (6) Number of hospitalizations: The number of hospitalizations due to diabetes-related diseases within the past 1 year, divided into ≤ 2 times and >2 times. (7) Drinking history: Regular drinking history (drinking at least once per week for ≥ 6 months) was defined as “yes”, otherwise as “no”. (8) Gallstones: It was determined based on the results of abdominal ultrasound or CT imaging examinations.

Laboratory indices: Fasting blood glucose (FBG), postprandial blood glucose (PBG), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). All measurements were based on the first fasting venous blood sample taken after admission.

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software. Count data were reported as frequency (%) and were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and subjected to independent samples t test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine the independent risk factors associated with the occurrence of ADRs in diabetic patients. The logistic regression equation was established: P= ExpΣBiXi/1+ ExpΣBiXi, and the sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of the model in predicting the occurrence of ADRs were evaluated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is a common metabolic disease in clinical practice, characterized by a high incidence and numerous comorbidities. Since there is currently no specific drug that can completely cure diabetes, it can only be treated by long-term administration of insulin and hypoglycemic drugs. However, these drugs inevitably cause varying degrees of ADRs when controlling glycemic, which not only increases the patient’s suffering but also severely increases the mortality risk of underlying disease.11,12 Therefore, identifying the influencing factors that cause the occurrence of ADRs and constructing risk prediction models may help to reduce the occurrence of ADRs, which is of great significance for improving the prognosis of patients.

Previous studies have concluded that advanced age, impaired liver and kidney function, and alcohol consumption can increase the risk of ADRs in patients with T2DM.13 The globe is currently approaching a period of rapid aging. The proportion of middle-aged and elderly people with diabetes is increasing. The elderly have a higher risk of diabetes, particularly T2DM, due to combined effects of increased insulin resistance and decreased pancreatic islet cell function with aging. Accordingly, older patients with diabetes have an increased risk of ADRs.14,15 The elderly often suffer from various diseases, such as impaired liver and kidney function and malnutrition. In addition, the distribution, metabolism and excretion of drugs in the elderly’s bodies undergo significant changes. The incidence of diabetes comorbidities is relatively high. The occurrence of comorbidities not only affects the quality of life of patients, but also may shorten their life cycle.16 Diabetic patients often have multiple medication regimens. Compared to a single drug, multiple drugs have lower metabolic rates in the body and have the probability of drug-induced side effects and drug–drug interactions, thus resulting in a higher incidence of ADRs.17 In addition, the frequency and severity of ADRs tend to increase disproportionately with the number of drugs taken. Other coexisting diseases may also influence susceptibility to ADRs. Diabetes is often accompanied by multiple comorbidities, which often affect the metabolic processes of drugs in the body and may increase the incidence of ADRs.18 Obesity is one of the most important factors affecting the change of diabetes. Long-term obesity not only causes the body to be in a state of micro-inflammation, but also affects the metabolism of drugs by affecting the function of liver and kidney. Therefore, monitoring changes in BMI may help indicate the occurrence of ADRs to diabetic medications.19

This study found that the independent risk factors for ADRs in diabetes patients were Age ≥ 60 years old, three or more type medication, three or more comorbidities, abnormal BMI (> 24 or < 18 kg/m2), and length of hospitalization > 7 days (P<0.05). Specifically, the risks of ADRs for patients aged 60 or above, those with three or more comorbidities, those with abnormal BMI (> 24 or < 18 kg/m2), and those with length of hospitalization > 7 days were 2.713 times (OR = 2.713, 95% CI: 1.211–6.124), 2.456 times (OR = 2.456, 95% CI: 1.093–5.519), 1.948 times (OR = 1.948, 95% CI: 1.160–3.275), and 1.574 times (OR = 1.574, 95% CI: 1.064–2.324), respectively. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on the analysis of single category adverse reactions of specific hypoglycemic drugs and merely remained at the level of scattered summarization of risk factors, the core innovation and clinical significance of this study lie in: covering all types of medication scenarios for diabetic patients, for the first time incorporating the indicator of hospitalization (which can be obtained upon admission) into independent predictive factors, constructing a comprehensive risk prediction model that can be directly used for rapid clinical screening, and filling the gap in the quantitative assessment tools for adverse reactions in the full medication scenarios of T2DM patients. Previous related studies have provided methodological and practical references for model construction, but there are clear limitations in the scenarios. Rodgers et al based on electronic health records and used the pre-event rate correction method to conduct a correction analysis on the risk of ADRs in patients with T2DM.20 They confirmed that electronic risk assessment can assist clinical decision-making, but this study focused on methodological correction and did not form a simple predictive tool that can be directly used in clinical practice. Yue et al constructed a risk prediction model that only focused on the gastrointestinal adverse reactions of semaglutide, a specific hypoglycemic drug.21 The included variables were mainly based on the patients’ basal metabolic indicators, and it was unable to meet the comprehensive risk assessment requirements for all types of combined medications in diabetic patients.

From the perspective of public health and pharmacovigilance, the effective management of adverse drug reactions in diabetes patients is of great significance. The global number of diabetes patients is continuously increasing. Diabetic patients often suffer from multiple comorbidities and need to take combined medications for a long time. ADRs not only affect the treatment compliance and quality of life of individual patients, but also increase the unnecessary occupation of medical resources and the burden on health and economy. Strengthening the monitoring of ADRs and risk management is of great significance for improving patient prognosis and alleviating public health pressure. Kim et al conducted a disproportionality analysis of infection-related adverse reactions to anti-diabetic drugs based on the global pharmacovigilance database. They found that combination therapy significantly increased the risk of infection compared to monotherapy (aROR for two drugs combined = 1.24, aROR for three or more drugs = 1.42), highlighting the necessity of strengthening pharmacovigilance in the context of increasingly common polypharmacy.22 The AI-driven pharmacovigilance framework developed by Alsuhibani provides a systematic technical approach for the safety monitoring of anti-diabetes drugs by integrating FDA drug labels and FAERS real-world adverse event data. This demonstrates the development direction of the transformation of the pharmacovigilance system from passive reporting to active monitoring.23 In this study, the use of ≥ 3 types of drugs was an independent risk factor for adverse drug reactions (OR = 2.673), and factors such as older age, multiple comorbidities, and long hospitalization further amplified the risk. This provides direct data support for the necessity of the above drug vigilance. By strengthening active monitoring for patients with multiple medications, advanced age, and multiple comorbidities, it is possible to achieve early warning and reduce the occurrence of adverse reactions.

Previous surveys have shown that China has the highest number of diabetics in the world. Diabetes and its chronic comorbidities have become the third leading cause of death, after cancer and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Diabetes has become a hot topic of research among medical scholars at present.5 The occurrence of ADRs in diabetic patients not only affect their quality of life, but also reduce their initiative and compliance to take medication, thereby impacting the efficacy of the treatment and may even endanger the patient’s life.24 Constructing risk models to predict the risk of ADRs can help reduce the occurrence of such events. In this study, the logistic regression prediction model is P=1/1+ExpΣ (4.337–1.003X1–0.983X2–0.899X3–1.667X4–0.453X5), where the closer the P value is to 1, the greater the possibility of ADRs occurring in the patient. We found that the accuracy of this probability model in predicting the occurrence of ADRs in diabetic patients was, the sensitivity was 83.93%, and the specificity was 85.86%. This suggested that the risk model had good predictive efficacy in predicting the risk of ADRs in diabetic patients. It has been confirmed that women, advanced age, more comorbidities, more types of medications, longer hospitalization, and more admissions may be risk factors for the occurrence of ADRs in elderly patients with T2DM and should be given clinical attention.25 Therefore, constructing a prediction model for the occurrence of ADRs in T2DM patients based on multi-factorial logistic regression analysis can help improve the prognosis of these patients.

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective observational study, causal inference is limited and there may be unmeasured confounding variables (such as genetic factors, lifestyle differences, medication adherence levels, etc) that may simultaneously affect medication selection and the risk of adverse reactions. Secondly, the study was a single-center, small-sample study (n = 254), which may have selection bias. The generalizability of the results needs to be verified. In the future, we will expand the experimental subjects and research duration to perform a more in-depth investigation of the potential risk factors for adverse drug reactions in diabetic patients. By conducting multi-center, large-sample prospective cohort studies, the stability and extrapolation ability of the risk factors and prediction models discovered in this study will be verified. Furthermore, the latest advancements in the field of predictive pharmacovigilance indicated that artificial intelligence techniques (such as XGBoost, random forest) that integrated multi-source data (electronic medical records, medication records, etc) outperformed traditional logistic regression in the detection of adverse reactions.26 This provides the following guidance for future directions of this study: using the logistic model as the baseline, compare the incremental predictive value of machine learning models; Establishing a real-time dynamic risk assessment system using AI adaptive learning; Optimize the model by incorporating more predictive variables (drug interactions and compliance patterns). Subsequently, a machine learning model will be constructed and compared with the model system in this study.

Conclusions

Through the retrospective analysis of 254 patients with T2DM, the following key findings were obtained: insulin hypoglycemic drugs were the most common drug category leading to ADRs (33.93%), and Age ≥60 years old, three or more type medication, three or more comorbidities, abnormal BMI (> 24 or < 18 kg/m2), and length of hospitalization > 7 days were independent risk factors for ADRs (P<0.05). The constructed multivariate logistic regression prediction model has certain predictive performance (accuracy 85.43%, sensitivity 83.93%, specificity 85.86%). There were 28 false positives (14.14%) and 9 false negatives (16.07%) in this model. False positives can cause unnecessary intervention and anxiety, while false negatives may delay prevention. Clinically, it should be judged based on specific circumstances, and patients with critical values should be monitored more closely. This model is only used as an auxiliary decision-making tool.

The clinical significance of this model lies in its ability to quickly identify high-risk groups of ADRs at the early stage of admission of patients by using easily available clinical indicators such as age, type of medication, number of comorbidities, BMI and length of hospitalization. This provides quantitative basis for clinicians to optimize medication programs and strengthen drug use monitoring, thus helping to improve drug safety for patients with T2DM and promoting evidence-based individualized decision-making.
