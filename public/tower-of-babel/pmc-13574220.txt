A Systems View of Posterior Capsule Opacification: TGF-β/ROS Signaling, Mitochondrial Homeostasis, and Injury/Aging Cues—Therapeutic Opportunities
Authors: Ke Xu, Rong Huang, Hang Xie, Lei Du, Meng Liu, Jianyong Wang, Changzheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Posterior capsule opacification is a common long-term complication of cataract surgery that can cause persistent visual impairment in some patients despite advances in surgical techniques and IOL design. Most current studies focus on single signaling pathways or isolated time windows, showing benefits in some models but with limited overall translation. This review organizes existing evidence into a pathological network in which TGF-β signaling initiation and amplification, mitochondrial dysfunction, and microenvironmental cues related to surgical trauma and aging collectively shape the susceptibility, progression, and persistence of posterior capsule opacification and outlines potential opportunities for combination therapy, time-sensitive intervention windows, and local delivery.

Although cataract surgery techniques are generally well established, posterior capsule opacification (PCO) remains a common cause of compromised long-term visual quality after surgery.1 Although IOL materials and designs continue to be refined, pharmacological prevention and treatment of PCO have progressed relatively slowly and have become an significant bottleneck in clinical practice.2–4

This bottleneck suggests that, although the central role of the TGF-β/Smad signaling axis in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) of lens epithelial cells (LECs) has been firmly established,5–7 strategies focusing on a single molecular node may be insufficient to achieve durable PCO prevention.8–14 Importantly, residual LECs are now recognized not only as the cellular substrate for lens maintenance and regeneration, but also as the primary source of fibrotic transformation after cataract surgery, giving rise to PCO.1,7,15,16 In this context, PCO may be better understood as a pathological process driven by multilayered signaling networks together with tissue microenvironmental cues, rather than the output of a single pathway.

Building on previous work, this review organizes current data on PCO into a temporal network in which the initiation and amplification of TGF-β signaling, mitochondrial homeostasis in the stabilization and maintenance of EMT, and surgical- and aging-related microenvironmental cues interact during disease onset and progression. Within this framework, we review currently reported intervention attempts, ranging from intraoperative prophylaxis to early postoperative prevention and strategies aimed at limiting progression toward fibrotic PCO, most of which are still largely preclinical, and relate these strategies to the signaling and mitochondrial processes they are intended to modulate.2–4,10,17–21

Signaling Targets and Early Amplification: TGF-β/Smad, Parallel Noncanonical Pathways, and Amplifiers

Within the framework outlined elsewhere in this article, the development of PCO begins with a phase dominated by signal initiation and early amplification, in which the key event is EMT of residual LECs in response to TGF-β–related stimuli. In this phase, the canonical TGF-β/Smad pathway is typically regarded as the principal driver, while multiple noncanonical pathways run in parallel and are coupled into a signaling network whose output is further amplified by reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby triggering downstream effects. We discuss these three components in turn, drawing on studies in PCO models and pointing out where they have been considered as potential intervention points.

TGF-β/Smad: The Canonical Signaling Pathway Driving EMT in LECs

The canonical TGF-β/Smad pathway is widely regarded as a principal driver of EMT in residual LECs. Mechanistically, TGF-β binds to TGF-β receptor II and recruits TGF-β receptor I, triggering C-terminal phosphorylation of Smad2/3, formation of Smad2/3–Smad4 complexes, and their translocation to the nucleus.6,22 In the nucleus, these complexes regulate the transcription of fibrosis-related genes such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), type I collagen, and fibronectin, partly through cooperation with co-activators including p300/CBP. This transcriptional program is accompanied by the disruption of cell–cell junctions, stress–fiber formation, and progressive capsular wrinkling and contraction, thereby promoting EMT progression and fibrotic PCO development.1,7

As a key effector in this pathway, Smad3 plays a decisive role in EMT initiation. Studies have shown that the loss of Smad3 markedly attenuates the upregulation of α-SMA and fibronectin and suppresses extracellular matrix deposition.7,23 More generally, TGF-β signaling is negatively regulated by the inducible inhibitory Smad, Smad7, which suppresses signal transduction through multiple mechanisms, including interfering with Smad2/3 recruitment, promoting receptor dephosphorylation, and preventing functional Smad complexes from interacting with DNA in the nucleus.6 In other fibrotic models, sustained TGF-β stimulation and oxidative stress can dysregulate this negative feedback mechanism, impair the inhibitory loop, and thereby promote persistent fibrosis. However, direct evidence for this pattern in LEC/PCO systems remains limited.6,24,25

The upstream activation of TGF-β is also regulated by the αV integrin family. In mouse postcataract surgery models, the upregulation of αV integrins is essential for activating latent TGF-β. In mice with lens epithelium–specific deletion of αV integrin or the β8 subunit, postoperative Smad3 phosphorylation and fibrotic responses are markedly reduced, whereas exogenous active TGF-β can restore these responses.13,26 Meanwhile, in rat LEC explants, bone morphogenetic protein 7, an endogenous antagonist of TGF-β signaling, induces early phosphorylation of Smad1/5 and counteracts Smad2/3 signaling, thereby suppressing TGF-β2–induced α-SMA upregulation, capsular wrinkling, and LEC loss.5

Moreover, the downstream effects of the canonical TGF-β/Smad pathway are also subject to local histone acetylation at fibrosis-related genes. After Smad2/3–Smad4 complexes enter the nucleus, their transcriptional activity depends not only on the recruitment of co-activators such as p300/CBP,6 but also on the local chromatin structure at target promoters.27,28 In human LEC line, increased histone H4 acetylation at the ACTA2 promoter accompanies α-SMA upregulation and myofibroblastic differentiation, indicating that promoter-level chromatin modifications of fibrosis-related genes participate in regulating EMT-associated transcriptional programs (Fig. 1).28

Parallel Noncanonical Pathways: The “Networked” Nature of Signaling in LEC EMT

Current evidence suggests that the canonical TGF-β/Smad pathway functions as the central axis of EMT, while multiple noncanonical pathways—including ErbB–ERK, Rho/ROCK–MRTF, β-catenin/CBP, integrin–FAK, YAP, and CTGF—operate in parallel and can be organized by functional role rather than as isolated branches. Pathways involved in early cytoskeletal remodeling and adhesion—including ErbB–ERK and integrin–FAK—contribute to EMT initiation. ROS, generated in part by NOX4, acts as a central amplifier that enhances ERK1/2, RhoA/ROCK, and β-catenin signaling, and may also provide an indirect upstream input to TGF-β/Smad activation. ERK1/2 represents another convergence node, receiving input from both ErbB-dependent and MEK-associated signaling. At the transcriptional maintenance level, Smad3 intersects with noncanonical branches by influencing β-catenin localization and MRTF-A nuclear translocation, while β-catenin/CBP signaling may reciprocally affect Smad3 phosphorylation and TGF-β receptor expression, potentially sustaining profibrotic signaling over time. Together, these pathways intersect at multiple nodes, giving rise to a networked pattern of signaling in EMT.

The ErbB–ERK Axis and ROS Amplification

Evidence from primary embryonic chick LEC cultures showed that TGF-β acts not only through the canonical Smad pathway, but also enhances ERK phosphorylation within 1.5 hours, and this enhancement depends on endogenous ErbB receptor kinase activity. Inhibitors of ErbB or ERK markedly reduce the TGF-β–induced upregulation of early (fibronectin) and late (α-SMA) myofibroblast markers, indicating that this noncanonical pathway is critical for myofibroblast lineage commitment.9 Importantly, TGF-β–generated ROS can further amplify ERK signaling, coupling oxidative cues to receptor tyrosine kinase networks and creating a ROS–ERK synergy that augments the profibrotic effects of TGF-β.9

RhoA/ROCK–MRTF and the Actin Cytoskeletal Program

Studies in rat lens epithelial explants showed that TGF-β2 activates RhoA/ROCK, promoting the polymerization of G-actin into F-actin and the formation of stress fibers. As the pool of free G-actin decreases, MRTF-A is released from actin complexes and translocates into the nucleus, where it cooperates with its transcriptional partner SRF to initiate the expression of myofibroblast genes such as ACTA2 (α-SMA). The inhibition of ROCK or the direct inhibition of MRTF-A blocks nuclear entry of MRTF-A and suppresses α-SMA upregulation.29,30 In addition, ROS facilitates this process: ROS activates RhoA, further enhancing ROCK activity, promoting F-actin formation, and thereby contributing to the progression of EMT.29

Evidence from mouse LEC explants indicates that the absence or inhibition of matrix metallopeptidase 9 alters the expression of cytoskeleton-associated proteins such as FAK, LIMK, and MLC2, which restricts the nuclear localization of MRTF-A and prevents the accumulation of α-SMA protein.31 At the level of the actin cytoskeleton, studies in primary and cell-line mouse LECs have shown that tropomyosin isoforms stabilize and promote the maturation of stress fibers: the combined knockdown of Tpm1/2 reverses TGF-β2–induced stress fiber formation and α-SMA upregulation,32 and the selective inhibition of Tpm3.1 (using TR100 or genetic deletion) also markedly attenuates cytoskeletal remodeling and EMT marker expression.33

A commonly used explanation is that RhoA/ROCK converts extracellular TGF-β2 and ROS signals into contractile stress fibers capable of bearing tension, while MRTF-A transduces these mechanical changes into the nucleus and initiates the expression of genes, such as α-SMA, consolidating a stable contractile phenotype.29,30,32–34 In addition, studies in rat LEC explants indicate that Smad3 inhibition can attenuate TGF-β–induced MRTF-A nuclear translocation, suggesting that canonical Smad3 signaling may also influence this cytoskeletal branch.35

Wnt/β-Catenin Drives EMT Through CBP Rather Than TCF and Cooperates With Smad

Wnt/β-catenin signaling has also been implicated in TGF-β–induced lens EMT, and TGF-β has been reported to promote the expression of several Wnt ligands and Frizzled receptors during cataract development,36 suggesting that TGF-β may enhance upstream Wnt ligand–Frizzled receptor signaling, thereby contributing to β-catenin activation. In rat LEC explants, CBP was shown to function as a transcriptional co-activator for β-catenin. Selective inhibition of the interaction between β-catenin and CBP (e.g., with ICG-001) markedly attenuates TGF-β2–induced cytoskeletal remodeling and upregulation of fibrotic markers, as reflected by reduced stress fibers and α-SMA together with preserved E-cadherin. In contrast, blocking the interaction between β-catenin and TCF does not produce similar effects, indicating that, in LECs, β-catenin mainly signals through CBP rather than through the canonical Wnt/β-catenin–TCF pathway to drive EMT.11 In the human LEC line HLE-B3, it was further shown that ROS can also activate β-catenin through ERK, JNK, and related pathways, promoting the expression of EMT-associated genes.37

In addition, studies in rat LEC explants suggest a functional interplay between β-catenin and Smad3. The inhibition of Smad3 prevents TGF-β–induced β-catenin redistribution and nuclear localization, whereas the inhibition of β-catenin/CBP signaling reduces Smad3 phosphorylation and TGF-β receptor expression, indicating a functional interdependence between β-catenin and Smad3 during LEC EMT.11,35

Beyond co-activator recruitment, the transcriptional output of the Smad3–β-catenin/CBP module may also be shaped by local chromatin accessibility. In human LECs, aspirin attenuates the TGF-β2–induced enrichment of H3K56ac/H3K122ac and associated transcriptional complexes at the ACTA2 and type I collagen promoters, while Smad2/3 phosphorylation is not markedly affected.27 These findings suggest that promoter acetylation may act as a downstream chromatin-regulatory step that modulates profibrotic transcription after upstream Smad activation. Although this mechanism has not been directly demonstrated in LEC/PCO models, salicylate, a major metabolite of aspirin, has been reported in nonlens systems to inhibit CBP/p300 acetyltransferase activity by competing with acetyl-coenzyme A, suggesting a possible route through which aspirin-related compounds may influence CBP/p300-dependent chromatin regulation.38

The Roles of Integrin–FAK Signaling and Matrix Remodeling in EMT

Adhesion and mechanotransduction cues also contribute to the noncanonical signaling network. The engagement of integrins with the extracellular matrix recruits and activates FAK at focal adhesions, converting adhesive and mechanical stimuli into cues that regulate EMT.12,13 On the one hand, in HLE-B3 cells, TGF-β2 was shown to induce fibronectin production and increase surface α5β1 integrin, and adhesion of α5β1 to fibronectin was shown to activate focal adhesion signaling and FAK. Blocking α5β1 or inhibiting FAK reduced LEC migration, a finding validated in a rabbit postoperative PCO model.12 On the other hand, in a mouse cataract-surgery model, αV-class integrins were found to be upregulated in residual LECs and to activate latent TGF-β; the loss of αV integrins was found to decrease postoperative Smad3 phosphorylation and expression of fibrotic markers.13

YAP, CTGF, and Combined Device–Drug Strategies

YAP also participates in noncanonical TGF-β signaling and contributes to EMT, and its inhibition reduces the expression of α-SMA and fibronectin and suppresses EMT.14 In the human LEC line SRA01/04, TGF-β has been reported to engage YAP, potentially through MEK/ERK-associated signaling, promoting its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and enhancing its transcriptional activity.8 YAP may also contribute to profibrotic signaling in part through connective tissue growth factor (CTGF).14,39 Work in SRA01/04 cells and rat PCO models also showed that adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated CTGF-short hairpin RNA significantly reduces PCO-related changes.39

Amplifiers: ROS-Driven Cascades in LEC EMT

In rat lens epithelial explants, TGF-β was shown to rapidly induce the expression and activation of NOX4, a member of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase family, leading to ROS production, particularly hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).10 In breast epithelial cell lines, it was found that, after TGF-β stimulation, Smad3 is required to promote NOX4 expression and thereby drive ROS production.40 Work in a human LEC line showed that ROS load accumulates quickly, reaching markedly elevated levels within 8 hours.41 At the same time, NOX4 protein expression becomes detectable as early as 6 to 8 hours and progressively shifts in subcellular localization from the cytoplasm to the nucleus; by 48 hours, NOX4 signals show strong colocalization with α-SMA, indicating close temporal and spatial alignment with EMT progression.10 Although NOX4 is an important source of TGF-β2–associated ROS in lens EMT, additional NOX4-independent ROS sources may also contribute; however, their precise identity and relative contribution remain incompletely defined in current lens models.42 Taken together, available evidence suggests that ROS functions as a redox amplifier of TGF-β signaling in LEC EMT.10,41,42

Notably, exogenous oxidative stress can also act as a trigger. UVB exposure upregulates Otx2 across mouse and human lens explants and the SRA01/04 cell line. In SRA01/04 cells, H2O2 treatment dose dependently increases Otx2 expression, supporting H2O2 as a mediator of UVB-induced Otx2 upregulation. Lentiviral Otx2 overexpression in SRA01/04 cells significantly increases TGFβ2 mRNA expression, enhances Smad2/3 phosphorylation, and induces EMT markers such as α-SMA and fibronectin.43 Together, these findings suggest that ROS-related oxidative stress may act upstream of TGF-β/Smad activation in LECs, with Otx2-related transcriptional changes providing a possible link between oxidative stress and TGFβ-associated EMT signaling.

In terms of antioxidant interventions, exogenous antioxidant treatment such as glutathione (GSH) or catalase in cultured lenses markedly reduces TGF-β–induced opacification, capsular wrinkling, α-SMA upregulation, and the decline of Pax6, a transcription factor associated with maintenance of the epithelial phenotype in cultured rat LEC explants.44 Also in human LEC lines, addition of the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine clears TGF-β–induced ROS, reduces the upregulation of EMT markers, and inhibits LEC migration and apoptosis.41

Overall, antioxidant or NOX4-directed interventions reduce ROS levels, EMT marker expression, and lens opacification, pointing to redox modulation as a potential therapeutic approach (Fig. 2).41,44

Mitochondrial Homeostasis Determines the Persistence of PCO

TGF-β/ROS signaling mainly governs the triggering and early amplification of PCO, whereas the persistence of EMT is more closely linked to mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox homeostasis. At the same time, these processes are likely to overlap and influence one another during disease progression. ROS generated during TGF-β signaling can perturb mitochondrial function, and mitochondrial dysfunction may further increase mtROS production and reinforce redox-sensitive profibrotic signaling. Beyond mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), mitochondrial alterations may also involve broader changes in cellular metabolic and redox states.

Anatomically, mitochondria are largely retained in the anterior monolayer of LECs, while fiber cells progressively remove their organelles, forming the central organelle-free zone.45 Thus, imbalances in mitochondrial energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial quality control are expected to occur predominantly in LECs that preserve their organelles rather than in mature cortical fibers.46–50 Available data suggest that mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)/adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, mtROS load, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) stability, and the status of mitochondrial quality control jointly influence the persistence of EMT.

Mitochondrial Structural and Functional Changes Induced by TGF-β2

One of the more salient changes after TGF-β2 stimulation is a parallel decrease in ΔΨm and ATP-generating capacity: a reduction in ΔΨm indicates a weakened electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane, and ATP synthesis decreases accordingly.51 At the ultrastructural level, mitochondrial cristae often shift from tightly packed folds to a sparse configuration and may even exhibit fragmentation. These mitochondrial abnormalities typically emerge in parallel with increased EMT marker expression and enhanced migratory capacity and are interpreted as suggesting a functional link between mitochondrial homeostasis and the fibrotic phenotype.51 Intracellular ROS induced by TGF-β2 may contribute to this bioenergetic defect. Consistently, H2O2 exposure in the human LEC line HLE-B3 causes a dose-dependent decline in ΔΨm and intracellular ATP, indicating that ROS accumulation can compromise the mitochondrial electrochemical gradient and thereby weaken bioenergetic output.52

This TGF-β2–associated bioenergetic phenotype may be interpreted in light of LEC metabolic reprogramming. Although the lens as a whole relies predominantly on glycolysis, LECs at the anterior surface simultaneously possess a comparatively strong oxidative capacity. It has been shown that nearly one-half of epithelial ATP originates from oxidative metabolism, with the remainder generated through glycolysis; when mitochondrial oxidation becomes restricted, the epithelium can compensate by increasing anaerobic lactate production.49 In terms of antioxidant supply, the pentose phosphate pathway produces NADPH to sustain GSH regeneration. For example, dehydroascorbate can markedly enhance pentose phosphate pathway activity and thereby promote the reduction of glutathione disulfide to GSH. When this pathway is impaired, GSH depletion and loss of lens transparency occur more readily.46,47,50

When oxidative or metabolic load increases, LECs rely more on glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathway to maintain ATP and NADPH to support survival and biosynthetic needs under stress.46–48 However, if the NADPH supply and GSH regeneration are restricted, it is more prone to oxidative damage and continuous activation of signaling pathways, which is consistent with sustained EMT.9,10,47,53 In parallel, the modulation of key glycolytic enzymes such as PKM2 alters the balance between glycolytic and oxidative metabolism in LECs and affects EMT phenotypes.53,54 In postoperative models, inhibition of aldose reductase reduces EMT/PCO readouts.55 A proposed explanation is that, as an NADPH-dependent enzyme, aldose reductase competes with GSH reductase for NADPH. Suppressing aldose reductase activity may reduce this competition, allowing more NADPH to be directed toward the glutathione disulfide-to-GSH regeneration cycle and thus partially relieving oxidative and metabolic stress.47,56

Coupling Between mtROS and Nuclear Signaling

When mitochondrial function declines, electron leakage at complexes I and III increases superoxide production,57 and the resulting mtROS interact with nuclear signaling through several routes. First, H2O2 can oxidize and inhibit key phosphatases such as PTEN and PP2A, prolonging Akt/ERK signaling and stabilizing EMT.57 Second, when the mitochondrial metabolism shifts toward a more reduced state, elevated NADH and reduced NAD⁺ may limit the activity of sirtuins (SIRT), which rely on NAD⁺ as a substrate.58 In fibrotic models of the kidney and lung, SIRTs have been shown to directly modulate the transcriptional activity of TGF-β/Smad signaling. For example, SIRT1 associates with Smad3 and lowers its acetylation, suppressing Smad3-dependent transcription and reducing ECM and collagen expression,59 SIRT2 directly deacetylates Smad2/3, reducing their phosphorylation and nuclear localization,60 and SIRT3 limits mtROS and mtDNA damage, inhibiting TGF-β1–induced myofibroblast differentiation and pulmonary fibrosis.61 These observations raise the possibility that reduced NAD⁺ levels and restricted SIRT activity increase Smad2/3 acetylation and stabilize their transcriptional complexes, which could in turn enhance TGF-β/Smad transcriptional output and ECM expression; however, this speculation remains to be tested in LECs. Third, ROS can also promote the activation of nuclear factor-κB and activator protein-1 to some extent, increasing the transcription of ECM-related and inflammatory genes.1,57 Overall, these findings suggest a bidirectional link between TGF-β/ROS signaling and mitochondrial stress in LEC EMT. TGF-β2– or H2O2–related oxidative stress can impair mitochondrial function, as reflected by a decreased ΔΨm and reduced ATP production and cristae disruption, while mitochondrial dysfunction can increase electron leakage and mtROS generation. This potential feedback loop may help to convert transient TGF-β/ROS stimulation into a more sustained EMT state, although direct evidence for a complete TGF-β–ROS–mtROS circuit in LEC/PCO models remains limited.

Mitochondrial Quality Control Imbalance: Mitochondrial Fission/Fusion, Biogenesis, and Mitophagy

Imbalance of Mitochondrial Fission and Fusion

Mitochondrial fission and fusion jointly determine mitochondrial morphology, ROS production, and energetic properties. TGF-β2 stimulation has been reported to induce mitochondrial oxidative stress in SRA01/04 cells, accompanied by increased phosphorylation of the fission protein DRP1 at sites such as Ser616, thereby promoting mitochondrial fission and fragmentation.62 Fragmented mitochondria typically generate higher levels of ROS and are associated with enhanced migratory capacity.63 In contrast, MFN1/2 mediate outer membrane fusion, and OPA1 maintains cristae structure and inner membrane fusion, supporting a networked, low-oxidative and energy-efficient state.62,64,65 When the membrane potential decreases, OMA1 becomes activated and cleaves OPA1, leading to further cristae disruption and facilitating ROS accumulation.66–69

Imbalance of Mitochondrial Biogenesis

PGC-1α is a co-activator of mitochondrial biogenesis and maintains mtDNA replication and transcription by regulating the mitochondrial transcription factor TFAM.51 After TGF-β2 treatment, expression of PGC-1α and TFAM decreases, accompanied by a reduction in mtDNA copy number, indicating impaired stability and replicative capacity of the mitochondrial genome.62 Activation of the PGC-1α–TFAM axis enhances mtDNA replication and transcription, helps to maintain mtDNA copy number, restores ΔΨm and ATP levels, and lowers ROS load, while also reducing α-SMA and fibronectin expression and weakening migration and contraction. These findings are consistent with the notion that improving mitochondrial biogenesis and genome maintenance may help to attenuate EMT.51

Conceptually, targeting mitochondrial biogenesis and genome stability may act at a more upstream level of energy and redox homeostasis than short-term ROS scavenging, but whether such an approach provides more sustained benefits in current models remains to be determined.

Imbalance of Mitophagy

The PINK1/Parkin axis is a core quality control pathway for the removal of depolarized mitochondria. In canonical mitophagy, when the ΔΨm falls, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane and recruits Parkin, which ubiquitinates outer membrane substrates and, together with adaptor proteins such as p62, NDP52, and OPTN, targets damaged mitochondria to LC3-positive autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation.70,71 Consistent with this protective role, in 24-hour H2O2-treated HLE-B3 cells, PINK1 overexpression further increased Parkin and LC3B-II expression, promoted p62 clearance, and was accompanied by reduced intracellular and mtROS, a preserved ΔΨm, and decreased apoptosis, supporting PINK1-dependent mitophagy as a protective mitochondrial quality control response under oxidative stress.72 However, when mitophagic clearance is not successfully completed, dysfunctional mitochondria accumulate and ROS levels tend to rise.62,72 In the same study, sodium selenite–induced rat cataracts showed increased PINK1, Parkin, and LC3B-II, as well as increased p62; the authors suggested that long-term oxidative stress may decrease autophagic flux, leading to p62 accumulation.72 Similarly, a study of TGF-β2–treated SRA01/04 cells showed that, although PINK1/Parkin signaling and LC3 were increased, p62 was not reduced, indicating impaired autophagic clearance and incomplete removal of damaged mitochondria.62 Thus, under sustained oxidative or profibrotic stress, endogenous PINK1/Parkin activation may represent an attempted but insufficient quality control response, whereas forced PINK1 activation may partially restore mitophagic clearance and reduce ROS-related injury.

In addition to this ubiquitin adaptor–driven pathway, mammalian cells also use receptor-mediated mitophagy, with BNIP3, NIX (BNIP3L), FUNDC1, and BCL2L13 as representative receptors.70,73,74 These proteins reside in the outer mitochondrial membrane and expose a cytosolic LC3-interacting region, enabling damaged mitochondria to engage LC3-positive autophagic membranes directly, without p62/OPTN/NDP52, under conditions such as hypoxia, differentiation, or other stresses.70,71,73 Outside the lens, existing studies in retinal vascular endothelial cells, RPE, and retinal ganglion cells have characterized these receptor-mediated mitophagy pathways and their links to ROS and cell death in a relatively systematic manner.75–77 However, in LEC/PCO systems, it remains unclear whether these receptors are activated by TGF-β2 or by postoperative microenvironmental cues, and their causal relationship with EMT phenotypes has yet to be defined.

Cross-system studies (e.g., in cardiac muscle and alveolar epithelium) further indicate that, when PINK1 levels decline and mitophagy is insufficient, increased mitochondrial membrane permeability allows oxidatively damaged mtDNA fragments to leak into the cytosol and, in some cases, into the extracellular space.78,79 These fragments can enter endolysosomal compartments and be recognized by Toll-like receptor 9, activating nuclear factor-κB/interferon regulatory factor–dependent sterile inflammatory signaling and promoting the upregulation of mediators such as TGF-β and IL-6, amplifying fibrotic responses.78,79 To date, however, there is no direct evidence in LECs that mitophagy impairment leads to mtDNA leakage, triggering Toll-like receptor 9 signaling and promoting EMT. Given that the PCO capsular bag forms a relatively enclosed compartment in which cytokines and mtDNA are likely to accumulate locally, this environment may be particularly sensitive to this axis, although this remains to be demonstrated (Fig. 3).57,78,80

Microenvironmental Interactions

Surgical and aging-related microenvironments provide the biological context in which TGF-β/ROS signaling and mitochondrial stress responses are initiated, amplified, and sustained. Surgical injury can introduce acute mechanical, oxidative, and inflammatory cues that promote TGF-β activation and ROS generation,4,80–85 while ROS, as discussed elsewhere in this article, can secondarily affect mitochondrial function. Aging-related changes may act through partly distinct routes: intrinsic aging of LECs is associated with reduced mitochondrial efficiency, lower stress-buffering capacity, and increased susceptibility to mitochondrial dysfunction,57 whereas extracellular advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) accumulation may engage the AGE receptor RAGE to amplify TGF-β2–stimulated Smad activation and ROS generation.86,87 Through these combined effects, microenvironmental cues may influence both signaling activation and mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby contributing to the initiation and persistence of EMT during PCO progression.

Surgery-Related Factors

During cataract surgery, mechanical stimuli contribute to several stress signals. Cavitation generated by phacoemulsification can produce hydroxyl radicals and other ROS in the irrigation fluid, and the magnitude of this effect depends on parameters such as energy settings, irrigation/aspiration conditions, and the composition of the irrigating solution.4,82,83 In addition, mechanical stress is rapidly sensed by LECs and elicits calcium-signaling responses. The mechanosensitive channel TRPV4 in LECs responds to osmotic and stretch-related cues; mediates calcium influx, hemichannel opening, and ATP release; and thereby converts physical stimuli into intracellular signals.84,85

Following these intraoperative events, postoperative biochemical cues further reinforce the profibrotic microenvironment. Within 1 to 6 hours after surgery, residual LECs display increased expression of inflammatory mediators such as CXCL1, S100A9, cyclo-oxygenase-2, and LCN2, with levels peaking at around 24 hours.80 Postoperative aqueous humor TGF-β concentrations rise, providing sustained stimulation to residual LECs.81 In addition, FGFs are abundant in the vitreous, and after surgery the posterior capsule becomes more directly exposed to the vitreous.88–90 Under this FGF-rich condition, studies in explants and primary cultures suggest that FGF-2 sensitizes LECs to low-dose TGF-β2, facilitating coordinated increases in pSmad2/3 and ERK and promoting EMT.9,88,89

Aging-Related Factors

With advancing age, LECs exhibit multiple forms of mitochondrial dysfunction, including reduced efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation, decreased membrane potential and ATP levels, elevated mtROS, and imbalances in mtDNA copy number and damage repair; these changes are closely associated with cataract formation.57 Collectively, such mitochondrial alterations place cells on a background that is more prone to accumulating oxidative and metabolic stress, and in the presence of cataract-associated inflammation and TGF-β stimulation, may shift them more readily toward EMT. However, in human aged LECs, direct evidence linking age-related mitochondrial defects to increased EMT susceptibility remains limited, and future studies that measure both mitochondrial functional readouts and EMT markers in age-stratified samples may provide more definitive support.

Aging of the external microenvironment also enhances the profibrotic effects of TGF-β signaling. In human lens capsule–based studies, AGEs accumulate markedly in the anterior capsules of older individuals with cataract and potentiate TGF-β2–induced EMT, with levels positively correlated with α-SMA expression.91 The AGE receptor RAGE enhances TGF-β/Smad and ROS signaling, whereas loss or inhibition of RAGE has the opposite effect.86,87 From both intrinsic and extrinsic perspectives, aging lenses appear to be characterized by a reduced internal buffering capacity and an increased external profibrotic drive.

It is important to note, however, that the overall incidence of PCO in clinical follow-up does not increase monotonically with age. Studies indicate that PCO is often more common and more severe in children and younger patients, with postoperative PCO rates in infants approaching 100% if the posterior capsule is left intact92; among adults, younger age is also associated with a higher incidence of PCO.93 This is often viewed as reflecting the greater number and higher proliferative activity of residual LECs in younger eyes.94 Correspondingly, recent work on aged LECs shows that proliferation slows with aging and EMT responses become attenuated.95 Therefore, the mitochondrial considerations discussed in this section primarily refer to changes in the stress tolerance of individual LECs under aging conditions, whereas the influence of age on PCO risk likely also reflects age-dependent differences in the number and regenerative potential of residual LECs. Younger eyes may show a greater overall incidence of PCO because of a larger residual LEC pool and stronger proliferative activity, whereas aging-related mitochondrial dysfunction and AGE/RAGE signaling may mainly sensitize remaining LECs to profibrotic stress.

Timing of Surgical and Aging Influences

When viewed in a temporal context, residual LECs have been reported to mount a strong inflammatory response within 24 hours after surgery, whereas p-Smad3 activation becomes more apparent only after about 48 hours.80 At the same time, emerging human aqueous humor data suggest that cataract surgery may also be followed by a more prolonged inflammatory milieu, with elevated levels of inflammatory mediators, including CD40L, IL-7, MIP-1α/CCL3, and LIF, detected in donor samples collected 3 to 12 years after surgery.96 Although direct evidence linking these persistent mediators to PCO maintenance remains limited, LIF overexpression has been shown to promote EMT in other systems, suggesting that long-term inflammatory cues may have the potential to modulate EMT-related signaling networks.97 These findings suggest that, in an aging microenvironment characterized by reduced stress tolerance and enhanced profibrotic cues, surgery-associated inflammation may act not only as an early trigger, but also as a longer-term modifier of EMT-related signaling networks.

Multistage Intervention and Potential Translational Directions

We outline potential interventions in three time windows: intraoperatively, early postoperatively, and during progression toward fibrotic PCO. In the intraoperative and early postoperative phases, the aim is to raise the thresholds for triggering and amplifying profibrotic signaling, whereas during fibrotic progression the focus shifts toward limiting EMT persistence and restraining the stabilization of profibrotic phenotypes.

Intraoperative Prophylaxis

At the intraoperative stage, current strategies can be broadly divided into two categories. One focuses on refining capsular-related surgical maneuvers, such as anterior capsule polishing and capsular-bag cleaning, to reduce residual LECs and lessen early postoperative signaling input. Clinical and experimental data on the impact of anterior capsule polishing on PCO are inconsistent,3,98,99 but it has been suggested that differences in polishing technique and in the completeness of LEC removal may be important contributors to these discrepancies3,100–102 and warrant further prospective evaluation.

The second approach is to make modest adjustments to the composition of the irrigating solution. Possible modifications include adding stable antioxidant components to standard solutions and transiently lowering the effective TGF-β concentration. Clinically, balanced salt solutions enriched with oxidized GSH, bicarbonate, and glucose (such as BSS PLUS) are used to support endothelial and anterior segment homeostasis, and experimental work suggests that such antioxidant-enriched irrigating solutions can limit cavitation-related ROS and improve the stability of the intraoperative environment.4 Theoretically, brief local neutralization of TGF-β at the end of surgery might reduce the influence of acutely released cytokine on early EMT, a concept supported in animal studies, but confirmation in humans is still lacking.2

Early Postoperative Prophylaxis

From immediately after surgery through the first postoperative day, inflammation and oxidative stress are prominent. In this window, controlling anterior chamber inflammation and reducing ROS load may help to limit downstream amplification of profibrotic signaling (see Signaling Targets and Early Amplification: TGF-β/Smad, Parallel Noncanonical Pathways, and Amplifiers for mechanistic details). Clinically, topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, with corticosteroids when needed, are used to suppress postoperative anterior chamber inflammation.18 In in vitro and in animal models, N-acetylcysteine eye drops41 and small-molecule NOX4 inhibitors10 reduce ROS levels and attenuate TGF-β–induced EMT. Early postoperative pharmacological treatment may help to lower the activation threshold and blunt cascade amplification, but the available evidence does not yet justify recommending any specific drug or regimen solely for PCO prevention.

Preventing Progression Toward Fibrotic PCO

Beyond intraoperative and early postoperative prophylaxis, a later intervention window may aim to limit EMT persistence and prevent progression toward stable fibrotic PCO. At the mitochondrial level, activation of the PGC-1α–TFAM axis may help to preserve mtDNA maintenance, ΔΨm, ATP production, and redox balance under TGF-β2–associated stress.51,62 Modulation of mitochondrial fission–fusion dynamics and mitophagy may provide additional ways to reduce mitochondrial stress and weaken profibrotic phenotype maintenance in residual active LECs.62,66,72 At the transcriptional and epigenetic levels, modulation of histone acetylation and profibrotic transcriptional complexes may also help to limit phenotype stabilization, as illustrated by low-dose aspirin.27 Together, these strategies are better viewed as approaches to restrain fibrotic progression and EMT maintenance. Compared with perioperative prophylaxis, these approaches remain more exploratory and are supported mainly by mechanistic or preclinical evidence.

Delivery-Oriented Translational Strategies

Drug-Eluting IOLs

Drug-eluting IOLs are considered a promising approach and can carry either hydrophilic small molecules or larger biologics. Small-molecule agents, such as the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 and bromfenac-eluting IOLs, can be released in a sustained manner and suppress PCO.19,20 Larger molecules, including TGF-β–neutralizing antibodies, can be tethered to the IOL surface through covalent conjugation or nanocoating techniques.21 Current evidence remains largely based on in vitro and animal models.

Intracameral or Intravitreal Long-Acting Formulations

In addition to drug-eluting IOLs, in which the drug is built into the lens implant itself, intracameral or intravitreal long-acting formulations may serve as independent intraocular sustained-release depots. Unlike IOL-based delivery, their potential relevance to PCO would depend on whether the incorporated antiprofibrotic agents can achieve sufficient exposure within the capsular bag to modulate the signaling pathways discussed, including TGF-β–centered pathways. Such systems may also reduce the compliance burden and surface toxicity associated with frequent eye drops.17 However, current evidence mainly supports their role as sustained intraocular delivery platforms, and their specific value for PCO prevention still requires further validation.

Gene Therapy

For longer-term intervention, AAV-mediated gene silencing may be explored. LEC-targeting AAV serotypes103 can be engineered to deliver constructs such as CTGF-short hairpin RNA39 or a dominant-negative Smad3 mutant,23 with the aim of achieving prolonged, localized expression within the capsular bag after a single injection. These approaches remain at the preclinical stage, mainly in animal models or capsular-bag explants.

Conclusions

To provide a comparative overview of the intervention strategies discussed, Table 2 summarizes their representative current results, relative efficacy and limitations, and future clinical potential. Research on PCO is gradually shifting from a linear, single-pathway view toward an understanding that emphasizes network properties and temporal dynamics. Based on current evidence, PCO can be regarded as a dynamically evolving network disease in which TGF-β/ROS signaling initiates and amplifies profibrotic responses,10,25 mitochondrial homeostasis helps to determine the persistence of EMT,57,62 and surgical and aging-related microenvironmental cues jointly modulate the course of disease81,86,87,91 (Fig. 4). Seen in this way, future work will likely explore multitarget combinations, locally sustained treatments based on innovative delivery systems, and interventions tailored to timing and patient risk, and then test these approaches carefully for both efficacy and safety.
