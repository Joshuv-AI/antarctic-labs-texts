Contexts, mechanisms and outcomes of home-based palliative care implementation in low- and middle-income countries: A realist review
Authors: Duong Dai Le, Khoa Duy Duong, Anna Peeler, Mevhibe Hocaoglu, Charles Normand, Irene Higginson, Richard Harding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 2015, an estimated 16.4 million people in low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) died with serious health-related suffering requiring palliative care. Yet access to palliative care remains limited in LMICs, where the gaps are greatest. Home-based palliative care (HBPC) has been shown to improve access, enhance quality of life and reduce costs. However, evidence on implementing HBPC across the diverse contexts of LMICs remains scarce.AimTo identify which strategies, in which contexts, trigger which mechanisms to produce successful implementation outcomes for HBPC in LMICs.Data sources and MethodsFollowing RAMESES standards, we conducted a realist review to refine an initial programme theory developed through WHO documentation and the CFIR. We searched six databases (MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, EMBASE, Global Health, and CENTRAL) for home-based palliative care in LMICs. Evidence was selected based on relevance, richness, and rigour, using 17 “if-then” statements to synthesise Context-Mechanism-Outcome-Configurations (CMOCs).ResultsIn LMICs, patients with advanced illness and caregivers experience substantial multidimensional unmet needs within health systems that are underfunded and fragmented. We identified five CMOCs in which HBPC was found to be acceptable, feasible, and sustainable. (1) Community engagement: Programmes that actively engaged community members and local civil organisations reduced stigma surrounding palliative care, strengthened collective ownership, and improved programme acceptability and sustainability. (2) Volunteer involvement: Training community-rooted caregivers created trusted bridges between services and families, enhancing volunteer commitment and long-term viability. (3) Nurse leadership: Empowering nurses through training and expanded roles increased professional confidence and trust from patients and families, supporting delivery in resource-constrained settings. (4) Multidisciplinary team: Collaborative team care alleviated feelings of abandonment, fostered hope and reciprocity; encouraged patients and families to contribute back to programmes. (5) Holistic and uninterrupted support for caregivers: Continuous and holistic support for family carers improved confidence in home caregiving and enabled end-of-life care at home.ConclusionShared contextual similarities across LMICs suggest these components could support HBPC implementation in diverse resource constrained settings and potential for cross-national learning. To maximise successful implementation, investment for HBPC should prioritise enhancing community capacity and developing nurse leadership within the team care model.

Introduction

Palliative care needs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are vast and increasing rapidly.1,2 An estimated 16.4 million people died with serious health-related suffering in LMICs which required palliative care in 2015. 1 This number is projected to nearly double by 2060, representing 83% of global serious suffering due to aging and the shift to non-communicable diseases. 2 However, access to palliative care in LMICs remains deeply inequitable compared to high-income countries (HICs). 3 While available services in HIC cover up to 81% of the need, coverage in LMICs reaches only 10-37%. 4 In response, various strategies have been proposed to expand access and improve outcomes, including home-based palliative care (HBPC). 1

HBPC provides holistic support to patients with advanced disease and their families by addressing physical, psychological, and social needs in the homes. 5 Evidence shows that HBPC increases the likelihood of dying at home, reduces symptoms, and improves quality of life for patients.5–7 Programs that integrate specialist and primary care achieve these outcomes while also reducing costs, particularly when they include standardised interdisciplinary meetings, volunteer engagement, and early intervention.8,9 While multiple reviews have demonstrated the effectiveness of HBPC, most originate from HICs. 9 Only a recent integrative review of HBPC model from South-East-Asia found that current HBPC only focus on symptom management with limited family support. 10

However, a major gap remains: no synthesis has examined which components of HBPC work, why they work, and in which context they can be implemented successfully in LMIC countries. This gap limits our understanding of how HBPC models can be adapted and transferred to low-resource settings, where constrained resources require careful selection and modification of interventions. Addressing this gap would help ensure that LMICs can learn from existing models without relying on one-size-fits-all approaches developed in HICs.

Using a realist review approach, 11 this synthesis seeks to identify the contextual conditions and mechanisms that contribute to the successful implementation and integration of HBPC services within LMIC health systems. Rather than duplicating prior effectiveness reviews, this work focuses on generating explanatory insights: how HBPC models operate, what drives their success and how these lessons can inform context-sensitive adaptation in other LMICs where HBPC remains limited.

Methods

Realist synthesis seeks to understand the causal relationships between intervention components and the actors involved in interventions within specific contexts, thereby yielding specific outcomes. 11 Using critical realism as the theoretical lens, it aims to highlight the hidden mechanisms of how the elements or resources in specific context altered the decision-making process of the agents to cause the outcomes of interest. The final product of a realist review is a set of statements consisting of multiple configurations of contexts, mechanisms and outcomes (CMOCs).11–14 We reported terminology definition used in Appendix 1.

In addition, this realist synthesis was enriched by an implementation science perspective. After identifying the intervention components most likely to “work” in LMIC contexts, we examined the implementation outcomes and strategies that could further support their uptake, recognising that these essential elements are often underreported. Implementation outcomes refer to the effects of deliberate actions taken to introduce new treatments, practices, or services. 15 Implementation strategies are the methods or techniques used to enhance the adoption, implementation and sustainability of interventions. 16 To guide this aspect of the analysis, we drew on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR),17,18 a meta-theoretical framework that synthesises multiple implementation science theories and provides a comprehensive approach for examining implementation processes.

We followed the Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses: Evolving Standards (RAMESES) to guide the implementation and reporting of this synthesis.19,20 The review process involved 5 iterative steps as proposed by Pawson et al. (2005). 11 The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024487526).

Step 1: Identifying review questions and defining the scope of the review

Stakeholder engagement is a critical component of realist reviews, as their aim is to inform policy and decision-making. 20 To guide the review, we consulted a panel of seven stakeholders: six palliative care practitioners from Vietnam and one from the United States with extensive experience working in LMIC settings. The group brought a wide range of expertise—including palliative care service developers, managers, educators and researchers—and included five physicians, one nurse and one social worker. During the consultation, stakeholders discussed key questions and challenges related to implementing HBPC in LMICs and helped refine the research focus to ensure that the resulting CMOCs would be relevant to these contexts. A central concern highlighted by the panel was how HBPC can be implemented in settings with limited resources (e.g., lack of funding, staff) and consequently limited service provision. Based on this input, the review was guided by three focused research questions:1) In what contexts have HBPC models been implemented in LMICs?2) What context-specific mechanisms likely explain the successful implementation of HBPC in LMICs?3) What outcomes—linked to these contexts and mechanisms—have been reported in HBPC implementation in LMICs?

Step 2: Scoping literature and developing the initial programme theory (IPT)

We developed the initial programme theory (IPT)—a set of early assumptions about what works, for whom, in what circumstances and how11,19,20—by drawing on a preliminary search of the literature and existing policy documents on HBPC. We conducted an initial MEDLINE search (“home-based palliative care” AND “review”) to identify literature reviews and policy documents on HBPC implementation5,8,9,21–32; the majority of which originated from high-income country contexts.

The first author (DDL) iteratively read and coded relevant texts and, using abductive reasoning, 33 developed a series of 17 preliminary if–then statements about HBPC models (See Appendix 4). An if–then statement is a way of expressing a proposed causal pathway between context, mechanism and outcome. During this phase, CFIR17,18 was used as an analytical framework to ensure a comprehensive theory-informed approach; the IPT statements were mapped to CFIR domains (Appendix 4, Table 4).

The initial if–then statements were refined over two rounds of consultation with a stakeholder panel, focusing on their relevance to LMIC context. The same seven stakeholders reviewed the draft IPT through a survey and group discussion, rating the relevance of each statement on a 5-point Likert scale and providing qualitative feedback. Although most statements scored highly, four—those concerning community volunteers, involvement of local organisations, interconnected HBPC networks, and infrastructure adaptation—showed varied perceptions, suggesting limited shared understanding of mechanism or limited available evidence in these areas. Stakeholders also recommended adding nurses’ roles in HBPC and the influence of family caregivers in home-care decision-making to the CMOCs.

A follow-up online meeting was organised to discuss revisions and consolidate the statements for greater clarity and coherence. The resulting set of 17 refined if–then statements formed the IPT used to sensitise the analysis and guide the development of potential CMOCs.

Step 3: Searching for evidence

We conducted a systematic search across six databases: MEDLINE (Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online), CINAHL (Cumulated Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), PsycINFO, EMBASE (Excerpta Medica dataBASE), Global Health and CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) (See Appendix 2 for the full search strategy). The main search was completed on 17th September 2024. Search strategies were informed by previous reviews of HBPC,5,25,31 and search terms combined synonyms for three core concepts: palliative care, home care, and LMICs. Additional purposive searches—including further database searches and hand-searching—were carried out following established evidence-synthesis procedures.34,35

Step 4: Selecting and appraising studies, and data extraction

All identified citations were uploaded, deduplicated and managed in Covidence (Lumivero). Title–abstract and full-text screening were conducted independently by two reviewers (DDL and KDD), both of whom have medical training and experience in literature reviews. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Appendix 2 (Table 3). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers, with adjudication by a third reviewer (AP) when needed. The screening process is summarised in a PRISMA flow diagram 36 (See Figure 1).Figure 1.PRISMA flowchart: Article flow during the selection process of the studies.

PRISMA flowchart: Article flow during the selection process of the studies.

We appraised the quality of included studies based on relevance, richness and rigour11,37 (See Appendix 3). Relevance (whether it can contribute to theory building or testing) was assessed using criteria from Malone et al. (2012) 38 and Dada et al. (2023). 39 Richness (how much information relating to if-then statements the study contains) was assessed using criteria from Dada et al. (2023). 39 The richness and relevance were categorised as high, medium and low; in order to build the CMOCs to form program theory, the included studies which were classified as high in relevance and richness were prioritised for synthesis. Given the methodological heterogeneity of included studies, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT)40,41 was used to appraise rigour; however, exclusion decisions were made case-by-case, as realist reviews may draw on relevant fragments of data. Disagreements were resolved through team discussion.

We extracted information on HBPC services using a tool adapted from Malone et al. (2012) 38 (Appendix 3), capturing details on intervention components, contexts, and mechanisms. We also collected data on implementation outcomes and implementation strategies, guided by the CFIR framework.17,18 Using Proctor’s taxonomy, 15 we identified implementation outcomes when explicitly reported; when they were not, we inferred and classified based on the available study data. Definitions of implementation outcomes are provided in Appendix 1 (Table 2). To support consistency in terminology, implementation strategies were categorised according to the classifications proposed by Proctor and Waltz et al.16,42

Step 5: Synthesis and theory refinement and dissemination of findings

We utilised retroductive reasoning 33 to identify important domains of contexts, mechanisms and outcomes in each study. We coded data inductively from the text and quotes and deductively by comparing and contrasting with the if-then statements in IPT. Subsequently, we went through the rest of the studies to complete the CMOCs and look for new CMOCs until reaching theoretical saturation. 43 We then presented to stakeholders for feedback to refine the CMOCs to form the final program theory.

Only one included study explicitly defined implementation outcomes, identifying feasibility and acceptability based on adherence, survey response rates, patient or family satisfaction, and economic viability. 75 Across the remaining studies, implementation outcomes were not clearly specified, although most reported elements reflecting acceptability and feasibility, often demonstrated through satisfaction surveys and qualitative feedback; patterns consistent with early-stage service development.

CMOC 1: Engagement of community and local civil organisation (Figure 3)

Context

In LMICs, severe structural constraints limit access to essential services and care, a situation further compounded by widespread stigma surrounding palliative care. Socioeconomic inequalities restrict households’ access to housing, education, and healthcare,48,49,53,68,89 while families face shortages of professional caregivers alongside stigma and fears of “contagion” related to cancer and HIV.46,48–50,52,53,66 Understanding of palliative care is generally limited, and fear of discussing death as well as opiophobia further impede effective symptom management.44,46,48–53,66,88,89 Nevertheless, many communities demonstrate strong traditions of mutual reliance, religious or communal solidarity, and informal social support networks that continue to sustain families.46,48,53,66,67,89Figure 3.CMOC of community engagement.

CMOC of community engagement.

Mechanism

HBPC recognises the strengths within these community networks and actively involves community in providing care and practical support. Community regards people living with serious illness not as a private household burden, but as a shared community responsibility.53,67,68 When patients return home after treatment or enter the terminal stage, community members assist with resources, basic care and daily needs of patients and families, as well as providing social, financial supports and companionship.44,49,53,66,67,89 This intervention component leverages the local community’s sense of togetherness (M1) and mutual benefits (M2) to care for patients and families—members of the community who are suffering and in need.47,50,53,67,68,88“The patient is not only the responsibility of their houses. He is the responsibility of that community. The community has to intervene for the patient.” 53 (p6)

“The patient is not only the responsibility of their houses. He is the responsibility of that community. The community has to intervene for the patient.” 53 (p6)

Community supports foster reassurance (M3), which can help reduce stigma associated with palliative care.48,49,53,68,89 Trusted community figures—such as religious leaders (e.g., monks) 51 — who introduce or advocate for HBPC further strengthen reassurance (M3) among patients and families.51,53 As communities engage more fully, this reassurance helps to lessen stigma and fear of contagion, enabling families to be better prepared for death and end-of-life care.48,49,53 Conversely, limited community awareness of palliative care can reduce acceptance of HBPC, as seen in situations where minimal community involvement contributed to fear and distrust of receiving services at home.52,88

Outcome

Collective community support increases the acceptability (O1) of HBPC through the reassurance generated by practical support and the influence of trusted figures.45,46,48,49,51,53,66,89 The community involvement also enhances HBPC’s feasibility (O2), as patients and families gain access to essential non-clinical support that enables their basic daily needs to be met.44,48,51,53,67,88,89 Importantly, sustainability (O3) emerges through community ownership as success stories further create a positive feedback loop, strengthening trust44,45,50,53:“This [the activities of the clinic] is run by the community, not us as volunteers. If the community does not give money for the activities of the clinic, we will cut activities one by one. This is the responsibility of the community.” 53 (p6)

“This [the activities of the clinic] is run by the community, not us as volunteers. If the community does not give money for the activities of the clinic, we will cut activities one by one. This is the responsibility of the community.” 53 (p6)

Sustainability is reinforced as communities reduce reliance on external resources by contributing socially and financially. In some settings, neighbours have organised formal “Neighbours Networks” to coordinate volunteer training, supervision and fundraising. 50

CMOC 2: Community-rooted caregivers as bridges between services and patients, families (Figure 4)

Context

In LMICs, rising non-communicable conditions and aging populations are increasing demand for palliative care,48,50,51,59,71,86 yet underfunded systems and workforce shortages leave families struggling with significant physical, emotional and financial caregiving burden.48,51,53,58,59,61,68,86 In many collectivist settings, neighbourhood-based social structure connect households and provide (in)formal monitoring and mutual support, helping to improve service delivery and protect collective wellbeing.47,48,50,67,68,72,86Figure 4.CMOC of community-rooted caregiver involvement.

CMOC of community-rooted caregiver involvement.

Mechanism

HBPC models that rely on caregivers such as CHWs or volunteers, draw on these existing social structures, enabling them to act as bridges between families and formal services.47,48,50,53,59,67,68,72,86 CHWs are typically trained, formally designated and paid members of primary health programmes,47,60,70,72 while volunteers often have informal caregiving roles and receive none to minimal remuneration or structured training.47,50,53,55–57,66,67,69 Volunteers are selected from the same geographic or faith communities, trained to provide basic social and psychosocial support, and meet regularly with healthcare professionals for consultation.47,50,53,55,56 Despite these distinctions, we group CHWs and volunteers together as community-rooted caregivers because both are embedded in local networks and function as trusted intermediaries between families and HBPC services.

Building on the community-rooted strength, these caregivers foster a strong sense of trust (M7) with patients and their families.48,51,53,58,60,61,67,69 By recruiting from the same communities—particularly individuals with prior caregiving experience or involvement in religious groups—and providing hands-on training in basic palliative care, HBPC leverages pre-existing relationships and local knowledge.47,48,58,67 Because caregivers share context, understand local resources, and are already known to families, they are welcomed into homes and can act as coordinators, quickly recognising needs and linking patients and families to appropriate resources.48,51,53,58,60,69,71,72 As trust deepens over repeated visits, families become more willing to disclose sensitive concerns53,58,68,69:“Community caregivers narrated multiple instances wherein patients who were initially withdrawn or uncommunicative, turning their face away from family members and visitors, would open up to them over multiple visits. Patients gradually shared issues such as a sense of neglect, abuse, or the unfulfilled desires they hesitated to share with their kin” 68 (p346)

“Community caregivers narrated multiple instances wherein patients who were initially withdrawn or uncommunicative, turning their face away from family members and visitors, would open up to them over multiple visits. Patients gradually shared issues such as a sense of neglect, abuse, or the unfulfilled desires they hesitated to share with their kin” 68 (p346)

Their involvement also activates confidence (M4),48,56,71 respect (M5),55,61,71 and a strong sense of professionalism (M6)53,71,72—mechanism that help explain why community-rooted caregivers are effective in LMIC contexts. They provide non-medical support—including practical assistance, emotional support, and help with errands—as well as, in some models, selected healthcare tasks such as emergency assistance, basic nursing care, and psychosocial support.61,70,72

With sufficient training and supervision, volunteers gain confidence (M4) in their abilities and build trust with families; this confidence is reinforced by visible patient improvement and appreciation48,56,71:“The confidence the patients have in me gives me courage” 48 .(p6)

“The confidence the patients have in me gives me courage” 48 .(p6)

Appreciation—whether relational (e.g., positive reinforcement from teams and patients) or material (e.g., food, stipends)—further strengthens confidence (M4), respect (M5), and a sense of professionalism (M6).48,50,54–57,60,71,72,86 Respect (M5) arises both from external recognition and appreciation and from the self-actualisation derived through helping others.55,61,71 Volunteers emphasise the significance of their contributions—“small, small roles create big, big changes” 53 —which reinforces their motivation to continue.61,68

Many community-rooted caregivers describe strong intrinsic motivation and a desire to make a meaningful difference, reflecting an emerging sense of professional commitment (M6)53,71,72:“So, there is something that bring you till here… Yes, [you] need money. But you want to do a change for somebody.” 72 (p6)

“So, there is something that bring you till here… Yes, [you] need money. But you want to do a change for somebody.” 72 (p6)

This commitment is reinforced by personal connections to patients and, in some cases, by caregivers’ own past experiences with inadequate care.47,68,71,72 However, intrinsic motivation alone may not be sustainable; structured training, regular supervision, manageable workloads, and appropriate incentives are often needed to prevent frustration and maintain care quality.66,72

Outcome

Recruiting trusted, community-rooted volunteers—often with prior caregiving or religious involvement—and providing hands-on training fosters trust with families, increasing the acceptability (O1) of HBPC.47,51,53,55,58,60,69,71,72 Families feel the caregiving burden is shared within the neighbourhood and patients become more comfortable receiving care at home and opening up about deeper emotional concerns. 68 As community-rooted caregivers consolidate confidence, respect and professionalism (M4–M6) into a stable role identity, sustainability (O3) improves through continued volunteer engagement; in some programmes, former recipients later volunteer to ensure others can benefit.47,48,50,53,67 “Volunteers believe that the visible benefits to patients in turn enhance the trust of the community in the programme, which further motivates it to sustain and support it.” 53 p6

CMOC 3. Empowering nurses to lead home-based palliative care (Figure 5)

Context

In many LMICs, nurses are more available and geographically closer to patients’ homes than other health professionals.44–46,73,78,86 They are often socially recognised as compassionate and trustworthy professionals, yet remain undervalued within health systems and by the public.46,64,78,86 Their professional autonomy is limited; nurses frequently require permission from doctors to perform tasks deemed outside a narrowly defined scope of practice.78,86Figure 5.CMOC of nurse empowerment.

CMOC of nurse empowerment.

Mechanism

Nurses’ competence and confidence grow (M8) as HBPC models empower them through knowledge transfer and task-shifting, equipping them with specialised skills (e.g., ostomy care, lymphedema management) and the ability to “think out of the box” in resource-constrained settings.44–46,73–75,78,86 As their capability increases, nurses become more confident acting independently, developing creativity and professional growth beyond routine hospital roles.46,78,86 They provide a wide range of clinical and supportive services—such as monitoring vital signs, medication management, ostomy and lymphedema care, 24/7 telephone support, caregiver education and liaison with specialist teams44,45,59,64,73–77—alongside companionship, reassurance, and dignity-preserving care.46,63,64,78,86

Regular home visits, compassionate communication, and continuity of presence allow nurses to build close, family-like relationships with patients and caregivers.46,63,77,78,86 Families value nurses’ friendliness, timeliness, and listening skills.45,46,62,77,78 These interactions foster trust (M7), as patients and families come to view nurses as competent, reliable, and caring.58,63,78

At the same time, nurses derive satisfaction and a sense of higher purpose from the trust and recognition they receive from patients and families.46,76,78 They consistently report valuing patients’ acceptance above any formal awards. 78 These bonds of compassion and mutual appreciation reinforce nurses’ motivation and self-worth (M9),46,76,78 reflected in one nurse’s account:“In this field, I can plan, work independently and care for the needy. This gives me much more satisfaction.” 78 (p5)

“In this field, I can plan, work independently and care for the needy. This gives me much more satisfaction.” 78 (p5)

However, nurses also face substantial challenges, including low salaries, heavy workloads, and significant emotional burden.63,73,78,86 A major structural barrier is the lack of a clearly defined role and scope of practice within health policy, which restricts autonomy and limits professional and organisational development.64,78 Nevertheless, HBPC models that rely on nurses without adequate training, supervision, or compensation risk exacerbating workload pressures and may undermine patient trust and care quality.64,73

Outcome

When HBPC models prioritise empowering nurses to take a leading role, the service becomes more acceptable (O1), feasible (O2), and sustainable (O3). The relational trust nurses build through regular home visits, compassionate communication, and continuity of presence leads to greater acceptability (O1) and feasibility (O2), as patients and families feel comfortable welcoming nurses into their homes and confident receiving care there.44–46,58,63,75–78,86 Empowered nurses can also work beyond traditional clinical tasks—taking on management, coordination, and psychosocial support—which enhances the feasibility (O2) of delivering holistic HBPC.44–46,63,75,78 In turn, autonomy, mutual appreciation, strengthened motivation, and enhanced self-worth support nurses’ sustained commitment to HBPC, contributing to the sustainability (O3) of the model.44–46,75,76,78

CMOC 4: Multidisciplinary home care team (Figure 6)

Context

In many LMICs, underfunded and increasingly privatised health systems mean that patients with terminal illness often reach public services only at advanced stages.48,49,68 The burden of care thus shifts heavily to families, who face physical, financial, and social exhaustion under strong cultural expectations.53,79,84 Suffering becomes normalised, long waits foster a sense of abandonment, and reluctance to seek help compounds a vicious cycle of isolation and hopelessness.48,68 For many, the “wait for care” gradually turns into waiting for death48,68,84—reflecting under-care not only at the level of resources but also at the level of meaning.Figure 6.CMOC of multi-disciplinary teams providing care at home.

CMOC of multi-disciplinary teams providing care at home.

Mechanism

HBPC interrupts this cycle by replacing passive waiting with responsive, holistic care through multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) consisting of doctors, nurses, medical social workers, physiotherapists, and local volunteers.48,49,53,74,76,79,80,82,85 These models employ collaboration and task-shifting across multiple professions and involve volunteer networks—an intervention component described above—to provide comprehensive psychological and social support.48,49,53,74,76,79,82,85 They act as liaisons to local resources and offer routine home visits; in some settings, access is rapid or even available 24/7 via cellphones. This greater availability and breadth of support reduce waiting and foster reassurance (M3) for patients and families.48,49,53,68,84“For the couple, the community palliative care team always answered the phone or called them back, no matter the time of day. Through this accompaniment across time, this suffering does not so much disappear, but becomes endurable.” 68 (p345)

“For the couple, the community palliative care team always answered the phone or called them back, no matter the time of day. Through this accompaniment across time, this suffering does not so much disappear, but becomes endurable.” 68 (p345)

MDTs share a mindset that treats patients and families as whole persons, not just clinical cases defined by professional silos. Teams demonstrate understanding of suffering and, especially through volunteers with lived experience, often share similar backgrounds with patients and families.48,49,53,74,76,79,82,85 Care is delivered with openness, dignity, agency, and shared decision-making, shifting practice from a purely biomedical focus toward person-centred care—moving from cure to peace of mind.48,68 In one Indian study, this orientation drew on the local concept of samādhānam. 68 Because care aligns with patients’ and families’ values, they experience reassurance (M3) and renewed hope (M10), as the possibility of achieving peace of mind—captured in the cultural notion of samādhānam—becomes tangible.48,68,84“We instil a sense of hope….Before, hope was lost in pain.” 48 (p5)

“We instil a sense of hope….Before, hope was lost in pain.” 48 (p5)

MDTs also help patients and families pursue achievable goals for the patients’ remaining time—supporting self-determination (M11) rather than passive waiting for death.46,48,53,68,81,83–85 As one participant shared, drawing on the local notion of swatantrayam (self-determination):“The palliative care team does not see us as patients. Even the doctor does not see us as patients. We have a relationship with him and talk to him openly. He has the swatantryam to tell me and I have the swatantrayam to tell them. He doesn’t refer to me as viklang (disabled).” 68 (p345)

“The palliative care team does not see us as patients. Even the doctor does not see us as patients. We have a relationship with him and talk to him openly. He has the swatantryam to tell me and I have the swatantrayam to tell them. He doesn’t refer to me as viklang (disabled).” 68 (p345)

Through this consistent, person-centred, and values-aligned approach, MDTs foster trust (M7)—both in palliative care as a service and in the possibility of living meaningfully with serious illness, including at the end of life.46,48,53,68,81,83–85

Outcome

As a result, HBPC delivered through MDTs becomes both acceptable and deeply valued (O1), due not only to greater availability and quality of care but also to a shift in its cultural meaning.49,74,77,81,82,85 Patients who were initially withdrawn gradually open up to caregivers and disclose concerns they felt unable to share with family members or hospital staff.49,53,68,82 Following MDT support, families often experience a sense of reciprocity (M12), prompting them to help others in similar situations—either by volunteering or offering practical, day-to-day assistance.48,49,68,84 Motivated to “pay it forward,” many family members go on to join MDT as volunteers to help others achieve the “non-market”samādhānam.48,49,68,84 Consequently, sustainability (O3) is reinforced not only through financial or institutional inputs but also through ongoing community engagement and mutual support, which help sustain the programme even where formal system support is limited.53,68

CMOC 5: Holistic, continuous support for carers (Figure 7)

Context

In LMICs, family caregivers are often the primary source of care for patients with life-limiting conditions in underfunded and understaffed health systems.52,53,79–82,85 This responsibility places a heavy physical, psychological, financial, and social burden on them, which intensifies as patients’ conditions deteriorate.48,53,79,80,82,85Women—particularly wives, daughters, and daughters-in-law—are disproportionately affected due to cultural expectations, gender inequality, and family power dynamics.53,79,81Figure 7.CMOC of holistic and continuous care for carers.

CMOC of holistic and continuous care for carers.

Mechanism

HBPC teams provide caregivers with information and hands-on training—for example, wound care, symptom management, and responding to emergencies.53,80,81 Services also offer frequent and flexible support through liaison with local general practitioners and, in some cases, 24/7 hotlines.49,60,68,75,80,85 HBPC extends well beyond conventional clinical roles by offering assistance with financial planning, 53 spiritual support, 48 and “accompaniment across time”.53,68 This ongoing, reliable presence fosters reassurance (M3) for caregivers, who feel supported and no longer alone in managing complex needs.48,49,53,68,79,80,82,85“We [the home care team] are able to give regular care. [The patients] get the feeling that whatever is humanly possible is being done for them. Care-givers are well supported and they know they have someone to call when there is a difficulty, someone who can understand their problems. They are getting reassurance.” 53 (p6)

Care-givers are well supported and they know they have someone to call when there is a difficulty, someone who can understand their problems. They are getting reassurance.” 53 (p6)

Through continuous, need-responsive support, suffering does not disappear but becomes more manageable and shared.49,53,68,80,82,85 Feeling guided, backed by a responsive team, and equipped with practical skills enables caregivers to feel capable and empowered (M13) to reconnect with daily life, care for their loved ones, confront fears surrounding death, and carry out essential caregiving tasks—such as managing symptoms and administering medications—through the end of life.48,49,53,75,79,80 When well supported, family caregivers often describe satisfaction and pride in completing caregiving tasks at home. 48 “ … I learned how to measure his medicine and give it to him. Outcome

Together, these mechanisms help HBPC become both accepted (O1) and feasible (O2) within resource-constrained settings.53,68,75,79–82,85 When caregivers feel supported, confident, and reassured, they are more willing to maintain patients at home and to trust the quality of HBPC services.48,49,53,75,79,80 Conversely, insufficient support risks undermining this trust, leading families to prefer hospital readmission 52 —especially at the end of life—thereby reducing acceptability and feasibility of HBPC.

Discussion

Our realist synthesis examined how HBPC components influence key actors—patients, families, nurses, and community members—by triggering specific mechanisms within LMIC contexts. We identified five interrelated CMOCs (Figure 8), with mechanisms such as trust, reassurance, and hope operating across multiple models of care. Together, these findings shed light on how HBPC can expand access to high-quality palliative care in diverse LMIC settings. Overall implementation of HBPC in LMICs

The CFIR framework17,18 offers a structured way to examine HBPC implementation by identifying contextual determinants across the outer setting, inner setting, and individual characteristics, thereby supporting the analytical development of CMOCs. Its domains related to intervention characteristics, implementation processes, and outcomes also help elucidate how different elements of HBPC implementation interact. Across the included studies, we identified recurring contextual factors, including deep socio-economic inequalities, under-resourced health systems, late presentation to care, and long waiting times that shift caregiving responsibilities onto families in many LMIC settings.3,22,90 These shared contextual features highlight the value of South–South learning and experience exchange. 90 Previous lessons from HIV and cancer programmes demonstrate how this type of collaboration can empower stakeholders and shift mindsets.91–93 The similarity of contexts across the five CMOCs also suggests that these intervention components could be—and may be most effective when—implemented together as part of a comprehensive approach. An implementation science framework, such as CFIR,17,18 can help coordinate this integrated implementation by aligning components with local determinants and processes. Complementing this, evidence from High income countries also supports the effectiveness of interventions aligned with these five identified CMOCs.6,9,94 In a broader context, while these five identified CMOCs were derived from LMICs, they may also be applicable—if appropriately adapted—to resource constrained populations in High income settings.

Our mapping (Figure 9) further shows that the identified CMOCs explain HBPC implementation primarily through individual-level, intrinsic mechanisms—such as trust, altruism and professional autonomy—operating among patients, families, healthcare providers, and communities. Within the LMIC-focused literature identified in this review, far less attention is paid to mechanisms shaping decision-making at the system level, as the perspectives of programme managers and policymakers are largely absent despite their central role in influencing implementation. While intrinsic motivation may facilitate early implementation, long-term sustainability depends on translating these individual drivers into formal, government- and society-led mechanisms. 95 Policies are therefore needed to formalise HBPC within health systems, strengthen workforce capacity through training and ongoing support to prevent attrition, 88 and provide clear policy direction from health ministries to secure funding67,77 and establish standards for service scope, quality, and sustainability. 66

Current implementation strategies focus largely on capacity-building approaches, including stakeholder education (e.g., training MDTs, CHWs, volunteers, and family carers), interactive assistance (e.g., supervision by specialists or trainers), and evaluative or iterative strategies (e.g., feedback from service users, providers, and other stakeholders). While important, these strategies alone are insufficient to address outer-setting challenges beyond the practice of palliative care, such as chronic under-investment and fragmented health services. Moreover, WHO-recommended strategies—such as stakeholder engagement, consumer involvement, and localised needs assessments—remain scarce and warrant greater prioritisation. 27

Key gaps in the target literature remain, including limited data on implementation costs, financing mechanisms and referral pathways—echoing other reviews. 10 Successful HBPC models often integrate into existing public or primary care systems rather than create new ones.44,45 Although the CMOC of financial flexibility was not supported, effective models provided direct government subsidies,44,45 free services funded by central or local authorities, 79 or packaged support such as food vouchers and medication.49,82 Despite prevalent opiophobia in LMICs, mitigation strategies were rarely described. Only one model in Malaysia reported success through clear opioid procedures, public campaigns and policy advocacy to extend prescription duration.44,45

CMOCs and practice-oriented implications

Community engagement

Mechanisms of reciprocity and trust between communities and health programmes observed in our review echo findings from palliative care, maternal and child health initiatives in LMICs.39,96 Public health oriented palliative approaches can strengthen social networks and enable inclusive, sustainable care.22,97–100 In some rural settings where community is more cohesive, leveraging organic community structures may suffice. However, in urban areas with fragmented social cohesion, strategies may partner with formal organisations, local authorities and faith-based groups to maintain trust and reach. 101 HBPC should maintain an open-minded and collaborative partnerships with communities to avoid the caveat of turning the communities to merely recipient of services. 102 Additionally, the implementation of community initiatives in palliative care faces significant barriers rooted in social dynamics, power imbalances, cultural resistance to external aid and economic vulnerabilities.96,101

Based on the findings, we recommend that:• As community ownership may evolve over time and communities may adapt, reshape, or resist interventions, HBPC implementers should establish mechanisms for continuous dialogue with communities, allowing for iterative feedback and adaptation.• Policymakers should develop and implement policies that formally recognise and legitimise the role of communities in HBPC. These policies should account for how community involvement intersects with wider social determinants, such as housing conditions, access to social protection, and welfare systems.

Volunteer and CHW involvement

Volunteers and CHWs are central to HBPC, filling workforce gaps and extending reach and continuity in resource-limited settings.22,96,103 Many HBPC models build on existing CHW networks to leverage established community relationships, particularly in programmes serving people living with HIV or leprosy. 47 When adequately trained and supervised, they can identify emerging problems early and prevent avoidable hospitalisations.8,9 At the same time, volunteers and CHWs often face mistrust, emotional strain and logistical barriers.48,71 Evidence from this review highlights the importance of structured training, clear role definitions, emotional support, continuous supervision, appropriate remuneration, and visible integration into HBPC teams (as reflected in CMOC 4) to enhance legitimacy and prevent burnout.9,67,71,103 Conversely, assigning new responsibilities without adequate preparation can lead to role stress and moral distress. 72 In some settings, over-formalisation of volunteer roles has also weakened community ties and reduced volunteer commitment.47,72

Empowering nurses in HBPC

Evidence from diverse health systems indicates nurse-led models improve quality of life, satisfaction, continuity, and cost-effectiveness.104–108 In HBPC implementation, nurses can serve as educators, coordinators, and facilitators of patient empowerment, strengthening self-management and adherence. 106 However, in LMICs, nurse-led palliative models remain limited by restrictive scopes of practice, low public recognition, insufficient training, heavy administrative workloads, and cultural preferences for physician-led care.109,110 Some families may view the absence of physicians in HBPC as reduced quality of care.62,74

Hybrid models—nurse-coordinated MDTs supported by periodic physician involvement or telemedicine—can help mitigate these barriers.10,22,106,107 For successful HBPC implementation, nurses require ongoing training in communication, psychosocial support, symptom management, and self-care to build competence and trust.64,78,90 Without adequate preparation for expanded roles, they risk feeling unprepared and emotionally burdened. 72 Structured protocols, stress-reduction programmes, and supportive supervision are essential for maintaining resilience and preventing burnout. 78 To ensure sustainability, these empowerment strategies must be reinforced by system-level measures, including standardised and periodic HBPC training with ongoing supervision, building research capacity and policies that formalise and expand nurses’ job descriptions and scope of practice—granting greater autonomy, legitimacy, and stability for nursing leadership in HBPC.77,86,110–112

MDT homecare

Consistent with other reviews, studies in our review indicate that MDTs in HBPC across LMICs typically include physicians, nurses, and volunteers.10,22 Mechanisms of reassurance and enhancing self-determination in patients and caregivers are also observed in other reviews.6,94 Psychologists and social workers are less commonly integrated, likely reflecting constraints in workforce and investment, as well as mental health stigma.113,114 Addressing this gap requires capacity building in mental health across all team members and the integration of mental health services into HBPC. 22 For successful implementation, MDTs need palliative care training, interdisciplinary education, and ongoing supervision to maintain clinical proficiency.57,89 Training is essential both to prevent frustration among staff 73 and to build patient and family confidence in the MDT. 83 Ongoing supervision fosters a sense of ownership and interdependence among team members.46,54 Regular MDT meetings has been shown to strengthen communication and ensure continuity of care.8,9

Continuous support for caregivers

Our findings are consistent with existing literature showing that support, communication and training for family carers can facilitate care delivery in resource-constrained settings.22,90 Similar to findings in HIC, the reassurance of caregivers acts as a vital factor to ensuring HPBC is acceptable.31,115 Although HBPC often shifts more caregiving responsibilities onto families—especially in LMICs where formal support is limited—evidence shows that HBPC can benefit family caregivers by reducing distress and burden, and by improving quality of life, self-efficacy, and caregiving competence. 116 Similar benefits were observed in several studies within our review. However, care teams in LMICs should be aware that caregivers often under-report distress due to social norms and limited formal support, 10 and may therefore need to actively seek out and assess caregivers’ needs.

Continuity of support emerged as critical: family carers feel reassured and secure when services are consistently available—a factor strongly associated with enabling home deaths. 31 Continuous 24/7 support appears to be a key component of effective HBPC models,25,117 yet many programmes lack the infrastructure to provide it—highlighting the need for investment in secure, efficient information-sharing across teams.

Based on the findings, we recommend that:• HBPC guidance should include recommendations that address the multidimensional needs of family caregivers, alongside patient care. It should also incorporate measurable outcomes for both caregivers and patients to assess care quality and impact.

Methodological reflections

Through this review, we reflected on the theoretical complementarity between realist synthesis and implementation science 118 in strengthening the implementation of HBPC in LMICs. The use of an implementation science framework—the CFIR17,18—with its structured domains supported the analysis and interpretation of this realist review. Specifically, CFIR helped organise how different aspects of HBPC implementation relate to one another, including intervention features, implementation strategies, and resulting outcomes (Figure 9). By providing predefined domains, this framework also facilitated systematic data extraction, categorisation, and mapping, enabling identification of areas that are well examined, underexplored, or methodologically challenging within the existing literature. Within this structure, realist mechanisms explain how and why individuals and organisations respond to particular contexts and processes. Combining realist explanation with an implementation science framework therefore strengthened both explanatory depth and analytical structure, allowing clearer identification of gaps across implementation domains and opportunities to improve methodological rigour and reporting.

Despite this potential, implementation science remains underused in LMIC HBPC research. Standardised tools to assess implementation outcomes—such as acceptability, feasibility, and sustainability—are rarely applied. Outcomes are often inferred rather than directly examined (for example, acceptability inferred from caregiver satisfaction), and reporting of implementation strategies is inconsistent across studies. Greater use of implementation science methods in HBPC and palliative care research would strengthen the evidence base, support scale-up, and better inform policy decisions.119–121

A major strength of our approach is combining realist synthesis with the CFIR, enabling a systematic examination of the underlying mechanisms and contextual determinants influencing HBPC implementation in LMICs. By drawing on multiple data sources and mapping findings to frameworks such as CFIR, we offered a theory-driven explanation of not only what works, but also why and under which conditions. By utilising implementation concepts, we extracted critical insights from a field where such methodologies have rarely been applied.

However, some limitations remain. The review drew on a small set of extracted contexts, which were reported in English and many implementation outcomes were inferred from indirect indicators. As a result, some if–then propositions in the IPT could not be fully developed into CMOCs. Realist synthesis cannot uncover all mechanisms—especially in under-reported areas. Due to limited evidence, we grouped CHWs and volunteers into a single category for this analysis. Additionally, stakeholder consultation was limited by the absence of policy-level government representatives and by participation from a wider LMIC countries. While we tried to include senior clinicians with service-planning experience contributed valuable perspectives; however, this did not fully substitute for policymaker input. Broader regional representation across Asia may have provided additional contextual and governance-related insights.

Future research directions

Following the reflection of combining realist approach and implementation science in HBPC, we recommend that future research should extend realist approaches across a wider range of HBPC models in LMIC contexts, particularly nurse-led and volunteer-supported programmes. Studies should incorporate the perspectives of policymakers and health managers to better capture system-level mechanisms influencing implementation. Greater application of implementation science principles—including theory-driven designs, explicit implementation strategies, and direct measurement of implementation outcomes—is needed. The use of standardised measures for acceptability, feasibility, and sustainability, rather than indirect inference, would enhance scalability, comparability, and knowledge translation.

Conclusion

This realist synthesis offers a theory-driven account of how HBPC “work” in LMIC by identifying core mechanisms—hope, reassurance, trust, reciprocity, confidence, and empowerment—and mapping them to contextual determinants. We call for HBPC models centred on multidisciplinary teams, nurse-led care, community-based health workers, community engagement, and comprehensive caregiver support. Integrating implementation-science principles—clear implementation strategies and standardised outcome measures—into palliative care will strengthen evidence, guide scale-up, and support advocacy for equitable care in diverse settings worldwide.
