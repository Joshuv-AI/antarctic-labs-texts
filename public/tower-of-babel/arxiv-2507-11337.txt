Survey on the Farrell-Jones Conjecture
Lück, Wolfgang

Abstract
. This is a survey on the Farrell-Jones Conjecture about the algebraic K K - and L L -theory of groups rings and its applications to algebra, geometry, group theory, and topology.

1.
Introduction

The Farrell-Jones Conjecture is devoted to the algebraic
K
- and
L
-theory of the
group ring
RG
of a group
G
and a ring
R
. Certainly

K
- and
L
-theory are rather sophisticated theories. Group
rings are very difficult rings, for instance, they are in general not commutative, Noetherian,
or regular, and may have zero-divisors. So studying the algebraic

K
-theory and
L
-theory of group rings is hard and seems to be on the first glance a
very special problem. So why should one care?

The answer to this question is that information about the
K
- or
L
-theory of group
rings has many striking applications to algebra, geometry, group theory, topology, and
operator algebras and that meanwhile the Farrell-Jones Conjecture is known for a large
class of groups. In the sequel we call a group a
Farrell-Jones group
if it
satisfies the so called
Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
which does imply all the
variants of the Farrell-Jones Conjecture appearing in the literature. The class
{\mathcal{F}}\!{\mathcal{J}}

of Farrell-Jones groups contains many groups which “occur in daily life”, e.g.,
hyperbolic groups, finite dimensional CAT(0)-groups, lattices, fundamental groups of
manifolds of dimension
\leq 3
, and
S
-arithmetic groups. Moreover, it has very
interesting inheritance properties, for instance it is closed under passing to subgroups
and to directed colimits (with arbitrary structure maps). In particular it contains all
directed colimits (with arbitrary structure maps) of hyperbolic groups and hence a lot
of groups with exotics properties, e.g., groups with expanders.

There are many open conjectures in algebra, geometry, group theory, and topology which
do follow from the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture but for which the class for which they
were proved by other methods is much smaller than the class
{\mathcal{F}}\!{\mathcal{J}}
. Here is a
(incomplete) list of such conjectures or applications which we will discuss or for which
we will give references for a discussion:

•

Vanishing of projective class groups of integral group rings and of the Whitehead
group for torsionfree groups;

•

Classification of closed manifolds;

•

Conjectures due to Bass, Borel, Novikov, and Serre;

•

Poincaré duality groups;

•

Automorphism groups of aspherical closed manifolds;

•

Hyperbolic groups with spheres as boundary;

•

The stable version of the Cannon Conjecture.

So for the reader the applications are more accessible and interesting than to figure
out the complicated formulation of the Full-Farrell-Jones Conjecture itself. Therefore the reader
may concentrate on
Section
2
, Section
3
, Section
7
, Subsection
8.8
, Section
9
,
and Section
11
.

The original formulation of the Farrell-Jones Conjecture with rings as coefficients
appears in
[
44
, 1.6 on page 257]
.

This article can be viewed as an extract of the book
[
69
]
, where much
more information and references to the literature about the Farrell-Jones Conjecture
are given.

2.
The projective class group

2.1.
The definition of the projective class group

The
projective class group
K_{0}(S)
of a ring
S
is the abelian group which is
obtained from the Grothendieck construction applied to the abelian semigroup of isomorphism
classes of finitely generated projective
S
-modules under direct sum. Equivalently, it
can be described as the abelian group whose generators are isomorphism classes
[P]
of
finitely generated projective
S
-modules
P
and for every exact sequence

0\to P_{0}\to P_{1}\to P_{2}\to 0
of finitely generated projective
S
-modules we require
the relation
[P_{1}]=[P_{0}]+[P_{2}]
.

The second definition yields the following universal property of the projective class
group. Note that any finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
defines an element
[P]
in

K_{0}(S)
. An
additive invariant

(A,a)
for the category of finitely generated projective

S
-modules consists of an abelian group
A
and an assignment which associates to every
finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
an element
a(P)\in A
such that for every
exact sequence
0\to P_{0}\to P_{1}\to P_{2}\to 0
of finitely generated projective

S
-modules we have
a(P_{1})=a(P_{0})+a(P_{2})
. Then
K_{0}(S)
together with the assignment

P\mapsto[P]
is the
universal additive invariant
for the category of finitely generated
projective
S
-modules in the sense that for any additive invariant
(A,a)
there is
precisely one homomorphism of abelian groups
\Phi\colon K_{0}(S)\to A
such that

\Phi([P])=a(P)
holds for any finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
.

The
reduced projective class group
\widetilde{K}_{0}(S)
of a ring
S
is obtained
from
K_{0}(S)
by dividing out the subgroup generated by all finitely generated free

S
-modules. Any finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
defines an element
[P]
in

K_{0}(S)
and hence also a class
[P]
in
\widetilde{K}_{0}(S)
. The decisive property of

\widetilde{K}_{0}(S)
is that
[P]=0
holds in
\widetilde{K}_{0}(S)
if and only if
P
is

stably free
, i.e., there are natural numbers
m
and
n
satisfying

P\oplus S^{m}\cong_{S}S^{n}
. So roughly speaking,
[P]\in\widetilde{K}_{0}(S)
measures the
deviation of a finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
from being stably free.

2.2.
Wall’s finiteness obstruction

A
CW
-complex
X
is called
finitely dominated
if there is a finite
CW
-complex

Y
and maps
i\colon X\to Y
and
r\colon Y\to X
such that
r\circ i
is homotopic
to
\operatorname{id}_{X}
. Often one can construct a finitely dominated
CW
-complex with interesting
properties but needs to know whether it is homotopy equivalent to a finite

CW
-complex. This problem is decided by the
finiteness obstruction
due to Wall. A
finitely dominated connected
CW
-complex
X
with fundamental group
\pi
determines an
element
\widetilde{o}(X)\in\widetilde{K}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}\pi)
, which vanishes if and only if
X

is homotopy equivalent to a finite
CW
-complex. So it is interesting to know whether

\widetilde{K}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}\pi)
vanishes because then
\widetilde{o}(X)
is automatically
trivial.

The question whether a finitely dominated
CW
-complex is homotopy equivalent to a finite

CW
-complex appears naturally in the construction of closed manifolds with certain
properties, since a closed manifold is homotopy equivalent to a finite
CW
-complex, and
one may be able to construct a finitely dominated
CW
-complex as a first approximation
up to homotopy. The
Spherical Space
Form Problem
is a prominent example. It aims at
the classification of closed manifolds whose universal coverings are diffeomorphic or
homeomorphic to the standard sphere.

One can actually show for a finitely presented group
G
that

\widetilde{K}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}G)
vanishes if and only if every finitely dominated connected

CW
-complex with fundamental group isomorphic to
G
is homotopy equivalent to a finite

CW
-complex. So we have an algebraic assertion and a topological assertion for any finitely presented group

G
which turn out to be equivalent.

2.3.
Conjectures about the projective class group for torsionfree groups

Conjecture 2.1
.

The reduced projective class group
\widetilde{K}_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}G)
vanishes if
G
is torsionfree.

A ring
S
is called
regular
if it is
Noetherian
, i.e., every submodule of a
finitely generated
S
-module is finitely generated, and every
S
-module has a
finite-dimensional projective resolution. Since
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a regular ring,
Conjecture
2.1
is a special case of the following
more general conjecture.

Conjecture 2.2
.

If
G
is torsionfree and
R
is a regular ring, then the canonical map

K_{0}(R)\xrightarrow{\cong}K_{0}(RG)

is bijective.

In particular
\widetilde{K}_{0}(RG)
vanishes, if
G
is torsionfree and

R
is a principal ideal domain.

Conjecture 2.3

(Kaplansky)
.

If
R
is a field and the group
G
is torsionfree,
then the group ring
RG
has only trivial idempotents, namely,
0
and
1
.

A group
G
is called of type (FF) or (FP) respectively, if the trivial

{\mathbb{Z}}G
-module

possesses a finite dimensional resolution consisting of finitely
generated free
{\mathbb{Z}}G
-modules or finitely generated projective
{\mathbb{Z}}G
-modules respectively.

Conjecture 2.4

(Serre)
.

A group

is of type
(FF)
if it is of type
(FP)
.

Conjecture
2.3
follows from
Conjecture
2.2
, whereas
Conjecture
2.4
follows from
Conjecture

.
Conjectures

Definition 3.2

(Whitehead group)
.

Given a group
G
, the
Whitehead
group

\operatorname{Wh}(G)
is the quotient of
K_{1}({\mathbb{Z}}G)
by the subgroup generated by trivial

3.2.
The
s
-Cobordism Theorem

Given a closed manifold
M
, an
h
-cobordism
W
over
M
is a compact manifold

W
such that its boundary
\partial W
can be written as a disjoint union

\partial W=\partial_{0}W\amalg\partial_{1}W
, there is a preferred identification of
M
with

\partial_{0}W
, and the inclusions
\partial_{k}W\to W
are homotopy equivalences for

k=0,1
.

Theorem 3.3

(The
s
-Cobordism Theorem)
.

The set of isomorphism classes relative
M
of
h
-cobordisms over
M
can be
identified with
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi)
if
M
is connected, has dimension
\geq 5
, and
\pi

denotes its fundamental group.

Theorem
3.3
is astonishing, since the set of isomorphism classes of

h
-cobordism relative
M
over
M
a priori depends on
M
, whereas
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi)
depends
only on the fundamental group. In the classification of closed manifolds it is often a
key step to decide whether an
h
-cobordism
W
over
M
is trivial, i.e,
isomorphic relative
M
to
M\times[0,1]
, since this has the consequence that
M
and

\partial_{1}W
are isomorphic. It is not hard to show that
\operatorname{Wh}(\{1\})
is trivial which
implies together with the results above the Poincaré Conjecture in dimensions
\geq 5

which says that a closed manifold which is homotopic to
S^{n}
is homeomorphic to
S^{n}
.

One can show for a finitely presented group
G
and any natural number
n\geq 5
that

\operatorname{Wh}(G)
is trivial if and only if for every connected
n
-dimensional closed
manifold
M
with fundamental group isomorphic to
G
every
h
-cobordisms over
M
is
trivial, where one can work in the smooth, PL, or topological category.
So we have again an algebraic assertion and a topological assertion
for any finitely presented group
G
which turn out to be equivalent.

For more information and references about Theorem
3.3

we refer to
[
70
, Chapter 2]

Note that it is a corner stone in the so called
Surgery Program
aiming at the classification of manifolds.

3.3.
Simple homotopy equivalences

We have the inclusion of spaces

S^{n-2}\subset S^{n-1}_{+}\subset S^{n-1}\subset D^{n}

where
S^{n-1}_{+}\subset S^{n-1}
is the upper hemisphere. The pair

(D^{n},S^{n-1}_{+})
carries an obvious relative
CW
-structure. Namely,
attach an
(n-1)
-cell to
S^{n-1}_{+}
by the attaching map
\operatorname{id}\colon S^{n-2}\to S^{n-2}
to obtain
S^{n-1}
. Then we attach to
S^{n-1}
an
n
-cell by the
attaching map
\operatorname{id}\colon S^{n-1}\to S^{n-1}
to obtain
D^{n}
.
Let

be a
CW
-complex. Let
q\colon S^{n-1}_{+}\to X
be a map satisfying

q(S^{n-2})\subset X_{n-2}
and
q(S^{n-1}_{+})\subset X_{n-1}
.
Let
Y
be the space
D^{n}\cup_{q}X
, i.e., the pushout

\textstyle{S^{n-1}_{+}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{i}}
\textstyle{D^{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{q}}
\textstyle{Y}

where

Then

inherits a

for

and

.
Note that

is obtained from

by attaching one

and one

is a homotopy equivalence

We call

and say that

There is a map

.
The map

Definition 3.4

(Simple homotopy equivalence)
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a map of finite
CW
-complexes. We call it
a simple
homotopy equivalence

if there is a sequence of maps

X=X[0]\xrightarrow{f_{0}}X[1]\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X[2]\xrightarrow{f_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{n-1}}X[n]=Y

such that each
f_{i}
is an elementary expansion or elementary collapse and

f
is homotopic to the composite of the maps
f_{i}
.

The idea of the definition of a simple homotopy equivalence is that
such a map can be written as a composite of elementary maps, namely, elementary
expansions and collapses, which
are obviously homotopy equivalences and in some sense the smallest and
most elementary steps to pass from one finite
CW
-complex to another
without changing the homotopy type. If one works with simplicial
complexes, an elementary map has a purely combinatorial description.
An elementary collapse means to delete the interior of one simplex and one of its faces
that is not shared by another simplex.
So one can describe the passage from one finite simplicial complex to
another coming from a simple homotopy equivalence by finitely many
combinatorial data.

This approach is similar to the idea in knot theory that two knots are
equivalent if one can pass from one knot to the other by a sequence of
elementary moves, the so-called Reidemeister moves. A Reidemeister
move obviously does not change the equivalence class of a knot and,
indeed, it turns out that one can pass from one knot to a second knot
by a sequence of Reidemeister moves if and only if the two knots are equivalent,
The analogous statement is not true for
homotopy equivalences
f\colon X\to Y
of finite
CW
-complexes
because there is an obstruction for
f
to be simple, namely, its
Whitehead torsion
\tau(f)
which is an element in
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(Y))
.

Theorem 3.5

(Whitehead torsion and simple homotopy equivalences)
.

(i)

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a
homotopy equivalence of connected finite
CW
-complexes. Then its Whitehead torsion

\tau(f)\in\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(Y))
vanishes if and only if
f
is a simple homotopy
equivalence;

(ii)

Let
Y
be a connected finite

CW
-complex. Then for any element
u\in\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(Y))
there exists a homotopy
equivalence
f\colon X\to Y
with a connected finite
CW
-complex
X
as source
satisfying
\tau(f)=u
.

Theorem
3.5
implies for any finitely presented group
G

and any connected finite
CW
-complex
Y
with
\pi_{1}(Y)\cong G
that
\operatorname{Wh}(G)
is trivial
if and only if every homotopy equivalence
f\colon X\to Y
with a connected finite

CW
-complex
X
as source and
Y
as target is simple.

We mention the deep theorem due to Chapman
[
24
]
that every (not necessarily cellular) homeomorphism
of finite

be the inclusion of rings sending

to
t^{\pm 1}
. Define

j_{\pm}:=K_{1}(l_{\pm})\circ j\colon{N\!K}_{1}(R)\to K_{1}(R[t,t^{-1}]).

The homomorphism

sends the class
[P]
of a
finitely generated projective
R
-module

to the class

[r_{t}\otimes_{R}\operatorname{id}_{P}]
of the

-automorphism

that maps

to
ut\otimes p
. The homomorphism

N^{\prime}\colon\operatorname{Nil}_{0}(R)\to K_{1}(R[t])

sends the class
[f]
of the nilpotent
endomorphism

of the finitely generated projective

R
-module

to the class
[\operatorname{id}-r_{t}\otimes_{R}f]
of the

R[t]
-automorphism

\operatorname{id}-r_{t}\otimes_{R}f\colon R[t]\otimes_{R}P\to R[t]\otimes_{R}P,\quad u\otimes p~\mapsto~u\otimes p-ut\otimes f(p).

This is indeed an automorphism. Namely, if
f^{n+1}=0
, then an
inverse is given by
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(r_{t}\otimes_{R}f\right)^{k}
.
The composite of
N^{\prime}
with both
(\operatorname{ev}_{0})_{*}\colon K_{1}(R[t])\to K_{1}(R)
and
\iota\colon K_{0}(R)\to\operatorname{Nil}_{0}(R)
is trivial. Hence

N^{\prime}
induces a homomorphism

N\colon\widetilde{\operatorname{Nil}}_{0}(R)\to{N\!K}_{1}(R).

The proof of the following theorem can be found for instance
in

]
(for regular rings),

,

,

(Bass-Heller-Swan decomposition for
K_{1}
)
.

The following maps are

\displaystyle j\oplus K_{1}(i^{\prime})\colon{N\!K}_{1}(R)\oplus K_{1}(R)\xrightarrow{\cong}K_{1}(R[t]);

\displaystyle B\oplus K_{1}(i)\oplus j_{+}\oplus j_{-}\colon K_{0}(R)\oplus K_{1}(R)\oplus{N\!K}_{1}(R)\oplus{N\!K}_{1}(R)\xrightarrow{\cong}K_{1}(R[t,t^{-1}]).

Let

Remark 5.2

(Glimpse of a homological behavior of
K
-theory)
.

In the case that

is a regular ring, the Bass-Heller-Swan decomposition shades some
homological flavour on
K
-theory. Namely, observe the analogy between the two formulas

\displaystyle K_{n}(R[{\mathbb{Z}}])

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle K_{n-1}(R[\{1\}])\oplus K_{n}(R[\{1\}]);

\displaystyle H_{n}(B{\mathbb{Z}};A)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle H_{n-1}(B\{1\};A)\oplus H_{n}(B\{1\};A),

where in the second line we consider group homology with coefficients in some abelian
group

, which corresponds to the role of
R
in the first line. Analogously one gets
for two torsionfree groups

and
G_{1}
and a regular ring

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{n}(R[G_{0}\ast G_{1}])

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{n}(R[G_{0}])\oplus\widetilde{K}_{n}(R[G_{1}]);

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{n}(B(G_{0}\ast G_{1});A)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{n}(BG_{0};A)\oplus\widetilde{H}_{n}(BG_{1};A),

where
\widetilde{K}_{n}(RG)
is the cokernel of
K_{n}(R)=K_{n}(R[\{1\}])\to K_{n}(RG)
and

\widetilde{H}_{n}(BG;A)
is the cokernel of

\widetilde{H}_{n}(B\{1\};A)=H_{n}(\{\bullet\},A)\to H_{n}(BG;A)
.

Here and elsewhere

denotes the space consisting of one point. The space

is the
classifying space of the group
G
,
which is up to homotopy characterized by the property that it is a

CW
-complex with
\pi_{1}(BG)\cong G
whose universal covering
is contractible.

6.1.
Algebraic
L
-groups

A
ring with involution

S
is an
associative ring
S
with unit together with an
involution of rings

\overline{\ }\colon S\to S,\quad s\mapsto\overline{s},

i.e., a map satisfying
\overline{\overline{s}}=s
,
\overline{s_{0}+s_{1}}=\overline{s_{0}}+\overline{s_{1}}
,
\overline{s_{0}\cdot s_{1}}=\overline{s_{1}}\cdot\overline{s_{0}}
, and
\overline{1}=1
for
s_{0},s_{1}\in S
.
If
S
is commutative, we can equip it with the trivial involution
\overline{s}=s
.

Let
w\colon G\to\{\pm 1\}
be a group homomorphism.
Then the group ring
S=RG
inherits an involution, the so-called
w
-twisted involution

that sends
\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g
to
\sum_{g\in G}w(g)\cdot\overline{r_{g}}\cdot g^{-1}
.

Below we fix a ring
S
with involution. Module is to be understood as left module unless
explicitly stated otherwise. The main purpose of the involution is to ensure that the dual
of a left
S
-module can be viewed as a left
S
-module again. Namely, let
M
be a left

S
-module. Then
M^{*}:=\hom_{S}(M,S)
carries a canonical right
S
-module structure
given by
(fs)(m)=f(m)\cdot s
for a homomorphism of left
S
-modules
f\colon M\to S

and
m\in M
. The involution allows us to view
M^{*}=\hom_{S}(M,S)
as a left
S
-module,
namely, define
sf
for
s\in S
and
f\in M^{*}
by
(sf)(m):=f(m)\cdot\overline{s}

for
m\in M
.

Given a finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
, denote by

e(P)\colon P\xrightarrow{\cong}(P^{*})^{*}
the canonical isomorphism of left

S
-modules that sends
p\in P
to the element in
(P^{*})^{*}
given by

P^{*}\to S,\;f\mapsto\overline{f(p)}
. We will use it in the sequel to identify

P=(P^{*})^{*}
.

Definition 6.1

(Non-singular
\epsilon
-symmetric
form)
.

Let
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
. An
\epsilon
-symmetric form
over an associative
ring
S
with involution is a finitely generated projective
S
-module
P

together with an
S
-map
\phi\colon P\to P^{*}
such that the composite

P\xrightarrow{e(P)}(P^{*})^{*}\xrightarrow{\phi^{*}}P^{*}
agrees with

\epsilon\cdot\phi
.

We call an
\epsilon
-symmetric form
(P,\phi)

non-singular

if
\phi
is an isomorphism.

Definition 6.2

(The standard hyperbolic
\epsilon
-symmetric form)
.

Let
P
be a finitely generated projective
S
-module. The
standard hyperbolic

\epsilon
-symmetric form

H^{\epsilon}(P)
is given by the
S
-module
P\oplus P^{*}
and the

S
-isomorphism

\phi\colon(P\oplus P^{*})\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\epsilon&0\end{pmatrix}}P^{*}\oplus P\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}\oplus e(P)}P^{*}\oplus(P^{*})^{*}\xrightarrow{\gamma}(P\oplus P^{*})^{*}

where
\gamma
is the obvious
S
-isomorphism.

Let
P
be a finitely generated projective
S
-module. Define an involution of abelian groups

T=T(P)\colon\hom_{S}(P,P^{*})\to\hom_{S}(P,P^{*}),\quad u\mapsto u^{*}\circ e(P).

Define abelian groups

\displaystyle Q^{\epsilon}(P)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\ker\left((1-\epsilon\cdot T)\colon\hom_{S}(P,P^{*})\to\hom_{S}(P,P^{*})\right);

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}(P)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\operatorname{coker}\left((1-\epsilon\cdot T)\colon\hom_{S}(P,P^{*})\to\hom_{S}(P,P^{*})\right).

Definition 6.3

(Non-singular
\epsilon
-quadratic form)
.

Let
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
. An
\epsilon
-quadratic form

(P,\psi)

is a finitely generated projective
S
-module
P
together with an
element
\psi\in Q_{\epsilon}(P)
. It is called

non-singular
if the
associated
\epsilon
-symmetric form

(P,(1+\epsilon\cdot T)(\psi))

is non-singular, i.e.

(1+\epsilon\cdot T)(\psi)\colon P\to P^{*}
is bijective.

Definition 6.4

(The standard hyperbolic
\epsilon
-quadratic form)
.

Let
P
be a finitely generated projective
S
-module. The

standard hyperbolic
\epsilon
-quadratic form

H_{\epsilon}(P)
is given by the
S
-module
P\oplus P^{*}
and
the class in
Q_{\epsilon}(P\oplus P^{*})

of the
S
-homomorphism

\phi\colon(P\oplus P^{*})\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}}P^{*}\oplus P\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}\oplus e(P)}P^{*}\oplus(P^{*})^{*}\xrightarrow{\gamma}(P\oplus P^{*})^{*}

where
\gamma
is the obvious
S
-isomorphism.

Definition 6.5

(

-groups in even dimensions)
.

For an even integer
n=2k
define the abelian group
L^{p}_{n}(S)
,
called the
n
-th projective quadratic

-group,

to be the abelian group of
equivalence classes
[P,\psi]
of non-singular
(-1)^{k}
-quadratic
forms
(P,\psi)
, whose underlying

-module

is
a finitely generated projective
S
-module, with
respect to the following equivalence relation: We call

(P,\psi)
and
(P^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
equivalent or stably isomorphic
if and only if there exists finitely generated projective
S
-modules
Q
and

and an
isomorphism of non-singular
(-1)^{k}
-quadratic forms

(P,\psi)\oplus H_{(-1)^{k}}(Q)\cong(P^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})\oplus H_{(-1)^{k}}(Q^{\prime}).

Addition is given by the direct sum of two
(-1)^{k}
-quadratic forms.
The zero element is represented
by
[H_{(-1)^{k}}(Q)]
for any finitely generated projective
S

Note that

corresponds to

in the sense that it can be viewed as the
Grothendieck construction associated to non-singular
(-1)^{k}
-quadratic forms
(P,\psi)

in terms of automorphism,

-quadratic forms

If one replaces in the definition of

finitely generated projective by finitely

.

If

{\mathbb{Z}}G
with the involution sending
\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g
to

\sum_{g\in G}r_{g}\cdot g^{-1}
, there is also the

, where
one considers based free finitely generated

into account.

Actually, for every

, one can define

-groups

,
where we put

\displaystyle L_{n}^{s}({\mathbb{Z}}G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{\langle 2\rangle}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G);

\displaystyle L^{h}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{\langle 1\rangle}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G);

\displaystyle L^{p}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{\langle 0\rangle}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G).

For

\cdots\to L_{n}^{\langle j+1\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G)\to L_{n}^{\langle j\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G)\to\widehat{H}^{n}({\mathbb{Z}}/2;\widetilde{K}_{j}({\mathbb{Z}}G))\\

One defines

\displaystyle L^{\langle-\infty\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{colim}_{j\to-\infty}L_{n}^{\langle j\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G).

For a group homomorphism

, there are also versions

Note that by definition we have

for every

and

These decorations

, or

-theory and lead to

-theory. Namely, for geometric applications the decoration

-Cobordism Theorem

whereas for the Farrell-Jones Conjecture one needs to consider the decoration

because of the Shaneson splitting

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\langle j\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}[G\times{\mathbb{Z}}])

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\langle j\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G)\oplus L_{n-1}^{\langle j-1\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G);

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\langle-\infty\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}[G\times{\mathbb{Z}}])

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\langle-\infty\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G)\oplus L_{n-1}^{\langle-\infty\rangle}({\mathbb{Z}}G).

However, there is a favourite situation, where the decoration do not matter because of the
Rothenberg sequences.

Proposition 6.8
.

The Borel Conjecture

Definition 7.1

(Topologically rigid)

We call a closed topological manifold

The Poincaré Conjecture says that

A space

homotopy groups vanish, i.e.,

Given two aspherical

bijection. In particular, two aspherical

only if they have isomorphic fundamental groups and every isomorphism between their

Remark 7.11

(The Borel Conjecture versus Mostow rigidity)
.

Farrell-Jones
[

construct for given

a closed

whose sectional curvature lies in the

and a closed hyperbolic

such that

are homeomorphic but not

with exotic spheres. Note that by

were hyperbolic if we could take

Hence this example is remarkable in view of

which predicts for two closed hyperbolic manifolds

and any homotopy equivalence

One may view the Borel Conjecture as the topological version of
Mostow rigidity. The conclusion in the Borel Conjecture is weaker,
one gets only homeomorphisms and not isometric diffeomorphisms, but
the assumption is also weaker, since there are many more aspherical
closed topological manifolds than hyperbolic closed manifolds.

10.
Methods and strategies of proofs

We very briefly give some informations about the methods and strategies of the proofs of
the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
8.10
. The key
words are
assembly maps
,
controlled topology
,
flow spaces
, and

transfers
. More details of the strategies of proofs can be found in the survey
article by Bartels
[
3
]
or in
[
69
, Chapter 19]
.

It is rather amazing that the proof of the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture, whose statement
is of homotopy theoretic nature, uses input from geometric group theory and
dynamical systems. This becomes more puzzling if one observes that the Idempotent
Conjecture
2.3
is a purely ring theoretic statement and one
would expect that a proof of it uses only algebraic methods, whereas it turns out that its
proof for prominent classes of groups such a hyperbolic groups is carried out by proving
the
K
-theoretic part of the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
and not by internal algebraic methods.

10.1.
Assembly maps

Probably the basic idea of assembly maps goes back to Quinn
[
81
,
85
]
and
Loday
[
63
]
leading for instance to the
K
-theoretic assembly map

H_{n}(BG;{\mathbf{K}}_{R})\to K_{n}(RG)
. The
L
-theory version and a more algebraic version
was introduced by Ranicki
[
86
]
.
The basic and uniform approach to assembly as it appears in Section
8

is sometimes called the Davis-Lück approach and was developed in
[
29
]
.

Consider a covariant functor
{\mathbf{E}}\colon\EuR{Or}(G)\to\EuR{SPECTRA}
, where
\EuR{Or}(G)
is the
orbit category. It yields a
G
-homology theory
{\mathcal{H}}_{*}(-;{\mathbf{E}})
satisfying

H_{n}(G/H;{\mathbf{E}})=\pi_{n}({\mathbf{E}}(G/H))
for every subgroup
H\subseteq G
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The projection
E_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)\to G/G
induces the assembly map

(10.1)

H_{n}(E_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G);{\mathbf{E}})\to H_{n}(G/G;{\mathbf{E}})=\pi_{n}({\mathbf{E}}(G/G)),

and the various assembly maps appearing in
Section
8
come from specific choices for
{\mathbf{E}}
.

Let
\EuR{Or}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)
be
{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted orbit category, i.e., the full subcategory of

\EuR{Or}(G)
consisting of objects
G/H
with
H\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Then the assembly
map (
10.1
) can be identified with the map

(10.2)

\pi_{n}({\mathbf{p}})\colon\pi_{n}\left(\operatorname{hocolim}_{\EuR{Or}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)}{\mathbf{E}}\right)\to\pi_{n}({\mathbf{E}}(G/G))

where the map of spectra

{\mathbf{p}}\colon\operatorname{hocolim}_{\EuR{Or}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)}{\mathbf{E}}\to\operatorname{hocolim}_{\EuR{Or}(G)}{\mathbf{E}}={\mathbf{E}}(G/G)

comes from the inclusion of categories
\EuR{Or}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)\to\EuR{Or}(G)
and the fact that

G/G
is a terminal object in
\EuR{Or}(G)
. For more information about homotopy colimits and
the identification of the maps (
10.1
) and (
10.2
), we refer
to
[
29
, Sections 3 and 5]
.

This interpretation is one explanation for the name
assembly
. If the assembly
map (
10.2
) is bijective for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, or, equivalently,
the map
{\mathbf{p}}
above is a weak homotopy equivalence, we have a recipe to assemble

{\mathbf{E}}(G/G)
from its values
{\mathbf{E}}(G/H)
, where
H
runs through
{\mathcal{F}}
. The idea is that

{\mathcal{F}}
consists of well-understood subgroups, for which one knows the values
{\mathbf{E}}(G/H)

for
H\subseteq G
and hence
\operatorname{hocolim}_{\EuR{Or}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(G)}{\mathbf{E}}
, whereas
{\mathbf{E}}(G/G)
is
the object, which one wants to understand and is very hard to access.

One can describe the assembly maps also by a universal property. Roughly speaking
it is the best approximation of the functor
G/H\to\pi_{n}({\mathbf{E}}(G/H))
by a
G
-homology theory from the left,
see
[
29
, Section 6]
.

More information about assembly can be found for instance
in
[
68
]
and
[
69
, Chapter 18]
.

10.2.
Controlled topology

Let
M_{0}
and
N
be closed topological manifold of dimension
n\geq 5
equipped with a metric
generating the given topology. Then there exists
\epsilon>0
with the following
properties:

•

Let
M
be a closed manifold and
f\colon M\to N
be a homotopy equivalence which is

\epsilon
-controlled
in the sense that there exists a map

g\colon N\to M
and homotopies
h\colon g\circ f\simeq\operatorname{id}_{M}
and
k\colon f\circ g\simeq\operatorname{id}_{N}

such that for every
x\in M
the diameter of
f(h(\{x\}\times I))
and for every
y\in N
the diameter of

k(\{y\}\times I)
is bounded by
\epsilon
.

Then by the
\alpha
-Approximation
due to Chapman and Ferry
[
25
]

f
is homotopic to a homeomorphism and in
particular has trivial Whitehead torsion. So in order to prove that
N
is topological
rigid in the sense of Definition
7.1
, it suffices to show that a
given homotopy equivalence
g\colon M\to N
is homotopic to an
\epsilon
-controlled
homotopy equivalence. Roughly speaking, to achieve up to homotopy a homeomorphism, it
suffices to gain
\epsilon
-control.

•

An
h
-cobordism
(W;M_{0},M_{1},f_{0},f_{1})
over

M_{0}
is trivial and hence has vanishing Whitehead torsion if we can show that it
is
\epsilon
-controlled. This follows from the Thin
h
-Cobordism Theorem of
Quinn
[
82
, Theorem 2.7]
.
In particular in order to show that
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(N))

vanishes, it suffices to show because of the
s
-Cobordism
Theorem
3.3
that, for any
h
-cobordism

(W;M_{0},M_{1},f_{0},f_{1})
over
M_{0}
, there is an
h
-cobordism

(W^{\prime};M_{0},M^{\prime}_{1},f^{\prime}_{0},f^{\prime}_{1})
over
M_{0}
such that
(W;M_{0},M_{1},f_{0},f_{1})
and

(W^{\prime};M_{0},M^{\prime}_{1},f^{\prime}_{0},f^{\prime}_{1})
have the same Whitehead torsion and

(W^{\prime};M_{0},M^{\prime}_{1},f^{\prime}_{0},f^{\prime}_{1})
is
\epsilon
-controlled;

Hence to prove for a finitely presented torsionfree group
Conjecture
3.6

which predicts the vanishing of
\operatorname{Wh}(G)
, or the Borel
Conjecture
7.4
, which predicts the topological rigidity
of an aspherical closed manifold with fundamental group
G
, a promising strategy is to
gain control, i.e., turning an
h
-cobordism or a homotopy equivalence to an

\epsilon
-controlled one without changing the class associated to it in the Whitehead group.

This turns out to be a major breakthrough since it allows to bring in completely new methods, namely,
geometric methods, into the play. Note that the pioneering work introducing control ideas into assembly
was done by Farrell and Hsiang in several papers, including
[
37
,
39
]
.
These ideas were then extensively developed by Farrell and Jones, in
particular in their seminal papers
[
40
,
41
]
.

Remark 10.3

(Control-Strategy)
.

The considerations above lead to the following

Control-Strategy

for proving the Farrell-Jones Conjecture:

(i)

Interprete elements in the
target group
K_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G)
of the assembly map as a kind of cycles and the source of
the assembly map
H_{n}^{G}(E_{{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{Y}}}(G);{\mathbf{K}}_{R})
as
controlled cycles
, i.e.,
cycles satisfying certain control conditions related to the family
{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{Y}}
;

(ii)

Identify the assembly
map as a kind of
forget control map
;

(iii)

For a specific group
G
and the
family
{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{Y}}
, develop a strategy how to change a cycle without changing its class
in
K_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}G)
such that the new representative satisfies the necessary control
conditions to ensure that the it defines an element in

H_{n}^{G}(E_{{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{Y}}}(G);{\mathbf{K}}_{R})
. This proves surjectivity of the assembly map. One may
call this process
gaining control
;

(iv)

Use a relative version of
part
(iii)
to prove injectivity of the assembly
map. One may call this process
gaining relative control
.

The strategy for
L
-theory is completely analogous.

Example 10.4

(Singular homology)
.

Next we illustrate this strategy in a much easier and classical instance, namely,
singular homology, by repeating how one proves excision for it.

Let
X
be a topological space, and let

C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing}}(X;R)
be the singular
chain complex of
X
with coefficients in the ring
R
. Let

{\mathcal{U}}=\{U_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be a cover of
X
, i.e., a collection of subsets
U_{i}

such that the union of their interiors
U_{i}^{\circ}
is
X
. Denote by
S_{n}^{{\mathcal{U}}}(X)

the subset of the set
S_{n}(X)
of those singular
n
-simplices

\sigma\colon\Delta_{n}\to X
for which there exists
i\in I
satisfying

\operatorname{im}(\sigma)\subseteq U_{i}
. Let
C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing},{\mathcal{U}}}(X;R)
be the
R

subchain complex of
C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing}}(X;R)
whose
n
th chain module consists
of elements of the shape
\sum_{\sigma\in S^{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n}(X)}r_{\sigma}\cdot\sigma
. Let

i_{*}^{{\mathcal{U}}}\colon C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing},{\mathcal{U}}}(X;R)\to C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing}}(X;R)
be the inclusion. The main ingredient in the proof of
excision is to show that
i_{*}
is a homology equivalence. Then excision follows by
applying the result above to
{\mathcal{U}}=\{X\setminus A,B\}
for

A\subseteq B\subseteq X
with
\overline{A}\subseteq B^{\circ}
.

The proof that

i_{*}^{{\mathcal{U}}}\colon C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing},{\mathcal{U}}}(X;R)\to C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing}}(X;R)
is a homology equivalence is based on the construction
of the subdivision operator which subdivides
\Delta_{n}
into a bunch of smaller copies
of
\Delta_{n}
and replaces the singular simplex
\sigma\colon\Delta_{n}\to X
by the
sum of the singular simplices obtained by restricting to these smaller pieces. This
process does not change the homology class but can be used to arrange that the
representing cycle lies in
C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing},{\mathcal{U}}}(X;R)
. This implies
surjectivity of

H_{n}(i_{*}^{{\mathcal{U}}})\colon H_{n}(C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing},{\mathcal{U}}}(X;R))\to H_{n}(C_{*}^{\operatorname{sing}}(X;R))
. One obtains injectivity by applying these
construction to an
(n+1)
-simplex
\tau\colon\Delta_{n+1}\to X
, provided that the
restriction of
\tau
to faces of
\Delta_{n+1}
does already lie in
S_{n}^{{\mathcal{U}}}(X)
.

Roughly speaking, the process of gaining control is realized by subdivision.

For more information about controlled topology we refer for instance to
[
69
, Section 19.4
and Chapter 21]
.

10.3.
Flow spaces

To gain control, flow spaces come into play. Roughly speaking, one considers so called

geometric modules
over a metric space
X
which is essentially a collection of
finitely generated free
R
-modules
\{M_{x}\mid x\in X\}
which is locally finite, i.e.,
for
y\in X
there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
y
in
X
such that its support

\operatorname{supp}(M)=\{x\in X\mid M_{x}\not=\{0\}\}\cap U
is finite. Very roughly speaking,
if
X
comes with an appropriate flow,
one can apply the flow to arrange that the support has arbitrary
small diameter. Thus one gains control. Since the flow is continuous this does not change
the class represented in the
K
- or
L
-theory by such a geometric module (or quadratic form over it)
or by an
automorphisms of such a geometric module (or quadratic form over it).

The appropriate flow spaces come from the geometry of the group
G
under consideration.
Suppose that
G
is the fundamental group of a hyperbolic closed manifold
M
of dimension

d
. Then we take
X=\widetilde{M}
which is just the hyperbolic space
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
. The
basic idea is that for two points
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
and a point
z
on the
boundary
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
there exists
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}d(\Phi^{z}_{t}(x_{0}),\Phi^{z}_{t+\tau}(x_{1}))=0
, if
\Phi^{z}_{t}(x_{0})

denotes the point obtained by moving
x_{0}
to
z
along the geodesic ray starting at
x_{0}

to
z
for the time
t
.

For a survey on the construction of the appropriate flow spaces for certain classes of groups
we refer for instance to
[
69
, Chapter 22]
.

10.4.
Transfers

We at least describe how transfer maps occurred in the work Farrell-Jones. Consider a
closed hyperbolic manifold
M
of dimension
d\geq 5
. We want to show

\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))=0
. There is no flow on
M
but there is the geodesic flow on the sphere
tangent bundle
STM
. There is the transfer map

p^{*}\colon\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))\to\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(STM)).

It sends the Whitehead torsion of an

h
-cobordism
(W;M,M^{\prime},f,f^{\prime})
over
M
to the Whitehead torsion of the
h
-cobordism over

STM
obtain by the pullback construction associated to the projection

p\colon STM\to M
and an appropriate retraction
r\colon W\to M
. The flow on
STM

allows to show that
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(STM))
vanishes. The projection
p
induces an isomorphisms

p_{*}\colon\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(STM))\to\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))
such that

p_{*}\circ p^{*}\colon\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))\to\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))
is multiplication with the Euler
characteristic of the fiber of
p
which is
1-(-1)^{d}
. If
d
is odd, we conclude
that
p_{*}\circ p^{*}=2\cdot\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))}
and hence each element in
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))

has order at most
2
. Farrell-Jones replace
STM
by a certain semisphere bundle

q\colon S^{+}TM\to M
. Its fiber is the upper hemisphere which is contractible and has
therefore Euler characteristic
1
. This implies
q_{*}\circ q^{*}=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(M))}
. Since one can still show that
\operatorname{Wh}(\pi_{1}(S^{+}TM))
vanishes using

The role of the family

Consider an appropriate flow space

11.
Can the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture be true for all groups?

We are not aware of any group for which the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
is known to be false.

Here is a list of interesting groups for which the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
8.10

is open in general at the time of writing:

•

elementary amenable, amenable, or a-T-menable groups;

•

\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
for
n\geq 3
;

•

Artin groups;

•

Thompson’s groups
F
,
V
, and
T
;

•

Torsionfree one-relator groups;

•

Linear groups;

•

Subgroups of almost connected Lie groups;

•

Residually finite groups;

•

(Bi-)Automatic groups;

•

Locally indicable groups.

It is hard to believe that the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
is true for all groups, since there
have been so many prominent conjectures about groups which were open for some time and
for which finally counterexamples were found. On the other hand, the conjecture is known
for so many groups so that we currently have no strategy to find counterexamples.
There are many exotic properties of groups, where one may hope that they rule out the
Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
8.10
, e.g.,
groups with expanders, but for all these properties one knows examples of groups which possess
this property and which do satisfy the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
. One reason is
that often constructions of group with exotics properties are carried out as colimits of hyperbolic groups but
these do satisfy the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
because of
Theorem
8.12

(i)a

and
(ii)f
.

Actually, there is a non-trivial result which holds for all groups and is a consequence of the
Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
.

Remark 11.4

(Universal finitely presented groups)
.

The following observation is amusing that
the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
holds for all groups
if it holds for one specific finitely presented group
U
.

Namely, let
U
be a group that is universal finitely presented, i.e., any
finitely presented group is isomorphic to a subgroup of
G
. Such a group exists by
Higman
[
55
, page 456]
, and there is even a universal finitely presented
group which is the fundamental group of a
complement of an embedded
S^{3}
in
S^{5}
,
see
[
48
, Corollary 3.4]
.

Suppose that
U
satisfies the Full Farrell-Jones
Conjecture
8.10
. Let
G
be any group.
It is the directed union of its finitely generated subgroups.
Hence by Theorem
8.12
(
(ii)f
)
the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
8.10

holds for all groups if and only if it holds for all finitely generated groups.
Any finitely generated group can be written as a directed colimit of finitely presented groups.
Hence by Theorem
8.12
(
(ii)f
)
the Full Farrell-Jones Conjecture
8.10

holds for all finitely generated groups if and only if holds for all finitely presented groups.
Now the claim follow from Theorem
8.12
(
(ii)a
),
since every finitely presented group is a subgroup of
U
.
