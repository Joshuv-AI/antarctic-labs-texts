Energetic closure of the spatially resolved global food system
Maxwell Kaye, Graham K. MacDonald, Eric Galbraith

Abstract
The global food system provides the energy that supports human metabolism, with complex spatial interdependencies between food production, transformation, and consumption. Empirical food system data for these global processes are often fragmented and inconsistent, with only certain components captured in spatially resolved formats. Here we propose a flexible approach to allocating country-level food system data subnationally. We estimate the spatial patterns of food energy production and supply, which we compare to estimates of human metabolism based on average body size. We downscale these rates onto a one-degree resolution grid with 95 corresponding food types to derive an internally consistent, energy-conserving, and spatially resolved dataset. We show that national food supply varies linearly with metabolism per capita, with about half the variation in food supply explained by metabolic rates. Our data processing pipeline is openly available and can readily incorporate new inputs in order to advance trans-disciplinary food system modeling efforts.

Here we provide a flexible and adaptable yet comprehensive approach to studying the global food system from production to metabolism. Our data processing pipeline downscales national food production, consumption, and utilization data proportionally to local grid reference data. This approach robustly incorporates data updates and improvements, and leverages the physical conservation principle that all food that is produced must then be metabolized by humans, utilized in some other form (direct or indirect use by humans), or else lost from the food system. In other words, the global sum of all food sources equals that of food sinks. We use energy as a standard metric to quantify and compare food between different stages, locations, and food groups, and to put initial production and final metabolic end use into direct correspondence. We assemble data on marine fish catch, livestock production, crop production, animal feed consumption, and human food supply onto a unified gridded dataset to study how food flows from land and seascapes to end use. Finally, we approximate global human metabolic rates, and compare these with food supply at the national scale, closing the loop from food production to human bioenergetics.

Gridded food system patterns

Over the surface of the Earth, food production and consumption energy densities range from negligible to millions of Calories per km
2
per day (Figure

At first order, the spatial patterns of food production and final human metabolism are similar, with intensive production at or near the same locations where large population densities drive large metabolic fluxes. However, there are important differences in their distribution. The consumption distribution is unimodal with left-skew on log axes, intuitively reflecting that most metabolism occurs in population dense locations (especially urban areas), whereas the production distribution is multi-modal, reflecting the contrast of intensive agriculture with extensive pastureland and fisheries. This asymmetry between production and consumption is an important qualitative aspect of the spatially-resolved global food system, constraining the necessity for food to flow so that production can support consumption.

The geography of human metabolism of food

Food supply is the quantity of food theoretically available for for human consumption nationally. While food supply is often used as a proxy for diet, a significant fraction of available food is wasted and never consumed, hence we estimate actual human metabolisms and compare with food supply to follow calories through the entire food system. We find a linear correlation between metabolic rates and food supply at the national level (Figure
4
), which is highly significant (
p<10^{-23}
). As national metabolic rate per capita increases (according to body heights and weights), national food supply per capita increases proportionally, nearly at twice the rate. We note that our data sources for food supply (FAOSTAT Food Balance Sheets) and for height and weight demographics (Lancet pooled research reports
[
38
,
35
]
) are entirely independent of each other.

Figure 4:
Left: Correlation between human consumption of food (total metabolic rate = basal metabolic rate + active metabolism) and food supply in each country. Right: Correlation between log GDP per capita and the residual consumption of the food supply (the difference between amount of food metabolized and the amount of food available). For the countries under the zero line in the residual plot, either their food supplies are under-reported in the source data (FAOSTAT) or we are over estimating their metabolism in this study.

The fact that the best fit regression has a slope significantly greater than 1 indicates that a proportionally greater loss of food energy occurs in countries with larger per capita food supplies. The difference between food supply and the best fit linear prediction is positively correlated with national gross domestic product (GDP), consistent with there being greater food waste in high-income settings. However, the relationship between log GDP per capita and the unexplained component left over after the first regression (residual value in figure
4
) is weak (
r^{2}\approx 0.15
). Thus, although our analysis is consistent with richer countries being more wasteful, it suggests that at the global scale, the more important factor is that people in rich countries are larger. Overall these observations suggest a general tendency for humans to grow larger, while metabolizing a smaller fraction of the available food supply, as the food supply becomes more abundant.

Discussion

In this study, we have applied a relatively straightforward but holistic framework to infer the global spatial patterns of food energy production and consumption, including the metabolic rates from demographic data. Our analysis shows characteristic differences among the geographic distributions of different foods, highlighting the importance of accounting for heterogeneous role of distinct food types in global food system assessments and future projections. We found a linear relationship between metabolic rate and food supply at the national scale with a slope significantly greater than 1, which suggests a consistent tendency for food waste to grow as the per capita food supply increases.

Previous approaches to track food loss and waste throughout the global food system operated under the assumption that every individual consumes a constant number of Calories, ranging from 1995 kcal/person/day
[
26
]
to 2342 kcal/person/day
[
1
]
. By estimating this metabolism value as a function of demographic data and resolved as food items, rather than as a uniform distribution across the global population, our approach provides opportunity for more fine-grained analysis of a key component of the food system. Body mass plays a crucial role in consumption rates, which must be acknowledged in future projections, since the spread of the obesity epidemic might reasonably increase global consumption by the equivalent of 1 billion people
[
45
]
. Given that both number of people and size of people are non-negligible contributors to human consumption rates, we suggest that these two characteristics should be explicitly accounted for in future assessments of spatial metabolic distribution.

Our inclusion of all the food groups covered in the FAOSTAT database (Food Balance Sheets) enables direct comparison with metabolism data, although the precise data for each commodity should be improved in future assessments. An important next step would be to compare these two variables at the grid level with higher precision spatial surrogates or proxies. Metabolism surrogates would benefit from more precise local data, including heights, weights, ages, and diets
[
49
,
27
]
. Similarly production data would benefit from more precise surrogates for specific crops. For example, some of the GAEZ+2015 surrogate data (ie. ”cropsNES”) correspond to multiple unique crops (ie. apples and almonds), which are not necessarily spatially correlated, yet we distribute each included crop proportionally to the same reference data. Our approach is also limited by the fact that locations of food transformation (i.e. food processing plants) are not spatially explicit. Open source data should be developed that includes locations of food processing and key supply chain hubs
[
28
,
20
]
, to assist in downscaling production of processed foods and processing rates. Furthermore, spatial data on fresh water fish catch are difficult to incorporate in global studies, despite comprising a large share of global fish catch and playing a crucial role in diets worldwide
[
14
]
.

Our global food system assessment compiles data from multiple sources and across phases of the food system, hence there are many possible sources of uncertainty and inaccuracy. For example, we rely on calorie density conversion factors, which are assumed to be uniform for each food type, but which in reality vary geographically and at different degree of transformation or food preparation that are not accounted for in our pipeline. For example, the calorie density of a crop in the farm might be different than its density in the grocery store. On the consumption side, while demographic average weights and heights, as well as food supplies, were taken over different regions and distributed according to populations, these distributions are not necessarily uniform in reality. However, our approach facilitates easy updating, allowing for users to input new spatial demographic data. Future efforts to study how food flows from production to metabolism might adapt this method by distributing more accurate national data onto grid cells, using more detailed caloric conversion factors that have different values for different stages of the food system, and including better surrogate data for more precise spatial distributions.

Our analysis represents an important step toward expanding the breadth of spatially explicit global food system analysis from production to metabolism with a relatively detailed tracking of specific food groups. We hope that, by expanding the breadth of food system analysis from production to metabolism, across nearly all foods and with global coverage, our work contributes to an improved holistic understanding of the food system. Further work on the global food system can continue to reveal emergent behavior of the billions of actors in the food system, which cannot be gleaned from the study of the sum of its local parts.

We formulate a similar concept of food energy conservation, specifically accounting for utilization through the human food supply, processing, and feed data, and collect other SUA (Supply Utilization Accounts) variables such as Other Uses, Feed, Seed, Waste, Processing, Stocks, into a single scale factor used to match production energy with consumption energy. Food energy conservation

Our goal is to track food from production to consumption throughout the food system, therefore we assume the following ”Food Energy Conservation“ equations to be true. Given regions
i
, we have the following three equations:

\sum I_{i}=\sum E_{i}

(3)

\sum P_{i}=\sum C_{i}

(4)

I_{i}+P_{i}=E_{i}+C_{i}

(5)

where
I_{i}
,
P_{i}
,
E_{i},
and
C_{i}
denote a region’s imports, production, exports, and consumption/utilization, respectively. Equation
3
follows from the notion that the system is closed; all imported food must have been exported elsewhere. Equation
4
follows from the notion that the total production is equal to the total consumption. This is analogous to the FBS supply utilization equation (equation
1
). Equation
5
describes how food can enter a region only through production or imports, and it can leave only through consumption (including utilization, loss, and waste) or exports.

Notably, Equations
3
,
4
, and
5
are not dependent on food system nodes being grid cells. The system of equations describes the conservation of mass or energy of any closed food system network at any scale. This means that the equations hold whether the index set labels nations, grid cells, or any other food system network, as long as food only enters the system through
P_{i}
and only leaves through
C_{i}
, and trade is closed between nodes in the system.

In principle, the total import quantity should be equal to the total export quantity, since all exported food must go somewhere (except for relatively small amounts of food that are lost in transit). However, trade data asymmetries are known to exist due to re-exports, insurance policies, tariffs on imports, misreporting and under-reporting, among other factors
[
29
,
22
,
30
,
23
]
. This error is propagated into other areas of the FBS so that equation
1
is satisfied. In the FBS, global total import quantity exceeds total export quantity (by about 20 Megatons of food in 2015), which places approximately 14% of global food trade and 4% of the human food supply, in apparent violation of equation
3
. Since countries are more likely to accurately track and report their own imports
[
23
]
, we assume the import quantities to be more accurate, and therefore scale the export quantities to match so that equation
3
is satisfied. We proportionally scale all production values by 2% to as well, since the uncounted food must come from somewhere, so that equation
4
remains satisfied.

Next, we convert production, feed, and processing into units of Calories (kcal). For each food, the energy density is obtained using caloric conversion factors
[
10
,
29
]
. For a small number of food items where conversion factors were unavailable, we infer the energy density as the fraction of the FBS ’Food supply (kcal)’ divided by the ’Domestic Supply Quantity’ for each country and year. We then use this energy density to convert each element in the food balance sheet (production, processing, imports, exports, food, feed, etc.) to units of Calories/year.

Spatial data allocation

We next populate a 1-degree resolution global grid (approximately 10,000 km
2
per grid cell at the equator), with the goal of distributing FBS data into each grid cell, proportionally to local variables (surrogate variables). This grid is based on aggregating higher resolution data for each surrogate variable. We obtain gridded maps of crop production in year 2015 from GAEZ+2015
[
15
]
, which provides local surrogate data for production of crop foods in the FBS. Livestock counts are obtained from Gridded Livestock of the World
[
13
]
, which form the basis for LSU (Livestock Units) surrogates for both feed consumption as well as animal product food production. Estimates of wild fish capture rates from 2015 are obtained from the data-constrained BoatSv2 model
[
16
]
, which provide surrogate data for marine fishery production. The global human population map is obtained from WorldPop, providing the basis for food demand and metabolism per grid cell
[
48
]
. Our choice of a relatively coarse 1-degree resolution is intended to provide unified global perspective on the food system that captures subnational patterns without attempting to quantify local (e.g., city-level) flows. This resolution provides both the computational feasibility for polynomial time global grid analyses (such as analysis of the network of food flow between grid cells), as well as the granularity to visualize and compare the roles of population centers with large agricultural regions and hybrid areas, without being overwhelmed by fine-scale detail.

We then downscale country-level food production, feed, and food supply data from the 2015 FAO FBS proportionally to their associated grid-level surrogate variables. This procedure, known as dasymetric mapping, has a long history in cartography and geography
[
34
]
. We chose dasymetric mapping as our approach for spatial allocation and downscaling, as it is a simple and non-trivial downscaling algorithm: regional data is distributed proportionally to a single grid-level surrogate. We seek to enable study of the relationship across scales, and hence need to rely on both local information and regional data. By distributing each regional quantity proportionally to a single surrogate quantity, we ensure easy reproducibility and interpretability of results. We chose 2015 as a base year since it is the middle of the time-span represented by our input dataset. Table

Information about specific food types and categories, their proportion of the total food supply, and their correspondences with crop and livestock production surrogates are listed in the supplemental information. The foods which are distributed to the grid using a surrogate variable account for about 97% of the food supply and 99% of production reported by FAOSTAT for 2015. The remaining 1% of production corresponds to food items and types of utilization that lacked surrogate data, most importantly fresh water fish. We compute the net caloric production in each grid cell for each food type as the difference between the production and the food supply of that food. For some countries which have production values in the FBS, but no available corresponding surrogate variable in the country, production is distributed uniformly among grid cells.

Data and code availability

Our effort to map and understand the global food system from farm to metabolism endeavors to reduce data transformation friction, and further future progress on global food system assessment. In order to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of how diverse components of the food system interact, some level of data manipulation is necessary. We provide an open source minimalist approach to compiling such data, to encourage reproducibility and interpretability. Data and code are available online at https://github.com/12nad1/food_energy_grid.git.
