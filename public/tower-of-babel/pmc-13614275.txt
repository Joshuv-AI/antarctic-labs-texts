Cinerascensosides A and B, Two New Cytotoxic Sulfated Saponins From the Sea Cucumber Holothuria cinerascens
Authors: Tran Thi Hong Hanh, Ninh Thi Ngoc, Le Thi Vien, Nguyen Xuan Cuong, Dao Viet Ha, Nguyen Hoai Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwo new cytotoxic sulfated compounds, cinerascensosides A and B (1 and 2), alongside three described triterpene glycosides, holothurins A (3), B (4), and B3 (5), were separated from the sea cucumber Holothuria cinerascens. Their structures and absolute configurations were established through extensive spectroscopic analysis, DP4+ probability calculation, and ECD calculations. Furthermore, probable biosynthetic pathways of 1−5 were suggested. Among them, compound 4 exhibited the most potent cytotoxicity against the HepG2 cell line (IC50 = 2.60 µM), followed by compound 3 (IC50 = 2.80 µM). Compound 2 displayed cytotoxicity on the KB and SK‐LU‐1 cell lines (IC50 = 9.28 and 9.49 µM, respectively), whereas compound 3 exhibited potent activity (IC50 = 2.48 and 2.98 µM). Computational analyses, including molecular docking, network pharmacology, and molecular dynamics (MDs) simulations, collectively suggest a potential mechanism of action underlying the observed cytotoxic activity.

Introduction

Sea cucumbers are consumed as health foods in many countries worldwide [1]. Their medicinal and nutritional importance has attracted considerable interest from the scientific community [2, 3]. Numerous studies have been published regarding their chemical composition and biological activity [4]. The main secondary metabolites identified are triterpene glycosides, alongside phenols, peptides, polysaccharides, glycosaminoglycans, sphingolipids, glycolipids, phospholipids, and phosphatidylcholines [5, 6]. Notably, the oxidation patterns of the aglycones vary significantly between species [4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. A characteristic feature of these saponins is the attachment of the sugar chain at C‐3 of the aglycones, with a number of sugar units typically ranging from one to six [4, 12]. Several studies have demonstrated that sea cucumber saponins exhibit significant anticancer properties, impacting cell viability, apoptosis, proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, metastasis, and in vivo tumor growth [13, 14].

In Vietnam, sea cucumbers are widely utilized in traditional medicine to support their immune system and enhance vitality. Research conducted on species inhabiting Vietnamese waters has previously investigated their chemical constituents and bioactivity [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These studies highlight structural similarities among the major constituents across different species [4]. However, previous evaluations have primarily focused on the cytotoxicity of isolated compounds [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], which does not fully elucidate the multi‐targeted properties traditionally exploited in medicine. Recently, network pharmacology has emerged as a powerful tool for studying the “one drug, multiple targets” model, attracting growing interest within the international scientific community [15]. Integrating network pharmacology with molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, provides a strong scientific basis for predicting and demonstrating the interactions between a ligand and its biological targets [16, 17]. This computational approach can streamline the identification of biological targets, potentially shortening the duration and reducing the cost of subsequent biomedical experiments [18].

In our continued efforts to discover bioactive secondary metabolites from marine holothurians in Vietnamese waters [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], we recently investigated the chemical constituents of the sea cucumber Holothuria cinerascens. This led to the isolation of two new cytotoxic sulfated triterpene glycosides, cinerascensosides A and B (1 and 2), alongside the described compounds holothurins A (3), B (4) and B3 (5). In this work, we report the separation and structure characterization of the new compounds, including the determination of their relative and absolute configurations as well as their proposed biosynthetic pathways. Additionally, we employ molecular docking, network pharmacology, MD simulations to suggest a potential mechanism of action underlying the cytotoxic activity of these compounds.

Results and Discussion

Two new cytotoxic sulfated compounds, cinerascensosides A and B (1 and 2), together with known derivatives holothurins A (3), B (4), and B3 (5), were obtained from the sea cucumber H. cinerascens. Their structures (Figure 1), including the relative and absolute configurations, were validated by comprehensive study of their 1D, 2D NMR and HRESIMS data, supported by DP4+ probability and ECD calculations.

Structures of saponins 1−5.

Compound 1 was separated as a white powder. Its chemical formula was established as C41H63NaO16S by HRESIMS ion peaks at m/z 889.3646 [M+Na]+ (calcd for C41H63Na2O16S+, 889.3621), 867.3827 [M+H]+ (calcd for C41H64NaO16S+, 867.3807), and 845.4005 [M−Na+2H]+ (calcd. for C41H65O16S+, 845.3988), requiring 10 degrees of unsaturation. In the 1H NMR spectrum of 1 (see Table 1), the appearance of seven singlet methyl signals [δ H 1.07 (s, H3‐30), 1.08 (s, H3‐27), 1.12 (s, H3‐26), 1.23 (s, H3‐31), 1.28 (s, H3‐32), 1.37 (s, H3‐19) and 1.53 (s, H3‐21)] was identified, along with one olefinic (δ H 5.31, br d, J = 5.4 Hz, H‐11) and two oxymethine peaks [δ H 3.16 (1H, dd, J = 11.4, 4.2 Hz, H‐3), 4.25 (1H, t, J = 6.6 Hz, H‐22)]. Detailed study of the 13C NMR, COSY, HSQC, and HMBC experiments (Figure 2) allowed the full assignment of the 13C and 1H NMR data (see Tables 1 and 2), confirming the presence of the 9(11)‐ene and γ‐lactone moieties. A comparison of the NMR values of 1 with the data of holothurin B (4) showed a high degree of similarity, differing only in the signal for C‐12 (δ C 28.50 in 1 and δ C 71.4 in 4) [19]. DFT calculations of the aglycone chemical shifts at PCM/mPW1PW91/6‐311+G(d,p)//B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level yielded 100% probability for the aglycone of 1 (Table S2), identifying it as 22,25‐epoxyholosta‐9(11)‐ene‐3β,17α‐diol.

The ROESY cross‐peaks for the aglycone of 1 (see Figure 3) were consistent with those observed in compounds 3 [20] and 4 [19], indicating a typical holostane skeleton [21]. However, free rotation around the C‐20/C‐22 bond complicated the configurational assignment at C‐22. Newman projection analysis [22] of 20S,22R‐1a and 20S,22S‐1b isomers produced six possible rotamers (Figure S18).

2D and 3D conformations with key ROESY correlations of the aglycone of 1.

The ROESY correlations between H‐22/H‐16 and H‐22/H3‐21 (Figure 3) narrowed the candidates to two possible isomers 20S,22R‐ 1a1 and 20S,22S‐ 1b3 (Figure S26). Both calculated ECD spectra for 20S,22R‐1a1 and 20S,22S‐1b3 were similar to the experimental ECD spectra of 1 (Figure S28) and 4 (Figure S40), suggesting that the chromophore of compound 1 was similar to that of 4. Consequently, the calculated and experimental ECD data alone did not resolve the C‐22 configuration. To further determine the C‐22 configuration of 1, experimental NMR data were compared with calculated values for isomers 20S,22R‐1a1, 20S,22S‐1b3 (Tables 1 and S3). Despite an overall DP4+ probability of 100% for 20S,22S‐1b3, these data were insufficient to unambiguously assign the C‐22 stereocenter. Based on the stable conformations of the isomer 20S,22S‐1b3, ROESY distance analysis [23, 24] between H‐22 (δ H 4.25) and H‐16 (δ H 2.88) as well as H3‐21 (δH 1.53) was performed. Given the consistency between the interproton distances and the ROESY data (Figure S27), along with the match between the experimental ECD spectra of 1 (Figure S28) and 4 (Figure S40) [19], the absolute configuration at C‐22 of 1 was confirmed as S. The sugar chain data of 1 perfectly matched those of 4 (Table 2), including two anomeric protons at δ H 4.70 (H‐1′, d, J = 7.2 Hz) and 5.10 (H‐1′′, d, J = 7.8 Hz), which had interactions with anomeric carbons at δ C 105.23 (C‐1′) and 105.69 (C‐1′′) in the HSQC spectrum. The strong downfield shift of H‐4′ at δ H 5.16 as well as C‐4′ at δ C 75.93 (relative to that of the progenin 3‐O‐(β‐D‐quinovopyranosyl‐(1→2)‐β‐D‐xylopyranoside)‐holosta‐9(11)‐ene‐3β‐ol‐16‐one at δ C 71.0 [25]) confirmed the appearance of a sulfate moiety at C‐4′. Proton H‐1′′ (δ H 5.10) of the quinovose revealed an HMBC interaction with C‐2′ (δ C 82.84) of the xylose, indicating the attached position of the quinovose at C‐2′ of the xylose. The HMBC interaction of H‐1′ (δ H 4.70) of xylose with C‐3 (δ C 88.73) of the aglycone confirmed the O‐glycosidic linkage of the saccharide chain at this carbon. Consequently, the complete structure of cinerascensoside A was validated as 3‐O‐(β‐quinovopyranosyl‐(1→2)‐4‐O‐sodiumsulfate‐β‐xylopyranoside)‐22S,25‐epoxyholosta‐9(11)‐ene‐3β,17α‐diol.

Compound 2 was also separated as a white powder. Detailed analysis of the 13C NMR, COSY, 2D‐TOCSY, HSQC, and HMBC spectra enabled complete assignment of the NMR data for compound 2 (Tables 1 and 2; Figure 2). The presence of the 9(11)‐ene and γ‐lactone moieties indicated that the aglycone of 2 was similar to that of 1, suggesting a shared biogenesis (Figure S29). The ROESY spectrum showed correlations of H‐22 (δ H 4.24, t, J = 6.6 Hz) with H‐16a (δ H 2.88, dd, J = 14.4, 6.6 Hz) and H3‐21 (δH 1.53, s), together with the experimental ECD spectrum (Figure S18), which displayed a pattern similar to those of 1 (Figure S8) and 4 (Figure S34) [19], this confirmed the aglycone of compound 2 as 22S,25‐epoxyholosta‐9(11)‐ene‐3β,17α‐diol.

A molecular formula of 2 was determined as C54H85NaO26S by HRESIMS, indicating that it was 338 mass units greater than that of 1. Together with the appearance of four anomeric proton and carbon signals (Table 2), this confirmed that the two compounds differ in their sugar chains. Additionally, the spectroscopic data for compound 2 were reminiscent of those of compound 3, revealing a difference only in the signal for C‐12 (δ C 28.44 in 2 and δ C 71.8 in 3) [20]. The sugar chain of 2 (Table 2) matched that of 3, and the HMBC interaction of H‐1′ (δ H 4.66, d, J = 7.2 Hz) of the xylose unit with C‐3 (δ C 88.65) of the aglycone confirmed the O‐glycosidic linkage at C‐3. Therefore, cinerascensoside B was validated as 3‐O‐methyl‐β‐glucopyranosyl‐(1→3)‐β‐glucopyranosyl‐(1→4)‐β‐quinovopyranosyl‐(1→2)‐4‐O‐sodiumsulfate‐β‐xylopyranoside‐22S,25‐epoxyholosta‐9(11)‐ene‐3β,17α‐diol.

Regarding the plausible biosynthetic pathway of compounds 1−5 (Figure S29), 2,3‐oxidosqualene is first cyclized to parkeol, which undergoes C‐18 carboxylation and C‐3 glycosylation (R = S1 or S2) to form intermediate a. Subsequent C‐20/C‐21 oxidation produces intermediate b, which then undergoes a single dehydration to yield b1. Hydration, oxidation, and 18,20‐lactonization convert b1 into b2 and b1a. The latter undergoes ketalization and dehydration to form b1b, yielding compounds 1 (R = S1) and 2 (R = S2). Sequential hydration of intermediate c, followed by oxidation, 18,20‐lactonization, and ketalization, generates intermediates c1–c3, ultimately yielding compound 3 (R = S2). Oxidation and dehydration convert b2 into b3, which then undergoes hydration/hydroxylation to yield b3a. Ketalization of b3a produces b3b, which ultimately forms compounds 4 (R = S1) and 5 (R = S2).

In this study, the in vitro cytotoxic activities of 1−5 were evaluated on five human cancer cell lines (Table 3). Compound 4 exhibited the most potent cytotoxicity against the HepG2 cell line (IC50 = 2.6 µM), followed by compound 3 (IC50 = 2.80 µM). Compound 2 was effective against KB and SK‐LU‐1 cell lines (IC50 = 9.28 and 9.49 µM, respectively) whereas 3 was more active (IC50 = 2.48 and 2.98 µM). In addition, compounds 1−3 and 5 revealed cytotoxicity on the HEK‐293 human normal cell line with IC50 values ranging from 3.11 to11.60 µM whereas 4 was less active (IC50> 100 µM). The selectivity indexes (SI) of 1−5 toward the five cancer cell lines mentioned above were also calculated (see Table 3). Analysis of the chemical structures 1−5 suggested that the appearance of hydroxy groups at both C‐12 and C‐17 of their aglycones might contribute significantly to their cytotoxicity against the aforementioned cancer cell lines.

The potential cytotoxic molecular mechanisms of compounds 1−5 were explored using a network pharmacology approach (Figure S30). The top‐10 ranking hub genes were identified as AKT1, SRC, GAPDH, EGFR, SAT3, MAPK3, CASP3, JUN, MMP9, and HSP90AA1. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Figures S30 and S41) revealed significant associations with prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, general cancer pathways, and EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, which is highly consistent with the previously reported molecular mechanisms of holothurin A [13]. Based on these findings, AKT1 (RAC‐alpha serine/threonine‐protein kinase; PDB ID: 6HHF) [26] and EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor; PDB ID: 1XKK) [27] were specifically identified as key potential targets through the network pharmacology analysis and subsequently selected for molecular docking. Initially, the binding energy of ellipticine with the target proteins 6HHF (‐10.47 kcal mol−1) and 1XKK (‐9.27 kcal mol−1), is consistent with those for the reference ligands capivasertib (‐10.1 kcal mol−1 for 6HHF, ‐8.38 kcal mol−1 for 1XKK) and gefitinib (‐9.39 kcal mol−1 for 6HHF, ‐8.85 kcal mol−1 for 1XKK). As shown in Table S4 and Figures S31–S34, 1 revealed relevant binding energies of ‐11.35 and ‐8.28 kcal. mol−1 with 6HHF and 1XKK. For 6HHF complex, 1 had interactions with Glu85, Asn279, Tyr272, Phe293, Asp292, and Thr291, whereas in the 1XKK complex, it formed interactions with Val769, Asp770, Ser768, and Arg776. Compound 2 demonstrated a binding energy of ‐11.17 kcal. mol−1 when docked into 6HHF, where it interacted with Asp274, Asp292, Thr82, Tyr18, Asn54, and Gln79. In contrast, a binding energy of ‐7.21 kcal. mol−1 was observed for the 1XKK complex, with interactions involving Lys823, Leu1017, Gln791, and Arg776. Compound 4 showed binding energies of ‐11.01 with 6HHF and ‐8.31 kcal. mol−1 with 1XKK. For 6HHF complex, it had interactions with Tyr272, Gly16, and Glu17, whereas interactions with Arg776, Ser768, Asp770, and Val769 were observed in 1XKK. Compound 3 revealed relevant binding energies of ‐12.15 and ‐7.306 kcal. mol−1 with 6HHF and 1XKK. It formed interactions with Leu295, Tyr272, Gln79, Asn54, and Arg15 in the 6HHF, while interacting with Arg813, Asp770, Asn771, Lys852, Val774, Gln791, Asp1014, and Arg705 in the 1XKK complex. Based on the docking results, the compound 2‐AKT1 complex was selected for MD simulation. With 100 ns, MD simulation showed a stable complex, with consistent RMSD, Rg, RMSF, and H‐bond profiles (Figures S35–S38), supporting the enhanced stability of the AKT1‐compound 2 complex during the simulation. These results revealed that formation of the compound 2‐AKT1 complex, mediated by hydrogen bonds between the ligand and key amino acid residues (Figures S32 and S36), significantly reduced the flexibility of functionally important regions of the AKT1 protein and restricted its conformational changes. These findings are consistent with published reports on the biological activity of holothurin compounds [13, 14].

Conclusions

From the sea cucumber H. cinerascens, two new cytotoxic sulfated compounds, cinerascensosides A and B (1 and 2), together with holothurins A (3), B (4) and B3 (5), were purified. Their chemical structures were validated by extensive analysis of their 1D and 2D NMR and HRESI mass spectra, alongside quantum chemical calculations. Probable biosynthetic pathways for 1−5 were suggested. Among them, 4 revealed the most potent cytotoxicity against the HepG2 cell line (IC50 = 2.6 µM), followed by 3 (IC50 = 2.80 µM). Compound 2 displayed cytotoxic activity against the KB and SK‐LU‐1 cell lines, with IC50 values of 9.28 and 9.40 µM, whereas compound 3 exhibited potent activity with IC50 values of 2.48 and 2.98 µM. Network pharmacology, molecular docking analysis, and MD simulations confirmed a potential mechanism of action for the cytotoxic activity of compound 2.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

The materials used for the simple isolation were described previously in the literature [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Optical rotations were recorded with a P‐2000 (Jasco Inc., Japan) polarimeter. Infrared (IR) spectra were measured with a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FT‐IR Spectrometer. The 1D and 2D NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE NEO 600 and III HD 500 FT‐NMR spectrometers. High‐resolution time‐of‐flight mass spectra were recorded on MaXis II ETD (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) and Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) systems. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were measured with a Chirascan CD spectrometer (Applied Photophysics, UK).

Biological Material

The samples of H. cinerascens (Brandt, 1835) were collected in Lang Co, Hue, Vietnam, in July 2024 and confirmed by Professor Do Cong Thung (Institute of Science, Technology and Environment, VAST). A voucher specimen (No: DLB‐2024‐LC03) was deposited at the Institute of Chemistry, VAST, Vietnam.

Extraction and Isolation

The MeOH extract (12 g) obtained from fresh samples of H. cinerascens (2.5 kg) was prepared according to previously described methods [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. The aqueous part was separated on a Diaion HP‐20 column chromatography (CC), eluting stepwise with MeOH−H2O (0:100, 25:75, and 100:0, v/v) to afford four fractions W1‐W4. Fraction W4 (1.3 g) was crudely divided into five subfractions, W4A‐W4E, using silica gel CC and a gradient mobile phase of CH2Cl2−MeOH (8:1‐1:1, v/v). Subfraction W4C (0.45 g) was further separated into three subfractions, W4C1‐W4C3, using reversed‐phase (RP)‐silica gel CC with acetone−H2O (1:1.5, v/v). Subfraction W4C3 (50 mg) was processed to yield fraction W4C3A (20 mg), which was then subjected to RP‐silica gel CC with acetone−H2O (1:1, v/v) to afford compound 3 (4 mg). Subfraction W4D (0.5 g) was divided into six fractions, W4D1‐W4D6, via RP‐18 CC (MeOH−H2O, 13:1, v/v). ROESY Distance Analysis

ROESY signal integration was measured in Bruker TopSpin 4.3.0, applying the previously outlined procedure [23]. Volumes were transformed to interproton distances with the equation r i,j = r ref (Aref/Ai,j)1/6, where A is the integrated signal volume, geminal protons were chosen to calculate r ref (r ref ​ = 1.8 Å) [23, 24].

Computational Details

Computational studies were performed using Gaussian 16 package (Gaussian inc., Wallingford, CT, USA) [28] and Spartan’18 (version 1.4.5, Wavefunction Inc., Irvine, CA, USA). Gauge‐Independent Atomic Orbital (GIAO) method was used to calculate NMR shielding constants at the PCM/mPW1PW91/6‐311+G(d,p)//B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level for DP4+ analysis [29, 30, 31], following previously reported procedures [32, 33, 34]. For the electronic circular dichroism (ECD) calculations, the rotatory and oscillator strengths of the thirteen lowest excited states for each optimized conformer of 1 were calculated using TDDFT B3LYP/6‐31+G(d,p)//B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level. The CPCM model was employed to account for the solvent effects of methanol [35, 36]. All final theoretical ECD spectra were generated by Boltzmann averaging the spectra of each conformation. Calculated ECD curves were generated using SpecDis software (version 1.71, Berlin, Germany) [37] with a half‐bandwidth of 0.30–0.45 eV. The details of Gibbs Free Energies (Hatree), Boltzmann populations, and optimized Z‐matrixes of the aglycon of compound 1 are shown in Tables S1 and S5.

Docking Studies

The crystal structures of AKT1 (PDB: 6HHF) and EGRF (PDB: 1XKK) crystal structures were taken from the Protein Data Bank [26, 27]. Molecular docking studies were done using the previously described procedure [32, 33]. The reference ligands (capivasertib and gefitinib), together with compounds 1−5, were prepared for the docking study. AutoDock Vina 1.2.0 [38] software was used to conduct binding affinity analyses. Docking observations were then used to analyze compounds 1−5. UCSF Chimera and LigPlot+ tool [39] was used to visualize results.

Cytotoxic Assay

All compounds (1−5) were evaluated for cytotoxicity on five human cancer cell lines [epidermoid carcinoma (KB, RRID:CVCL_0372), hepatoma (HepG2, RRID:CVCL_0027), prostate cancer (LNCaP, RRID:CVCL_0395), lung adenocarcinoma (SK‐LU‐1, RRID:CVCL_0629), and mammary adenocarcinoma (MCF‐7, RRID:CVCL_0031)] and one noncancerous HEK‐293 (RRID:CVCL_0045) cell line using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) method, following established protocols [40, 41].

Network Pharmacological Analysis

A total of 381 predicted targets for compounds 1−5 and their aglycon were identified using the Swiss Target Prediction platform (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) [42], together with reported data [13, 14], while a comprehensive search across the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) gave 9449 targets associated with keyword “cytotoxic”. By using Draw Venn Diagram online (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/), 313 overlapping targets were found as potential cytotoxic targets. These overlapping targets were sent to protein‐protein interaction (PPI) analysis with the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/), and the resulting PPI network was subsequently examined using Cytoscape v3.10.4 [43]. Gene ontology/KEGG pathway analysis was performed using SHINEGO 0.85 [44].

MD Simulation

To study the conformational behavior and dynamic stability of the compound 2‐AKT complex, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed in explicit solvent using GROMACS 2023 [45]. The CHARMM36 force field was applied to the protein, while ligand parameters were derived via CgenFF (https://app.cgenff.com/). The system was solvated with TIP3P water, neutralized, energy‐minimized, and equilibrated under NVT and NPT ensembles prior to the production run [45]. The simulation results were analyzed using tools integrated within the GROMACS package.

Statistical Analysis

Each experiment was done in triplicate to validate the reliability of the obtained results and the data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). The IC50 data were calculated with nonlinear regression using Table­Curve 2D ver. 4 software.
