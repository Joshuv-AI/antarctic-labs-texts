New Statistical and Computational Results for Learning Junta Distributions
Lorenzo Beretta

Abstract
We study the problem of learning junta distributions on { ± 1 } n \{\pm 1\}^{n} , where a distribution is a k k -junta if its probability mass function depends on a subset of at most k k variables. We make two main contributions: • We show that learning k k -junta distributions is computationally equivalent to learning k k -parity functions with noise (LPN), a landmark problem in computational learning theory. • We design an algorithm for learning junta distributions whose statistical complexity is optimal, up to polylogarithmic factors. Computationally, our algorithm matches the complexity of previous (non-sample-optimal) algorithms. Combined, our two contributions imply that our algorithm cannot be significantly improved, statistically or computationally, barring a breakthrough for LPN.

Learning parity functions with noise.

Learning a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
under distribution
\mathcal{D}
with noise of rate
\eta<1/2

while observing pairs
(x,f(x)\cdot b)

is a Rademacher random variable with parameter

is a parity with relevant variables

if
f(x)=\prod_{i\in J}x_{i}
. Learning parities with noise under the uniform distribution (LPN) is a cornerstone problem in computational learning theory, serving as a foundation for several hardness results (see
Figure

).

Feldman, Gopalan, Khot, and Ponnuswami first demonstrated the central role of LPN by computationally reducing several fundamental problems in learning theory to it
[
Fel+06
,
Fel+09
]
. They showed that, under the uniform distribution, agnostically learning
k
-parity functions, learning
k
-junta functions (both with and without noise), and learning Boolean functions that admit a DNF representation of size
2^{k}
all reduce to learning
k
-parities with noise.
Furthermore, LPN can be reduced to numerous other learning problems: learning halfspaces
[
KK14
]
and ReLU functions
[
GKK19
]
under the Gaussian distribution, learning submodular functions
[
FKV13
]
and agnostically learning halfspaces under the uniform distribution
[

]
.

LPN is widely believed to be computationally hard. Kearns established that LPN requires
\Omega(n^{k})
constant-noise queries in the statistical query (SQ) model
[
Kea98
,
Bar+22
]
.
The learning with errors (LWE) problem, a generalization of LPN over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

[
Reg09
]
. Moreover, LPN itself has been leveraged as a hardness assumption in cryptographic constructions
[

]

The best known algorithm for sparse LPN (

\approx n^{0.8k}

[

,

]

[

]

Finally, for

for arbitrary

Truncated distributions.

Learning junta distributions connects with an emerging line of work on testing distributions truncated by low-degree polynomials.
A distribution is truncated by a function
f
if it is obtained by conditioning a ground distribution
x\sim\mathcal{D}
on the value of
f(x)
.
Recently, testing junta truncation
[

]

[

]

Apparently, a uniform distribution truncated by a junta function is a junta distribution, so junta truncation testing reduces to LJD.
Moreover, juntas are low-degree polynomials, so low-degree polynomial truncation is a generalization of junta truncation.

1.3
Technical Overview: Computational Complexity

In order to prove
Theorem

1
, we need to reduce learning parities with noise (LPN) to learning junta distributions (LJD) and vice versa.

1.3.1
Reducing LPN to LJD.

Let
(x,y)
be a sample-label input for LPN with ground truth
\chi_{J}(x)=\prod_{j\in J}x_{j}
.
The sample
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is distributed uniformly, whereas
y\in\{\pm 1\}
is distributed as
\chi_{J}(x)
flipped with probability
\eta<1/2
.

We implement a filtering strategy that, given LPN samples
(x,y)
, returns samples
z\sim\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is a junta distribution. Then, we show that learning
\mathcal{D}
suffices to recover
\chi_{J}
.
We define
z\leftarrow x
if
y=1
and re-sample
(x,y)
otherwise.
This ensures that
\mathcal{D}
is a mixture of two distributions: (i) The uniform distribution supported on
\{w\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\,|\,\chi_{J}(w)=1\}
; (ii) The uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}_{n}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
In this mixture (i) is weighted
\approx 1-2\eta
and (ii) is weighted
\approx 2\eta
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{D}
is a
k
-junta distribution with relevant variables
J
.

By running an exponential search, we can assume that we know
1-2\eta
up to a constant factor.
We run our LJD learner on
\mathcal{D}
with precision
\varepsilon\cdot(1-2\eta)
, and it returns
\mathcal{F}
such that
\left|\left|\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{D}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\cdot(1-2\eta)
.
Now, define a boolean function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}

as
f(x)=1
iff
\mathcal{F}(x)>1/2
.
Our estimate
\mathcal{F}
is precise enough so that
f
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[|f-\chi_{J}|\right]\leq\varepsilon
.
Notice that since we have query access to the probability mass function of
\mathcal{F}
, we also have query access to
f
.
In order to retrieve
J
from
f
, consider
f(w)\cdot f(w\oplus e_{j})
where
w\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is uniformly distributed and
w\oplus e_{j}
flips the
j
-th bit of
w
. By virtue of
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[|f-\chi_{J}|\right]\leq\varepsilon
, we must have that
f(w)\cdot f(w\oplus e_{j})
has a majority of
-1
for
j\in J
and a majority of
1
for
j\not\in J
. To conclude, we just test if
j\in J
for all
j\in[n]
.

1.3.2
Reducing LJD to LPN.

In order to reduce LJD to LPN we need to introduce an auxiliary problem:
learning noisy parity distributions
(LNPD).
We say that a junta distribution is a parity distribution with relevant variables
J
if it coincides with the uniform distribution over
\{w\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\,|\,\chi_{J}(w)=s\}
for a fixed
s\in\{\pm 1\}
.
We generalize this definition and say that a junta distribution is a noisy parity distribution if it is a mixture of a uniform distribution and a parity distribution.
By symmetry, the only non-zero coefficients in the Fourier spectrum of (the p.m.f. of) a noisy parity distribution
\mathcal{D}
with relevant variables
J

are
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\emptyset)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)
.
The reduction of LJD to LPN develops in two steps: First, we reduce LJD to LNPD; Second, we reduce LNPD to LPN.

Reducing LJD to LNPD.

The Fourier spectrum of a junta distribution can have as many as
2^{k}
non-zero coefficients, whereas a noisy parity distribution has at most two. The idea is to distill our input junta distribution
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{P}
so that
\mathcal{P}
is a noisy parity with relevant variables
J
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(J)=\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)
, for some fixed
J
.
Then, use the LNPD algorithm to learn
\mathcal{P}
.

To perform such distillation, we use an idea first introduced in
[
Fel+06
]
:
By carefully adding noise to the distribution
\mathcal{D}
it is possible to zero all Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{D}
besides
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\emptyset)
. Moreover, each
J\subseteq[n]
with
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)\neq 0
has a large enough chance of being left intact. Thus, a coupon-collector style argument ensures that we learn all non-zero Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{D}
in
\approx k\cdot 2^{k}
rounds.

Reducing LNPD to LPN.

For ease of presentation, we show how to reduce learning (non-noisy) parity distributions to learning parity functions (without noise). The reduction carries over to the noisy case.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a parity distribution over relevant variables
J
and such that
\chi_{J}(w)=1
for all
w\in\mathrm{supp}\,(\mathcal{D})
(the case
\chi_{J}(w)=-1
is symmetric). Let
z\sim\mathcal{D}
.
Fix
j\in[n]
and define
(x,y)
as follows: Flip the
j
-th bit of
z
with probability
1/2
and define that to be
x
; define
y=-1
if the
j
-th bit was flipped and
y=1
otherwise.
If
j\in J
, then
x
is distributed uniformly over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
y=\chi_{J}(x)
. Else, if
j\not\in J
then
y
is distributed uniformly on
\{\pm 1\}
and independently of
x
.

Then, we solve LPN on samples
(x,y)
.
If it turns out that
y
is a
k
-parity function of
x
then we apply the same technique used in the reduction from LPN to LJD above to retrieve
S
from
f
, and we find
J
.
If
j\not\in J
then
y
is not going to be a parity function of
x
and we need to redefine
\mathcal{D}
using some other
j\in[n]
. By scanning all
j\in[n]
, we are guaranteed that at some point
j\in J
holds.

1.4
Technical Overview: Sample-Optimal Algorithm

Our algorithm learns JDN with fewer samples than previous work; in particular, it replaces the term
2^{k}\log n
in the sample complexity of
[
Esc24
]
with
2^{k}+\log n
(see
Table

1
).
The goal of this overview is to show how to make the term
\log n
additive, rather than multiplicative.

Low-degree algorithms.

Most previous literature on junta distributions, as well as our own algorithm, make use of the low-degree algorithm of Linial, Mansour, and Nisan
[
LMN93
]
.
In a nutshell, the low-degree algorithm estimates individual Fourier coefficients and outputs a hypothesis function consistent with the estimated Fourier coefficients.
In
[
ABR16
]
, the authors pointed out that applying the low-degree algorithm to the junta distribution problem requires too many samples. Indeed, an accuracy of
\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-n/2}
on individual Fourier coefficients is necessary to deliver an
\varepsilon
error in
L_{2}
-norm.
To bypass this, Escudero introduced a thresholding of Fourier coefficients, thus ensuring that at most
2^{k}
coefficients are estimated to be nonzero
[
Esc24
]
.
In this work, we simply apply the low-degree algorithm to distributions over a small domain
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
, thus the standard analysis from
[
LMN93
]
suffices.

Our template algorithm.

Consider the following template.
We maintain a junta distribution
\mathcal{Q}
as our “best guess” for
\mathcal{D}
, along with its set of relevant variables
N\subseteq[n]
of size at most
k
.
We maintain the invariant that
N\subseteq J
, where
J
is the true set of relevant variables.
Given
S\supseteq N
, we can check if the marginals of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{D}
with respect to the variables in
S
(resp.
\mathcal{Q}|_{S}
and
\mathcal{D}|_{S}
) are close in total variation distance.
In order to check the above condition, we draw samples from
\mathcal{D}
, project them onto the coordinates in
S
and feed these samples to a closeness tester.

If
\left|\left|\mathcal{Q}|_{S}-\mathcal{D}|_{S}\right|\right|_{TV}\leq\varepsilon
for all
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
with
N\subseteq S
, then we can correctly output
\mathcal{Q}
.
Else, if we find
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
with
N\subseteq S
such that
\left|\left|\mathcal{Q}|_{S}-\mathcal{D}|_{S}\right|\right|_{TV}\leq\varepsilon
, then we learn the Fourier coefficients of
\mathcal{D}|_{S}
using the low-degree algorithm.
Then, we update the distribution
\mathcal{Q}
so that it includes the newly apprehended Fourier coefficients, and augment
N
accordingly.
Each iteration of this scheme increases the size of
N
, so we are done after
k
iterations.

Sample complexity analysis.

First, observe that while making multiple calls to an algorithm we can certainly re-use the same samples, and the price we pay is that we need to union bound over the error probability.
The low-degree algorithm operates on distributions over
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
, so the cost of an individual call is negligible. Moreover, we make at most
k
calls to it, so a standard boosting of the success probability increases its sample complexity by at most
\log k
.
On the other hand, we make up to
k\cdot n^{k}
calls to the closeness tester. Each individual call costs at least
\tilde{\Omega}(2^{k})
by known lower bounds
[
VV17
]
. So, we cannot afford a standard boosting of the success probability, as it would introduce a multiplicative term
\log(n^{k})=k\log n
.

To counter this, we develop a closeness tester that succeeds with probability
1-\delta
and uses only
s+\log 1/\delta
samples, where
s
is the support size.
The design of our tester uses the TV distance between the empirical probability mass function and the tested distribution as an estimator, borrowing ideas from
[
Dia+18
]
.

Preliminaries

We denote with
\Delta(A)
the set of probability distributions over the set
A
.
Given a probability distribution
\mathcal{P}\in\Delta(\{\pm 1\}^{n})
and a set
S\subseteq[n]
we denote with
\mathcal{P}|_{S}
the marginal of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to variables in
S
.
We overload the notation and denote with
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
its probability mass function (PMF).
We denote the uniform distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with
\mathcal{U}_{n}
.
Whenever not specified, expectations are taken with respect to the uniform distribution.

Fourier transform.

In this paper we use the Fourier transform over the boolean hypercube.
For a subset
A\subseteq[n]
we define the function
\chi_{A}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
such that
\chi_{A}(x)=\prod_{i\in A}x_{i}
.
Such functions form the Fourier basis of the vector space of functions
\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Notice that the Fourier basis is orthonormal with respect to the dot product
\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[f(x)\cdot g(x)\right]
.

Given a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}:2^{[n]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\widehat{f}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[f(x)\cdot\chi_{A}(x)\right]
. Thus,
f=\sum_{A\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(A)\cdot\chi_{A}
.
Finally, we have

\underset{x\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{2}(x)\right]=2^{-n}\cdot\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{A\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}^{2}(A).

Learning functions and distributions.

Fix a class of boolean functions
F\subseteq\{f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}\}
.
Consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes as input parameters
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and
\{(x_{i},f(x_{i}))\}_{i\in[m]}
where
x_{1}\dots x_{m}\{\pm 1\}^{n}
are i.i.d. samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
and
f\in F
is fixed. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}
outputs a function
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(x)\neq f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)\right]\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Then, we say that
\mathcal{A}
learns
F
using
S(n)
samples and
T(n)
time under the distribution
\mathcal{D}
if
\mathcal{A}
takes
S(n)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1},\delta^{-1})
samples and runs in time
T(n)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1},\delta^{-1})
.
Moreover, we say that
\mathcal{A}
learns
F
with noise of rate
\eta<1/2
if the label
f(x_{i})
in the samples

Lemma 2.1

Reducing Noisy Parity Distributions to Parity Functions with Noise

First, we define noisy parity distributions.

Definition 3.1

(Noisy parity distribution)
.

We say that a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is a noisy parity distribution with relevant variables
J\subseteq[n]
with noise rate
\eta<1/2
if

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(S)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\begin{cases}1&\text{if $S=\emptyset$}\\
\pm(1-2\eta)&\text{if $S=J$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

Observation 1
.

A noisy parity distribution with noise rate
\eta=0
corresponds to the uniform distribution truncated by a parity function (i.e.,
x\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}
conditioned on either
\chi_{J}(x)=\pm 1
). We call such distribution a

and highlight that the PMF of a parity distribution is, indeed, a parity function (up to multiplying it by
-1
).
Moreover, a noisy partiy distribution over set
J
with noise rate
\eta>0
coincides with the mixture
(1-2\eta)\cdot\mathcal{P}+2\eta\cdot\mathcal{U}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a parity distribution.

The bulk of this section is spent proving the next lemma, which reduces learning noisy parity distributions to learning parity functions with noise.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that learns

k
-parity functions
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
under the uniform distribution with noise of rate
\eta<1/2
and uses

Then, there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that learns noisy
k
-parity distributions over

with noise rate
\eta<1/2
. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
uses at most
\mathrm{poly}(2^{k},n)\cdot T\left(n,k,(1-2\eta)^{-1}\right)

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a noisy parity distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with relevant variables

and noise rate
\eta<1/2

Then, there exists an algorithm that takes as input

Proof.

By

3.1
, we have
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)\in\{\pm 2^{-n}\cdot(1-2\eta)\}

We have

.
Let

\eta\leq\bar{\eta}<1/2
,

Standard concentration bounds imply that

Since

At each step, we have that, for

Once we have successfully approximated

(Find Parity with Queries)

3.2
Reducing Junta Distributions to Noisy Parity Distributions

In this section, we reduce the problem of learning junta distributions to that of learning noisy parity distributions. The main result of this section is the next lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that learns a noisy
k
-parity distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with noise rate
\eta<1/2
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
uses
T\left(n,k,(1-2\eta)^{-1}\right)
time.
Then, there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that learns a
k
-junta distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
uses at most
\mathrm{poly}(2^{k},n)\cdot T\left(n,k,O(2^{k/2})\right)
time.

To prove
Lemma

3.5
, we mimic the techniques used in
[
Fel+06
]
to reduce learning junta functions with noise to learning parity functions with noise.
Here is a summary of this technique.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be the junta distribution with relevant variables
J^{\star}
that we would like to learn. We introduce some noise that, with large enough probability, zeros all Fourier coefficient of
\mathcal{D}
besides
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\emptyset)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)
for some
J\subseteq J^{\star}
.
Once all coefficients besides these two are zero, we are left exactly with a noisy parity distribution.
As long as we manage to isolate all
J\subseteq J^{\star}
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)
is high enough, we can learn
\mathcal{D}
.

In order to implement this scheme we need the following lemmas that implement noise injection.
Throughout the section we identify
\{0,1\}^{n}\sim 2^{[n]}
so that Fourier coefficients are (also) indexed by boolean vectors. For
J\subseteq[n]
, denote with
\mathbbm{1}_{J}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
the vector such that
\mathbbm{1}_{J}[i]=1
iff
i\in J
.
We denote with
\oplus
the sum modulo
2
.
We overload the notation
\mathcal{U}_{n}
so that it represent the uniform distribution both on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Lemma 3.6

(Essentially Lemma 1 in the full version of
[
Fel+06
]
)
.

Let
A\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}
and let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function over the boolean hypercube. Then, for each
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we have the identity

f_{\mathcal{A}}(x):=\underset{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x\oplus A^{\top}p)\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
Aa=0^{m}\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}(a)\chi_{a}(x).

Proof.

First, notice that for each
a\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\chi_{a}(A^{\top}p)=(-1)^{\langle a,A^{\top}p\rangle}=(-1)^{\langle Aa,p\rangle},

thus
\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{m}}\left[\chi_{a}(A^{\top}p)\right]=1
if
Aa=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{m}}\left[\chi_{a}(A^{\top}p)\right]=0
otherwise.

\displaystyle\underset{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x\oplus A^{\top}p)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\widehat{f}(a)\cdot\underset{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi_{a}\left(x\oplus A^{\top}p\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\widehat{f}(a)\cdot\chi_{a}(x)\cdot\underset{p\sim\mathcal{U}_{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi_{a}\left(A^{\top}p\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
Aa=0^{m}\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}(a)\chi_{a}(x).

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a
k
-junta distribution with relevant variables

and

2^{-n},&\text{if $I=\emptyset$}\\

Namely,

is a noisy parity distribution with relevant variables

By

Lemma 3.8
.

Given a probability mass function
\mathcal{D}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}

Lemma 3.9
.

Lemma 3.10

4.1
High-Confidence Tolerant Identity Tester

The goal of this section is to develop a sample-efficient tolerant identity tester for the the high-probability regime. In particular, standard boosting of off-the-shelf identity testers to failure probability
\delta
incurs a multiplicative overhead of

\log\delta^{-1}

To this end, we mimic the techniques used in

Definition 4.1

(Tolerant Identity Tester)
.

We say that a algorithm

is an
\ell_{1}

(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant identity tester for the distribution
\mathcal{P}
with sample complexity

For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
, let

be i.i.d. samples from

•

If

returns “close” with probability at least

•

If
||\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{P}||_{1}\geq\varepsilon_{2}
then
T(x_{1}\dots x_{m})
returns “far” with probability at least

Recall the “Bernstein” version of the standard bounded differences (McDiarmid) inequality.

Lemma 4.2

[

]
)
.

Let

be independent random variables taking values in the set

Let

be a function of

so that for every
j\in[m]

and

, we have that:

|f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{j},\ldots,y_{m})-f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{m})|\leq B\;.

Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[f(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})-\mathbb{E}[f]\geq z\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-2z^{2}}{{m}B^{2}}\right)\;.

Now we can prove the main result of this section.

Lemma 4.3

(High-confidence tolerant identity tester)
.

For any
\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta>0

\mathcal{D}
with support
[s]

(\alpha,\alpha+\varepsilon)

Proof.

We define the same estimator used in
[

]
and we analyze its mean and concentration similarly to
[

]
.
Let

and

be i.i.d. sample from

Let

Given a distribution

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a probability distribution supported on

In the following we prove that with probability

S_{\mathcal{P}}(X)=\left|\left|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{D}\right|\right|_{1}\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Then, to obtain the desired tolerant tester, we return “close” if

and return “far” otherwise.
By triangle inequality, we have

\left|\left|\frac{X}{m}-\mathcal{P}\right|\right|_{1}=\left|\left|\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{P}\right|\right|_{1}\pm\left|\left|\frac{X}{m}-\mathcal{D}\right|\right|_{1}.

First, we bound the expectation of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\mathcal{D}}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{X_{i}}{m}-\mathcal{D}(i)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{X_{i}}{m}-\mathcal{D}(i)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\sqrt{\frac{m\cdot\mathcal{D}(i)}{m^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\sqrt{\frac{m\cdot\frac{1}{s}}{m^{2}}}

(3)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{s}{m}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Then, we show that

has Lipschitz constant

Therefore,

.

\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(J)\right]=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i\in[m]}\cdot\mathrm{Var}\left[2^{-k}\cdot\chi_{J}(x_{i})\right]=\frac{2^{-2k}}{m}\cdot\mathrm{Var}\left[\chi_{S}(x_{1})\right]\leq\frac{2^{-2k}}{m}.

Thus, by standard concentration bounds we have that, for each
J\subset\{\pm 1\}^{k}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(J)-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)\right|>\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-k}\right]\leq 2^{-k}\cdot\delta.

By taking a union bound over all
J\subseteq[k]
we obtain:

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{J\subseteq[k]}\left|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(J)-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(J)\right|>\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-k}\right]\leq\delta.

(4)

The values
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy the desiderata, however we might have that
\mathcal{P}
, the inverse Fourier transform of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, is not a probability distribution.
On the other hand, conditioning on the previous step succeeding, we know that there exists a probability distribution
\mathcal{Q}
such that
\left|\left|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\right|\right|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-k}
.
We write a linear program that searches for a distribution
\mathcal{Q}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\right|\right|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-k}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\mathcal{Q}=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(x)\geq 0\;\text{ for all }\;x\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}.

Since each coefficient of
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
is a linear function of
\mathcal{Q}
we can solve the above LP in time
\mathrm{poly}(2^{k})
. Triangle inequality and
Equation

4
imply our desiderata.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{D}
be distributions over
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
with
\max_{a\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}}|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(a)|\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-3k/2}
, then
||\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{D}||_{1}\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

\displaystyle||\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{D}||^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\cdot||\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{D}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{2k}\cdot\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(a))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2^{3k}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

∎
