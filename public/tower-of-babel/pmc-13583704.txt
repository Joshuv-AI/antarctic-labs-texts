Acute cholecystitis in high-risk patients: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and practice management guideline from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST)
Authors: Asanthi Ratnasekera, Rishi Rattan, Bishwajit Bhattacharya, Zugui Zhang, Madison Harris, Sirivan S Seng, Connor Magura, Christopher A Butts, Judy Rabinowitz, Jordan Michael Kirsch, Ida Molavi, Haytham Kaafarani, Tejal S Brahmbhatt, Michael W Cripps, Nikolay Bugaev, John J Como

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk patients with acute calculous cholecystitis (ACC) require an individualized approach and may not be eligible for cholecystectomy. The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the management of ACC in patients with either high operative risk or delayed presentation.MethodsAn evidence-based systematic review was performed to answer the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes (PICO) questions. This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024610156). An academic health science librarian and lead authors developed the search strategy. The search was conducted in Medline (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), Cochrane CENTRAL (Ovid), and Web of Science. The search strategy was written for Medline and translated using each database’s syntax, subject headings, and search fields. An initial search, conducted on August 3, 2020, was updated on June 5, 2025.ResultsA total of 25 studies were identified. We conditionally recommend that high-risk patients with ACC undergo treatment with antibiotics alone versus using percutaneous cholecystostomy (PC) and antibiotics. No recommendation was made to treat patients with ACC with more than 5 days of symptoms with either cholecystectomy or antibiotics. No recommendation was made to treat patients with ACC with more than 5 days of symptoms with PC or antibiotics. We conditionally recommend removing the PC catheter without performing cholecystectomy after resolution of ACC in high-risk patients who have evidence of cystic duct patency.ConclusionRisks of cholecystectomy and PC must be balanced in patients who are high-risk for operative management.Level of evidenceLevel III, systematic review and meta-analysis.Keywords: cholecystitis, cholecystectomy

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), ideally within 72 hours, is the gold standard treatment of acute calculous cholecystitis (ACC).1 However, management in high-risk populations poses a challenge.1–4 High-risk patients may necessitate an individualized approach. A ‘high-risk’ patient is defined by various risk stratification models and scores. Risks may be defined by severity of gallbladder disease using Tokyo definitions,4 or various severity of illness scores such as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score,5 or Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores.6 Current guidelines recommend considering a risk-stratified approach to non-operative management.3 4 7

The widely accepted 2018 Tokyo Guidelines (TG 18) and World Society of Emergency Surgery 2020 guidelines assess early LC compared with percutaneous cholecystostomy (PC), in various grades of severity of cholecystitis and in high-risk patients.1 4 However, there is a gap in evidence-based guidelines regarding the utilization of non-operative management strategies of antibiotics and/or PC for drainage in high-risk patients or patients with delayed presentations, and subsequent management of those who had PC drainage. To address this need, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to develop evidence-based recommendations.

Objectives

A working group of Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma members, including the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-trained methodologists, acute care surgeons, a medical librarian, and a statistician conducted this project using GRADE methodology.8 The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate management of ACC in patients with either high operative risk or delayed presentation with non-operative management strategies.

Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome questions

Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) questions were formulated by consensus after a preliminary, informal literature search but before a systematic search.

Selection of outcome measures

Authors independently voted on a nine-point scale for candidate outcomes.8 Outcomes with scores 7 to 9 were considered critical, 4 to 6 important, and 1 to 3 were of limited importance and excluded. Outcomes of mortality, septic shock, intra-abdominal infections, unplanned interventions, and recurrent biliary complications were considered critical outcomes (table 1).

Methods

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024610156). This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement (PRISMA checklist, online supplemental table 1).9 An academic health science librarian (JR) and lead authors (AR, JJC, and NB) developed the search strategy. Controlled vocabulary subject headings and text words were used for the concepts of acute cholecystitis, cholecystostomy, cholecystectomy, and their synonyms. The search was conducted in Medline (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), Cochrane CENTRAL (Ovid), and Web of Science. The search strategy was written for Medline and translated using each database’s syntax, subject headings, and search fields. (online supplemental table 2) No limits were applied. An initial search, conducted on August 3, 2020, was updated on June 5, 2025. Bibliographies of relevant studies were also searched. Results were uploaded to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne) via EndNote V.21 (Clarivate, London) for deduplication, screening, and full-text evaluation.10 The Retraction Watch database was checked for retractions.11 Only peer-reviewed, prospective observational, retrospective, and randomized studies that included both patients receiving the intervention and comparator were eligible for review. Case series and reports, commentaries, studies not in the English language, and non-clinical studies were excluded. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were independently reviewed in duplicate, with any conflicts adjudicated by a third author.

Statistical methodology

Data were independently extracted in duplicate using Excel v16 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington State, USA). A biostatistician (ZZ) developed the statistical plan and completed the analyses. Meta-analyses were conducted when at least three studies reported events in both intervention and comparator groups.12 For dichotomous outcomes, ORs with 95% CIs were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method. Both common/fixed-effects and random-effects models were applied, with priority to the latter when significant heterogeneity was detected (I2>30%). For continuous outcomes, the mean differences were calculated using random-effects models to account for anticipated clinical heterogeneity.13 Fixed-effects models were computed as sensitivity analyses, where appropriate.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q-test. Heterogeneity was interpreted as follows: I² <25% (low), 25% to 50% (moderate), and >50% (high).14 15 The Q-test assessed whether observed heterogeneity exceeded that expected by chance, with p value <0.10 considered statistically significant.16 17

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test, with p value <0.05 suggestive of bias or small-study effects.16 In such cases, the trim-and-fill method was applied to account for potentially missing studies.16 To address the robustness of the pooled effect size, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted wherein one study at a time is iteratively removed to analyze whether any single study disproportionately influences overall results. All analyses were conducted using R software (V.2024.09.0), using the meta (V.5.5-0) and metafor (V.3.4-0) packages.14 18 Risk of bias was assessed using Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies (ROBINS) for non-randomized studies and risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (ROB2) for randomized controlled trial (RCT).15 19–21

All authors voted on final recommendations, considering the results and quality of the meta-analyses and the balance between benefits and harms, cost considerations, and patient preference.22 23

Voting of recommendations was performed confidentially and anonymously. Initial voting resulted in discordance. A modified Delphi method was implemented, taking into account the literature reviewed, the meta-analysis results, patient values, cost considerations, and collective expert judgment of the author panel. The authors met virtually where all of the above was reviewed, with an opportunity to discuss opinions. After a second round of voting with significantly less discordance, a final meeting was held to review and confirm recommendations by consensus (table 2).

Results

The search yielded 12 390 studies, of which 25 met inclusion criteria (figure 1, online supplemental table 3).24

PICO 1: in patients with ACC who are considered high risk for cholecystectomy due to acute illness or chronic medical conditions (P), should PC be performed in addition to administration of antibiotics (I) versus administration of antibiotics alone (C) to reduce the incidences of mortality, septic shock, intra-abdominal infections, need for unplanned interventions, or recurrent biliary complications (O)?

Qualitative analysis

Eight non-randomized and one randomized study were included.25–33 The studies had varying results; however, in many cases, the outcomes were unadjusted for disease severity and comorbidities. Hatzidakis et al conducted a randomized trial and concluded that PC did not decrease mortality (18% vs 13%, p>0.05).25 Although some included studies delineated no significant mortality differences between those who received PC or antibiotics only,26 30 others demonstrated a lower mortality in the antibiotics group.27 28 33 These studies noted that more severely ill patients received PC rather than antibiotics alone. Asmar et al studied frail, geriatric patients, indirectly finding that PC had a higher 6-month mortality. There was a higher incidence of sepsis in the antibiotics (4%) and PC (5%) groups versus the cholecystectomy group (3%, p<0.001).33

Endo et al examined 30-day and 90-day mortality between cholecystectomy, PC, or antibiotics, stratified by patient severity.29 The authors concluded that gallbladder drainage may play a role in groups with Tokyo Guidelines 2013 grade 3 severity, who have concomitant jaundice, neurologic dysfunction, and respiratory dysfunction, all of which were associated with increased mortality. However, a primary cholecystectomy could be performed safely in those with grade 3 ACC without those risk factors. Ozdemir et al demonstrated a lower 30-day mortality in high-risk patients, defined as APACHE II score ≥12 who received a PC (7% vs 10%).31

Many studies demonstrated that PC was associated with increased biliary-related complications; however, antibiotics alone had a higher recurrence of disease.26–29 In the study conducted by Janssen et al, patients who only received antibiotics had a higher recurrence of ACC versus those who had PC (33% vs 8%, p value non-significant).34 Joliat et al reported that among elderly patients, treatment failure or recurrence requiring emergent cholecystectomy occurred in 35.7% of those who underwent PC versus 43% of those treated with antibiotics alone.28

Quantitative analysis

Mortality

Six of the nine studies included reported mortality outcomes.25–31 The pooled analysis using a fixed-effects model demonstrated no significant differences in mortality between the PC plus antibiotics group and the antibiotics-only group (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.68 to 1.56, p=0.88). There was moderate heterogeneity (I2=30.2%, p=0.20) with no significant publication bias detected (Egger’s test: t=2.10, df=4, p=0.10) (figure 2A). Most studies reported 30-day mortality,26 27 30 whereas Joliat et al28 and Endo et al29 reported 90-day mortality. Ozdemir et al reported mortality during the study period, which spanned 13 years.31

Septic shock

Four studies reported on septic shock events.26 27 31 33 The random-effects model was used due to moderate to substantial heterogeneity (I²= 66.4%, p=0.03). The pooled result did not show a statistically significant difference between groups (OR 1.53; 95% CI 0.21 to 10.98, p=0.67), and the wide CI reflects substantial uncertainty and variability across studies (figure 2B). No publication bias was detected (Egger’s test: t=−0.47, df=2, p=0.68).

Intra-abdominal infection

Five studies were included for intra-abdominal infection.26 30–33 The random-effects model was used due to significant heterogeneity (I² = 67%, p=0.02). The analysis did not reach statistical significance, indicating a high level of variation in the studies (OR 2.36; 95% CI 0.91 to 6.07; p=0.07), but the wide interval suggests substantial uncertainty (figure 2C). The Egger’s test did not indicate publication bias (t=0.44, df=3, p=0.69), and the slope of the funnel plot regression line is close to zero.

Recurrent biliary complications

Seven studies were included for recurrent biliary complications analysis.26–28 30–33 The random-effects model did not demonstrate statistical significance between groups (OR 1.97; 95% CI 0.90 to 4.33; p=0.09) with a wider CI that crosses 1, suggesting uncertainty due to study variability. (figure 2D) Heterogeneity is statistically significant and substantial (I²: 70.5%, p=0.002), indicating that study results vary beyond chance. No evidence of publication bias was detected. (Egger’s Test: t=0.79, df=5, p=0.46)

Unplanned interventions

There were three studies eligible for this analysis.26 30 31 There were no statistically significant differences in the antibiotics alone group in comparison to the PC in combination of antibiotics group (OR 8.86; 95% CI 0.62 to 127.42; p=0.11). However, CI was wide, indicating uncertainty likely due to small sample size and heterogeneity. (figure 2E) Heterogeneity was significant (I² = 78.5%, p=0.009). No evidence of publication bias was detected (Egger’s Test: t=2.71, df=1, p=0.22).

Grading of evidence

The risk of bias is serious (online supplemental figure 1a, b). There was low risk for inconsistency among mortality outcomes given low heterogeneity with narrow, overlapping CI. Based on few event rates and heterogeneous populations across studies, there was serious indirectness and imprecision. Publication bias was not a concern.

For septic shock, there was serious risk of inconsistency given high heterogeneity and a wide CI, indirectness, and imprecision as the CI crosses the threshold with a low absolute effect. The remaining outcomes have serious risk of inconsistency and imprecision. There was a large effect size noted with intra-abdominal infections and unplanned interventions (online supplemental table 4).

PICO 2: in patients presenting to the hospital with delayed presentation (>5 days since onset of symptoms) of ACC (P), should cholecystectomy be performed (I) versus administration of antibiotics alone without cholecystectomy (C) to reduce the incidences of mortality, septic shock, intra-abdominal infection, need for unplanned interventions, or recurrent biliary complications (O)?

Qualitative analysis

There were only two retrospective studies identified.35 36 A meta-analysis was not performed due to having a low number of studies and events. Tan et al studied outcomes between same admission and delayed cholecystectomy in patients with >7 days of symptoms.35 Delayed cholecystectomy was performed from 4 weeks to 24 weeks after discharge. There was a higher intraoperative severity and technical difficulty in the same admission group. However, there were no differences in overall complications (delayed 17% vs same admission 14%, p=0.81), intraoperative mortality (0%), or intraoperative morbidity (delayed 2% vs same admission 0%, p=0.49). Bundgaard et al evaluated early cholecystectomy, stratified by time to operative intervention, and delayed cholecystectomy and found no differences in stated outcomes.

Grading of evidence

Both studies were at risk of bias, indirectness due to symptom duration, and imprecision due to small event rate. Therefore, the certainty of evidence is very low. (online supplemental figure 1a).

PICO 3: in patients presenting to the hospital with delayed presentation (>5 days since onset of symptoms) of ACC (P), in addition to administration of antibiotics, should cholecystectomy be performed (I) versus performing a PC (C), to reduce the incidences of mortality, intra-abdominal infections, need for unplanned interventions, or recurrent biliary complications (O)?

Qualitative analysis

Two studies were included.34 37 In the study of Abi Haidar et al, mean symptom onset was 8 days.37 PC was associated with longer intensive care unit stays (6 days vs 2 days, p=0.08), longer hospital stays (20 days vs 12 days, p<0.001), more complications per patient (3 vs 2, p=0.01), and more readmissions (31% vs 13%, p=0.006). However, in a multivariable regression analysis controlling for Charlson Comorbidity Index and age, there was no difference in mortality association (HR 1.8 (0.8 to 3.7)).

Karakayali et al studied low-risk patients with ACC who presented ≥72 hours after symptom onset.34 They compared early cholecystectomy to PC followed by cholecystectomy ≥4 weeks later. The authors found fewer complications after PC (9% vs 35%, p=0.003), shorter postoperative length of stay (PC 3 days vs 5 days, p=0.01), and lower conversion to open (PC 19% vs 40%, p=0.029). There were five biliary-related complications in the cholecystectomy group compared with one in the PC group.

Grading of evidence

In aggregate, these two studies were heterogeneous in nature. A meta-analysis was not performed due to having a low number of studies and events. The studies demonstrated indirectness, as their timing of symptom onset did not align with that specified in our PICO question. There are risks of bias associated with the studies (online supplemental figure 1a). Therefore, the results are inconsistent and, therefore, inconclusive.

PICO 4: in patients who are at chronically high risk for surgery, who have already received PC and have evidence of cystic duct patency (P), should the PC be removed electively after the resolution of ACC (I) versus leaving the PC in place and performing elective cholecystectomy (C) to reduce the incidences of intra-abdominal infections, need for unplanned interventions, or recurrent biliary complications (O)?

Qualitative analysis

12 retrospective studies, 11 of which were single-center, were included.38–49 The studies varied in reported results. Higher incidences of biliary-related complications and longer operative times were reported in patients who had PC left in situ prior to performing a cholecystectomy in several included studies.39–42 44 However, Tolan et al demonstrated no recurrences in a mean follow-up period of 17 months among patients who did not have a cholecystectomy after removal of PC.38 Fleming et al concluded that nearly 90% of patients with acute cholecystitis did not have sepsis after PC removal.43 In contrast, studies indicated that in patients who had a cholecystectomy after PC, there was a lower risk of recurrence, mortality, and other complications.45 46 When the PC was removed without a subsequent cholecystectomy, higher reported biliary events47 49 and readmissions48 were observed.

Quantitative analysis

Intra-abdominal infection

In the four studies included for the fixed model pooled analysis, a lower risk of intra-abdominal infections with PC removal without subsequent cholecystectomy was demonstrated (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.65, p=0.007) (figure 3A). Heterogeneity was low (I2 21.4%, p=0.28). Egger’s test (p=0.03) suggested some publication bias.

Need for unplanned interventions

The fixed model analysis demonstrated increased risk of unplanned interventions after PC removal in the pooled analysis of the six studies included (OR 2.23, 95% CI 1.28 to 3.90, p=0.005) (figure 3B). There was low heterogeneity (I² = 31.3%). Egger’s test (p=0.08) suggested no publication bias.

Recurrent biliary complications

11 studies were included. The random-effects model showed increased risk of recurrent biliary events in patients who had the PC left in place and subsequently underwent elective cholecystectomy, compared with those managed with PC removal alone (OR 3.01, 95% CI 1.58 to 5.73; p<0.01) (figure 3C). There was high heterogeneity (I² = 71.8%, p=0.0001). Egger’s test did not identify significant publication bias (p=0.78).

Grading of evidence

There was serious risk of bias for all outcomes (online supplemental figure 1a). For the outcomes of intra-abdominal infection and unplanned interventions, there was low risk of other factors downgrading certainty. Overall certainty was deemed low due to risk of bias. There was serious risk of bias and inconsistency when assessing the outcome of recurrent biliary complications. There were large effect sizes noted in the outcomes of unplanned interventions and recurrent biliary complications. Thus, the certainty of evidence was deemed very low (online supplemental table 5).

Recommendations

PICO 1

We conditionally recommend that high-risk patients with ACC undergo treatment with antibiotics alone.

PICO 2

No recommendation was made to treat patients with ACC with more than 5 days of symptoms with either cholecystectomy or antibiotics.

PICO 3

No recommendation was made to treat patients with ACC with more than 5 days of symptoms with PC or antibiotics.

PICO 4

We conditionally recommend removing the PC catheter without performing cholecystectomy after resolution of ACC in high-risk patients who have evidence of cystic duct patency.

Discussion

The TG 18 recommend early cholecystectomy for patients who are deemed acceptable risk, defined as a CCI of ≤5 and ASA physical status classification system score of ≤2 in grade 1 and 2 acute cholecystitis.4 TG 18 further recommends utilization of antibiotics and general supportive care with observation alone if patients meet criteria of CCI 6 or greater and/or ASA class III or greater in grade 1 cholecystitis. In grade 2 and higher grades of cholecystitis, TG 18 recommend antibiotics and gallbladder drainage with delayed cholecystectomy in the high-risk patient population. The 2020 WSES guidelines recommend early cholecystectomy over PC for high-risk surgical patients. However, the guideline acknowledges there are varying definitions of the high-risk patient. The guideline also refers to the CHOCOLATE trial, the only multicenter randomized control trial, comparing cholecystectomy to PC in high-risk surgical patients defined by APPACHE II score of 7 or more.50 This study demonstrated no differences in mortality, but increased risk of PC-related complications. In formulating our PICO questions for this practice management guideline, we aimed to address areas that were not assessed in previous guidelines. Our objective was to investigate studies which compared high-risk surgical patients who had antibiotics alone in comparison to PC. In our pooled analysis, there were no appreciable differences in mortality, septic shock, intra-abdominal infection, or recurrent biliary complications. However, the data suggested an increased risk of unplanned interventions in the PC group. In already compromised and high-risk surgical patients, unplanned interventions carry a high stake for further complications and subsequent decompensation. The increased risk of unplanned interventions was observed secondary to catheter dislodgements.25 26 31 Further, large effect sizes were observed in outcomes related to intra-abdominal infections and unplanned interventions in the PC group and were taken into consideration when submitting our recommendation for PICO 1. Catheter dislodgement is a common complication after PC, especially in critically ill populations where neurologic changes may lead to behavioral issues or appropriate long-term care for drains by various caregivers in various healthcare settings may be challenging. Although there was no appreciable risk of mortality in the pooled analysis, the study conducted by Asmar et al which was not included in the meta-analysis due to lack of clear numerical outcomes had the largest sample size.33 The authors demonstrated the PC group had a twofold risk of increased mortality risk at 6 months, which was higher than that in the antibiotic group. The clinical condition of the patient must be emphasized in using antibiotics as an initial approach. A recent systematic review published in JAMA Surgery concluded that PC should only be reserved for those who are considered poor surgical candidates, those with concomitant sepsis, or those who had failure of antibiotic treatment.51 This systematic review addressed a similar question to our PICO 1; however, the review included patients with acalculous cholecystitis, malignancy, and a wide range of illness severity. Similarly, we recommend antibiotics as an initial treatment option for high-risk patients. However, a PC in addition to antibiotics should be used to obtain source control in the event of hemodynamic compromise or collapse during the treatment period, which would indicate treatment failure with antibiotics alone.

In evaluating the existing guidelines, early cholecystectomy was recommended in patients who present with symptom onset of <7 days and ideally within 72 hours.4 In addressing PICO 2, we aimed to assess whether antibiotics alone would be beneficial for those presenting in a delayed fashion. This PICO did not address the high-risk surgical patient but the patient who presented in a delayed fashion. There are no existing guidelines assessing this clinical question. We used a symptom onset timing of 5 days based on the literature and as an intermediate duration between 72 hours and 7 days.52 There were only two studies addressing this PICO. There were no differences in mortality, and other critical outcomes were not addressed. Therefore, the authors could not make a recommendation.

We addressed cholecystectomy or PC in those with delayed presentation >5 days of symptom onset for PICO 3 and again only found two eligible studies. These two studies had contradictory results. This PICO again did not address the high-risk surgical patient. Therefore, no recommendation could be made.

Our analysis for PICO 4 demonstrated that removing a PC after resolution of ACC without cholecystectomy incurred an increased risk of unplanned interventions. On the other hand, there was a decreased risk of intra-abdominal infection and recurrent biliary complications. The overall certainty of evidence was very low. The combined consensus recommendations from WSES and two other international societies recommended cholecystectomy after PC.53 However, existing guidelines do not clearly delineate whether PC should be removed after resolution of ACC with and without planned surgery. The decision to remove a PC after resolution of ACC is highly dependent on biomarkers and symptomatology, as well as risk of future surgery for a particular individual. The increased risk of unplanned interventions was seen due to catheter complications such as dislodgements and bile leakages across all studies included. These complications could pose a significant burden to quality of life as well as expose the patient to hospital readmissions and consequences related to re-interventions. However, removing the catheter incurred a lower risk of intra-abdominal infections and biliary complications, likely related to the underlying rationale for removing the catheter after resolution of ACC with a patent cystic duct. Yet, removing a PC without planned surgery may cause recurrence of ACC due to an underlying impaired gallbladder. Bala et al demonstrated that older age, presence of coronary artery disease, elevated alkaline phosphatases, and septic shock at admission were independent predictors of using PC as a definitive treatment without an interval cholecystectomy.41 These risks must be balanced against the benefits of performing surgery. Further heterogeneity of severity of ACC requiring a PC proves difficult to standardize management. Alvino et al demonstrated that PC placement is associated with a low mortality (0.35%) and successful symptom resolution in 91% of patients.44 Using risk assessment calculators may inform the clinician on how to proceed in high-risk surgical patients. Therefore, the authors made a conditional recommendation for removing the PC after resolution of ACC in patients with a patent cystic duct.

Limitations

There are several limitations to our analysis. First, most of our included studies were retrospective in nature, with the exception of one RCT, which was published in 2002 and had serious risks of bias. There was wide heterogeneity within the included studies regarding study populations, including surgical risk assessments, comorbidities, and grade of cholecystitis. Interventions such as choice of antibiotics used and indirectness to timing of interventions in relation to our PICO questions also differed between studies. We were unable to perform a pooled analysis for PICO 2 and PICO 3 given the lack of robust studies. PICO 2 and 3 did not address high-risk patients. The events across all pooled analyses were low. Due to the variability in the time frames used to report mortality and complications across the studies, the authors are unable to provide evidence-based recommendations regarding the follow-up necessary for patients receiving the treatments outlined in this guideline.

Despite the above limitations, this systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the best available evidence pertaining to our investigative questions. Although robust evidence was not available to provide strong recommendations for each PICO, clinicians should recognize that the use of PC, especially in high-risk surgical patients may carry a risk of complications such as unplanned interventions, which could pose further threats to an already compromised patient. We acknowledge WSES guidelines and TG 18 recommendations to transfer to a center with biliary expertise. There is existing literature on other modalities of gallbladder drainage techniques, which was not studied or discussed in this analysis. The authors made a collective decision to address other gallbladder drainage techniques in a future practice management guideline when more published evidence is available. We cannot make any recommendations regarding the use of antibiotics alone and PC for those who presented in a delayed fashion. Clinicians must weigh risks and benefits of conservative management and surgery. The management of high-risk patients is complex, and these varying clinical conditions are frequently encountered by the acute care surgeon. The clinical nuances addressed in our PICO questions and the lack of available evidence to make any strong recommendations further highlight opportunities for further study in these areas.
