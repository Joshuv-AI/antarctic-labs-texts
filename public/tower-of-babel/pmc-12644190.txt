Short-term outcomes of endoscopic intermuscular dissection for early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration: a single-center experience from China
Authors: T. Yang, J. Qi, X. Lin, L. Shi, F. Li, Q. Wu, L. Huang, D. Fan, J. Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and study aimEndoscopic intermuscular dissection (EID) is an emerging technique for resecting early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration. This study reports the short-term outcomes of EID for early rectal cancer from a single-center experience in China.Patients and methodsBetween January 2024 and March 2025, 12 patients diagnosed with rectal malignant tumors, with CT staging ≤ T2 and no lymph node metastasis underwent EID. For lesions staged as cT2, endoscopic ultrasound confirmation of no muscularis propria invasion was required prior to EID for therapeutic resection at our center. All patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team and provided informed consent. The primary outcomes included technical success (defined as macroscopic complete en bloc resection without major intraprocedural complications), postoperative complications, and tumor-free resection margins. Secondary outcomes included hospital stay duration, follow-up completion rates, and short-term oncological outcomes.ResultsTechnical success was achieved in 11 of 12 patients (91.7%), with one patient experiencing an intraprocedural perforation. En bloc resection margins were confirmed histopathologically in all cases. One patient (8.3%) experienced delayed perforation requiring additional surgery. All lesions were confirmed as pT1b adenocarcinoma on final pathology. Among patients who reached follow-up timepoints, 6-month follow-up completion was 100% (9/9 eligible patients) and 12-month follow-up completion was 50.0% (2/4 eligible patients). No tumor recurrence was observed in patients with available follow-up data during a median follow-up of 9 months (range 3–16 months).ConclusionThis preliminary single-center experience suggests that EID may be a feasible technique for carefully selected cases of resecting early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration, demonstrating acceptable rates of complete resection and reasonable short-term safety profiles. However, the small sample size, staging limitations, and short follow-up period mean that these findings require cautious interpretation. Larger multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are necessary to establish the role of EID in the treatment algorithm for early rectal cancer.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer represents a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with rectal cancer constituting a significant proportion of cases [1]. Early detection and intervention are critical for improving survival rates and reducing morbidity [2]. Endoscopic resection techniques have evolved as minimally invasive approaches for managing early-stage rectal cancers, offering alternatives to conventional surgery while preserving organ function and reducing treatment-related complications [3, 4].

Endoscopic intermuscular dissection (EID) represents a novel technique that enables resection of early rectal cancer by dissecting within the intermuscular space between the longitudinal and circular muscle layers [5]. This approach shows particular promise for treating T1b rectal tumors, especially in cases where conventional endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) or endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) may be limited by tumor depth, size, or fibrosis [6]. EID is primarily indicated for T1N0 lesions confined to the submucosal layer [7]. However, accurate preoperative staging of early rectal cancers remains challenging, with CT imaging frequently overstaging lesions as a result of its limited resolution for assessing tumor depth [8–10]. This staging limitation necessitates the use of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) to provide more precise assessment of tumor invasion depth, particularly for lesions that appear as T2 on CT imaging but may be confined to the submucosal layer [11]. The combination of CT and EUS evaluation allows for more accurate identification of true T1 lesions suitable for EID, even when initial CT staging suggests deeper invasion.

Previous research has demonstrated that in rectal lesions, residual malignancy occurs in approximately 19% of cases following non-curative ESD in surgical resection specimens [12]. This finding highlights the potential for tumor persistence after ESD treatment for early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration. While full-thickness local excision techniques such as transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) can overcome the limitation of incomplete resection seen with ESD, these approaches are associated with higher morbidity and may not be suitable for all patients, particularly those with significant comorbidities or those preferring less invasive approaches [13, 14]. EID provides a therapeutic avenue for achieving complete tumor resection while potentially reducing the risks of perforation and local recurrence associated with more extensive resection methods.

Despite its potential advantages, EID remains relatively novel and has been less extensively investigated compared to established techniques such as ESD or EMR [7]. The safety, feasibility, and efficacy of EID in managing early rectal cancer have not been fully established, and additional clinical experience is needed to contribute to the growing evidence base before larger multicenter studies can be undertaken. Reports on short-term outcomes are essential to evaluate procedural success, complication rates, and early oncological efficacy of this emerging technique.

In this single-center study from China, we present our initial experience with EID for early rectal cancer between January 2024 and March 2025. The primary objective was to assess the short-term outcomes of EID regarding procedural safety, postoperative complications, and early oncological efficacy, as measured by tumor-free resection margins and recurrence rates. This investigation contributes to the growing body of evidence on EID and provides insights into patient selection criteria, technical considerations, and limitations of this emerging technique.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

From January 2024 to March 2025, 12 patients diagnosed with rectal malignant tumors with CT staging ≤ T2 and no evidence of lymph node metastasis (N0) underwent EID for therapeutic resection at our center. For lesions initially staged as cT2 on CT imaging, mandatory EUS evaluation was performed to confirm that the tumor was confined to the submucosal layer without muscularis propria invasion. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-Sen University (No. E2024355).

All patients underwent preoperative evaluation including CT scanning and EUS. CT has well-documented limitations in accurately staging early rectal cancers and tends to overstage lesions [15]. Therefore, all lesions initially staged as cT2 on CT imaging underwent mandatory EUS evaluation to assess the precise depth of tumor invasion and to exclude muscularis propria involvement. Only lesions confirmed to be confined to the submucosal layer on EUS were considered suitable for EID. This approach ensured that only true T1 lesions (confined to submucosa) were treated with EID, despite initial CT staging suggesting T2 disease. The accuracy of this approach was validated by final pathological examination, which confirmed pT1b disease in all cases.

Procedure and management

The EID procedure was first described by Leon Moons from the Netherlands [7]. Our center subsequently refined this technique [16, 17]. The procedure involves mucosal incision, intermuscular dissection, and vessel coagulation, with access to the intermuscular space achieved through partial myotomy of the inner circular muscle layer. The dissection is completed in two planes: a distal plane immediately above the inner circular muscle layer and a proximal plane of resection below the lesion and the circular muscle, ensuring clear visualization of the longitudinal muscle layer following the procedure (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Procedural steps of endoscopic intermuscular dissection for early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcomes assessed were technical success (defined as macroscopic complete en bloc resection without major intraprocedural complications), postoperative complications, and tumor-free resection margins. Secondary outcomes included hospital stay duration, follow-up completion rates, and short-term oncological outcomes. Postoperative complications were defined as adverse events resulting in prolonged hospitalization, readmission, or requiring intervention (e.g., blood transfusion, endoscopy, or surgery). These were classified according to the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy criteria [18]. Tumor-free resection margins were determined histopathologically, with en bloc resection defined as the absence of tumor cells at both lateral and deep resection margins [19].

Follow-up colonoscopy for scar surveillance was scheduled at 6 months and 1 year post procedure. Scar biopsies were routinely obtained. CT scans were performed at 6 months and 1 year post procedure to evaluate potential lymph node metastasis. Patients referred for additional surgery were excluded from scar surveillance protocols.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed for data analysis. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Technical success and complications

Technical success, defined as macroscopic complete en bloc resection without major intraprocedural complications, was achieved in 11 of 12 patients (91.7%). One patient experienced an intraprocedural perforation that was successfully managed with endoscopic metallic clip closure, followed by fasting and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Postoperative complications occurred in one case: one patient developed a delayed perforation requiring emergency laparoscopic repair, which was associated with infection and prolonged hospitalization. The mean total hospital stay for the entire cohort was 9.33 ± 4.23 days, which included preoperative preparation and evaluation. The mean postoperative hospital stay was 4.92 ± 3.92 days, which is more consistent with expectations for a minimally invasive endoscopic procedure (Table 2). Pathology results and follow-up

Histopathological assessment confirmed en bloc resection margins in all cases, indicating complete tumor removal with no evidence of neoplastic cells at either lateral or deep resection margins.

All 12 patients were diagnosed with submucosal adenocarcinoma (pT1b) on final pathological examination. This confirmed the accuracy of our staging approach, as the eight patients initially staged as cT2 by CT were correctly identified as having submucosal disease through EUS evaluation, with final pathology confirming no muscularis propria invasion. Histological classification revealed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma in 1 patient (8.3%), moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma in 10 patients (83.3%), and well-differentiated adenocarcinoma in 1 patient (8.3%). Additional histological prognostic markers were evaluated: lymphovascular invasion was absent in all cases, perineural invasion was not identified, and tumor budding was low grade in all evaluated specimens. During the follow-up period, no tumor recurrence was observed among the patients with available assessment data (Table 3). Discussion

Endoscopic intermuscular dissection represents a significant advancement in the minimally invasive management of early rectal cancer. By enabling resection within the intermuscular space, EID addresses several limitations of conventional endoscopic techniques, including ESD and EMR, which are often constrained by tumor depth, size, or fibrosis [6, 7]. The ability to distinguish between the longitudinal and circular muscle layers facilitates precise tumor removal while reducing the risk of perforation and local recurrence, which remain common concerns with more superficial resection methods [20, 21].

The technical success rate of 91.7% and en bloc resection margins observed in our study underscores the therapeutic precision of EID. Unlike ESD, which is primarily limited to mucosal and superficial submucosal lesions, EID provides a viable option for tumors extending deeper into the submucosa in carefully selected cases [7, 22]. This capability is particularly valuable in early rectal cancer management, where complete tumor removal with clear margins is essential for preventing local recurrence and avoiding radical surgery [22]. The absence of tumor remnants at the vertical resection margins in our cohort further highlights the oncological efficacy of EID, offering a potentially curative approach for carefully selected patients.

When compared to other local excision techniques, EID occupies a unique position in the treatment algorithm. Compared to ESD and EMR, EID offers deeper resection capability while maintaining endoscopic access. When compared to full-thickness excision techniques such as TEM or transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS), EID offers potentially lower morbidity while achieving complete resection in the intermuscular plane. However, full-thickness techniques provide more comprehensive resection and may be more appropriate for larger or more advanced lesions [23–26]. From a technical perspective, EID bridges the gap between endoscopic and surgical techniques, combining the minimally invasive benefits of endoscopy with the oncological thoroughness of complete tumor resection beneath the submucosa. This approach not only preserves organ function but also reduces the morbidity associated with traditional surgical interventions, such as low anterior resection or abdominoperineal excision. For patients with early rectal cancer without lymph node metastasis, EID offers a tailored therapeutic option that balances oncological safety with quality-of-life considerations, particularly in cases where radical surgery might represent overtreatment.

The apparent discrepancy between initial CT staging (cT2 in eight patients) and final pathology (pT1b in all patients) actually validates our staging approach rather than representing a limitation. CT is known to overstage early rectal cancers [10, 27], which is why we implemented mandatory EUS evaluation for all cT2 lesions to confirm submucosal confinement. The EUS findings correctly identified these lesions as being confined to the submucosal layer, which was subsequently confirmed by final pathology. This demonstrates the importance of combining CT and EUS for accurate staging of early rectal cancers and supports our patient selection criteria.

The mean total hospital stay of 9.33 ± 4.23 days included both preoperative and postoperative periods. The postoperative hospital stay of 4.92 ± 3.92 days is more appropriate for evaluating procedural morbidity and is consistent with expectations for advanced endoscopic procedures. Several factors contributed to the postoperative stay duration, including institutional learning curve and conservative post-procedural monitoring protocols, patient age and comorbidities, and the impact of complications requiring extended monitoring and treatment. Quality improvement initiatives have been implemented to optimize care pathways and reduce future hospital stays while maintaining safety standards.

However, the innovative nature of EID also necessitates careful consideration of its learning curve and technical demands. On the basis of our experience and available literature, endoscopists with extensive ESD experience require approximately 10–15 EID cases to achieve technical proficiency. We recommend minimum requirements including > 100 ESD procedures, formal training with experienced operators, careful case selection, surgical backup availability, and gradual complexity progression. The procedure requires advanced endoscopic skills and thorough familiarity with intermuscular anatomy, which may limit its widespread adoption in the short term. Specific challenges, such as identifying the intermuscular plane and managing intraoperative bleeding, highlight the need for comprehensive training programs and standardized protocols.

Our study has several important limitations. The small sample size of 12 patients limits statistical power and generalizability. Among patients who reached appropriate follow-up timepoints, the 6-month follow-up completion rate was excellent (100%), demonstrating good patient compliance. However, only four patients have reached the 12-month timepoint, with 50% completion rate, limiting our ability to assess longer-term outcomes. Three patients had not yet reached the 6-month timepoint at the time of analysis, reflecting the recent implementation of this technique. Furthermore, the limited follow-up duration precludes definitive conclusions regarding long-term oncological outcomes. The absence of MRI in our staging protocol and reliance on CT scanning represents a significant methodological limitation. The single-center design may introduce selection bias. The lack of comparative analysis with other treatment modalities limits our ability to position EID within the treatment algorithm.

In conclusion, our single-center experience suggests that EID may represent a feasible technique for the management of carefully selected cases of early rectal cancer with deep submucosal infiltration, demonstrating acceptable rates of complete tumor resection and reasonable short-term safety profiles. However, these preliminary results should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the study’s inherent limitations. The significant discrepancy between clinical and pathological staging emphasizes the need for improved preoperative assessment protocols. Future multicenter, prospective investigations with larger cohorts, extended follow-up periods, and comparative designs are necessary to establish the role of EID in the treatment algorithm and define optimal patient selection criteria.
