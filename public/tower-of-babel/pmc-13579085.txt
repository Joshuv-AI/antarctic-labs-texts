S100A1 Promotes MDM2‐Mediated KLF15 Ubiquitination to Regulate ID1‐Driven Tubular Injury in Diabetic Kidney Disease
Authors: Zhitao Zeng, Jiazhen Shang, Shouyu Chai, Xia Zhang, Xincong Lv, Xiaotian Han, Yifan Xu, Baoze Ma, Zhimei Lv, Rong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the leading cause of end‐stage renal disease globally. Tubular injury represents an early pathological hallmark of DKD and a core driver of disease progression. By comparing single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data of kidney samples from healthy controls and DKD patients, we found significantly upregulated S100 calcium‐binding protein A1 (S100A1) expression in the proximal tubules of DKD patients, while its role and regulatory mechanism in DKD pathogenesis remain largely unclear. Analysis of renal biopsy specimens from DKD patients showed that S100A1 was markedly upregulated in proximal tubules, and its expression level was significantly correlated with 24‐h proteinuria, serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In vitro, S100A1 promoted tubular injury, inflammatory cytokine release, and fibrotic responses in high glucose‐stimulated human proximal tubular epithelial cells (HK‐2). Mechanistically, S100A1 enhanced the interaction between E3 ubiquitin ligase MDM2 and transcription factor Krüppel‐like factor 15 (KLF15), induced K48‐linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of KLF15, relieved KLF15‐mediated transcriptional repression of inhibitor of DNA binding 1 (ID1), and ultimately aggravated tubular injury. In vivo, proximal tubule‐specific knockdown of S100A1 attenuated renal injury, inflammatory infiltration, and fibrosis in diabetic mice. Our findings reveal a novel S100A1‐MDM2‐KLF15‐ID1 regulatory axis in DKD pathogenesis. Targeting S100A1 might be a potential therapy for proximal tubular injury in DKD.Keywords: diabetic kidney disease, ID1, KLF15, MDM2, proximal tubular injury, S100A1, ubiquitination

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), the most severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus, is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide [1, 2]. Epidemiological data show that approximately 589 million adults globally had diabetes in 2024, and this number is projected to reach 853 million by 2050, with 30%–40% developing DKD [3]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that renal tubular injury represents a core pathological hallmark of DKD and contributes substantially to disease progression [4, 5, 6]. Given the close association between tubulointerstitial damage, progressive renal function loss, and adverse renal outcomes, tubular epithelial cell dysfunction is widely recognized as a key pathogenic driver of DKD [7]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia directly induces renal tubular epithelial cell injury, which accelerates glomerulosclerosis via tubule‐glomerular crosstalk and forms a vicious cycle driving DKD progression [8, 9, 10]. Thus, exploring novel molecular mechanisms underlying DKD tubular injury is imperative.

S100 calcium‐binding protein A1 (S100A1) is a prototypical member of the S100 protein family containing two EF‐hand calcium‐binding domains [11, 12, 13]. It lacks intrinsic enzymatic activity and exerts functions by undergoing conformational changes upon calcium binding and interacting with specific target proteins to participate in calcium signaling, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses [14, 15]. However, the role of S100A1 in DKD remains poorly understood.

In this study, by comparing our single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data [16] between normal and DKD human kidney samples, we found a significant increase in S100A1 expression in proximal tubules of DKD. Through both in vitro and in vivo experiments, we confirmed that S100A1 plays a critical role in proximal tubule injury through promoting inflammation and fibrosis. Mechanistically, we revealed that S100A1 promotes Krüppel‐like factor 15 (KLF15) degradation by enhancing the interaction between MDM2 and KLF15, thereby relieving the transcriptional inhibition of inhibitor of DNA binding 1 (ID1) and ultimately facilitating the progression of DKD. The results of our study indicate that S100A1 plays a pivotal role in DKD and may serve as a potential therapeutic target for this disease.

Materials and Methods

Human Samples

Renal biopsy specimens from patients with DKD were obtained via percutaneous renal biopsy from the Department of Nephrology, Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. Normal control samples were collected from healthy renal tissues by wedge biopsy during nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University and strictly followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell Culture and Treatments

HK‐2 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were subsequently randomly divided into normal glucose group (NG, 5.5 mmol/L D‐glucose) and high glucose group (HG, 30 mmol/L D‐glucose). High osmolarity group (HO, 5.5 mmol/L D‐glucose plus 24.5 mmol/L mannitol) was used as an osmolarity control.

Gene Overexpression and Knockdown

S100A1 siRNA, ID1 siRNA, KLF15 siRNA, MDM2 siRNA and their corresponding negative controls were synthesized by GenePharma Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences used in the studies are as follows: S100A1‐siRNA 5′‐CUGUGGACAAGGUGAUGAATT‐3′; ID1‐siRNA 5′‐GUUGGAGCUGAACUCGGAATT‐3′; MDM2‐siRNA 5′‐GCCAGUAUAUUAUGACUAATT‐3′; KLF15‐siRNA 5′‐CUUUGGGUGAUCCUGAUGATT‐3′; NC‐siRNA 5′ UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT‐3′.

Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured HK‐2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Biotechnology, China). The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop‐2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Evo M‐MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (Accurate Biotechnology, China). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate Biotechnology, China) on a LightCycler 480 II Real‐Time PCR System (Roche, USA). β‐actin was used as the internal reference, and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in this study are detailed in Table S1.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from cells and kidney tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Epizyme Biotech, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors. After centrifugation, protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). Proteins were separated by 10% or 12% SDS‐PAGE gels and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Epizyme Biotech, China), and the gray values of the bands were analyzed using ImageJ software (RRID:SCR_003070).

The primary antibodies used in this study included: β‐actin (66009‐1‐Ig, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2687938); S100A1 (A23494, Abclonal); MDM2 (27883‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2881003); KLF15 (13749‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2132428); ID1 (18475‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2248812); IL‐1β (YT5201, Immunoway); IL‐6 (TD6087S, Abmart, RRID:AB_2936896); TNF‐α (A11534, Abclonal, RRID:AB_2758597); Fibronectin (db13198, Diagbio); Collagen I (db11923, Diagbio); Collagen IV (TP51657, Abmart); KIM‐1 (30948‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_3669790); Flag (20543‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_11232216); MYC (60003‐2‐Ig, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2734122); Ubiquitin (#3936, Cell Signaling Technology, RRID:AB_331292).

Immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemistry Assay

Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization and antigen retrieval prior to analysis. For immunohistochemical analysis: Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against S100A1 (A23494, Abclonal), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. The sections were then developed with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin. For immunofluorescence analysis, sections were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies against S100A1 (A23494, Abclonal), KIM‐1 (30948‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_3669790), or F4/80 (28463‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2881149). Fluorescein‐labeled Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL; FL‐1321, Vector Laboratories) was used as a proximal tubular marker when indicated. After washing, sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies, including Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (ab150076, Abcam, RRID:AB_2782993), Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG (ab150113, Abcam, RRID:AB_2576208), or Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (ab150077, Abcam, RRID:AB_2630356). Images were captured using an Olympus BX63 microscope (Japan).

Luciferase Reporter Assay

HK‐2 cells (RRID:CVCL_0302) were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then co‐transfected with the ID1 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid and either the KLF15 overexpression plasmid or the corresponding empty vector using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). The Renilla luciferase plasmid was co‐transfected as an internal control. After 48 h of transfection, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were detected using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

ChIP

HK‐2 cells were cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 37°C, quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min, and lysed in SDS lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). Chromatin was sheared by sonication (10 s ON/10 s OFF, 20 cycles, 20 W) to 200–800 bp fragments and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted 10‐fold in ChIP dilution buffer (Beyotime, China), and 1% was reserved as input. After pre‐clearing with Protein A/G agarose (Beyotime, China), samples were incubated with anti‐KLF15 antibody (13749‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2132428) or IgG overnight at 4°C. Immunocomplexes were captured with Protein A/G agarose for 1 h, washed sequentially with low‐salt, high‐salt, LiCl, and TE buffers, and eluted in 1% SDS/0.1 M NaHCO3. Cross‐links were reversed at 65°C for 4 h, followed by proteinase K digestion at 45°C for 1 h. DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR. KLF15 enrichment at the ID1 promoter was normalized to input DNA and expressed as percentage of input. The primer sequences used for the ID1 promoter were as follows: forward, 5′‐GTCGTCTTTCCACACTGCGAG‐3′; reverse, 5′‐TTATAAACACGCCACGGCCC‐3′.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay

After treatment, HK‐2 cells were lysed on ice for 15 min in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Epizyme, China). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification. For each immunoprecipitation reaction, 500–1000 μg of total protein was incubated with primary antibodies against MDM2 (27883‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2881003), or with normal IgG control (30000‐0‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2819035) overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. The antibody–protein complexes were then captured using Protein A/G magnetic beads (Epizyme Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After washing, the bound proteins were eluted with SDS loading buffer and denatured at 100°C for 10 min. Input lysates and immunoprecipitates were subjected to SDS‐PAGE and Western blot analysis.

Ubiquitination Assay

Ubiquitination assays were performed in HK‐2 cells transfected with Flag‐KLF15 and the indicated plasmids or siRNAs. To examine S100A1‐induced KLF15 ubiquitination, cells were co‐transfected with Flag‐KLF15 and S100A1 plasmid. To assess the requirement of MDM2, cells were co‐transfected with Flag‐KLF15, S100A1 plasmid, and si‐MDM2 or si‐NC. Cells were treated with MG132 for 6 h before harvest and then lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Flag‐KLF15 was immunoprecipitated using anti‐Flag antibody (20543‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_11232216), and KLF15 ubiquitination was detected by western blot with an anti‐ubiquitin antibody. For ubiquitin linkage analysis, HK‐2 cells were co‐transfected with Flag‐KLF15, S100A1 plasmid, and MYC‐Ub‐WT, MYC‐Ub‐K48, or MYC‐Ub‐K63 plasmids. After immunoprecipitation with anti‐Flag antibody (20543‐1‐AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_11232216), KLF15‐associated ubiquitin chains were detected by Western blot with an anti‐MYC antibody (60003‐2‐Ig, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2734122).

Animal Studies

All animal experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University and were approved by this committee. Male C57BL/6 mice (RRID:MGI:2159769) were selected because they are more susceptible to developing DKD than females [17]. Male C57BL/6J mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water. To establish the diabetic kidney injury model, mice were fed a high‐fat diet (HFD) starting at 8 weeks of age for 4 weeks and then received intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg/day) for 5 consecutive days. Control mice were injected with an equal volume of sodium citrate buffer. Fasting blood glucose levels were monitored weekly after STZ injection, and mice with sustained fasting blood glucose levels above 300 mg/dL were considered diabetic. At 20 weeks after STZ injection (the end of the experimental period), all mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). After complete cessation of respiration and heartbeat was confirmed, cervical dislocation was performed as secondary confirmation of death, and blood, urine, and whole kidney tissues were immediately harvested for subsequent analyzes.

Adeno‐Associated Virus Vectors (AAV9) Delivery

To achieve proximal tubule‐targeted knockdown of S100A1, a recombinant AAV9 vector was generated in which S100A1‐specific shRNA was driven by the Ggt1 promoter and was designated AAV9‐Ggt1‐shS100a1. An AAV9 vector expressing scrambled shRNA was used as the negative control (AAV9‐NC). Mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): Con+AAV9‐NC, Con+AAV9‐shS100a1, STZ + AAV9‐NC, STZ + AAV9‐shS100a1. Four weeks before STZ injection, mice received AAV9‐Ggt1‐shS100a1 or AAV9‐NC via tail vein injection at a dose of 1 × 1012 viral genomes per mouse. At 20 weeks after STZ injection, mice were euthanized, and blood, urine, and whole kidney tissues were harvested for further analyzes.

Histopathological Analysis of the Kidney

Kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE), periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS), and Masson's trichrome staining were performed according to standard protocols to evaluate renal histopathological changes and collagen deposition. Tubulointerstitial injury was evaluated using randomly selected non‐overlapping cortical fields. The severity of tubulointerstitial injury was graded from 0 to 4 as follows: 0, no lesion; 1, injury involving < 25% of the field; 2, injury involving 25%–50% of the field; 3, injury involving 50%–75% of the field; and 4, injury involving > 75% of the field. Approximately 10 non‐overlapping cortical fields were analyzed per kidney from each mouse. All histological assessments were performed in an observer‐blinded manner.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two‐tailed Student's t‐tests. Differences between multiple groups were determined using one‐way ANOVA or two‐way ANOVA. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (RRID:SCR_002798) and Adobe Illustrator (RRID:SCR_010279).

Results

S100A1 Is Increased in Proximal Tubules of DKD

In our previous study [16], by performing scRNA‐seq using renal biopsies from three patients with DKD and normal tissues from three nephrectomy samples, we found elevated expression of S100A1 in proximal tubules of DKD (Figure 1A). To further validate this cell‐type‐specific finding, we first interrogated the Kidney Interactive Transcriptomics (KIT) database (http://humphreyslab.com/SingleCell/), which similarly showed increased S100A1 expression in proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) of DKD patients (Figure 1B). We then analyzed the Nephroseq database and observed increased S100A1 expression in DKD tubulointerstitial samples, further supporting the association between S100A1 upregulation and diabetic kidney injury at the tissue level (Figure S1A,B).

We next assessed S100A1 protein levels in kidney tissues from DKD patients. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that the intensity of S100A1 in proximal tubular epithelial cells was markedly higher in DKD patients than in healthy controls (Figure 1C). Correlation analysis indicated that tubular S100A1 expression was positively correlated with 24‐h proteinuria (r = 0.5523, p = 0.0116) and serum creatinine (r = 0.5591, p = 0.0104), and negatively correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; r = −0.5056, p = 0.0229) (Figure 1D). Immunofluorescence co‐staining further confirmed that S100A1 colocalized with Lotus Tetragonolobus Lectin (LTL), a marker of proximal tubules, in DKD kidneys (Figure 1E). These results demonstrate that S100A1 is upregulated in proximal tubules of DKD patients.

S100A1 Is Involved in High Glucose‐Induced HK‐2 Cell Injury

To mimic the hyperglycemic condition of DKD in vitro, we exposed HK‐2 cells to high glucose. Western blot and real‐time PCR analyzes revealed that high glucose stimulation significantly upregulated S100A1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 2A,B), which was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Figure 2C). To investigate the function of S100A1 in HK‐2 cells, we designed and transfected small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting S100A1 and confirmed the knockdown efficiency by Western blot and real‐time PCR (Figure S2A). We subsequently assessed the protein expression of kidney injury molecule‐1 (KIM‐1), an established marker of proximal tubular injury. Silencing S100A1 significantly attenuated the high glucose‐induced upregulation of KIM‐1 (Figure 2D), thereby ameliorating HK‐2 cell injury. We then generated an S100A1‐overexpressing cell line and validated the overexpression efficiency by Western blot and real‐time PCR (Figure S2B). Overexpression of S100A1 markedly increased KIM‐1 protein levels (Figure 3A), suggesting that S100A1 exacerbates HK‐2 cell injury.

It has been reported that S100A1 participates in the regulation of inflammation and fibrosis [11, 15], both of which are closely associated with diabetic proximal tubular injury. We examined the mRNA and protein expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1) in both S100A1‐knockdown and S100A1‐overexpressing HK‐2 cells. Silencing S100A1 significantly suppressed the high glucose‐induced inflammatory response (Figure 2E,F), whereas overexpression of S100A1 markedly upregulated the expression of these cytokines (Figure 3B,C). We observed that S100A1 overexpression substantially promoted the upregulation of fibrosis‐related markers, including collagen I, collagen IV, and fibronectin (Figure 3D), while S100A1 silencing significantly inhibited high glucose‐induced production of collagen I, collagen IV, and fibronectin (Figure 2G). Taken together, these results indicate that S100A1 might promote high glucose‐induced proximal tubular epithelial cell injury through inducing inflammatory activation and profibrotic responses.

S100A1 Promoted HK‐2 Cell Injury Through Upregulating ID1

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of S100A1 in HK‐2 cell injury, transcriptome sequencing was performed using S100A1‐overexpressing and control cells. Compared with the control group, a total of 353 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2FoldChange| > 0.8, p < 0.05) were identified in the S100A1OE group, comprising 105 upregulated and 248 downregulated genes (Figure 4A). Among the identified DEGs, the inhibitor of DNA binding 1 (ID1) gene exhibited significant upregulation (Figure 4B). Given prior evidence implicating ID1 in the regulation of inflammation and fibrosis [18, 19], we selected ID1 as a key candidate gene for further investigation.

To further explore the pathological relevance of ID1 in DKD, we examined ID1 expression in high glucose‐stimulated HK‐2 cells. We found that high glucose treatment significantly upregulated ID1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4C). We next examined whether S100A1 regulates ID1 expression. Western blot and real‐time PCR were employed to assess ID1 protein and mRNA expression in HK‐2 cells after S100A1 overexpression or knockdown. The results showed that S100A1 knockdown decreased ID1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4D), whereas S100A1 overexpression increased ID1 mRNA and protein expression (Figure 4E), indicating that S100A1 positively regulates ID1 expression. The functional role of ID1 in high glucose‐induced HK‐2 cell injury was then evaluated by ID1 knockdown. Western blot analysis revealed that ID1 knockdown inhibited high glucose‐induced KIM‐1 upregulation (Figure 4F). In addition, ID1 knockdown reversed the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and MCP‐1, and fibrosis‐related markers, including collagen I, collagen IV, and fibronectin induced by high glucose, alleviating HK‐2 cell injury (Figure 4G–I). Finally, ID1 was silenced in S100A1‐overexpressing HK‐2 cells to determine whether ID1 mediates the effects of S100A1. Western blot and real‐time PCR analyzes revealed that ID1 knockdown significantly attenuated the S100A1‐induced upregulation of inflammation‐ and fibrosis‐related markers, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, fibronectin, collagen I, collagen IV, and KIM‐1 (Figure S3). Together, these data suggest that S100A1 promotes inflammatory activation and profibrotic responses in HK‐2 cells through upregulation of ID1, and that ID1 is a critical mediator of S100A1‐induced pathological effects.

S100A1 Upregulates ID1 by Promoting MDM2‐Dependent Ubiquitination and Degradation of KLF15

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of S100A1 regulating ID1, we identified upstream transcription factors governing ID1 transcription using the JASPAR database. Bioinformatic analysis revealed multiple putative binding sites for KLF15 in the ID1 promoter, suggesting that KLF15 may serve as a critical transcriptional regulator of ID1. To determine whether KLF15 regulates ID1 promoter activity, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was performed. KLF15 overexpression significantly reduced ID1 promoter activity in HK‐2 cells (Figure 5A), indicating that KLF15 negatively regulates ID1 transcription. To further validate the binding of KLF15 to the ID1 promoter, ChIP assays were performed. ChIP‐qPCR analysis showed significant enrichment of KLF15 at the ID1 promoter region compared with the IgG control (Figure 5B). Consistently, KLF15 knockdown increased ID1 mRNA and protein expression in HK‐2 cells, whereas KLF15 overexpression produced the opposite effect (Figure 5C,D, Figure S4A,B). These findings demonstrate that KLF15 directly binds to the ID1 promoter and negatively regulates ID1 transcription.

After establishing that S100A1 regulates ID1 expression through KLF15, we further investigated how S100A1 modulates KLF15 expression. S100A1 overexpression and knockdown did not significantly alter KLF15 mRNA levels, but both markedly affected KLF15 protein abundance (Figure 5E,F). Specifically, S100A1 overexpression reduced KLF15 protein levels, whereas S100A1 knockdown increased KLF15 protein expression (Figure 5F), suggesting that S100A1 regulates KLF15 mainly through a post‐translational mechanism. Cycloheximide chase assays further showed that S100A1 overexpression accelerated KLF15 protein degradation and shortened its half‐life (Figure 5G). Therefore, we used the proteasome inhibitor MG132, the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), and the calpain inhibitor calpeptin to elucidate the degradation pathway of the KLF15 protein. We observed that MG132 markedly rescued the reduction in KLF15 caused by S100A1 overexpression, whereas chloroquine and calpeptin had no comparable effect (Figure 5H). These findings suggest that S100A1 decreases KLF15 protein stability mainly through a pathway dependent on the proteasome.

Since proteasome‐dependent degradation is closely associated with ubiquitination, and KLF15 has been reported to undergo ubiquitination [20, 21], we next examined whether S100A1 promotes KLF15 ubiquitination. Ubiquitination assays revealed that S100A1 overexpression significantly increased the ubiquitination level of KLF15 (Figure 6A). To further determine the ubiquitin linkage type involved in this process, HK‐2 cells were co‐transfected with MYC‐Ub‐WT, MYC‐Ub‐K48, or MYC‐Ub‐K63 together with S100A1 and Flag‐KLF15. The results showed that S100A1 predominantly enhanced K48‐linked, but not K63‐linked, ubiquitination of KLF15 (Figure 6B).

To identify the potential E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for KLF15 ubiquitination, we searched the UbiBrowser database using KLF15 as the substrate, and MDM2 was predicted as a candidate E3 ligase for KLF15 (Figure 6C). Subsequently Co‐IP assays confirmed that MDM2 interacted with KLF15, and this interaction was enhanced upon S100A1 overexpression (Figure 6D). To determine whether MDM2 is required for S100A1‐induced KLF15 ubiquitination, we knocked down MDM2 in S100A1‐overexpressing HK‐2 cells. Western blot results confirmed that si‐MDM2 had good knockdown efficiency (Figure S4C). Ubiquitination assays showed that S100A1 overexpression increased KLF15 ubiquitination, whereas MDM2 knockdown markedly attenuated this effect. Consistently, the results of Western blot confirmed that S100A1 overexpression reduced the total protein level of KLF15, and this reduction was effectively reversed by MDM2 knockdown (Figure 6F). Collectively, these findings indicate that S100A1 promotes KLF15 degradation by enhancing the interaction between MDM2 and KLF15 and inducing MDM2‐dependent K48‐linked ubiquitination of KLF15.

Knockdown of S100A1 in Proximal Tubules Alleviates Kidney Injury in STZ‐Induced Diabetic Mice

To further define the in vivo role of S100A1 in diabetic kidney injury, we generated a recombinant AAV9‐Ggt1‐shS100a1 vector, in which shS100A1 expression was driven by the proximal tubule‐specific Ggt1 promoter to achieve targeted knockdown of S100A1 in renal proximal tubules. The virus was delivered to mice via tail vein injection 4 weeks before STZ‐induced diabetic modeling. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient knockdown of S100A1 in AAV9‐shS100a1‐treated kidneys (Figure 7A). Immunofluorescence staining further showed that S100A1 was predominantly localized in LTL‐positive proximal tubules and was markedly upregulated after STZ treatment (Figure 7B). STZ‐treated mice developed marked hyperglycemia, whereas AAV9‐shS100a1 administration did not significantly affect blood glucose levels relative to AAV9‐NC treatment in diabetic mice (Figure 7C). In contrast, STZ markedly increased the urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), and proximal tubule‐targeted S100A1 knockdown significantly reduced UACR in diabetic mice (Figure 7D), suggesting that S100A1 knockdown attenuated diabetic renal injury without altering systemic hyperglycemia.

Pronounced histological alterations were also detected. As shown by HE, PAS, and Masson's trichrome staining, STZ‐treated mice receiving AAV9‐NC exhibited marked tubular dilation, tubular epithelial injury, disruption of tubular architecture, increased PAS‐positive matrix deposition, interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. These pathological changes were markedly attenuated in diabetic mice treated with AAV9‐shS100a1. Semiquantitative analysis further showed that the tubulointerstitial injury score was significantly reduced after S100A1 knockdown (Figure 7E,F). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that KIM‐1 expression was markedly increased in diabetic kidneys, supporting the presence of proximal tubular epithelial cell injury in STZ‐induced diabetic kidneys (Figure 7G). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that STZ treatment increased the protein levels of KIM‐1, IL‐1β, IL‐6, collagen I, fibronectin, and ID1, whereas AAV9‐shS100a1 treatment markedly reduced their expression in diabetic kidneys (Figure 7H). In addition, F4/80 immunofluorescence staining showed increased macrophage infiltration in STZ‐treated kidneys, which was substantially attenuated by S100A1 knockdown (Figure 7I). Collectively, these findings indicate that proximal tubule‐targeted knockdown of S100A1 attenuates STZ‐induced diabetic kidney injury by suppressing tubular injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, fibrosis, and ID1 expression.

Discussion

In recent years, single‐cell transcriptomic analysis has provided important insights into cell‐type‐specific molecular changes in DKD [22, 23]. Through the analysis of our previous scRNA‐sequencing data [16], we identified that S100A1 was upregulated in proximal tubular epithelial cells from DKD patients. Consistent with this finding, analysis of the KIT public database showed increased S100A1 expression in DKD PTECs, suggesting that S100A1 may be involved in proximal tubular injury during DKD progression. Based on these observations, we further investigated the functional role and molecular mechanism of S100A1 in diabetic tubular injury. Our results demonstrated that S100A1 might contribute to proximal tubular injury by inducing inflammatory activation and fibrotic responses. Mechanistically, S100A1 enhanced the interaction between MDM2 and KLF15, thereby promoting K48‐linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of KLF15. This process relieved the transcriptional repression of ID1 by KLF15, leading to increased ID1 expression and contributing to proximal tubular epithelial cell injury and DKD progression. In vivo, proximal tubule‐targeted knockdown of S100A1 markedly attenuated HFD/STZ‐induced diabetic kidney injury (Figure 8).

Schematic illustration of the role of S100A1 in diabetic proximal tubular injury. High glucose induces S100A1 upregulation in proximal tubular epithelial cells. Increased S100A1 enhances the interaction between MDM2 and KLF15, promoting MDM2‐mediated K48‐linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of KLF15. Reduced KLF15 stability relieves KLF15‐dependent transcriptional repression of ID1, leading to ID1 upregulation. Increased ID1 expression promotes inflammatory activation and profibrotic responses, thereby contributing to proximal tubular epithelial cell injury and DKD progression.

Although glomerular injury and albuminuria have long been considered central pathological features of DKD, emerging studies have reported that renal tubular injury is a critical contributor to the development and progression of DKD [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Evidence indicates that PTECs are highly susceptible to hyperglycemic stress and actively participate in renal inflammation, oxidative stress, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis [9, 24, 29, 30]. Therefore, identifying dysregulated molecules in PTECs may help clarify the mechanisms linking diabetic metabolic stress to tubulointerstitial injury. S100A1 is a member of the S100 calcium‐binding protein family and was initially characterized as an intracellular Ca2+‐binding protein that regulates cellular function through Ca2+‐dependent interactions with multiple downstream effector proteins [31, 32]. Although previous studies have mainly focused on its role in cardiac calcium handling and myocardial contractility, several reports suggest that S100A1 may also participate in stress‐related inflammatory responses. Yu et al. [33] reported that S100A1 regulated hypoxia‐induced inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in H9c2 cardiomyocytes through the TLR4/ROS/NF‐κB pathway. Bai et al. [15] further showed that S100A1 overexpression aggravated LPS‐induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, whereas S100A1 silencing attenuated these effects. In addition, other S100 family members have been implicated in renal tubular inflammation and fibrosis [34, 35]; for example, S100A8/A9 was reported to be increased in renal tubular epithelial cells in diabetic nephropathy and to promote renal interstitial fibrosis through activation of the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway [36]. However, the role of S100A1 in DKD remains poorly understood. In the present study, we observed that S100A1 was increased in DKD PTECs and functionally promoted proximal tubular epithelial injury, inflammatory cytokine expression, and fibrotic responses, whereas S100A1 knockdown exerted protective effects both in vitro and in vivo. These results extend the pathological relevance of S100 family proteins to S100A1 in diabetic tubular injury and suggest that S100A1 may serve as an important PTEC‐associated mediator of inflammation and fibrotic remodeling in DKD. Notably, all in vitro mechanistic experiments in this study were conducted using the immortalized human proximal tubular epithelial cell line HK‐2. Although HK‐2 cells are a classic and widely used cell model for exploring diabetic renal tubular injury, it cannot fully mimic the phenotypic and functional features of primary renal tubular epithelial cells [37], nor can it reproduce the complex cellular crosstalk and tissue microenvironment of the kidney in vivo [38, 39]. This may restrict the physiological relevance of our in vitro mechanistic conclusions, and further validation in primary cells is warranted in future research. In addition, our review of multiple single‐cell datasets revealed that S100A1 is not exclusively expressed in proximal tubular epithelial cells, but is also present in other cell types, such as podocytes, and endothelial cells. Thus, we cannot exclude the potential role of S100A1 in these additional cell types. In future studies, we aim to further investigate the function of S100A1 across different cell types. Additionally, all in vivo experiments in this study were performed exclusively in male C57BL/6J mice. This design was primarily based on previous evidence that male C57BL/6 mice exhibit higher susceptibility to streptozotocin‐induced diabetic kidney injury and more stable renal pathological phenotypes, which helps reduce intragroup variation and ensure the robustness of modeling [17, 40]. Nevertheless, this study cannot address potential sex‐dependent differences in the S100A1‐MDM2‐KLF15‐ID1 regulatory axis. Accumulating studies have increasingly recognized sex as a critical biological variable that affects the pathogenesis progression and therapeutic response of DKD, and sexual dimorphism in renal injury has been widely documented in both diabetic patients and animal models [41, 42, 43, 44]. Therefore, future studies incorporating female animal models are warranted to systematically compare the sex‐specific effects of this regulatory axis, so as to further improve the generalizability and clinical translational value of our findings. Furthermore, although the 20‐week observational endpoint after STZ injection is a reasonable and well‐established DKD model, this model primarily recapitulates the early‐to‐moderate stage of human DKD, and cannot fully replicate the severe progressive fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis and renal function decline observed in advanced human DKD. Besides, AAV‐mediated S100A1 knockdown was administered at the early phase of diabetes modeling, which demonstrates that S100A1 blockade can prevent or attenuate the development of tubular injury. However, whether S100A1 inhibition can reverse already established tubular fibrosis and inflammation at later disease stages remains to be elucidated in future studies.

To further identify the downstream effectors responsible for S100A1‐induced proximal tubular epithelial cell injury, we performed transcriptomic analysis of control and S100A1 overexpressing HK‐2 cells. Using this strategy, we identified ID1 as a markedly upregulated gene in S100A1 overexpressing HK‐2 cells. ID1 belongs to the inhibitor of DNA binding family and functions as a helix–loop–helix transcriptional regulator [18, 45]. Li et al. [46] first reported that ID1 was rapidly induced in human proximal tubular epithelial cells after TGF‐β1 stimulation and mediated tubular epithelial dedifferentiation by suppressing E‐cadherin and ZO‐1 expression. The same group later showed that ID1 was selectively induced in injured renal tubular epithelial cells and promoted peritubular inflammation and renal fibrogenesis [47]. Based on these findings and our transcriptomic results, we focused on ID1 as a potential downstream effector of S100A1 in diabetic proximal tubular injury. In our study, ID1 expression was increased after S100A1 overexpression, consistent with our transcriptomic data. Subsequently, we determined whether ID1 mediates renal tubular injury, and we observed that ID1 knockdown decreased the levels of inflammatory cytokines and fibrosis‐related markers in HK‐2 cells, consistent with previous studies [47]. Functional rescue experiments showed that ID1 silencing significantly attenuated S100A1‐induced inflammatory activation and fibrotic responses, alleviating HK‐2 cell injury. These results indicate that ID1 is not simply a differentially expressed gene induced by S100A1, but a functional downstream effector that mediates the damaging effects of S100A1 in tubular epithelial cells. Thus, the S100A1‐induced increase in ID1 may represent an important molecular event linking diabetic stress to tubular epithelial injury and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Although ID1 was identified from transcriptomic analysis and functionally validated as an important downstream effector of S100A1, S100A1 may also regulate other target genes involved in diabetic proximal tubular injury. Therefore, the S100A1‐ID1 axis identified in this study should be interpreted as a key regulatory pathway rather than the only mechanism by which S100A1 promotes tubular epithelial injury, inflammatory activation, and profibrotic responses. Future studies are needed to determine whether additional S100A1‐regulated genes also contribute to DKD‐associated tubular injury.

Because ID1 was identified at the transcriptomic level and its mRNA expression was increased after S100A1 overexpression, we next asked whether transcriptional regulation might contribute to S100A1‐induced ID1 upregulation. S100A1 does not possess intrinsic transcriptional regulatory activity and mainly exerts its biological functions through protein–protein interactions, suggesting that it may regulate ID1 indirectly through transcriptional regulators. Therefore, we used the JASPAR database to predict potential transcription factors that could bind to the ID1 promoter and identified KLF15 as the highest‐ranked candidate. KLF15 is an important transcription factor involved in metabolic homeostasis, inflammatory regulation, and tissue remodeling [48, 49]. Previous studies showed that loss of KLF15 in renal stromal cells aggravated kidney fibrosis [50], while proximal tubule‐specific deletion of KLF15 exacerbated tubular injury and subsequent fibrotic remodeling [51]. In addition, KLF15 has been reported to function as a transcriptional repressor by directly binding to gene promoter regions [52, 53, 54]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that KLF15 suppressed ID1 promoter activity and reduced ID1 expression, indicating that KLF15 acts as an upstream transcriptional repressor of ID1 in tubular epithelial cells. Importantly, S100A1 did not significantly alter KLF15 mRNA expression but markedly reduced KLF15 protein abundance, suggesting that S100A1 regulates KLF15 mainly at the post‐translational level. Cycloheximide chase and MG132 rescue experiments further indicated that S100A1 decreased KLF15 protein stability through a proteasome‐dependent mechanism.

Protein stability controlled by the ubiquitin‐proteasome system is an important mechanism for regulating transcription factor activity. To identify a potential E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in S100A1‐mediated KLF15 degradation, we performed database screening and selected MDM2 for further validation. MDM2 is a well‐characterized E3 ubiquitin ligase that has been implicated in the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of multiple substrates [55, 56]. Fu et al. [57] reported that MDM2 can interact with FOXO1 and FOXO3A, respectively, and promote the ubiquitination and degradation of these FOXO transcription factors in a manner dependent on its E3 ligase activity. In another study, Chi et al. [58] showed that MDM2 directly interacts with the transcription factor RUNX3 and promotes its ubiquitination, nuclear export, and proteasomal degradation. These findings indicate that MDM2 can regulate transcription factor stability through substrate recognition and ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis. In our study, we found that S100A1 enhanced the interaction between MDM2 and KLF15, thereby promoting MDM2‐mediated K48‐linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of KLF15. The resulting reduction in KLF15 stability relieved KLF15‐dependent transcriptional repression of ID1 and increased ID1 expression. Although our data support the involvement of MDM2 in S100A1 induced KLF15 ubiquitination and degradation, MDM2 may not be the only E3 ubiquitin ligase regulating KLF15 protein stability under diabetic conditions. Future studies using systematic E3 ligase screening and mutational analysis of KLF15 ubiquitination residues would help further define the complete ubiquitination mechanism controlling KLF15 stability.

In summary, our study reveals a previously unrecognized role of S100A1 in diabetic tubular injury. S100A1 upregulation in DKD PTECs contributes to tubular epithelial injury, inflammatory activation, and fibrosis by disrupting KLF15‐dependent repression of ID1 through MDM2‐mediated regulation of KLF15 stability. These findings expand the current understanding of tubular cell injury in DKD and suggest that targeting S100A1 may provide a potential strategy for limiting tubulointerstitial damage in diabetic kidney disease.
