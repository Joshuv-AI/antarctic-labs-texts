Transmembrane glycoprotein BSG serves a dual role as a prognostic and immunological modulator in the tumor microenvironment of lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xiaofei Zhang, Qiliang Peng, Wenlang Zhu, Jianguo Zhang, Zhengfei Zhu, Kuaile Zhao, Peiyi Liu, Weixin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•BSG is significantly upregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissues.•High BSG expression is an independent prognostic factor for poor survival in LUAD.•BSG expression negatively correlates with the infiltration of key anti-tumor immune cells.•BSG is closely associated with immune checkpoint molecules and chemokine networks.•Experimental validation confirms BSG's protein expression and prognostic relevance in LUAD.Keywords: BSG, Prognosis, Lung adenocarcinoma, Tumor microenvironment, Biomarker

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally, characterized by extraordinarily high morbidity and mortality. For decades, it has remained a leading contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide, with 75–80% of cases classified as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common pathological subtype of NSCLC, further stands as the predominant pathological type across all lung cancer cases [2]. Regrettably, LUAD is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor prognosis. Even with recent advancements in therapeutic technologies, its overall survival rate remains disappointingly low [3]. Despite ongoing efforts to advance individualized and precise treatment strategies, the lack of effective tools to identify patients at highest risk persists—largely due to the marked heterogeneity of LUAD [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore more robust approaches to deepen our understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving LUAD and optimize individualized, precise treatment.

Basigin (BSG), also known as CD147, is a key transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily [5]. Initially identified as a potent inducer of extracellular matrix metalloproteinases, it is also referred to as EMMPRIN [6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that BSG actively participates in the development and growth of physiological tissues, while playing a critical role in reactive processes such as inflammation, immunity, and tissue repair [7]. Accumulating evidence highlighting the involvement of BSG expression in tumor progression and prognosis has established it as a relevant tumor biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, while also validating its potential as a promising therapeutic target in oncology [8]. Additionally, BSG functions as a key mediator of inflammatory and immune responses, suggesting it may offer therapeutic opportunities for immune-related diseases and tumor immunotherapy [9]. However, the immunological role, expression profile, and prognostic value of BSG in LUAD remain incompletely understood, warranting further investigation.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a comprehensive study to systematically elucidate the associations of BSG expression with clinical relevance and tumor immunology in LUAD. First, we analyzed the expression patterns of BSG in normal tissues, cancer cells, and cancer tissues by integrating data from multiple databases. Subsequently, we evaluated the clinical relevance, diagnostic efficacy, and prognostic significance of BSG expression in LUAD. Furthermore, we performed network analysis and functional enrichment analysis of BSG to further uncover the potential mechanisms underlying LUAD progression. Notably, we examined the immune landscape linked to BSG expression in LUAD, with a specific focus on correlations between BSG expression and immune cell infiltration levels, copy number variations, immune microenvironment scores, immune checkpoint-related genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes. Finally, to verify the expression pattern of BSG in LUAD and its potential clinical prognostic value, we performed validation experiments at the cellular and tissue levels.

Materials and methods

Sample information

Gene expression matrices and corresponding clinical data for various tumor and normal tissues were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases [10,11]. BSG expression data across different tumor cell lines were acquired from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database [12]. The GSE31210 dataset, downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, was used as an independent validation cohort [13]. The GSE31210 dataset retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database was adopted as the independent validation cohort. This cohort included 226 lung cancer patients with complete clinical and follow-up data. In terms of tumor stage, most cases were classified as stage I–II, while a small proportion belonged to stage III. The median follow-up time of all enrolled participants was 54 months, which guaranteed sufficient follow-up duration for prognostic assessment. Data on immune-infiltrating cells were extracted from the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) database [14]. For subsequent analyses, RNA-seq data originally formatted as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) were converted to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) and used for statistical analysis. LUAD samples were stratified into low BSG expression and high BSG expression groups based on BSG mRNA expression levels. Specifically, outliers were defined as values lying beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) above the third quartile or below the first quartile. These outliers were retained in the plots and included in all statistical analyses.

The abbreviations of tumor names and their corresponding meanings are listed below: adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), brain lower grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), LUAD, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), mesothelioma (MESO), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), sarcoma (SARC), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), thymoma (THYM), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), and uveal melanoma (UVM).

Expression analysis of BSG

Differences in BSG mRNA expression between tumor and normal tissues were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with data integrated from TCGA and GTEx databases. BSG expression levels across various tumor cell lines were analyzed based on CCLE data. Additionally, the distribution and subcellular localization of BSG protein were evaluated using immunofluorescence staining data from the HPA database. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, and the "ggplot2″ package was used for visualizing the results.

Association analysis of BSG with clinical characteristics

Correlations between BSG expression and clinical characteristics of LUAD (from TCGA) were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The clinical characteristics included T classification, N classification, M classification, clinical stage, gender, age at diagnosis, and smoking status. Boxplots generated with the R package "ggplot2″ were used for visualization.

Diagnostic analysis of BSG

The diagnostic efficacy of BSG for LUAD was evaluated using RNA-seq data from TCGA. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the "pROC" and "ggplot2" packages in R, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify diagnostic performance [15]. Consistent with conventional standards, an AUC value > 0.7 was considered indicative of relatively high diagnostic power.

Prognostic analysis of BSG

The prognostic significance of BSG for overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) across multiple cancers was evaluated via Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis, with results presented as forest plots. Log-rank P-values and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. The R package "survival" was used for statistical analysis, and "survminer" was used for visualization. Furthermore, the GSE31210 cohort was used as an independent validation set to analyze the prognostic value of BSG expression for LUAD OS and relapse-free survival (RFS). To determine whether BSG is an independent prognostic factor for LUAD, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed for BSG expression and other clinical variables using the "survival" package. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. Finally, a nomogram for predicting LUAD prognosis was constructed using the R package "RMS" [16], and calibration curves were generated to compare the predicted probabilities with the actual probabilities of 1-year, 3-year and 5-year OS.

Network and functional enrichment analysis of BSG

To explore the potential functions of BSG and the underlying mechanisms driving LUAD progression, network and functional enrichment analyses were performed. The STRING database was used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, and the GeneMANIA database was employed to establish a gene-gene interaction network [17,18]. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, were conducted using the R package "clusterProfiler" [19,20]. GO terms were categorized into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). A sub-network was constructed using the most significant module molecules from the STRING PPI network. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the expression correlations between BSG and other molecules in the sub-network, with results visualized via heatmaps and scatter plots. Additionally, survival maps were generated to illustrate the associations between the expression of BSG-interacting proteins and LUAD survival.

Differential expression analysis of BSG in LUAD

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with BSG in the TCGA-LUAD cohort were identified using the LinkFinder module of the LinkedOmics database [21]. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the correlations of these DEGs with BSG, and the top 50 most significantly correlated genes were visualized as heatmaps and volcano plots. Survival maps of OS and disease-free survival (DFS) were also generated to reveal the associations between these DEGs and LUAD survival. Furthermore, GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were performed for these DEGs using the R package "clusterProfiler".

Association analysis of BSG with immune microenvironment

To clarify the relationship between BSG and immune cells in the LUAD tumor microenvironment(TME), multiple algorithms (CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, MCPCOUNTER, QUANTSEQ, TIDE, TIMER, and XCELL) integrated in the TIMER database were used to quantify immune cell infiltration. For further exploration, the correlation between BSG expression and the infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types in LUAD was evaluated using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) method via the R package "GSVA" [22], with results displayed as lollipop charts and scatter plots. Additionally, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare immune cell infiltration levels between the low and high BSG expression groups, and boxplots were used for visualization. The TIMER database was also used to assess the correlation between BSG copy number variations (CNVs) and the abundance of six immune cell types (B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells). Moreover, the relationships between BSG expression and immune microenvironment scores (stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score) were explored using the R package "estimate", with results presented as heatmaps [23].

Association analysis of BSG with immune-related genes

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to explore the correlations between BSG expression and immune checkpoint proteins (including immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive genes) in LUAD, with results visualized via heatmaps and radar maps. Additionally, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations of BSG with chemokines, chemokine receptors, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) genes in LUAD, and the results were also presented as heatmaps and radar maps. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Cell immunohistochemistry(IHC) staining

The human LAD cell line A549 was routinely cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂. When the cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, they were seeded at an appropriate density into a 24-well plate pre-placed with cell slides. Subsequent experiments were performed after the cells adhered and reached 70–80% confluence. After gentle rinsing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min (only required for detecting nuclear antigens). After blocking with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min, primary antibody working solutions were applied, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, the EnVision™ FLEX+ HRP-labeled secondary antibody (Dako, K8002) was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. DAB was used for chromogenic development, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Finally, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Stained sections were observed under an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100), and images were captured using a digital imaging system. Protein expression localization was evaluated by two independent observers using a double-blind method, and its distribution in the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus was recorded. At least five random fields per slide were selected for statistical analysis. Each batch of experiments included negative controls (PBS or isotype IgG instead of the primary antibody) and positive controls (tissue sections with known positive expression) to ensure staining specificity and experimental consistency.

IHC detection of BSG in LUAD tissues

IHC was performed to determine the expression level and subcellular localization of BSG protein in LUAD tissues, with all procedures strictly following standardized operating protocols. A total of 90 paired LUAD tissues and corresponding adjacent normal lung tissues, all pathologically confirmed, were assembled into a tissue microarray (HLugA180Su13) for high-throughput IHC analysis. A monoclonal antibody against BSG was used, and its optimal working concentration was determined as 1:3000 via preliminary gradient dilution tests; antigen retrieval was implemented using the EDTA-based method, followed by incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. For signal detection, the EnVision™ FLEX+ Detection System (Dako) was applied as the secondary antibody reagent, combined with a background reducing agent (Dako) to minimize non-specific staining. The experiment was conducted using key instruments including the Autostainer Link 48 automated IHC staining system (Dako), PT Link automated IHC pretreatment system (Dako), LEICA ST5020 automated dewaxing machine, Aperio XT whole-slide scanner (LEICA), and constant-temperature drying oven. Tissue sections were baked at 63 °C for 1 h, dewaxed in two xylene baths (15 min each) and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, then subjected to EDTA-mediated antigen retrieval and natural cooling for over 10 min. After PBS rinsing, sections were incubated with the primary antibody solution at 4 °C overnight, rewarmed at room temperature for 45 min, and processed for secondary antibody binding and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) color development on an automated stainer, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining for 1 min, acidified ethanol differentiation, tap water bluing, and mounting with neutral resin after air-drying. Staining results were independently evaluated by two senior pathologists using a double-blind method; for each tissue spot, tissue type and pathological subtype were recorded, with semi-quantitative scoring performed for staining intensity (0 = negative, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong) and positive cell percentage (0–100%) in the cytoplasm, cell membrane, and nucleus, respectively. Non-specific staining signals in macrophages and stromal cells were excluded, and specimens with insufficient tissue or ambiguous structure were labeled as unevaluable and excluded from subsequent analysis. An additional orientation spot was included only to facilitate array layout identification and was not used for quantitative analysis. Therefore, the final statistical analyses were based on IHC scoring data from 180 valid tissue spots, with all samples subjected to standardized processing to ensure result comparability and reliability. The research was conducted in accordance with approved protocols and guidelines, with approval number 050,432-4–2108.

Cellular validation experiments

Human normal bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells, A549 cells and human embryonic kidney 293T cells were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum, penicillin and streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments. siRNAs targeting BSG, negative control siRNA and BSG overexpression plasmids were constructed. Cell transfection was performed with Lipofectamine 3000, and cells were cultured for 48 h after transfection. qRT-PCR was used to detect the mRNA expression of BSG and screen out the most efficient siRNAs.

A commercial kit was applied to separate cytoplasmic, membrane and nuclear protein fractions. Western blot was conducted to detect the expression and subcellular localization of BSG protein, and band intensity was quantified by grayscale analysis. Colony formation assay was used to evaluate cell proliferation ability. Transwell assays were performed to examine cell migration and invasion; Matrigel was pre-coated in the upper chamber for invasion assay.

All experiments were repeated three times. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Independent t-test was used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA combined with Tukey’s test was applied for multiple-group comparisons. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The expression characteristics of BSG

We first analyzed physiologic BSG gene expression levels across normal tissues, using the GTEx database. As plotted in Fig.1A, BSG has different expression levels in different tissues with highest level in testis and lowest level in liver. We then explored the expression levels across different cell lines from CCLE database. The results indicated that the BSG expression levels varied across different cell lines with highest expressed level in LGG cell lines (Fig.1B). Furthermore, we assessed BSG mRNA expression levels in TCGA tumors and adjacent normal tissues. Our research exhibited that the mRNA expression levels of BSG were increased in 14 of the 33 cancers compared with normal tissues, including BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, ESCA, HNSC, KICH, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PRAD, STAD, THCA, and UCEC, while the expression of BSG significantly decreased in COAD and READ (Fig.1C). In addition, by integrating TCGA and GTEx databases, the results revealed that BSG was significantly upregulated in 25 of the 33 cancers including ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PAAD, PRAD, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THCA, THYM, and UCEC, compared with those in normal tissues (Fig.1D). By contrast, BSG expression was significantly downregulated in LAML than in normal tissues.

Correlation of BSG expression with clinicopathological parameters in LUAD

Afterwards, the associations between BSG expression and the clinicopathological parameters were evaluate by employing the TCGA-LUAD data. The expression of BSG was significantly higher in LUAD samples than in normal lung tissues (P < 0.01) (Fig.2A). We also found that BSG expression was strongly higher in LUAD samples than in paired adjacent normal tissues (P < 0.01) (Fig.2B). Moreover, The Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test revealed that the expression of BSG was strongly correlated with the T stage (T2 vs. T1 and T4 vs. T1, Fig.2C), N stage (N2 vs. N0, Fig.2D), and pathologic stage (Stage III vs. Stage I, Fig.2F). However, no significant association was found between BSG expression with M stage (Fig.2E), gender (Fig.2G), age (Fig.2H), and smoking status (Fig.2I). In conclusion, BSG is upregulated in LUAD and may play a key role in the carcinogenesis of LUAD.

Diagnostic power of BSG expression in LUAD

Diagnostic study was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic value of BSG for cancer diagnosis. The results showed that BSG had moderate performances for the detection of multiple cancers according to AUC values in ROC curves, including ACC (AUC = 0.916), BLCA (AUC = 0.674), BRCA (AUC = 0.823), COAD (AUC = 0.844), DLBC (AUC = 0.795), GBM (AUC = 0.864), HNSC (AUC = 0.882), KICH (AUC = 0.962), KIRC (AUC = 0.519), KIRP (AUC = 0.772), LAML (AUC = 0.958), LIHC (AUC = 0.960), LUAD (AUC = 0.624), LUSC (AUC = 0.682), PAAD (AUC = 0.986), PRAD (AUC = 0.721), READ (AUC = 0.671), STAD (AUC = 0.789), THCA (AUC = 0.689), and UCEC (AUC = 0.801) (Fig.3). BSG showed relatively high diagnostic performance in several cancer types; however, its diagnostic value in LUAD was moderate, with an AUC of 0.624. Therefore, BSG may be more suitable as a complementary biomarker rather than a standalone diagnostic marker for LUAD.

Prognostic value of BSG expression in LUAD

To further evaluate the prognostic potential of BSG in cancers, we performed a survival association analysis, including OS, DSS, and PFI, by the Kaplan–Meier method and univariate Cox regression. For the results of univariate Cox regression analyses, the OS results indicated that BSG was a risk factor for poor prognosis of LGG, LIHC, LUAD, and SKCM, while BSG may have protective effects in the patients with KIRP and UCEC (Fig.4A). Moreover, upregulated BSG expression was also remarkably associated with poor DSS in LGG, LIHC, LUAD, and SKCM, while high BSG expression was significantly related to good DSS in KIRP and UCEC (Fig.4B). In addition, overexpressed BSG was related to decreased PFI in HNSC, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, and UCS while overexpressed BSG was related to prolonged PFI in UCEC (Fig.4C). Notably, some cancer types, such as KICH and UCS, have relatively small sample sizes; therefore, conclusions drawn from these small-sample analyses should be interpreted with caution. Despite this, the overall patterns of BSG’s prognostic associations remain consistent and informative across larger cohorts.

According to the univariate analysis, the variables of T stage, N stage, M stage, pathologic stage, and BSG expression were significantly related to the OS of LUAD patients (P < 0.05). When integrating these variables into multivariate Cox regression analysis, T stage, N stage, M stage, and BSG expression remained as independent prognostic factors for OS in LUAD patients (P < 0.05).

Network and functional enrichment analyses of BSG in LUAD

To study the molecular mechanism of BSG in tumorigenesis and development, we filtered out the known BSG-interacting proteins and the BSG expression correlated genes for functional enrichment analyses. As shown in Fig-sup.1, a PPI network involved in BSG-interacting proteins based on STRING database was constructed. The GO results displayed that BSG-interacting proteins had significant regulation on leukocyte migration, carboxylic acid transmembrane transport for the BP level, basolateral plasma membrane, basal plasma membrane for the CC level, and monocarboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity, carboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity for the MF level (Fig-sup.1C). In addition, KEGG pathway analysis showed that BSG-interacting proteins were significantly enriched in pathways including IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and non-small cell lung cancer (Fig-sup.1D).

Furthermore, a gene-gene network associating BSG related genes was set up based on Genemania database (Fig-sup.1B). GO annotations demonstrated that these genes were significantly involved in carboxylic acid transmembrane transport, organic acid transmembrane transport at the BP level, highly associated with basolateral plasma membrane, basal plasma membrane at the CC level, and mainly enriched in carboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity, organic acid transmembrane transporter activity at the MF level (Fig-sup.1E). Based on KEGG pathway analysis, BSG related genes were significantly enhanced for ferroptosis, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, cAMP signaling pathway, and central carbon metabolism in cancer (Fig-sup.1F).

Sub-network and significant BSG-interacting proteins in LUAD

Afterwards, a sub-network was established with the most significant module molecules in the STRING PPI network including BSG, SLC3A2, SLC16A3, SLC16A1, SDC1, PPIB, PPIA, MMP1, ITGB1, CD44, and CAV1 (Fig-sup.2A). Heatmap confirmed that these genes interacted significantly LUAD (Fig-sup.2B). In LUAD, we found that the expression of BSG had a significant relationship with CAV1 (R = −0.11, P = 0.013, Fig-sup.2C), CD44 (R = −0.13, P = 0.0049, Fig-sup.2D), ITGB1 (R = 0.12, P = 0.011, Fig-sup.2E), PPIA (R = 0.19, P = 3.4e-05, Fig-sup.2G), PPIB (R = 0.3, P = 2.4e-11, Fig-sup.2H), SDC1 (R = 0.13, P = 0.0058, Fig-sup.2I), SLC3A2 (R = 0.12, P = 0.01, Fig-sup.2J), and SLC3A3 (R = 0.23, P = 3.6e-07, Fig-sup.2L), while the BSG mRNA expression did not correlate with MMP1 (R = −0.018, P = 0.7, Fig-sup.2F), SLC16A1 (R = −0.059, P = 0.19, Fig-sup.2K). We then assessed the associations between these genes and survival outcome in LUAD. The survival maps of OS and DFS suggested that these genes also had predictive value for survival of LUAD patients (Fig-sup.2M).

Correlation between gene differential expression and BSG in LUAD

NUsing the online LinkedOmics database, the differentially expressed genes associated with BSG in TCGA-LUAD cohort were screened. The top 50 genes significantly positively or negatively correlated with BSG were shown in the volcano (Fig-sup.3A) and heatmap plot (Fig-sup.3B-C). Afterwards, the prognostic value of the correlated genes in LUAD regarding OS and DFS was evaluated. Survival maps of the top 50 genes positively and negatively associated with BSG in LUAD were plotted in Fig-sup.3D (OS) and Fig-sup.3E (DFS). It is worth noting that the correlated genes were also highly related to the survival outcomes of LUAD patients, suggesting they may play a synergistic role with BSG in LUAD. Furthermore, functional enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the correlated genes involved in LUAD. GO functional annotations demonstrated that these genes were involved in key molecules and activity of key molecules including protein serine/threonine kinase activity, protein serine kinase activity, mitochondrial protein-containing complex and oxidoreductase complex (Fig-sup.3F). In addition, KEGG enrichment revealed that these genes were significantly associated with a series of important pathways, such as non-small cell lung cancer, oxidative phosphorylation, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, B cell receptor signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig-sup.3G).

Correlation of BSG expression with immune infiltration level in LUAD

Next, we assessed the associations between BSG expression and 46 tumor-infiltrating immune cells in LUAD based on different algorithms. The results revealed that elevated BSG maintained a significant negative correlation with most of the tumor-infiltrating immune cells in LUAD (P < 0.05, Fig-sup.4A).

Based on ssGSEA method, we further evaluated the relationship of BSG mRNA expression with immune infiltration level in LUAD. As shown in Fig-sup.4B, the associations between the infiltration of 24 immune cells and BSG mRNA expression were plotted by lollipop chart based on Spearman correlation coefficients and P-values. The results suggested that BSG mRNA expression significantly positively correlated with the infiltration of NK CD56bright cells, NK cells, and NK CD56dim cells, while BSG mRNA expression significantly negatively correlated with the infiltration of T helper cells, Tcm, B cells, T cells, Tem, macrophages, Th1 cells, and cytotoxic cells.

In addition, we also compared the immune infiltration levels of immune cells between different BSG expression groups to determine whether the tumor immune microenvironment is different between high- and low-BSG expression groups in LUAD. The results indicated that these immune cells were quite different between BSG high or low tumors in LUAD including T cells, B cells, eosinophils, macrophages, T helper cells, Tcm, Tem, Th1 cells, and Th2 cells (Fig-sup.4C).

A heatmap was plotted to further reveal the correlation of BSG expression level with immune cells in LUAD (Fig-sup.4D).

Correlation of BSG expression with CNV and immune microenvironment scores in LUAD

The TIMER tool was employed to link the genetic CNV of BSG with the relative abundance of tumor infiltrating cells in LUAD, including the variations in deep deletion, arm-level deletion, diploid/normal, arm-level gain, and high amplification. It was found that BSG CNV has a strong correlation with the infiltration levels of B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in LUAD (Fig-sup.5A).

Afterwards, relationships between BSG expression and immune microenvironment scores including stromal score, immune score, and estimate score were explored with the “estimate” method. As displayed by the heatmap in Fig-sup.5B, BSG expression appeared negatively associated with all of the stromal, immune, and estimate scores in almost all cancer types (P < 0.05). In LUAD, we found that the expression of BSG had a negative relationship with stromal score (R = −0.190, P < 0.001, Fig-sup.5C), immune score (R = −0.193, P < 0.001, Fig-sup.5D), and estimate score (R = −0.210, P < 0.001, Fig-sup.5E).

Correlation of BSG expression with immune checkpoint proteins in LUAD

To explore the potential of BSG in immunotherapy, we evaluated the correlations between BSG expression and immune checkpoint proteins including immunosuppressive genes and immunostimulating genes. Notably, we observed by the heatmaps that the expression of BSG negatively correlated with most immunosuppressive and immunostimulating genes across most tumor types except LIHC and PCPG (Fig-sup.6A-B). Moreover, the correlations between BSG expression and immune checkpoint related genes in LUAD were displayed by the radar charts (Fig-sup.6C-D). The results revealed that most immune checkpoint proteins were negatively correlated with BSG (P < 0.05).

Correlation of BSG expression with chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes in LUAD

Furthermore, gene co-expression analysis was performed to assess the association between BSG expression and chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes. The resulting heatmaps indicated that most chemokines and chemokine receptors were co-expressed with BSG (Fig-sup.7A-B) and the majority were negatively correlated with BSG across most tumor types, except DLBC, LIHC, and PCPG (P < 0.05). In addition, BSG expression was positively or negatively correlated with most MHC genes in most cancer types (Fig-sup.7C). In LUAD, the resulting radar charts suggested that BSG expression negatively correlated with most chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes (P < 0.05, Fig-sup.7 d-F).

Cellular and tissue validation of BSG expression and prognostic value in LUAD

To verify the expression pattern of BSG in LUAD and its potential clinical prognostic value, we performed validation experiments at the cellular and tissue levels. Fig. 6 characterizes BSG expression at the cellular and tissue levels, quantifies its differential expression between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues, and evaluates its prognostic significance in LUAD, along with COX regression analyses of clinicopathological factors.

. Cellular and tissue validation of BSG expression and prognostic value in LUAD. (A-D) Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results of BSG in A549 cells. (E) Tissue microarray constructed using patient-derived clinical tissues. (F-K) Representative IHC images of BSG in tumor tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues from three patients. (L) Statistical analysis of BSG expression differences between LUAD tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues. (M) Correlation analysis between BSG expression levels and the overall prognosis of patients with LUAD. (N—O) Univariate (N) and multivariate (O) COX regression analyses of BSG expression.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results of A549 cells showed that the expression localization of BSG was cytoplasmic positive and membranous positive, with nuclear positivity observed in a subset of cells (Fig. 6A–D). A tissue microarray was constructed using patient-derived clinical tissues (Fig. 6E) for high-throughput analysis. Representative IHC images of paired tumor/adjacent non-tumor tissues from three patients (Fig. 6F–K) revealed distinct BSG staining intensities between the two tissue types. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6L) validated significantly dysregulated BSG expression in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (P < 0.05).

To validate the prognostic significance of BSG in LUAD, we first analyzed patient survival using Kaplan–Meier curves. Patients with high BSG expression exhibited significantly worse overall survival compared to those with low expression (Fig. 6M), consistent with our bioinformatics findings. To further quantify these associations, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 6N), which revealed that high BSG expression (HR = 1.944, 95% CI: 1.096–3.448, P = 0.0231), larger tumor size, and advanced TNM stage were significantly associated with poorer overall survival, whereas sex, age, tumor grade, and lymph node metastasis were not significant. Building on these results, multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 6O) showed that tumor size remained a strong independent prognostic factor, while BSG expression demonstrated a marginal trend toward poor prognosis (HR = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.95–3.03, P = 0.077). TNM stage lost significance in the multivariate model. Overall, these findings suggest that BSG may contribute to LUAD progression and patient outcomes, with tumor size as the most robust independent predictor and BSG providing additional prognostic insight.

Expression and subcellular localization of BSG in BEAS-2b and A549 cells

We characterized the expression and subcellular localization of BSG in human normal bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells and A549 cells. qRT-PCR was performed to measure the relative mRNA abundance of BSG, and the results demonstrated that BSG mRNA expression was markedly higher in A549 cells than in BEAS-2B cells. Membrane, cytoplasmic, nuclear fractions and whole-cell lysates were separated by differential centrifugation, and Western blot was used to detect BSG protein expression across distinct subcellular compartments. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, histone H3 and GAPDH served as specific internal controls for the cell membrane, nucleus, and cytoplasm/whole cell, respectively. Immunoblotting results verified that BSG predominantly localized to the cell membrane and cytoplasm, with low and equivalent nuclear BSG expression observed in both cell lines. Densitometric quantification of BSG bands from each fraction was conducted and normalized to the corresponding reference proteins. Quantitative analysis of membrane and cytoplasmic fractions both revealed significantly elevated BSG protein levels in A549 cells relative to BEAS-2B cells (***p < 0.001). Nuclear fraction quantification showed extremely low BSG protein abundance without statistically significant differences between the two cell lines (ns). Densitometric measurement of total BSG protein in whole-cell lysates further confirmed substantially higher total BSG expression in A549 cells (***p < 0.001). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, See Figure Supplement 8.

BSG modulates proliferation, migration and invasion of A549 cells

Figure Supplement 9 illustrates the regulatory role of BSG in the proliferation, migration and invasion of A549 cells. Firstly, four siRNAs targeting BSG were transfected into 293T cells, and qRT-PCR analysis after 48 h of culture demonstrated that BSG siRNA-1 and siRNA-2 possessed the highest knockdown efficiency (***p < 0.001 versus the NC siRNA group). We further validated their interfering effects in A549 cells, and both siRNAs were confirmed to dramatically downregulate BSG mRNA expression (***p < 0.001). In addition, transfection with the BSG overexpression plasmid markedly increased BSG expression in A549 cells compared with the empty vector group (***p < 0.001). Subsequently, we set up six experimental groups including Blank, Vector, BSG OE, NC siRNA, BSG siRNA-1 and BSG siRNA-2. Representative images and quantitative analyses of colony formation, Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that BSG overexpression significantly promoted the clonogenic potential, migration and invasion abilities of A549 cells (**p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 vs Blank group). In contrast, silencing BSG obviously suppressed these malignant biological behaviors (#p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01 vs NC siRNA group). All experiments were independently repeated three times, and all quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation after normalization using the Blank group as the reference.

Discussion

Despite substantial advancements in diagnostic technologies and therapeutic strategies, LUAD remains a formidable challenge for medical oncologists worldwide. The urgent need to identify effective prognostic and therapeutic markers to guide LUAD intervention is therefore undeniable. Accumulating evidence has linked the transmembrane glycoprotein BSG to the development and progression of multiple diseases, including various cancers. However, while BSG’s role in tumorigenesis and prognosis has been partially characterized in some cancer types, its specific functions and underlying mechanisms in LUAD remain incompletely elucidated. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted the first comprehensive analysis of BSG in LUAD, systematically investigating its expression patterns, diagnostic efficacy, prognostic value, functional pathways, and immune-related landscape.

To thoroughly characterize BSG expression across physiological and pathological contexts, we integrated data from multiple databases (GTEx, CCLE, and TCGA). Our findings revealed that BSG is widely expressed in normal human tissues and significantly upregulated in the majority of tumor types, suggesting it may act as an oncogene driving tumor progression. Notably, in LUAD, BSG expression was strongly associated with key clinicopathological parameters (T stage, N stage, and pathologic stage), and its overexpression correlated significantly with poor outcomes in terms of OS, DSS, and PFI. The prognostic value of BSG was further validated using an independent GEO cohort (GSE31210), confirming its applicability across diverse LUAD populations. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further identified BSG as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD. To translate this finding into clinical utility, we constructed a nomogram integrating BSG with other prognostic variables (T stage, N stage, M stage) to predict personalized 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in LUAD patients. While BSG demonstrated high diagnostic performance in several cancer types, such as KICH and PAAD, the AUC for LUAD was 0.624, indicating only moderate diagnostic accuracy. This relatively lower value may reflect the complex molecular heterogeneity of LUAD, overlapping expression patterns with normal tissues, and the involvement of multiple pathways in tumor initiation, which can limit the discriminative power of a single biomarker. Nevertheless, despite its moderate AUC, BSG expression is significantly associated with clinical stage, patient prognosis, and immune infiltration patterns in LUAD, supporting its potential utility as a complementary biomarker rather than a standalone diagnostic indicator.

To explore how BSG regulates LUAD progression and prognosis, we performed functional enrichment analyses of BSG-related genes. Our results demonstrated that BSG is intricately involved in multiple critical signaling pathways, highlighting its central role in LUAD pathogenesis. Most directly, BSG-related genes were significantly enriched in the “non-small cell lung cancer” pathway, establishing a direct link between BSG and LUAD development. Additionally, these genes were associated with pathways well-documented in immunotherapy (IL-17 signaling, B cell receptor signaling, T cell receptor signaling, and PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway) [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent programmed necrosis, is now widely recognized as a key factor in the development and progression of various cancers especially in lung cancer [28]. Targeting ferroptosis pathway may provide a potential strategy for the treatment of lung cancer [29]. BSG-related genes were also highly linked with EGFR-TKI resistance pathway, which is a well-studied pathway with important roles in the targeted therapy of EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer [30]. Increasing evidence supports a concept that PI3K-Akt signaling is of great importance in regulating tumor development, growth, and proliferation of LUAD, and targeting PI3K-Akt pathway may be meaningful in personalized treatment of LUAD [31]. In addition, BSG-related genes were also closely related to some other key pathways, which were also highly involved in cancer initiation and progression [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. Moreover, BSG exerts a vital regulatory effect on tumor glycolysis and lactate metabolism by interacting with monocarboxylate transporters MCT1 and MCT4. A recent mechanistic study confirmed that BSG acts as a positive regulator to enhance the transport activity of MCT1 and MCT4, while the monoclonal antibody targeting BSG could reverse this regulatory effect, inhibit intracellular lactate efflux, and further remodel the metabolic landscape of tumor cells and T cells in NSCLC. This metabolic reprogramming induced by BSG blockade ultimately relieves lactate-mediated immunosuppression and enhances anti-tumor immune responses, which is consistent with our enrichment results showing the close correlation between BSG and metabolic pathways in LUAD [37]. Of note, a recent experimental study confirmed that silencing CD147 could suppress the proliferation, migration and invasion of LUAD cells and reverse lipid metabolism disorders via blocking the Rap1 signaling pathway, further verifying the regulatory effect of BSG on core malignant phenotypes and metabolic reprogramming in LUAD [38]. The functional enrichment results may provide some clues on the mechanisms involved in the survival and immunology of LUAD. Together, these findings suggest BSG plays a pivotal role in tumor immunity and may serve as a key biomarker for guiding immunotherapy.

A key finding of our study is that BSG expression is significantly associated with immune cell infiltration in the LUAD TME. Specifically, BSG expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of NK cells (including CD56bright and CD56dim subsets) but negatively correlated with infiltration of T helper cells, central memory T cells (Tcm), B cells, total T cells, effector memory T cells (Tem), macrophages, Th1 cells, and cytotoxic cells. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells are core components of the TME and govern every stage of tumor progression and immune response [39]. For example, in most cancers, T cells are a key component of the adaptive immune system and are the main force of the anti-tumor army in the immune system [40]. B cells are another major component of TME with important roles in shaping lung cancer development, and exploration of B cells function within TME will provide improved therapeutic strategies to target this important subset of immune cells [41]. NK cells, a crucial component of the innate immune system, are one of the first lines of defense against infections and malignancies [42]. Macrophages are a key component of anti-tumor immunity as they can directly clear tumor cells and improve the phagocytic ability of immune cells and function as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity [43]. Consistent with our observation that BSG is negatively correlated with macrophage infiltration, a high-level study revealed that Lys148 dimethylation of CD147 (CD147-K148me2), a classic post-translational modification of BSG, could drive the crosstalk between tumor cells and macrophages. Mechanically, CD147-K148me2 promoted the binding of CyPA to CD147, activated the p38-ZBTB32 cascade to upregulate CCL5 transcription and secretion, and subsequently recruited M2-like tumor-associated macrophages to construct an immunosuppressive microenvironment through the CCL5/CCR5 axis, ultimately accelerating tumor progression [44]. In line with these conclusions, antibody-mediated inhibition of BSG has been proven to increase the proportion of CD3+ T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and dendritic cells in the TME, reduce the abundance of immunosuppressive Treg cells, and exert synergistic anti-tumor effects when combined with PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade in NSCLC [37]. These findings support that BSG may affect the prognosis and immunotherapy efficacy of LUAD patients partly by regulating and recruiting immune infiltrating cells.

Our analysis further revealed robust associations between BSG expression and immune-related genes—including immune checkpoint proteins, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes—across most tumor types, particularly LUAD. Immune checkpoint proteins have been recognized as important regulators of the immune system and can maintain self-tolerance, prevent autoimmune responses, and minimize tissue damage by controlling the duration and intensity of immune responses [45]. Targeting immune checkpoint proteins has now been universally acknowledged as a significant breakthrough in tumor therapy [46]. Chemokines, as mediators of immune cell trafficking, play a significant part in directing immune cell migration, inducing an immune response and participating in immune regulation and associated pathological responses [47]. Chemokine receptors are crucial regulatory components of the chemokine network, playing a guiding role in the coordination of cell trafficking in a lot of cellular activities [48]. Manipulation of chemokine and chemokine receptor signaling can reshape the immune phenotype in the TME to improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy [49]. Emerging evidence has supported that MHC genes play a crucial part in both innate and adaptive immune responses [50]. MHC genes can be mainly divided into MHC class I and MHC class II molecules, which can activate the adaptive immune system via presenting antigens to CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, respectively [51]. Targeting the MHC class I and class II pathway may provide many opportunities for developing new modalities of immunotherapy [52]. Existing research also demonstrated that blocking BSG could upregulate the expression of MHC class II molecules in CD4+ T cells, elevate the expression of effector molecules such as IFNG, CCL4 and XCL1 in CD8+ T cells, and downregulate the markers of suppressive immune cells, further confirming that BSG can remodel anti-tumor immunity by modulating the expression of a series of immune-related genes [37]. These correlations indicate that BSG may modulate immune responses in LUAD by regulating the expression of these immune-related genes.

The immunohistochemical validation and cell functional assay results further confirm the clinical relevance of BSG in LUAD, which is consistent with our bioinformatics analysis based on multi-omics databases. The cytoplasmic and membranous localization of BSG in A549 cells aligns with its known biological functions, as BSG is a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in regulating cell adhesion, invasion, and metabolic reprogramming—key processes in tumor progression. Quantitative analysis of tissue microarrays verified significantly dysregulated BSG expression between LUAD and adjacent normal tissues, reinforcing the notion that BSG upregulation is a characteristic event in LUAD carcinogenesis. Importantly, high BSG expression was associated with unfavorable overall survival in clinical samples, which validated the prognostic value of BSG identified in TCGA and GEO cohorts, and further supported its potential as an independent prognostic biomarker. Although tumor size remained the most robust independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis, the marginal trend of BSG with poor prognosis (P = 0.0766) may be attributed to the limited sample size, warranting validation in larger cohorts. Of note, patient-derived NSCLC organoid (NSCLC-PDO) models have verified that targeting BSG presents superior anti-tumor activity compared with single inhibition of MCT1, MCT4 or LDHA, fully demonstrating the clinical translational potential of BSG-targeted therapy for LUAD [37]. These findings support the prognostic relevance of BSG in LUAD, although larger cohorts are needed to confirm its independent prognostic value.

A key insight from our study is that BSG exhibits a dual role in LUAD, acting both as a prognostic biomarker and as a modulator of the tumor immune microenvironment. Elevated BSG expression was consistently associated with worse clinical outcomes, including OS, DSS, and PFI, across TCGA and GEO cohorts. Concurrently, BSG expression was significantly correlated with the infiltration of multiple immune cell types, including positive correlations with NK cell subsets and negative correlations with T cells, B cells, macrophages, and Th1 cells. Moreover, BSG expression was associated with immune checkpoint proteins, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes. These findings suggest that the prognostic impact of BSG may be at least partially mediated through its immunomodulatory functions: by shaping the immune composition and activity within the TME, BSG can influence tumor progression and patient outcomes. Therefore, the “dual role” of BSG reflects its capacity to both directly indicate prognosis and indirectly affect tumor behavior via immune regulation, providing a mechanistic link between these two functions.

Limitations

Despite the comprehensive multi-omics analyses and validation experiments performed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we used multiple datasets, the sample size in some analyses remains limited, which may affect statistical power. Second, while correlative analyses suggest associations between BSG expression, prognosis, and immune infiltration, direct mechanistic experiments are lacking and are required to confirm causal relationships. Third, validation in clinical cohorts treated with immunotherapy is not available, limiting the ability to directly translate our findings to immunotherapy outcomes. In addition, we have not further explored the crosstalk between BSG-mediated lactate metabolism, ferroptosis and immune regulation in LUAD. Combining organoid models and in vivo animal experiments to clarify the upstream and downstream regulatory networks of BSG will help to fully reveal its mechanism of action [37]. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, functional experiments, and immunotherapy-treated patients will be critical to fully elucidate the role of BSG in LUAD.

Conclusions

In summary, our comprehensive analysis, supplemented by in vitro cellular and clinical tissue IHC validation, confirms that BSG is a promising prognostic biomarker and a candidate immune-related biomarker in LUAD. IHC results verified BSG’s cytoplasmic/membranous localization, dysregulated expression in LUAD tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues, and association with poor overall survival, reinforcing the reliability of our bioinformatics findings. Furthermore, multi-omics and functional enrichment analyses indicate BSG may drive LUAD progression and modulate tumor immunity by regulating anti-tumor immune signaling pathways and interacting with key immune-related genes. However, follow-up mechanistic experiments and large-scale prospective clinical studies are essential to validate BSG’s functional role in LUAD progression and TME remodeling.
