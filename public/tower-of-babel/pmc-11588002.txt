Small Brains: Body Shape Constrains Tissue Allocation to the Central Nervous System in Ant‐Mimicking Spiders
Authors: Michael B. J. Kelly, Vanessa Penna‐Gonçalves, Nikolas J. Willmott, Donald James McLean, Jay R. Black, Jonas O. Wolff, Marie E. Herberstein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn Batesian mimicry, mimetic traits are not always as convincing as predicted by theory—in fact, inaccurate mimicry with only a superficial model resemblance is common and taxonomically widespread. The “selection trade‐offs hypothesis” proposes a life‐history trade‐off between accurate mimetic traits and one or more vital biological functions. Here, using an accurate myrmecomorphic (ant‐mimicking) jumping spider species, Myrmarachne smaragdina, we investigate how myrmecomorphic modifications to the body shape impact the internal anatomy in a way that could be functionally limiting. Specifically, via x‐ray micro‐computed tomography (microCT), we quantify how the spider's constricted prosoma, which emulates the head and thorax of ants, impacts the size of the central nervous system (CNS) and the venom glands. Although, relative to their whole‐body mass, we found no significant difference in venom gland volume, the CNS of the ant‐mimicking jumping spider was significantly smaller when compared with a relatively closely related non‐mimic jumping spider, indicating that some trade‐off between mimic accuracy and size of neural anatomy, as articulated by the “selection trade‐offs hypothesis,” is a possibility. Our explorative evidence enables and encourages broader investigation of how variable mimic accuracy impacts the neuroanatomy in ant mimics as a direct test of the “selection trade‐offs hypothesis.”

Introduction

Batesian mimics gain protection from predators by resembling the phenotype of a defended prey model (McIver and Stonedahl 1993). Theoretically, the more accurately a mimic resembles its model, the greater the selective benefit (Gilbert 2005), and thus, natural selection is predicted to refine mimic fidelity while eliminating inaccurate mimics from the population (Mappes and Alatalo 1997). Paradoxically, there are many instances of inaccurate mimics (Sherratt and Peet‐Paré 2017; Cushing 2012). Inaccurate mimics generally possess minimal or no morphological modifications, usually only approximating their model via size and coloration, whereas accurate mimics combine aspects of size and coloration with often extreme morphological adaptations that vastly improve their mimetic resemblance (Moya‐Laraño et al. 2013; Pekár 2014). For instance, many species of hoverflies (model: bees and wasps; Edmunds 2000), nonvenomous kingsnakes (model: coral snakes; Kikuchi and Pfennig 2010), and myrmecomorphic (i.e., ant‐mimicking) spiders (model: ants; McIver and Stonedahl 1993; Cushing 1997) inaccurately resemble their defended models. In fact, Mokkonen and Lindstedt (2016) suggest that perfect resemblance may be more an exception than a rule and thus presents a challenge to the theory of mimicry and evolutionary theory more broadly. The evolutionary paradox of widespread inaccurate mimicry in many Batesian mimicry systems currently remains an intensely debated enigma (Kikuchi and Pfennig 2013; McLean et al. 2019) and begs the question why mimic fidelity, or accuracy, has not improved through natural selection (Edmunds 2000, 2006; Sherratt 2002; Gilbert 2005; Bain et al. 2007; Penney et al. 2012). However, if inaccurate mimicry is an evolutionary stable state, this would suggest that opposing or balancing selective forces must exist (Gilbert 2005), and this has resulted in the proposition of numerous, non‐mutually exclusive hypotheses (reviewed in McLean et al. 2019).

The “selection trade‐offs hypothesis” predicts that inaccurate mimicry is the result of life‐history trade‐offs between the expression of mimetic traits and another function such as thermoregulation, fecundity, foraging ability, or mate choice (Pfennig and Kikuchi 2012; Taylor, Reader, and Gilbert 2016; McLean et al. 2019). These trade‐offs would result in an adaptive peak for inaccurate mimicry, where inaccurate mimicry is locally more adaptive than accurate mimicry (Holen and Johnstone 2004; Kikuchi and Pfennig 2013).

Myrmecomorphic spiders (spiders: RRID:NCBITaxon_74971) are an excellent study system to investigate such potential trade‐offs because ant mimicry is widespread, varies considerably in its expression, and often exhibits evolutionary reversals from accurate toward inaccurate states (Reiskind 1971; Cushing 1997; Pekár 2014; Kelly et al. 2024). Many predators avoid ants, as they possess powerful mandibles, poison‐injecting stings, and various defensive secretions (Nelson and Jackson 2006b) and are aggressive toward intruders, often attacking collectively (Pekár and Jarab 2011). Jumping spiders (Salticidae) have evolved myrmecomorphy at least 12 times independently (Pekár 2014; Maddison 2015). The jumping spider genus Myrmarachne is the largest genus of jumping spiders and comprises more than 200 described species that have evolved numerous myrmecomorphic traits contributing to their striking morphological resemblance to ants, including deep constrictions to the prosoma (Hashimoto et al. 2020). As in all spiders, including jumping spiders, the cephalic and thoracic segments are fused to form the prosoma (or cephalothorax), whereas ants possess distinct head and thorax tagmata separated by a “neck” or pronotum. Higher levels of myrmecomorphic accuracy have been ascribed to spiders via the evolution of a narrowed prosoma (Edmunds 2006; Durkee, Weiss, and Uma 2011; Nelson 2012; Ceccarelli 2013; Candiani and Bonaldo 2017) with constrictions located posterior to the posterior lateral eyes (Edmunds 2006; Pekár 2014; Kelly et al. 2021). These constrictions produce the appearance of a separate head and thorax, characteristic of ant morphology (Hashimoto et al. 2020). Although these morphological modifications to the prosoma intensify the illusion of ant‐likeness in spiders, they may simultaneously reduce the volume of the prosoma relative to spiders with non‐constricted prosomata.

There are several important functions that could be directly impacted by the constriction of the prosoma in ant mimics: (1) Nervous integration—the highly condensed central nervous system (CNS) is located exclusively in the prosoma (Foelix 2011) and consists of highly fused segmental neuromeres: the protocerebrum, the deutocerebrum, the tritocerebrum (i.e., collectively the brain) and neuromeres connected to the walking legs, as well as the opisthosomal neuromere (i.e., the ventral nerve cord; Steinhoff et al. 2017; Steinhoff, Harzsch, and Uhl 2023); (2) prey immobilization and feeding: venom is injected into their prey from the venom gland via the cheliceral fang ducts, and ingestion of food is carried out via the pharynx, the oesophagus, and the sucking stomach; and (3) locomotion (including running, jumping, courtship and prey capture): Leg extension is predominantly achieved via the spider's hydraulic system, controlled by extensive musculature in the prosoma (Kropf 2013; Hashimoto et al. 2020).

Studies of the CNS (synganglion) are relatively sparse in spiders, especially compared to studies of the insect CNS (Auletta et al. 2020; Rivera‐Quiroz and Miller 2022). However, the general CNS anatomy is largely conserved across spiders, with some variation in the number and structure of neuropils (i.e., clusters of neurites where synapses are present in higher density), particularly in the protocerebrum and the connectivity of the secondary visual pathway neuropils (Steinhoff et al. 2020; Steinhoff, Harzsch, and Uhl 2023; Long 2021). In arthropods, such as spiders, the CNS can be divided into two major compact ganglia: the supraesophageal and subesophageal ganglionic masses. The supraesophageal ganglion includes the protocerebrum and a part of the cheliceral neuropil, whereas the subesophageal ganglion (SOG) includes the other half of the cheliceral neuropil, the pedipalpal neuropil, and the ventral nerve cord (leg neuropils + opisthosomal neuropil). The brain consists of the protocerebrum, deutocerebrum, and tritocerebrum, whereas the ventral nerve cord consists of leg and opisthosomal neuropils (Steinhoff, Harzsch, and Uhl 2023). Some neuropils in the protocerebrum are associated with higher order processing, such as the arcuate body and mushroom bodies. Our aim was to investigate whether the “selection trade‐offs hypothesis” may offer a suitable explanation for inaccurate myrmecomorphy. Due to the cost and time‐consuming nature of producing and analyzing micro‐computed tomography (microCT) data, particularly segmenting images, which can take days for just a single specimen, we focused on exploring the potential for a trade‐off between ant mimicry and the volume of internal organs such as the CNS and venom glands, by comparing a non‐mimetic species of jumping spider and an accurate ant‐mimicking species. Although there have been no previous studies investigating ant‐mimicking species in this context, we predict that the sizes of one or potentially both of these organs would be reduced due to the impact on the carapace of the extensively altered phenotypes of accurate myrmecomorphic spiders. We further predict that some components of the CNS may be reduced more than others as tissue allocation to different brain regions may be under differential selection pressure (Stafstrom, Michalik, and Hebets 2017). We used x‐ray microCT, an imaging technique suitable for identifying and quantifying neuropil volumes (Sombke et al. 2015). We compared absolute and proportional CNS and venom gland volumes to whole‐body mass and prosoma volume between Myrmarachne smaragdina (a strongly myrmecomorphic spider) and Astia hariola (a non‐myrmecomorphic spider). These two species were selected as they are readily available for collection, but importantly because of their relatively close phylogenetic relationship and disparate morphologies. Myrmarachne and Astia both belong to the Astioida clade and have been identified as members of sister tribes (respectively, Myrmarachnini and Astiini), thus reducing potential sources of brain structure variation as a consequence of increased phylogenetic distance (Maddison 2015). A. hariola possesses the basal stocky square‐like morphology of jumping spiders, whereas M. smaragdina has evolved a strong ant‐like phenotype (Figure 1) and has been assessed as an accurate ant mimic, with particularly strong constrictions to the prosoma, based on a comprehensive morphological evaluation (Kelly et al. 2024).

Materials and Methods

Collection and Observation

The two species of focus in this study were collected by hand using a visual search of foliage and tree trunks. The non‐mimic species, A. hariola (n = 11, including 8 females and 3 males), were obtained from the Macquarie University Wallumattagal campus, Balaclava Rd, Macquarie Park, New South Wales (−33.770338, 151.113395) on the 28th of April and 4th of May 2021. The ant‐mimicking species, M. smaragdina (n = 9, including seven females and two males), were obtained from the Townsville Palmetum, University Rd, Douglas, Queensland (−19.309270, 146.765085) on the 20th and 21st of May 2021. Both species were returned to Macquarie University alive and weighed and measured prior to preparation for microCT scanning. Spiders were individually housed in plastic vials (diameter 4 cm, height 6.5 cm) with a small piece of smooth bark and a moistened cotton bud allowing access to water ad libitum. Spiders were imaged using a Canon camera (Model: EOS 80D) mounted to a microscope (Model: Motic SMZ‐171) and weighed (in grams; to four decimal places) using a Mettler Toledo Analytical Balance (Model: ML204T/00). The images produced were then used to measure prosoma length, prosoma width, and total body length using ImageJ v1.51 (Schneider, Rasband, and Eliceiri 2012; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Measurements of the prosoma and body length exclude the chelicerae. All measurements were recorded in millimeters (three decimal places). Further details of collection and processing data can be found in Dataset S1 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13269406).

Sample Preparation for microCT Scanning

All spiders were processed for microCT scanning 2–7 days following collection. The spiders were not fed for 2 days prior to fixation, in an attempt to reduce any potential effect of feeding on the venom gland size or the quality of the microCT scans. Our protocol follows Steinhoff et al. (2017) with minor changes. In summary, spider bodies were dissected and fixed in Bouin's solution fixative (Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA) and stained in 1% iodine solution stain (iodine, resublimated in 99.8% ethanol; Sigma‐Aldrich, Missouri, USA) before being scanned on a Phoenix Nanotom M (Waygate Technologies, Germany) micro‐/nano‐CT scanner. All samples underwent a preliminary short scan to determine if nervous tissue shrinkage had occurred and to check the quality of the fixation and staining process. Those specimens that were in good condition underwent longer scans and were used in our analyses, whereas those with obvious damage due to the preparation process were excluded. The resolution (pixel size) of scans used for segmentations was 3.41 µm (±0.01). For extended methodology, see Supporting Information Section I (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13269406).

Segmentation of microCT Scans

Tiff stacks of microCT scan images were converted to Analyze 7.5 files using ImageJ v1.51 and then imported into the segmentation software ITK‐SNAP v3.8.0 (Yushkevich et al. 2006; www.itksnap.org). A total of 10 internal anatomical structures of the spiders’ prosoma were manually segmented and volumetrically measured, inclusive of 9 neural structures—collectively referred to here as the CNS—and the venom glands. Each structure was identified and mapped manually every 3–10 images. The number of images mapped for each of the different structures was influenced by two factors: (1) depending on the structure's size (i.e., the smaller visual neuropils are only present for 10–30 images; therefore, we could map them over almost every image, whereas the major ganglionic masses were present for several hundred images); and/or (2) the frequency of anatomical change (i.e., the major ganglionic masses showed relatively little anatomical alteration throughout the images, relative to the smaller structures, and could be mapped less frequently still allowing for the interpolation tool in ITK‐snap to confidently provide an accurate volume measurement). Finally, as noted above, the structures, when needed (particularly the major ganglionic masses and the venom glands), were completed using the interpolation tool provided in ITK‐SNAP and manually checked for any interpolation error. Total prosoma volume was quantified for A. hariola (non‐mimic; n = 11); M. smaragdina (mimic; n = 9) by implementing either the semi‐automatic segmenting tool or manually segmented (slice by slice contouring) in ITK‐SNAP or the masking tool with manual segmentation editing in Amira (v2020.2; ThermoFisher Scientific, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) to select the entire prosomal internal space. Any portions of appendages remaining after the dissection process, including the legs, pedipalps, and chelicerae, were manually removed using the segmentation software following interpolation of the segmented prosoma images. To test repeatability of the segmentation, two people each segmented the same scans of three structures (the protocerebral neuropils [PN] and the SOG (PNSOG), venom glands, and the arcuate body) across the two study species. Repeatability was estimated using the rptR package (Stoffel, Nakagawa, and Schielzeth 2019) in R v4.1.3 (R Development Core Team 2018).

Data Analysis

To determine whether there was any volume change in the total allocation of tissue to the CNS and venom glands, we analyzed them as a proportion of their whole‐body mass, because brain size generally scales allometrically with body mass, and spiders are no exception to this pattern (Quesada et al. 2011). We would also expect venom glands to scale with body size, as larger spiders presumably subdue larger prey. Additionally, we analyzed whether any structures of the CNS, or the combined visual neuropil volume, differ as a proportion of the total CNS volume between the mimic and the non‐mimic. Supporting Information Section II (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13269406), describes the equation to convert pixel size to volume (mm3) for the relevant structures. All individual volumetric data can be found in Dataset S1.

Relative Size of Neural Tissue and Venom Glands

To test for differences in proportional volumes of neural tissues between ant mimics and non‐mimics, we fitted reduced major axis (RMA, aka standardized major axis, SMA) linear models to log total CNS volume and log body mass for A. hariola and M. smaragdina. We also fitted RMA linear models to log venom glands volume and log body mass for both species. The choice of ordinary least squares (OLS) or RMA regression for testing allometric hypotheses depends on the nature of the biological question (Dharmarathne et al. 2023). An important assumption of OLS linear models is that the dependent variable is a function of the independent variable; there is evidence from birds that brain size does not depend directly on body size (Montgomery, Mundy, and Barton 2016), so OLS is inappropriate here. Assuming that the slopes of the allometric lines for both species are approximately equal, the elevations of the lines indicate the relative volumes of neural tissue and venom gland size in each species, with a lower elevation (or y‐intercept) indicating a relatively smaller investment in neural tissue or venom gland size. We used a likelihood ratio test for common slope and a Wald test for common elevation (Warton et al. 2006). Analysis was performed using the R package smatr (Warton et al. 2012).

The Nine Measured Internal Anatomical CNS Structures, and the Combined Visual Neuropils, Volumes Relative to Total CNS Volume

To compare the relative sizes of the CNS structures between species, we calculated the binary logarithm (log2) of the ratio of the proportional volumes (see equations, Supporting Information Section III: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13269406) for the two species. Effect sizes as log2 ratios have a straightforward interpretation: An effect size of 1 means the structure occupies double the proportion of the CNS in M. smaragdina compared to A. hariola, and an effect size of 0 means they occupy the same proportion, whereas an effect size of −1 means the structure occupies double the proportion in A. hariola. We generated the confidence intervals of the log ratios using adjusted bootstrapped percentile intervals, using the R package Durga (Khan and McLean 2024;). Because we performed multiple comparisons, we estimated the 99.5% confidence intervals, which is analogous to applying a Bonferroni correction for 10 comparisons. Calculations were performed in R v4.1.3 (R Development Core Team 2018; see Supporting Information File 1—R project files for scripts used for our analyses [https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13269406]).

Data Analysis

Relative Size of Neural Tissue and Venom Glands

Biometric Data

Here we used an ANCOVA‐like analysis to compare total CNS volume between A. hariola and M. smaragdina, while accounting for variation due to body size. Note that a single individual of M. smaragdina was excluded from this analysis as the body mass was recorded incorrectly during processing.

Absolute Volumes

The absolute volumes of the total CNS, each of the nine structures of the CNS, the venom glands, and the total prosoma volume were all on average larger in A. hariola (n = 11) than M. smaragdina (n = 9; see Dataset 1 for raw values and Table S2 for absolute minimum, maximum, means, and sample standard deviation [±]).

Relative Size of Neural Tissue and Venom Glands

To determine allocation into neural tissue relative to whole‐body mass of the ant mimic M. smaragdina and the non‐mimic A. hariola, we compared the elevations of their respective CNS‐body allometric lines. However, a difference in elevations is only meaningful if the slopes are not significantly different, so we first used a likelihood ratio to test for common slopes. We found no evidence that the slopes differed between the species (χ 2(1) = 0.88, p = 0.35) and a Wald test provided evidence that the elevations differed (W(1) = 24.57, p < 0.0001). The total CNS volume was smaller in M. smaragdina than in A. hariola (Figure 3A). The two SMA allometric slopes differed in elevation by 0.15, which means that for a given body mass, the CNS of A. hariola is approximately 142% the volume of the CNS of M. smaragdina, or conversely, the volume of the CNS of M. smaragdina is 70% the volume of the CNS of A. hariola. We performed the same analysis for the venom glands volume relative to whole‐body mass and found no difference in the allometric slopes between the two species (χ 2(1) = 0.027, p = 0.87); however, we found no significant difference in venom gland volume between the two species (W(1) = 0.82, p = 0.36; Figure 3B). The two allometric slopes differed in elevation by 0.13, which means that for a given body mass, A. hariola allocates around 134% more tissue into venom glands than M. smaragdina, or conversely, the venom glands of M. smaragdina are 74% of the volume of the venom glands of A. hariola.

DISCUSSION

Here we investigated the impact of morphological modifications in ant‐mimicking spiders (M. smaragdina), such as the deep prosomal constrictions, on the size of the CNS and venom glands. Our comparison of the CNS and venom gland volumes revealed a significant reduction in overall neural volume relative to body mass in the ant mimic, which may indicate a trade‐off between CNS volume with morphological adaptations to accurate myrmecomorphy. Venom gland volume, however, did not differ significantly between the two species.

Morphological and Ecological Impact on Brain Size and Structure

Although we found an overall reduction in CNS volume in M. smaragdina, at this point, it is not clear what the fitness impact of a reduced CNS volume might be, as some CNS regions were larger (as a proportion of CNS volume) in M. smaragdina than in A. hariola. The relatively larger regions were the first‐ and second‐order visual neuropils of the anterior median eyes and the arcuate body. The latter structure is a higher order integration center associated with locomotion control (Steinhoff et al. 2017; Caponera et al. 2021) and cognitive functions, including learning and memory (Loesel et al. 2011; Strausfeld 2012; Liedtke and Schneider 2017; Steinhoff et al. 2017), and which acts as a third‐order visual processing center (Menda et al. 2014; Auletta et al. 2020). Whether the observed reduction in CNS volume was mitigated by an increase in neuron density is not known and requires further study using different imaging techniques. Little is known about neuron numbers in spiders, but in birds and mammals, cognitive diversity (viewed as an improved ability to perform specific cognitive tasks) is directly related to higher neuron numbers (Herculano‐Houzel 2017). The relative predominance of the arcuate body and the first‐ and second‐order visual neuropils of the anterior median eyes in the CNS of M. smaragdina may reflect elements of their life‐history and increase their ability to obtain and process critical visual information. Visual information is vital to a jumping spider for discriminating among conspecifics, prey, and potential predators (Jackson and Pollard 1996). Given that M. smaragdina live in proximity of their dangerous and aggressive models, the green weaver ant, Oecophylla smaragdina, they may require cognitive processes capable of accurately identifying the weaver ant's presence to avoid potential attacks and to distinguish their myrmecomorphic conspecifics for competition and courtship. M. smaragdina can discriminate between its model and other ant mimics (e.g., the alydid bug Riptortus serripes; Ceccarelli 2009), whereas non‐mimic jumping spiders seem incapable of making this distinction (Nelson and Jackson 2006a, 2006b). Similarly, Myrmarachne assimilis, another known mimic of O. smaragdina in the Philippines, uses visual cues alone to discriminate among prey, conspecifics, and their exceptionally aggressive model (Nelson and Jackson 2007).

Behavioral Implications

Differences in the behavioral repertoire between these two species may also explain why the arcuate body is proportionally larger in M. smaragdina, which appears to perform more complex behaviors. A. hariola are noted as opportunistic sit‐and‐wait predators that commonly live under the bark of Eucalyptus trees (Myrtaceae; Hawkeswood 2003) and display a direct, punctuated locomotion style typical of non‐mimetic jumping spiders. In contrast, Myrmarachne are active hunters that constantly display numerous ant‐like locomotive behaviors, such as a stop–start, winding trajectory, and the repeated raising of the first pair of legs to simulate the antennae of ants (Shamble et al. 2017). Although one report indicates that A. hariola are “usually nocturnal” and are sit‐and‐wait predators (see Hawkeswood 2003), we cannot confirm an explicitly nocturnal lifestyle from any other literature source, and our own observations during the collection of A. hariola indicate that they are frequently found hunting on tree trunks during the day. Regardless, the most likely consequence of nocturnal versus diurnal activity on elements of the CNS would relate to the size of the visual neuropils. We found only significant differences in the proportional size of the neuropils associated with the anterior eyes in the mimic species (which may relate to the importance of visual acuity discussed in the previous section) and that the second‐order visual neuropils were larger in the non‐mimic species. However, these volume differences in visual neuropils would have little effect on the overall CNS mass, and thus, our results here.

The reduction of the CNS in M. smaragdina may not be the only trade‐off associated with ant mimicry. Hashimoto et al. (2020) showed that there appears to be a trade‐off between myrmecomorphic jumping spiders’ ability to jump and, in turn, their prey‐capture capabilities, likely due to negative effects on the control of the hydraulic pressure produced in the spiders’ modified ant‐like prosoma. Relative to non‐mimic jumping spiders, they found that jumping and prey‐capture success were reduced in Myrmarachne spp., and these costs were more pronounced when there was greater morphological modification. Although we did not test jumping performance here, the slender morphology of M. smaragdina, in‐line with the results of Hashimoto et al. (2020), suggests that jumping costs are likely. This warrants further investigation to better understand the multifaceted costs of mimicry.

Compensating for Space Constraints

Dependent on the spider's ecology, a reduction in neural tissue due to morphological constraints is expected for brain regions of lower importance (Stafstrom, Michalik, and Hebets 2017), while maintaining the size of more critical CNS regions. This is supported by our finding that the arcuate body, a vitally important higher order integration center, was proportionally larger in M. smaragdina than in A. hariola. Alternatively, but not mutually exclusive, compensation for space constraints in the prosoma is the possibility that the CNS may extend into the coxae of the legs and pedipalps, which is observed in miniaturized spider species (Quesada et al. 2011). We have not seen any evidence of nervous tissue in the coxae, but this compensatory option warrants further investigation in ant‐mimicking spiders.

CONCLUSIONS

The aim of our study was to confirm whether the “selection trade‐offs hypothesis” is a plausible explanation for the maintenance of inaccurate ant mimicry. The reduction in CNS volume in an accurate ant mimic supports this hypothesis and indicates an overall space constraint in M. smaragdina due to high levels of mimetic accuracy. We also have evidence, at least in these two studied species, of compensation in terms of a relative increase of some CNS features (e.g., the arcuate body). Our findings provide support for the idea that a trade‐off between accurate ant‐like morphology and internal anatomy warrants further, more broad‐scaled studies. Future study could include the full range of morphologies from non‐mimic to inaccurate mimic and highly accurate mimic in a phylogenetically corrected approach, to conclusively determine that this trade‐off with CNS and potentially other structures such as venom glands offers a viable explanation for explaining why ant‐mimicry is so variable, evolved so dynamically, and why inaccurate mimicry is so abundant.
