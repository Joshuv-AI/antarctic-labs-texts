Ixazomib with chemotherapy for childhood relapsed acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a TACL consortium report∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•The oral formulation of ixazomib is accepted and well tolerated by children with R/R ALL.•Ixazomib, in addition to standard relapsed chemotherapy, is tolerable and has a promising early efficacy signal in R/R ALL.

Introduction

There has been steady incremental improvement in outcomes for children and young adults with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) since the middle of the 20th century.1 However, patients continue to experience disease relapse, and death from disease is still one of the leading contributors to childhood mortality in the Western World.1,2 Although newer immunotherapies have revolutionized ALL therapy, their broader use is limited by toxicities, availability, disease resistance, cost, and dependence on expression of specific disease-associated targets.

First and second generation IV proteasome inhibitors, such as bortezomib and carfilzomib, have demonstrated therapeutic activity in a broad range of hematologic malignancies including multiple myeloma, follicular lymphoma, and ALL.3 Proteasome inhibitors sensitize malignant cells to the anticancer effects of radiation and chemotherapy and impart direct cytotoxicity through initiation of apoptosis.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 They are also generally well tolerated because normal hematopoietic progenitors such as CD34 hematopoietic stem cells appear to be resistant to their toxic effects.10,11 The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway is essential for the degradation of intracellular proteins in eukaryotic cells. Inhibitors of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway exert their effect by inhibiting the β5 of the 20S proteasome and stabilize many cell cycle–dependent proteins such as NF-κβ, p53, Bax, p27 and p21, which are largely overexpressed in leukemia cells.12 Proteasome inhibitors also suppress immunoproteasomes, which are responsible for processing antigens for presentation on major histocompatibility class 1 molecules.13 Preclinical studies of bortezomib and carfilzomib have shown disease efficacy in model systems of childhood ALL including leukemia cell lines, primary ALL lymphoblasts, and in mouse xenografts.5, 6, 7,14 Bortezomib and carfilzomib have also been shown to be safe and effective in early phase clinical studies in relapsed pediatric ALL, and bortezomib has been shown to be safe and effective in a randomized controlled study of de novo pediatric T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (LL), becoming a standard-of-care drug for that disease.15, 16, 17, 18

Ixazomib (MLN 9708) is a newer oral proteasome inhibitor that is currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as a 2.2 mg/m2 dose once daily on days 1, 8, and 15 of 28-day cycles in combination with lenalidomide and dexamethasone for the treatment of multiple myeloma in patients who have received at least 1 previous therapy.19 In preclinical studies, ixazomib has shown increased potency and superior efficacy over bortezomib due to its shorter proteasome disassociation half-life that leads to a higher tumor-to-blood ratio of proteasome inhibition.20, 21, 22 Additional advantages of ixazomib over bortezomib or carfilzomib are that it can be administered in a highly bioavailable oral formulation and has significantly lower incidence of peripheral neuropathy.23

The purpose of this study was to estimate the maximum tolerated dose and recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) for the capsule and liquid formulations of ixazomib in combination with a standard backbone of chemotherapy in children, adolescents, and young adults with relapsed and refractory (R/R) ALL and LL. In the phase 2 portion, the purpose was to estimate the efficacy of this treatment in the same population of patients.

Methods

This multi-institutional study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03817320) was performed through the Therapeutic Advances in Childhood Leukemia and Lymphoma (TACL) consortium. The study was approved by the institutional review board at all participating TACL centers. Consent was obtained from all participants, which included parental permission and minor child assent (if age appropriate).

Eligibility

Eligible patients were those with R/R ALL or LL aged <22 years of age with ≥5% blasts (by morphology) in the bone marrow and at least 1 previous treatment attempt. Patients with LL were required to have measurable disease as defined in the revised Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guideline (version 1.1).24 Patients with concurrent extramedullary disease, or central nervous system (CNS) involvement, were also eligible. Patients with mixed phenotype acute leukemia or mature B leukemia were not eligible. Infants and patients with Down syndrome (DS) were eligible to be enrolled on a separate stratum at 1 lower dose level (DL).

A Lansky/Karnofsky performance score of ≥50 was required at study entry, as well as stable organ function including a cardiac left ventricular ejection fraction of ≥50%. Patients had to be >90 days after hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) and without active graft-versus-host disease and were not receiving treatment for graft-versus-host disease. Exclusion criteria included patients with grade ≥2 sensory or motor neuropathy (as defined by the Modified Balis Scale of Pediatric Neuropathies) or if they were unable to receive any of the drugs used in the study due to intolerance or allergy (although asparaginase Erwinia chrysanthemi [recombinant]-rywn could be substituted for pegaspargase or calaspargase).

Treatment

The treatment regimen, comprising 2 induction blocks and maintenance chemotherapy, is shown in Figure 1. Ixazomib was combined with a VXLD (vincristine, dexamethasone, asparaginase, and doxorubicin) backbone in block 1. After block 1, chemotherapy was completed, patients who attained a complete response (CR), CR with minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity (CR MRD−), or CR with incomplete count recovery (see full definitions in the supplemental Methods) In brief, for patients with ALL, primary response evaluation was measured by bone marrow morphology with measured blasts of <5% constituting a CR; MRD assessment was performed by identifying leukemic blasts by flow cytometry or molecular testing (eg, polymerase chain reaction, next-generation sequencing, etc) Patients in CR had the option to receive block 2 intensive chemotherapy or maintenance chemotherapy with ixazomib. Patients with a partial response or stable disease continued with block 2 therapy. After completion of block 2 therapy, patients with CR/CR MRD−/CR with incomplete count recovery/partial response could participate in the optional maintenance cycle for up to 4 cycles. Intrathecal therapy was administered throughout the treatment with agents and dosing according to the patient’s CNS disease status upon study entry. Ixazomib was given orally on days 1, 4, 8, and 11 in block 1, and days 1, 4, 8, 15, and 18 of block 2 therapy. In the optional maintenance block, ixazomib was given on a weekly basis (3 weeks on, 1 week off) on days 1, 8, and 15. The medication was administered on an empty stomach. Antifungal (per institutional standard) and antibacterial prophylaxis (levofloxacin) was mandated. Statistics

Disease evaluations were obtained in all study participants at the end of block 1. Patients with ALL underwent a bone marrow aspiration. The disease evaluation occurred no sooner than day 29 and was delayed no later than day 36 in patients with pancytopenia on day 29. Patients with a hypoplastic marrow at the time of the disease evaluation could undergo repeat marrow evaluation every 7 to 21 days depending on peripheral blood count recovery and remission assessed at the later time point.

Patients who received all, or part of, the study treatment and were able to undergo disease response evaluation at the end of block 1 were considered evaluable for response. Additionally, patients were evaluable if they met the definition of progressive disease, or if they died from a possibly, probably, or definitely ixazomib-related dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) during the first cycle. Patients who were not evaluable (NE) for response were replaced.

Overall survival (OS) is defined as the time from study entry to death of any cause or last follow-up if the patient remained alive as of 8 October 2025. OS is summarized with Kaplan-Meier survival estimates and the Greenwood formula for the standard error and is compared with the log-rank test.

Two DLs were tested in the phase 1 portion of the study: DL1 was 1.6 mg/m2 per dose, and DL2 was 2 mg/m2 per dose, using a 3+3 dose escalation design. The design targeted DLT rates of 15% to 20%, which is considered acceptable for this backbone regimen for relapsed ALL.25

Sample size for the phase 2 expansion was 12 to 18 response evaluable patients, including the response evaluable patients treated at the RP2D and enrolled in phase 1. Approximately 9 to 12 DLT-evaluable patients were planned to be enrolled and treated for a minimum of 1 block (28-day cycle) of therapy. Unless toxicity requiring premature drug discontinuation occurred during block 1, each patient had to receive at least 4 weeks of treatment.

Toxicity

The primary safety end point was the presence of DLT during block 1 of chemotherapy. Toxicities were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria version 5.0.26 Patients who received at least 1 dose of ixazomib during block 1 with at least 75% of the required dose of ixazomib were evaluable for DLT unless the patient discontinued block 1 of therapy early due to a DLT, or treatment was terminated for toxicity or intolerability. Toxicity data from infants and patients with DS were collected but not used in the DLT assessment or safety analysis.

The occurrence of toxic death (TD), defined as treatment-related mortality at any time during protocol therapy, was a primary end point for safety monitoring. The criterion for an unacceptable rate of TDs was to be satisfied if the following fractions of TDs out of total patients treated were exceeded: ≥1 of 5, ≥2 of 12, ≥3 of 20, or ≥4 of 28.

Pharmacokinetics (PKs)

Blood samples were collected from all patients enrolled in phase 1 for determination of plasma concentrations of ixazomib. For patients with a body weight of <20 kg, samples were collected on day 1 of block 1 at 72 hours after ixazomib dosing, and on day 11 of block 1. Samples were collected before ixazomib dosing and then after dosing at 0.5, 4, and 72 hours. For patients with a body weight of ≥20 kg, samples were collected on day 1 of block 1 at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, and 72 hours after ixazomib dosing, and on day 11 of block 1 samples were collected before the ixazomib dose and then after dosing at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, 72, and 264 hours.

Results

Between January 2019 and December 2023, 24 patients were enrolled across 10 TACL sites. Patient characteristics and disease status are shown in Table 1. Twenty-three evaluable patients were enrolled in stratum A. One patient with DS was enrolled in the descriptive only, DS stratum B. The median age at enrollment was 8.2 years (range, 1.5-20.5) and 67% of patients were of Hispanic ethnicity, consistent with the ethnicity distribution of enrolling sites. There were 8 patients (34%) with CNS disease and 3 patients (13%) with non-CNS extramedullary disease. Patients constituted a heavily pretreated population with a median of 3 previous lines of therapy. In the phase 1 portion of this study, 10 patients were treated, 9 in stratum A (3 at DL1 and 6 at DL2) and 1 patient with DS enrolled on stratum B (at DL1). All patients in the phase 1 study were evaluable for toxicity/DLT and response and there were no DLTs. Stratum A analysis determined that the RP2D of ixazomib combined with chemotherapy in this population was 2 mg/m2 per dose (DL2). In the phase 2 portion of the study, 14 patients were treated at the RP2D (DL2). All 14 patients were evaluable for DLT, and 2 patients experienced a DLT. There were also 2 patients who died of infectious toxicities before day 15 and were considered NE for response and were replaced. Thus, there were 23 patients (phase 1, n = 9; phase 2, n = 14) evaluable for toxicity and 21 patients (phase 1, n = 9; phase 2, n = 12) evaluable for response.

AEs attributable to backbone chemotherapy were similar to those attributable to ixazomib (supplemental Table 1). These included febrile neutropenia, gastrointestinal events, and sepsis.

The patient with DS developed grade 4 sepsis, considered possibly related to ixazomib and possibly related to backbone chemotherapy. The patient required admission to the pediatric intensive care unit on day 14 of protocol therapy but fully recovered. This patient also developed grade 3 hyperglycemia on day 3 and was treated with insulin, improving to grade 2 hyperglycemia by day 5. Hyperglycemia was thought unlikely related to ixazomib and likely related to the backbone dexamethasone.

DLTs

No DLTs were noted in phase 1. In phase 2, 2 patients met DLT criteria, but these did not affect the RP2D. One such patient experienced grade 4 enterocolitis and grade 4 sepsis that was deemed possibly related to ixazomib and probably related to the backbone chemotherapy. The second patient developed grade 4 sepsis and grade 5 enterocolitis (possibly due to ixazomib and probably due to the backbone).

TDs

In addition to the patients in phase 2 who died while meeting DLT criteria, there were 2 additional patients who experienced a TD due to infection before day 15 of block 1 chemotherapy. The first patient died on day 11 of block 1 due to grade 5 sepsis and cerebral edema caused by Bacillus cereus that was possibly related to ixazomib and the backbone chemotherapy. A second patient died on day 13 of block 1 due to grade 5 septic shock from Escherichia coli. The occurrence of TDs did not cross the protocol safety monitoring boundary.

One patient experienced multiple grade 4 AEs including enterocolitis, multiorgan failure, sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and respiratory failure during block 1 of chemotherapy. The patient also experienced a grade 3 methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection and grade 3 tracheitis. The patient recovered from this illness and experienced a CR, was then removed from protocol therapy, and subsequently completed 2 courses of off-protocol bridging therapy followed by CAR-T therapy.

Outcome

The 2-year OS for patients treated at DL2 was 60% (95% confidence interval [CI], 35.7-77.6; Figure 2A). There were 5 patients treated at DL2 who experienced a CR and went on to receive more than block 1 protocol therapy. Of 18 patients eligible to receive block 2, 4 patients began such therapy, 2 of whom proceeded to alternative therapies (HSCT and CAR-T therapy) before finishing block 2. Five additional patients proceeded to HSCT within 60 days of completing block 1, and 3 patients proceeded to CAR-T therapy because they were using block 1 as a bridge. One patient received a single protocol-directed maintenance block after block 1 chemotherapy during which time no grade ≥3 toxicities occurred.

After completion of protocol therapy, 14 patients (58%) subsequently received a HSCT. This included 3 patients treated at DL1, 1 of whom had DS. Among 12 patients treated at DL2 who achieved CR, 9 (75%) subsequently underwent HSCT.

PKs

Final determination of PK parameters was made using samples from day 11 of block 1 (supplemental Table 2). The median time to maximal concentration was consistent across DLs at 4 and 3 hours for DL1 and DL2, respectively. The median highest concentration and area under the curve were 15.9 ng/mL and 449.1 h·ng/mL, respectively, for DL1, and as expected increased to 72.6 ng/mL and 1366.7 h·ng/mL, respectively, for DL2. The half-life was consistent across DLs at 119.2 hours and 115 hours for DL1 and DL2, respectively. Although numbers are small and interpatient variation is large, the PK of our patients was reasonably consistent with that recorded in adults with multiple myeloma who received similar doses of ixazomib but with lenolidomide and dexamethasone.27 In direct comparison, our patients’ time to maximal concentration was slightly longer (3-4 hours vs 1-1.5 hours), highest concentration was slightly higher (15.9-72.6 ng/mL vs 5.7-31.4 ng/mL), and half-life was slightly shorter (119.2-115 hours vs 205-157 hours).27

Ixazomib palatability

Fifteen patients (68%) received the capsule, and 7 patients (32%) received the liquid formulation of ixazomib. Of patients who took the capsule, 10 patients (67%) rated it as neutral, and 5 (33%) rated it as less than neutral. Of patients who received the liquid, 3 patients (43%) rated it as neutral, and 4 (57%) rated it as less than neutral (supplemental Table 3). Small sample size makes it difficult to make significant conclusions about palatability, but no patient came off protocol therapy due to inability to take the oral ixazomib.

Discussion

This study determined that, in children with R/R ALL, the addition of 4 doses of ixazomib combined with standard reinduction chemotherapy had an acceptable safety profile and achieved a primary response end point (CR) of 67%, with a 58% MRD-negativity rate. Ixazomib was also palatable in this population (supplemental Table 3). The RP2D was the higher DL tested (DL2): 2 mg/m2 per dose. Two-year OS for patients attaining a CR was 83% (Figure 2B). PKs showed that ixazomib clearance was similar to that is adults with multiple myeloma treated with ixazomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone. Limited palatability data showed that the oral versions of ixazomib were tolerable for most patients.

Although outcomes for children, adolescents, and young adults with first-relapse lower-risk precursor B-cell ALLs are rapidly improving, the prognosis remains poor for higher-risk first-relapse precursor B-cell and T-cell ALL or any ALL in second or greater relapse.2,28,29 In addition, most of the recent therapeutic advances in this field revolve around small-molecule inhibitors or immunotherapies with specific leukemic targets, leaving those who experience disease relapse after such therapies or those without such targets with few options. All eukaryotic cells rely on proteasomes for protein turnover and thus targets for proteasome inhibitors are universal.4 Cells with high metabolic rates, such as leukemia cells, are more susceptible to proteasome inhibitors in comparison with nonmalignant cells. Notably, proteasome inhibitors have shown preclinical activity in a wide array of cancers.4 This study of ixazomib in children with relapsed ALL/LL marked yet another step toward optimization of this therapeutic modality, ultimately demonstrating that, within the context of a phase 1/2 study, ixazomib in combination with induction chemotherapy can be well tolerated and effective.

The CR rates in phase 1 studies of small-molecule inhibitors on 3- to 4-drug reinduction platforms (eg, vincristine, corticosteroid, asparaginase with or without an anthracycline) for high-risk or multiply relapsed ALL consistently range from 40% to 55%.30, 31, 32, 33 A few studies using reinduction chemotherapy with the first generation, IV reversible proteasome inhibitor bortezomib have exceeded that level, with CR rates between 67% and 80%.17,34,35 Although early generation IV proteasome inhibitors offer benefit to children with multiply relapsed ALL without the need for a specific drug target, there are opportunities for improvement with this class of agents and some limitations have been demonstrated. For example, the second-generation, irreversible proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib failed to demonstrate comparable success when it induced a 50% CR rate in a similar population of patients.33 Furthermore, despite the efficacy of bortezomib in combination with induction chemotherapy inducing CRs and longer term remissions, 2- to 3-year OS rates have continued to be poor.36 Importantly, proteasome inhibitors have traditionally been given 4 to 5 times during a cycle, usually on days not overlapping with other chemotherapy, causing increased expense and inconvenience.36 Although ours is a relatively small phase 1/2 study, several of these challenges noted in earlier proteasome trials are overcome with the use of the oral proteasome inhibitor ixazomib.

In this trial, the CR rate among 21 evaluable patients was 67% (14 CRs), with 9 patients (43% of total patients; 64% of patients who achieved CR) achieving MRD-negative status. This is comparable with the results of trials of bortezomib.16,17,34,35 However, in our trial, remission durability appears to be improved. After completion of therapy on our trial, 58% of patients received consolidative HSCT and the 2-year OS for those treated at the RP2D was 60%. This is comparable with the percentage of patients going onto consolidative HSCT (51%) and 1-year OS (59%) on the AALL07P1 trial, (a phase 2 trial with bortezomib) despite that study being limited to children in first relapse of ALL, whereas our study included heavily pretreated patients with multiple relapses, many of whom had undergone previous HSCT.16

On this trial, ixazomib was given on days of the cycle similar to previous proteasome inhibitor schedules; that is, in block 1, on days 1, 4, 8, and 11; and in block 2, on days 1, 4, 8, 15, and 18. Oral administration not only allowed less entry into a central line in this highly immunocompromised population but also avoided additional days of access or travel to a clinic/infusion center on days not traditionally used for backbone chemotherapy administration. All patients on our trial accepted the oral formulation and more than half (59%) rated the palatability as at least “neutral.”

AEs were largely expected, reversible, and commonly observed with the intensive chemotherapy backbone used on this study. Only a single patient experienced grade ≥3 peripheral neuropathy, a toxicity commonly observed in patients receiving other proteasome inhibitors.37 The generally low incidence of neuropathy on this study is consistent with other studies of ixazomib, which also report lower incidences of peripheral neuropathy in comparison with other proteasome inhibitors. There were 4 TDs (16.7%), which is not unexpected in a heavily pretreated relapsed pediatric ALL population receiving an intensive chemotherapy backbone regimen for which published induction deaths generally range from 6% to 25%.17,31,33,38 Of note, however, there was a relatively frequent incidence of high-grade gastrointestinal toxicities including 2 grade 4 and 1 grade 5 occurrences of enterocolitis. Gastrointestinal toxicities are well known to occur with all approved proteasome inhibitors.39,40 The mechanism of such toxicity is not well elucidated but preclinical studies suggest possible drug-induced increase in proinflammatory cytokines in the intestinal epithelium, dysregulation of the gut microbiome, and neuropathy of the gastrointestinal tract as potential contributor to proteasome inhibitor gastrointestinal toxicity. Given that ixazomib is oral, it is possible that these conditions are exacerbated by the route of administration.39,40 The protective course of action is not clear, but gastrointestinal complaints during ixazomib therapy should prompt close medical attention.

Overall, this study showed that ixazomib, when combined with dexamethasone, asparaginase, doxorubicin, and vincristine, can be tolerable and effective for children and adolescents with relapsed ALL. This conclusion is in the context of a phase 1/2 investigation and limitations intrinsic to the study design include a small number of patients; care given at limited sites, which have expertise in providing care to children and adolescents with intensive and experimental therapies; a heterogenous population; and a relatively short follow-up period. However, a clear signal of efficacy has been detected, warranting additional larger studies or studies of the use of ixazomib in less intensive phases of therapy such as maintenance therapy or as a bridge to HSCT or immunotherapy.
