The Human Mind in Dialogue With its Replica AI – a Historical Perspective
Authors: Sven Hroar Klempe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough artificial intelligence has exploded for the last couple of years, the idea of comparing the human mind with a computer is not new. One may say that Alan Turing started this movement, and this idea got off to a flying start in psychology through the symposium arranged by the “Special Interest Group in Information Theory” at Massachusetts Institute of Technology Sept. 10–11, 1956. This symposium was sponsored by IBM, and Noam Chomsky presented for the first time his ideas about a transformational generative grammar. There were several contributors to the cognitive revolution, but Chomsky’s presentation of a linguistic system with a level of precision comparable with mathematics was symptomatic for how a cognitive model for the human mind aimed to be conceptualized. This has brought the question about artificial intelligence to the core of psychology, namely how it can be understood as a model for the human mind. Psychology and its understanding of the human mind have changed along the last 500 years of the Western psychology. After a short presentation of some of the premises for artificial intelligence of today and some aspects of the idea behind the computational mind, these perspectives are compared with the very early use of the term ‘psychology’ in the early 16th century. The role of classical logic as a model for the human mind was at stake back then and one of the main factors that paved the way for psychology was that Aristotelian logic was criticized and questioned as a model for the human mind. On the other hand, the importance of classical logic has never been completely outdated Consequently, for the last 500 years Western psychology can in many ways be characterized as an incessant battle about the role of logic in the understanding of the human mind. However, the conclusion is that AI of today presupposes an understanding of the human mind that has been outdated for more than 500 years.Keywords: Machinery intelligence, Functional explanations, Calculation systems, Logic, Neural activity, Metaphysics, Spikes

There is no doubt that Alan Turing (1912–1954) represents a crucial starting point for developing what we today call artificial intelligence (AI). He did not apply that term, but posed and tried to answer the question: “Can machines think?” This was done in his ground-breaking article from 1950, entitled “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (Turing, 1950, p. 433). In this article he also tried to define a criterion for deciding if a machine can think or not. Although there is a discussion to what extent he was able or intended to define this criterion (Copeland, 2000/2003), he talked about a machinery produced imitation game that very well may function as if computing machinery may think. Since then, the so-called Turing Test has been understood as if “the machine [try to] pretend to be a man […] it will pass only if the pretence is reasonably convincing” (Copeland, 2000/2003, p. 8/526).

This challenged psychology in the nineteen fifties. Behaviourism dominated American psychology and intelligence was very much defined in line with a behaviouristic learning process. Thus, Turing’s machinery game followed a quite straight Stimuli/Response model as a model for learning, and there is a silent agreement about 1956 as the year when cognitive science was born. This included a deep interest in achieving Artificial Intelligence as well (Copeland, 2000/2003; Miller, 2003). John McCarthy’s “Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence” was arranged that year. According to Copeland (2000/2003), this arrangement “gave the emerging field its name” (p. 1/519). This project included also Marvin Minsky and Claude Shannon, who both had been important contributors in developing information theory at the time. In September the same year, Massachusetts Institute of Technology supported by IBM arranged a seminar, in which the first day, the 10th, concentrated on information theory and coding. According to Miller (2003), it was the second day, the 11th Sept. when Noam Chomsky presented his forthcoming thesis Syntactic Structures (Chomsky 1957/1975), the birth of cognitive sciences took place. Miller said so because “Chomsky was the first linguist to back up the claim [saying] that language has all the precision of mathematics” (Miller, 2003, pp. 142–143). However, Chomsky’s later introduction of the concept of competence (Chomsky, 1965/1976) became a stumble block for understanding artificial intelligence as a model for human thinking. Competence refers to predisposed human knowledge of grammaticalness, which presupposes an active human creative mind that is free to express “indefinitely many thoughts” (Chomsky, 1965/1976, p. 6).

The idea of machinery intelligence intensified tremendously after the Second World War. The main question was to what extent a machinery entity could have the capacity of replicating human intelligence. The two underlying questions related to this concerned the nature of human cognition on the one hand and the potential in machinery intelligence on the other. The bridge between machinery and human spheres has always been related to the role of mathematics and logic. Moreover, the latter may very well also lead to the core of a historical understanding of the human mind, namely to what extent it is governed by logical reasoning described and modelled by mathematics. These are in other words the questions that are pursued in this paper, namely to what extent there are in fact connections between the human mind and the way AI is working today. From a historical perspective, the understanding of thinking, consciousness and the mind has been split up in rather different, and even contradictory perspectives. This is why it is important to consider some historical aspects, as there have been presented different perspectives on the nature of the human mind in the Western history of psychology.

Different Forms of Explanations

There is, however, another aspect that immediate appears as a fundamental question when we talk about artificial intelligence, and that is how to define it. A definition can very well be regarded as an explanation, as the aim of both is to achieve an understanding by means of uniting two different statements (Toulmin, 1963, p. 18). Alan Turing’s article from 1950 is a good example. It has been stated that Turing did not offer a definition of machinery intelligence (Copeland, 2000/2003, p. 4/522). However, there are several different types of definitions, and the Turing Test is a typical example of what is called an operational definition (Føllesdal & Walløe, 1977/2017, p. 238), in which definiendum is defined by an operation, i.e. passing the Turing Test. This definition is at the same time an explanation of an intelligent act, namely if something appears as an intelligent act, it must be regarded as an intelligent act. This is a functional explanation, as fulfilling its function counts as a sufficient reason for what it is (Elster, 1979).

Functional explanations have been central in psychology as long as it has pretended to be a science, quite simply because consciousness and thinking cannot be observed or measured directly. Consequently, functional explanations appear most consistently in behaviourism, which disregards all psychological terms, but just observable terms. William James’ understanding of feelings may also count as an illuminating example, as James says: “My thesis […] is that the bodily changes follow directly the PERCEPTION of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes that occur IS the emotion” (James, 1884, pp. 189–190, original italics). This has normally been interpreted as if emotions do not exist unless they are expressed through an observable behaviour. Thus, according to James, emotions can primarily be explained as a function of behaviour.

This theory is also called the James-Lange theory because the Danish physicist and neurologist Carl Georg Lange (1834–1900) published a comparable theory in a thesis from 1885, entitled: “Om Sindsbevægelser” (Wassmann, 2010). In English: “On Emotions: A Psycho-Physiological Study”, which is a direct translation of the German version published in Leipzig in 1887 (Wassmann, 2010, p. 990). However, Lange was a physicist and the founding father of Danish neurology. He was primarily interested in finding physiological processes related to emotions. “Lange held that vascular changes were essential to generate emotional feelings” (Wassmann, 2010, p. 977). His stand was comparable with James’, as they both doubted emotions solely being entrenched in the mind. In contrast to James who just referred to physiological investigation as an option, Lange pursued the measurable physiological activities that could be related to emotions. Consequently, Lange did not present a functional explanation of emotions, but searched for a causal explanation, in which demonstrated physiological processes had impact on the mind. Although James hoped to do the same in his statement, he did not refer to anything else than behaviour. In this perspective, there are differences between William James’ theory of emotions from 1884 and Carl Georg Lange’s theory of emotions from 1885. The most important reason is that James was highly influenced by the Philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce, whom he met already in the 1860ies. Peirce founded the so-called American pragmatism. James is often regarded as a cofounder of this philosophical position, and it had tremendous impact on American psychology as William James actively promoted pragmatism (James, 2017). But it was Peirce who formulated the so-called pragmatic thesis for the first time in his seminal article from 1878, entitled “How to Make our Ideas Clear”. In this article, Pierce concluded:

It appears then, that the rule for attaining the third grade of clearness of apprehension is as follows: Consider what effects, which might be conceivable have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object. (Peirce, 1878/1986, p. 266.)

Peirce says here that an object is a function of the effect an object may have on our conception of it. This is a statement James very often referred to, and the application of this thesis may very well end up in functional explanations, as it does not refer to a cause, but to the experienced outcome. The difference between Peirce and James, however, is that Peirce underlined the process of conceptualizing, whereas James stressed the observable effect.

The Computational Mind

This said, there should be two very different perspectives on how to understand the relationship between human and machinery thinking. The Turing Test only asks if a machine is experienced as if it thinks. This indicates a functional explanation of the relationship between human and machinery thinking. A neuroscientific perspective, on the other hand, may rather suggest that human thinking can be modelled and explained by means of machinery thinking. Those two perspectives are very different. The one says that Artificial Intelligence functions as if it is human thinking, whereas the other says that human thinking works in the same way as machinery thinking.

Already in the late eighties, one of the students of Noam Chomsky, Ray S. Jackendoff (1945 -) published a seminal study on the relationship between a Chomsky-inspired understanding of the consciousness and the concept of the computational mind (Jackendoff, 1987). There he gives a clear presentation of what the computational mind is supposed to be about: “The computational theory of mind grows out of the conception of the brain as an information processing device, analogous to a computer” (Jackendoff, 1987, p. 15, italics added). Although this was formulated almost 40 years ago, it is still valid as a common prevalent understanding of the relationship between Artificial Intelligence and the human mind. Computers are considered as comparable with the human mind in the sense that they work in comparable ways. Jackendoff, however, argued against this perspective by referring to the creative activity of the human mind, which is easily traceable in human’s use of language, but also in other sorts of human expressions, like in music (Jackendoff, 1987). Yet, the free creative will as such is not so much a factor in computers unless they hallucinate, which may appear as a problem. The human factor in human-computer interaction conferences sponsored by the computer industry is normally limited to how humans make use of their own creativity in the interaction with computers (se for example: CHI2023 https://programs.sigchi.org/chi/2023/program/all#97348), In line with this, a more or less unarticulated premise for human-computer interactions may seem to presuppose a concurrence of the rationality of the human mind and the rationality that governs computers.

This is the question to be pursued here, namely if the hypothesis is true saying that the human mind is only governed by classical logic. An acceptance of the functional explanation of intelligence, such as the Turing test, will easily confirm this hypothesis. But such a confirmation may very well include that the hypothesis is just apparently corroborated. If so, an error called Type 2 in statistics can very well be the case, namely that the test of the hypothesis fails to reject the hypothesis. In this case, however, the hypothesis cannot be statistically tested. Instead, this paper will look at some historical aspects of European psychology to see if the understanding of the human mind during the last 500 years can be reduced to something comparable with a computational understanding of the mind.

Different Calculation Systems

Computation concerns primarily mathematical calculations. This may appear as an overwhelming field with different systems. Normally, we refer to a decimal system, in which we operate with ten different numbers or digits. From a mathematical perspective, some would say the twelve-digit system should work better than the decimal system. The twelve-digit system is also supposed to be the oldest one, and we find reminiscences of it in our calculations of time. Anyway, computers apply neither of the two systems, but a third one, namely the binary number system. In the binary system the numbers or digits are narrowed down to be just two: Zero (0) and one (1). The binary system is almost impossible to work with for a human mind, but for an electronic mind, it is just perfect, as the electronic information is translated and then reduced to be represented by just ‘on’ (as 1) and ‘off’ (as 0).

For Turing and his colleagues in the early 1950ies, one of the crucial questions for machinery intelligence was the speed in which the machine could count. In the 40ies and the 50ies, all machinery calculations took a lot of time. The more complicated calculations, the more time it consumed. In this perspective, they said the practical use of machinery intelligence belonged to the future – around within 50 years, they hoped (Copeland, 2000/2003). And they were right. Around the year 2000, the microchips performed calculations very, very fast, and they also became so small that even cell phones were able to perform processes that required enormous computers just 30 years earlier.

Another important aspect of the binary number system is that it reflects one of the most important criteria for logical reasoning, namely that a declarative statement must be either true or not true (Snell, 1982, p. 192). This is a sine qua non – an absolute necessary requirement in classical logic. This correspondence between a fundamental criterion for logical reasoning and the binary number system is in principle arbitrary. However, this is not only a convenient coincidence, but it points to the core of the question about machinery intelligence; namely if human intelligence can be defined in terms of classical logical thinking.

Digits and Thinking

Before examining the role of logical thinking in human intelligence, we must dwell with the two types of explanations that are presented above. As mentioned, with a functionalistic approach to machinery intelligence, it does not matter if the machine is based on a type of logic completely different from the human mind’s reasoning, or not. With this approach, a machine is intelligent if it is experienced as intelligent by a human being. However, the neuropsychological perspective on this, which follows up Carl Langes’ project, but also dominates very much of the research that aims at investigating the computational mind, aspires towards tracing a causal connection between the human nerve-system and the human mind. Moreover, this research in combination with the label ‘the computational mind’ aims in other words with not only regarding the computer as a model for human thinking, but also how the nerve-system functions.

Some would say McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 “were the first to suggest, “neural activity is computation, and that neural activity can explain cognition” (Piccinini & Bahar, 2013, p. 454). However, this depends on how computation is understood. A computer of today is solely based on mathematical calculations by means of digits. In this sense, this statement is suggested to be true. The role of digits forms the core in a discussion about the computational mind, namely to what extent it can be characterized as digital or analogue. The main and crucial difference between a digital and an analogue system is that the former is characterized by operating with separate and discontinuous digits, whereas analogue processes are characterized by continuity. This does not imply, however, that only digital calculations are mathematical. Infinitesimal calculation, for example, which forms the basis for differential equations present continuity, and are crucial in all mathematical calculations and models in physics. In other words, this discussion is not a question about applying mathematics as such in the understanding of human cognition, but to what extent the human mind does in fact operate in the same way as a computer calculates.

In line with this, logic may also refer to different ways of reasoning. However, in a historical perspective the term refers to what is called “classical logic” (Shapiro & Kissel 2024). When logic was referred to above as comparable with the binary number system, it is restricted to classical logic. The similarities between the two are completely arbitrary, but they appear as similar because they both refer to an either/or: true or not true, 0 or 1, On or Off. Classical logic is first based on deductions, which implies a general valid statement as the first premise, (‘All humans are mortal’), a very specific statement as the second premise, (‘Socrates is a human’), and a conclusion, which is given by necessity (‘Socrates is mortal). Such an inference is either true or false, and a third outcome is not possible – tertium non datur. The revival of this classical logic is recognizable in Popper’s theory of falsification, in which the general first premise is the hypothesis, and the second premise must be a falsifiable statement (Popper, 1980). However there exists also a “deviant” logic (Borchert, 2006), which problematizes classical logic and opens for a third outcome. Deviant logic tells us that to be logical is a more open question.

A Historical Perspective on the Term ‘psychology’

Most likely, humans must have reflected on the nature of the human soul from the very beginning. However, when we look at the understanding of the soul in the ancient Greek, it is obvious that the soul was regarded as consisting of two parts: the human mental capacity (animus) and its spiritual part (anima, see Mengal, 2005). Aristotle’s thesis on the soul discusses the human mental capacities which can be divided into two main parts: Thinking and sensation. The border between those two capacities is difficult to define, so he is primarily discussing the relationship between them, but he concentrates on thinking. He also has a small comment on the spiritual aspects of the soul. After having discussed the two capacities of thinking and sensation, he points at another part of the soul which do not expire when the body dies, but this part is not of interest to discuss here, he says (Aristotle 1907, p. 57; II.2, 413b). It is an open question if this thesis On the Soul of Aristotle is a psychological thesis. Most likely, it is not, as he is not discussing other crucial psychological aspects, like feelings, for example. This does not mean he is not aware of feelings, as they are thoroughly discussed in the Rhetoric and On Poetry. The aim of On the Soul is most likely to make a foundation for his philosophical project, which included his metaphysics (Klempe, 2020). In line with this, logic is close to metaphysics in the sense that both investigate truth: logic evaluates truth in propositions and inferences, and metaphysics decide the level of truth in our conception of the world as such.

Some would say logic is about the laws that govern thinking (Alnes, 2025). This understanding implies a very close and almost inseparable connection between psychology and logic, which the theory of the computational mind also assumes. However, one of the factors that paved the way for defining psychology as an independent science in the sixteenth century was a new understanding of the human’s capacity of thinking as something that goes beyond the rules for classical logic. The Dutch Humanist Rodolphus Agricola (1443/44–1485) was one of those who presented this new perspective in De Inventione Dialectica libri tres, published posthumously in 1497. In this thesis he argues systematically for how the classical Aristotelian logic can be reconciled with a rhetorical discourse (Muller, 2001). According to Aristotle, the two subjects stand in opposition to each other, as logic is based on deductions, whereas in rhetoric induction, examples and incomplete inferences are fully accepted. This strict border was challenged by Agricola, and classical logic was slightly dethroned as a source for understanding human thinking.

For some decades, the Croatian poet and humanist Marco Maruliç (1450–1524) was regarded to be the first one to apply the neologism ‘psychology’ at the beginning of the sixteenth century when he allegedly published his work Psichologia de ratione animae humanae (Psychology, The Rationality of the Human Soul) (Krstic, 1964). However, this work has never been found, so this title is what stands left after Maruliç. Instead, Janssen and Hubbard (2021) have published parts of another manuscript from the same time, namely 1525. This is authored by the Italian humanist Pier Nicola Castellani (unknown born and death). In this translated excerpt Castellani says, “the whole science about the soul [tota scientia de anima] […] is called Psychology [psychologia]” (Castellani in Janssen & Hubbard, 2021, p. 185, emphases in the original). This is elaborated on and more exactly defined by saying, “Psychology [psychologia] is said by the Greeks to be in the middle between Physics and Metaphysics” (Castellani in Janssen & Hubbard, 2021, p. 185.) There is also an essential phrase later in this excerpt, saying, “Wherefore in this way the science about the soul [scientia de anima] is of a middle quality, just as mathematics and astronomy are of a middle quality” (Castellani in Janssen & Hubbard, 2021, p. 185). In other words, psychology was in the early sixteenth century understood as a science that was in the middle between physics and metaphysics, which implies that the soul was understood as a science that either united the spiritual and material parts of the human nature or a science beyond metaphysics and physics. The former understanding is most likely, as psychology gradually became a science of the interaction between mind and sensation, which is entrenched in the body (Klempe, 2020).

Psychology and the Entrance of Humanism and Subjectivity

The movement of humanism provided this new understanding of the human being, in which the soul is not limited to concern just the human mind, but rather the interchange between the body and the mind. Thus, the soul was considered to permeate the whole body (Petersen, 1921/2015). This is most likely what lies behind Castellani’s formulation saying that psychology is a science that exists “in the middle between Physics and Metaphysics” (Castellani in Janssen & Hubbard, 2021, p. 185). In other words, when Aristotle talks about the intellect as representing a distinct faculty of the soul separated from the body (Aristotle 1907, p. 57; II.2, 413b), he emphasizes that humans have an intellectual capacity to execute logical inferences that are abstracted from sensation and feelings. But neither the intellect nor the soul is limited to just this capacity. Consequently, human thinking cannot be restricted to classical logic.

There is another crucial aspect embedded in the statement saying that psychology is a science standing in the middle between metaphysics and physics. It is hard to tell which relationship psychology may have to each one of them. On the one hand psychology was regarded as being close to both, but it also stands in opposition to both. The key to understand this lies partly in Castellini’s comparison of psychology with mathematics and astronomy. Most likely, this reflects the role of the so-called seven liberal arts had in the medieval time. These arts had dominated all higher education as a propaedeutic group of disciplines all students had to go through. This group of seven different subjects was divided in two: (1) trivium, consisting of grammar, rhetoric and dialectic; (2) quadrivium, consisting of arithmetic, geometry, astronomy and music. The seven liberal arts reflected a Pythagorean-Platonic type of metaphysics that had dominated. Quadrivium was regarded as the most important group of disciplines. They regarded the essence of each one of the subjects in quadrivium to be magnitude and numbers. Arithmetic represents magnitude by itself. Geometry represents immobile magnitude, astronomy the mobile magnitude, and music the relation between magnitudes (Hugh of St. Victor, 1961, p. 67).

However, when Humanism emerged as a movement in Europe after the 13th century, trivium became gradually even as important as quadrivium, and this change in European thinking paved the way for focusing on psychology, which rather exists in the middle of, not only metaphysics and physics, but all human experiences of the world and the individual. This changed the focus from objectivity to subjectivity, and the appearance of the term ‘psychology’ is at the same time an acceptance of subjectivity in European thinking. The Reformation of the church is the best example, as it changed focus from the church to the individual’s personal faith.

Three Different Perspectives on Psychology in Relation to Logic and Metaphysics (1568–1576)

In line with these changes, some confusions about the role and the content of metaphysics in philosophy can be traced. Since metaphysics is supposed to form the basis for all other sciences, very many sciences were reconsidered and placed in jeopardy for more than 200 years. In the 16th and 17th centuries psychology existed more or less as a ghost discipline in the background, and very few scholars dared to apply the term. The British empiricists are probably the best example. But also, in the Latin countries very few scholars used this term. As Janssen and Hubbard (2021) points at, the north German abbed Gerhard Synellius (1470–1552) also developed a psychology in his Hortulus oratorium, of which we only have preserved a second edition from 1525, but most likely the first edition can be traced back to 1512 (Janssen & Hubbard, 2021). Thus, the neologism ‘psychology’ was widespread in Europe already at the beginning of the sixteenth century, but it was a challenge to find exactly what it was supposed to be about and how it could be related to other disciplines.

The German pedagogue and polymath Johann Thomas Freigius’ publications in the time span between 1568 and 1576 can illustrate the confusion in a quite illuminative way. In this timespan of just 8 years, he published three different schemes about how the different philosophical disciplines could be sorted. The first from 1568 (see Fig. 1) presents the seven liberal arts in the same way as it had been distributed for more than 700 years before him:

Freigius 1568, after Lamanna, 2010, p. 309

The medieval seven liberal arts are prevailed and kept intact by featuring trivium at the top and quadrivium at the bottom. In the middle, we see metaphysics included in the same group as “Libri de animi”. The fact that they are grouped together underlines the suggestion that Aristotle’s thesis On the Soul (Aristotle, 1907) was primarily considered as a basis for metaphysics. A third aspect of this scheme is that logic is not included. This might be an effect of the fact that Freigius had met Pierre de la Ramée (1515–1572), or Petrus Ramus, who was a famous convert to Calvinism at that time because he had started a big movement against Aristotelian logic. Consequently, Ramée followed up Agricola’s work on not making a clear distinction between rhetoric and logic. The combination of the two had partly dominated the “dialectica” in trivium for a long time, and in this scheme, dialectics seem to have taken over for logic, as the latter has obviously been left out.

In this scheme from 1574 (see Fig. 2) the term ‘psychologia’ is applied. The interesting coincidence with the use of this term is that the seven liberal arts in its original form has evaporated completely. Another interesting aspect is the inclusion of ‘logica’. There are reasons to underline that it is said to be a part of metaphysics: “Rationem, Logica sub qua metaphysica” (Freigius in Lamanna, 2010, p. 310). There are no reasons to think that Freigius changed his understanding of logic, as it appears under a headline referring to linguistic based instruments. Mathematics is, however, in a completely different category related to nature and the physical reality. Psychology, on the other hand, appears under “Mores, ut Ethica”, which refers to the ethical reality.

Freigius 1574, after Lamanna, 2010, p. 310

The third scheme of Freigius (see Fig. 3) is from 1576 and represents a quite new understanding of the different disciplines. Psychology does not belong to the ethical sphere anymore, but rather to the sensational, physical sphere (“qualitatem=Physica”). Music belongs to the same category, and is no longer regarded as a mathematical science, but as a sensational and physical science. The same is true for astronomy (“astrologia”). Mathematics is reduced to consisting of arithmetic and geometry. Trivium is almost kept intact, except for dialectics, which is replaced with “Poetica”. There are reasons to highlight that logic here belongs to metaphysics. This does not necessarily represent a change in Freigius’ understanding of logic, but rather as an expression for a change in the understanding of metaphysics. The latter is still supposed to form the basis for scientific knowledge, but this basis does no longer represent an indisputable truth based on just unequivocal statements, but includes examples and conclusions based on more unclear premises instead.

Freigius 1576, after Luccio, 2013 p.7

The differences between these schemes of Freigius from the latter half of the sixteenth century summarize in many ways the crucial changes in European ideas that took place during the sixteenth century. All these factors pointed towards the European modernity. Consequently, European modernity must be understood in close connection with the entrance of psychology as a more specified discipline. The biggest change that opens for modernity is that metaphysics is no longer based on abstract sciences like mathematics and classical logic discerned from physics and the physical part of the world but instead seems to include the human mind’s way of processing sensible qualities. This is a consequence of what Castellini highlighted, namely that psychology stands in the middle of metaphysics and physics. This paves the way for empiricism as a kind of new metaphysics in philosophy. And this forms also the basis for artificial intelligence of today, as it represents primarily linguistic models based on an almost endlessly amount of big data. In line with this, the renaissance psychology founded one important aspect of the basis for artificial intelligence of today, as it allowed relying on sense impressions as such.

Artificial intelligence, or more correctly, machinery thinking, presupposes that thinking is defined and governed by the rules of classical logic. However, the renaissance psychology stands in direct contradiction to this assumption, as it instead challenges classical logic. Neither Agricola nor Ramée accepted any clear distinction between logic and rhetoric. This implies that examples, enthymemes (i.e. inferences with unclear premises), and inductions have all to be included in what we can accept as logical inferences. Especially induction is what Francis Bacon (1561–1626) built upon in his New Organon (Bacon, 2000) – the new logic from 1620, which formed the philosophical basis for British empiricism. AI is also based on inductions, as even big data is limited. Thus, the linguistic models that are applied in AI must induce and generalize. AI requires that all new statements can be generated from a limited amount, which big data after all represent.

The Digital vs. the Analogue

Artificial Intelligence faces today challenges comparable with challenges European intellectuals faced in early modernity. One of these is the role of digits and to what extent digits in mathematics represent an adequate understanding of the nature of humans. A digit is not restricted to the binary number system but is a term that refers to a distinct differentiation of each number. A string of digits represents a discontinuity, as the transition from one digit to another includes an abruption or a jump. Not all mathematics are digital in this sense. There is also a long historical tradition for the so-called infinitesimal calculus (Baron, 1969), which says there can be a continuous mathematical line from one digit – for example 0 – to another digit – for example 1.

Since psychology was said to represent a merge of thinking and sensation, the empirical orientation in European modernity was a consequence of the entrance of psychology as an independent academic discipline. This called for a need to find mathematical expressions for the continuity that obviously exists in nature. It was on this basis the so-called differential calculus was invented late in the seventeenth century. Both Isaac Newton (1643–1727) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) discovered independently this type of calculus. The two developed this mathematics in slightly different ways as well. Newton was interested in capturing bodies’ movement in physics, whereas Leibniz was more interested in capturing the unity and continuity between everything existing in this world. Thus, the differential calculus was supposed to modeling the dynamics in continuity and movements that permeates the whole world.

This type of calculus became crucial for the development of natural science, and it is still the most applicable mathematical device in very many research areas. Although it has dominated modeling in natural sciences, it has also played an important role in the development of psychology, especially before 1900. However, in this context a certain aspect of it that can enlighten our understanding of machinery intelligence. Although a computer is the best device to perform differential calculations, its logic is not based on differential calculus, as its logic is based on digits. This demonstrates the fundamental distinction between what is digital and what is analog, namely that the former is based on a mathematical system that operates with distinct digits, whereas the latter must be based on a system that requires seamless continuity. Differential calculations may contribute to produce the latter, but computers based on this system are not available yet, although the future-oriented quantum-computers aim to apply this type of calculations to process data.

However, so far, these computers are not fully available, and it is still an open question if they anyway will manage to provide a complete replica of the human mind. Still, there is a saying among scientists, which is in line with what the American psychologist Karl Lashley formulated in 1958, namely that the “brain is an analogical machine, not digital” (Piccinini & Bahar, 2013, p. 453). Piccinini and Bahar are anyway discussing to what extent the human nervous system can be digital, and after having evaluated research on this, their conclusion is quite clear: “typical neural signals are neither continuous signals nor strings of digits” (Piccinini &Bahar, 2013, p. 453). They follow up Lange’s search for a strong connection between the neural system and human cognition, but to understand the neural system, we must search beyond the digital and maybe also beyond the analogical sphere.

The Acceptance of Psychology in Philosophy

Although psychology is a term that can be traced back to the early sixteenth century, it took more than 200 years to be an acceptable term in the philosophical discourse. The German enlightenment philosopher Christian Wolff (1679–1754) was the one that included psychology in philosophy by publishing Psychologia empirica in 1732. Wolff was a highly respected professor at the Protestant university in Halle, but he provoked the pietists there so much that he had to flee the city and accepted a chair at the Protestant University in Marburg instead. Wolff had been a friend of Leibniz and posited a chair in mathematics and physics in Halle. But he was also highly inspired by Leibniz’ aim of restoring metaphysics adjusted to the new times, in which empirical and experimental approaches was on the way to be a fashion in Europe. Leibniz had tried to write a renewed metaphysics, but he had not completed this effort. When Wolff came to Marburg, ‘psychology’ had been a well-known term since the earlier rector of this university, Rudolph Goclenius (1547–1628), had published a popular anthology on psychology already in 1590. This was about the human nature seen from both theological and philosophical perspectives (Mengal, 2005).

One of the reasons for regarding Wolff as the philosopher of the enlightenment is that the more than twenty books he published in Halle were all in German and quite popular. He started up a new writing project In Marburg, in which the aim was to work out a metaphysics in Latin. One factor that triggered this project was probably that Goclenius was also the one who had coined the neologism ‘ontology’. He did so in an encyclopedic article from 1610 (Klempe, 2020). When Wolff aimed at systematizing a new metaphysics, he published a volume on ontology in 1730, on cosmology in 1731, on empirical psychology in 1732, on rational psychology in 1734, and finally two volumes on natural theology in 1736 and 1737, respectively. These 6 volumes constituted the complete system of metaphysics, and this row of publications made psychology to one of the core aspects of metaphysics in the eighteenth century.

Just before the publication on ontology from 1730, he had also published a volume on logic. Most likely, his intention was to include logic as a part of metaphysics, but at the end, it was not included. The most important and revolutionizing part of this metaphysics was the inclusion of Psychologia empirica (Wolff, 1732). This is a treatise that aims at explaining how sensation can be a reliable source for acquiring scientifically valid knowledge. This reflected the core of the turning point in modernity; specifically, that scientific knowledge can be based on empirical investigations. The Wolffian understanding of metaphysics became very popular in North Europe in the eighteenth century. Very many scholars in philosophy wrote a textbook on metaphysics following the same pattern as Wolff had prescribed. One of these was Alexander Baumgarten (1714–1762), who wrote a one-volume Metaphysics in 1739. A German translation of this was the textbook Immanuel Kant used for his students when he lectured on metaphysics (Klempe, 2025).

Competing Perspectives on Psychology ss a Science

It is obvious that logic was important to Wolff. But he argued for making natural science empirical. One of his students, the Russian polymath and chemist, Lev Lomonosov (1711–1765) “refers to Wolff as the one who inspired him to not accept any theories unless they were rooted in experiments” (Klempe, 2025, p. 34). Wolff’s intention with including psychologia empirica as a part of metaphysics was to justify sense-based observations as a necessary source for acquiring scientific knowledge. But it was of course not a sufficient reason, as observations and sense impressions must be validated by logical inferences and other critical operations. A lot of empirical research was performed during the eighteenth century – also in psychology. This is best documented in a series in ten volumes presenting results from empirical research in psychology. Karl Philipp Moritz founded and edited this series, which was published annually under the label “Magazine for Experimental Knowledge of the Soul (Know yourself)”, from 1783 to 1793 (Klempe, 2025, p. 73).

Although Alexander Baumgarten followed up Wolff with writing a Wolff-inspired textbook on metaphysics, his interest was not in natural sciences, but in poetry. His treatise on poetry from 1735 opened for a peculiar understanding on the role of language, which may very clearly contradict classical logic (Baumgarten 1735/1968). Baumgarten made a distinction between intensive and extensive conceptions. The former refers to classical, deductive logic, which represents intensive clearness by narrowing down and limiting the content of the concept. Extensive clearness, however, refers to the opposite quality of a concept, namely to emphasize the richness of a concept: “§ XVI If the conception in A is conceived as richer than in B, C, D etc., and yet they all are confusing, then A will be EXTENSIVELY CLEARER than the others” (Baumgarten 1735/1968, p. 95). Extensive clearness implies that A appears as dim and obscure. This is not a negative outcome, as it stimulates the reader or listener’s sensitivity. Thus, Baumgarten makes a clear distinction between the sensitive, which implies an attention to the richness in an expression, and the sensual, which is an undifferentiated impression of something. Dimness and obscurity may also be present in arithmetic, as the number 10 is more definite than 2 + 2+3 + 3, although they express the same, namely 10. By challenging logic and mathematics in this way, but also by combining this with the aspect of beauty, Baumgarten also founded the basis for modern aesthetics in general. Here he also coined the term “aestheticological”, which refers to extensive clarity as a sensitive dimension of the process of perception (Baumgarten, 2007). Both the term and Baumgarten’s understanding of the poetic language characterized by extensive clarity, challenge classical logic. Moreover, they challenge a perspective saying that human thinking is governed by the rules of classical logic.

The Free Will

Another challenge for philosophy in the early Western modernity was how to justify the human free will. This appeared as an important factor in the Protestant theology, as the individual was expected to have a free choice, not only to be a Christian, but also to execute good and evil acts. René Descartes (1596–1750) probably gave the critical defence for a human free will in this respect. He pointed at the fact that a human being may very well make errors, but they, he says, “arise from this cause alone, that I do not restrain the will, which is of much wider range than the understanding” (Descartes, 1912, p. 114). Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) partially followed Descartes’ way of reasoning by saying, God “determines our will to choose what appears as best, yet without necessitating it” (Leibniz, 1998, p. 81). Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), on the other hand, connects the free will to the sphere of morality, and discusses it a lot, not least in the Critique of Pure Reason, as the free will by necessity stands in contradiction to causality. He prevails causality as a necessary aspect of the understanding of nature, yet Freedom nevertheless exists as a regulative transcendental idea: “Freedom is treated here only as a transcendental idea, through which reason thinks of the series of conditions in appearance starting absolutely through what is sensibly unconditioned, but thereby involves itself in an antinomy following its own laws, which it prescribes for the empirical use of the understanding (Kant, 1781/1998, p. 546(A558/B586). We are still captured in this antinomy, but also in a favour of the free will, which is also embedded in Chomsky’s conception of creativity (Chomsky, 1965/1976).

Kant and Herbart

Even though Kant applied Baumgarten’s metaphysics as the preferred textbook for his students, he did not agree too much with Baumgarten’s thinking. Nevertheless, Kant’s considerations on the body and the mind are interesting in this context. Kant would not make a clear distinction between the two, so, after having thrown empirical psychology out of metaphysics in his Critique of Pure Reason, he suggested that empirical psychology should be subordinated to rational physiology instead (Kant 1781/1998, p. 699, A846/B874). In his lectures on anthropology, he refers to ganglia, which are the collection of nerve cells that transmit impulses from the central nervous system to control the organs (Klempe, 2025, p. 74). According to Kant’s understanding of the contemporaneous Scottish medical doctor James Johnstone (1730–1802), “the ganglia are to be considered as little brains, which, however, all remain under the brain’s control” (Kant, 2012, p. 463).

The distinction between psychology and physiology remained an open question in psychology during the whole 19th century. In line with this, the successor to Kant’s chair in Königsberg, Johann Friedrich Herbart (1776–1842) followed Kant by focusing on a dynamic understanding of mental representations (Vorstellungen). Whereas Kant ended up with the twelve categories of understanding as the inevitable basis for what we can count as true cognition, Herbart focused much more on the processes that produce the representations or ideas we may have. Although we have in fact an abundance of ideas and representations, not all of them can be present in one’s mind at the same time. In fact, just one can dominate the mind, but at the same time, we experience also that another idea goes along in a dialogue with the one that is dominating. Moreover, other associations and thoughts can replace both. Thus, the question for him was to exam these dynamics of the mind that in fact produce the different opinions and attitudes we may have, but also the fact that they may change.

There are a couple of aspects in Herbart’s understanding about the mind which are relevant in this context. One is how he envisaged the mind to go through processes. This stands in contradiction to the more static faculty psychology Wolff presented and Kant assumed and indirectly followed. The other is Herbart’s suggestion of a mathematical equation for modelling this process. Since just one of the two ideas one may have in mind can be dominant, the two must have different weights or strengths.

Herbart was influenced by Newton, who applied the two terms ‘mechanics’ and ‘equilibrium’ in physics. The former refers to the conflicts that exist between different forces in nature, as they are pulling in different directions. This makes a dynamic understanding of nature, and it explains why things are changing all the time. To express this mathematically he applied differential equations, which can express the continuous changes that exist when a body is moving in a certain speed. Equilibrium refers to a situation, in which movement ceases, and the body stands still. That is when all the active forces are in balance with each other.

Herbart envisaged a similar process in the mind, as our thoughts always are in movement and may change. But when we have made up our mind, we stand left with a stable dominant opinion. This can very well be contrasted with an opposite opinion, which is refused, but anyway exists in balance with the dominant one and therefore establishes an equilibrium of the mind. Moreover, there exist an abundance of ideas, opinions and thoughts in one’s memory, but to preserve the balanced equilibrium between one dominating and the secondary subordinated idea, the other are all inhibited. This understanding of the mind assumes that the ideas are charged with forces by having different strengths and consequently are comparable with mechanics in physics. On this basis, Herbart also envisaged that differential equations could be able to express this process of the dynamics of the forces of human ideas. The problem, however, was that it is impossible to measure the strength of an idea, and by this calculate how the battle between the different ideas may end up.

Classical and Connectionist Architecture of the Mental State

Several scholars have criticized Herbart’s understanding of the process of producing ideas in the human mind. Nevertheless, it had an impact on experimental psychology in the nineteenth century but was almost completely ignored in psychology in the twentieth century. However, at the end of the twentieth century comparable thoughts appeared without any references to Johan Friedrich Herbart, though. Nevertheless, the idea of neurons can be traced back to the time when Herbart was still alive, but the study of neurons appeared with the electron microscope in the 1950ies. Since then, it has been confirmed that a neuron is electrically charged with a microscopic voltage – both negative and positive. Consequently, the electric communication between the united neurons forms the synapses, but also the nerve network that unites brain and body. Since the 1980ies, one of the harshest discussions in artificial intelligence has been about how to understand the role of neurons in the human mind.

When Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) suggested a distinction between classical and what they called the connectionist architecture of the representational mental state, they argued for a classical architecture, which is “committed to a symbol-level of representation, or to a ‘language of thought’: i.e., to representational states that have combinatorial syntactic and semantic structure” (Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988, p. 3). Connectionism, on the other hand, “may provide an account of the neural (or ‘abstract neurological’) structures” (Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988, p. 3). In other words, this distinction reflects the classical distinction between top-down and bottom-up perspectives, in which Fodor and Pylyshyn argued for a creative and free human factor that can never be reduced to just positive and negative electric voltages in the neurons. The two different perspectives require two very different approaches in research. The classical architecture implies that the understanding of meaning in a sentence can only be provided by a living human mind, which can recognize meaning as such. Connectionism, on the other hand, presupposes that the mind’s awareness of meaning can be detected by measuring voltage activities in the highly complex network of neurons.

Back to the 19th Century’s Experimental Psychology

The understanding that lies behind Fodor and Pylyshyn’s (1988) ‘classical architecture’ is more in line with the 19th Century’s understanding, as it was completely impossible to measure brain activity back then. Gustav Theodor Fechner (1801–1887) took this fact seriously into account. He tried to develop an experimental approach in which it is clarified what is measurable and what is not. This is the background for his Psychophysics (Fechner, 1860). Fechner thought that there are strong connections in the whole physical and mental world. Consequently, he suggested that there are no clear distinctions between nature and culture. Nevertheless, there is a crucial principal distinction between the two anyway, as nature by necessity appears as an object for a human, whereas the cultural product oneself is a part of represents a subjective creation. His psychophysics (Fechner, 1860) is an effort to take this distinction seriously, which implies that we can measure an object (i.e. physics), but when we perceive the object, a subjective process takes place within an individual’s immeasurable human mind. Consequently, the connection between our understanding of the object and the object itself must be there, but they are not completely coinciding. Thus, there must be a fundamental difference between a subject and an object, although there must also be some kind of connections as well.

This forms the background for the so-called Fechner-Weber’s law. Fechner talked only about Weber’s law. The reason is quite simply that Weber had already found that there is a distinction between the measurable physical pressure on the skin, and the subjectively perceived pressure. Weber explained this with differences in density of nerve cells. Fechner added just a mathematical expression of this difference, as he applied a differential equation to say that the physical pressure on the skin forms linear increases of the pressure, whereas the impression of the same physical pressure is not increased in the same way, but instead gradually flattens out. The latter can be expressed mathematically with the logarithm of the same measured series of numbers. The Gap Between Physiology and Mind Today

There is a long tradition in not making a clear distinction between psychology and physiology. Although this tradition can be traced back to Kant, it is most likely older than that. As mentioned, the first use of the term ‘psychology’ in the sixteenth century emphasized that psychology is in between metaphysics and physics. Fechner is a spokesman for this tradition. Yet Fechner was also a spokesman for a principal distinction between what is measurable (physics) and what is not measurable (the subjective mind). It was this latter principal distinction that was ignored when Turing started with construing machinery intelligence, and this distinction is not very much included in the research in neural computation (Piccinini & Bahar, 2013). The idea behind neural computation is a corresponding connection between the neural system and the system that governs computers, but also that the system that governs the human mind coincides with the neural system.

Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) may stand as representatives for an opposite position, as they mean meaning must be freed from physically entrenched explanations. They represent a sort of phenomenology that was included in experimental psychology since the latter half of the 19th century. This phenomenological perspective was also an important part of the so-called cognitive revolution, as the rationalist Noam Chomsky was a dominating figure in that movement already when it was established in the middle of the nineteen fifties. In a computational theory of cognition today, the crucial question is not only to what extent the neural system is different from the system that govern human thinking, but rather if the system that govern both is in fact comparable with computers.

This said, Piccinini & Bahar’s (2013) conclusion is very clear when they say that “typical neural signals are neither continuous signals nor strings of digits” (p. 453). Instead, they refer to the so-called ‘neural spike’, which is a brief, rapid electrical pulse generated by a neuron to communicate with other neurons (Yamazaki et al., 2022). According to these authors spiking neural networks (SNN) represents the third generation of deep network theories that might bridge the gap between neuroscience and machine learning. However, according to Piccinini & Bahar (2013), the type of calculation that governs computers cannot match the human neural system. “It follows that neural computation is sui generis” (Piccinini & Bahar, 2013, p. 453). The reason for saying so is that the neural spikes have an unpredictable nature: “Whereas digital computational events are temporally discrete, spike times are values of continuous variables” (Piccinini & Bahar, 2013, p. 471). In other words, although we are approaching a more exact understanding of the neural system and its similarities with the human mind, we are not exactly there. Consequently, we may very well conclude that Artificial Intelligence cannot be called a replica of the human mind. The argument for this conclusion is embedded in this historical presentation, which demonstrates that the envisaged human mind in European psychology seems to go far beyond classical logic and therefore contradicts very much the hypothesis saying that the human mind is only governed by classical logic. The hypothesis is not directly falsified, but the historical facts speak for this, and the lack of rejecting the hypothesis most likely includes a Type 2 Error, namely that the hypothesis might be false although it has not been completely falsified, as it anyway seems to contradict the historical facts of saying how the human mind has been envisaged.

When it comes to the future, it is hard to tell what the outcome of the forthcoming quantum computers will be. It is true, though, that spikes in neurons are apparently comparable with the processes of superpositions and quantum leaps in atoms, as they are all unpredictable. If so, there can maybe be a closer connection between human thinking and machinery thinking when such computers are fully developed. However, the fact that unpredictability characterizes the free will, and we find aspects of unpredictability in both spikes and quantum leaps, does not necessarily imply that the unpredictability has the same character in all the three domains. If there are concealed differences, we may still face a Type 2 Error. If so, a machinery entrenched artificial intelligence seems to be unattainable, also in the future.

Conclusions

The main topic in this paper has been to examine to what extent the human mind is governed by classical Aristotelian logic. This is a core question in a discussion of the computational mind, as the computers’ system of today follow an either-or system as 0/1, on/off, true/false form the basic units in their electronic system. Seen from a historical perspective, the role of formal logic has not dominated the psychological understanding of the human mind. The more unpredictable symbol understanding, which for example Alexander Baumgarten saw as a precondition for understanding poetry, has in different ways dominated the psychological understanding of the human mind. Although classical symbol architecture of the mind and a more neuroscientific understanding in terms of connectionism seem to approach each other, there is no complete concurrence between the two perspectives on the human mind.

Even the unpredictable aspect of humans’ free will seems to be a factor in neurons’ behavior, as their so-called spikes, or their measurable electrical pulses also seem to be unpredictable. On this basis, digital based hardware can never produce a spiking neural network. Therefore, the Fechnerian principally distinction between the subjective human mind and measurable object is still valid, as the unpredictable free will always generate an unpredictable factor of the individual’s symbolic understanding of a sign. Moreover, when spiking neural networks (SNN) refer to excitatory and inhibitory connections, it is easy to associate Herbart’s idea about representations as being present or inhibited. This said, one may conclude that Artificial Intelligence, or more correctly, machinery thinking, presupposes that thinking is defined and governed by the rules of classical logic. However, the renaissance psychology stands in direct contradiction to this assumption, as it instead challenged classical logic by not accepting a distinction between logic and rhetoric.
