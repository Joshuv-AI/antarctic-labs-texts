Nondestructive removal of superhydrophobic coatings by an electric switch
Authors: Shuangyan Huang, Qilin Yue, Jiahao Lv, Yanan Wang, Hailei Yang, Qisheng He, Jianwen Zhang, Yong Wei, Shanlin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtensive efforts have been made to enhance the durability of superhydrophobic coatings by adhesives, yet it raises a challenge to nondestructively remove the failed coatings. Here, we propose a nondestructively removable superhydrophobic (NRS) coating by an electrical switch, inspired by the nondestructive ecdysis of snakes. To achieve this, we sequentially spray i-layer and e-layer using electro-responsively epoxy-based ionic adhesive and superhydrophobic CNT coating, respectively. Its adhesion energy to stainless steel sheets significantly declines from 2.46 kJ m−2 to 0.01 kJ m−2 (about 99.6% of removal efficiency), through the disordered-ordered switching of free ions, only applying 90 V DC voltage for 3 min. This enables it to be nondestructively removed from the substrates even after 10 recoating cycles. This work offers a common strategy to sustainably protect the metal substrates and facilitates their recycling, showing great potential in green manufacturing and surface protection for large-scale equipment.Subject terms: Bioinspired materials, Structural properties, Electronic materials, Organic molecules in materials science

Introduction

Coating is one of the important measures for metal surface protection, and holds a significant position in the field of corrosion prevention1–3. However, traditional coatings are hard to deal with the long-term service requirements of equipment in complex environments, limited by their poor water and salt resistances4–6. Therefore, superhydrophobic coatings have been extensively developed to address this challenge7–9. Its micro-nanostructures and low surface energy exhibit exceptional properties such as anti-corrosion10–12, self-cleaning13–15 and anti-icing16–18, showing great potential in various fields including petrochemical, aerospace, marine, and shipping industries19–21. Despite this, their wide applications are still limited by the poor durability, such as the fragility of micro-nanostructures and the weak adhesion to substrates22. Thus, active regeneration23–25, passive resistance26–28 and their combined strategies have been widely reported to achieve the long-term services, such as the armour strategy29 and the cellular coating30.

Although these efforts have significantly enhanced the application potentials of superhydrophobic coatings31, the currently reported ones still struggle to satisfy the long-term sustainable service requirements of equipment. Therefore, it becomes inevitable to remove and recoat the coating after its failure. Based on this, both the complicated structural design and the strong adhesion of resins bring great challenges to the removal of the coatings after failure. However, conventional removal techniques, such as blasting treatment or solvent immersion, are extremely energy-consuming and prone to generating hazardous wastes32–34. More importantly, these methods irreversibly damage the underlying substrates during the removal process35, severely restricting the reuse and recycling of the expensive metal substrates. This seems to be disastrous for the sustainable utilization of large-scale engineering equipment and facilities. Thus, it is an urgent demand to develop a durable and nondestructively removable superhydrophobic (NRS) coating to address long-term sustainable applications.

In nature, most animals have the ability to use ecdysis to repair and regenerate their skin, as represented by snakes. This nondestructive replacement achieves a sustainable protective effect, providing an important guarantee for the continuation of its life. The ecdysis of snakes provides an evolutionary optimum for adaptive surface renewal, wherein the epidermis is shed in an integrated manner to fulfill dual functional demands: enabling body growth and removing surface contaminants along with ectoparasites36. As shown in Fig. 1a, during the ecdysis, the secreted hormone tunes the migration of Na⁺ and K⁺ within the germinal layer cells via ion pumps that induce changes in membrane potentials. The generated bioelectrical signals subsequently trigger the gene expressions to synthesize specific enzymes, which selectively degrade the intermediate extracellular matrix, resulting in controllable removal of the outer epidermis37.

The robust adhesion force makes it difficult to remove from the SS sheet, which easily causes severe damage to the surface during the mechanical removal. d Digital photographs demonstrate that the NRS coating on the SS sheet is easily to be removed after applying 90 V DC voltage for 3 min, and it does not cause visible damage to SS sheet.

Inspired by this, we proposed that the coatings could be nondestructively removed using an electrical switch induced by free ion migration. Figure 1b shows the design principle of the NRS coating on a metal substrate, containing an ionic conductive i-layer that can regulate the interfacial adhesion force through the ordered migration of ions and a superhydrophobic e-layer with a conductive property. In the natural state, the disordered free ions in the i-layer can enhance its adhesion to the e-layer and the metal substrate, thereby improving the durability. When removal is needed, a DC voltage is applied to the e-layer and substrate. The disordered free ions directionally migrate to the opposite electrodes to form ordered ion arrangements, and thus double electric layers (DELs) are generated at the two interfaces of the i-layer. Then, rapid interfacial electrochemical reactions of ion carriers generate a great deal of gases at the i-layer and metal substrate interface38,39. This directly induces expansion stress and significantly changes the contact state between the i-layer and substrate, which destroys the strong interfacial interactions, including interlocked forces, van der Waals’ forces, and hydrogen bonding interactions. Consequently, we only need to overcome a tiny frictional force to achieve nondestructive removal of the coatings.

Results and discussion

To achieve this design, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1, we first spray the i-layer, a functionalized epoxy-based ionic adhesive typically containing 70.0 g epoxy prepolymer E51, 18.9 g polyethylene glycol, 18.9 g 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis [(trifluoromethyl) sulfonyl] imide ([Bmim][TFSI]), and 18.2 g curing agent, as the primer on stainless steel (SS) sheet. That provides high ionic conductivity of 8.82 × 10−5 S m−1 and strong lap-joint strength up to 8.04 MPa (Supplementary Fig. S2). After semi-curing at 65 °C for 25 min, the e-layer, typically containing 1000 mL fluorine-modified multi-walled carbon nanotubes suspension, 63.5 g epoxy prepolymer E51, and 16.5 g curing agent (Supplementary Fig. S3), is sprayed as the functional topcoat to construct the micro-nanostructures (Supplementary Fig. S4). That exhibits low volume resistivity of below 0.26 Ω m and a high water contact angle (WCA) of over 160°. Finally, the NRS coating is prepared on the SS sheet after curing at 65 °C for 3 h (Fig. 1c), which can completely rebound an impacting water droplet without any residue (Supplementary Fig. S5). The strong adhesion strength of the i-layer endows our NRS coating with difficulty being mechanically removed from the SS sheet, which in turn easily causes surface damage. In contrast, the NRS coating can be completely removed without any damage or residue by only applying 90 V DC voltage for 3 min (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S6), just like that its adhesion strength was controlled by an electric switch. More details see Supplementary Movie S1.

Figure 2a quantifies the improvement of the spray amount of e-layer on wettability of the NRS coatings. As the spray dosage increases from 0.3 mg mm−² to 2.1 mg mm-², it shows a significant increase in WCA from 120.0° to 151.0° and a decrease in rolling angle (RA) from 80.7° to 9.0°. This transition from the Wenzel to the Cassie–Baxter wetting state is generally regarded as the reduction in the effective solid–liquid contact fraction and a corresponding increase in the trapped air fraction at the interface40,41, consistent with the observation that continuous and well-developed micro-nanostructures become more pronounced with increasing spray amount of the e-layer (Supplementary Fig. S7). Eventually, the WCA can be increased to 165.0° with RA of 4.7° after spraying 9.5 mg mm-² of e-layer. Moreover, this structural integrity brought about by the amount of spraying also has a conspicuous impact on the nondestructive removal of the coatings. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, we measured the adhesion strength of the NRS coating with a typical length, width, and thickness of 5.0 mm, 5.0 mm, and 0.3 mm, using a home-made pushing test on an electronic universal testing machine with a pushing speed of 5 mm min−1. Figure 2c lists the significant differences in adhesion strength of the NRS coatings with spraying 3.3 mg mm-² e-layer before and after applying voltage. For instance, after applying a DC voltage of only 90 V for 3 min, the maximum thrust drops from 58.9 N to 10.0 N, and the displacement remarkably decreases from 1.29 mm to 0.25 mm.

To more accurately quantify the energy consumption required for the coating to overcome adhesion during the removal process, we defined the normalized adhesion energy (P), which is calculated as the ratio of the total energy dissipated during the removal process (W, usually obtained by integrating the area enclosed by the pushing force-displacement curve that is accurately calculated by Origin software) and the actual adhesion area (A) of the coating and substrate, that is P = W/A. Based on this, we further evaluated the removal efficiency (Ψ) of the coating after electrical treatment using formula (1).

where Pin is the initial normalized adhesion energy of the coating without applying voltage, Pre is the residual normalized adhesion energy after applying α V DC voltage for β min. Based on this, we can be surprised to find that the Ψ[90V, 3min] of the NRS coating with spraying 3.3 mg mm-² e-layer was as high as 99.6% with Pin = 2.46 kJ m−2 and Pre = 0.01 kJ m−2, which provides a guarantee for nondestructive removal and then left a clean SS sheet (Supplementary Fig. S8a). Yet it is worthy that its Ψ[90V, 3min] decreases to 83.8% when applying with the opposite DC voltage, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S8b, although this can also result in a nondestructive and clean substrate. This observation implies that the interfacial electrochemical reactions of [TFSI]- anions are more conducive to the electrical removal. Thus, the DC voltage application mode in the following text is operated by connecting the cathode to the e-layer and the anode to the substrate.

In addition, we also quantified the influence of DC voltage and application time on the normalized adhesion energy and removal efficiency in Fig. 2d. The Ψ can be exceeded by an astonishing 99% after only providing 30 V for 15 min, 60 V for 10 min, or 90 V for 3 min. More details see Supplementary Fig. S9. In the subsequent texts, all experiments adopted 90 V for 3 min as the power supply parameter unless otherwise specified to further evaluate the removal efficiency with different process parameters, such as the spray dosage of e-layer, the thickness of i-layer, and the adhesion area. As shown in Fig. 2e, the Ψ[90V, 3min] reveals a trend of increasing and then decreasing with the increase of the spray dosage of e-layer, and it increases up to 99% when the spray dosage of e-layer increases to between 0.9-5.9 mg mm-². More details see Supplementary Fig. S10. The increase in Ψ[90V, 3min] is mainly due to the fact that the conductive networks in the e-layer become more continuous and complete, which facilitates the horizontal transmission of charges as the spray dosage of the e-layer increases. Such as the horizontal surface resistance (Re) gradually decreases from 30.9 kΩ to 0.9 kΩ when the spray dosage ranges from 0.3 mg mm−2 to 9.5 mg mm−2 (Supplementary Fig. S11a). However, the z-direction resistance (Rz) (Supplementary Fig. S11b) inevitably increases as Re decreases, which partially restricts the longitudinal transmission of charges. Thus, it is observed that as the spray dosage increases from 3.3 mg mm−2 to 9.5 mg mm−2, the Rz increases from 0.4 kΩ to 4.0 kΩ, and the Ψ[90V, 3min] slightly decreases from 99.6% to 97.9% (Supplementary Fig. S11c). Figure 2f displays that the i-layer thickness has no significant effect on electrical removal efficiency, such as its Ψ[90V, 3min] remains at high values of 95.6%, 99.6%, and 99.0% with i-layer thicknesses of 0.1 mm, 0.3 mm, and 0.6 mm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S12). This is because the electrical removal is determined by the interfacial ion accumulation and the following electrochemical reactions. This process is independent of the i-layer thickness; it is primarily governed by the surface microstructures of the substrate and the ionic conductivity of the i-layer.

Moreover, we also inspected the influence of adhesion area on electrical removal efficiency in Fig. 2g. The Pre slightly increases with adhesion area, yet it is only 0.07 kJ m−2 even the adhesion area reaches 100 mm2, and the Ψ[90V, 3min] still reaches 97.9% (Supplementary Fig. S13). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, finite element analysis was performed to evaluate the surface potential distribution on the e-layer during the electrical removal process. There is a slight reduction in surface potential within a large area of 10000 cm2 (Supplementary Fig. S14), and the experimental results also reveal that the potential at the position 10 cm away from the central electrode slightly declines from 90.0 V to 86.7 V (Supplementary Fig. S15). This discovery indicates that the electrical removal of the NRS coatings on ultra-large equipment or facilities may be overcome through designing an e-layer with lower bulk resistance or applying gridded voltages on the e-layer to reduce the degree of surface potential decrease. In addition, the NRS coating was processed on nickel (Ni) alloy, aluminum (Al) alloy, zinc (Zn) alloy and tinplate. The Pin values are greater than 2.2 kJ m−2 and the corresponding Ψ[90V, 3min] achieve 99.3%, 99.2%, 99.6% and 99.1%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S16). The excellent electrical removal across all tested metal substrates suggests the removal of superhydrophobic coatings by an electric switch is a widely applicable strategy to sustainably protect metal substrates.

Significantly, the ionic liquid [Bmim][TFSI] can not only reduce adhesion strength through the ordered migration of free ions, but also through mediated plasticization, further increasing the energy dissipation and mitigating the localized stress and adhesion-driven wear (Supplementary Fig. S17). As shown in Fig. 3a, the NRS coating (with [Bmim][TFSI]) maintains outstanding superhydrophobicity (WCA > 150° and RA < 10°) even after 90 abrasion cycles, while it can only last for 50 abrasion cycles when the pure epoxy is used. This result can also be evidenced by the evolution of the surface microstructures during the sandpaper abrasion test (Supplementary Fig. S18). In spite of micro-scaled scratches being generated and the e-layer thickness gradually decreasing with abrasion cycles (Supplementary Fig. S19), the hierarchical micro-nanostructures remain intact in the first 80 abrasion cycles, thus the Cassie-Baxter wetting state is still maintained. Also, Fig. 3b exhibits that the Ψ[90V, 3min] still exceeds 98% although the Re increases from 1.3 kΩ to 200.5 kΩ after 100 abrasion cycles (Supplementary Fig. S20), and a very clean SS surface without residue and damage is obtained (Fig. 3c). This distinctive combination of outstanding durability and nondestructive electrical removal offers significant advantages for sustainable coating applications, both reducing the removal time and the negative effects on the environment compared with conventional removal techniques.

The neutral salt spray test is used to assess the corrosion resistance of the protective functional coatings on tinplate substrates. Figure 3d presents the corrosion behavior of pristine tinplate and tinplate coated with pure epoxy, i-layer, e-layer, and NRS coatings. As a control, the pristine tinplate is almost entirely corroded, and about 98% of the surface is covered with corrosion products after only 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S21a). In terms of the coatings without the e-layer, the physical barrier effect of pure epoxy or i-layer effectively enhances the corrosion resistance. However, the absence of superhydrophobicity results in typical corrosion after 240 h (Supplementary Fig. S21b, c). Moreover, the protective capacity of pure epoxy is superior to that of i-layer, the corresponding corrosion areas are about 5% and 14%, respectively, which is attributed to42,43. In contrast, the tinplate coated with only e-layer or NRS coating shows excellent superhydrophobicity, that effectively repels the salt vapor (Supplementary Fig. S21d, e). Consequently, the e-layer and NRS coating demonstrate superior corrosion resistance and no corrosion point is observed even after 480 h. Moreover, the corrosion protection of NRS coating can also be evidenced by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement, it agrees well with the salt spray test (Supplementary Fig. S22). Additionally, the NRS coating shows great potential in saline environments. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S23, the NRS coating still exhibits excellent superhydrophobicity even after immersion in 3.5 wt% saline water for 48 h, with WCA of 157.6° and RA of 6.2°; the Pin is still as high as 1.97 kJ m2 after immersion for 48 h and Pre significantly drops to 0.01 kJ m2 after applying 90 V DC voltage for 3 min. Therefore, it can be concluded that the NRS coatings simultaneously possess extraordinary durability and protective capacity that is crucial in practical applications.

To further demonstrate that our NRS coatings maintain sustainable nondestructive removal and high adhesion after recoating, we surveyed the removal efficiency of a newly fabricated NRS coating on SS sheet. As shown in Fig. 4a, the Pin varies in a tiny range from 2.18 kJ m−2 to 3.91 kJ m−2 and the Ψ[90V, 3min] is still higher than 98% in 10 cycles of recoating and removal, which is equivalent to that of a fresh SS sheet (Supplementary Fig. S24). Moreover, the surface morphology exhibits that there is no discovery of any damage or impurity remaining on the 10th removed SS surface (Fig. 4b). Compared with the surface resistance of the fresh SS sheet, there is almost no change for the surface after 5th and 10th removal (Supplementary Fig. S25). The full X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra show the element species and the peak intensities are identical for the fresh SS surface and that of after 5th and 10th electrical removal (Fig. 4c). Actually, the anodic interfacial electrochemical reactions of [TFSI]- anions mainly generate gases, and it shows a weak impact on metal substrates (Supplementary Fig. S26 and S27)44,45. More details see Supplementary Movie S2. Thus, it is merely observed a slight decrease in the peak intensity for zero valent metals (Fe0, at 707.2 eV; Cr0, at 574.2 eV) (Fig. 4d), a slight decrease in peak intensity for metal oxides (MeOn, at 530.1 eV), and increase for metal hydroxides (Me(OH)n, at 532.0 eV) (Fig. 4e). More details see Supplementary Fig. S28. To sum up, our NRS coatings endow the protection system with excellent recyclability and sustainability, it can be fully nondestructively removed, and the metal substrates can be directly reused or recycled when their life inevitably comes to an end.

In summary, inspired by the nondestructive ecdysis of a snake, this work achieves the nondestructive removal of superhydrophobic coatings by an electric switch. The NRS coatings can be readily integrated by the i-layer and e-layer. Though an initial exploration, this work highlights the efficacy of NRS coatings in anti-corrosion, service durability, and nondestructive electrical removal. It is believed that this work opens an avenue for sustainable applications of coatings, including the removal of failed coatings, the reprocessing of new coatings, and the recycling of the protective substrates.

Methods

Materials

Polyethylene glycol with a molecular weight ca. 400 g mol−1 (PEG400, 99.5%) and the ionic liquid 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis [(trifluoromethyl) sulfonyl] imide ([Bmim][TFSI], 98%) were provided by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemistry Technology Co., Ltd. The epoxy prepolymer (E51, η = 16000 mPa s, Mn = 8000 g mol−1, epoxy value = 195 g mol−1) and the curing agent (DBGE, amine value = 700 mg KOH g−1) were purchased from Shanghai Autun Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. The ethanol (EtOH, 99.99%), 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane (PFDTES, 97%), deionized (DI) water, and ammonia water (NH3·H2O, 28 wt%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) with a diameter of 5-15 nm, length of 10-30 μm were obtained from Nanjing Superwetting Advanced Materials Technology Co., Ltd. The tinplate and SS sheets were sourced from Nanjing Xinhang Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. All the chemicals and materials were used as received without any further treatment.

Fabrication of e-layer paint

The e-layer paint was fabricated by a traditional fluorination strategy46,47. Typically, 100.0 g MWCNTs, 500 mL NH3·H2O, and 1000 mL DI water were added in 10000 mL EtOH and ultrasonicated for 10 min to gain the activated MWCNTs suspension. Then, 60 mL PFDTES was slowly added and stirred for 24 h to obtain a fluorine-modified MWCNTs (F-MWCNTs) suspension. When ready to use, 63.5 g E51 and 16.5 g DBGE curing agent were successively dissolved into 1000 mL F-MWCNTs suspension by vigorously stirring at 600 rpm for 30 min to obtain the e-layer paint.

Fabrication of i-layer paint

The i-layer was prepared via a simple procedure. First, 70.0 g E51 was added into 100 mL EtOH and stirred at 300 rpm for 20 min. Then, 18.9 g PEG400 and 18.9 g [Bmim][TFSI] were added to the mixture and stirred for 20 min, where the [Bmim][TFSI] is responsible for providing ion carriers, and the PEG400 serves as the soft segment and provides ion-dipole interactions with [Bmim][TFSI] to ensure the ion transportation. Subsequently, 18.2 g DBGE was dropped into the mixture and continuously stirred for 20 min to obtain the i-layer paint.

Fabrication of NRS coating

The fabrication of the NRS coatings was achieved by a spraying approach48–50. Initially, the i-layer paint was sprayed on the substrate to form an ionic layer as the primer; it was then thermally treated at 65 °C for 25 minutes to achieve a semi-cured state. After that, the e-layer was sprayed as the functional topcoat to construct micro-nanostructures. After curing at 65 °C for 3 h, the NRS coating was finally prepared on the substrate.

Abrasion test of NRS coatings

During the measurement, the NRS coating with a typical length, width, and thickness of 5.0 mm, 5.0 mm, and 0.3 mm was processed on an SS sheet by the above-mentioned spraying approach, and it was horizontally placed on a 1000 mesh sandpaper to ensure the e-layer was completely in contact with the sandpaper, and a 100 g weight was loaded on the SS sheet. During the abrasion test, the abrasion distance was set as 15 cm for each cycle. After each abrasion cycle, the wettability and electrical removal were tested.

Anti-corrosion test of NRS coatings

The salt spray test was conducted in a salt spray chamber (YW-40, Shanghai Lihong Electric Co., Ltd.), the temperature was set as 35 ± 2 °C with spraying 5 wt% NaCl neutral aqueous solution. The corrosion area was calculated using ImageJ software. The corrosion protection of NRS coating was evidenced by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E instrument) with a frequency range from 10−2 Hz to 105 Hz and an amplitude of 5 mV. During the test, the samples were immersed in the 3.5 wt% NaCl neutral aqueous solution and were characterized by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E instrument) using a three-electrode system, where the sample, a platinum sheet, and a Hg/HgCl2 electrode saturated with KCl (+0.2415 V vs. SHE) were used as the working electrode, counter electrode, and reference electrode, respectively.

COMSOL simulation

To investigate the electric potential distribution on NRS coating surfaces during the electrical removal, a parallel-plate capacitor model was constructed using COMSOL Multiphysics to simulate the structure of the NRS coating. In this model, the dimension of the i-layer was set as 100 cm × 100 cm × 0.3 mm, the volume resistivity of the e-layer was set as 0.26 Ω m, and the ionic conductivity of i-layer was set as 8.82×10−5 S m−1. During the simulation, a 90 V positive potential was applied at the middle position of the e-layer, and the substrate was grounded; then, the Electric Currents module was employed to study potential distribution.

Other general measurements

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken by a Tescan MIRA LMS instrument. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer at room temperature. The XPS analysis was carried out using a K-Alpha electron spectrometer. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Thermo Fisher Talos F200S G2) was used to characterize the morphology and structure of MWCNTs. The ionic conductivity (σ) of the i-layer was tested by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E instrument), which can be calculated by σ = l/(RS), where l, S, and R represent the thickness, area, and measured bulk resistance of the i-layer, respectively. The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was performed using a Netzsch DSC 200F3 instrument (Germany) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under N2 atmosphere. The volume resistivity (ρ) of the e-layer was measured by a four-point probe (FT-340, Ningbo Rike Micro-Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd). The water contact angle (WCA, 6 μL water droplet) and rolling angle (RA, 10 μL water droplet) were determined using a DSA 30 contact angle meter; the average WCA and RA values were calculated by measuring 3 distinct positions across the coating surface at room temperature under 50% relative humidity. Time-lapse images and videos were obtained using the Photron FASTCAM Mini UX100 type high-speed camera equipped with a Navitar 6000 zoom lens. The surface profiles and surface roughness (Ra) were measured via a 3D optical topography (Contour GT-K, Bruker). The optical microscopy (OM) images were obtained through a WMJ-9590 metallographic microscope (Shanghai WUMO Optical Instrument Co., Ltd). The horizontal surface resistance (Re) and z-direction resistance (Rz) of the e-layer were measured using a two-probe method with a digital multimeter (Keithley DMM6500).
