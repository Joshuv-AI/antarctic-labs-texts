Decomposing Elements of Problem Solving: What "Math" Does RL Teach?
Tian Qin, Core Francisco Park, Mujin Kwun, Aaron Walsman, Eran Malach, Nikhil Anand, Hidenori Tanaka, David Alvarez-Melis

Abstract
Mathematical reasoning tasks have become prominent benchmarks for assessing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, especially with reinforcement learning (RL) methods such as GRPO showing significant performance gains. However, accuracy metrics alone do not support fine-grained assessment of capabilities and fail to reveal which problem-solving skills have been internalized. To better understand these capabilities, we propose to decompose problem solving into fundamental capabilities: Plan (mapping questions to sequences of steps), Execute (correctly performing solution steps), and Verify (identifying the correctness of a solution). Empirically, we find that GRPO mainly enhances the execution skill—improving execution robustness on problems the model already knows how to solve—a phenomenon we call temperature distillation . More importantly, we show that RL-trained models struggle with fundamentally new problems, hitting a ‘coverage wall’ due to insufficient planning skills. To explore RL’s impact more deeply, we construct a minimal, synthetic solution-tree navigation task as an analogy for mathematical problem-solving. This controlled setup replicates our empirical findings, confirming RL primarily boosts execution robustness. Importantly, in this setting, we identify conditions under which RL can potentially overcome the coverage wall through improved exploration and generalization to new solution paths. Our findings provide insights into the role of RL in enhancing LLM reasoning, expose key limitations, and suggest a path toward overcoming these barriers. Code is available at https://github.com/cfpark00/RL-Wall .

Recent months have seen rapid gains in LLM mathematical reasoning, driven in large part by reinforcement learning (RL) methods like the simple yet effective GRPO
[
1
]
. This progress has largely been benchmarked using a single evaluation metric: Pass@1 accuracy—the fraction of problems for which the model produces a correct solution on its first attempt. This mirrors a long-standing tendency in human cognition research to rely on simple, aggregate metrics—such as standardized test scores
[
2
,
3
]
or IQ measures
[
4
]
—to evaluate complex capabilities. These metrics persist in part because they are easy to interpret and compare across individuals
[
5
,
6
]
. While convenient, relying on a single accuracy score like Pass@1 to evaluate LLM reasoning has two important limitations.
First, it is
monolithic
: too coarse-grained to reveal the nature of model failures or the specific reasoning skills being improved, such as whether RL enhances planning, computation, error-checking, or merely reinforces existing knowledge. Second, it evaluates greedy decoding, whereas real-world deployments—especially those requiring complex reasoning—often rely on stochastic sampling (e.g., temperature decoding, best-of-N selection) to increase diversity or robustness. To truly understand the impact of RL on reasoning, we need evaluation methods that go beyond aggregate accuracy and capture how models navigate the problem-solving process.

To address these limitations, we adopt a finer-grained evaluation framework aimed at understanding two key questions: (i) are the improvements from GRPO uniform across problems?, and (ii) what specific aspects of reasoning does GRPO improve?
By breaking down accuracy per problem, we find that GRPO is most effective on problems the model already knows how to solve (Section
3
). On these problems, its effect is to make the correct solution more likely regardless of sampling temperature—a phenomenon we call
temperature distillation
. This enhances execution robustness under sampling but does little to help with fundamentally novel problems, exposing a
coverage wall
, defined as the limit of Pass@
K
as
K\rightarrow\infty
(Fig.
1
c
). To study (ii) we introduce a more fine-grained framework for analyzing reasoning, viewing problem solving as a search process in a solution tree (Section
4
). This perspective decomposes reasoning into three core skills (Fig.
1
a
): Plan (mapping the question to a sequence of actions indicating where to go in the tree, Execute (carrying out those steps correctly by performing the navigation), and Verify (detecting and correcting errors). Using this lens, we find that even small models often plan well but frequently fail in execution, making logical and factual errors. GRPO, we show, primarily strengthens execution.

To better isolate and understand RL’s effects, we construct a minimal, synthetic setting that abstracts mathematical problem solving. (Fig.
1
b
, Sec.
5
). Here, the model needs to map the question to a sequence of actions (i.e., plan) and execute the sequence of actions to provide the correct answer. This controlled environment closely mirrors our proposed framework, while allowing control over the underlying data generation process. We find that this synthetic setting faithfully reproduces our empirical observations. Crucially, the setup also reveals properties of the pretraining and RL data which allows RL to break through the coverage wall—namely, when the model is able to explore new actions and apply them to structurally similar problems.

Our study provides insights into RL’s role in enhancing LLM reasoning. Our contributions are:

•

Reasoning Decomposition:
We propose a framework that decomposes math problem solving into three capabilities: planning, execution, and verification.

•

Empirical Analysis of RL:
In the proposed decomposition, we show that GRPO primarily improves execution on known problems, leading to a temperature distillation effect. However, GRPO fails to help model solve previously unsolved problems, revealing a coverage wall.

•

Synthetic Validation:
We construct a minimal synthetic task that closely reflects the fundamental elements of problem solving and replicates empirical findings. We reveal some data properties under which RL can overcome the coverage wall.

3.3
GRPO Does Not Unlock Previously Unsolvable Problems

A central question we examine is whether reinforcement learning enables the model to solve problems it previously could not. We address this using two complementary approaches: total coverage and fine-grained matched problem analysis.

Coverage.

We define
\text{Coverage}(K,T^{*})
as the number of problems for which at least one of the
K
samples generated at optimal temperature
T^{*}
is correct.
In practice, the number of solvable problems grows roughly log-linearly with
K

[
48
]
,
so
K
is typically chosen as large as computational constraints allow based on task difficulty. Here we use
K=64
for the MATH-500 (see Appendix
C
for justification). To isolate coverage from temperature sensitivity—a key confounder due to the temperature distillation effect—coverage requires the optimal sampling temperature for each model. To account sampling stochasticity, we run best-of-64 sampling across 5 random seeds for each problem, both before and after GRPO. Fig.
2
(

The key observation is that GRPO does not significantly increase coverage. However, coverage alone offers an incomplete view: it doesn’t reveal
which
problems are being solved. Two models with similar coverage could succeed on entirely different subsets, implying a trade-off in problem-solving behavior. We explore this further by analyzing per-problem solution rates.

To study fine-grained changes in problem solvability from GRPO, we conduct a

4
Elements of Problem Solving

So far, we’ve seen that RL primarily results in temperature distillation and fails to overcome the coverage wall. To understand why, we decompose math problem solving into three core components (Section
4.1
) and use this framework to analyze how RL shapes model behavior (Sections
4.2
and
4.3
).

4.1
Plan, Execute, and Verify

We conceptualize math problem solving as traversing through a solution tree, where each node represents a possible intermediate step. Within this framework, we identify three essential capabilities:

•

Plan: Mapping a problem to a sequence of solution steps indicating where to go in the tree. Planning is non-trivial most problems don’t explicitly indicate the necessary steps to solve them.

•

Execute: Correctly carrying out these steps, which requires mathematical knowledge, contextual reasoning, and logical consistency.

•

Verify: Detecting and correcting errors in the solution path. While large models can sometimes backtrack or reflect, such behaviors are limited in smaller models. Verification is an active area of recent research
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

Here we focus on the Plan and Execute components. Our goal is to identify where models succeed or fail in these steps and, most importantly, whether RL enhances these abilities.

4.3
Reasoning with Spurious Correlations

Spurious Correlations.

Why do models often fail to execute solution steps correctly? Understanding these failures would shed light on the limits of their reasoning capabilities. To this end, we implement a visualization tool (See Appendix
F
) that convert model’s solution roll out into a solution tree, branching whenever generations diverge. Using this visualization tool, we inspect model’s solution tree across 30 different MATH-500 questions. A consistent pattern emerges: models apply mathematical and logical steps
probabilistically
, often influenced by spurious correlations such as formatting or phrasing in the chain-of-thought. For instance, Fig.
5
(
left
) shows Qwen2.5-1.5B’s generation tree on a problem where solutions diverge based on whether the word “unit" or “block" is used. Although the wording choice is semantically irrelevant, it significantly affects whether the model produces a correct solution. This suggests that execution accuracy is driven more by superficial patterns in the training data than by robust reasoning. Despite strong benchmark scores (1.5B achieves
\geq 50\%
on high shcool MATH-500,
\geq 70\%
on grade-school GSM8K)—these models still make basic factual and logical errors—many of which would be obvious to elementary school students (see additional examples in Appendix
F
). We hypothesize that temperature distillation mitigates these issues by encouraging solution traces that are less sensitive to such spurious cues, improving consistency across generations.

Partial Repair via GRPO.

To test this hypothesis, we re-visit solution traces previously identified as containing execution errors (Section
4.2
) and label them with:

1.

whether they contain a basic factual math error (e.g., claiming that a triangle has six sides)

2.

the difficulty level of the mistaken fact (elementary or high-school level)

3.

whether there is a basic logical error (e.g., a contradiction or a
non-sequitur
)

We use GPT-4.1-mini to generate these annotations pre- and post-GRPO. Appendix
G
includes annotation prompts and sample outputs. Fig.
5
(
right
) shows the relative reduction in error rates, normalized to the number of pre-GRPO mistakes (

Taken together, these results reinforce and provide an explanation for our earlier findings: GRPO improves execution be reducing low-level errors and increasing internal consistency—but it does not enhance planning or expand mathematical understanding.

5.1
Motivation

Our empirical analysis of RLVR’s impact on LLM mathematical problem solving offers a more nuanced understanding of the types of improvements RL delivers. However, the complexity of the training data of language models as well as the given math problems complicate the understanding of why and when we should expect the behavioral change observed in RLVR on MATH. This is especially important as there are clearly cases where RL reveals additional problems in both classical RL

5.2
Synthetic Setup

The ideal synthetic setup should be a good analogy to the original setup in hand and reproduce core observed phenomena. Given these conditions are met, it is preferable to have the simplest setup possible. Here, we aim to capture these aspects of mathematical problem solving:

1.

There exists a ground truth action sequence to apply on the problem state, and one could deduce this action sequence from the problem statement in a learnable way.

2.

There exists a vast pretraining data with biases, spurious correlations and irrelevant context.

3.

The RL training and eval datasets, while not necessarily matching the pre-training distribution, are sampled i.i.d. from the same distribution—much like the questions in MATH
[
12
]
.

And we attempt to reproduce (See Sec.
3.1
):

1.

Temperature Distillation:
RL reduces the performance gap between sampling at low temperature and high temperature.

2.

Limited Coverage Increase:
RL shows a relatively modest coverage increase compared to Pass@1.

3.

RL generalization gap:
RL demonstrates significant generalization gap between the training set reward and the eval set reward.

The synthetic setup we designed is described in Fig.
6
. A “math question" in this setup is defined as a set of actions to apply to an initial state where an action changes the state to a target state as described in the state table in Fig.
6
a). The problem is solved by internalizing this state-action pair table, and applying them to the given initial state. However, the set of actions to apply is not directly given in the question statement, just like a math problem where the actions to be performed should be carefully deduced by thinking about the problem. Here, to make an analogy of this process, we encode the action sequence into sequence of “problem states" which are also determined by a transition table. Thus, in order to solve the question correctly, the model must reconstruct the action sequence from the problem state transitions and apply them to the given state. In order to simulate spurious correlation, we generate a “context" token for each sampled action which is highly correlated with the action number for the pre-training set, but not for the RL/eval set. Additionally, we add irrelevant context to the question part to allow memorization solutions as well. Overall, our synthetic data is designed to capture aspects of problem solving by having a more complex but accurate function to learn and spurious correlations to learn from. See Appendix
H
for hyperparameters and further details about training, RL and eval data distributions as well as properties of the trained autoregressive model such as the choice of tokenizer or training hyperparameters.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

This work proposes a decomposition of mathematical reasoning in LLMs into three core capabilities: planning, execution, and verification. Using this framework, we conduct a in-depth analysis of reinforcement learning (specifically GRPO) and find that it primarily enhances execution robustness—making correct solutions more reproducible under sampling (a phenomenon we term temperature distillation)—but does not improve planning or expand the set of problems the model can solve (coverage). To further isolate this behavior, we design a minimal synthetic environment that mirrors our decomposition and replicates empirical observations. Crucially, this controlled setting reveals that RL can overcome the coverage wall when the model is exposed to actionable patterns that generalize across structurally similar problems.

Limitations and Future Work.

Our study is limited to small-scale models and public datasets, and therefore cannot rule out the possibility of emergent behaviors in larger models trained with more extensive data. It is also plausible that curriculum design or expanded RL datasets could improve planning and coverage. Furthermore, while the synthetic environment reveals important mechanisms, its constrained state space makes it more susceptible to coverage improvements through brute-force sampling, which may not reflect the difficulty of generalization in real-world settings. Despite these limitations, our decomposition offers a conceptual tool for reasoning-focused analysis. We hope it encourages future work to move beyond aggregate accuracy and investigate how specific reasoning abilities emerge, interact, and improve under different training data and paradigms.

Grading Rubric:

(1). Does the solution contain basic mathematical factual mistakes? Here we are talking about simple plain mathematical facts that are in direct contradiction to well-known mathematical knowledge. Data

Here, we explain the details of the synthetic data generated in our study in Sec.
5
. The framework is designed to create a controllable environment that mimics critical aspects of complex problem-solving, such as deducing a sequence of operations and handling spurious correlations or irrelevant information. The core task involves navigating a discrete state space by applying actions, where the correct action sequence must be inferred from an auxiliary sequence of “problem states.”

The synthetic environment is built upon several key entities, visually represented in Fig.
6
a) of the main text:

•

States (
S_{x}
)
: These represent the fundamental state of the problem instance. The model’s goal is to correctly predict the trajectory of these states. Each state
s
is an integer from the set
\{0,1,\dots,N_{S}-1\}
, where
N_{S}
is the total number of unique states.

•

Actions (
A_{x}
)
: These are discrete operations that cause transitions between states
S_{x}
. Each action
a
is an integer from the set
\{0,1,\dots,N_{A}-1\}
, where
N_{A}
is the total number of unique actions.

•

Problem States (
P_{x}
)
: This sequence of tokens serves as an indirect instruction or “problem description.” The model must learn to interpret the transitions between problem states to infer the appropriate action
A_{x}
to apply to the current state
S_{x}
. Each problem state
p
is an integer from the set
\{0,1,\dots,N_{P}-1\}
, where
N_{P}
is the total number of unique problem states.

•

Spurious Contexts (
C_{x}
)
: These are tokens associated with each chosen action
A_{x}
. Their statistical properties are deliberately manipulated between different data splits:

–

During
pre-training
(the ‘train’ split), the context
c
for a given action
a
is sampled from a non-uniform distribution
P_{\beta}(c|a)
. This distribution is specifically engineered to create strong, yet spurious, correlations between certain contexts and actions, acting as a potential distractor or misleading heuristic for the model. We specify this distribution in the next paragraph.

–

During
Reinforcement Learning (RL) training
(the ‘train_rl’ split) and
evaluation
(the ‘eval’ split), these spurious correlations are removed. The context
c
for a given action
a
is sampled from a uniform distribution,
P(c|a)=1/N_{C}
, where
N_{C}
is the total number of unique context tokens.

•

Irrelevant Contexts (
I_{x}
)
: A fixed number of randomly generated tokens are appended to the input prompt. These tokens have no causal relationship with the problem’s solution and are included to test the model’s ability to ignore irrelevant information and focus on salient parts of the input. We specify the parametrization in the next paragraph.

The data generation process is controlled by a set of parameters. Their default values are as follows:

•

n_{\text{dim}}
(Default: 20): This parameter defines the dimensionality of the primary entities. Specifically, the number of distinct actions
N_{A}
is set to
n_{\text{dim}}
.

•

n_{\text{ctx}}
(Default: 20): This parameter sets the size of other entity spaces. For all experiments explored, we set the number of distinct states
N_{S}
, the number of distinct problem states
N_{P}
, and the number of distinct context tokens
N_{C}
all equal to
n_{\text{ctx}}
.
Thus, by default:
N_{A}=20
,
N_{S}=20
,
N_{P}=20
,
N_{C}=20
.

•

n_{\text{irr\_ctx}}
(Default: 10): Specifies the quantity of irrelevant context tokens (‘num_irr_context’) to be included in each problem prompt.

•

k_{\text{min}}
(Default: 5): The minimum number of actions (‘min_actions_per_problem‘) that will constitute a single problem instance.

•

k_{\text{max}}
(Default: 10): The maximum number of actions (‘max_actions_per_problem’) for a single problem instance. The actual number of actions for each problem is chosen uniformly at random from the range
[k_{\text{min}},k_{\text{max}}]
.

•

\alpha_{\text{train}}
(Default: 0.3): This is the concentration parameter for the Dirichlet distribution used to generate the action probabilities
P_{\alpha}(A_{i}|S_{i})
for the
pre-training (‘train’) split
. Specifically, for each state

\boldsymbol{\alpha}

•

pre-training (‘train’) split

\boldsymbol{\beta}

This generation pipeline produces datasets (‘train’, ‘train_rl’, ‘eval’) with controlled statistical properties. The ‘train’ set has biased action priors and spurious action-context correlations, designed to reflect complexities and pitfalls in real-world pre-training data. The ‘train_rl’ and ‘eval’ sets, by contrast, has uniform action and context sampling more suitable for thorough evaluation of evaluating generalization beyond learned spurious cues. This latter also provides a cleaner signal for RL.
