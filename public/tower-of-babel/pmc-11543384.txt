CGG Repeat Expansion in NOTCH2NLC Causing Overlapping Oculopharyngodistal Myopathy and Neuronal Intranuclear Inclusion Disease With Diffusion Weighted Imaging Abnormality in the Cerebellum
Authors: Jing Ma, Huiqiu Zhang, Bing Meng, Jiangbo Qin, Hongye Liu, Xiaomin Pang, Rongjuan Zhao, Juan Wang, Xueli Chang, Junhong Guo, Wei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeCGG repeat expansion in the 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) of the Notch 2 N-terminal-like C gene (NOTCH2NLC) has been associated with neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) and oculopharyngodistal myopathy type 3 (OPDM3). Few OPDM3 patients have been reported. This report describes two OPDM3 patients with novel imaging findings who presented the typical features of NIID, and reviews all OPDM3 cases available in the literature.MethodsThe available clinical, imaging, and pathological information was reviewed and investigated. CGG repeat expansion in the 5'UTR of NOTCH2NLC was tested using the repeat-primed polymerase chain reaction (PCR), followed by the fluorescence amplicon-length PCR to determine the number of CGG repeats.ResultsOur two OPDM3 patients and most patients reported in the literature developed the typical clinical characteristics of NIID, including leukoencephalopathy, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive deterioration, pigmentary retinopathy, ataxia, tremor, acute encephalitis-like episodes, pigmentary retinopathy, miosis, and sensorineural hearing loss. In addition to typical imaging findings of NIID, our two patients exhibited diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) hyperintensities in the middle cerebellar peduncles, which have not been described previously. Muscle biopsies revealed rimmed vacuoles and p62-positive intranuclear inclusions in the myofibers in both patients. The skin biopsy performed in one patient detected typical eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions. Genetic analysis identified CGG repeat expansion in NOTCH2NLC as the causative mutation in the two patients.ConclusionsOur two patients with OPDM3 had clinical characteristics of NIID and exhibited DWI abnormality in the cerebellum. Our results indicate that OPDM3 is within the spectrum of NIID and that DWI hyperintensities in the cerebellum are helpful for diagnosing NIID or OPDM3.

INTRODUCTION

The human-specific Notch 2 N-terminal-like C gene (NOTCH2NLC) and its two paralogs (NOTCH2NLA and NOTCH2NLB) encode Notch-related proteins that enhance Notch signaling.1 These genes may contribute to the development of the human neocortex.1 NOTCH2NLC contains the repeat sequence (CGG)9(GGA)2(CGG)2 in its 5′ untranslated region (5'UTR), and such CGG repeat expansion is associated with neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID; MIM 603472).23

NIID is a slowly progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by eosinophilic hyaline intranuclear inclusions in the central and peripheral nervous systems and also in various visceral organs.4 Eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions identified in skin biopsies are regarded as the pathological hallmark for a diagnosis of NIID.2345 The clinical manifestations of NIID are highly variable, and include dementia, limb weakness, parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, autonomic dysfunction, retinal degeneration, sensorineural hearing loss, and seizure.678 NIID cases can be categorized into dementia-dominant and limb-weakness-dominant subtypes according to their main clinical presentations.4 Most cases of the limb-weakness-dominant phenotype have been ascribed to neurogenic weakness.4 It seems that muscle is not affected in NIID. However, while intranuclear inclusions are rare, they do exist in myofibers of patients with NIID.4910 The increase in the number of reported cases has led to myopathy also being described in patients with CGG repeat expansions in NOTCH2NLC.510111213

Oculopharyngodistal myopathy (OPDM) is a rare inherited myopathy characterized by progressive distal limb weakness, ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, bulbar muscle weakness, and rimmed vacuoles in muscle biopsies.14 CGG repeat expansions in the noncoding regions of two genes, LRP12 and GIPC1, have been reported to be causative for OPDM type 1 (OPDM1; MIM 164310) and OPDM type 2 (OPDM2; MIM 618940), respectively.1516 CGG repeat expansion in NOTCH2NLC has also recently been identified as a causative mutation of OPDM (OPDM type 3, OPDM3; MIM 619473).51112 Very few patients with OPDM3 have been reported: only four index cases with a family history and nine sporadic cases.5111217 OPDM3 shares pathological and clinical features with NIID, such as leukoencephalopathy, retinal pigmentary degeneration, ataxia, tremor, and peripheral neuropathy.51112 This means that it is reasonable to assume that OPDM3 is not a disease entity independent of NIID.11

Here we report two additional sporadic patients of OPDM3 with leukoencephalopathy and peripheral neuropathy. We report for the first time a patient suffering from typical OPDM and typical NIID caused by CGG repeat expansion in NOTCH2NLC, which suggests that OPDM3 falls within the spectrum of NIID. In addition, the two patients had diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) abnormalities in the cerebellum, which have not been reported before and may have value in diagnosing OPDM3 or NIID. We also reviewed all published data and here provided an overview of the clinical, histological, and genetic features of OPDM3.

METHODS

Patients

Two sporadic patients with OPDM3 were recruited. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (approval number K165). Informed consent for study participation, sample collection, and reviewing medical records was obtained from the participants. Medical records and imaging data were reviewed by a senior neurologist and a radiologist, respectively.

Muscle biopsy, skin biopsy, and immunofluorescence staining

A muscle biopsy and pathological investigation was performed for the two patients as described previously.18 Muscle specimens were obtained from the left quadriceps femoris of Patient 1 and from the right biceps brachii of Patient 2. Immunofluorescence staining was conducted on frozen muscle sections with antibodies against p-62 (ab56416, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and NOTCH2NLC protein (ab233287, Abcam). The nuclei were counter-stained with DAPI.

For electron microscopy, a portion of the muscle specimens was initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, subsequently fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, and embedded in Epon 812 (45345, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Ultrathin sections were examined using an electron microscope (JEOL-1400Flash, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

A skin biopsy of the left thigh (together with a muscle biopsy) was performed in one of the OPDM3 patients (Patient 1). The obtained specimen was fixed using formalin and embedded in paraffin. Skin sections with a thickness of 4 µm were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, anti-p62 antibody (ab207305, Abcam), and anti-ubiquitin antibody (ab140601, Abcam).

Molecular genetic analysis

DNA was extracted using the CWE9600 Automated Nucleic Acid Extraction System using Blood DNA Kit V2 (CWE2100, CWBiotech, Taizhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed in the two patients according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as reported previously.18 The CGG repeat expansion in the 5'UTR of NOTCH2NLC was tested using repeat-primed polymerase chain reaction (RP-PCR) protocols as described previously.19 A sawtooth tail pattern in the electropherogram was considered to indicate the disease-associated repeat expansion. The fluorescence amplicon-length PCR (AL-PCR) was performed at a commercial company (Beijing Microread Genetech, Beijing, China) to screen the CGG expansion in the 5'UTR of NOTCH2NLC. Since the CGG repeat expansions in LRP12 and GIPC1 are also associated with OPDM, the CGG repeats in the two genes were also evaluated using the RP-PCR.

RESULTS

Clinical features

The two sporadic patients investigated in this study were born to nonconsanguineous parents. Their clinical features are listed in Table 1 and compared with all previously reported cases.

Patient 1

Patient 1 was a 26-year-old male who complained of night blindness and difficulty in squatting 10 years previously (at age 16 years). The following year (age 17 years) he noticed hand weakness. During the following 8 years (from ages 17 to 24 years) the patient experienced nine recurrent encephalitic episodes of headache, nausea, vomiting, fever, and disturbance of consciousness. These episodes had durations ranging from several hours to 3 days. Simultaneously, deterioration of learning ability, reduced visual acuity, slurred speech, and muscle atrophy in distal limbs were gradually noted. Patient 1 had no siblings, his father had essential tremor, and there was no family history of muscle weakness or symptoms related to brain disorders in his immediate family (Supplementary Fig. 1A in the online-only Data Supplement).

A neurological physical examination of Patient 1 revealed ptosis, external ophthalmoplegia, reduced pupillary light reflexes, dysphagia, dysarthria, facial palsy, hypotonia of all four limbs, predominant weakness of distal limbs, cerebellar ataxia, steppage gait, diminished tendon reflexes, and pes arcuatus (Fig. 1A-E). Diplopia and miosis were not observed. The Medical Research Council (MRC) scale values were grades 2–4 for muscles in the distal limbs, and grade 4 or 5 in the proximal limbs. The Babinski signs were negative, and the findings of a sensory examination were normal.

Elevation of serum creatine kinase (684 IU/L) was noted. Twenty-four-hour urinary protein quantification revealed an increased protein level of 1.39 g/day. The results of the other laboratory tests were unremarkable. The patient scored 30/30 on the Mini-Mental State Examination and 22/30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. The best-corrected visual acuity was 0.6 in the right eye and 0.4 in the left eye. Visual field testing performed by Octopus 101 perimetry showed diffuse reductions in mean light sensitivity of 6.0 and 3.9 dB in the right and left eyes, respectively (Fig. 1F). Ophthalmoscopic examinations revealed bilateral pigmentary retinopathy (Fig. 1G). Pure-tone audiometry performed in a standard environment for both ears showed bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, with a significant loss of 45–50 dB at 4 kHz. The brainstem auditory evoked potential could not be elicited by auditory stimulation at up to 96 dB nHL bilaterally.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) revealed reductions in the motor nerve conduction velocity (MNCV), sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV), and compound muscle action potential (CMAP) (Table 2). Electromyography (EMG) recordings revealed neurogenic changes.

Muscle magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy in the legs, with muscles of the lower leg being affected more severely than those of the thigh (Fig. 2). Brain MRI detected cerebellar atrophy and hyperintensities in the cerebral white matter, corpus callosum, medial parts of the cerebellar hemisphere beside the vermis (the paravermal area), and middle cerebellar peduncles on both fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and DWI images (Fig. 2), which differs from previous reports.20

Patient 2

Patient 2 was a 39-year-old male who presented with lower limb weakness and had experienced difficulty climbing stairs and squatting 8 years previously. At the age of 35 years the patient exhibited hand weakness, slurred speech, and swallowing difficulty. His clinical symptoms had gradually worsened. The proband’s 18-year-old son exhibited learning difficulty, and a physical examination revealed decreased deep tendon reflexes. The proband’s elder daughter and father were asymptomatic, with normal findings in physical examinations (Supplementary Fig. 1B in the online-only Data Supplement).

A physical examination of Patient 2 revealed ptosis, external ophthalmoplegia, miosis, reduced pupillary light reflexes, dysphagia, dysarthria, facial palsy, predominant weakness of distal limbs, and steppage gait (Fig. 1H and I). Diplopia was absent, and his muscle tone was normal. The MRC scale values were grade 3 or 4 for muscles in the distal limbs, and grade 4 or 5 in the proximal limbs. Sensory examination findings and tendon reflexes were normal, and the Babinski signs were negative.

Serum creatine kinase was elevated (903 IU/L), while the results of other laboratory tests were unremarkable. NCS revealed reductions in the MNCV, SNCV, and CMAP (Table 2). EMG recordings showed mixed neurogenic and myopathic changes.

Muscle MRI revealed fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy in the legs, and muscles of the lower leg were affected more severely than those of the thigh (Fig. 2). Brain MRI hyperintensities were detected in the cerebral white matter, corpus callosum, and middle cerebellar peduncles on both FLAIR and DWI images (Fig. 2), which were similar to the findings for Patient 1.

Pathological changes

Muscle biopsies in the two patients revealed severe fiber-size variation, endomysial fibrosis, rimmed vacuoles, fiber splitting, necrosis and regeneration, and increased central nuclei, indicating myopathic changes (Fig. 3A-D, I, J). The findings of angular fiber clustering, targetoid fibers, and fiber grouping suggested neuropathic changes (Fig. 3A, E, F, K). Immunofluorescence staining revealed p62-positive protein deposits in myofiber nuclei (Fig. 3G and L). NOTCH2NLC proteins were also detected in the nuclei (Fig. 3H and M). However, colocalization of p62 and NOTCH2NLC proteins was not found.

A skin biopsy detected typical eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions in the fibroblasts, fat cells, and ductal epithelial cells of sweat glands in Patient 1 (Fig. 4A and B). These eosinophilic inclusions were positive for p62 and ubiquitin (Fig. 4C-F). Electron microscopy of a muscle sample from Patient 2 showed intranuclear inclusions (Fig. 4G and H). Such intranuclear inclusions were not found in a muscle sample from Patient 1, and we did not perform electron microscopy on his skin sample.

Molecular genetic analysis

WES performed in the two patients to accurately localize the causative disease gene did not identify any relevant mutations. Subsequent NOTCH2NLC genotyping using the RP-PCR revealed abnormal CGG expansions in its 5'UTR, appearing in the typical sawtooth tail pattern (Fig. 5A). The AL-PCR revealed that the numbers of CGG repeat expansions were 127 and 159 for the 2 patients (Fig. 5B). The patients did not exhibit CGG repeat expansion in LRP12 or GIPC1. The AL-PCR was also performed on the father of Patient 1, which revealed the 11 and 93 CGG repeat expansions in 2 alleles.

DISCUSSION

CGG repeat expansion in the 5'UTR of NOTCH2NLC has been identified as the causative mutation of NIID and OPDM3.2351112 The present study has characterized two new sporadic cases of OPDM3 with features suggesting a diagnosis of NIID, indicating that OPDM3 is within the spectrum of NIID.

The diagnosis of OPDM3 for the two patients was supported by clinical, pathological, and genetic data. Both patients presented with progressive myopathy with ocular, facial, pharyngeal, and distal limb muscle involvement, and carried CGG repeat expansion in NOTCH2NLC. The muscle biopsies revealed rimmed vacuoles and p62-positive intranuclear inclusions in the myofibers, which is consistent with the known pathological features of OPDM.121516

The two patients also had clinical characteristics of NIID, including leukoencephalopathy and peripheral neuropathy. In addition, the presentations of Patient 1 included cognitive deterioration, ataxia, acute encephalitis-like episodes, pigmentary retinopathy, and sensorineural hearing loss, which are typical of NIID, in combination with intranuclear inclusions in skin biopsy samples and genetic data.482122 Patient 2 presented with miosis, which is also prevalent in NIID.4 Most patients with OPDM3 reported in the literature developed neurological manifestations (Table 1), with, for example, leukoencephalopathy as well as peripheral neuropathy being seen in 8 of 11 OPDM3 patients. It seems that only one patient (designated S3 in Table 1) has presented with myopathy without involvement of the nervous system. However, that patient did not undergo an ophthalmoscopic examination or pure-tone audiometry, and therefore the assessment was not comprehensive. Furthermore, in contrast with those having OPDM3, only a few patients with OPDM1 or OPDM2 have had neurological abnormalities.1516 We therefore suggest that OPDM3 is within the spectrum of NIID.

The characteristic inclusions in visceral organs are widely distributed in NIID, including cardiac myocytes, adrenal cortex and medulla cells, hepatocytes, lipocytes, skeletal-muscle cells, smooth-muscle cells, and renal tubular epithelial cells.9 Therefore, other organs may also be damaged in addition to the nervous system and muscle. For example, Patient 1 developed proteinuria (1.39 g/day), indicating kidney involvement. Ji et al.17 reported another OPDM3 patient with proteinuria (3.22 g/day), for which a renal biopsy revealed segmental sclerosis, localized tubular atrophy, and eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions in renal tubular epithelial cells. This indicates that it is not reasonable to name the diseases based on the visceral organs involved, such as OPDM3 and the created name NOTCH2NLC-related repeat expansion nephropathy. Some authors have even argued that NIID does not describe the nature of the disease; for example, Chen et al.8 has proposed that systemic intranuclear inclusion disease is a more-appropriate name than NIID.

The clinical manifestations associated with NOTCH2NLC are highly variable, including dementia, parkinsonism, peripheral neuropathy and myopathy, leukoencephalopathy, and essential tremor and so on.23 The number of CGG repeats seems to be associated with different phenotypes.23 Previous studies have shown that the limb-weakness-dominant phenotype of NIID has the longest CGG repeat expansion, usually more than 200 repeats, and the number of repeats in the dementia-dominant phenotype generally ranges from 100 to 200, followed by the parkinsonism-dominant phenotype with fewer than 100 repeats.22425 However, the number of trinucleotide repeats in OPDM3 has usually ranged from 100 to 200 (Table 1), overlapping with that in the dementia-dominant phenotype. From the perspective of the number of CGG repeats, OPDM3 and dementia-dominant phenotype of NIID share similar pathogenic factors, further supporting the notion that OPDM3 is within the spectrum of NIID.

Hyperintensity along the corticomedullary junction in DWI has been described as a specific MRI finding of NIID (Supplementary Fig. 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).4 It seems that the DWI hyperintensity extends only along the corticomedullary junction between gray and white matter, and does not expand into deep white matter even during the late disease stage.4 Other imaging findings on brain MRI include abnormal DWI signals in the corpus callosum, as well as hyperintensities involving the cerebral white matter, corpus callosum, middle cerebellar peduncles, and paravermal area on T2-weighted and FLAIR images.42026 The imaging features for patients with OPDM3 are similar to those with NIID.51112 The two patients in the present study had a distinctive neuroimaging pattern. First, our two patients had DWI abnormalities in the middle cerebellar peduncles and/or paravermal area, which have not been reported before. Second, the DWI hyperintensity extended into deep white matter in Patient 1. Different autopsy cases of NIID showed that hyperintensities in DWI were correlated with spongiosis.2728 This is similar to what happens in Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.29 In our patients, the DWI abnormalities in the middle cerebellar peduncles, paravermal area, and/or deep white matter indicated that spongiosis might occur in wide regions beyond the corticomedullary junction. DWI hyperintensities in the cerebellum have not been found in other diseases except FXTAS (fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome), which is very rare.30 Therefore, this imaging sign is a specific marker in the diagnosis of NIID or OPDM3 and may provide a more-comprehensive understanding of the specific imaging features of the disease.

In conclusion, our two patients with OPDM3 exhibited distinct clinical and imaging features, including the involvement of both central and peripheral nervous systems. Our results indicate that OPDM3 is within the spectrum of NIID and that DWI hyperintensities in the cerebellum are helpful for diagnosing NIID or OPDM3.
