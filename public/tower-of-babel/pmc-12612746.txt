Prevalence of obsessive-compulsive disorder and related healthcare utilisation in China: a cross-sectional epidemiological survey
Authors: Bangshan Liu, Junzhe Cheng, Zhaorui Liu, Yueqin Huang, Shazia Rehman, Minxue Shen, Jin Liu, Yumeng Ju, Yaqin Yu, Xiufeng Xu, Zhizhong Wang, Yifeng Xu, Tao Li, Guangming Xu, Xiangdong Xu, Limin Wang, Yongping Yan, Shuiyuan Xiao, Tingting Zhang, Jie Yan, Lingjiang Li, Huifang Yin, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is classified by the World Health Organization as 1 of the 10 most disabling conditions. However, nationally representative epidemiological data on OCD are not yet available in China.AimsTo investigate the prevalence, comorbidity, role impairment and healthcare utilisation of OCD in China.MethodsThe present study used a multistage clustered area probability sample to obtain representative population-based data of adults from 157 nationwide disease surveillance points across 31 provinces in China. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews with respondents to collect information based on the Composite International Diagnostic Interview 3.0. Data weighting was performed to account for differential selection probabilities and response rates, and to post-stratify the sample to ensure its representativeness of the population in China.ResultsA total of 28 140 respondents (12 537 (44.55%) males and 15 603 (55.45%) females) completed the diagnostic interview. Two-thirds of the respondents with lifetime OCD had comorbid mental disorders, with OCD typically emerging later than the comorbidities. The most common comorbidities were mood disorders (39.67%, odds ratio (OR): 9.60, 95% confidence interval (CI 7.35 to 12.53) and anxiety disorders (32.75%, OR: 13.33, 95% CI 10.14 to 17.52). Overall, 588 (weighted 58.19%) respondents with obsessions or compulsions experienced role impairment, which was most severe in those reporting unspecified symptoms. Only 46 (6.74%) respondents with lifetime OCD and 28 (6.48%) with 12-month OCD received any healthcare services for their conditions.ConclusionsThe weighted lifetime and 12-month prevalence of OCD were 2.43% and 1.63%, respectively. Most patients with OCD reported comorbid mental disorders and role impairment, but very few sought healthcare services. National programmes to expand service coverage and increase awareness of OCD are essential to meet healthcare needs in China.Keywords: Psychiatry, Cross-Sectional Studies, Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder, Epidemiologic Studies

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Previous epidemiological studies have reported notably low prevalence rates of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) in China compared with Western countries. A national epidemiological survey in 1982 reported an OCD prevalence of 0.03% among 38 136 respondents from 12 regions in China. This discrepancy may be due to the non-standardised screening tools and under-detection. An epidemiological survey in 2009 reported an adjusted 1-month prevalence of 0.08% among 16 577 respondents from four city-provinces in China. However, no nationally representative studies on the prevalence of OCD in China have been conducted to date.

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a debilitating mental disorder characterised by obsessions and/or compulsions (O/C). Obsessions are repetitive, intrusive, persistent thoughts, images or impulses, while compulsions are repetitive behavioural or mental acts that the individual feels driven to perform to achieve a sense of ‘completeness’.1 OCD is ranked among the top 10 most disabling disorders by the World Health Organization.2 It significantly impairs quality of life and is frequently associated with psychiatric comorbidities, often leading to serious health consequences.

OCD is a heterogeneous condition. Patients may experience symptoms across different dimensions, such as contamination, checking, ordering and hoarding, which are closely related to the functional outcome and therapeutic response.3 4 Each dimension comprises both obsessions and compulsions. Traditional cognitive-behavioural theories interpret compulsions as responses to obsessions,5 but clinical evidence suggests that the obsessive and compulsive symptoms within the same dimension can exist and develop independently.46,8 Therefore, comprehensive assessments of how different dimensions of obsessions and compulsions are distributed in patients with OCD and how they correlate with functional outcomes may provide new perspectives on the management of OCD and personalised treatment for specific OCD symptoms.

Current evidence suggests that OCD poses a significant threat to the mental health of Chinese residents and causes a substantial economic burden to individuals and society. The estimated annual per capita cost is approximately US$3465.88, and the estimated total yearly cost of OCD in China is approximately US$5.34 billion.9 Multiple studies have reported OCD as one of the major mental disorders in specific populations in China, such as outpatients10 and adolescents,11,13 highlighting the importance and urgency of effective management. However, these studies did not representatively describe the prevalence and distribution of OCD in China due to limited generalisability. To date, no nationally representative epidemiological data on the prevalence of OCD and related healthcare utilisation in China are available.

The China Mental Health Survey (CMHS) is the first nationally representative survey in China to investigate the epidemiology of mental disorders, conducted in 2013–2015.14 Proper interpretation of CMHS data offers a valuable reference for health practitioners. Thus, the present study used data from the CMHS to investigate the prevalence, comorbidity and role impairment associated with OCD, as well as patterns of healthcare utilisation in China. To establish a nationally representative epidemiological description of OCD, the present study seeks to (1) investigate the prevalence of obsessions, compulsions and OCD in China, (2) investigate the comorbid mental disorders associated with OCD, (3) assess the role impairment associated with OCD and (4) assess the healthcare utilisation in patients with OCD in China, in the hope of offering a comprehensive epidemiological insight of OCD in China and bridging the substantial gap in national data.

Methods

Study design and respondents

CMHS is an extensive population-based cross-sectional epidemiological survey of mental disorders in China. With the same sampling frame as the Disease Surveillance Point (DSP) system of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), it used a multistage clustered-area probability sampling method across 157 nationally representative population-based DSPs, covering districts and counties in all 31 provinces of China. A total of 40 964 households from 1256 communities or villages in 628 districts or towns of the 157 DSPs were selected using the multistage proportional to population size sampling method (figure 1). The survey was conducted from 22 July 2013 to 5 March 2015. Detailed sampling procedures are provided in online supplemental S1. People who met the following criteria were eligible for this study: (1) Chinese nationals, (2) aged≥18 years and (3) residents of the selected households for ≥6 months in the preceding year. Exclusion criteria were (1) individuals residing in institutions (ie, workplaces, construction sites, schools, hospitals, military areas and care facilities), (2) individuals with communication difficulties and (3) pregnant women, as routine blood collection could raise health concerns (CDC Chronic Diseases and Risk Factors Surveillance). Detailed procedures and ethical information are provided in online supplemental S2.

Procedures

Respondents were interviewed using the Composite International Diagnostic Interview 3.0 (CIDI 3.0, Chinese version),15 a fully structured clinical diagnostic interview designed to assess whether a person meets the diagnostic criteria for mental disorders by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) at any point in their lifetime or within 12 months preceding the interview day.16 Well-trained lay interviewers could use CIDI 3.0 to diagnose obsession, compulsion and OCD. In CMHS, a computer-assisted personal interview (CAPI) version of CIDI 3.0 was used to simplify data collection.

For the OCD module of CIDI 3.0, the respondents were asked to evaluate their obsessions (repeated unpleasant thoughts, images or impulses) and compulsions (repetitive behaviours). Obsessions included five symptoms: (1) contamination (concerns about dirt, germs or contamination), (2) checking (concerns about causing harm), (3) ordering (concerns about symmetry and order), (4) hoarding (concerns about saving or keeping things) and (5) other obsessions (eg, disturbing moral or sexual thoughts, or other recurrent unpleasant thoughts); compulsions also included five symptoms: (1) contamination (repeated washing or cleaning), (2) checking (repeated checking of things to make sure there is no harm or injury), (3) ordering (repeated ordering or touching things), (4) hoarding (always saving or keeping things) and (5) other compulsions (eg, repeated moral argument in mind, repeated praying for forgiveness or other repeated behaviours) (online supplemental S3). Respondents also specified other recurrent unpleasant thoughts or repeated behaviours for review by psychiatrists. Medical-related and substance-related exclusion criteria were considered when establishing the diagnosis according to DSM-IV. Further inquiry was conducted for respondents meeting the DSM-IV lifetime criteria for OCD to collect information about the age of onset, course of disease and recency of the disorder in the past 12 months. The diagnoses and age of onset of other mental disorders (eg, mood disorders, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, impulse-control disorders and eating disorders) were obtained using other modules of CIDI 3.0 and used to investigate the comorbidity and temporal order of OCD and other mental disorders.

The Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS, Chinese version) was used to assess OCD-related role impairment.17 The SDS includes four items: household duties, work, relationships and social life. All items were rated using a visual analogue scale from 0 to 10 (0: none; 1–3: mild; 4–6: moderate; 7–9: severe; and 10: very severe). Respondents with OCD were asked about their OCD-induced role impairment during the worst month of the past year and the total number of days they were completely incapable of working or performing routine activities due to OCD.

Respondents were also asked whether and when they received healthcare services for their OCD symptoms. Healthcare service providers were divided into five categories: (1) specialised psychiatric hospitals, with healthcare professionals being psychiatrists, psychologists or counsellors in specialised psychiatric hospitals; (2) the department of clinical psychology in general hospitals, with healthcare professionals being psychiatrists, psychologists or counsellors in the department of clinical psychology in general hospitals; (3) traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) hospitals or the department of TCM in general hospitals, with healthcare professionals being TCM specialists; (4) other departments in general hospitals, with professionals being specialists of departments other than the psychiatry department in general hospitals; and (5) other healthcare facilities, with service providers being religious staff, social workers, counsellors, online professionals or self-help groups in non-mental health and non-healthcare settings.

A total of 824 qualified interviewers trained by the Institute of Social Science Survey, Peking University (Beijing, China) conducted face-to-face interviews with the patients using the CAPI version of CIDI 3.0. In CMHS, measures were taken to ensure the validity of the epidemiological data, as previously described.14 All interviewers underwent training programmes, including interview techniques and CAPI pretraining, and passed both written and mock interview examinations. During the survey, independent quality supervisors used random audio recordings of CIDI interviews and phone back-checks to verify adherence to standardised procedures. Further details on quality control measures have been reported in published articles.14 18 19

The CMHS surveyed a total of 38 593 households. Of these households, 32 552 respondents were selected and invited to complete the CIDI 3.0 interview, and 28 140 (86.4%) completed the interview.

Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive analyses were used for the analysis of weighted prevalence, comorbidity, symptom severity, role impairment and healthcare utilisation of respondents diagnosed with OCD. Various weighting methods were employed to estimate weighted prevalence, including sampling, non-response and post-stratification weights. Post-stratification weights were calculated based on the distribution data of age, gender and residence from China’s 2010 population census. Taylor’s linearisation was used to calculate the standard error in weighted prevalence by adjusting for sampling weights and data clustering. All the reported prevalences or rates were weighted in this study. Logistic regression analyses, adjusted for sampling weights and data clustering, were used to analyse the association between sociodemographic characteristics and lifetime and 12-month prevalences of OCD, the association between gender and age of OCD onset and the association between lifetime OCD and comorbid mental disorders. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to examine the impact of obsessions and compulsions on role impairment in respondents with OCD. Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, work status, region, place of residence (urban/rural) and marital status, were analysed as independent variables in logistic regression analyses. A two-tailed p<0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. All the analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (series number: 70303445). Details of statistical analysis can be found in our previous publications.14

Interestingly, respondents with less frequent obsessions or compulsions demonstrated a higher susceptibility to being diagnosed with OCD, indicating that those with ‘other’ obsessions and compulsions might have a higher conditional probability of being diagnosed with OCD. The increase in the number of OCD symptoms was also found to be associated with a significantly higher conditional probability of lifetime OCD, with rates rising from 8.27% to 36.29%. The prevalences of any obsession and any compulsion among patients with OCD were 77.98% and 81.64%, with hoarding and checking being the most prevalent dimensions for obsession and compulsion, respectively. ‘Other obsessions’ (21.01%) and ‘other compulsions’ (28.88%) were reported as the least frequent symptoms in patients with OCD.

The age of OCD onset, stratified by gender, is presented in online supplemental figure 1. The study found no significant gender difference in the age of OCD onset (odds ratio (OR)=1.05, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.78 to 1.42). Early-onset OCD occurred in both male and female respondents, with OCD onset before age 30 in more than 50% of patients.

Comorbidities of OCD

Approximately two-thirds of respondents (66.69%) with lifetime OCD met the criteria for at least one other lifetime mental disorder (table 3). The most common comorbid mental disorders among individuals with OCD were mood disorders (39.67%), followed by anxiety disorders (32.75%), substance use disorders (16.92%), impulse-control disorders (10.97%) and eating disorders (1.59%). 17 of the 18 comorbid mental disorders were found to be significantly associated with OCD after adjustment for sociodemographic characteristics. Anxiety disorders and mood disorders had higher ORs, ranging from 8.76 to 16.45 and 5.76 to 15.37, respectively, followed by eating disorders (OR: 4.91 to 17.00), impulse-control disorders (OR: 8.56 to 9.22) and substance use disorders (OR: 2.74 to 8.25).

In more than half of the respondents (56.22%), the onset of OCD occurred after other mental disorders. Among those with comorbid anxiety disorders, 77.67% developed OCD following anxiety disorders, while panic disorder was more likely to emerge in the presence of OCD (49.83%). In more than half of patients with mood disorders (54.58%), OCD occurred before the onset of mood disorders, while bipolar II disorder often occurred at the same time as OCD (58.25%). For substance use disorders, the majority (53.31%) occurred before OCD. Among patients with comorbid eating disorders, the proportion with first OCD (46.11%) was comparable to that with secondary OCD (51.37%). Additional details on comorbid mental disorders not explicitly categorised can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Role impairment in respondents with O/C by SDS category

Most respondents with O/C (58.19%) experienced role impairment across one or more SDS domains. Specifically, 2% reported mild role impairment, 15.89% reported moderate role impairment, 9.99% reported severe role impairment and 2.16% reported very severe role impairment after data weighting. A positive correlation was found between the number of O/C and the severity of role impairment across all SDS domains: as the number of O/C increased, the likelihood of severe or very severe impairment also increased in each SDS domain (figure 2).

Regression analysis between severity of role impairment and O/C dimension

Multiple linear regression analyses showed that role impairment in different domains was associated with different O/C dimensions (online supplemental table 2). Compulsive contamination (β=0.15) was most strongly associated with household duty impairment, followed by ‘other compulsions’ (β=0.12) and ‘other obsessions’ (β=0.07). ‘Other compulsions’ (β=0.12) were most strongly associated with work impairment, followed by obsessive checking (β=0.09) and ‘other obsessions’ (β=0.09). Obsessive checking (β=0.10) and ‘other compulsions’ (β=0.10) were most strongly associated with relationship impairment, followed by ‘other obsessions’ (β=0.09). ‘Other obsessions’ (β=0.12) were most strongly associated with social life impairment, followed by obsessive checking (β=0.09), ‘other compulsions’ (β=0.09) and compulsive contamination (β=0.08). Interestingly, compulsive checking was associated with lower severity of household duty impairment (β=−0.12), work impairment (β=−0.08) and relationship impairment (β=−0.09).

Healthcare utilisation among patients with lifetime and 12-month OCD

The healthcare utilisation among patients with lifetime and 12-month OCD is presented in online supplemental table 3. The mean (standard deviation (SD)) age of OCD onset was 29.28 (17.40) and 29.03 (17.63) years for patients with lifetime and 12 month OCD, respectively, whereas the mean (SD) age at first healthcare utilisation was 40.61 (16.06) and 37.27 (15.05) years, respectively. Among respondents, 46 (6.74%) of 622 with lifetime OCD and 28 (6.48%) of 410 with 12-month OCD had received treatment in any healthcare facility (online supplemental table 3).

The characteristics of healthcare utilisation among patients with any O/C dimensions are presented in online supplemental table 4. Overall, the lifetime weighted rate of healthcare utilisation in respondents with any O/C dimensions was 3.10%, with 4.30% and 3.35% for obsessive and compulsive symptoms, respectively. Utilisation rates varied by O/C dimension: patients with ‘other obsessions’ (6.71%) and ‘other compulsions’ (7.73%) showed the highest rates of healthcare utilisation, whereas those with hoarding exhibited the lowest rates (3.52% for obsessive hoarding and 3.91% for compulsive hoarding). An increase in the number of OCD symptoms was associated with higher rates of healthcare utilisation among respondents with any O/C dimensions.

Discussion

Main findings

This study represents the first nationwide epidemiological investigation of the prevalence, comorbid mental disorders and functional impairment of OCD, as well as related healthcare utilisation in China. We explored the epidemiology of obsessions and compulsions to inform the development of preventive and management strategies for OCD. The findings also highlighted that only a small proportion of patients diagnosed with OCD received adequate healthcare services, primarily from specialised mental health services. This calls for additional national-level measures to improve the availability, accessibility and acceptability of healthcare services and provide better healthcare education to patients with OCD in China.

The lifetime and 12-month prevalence of OCD in China were 2.43% and 1.63%, similar to 2.3% and 1.2% in the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication20 and slightly lower than 3.8% and 2.7% in the 2007 Australian National Survey of Mental Health and Well-being.21 A study in Konya, Turkey, reported a 12-month prevalence rate of 3.0%,22 while the Singapore Mental Health Study reported a lifetime and 12-month prevalence of 3.0% and 1.1%, respectively.23 These differences may reflect distinct cultural beliefs, stigma surrounding mental health and disparities in socioeconomic development. The sociodemographic characteristics associated with OCD in the present study differed from existing epidemiological data; for instance, the mean (SD) age of OCD onset was 29.2 (17.4) years, later than that reported in Turkey,22 the USA20 and some Asian countries.23 The present study found no significant gender differences in the lifetime and 12-month prevalence of OCD or the age of OCD onset, which contrasts with some prior studies20 but aligns with others.23,25 Both lifetime and 12-month OCD prevalences showed no significant difference between age groups. While most studies report lower prevalence in older adults,23 26 in the present study, we observed only marginally lower rates in this group (lifetime 1.93%, 12-month 1.38%), without statistical significance. This study also found a significant association between being separated, widowed or divorced and higher lifetime OCD prevalence. However, given the cross-sectional design, causality between marital breakdown and OCD onset cannot be inferred, despite observed role impairment in relationships among respondents with O/C symptoms.

The present study showed that the lifetime prevalence of any O/C dimensions was 14.28%, and the lifetime weighted prevalences of obsessions and compulsions were 9.23% and 9.96%, respectively. The most prevalent O/C dimension was hoarding, with a prevalence of 5.50%, consistent with previous studies in the Asian population.23 Checking (7.8%) was the most common O/C dimension in the European population,27 and checking (15.4%) and hoarding (14.4%) were identified as the most common O/C dimensions in the USA.20 It is also suggested that O/C dimensions remain transitory and may be associated with genetic, neural and sociocultural factors.28 29

The present study also revealed substantial comorbidity of mental disorders among patients with a history of OCD. Mood disorders were the most common comorbidities, while anxiety disorders showed the strongest associations, consistent with prior studies in Asia.23 Earlier research indicates that OCD tends to manifest at a later age compared with its comorbid disorders,20 which highlights the significance of OCD prevention in patients with mental disorders. Notably, the comorbidity rate of OCD with drug use disorders was much lower in China (2.45%) compared with the USA (21.7%) and the UK (25.6%), likely reflecting the strict drug policy and sociocultural differences.20 26

In addition, the present study suggested that the majority of respondents diagnosed with OCD experience role impairment. The results revealed that specific obsessions and compulsions significantly impacted role functioning; specifically, ‘other obsessions’ and ‘other compulsions’ were found to be associated with the most severe role impairment, followed by obsessive checking and compulsive contamination. In contrast, ordering and hoarding were not significantly associated with role impairment. Meanwhile, epidemiological studies showed that respondents with ‘other’ obsessions and compulsions had the highest conditional probability of developing OCD. The present study suggests that patients with OCD are more likely to experience significant impairment in their daily functioning, especially for those with O/C, which leads to the most severe role impairment. Furthermore, these patients exhibit a greater prevalence of lifetime OCD compared with those with less severe impairment. Hence, healthcare professionals should consider the unique manifestations of OCD and develop individualised intervention strategies.

The present study indicates that compulsive checking behaviours might be associated with less severe role impairment in the O/C dimensions. Our analysis revealed that respondents displaying compulsive checking behaviours experienced fewer limitations in their household tasks, work responsibilities and interpersonal relationships compared with those with ‘other’ obsessive-compulsive behaviours. Previous studies have indicated that patients with compulsive checking also showed impaired prospective memory, which refers to the formation of intentions, plans and commitments and the subsequent recall and execution of them in an appropriate context.29,31 Memory deficits and/or lack of confidence in memory can lead to the failure to distinguish between actual and imagined events.32 In such cases, patients may use compulsive checking to reverify the plans or events and reduce the anxiety arising from memory deficits.33 34 Therefore, compulsive checking may be associated with less severe role impairment among all dimensions.

Moreover, only a small proportion of patients with lifetime (6.74%) and 12-month (6.48%) OCD reported using healthcare services in any facility. This percentage is significantly higher in the USA, with 72.7% and 49.2% of patients using healthcare services for the same conditions.20 There was also a considerable time gap between the onset of OCD and the first healthcare utilisation. In the present study, respondents with 12-month OCD showed a relatively low rate of healthcare utilisation in specialised psychiatric hospitals, which might be attributed to the stigma and a lack of awareness of OCD.35 Therefore, it is crucial to formulate national programmes of public education on mental health and improve healthcare services for patients with OCD in China. It is also important to note that some DSM-5-defined subgroups, such as patients with poor insight, may contribute to the significantly low treatment rates observed in this study, as they often underestimate the severity of their symptoms or perceive them as ego-syntonic, which can delay or prevent them from seeking healthcare. This highlights that both limited healthcare access and patient-related factors contribute to unmet treatment needs.

This study also suggested that the severity of obsessive-compulsive symptoms might be associated with the diagnosis of OCD and healthcare utilisation. We found that respondents with obsessive checking, compulsive contamination and ‘other’ obsessions and compulsions, which caused the most severe role impairment, had the highest conditional probability of lifetime OCD and the highest rate of healthcare utilisation for any O/C dimension. The respondents with hoarding and compulsive checking, which may lead to milder role impairment, showed a low conditional probability of lifetime OCD and a low rate of healthcare utilisation. These findings indicate that it is essential to consider the multidimensional and heterogeneous characteristics of obsessive and compulsive symptoms.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, the participants were household residents, with pregnant women and institutionalised people excluded. This might have limited the generalisability of our findings and could have introduced biases in the selection process, which might lead to an underestimation of the prevalence of OCD. Future research should include these subgroups to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of OCD in China. Second, the lifetime prevalence and age of OCD onset might be affected by recall bias due to the cross-sectional design. Longitudinal study designs are important to track temporal changes in OCD prevalence and healthcare utilisation. Third, the study used the CIDI 3.0, a diagnostic tool based on the DSM-IV criteria, to diagnose OCD. Despite the robustness of this tool, it may not reflect the complete range of OCD symptoms described in the revised DSM-5, which includes expanded symptom dimensions and specifiers (eg, with poor insight, with tic-related) and may lead to higher and more clinically nuanced prevalence estimates. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that DSM-5-based surveys tend to yield slightly higher prevalence rates compared with DSM-IV-based studies, particularly when subthreshold or subtype presentations are considered.36 Thus, our prevalence estimates may be conservative and should be interpreted in light of this diagnostic framework. Future nationwide epidemiological surveys based on the new diagnosis criteria are needed to provide more precise and clinically relevant estimates of OCD prevalence in China.

Implications

This comprehensive investigation of OCD in China has several implications. First, this study highlights the urgent need to improve mental health services and public awareness of OCD, especially among vulnerable populations. The low healthcare utilisation rates highlight the need to improve healthcare coverage. Second, the high comorbidity rates of OCD and other mental disorders complicate management and treatment, emphasising the need for effective, safe and targeted intervention strategies. Third, about 12% of respondents with O/C who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for OCD might also experience role impairment and require help, which should be considered when developing mental health policies and initiatives in China. Fourth, given the dynamic changes in mental health awareness, healthcare utilisation and public policy in China over the past decade, these estimates may not fully reflect current trends. Therefore, periodic large-scale epidemiological surveys are essential to ensure timely monitoring of mental health needs, enhance the responsiveness of healthcare systems and inform evidence-based policymaking tailored to OCD and related disorders in China.

Conclusions

The present study is the first nationally representative epidemiological survey of OCD in China. The findings demonstrated that the lifetime and 12-month prevalence of OCD in China were 2.43% and 1.63%, respectively, and highlighted the functional impairment and comorbidities related to OCD. This study provides insight into OCD-related healthcare utilisation, which may facilitate the formulation of future policies and practices in China and other middle- and low-income countries. This study also suggests that based on the OCD-related healthcare utilisation status in China, the availability, accessibility and acceptability of mental health services remain insufficient. More financial investment is also required to expand health service coverage and improve public education to promote effective use of healthcare services.
