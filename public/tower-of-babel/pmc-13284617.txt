Can Response Outcomes Predict Survival in Paediatric Patients Receiving Treatment for Relapsed and Refractory Rhabdomyosarcoma? Results From the Living‐REFoRMS Systematic Review
Authors: Preethi Muthukumar, Lucy Shepherd, Sumayya Anwer, Connor Evans, Jessica E Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe relationship between radiological response and survival outcomes has been studied in upfront treatment in paediatric rhabdomyosarcoma and adult cancer settings. However, the extent of the relationship between these outcomes is unknown in children and young people (CYP) with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma. Studies included in the Living‐REFoRMS systematic review were analysed using Pearson correlation and linear regression to assess the relationship between objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR), and progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of removing cohorts that only included one evaluable CYP with rhabdomyosarcoma. Thirty‐one studies (18% of total Living‐REFoRMS dataset) reporting 46 cohorts with a total of 419 evaluable CYP were eligible for this analysis. Forty‐eight percent of cohorts included only one evaluable CYP with rhabdomyosarcoma. ORR and DCR were positively correlated with PFS (p < 0.0001). ORR and DCR were positively correlated with OS, though this was not statistically significant in ORR (p = 0.276). All correlations were statistically significant in the sensitivity analyses, highlighting the impact that cohorts including a single CYP with rhabdomyosarcoma evaluable for response had on the results. These results illustrate a positive correlation between tumour shrinkage and control, and the duration of survival in relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma. We encourage the reporting of both ORR and DCR (or the elements required to calculate these) by tumour type within study reports to facilitate these aspects of clinical decision making.Keywords: children and young people, radiological response, relapsed/refractory cancer, rhabdomyosarcoma, survival

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma is a high‐grade, malignant neoplasm most commonly seen in paediatric, teenage and young adult patients. One third of children and young people (CYP) with rhabdomyosarcoma will experience relapsed or refractory disease [1]. For this population, outcomes are poor with only one in five CYP being cured [2]. Treatment options, especially after first relapse, are limited, and many patients will subsequently consider enrolling onto an early‐phase trial [3].

The Living‐REFoRMS project uses living systematic review methodology to identify, synthesise and evaluate the literature available from early phase trials in CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma, routinely incorporating new evidence as it becomes available. Up to 25/10/2024, it includes 175 early phase studies of interventions for CYP with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma since 2000 [4, 5]. The aim of the overarching project is to develop an up‐to‐date, detailed synthesis of evidence that will inform clinicians, researchers and patients when it comes to making decisions about treatment.

Most of these studies measure the success of a treatment by the radiological response in the tumour. This can be specific to the study but commonly includes an objective response rate (ORR) as defined by RECIST criteria [6]. However, a valid question is how likely it is that an ORR translates to an increased duration of survival. Knowing this may influence decision‐making when considering treatment options.

Research to understand the relationship between radiological response and survival has been conducted but has predominantly focused on adult cancer. A systematic review by Cooper et al. identified 63 studies across 20 tumour types (mostly advanced or metastatic) that evaluated the relationship between radiological response and survival using meta‐regression and correlation analysis. They found that the strength of the association between overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS) and ORR (proportion of complete response [CR] and partial response [PR]) was variable, especially for PFS and ORR. The authors concluded that radiological response may not be a reliable surrogate for survival outcomes [7].

In newly diagnosed rhabdomyosarcoma, studies have also explored the association between radiological response and survival [8, 9]. Rodeberg et al. compared response rates (CR versus PR/no response [NR]) with OS and failure‐free survival (FFS) in 419 CYP who were enrolled into the IRS‐IV study. The study did not find a statistically significant relationship between the risk of failure or death and CR versus PR/NR. Meanwhile, Lautz et al. assessed the relationship between response rates, OS and FFS in two Children's Oncology Group studies; while 5‐year FFS was greater for those achieving a CR compared to PR/NR, this was not statistically significant. Furthermore, radiological response was not associated with OS. Although research to date suggests that there is a limited relationship between survival outcomes and response rates in the upfront setting, there is little evidence in the relapsed/refractory setting.

The Living‐REFoRMS systematic review parent group collaborated closely with our team in the design and conduct of our research. They felt that OS and PFS were the most important outcomes to collect in the reviews. As these survival outcomes are not routinely collected in early phase studies, this analysis aims to explore the correlation between response rates and patient survival measured in early phase studies including CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma and patient survival.

Methods

This analysis uses data from the Living‐REFoRMS systematic review. In brief, the systematic review includes early phase studies of interventions for CYP under the age of 18 years with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma, published after 2000. Screening, data extraction and quality assessment were conducted by two reviewers. Following the baseline systematic review [4], searches were re‐run approximately every 6 months and newly identified studies were included in the Living‐REFoRMS dataset. Further details on the Living‐REFoRMS systematic review have been published elsewhere [4, 5].

For this analysis, studies from the Living‐REFoRMS systematic review were reviewed, and those that reported both radiological response and median survival outcomes were eligible for inclusion. Primary outcomes of interest in this analysis were ORR, disease control rate (DCR), PFS and OS.

Radiological response was most commonly categorised as one of the following: CR, PR, stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). In some studies, minor response (MR) was reported; as the MR criteria is similar to PR in RECIST, patients achieving a MR were classified as having a PR in this analysis. While we acknowledge the nuances between PFS, time to progression and event‐free survival, for the purposes of the analysis they were considered the same. Additional information on the criteria used for assessing response (e.g., RECIST criteria) and when response was measured were extracted. When not directly reported, ORR and DCR (percentage of participants achieving a CR, PR or SD) were calculated for each study. Some studies only stated that there were ‘no objective responses’ and therefore DCR could not be calculated.

Studies were excluded if the number of CYP with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma who were evaluable for response was not reported, as regression analyses were weighted on sample size. Patients who were disease‐free at the start of the trials were also excluded from our analysis as they could not have an evaluable tumour response. Where studies reported both response at a particular time point (i.e., two cycles) and best ORR, the response at a particular time point was used in the analysis.

The relationship between response and survival outcomes was assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient [10]. The Pearson correlation was chosen as the presence of tied observations (i.e., observations with the same values) in the data prevented the use of more robust rank‐based correlation methods. These tied observations were particularly seen at the extremes as many of the cohorts only included a single‐patient (and therefore, response could only be 0% or 100%). Linear regression models were fit to the data to explore the relationship further. Due to the sparsity of data, sophisticated methods such as multivariate meta‐analysis [11], where evidence is borrowed across studies, were not explored further. p values below the threshold of 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Post hoc sensitivity analyses were considered after the results for the main analyses were completed. Data transformations were also explored in order to find an appropriate model fit. All analyses were conducted in base R version 4.5.1 [12], with the car companion package [13]. All plots were constructed using the ggplot2 package [14].

Results

Study and Patient Characteristics

Thirty‐one studies comprising 46 cohorts including 419 evaluable CYP with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma from the Living‐REFoRMS dataset provided data on both response rates and survival outcomes and were included in this analysis. This reflects 18% of the total Living‐REFoRMS studies. A summary of characteristics of the included studies in the present analysis can be found in Table S1. Further details of the studies can be found on the REFoRMS webpage and publications [4, 5]. Table 1 presents a comparison of the characteristics of the studies included in this analysis to the overall Living‐REFoRMS dataset. Studies included in this analysis were generally similar to the Living‐REFoRMS dataset, although this analysis did include more studies in rhabdomyosarcoma patients only, and included a greater proportion of cohorts with only one evaluable CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma.

Studies primarily included multiple tumour types, so the number of evaluable CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma in each cohort was low (median 2, range 1–58), and 48% of cohorts included a single evaluable patient with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma. This analysis included mostly Phase 2 (n = 12) or Phase 1 (n = 7) studies; there were five molecular registry studies, which contributed 16 cohorts to the analysis (35%). Cohorts primarily investigated novel agents (n = 24, 52%), either alone (n = 11) or in combination with other therapies (n = 13). Single and multi‐agent standard systemic therapies were investigated in 14 cohorts. Other therapies, including stem cell transplant and metronomic chemotherapy, were evaluated in eight cohorts.

Studies of multiple tumour types often did not report patient characteristics specifically for rhabdomyosarcoma. Where patient characteristics were available for rhabdomyosarcoma patients, this was often reported for the number of patients that were enrolled, rather than those who were evaluable for response. Of patients who were enrolled in the included studies and where the type of rhabdomyosarcoma was reported, the majority of CYP had alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (n = 171, 68%), while 81 were diagnosed with embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (32%). In the 163 CYP with rhabdomyosarcoma where relapse status was reported, 109 (67%) were receiving treatment for first relapse, 14 (9%) for second relapse and 40 (25%) for refractory disease.

In all cohorts, the ORR was either reported or was able to be calculated; the DCR was reported or calculated in 43 cohorts. Data on the median PFS or OS were available in 43 cohorts and 20 cohorts, respectively.

Statistical Analyses

The correlations between survival outcomes (OS and PFS) and response rates (ORR and DCR) were calculated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (Table 2). All data points were assumed to be equal, with no weights assigned on sample size. ORR and DCR in cohorts with a single patient have to be either 0% or 100%, which may introduce bias towards the two extremes. Therefore, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted that excluded all single‐participant cohorts. Transformations were attempted on the dataset with a log‐transformation on survival outcomes and a logit‐transformation on response outcomes. As data transformations did not improve the model fit or correlation results, they were not selected due to parsimony (where simpler models are preferred over more complex models if the latter do not substantially improve the model fit).

The number of evaluable patients remained consistent across the different analyses, irrespective of the number of cohorts included. As presented in Table 2, there was a statistically significant, positive correlation between median PFS and both ORR (r = 0.664, p < 0.0001) and DCR (r = 0.670, p < 0.0001) for the main analysis. The correlation calculated in the sensitivity analysis was consistent with the main analysis (r = 0.759, p < 0.0001 for median PFS vs. ORR; and r = 0.686, p = 0.001 for median PFS vs. DCR).

There was a statistically significant, positive correlation between median OS and DCR (r = 0.492, p = 0.038) that was further strengthened during the sensitivity analysis (r = 0.783, p = 0.002). The relationship between DCR and OS was strongest in all of our analyses, reflecting the importance of accounting for SD when considering the relationship between response and survival in this setting. The correlation between median OS and ORR was not statistically significant (p = 0.276); however, the positive correlation for the sensitivity analysis was statistically significant (r = 0.708, p = 0.003). The difference in correlations in the main and sensitivity analyses could be attributed to the fewer number of cohorts included in the analyses. As there were fewer cohorts, the included cohorts which had only one evaluable patient with rhabdomyosarcoma, where response rates are at the extremes may have had a greater impact in the correlation than they should.

In addition, an exploratory analysis fitting a weighted linear regression model to the data was conducted to assess the relationship between response and survival outcomes. Observations were weighted by the inverse sample size, with larger cohorts having more influence. Results for the weighted linear regression models are provided in Table 3. The regression coefficient was significant for all four models, further suggesting that response and survival outcomes are correlated, with survival increasing with an increase in response rate. The regression coefficient can be interpreted as follows: a 10% increase in DCR is associated with an increase in PFS by 0.56 months, and an increase in overall survival by 1.4 months. The results for the sensitivity analysis, where cohorts with only one patient with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma in them were removed, were consistent with the main analysis, although removing these cohorts improves the model fit.

Discussion

Using the Living‐REFoRMS dataset, this study analysed the relationship between response rates and survival from 31 studies including 419 CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma. In the main correlation analyses, our results show that there is a significant relationship between DCR and PFS (p < 0.0001) and OS (p = 0.038). While the relationship between ORR and PFS was significant (p < 0.0001), the relationship with OS was not (p = 0.276).

Both DCR and ORR are strongly correlated with OS and PFS. CYP can live with SD for a significant duration, and early tumour shrinkage may not always translate to sustained response. This allows us to conclude that DCR should be considered when evaluating the benefit of therapies and could be potentially more clinically relevant and meaningful to families and practitioners. This is dependent on the clinical picture for each CYP—those with asymptomatic disease may benefit most from disease control, whilst those experiencing challenging symptoms may prefer an approach focused on reducing tumour size to alleviate these. Given these clinical implications of the different outcomes, we strongly recommend reporting of DCR and ORR in future studies and consideration of which of these reflects the most important primary outcome given the study aims.

One of the main limitations of this dataset, and a wider reflection of conducting clinical trials in rare disease areas, is the large proportion of studies including a single patient with disease type—in this case, rhabdomyosarcoma. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the impact of studies including a single patient with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma (who were typically enrolled in basket trials including CYP with multiple tumour types, or molecular registry studies), as the inclusion of these could introduce bias to the extremes. Removing studies including only one evaluable CYP with relapsed or refractory rhabdomyosarcoma made the correlation between response and survival outcomes more pronounced. This is seen when calculating the correlation between median OS and ORR, where removing these studies makes the correlation statistically significant. The Pearson correlation is more sensitive to outliers particularly those with high leverage [15] (i.e., observations with extreme/outlying values of the independent variable—in this case ORR and DCR) and/or coincidental outliers [16, 17]. The presence of a high number of tied observations prevented the use of more robust rank‐based correlations such as Spearman's correlation [18]. While studies with a single CYP provide useful data on which therapies have been explored in relapsed/refractory rhabdomyosarcoma, they do impact analyses, especially as they account for a large proportion of the dataset used in this study.

While we acknowledge that the main aim in early phase trials is often to assess the safety of a treatment, not necessarily its effectiveness, the reporting of response rates and survival outcomes in the included early phase trials was often limited. This was especially the case in basket trials including multiple tumour types. This meant that overall, we could only include 18% of studies from the Living‐REFoRMS dataset. While the studies included in this analysis were generally similar compared to the entire Living‐REFoRMS dataset (see Table 1), the inclusion of only a subset of studies could introduce bias into the analysis. Studies that did not report more comprehensive data (and therefore could not be included in the analysis) may have shown less promising results for and young people with CYP with rhabdomyosarcoma and therefore not have reported in detail what the outcomes were.

Furthermore, in many studies included in the analysis, there was incomplete reporting of outcomes for non‐responders, with results primarily focusing on patients who achieved a CR or PR, or simply reporting that there was no ‘objective response’. Given that DCR is strongly correlated with survival outcomes, future early phase trials should report the number of patients achieving SD or DCR by tumour type. In the Living‐REFoRMS project, we will report both DCR and ORR going forward.

In addition, many of the studies included in the Living‐REFoRMS dataset could not be included in this analysis as they did not report the number of patients with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma who were evaluable for response. In addition, survival endpoints are often aggregated across tumour types, which may not be meaningful given the differing biologies of paediatric and adolescent cancers. We encourage greater granularity in the reporting of the number of evaluable patients, response rates (including ORR, DCR and the number of SD and PD responses) and survival outcomes (including proportion and/or median PFS/OS) by individual tumour types in early phase trials where this data is collected. Reporting all available data is especially critical in small populations like relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma, where each participant may contribute meaningful information to secondary analyses such as this, and focused trials are rare.

Finally, there was limited reporting of patient characteristics specific to rhabdomyosarcoma in the included studies. Where data are reported, there is some suggestion that the included patients had poorer histology (alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma), but they were mostly at first relapse. This is important as the significance of tumour response to therapy at relapse is known to be closely associated with a patient's disease, including number of relapses and prior therapy [19, 20]. Additional analyses evaluating the impact of patient characteristics on the relationship between response and survival are warranted.

This paper presents a comprehensive analysis using data from the Living‐REFoRMS project, an extensive systematic review programme performed by evidence synthesis experts. While previous research has explored the relationship between response and survival in newly‐diagnosed rhabdomyosarcoma [8, 9], this analysis provides new insights into this relationship in the relapsed/refractory setting. As the Living‐REFoRMS database uses data from published studies, the analysis is based on aggregate data, and we therefore acknowledge that study‐level comparisons may not necessarily translate to similar relationships at an individual level, especially when accounting for differences in patient and tumour characteristics. Many of these challenges could be overcome by individual‐patient data analyses of this dataset; however, the resources required to achieve this would be prohibitive. Finally, given the small sample sizes in the majority of studies, the relationship between response and survival is likely to be driven by a small number of larger studies, including Mascarenhas et al. [21] which only included patients at first relapse.

Conclusion

Using aggregate data collected within the Living‐REFoRMS dataset of early phase trials, we have established that response rates (particularly disease control) and survival duration are strongly correlated in CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma. These findings support the clinical value of disease stabilisation and highlight the need for standardised, granular reporting of response rates and consistently provided survival outcomes in order to strengthen the evidence base in this setting. Nonetheless, this analysis supports clinical decision making based on response rates—including disease control—for CYP with relapsed and refractory rhabdomyosarcoma.
