Cohomology of compactified Jacobians for locally planar integral curves
Junliang Shen

Abstract
. This article surveys some recent developments on the cohomology of the compactified Jacobian associated with a locally planar integral curve. Topics discussed here include the Ngô support theorem, the perverse filtration, connections to the Hilbert schemes, and cohomological structures induced by the Arinkin–Fourier–Mukai transform.

Introduction

Throughout, we work over the complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
.

For a nonsingular irreducible projective curve
C
of genus
g
, the space of holomorphic differential forms is a
g
-dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})
. The homology lattice
H_{1}(C,{\mathbb{Z}})
embeds naturally in the dual vector space
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})^{\ast}
via integration

[\gamma]\in H_{1}(C,{\mathbb{Z}})\mapsto\left(\omega\mapsto\int_{\gamma}\omega\right)\in H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{1})^{\ast}.

The associated quotient
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})/H_{1}(C,{\mathbb{Z}})
is a
g
-dimensional complex torus, called the Jacobian
J_{C}
of the curve
C
.

The Jacobian is naturally an abelian variety — a complex torus which can be realized as an algebraic variety. An algebraic interpretation of
J_{C}
is given by the Picard variety parameterizing degree 0 line bundles on the curve
C
,

J_{C}=\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(C):=\{\mathrm{degree~0~line~bundles~on~C}\}.

This modular description is often regarded as the definition of the Jacobian
J_{C}
in algebraic geometry.

For a singular curve
C
, the Jacobian variety
J_{C}
(parameterizing degree 0 line bundles) is no longer proper.
1
1

1

The variety
J_{C}
for a singular curve
C
is sometimes called the generalized Jacobian.
If the curve
C
is integral (
i.e.
reduced and irreducible), there is a natural modular compactification of
J_{C}
— by
[
2
]
there is a fine projective moduli space
\overline{J}_{C}
parameterizing torsion free sheaves of rank 1 and degree 0 on the integral curve
C
; this is called the compactified Jacobian associated with
C
. Furthermore, we know from
[
1
,
29
]
that
\overline{J}_{C}
is irreducible if and only if the integral curve
C
is locally planar (
i.e.
each analytic neighborhood of a singular point of
C
can be embedded in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
). In this case, the compactified Jacobian

J_{C}\subset\overline{J}_{C}

is an “honest” compactification of
J_{C}
: it contains
J_{C}
as a Zariski open and dense subset, and the boundary is given by those rank 1 torsion free sheaves that fail to be line bundles.

In this survey article, we focus on the compactified Jacobian associated with a locally planar integral curve
C
. There are (at least) three reasons for such a choice. First, it is a fundamental and simple geometric object,
e.g.
it is integral and
l.c.i.
, and there is no complicated stability condition involved as in other moduli problems in algebraic geometry. Second, it carries rich structures that lie in the crossroads of many branches of mathematics. Third, many new tools have been developed in recent years to study them,
e.g.
perverse sheaves, derived categories, representation theory, knot/link theory,
etc
.

Let me give some examples of the second point above to illustrate that the cohomology of the compactified Jacobian associated with a locally planar integral curve has rich connections:

\bullet

Many compactified Jacobians are geometric models of affine Springer fibers, whose cohomology admit interesting representations
[
30
,
48
,
49
,
20
]
.

\bullet

The topology of a planar singular point is characterized by the associated link; the cohomology of the compactified Jacobian of a locally planar curve is expected to detect deep link invariants of the singularities
[
50
,
53
]
;

\bullet

Compactified Jacobians associated with spectral curves (which are automatically locally planar) arise as Hitchin fibers; therefore, the local cohomological study of the Hitchin fibration is reduced to the study of the cohomology of certain compactified Jacobians
[
46
,
15
,
36
]
;

\bullet

Cohomology of compactified Jacobians are closely related to the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants in enumerative geometry
[
40
,
41
,
44
,
16
]
;

\bullet

Compactified Jacobians are natural geometric degenerations of abelian varieities; therefore they can be used to test conjectures for abelian fibrations with singular fibers
[

,

.

An important structure that is related to all the stories above is the
perverse filtration
. In short, the cohomology
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
is not only a graded vector space, but is naturally
filtered
:

(1)

P_{0}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{1}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{2}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset\cdots\subset H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

This filtration endows the cohomology a “hidden grading”, which plays a crucial role in the connections to geometric representation theory, link invariants, Gopakumar–Vafa invariants
etc
mentioned above.

The perverse filtration for a general projective morphism was used in the seminal paper of de Cataldo–Migliorini
[
11
]
in their proof of the decomposition theorem
[
8
]
via classical Hodge theory. Then de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini
[
13
]
discovered that the perverse filtration associated with the Hitchin fibration plays a crucial role in non-abelian Hodge theory, and formulated the (now proven)
P=W
conjecture
[
37
,
22
,
38
,
23
]
. The perverse filtration (
1
) for the compactified Jacobian is viewed as a “local analog” of the perverse filtration for the Hitchin fibration, which seems to be more mysterious. For example, the cohomology of the moduli space of stable Higgs bundles is generated by
tautological classes
; as a key step in all the known proofs of the
P=W
conjecture, the perverse filtration of the Hitchin fibration can be completely described in terms of the taugological classes. However, for the compactified Jacobian associated with a general locally planar integral curve, it is not clear how to present all classes in the cohomology explicitly; therefore it is more difficult to describe the perverse filtration. Furthermore, the non-abelian Hodge theory for the moduli space of stable Higgs bundles is well-understood; motivated by connections to link theory, Shende
[
53
]
proposed that the perverse filtration (
1
) should also be matched with the weight filtration of some
Betti moduli space
under a version of non-abelian Hodge theory; see also
[
21
, Conjecture 6.16 and Remark 6.17]
. Although this proposal has been studied intensively after its appearance (see
e.g.

[
9
,
59
,
58
]
), it is still mysterious what is the correct geometric formulation of the non-abelian Hodge theory for an arbitrary planar singularity.

These notes present several topics on recent developments in the cohomological study of compactified Jacobians. We focus on the particularly accessible yet rich case of locally planar integral curves. The goal is to illustrate some new ideas and techniques emerging in this area, in which the perverse filtration plays a central role.

Plan of the following sections

Section
1
focuses on the support theorem of Ngô; the support theorem is a fundamental new input in the modern cohomological study of compactified Jacobians. As a consequence, the cohomology of the compactified Jacobian carries a
canonical
perverse filtration, introduced by Maulik–Yun; we discuss this filtration, its properties, and some open questions in Section
2
. In Section
3

The last section discusses the Arinkin–Fourier–Mukai transform for the compactified Jacobian which extends the classical Fourier–Mukai transform for abelian varieties; we explain how this duality for the derived categories of coherent sheaves yields new structural results for the perverse filtration associated with the compactified Jacobian.

1.
Ngô support theorem

1.1.
Compactfied Jacobian fibrations

One of the first constructions of compactified Jacobians by Igusa
[
25
]
viewed the compactified Jacobian for a singular curves as the limit of a family of Jacobian varieties for nonsingular curves. The purpose of this section is to explain that the cohomology of the compactified Jacobian is also the “limit” of the cohomology of Jacobians for nonsinguar curves. A precise formulation requires the decomposition theorem of Beilinson, Bernstein, Deligne, and Gabber
[
8
]
and its refinement due to Ngô
[
46
]
.

To state the main result (Theorem
1.1
) of this section, we first introduce some notation. We fix
C
to be a locally planar integral curve, and we include it in a “large enough” family as follows. We let
p:{\mathcal{C}}\to B
be a flat family of integral projective curves of arithmetic genus
g
with planar singularities over a nonsingular base
B
, where
C\subset{\mathcal{C}}
is the fiber over a point
0\in B
. We denote by
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
the compactified Jacobian fibration, and assume that the total space
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
is nonsingular. In particular,
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
is flat with integral fibers of dimension
g
. The family
\pi:\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\to B
contains
\overline{J}_{C}=\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{0}}
as a fiber over
0\in B
, and a general fiber is the Jacobian variety
J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}
associated with a nonsingular curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
. By
[
41
, Proposition 3.5]
, such a family always exists.

We denote by
p^{\circ}:{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
the locus of nonsingular curves with
\pi^{\circ}:J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}\to B^{\circ}
the smooth Jacobian fibration. For any nonsingular curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
with
b\in B^{\circ}
, its total cohomology is the exterior algebra of the first cohomology of the curve,

H^{*}(J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\wedge^{*}H^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{b},{\mathbb{Q}}).

A relative version of this result is given by Deligne’s decomposition theorem for smooth maps:

(2)

R\pi^{\circ}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}[-i]\in D^{b}_{c}(B^{\circ}).

Here every term in the righ-hand side is a (shifted) local system, and the isomorphism holds in the bounded derived category
D^{b}_{c}(B^{\circ})
of constructible sheaves on
B^{\circ}
.

The following theorem extends (
2
) to all the singular fibers via the intermediate extension of perverse sheaves. It is a direct application of Ngô’s support theorem
[
46
]
; see
[
41
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

(3)

R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}\mathrm{IC}_{B}\left(\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}\right)[-i-\mathrm{dim}B]\in D^{b}_{c}(B).

Here
\mathrm{IC}_{B}({\mathcal{L}})
stands for the intermediate extension of a local system
{\mathcal{L}}
defined over a Zariski open subset of
B
. Since conventionally the intermediate extension of a local system is a perverse sheaf, the extra shift of
-\mathrm{dim}B
is to make the indices still lie in
[0,2g]
.

The two sides of (
3
) are of different flovors: the left-hand side collects cohomology of all the fibers
\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}
; the right-hand side only depends on the nonsingular curves
p^{\circ}:{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
. In other words, it shows that the cohomology
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
is completely determined by the collection of
H^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{b},{\mathbb{Q}})
for the nonsingular curves.

1.2.
Decomposition Theorem

Let
f:X\to B
be a proper morphism between nonsingular varieties with equidimensional fibers. By the decomposition theorem
[
8
]
, we have

(4)

Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X})[-i]\in D^{b}_{c}(B).

Here
{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(-)
stands for the perverse cohomology — taking the cohomology with respect to the perverse
t
-structure:

{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(-):D^{b}_{c}(B)\to\mathrm{Perv}(B).

A deep result of
[
8
]
is that each perverse cohomology on the right-hand side is not only a pervese sheaf, but also semisimple. Recall that a simple perverse sheaf is of the form
\mathrm{IC}_{Z}({\mathcal{L}})
where

(a)

Z
is an irreducible closed subset of
B
,

(b)

{\mathcal{L}}
is an irreducible local system defined on an open subset
U
in the smooth locus of
Z
, and

(c)

\mathrm{IC}_{Z}(-)
stands for the composition of the intermediate extension from
U
to
Z
, and the extension by
0
from
Z
to
B
.

The closed subset
Z
is called the
support
of the simple perverse sheaf
\mathrm{IC}_{Z}({\mathcal{L}})
. By definition, a semisimple perverse sheaf is a direct sum of simple perverse sheaves. An important invariant of the decomposition theorem for
f:X\to B
is the collection of the supports given by all the simple summands that arise in the right-hand side of (
4
).

Now we revisit Theorem
1.1
from the perspective of supports. As a general result following from the decomposition theorem, the right-hand side of (
3
) must appear in the decomposition of the left-hand side. In fact,
R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
is semisimple, and the intermediate extensions of the local systems
\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}
have to appear by (
2
). In other words, the terms in the right-hand side are exactly all the summands with full support
B
, and Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to saying that there is no summand with smaller support.

In general, it is very difficult to calculate all the supports for a proper morphism. For a large class of abelian fibrations (a proper map whose general fiber is an abelian variety), Ngô developed a framework in his proof of the fundamental lemma of the Langlands program
[
46
]
which obtains a strong control of the supports. In the following, we discuss the ideas of Ngô and explain the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.3.
Ngô’s support theorem

The idea of Ngô was built on the Goresky–MacPherson inequality (see
[
46
, Section 7]
) which we briefly reviewed. Let
f:X\to B
be a proper morphism as in Section
1.2
where
d
is the relative dimension.

Proposition 1.2

(Goresky–MacPherson inequality)
.

Let
Z\subset B
be a support of the decomposition of
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}
, then the following hold.

(a)

We have

\mathrm{codim}_{B}(Z)\leq d.

(b)

If a support
Z
reaches equality in (a), then
Z
can be detected by the constructible sheaf
R^{2d}f_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}
; more precisely, there is an open
U\subset B
with
U\cap Z\neq\emptyset
such that a direct sum component of
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}|_{U}
is supported on
U\cap Z
.

The spirit of the proposition is that a support cannot be “too small”. The proof relies on a clever use of the Verdier dualizing functor, combined with the fact that
Rf_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}[\mathrm{dim}X]
is self-dual, and is concentrated in the degrees in
[-\mathrm{dim}X,-\mathrm{dim}X+2d]
.

For example, the proposition implies that the decomposition theorem associated with a family of curves can have support at worst at codimension 1 in the base. However, the larger the fiber dimension
d
is, the less powerful the proposition is.

Ngô observed that if the fibration
f:X\to B
admits a fiberwise symmetry given by a commutative group scheme, then the inequality of Proposition
1.2
can be greatly imporved. Ngô’s framework is for
\delta
-regular
weak abelian fibrations
; for convenience, we focus on the compactified Jacobian fibration
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
of Section
1.1
.

Recall that the relative Jacobian

J_{\mathcal{C}}:=\{(C_{b},L)~\Big|~L\in\mathrm{Pic}^{0}({\mathcal{C}}_{b}),~b\in B\}

is a group scheme over
B
; it is a Zariski open subset of the relative compactified Jacobian

J_{\mathcal{C}}\subset\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}.

Moreover, this group scheme acts on
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
via tensor product:

(5)

a:J_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}},\quad\left((C_{b},L),(C_{b},F)\right)\mapsto(C_{b},F\otimes L).

Now for any point
b\in B
, we let
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{b}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
be the normalization, where the genus of the nonsingular curve
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{b}
is the geometric genus of the possibly singular
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
. The commutative group
J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}
can be expressed as an extension

1\to R_{b}\to J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}\to J_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}}\to 1,

known as the Chevalley decomposition. The map
J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}\to J_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{b}}
is given by the pullback of degree 0 line bundles along the normalization map
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{b}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
, and the first factor
R_{b}
is an affine group scheme. The dimension of
R_{b}
, which is simply the difference of the arithmetic and the geometric genera of
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
, define a function on the base

\delta:B\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\quad b\mapsto\mathrm{dim}R_{b}.

Let
Z\subset B
be an irreducible closed subset. We define the
\delta
-value of
Z
as
\delta(b)
with
b
a very general point in
Z
; equivalently, by semicontinuity we have

\delta_{Z}:=\mathrm{min}\{\mathrm{dim}R_{b}~\Big|~b\in Z\}.

Using the fiberwise symmetry (
5
) and a purity argument, Ngô observed that the Goresky–MacPherson inequality can be further “squeezed”. In the case of the compactified Jacobian fibration
\pi:\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\to B
, Ngô’s support theorem provides an enhancement of Proposition
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3

(Ngô support theorem
[
46
]
)
.

Let
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
be the compactified Jacobian fibration as above, and let
Z\subset B
be a support of the decomposition of
R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
.

(a)

We have

\mathrm{codim}_{B}(Z)\leq\delta_{Z}.

(b)

If a support
Z
reaches equality in (a), then
Z
can be detected by the constructible sheaf
R^{2g}\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
.

Here the part (b) is in the same sense as Proposition
1.2
(b).

We conclude this section by explaining that Theorem
1.1
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof sketch of Theorem
1.1
.

Since the total space
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
is nonsingular, we have the classical Severi inequality, which says any irreducible subset
Z\subset B
satisfies

(6)

\mathrm{codim}_{B}(Z)\geq\delta_{Z}.

Intuitively, the smoothness of
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
guarantees that the
\delta
-value of
Z
cannot be “too large” comparing to its codimension — the reason is that the base
B
, viewed as a slice in the moduli stack of genus
g
curves, has to meet transversally to every
\delta
-stratum; see
[
15
, Theorem 7.3]
or
[
36
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Now we assume that
Z\subset B
is a support. Since (
6
) holds for any
Z
, we obtain from Theorem
1.3
(a) that the support
Z
has to be of codimension
\delta_{Z}
. Then by the part (b), it can be detected by the sheaf
R^{2g}\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
. Since every fiber
\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}
is irreducible, we know that
H^{2g}(\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}},{\mathbb{Q}})
is 1-dimensional; in particular
R^{2g}\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
is a rank 1 local system on the base
B
.
2
2

2

In fact, this is a trivial local system; but it is not needed in the proof of the theorem.
This concludes that
Z=B
.
∎

2.
Perverse filtrations

2.1.
Definition

Let
C
be a locally planar integral curve of arithmetic genus
g
. The perverse filtration on
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
can be thought of as the “limit” of the decomposition

(7)

H^{*}({J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}\wedge^{i}H^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{b},{\mathbb{Q}})

for nonsingular curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
, which we explain in the following.

As before, we put
C={\mathcal{C}}_{0}
in a family
{\mathcal{C}}\to B
as in Section
1
. Then the decomposition (
7
) for nonsingular curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}
specializes
naïvely
to the cohomological decomposition

(8)

H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}H^{i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Obviously, this is a boring specialization. Alternatively, the local system formed by

H^{i}(J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\wedge^{i}H^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{b},{\mathbb{Q}}),\quad b\in B^{\circ}

can be extended over the singular fibers via intermediate extension as in the right-hand side of Theorem
1.1
; if we take the stalk for both sides over
0\in B
, we obtain in an interesting way of decomposing
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
into
2g+1
pieces:

(9)

H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}Q_{i},

where each vector space
Q_{i}
is the stalk of the summand
\mathrm{IC}_{B}\left(\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}\right)[-i-\mathrm{dim}B]
on the right-hand side of (
3
). This decomposition turned out to be highly different from the cohomological decomposition (
8
), which refines the cohomology group with a second myterious grading other than the cohomological one.

Definition 2.1
.

The perverse filtration

(10)

P_{0}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{1}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset P_{2}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset\cdots\subset P_{2g}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\subset H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})

is an increasing filtration given by

P_{k}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}):=\bigoplus_{i\leq k}Q_{i}\subset H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Although (
3
) gives a decomposition, we note that it is not canonical, therefore it does not induce a canonical decomposition of
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
. Instead, the filtration defined above is always canonical, as the sheaf-theoretic filtration

\bigoplus_{i=0}^{k}\mathrm{IC}_{B}\left(\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}}\right)[-i-\mathrm{dim}B]\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}

is induced by the perverse truncation functor

{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq k+\dim B}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}.

Remark 2.2
.

More discussions on possible interesting splittings of the perverse filtration will be in Section
2.4
.

Now we have defined a filtration on
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
dependent on a choice of embedding
C
into a family
{\mathcal{C}}\to B
. In the next section, we show that the perverse filtration in fact is independent of that choice — in particular, it defines an intrinsic structure on the cohomology
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
.

We conclude this section by explaining that why the decompositions (
7
) and (
9
) are different in general. Since
\overline{J}_{C}
is singular, the cohomology
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
may fail the numerical Poincaré duality,
i.e.
, there exists
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
such that

\mathrm{dim}H^{i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\neq\mathrm{dim}H^{2g-i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

On the other hand, by the relative Hard Lefschetz, we always have

\mathrm{dim}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{i}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})=\mathrm{dim}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{2g-i}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

For example, let
C
be a rational curve with a singular point given by
x^{3}-y^{4}=0
. By the discussion of
[
41
, Section 1.5]
, we have

\sum_{i=0}^{6}\mathrm{dim}H^{i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})q^{i}=1+q^{2}+2q^{4}+q^{6}

and

\sum_{i=0}^{6}\mathrm{dim}\mathrm{Gr}^{P}_{i}H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})q^{i}=1+q^{2}+q^{3}+q^{4}+q^{6}.

2.2.
Independence of the family

We outline a proof that the perverse filtration defined in Section
2.1
is intrinsic,
i.e.
, it only depends on the locally planar integral curve
C
. We follow
[
41
, Section 3.8]

We fix the curve

defined via

Since the perverse filtration only depends on an analytic neighborhood of the point

\iota:B\to V
realizing
{\mathcal{C}}\to B
as the pullback of
{\mathcal{C}}_{V}\to V

\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B,\quad\pi_{V}:\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{V}}\to V

be the corresponding compactified Jacobian fibrations.

We pick isomorphisms

R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}[-i]\in D^{b}_{c}(B)

and

R\pi_{V*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{V}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{i}[-i]\in D^{b}_{c}(V)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{i},{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{i}
are semisimple perverse sheaves on

\iota^{*}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}=R\pi_{V*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{V}}}\in D^{b}_{c}(V)

which yields isomorphisms

(11)

\bigoplus_{i}\iota^{*}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}[-i]\simeq\iota^{*}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}=R\pi_{V*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{V}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{i}[-i]\in D^{b}_{c}(V).

Here the first and the third isomorphisms are non-canonical. For our purpose, we need to show that the second canonical isomorphism yields and isomorphism of the perverse filtrations on

Since each term on the right-hand side of (

\iota^{*}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
over the locus

of nonsingular curves, we conclude that they are isomorphic, which completes the proof. ∎

2.3.
A local P=W conjecture

A fascinating yet still mysterious feature of the perverse filtration for
\overline{J}_{C}
is its conjectural connection to invariants associated with links. This serves as our motivation for exploring structures of the perverse filtration. This conjecture was proposed by Shende
[
53
]
aiming at unifying two stories as we briefly discuss below.

2.3.1.
Story 1: the P=W conjecture for the Hitchin fibration

Let
\Sigma
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
g\geq 2
. Given two coprime integers
r,d
(which will stand for the rank and the degree), there are two moduli spaces associated with such data: the moduli space
M_{\mathrm{Higgs}}
of stable Higgs bundles of rank
r
and degree
d
, and the corresponding character variety
M_{\mathrm{char}}
parameterizing
d
-twisted rank
r
complex representations of the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
. These two moduli spaces are of different flavors. The variety
M_{\mathrm{Higgs}}
underlies an algebraically complete integrable system, known as the Hitchin fibration

h:M_{\mathrm{Higgs}}\to A\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dim}M_{\mathrm{Higgs}}};

the character variety
M_{\mathrm{char}}
is an affine variety only dependent on the topology of
\Sigma
. The non-abelian Hodge theory gives a diffeomorphism of these two moduli spaces. The
P=W
conjecture of de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini
[
13
]
, proven recently
[
37
,
22
,
38
]
, says that the non-abelian Hodge theory exchanges the perverse filtration associated with the Hitchin fibration and the weight filtration associated with the mixed Hodge structure on
M_{\mathrm{char}}
:

(15)

``P=W":\quad P_{k}H^{*}(M_{\mathrm{Higgs}},{\mathbb{Q}})=W_{2k}H^{*}(M_{\mathrm{char}},{\mathbb{Q}}).

In particular, certain algebro-geometric invariants (
e.g.
the invariants encoded in the perverse filtration) of the Hitchin fibration can be completely understood by the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
of the topological surface
\Sigma
.

2.3.2.
Story 2: link invariants associated with planar singularities

For a planar singular point
p\in C
, we can embed the neighborhood of the point

into
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
. If we use a small 3-dimensional sphere

to cut it, we obtain a link

A natural question is how the

. This question was first proposed by Pandharipende using the Hilbert schemes (whose connection to the compactified Jacobian will be discussed in Section

. Oblomkov–Shende’s conjecture relate the invariants associated with the Hilbert schemes

to the HOMFLY polynomial of the links

, which connects algebro-geometric invariants associated with a planar singularity with the
Khovanov-Rozansky homology

[
26
]
of the link. We assume that

is a rational curve with a unique planar singular point
p\in C
.

Alternatively, we can work with the local compactified Jacobian associated with a planar singular point; we work with this global version only for conenience.
With this assumption, a version of the Oblomkov–Rasmussen–Shende conjecture is the following.

2.3.3.

As in Section

2.4.
Predictions and known results

First, the weight filtration for any algebraic variety is compactible with the cup-product; Shende’s proposal predicts that the same holds for the perverse filtration (
10
).

Prediction A.
The perverse filtration (
10
) is compatible with the cup-product,
i.e.

\cup:P_{i}H^{d}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\times P_{i^{\prime}}H^{d^{\prime}}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\to P_{i+i^{\prime}}H^{d+d^{\prime}}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Second, recall that by a result of Shende
[
55

the weight filtration

for the character variety

is of Hodge–Tate type; more precisely, the (decreasing) Hodge filtration

splits the (increasing) weight filtration:

H^{*}(M_{\mathrm{char}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{k,d}\left(W_{2k}\cap F^{k}\right)H^{d}(M_{\mathrm{char}},{\mathbb{Q}}).

This provides the weight filtration
W_{\bullet}H^{*}(M_{\mathrm{char}},{\mathbb{Q}})
a multiplicative splitting. If one believes that the local character variety

associated with a link is also of Hodge–Tate type, then the following should hold.

Prediction B.
The perverse filtration (
10
) admits a multiplicative splitting.

are not multiplicative. This is due to the fact that the perverse

3.
Hilbert schemes

3.1.
Compactified Jacobians vs Hilbert schemes

The purpose of this section is to explain the connection between compactified Jacobians and Hilbert schemes of points. For a nonsingular curve, this is the classical Macdonald formula; for locally planar integral curves, this connection holds in a subtle way where the perverse filtration comes into play naturally, as proven in the work of Maulik–Yun
[
41
]
and Migliorini–Shende
[

Let

Theorem 3.1

(
[

,
44
]

There is an isomorphism of graded

Expanding (

H^{d}(C^{[k]},{\mathbb{Q}})\simeq\mathrm{Gr}_{k}^{P}H^{d}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})\oplus\mathrm{[lower~terms]}.

Here, the lower terms contain those that have either a smaller perverse degree or a smaller cohomological degree.

3.3.
A natural splitting

Theorem

For convenience, we work with

[\mu_{-}([\mathrm{pt}]),\mu_{+}([C])]=[\mu_{-}([C]),\mu_{+}([\mathrm{pt}])]=\mathrm{id}.

Define

(a)

There is a natural isomorphism

W\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}[\mu_{+}([\mathrm{pt}]),\mu_{+}([C])]\xrightarrow{~\simeq~}V(C).

(b)

The generalized Abel–Jacobi map (
20
) induces an isomorphism

\mathrm{AJ}_{*}:W\xrightarrow{~\simeq~}H_{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Since
V(C)
is naturally bigraded, the primitive space
W\subset V(C)
is also bigraded

W=\bigoplus_{k,i}W_{k,i},\quad W_{k,i}:=W\cap H_{i}(C^{[k]},{\mathbb{Q}}).

Theorem
3.3
constructs a geometric bigraded decomposition

(21)

H_{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})=\bigoplus_{k,i}D_{k}H_{i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}),\quad D_{k}H_{i}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}}):=\mathrm{AJ}_{*}\left(W_{k,i}\right).

This decomposition turned out to describe the perverse filtration.

Proposition 3.4

(Rennemo
[
52
]
)
.

Dualizing the decomposition (
21
) yields a splitting of the perverse filtration (
10
).

Rennemo asked in
[
52
, Question 1.4]
whether the (dual of the)
D
-decomposition of
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
is compactible with the cup-product; this construction provides a natural candidate for the desired splitting of Conjecture
2.7
.

(1)

The correspondence between the Hilbert schemes and the perverse filtration for the compactified Jacobian is closely related to the correspondence between Pandharipande–Thomas invariants and the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants for Calabi–Yau 3-folds

Both Theorems

and

were generalized to locally planar reduced curves. Theorem

4.
Derived equivalences

4.1.
Perverse filtrations revisited

A challenge in studying the perverse filtration (
10
) is the abstract nature of the perverse truncation functor (
14
), which in general is difficult to compute. A geometric description of the perverse filtration (
10
) was introduced in Section
3.3
using the Hilbert schemes. The purpose of this section is to introduce another description of (
10
) using the Arinkin–Fourier–Mukai transform of the derived categories of
coherent sheaves
. As an application, we explain in Section
4.3
a proof of Theorem
2.6
— the perverse filtration associated with
H^{*}(\overline{J}_{C},{\mathbb{Q}})
is multiplicative.

The strategy is to generalize the ideas of Beauville
[
6
,
7
]
and Deninger–Murre
[
17
]
which characterized the Leray filtration of an abelian scheme using the Fourier–Mukai transform.

In the following, we first briefly recall the work of Beauville and Deninger–Murre in the special case of a Jacobian fibration. Let
p:{\mathcal{C}}\to B
be a smooth family of genus
g
curves, and let
\pi:J_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
be the Jacobian fibration; it is an abelian scheme. We consider the decomposition theorem

(22)

R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{C}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathcal{C}}[-i]

where every term on the right-hand side is a (shifted) local system. The observation of
[
6
,
7
,
17
]
is that the decomposition (
22
) is of a
motivic
nature, where the algebraic cycles are produced by the Fourier–Mukai transform. To explain this, we recall the normalized Poincaré line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}
on the relative product
J_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}J_{\mathcal{C}}
which induces the derived equivalences

\mathrm{FM}_{{\mathcal{P}}}:D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({J}_{\mathcal{C}})\xrightarrow{\simeq}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({J}_{\mathcal{C}}),\quad\mathrm{FM}_{{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}}:D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({J}_{\mathcal{C}})\xrightarrow{\simeq}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({J}_{\mathcal{C}})

where we use
{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
to denote the kernel of the inverse of the Fourier–Mukai transform
\mathrm{FM}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

The normalized Poincaré line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}
induces a cycle class

\mathfrak{F}=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{F}_{i}\in\mathrm{CH}^{*}(J_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}J_{\mathcal{C}})_{\mathbb{Q}},\quad\mathfrak{F}_{i}:=\mathrm{ch}_{i}(\mathfrak{F})\in\mathrm{CH}^{i}(J_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}J_{\mathcal{C}})_{\mathbb{Q}},

which gives a correspondence

\mathfrak{F}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}.

The inverse
{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
induces the inverse of the correspondence
\mathfrak{F}
:

\mathfrak{F}^{-1}=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}

which is the inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
. The operators
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
are regarded as the Fourier transforms.

We consider the correspondence given by

\mathfrak{q}_{i}:=\mathfrak{F}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{2g-i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}

where
\circ
is the composition of correspondences. Based on the ideas of Beauville
[
6
,
7
]
, Deninger–Murre
[
17
]
proves the following result.

Theorem 4.1

(Deninger–Murre
[
17
]
)
.

There is a canonical decomposition (
22
) such that the image of the correspondence

\mathfrak{q}_{i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}

recovers the
i
-th summand

\wedge^{i}R^{1}p_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathcal{C}}[-i]\subset R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{\mathcal{C}}}.

Consequently, the correspondence given by

\mathfrak{l}_{k}:=\sum_{i\leq k}\mathfrak{q}_{i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{\mathcal{C}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{\mathcal{C}}}

recovers the classical truncation functor

\tau_{\leq k}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{\mathcal{C}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{J_{\mathcal{C}}}.

Now we consider the compactified Jacobian fibraiton
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
associated with a family of locally planar integral curves
p:{\mathcal{C}}\to B
with
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
nonsingular. As we will introduce in Section
4.2
, the normalized Poincaré line bundle for nonsingular curves admits an extension to a Cohen–Macaulay sheaf by Arinkin
[
3
]
, which induces Fourier transforms
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
. Analogously to the smooth case, we consider the correspondence

\mathfrak{q}_{i}:=\mathfrak{F}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{2g-i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}.

Theorem 4.2

(Maulik–Shen–Yin
[
38
]
)
.

The operator

\mathfrak{p}_{k}:=\sum_{i\leq k}\mathfrak{q}_{i}:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}}

realizes the perverse truncation functor

{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq k+\dim B}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}.

Theorem
4.2
suggests that the complexity of the perverse truncation functor over the singular locus
B\setminus B^{\circ}
is matched with the complexity of the Arinkin sheaf over
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}
. We discuss more details about the Arinkin sheaf in Section
4.2
; then in Section
4.3
we sketch a proof for a sheaf-theoretic multiplicativity of the perverse truncation functor which implies Theorem
2.6
.

4.2.
Arinkin–Fourier–Mukai transforms

As we saw in Section
4.1
, a key geometric input in the characterization of the perverse truncation functor in Theorem
4.2
is Arinkin’s Fourier–Mukai kernel

{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathrm{Coh}\left(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\right).

Let

{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}\in\mathrm{Pic}\left(J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}\times_{B^{\circ}}J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\circ}}\right)

be the normalized Poincaré line bundle over the smooth locus. It is not hard to show that this bundle can be extended to a line bundle
{\mathcal{P}}^{\heartsuit}
on the larger open subset

(23)

J_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\cup\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\hookrightarrow\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}

In fact, over a pair of degree 0 line bundles

(L_{1},L_{2})\in J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}\times J_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}}

on a nonsingular curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
, the fiber of the normalized Poincaré line bundle is given by

{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}\Big|_{(L_{1},L_{2})}=\mathrm{det}\mathrm{R}\Gamma({\mathcal{C}}_{b},L_{1}\otimes L_{2})\otimes\mathrm{det}\mathrm{R}\Gamma({\mathcal{C}}_{b},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{b}})\otimes\mathrm{det}\mathrm{R}\Gamma({\mathcal{C}}_{b},L_{1})^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{det}\mathrm{R}\Gamma({\mathcal{C}}_{b},L_{2})^{-1}.

This formula makes sense for singular curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{b}
as long as one of
L_{1},L_{2}
is still a line bundle; however, in the boundary where both factors are not line bundles (but only rank 1 torsion free sheaves), their (derived) tensor product may not be bounded. Therefore the first term in the right-hand side is not well-defined.

Nevertheless, if we use
j
to denote the open embedding (
23
), Arinkin showed in
[
3
]
that
{\mathcal{P}}:=j_{*}{\mathcal{P}}^{\heartsuit}
is a Cohen–Macaulay coherent sheaf on
\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}
; he further proved that
{\mathcal{P}}
induces a derived auto-equivalence

\mathrm{FM}_{{\mathcal{P}}}:D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})\xrightarrow{~~\simeq~~}D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}),

whose inverse is induced by the kernel

{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}:={\mathcal{P}}^{*}\otimes\pi_{2}^{*}\omega_{\pi}[g],\quad{\mathcal{P}}^{*}:={{\mathcal{H}}}om({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}).

Here
\omega_{\pi}
is the relative canonical bundle with respect to
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
, and
\pi_{2}:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
is the natural morphism.

Unlike the smooth case, the Fourier–Mukai kernels
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
are
not
even perfect complexes. Therefore, defining the cycles of the Fourier transforms

\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\in\mathrm{CH}_{*}\left(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)_{\mathbb{Q}}

on the singular total space relies on the Baum–Fulton–MacPherson map in the Riemann-Roch formula for singular varieties. Although it is very difficult to explicitly calculate
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}
due to the singularities, one can prove the following quadratic “Fourier vanishing” relations

\mathfrak{F}^{-1}_{i}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{j}=0,\quad i+j<2g

in the Chow group. Eventually Theorem
4.2
was deduced from these relations. We note that a crucial geometric input in proving these relations is again the Severi inequality (
6
) — a key player in the proof of the support theorem in Section
1.3
!

4.3.
Multiplicativity via the Fourier transform

We conclude by explaining the proof of Theorem
2.6
. In fact, it follows from a stronger sheaf-theoretic result.

Theorem 4.3

(Maulik–Shen–Yin
[
38
]
)
.

Let
p:{\mathcal{C}}\to B
and
\pi:\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B
be as in Section
4.1
. Then the cup-product

\cup:R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\otimes R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\to R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}

is compatible with
the perverse truncation functor in the sense that

\cup:{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq k+\dim B}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\otimes{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq k^{\prime}+\dim B}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\to{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq k+k^{\prime}+\dim B}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}.

It is clear that Theorem
4.3
implies Theorem
2.6
: if
C
is a fiber
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
over
0\in B
, we obtain Theorem
2.6
by taking the stalk over
0\in B
.

The idea of the proof of Theorem
4.3
is to use the interaction between the Fourier transform
\mathfrak{F}
and the perverse truncation functor
{{}^{\mathfrak{p}}}\tau_{\leq\bullet}R\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}
. Such a connection is a consequence of Theorem
4.2
, as the the perverse truncation functor admits an explicit formula in terms of the Fourier transforms. This connection leads to the observation that, in order to prove the multiplicativity, it suffces to control the size of the support of the convolution kernel

{\mathcal{K}}\in D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}\left(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\right);

i.e.

{\mathcal{K}}
is the Fourier–Mukai kernel for the convolution product

\ast^{\mathcal{K}}:D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})\otimes D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})\to D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})

which exchanges with the tensor product

\otimes:D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})\otimes D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})\to D^{b}(\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}})

under the Fourier–Mukai transforms
\mathrm{FM}_{\mathcal{P}}
,
\mathrm{FM}_{{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}}
.

Finally, Theorem
4.3
follows from the fact that

\mathrm{codim}_{\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}\times_{B}\overline{J}_{\mathcal{C}}}\left(\mathrm{supp}({\mathcal{K}})\right)=g,

whose proof relies (again!) on the Severi inequality (
6
).
