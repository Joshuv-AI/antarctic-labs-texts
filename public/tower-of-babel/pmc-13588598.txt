Exploring the Challenges and Barriers to Telenursing in the Riyadh Region, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Areej Safar Dukhi Alotaibi, Amira Yahia Boshra, Faizan Kashoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aims to examine and analyse the barriers encountered in the implementation of telenursing within hospitals in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.DesignA quantitative cross‐sectional design was adopted.MethodsRecruited 356 nurses via convenience sampling who completed an online questionnaire assessing the barriers to telenursing practice. A validated questionnaire was used to collect data related to demographic variables, perceptions and barriers towards telenursing.ResultsThe majority of nurses were male (n = 209, 58.7%). In terms of age distribution, 37.4% (n = 133) were aged 26–30 years, and 62.6% held a bachelor's degree. Nurses expressed agreement, ranging from 83.1% (n = 296) to 87.1% (n = 310), that telenursing would improve healthcare services, reduce costs and enhance professional development. Furthermore, the majority of participants identified a lack of financial support (strongly agreed, n = 260, 73.0%) as the primary barrier to telenursing, whereas the least reported barrier pertained to language and cultural issues. The association between demographic variables, namely age, education and gender, and the identified barriers was not statistically significant following Bonferroni adjustment.ConclusionThis study identified key barriers to telenursing implementation in Riyadh, including limited reimbursement, lack of collaboration among healthcare providers and resistance to technology adoption. Although nurses recognize the benefits of telenursing, addressing these barriers through policy interventions, enhanced training and improved infrastructure may support the integration of telenursing services into the Saudi healthcare system, aligning with Vision 2030 objectives.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareFindings suggest that nursing leaders and policymakers should prioritize establishing clear reimbursement mechanisms for telenursing, fostering interprofessional communication frameworks and integrating telenursing competencies into nursing curricula to enhance workforce readiness for digital health delivery.ImpactThis study addresses the gap in nursing‐specific telenursing barriers in Saudi Arabia. The main findings indicate that systemic rather than demographic factors predominate, suggesting that broad organizational and policy‐level interventions are warranted. The research will impact nurse managers, educators and health policymakers involved in telehealth expansion under Saudi Vision 2030.Reporting MethodThis study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies.Patient or Public ContributionNo contribution from patient or public; this study focuses on nurses working in a health care setting.

Introduction

The delivery of healthcare is undergoing a significant transformation due to the emergence of telenursing and home telenursing. Telenursing facilitates nursing procedures and provides nursing services to remote area (Johnson et al. 2015). It is defined as patient‐to‐nurse communication via mobile devices, computers, applications, video technology and remote patient monitoring (Asiri and Househ 2016). According to the American Nurses Association (ANA), telenursing is the use of technology to deliver nursing care and conduct nursing practices, representing a specialized subset of telehealth where the primary focus is specifically on the profession of nursing (Chang et al. 2021).

Significant research gaps remain in the Middle East related to perception and barriers towards telenursing. A systematic review of telemedicine adoption in Middle Eastern countries identified six major barrier dimensions: cultural, legal and regulatory, financial, individual, organizational and technological (Alajlani and Clarke 2013). However, most existing studies in Saudi Arabia have focused primarily on physicians' perspectives (Alaboudi et al. 2016) or general healthcare practitioners' attitudes towards telemedicine (Ahmed et al. 2021). There are limited studies that report nurses' experiences and perceived barriers towards nurses in Saudi Arabia. This represents a critical gap, as nurses constitute the largest segment of the healthcare workforce and are essential frontline providers in telehealth service delivery. Nurses engaging in telenursing must perform continuous patient monitoring, health education and care coordination through virtual modalities (Koivunen and Saranto 2018). Consequently, the barriers nurses encounter such as assessing patients without physical examination, and navigating technology while maintaining patient safety are qualitatively distinct and warrant independent investigation.

The COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated telehealth adoption globally and the specific barriers Saudi nurses encounter when implementing telenursing services remains scarce. A recent systematic analysis reported that limited researches were conducted to examine the transformative nature of telehealth with a specific focus on nursing in KSA (Al Baalharith et al. 2022). The successful implementation of Saudi Vision 2030's digital health initiatives requires a comprehensive understanding of nursing‐specific barriers to inform targeted training programs, organizational policies and quality assurance frameworks. Additionally, existing studies have predominantly employed qualitative or mixed‐methods approaches with small sample sizes; there is a paucity of quantitative research examining the relationship between nurse demographic characteristics and specific barriers to telenursing implementation. The present study addresses these gaps by providing large‐scale quantitative evidence of nursing‐specific barriers and their demographic correlates. Therefore, we have three fold objectives of this study first to examine the perceptions of telenursing among nurses working in hospitals in Riyadh. Second is to explore the challenges and barriers to telenursing in Riyadh. Third is to determine the relationship between sociodemographic data and barriers to telenursing in Riyadh. We hypothesised that nurses in Riyadh hospitals would hold generally favourable perceptions of telenursing while still identifying notable barriers, particularly inadequate reimbursement, limited interprofessional communication and difficulties in remote patient assessment. We further hypothesised that perceived barriers would not differ substantially by age, sex or educational level after correction for multiple comparisons.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

A quantitative cross‐sectional design was adopted owing to its ability to allow concurrent comparisons of several parameters. Convenience sampling was used to recruit nurse managers and staff nurses among the total population of 1500 in the second health cluster. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Saudi nationality; (b) current employment as a staff nurse, head nurse, nurse director, nurse supervisor or nurse manager in a hospital within the Riyadh second health cluster; and (c) willingness to participate. Nurses were eligible regardless of whether they had prior telenursing experience or training; prior experience was treated as a study variable rather than an eligibility requirement. Nurses who were working or had previously worked in telenursing were not selectively recruited, but their experience status was recorded to permit subgroup description and exploratory comparison.

The online questionnaire link was distributed to approximately 520 potentially eligible nurses across healthcare facilities in the Riyadh second health cluster via WhatsApp and Twitter over an 8‐week period (December 2023 to February 2024). Of these, 412 nurses accessed the survey link. After screening for eligibility (Saudi nationality, current employment as a nurse or nurse manager in the target facilities), 380 met the inclusion criteria. Twenty‐four participants were excluded due to incomplete responses (< 50% completion), resulting in 356 complete questionnaires available for analysis (response rate: 68.5% of those invited; 93.7% of eligible participants). No missing data were present in the final analytic dataset because incomplete responses were excluded prior to analysis. Figure 1 presents the STROBE participant flow diagram.

A Sample Size

The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a population proportion in a finite population (Charan and Biswas 2013): Step1.n0=Z2×P×1−P/d2 Step2.n=n0/1+n0−1/N where n = required sample size; Z = critical value = 1.96 for a 95% confidence level; P = expected proportion = 0.50 (conservative estimate, as the prevalence of specific barrier perceptions was unknown); d = precision (margin of error) = 0.05; and N = finite population size = 1500.

Substituting the values: n 0 = (1.96)2 × 0.50 × 0.50/(0.05)2 = 384.16. Applying the finite population correction: n = 384.16/[1 + (383.16/1500)] = 384.16/1.255 = 306. Therefore, the minimum required sample was 306. To account for an anticipated 15% non‐response or incomplete submission rate, the target was increased to 352. During the 8‐week data collection period, 356 complete responses were obtained, exceeding both the minimum required sample and the adjusted target.

Data Collection

Data collection was carried out between December 2023 and February 2024 (8‐week period). During this period, 356 nurses were eligible to participate. The questionnaire used in this study was originally developed by Carius et al. (2016) for assessing telemedicine barriers and was adapted and translated into Arabic to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness for the Saudi context. Permission to use and adapt the instrument was obtained from the original author via email correspondence.

The adapted questionnaire consisted of three sections:

Section 1: Sociodemographic characteristics. This section assessed participants' sex, age group (20–25, 26–30, 31–45, > 45 years), educational level (Diploma, Bachelor, Master, PhD), training on telenursing (Yes/No) and telenursing experience (Yes/No). Training on telenursing was measured by a single yes/no item: ‘Have you received any training or education on telenursing?’ The questionnaire did not capture the duration, content, recency, provider (institutional vs. self‐directed) or format (formal coursework vs. informal briefing) of training. Therefore, the 98.9% training prevalence may encompass a wide spectrum of exposure intensity, from brief orientation sessions to comprehensive certification programs. The substantial gap between training prevalence (98.9%) and actual telenursing experience (27.8%) should be interpreted cautiously; it may reflect mandatory but superficial institutional orientations rather than a meaningful training‐practice disconnect.

Section 2: Perceptions of telenursing. This section included five items assessing nurses' perceptions of telenursing effectiveness, access improvement, cost reduction, chronic condition monitoring and professional development opportunities. Items were scored on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, 4 = strongly agree).

Section 3: Challenges and barriers to telenursing. This section included six items assessing barriers: lack of collaboration/communication, language/cultural barriers, difficulty assessing patients remotely, difficulty establishing rapport, resistance to technology adoption and limited reimbursement/financial support. Items were scored using the same 4‐point Likert scale.

The questionnaire was distributed online through social media applications (WhatsApp, Twitter) using a secure online link (Google Forms) to healthcare facilities in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, including King Fahad Medical City and Al Yamamah Hospital. Distribution followed a convenience sampling approach, with clear instructions provided to participants. Reminders were sent at 2‐week intervals to encourage participation. The estimated completion time was 15–20 min. All responses were kept anonymous and securely stored. Data were reviewed for completeness and accuracy before analysis. This process continued until the desired sample size was achieved.

Translation and Cross‐Cultural Adaptation

The questionnaire was originally developed in English by Carius et al. (2016) as part of a holistic competence model for telenursing practice. Permission to use and adapt the instrument was obtained from the original author via email. Following the back‐translation model (Brislin 1970), the instrument was forward‐translated from English to Arabic by a bilingual nursing expert, then back‐translated into English by a second bilingual expert blinded to the original version. The research team compared the original and back‐translated versions for semantic and conceptual equivalence, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third bilingual expert until consensus was reached.

Construct Validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted on the 11 Likert‐scale items of the Telenursing Perceptions and Barriers Questionnaire using the full study sample (N = 356). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.873, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ 2 = 2758.200, df = 55, p < 0.001), confirming the suitability of the correlation matrix for factor analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation was employed, yielding a clean two‐factor structure that explained 72.66% of the total variance. The rotated component matrix demonstrated excellent simple structure: Factor 1 (challenges and barriers) comprised all six barrier items—lack of collaboration (0.895), resistance to technology (0.895), difficulty establishing rapport (0.888), difficulty in remote assessment (0.885), language/cultural barriers (0.862) and limited reimbursement (0.709) with loadings ranging from 0.709 to 0.895. Factor 2 (positive perceptions of telenursing) comprised all five perception items—cost reduction (0.847), effectiveness (0.854), chronic condition management (0.836), professional development (0.829) and improved access (0.791) with loadings ranging from 0.791 to 0.854. All factor loadings exceeded the 0.50 retention threshold (Watkins 2018), and no significant cross‐loadings were observed, supporting the clear distinction between the two constructs. These findings provide robust construct validity evidence for the adapted instrument, confirming that perceptions of telenursing and perceived barriers represent two distinct yet complementary dimensions.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was confirmed with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.873 for the perception scale and 0.769 for the barrier scale, indicating good reliability.

Data Analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26 was utilized to analyse the collected data and test the research hypotheses. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the sociodemographic characteristics, and means and SDs to present the study fields of the participants. Cronbach's alpha values were calculated, reaching 0.873 for the perception scale and 0.769 for the barrier scale, demonstrating good internal consistency. Each questionnaire item was measured on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). Likert‐scale items constitute ordinal data, wherein the intervals between response categories cannot be assumed equal (Liddell and Kruschke 2018). Therefore, nonparametric statistical tests, which analyse ranks rather than raw scores, were selected as the appropriate analytical approach. The Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted and confirmed significant deviation from normal distribution for all variables (p < 0.001); however, the decision to employ nonparametric tests was based primarily on the ordinal measurement level and the research question rather than on normality testing alone. Therefore, non‐parametric statistical tests were employed for all subsequent analyses. Statistical tests: Appropriate nonparametric tests were selected based on the nature of the independent variables: the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to compare barrier scores across multi‐category demographic groups (age, education), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two‐group comparisons (gender). These tests evaluate whether the distribution of barrier scores differs across demographic groups, consistent with the study's comparative objectives. Multiple comparison correction: Given that 18 statistical comparisons were performed (6 barriers × 3 demographic variables), the Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error inflation. The corrected significance level was calculated as α = 0.05/18 = 0.0028. Both item‐level and scale‐level analyses were conducted. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations) were calculated for each individual item to permit granular interpretation of specific barriers and perceptions. For inferential analyses, the Kruskal–Wallis H and Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to each barrier item separately, as the research objectives focused on identifying which specific barriers varied across demographic groups. Additionally, composite subscale scores were computed based on the EFA‐derived two‐factor structure (organizational/systemic barriers and clinical/technical barriers), and Kruskal–Wallis tests were repeated on these subscales; the pattern of results was consistent with the item‐level analyses. The Cronbach's alpha values reported (0.873 for perceptions, 0.769 for barriers) refer to the full scales and support the reliability of the composite scores for descriptive and exploratory purposes. Effect size calculation: For Kruskal–Wallis H tests, eta‐squared (η 2) was calculated as H/(N − 1) to estimate effect size. For Mann–Whitney U tests, the rank‐biserial correlation (r) was calculated as Z/√N to estimate effect size.

Bonferroni Correction Results

Given that 18 statistical comparisons were performed (6 barriers × 3 demographic variables), the Bonferroni‐corrected significance level was α = 0.05/18 = 0.0028. After applying this correction, none of the associations remained statistically significant. This suggests that the initial significant findings at the conventional α = 0.05 level may be attributable to Type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons.

Supplementary multivariable linear regression was conducted using composite mean perception and barrier scores as outcomes. For perceptions, the model explained 12.9% of the variance, R 2 = 0.129, adjusted R 2 = 0.106, F(9, 346) = 5.69, p < 0.001. Training status was the strongest predictor; nurses without training had lower perception scores than those with training, B = −0.84, 95% CI [−1.16, −0.52], p < 0.001. However, this estimate is unreliable because only four participants lacked training. Education also showed a nominal association, with bachelor‐prepared nurses reporting lower perception scores than diploma‐prepared nurses, B = −0.12, p = 0.005. Age, sex and telenursing experience were not independently associated with perceptions.

For barriers, the model explained 6.6% of the variance, R 2 = 0.066, adjusted R 2 = 0.041, F(9, 346) = 2.71, p = 0.005. No age or sex differences were observed. Education showed nominal associations, with bachelor and master‐prepared nurses reporting lower barrier scores than diploma‐prepared nurses, B = −0.18, p = 0.012 and B = −0.22, p = 0.030, respectively. Telenursing experience approached but did not reach statistical significance, B = 0.14, p = 0.052. Training status again showed an unstable association, B = −0.62, p = 0.020.

Taken together, these supplementary results are consistent with the primary nonparametric analyses. Sociodemographic characteristics did not show robust independent associations with telenursing barriers. The only consistent predictor, lack of training, should not be overinterpreted because of the very small number of untrained nurses. No additional corrections were applied to these exploratory regression models because the Bonferroni‐corrected nonparametric analyses remained the primary inferential basis.

Discussion

This study examined nurses' perceptions of telenursing and identified key barriers to its implementation in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. As shown in Table 2, nurses showed positive perceptions of telenursing, with mean scores above 3.8 on a 4‐point scale for all perception items. The highest‐rated perceptions were that telenursing is an effective way to provide healthcare services (M = 3.86) and can monitor chronic conditions (M = 3.84). However, as presented in Table 3, significant barriers were identified, with limited reimbursement or financial support rated as the most substantial obstacle (M = 3.70), followed by lack of collaboration or communication with other healthcare providers (M = 3.59). Moreover, several associations between demographic characteristics and barriers were nominally significant at the conventional α = 0.05 level; none survived Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.0028). This finding suggests that barriers to telenursing are relatively uniform across different nurse demographics, implying that systemic and organizational interventions rather than demographically targeted approaches may be most effective for barrier reduction. The small effect sizes (η 2 = 0.022–0.032) further indicate limited practical significance of any demographic differences, even before multiplicity adjustment (Cohen 1992). These results align with the broader telehealth literature, where provider demographic characteristics often show weaker associations with technology adoption barriers than do organizational and infrastructural factors (Koivunen and Saranto 2018). The study employed bivariate nonparametric comparisons rather than multivariable models. Consequently, the analyses did not adjust for potential confounders such as years of nursing experience, facility type or the interaction between training and experience. Multivariable linear regression consistently provided similar results while accounting for clustering and covariate effects.

The present study makes several distinct contributions to the telenursing literature. First, while previous Saudi‐based research has predominantly examined telehealth from the perspectives of physicians or mixed healthcare practitioner groups (Alaboudi et al. 2016; Albarrak et al. 2021; Al‐Wathinani et al. 2024; AlMojaibel et al. 2025). This study specifically targets nurses the largest segment of the healthcare workforce and the frontline providers in telehealth delivery. This nursing‐specific focus is critical because nurses engaging in telenursing must perform functions that traditionally rely heavily on physical presence and direct observation, encountering barriers distinct from those faced by other healthcare professionals (Duka et al. 2026). Second, the quantitative design and comparatively large sample size (N = 356) provide empirical weight to barrier identification that prior qualitative or small‐scale mixed‐methods studies in the region could not establish. Third, the finding that barriers are relatively consistent across demographic groups after appropriate correction for multiple comparisons suggests that interventions need not be demographically tailored but rather systemically oriented.

Comparative analysis reveals both convergence and divergence with international findings. The identification of limited reimbursement as the highest‐rated barrier (M = 3.70) aligns with the Pan American Health Organization's classification of economic barriers as primary obstacles to telehealth implementation and with systematic reviews identifying billing and reimbursement issues as pervasive across nursing and allied health professions (Zhang et al. 2023). However, the prominence of this barrier in the Saudi context is notable given the Ministry of Health's provision of free healthcare; it suggests that reimbursement concerns may reflect inadequate institutional funding mechanisms for telenursing services specifically, rather than patient‐borne costs. The barrier of lack of collaboration or communication (M = 3.59) aligns with Grinberg and Sela (2023) findings that nurses perceive face‐to‐face nursing as higher quality regarding professional treatment. Resistance to technology adoption (M = 3.55) contrasts with Assaye et al. (2023), who reported 60.9% positive impressions of telemedicine among healthcare professionals. In our study, higher barrier scores may reflect the specific challenges of telenursing where technological resistance intersects with concerns about clinical assessment quality rather than general telemedicine attitudes. The relatively lower rating of language and cultural barriers (M = 3.49), whereas still considerable, diverges from Alajlani and Clarke (2013), who identified cultural barriers as major dimensions in Middle Eastern telemedicine adoption. This discrepancy may indicate that cultural barriers are more salient for physician‐patient interactions than for nurse‐led telenursing in the Saudi context.

The finding that nurses perceive telenursing as effective for healthcare delivery aligns with previous research. Glinkowski et al. (2013) demonstrated that telehealth technology enhances nurses' productivity by minimizing travel time to distant locations, thereby increasing the number of cases they can handle daily. Similarly, Albagmi et al. (2025) reported high satisfaction levels among both patients and healthcare practitioners with telemedicine services in Saudi Arabia. The positive perceptions regarding cost reduction identified in this study (M = 3.80) are supported by Gidora et al. (2019), who found that telenursing triage and advice services can significantly reduce healthcare costs by remotely providing required services. Furthermore, Asbath et al. (2025) reported that telenursing is a readily implementable, secure, cost‐effective and efficient intervention that enhances health outcomes.

The barriers identified in this study are consistent with the international literature. The finding that lack of collaboration and communication with other healthcare providers represents a significant barrier (M = 3.59) echoes the work of Grinberg and Sela (2023), who found that nurses perceive face‐to‐face nursing as having higher quality than telenursing. Cultural and language barriers (M = 3.49) identified in this study align with Chang et al. (2021), who reported that practical execution of telenursing services is affected by cultural differences between patients and healthcare staff. The resistance to technology adoption (M = 3.55) found in this study contrasts somewhat with Koivunen and Saranto (2018), who identified the greatest number of facilitators and barriers related to nurses' job operations and challenges of adapting to technological methods. However, Assaye et al. (2023) reported that 60.9% of healthcare professionals held positive impressions of telemedicine, suggesting that resistance may be overcome with appropriate training and support.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. The use of a validated questionnaire (Carius et al. 2016) adapted for the Saudi context ensures reliability and comparability of findings. The sample size of 356 nurses exceeded the minimum required sample of 306, providing adequate statistical power for the primary analyses. The application of appropriate non‐parametric statistical tests, following confirmation of non‐normal data distribution via Shapiro–Wilk testing, ensures the validity of inferential statistics. Furthermore, the inclusion of effect size calculations and Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons demonstrates methodological rigour.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships between demographic characteristics and barriers. The use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, potentially overrepresenting nurses with greater interest in technology. The study was conducted in a single region (Riyadh), limiting generalizability to other regions of Saudi Arabia. Additionally, the Bonferroni correction, whereas controlling Type I error, is conservative and may increase Type II error rates. Finally, the self‐reported nature of the data may introduce social desirability bias.

The assumption that all observations are independent may be questionable because participants were nested within healthcare facilities. Nurses working in the same hospital likely experience shared organizational cultures, technology platforms and policy environments. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and design effect (Deff = 1 + (m − 1) × ICC) could not be calculated because facility identifiers were not collected (Killip et al. 2004). Future studies should record facility membership and employ multilevel models or adjust standard errors for clustering to obtain valid inferential statistics.

The measurement of telenursing training was limited to a binary yes/no item without capturing duration, depth, recency or format. Consequently, the high training prevalence (98.9%) may mask considerable heterogeneity in training quality and intensity. The apparent training–practice gap should not be interpreted as evidence of training failure without more granular measurement.

Participants were recruited from multiple healthcare facilities within the Riyadh second health cluster, including King Fahad Medical City and Al Yamamah Hospital. Nurses within the same facility may share common organizational policies, technology infrastructures, training programs and reimbursement arrangements, potentially violating the assumption of independence between observations. Facility identifiers were not recorded in a manner permitting multilevel analysis; therefore, the potential clustering effect could not be statistically accounted for in the present analysis.

Convenience sampling was employed due to the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame of nurses with email addresses and the exploratory nature of the study. This approach introduces risks of selection bias, self‐selection bias and coverage bias, as nurses with greater interest in technology or telenursing may have been more likely to participate (Stratton 2021). Recruitment through social media platforms (WhatsApp, Twitter) and online forms may further underrepresent nurses who are less digitally engaged. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the perspectives of the recruited sample rather than as representative of all nurses in Riyadh or Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

The implementation of telenursing in Riyadh is crucial. Many nurses are aware of telenursing applications and their benefits, including the potential reduction of healthcare costs and time consumption and ease of healthcare services. Several barriers are reported, such as insufficient coordination or communication among healthcare providers, challenges in remotely diagnosing and monitoring patients and resistance to change or unwillingness to embrace new technology. Addressing these barriers may facilitate the successful integration of telenursing into the Saudi healthcare system, though longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to confirm effective strategies.

Future Research

Future research could examine the difficulties and obstacles that telenursing faces in various parts of Saudi Arabia or other Gulf nations. Finding regionally specific problems and best practices that could guide national strategies would be made easier with this. Performing further studies among various healthcare workers in other medical service areas in the country, such as physicians and pharmacists, to assess the degree of telenursing success in the healthcare systems across the country. Comparative regional studies with larger sample sizes (target N = 400+) would provide more robust evidence. Additionally, future studies should explore organizational and systemic factors beyond demographic characteristics that may influence telenursing barriers, such as institutional support, technological infrastructure and training quality. Longitudinal designs would also be valuable for understanding how barriers evolve over time as telenursing becomes more integrated into healthcare delivery.
