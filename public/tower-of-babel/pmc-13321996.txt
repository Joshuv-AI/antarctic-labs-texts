Pharmacological inhibition of G protein–coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2) by paroxetine attenuates acetaminophen-induced hepatotoxicity
Authors: Daram Yang, Hee-Won Moon, Jong-Won Kim, Bumseok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcetaminophen (APAP) overdose is a leading cause of acute liver injury and is associated with high mortality. G protein–coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2) is a widely expressed serine/threonine kinase involved in the regulation of multiple cellular signaling pathways. Dysregulation of GRK2 expression has been linked to numerous pathological states, highlighting its potential contribution to disease mechanisms. However, its involvement in drug-induced acute liver injury is yet to be elucidated. This study explored the hepatoprotective effects of paroxetine, a GRK2 inhibitor, in the context of APAP-induced liver injury. Male C57BL/6 mice received an intraperitoneal injection of APAP (300 mg/kg), followed by oral administration of paroxetine (10 mg/kg) 30 min afterward. Paroxetine markedly decreased serum alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase concentrations and reduced APAP-induced hepatocellular apoptosis and inflammation. Paroxetine also diminished hepatic neutrophil accumulation and decreased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Furthermore, GRK2 inhibition alleviated oxidative stress, as evidenced by lower hepatic malondialdehyde concentrations and a partially restored glutathione (GSH)/GSH disulfide ratio. Importantly, inhibition of GRK2 resulted in a decrease in hepatic phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK). Collectively, these results indicate that pharmacological inhibition of GRK2 by paroxetine confers protection against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, potentially mediated by modulation of ERK signaling.Keywords: Acetaminophen, Extracellular signal-regulated, MAP kinases, G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2, Hepatotoxicity, Paroxetine

INTRODUCTION

Acetaminophen (APAP) is a widely used over-the-counter analgesic and antipyretic medication [1]. Although considered safe at recommended therapeutic doses, excessive intake of APAP (> 10–15 g in adults or > 150 mg/kg in children) results in severe hepatotoxicity due to the accumulation of a toxic metabolite [2,3]. APAP-induced hepatotoxicity accounts for a substantial proportion of drug-related liver injury and continues to pose a significant challenge in clinical toxicology. Although N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is the mainstream treatment for APAP overdose, its therapeutic efficacy depends heavily on timely administration [4]. If there is a delay in medical intervention, liver transplantation often becomes the only viable strategy for improving prognosis in patients presenting with APAP-induced liver failure [4,5]. These clinical limitations underscore an urgent need to identify alternative treatments that can prevent or reverse hepatocellular damage, especially at more advanced disease stages. In light of these challenges, there is growing interest in discovering novel molecular targets implicated in the pathogenesis of APAP-induced liver injury.

To elucidate the underlying pathological mechanisms of APAP-induced hepatotoxicity, it is essential to examine its hepatic metabolism. Under physiological conditions, over 90% of APAP undergoes phase II conjugation in the liver to yield glucuronide and sulfate derivatives, which are eliminated in the urine. An insignificant amount (~2%) of APAP is excreted unchanged, while 5%–10% is metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, predominantly CYP2E1, producing N-acetyl-p-benzoquinoneimine (NAPQI), a highly reactive electrophilic compound [4]. At therapeutic levels, NAPQI is effectively neutralized by conjugation with glutathione (GSH), resulting in a water-soluble metabolite excreted in bile. However, during APAP overdose, phase II detoxification capacity is overwhelmed, causing excessive NAPQI accumulation, rapid depletion of GSH, and covalent binding of NAPQI to cytosolic and particularly mitochondrial protein sulfhydryl groups [6,7]. Mitochondrial dysfunction becomes a central event in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity by promoting increased oxidative stress, ATP depletion, and subsequent necrosis of hepatocytes. This cellular injury triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which recruit immune cells such as neutrophils and amplify the inflammatory cascade [8,9]. Furthermore, overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) aggravates oxidative injury and promotes hepatocyte apoptosis, thereby worsening liver damage and significantly impairing immune regulation [10].

G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRK) represent a family of serine/threonine kinases that selectively phosphorylate agonist-activated G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) [11-13]. GPCRs play a critical regulatory role in numerous physiological processes and constitute the most widely targeted protein family by drugs approved in the United States and the European Union [14,15]. Within the GRK family, GRK2 is broadly distributed and has been implicated in diverse cellular signaling pathways related to metabolism, inflammation, and cardiovascular regulation [13]. Research has shown that GRK2 expression levels are dysregulated in several pathological conditions, such as heart failure, obesity, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation [16-19]. Notably, Cruces-Sande et al. [20] reported increased GRK2 expression in liver biopsies obtained from patients with steatosis and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). These findings indicate a likely role for GRK2 in hepatic metabolic disturbances and inflammatory processes. Nevertheless, its definitive function in APAP-induced liver injury has yet to be established.

Paroxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) commonly administered as an antidepressant, has emerged as a potent inhibitor of GRK2 [21,22]. It binds directly to GRK2, resulting in its functional inhibition and subsequent alterations in GPCR signaling pathways [23]. Considering the involvement of GRK2 in various liver diseases, targeting GRK2 with paroxetine might represent a promising therapeutic strategy to reduce APAP-induced hepatotoxicity.

This study aimed to investigate the impact of GRK2 inhibition on APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. Specifically, we examined the role of GRK2 in mediating APAP-induced liver injury and whether its pharmacological inhibition by paroxetine protects against hepatocyte damage. Clarifying GRK2 signaling in this context may support the development of new therapeutic strategies to prevent liver failure caused by APAP overdose.

METHODS

Animal experimentation

Five-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were acquired from Taconic, Inc. (Samtako Bio Korea). The animals were kept in a controlled environment at 24°C ± 3°C and 50% ± 5% humidity, with unrestricted access to a sterilized standard diet and water ad libitum. All animal studies were approved and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Ethics Committees of Jeonbuk National University (CBNU 2018-115).

Mice were allocated into four experimental groups: (ⅰ) mice injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and given dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) via oral gavage (PBS-DMSO, n = 6); (ⅱ) mice injected i.p. with PBS and administered paroxetine (Ark Pharm), a GRK2 inhibitor, by oral gavage (PBS-paroxetine, n = 6); (ⅲ) mice injected i.p. with APAP (Sigma-Aldrich) and treated with DMSO by oral gavage (APAP-DMSO, n = 6); and (ⅳ) mice injected i.p. with APAP and given paroxetine via oral gavage (APAP-paroxetine, n = 6). All mice received a single i.p. dose of 300 mg/kg APAP or PBS after undergoing a 16-h fasting period. A single oral dose of 10 mg/kg paroxetine or DMSO was administered to the mice 30 min following APAP injection, with the dose selected based on previously reported in vivo studies [24,25] and adjusted to be appropriate for the acute single-dose design of our APAP injury model. Mice were euthanized 24 h after paroxetine administration.

Primary cell isolation

Mouse primary hepatocytes and Kupffer cells were isolated from the livers of C57BL/6 mice 24 h after i.p. injection with either PBS or APAP (300 mg/kg), following established protocols [26]. For hepatocyte isolation, mice underwent liver perfusion and enzymatic digestion using collagenase IV (LS004189; Worthington Biochemical Corporation). The liver cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 rpm for 2 min. The collected cell pellet was subjected to an additional filtration using a 40 μm strainer and centrifugation. The final pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, 12-604F, Lonza) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, MP Biomedicals) and seeded on a cell culture plate coated with collagen type I (354236; Corning).

To isolate Kupffer cells, digested liver cells were passed through a strainer and subjected to centrifugation at 500 rpm for 2 min. The supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 1,800 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 20% Opti-Prep (D1556; Sigma-Aldrich) prepared in Hank’s balanced salt solution. An 11.5% Opti-Prep layer was gently placed on top of the suspension and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min without brake. Kupffer cells were isolated using magnetic cell sorting with the F4/80 antibody and resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (12-702F; Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS prior to cell culture.

Cell culture and GRK2 knockdown using siRNA

Alpha mouse liver 12 (AML12) cells were maintained in DMEM/Ham's F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Lonza, 12-719F) containing 10% FBS, 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium-Pyruvate Supplement (Welgene), 40 ng/ml dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), and an antibiotic-antimycotic solution. Huh-7 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

For GRK2 silencing, AML12 and Huh-7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. Cells were transfected with Silencer Select Adrbk1 (GRK2) siRNA (4390771; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Opti-MEM (Gibco), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Silencer Select Negative Control #1 siRNA (4390843; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the non-targeting control.

After transfection, cells were maintained in Opti-MEM for 6 h, then the medium was replaced with complete DMEM containing 10% FBS and antibiotics for an additional 24 h. Cells were then treated with 10 mM APAP prepared in serum-free medium. Analyses were performed 24 h after APAP exposure.

Cell viability assay

AML12 and Huh-7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight to allow stabilization. For paroxetine cytotoxicity assays, paroxetine at the designated concentrations was added to each well and incubated for 24 h. For experiments involving GRK2 knockdown, cells were transfected with siRNA according to the procedure described above. Subsequently, 10 μl of Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (CCK-8, Dojindo) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an Emax spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measurements

Blood samples were collected and centrifuged twice at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to obtain serum. AML12 and Huh-7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates, maintained in DMEM/F-12, and treated with 10 mM APAP under conditions of either paroxetine treatment or siGRK2 transfection. After incubation for 24 h, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min to eliminate cell debris. Serum ALT and AST concentrations in serum and cell supernatants were determined using the AM101-K assay kit (ASAN Pharmaceutical). All steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ALT and AST activities were quantified by measuring absorbance at 490 nm on an EMax spectrophotometer.

Histopathologic examination

For histopathological evaluation by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, liver tissues were fixed in a neutral buffered formalin solution, routinely processed, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded liver tissues were cut into 4 μm thick sections using a microtome (HM-340E, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mounted on glass slides. H&E staining was performed according to standard procedures.

Apoptotic hepatocytes were identified by a terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay on paraffin-embedded sections. The TUNEL assay was performed using the ApopTaq Peroxidase In Situ Apoptosis Detection Kit (S7100, Sigma-Aldrich) by following the manufacturer's protocol. Positive staining was developed with 3,3'-diaminobenzidine substrate, and cell nuclei were counterstained with methyl green dye. TUNEL-positive hepatocytes were quantified as the percentage of positively stained area per high-power field (HPF). For each animal, 10 HPFs per liver tissue were evaluated. Data were expressed as the proportion of TUNEL-positive areas. Images of entire liver sections were captured using a light microscope (BX53F, Olympus) and analyzed with digital imaging software (analySIS TS, Olympus).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) assay

IHC staining was conducted on liver tissue sections. For IHC staining, tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration. Sections were incubated in 3% H₂O₂ in methanol for 20 min to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed using antigen retrieval buffer (ab93684, Abcam) at 100°C for 30 min, followed by cooling at room temperature (RT) for 20 min. Sections were subsequently incubated with a commercial blocking solution (Super Block; AAA125, ScyTek Laboratories, Inc.) to reduce non-specific background staining. Afterwards, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-mouse myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibody (ab9535, Abcam) at a 1:25 dilution or anti-4 hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) antibody (ab46545, Abcam) at a 1:200 dilution in antibody diluent (E09-300, GBI Labs). Sections were then incubated with a horse anti-mouse/rabbit IgG (ImmPRESS Universal Polymer Kit Peroxidase; MP-7500, Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at RT. Immunoreactivity was detected using 3-amino-9-ethylcarbozole substrate (SK-4200, Vector Laboratories). Counterstaining was performed with 50% hematoxylin, after which the sections were mounted. Subsequently, stained sections were evaluated with a light microscope (BX53F, Olympus) and digitally processed using imaging software (analySIS TS, Olympus). The positive area was quantified as the mean percentage of the area with positive staining per field in liver sections.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

MDA concentrations were measured from liver tissue using the OxiSelect TBARS Assay Kit (STA-330, Cell Biolabs Inc.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction. Liver samples were homogenized in 1X butylated hydroxytoluene solution. The supernatant was subsequently combined with a sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) lysis solution and mixed thoroughly. Thiobarbituric acid reagent was then introduced, and the samples were incubated at 95°C for 45 min. Afterwards, samples were placed on ice for 5 min and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was extracted and combined with n-butanol (Daejung Chemicals), followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was added to 96-well plates. Absorbance at 562 nm was recorded using an EMax spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

GSH assay

The GSH to GSSG ratio was determined in liver tissue using the GSSG/GSH Quantification Kit (Dojindo) as per to the manufacturer’s instruction. Liver tissues were homogenized in 5% 5-sulfosalicylic acid solution. The collected supernatant was diluted to 0.5% prior to analysis. GSSG samples were prepared with the addition of a masking solution. Absorbance at 405 nm was determined with an EMax spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissue using the Tri-RNA Reagent (Favorgen) and Hybrid-R RNA purification Kit (GeneAll) as specified by the manufacturer’s instruction. RNA concentrations were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Synthesis of single-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) was carried out using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover, following the supplier's protocol. qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) using the synthesized cDNA. All primers used for qRT-PCR were obtained from Bioneer. For normalization, the relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated using Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (RPLP0) as the reference gene. Detailed primer sequences employed in this study are provided in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Liver tissue samples were lysed in T-PER reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min to separate supernatants. Protein content in the supernatants was quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Proteins were subjected to electrophoresis on 8%–10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were incubated in blotting-grade Blocker (Bio-Rad) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 2 h at RT to block nonspecific binding. Following blocking, they were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2 (9102S, Cell Signaling), phospho-ERK 1/2 (p-ERK 1/2) (9101S, Cell Signaling), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) (9252S, Cell Signaling), phospho-JNK (p-JNK) (4668S, Cell Signaling), GRK2 (sc-13143, Santa Cruz), and β-actin (sc47778, Santa Cruz), prepared in EveryBlot blocking buffer (Bio-Rad). After thorough washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated for 1 h at RT with either goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (1:4,000; ADI-SAB-300-J, Enzo) or goat anti-mouse IgG polyclonal antibody (1:4,000; ADI-SAB-100-J, Enzo). Detection of protein bands was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Clarity Western ECL Substrate; Bio-Rad), and signals were captured with an ImageQuant LAS 500 system (GE Healthcare).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The protein levels of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 in serum were quantified using ELISA Kits (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Briefly, coated plates were rinsed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS/T), and each well was subsequently blocked with a diluent buffer for 1 h at RT. Serum samples were then added to the wells and allowed to incubate for 2 h. Following incubation, each well was washed with PBS/T before the addition of a detection antibody, which was incubated for 1 h. After additional washes, horseradish peroxidase was applied to each well and incubated in the dark for 30 min. 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added to the plate, which was incubated for a maximum of 15 min. Absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently determined using an EMax spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

Statistical analysis

RESULTS

GRK2 inhibition reduces hepatic cytotoxicity caused by APAP treatment in vitro

To assess the inhibitory effect of paroxetine, mice received oral administration of either DMSO or paroxetine (10 mg/kg) 30 min after APAP injection. Hepatic GRK2 expression was evaluated by Western blotting 24 h after paroxetine treatment. APAP injection led to an increase in hepatic GRK2 expression, but this elevation was significantly reduced in the group treated with paroxetine (Fig. 1A). Additionally, to clarify which liver cell types demonstrate significant alterations in Grk2 expression after APAP exposure, Grk2 mRNA levels were quantified in hepatocytes and Kupffer cells isolated from mice injected with either PBS or APAP (300 mg/kg). In the PBS group, Grk2 mRNA levels were notably higher in Kupffer cells than in hepatocytes; however, in APAP-injected mice, this pattern was reversed (Fig. 1B). The elevated Grk2 expression in hepatocytes following APAP administration aligned with our in vivo data. We next evaluated the cytotoxic effects of paroxetine and direct GRK2 silencing (siGRK2) on the hepatocyte cell lines AML12 and Huh-7 (Fig. 1C). To determine whether the protective actions of paroxetine are consistent with those produced by direct GRK2 suppression, siGRK2-mediated knockdown was also examined. Concentrations of paroxetine at 5 μM for AML12 and 10 μM for Huh-7 were determined to be non-cytotoxic and did not produce significant changes in cell viability. The maximally non-cytotoxic doses of siGRK2 were determined to be 30 nM for AML12 cells and 40 nM for Huh-7 cells. Under APAP (10 mM)-induced hepatotoxic conditions, paroxetine exposure led to a dose-dependent and statistically significant reduction in ALT levels in AML12 cells, whereas Huh-7 cells showed a similar decreasing trend that did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1D). siGRK2 transfection also resulted in significant reductions in ALT levels at both intermediate and high siRNA doses in AML12 and Huh-7 cells. These findings indicate that the hepatoprotective effects of paroxetine parallel those achieved by direct GRK2 silencing, suggesting that its protective actions are closely linked to GRK2 inhibition. Taken together, these results demonstrate that paroxetine alleviates APAP-induced hepatotoxicity in vitro by inhibiting GRK2 expression and lowering liver injury enzymes.

GRK2 inhibition ameliorates APAP-induced histopathological alterations in the liver

Serological and histopathological analyses were performed to determine whether GRK2 inhibition influences APAP-induced hepatotoxicity in vivo and in vitro. Significant increases in serum ALT and AST levels were observed in the APAP-DMSO group compared to the PBS controls (Fig. 2A). Paroxetine administration significantly counteracted these elevations. As depicted in Fig. 2B, paroxetine administration markedly attenuated APAP-induced hepatocellular injury and necrosis. TUNEL staining showed a reduction in hepatocellular apoptosis in the APAP-paroxetine group compared to the APAP-DMSO group, as indicated by a smaller positive area (Fig. 2C). IHC analysis of MPO, a neutrophil marker, revealed that APAP treatment promoted neutrophil infiltration in the liver, which was reflected by an increased positive area (Fig. 2D). Notably, this MPO-positive area was significantly diminished after paroxetine administration. Collectively, GRK2 inhibition by paroxetine reduces both hepatocellular apoptosis and neutrophil infiltration in the liver of APAP-injected mice.

GRK2 inhibition reduces hepatic inflammatory responses triggered by APAP treatment

To determine whether GRK2 inhibition influences hepatic inflammatory responses during APAP-induced liver injury, we initially measured the mRNA expression levels of major pro-inflammatory cytokines. The hepatic mRNA levels of Tnf-α, Il-1β, and Il-6 were markedly elevated in the APAP-DMSO group compared to the PBS groups (Fig. 3A). Administration of paroxetine significantly downregulated these pro-inflammatory cytokines that were increased by APAP injection. Additionally, ELISA analysis demonstrated that hepatic protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly lower in the APAP-paroxetine group compared with the APAP-DMSO group (Fig. 3B). The hepatic mRNA levels of C-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 2 (Ccl2), C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand (Cxcl)1, and Cxcl2, which act as potent chemoattractants for monocytes and neutrophils, were significantly decreased in the APAP-paroxetine group compared to the APAP-DMSO group (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these results indicate that GRK2 inhibition suppresses hepatic inflammation by reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in APAP-injected mice.

GRK2 inhibition regulates oxidative stress during APAP metabolism

To assess the impact of GRK2 inhibition on ROS generated throughout APAP metabolism, we examined hepatic mRNA levels of key inducible factors and enzymes involved in ROS regulation (Fig. 4A). Expression of hepatic mRNA for nitric oxide synthase 2 (Nos2) and glutamate–cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (Gclc) was significantly decreased in the APAP-paroxetine group in comparison to the APAP-DMSO group. Hepatic mRNA levels of heme oxygenase-1 (Ho-1) were slightly higher in the APAP-paroxetine group than in the APAP-DMSO group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Hepatic mRNA levels of glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (Gclm) and NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (Nqo1) did not differ significantly between the two groups. To complement these transcriptional findings, we next evaluated tissue-level oxidative stress. IHC for 4-HNE, a representative marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, showed that APAP markedly increased 4-HNE–positive staining (arrow), whereas paroxetine treatment significantly reduced this oxidative response (Fig. 4B). Consistent with these findings, hepatic MDA levels, which represent a marker of lipid peroxidation, were notably increased in the APAP-DMSO group relative to the PBS groups (Fig. 4C). Paroxetine treatment significantly attenuated APAP-induced elevations in MDA levels. Moreover, the hepatic GSH/GSSG ratio, an indicator of GSH oxidation during APAP metabolism, was reduced in the APAP-DMSO group in comparison with the PBS groups (Fig. 4D). The GSH/GSSG ratio was moderately higher in the APAP-paroxetine group compared to the APAP-DMSO group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance. Together, these data indicate that GRK2 inhibition mitigates oxidative stress arising from APAP metabolism by modulating ROS-related gene expression and maintaining redox balance.

GRK2 inhibition modulates ERK 1/2 phosphorylation in APAP-induced liver injury

APAP-induced liver injury is primarily facilitated through the phosphorylation of JNK and/or ERK components within the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [27,28]. To clarify the signaling pathway influenced by GRK2, we utilized Western blot analysis. The phosphorylation levels of both JNK and ERK 1/2 were markedly elevated in the livers of the APAP-DMSO group compared to the PBS-DMSO group (Fig. 5). Notably, paroxetine administration significantly decreased hepatic p-ERK 1/2 protein levels, whereas p-JNK remained unaffected. These findings indicate that the ERK pathway may be involved in the protective effects of GRK2 inhibition on APAP-induced liver injury.

DISCUSSION

In recent years, extensive research has investigated the involvement of GRK2 in multiple pathological states, such as inflammation, insulin resistance, hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis, heart failure, cancer, and NASH [16,29-32]. Nonetheless, knowledge concerning the function of GRK2 in hepatotoxicity is still limited. Our results reveal that inhibiting GRK2 offers protective benefits against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity in a mouse model. We detected altered GRK2 protein levels in APAP-treated mice, implying a potential role for GRK2 in the progression of APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. GRK2 is recognized for its high expression within the immune system, where it acts as a key modulator of immune cell activation during inflammation [12]. As anticipated, Grk2 mRNA expression was substantially higher in Kupffer cells than in hepatocytes; however, this trend was reversed after APAP exposure (Fig. 1A, B). Because GRK2 expression increased both in whole liver tissue in vivo and in APAP-treated hepatocytes in vitro, we focused primarily on hepatocyte-derived GRK2 signaling as the main mechanistic axis in this study. In parallel, GRK2 expression decreased in APAP-treated Kupffer cells, suggesting a cell type–specific regulatory response. Multiple studies have shown that Kupffer cells are actively involved in the metabolism of APAP by orchestrating inflammatory responses and exacerbating hepatocyte damage [33,34]. Although our experimental design does not allow us to delineate the relative contributions of hepatocyte and Kupffer cell GRK2, we acknowledge that the reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines observed in vivo may also reflect indirect effects on Kupffer cell activation. Paroxetine-mediated alleviation of hepatocyte injury or stress signaling could secondarily attenuate immune cell–driven inflammation. Future studies employing cell type–specific GRK2 regulation models will be necessary to define the direct role of GRK2 in Kupffer cell–mediated hepatic inflammation.

ERK activation is known to inhibit GRK2 activity [35], but the mechanisms by which GRK2 regulates ERK signaling are not yet well understood. Previous research has demonstrated that inhibition of GRK2 results in elevated ERK activation across diverse experimental conditions. For instance, splenocytes from GRK2 heterozygous mice exhibited increased ERK activation after stimulation with the chemokine CXCL12, which correlated with GRK2’s interaction with MAPK, a kinase upstream of ERK [36]. Similarly, myeloid-specific GRK2 knockout (KO) mice showed higher ERK activity following administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [37]. In colon epithelial cells exposed to GRK2-targeted silencing oligonucleotides (siGRK2), p-ERK levels rose after TNF-α treatment [38]. However, studies in GRK2 hemizygous mice fed a methionine-choline-deficient diet for four weeks reported no changes in ERK activity [20]. Contrasting with these previous findings, our results revealed that GRK2 inhibition via paroxetine led to decreased ERK activity, which had initially increased in response to APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. This divergent outcome indicates that the regulatory effect of GRK2 on ERK signaling may be influenced by the specific experimental context or the mode of GRK2 inhibition. A significant contributing factor may be the methodological differences: earlier studies primarily employed genetic models, including GRK2 heterozygous and myeloid-specific GRK2 KO mice, or siGRK2, whereas our study relied on pharmacological inhibition with paroxetine. Therefore, the use of paroxetine as a GRK2 inhibitor may account in part for the contrasting ERK activity observed among studies. Notably, paroxetine has also been reported to diminish T cell infiltration and joint inflammation in collagen-induced arthritis rats, likely by inhibiting GRK2 and decreasing ERK phosphorylation [39]. In addition, in BV2 microglial cells, pretreatment with paroxetine followed by LPS stimulation resulted in reduced ERK activity [40]. Interestingly, although both myeloid-specific GRK2 KO mice and paroxetine-treated BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS, the resulting ERK activity differed, underscoring that the method of GRK2 suppression—whether through genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition—may have a substantial impact on downstream signaling [37,40]. It is also important to consider that, as a SSRI, paroxetine may exert off-target effects aside from inhibiting GRK2. Consequently, further studies employing more specific GRK2 inhibitors are needed to elucidate the exact relationship between GRK2 and ERK signaling in the setting of APAP-induced hepatotoxicity.

In this study, we assessed the phosphorylation status of two key MAPK signaling components, ERK and JNK, to elucidate mechanisms underlying the protective effect of GRK2 inhibition against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. Although JNK phosphorylation remained unaltered after paroxetine administration, a significant decrease in p-ERK levels was detected, suggesting that ERK signaling may be a relevant pathway through which GRK2 inhibition exerts its protective effects. Prior research has established that neutrophil migration in inflamed intestinal tissue is ERK-dependent [41], and blockade of ERK activation by a melatonin antagonist reduces neutrophil recruitment at injury sites in zebrafish [42]. Aligning with these reports, our results demonstrated that reduced p-ERK levels were associated with diminished neutrophil infiltration, verified by MPO immunostaining, and attenuated hepatocyte apoptosis, as indicated by TUNEL staining. Moreover, paroxetine treatment led to downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, at both the mRNA and protein levels. We additionally examined NF-κB phosphorylation to determine whether GRK2 inhibition affects broader inflammatory signaling beyond the MAPK pathway. APAP increased NF-κB phosphorylation, and paroxetine showed a modest but non-significant reduction in this response (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that NF-κB is unlikely to be a predominant contributor in our model. Considering the established function of ERK signaling in regulating inflammatory gene expression and apoptosis, these findings raise the possibility that modulation of ERK activity represents one of the mechanisms contributing to the hepatoprotective effects of GRK2 inhibition during APAP-induced liver injury. Although our study did not evaluate direct downstream mediators of ERK, the observed association between diminished ERK phosphorylation and lower liver injury enzymes supports this interpretation. Further investigations are required to identify downstream ERK targets and to determine whether GRK2 directly modulates the ERK cascade under oxidative and inflammatory stress.

Our findings underscore GRK2 as a viable therapeutic target for alleviating APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. Pharmacological inhibition of GRK2 by paroxetine mitigated liver injury and inflammation in vivo and in vitro, supporting a regulatory function of GRK2 in stress-related hepatic injury. While the detailed molecular pathways have yet to be clarified, our results furnish foundational evidence that interference with GRK2–ERK signaling may represent a novel approach for managing drug-induced liver injury. Considering the scarcity of effective treatments for APAP overdose aside from NAC, GRK2-focused interventions may have translational potential to broaden available therapeutic options for acute toxic liver injury.
