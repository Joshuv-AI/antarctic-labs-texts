Natural hazards and population impact in Sri Lanka, 2010–2025
Authors: Dewarahandhi Kavishka Madushan De Silva, Sudath Shirly Pathmasiri Warnakulasuriya, Samath D. Dharmaratne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSri Lanka is highly vulnerable to climate-sensitive natural hazards, yet long-term national evidence on disaster patterns and human impacts remains fragmented. Robust synthesis of historical disaster data is essential to inform prevention, preparedness, and public health resilience. This study aimed to characterize temporal trends, dominant hazard types, and population-level impacts of natural hazards in Sri Lanka from 2010 to 2025.MethodsA retrospective secondary analysis was conducted using publicly available humanitarian reports published between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2025, retrieved from the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT), maintained by the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters. Reports describing natural hazard events in Sri Lanka and documenting hazard type, date, location, affected population, displacement, or mortality were included. Events were cross-checked to avoid duplication. Descriptive epidemiological analysis summarized disaster frequency, temporal patterns, and aggregate human impacts.ResultsBetween 2010 and 2025, natural hazards affected 14,477,357 people, displaced 1,497 277 individuals, and caused 2,218 deaths with 59 reported events. Floods, frequently occurring in combination with landslides, were the most frequent and impactful hazards, accounting for over 6 million affected individuals and 479 deaths. Cyclone-related disasters, although less frequent, were associated with the highest mortality (823 deaths). Droughts affected more than 6.5 million people but were rarely linked to displacement. Substantial inter-annual variability was observed, with major peaks in 2011, 2014, 2016, and 2025.ConclusionsNatural hazards impose a sustained and substantial public health burden in Sri Lanka, predominantly driven by hydrometeorological hazards. Strengthening national disaster surveillance systems, integrating health impact reporting into disaster databases, and prioritizing flood- and landslide-risk reduction are critical to enhance population resilience aimed at increasing climate variability.

Background

Natural hazards are sudden or slowly developing hazardous physical events caused by natural processes of the Earth’s system [1]. These events include hydrometeorological hazards (such as floods, cyclones, storm surges, and droughts), geological hazards (including earthquakes, landslides, and tsunamis), as well as climate-related hazards such as heatwaves and wildfires [1–3]. Natural hazards can produce immediate loss of life and long-term adverse health, social, and economic consequences through the disruption of health services, food and water systems, housing, and livelihoods [4]. Globally, these events impose substantial strain on health and economic systems by damaging infrastructure, interrupting essential services, and diverting limited resources toward emergency response and recovery [2].

Sri Lanka is a tropical island located in South Asia (Fig. 1) with diverse climatic zones and complex geographical features, rendering it highly vulnerable to a range of natural hazards [5]. The frequently reported natural hazards in Sri Lanka are seasonal and localized flooding events, often occurring in association with landslides. Tropical cyclones accompanied by storm surges, recurrent droughts, and periodic flooding are also common in the country [6–7]. These events result in deaths, injuries, population displacements, and extensive damage to housing, transport, infrastructure, and agriculture, with rural and low-income communities disproportionately affected. Monitoring and reporting are primarily conducted through the Disaster Management Centre (DMC) and the National Disaster Management Information System (NDMIS), which consistently identify flooding and landslides as the predominant risks in Sri Lanka.

Despite the frequency and impacts of natural hazards, Sri Lanka lacks comprehensive, consistent, and accessible long-term national hazard data [6]. Previous studies have documented limitations in disaster data collection, storage, and inter-agency data sharing, which constrain longitudinal analyses and evidence-based policymaking [7]. The lack of standardized historical records limits the ability to accurately assess national trends in disaster frequency and associated human impacts, including mortality, displacements, affected populations, and broader socio-economic consequences as well as both immediate and long-term public health impacts and broader socio-economic consequences.

Recent catastrophic events, including major cyclone Dithwa in 2025 and extreme monsoon-related flooding, continue to cause substantial and at times rapidly escalating risks for the island nation. The recurrence and intensity of such events underscore the need for a robust disaster surveillance system capable of systematically analyzing disaster occurrence and impacts to strengthen preventive strategies, preparedness planning, and health system resilience in the context of increasing climate vulnerability.

Accordingly, this study aimed to retrospectively synthesize 16 years of historical data on natural hazards in Sri Lanka. By consolidating and analyzing multi-year records where previously only fragmented records existed, the study sought to (i) describe temporal and spatial patterns of disaster occurrence, (ii) quantify reported human and population-level impacts, and (iii) identify gaps in national disaster surveillance and reporting that should be prioritized to strengthen disaster-related public-health planning and research.

Methods

Study designs

This study employed a retrospective descriptive design using secondary humanitarian data to characterize the occurrence and population impacts of natural hazards in Sri Lanka between 1st of January 2010, and 31st of December 2025, based on data availability at the time of extraction. The study aimed to synthesize routinely reported disaster information to describe disaster types, temporal trends, and associated human impacts at the national level.

Data source

Primary disaster data were obtained from the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT), maintained by the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED). EM-DAT is an internationally recognized disaster database that systematically records natural and technological disasters and includes standardized information on disaster occurrence, mortality, affected populations, and displacement.

To improve data completeness and verify reported events, EM-DAT records were cross-checked against reports available from ReliefWeb, operated by the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), and Sri Lanka’s National Disaster Management Information System (NDMIS), which is based on the DesInventar framework developed by the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR). ReliefWeb reports were additionally used to clarify event characteristics, contextual details, and discrepancies in event reporting where necessary.

A UN Secretariat entity mandated to coordinate international humanitarian responses during emergencies and disasters. ReliefWeb was selected for cross-checking due to its detailed narrative reports, which provide granular information on disaster characteristics, including affected populations, displacement, and situational context variables that are often incompletely captured in structured global disaster databases. Furthermore, ReliefWeb aggregates reports from multiple validated sources, including the Sri Lankan Disaster Management Centre, the World Bank, and the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, among other humanitarian agencies. This multi-source integration enhances the reliability of reported disaster impacts, although variations in reporting standards, thresholds, and completeness across contributing organizations are acknowledged.

Sri Lanka’s National Disaster Management Information System, developed based on the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction DesInventar framework, provides nationally reported disaster data; however, publicly accessible datasets remain limited in temporal coverage and completeness. The triangulation of international, humanitarian, and national disaster databases was used to improve consistency, reduce duplication, and minimize potential reporting bias across the study period.

Inclusion criteria

Records data were included if they: reported natural hazardous events occurring in Sri Lanka published between 2010 and 2025on EM-DAT; and contained one of these variables: disaster type, date and year of occurrence, affected population, injuries, deaths, or displacements. Natural hazards were defined in accordance with internationally recognized classifications [8], including hydrometeorological and geological hazards.

Exclusion criteria

Records were excluded if they focused exclusively on conflicts, economic crises, or epidemics without an associated natural hazard component.

Search strategy and data extraction

A structured search was conducted on EM-DAT using the country filter as Sri Lanka, and a time frame filter from 2010 to 2025. Searches were performed using disaster-related keywords (e.g., floods, droughts, landslides, cyclones, tsunamis, heatwaves). Data were further cross-checked with Sri Lanka’s National Disaster Management Information System and ReliefWeb by using the same keywords.

Data extraction was conducted by a single researcher using a standardized Microsoft Excel extraction framework. Extracted variables included year of the occurrence, type of the disaster, geographic location, number of people affected, mortality and injury counts (if reported), displacement figures, and source organization. When multiple reports referred to the same disaster event, data were cross-checked and consolidated to avoid duplication. The use of a single extractor was recognized as a potential limitation and was mitigated by systematic cross-referencing of reports.

Data management and quality assurance

Extracted data were compiled, cleaned, and reviewed before the analysis. Duplicate records were removed, and inconsistencies across reports were resolved through triangulation of sources. Missing data were handled on a variable-specific basis, with analysis conducted using available case reporting for each outcome. No imputation was performed.

Statical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS V.26. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentages, and annual counts, were used to summarize disaster types, temporal trends, disaster occurrence, and aggregate human impacts. Given the descriptive nature of the study and reliance on aggregated secondary data, inferential or causal analyses were not performed.

Ethical considerations

The current study utilized publicly available de-identified secondary data and did not involve human participants, personal identifiers, or confidential information. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were waived. However, the study complies with principles of data transparency, responsible data use, and acknowledgment of original data sources.

Results

As illustrated in Fig. 2, Sri Lanka experienced six major types of disasters with varying temporal patterns between 2010 and 2025, including floods, floods with landslides, severe storms, cyclones with floods and landslides, droughts, and landslides only. A total of 59 disaster events were reported in Sri Lanka during this period. Flooding was the most recurrent hazard, with isolated flood events in fourteen different years (2010, 2011, 2013, 2014, 2015, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2024, and 2025). Multi-hazard events involving floods and landslides were the most frequently observed combined disasters, reported across fourteen years, indicating a persistent co-occurrence of these events throughout the study period. Only tropical cyclones without any associated events were infrequently reported, with occurrence limited to 2012, 2013, 2020, 2021 and 2022. Cyclone-related events, typically accompanied by flooding and landslides, were reported majorly in 2024 and 2025, without a clear periodic pattern. Drought events were observed sporadically across four years (2012, 2014, 2017, and 2020). Landslides occurring independently were rare and were documented only once, in 2014.

Human impact of disasters

Human impact of disasters by disaster type

The human impact of disasters varied substantially by disaster type with respect to the size of the affected population, displacement, and mortality (Table 1). Floods occurring in isolation affected over 1.9 million people, resulting in the displacement of 283,428 people and 268 reported deaths. In contrast, flood events accompanied by landslides produced the greatest overall impact, affecting over 6.4 million people, displacing 894,179 individuals, and resulting in 479 deaths.

Compound disasters involving cyclones in combination with flooding and landslides were associated with the highest mortality burden, accounting for 823 deaths, while affecting approximately 1.7 million people and displacing 319,670 individuals. Drought events affected over 6.5 million people. Landslides occurring independently had a limited population impact, affecting 1,797 people with 330 reported displacements and resulting in 241 deaths.

Human impact of disasters by year of occurrence

As shown in Fig. 3, natural hazards affected a total of 14,477,357 people, displaced 1,497,277 individuals, and caused 2,218 deaths in Sri Lanka from 2010 to 2025. The highest number of affected people was reported in 2014 (3,005,826), followed by 2018 (3,036,712) and 2012 (2,316,021). Displacement was greatest in 2011, with 663,696 displaced people, followed by 2010 (282,291) and 2025 (233,015). The highest number of deaths was recorded in 2025 (854 deaths), followed by 2014 (336 deaths) and 2017 (329 deaths). The year 2015 reported the lowest overall impact, with 27,309 affected people, 664 displaced individuals, and 10 deaths.

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to provide a consolidated national overview of natural hazards in Sri Lanka over 16 years, highlighting clear temporal patterns, dominant hazard types, and substantial population-level impacts. Floods, particularly when combined with landslides, emerged as the most frequent and consequential disasters. These findings reinforce Sri Lanka’s classification as a hydrometeorological disaster-prone country and align with regional risk profiles in South and Southeast Asia [9].

Flood-related disasters accounted for the largest proportion of events and affected the largest population. The predominance of floods and flood-associated landslides is consistent with Sri Lanka’s monsoonal climate, high rainfall variability, and increasing land-use pressures in the vulnerable district [9]. In South Asia, floods affected approximately 750 million people between 2000 and 2021, representing nearly half of the global flood-affected population [10]. India, Bangladesh, and Nepal consistently report floods as the leading cause of disaster-related displacement, accounting for over 60% of annual disaster displacements in the region [10, 11]. However, the magnitude of people affected by flood–landslide combinations in Sri Lanka appears proportionally higher than that reported in several middle-income Asian countries. This suggested heightened exposure linked to settlement patterns, slope instability, and population density in hazard-prone areas in Sri Lanka when compared with other Asian countries.

Cyclone-related disasters, although less frequent, are associated with the highest mortality burden. This pattern mirrors evidence from coastal South Asia, where cyclones contribute fewer than 10% of disaster events but account for over 30% of disaster-related deaths [12, 13]. In the Bay of Bengal region alone, tropical cyclones caused more than 500,000 deaths between 1970 and 2019, despite advances in forecasting [13, 14]. The rising mortality observed in recent cyclone-associated events in Sri Lanka, particularly in 2024 and 2025, may reflect intensifying storm severity linked to climate change, as well as residual gaps in early warning dissemination and evacuation compliance.

Droughts affected a large population but resulted in comparatively lower immediate mortality and no reported displacement. Globally, droughts accounted for approximately 35% of disaster-affected populations between 2000 and 2020, while contributing to less than 5% of reported disaster-related deaths [10].

The observed pattern is consistent with evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, indicating that droughts predominantly produce indirect health consequences mediated through food insecurity, livelihood disruption, and long-term nutritional stress rather than acute fatalities [15]. The absence of reported displacement during drought events likely reflects under-documentation rather than true non-occurrence, as slow-onset disasters are frequently under-represented in humanitarian reporting systems due to gradual onset, diffuse geographic distribution, and weaker emergency response triggers.

The temporal analysis demonstrated substantial inter-annual variability, with the peak impacts observed in 2011, 2014, 2018, and 2025. These peak years coincide with documented extreme monsoon anomalies and major flood episodes across South Asia, where observational records indicate a 10–20% increase in rainfall intensity over recent decades [16]. The sharp increase in disaster-related mortality in 2025 represents an anomalous event relative to preceding annual averages and may reflect an unusually severe combination of hazard intensity, exposure, and population vulnerability. Comparable upward trajectories in disaster-related mortality have been reported across parts of Southeast Asia, where climate-driven hydrometeorological extremes have contributed to an estimated 25% increase in flood-related deaths since 2010 [17].

The minimal disaster impact recorded in 2015 and 2022 across all indicators may reflect genuine climatic variability; however, it is also consistent with pandemic-related disruptions to disaster surveillance and humanitarian reporting systems. During the COVID-19 period, institutional priorities shifted toward public health emergency response, potentially reducing detection, verification, and reporting of disaster events. Global estimates indicate that disaster reporting completeness declined by approximately 15–20% during 2020–2021 [17, 18]. These findings underscore the susceptibility of secondary disaster datasets to systemic shocks affecting monitoring infrastructure and data governance.

The cumulative burden identified in this study - nearly 14.5 million affected individuals, nearly 1.5 million displaced persons, and more than 2000 deaths- demonstrates the sustained public health significance of natural hazards in Sri Lanka. When compared with island nations of similar population scale, such as the Philippines and Madagascar, Sri Lanka demonstrates a comparable magnitude of affected population but a relatively higher displacement-to-death ratio. For example, the Philippines reported approximately 18 million disaster-affected individuals annually during the same period, with proportionally lower displacement-associated mortality [18]. This pattern suggests effective short-term survival outcomes alongside persistent vulnerability affecting housing security and livelihood stability.

The study identifies critical limitations in national disaster surveillance capacity. Several disaster categories lacked complete reporting of affected populations, displacement, and mortality. Comparable reporting limitations have been reported across low- and middle-income countries, where fewer than 60% of disaster events include complete health impact metrics [19]. These gaps constrain robust longitudinal analyses and hinder evidence-based disaster risk reduction planning.

Strengthening disaster surveillance requires harmonized national reporting frameworks, integration of health sector information systems, and routine across-agency data validation mechanisms. Evidence from regional counterparts such as Vietnam and Thailand demonstrates that centralized disaster information platforms can improve reporting completeness by more than 30% within five years of implementation [20]. Adopting interoperable, multi-sector surveillance architectures would substantially enhance national preparedness, risk assessment accuracy, and resilience.

Strengths and limitations

The principal strength of this study is its extended observation period and the use of an internationally recognized humanitarian data repository (EM-DAT), enabling consolidation of fragmented reports into a comprehensive longitudinal overview of disaster trends in Sri Lanka. However, the findings are constrained by reliance on secondary data sources, potential reporting bias, and inconsistent operational definitions across datasets. Mortality and displacement estimate likely underestimate true impacts, particularly for slow-onset disasters and geographically remote populations.

Public health implications

These findings emphasize the urgent need to prioritize flood and landslide risk reduction within national public health and disaster management strategies. Evidence from comparable settings suggests that investments in early warning systems, risk-informed land use planning, and community preparedness programs can reduce disaster mortality by up to 40%. Integrating disaster surveillance within routine health information systems would enable real-time assessment of health impacts, facilitate targeted resource allocation, and strengthen adaptive health system resilience.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations related to the use of secondary disaster data. First, this study relied on secondary disaster databases, which are subject to variations in reporting standards, inclusion thresholds, and event classification methodologies across sources. Although EM-DAT served as the primary dataset, and records were triangulated with ReliefWeb and Sri Lanka’s National Disaster Management Information System, discrepancies in event counts and impact estimates were observed between databases. These differences likely reflect variations in reporting practices, thresholds for disaster inclusion, and timing of data updates.

In addition, the limited availability of comparable national and global datasets constrained comprehensive cross-validation. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, particularly with regard to absolute event counts; however, they are less likely to substantially alter the overall patterns of major disaster impacts described.

Conclusions

Natural hazards represent a sustained public health challenge in Sri Lanka rather than isolated episodic events. This retrospective analysis demonstrates that climate-sensitive hazards, particularly hydrometeorological events, account for a substantial proportion of disaster occurrence and associated population impacts. The analysis underscores the critical role of robust disaster surveillance in supporting evidence-informed public health decision-making. Vulnerability in reporting incompleteness and consistency limits the accuracy of the assessment of temporal trends and population impacts, thereby constraining the effectiveness of disaster risk reduction and preparedness strategies. Strengthening national disaster data systems and improving their integration with health information platforms would enhance situational awareness and support more responsive decision-making.

From a policy and planning perspective, the study supports a transition from predominantly reactive disaster response models toward prevention, mitigation, and preparedness -oriented approaches. Greater alignment between disaster risk reduction frameworks and public health planning is necessary to mitigate avoidable health impacts and safeguard population well-being in the context of increasing climate variability and disaster frequency.
