Bilateral symmetrical extradural hematoma in an older person: A case report

Introduction

The occurrence of bilateral extradural hematomas (EDH) is a rare consequence of craniocerebral trauma and its incidence in various studies varies from 2 to 25 % [[1], [2]]. An acute symmetrical bilateral epidural hematoma is rarely reported [3]. We discuss the case of an 82-year-old patient whose surgical approach resulted in a good clinical and radiological evolution. All our work was reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria and guidelines [9].

Case report

The patient was an 82-year-old man, treated for benign prostatic hypertrophy, on an alpha-blocker. He suffered an accidental fall down five steps, resulting in an isolated head injury with initial loss of consciousness and vomiting. He was taken to the emergency department immediately after the fall for in-patient management.

On admission, we found a somnolent patient with a Glasgow score of 14/15, his pupils were reactive and symmetrical, he was mobilizing all four limbs, and he presented with bi-parietal ecchymoses, with no notion of a cerebral wound or orificial discharge.

His computed tomography (CT) scan taken at H2 of the trauma showed bilateral, symmetrical parietal EDH measuring 25 mm on the right and 23.5 mm on the left, with subarachnoid hemorrhage in a few right parietal sulci [Fig. 1]. The bone window showed fracture lines of both parietal bones [Fig. 2].

Cerebral scan in non-injected axial slices showing bilateral parietal ED.

Axial bone sections showing both right and left parietal fractures.

We proceeded to evacuate the left and right extra-dural hematoma in a single operation, turning the patient's head over. The incision was linear, about 6 cm in length, opposite the parietal hump, and the parietal fractures were at the origin of the bleeding. The bone flap was centered on the EDH, and after exposing and evacuating it, we ensured hemostasis, then suspended the dura and replaced the bone.

The choice of the left side as the first step was to decompress the dominant cerebral hemisphere.

The patient was extubated in the operating room, and regained consciousness with a GCS score of 15/15, with no sensory-motor deficits. The follow-up CT scan was satisfactory [Fig. 3].

Discussion

The pathophysiology of bilateral hematomas is unclear, as it has been suggested that the force of impact on head trauma can produce bilateral hematomas, particularly when the impact waves are predominantly in the anteroposterior rather than lateral direction [4]. The dura mater is detached from two locations by a single directed force. Detachment of the dura mater can occur at the site of impact by inward or outward flexion of the skull [2]. A further hypothesis is that this phenomenon can also occur due to extension of the fracture line on the midline, leading to bilateral EDH below the fracture line [4,5].

The particularity of our case is that in elderly subjects, cerebral atrophy delays the increase in intracranial pressure, and symptomatic hematomas are therefore large from the outset. Hemostasis tests may be disturbed, and thrombocytopenia may be present.

Bilateral extradural hematomas are associated with high mortality [6,7] this is due to the reduced lucid interval in these patients, followed by the GCS score which is generally decreased. These patients deteriorate more often than individuals with unilateral hematomas [3,4,7,8]. There are two types of bilateral EDH, in the first type which is the most common, the bleeding is venous in nature and occurs as a delayed phenomenon, whereas in the second type, the bleeding is arterial in nature [4], a lesion of the bilateral middle meningeal arteries could be the cause of bilateral epidural hematomas [3].

Surgical management requires rapid emergency cerebral decompression. Preferably, two bilateral craniotomies should be performed simultaneously by two groups of neurosurgeons to reduce operative time and improve prognosis [3].

However, it is not always possible to have two teams of neurosurgeons, so it is preferable to start on the side of the dominant hemisphere.

A surgical technique using a single bicortical incision has been reported in the literature. The head is placed in a neutral position, then after incision and decapsulation, a bilateral craniotomy is performed, followed by evacuation of clotted blood and suspension of the dura mater to avoid recurrence after exposure of both sides. The brain parenchyma is freed as soon as the flap is made, even before the blood clot is evacuated.

Post-operative clinical condition depends on the initial Glascow score, the quality of the surgical procedure and the rapidity of management. The patient requires strict neurological monitoring for the first 24 h post-operatively, and general clinical monitoring throughout the hospital stay.

Conclusion

Bilateral and symmetrical EDH are extremely rare. Even more so in subjects over the age of 80.

The management of these cases requires careful planning, adequate exposure, a judicious and rapid surgical approach and good time management to achieve good results.
