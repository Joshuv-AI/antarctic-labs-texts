The relationship between lipid accumulation products and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease among adults in the North of Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Catherine Behzad, Negin Atashi, Nasim Hafezi, Nima Motamed, Mansooreh Maadi, Elham Sobhrakhshankhah, Zainab Shateri, Farhad Zamani, Azam Doustmohammadian, Mehran Nouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsights into the relationship between adiposity and lipid metabolism are provided by the lipid accumulation product (LAP) index, which is particularly relevant in the context of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Therefore, the purpose of this research was to evaluate the relationship between the LAP index and NAFLD in the Iranian population.MethodsThe current cross-sectional study (n = 3,120) was conducted in the second stage of the Amol Cohort Study. NAFLD was confirmed to use ultrasound imaging. The values of the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) and LAP were obtained using the corresponding equations. Additionally, a multivariate logistic regression was performed to examine the relationship between LAP tertiles and both NAFLD and HSI.ResultsThe findings showed that there was a 1.8% increase in the odds of NAFLD and a 1.6% increase in the odds of HSI for each unit change in LAP score (P < 0.001 for both) in the adjusted model. Also, the analysis based on LAP tertiles found higher odds of NAFLD and HSI in the second tertile (NAFLD: odds ratio (OR) = 2.042, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.578–2.642, P < 0.001 - HSI: OR = 1.765, 95% CI: 1.283–2.428, P < 0.001) and last tertile (NAFLD: OR = 3.607, 95% CI: 2.665–4.880, P < 0.001 - HSI: OR = 3.406, 95% CI: 2.282–5.083, P < 0.001) of LAP compared to the first tertile in the adjusted model.ConclusionsThe present research provides robust evidence that a higher LAP index is strongly associated with higher odds of NAFLD and hepatic steatosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04353-5.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) refers to a range of liver conditions that include non-inflammatory isolated steatosis, which is characterized by the accumulation of triglycerides (TG) in liver cells. This is identified by the presence of lipid droplets in the cytoplasm of more than 5% of liver cells [1, 2]. Histologically, NAFLD encompasses a spectrum of conditions, ranging from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and further progressing to cirrhosis, hepatic failure, advanced fibrosis, and potentially hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 3–6].

NAFLD is the most common chronic liver disease (CLD) globally, impacting approximately 25–33% of the worldwide population [7]. A global survey conducted in 2016 revealed that the incidence of NAFLD diagnosed through imaging is more than 25%, with rates of 23.71% in Europe, 27.37% in Asia, 24.13% in North America, 30.45% in South America, and 31.79% in the Middle East [8]. According to a 2024 meta-analysis, the prevalence of NAFLD among Iranian adults is approximately 35%, indicating a high and rising burden of disease at the national level [9]. Consequently, NAFLD is now widely acknowledged as the primary cause of CLD. Meanwhile, NASH is the second leading cause of cirrhosis among adults on the United States liver transplant waiting list. Projections indicate that NASH will likely surpass other causes and become the leading cause within the next ten years [10, 11].

Metabolic disorders play a crucial role in the onset and advancement of NAFLD [12]. Given that NAFLD is a chronic condition that develops over many years, regular screening and timely diagnosis in young individuals can help prevent serious complications, such as hepatocellular carcinoma and cirrhosis [13]. While liver biopsy remains the preferred method for diagnosing liver cirrhosis and NAFLD, its invasive nature renders it impractical for routine follow-up assessments [14]. Consequently, several studies have endeavored to discover effective and streamlined alternatives for epidemiological studies and comprehensive population health surveillance. These alternatives are aimed at enabling early detection of individuals at a heightened risk of developing NAFLD [15, 16]. Several biochemical and anthropometric indices, as well as combinations of these indices, have been developed to assess the risk of NAFLD in this context [17]. Amongst these markers, lipid-related and obesity indicators are considered to be the most effective in anticipating NAFLD and are frequently utilized in epidemiological research [17].

The lipid accumulation product (LAP) index is a novel biomarker for quantifying excessive lipid accumulation. It shows promise for predicting metabolic abnormalities [18]. The LAP index is easily calculated by readily available clinical measures, such as fasting TG and waist circumference (WC). This accessibility makes it a valuable tool for both epidemiological and clinical applications [18, 19].

Insights into the relationship between adiposity and lipid metabolism are provided by this index, which is particularly relevant in the context of NAFLD. The LAP reflects visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, both of which are key contributors to the pathogenesis of NAFLD [20, 21]. Furthermore, the LAP index is regarded as a feasible alternative for detecting NAFLD due to its non-invasive nature and low cost, in contrast to invasive and costly procedures like liver biopsy [22]. Previous studies have shown a significant association between LAP and NAFLD. Several studies, including the study by Dai et al. [13] and the study by Huang et al. study [23], have demonstrated that LAP may be a useful predictor of NAFLD. However, findings remain inconsistent across different ethnicities and age groups [24, 25], and no study has examined this association in the Iranian population, which is the focus of the present study.

Therefore, the purpose of this research was to evaluate the relationship between the LAP index and NAFLD. The study aimed to develop efficient and affordable screening tools that can predict and assess the risk of NAFLD, ultimately improving early detection and intervention strategies for this common chronic liver condition.

Materials and methods

Study population

The current cross-sectional study was conducted from the second stage of the Amol Cohort Study (AmolCS), a large cohort study in the northern region of Iran, especially in the city of Amol. The AmolCS consisted of individuals aged over 18 years who were monitored for diabetes mellitus (DM), metabolic syndrome (MetS), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and NAFLD. Sampling was done from 16 urban health centers and 25 rural health centers into 16 strata based on age and gender. Age groups were defined in ten-year intervals, from 18 to 89 years. In the current study, the people of each stratum were selected in proportion to the population size within that stratum. The selection process was done through a simple randomization method, as described in the study by Zamani et al. [26, 27].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this study, a total of 5,147 people over 18 years of age were examined. A total of 459 participants were excluded due to various reasons, including death (n = 77), migration (n = 25), or non-cooperation (n = 357) in the second phase. Furthermore, a total of 486 participants with a history of alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, specific dietary restrictions, malignancy, thyroid disorders, autoimmune diseases, or missing/unreliable data on physical activity were excluded from the study. Additionally, 153 pregnant or lactating women were also excluded. A total of 929 participants were excluded from the study due to missing data on abdominal ultrasonography, incomplete dietary questionnaires, or implausible energy intake values based on Willett’s thresholds (i.e., < 500 or > 3,500 kcal/day for women, and < 800 or > 4,200 kcal/day for men). Finally, 3,120 subjects were eligible for analysis (Fig. 1).

Anthropometric and clinical history assessments

Trained personnel conducted anthropometric assessments following standardized protocols. Weight and height were measured using calibrated digital scales and stadiometers, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Anthropometric variables, such as hip circumference (HC) and WC, were recorded. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest using a non-elastic measuring tape. Also, the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was obtained by dividing WC (in meters) by height (in meters). In addition, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was determined by dividing WC (in meters) by HC (in meters). Moreover, the body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing an individual’s weight (in kilograms) by their height (in meters) squared.

A checklist was completed by all participants to gather demographic, lifestyle, and clinical history information. The checklist included the following details: sex, age, residency area, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and usage of glucose-lowering medications, lipid-lowering medications, and anti-hypertensive medications, as well as family history of chronic diseases. Using a validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire, physical activity level was assessed [2].

Laboratory assessments

After a fasting period of 12 h, venous blood specimens were collected from each participant in the morning to assess their lipid profiles and fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels. Samples were processed within 2 h of collection, following standard protocols to ensure the stability of measurements. Following the protocol utilizing the BS200 Auto analyzer (Mindray, China), various tests were conducted including vitamin D, C-reactive protein (CRP), thyroxine (T4), triiodothyronine (T3), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), TG, FBS. The Friedewald equation was used to calculate serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Nutritional assessments

The participants’ typical diet was assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) comprised of 168 items, the validity of which has been previously established [28]. Trained interviewers gathered information about the participants’ diet. They recorded the quantity and frequency of each food consumed in the recent past. This information was then changed to grams per day using household measurements [29]. Finally, the data pertaining to each food item was extracted using Nutritionist IV software (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA). The software was designed specifically for Iranian foods [30].

Definition of NAFLD

NAFLD was confirmed to use ultrasound imaging. The imaging showed increased echogenicity in the liver, as well as abnormal appearance of the diaphragm and hepatic arteries. These findings were observed in a patient who did not have viral or hereditary liver diseases, did not have drug-induced steatosis, and did not consume excessive alcohol. A linear array transducer with a frequency range of 3–5 MHz was used to capture intercostal, lateral, longitudinal, sagittal, and lateral views. The subjects were in a fasting state when a single experienced radiologist performed the diagnosis using ultrasound sonography. Also, the radiologist followed standardized protocols to ensure consistency in measurements.

Definition of MetS

Using the criteria established by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III, MetS was identified [9]. The diagnosis criteria were the following:

Calculation of indices

Although abdominal ultrasonography is the standard imaging tool for NAFLD detection, its application may be limited in large-scale screening due to cost, operator dependence, and lack of accessibility in certain healthcare settings. The Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI), derived from routine clinical parameters, offers a feasible alternative for non-invasive risk assessment. In this study, HSI was used to approximate NAFLD status in the absence of imaging data, and its association with LAP was analyzed to explore their complementary roles in identifying individuals at risk of hepatic steatosis. Statistical analysis

At first, the patient characteristics were grouped into tertiles based on the LAP. The descriptive statistics were reported as percentage for categorical variables and the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the assessment of normality was conducted. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was utilized to compare LAP tertiles across different variables, followed by Tukey’s test.

A multivariate logistic regression was performed to examine the relationship between LAP tertiles and both NAFLD and HSI. Both crude and adjusted models were used to assess this association. Several covariates, such as energy intake, fiber intake, gender, residential area, current smoking status, alcohol consumption, family history of chronic diseases, physical activity level, MetS, and use of lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering, and anti-hypertensive medications were included in the adjusted model. Subgroup analyses were performed based on age, gender, and BMI, which are well-known effect modifiers in metabolic-related outcomes and have been commonly used in prior research on NAFLD and LAP. Other variables showing baseline differences were adjusted for multivariable models, but not used for stratification to maintain statistical robustness and avoid multiple comparison issues.

We assessed the areas under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate LAP’s performance. The Youden index was employed to determine the optimal cutoff point, and sensitivity and specificity were determined at known cut-off points.

SPSS (version 23) was used for data analysis. Also, R software (R version 4.4.1) was applied to depict all figures. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed to have statistical significance.

Relationship between NAFLD and HSI with LAP

Discussion

The findings from the current study indicated a significant positive relationship between LAP and the odds of NAFLD, as well as the HSI. LAP was positively associated with NAFLD across different genders, ages, and BMI categories. However, LAP showed a significant positive relationship with the HSI specifically in males, individuals under 45 years old, and those with a BMI under 30. No association was observed between LAP and HSI in females, individuals over 45 years of age, or obese participants.

NAFLD, which is the most common chronic liver disease, affects approximately 25% of the adult population worldwide [8]. There is a growing number of studies that aim to identify practical and reliable predictors for NAFLD, which is crucial in clinical settings. NAFLD is strongly associated with type 2 DM (T2DM), obesity, and MetS. Consequently, it is recommended to screen individuals with these conditions for NAFLD [32, 33]. In recent years, numerous studies have confirmed a strong link between LAP index and cardiometabolic risk factors [34, 35]. For example, Taverna et al. conducted a cross-sectional study that demonstrated the LAP index’s exceptional accuracy in diagnosing MetS, which is also closely linked to NAFLD [36, 37]. LAP index has been proven to have a positive correlation with the risk of NAFLD [23, 38], and serves as a reliable indicator for hepatic steatosis [39, 40]. These findings align with the outcomes of our cross-sectional study.

Also, LAP has demonstrated considerable utility not only in identifying NAFLD but also as a marker for metabolic dysfunction across various populations and conditions. For instance, in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), LAP has been correlated with MetS in normal-weight and overweight women and cardiovascular risk, offering a practical tool for early risk stratification [41, 42].

Motamed et al. discovered that WC is a highly effective diagnostic tool for identifying NAFLD, with an AUC of 0.85 and a 95% CI of 0.84–0.86 [43]. An increase in WC is indicative of the accumulation of excessive visceral fat. This can lead to the synthesis of cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin 6 (IL-6), which trigger inflammation and the infiltration of macrophages. As a result, liver inflammation and fibrosis are exacerbated [44]. Furthermore, elevated levels of TG can promote the transportation of free fatty acids (FFA) to the liver, thus contributing to the development of NAFLD [45]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that omega-3 fatty acids can effectively decrease liver steatosis by reducing TG levels. This provides additional evidence supporting the notion that elevated TG plays a significant role in the development of NAFLD [46]. Consequently, LAP, calculated from TG and WC, is closely associated with hepatic pathological changes and NAFLD. Also, studies have found a significant link between the severity of hepatic steatosis and the levels of ALT and AST in children who are overweight or obese [47] with elevated liver enzymes often indicating early NAFLD diagnosis [48].

According to Ulasoglu et al., around 21% of patients with biopsy-confirmed NAFLD were found to have normal levels of liver enzymes [49, 50], reinforcing the notion that aminotransferase levels alone are not definitive indicators of liver damage [50–52]. Moreover, conditions such as Wilson’s disease, autoimmune hepatitis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, hereditary hemochromatosis, hepatitis C, hepatitis B, and drug-induced liver injury can also cause elevated transaminase levels [53].

Contrary to our findings, Liu et al. reported a more pronounced correlation between LAP and NAFLD in females as opposed to males [38]. This difference could be attributed to the way estrogen affects vascular inflammation, lipid metabolism, plaque progression, and cell growth in premenopausal women. Menopause causes biological and physiological changes, including glucose intolerance, dyslipidemia, and the accumulation of visceral fat. These changes are closely linked to NAFLD, CVD, and increased insulin resistance (IR) [54].

Sex differences in the utility of LAP as a marker for metabolic risk have been reported [25], with menopausal status playing a critical role in modulating these differences [54]. Menopause is associated with hormonal changes that lead to increased central adiposity and altered lipid metabolism, factors that can influence LAP values and their predictive power [55, 56]. Studies in diverse populations have shown that postmenopausal women have greater central adiposity, dyslipidemia, increased inflammation, and a higher prevalence of MetS than premenopausal women [57–59]. This suggests that LAP performance may differ by menopausal status, highlighting that stratification by menopause could improve LAP’s precision and clinical utility in risk assessment.

The stronger association between LAP and prevalent NAFLD observed in younger and lower-BMI individuals may be explained by several factors. Younger individuals may exhibit greater hepatic sensitivity to lipid accumulation or may not yet present other competing metabolic comorbidities, making the impact of LAP on NAFLD more apparent. Similarly, in lower-BMI populations, LAP might better capture subtle visceral adiposity and metabolic disturbances that are not reflected by BMI alone, highlighting its role as a sensitive indicator of hepatic fat deposition even among non-obese individuals. These findings suggest that LAP could be particularly useful for early risk stratification in populations traditionally considered at lower risk.

One of the main strengths of this study is its large sample size, comprising individuals from a northern Iranian population who underwent liver ultrasound, which allowed us to evaluate the diagnostic performance of LAP for ultrasound-confirmed NAFLD. In our cohort, the optimal LAP cutoff for identifying NAFLD was 0.791. Comparable studies in other populations have reported varying thresholds and diagnostic performance. For instance, in a Chinese cohort, LAP demonstrated sex-specific AUC values of 0.843 for men and 0.887 for women [60]. In a US (NHANES) cohort, an AUC of 0.813 was reported for LAP in predicting as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatosis liver disease (MASLD) [61]. Additionally, a Turkish cohort study reported a LAP cutoff of 42.7 for the prediction of NAFLD [62].

Additionally, we adjusted for potential confounders in regression models. However, we should acknowledge some limitations. The cross-sectional design of the study hinders our capacity to establish causality in the observed associations. The study only evaluates the association between LAP and NAFLD, and causal relationships could not be determined, necessitating prospective studies for confirmation. Furthermore, the results should be cautiously generalized to other regions, as the research was conducted in northern Iran.

Although our study was originally conducted using the NAFLD framework, it is important to note that this condition has recently been redefined as MASLD. While we did not collect all criteria required for a formal MASLD diagnosis, such as insulin resistance markers or hs-CRP, most of our participants with hepatic steatosis also exhibited metabolic risk factors, including elevated BMI, triglycerides, and fasting glucose. Thus, our population largely aligns with the conceptual basis of MASLD, and our findings remain relevant in the context of this updated classification.

Conclusions

The present research provides robust evidence that a higher LAP index significantly increases the risk of NAFLD and hepatic steatosis. The LAP measurement is simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective, making it suitable for large-scale application in hepatic steatosis and early-stage NAFLD screening. Consequently, these findings could facilitate more rapid screening and diagnosis of NAFLD in clinical practice. Implementing LAP as a standard screening tool could improve early detection rates, allowing for timely intervention and management, potentially reducing the progression to more severe liver diseases. Furthermore, this approach could lead to better resource allocation in healthcare settings, emphasizing preventive measures and reducing the need for more invasive diagnostic procedures. Therefore, the LAP index stands out as a promising marker to improve clinical outcomes in patients at risk for NAFLD, ultimately contributing to better overall public health outcomes.
