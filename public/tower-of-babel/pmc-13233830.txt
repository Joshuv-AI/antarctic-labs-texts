Prostaglandin E2 alleviates acetaminophen-induced liver injury through DDIT4-enhanced autophagy regulated by circLima1/miR-486
Authors: Chao Chen, Jun Guan, Shanshan Wu, Meng Hong, Yanli Ren, Guodi Wu, Jing Wang, Xinyu Gu, Zhi Chen, Haihong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcetaminophen (APAP) overdose is a leading cause of acute liver injury (AILI). While Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) protects against various forms of acute hepatitis, its underlying mechanisms and the role of circRNA-mediated regulatory networks in AILI remain to be elucidated. AILI was induced in mice by administration of 500 mg/kg APAP, with or without 16,16-dimethyl PGE2 (dmPGE2) pretreatment. Hepatic transcriptomic landscapes (circRNA, miRNA, and mRNA) were characterized by high-throughput sequencing. The circLima1/miR-486/Ddit4 axis was validated using dual-luciferase assays and hepatocyte-specific AAV8-mediated knockdown. Autophagic flux was monitored via mRFP-EGFP-LC3 tandem reporting, and the functional necessity of autophagy was confirmed using the inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA). dmPGE2 treatment reduced serum transaminases and suppressed hepatic p-JNK activation in APAP-challenged mice. Transcriptomic profiling and qPCR identified Ddit4 as a key responder, which was confirmed in primary hepatocytes. Mechanistically, dmPGE2-induced circLima1 sponges miR-486a/b-3p to upregulate DDIT4, leading to inhibited mTOR phosphorylation and enhanced autophagic flux. AAV-mediated knockdown of circLima1 or Ddit4, as well as pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with 3-MA, abolished the hepatoprotective effects of dmPGE2. dmPGE2 alleviates AILI by activating the circLima1/miR-486/DDIT4 axis to promote protective autophagy. These findings identify the circLima1/DDIT4 pathway as a potential therapeutic target for drug-induced liver injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52049-x.Keywords: Prostaglandin E2, Acetaminophen, Acute liver injury, circLima1, DDIT4, AutophagySubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Molecular biology

Introduction

PGE2, a bioactive lipid synthesized from arachidonic acid (AA) via the sequential action of cyclooxygenase (COX) and prostaglandin E synthase (PGES)1, is one of the most abundant prostaglandins in the human body and exerts diverse biological effects2. The downregulation of PGE2 in skeletal muscle tissue with overexpression of 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH) leads to muscle atrophy and reduction of autophagy in young mice3. Injection of PGE2 reverses aged muscle stem cell dysfunction, leading to increased regeneration and strength4. Prior research has shown that dmPGE2, a metabolically stable PGE2 analog with physiological effects comparable to endogenous PGE2, exhibits hepatoprotective properties in animal models of Acetaminophen (APAP)-induced liver injury(AILI)5. Research shows that APAP overdose induces autophagy, which attenuates APAP-induced liver cell death by removing damaged mitochondria and APAP-protein adducts (APAP-ADs)6. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the alleviation effect of PGE2 on acute liver injury remain unclear. PGE2 might protect hepatocytes by inducing autophagy and restoring mitochondrial function.

The circRNA/miRNA/mRNA network is reported to play an essential role in AILI7. CircRNAs are a class of highly conserved noncoding RNAs characterized by covalently closed-loop structures lacking polyadenylated tail and 5′-to-3′ polarity8. These molecules, formed through back-splicing, demonstrate greater stability than linear RNAs due to their resistance to exonuclease activity and are increasingly recognized as potential diagnostic biomarkers for various diseases9,10. Functionally, circRNAs modulate gene expression through mechanisms such as miRNA sequestration, interactions with RNA-binding proteins, and limited peptide translation11. CircRNAs bind to complementary miRNAs and inhibit their regulatory effects on target genes12,13. This mechanism is facilitated by the presence of complementary sequences within circRNAs specific to miRNAs, which enables the formation of stable RNA-miRNA complexes that exhibit resistance to degradation8,14. Consequently, by acting as miRNA sponges, circRNAs sequester miRNAs and prevent their interaction with target genes, thereby resulting in target gene derepression15. Thus, through this miRNA sponge mechanism, circRNAs post-transcriptionally regulate miRNA function and are consequently pivotal regulators of gene expression in cellular processes16. In AILI, circ-CBFB has been identified as a miR-185-5p sponge, modulating p66Shc expression and mitochondrial dynamics17. miRNAs also play pivotal roles in AILI. As key regulators of APAP metabolism, cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP450s) were regulated by specific miRNAs. For instance, miR-27b binds to the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of CYP3A4, downregulating its expression in HEK293 cells18. Serum levels of miR-122-5p, miR-378a-5p, miR-125b-5p, and miR-27b-3p are elevated in children with APAP overdose compared to healthy controls. Papageorgiou et al. further demonstrated that miR-375 modulates UGT1A1 expression, which increased the risk for APAP-induced ALF19. However, the circRNA/miRNA/mRNA networks involved in PGE2-mediated mitigation of AILI remain unexplored.

Collectively, while the link between noncoding RNAs and liver diseases is increasingly acknowledged, the precise regulatory functions of circRNA and miRNA in dmPGE2-mediated alleviation of AILI remain unclear. To investigate the role of it underlying PGE2-mediated alleviation of AILI, we performed sequencing of circRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs in liver tissues from mice treated with APAP and dmPGE2. Through high-throughput sequencing, we aimed to elucidate the role of circRNA/miRNA/mRNA interactions in the mechanism by which dmPGE2 alleviates AILI.

Materials and methods

Animal models and treatment regimen

Male C57BL/6J mice (wild-type, WT), aged 8 weeks and weighing 20 ± 2 g, were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal (Shanghai, China). Mice were acclimatized in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment for 1 week prior to experimentation. To establish the acute liver injury (AILI) model, mice were fasted for 15 h overnight and subsequently injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 500 mg/kg APAP dissolved in warm saline. Control mice received equivalent volumes of saline vehicle.

Based on our preliminary toxicity and protection assessments (Supplementary Fig. S1), multiple treatment regimens were assigned: Standard Pre-treatment: 16,16-dimethyl PGE2 (dmPGE2; MCE, HY-106420) was administered i.p. at an optimal dose of 300 μg/kg (unless otherwise indicated for dose-gradient assays) 1 h prior to APAP challenge. Therapeutic Window Assessment: dmPGE2 (300 μg/kg) was administered at − 1 h, 1 h, 2 h, or 3 h relative to the APAP challenge. Autophagy Inhibition (3-MA): For in vivo mechanistic validation, the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA, 15 mg/kg) was administered i.p. 2 h prior to APAP exposure (i.e., 1 h before dmPGE2 pretreatment).

For the primary mechanistic and histological analyses, liver and serum samples were harvested exactly 6 h post-APAP administration. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were quantified using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7180, Japan) according to standard clinical procedures. For survival assessments, mice were monitored continuously for up to 72 h following a lethal APAP challenge. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University and conducted in strict adherence to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Cell culture and in vitro treatments

The murine hepatoma cell line Hepa1-6 (ATCC, CRL-1830) and the human hepatoma cell line Hep3B (ATCC, HB-8064) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For in vitro injury modeling, cells were challenged with 10 mM APAP for 24 h. To evaluate hepatoprotection, 2 μM PGE2 (MCE, HY-101952) was supplemented 1 h prior to APAP exposure. The Hepa1-6 cell line was used to verify the conserved murine circLima1/miR-486/mRNA regulatory axis, whereas the Hep3B line was utilized for Seahorse metabolic assays and autophagic puncta quantification. For autophagic flux validation, rapamycin (10 μM, MCE) was co-administered as a positive inducer. Additionally, transient knockdown of DDIT4 in Hep3B cells was achieved using specific siRNAs transfected via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol 24 h prior to the APAP challenge. All in vitro experiments were performed in biological triplicate (n = 3).

Isolation of primary murine hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells (NPCs)

To validate the hepatocyte-specific expression of DDIT4 ex vivo, primary murine hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) were isolated from dmPGE2-treated AILI mice utilizing a standard two-step collagenase perfusion technique. Briefly, the liver was perfused in situ through the portal vein with EGTA-chelating buffer, followed by digestion with 0.05% Collagenase Type IV (Worthington). The digested cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 50 × g for 3 min to pellet the parenchymal hepatocytes. The remaining supernatant was further centrifuged at 500 × g for 7 min to collect the NPC fraction. The purity and viability of isolated fractions were confirmed via Trypan blue exclusion prior to immediate downstream immunoblotting.

Histological analysis

Liver tissues from the left lobe were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4 μm thickness), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To assess the severity of APAP-induced liver injury, the area of centrilobular coagulative necrosis and ballooning degeneration was quantified using the Irregular AOI tool in Image-Pro Plus software. Five distinct, randomly selected fields (at 400 × magnification) per slide from each sample were evaluated to calculate the average percentage of the necrotic area relative to the total field area.

Transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) supplemented with RNase-free DNase I. For each group, 5 μg of total RNA from three biological replicates was utilized to construct sequencing libraries. Briefly, for mRNA and circRNA sequencing, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted prior to library construction, whereas small RNA libraries were specifically generated for miRNA profiling. The libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing reads containing > 10% unknown bases or > 30% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 20) were filtered out. The resulting clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome (mm39) using the STAR alignment algorithm. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package, with statistical significance strictly defined as an adjusted P-value < 0.05. Novel circRNAs (including circLima1, circSlc25a15, and circLsm14a) were identified and annotated utilizing the CIRCpedia v3 database (Supplementary Datasets S1 and S2). The high-throughput sequencing datasets generated in this study have been deposited in the GEO database (GSE241509, GSE241510, and GSE241511). Detailed overviews of the sequencing data, mapping statistics, and all primer/oligonucleotide sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S6.

For downstream bioinformatic analyses, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of differentially expressed mRNAs was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape, and core hub genes were extracted utilizing the cytoHubba plugin based on maximal clique centrality (MCC) scores. Finally, the conserved ceRNA network was predicted via the miRanda algorithm, applying highly stringent thresholds (pairing score ≥ 140, maximum binding free energy ≤ −17 kcal/mol) to identify the specific circLima1/miR-486/Ddit4 regulatory axis.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK-293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were seeded into 24-well plates to reach 60–70% confluence prior to transfection. To validate the direct binding interaction between the circular RNA and its target miRNA, wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) reporter plasmids harboring the full-length sequence of circLima1 were constructed. Cells were transiently co-transfected with specific miR-486a/b-3p mimics (or negative control mimics) alongside 0.1 μg of either the circLima1-WT or circLima1-Mut reporter plasmids utilizing Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. At 48 h post-transfection, Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were quantified using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, China) on a VICTOR Nivo Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing the Firefly luminescence to the internal Renilla luminescence.

Hepatocyte-specific in vivo knockdown via rAAV8

To achieve hepatocyte-specific in vivo knockdown of circLima1 and Ddit4, customized recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (rAAV8) vectors were constructed and packaged by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). For Ddit4, the optimal shRNA sequence (5′-GTTAAGTTCTGCCAACTCTTC-3′) was selected following in vitro screening for maximum knockdown efficiency. For circLima1, an shRNA specifically targeting its unique back-splicing junction (5′-AACCAAACCAAGAACCCTG-3′) was custom-designed to ensure silencing specificity without affecting its linear cognate mRNA.These specific oligonucleotides, alongside a scrambled negative control sequence (5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′), were cloned into the GPAAV-TBG-eGFP-5′miR30-MCS-3′miR30-WPRE vector. This vector utilizes a thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) promoter to restrict target gene knockdown specifically to parenchymal hepatocytes. The final viral titers, as determined by quantitative PCR, were 1.09 × 1013 VG/mL for AAV-sh-circLima1 and 1.03 × 1013 VG/mL for AAV-sh-Ddit4. Mice were injected intravenously via the tail vein at a dose of 2 × 1011 VG/mouse and housed for 3 weeks to ensure stable and sufficient hepatic expression prior to APAP modeling.

Immunoblotting

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were resolved via 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Merck, USA). After blocking, membranes were probed with primary antibodies against mTOR (1:2000), p-mTOR (1:2000), DDIT4 (1:2000), p62 (1:2000), LC3 (1:2000), GAPDH (1:5000), JNK (1:1000, Proteintech), p-JNK (1:1000, Proteintech), and β-actin (1:2000, Proteintech). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and quantified via ImageJ software (v1.53). Original uncropped blots are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Quantification of autophagic flux via tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3

Autophagic flux was dynamically monitored utilizing a tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 system. Briefly, Hep3B cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding mRFP-EGFP-LC3 (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China). In this system, autophagosomes display dual fluorescence appearing as yellow puncta (EGFP + / mRFP +), while autolysosomes emit exclusively red fluorescence (EGFP- / mRFP +) due to the quenching of EGFP in acidic lysosomal environments. Following the respective treatments described above, cells were imaged hourly at 40 × magnification using the Operetta CLS™ High-Content Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). Automated puncta quantification was executed via Harmony software (v4.9) applying strict detection thresholds: radius ≤ 5 pixels, contrast > 0.1, and uncorrected spot-to-region intensity ratio > 1.5.

Mitochondrial function assessment

Cellular metabolic parameters were evaluated utilizing the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Hep3B cells were seeded into XF96 microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight. Prior to the assay, the culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF base medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 2 mM L-glutamine (pH adjusted to 7.4). Cells were then incubated in a non-CO₂ incubator at 37 °C for 1 h.

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were dynamically measured using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit. During the assay, specific metabolic inhibitors were sequentially injected at optimized final concentrations: oligomycin (1.0 μM), FCCP (1.0 μM), and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA, 0.5 μM). Data were acquired and analyzed using Wave software (Agilent Technologies), and the metabolic rates were normalized to the initial cell seeding density.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Prior to parametric testing, data were evaluated for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test and assessed for homogeneity of variance.

For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls (SNK) post-hoc test was employed. If data violated the assumptions of normality, non-parametric alternatives (such as the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test) were applied. Bivariate correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

dmPGE2 alleviates AILI in vivo

As outlined in the experimental timeline (Fig. 1A), H&E staining revealed that APAP induced severe centrilobular coagulative necrosis (Fig. 1C, red arrow) and ballooning degeneration (Fig. 1C, black arrow). Pre-treatment with dmPGE2 significantly reduced the necrotic area (Fig. 1D) and serum AST/ALT levels (Fig. 1B), with 300 μg/kg identified as the optimal protective dose. Therapeutic administration of dmPGE2 significantly reduced transaminase leakage when given at 1 or 2 h post-APAP (Fig. 1E). However, this protection was lost when delayed to 3 h (Fig. 1E). Mechanistically, dmPGE2 did not alter hepatic glutathione (GSH) levels (Fig. 1F). Instead, Western blot analysis at 6 h demonstrated that dmPGE2 effectively suppressed the pathogenic activation of phosphorylated JNK (Fig. 1G,H). Thus, dmPGE2 mitigates AILI by intercepting the hepatocyte-intrinsic JNK cascade independently of GSH metabolism.

Transcriptomic profiling and identification of candidate circRNAs in dmPGE2-treated AILI mice

Hepatic circRNA profiles among the CON, APAP, and APAP + dmPGE2 (PGE2) groups were evaluated via high-throughput sequencing. The identified circRNAs were widely distributed across 21 chromosomes (Fig. 2A) and primarily originated from exonic regions (Fig. 2B), with lengths predominantly ranging from 201 to 400 nt (Fig. 2C).

Hierarchical clustering revealed distinct transcriptional patterns across the groups (Fig. 2D). To identify key regulatory circRNAs, we initially selected four top candidates from the volcano plot (circKcnn2, circTrpc6, circLima1, and circSlc25a15) based on their significant differential expression in the PGE2 vs. APAP comparison (Fig. 2E). Subsequent RT-qPCR validation in an independent murine cohort confirmed that three of these candidates-circLima1, circSlc25a15, and circKcnn2-were significantly upregulated following dmPGE2 treatment in AILI mice (Fig. 2F). The fourth candidate, circTrpc6, did not show consistent changes and was excluded from further analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3). Additionally, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed circRNAs highlighted pathways associated with cellular metabolism and stress responses (Fig. 2G,H). This sequential screening process yielded three robust circRNA candidates for downstream functional validation.

Transcriptomic profiling of miRNAs in dmPGE2-treated AILI mice

Small RNA sequencing of liver tissues yielded 0.58–0.68 million high-quality reads per group, mapping to the mouse reference genome at rates of 33.91–38.56% (Supplementary Table S4). The identified small RNAs displayed a typical mature miRNA length distribution peaking at 22 nt (Fig. 3A), with comparable overall transcript abundance (TPM) across all groups (Fig. 3B).

Hierarchical clustering of the differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) revealed distinct transcriptional signatures across the treatments (Fig. 3C). Pairwise comparisons identified 49 DEmiRNAs in the APAP vs. CON group, and 26 DEmiRNAs in the PGE2 vs. APAP group (Fig. 3D). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEmiRNA target genes indicated that APAP exposure predominantly altered cellular component-related terms, whereas dmPGE2 intervention primarily modulated broader cellular processes and signaling pathways (Fig. 3E,F). These miRNA expression profiles provided the basis for constructing the downstream ceRNA regulatory network.

Transcriptomic profiling and identification of candidate mRNAs in dmPGE2-treated AILI mice

Among these 12 intersected genes, Ddit4, Retreg1, and Gadd45g were initially prioritized based on their progressive upregulation across the CON, APAP, and APAP + dmPGE2 groups in the RNA-seq dataset (Fig. 4C). Subsequent RT-qPCR validation confirmed the significant upregulation of Ddit4 and Retreg1 following dmPGE2 treatment. In contrast, Gadd45g expression did not significantly differ between the APAP and APAP + dmPGE2 groups (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether conventional global stress mediators were involved in this protection, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the DEmRNAs (Fig. 4E). The expression levels of the top 10 theoretical hub genes (e.g., Hsp90ab1, Hspa1a) remained unaltered by dmPGE2 treatment (Fig. 4F). This allowed us to exclude these classic stress responders and narrow our mechanistic focus to Ddit4 as the primary responsive candidate for downstream functional evaluation.

The hepatocyte-specific circLima1/DDIT4 axis is essential for dmPGE2-mediated hepatoprotection

To evaluate the functional role of the dmPGE2-mediated network in vivo, we utilized a hepatocyte-specific adeno-associated virus (AAV8) delivery system driven by the thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) promoter (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, primary cells isolated at 6 h post-APAP + dmPGE2 treatment showed that DDIT4 is predominantly enriched in hepatocytes rather than non-parenchymal cells (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Targeted knockdown of hepatocyte Ddit4 (AAV-sh-Ddit4) abolished the ability of dmPGE2 to suppress transaminase leakage (Fig. 5B) and p-JNK activation (Fig. 5C,D). Consequently, the hepatoprotective effects of dmPGE2 were reversed, with persistent severe centrilobular necrosis observed in the AAV-sh-Ddit4 group compared to the AAV-sh-Neg control (Fig. 5E). These functional genetic data indicate that DDIT4 is a necessary downstream effector of dmPGE2.

Next, we investigated the upstream regulation of Ddit4. Bioinformatic analysis predicted a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network involving circLima1 and miR-486a/b-3p (Fig. 5F), with complementary binding sites among these transcripts (Fig. 5G). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed this physical interaction: miR-486 mimics significantly reduced the luminescence of the wild-type circLima1 reporter, whereas the mutant construct remained unaffected (Fig. 5H).

To validate this regulatory axis in vivo, we administered AAV-sh-circLima1. Hepatocyte-specific knockdown of circLima1 triggered the expected molecular cascade: an upregulation of miR-486a/b-3p and the subsequent suppression of Ddit4 (Fig. 5I). Consistent with the Ddit4 knockdown phenotype, silencing circLima1 abrogated the hepatoprotective effects of dmPGE2, resulting in elevated AST/ALT levels (Fig. 5J) and severe hepatic injury (Fig. 5E). Together, these findings demonstrate that dmPGE2 alleviates AILI by activating the circLima1/miR-486/DDIT4 regulatory axis.

dmPGE2 enhances hepatoprotective autophagy via the DDIT4/mTOR axis

To monitor autophagic flux in vitro, Hep3B cells were stably transduced with an mRFP-EGFP-LC3 reporter. Compared to APAP alone, co-treatment with PGE2 significantly increased the accumulation of both yellow puncta (autophagosomes) and red puncta (autolysosomes), indicating enhanced autophagic flux (Fig. 6A–D). Western blot analysis confirmed that PGE2 upregulated DDIT4, suppressed mTOR phosphorylation, and promoted p62 degradation alongside LC3-II conversion (Fig. 6E). Notably, siRNA-mediated knockdown of DDIT4 abrogated this PGE2-induced autophagic enhancement (Fig. 6F,G), identifying DDIT4 as the requisite link between PGE2 treatment and autophagy activation.

To validate the functional necessity of this mechanism in vivo, we utilized the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) in combination with dmPGE2. Mice were treated according to a sequential pharmacological timeline (3-MA at −2 h, dmPGE2 at −1 h, APAP at 0 h). While 3-MA administration alone did not exacerbate baseline APAP toxicity, its co-administration with dmPGE2 abolished the reduction in serum AST/ALT levels (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, pharmacological blockade of autophagy reversed the survival benefits of dmPGE2 following a lethal APAP challenge (Fig. 6I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dmPGE2-mediated hepatoprotection relies on the activation of the DDIT4-driven autophagy axis.

Discussion

In the present study, we employed AILI animal models and cell models to identify the key gene DDIT4, which exhibited a continuous increase in expression following APAP or APAP + PGE2/dmPGE2 treatment. We propose that PGE2 induces DDIT4 via the regulatory axis network involving circLima1, miR-486, and DDIT4. The upregulation of DDIT4 in hepatocytes, in turn, inhibits downstream mTORC1, thereby enhancing autophagy. Consequently, APAP-ADs, which play a critical role in APAP-induced hepatocyte necrosis20,21 by impaired mitochondrial function, can be removed through autophagy induced by DDIT422. Sustained JNK activation and the subsequent mitochondrial permeability transition (MPT) are central drivers of APAP-induced necrosis. Our in vivo rescue data firmly establish the dmPGE2-induced circLima1/DDIT4 axis as a crucial upstream regulator that effectively suppresses this pathogenic JNK-driven mitochondrial damage.

APAP is considered safe for alleviating fever and pain at therapeutic doses, but APAP overdose represents the leading cause of acute liver failure23. APAP is metabolized by cytochrome P-450 2E1 (CYP2E1) in hepatocytes to generate N-acetyl-p-benzoquinoneimine (NAPQI)24 while excess NAPQI covalently binds to intracellular proteins, forming APAP-ADs, which contribute to hepatocyte damage25. In clinical practice, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) serves as the antidote for APAP overdose by acting as a precursor for glutathione (GSH) synthesis, thereby facilitating the detoxification of excess NAPQI26. Notably, NAC demonstrates maximal efficacy when administered early following APAP overdose, as it does not reverse existing APAP-ADs formation. Recent studies using a zebrafish model of APAP toxicity have shown that co-administration of NAC with PGE2 extends the therapeutic window, attenuates hepatocyte apoptosis, normalizes serum transaminase levels, and significantly reduces mortality27.

This study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the cytoprotective mechanisms of dmPGE2 in AILI mice. APAP primarily undergoes II phase metabolism, forming inactive metabolites, while a small fraction is metabolized into NAPQI28, leading to APAP-ADs formation29 and hepatotoxicity30–32. DDIT4, a 232-amino-acid protein, is cytoplasmic33 under normal conditions but can also localize to the cell membrane and mitochondria34,35, which typically responds to stressors like hypoxia36, ischemia37, DNA damage33, and ROS activation38. mTORC1, the primary target of DDIT4, is a crucial regulator of intracellular synthesis and metabolism, including enhancing protein production39, promoting lipid synthesis40, impeding the formation of autophagosomes41, and curtailing autophagy42. Induction of DDIT4 suppressed mTORC1 activity to induce autophagy by preserving the function of the tuberous sclerosis protein 1/2 complex (TSC1/2)43. Autophagy can eliminate damaged mitochondria and maintain mitochondrial homeostasis44, as in liver injury induced by alcohol or APAP45. In our research, the induction of DDIT4 is observed in mice treated with APAP. DDIT4 is upregulated by various hormones and growth factors, including glucocorticoids46, insulin47, catecholamines48, aldosterone49, growth hormones50, and melatonin51. The study shows that the downregulation of PGE2 in skeletal muscle tissue with overexpression of 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH) leads to muscle atrophy and reduction of autophagy in young mice3, indicating that the relationship between PGE2, DDIT4, autophagy, and muscle atrophy requires further investigation.

Our findings also represent a significant step toward identifying new therapeutic targets in AILI. As the only clinically approved antidote against APAP overdose, NAC supports hepatic GSH synthesis and prevents APAP-ADs formation but does not act directly on NAPQI nor remove APAP-ADs26,52. Research shows that APAP overdose induces autophagy, which attenuates AILI by removing damaged mitochondria and APAP-ADs6. We found that dmPGE2 induced autophagy in the liver from AILI mice, indicating that targeting the PGE2/circLima1/miR-486/DDIT4 axis might induce selective autophagy, particularly in liver tissue impaired by APAP overdose.

However, our research still has limitations. DDIT4 is upregulated under stimulation such as DNA damage and ROS activation caused by overdose of APAP36,48,51. Our data showed that Ddit4 was upregulated by circLima1/miRNA-486a/b in the dmPGE2 + APAP group, but both DDIT4 and miRNA-486a/b were upregulated in the APAP group (Fig. 5I & Supplementary Fig. S5). Moreover, dmPGE2 enhanced the upregulation of DDIT4 in AILI mice. However, it did not significantly increase the expression of DDIT4 in normal mice (Fig. 6D). So we supposed that DDIT4 is also regulated by other molecules, which remains unverified in the present work. Regarding the in vitro models, the 24 h treatment served as a compensatory window to observe signaling events in hepatoma cells despite their limited metabolic capacity. Importantly, these findings were strictly cross-validated by our in vivo experiments and primary hepatocyte analysis to ensure physiological relevance. In AILI mice treated with dmPGE2, downregulated miRNA-486a/b through circLima1 relieved the suppression of upregulation of DDIT4 induced by APAP. In normal mice, there is no suppression of DDIT4 by miRNA-486a/b. So, the dmPGE2 did not upregulate DDIT4 directly. The mechanism for the regulation of DDIT4 will be clarified in our future research. Our research did not confirm sufficiency or exclusivity of DDIT4 in PGE2 protection on AILI mice, while other pathways might be involved in PGE2-induced autophagy enhancement. In vivo knockdown or overexpression experiments would better confirm the roles of DDIT4 or circLima1, which we will do in the future. Moreover, only 119 DEmRNAs are identified in PGE2 vs. APAP, compared to 1437 in APAP vs. CON, which indicates PGE2 alleviates liver injury significantly but relatively few changes in gene expression. The APAP-induced liver injury involves multiple pathways and genes. DmPGE2 influences downstream pathways such as autophagy instead of upstream pathway such as GSH metabolism. So there are few changes in gene expression of dmPGE2 + APAP vs. APAP. Further research is required to clarify its mechanism.

In summary, this study investigated the protective effects of PGE2 against APAP-induced acute liver injury. We observed a significant increase in DDIT4 expression in both the APAP model and following PGE2 treatment. Notably, DDIT4 was found to induce autophagy by inhibiting mTORC1 activity, with its regulation mediated through the interaction of circLima1 and miRNA-486.
