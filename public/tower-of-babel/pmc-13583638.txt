Awareness, Willingness and Self-Reported Eligibility Characteristics for Blood Donation Among Young Undergraduates of David Umahi Federal University of Health Sciences, Uburu, Ebonyi State, Nigeria
Authors: Eric Okorie Nwaze, Francis Idenyi Onwe, Victor Sochima Ngwuta, Josephine Adejoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Nigeria continues to face shortages of safe blood, with substantial reliance on family replacement donors. University students are young, accessible and potentially suitable for repeat voluntary donation, but institution-specific evidence is needed to guide safe campus donor mobilisation. This study assessed awareness, knowledge, willingness, previous donation practice and self-reported eligibility characteristics for blood donation among undergraduates of David Umahi Federal University of Health Sciences (DUFUHS), Uburu, Nigeria.Methodology:A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among DUFUHS undergraduates in September 2025. The minimum sample size was 334 after finite population correction; 352 students were approached to allow for incomplete or unusable questionnaires, and 334 complete questionnaires were analysed, giving a usable response rate of 94.9%. Data were collected using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire adapted from previous blood donation studies. Variables included socio-demographic characteristics, awareness, knowledge, willingness, previous donation and self-reported eligibility characteristics. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and associations were assessed using chi-square or Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests at p < 0.05.Results:The mean age was 19.43 ± 2.98 years, and 248 (74.2%) respondents were female. Awareness of the blood donation process was reported by 172 (51.6%), while 162 (48.4%) were familiar with eligibility requirements. Only 51 (15.4%) were likely to donate within six months, 69 (20.7%) met the predefined willingness criterion, and 310 (92.9%) had never participated in blood donation drives.Conclusions:Awareness was moderate, but willingness and previous donation practice were low. DUFUHS should implement regular campus blood donation drives, peer donor clubs, targeted social media education and formal collaboration with authorised blood transfusion services.

Background

Blood transfusion services remain an indispensable component of modern health systems and are central to the management of trauma, obstetric haemorrhage, major surgery, malignancies and severe anaemia. Blood and blood components remain life-saving resources for which no artificial substitute is routinely available. The World Health Organization has reported that about 118.54 million blood donations are collected worldwide, with a disproportionate share collected in high-income countries despite higher transfusion-related burdens in many low- and middle-income settings.[1]

Nigeria continues to experience blood supply shortages and remains substantially dependent on family replacement and other non-regular donor systems. This weakens sustainability and can compromise transfusion safety. National and global blood safety strategies therefore emphasise voluntary, non-remunerated and regular blood donation as the preferred pathway to a safe and adequate blood supply.[1-4]

University students are a strategic population for voluntary blood donor recruitment because they are generally young, accessible, educated and potentially available for repeat donation. However, evidence from Nigeria shows a persistent gap between knowledge or favourable attitudes and actual donation practice. Salaudeen and Odeh reported suboptimal voluntary donation behaviour among tertiary institution students, while Nwabueze and colleagues found high awareness among medical and pharmaceutical science students but limited practice.[6,7] A recent study among Ambrose Alli University students similarly reported fair knowledge and positive attitude but poor donation practice, with only 13.6% reporting previous donation.[8]

David Umahi Federal University of Health Sciences (DUFUHS), Uburu, Ebonyi State, provides a relevant institutional context because its students are being trained within health-related disciplines and may be expected to support health promotion activities, including voluntary blood donation. Institution-specific evidence is needed to guide campus donor mobilisation, blood donation education, donor clubs and partnerships with blood transfusion services.

This study therefore assessed awareness, knowledge, willingness, previous blood donation practice and self-reported eligibility characteristics for blood donation among DUFUHS undergraduate students. The title and terminology were revised to make clear that the study did not clinically determine blood donor eligibility, but only assessed self-reported characteristics relevant to donor screening.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional study design was adopted to assess awareness, knowledge, willingness, previous donation practice and self-reported eligibility characteristics for blood donation among undergraduate students of DUFUHS, Uburu, Ebonyi State, Nigeria. The design was appropriate for estimating these variables at a single point in time, but it does not permit causal inference.

Study area

The study was conducted at David Umahi Federal University of Health Sciences, Uburu, Ebonyi State, Nigeria. DUFUHS is a specialised federal university established to train health professionals across Medicine and Surgery, Nursing Sciences, Pharmaceutical Sciences, Allied Health Sciences, Health Sciences and Technology and related programmes. The university operates a single-campus system with defined academic programmes and academic-year cohorts.

Study population

The study population comprised registered undergraduate students of DUFUHS across faculties and academic levels at the time of data collection. The undergraduate student population was approximately 2,500 based on records obtained from the Students' Affairs Division.

Eligibility criteria

Registered undergraduate students who were present on campus during the study period and who provided informed consent were eligible to participate. Postgraduate students, visiting students, students who declined consent and students who were ill or unable to complete the questionnaire were excluded.

Sample size determination

The minimum sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula n0 = Z2p(1-p)/d2, where Z was 1.96 at a 95% confidence level, p was set at 0.50 because no prior DUFUHS estimate was available, and d was 0.05. The initial sample size was 384.16, rounded to 385. Because the target undergraduate population was less than 10,000, finite population correction was applied using N = 2,500, giving n = 333.67, rounded to 334. The calculated minimum sample size was therefore 334 students. Although the required sample size was 334, 352 students were approached to allow for incomplete or unusable questionnaires; 334 complete questionnaires were finally analysed, corresponding to a usable response rate of 94.9%.

Sampling technique

Students were recruited using campus-based consecutive sampling of eligible students who were available during approved lecture-free periods and other class-contact periods. No random selection was performed. Questionnaires were administered across available undergraduate groups and programmes to improve coverage of the institutional population, but the sampling procedure remains non-probability based. This limitation has been acknowledged because it may introduce selection bias and may restrict generalisability beyond the participating students.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire adapted from previous blood donation knowledge, attitude and practice instruments used among Nigerian university students and health-related student populations.[6-8] The questionnaire contained sections on socio-demographic characteristics, awareness of blood donation, knowledge of donation guidelines, willingness to donate blood, previous donation practice and self-reported eligibility characteristics. Items included closed-ended questions and Likert-type responses.

Pre-testing and validation of the instrument

The questionnaire was reviewed by public health and haematology-oriented academics for face and content validity, then pre-tested among students in a comparable institutional setting to assess clarity, sequencing and ease of completion. Ambiguous items were revised before final administration. The instrument was not presented as a conventional validated eligibility or socio-economic status scale; rather, it was used to describe awareness, willingness, donation practice and selected self-reported characteristics relevant to donor screening.

Operational definitions

Awareness of blood donation was defined as a "Yes" response to having heard of or being aware of the blood donation process. Knowledge of eligibility requirements was defined as a "Yes" response to being familiar with donor eligibility requirements and was supplemented by specific knowledge items on donation interval and annual donation frequency. Willingness to donate was assessed in two ways: likelihood of donating within the next six months and a Likert-scale willingness score, dichotomised as willing for scale responses of 4-5 and unwilling/neutral for responses of 1-3. Previous blood donation practice was defined as any self-reported previous blood donation or participation in a blood donation drive. Self-reported eligibility characteristics referred to respondent-reported weight category, medical condition, blood group and health-related donation responses. Actual donor eligibility was not clinically determined because haemoglobin level, blood pressure, pregnancy or breastfeeding status, current medication use, recent illness, risk exposure history and screening for transfusion-transmissible infections were not objectively assessed.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected in September 2025 by trained research assistants using paper-based questionnaire administration. Participants were briefed on the purpose of the study, informed that participation was voluntary and assured of confidentiality. Questionnaires were completed individually and retrieved immediately to reduce missing data.

Data management and analysis

Data were entered, cleaned and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations were used for descriptive analysis. Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables and previous blood donation practice. Fisher's exact or Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests were used where expected cell counts were small. Cramer's V was specified as the effect-size statistic for multi-category associations where applicable. Multivariable logistic regression was not presented because the number of donation-event outcomes was small and several programme-level categories had sparse cells; the inferential results were therefore restricted to bivariate associations.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the DUFUHS University Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: DUFUHS/UREC/2025/0013 dated September 4, 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all respondents. No names or identifying numbers were collected on the analysis dataset. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that refusal or withdrawal would attract no penalty. Data were stored securely and accessed only by the research team.

Of 352 undergraduate students approached, 352 consented and received questionnaires. Eighteen questionnaires were excluded because they were incomplete or unusable. The final analysis included 334 complete questionnaires, giving a usable response rate of 94.9%.

Discussion

This study assessed awareness, knowledge, willingness, previous donation practice and self-reported eligibility characteristics for blood donation among undergraduate students of David Umahi Federal University of Health Sciences (DUFUHS), Uburu, Ebonyi State. The findings show a pattern that is important for blood donor mobilisation in a university setting: moderate awareness of the blood donation process, limited knowledge of some eligibility requirements, low willingness to donate within the near future, very low previous donation practice and low participation in organised blood donation drives. The overall pattern indicates a gap between recognising the social and clinical importance of blood donation and translating that recognition into actual voluntary donor behaviour.

The finding of low previous donation practice is consistent with several Nigerian studies. Salaudeen and Odeh reported that voluntary blood donation behaviour among tertiary institution students remained suboptimal despite awareness of blood donation.[6] Nwabueze and colleagues also reported high awareness among medical and pharmaceutical science students in Nnamdi Azikiwe University, but concluded that donation practice was low.[7] Similarly, Saka and colleagues found that only 13.6% of Ambrose Alli University students had donated blood, despite fair knowledge and positive attitude.[8] These Nigerian findings suggest that awareness alone is not sufficient to generate a reliable pool of voluntary student donors. The present study therefore supports the argument that blood donation programmes in universities should move beyond general sensitisation to structured behavioural, institutional and service-delivery interventions.

The socio-demographic profile of the respondents is relevant to the interpretation of the findings. The respondents were predominantly young undergraduates, with a mean age of 19.43 years, and most were within the age range generally considered suitable for potential blood donor recruitment after appropriate screening. However, a large proportion were in the lower academic levels. This may partly explain the observed gaps in knowledge and practice, because early-stage students may have had less exposure to clinical training, hospital environments, transfusion-related health education and patient-care experiences. In contrast, students in higher academic levels may have greater exposure to clinical settings, patient needs, blood transfusion services and peer discussions around donation. Studies among university and healthcare-related student populations have similarly suggested that educational exposure and health-related training may influence knowledge, attitude and blood donation behaviour.[9,10]

Awareness of the general blood donation process was moderate in this study, but knowledge of specific eligibility requirements was weaker. Although many respondents recognised that blood donation is important to society, fewer were familiar with practical requirements such as minimum weight, donation interval and other fitness criteria. This distinction is important. General awareness may create positive attitudes, but inadequate knowledge of eligibility criteria can lead to fear, uncertainty, self-exclusion or unsafe assumptions about who can donate. Previous studies have shown that students and young adults may express favourable attitudes towards blood donation while still having poor understanding of donation guidelines and low participation in actual donation.[11,12] The implication is that future awareness campaigns in DUFUHS should not only state that blood donation saves lives, but should also explain who can donate, how often donation can be done, what screening involves, and what safeguards exist for donors and recipients.

The sources of information reported by respondents also have practical implications. University programmes and social media were important sources of information, while friends, family and mass media also contributed. This suggests that DUFUHS already has communication channels that can be strengthened for donor education. Social media is particularly relevant because students are likely to engage with digital platforms, but messages delivered through social media must be accurate, repeated and linked to real donation opportunities. University-based health talks, orientation programmes, class briefings,

departmental associations, student union platforms and hostel-based peer education could provide more structured reinforcement. Evidence from blood donor behaviour research indicates that convenience, accessibility, repeated invitation and trusted information can influence donation intention and practice.[13,14]

Willingness to donate blood was low in the present study. Only a minority of respondents indicated likelihood of donating within six months, and the proportion showing overall willingness was also low. This is concerning because university students are often viewed as a potentially sustainable donor pool due to their age, accessibility and capacity for repeat donation. However, the finding is not unexpected. Similar studies in Nigeria and other low- and middle-income settings have reported that willingness to donate is frequently limited by fear of pain, fear of weakness, concern about adverse health effects, lack of opportunity, lack of knowledge and low confidence in personal eligibility.[15,16] In the present study, personal health concerns and fear-related issues were among the reasons for unwillingness, while lack of opportunity and limited awareness were also evident. These barriers are modifiable and should be addressed through targeted educational and service interventions.

A notable finding is that many respondents believed blood donation could make a difference and many would encourage others to donate, even though personal willingness and previous practice were low. This suggests a gap between social endorsement and personal action. Students may support the idea of blood donation in principle but hesitate when faced with the personal act of donating. This gap has been described in behavioural studies of blood donation, where intention and actual donation are influenced not only by knowledge but also by perceived risk, fear, convenience, social norms, past experience and availability of donation opportunities.[12,13,17] In DUFUHS, peer-led approaches may be useful because students who are already informed or who have previously donated can serve as ambassadors to reduce fear, normalise donation and encourage first-time donors.

Associations between academic level, perceived social class, and course of study and previous blood donation should be interpreted cautiously. As a cross-sectional study, causal inference is inappropriate. Observed differences may reflect clinical exposure, peer networks, curriculum content, professional identity, or awareness of transfusion needs, warranting further prospective research.

The assessment of eligibility is an important limitation and should be interpreted carefully. True donor eligibility requires objective pre-donation screening, including haemoglobin measurement, blood pressure assessment, pregnancy and breastfeeding status where applicable, recent illness, medication use, transfusion history, high-risk exposure assessment and screening for transfusion-transmissible infections.[18] In this study, these variables were self-reported and incomplete. The findings should therefore be interpreted as self-reported eligibility characteristics rather than actual donor eligibility. This distinction is important for ethical reporting and for safe donor recruitment. It also means that the proportion of students who appear eligible from questionnaire responses may differ from the proportion who would pass formal pre-donation screening.

Self-reported blood group findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although O-positive predominance is broadly consistent with Nigerian population patterns, the reported O-negative proportion (10.3%) appears higher than expected and requires laboratory confirmation. Errors may arise from recall, misunderstanding of Rhesus status, or data entry. Therefore, prospective donors should undergo standard blood grouping and screening by an authorised transfusion service. The low prevalence of previous blood donation also highlights an institutional opportunity. Regular campus blood drives, student donor clubs, peer ambassadors, social media campaigns, donor recognition and retention strategies could improve participation. As a health sciences university, DUFUHS can promote voluntary, non-remunerated donation as part of professional and public health responsibility.

These recommendations align with the national emphasis on voluntary, unpaid and regular donors as the safest donor group and with Nigerian donor guidance requiring donors to be healthy, aged 18-65 years, weigh 50 kg and above and undergo haemoglobin or fitness assessment before donation.[3] The focus should be on safe, voluntary, and repeat donation. Health education should address fears, explain donor screening, and provide supportive counselling for deferred students to encourage future participation.

The study provides institution-specific evidence on blood donation awareness and practices among DUFUHS undergraduates, supported by a good sample size, high response rate, and structured questionnaire. However, self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases, while eligibility and blood groups were not objectively verified. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and perceived social class was subjective. Overall, despite moderate awareness, low willingness and previous donation remain challenges, highlighting the need for sustained campus donation drives, peer-led education, social media engagement, and collaboration with authorised transfusion services.

Conclusion

Among DUFUHS undergraduates, awareness of blood donation was moderate, but willingness to donate and previous participation in blood donation drives were low. Several socio-demographic and educational variables were associated with previous donation practice, but they should not be interpreted as causal determinants. Self-reported eligibility characteristics should not be interpreted as clinical donor eligibility. The findings support structured campus-based blood donation drives, donor clubs, peer ambassador programmes, social media education and formal partnership with blood transfusion services. Future studies should include objective pre-donation screening, validated socio-economic status measurement and individual-level multivariable analysis to better define factors independently associated with willingness and donation practice.
