Bringing Everyone to the Table: An Experimental Study of LLM-Facilitated Group Decision Making DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX ISBN: 978-1-4503-XXXX-X/18/06
Mohammed Alsobay, David M. Rothschild, Jake M. Hofman, Daniel G. Goldstein

Abstract
. Group decision-making often suffers from uneven information sharing, hindering decision quality. While large language models (LLMs) have been widely studied as aids for individuals, their potential to support groups of users, potentially as facilitators, is relatively underexplored. We present a pre-registered randomized experiment with 1,475 participants assigned to 281 five-person groups completing a hidden profile task—selecting an optimal city for a hypothetical sporting event—under one of four facilitation conditions: no facilitation, a one-time message prompting information sharing, a human facilitator, or an LLM (GPT-4o) facilitator. We find that LLM facilitation increases information shared within a discussion by raising the minimum level of engagement with the task among group members, and that these gains come at limited cost in terms of participants’ attitudes towards the task, their group, or their facilitator. Whether by human or AI, there is no significant effect of facilitation on the final decision outcome, suggesting that even substantial but partial increases in information sharing are insufficient to overcome the hidden profile effect studied. To support further research into how LLM-based interfaces can support the future of collaborative decision making, we release our experimental platform, the Group-AI Interaction Laboratory (GRAIL), as an open-source tool.

Integrating LLMs as active participants in discussions offers the opportunity to steer conversations in real time. As facilitators, LLMs may excel in aspects of facilitation that would be taxing for their human counterparts, particularly in managing discussions that proceed at a high volume and rapid pace. Furthermore, LLMs operate without the social anxieties that frequently inhibit human facilitators from addressing necessary confrontation and other uncomfortable situations. In this direction, recent work has addressed the design principles underlying such active LLM-based interventions

We also contribute an open-source interface to facilitate the study of group-AI interaction in conversational tasks.

2.
Related Work

In this work, we study the impact of LLM facilitation on group decision making discussions. Our research is motivated by the well-documented biases and communication failures exhibited by groups in settings of asymmetric information, and builds upon previous work in the design of group-AI interaction and group decision support technologies. We contribute to these lines of research by complementing existing user studies and “in-silico” simulations with large-scale, pre-registered experimental evidence describing the impact of LLM facilitation on group behavior while completing a challenging hidden profile task.

2.1.
The Hidden Profile Task Paradigm of Group Decision-making Research

Since the seminal work of Stasser and Titus
(
Stasser1985-em
)
, hidden profile tasks (HPTs) have been used extensively to study the dynamics of group decision making
(
Stasser2003-kw
;
Lu2012-hh
;
Sohrab2015-bs
;
Reimer2010-no
;
Brodbeck2007-ae
)
. In an HPT, a group of decision makers is tasked with selecting one of several predefined options, with information about the options asymmetrically distributed among group members. Each group member receives a distinct information set (or “profile”) about the various options, typically delivered as a list of facts about each option. The visibility (public/shared/private) and polarity (positive/negative/neutral) of pieces of information and their distribution among group members is deliberately structured such that individually accessible information suggests one alternative as optimal, while the full information set would identify a different option as superior. This information asymmetry creates a decision scenario where optimal group performance requires effective information exchange and integration rather than simple majority voting or averaging of individual preferences.

One familiar with the literature on the "wisdom of the crowd"
(
Surowiecki2005-qv
)
and collective intelligence
(
Woolley_undated-rg
)
may intuitively expect groups to outperform individuals in decision making tasks, including HPTs. However, groups do not exhaustively share information, and instead sample which information to share, due to effort and time constraints
(
Stasser1985-em
)
. The distribution of information under the HPT induces two key information sharing biases that hinder group progress. In groups completing an HPT, shared information tends to be surfaced more frequently than private information (shared information bias
(
Nicholson2021-gk
;
Larson1996-zt
)
), and participants are more likely to share information that supports the option that is appealing under their personal information set (individual preference bias
(
Kelly1999-wk
;
Schulz-Hardt2016-og
)
). More broadly, while groups can exhibit synergy
(
Larson2010-mo
)
, collectively achieving better outcomes than any individual member could on their own, they can also experience social phenomena that affect their performance, such as social loafing
(
Jackson1985-fl
;
Simms2014-qp
;
Stieglitz2022-ts
;
Alnuaimi2010-mc
;
Liden2004-yr
;
Latane1979-kb
)
, groupthink
(
Baron2005-ff
)
, and communication apprehension
(
McCROSKEY1977-sm
;
Nicholson2021-gk
)
.

2.2.
Group-AI Interaction

Algorithms have been used to influence group behavior with the goal of overcoming detrimental group processes, ranging in sophistication from simple, noisy bots that improve coordination in networked groups
(
Christakis2008-xv
)
, to graph neural networks that rewire human networks to promote cooperation
(
McKee2023-hn
)
and variational autoencoders used to influence group dynamics in a hidden profile task
(
Pescetelli2021-nk
)
.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been explored as a tool to influence group dynamics and outcomes in a range of settings. For example, Tessler et al.’s LLM-based “Habermas Machine” iteratively generates consensus statements for social and political issues
(
Tessler2024-hq
)
, and Argyle et al. have used LLMs to suggest ways that messages can be rephrased to increase the sense of feeling understood in divisive conversations
(
Argyle2023-st
)
. In these applications, the LLM serves as a tool that provides input to the group in a highly structured format, rather than as an active participant in a group’s interactions. As Mao et al. and Do et al. emphasize, transitioning from structured forms of support to a free-form, multi-user chat setting expands the design space from
what
to say, to include the
when
and
who

(
Mao2024-tb
;
Do2022-am
)
. To address the complexity of group-AI interaction in chat-based settings, Mao et al. and Papachristou et al. introduce multi-component LLM systems in which each component tackles a single facet of interaction (e.g. timing, content, recipient selection, intent extraction, and coordination). Likely owing to the practical complexity of experiments involving simultaneous group interaction, these and other studies typically apply their systems to dyadic group settings, evaluate them in scenarios simulated with auxiliary LLMs, or study their acceptance in user studies of limited scale
(
Mao2024-tb
;
Papachristou2023-zg
;
Shamekhi2019-ht
;
Zhang2024-gi
;
He2024-vk
;
Kim2020-xm
)
. In a larger experiment, Chiang et al. study the effect of LLM-powered devil’s advocacy–a specific facilitative technique–on decision-making in groups of 2-3 members
(
Chiang2024-cz
)
. We build upon and extend this literature by conducting a pre-registered, large scale (281 groups), highly interactive (5-6 members per group) experiment studying the role of single LLMs as general purpose facilitators in hidden profile tasks, a setting that is particularly challenging due to its asymmetric and adversarial information landscape.

2.3.
Computational Approaches to Group Facilitation

Broadly put, the role of a facilitator is to enact process and content interventions that help a group achieve its objectives
(
Viller1991-uz
;
MirandaUnknown-ah
)
. One form of process-focused support is to impose structure on a group’s conversation by, for example, imposing an agenda for the discussion, or by managing specific formats of discussion such as the Delphi method and nominal group technique
(
Fink1984-bx
)
. Conversationally, effective facilitators typically fulfill their role through actions such as timekeeping, managing participation, resolving conflict, and synthesizing opinions.

In 1993, Phillips & Phillips wrote "the key functions of the facilitator, observing, attending, maintaining awareness of feelings, and intervening, cannot, of course, be carried out by computers…"
(
Phillips1993-fb
)
. Since then, computational approaches to these functions have arguably progressed to the level of practical utility, and a rich body of work in the CSCW and broader HCI communities on group decision support systems (GDSSs) and conversational agents (CAs) has explored technological solutions to enact the structural and conversational roles of the facilitator in group settings. For example, Kim et al. demonstrated the utility of CAs that interactively add structure (e.g., turn-taking) to group debates
(
Kim2021-qw
)
, while Do et al. explored how agents can sensitively provide feedback to underperforming group members
(
Do2022-am
)
, and Shamekhi et al. studied how embodiment influences the perceived rapport, trust, intelligence, power, and efficacy of facilitative agents in decision-making groups
(
Shamekhi2018-gs
;
Shamekhi2019-ht
)
.

LLMs may potentially allow us to easily conduct these facilitatory functions with greater fidelity and scale, and across a wide range of domains, democratizing access to facilitative experiences in the process. Professional facilitators, such as those found on
Find a Facilitator
, command daily fees in the range of $1,500-$3,000/day
1
1

1

, which may be out of reach for groups in a wide range of decision making settings. The comparatively low (and decreasing) marginal cost of LLM-based systems may provide a competitively effective, more affordable alternative.

3.
Methods

3.1.
Experiment Design & Implementation

In this pre-registered experiment (
AsPredicted #192061
), 1,475 participants were recruited from Prolific and randomly assigned into groups of five to complete a hidden profile task. Participants were compensated a flat amount of $3.75 for full completion of the study, and the median hourly compensation rate was $14.40/hr. The study was approved by the Microsoft Research Institutional Review Board (MSR IRB; Record #10883), and all participants provided explicit consent to participate in this study. For recruitment materials, exclusion criteria, and minor deviations from pre-registration, please see Appendices
A1
and
A2
.

3.1.1.
Facilitation

The focal manipulation of this study was the availability of facilitative support to assist groups during deliberation. Each group was randomly assigned to receive one of four facilitation treatments:

(1)

No facilitation
(“None”, N=70 groups), in which a group completed the task without any facilitative support.

(2)

A one-time message
(“Message”, N=71 groups), in which the group was shown a reminder that “People may have different information about what is being discussed in this meeting, so encourage everyone to share all of the relevant information they have.” This reminder was shown at the beginning of the group’s deliberation period, and disappeared once the discussion began. This active control condition, previously studied by Stasser et al.
(
Stasser1995-gs
;
Stasser2000-yo
)
, is akin to an educational poster hanging in a meeting room.

(3)

Human facilitation
(“Human”, N=70 groups), in which a sixth human participant was added to the group to act as a facilitator. This treatment is akin to an actual decision-making group tasking a random member to assume the role of facilitator.

(4)

LLM facilitation
(“LLM”, N=70 groups), in which an LLM (GPT-4o) acted as a facilitator.

Neither the human nor LLM facilitator was provided with information relevant to any of the options in the hidden profile task. Both facilitators were presented with the same core prompt to “first, make sure that everyone is heard from and shares what they know and, second, act as a scoreboard and keep track of pros and cons”, and were also given the reminder presented in the “Message” facilitation condition (in the "Human" and "LLM" conditions, the message was only provided to the facilitator, not the participants). If the group was assigned to have an LLM facilitator, the LLM sent a message to the group every 90 seconds, in addition to responding every time it was directly addressed by the group; each request sent to the LLM includes the full timestamped transcript preceding the request, as well as general information about meeting objectives, attendees, and time elapsed/remaining. For implementation details of each facilitative condition, including screenshots, please see Appendix
A3
. The implementation of the LLM facilitator and its prompt structure are further detailed in Appendix
A4
.

3.1.2.
The Hidden Profile Task

In this experiment’s hidden profile task, groups were tasked with selecting one of three fictional cities—Eldoron, Myloria, and Cragnio—to host an international sporting event akin to the Olympics. Each city was described by 10 different facts, and facts had five categories: weather, tourism, transportation, community, and a miscellaneous category for neutral information that did not plausibly influence the decision to host a sporting event. Each fact about a city had a positive (e.g., “friendly and welcoming residents”), negative (e.g., “higher than average humidity”), or neutral valence (e.g., “considered a hub for biotech in the region”), validated through a pre-experiment survey. The structure of the task, including group size, number of options, and information architecture, was modeled after the “Grogan Air” hidden profile task
(
AmesUnknown-vy
)
, with the content modified for the international sporting event context. For details of the hidden profile task design and valence measurement survey, please see Appendix
A5
.

Under full information and a unit-weight linear model (UWLM) of utility
(
Gigerenzer1996-tu
)
which assigns a score of 1 to positive facts, 0 to neutral facts, and -1 to negative facts, Eldoron is the most favorable option with a total score of 6, followed by Myloria with a score of 2, and Cragnio with a score of 0. However, each group member only had access to a subset of facts; facts could either be public (available to all group members), shared (available to multiple members), or private (available to only one member). To obscure the utility of Eldoron and create the "hidden" profile, the polarity (positive/negative/neutral) and visibility (public/shared/private) of the facts were manipulated such that Myloria was the most appealing option in each individual information set, as detailed in Table

. For the full list of facts and the distribution of information among the participants, please see Figure

3.1.3.
Experimental flow

During onboarding, participants are informed of the purpose and duration of the meeting, the size of their group, and the availability and type of facilitator supporting their group, if they have been assigned one. After reviewing the instructions and completing a brief comprehension check, each group proceeds through three stages of the experiment, illustrated in Figure
1
, using the chat interface illustrated in Figure
2
:

(1)

Icebreaker:
Groups begin by spending 1 minute introducing themselves to each other, using the same chat interface they will use for the main decision making task. During this stage, participants gain familiarity with the interface, allowing them to immediately begin the decision-making discussion in the next stage. Participants are not shown any task-relevant information during this stage. While human facilitators may participate in the icebreaker stage, the LLM facilitator does not.

(2)

Decision Making Discussion:
After the icebreaker stage, each participant is shown their personal information set, and the group is given 10 minutes to discuss the task. Groups must stay for the entirety of the 10 minutes. During this stage, facilitators in the "Human" and "LLM" conditions communicate with their group through the same chat window the group is using for their discussion. Instead of receiving task-specific information, as is shown to the five "decision making" participants, the human facilitator’s interface shows the facilitator’s instructions.

(3)

Post-task Survey:
After the decision making discussion, all participants (including human facilitators) complete an exit survey individually. They are asked to report the decision made by the group, as well as their subjective evaluations of the task (as measured by the NASA Task Load Index
(
Hart1988-rg
)
), their group, and their facilitator (if they were assigned one).

On the right half, the group communicates through a shared chat window; participants can use “@” tags to directly address others, including the facilitator.

Within each group, participants are randomly assigned colors ("Blue", "Pink", "Green", "Orange", "Red") as nicknames; across all groups, each color is associated with an information set (which includes all public information, and any shared or private information assigned to them), presented in "personal briefs". Through the interface shown in Figure

, participants can view their personal brief on the left side of the screen, and communicate through a chat window on the right side of the screen. The interface is configured to disable direct copying and pasting of the information presented in the personal brief, preserving the limitations on information sharing that make it necessary to choose which information to share, and preventing participants from easily sharing all available facts or copy-pasting the content into an external LLM to be summarized.

After completing the task, each participant individually fills out an exit survey. In this survey, they report which option their group had agreed upon and indicate how much they personally agreed with the group’s decision. They also provide their subjective attitudes toward their experience of the task itself, which are assessed using the NASA Task Load Index
(
Hart1988-rg
)
. Finally, participants are asked about their subjective attitudes toward their group, as well as their views on the facilitator, if one was present during the task. For screenshots of the introduction and exit survey, please refer to Appendix
A7
.

3.2.
Outcome Measures and Data Processing

Our pre-registered primary outcome is the volume of information covered within each group, operationalized as the total number of distinct facts mentioned during the group’s discussion. To quantify this outcome, each message sent by participants is annotated by GPT-4o to identify all directly or indirectly mentioned facts (for further details, please refer to Appendix
A6
). Exploratory analyses examine additional dimensions including the density and diversity of information sharing, as well as self-reported participant attitudes toward the task, their group, and, where applicable, their facilitator.

Given the sensitivity of the decision-making task to private information that may be lost to a group if a participant leaves the study, our pre-registered inclusion criteria are that each participant sends at least one message, the group collectively sends at least 10 messages, and that all participants submit the task completion code (displayed at the end of the study) on Prolific. Groups were sampled from Prolific until the pre-registered count of 70 valid groups per experimental condition was met; 7 groups in each of the “None”, “Message”, and “LLM” and 15 groups in the “Human” treatments failed to satisfy the inclusion criteria, and one extra group was sampled from the “Message” condition as a consequence of ensuring that groups could be assigned to any treatment with equal probability. For more details, please refer to Appendix
A2
.

4.
Results

4.1.
LLM facilitation increases information sharing in group discussion

Our primary, pre-registered analysis investigates how information “coverage”, defined as the number of distinct facts mentioned at least once throughout a group’s discussion (ranging from 0 to 30), varies with facilitation type. Our focus on information coverage is motivated by the fact that information aggregation is a necessary (albeit insufficient, as we discuss in Section
4.2
) condition for effective decision-making not only in our instantiation of the HPT, but across a wide range of HPT designs and group performance evaluation schemes
(
Mesmer-Magnus2009-fa
)
.

As illustrated in Figure

As pre-registered, these tests are one-sided, with sample size chosen based on pilot study data to achieve 70% power in detecting a difference of 2 facts shared at a significance level of 5%; all other analyses in this work are exploratory, and all subsequent tests are two-sided.

Despite LLM facilitators eliciting more facts from groups, we detect no significant difference in the average number of overall messages sent by the group members (excluding the facilitators) between treatments (F(3,277) = 0.46, p = 0.71), and find that LLM-facilitated discussions are more information dense than unfacilitated discussions on average, where density is defined as the ratio of covered facts to the number of non-facilitator messages in the discussion (

By the volume and length of their messages, human facilitators appear to adopt a more conversational style than their LLM counterparts. Human facilitators communicate more frequently, sending 9 messages to their group on average, while the LLM facilitator sends an average of 7 messages (by design, as detailed in Appendix

content
of the facilitator messages, we find that one potential mechanism driving the increase in information sharing is that of social pressure and task participation. In contrast to a human facilitator, an LLM may be more likely to directly engage individual group members (e.g., “Red, we haven’t heard from you…”), and these direct call-outs are observable by other team members, potentially increasing social pressure on the targeted group member to engage with the task and minimizing social loafing
(
Jackson1985-fl
)

On average, 25% of a human facilitator’s messages in a discussion directly address a group member, while LLM facilitators do so in 68% of the messages they share within a single discussion; while the LLM facilitator directly addressed a group member at least once in every group it interacted with, only 77% of human facilitators ever did so.

In parallel, LLM facilitation correlates with an increase in the minimum number of new facts (i.e., not previously covered in the group’s discussion) shared by any member of the group (
\Delta
= 0.41, 95% CI [0.145, 0.684], t(138) = 3.04, p = 0.003), but not the maximum number of new facts shared by an individual (as illustrated in Figures

all

\chi^{2}(3,N=281)
= 8.10, p=0.044). This complete participation is crucial in HPT settings of asymmetric information, as key facts may only be known to a single person.

4.2.
Does additional information improve decision making?

Defining a group’s collective choice as the option chosen by a majority, Eldoron (the optimal choice) was chosen by 31% of unfacilitated groups, 21% of groups presented with the one-time message, 30% of groups facilitated by a human, and 23% of groups facilitated by an LLM; however, these proportions are not statistically distinguishable from one another (
\chi^{2}(3,N=281)
= 2.85, p = 0.42). While unaffected by the facilitative treatments, these proportions of optimal outcomes are in line with those reported for the hidden profile task upon which ours is based
(
AmesUnknown-vy
)
. If groups strictly followed UWLM to make their decision (implying perfect perception of fact polarity, exact tabulation of scores based on covered information, and selecting the option with the highest score), Eldoron would have been chosen by 60% of unfacilitated groups, 48% of groups presented with the one-time message, 47% of groups facilitated by a human, and 63% of groups facilitated by an LLM.

To probe why actual group decisions diverged from the UWLM-optimal selections, we conducted a multinomial logistic regression of group choices on the options’ UWLM scores, calculated solely from information surfaced during each group’s discussion. With Myloria (the “bait”) as the baseline option, the odds of choosing Eldoron (the optimal option) increased as its score increased (
\beta=0.395
, 95% CI [0.245, 0.545], p
<
0.001) and decreased with increasing Myloria score (
\beta=-0.734
, 95% CI [-0.919, -0.548], p
<
0.001), while the score of Cragnio did not influence this contrast (
\beta=0.054
, 95% CI [-0.170, 0.279], p = 0.64). Similarly, the odds of choosing Cragnio increased as its score increased (
\beta=0.417
, 95% CI [0.136, 0.698], p = 0.004) and decreased with increasing Myloria score (
\beta=-0.649
, 95% CI [-0.843, -0.455], p
<
0.001), while the score of Eldoron did not influence this contrast (
\beta=-0.043
, 95% CI [-0.236, 0.150], p = 0.66). In both comparisons, the magnitude of the effect of Myloria’s score exceeded that of the alternative city by 86% (vs. Eldoron) and 56% (vs. Cragnio), suggesting that groups disproportionately weighted information about Myloria compared to the other cities. Although the overall directional pattern aligns with UWLM-based decision making, this asymmetric weighting could partially explain groups’ deviations from optimal UWLM choices. Conceptually, this finding is in agreement with Xiao et al.  who argue that hidden profile decision making is driven more by information
use
than
coverage

(
Xiao2016-dp
)
; as Dennis puts it, “you can lead a group to information, but you can’t make it think”
(
Dennis1996-ge
)
. While our design does not directly identify the mechanism underlying this effect, it may reflect an individual-level preference bias. Since each participant initially sees Myloria as the most appealing option in their personal information set, they may remain individually inclined to focus on Myloria in their
use
of the covered facts, thereby influencing group-level decision making.

4.3.
Perceptions of the task, group, and facilitator

As measured by the post-task survey, we find no differences between conditions in participants’ perceptions of the task (measured using the NASA Task Load Index
(
Hart1988-rg
)
, Figure
5
) or their group (Figure
6
[A-D]). When asked to evaluate their facilitator, the LLM was reported as more effective at both information elicitation and synthesis, though also more distracting than the human facilitator (Figure
6
[E-H]).

Participants were also asked, "You completed the task with [no, a human, an AI] facilitator. If you were to repeat the task, which type of facilitation would you prefer?", as illustrated in Figure
7
. Overall, participants in all conditions showed a strong preference for repeating their initial experience, suggesting that openness to AI-based facilitators may emerge primarily after direct exposure. However, an intriguing asymmetry emerged: those who experienced LLM facilitation expressed openness toward future facilitation of either type—human or AI—while participants who interacted with a human facilitator were notably less willing to consider AI facilitation. One possible explanation for this asymmetry is that participants under human facilitation may attribute their positive experience specifically to the skills or qualities of the human facilitator, while those facilitated by an LLM may attribute benefits more broadly—both to the LLM itself and to the practice of facilitation in general. If true, this speculated mechanism would imply that openness to AI-based facilitators may primarily emerge after direct exposure, highlighting the importance of firsthand experience in shaping attitudes toward AI facilitation.

Together, these results suggest that the gains in information sharing arising from LLM facilitation come at limited cost in terms of subjective experience, at least as operationalized by our various survey instruments.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
What value did LLM facilitation add to the deliberative process?

Through our experiment, we demonstrated that LLM facilitators were able to elicit more facts from the groups they supported and that groups appreciated the LLM’s synthesis of information, with limited impact on how group members perceived the task or their groups. However, despite the additional information that groups were able to share, the likelihood of choosing the optimal choice was unaffected.

One possibility is that time spent sharing additional information came at the expense of time that would be spent discussing and analyzing the aggregated information. A second possibility is that the additional information surfaced is not useful in resolving the hidden profile. A third possibility is that the decision-making biases in effect are too overwhelming to overcome with an additional 2-3 more facts on average; as illustrated in our analysis of the effect of UWLM scores on decisions made, participant judgment may have been skewed towards Myloria. Finally, we deliberately avoided prompting the LLM to provide a decision, to preserve its role as an aid and maximize the group’s sense of ownership over the decision; an LLM prompted to more directly influence the decision-making process could potentially mitigate these biases by explicitly highlighting critical information, or possibly introduce new biases by making direct recommendations based on surfaced information.

5.2.
Designing more effective LLM facilitators

The design of our LLM facilitator was intentionally simple: it interjected with a message to the whole group on a fixed 90 second interval, with no information other than the group transcript and generic instructions to gather and summarize information. Through this basic design, we demonstrate the potential of domain-agnostic, low-cost solutions that could inspire the development of more specialized systems.

Abstractly, the advantages of LLMs in facilitative settings can be classified into “substrate” advantages, those that are attributable to an algorithm/computer’s ability to process text at high volume and speed, and “analytical & behavioral” advantages, such as recognizing when participants have been quiet (overcoming human cognitive biases such as omission neglect
(
Kardes2006-rc
)
) or intervening without experiencing social discomfort. Under this conceptual framework, LLM facilitators can be designed to more acutely seize these advantages. For instance, beyond addressing the entire group simultaneously, facilitative systems could also engage participants individually. This capability would allow the facilitator to privately deliver feedback (as studied by Do et al.
(
Do2022-am
)
), discuss participants’ concerns and motivations, or deliver tailored messages to each individual to align them with the group, at scale. Additionally, adopting a modular approach, such as the MUCA framework
(
Mao2024-tb
)
, could facilitate the integration of specialized LLMs with distinct emphases—for instance, emotional intelligence or content analysis—that communicate internally to synthesize and deliver comprehensive facilitation. Further, LLMs in facilitative settings can be given more detail about the exact objective of the meeting, and provided structure on how best to accomplish those specific goals; owing to their interactivity and broad knowledge bases, LLMs can also be configured to adapt to a group’s
changing
objectives, modifying their facilitative strategy to match the trajectory of the meeting.

5.3.
The need for a systematic exploration of group-AI interaction and task design space

While our study demonstrates noteworthy dynamics of group-AI interaction and the promise of LLMs as facilitators in group discussions, our results are likely contingent on the design of our experiment. Beyond the implementation of the LLM facilitator and its interface, as discussed in Section
5.2
, contextual factors such as time pressure
(
Kelly1999-wk
;
Kelly2004-dj
)
, group size
(
Cruz1997-dy
)
, and communication modality (chat, video, audio, face-to-face)
(
Shirani2006-rk
;
Lam2000-pr
;
Kerr2009-ws
)
may significantly influence the efficacy of LLM facilitation.

The characteristics of the hidden profile task itself further underscore the need for systematic exploration. Key task-related factors include the domain (e.g., neutral settings such as fictional cities vs. potentially contentious topics)
(
Manata2019-ix
)
, the total information load
(
Stasser1987-cy
)
, and the difficulty of discerning the hidden optimal option
(
Schulz-Hardt2006-os
;
Brodbeck2002-in
)
. More fundamentally, our hidden profile setting is a collaborative one; as previous research has noted, the assumptions underlying our understanding of its group dynamics (e.g. cooperation vs competition, unbiased and objective sharing of information, etc.) may not extend to other settings
(
Wittenbaum2004-oe
;
Toma2009-qm
)
. In more discordant settings, Tessler et al. demonstrate the ability of algorithmic support to foster post-hoc alignment of divergent group perspectives through static statements
(
Tessler2024-hq
)
; interactive LLM-based systems may complement such approaches by steering discussions towards consensus in real time.

In sum, the density of factors influencing LLM-facilitated group interactions necessitate a systematic and integrative approach to future research
(
Almaatouq2022-ml
)
. By carefully examining these dimensions simultaneously, the field can advance towards more robust and generalizable insights into effective group-AI facilitation.

5.4.
Open data and open-source software to support group-AI interaction research

The hidden profile task paradigm is specifically designed to expose challenges in group decision-making, highlighting how biases in information sharing and discussion can prevent groups from effectively utilizing distributed knowledge, even when extensive information pooling occurs. Given the ubiquity of similar scenarios in real-world group decision-making contexts, developing facilitative technologies that mitigate such failures is a challenge of practical importance.

Our dataset, comprised of 14,343 messages shared by 1,475 participants across 281 groups, provides a rich resource for researchers exploring facilitative technologies and group decision-making. Alongside the transcripts, we provide all LLM requests, complete responses with LLM-generated rationales, and related metadata, enabling researchers to conveniently perform post-hoc analyses or simulate alternative facilitation prompts.

In addition to this dataset, we introduce the Group-AI Interaction Laboratory (GRAIL), our open-source experimental platform based on the Empirica framework
(
Almaatouq2021-cs
)
. By providing connectivity to an LLM and text files containing participant-specific stimuli, researchers can use our framework to explore questions of group-AI interaction in a way that grants them flexibility in the design of the task and the details of the LLM’s interactions with the group while relieving them of the technical difficulties of conducting highly-interactive behavioral experiments with groups of simultaneous participants.

6.
Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the practical capacity of LLMs to
actively
support group decision making discussions by directly engaging group members and prompting participation as a discussion evolves, as well as effectively summarizing rapid, information-dense communication. By raising the minimum level of individual task engagement within groups, the LLM facilitator significantly increased both the breadth of information at a group’s disposal, and the likelihood that
all
group members were heard from—an outcome that is critical in settings with asymmetric information, where a single member’s private knowledge may be pivotal.

Decision-making, whether performed individually or collaboratively, is a complex, multi-stage process. Although the increased information sharing observed did not translate directly into better final decisions in our experiment, this outcome highlights the inherent complexity and context dependence of group decision-making. It also underscores the potential value of integrating computational support that focuses distinctly on each stage of the deliberative process, from initial information gathering through to evaluation and consensus formation.

By encouraging broader participation within groups, the LLM facilitator helps groups consider a wider range of perspectives, particularly from group members who might otherwise remain passive. In practical scenarios analogous to hidden profile tasks—such as hiring committees—this form of facilitation may play an important role in mitigating biases through promoting conversational equity. Moreover, continued development of computational facilitation technologies could democratize access to these organizational techniques, making them available to communities unable to afford professional facilitation services.

Our work builds upon an extensive body of research within the CSCW and broader HCI communities studying the effectiveness of group decision support systems, which have historically incorporated a wide range of computational methods. The novel affordances of LLMs present an opportunity to increase the fidelity and adaptivity of these systems, and it is our hope that the robust empirical evidence of this potential that we have presented here inspires future work in this direction, and that our open-source experimental platform enables researchers to systematically explore and extend these potential benefits.

A4.
Facilitator LLM Prompting and Prompt Formatting

To implement the LLM facilitation condition, we configured the experimental platform to automatically query GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) every 90 seconds during the 10-minute decision-making task (i.e., at the 1.5, 3, 4.5, 6, 7.5, and 9 minute marks, for a total of 6 queries over the duration of the task). A5.
Hidden Profile Task Design

The hidden profile task used in this study was a modified version of the “Grogan Air” team decision exercise developed by Prof. Daniel Ames at Columbia Business School
[
59
]
, in which groups of 5 members review information about 3 potential candidates for an executive role at a fictional airline. The structure of the individual information sets was preserved (i.e., the polarity and visibility of facts), while the content of the facts in the information set was changed to describe fictional candidate cities to host an international sporting event akin to the Olympics. To direct groups’ focus to the facts at hand, participants were informed that “all three cities are equally qualified and capable of hosting the event from a sports infrastructure perspective, but may differ in non-critical ways” at the beginning of their individual reports.

As in “Grogan Air”, there is a total of 30 facts, with 10 facts describing each of the three options (Eldoron, Myloria, and Cragnio) with each individual group member’s information set including a subset of 14-15 facts. Figure
A5
shows the content of each city’s fact set, and the distribution of the facts among each of the group’s 5 members (referred to as Pink, Red, Orange, Blue, and Green).

Figure A5.
Hidden profile task information distribution:
Each of the 3 candidate cities is described by 10 facts. In turn, each fact is defined by its polarity (red for negative, grey for neutral, and green for positive) and visibility, indicated by the check marks showing which group members (Pink, Red, Orange, Blue, Green) it is visible to.

Following the structure of “Grogan Air”, we aimed to phrase facts that would be widely perceived as negatively or positively affecting a city’s ability to host such an event, as well as facts that would not plausibly affect a host city’s eligibility. To validate whether these facts were perceived as intended, 119 Prolific participants (who did not participate in the main experiment) were randomly assigned to one of the five information sets, presented with the same instructions provided in the main experiment, and asked to rate each fact on a Likert scale of 1 (“very negative”) to 7 (“very positive”), with 4 labeled as “Neutral” (see Figure

As shown in Figure

, facts about the cities were generally perceived as intended, with the exception of some neutral facts leaning towards positive. Out of 30 facts, 25 were rated as “dealbreakers” by less than 5% of respondents, with the most “dealbreaking” fact being “In the past, residents have complained about noise from large events”, as rated by 17% of respondents; in aggregate, these results suggest the absence of overwhelmingly influential facts.

A6.
Using an auxiliary LLM to detect fact mentions in group discussion

The hidden profile task used in the study includes a total of 30 facts. To detect when these facts are mentioned in a group’s discussion, we prompt GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) with instructions for the fact detection task and a list of all 30 facts (as shown in Appendix
A6.1
below). Each group discussion is processed as a single transcript, rather than processing each message individually, to allow the LLM to detect implicit references, e.g., saying "yeah but the parking there is pretty bad" in response to an earlier mention of Cragnio.

The LLM’s fact detection performance was validated against a set of 5 randomly selected group transcripts manually labeled with mentioned facts by the authors; this validation set included a total of 272 messages, 177 of which included no facts, 70 of which included 1 fact, and 25 of which included more than 1 fact. The lists of facts detected by the humans and LLM annotators match exactly in 95% of the 272 test cases; all 14 cases in which the annotators differed involved a single fact that the human annotator detected as an implicit mention, while the LLM annotator did not. In general, these findings suggest that the LLM fact detection measure works acceptably well; any peculiarities in its performance would likely affect conversations across the different experimental treatments similarly, and thus not affect the differences we observe between treatments.
