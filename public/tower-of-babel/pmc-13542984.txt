Enrollment in a General Nutrition Course Is Associated With Higher Nutrition Knowledge Scores Among University Students
Authors: Hadia Radwan, Hayder Hasan, Dana N Abdelrahim, Falak Zeb, MoezAlIslam E Faris, Tareq Osaili, Mona Hashim, Leila Cheikh Ismail, Reyad Shaker Obaid, Salma Abu‐Qiyas, Dimitrios Papandreou, Nadia Alkalbani, Farah Naja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and MethodsThis study examined the association between completion of a general nutrition course (GNC) and nutrition knowledge among university students in the United Arab Emirates (UAE). A cross‐sectional online survey was conducted at the University of Sharjah. Nutrition knowledge was compared between students who completed the GNC (GNC group, n = 249) and those who did not (non‐GNC group, n = 561). The elective course comprised three modules covering macronutrients and micronutrients, selected nutrition topics, and common nutrition‐related diseases. At the end of the academic semester, participants completed the validated 88‐item General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire‐Revised (GNKQ‐R), which assesses dietary recommendations, food groups and nutrient sources, healthy food choices, and diet‐related diseases and weight management. Each correct response was awarded 1 point, for a maximum score of 88.ResultsOverall, 16.4% of participants demonstrated good nutrition knowledge (≥ 64/88). This proportion was higher in the GNC group than in the non‐GNC group (26.5% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.001). The GNC group also had a higher mean total knowledge score (55.19 ± 12.50 vs. 52.55 ± 9.75, p = 0.001; Cohen's d = 0.25), with differences observed in three of the four GNKQ‐R sections. In the full multivariable model, GNC completion, high Body Mass Index (BMI), enrollment in medical or health‐related majors, and more positive self‐rated health were associated with higher total scores.ConclusionsCompletion of a general nutrition course was associated with higher nutrition knowledge among university students; however, the magnitude of the difference was modest and overall knowledge levels remained low. Longitudinal and intervention‐based studies are needed to assess knowledge retention and whether knowledge differences translate into lifelong dietary behavior change.Keywords: diet‐disease relationship, dietary recommendations, general nutrition course, nutrition education, nutrition knowledge

Introduction

Nutrition‐related knowledge is a key determinant of healthy eating practices and weight management [1]. Higher nutrition knowledge has been linked to healthier food choices and a lower risk of nutrition‐related diseases [2]. The Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change proposes that behavior change can be promoted by strengthening individuals' knowledge and beliefs [3]. Therefore, nutrition education plays a central role in improving dietary behaviors and promoting long‐term health [4].

A growing body of evidence suggests a positive correlation between nutritional knowledge and healthier eating habits. For example, university students in Kuwait and Japan with higher nutrition knowledge reported greater consumption of vegetables and lower intake of fast foods [5, 6]. Similarly, school and university‐based nutritional educational programs have been shown to improve students' food choices and eating behaviors [7, 8]. However, the extent of the relationship between knowledge and dietary behavior varies depending on demographic characteristics, program quality, and contextual influences [8, 9]. In addition, knowledge alone does not always translate into behavior change, as environment and cultural factors often play critical roles [10, 11].

University students represent a key group for nutritional education because they are at a transitional stage where lifelong dietary habits are formed. During this period, academic pressure, limited time, peer influence, irregular sleep, and the easy availability of fast foods often shape students’ eating behaviors [12, 13]. Such behaviors are marked by meal skipping, frequent fast‐food consumption, low physical activity, and low intake of fruits and vegetables, which together could potentially increase the risk of nutrition‐related diseases [14, 15]. The eating habits prevalent among university students underscore the importance of nutrition education and the potential for improved dietary habits in this age group.

General Nutrition Courses (GNCs) have shown potential in enhancing students' understanding of nutrition concepts. Research from different regions, including Turkey and Canada, has reported that undergraduates who completed a GNC achieved higher knowledge scores and relied more on credible nutrition information sources [16, 17]. However, despite evidence of the benefits of such courses, many studies still find that university students lack sufficient knowledge about key nutrition topics, such as fruit and vegetable intake, dietary fat quality, and food labeling, highlighting the need for better educational approaches [18].

In the UAE, university students commonly report irregular meal patterns, low fruit and vegetable intake, and frequent consumption of fast food and sugar‐sweetened beverages [19]. These findings support the need to examine structured nutrition education within higher education. To our knowledge, evidence examining the association between completion of a general nutrition course and nutrition knowledge among university students in the UAE remains limited. Therefore, this study examined the association between completion of a GNC and nutrition knowledge among students at the University of Sharjah. We hypothesized that students who completed the GNC would have higher nutrition knowledge scores than those who did not. This study is among the first in the Gulf region to examine the association between a structured general nutrition course and nutrition knowledge in a large, multidisciplinary university population. It also identifies sections in which knowledge gaps persisted despite course exposure.

Methodology

Study Design

A cross‐sectional design was used to assess nutrition knowledge among students at the University of Sharjah, which offers health‐related programs (e.g., Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacy, and Health Sciences) and non‐health‐related programs (e.g., Engineering, Business, Arts, and Social Sciences). The study compared students who completed the GNC during the spring 2021–2022 semester (GNC group, n = 249) with students enrolled in other courses who had not previously completed the GNC (non‐GNC group, n = 561). Data were collected in May 2022, shortly after GNC students completed the course and during the final‐examination preparation period. No predefined retention interval was included; therefore, the assessment reflects knowledge shortly after course completion. Ethical approval was granted by the University Research Ethics Committee (REC‐21‐04‐28‐01‐S), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sampling

The questionnaire link was distributed through the university email system to all registered undergraduate students during the study semester. Eligible participants were undergraduate students aged 18 years or older who could read English or Arabic and provided electronic informed consent. Students majoring in Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics were excluded because they received advanced nutrition education. For the non‐GNC comparison group, students who had previously completed the GNC were also excluded. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded. Participation was voluntary and anonymous.

Description of the General Nutrition Course

The GNC is an elective course offered in English and Arabic to first‐year students from health‐related and non‐health‐related majors, except students in the Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics program. The course comprises 15 lectures organized into three modules. The first module (seven lectures) addresses macronutrient and micronutrient functions, deficiency signs and symptoms, dietary requirements, and food sources. The second module (four lectures) covers dietary guidelines, food safety, food labeling, and physical fitness. The third module (four lectures) addresses common nutrition‐related diseases, including their pathophysiology, risk factors, and dietary management.

Data Collection Tool

Nutrition knowledge was assessed using the validated 88‐item General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire‐Revised (GNKQ‐R) [20, 21], administered as a self‐completed online questionnaire through Google Forms. Before data collection, the questionnaire was translated into Arabic and back‐translated into English by two bilingual experts. The original and back‐translated versions were compared for consistency. A panel of five academic nutritionists reviewed the instrument for content validity, clarity, and cultural appropriateness, and a pilot test with 30 students assessed face validity and cultural suitability. Internal consistency in the study sample was acceptable (Cronbach's α = 0.87 overall; 0.74–0.82 across sections).

The GNKQ‐R comprises four sections: (I) dietary recommendations (18 items); (II) food groups and nutrient sources (36 items); (III) healthy food choices (13 items); and (IV) diet‐related diseases and weight management (21 items). Each correct response received one point, for a maximum score of 88. Scores ≥ 64/88 (72.7%) were classified as good knowledge, consistent with previous GNKQ‐R studies and to facilitate comparison with earlier populations [20, 21].

The questionnaire also included sociodemographic information (sex, marital status, parental status, nationality, academic major, self‐rated health). Participants were asked to report their weight and height. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2) and classified according to World Health Organization criteria as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5‐24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0‐29.9 kg/m2), or obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [22].

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± standard deviations. Independent‐samples t tests and chi‐square tests were used for between‐group comparisons. Cohen's d was calculated for continuous‐score comparisons. For the total score and each GNKQ‐R section, a full multivariable linear regression model was specified a priori, with GNC completion, sex, BMI category, self‐rated health, marital status, parental status, nationality, and academic major entered simultaneously, irrespective of univariable significance. Unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), p values, and variance inflation factors (VIFs) are reported. Residual normality was assessed using Q‐Q plots and the Shapiro‐Wilk test, and constant variance was assessed using residual‐versus‐fitted‐value plots. Because mild heteroscedasticity was observed, final inferences were based on heteroscedasticity‐robust (HC3) standard errors. For the 88 item‐level comparisons, a Bonferroni‐adjusted threshold of p < 0.00057 (0.05/88) was applied. Item‐level associations with nominal p < 0.05 that did not meet the adjusted threshold were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. Statistical significance for the primary total and section analyzes was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 810 students participated in the study, including 249 (30.7%) in the GNC group and 561 (69.3%) in the non‐GNC group. Of the 3200 eligible students, 810 completed responses were received, yielding a response rate of 25.3%. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. The majority of the participants were females (75.9%), Arab nationals (93.3%), single (93.6%), and either under or normal weight (70.6%). Approximately two‐thirds (67.4%) were enrolled in health‐related majors. A significantly higher percentage of GNC participants were from non‐health‐related majors compared with the non‐GNC group (39.4% vs. 29.6%, p = 0.004). No other significant sociodemographic differences were observed between the groups.

Table 2 presents the mean nutrition knowledge scores, standardized GNKQ‐R scores, and standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d) for the total score and each GNKQ‐R section for the total sample and the GNC group (n = 249) and the non‐GNC group (n = 561). The total mean ± SD nutrition knowledge score was 53.36 ± 10.73 out of 88, equivalent to 60.64 ± 12.19 on a standardized 0‐100 scale. Students in the GNC group had a significantly higher mean ± SD total score than those in the non‐GNC group (55.19 ± 12.50 vs. 52.55 ± 9.75 out of 88; 62.72 ± 14.20 vs. 59.72 ± 11.07 on the 0–100 scale; p = 0.001), representing an absolute difference of 2.64 points out of 88. However, the effect size was small (Cohen's d = 0.25). Subsection analyzes demonstrated that the GNC group scored significantly higher mean ± SD scores compared with the non‐GNC group across three out of four sections of the GNKQ‐R questionnaire. Specifically, higher scores were observed in Section 1: Dietary Recommendations (12.21 ± 2.30 vs. 11.47 ± 2.09, out of 88; 67.83 ± 12.78 vs. 63.72 ± 11.61 on the 0‐100 scale; p = 0.020), Section 2: Food Groups and Nutrient Sources (22.42 ± 4.96 vs. 21.35 ± 4.29 out of 88; 62.28 ± 13.78 vs. 59.31 ± 11.92 on the 0–100 scale; p = 0.017), and Section 4: Diet‐Related Diseases and Weight Management (13.29 ± 4.61 vs. 12.55 ± 3.77 out of 88; 63.29 ± 21.95 vs. 59.76 ± 17.95 on the 0‐100 scale; p = 0.016). Despite statistical significance, small effect sizes were observed for dietary recommendations (d = 0.34) and food groups (d = 0.24), whereas the effect size for diet‐related diseases was very small (d = 0.18). In contrast, the difference in Section 3: Healthy Food Choices was negligible (7.27 ± 3.07 vs. 7.17 ± 2.51 out of 88; 55.92 ± 23.62 vs. 55.15 ± 19.31 on the 0–100 scale; p = 0.016; d = 0.04) and did not reach statistical significance. Thus, while GNC completion was consistently associated with higher continuous knowledge scores, the practical magnitude of these section‐level differences remained modest.

Table 2 also presents the proportion of students classified as having “good” versus “poor” nutrition knowledge (n = 810). Only 16.4% (n = 133) of participants met the threshold for good knowledge (≥ 64/88), whereas the vast majority (83.6%, n = 677) were classified as having poor knowledge (< 64/88). The prevalence of good knowledge was significantly higher among participants in the GNC group than in the non‐GNC group (26.5%, n = 66 vs. 11.9%, n = 67; p < 0.001). Participants who completed the GNC were more than two and a half times as likely to attain the good‐knowledge threshold compared with those who had not taken the course (unadjusted OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.82–3.89).

Table 3 presents the significant item‐level results of the correct responses between groups. Significant between‐group differences were identified for 27 of the 88 questionnaire items (p < 0.05). In the dietary recommendations section, the GNC students compared with the non‐GNC students more accurately identified the need to increase fruit intake (87.6% vs. 78.3%, p = 0.001), the importance of reducing processed red meat (79.5% vs. 65.6%, p = 0.001), the selection of wholegrain and high‐fiber foods as preferred options (63.9% vs. 48.0%, p = 0.001), and correctly identified the “five‐a‐day” fruit and vegetable recommendation more frequently (18.5% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.025). In addition, the GNC students correctly identified that unsaturated fats should not be reduced (83.1% vs. 70.8%, p = 0.001), that trans fats should be reduced (54.2% vs. 34.4%, p = 0.001), and that reduced‐fat dairy products are the recommended choice (56.6% vs. 46.9%, p = 0.010).

Table 4 displays the multivariate linear regression models examining factors associated with total nutrition knowledge and individual GNKQ‐R section scores. Course completion (GNC group) was independently associated with a higher total nutrition knowledge score (B = 2.91, β = 0.128, 95% CI: 1.18–4.64, p = 0.001). GNC completion was also positively associated with dietary recommendations (B = 0.71, β = 0.151, 95% CI: 0.35–1.06, p < 0.001), food groups and nutrient sources (B = 1.17, β = 0.121, 95% CI: 0.46–1.88, p = 0.001), and diet‐related diseases and weight management (B = 0.90, β = 0.105, 95% CI: 0.26–1.55, p = 0.006). No significant association was observed between GNC completion and healthy food choices (B = 0.13, β = 0.026, 95% CI: −0.31 to 0.56, p = 0.567).

Discussion

This study examined the association between GNC completion and nutrition knowledge among university students in the UAE. The results showed students who completed the GNC had higher total knowledge scores and higher scores in three of the four GNKQ‐R sections than those who did not take the course. Although the overall proportion of students with “good knowledge” was modest, the observed differences highlight the importance of structured nutrition education in the university setting. The findings of this study are consistent with international research reporting higher nutrition knowledge following formal nutrition education. A systematic review of 18 studies found that nutrition education was associated with higher knowledge among medical students and residents [23]. Similarly, studies in the United Kingdom and the United States have reported significant gains in knowledge with higher GNKQ scores following structured nutrition courses [20, 24]. Other studies reported comparable results where university students in the in the Netherlands and Iran exposed to targeted nutrition courses increased their ability to interpret dietary information and recognize credible nutrition sources [25, 26]. Together, these studies support the growing evidence that structured educational interventions can contribute to better nutrition knowledge among university students.

Despite the positive association observed, this study also identified persistent gaps in participants’ knowledge of specific nutrition concepts. Even among students who completed the GNC, a considerable proportion were unable to correctly identify recommendations related to fruit and vegetable intake, dietary fat quality, and fiber consumption. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that university students and young adults and university students, frequently demonstrate inadequate knowledge of dietary guidelines, particularly regarding recommended fruit and vegetable intake, the health effects and recommendations for different types of dietary fats, and the role of dietary fiber in a healthy diet [17, 20, 27, 28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that nutrition education may benefit from incorporating practical and interactive teaching strategies that help students apply nutrition knowledge in everyday settings, including food preparation, meal planning, interpretation of nutrition labels, and healthier food selection [29, 30].

It should also be noted that the GNKQ‐R combines items reflecting broad public nutrition‐literacy items (e.g., the recommendation to increase fruit intake) with more technical, course‐adjacent items (e.g., the predominant fatty acid in olive oil, calcium equivalence in skimmed and whole milk, glycemic‐index classification, and trans‐fat sources that are more directly taught within the GNC curriculum). Differences on broad‐literacy items may be more generalizable, whereas differences on technical items may partly reflect direct recall of material taught in the GNC. This construct overlap should be considered when interpreting the educational significance of the findings. Future studies should ideally classify GNKQ‐R items into course‐aligned and general‐literacy items a priori to disentangle these two forms of knowledge.

An interesting finding in this study was that high BMI and more positive self‐rated health were independently associated with higher total knowledge scores. Individuals with a higher BMI may have greater prior exposure to weight‐management counseling or nutrition information [31, 32]. Self‐rated health perception was also associated with higher nutrition knowledge in this study. This finding is consistent with studies showing that individuals who perceive their health positively tend to have better health literacy and engage in healthier behaviors [33]. Students in health‐related majors also had higher knowledge scores, likely due to both curriculum exposure and increased interest in health‐related topics. However, the benefits of GNC are not limited to health science students; exposure to basic nutrition education can equip non‐health majors with essential skills for personal well‐being and informed food decision‐making [34].

The findings have potential implications for higher education, but they do not establish that curriculum integration will change dietary behavior or health outcomes. Universities considering nutrition education may evaluate whether a full elective course, shorter embedded modules, or blended delivery is most appropriate for their student population and resources.

From a policy and implementation perspective, despite reaching statistical significance, the modest absolute difference in mean scores indicates that a single elective course, while beneficial, is insufficient on its own to bring most students to a level of ‘good’ nutrition knowledge. Our findings indicate that gains were concentrated in specific sections (dietary recommendations, food groups and nutrient sources, and diet‐disease relationships) rather than uniformly across all content areas. Curriculum designers may therefore consider strengthening applied, skills‐based content in areas where gaps persisted, including food‐label interpretation, portion estimation, and meal planning, where knowledge gaps were most persistent even among GNC completers, rather than assuming that broad topic coverage alone will close these gaps. These approaches should be tested rather than assumed to be effective.

From a clinical and student health services perspective, campus health services and university dietitians could use the identified gaps to develop targeted educational messages on fruit and vegetable recommendations, dietary fat quality, and fiber intake for students across all majors.

Future research should use longitudinal or pre‐post designs that track the same students before and after course completion and include an appropriate retention interval, assess dietary behavior and food‐purchasing outcomes, and measure informal nutrition‐information exposure so that the independent contribution of formal coursework can be evaluated more clearly.

Strengths and Limitations

This study included a relatively large, multidisciplinary student sample and used the validated GNKQ‐R, allowing comparison with international research. Moreover, the study is among the first in the UAE to evaluate the association of a structured GNC with student knowledge, providing culturally relevant insights. However, the findings should be interpreted in light of important limitations. The non‐random convenience sample and 25.3% response rate create the possibility of selection and nonresponse bias, which inherently limits the direct generalizability of the findings to the broader student population. Moreover, female participants were overrepresented, and institutional registrar data were not available to formally compare respondents with the full eligible student population. Although the GNC and non‐GNC groups were similar on most measured characteristics, they differed in academic‐major distribution. The cross‐sectional, non‐randomized design also precludes causal inference, and adjustment for measured covariates cannot eliminate confounding from pre‐existing interest, motivation, or prior nutrition exposure. Hence, the results should not be interpreted as evidence that the GNC caused the observed differences.

Moreover, the online, unproctored administration of the GNKQ‐R means that some participants may have consulted external sources while answering and may have affected measured scores or group differences. Furthermore, the questionnaire did not capture students’ exposure to nutrition information from informal sources outside the course setting (e.g., social media, healthcare providers, family, fitness influencers), which may be differentially distributed between GNC and non‐GNC students. In addition, data in this study were self‐reported and therefore could be subject to recall or social desirability bias; specifically, BMI was calculated from self‐reported height and weight rather than direct measurements.

The ≥ 64/88 threshold was retained to facilitate comparison with earlier population studies using GNKQ‐R. However, dichotomization at a single cutoff can oversimplify a continuous construct and obscure meaningful variation within the “poor knowledge” group.

So residual diagnostics indicated modest departures from normality (Shapiro‐Wilk W = 0.961–0.980, all p < 0.001) and mild heteroscedasticity. VIFs were below 2.30. Final regression inferences were therefore based on HC3 robust standard errors, and the substantive pattern of associations was unchanged. Finally, the large number of item‐level comparisons increases the risk of Type I error; the Bonferroni correction reduced this risk, and unadjusted item‐level findings are interpreted as exploratory.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that completion of a general nutrition course was associated with higher nutrition knowledge among university students in the UAE, but the magnitude of the difference was modest and overall knowledge levels remained low. In the full multivariable analysis, GNC completion, higher BMI, enrollment in medical or health‐related majors, and more positive self‐rated health were associated with higher total scores. Despite these encouraging associations, overall knowledge levels remained moderate, with significant gaps in key areas such as fruit and vegetable intake, dietary fiber, and fat quality. These findings support further investigation into structured, evidence‐based nutrition education in higher education curricula, not just for students in health‐related fields but for all undergraduates. As this study assessed only knowledge scores at a single time point and did not evaluate dietary behavior, long‐term knowledge retention, or health outcomes, these implications cannot be inferred and should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating. Longitudinal and intervention‐based studies are needed to determine whether structured nutrition education produces long‐lasting dietary behavior changes.
