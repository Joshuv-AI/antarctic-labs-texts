Early secondary outcome analysis in multi-phase randomised-controlled trials, a pragmatic approach: the Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI)
Authors: Kalyanaraman Kumaran, Jean-Patrice Baillargeon, Catherine S Birken, Cindy-Lee Dennis, Jianxia Fan, William D Fraser, Hefeng Huang, Rayjean J Hung, Kumar Gavali Suryanarayana, Stephen J Lye, Shane A Norris, Stephen G Matthews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe extended time frame of some trials can cause significant challenges in delaying all analyses until the primary outcome is attained. This may particularly affect any mechanistic studies linked to secondary outcomes, as well as impact the involvement of trainees and junior investigators in the project. This manuscript describes the strategy and process by which the leadership of the Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI) developed an analysis approach.MethodsThe study involved the engagement of an expert panel with broad knowledge in disciplines relevant to the study.ResultsSeveral reviewers emphasised the importance of undertaking analysis only after the primary outcome has been achieved. However, the majority of reviewers highlighted that this was not practical in the context of the HeLTI consortium because valuable interim analysis would be missed.ConclusionAfter careful consideration of the issue and feedback received, the HeLTI leadership agreed to an approach that allowed publishing between-group comparisons of secondary outcomes and linked mechanistic studies before the primary outcome, using an a priori agreed phased approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-026-09737-w.Keywords: Clinical trials, Clinical trial methodology, Trial analysis

Background

The long-term nature of some studies, particularly randomised or non-randomised trials, causes challenges in withholding all analyses until the primary outcome is reached. This may especially impact mechanistic studies linked to secondary outcomes. This manuscript discusses the data analysis approach that will be applied to the trials as part of the international Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI) consortium and the process involved in selecting this approach.

The healthy life trajectories initiative

The Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI) was established in 2017 through a unique collaboration between national funding agencies in Canada, China, India, South Africa and the World Health Organization (WHO). The initiative is founded on a developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) concept and aims to evaluate the effect of an integrated 4-phase intervention, starting preconceptionally and continuing through pregnancy, infancy and early childhood, on reducing childhood adiposity (fat mass index) and overweight and obesity (OWO) and promoting early childhood development (ECD). Approximately 22,000 women are being recruited into trials in Shanghai (China), Mysore (India), Soweto (South Africa), and across Canada. Women who conceive (N ~ 10,000) are followed through pregnancy and their children through to 5 years. The overall HeLTI hypothesis is that an intervention aimed to [1] address sub-optimal maternal weight, nutrition and health behaviours; [2] provide psychosocial support to reduce maternal stress and promote mental health; [3] optimise infant nutrition, physical activity and sleep; and [4] promote parenting skills will reduce the intergenerational risk of excess childhood adiposity and OWO and improve ECD outcomes.

The overall goal of the HeLTI consortium is to generate evidence that will inform national and global policy and decision-making for improving parental and child health and preventing non-communicable diseases (NCDs) throughout the lifespan. Equally important is the development of evidence-informed ECD policies. Given that maternal and paternal health have transgenerational effects on child development and health [1], this initiative aims to develop gender-responsive evidence for interventions and policies aimed at the needs of women, men, girls and boys. The engagement of multiple countries promotes international collaborations through a multifaceted, multidisciplinary approach that spans from basic science to public health research and policy as well as an enhanced understanding of the results and their implications in different contexts. The collaboration enables comparative analysis of interventions, harmonised data, assessment of biological mechanisms and biological-environmental interactions in various populations and the exchange and stimulation of scientific ideas and the sharing of expertise. The initiative also provides opportunities for early-career researchers and trainees to collaborate with senior scientists and develop their research skills, including implementation and quality assurance of large intervention cohorts as well as data management and analysis.

For each country, the joint leadership team includes a lead from the participating country and a co-lead from Canada, and 2 co-leads from Canada for the Canadian trial. In China, India and South Africa, the trials are funded by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC), the Department of Biotechnology (DBT) and the South African Medical Research Council (SAMRC), respectively. The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) has contributed funds to all trials through grants to Canadian collaborating institutions and for the Canadian trial. The HeLTI consortium has a collective governance structure for managing the trials. The two co-lead PIs of each of the four HeLTI trials form the Research Committee (RC), which is responsible for overall consortium management and oversight, liaising with working groups and reporting to the HeLTI Council (comprising representatives from all funders and a WHO secretariat). Working groups, including investigators from all countries, chaired by a RC member, develop harmonised protocols covering biospecimens, data collection, interventions, cohort retention, data management, capacity building and knowledge translation. Each country project team has a Steering Committee, comprising the two PIs and selected co-PIs, that addresses daily operations and reports to the RC, as well as liaising with participating institutions and community representatives. The four HeLTI trials have a joint international Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) comprising independent experts, with representation from the four countries involved. This initiative is supported through a novel partnership with the WHO that provides technical support and coordination. All study protocols are registered and published in BMJ Open [2–5].

HeLTI trial designs

The trials in China and India have adopted a cluster randomised design, while the trials in Canada and South Africa are based on individual randomisation. All trials have implemented interventions in phases across the preconception period, pregnancy, infancy (0–2 years) and early childhood (3–5 years). While key intervention domains have been harmonised across country sites, they have been tailored to local socio-cultural contexts. The primary outcome measure in all trials is an adiposity measure (fat mass index/overweight and obesity) in children at 5 years of age. Additional major outcomes in all projects include ECD measures and other anthropometric indices, including BMI trajectory and cardiometabolic measures. Diverse maternal and paternal outcomes are assessed across the intervention phases, including physical and mental health, health behaviours and parenting skills. Economic and process evaluations are also being undertaken.

The trials are approximately 10-year projects that included 18–24 months of set-up and formative work. Study development across the consortium was initiated in December 2016. Given the differing start dates (due to funding approvals and the COVID-19 pandemic), the study timelines for the four sites varied. Details are provided in Table 1. Basic statistical analysis plans (SAPs) have been included in the site-specific protocol papers that have all been published [1–4]. As of March 2026, all trials have completed recruitment.

The challenge

Given the long-term nature of the trials (~ 10 years), the four phases of the intervention, and the multiple consortium objectives, the Research Committee identified the challenge of undertaking mechanistic studies and studies that evaluate secondary outcomes that might require between-group comparisons before publishing the primary outcome. The following problem statement was articulated for further consideration by the HeLTI Research Committee:

Would analysing and publishing between-group comparisons of secondary outcomes, either a subsample or from all mothers/infants, from one cohort or from all cohorts as a pooled analysis, in advance of the primary outcome data of the trials being captured, analysed and published, result in ethical or other dilemmas that might undermine the integrity of the study design and prejudice the likelihood of primary study outcomes being published in a high-impact journal?

For example, what would be the implications of analysing and publishing birthweight data and epigenetic measures by study group in order to report the effects of the preconception or pregnancy phases of the intervention before the end of the study and collection of the primary outcome measures, and while postnatal interventions are still being implemented according to randomisation? See Additional file 1 for more examples of mechanistic studies.

Since different interventions are progressively implemented in each phase (preconception, pregnancy, infancy and early childhood), some important outcome measures, such as birthweight, will be available between years 3 and 5 of the studies. However, postnatal interventions will still continue to be implemented and final outcome measures (body composition and early childhood development) will not have been completed.

Ethical considerations

Examining mechanistic pathways with clinical relevance and other intermediate clinical outcomes among the study populations by study arm before analyses of the primary outcomes would obligate the research teams to inform participants and communities of potential benefits/harms of being randomised to one arm or the other before delivery of postnatal interventions has been completed. Depending on the results, this may result in unexpected responses from the communities and possibly decreased continuing engagement in the study when postnatal interventions are still being delivered. This may be especially true in settings where low levels of literacy are prevalent and maintaining consent for the study is difficult.

Analyses could discover a between-group difference that is so clinically significant that the trial is stopped. This may be unlikely, but the DSMB would need to review such findings and, with stopping rules agreed on a priori, consider the implications. The DSMB is aware of the plans for analysis by phase prior to publication of the primary outcome.

Statistical considerations: Intermediate analyses could result in criticism that a number of between-group comparisons were made and published before the actual between-group comparisons of the primary outcome. Therefore, adjustments to the significance level would be needed despite the secondary nature of the intermediate analyses.

Publication of the primary outcomes of the trial (adiposity and cardio-metabolic risk markers at child age 5) may be jeopardised: Early reporting of birthweight and other pregnancy outcomes by study arm, in addition to mechanistic outcome measures, would communicate to the scientific community that the preconception and pregnancy interventions have or have not improved outcomes. These outcomes are critical intermediaries in the obesity/NCD/ECD pathways. Early reporting of these outcomes by study arm could impact the primary outcomes being considered ‘old news’. This will be even more likely if there are significant differences in mechanistic pathways. As such, reporting of the main trial outcomes would be less impactful as a result of unblinded analyses of intermediate outcomes by study groups. The chances of the primary outcomes being published in a high-impact journal would, therefore, be diminished.

The Research Committee worked with the WHO to identify a group of international experts in clinical trials/epidemiology, statistics, ethics, and DOHaD science, as well as journal editors and research funders. The problem statement and supporting document were shared with the international expert group and their views were collated (April–June 2018). Trial timelines are presented in Table 1. Specific perspectives were requested from domain experts (Additional file 3). We invited 25 global experts in various fields (11 in clinical trials/epidemiology, 4 in statistics, 3 in ethics, 3 in DOHaD science, 3 journal editors, and 1 research funder) to provide feedback. Of these, five declined the invitation to provide feedback and we did not receive a response from five invitees. A total of 15 experts agreed to provide feedback, and ultimately, we received feedback from 13 experts (7 in clinical trials/epidemiology, 2 in statistics, 1 in ethics, 2 in DOHaD science, and 1 in research funder).

Results

Expert panel feedback

Expert panel members commended the HeLTI teams for reaching out to the research community for input. They also highlighted the importance of the HeLTI studies. As no similar studies have been undertaken previously, it was felt that the HeLTI consortium should break new ground. It was identified that given the very long nature of the studies, there would be changes in the environments in which the studies are conducted that cannot be predicted or controlled. However, it was suggested that as long as any changes implemented were carefully documented, the HeLTI consortium would successfully answer the question of whether interventions initiated preconceptionally could impact childhood adiposity.

Firstly, I’d like to commend you on giving such careful consideration to this issue and preparing such a thoughtful document setting out various advantages and disadvantages. (Reviewer 2).

I firmly believe that HeLTI sets the scene for a truly innovative approach to the issue of DOHaD. (Reviewer 11).

Overall, eight of the external experts were generally supportive of publishing between-group comparisons of mechanistic studies and secondary outcomes before the trial primary outcomes, suggesting that a pragmatic approach was appropriate. Two external experts stated that it would not be appropriate to undertake between-group analyses before the primary endpoint was reached. Three experts considered the pros and cons of publishing secondary outcomes and raised issues for consideration but did not state a categorical opinion. Additional file 4 includes all the specific comments from experts (provided with permission).

Numerous experts felt that, given the scale of the initiative, emphasis should not be placed on one large ‘trial’ paper at the end of the study. It was suggested that such an approach would reduce the interest of other researchers, including early career researchers (ECRs) and trainees, of being involved in the consortium. Further, it may reduce the ability to secure funding for the analysis of secondary and mechanistic outcomes as well as the overall programme.

While we all want high-impact papers, I think that having a body of work from this trial rather than one high-impact paper at the end would be better output and more informative about the intervention. (Reviewer 4).

The impact of analysing the outcomes of one intervention phase prior to the completion of interventions in the next phase of the trial was considered. Some felt that this would not represent a major study limitation as each phase of the intervention was independent of the success of a previous phase. Importantly, it was highlighted that while interim analysis might show differences between groups, it is unlikely that these would necessarily predict the primary outcome. Indeed, one reviewer suggested that previous studies that did not show a significant intervention effect on long-term outcomes would have perhaps benefitted from an understanding as to whether the intervention had an earlier impact. Another reviewer felt that although some interventions may not show early positive effects, they could potentially demonstrate impact at a later time. The reviewers also thought that the potentially adaptive study design of the HeLTI trial negated some of these concerns.

Yes, if you find differences in birthweight, the intervention has had an effect, but if you do not find differences in birthweight, you still have no idea whether there was contrast between intervention and control. (Reviewer 12).

It was suggested that informing participants of study results from interim analyses would positively impact participant engagement and retention. Generally, it was felt that this could be managed through open communication.

Several reviewers felt that while the mechanistic studies linked to secondary outcomes would provide important new knowledge, they would unlikely predict the intervention effects on the primary outcome. It was also suggested that identifying intervention effects on interim outcomes would positively impact future funding.

I don’t think that there would be any implications if the early mechanistic studies were negative. Strongly positive studies in a 10% subsample would need replication in the full study at completion. (Reviewer 9).

In general, reviewers felt that if the analysis plan was outlined a priori, then such an approach would be more acceptable. It was also felt that reference to the interim analysis should be included in the ethics applications, consent, and trial registration. Some reviewers suggested that adopting a Bayesian approach might be helpful and address some potential statistical challenges of an interim analysis.

In sum, I think that it is worth paying the statistical penalty of examining interim secondary outcomes. This penalty should be stated up front. (Reviewer 3).

Many reviewers highlighted the importance of an active Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) and suggested that it should be consulted on the analysis strategy. The DSMB would also need to be engaged in all decisions around phased analysis.

The DSMB has to play an important role in any decision on the reporting/ publishing of interim data of the trial and should be used to make independent decision in regards to safeguarding the integrity of the trial. Having a DSMB that oversees all the trials would be helpful in this regard. (Reviewer 8).

Three reviewers indicated that interim analysis of secondary outcomes was inconsistent with the trial design and may impact the ability to publish results in a high-impact journal. These are very important and valid viewpoints that require continuing consideration.

Publication of any secondary outcomes prior to the publication of the primary outcome would invalidate the trial, potentially influence the participants and the trial staff, and prejudice any high-ranking journal against publication. (Reviewer 6).

Doing so would undermine the integrity of the study design and jeopardise publication of the primary outcome findings. (Reviewer 7).

It was also felt that the ethical implications of continuing with the interventions while early effects were observed (or excluded) in one group were not significant, as neither the primary outcome nor the intervention in a subsequent phase depended upon the intervention’s success (or failure) in the previous phase.

Your second worry that ethics committees might halt the trial also seems redundant to me. The results of one phase that is completed cannot be stated to materially affect that next phase of intervention unless you have (foolishly) predicated your second phase upon a positive result in the first phase (e.g. you might set up a trial where an intervention in kids is based on randomising babies who had achieved a certain metric (e.g. birthweight) that was determined by the first phase. (Reviewer 1).

While several reviewers highlighted that the purist approach would be to undertake an analysis of the primary outcome before any interim analyses, the majority felt that this was not practical in the context of the HeLTI trials where very valuable interim analyses, including important mechanistic assessments, would be missed if such an approach was adopted.

Discussion

The HeLTI consortium is exceptional in many ways, particularly, as it involves four prospectively harmonised individually powered trials in four countries with a 10-year perspective. Consequently, the duration of the HeLTI trials differs from more traditional and shorter approaches—it is different from a standard drug trial given that it is impossible to blind participants and research staff delivering interventions. Numerous experts felt that, considering the scale of the initiative, emphasis cannot be simply placed on one large ‘trial’ paper at the end of the study. Further, such an approach would reduce the interest of other researchers, including early-career researchers (ECRs) and trainees, of being involved in the consortium. It may also reduce the ability to secure funding for the analysis of secondary and mechanistic outcomes. The impact of analysing the outcomes of one intervention phase prior to the completion of interventions in the next phase of the trial was considered. It was felt that this would not represent a major study limitation as each phase of the intervention was independent of the success of a previous phase. Importantly, while interim analyses might show differences between groups, or rule out an effect, it is unlikely that these would necessarily predict the primary outcome. Another possible option would be to identify multiple primary outcomes including at earlier stages. While it may be appropriate for some specific questions, in this instance our primary outcome was based on developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) concepts. We specifically selected a childhood marker of adult cardiometabolic risk. To our knowledge we are not aware of any earlier marker that would provide a similar level of confidence in terms of adult cardiometabolic risk.

The adverse ethical implications of publishing secondary outcomes are not seen to be significant. The HeLTI Research Committee agreed that any beneficial results should be shared with the participants. Indeed, an adaptive design will need to consider any interim results, including findings from ongoing process evaluation, and any new findings published in the literature. It was suggested that informing participants of study results from interim analyses may positively impact participant engagement and retention. Some reviewers highlighted the importance of an active DSMB and said they should be consulted on the analysis strategy. The HeLTI consortium has established an international DSMB that oversees all four studies. The DSMB meets regularly and has discussed the phased analysis plan. In contrast to publishing the primary outcome first in trials, there is some precedent for interim analysis within a long-term trial design. One reviewer referred to The Gambia Hepatitis Intervention Study, a trial of hepatitis B immunisation in infancy with a primary outcome of liver cancer in adulthood. Several papers have been published on sub-groups assessing interim outcomes, unblinding at various points prior to the primary outcome being reached. The study team is now preparing to evaluate the primary outcome, and there is no concern that earlier publications would jeopardise the trial [6]. The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Efficacy Guideline E9: Statistical Principles of Clinical Trials recommends a single primary outcome as ideal; however, there is guidance on how to accommodate multiple primary outcomes with careful consideration of the possibility of type 1 error for multiplicity of outcomes [7]. In our trial, we have chosen a single primary outcome; all the other outcomes will be treated as secondary outcomes. Our phased analysis may appear similar to an interim analysis; however, the outcomes we have chosen at each phase are independent of the final primary outcome. Given the principles of DOHaD, the primary outcome of adiposity is not dependent on the effectiveness of the intervention package on any of the earlier secondary outcomes. We will specify analyses to be undertaken in a detailed plan; any amendments will be completed prior to unblinding. Interim analyses in trials, especially adaptive clinical trials, are gaining wider acceptance and our trial conforms to similar principles [8]. For the HeLTI trials, final detailed country-specific SAPs with the decision incorporated are being developed and will be published on the trial website prior to the end of the first phase of unblinding (when all recruited participants either become pregnant or exit).

Conclusion

After careful consideration of the issue, the Research Committee unanimously agreed to take an approach that allowed for publishing between-group comparisons of secondary outcomes and linked mechanistic studies before the primary outcome. This would be undertaken using an a priori agreed phased approach. Specifically, it was decided that the analysis would be completed at the end of each intervention phase: preconception, pregnancy (perinatal period), infancy (0–2 years) and early childhood (3–5 years). Such a phased analysis will only be undertaken when all individuals in a study have completed that particular phase. This is an innovative and pragmatic solution to the challenge of undertaking interim analyses in a long-term public health study, and the process and outcome may benefit future planning of the evaluation of life course-centered complex interventions.
