Universal scaling limits at the spectral singularity of structured random matrices
Markus Ebke, Torben Krüger

Abstract
The empirical spectral distribution of Hermitian K × K K\times K -block random matrices converges to a deterministic density on the real line with a potential atom at the origin as the dimension of the blocks tends to infinity. In this model the variances of the entries depends on the block and the limiting density is determined by these variances. In the absence of an atom the density is either bounded or has a power law singularity at the origin. We determine all scaling limits of the spectral density on the eigenvalue spacing scale at this singularity for Gaussian matrices with block numbers K ≤ 3 K\leq 3 . The appropriate scaling for the universal limit is correctly predicted by the global eigenvalue density. For K = 3 K=3 the local one-point function exhibits an additional logarithmic singularity. The scaling limits depend only on the zero pattern within the variance profile, but not on the values of its positive entries.



Keywords and phrases:
Wigner-type matrix; eigenvalue distribution; microscopic scaling limit; supersymmetric method; superbosonization.

Introduction

A prominent theme within the theory of random matrices (RMT) is the phenomenon of universality: the empirical spectral distribution of large random matrices depends only on a few model features, such as the underlying symmetry class of the matrix or the presence of global constraints on the entries, not however on the details of the entry distributions. This phenomenon is observed on two complementary scales. On the global (macroscopic) scale, the empirical eigenvalue distributions converge to deterministic laws that reflect coarse structural information about the ensemble such as the covariance structure of the entries. Wigner’s semicircle law
[
57
]
and the Marchenko–Pastur law
[
42
]
are classical examples for which the limit of the empirical spectral measure depends only on the entry variances but not on their distributions. On the local (microscopic) spectral scale, after zooming to the typical eigenvalue spacing, universal correlation functions emerge: the sine kernel in the bulk
[
43
,
29
]
, Tracy–Widom/Airy statistics at soft edges
[
53
,
54
]
, Bessel-type laws at hard edges
[
52
]
, and the Pearcey-type correlations at cusps
[
10
]
. Both, global laws and local spectral universality, have by now been established for wide classes of random matrix ensembles far beyond the classical settings of Wigner matrices with i.i.d. entries, for which the sine kernel universality was proved in
[
49
,
51
,
24
]
.

Within this landscape of random matrix ensembles,
structured
or
Wigner-type
models play a central role, both to test theoretical hypotheses and to model applications with inhomogeneous settings, where the distribution of the entries depends on their position inside the matrix. These ensembles are Hermitian matrices
H=(h_{\alpha\beta})
with independent entries and a variance profile
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{\alpha\beta}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}
. The simplest instance are
N\times N
Wigner matrices with i.i.d. entries whose variances equal
\frac{1}{N}
.
Block variance profiles are of particular interest due to the transparent nature of their limiting spectral measure. In these models the matrix indices are grouped into
K
batches
I_{1},\dots,I_{K}
of size
N
and the variance profile only depends on the batch index, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{\alpha\beta}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}s_{ij}
for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in I_{i}\times I_{j}
. As
N
tends to infinity, the empirical spectral distribution
\rho
is governed by the associated vector Dyson equation and depends on
S=(s_{ij})
(see e.g.
[
3
]
for details and references).
This framework includes block models and multi-type networks (e.g. inhomogeneous Erdős–Rényi and stochastic block models
[
38
]
), block band matrices with applications in condensed matter physics
[
22
]
, graph Laplacians with community structure
[
44
,
12
]
, bipartite/chiral Hamiltonians
[
8
]
, as well as heteroskedastic noise models in high-dimensional statistics and signal processing
[
33
,
16
,
6
]
. Systematic analysis of the vector Dyson equation as well as bulk local spectral universality for Wigner-type matrices has been developed in
[
3
,
4
]
.

It is natural to ask for a classification of all local universality classes that can occur within such structured ensembles. We restrict our discussion here to block models with centred entries. Under sufficiently strong non-degeneracy assumptions on the variance profile
S
, e.g. when
S
is strictly positive, it was established in
[
4
]
that the limiting spectral measure is bounded, analytic, whenever positive, and approaches zero only at regular square root edges or cubic root cusps. The corresponding universality classes are the sine
[
4
]
, Airy
[
7
]
and Pearcey statistics
[
13
,
23
]
, respectively.
However, once zero blocks within the matrix are permitted
\rho
may have an atom at the origin or a power law singularity of the form
\rho(E)\sim\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{E}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{-\sigma}
for
E\to 0
. These singularities have been classified in
[
36
]
depending on the zero pattern of the variance profile. In particular,
\sigma=(\ell-1)/(\ell+1)
for some integer
1\leq\ell\leq K
. Some special cases are also considered in
[
35
]
, which additionally considers
N
-dependent variance profiles
S
with entries that are uniformly bounded from above and below.
A related model is the linearization of covariance matrices
XX^{*}
, where
X
are
\lambda
-shaped matrices, which are rectangular with independent complex entries whose variance profile has ones and zeros in the shape of a Young-diagram. The study of the eigenvalue distribution of these ensembles is closely related to the combinatorics of plane trees
[
17
,
9
]
.
The question which local spectral universality class is associated to the singularity with given
\ell
however has not been addressed in previous works. Only numerical evidence for the conjectured spacing scale
\eta_{N}\sim N^{-(\ell+1)/2}
at the singularities for
K=2
and
K=3
blocks has been provided in
[
36
]
.

In the present work we address the question of local spectral statistics for
K=2,3
with Gaussian entries and derive a formula for the spectral density at the singularity on the eigenvalue spacing scale. For
K=2
we show that the global power law, dictated by the density
\rho
, persists down to the microscopic scale. With
\ell=1
this yields a new universality class whose limiting one-point function is expressed in terms of Meijer
G
and generalized hypergeometric functions.
In the
K=3
case with
\ell=2
a new universality class is established at a typical spacing scale
\sim N^{-2}
, whose local one-point function is expressed in terms of modified Bessel functions. In this case, the local density has a logarithmic singularity at the origin suggesting a particularly strong affinity of the smallest singular value to the origin. In both cases the limits depend only on the divergence type of the macroscopic density and not on the specific values of the non-zero entries within the variance profile
S
.
Furthermore, we consider variants of
K=2,3
where entries of the variance profile vanish as
N\to\infty
.
This allows us to interpolate between our new results for the
K=2
case and the well-known results from
[
27
]
for the chiral Gaussian Unitary Ensemble.
We also examine an analogous limit for
K=3
.

For the proofs we employ the supersymmetric method, including superbosonization, to derive finite-
N
representations for the expected trace of the resolvent of
H
.
The supersymmetric method has been used for a variety of random matrix ensembles including random band matrices
[
19
,
20
,
47
,
50
]
, interpolating ensembles
[
45
,
1
]
, the shifted Ginibre ensemble
[
15
,
14
,
46
,
41
]
and chiral random matrices
[
34
]
.
The superbosonization formula was used in
[
11
]
to derive the eigenvalue distribution for almost diagonal matrices, i.e.
N=1
and
S_{ij}\to 0
for
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{i-j}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\to\infty
in our notation.
To determine the local scaling limit of the eigenvalue density at the singularity of the density of states, we rescale the integrals of the finite-
N
representations to accommodate for the divergent behaviour of the resolvent at the singularity. Here, additional symmetries in the resulting action at the spacing scale emerge. After a higher order saddle point analysis these symmetries are integrated out leading to the special functions within the final formulas.

In Section

.

We investigate the weak non-chirality regime in Section
6
, here we start again with the finite

4
and compute its asymptotics for an
N

S
.
In Appendix

we summarize the saddle-point method and state the formulas that we use in Section
4
and Section
6
.

2
Main Results

We consider Hermitian
K\times K
-block random matrices with block dimension
N
that have independent centred Gaussian entries.
The block variance profile is
S=(s_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{K}
with
s_{ij}=s_{ji}\geq 0
.
This model is conveniently written in tensor notation as

H=\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\sqrt{\frac{s_{ij}}{2}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({E_{ij}\otimes X_{ij}+E_{ji}\otimes X_{ij}^{*}}\right)}}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times K}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{KN\times KN}\,,

(2.1)

where
E_{ij}=(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl})_{k,l=1}^{K}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{K\times K}
and
X_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
are independent complex
N\times N
Ginibre matrices.
A Ginibre matrix
X=(x_{kl})_{k,l=1}^{N}
has centred i.i.d. Gaussian entries with
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{x_{kl}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}
and
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}x_{kl}^{2}=0
.

We define the normalized one-point function (also called the density of states)
\rho_{N}
of
H
and the asymptotic density of states
\rho_{\infty}
as

\rho_{N}:=\frac{1}{KN}\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{\lambda\in\spec(H)}\delta_{\lambda}}\right]}}\,,\qquad\rho_{\infty}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\rho_{N}\,,

respectively, where the sum over the eigenvalues
\lambda\in\spec(H)
of
H
is taken with multiplicity.
The density of states is connected to the resolvent of
H
through the Stieltjes transform

\frac{1}{KN}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}}\right]}}=\frac{1}{KN}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{\lambda\in\spec(H)}\frac{1}{\lambda-z}}\right]}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho_{N}(\mathrm{d}E)}{E-z}\,,\qquad\text{for }z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}.

Here
\frac{1}{H-z}
denotes the resolvent of
H
, i.e. the matrix inverse of
H-z\Id_{KN}
where
\Id_{KN}
is the
KN\times KN
identity matrix.
The trace
\Tr
is the sum over all diagonal entries.
The density can be recovered using the inversion formula

\rho_{N}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{1}{KN}\Im\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-(E+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}}\right)}}}\right]}}

(2.2)

which holds if the limit on the right-hand side exists.
As a first step towards analyzing the density
\rho_{N}
, we derive an exact expression for the expectation of the resolvent at finite
N
.
The proof is given in Section
3
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
H
be given by (
2.1
).
Then for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Im(z)>0
and all
N\in\mathbb{N}
the expectation of the resolvent has the integral representation

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}}\right]}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}N^{K+1}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{K}}\int_{(0,\infty)^{K}}\mathrm{d}a\oint^{K}\mathrm{d}b\,\mathrm{e}^{-NI(a)+NI(b)}\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag_{i=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}}\right]}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}a_{j},

where

is the

\oint^{K}\mathrm{d}b:=\oint\mathrm{d}b_{1}\dots\oint\mathrm{d}b_{K}

is a counter-clockwise contour integral over the circle around

The function

in the exponent is

I(a):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}s_{ij}a_{j}a_{i}-\mathrm{i}z\sum_{i=1}^{K}a_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log(a_{i}).

(2.4)

For Wigner-type matrices it is well known that (under certain assumptions on the entry distribution, see

,
3

\rho_{\infty}
can be expressed as

\rho_{\infty}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\Re\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}a_{i}(E+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon),

where
a(z)=(a_{1}(z),\dots,a_{K}(z))
is the unique solution of the quadratic vector equation
\nabla I(a(z))=0
such that
\Re a_{i}(z)>0
for all
i=1,\dots,K
.

In the following, we derive the microscopic scaling limit at the origin.
We assume that the variance profile
S
is irreducible, since otherwise the spectral measure of
H
is a convex combination of the spectral measures of its irreducible components. Furthermore, from
[
36
]
we know that the asymptotic density of states has an isolated atom at zero whenever the variance profile
S
does not have support. In this case the asymptotic eigenvalue statistics of
H
in a vicinity of the origin is trivial. Therefore we assume in the following that
S
has support, i.e. that there is a permutation matrix
P\in\{0,1\}^{K\times K}
such that
S\geq\varepsilon P
entry wise for some
\varepsilon>0
. In this case
\rho_{\infty}
has a density on the real line that we will denote by the same symbol, i.e.
\rho_{\infty}(\mathrm{d}E)=\rho_{\infty}(E)\mathrm{d}E
.

From the classification of singularities of
\rho_{\infty}
in
[
36
]
we know that the asymptotic density of states has a polynomial singularity at the origin such that the limit

\vartheta:=\lim_{E\to 0}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{E}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell+1}}\rho_{\infty}(E)\in(0,\infty)

(2.6)

is finite and strictly positive, where
\ell\leq K
is a positive integer that depends on the location of zero and non-zero entries within the variance profile
S
.
From the above relation (
2.6
) we deduce that

\lim_{N\to\infty}KN\int_{-\eta_{N}/2}^{\eta_{N}/2}\rho_{\infty}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E=1\,,\qquad\text{for }\eta_{N}:=2\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\vartheta KN(\ell+1)}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{-\frac{\ell+1}{2}}.

(2.7)

We interpret
\eta_{N}
as the local spacing scale, i.e. the typical distance between consecutive eigenvalues at the origin.

For
K=1
we have
\ell=1
and
H
is a GUE matrix, up to a constant.
In this case the spacing scale is proportional to
\frac{1}{N}
and the local eigenvalue one-point function
\lim_{N\to\infty}KN\eta_{N}\rho_{N}(\eta_{N}x)
at the origin is constant, i.e. independent of
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
For
S=1
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-N^{-1}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}=\mathrm{i}.

(2.8)

For
K=2
three cases occur.
When all entries of
S
are non-zero the density
\rho_{\infty}
remains bounded and
\ell=1
.
In this case the local eigenvalue statistics at the origin is given by the sine kernel process and the local eigenvalue one-point function is again constant.
Bounded density with
\ell=1
also occurs when the diagonal entries of
S
vanish while the off-diagonal entries are positive.
The corresponding matrix ensemble is called the
chiral GUE
and was introduced in
[
48
,
55
,
56
]
as a model for the Dirac operator in quantum chromodynamics.
Its eigenvalue distribution is a determinantal point process whose kernel can be written in terms of Laguerre polynomials.
The macroscopic limiting density is the semi-circle law, the microscopic scaling limit in the bulk is the sine kernel except at the origin where the limiting kernel can be written in terms of Bessel-
J
functions
[
27
]
.
Here the scaling limit of the one-point function is

\lim_{N\to\infty}\rho_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{\xi}{2N}}\right)}}=\frac{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}}{2}(J_{0}(\xi)^{2}+J_{1}(\xi)^{2}).

(2.9)

The remaining case with
K=2
block indices is
\ell=2
, where up to permutation of the block indices the variance profile takes the form

S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}\\
s_{12}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{with }\;s_{11},s_{12}>0.

(2.10)

As we will see in Theorem
2.2
below the eigenvalue one-point function on the local spacing scale
N^{-3/2}
around the origin is non-trivial in this situation.

For
K=3
block indices with a variance profile
S
that has support there are only two cases. Either the variance profile is fully indecomposable (see
[
36
]
for the precise definition) or it is, up to permutation of the block indices, of the form

S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}&s_{13}\\
s_{12}&s_{22}&0\\
s_{13}&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{with }\;s_{11}\geq 0\;\text{ and }\;s_{12},s_{13},s_{22}>0.

(2.11)

In the former case, we have
\ell=1
and the local eigenvalue one-point function is constant.
In the latter,
\ell=3
with a local spacing scale of
N^{-2}
.

Our main theorem provides the universal scaling limits of the expectation of the resolvent for the two non-trivial cases (

) and (
2.11
), the proof is given in Section

Remark 2.3
.

The Meijer
G
-function also appears in the local one-point function for products of random matrices.
As an example, consider the Hermitized product
G_{m}^{*}\dots G_{1}^{*}HG_{1}\dots G_{m}

K_{\text{Meijer}}^{M}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}u\,G^{1,0}_{0,M+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\begin{matrix}-\\

Remark 2.5
.

In the
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\to 0
asymptotics (

) for

, we see that the microscopic one-point function has a logarithmic singularity and we may interpret this singularity as an attraction of the eigenvalues to the origin.
For comparison, let us consider a 1-dimensional log-gas in an external potential
V
with a local attraction due to the insertion of a point charge (also called a root-type Fisher-Hartwig singularity).
The corresponding unitary ensemble of random matrices has a probability density proportional to
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\det(M)}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-N\Tr(V(M))}\mathrm{d}M
, where
M
is a Hermitian
N\times N
-matrix and
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
is the strength of the charge inserted at the origin.
If
\alpha<0
, the charge is attractive.
Note that the point charge affects the eigenvalue distribution only on the microscopic scale, because
\alpha
does not scale with
N
.
It is known from
[
37
]
that the microscopic scaling limit at the origin is universal for a large class of potentials
V
.
However, one of the assumptions on
V
is that the equilibrium measure
\rho_{\infty}
is positive and finite at the origin, unlike our model.
The limiting correlation kernel is given in terms of Bessel-
J
functions (but is different from the hard edge Bessel-kernel) and for
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\to 0
the microscopic one-point function is asymptotically proportional to
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{\alpha}
.
Hence, there is a divergence but not a logarithmic divergence when
\alpha<0
.
This comparison suggests that the local attraction in our case is weaker than the attraction due to a charge of opposite sign.

The asymptotic behaviour for
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\to\infty

\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{-\frac{l-1}{l+1}}

\rho_{\infty}
dictates the asymptotics.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\rho_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{\xi}{3N}}\right)}}=\frac{2}{3}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\xi}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({J_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{2}{3}\xi}\right)}}^{2}+J_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{2}{3}\xi}\right)}}^{2}}\right)}}+\frac{1}{3\pi}.

This combination of
\frac{2}{3}
times the chiral GUE one-point function plus
\frac{1}{3}
times a constant is expected, since an eigenvalue of the matrix

H=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&A\\
0&B&0\\
A^{*}&0&0\end{pmatrix}

is either an eigenvalue of
\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
A^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}
or an eigenvalue of
B
.
These blocks also correspond to the irreducible components of
S
.
In our model, (
2.1
)
B
is a GUE matrix and so its local one-point function at the origin is constant.

3
Integral Representation for Finite
N

In this section we prove Lemma
2.1
.
We use the supersymmetric method and the superbosonization formula similar to the example in
[
11
]
, but our notation is closer to
[
15
]
so that we can apply the superbosonization formula from
[
39
]
.

3.1
SUSY Representation

We start with the identity

A^{-1}=\det(A)\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}[Dx]\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{*}Ax}xx^{*}\,,\qquad[Dx]:=\prod_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\!\Re x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\!\Im x_{i}}{\pi}

(3.1)

which holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
\Re\spec(A)\subset(0,\infty)
, where
\spec(A)
is the set of eigenvalues of
A
.
Note that we write
x^{*}
for the row vector of complex conjugates
\overline{x_{1}}\!\,,\dots,\overline{x_{n}}\!\,
and that we multiply vectors and matrices row-by-column.
In particular,
x^{*}Ax=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\overline{x_{i}}\!\,A_{ij}x_{j}
is a
1\times 1
-matrix which we interpret as a scalar and
xx^{*}
is the
n\times n
-matrix with entries
(xx^{*})_{ij}=x_{i}\overline{x_{j}}\!\,
for
i,j=1,\dots,n
.

Next, we express the determinant of
A
using
Grassmann variables
.
Let
\xi_{i}
and
\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
be anti-commuting variables that satisfy

\xi_{j}\xi_{i}=-\xi_{i}\xi_{j},\qquad\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j}\xi_{i}=-\xi_{i}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j},\qquad\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}=-\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j}\qquad\text{for all }i,j=1,\dots,n.

In particular,
\xi_{i}^{2}=\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}^{2}=0
.
Additionally, we introduce the derivatives
\partial_{\xi_{i}}
and
\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}}
as

\partial_{\xi_{i}}\xi_{j}=\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j}=\delta_{i,j},\qquad\partial_{\xi_{i}}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{j}=\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}}\xi_{j}=0,\qquad\partial_{\xi_{i}}1=\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}}1=0.

The determinant identity is then (see e.g.
[
32
, Sec. 5.13]
, but note that we use a different sign convention)

\det(A)=[\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]\,\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{*}A\xi}\,,\qquad[\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]:=\partial_{\xi_{1}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{1}}\dots\partial_{\xi_{n}}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,_{n}}\,,

(3.2)

where the exponential of anti-commuting variables is defined by its (terminating) Taylor series,
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})^{\top}
is a column vector while
\xi^{*}=(\overline{\xi}\!\,_{1},\dots,\overline{\xi}\!\,_{n})
is a row vector and
\xi^{*}A\xi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\overline{\xi}\!\,_{i}A_{ij}\xi_{j}
.

When combining the identities (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), it is useful to introduce the
n\times(1|1)
-matrix
\Psi=(x,\xi)
and the
(1|1)\times n
-matrix
\Psi^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{*}\\
\xi^{*}\end{pmatrix}
.
The dimension
(p|q)
denotes the presence of
p\in\mathbb{N}
commuting and
q\in\mathbb{N}
anti-commuting components.
Then

x^{*}Ax-\xi^{*}A\xi=\Str\begin{pmatrix}x^{*}Ax&x^{*}A\xi\\
\xi^{*}Ax&\xi^{*}A\xi\end{pmatrix}=\Str(\Psi^{*}A\Psi),

(3.3)

where the
supertrace

\Str
of a
(p|q)\times(p|q)
-supermatrix is defined as the scalar

\Str\begin{pmatrix}a&\sigma\\
\tau&b\end{pmatrix}:=\Tr(a)-\Tr(b).

The
p\times p
-block
a
is usually called the
boson-boson
block and the
q\times q
-block
b
is called the
fermion-fermion
block, both have commuting entries.
The
p\times q
-block
\sigma
and the
q\times p
-block
\tau
contain Grassmann variables.
Observe that we only need the case
p=q=1
.

Using the cyclic invariance of the trace and the anti-commutativity of
\xi
and
\xi^{*}
yields

x^{*}Ax-\xi^{*}A\xi=\Tr(Axx^{*})+\Tr(A\xi\xi^{*})=\Tr(A\Psi\Psi^{*}).

(3.4)

We conclude that
\Str(\Psi^{*}A\Psi)=\Tr(A\Psi\Psi^{*})
, where
\Psi^{*}A\Psi
is a
(1|1)\times(1|1)
-supermatrix while
\Psi\Psi^{*}=xx^{*}+\xi\xi^{*}
is a
n\times n
-matrix whose entries contain linear combinations of commuting variables that are either complex numbers or quadratic expressions of Grassmann variables.

To summarize, for
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
with
\Re\spec(A)\subset(0,\infty)
we obtain the formula

\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({A^{-1}}\right)}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}[Dx][\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{*}Ax+\xi^{*}A\xi}\Tr(xx^{*})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}[Dx][\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]\,\mathrm{e}^{-\Str(\Psi^{*}A\Psi)}x^{*}x

(3.5)

and in particular, for
A=\mathrm{i}(H-z)
with
\Im(z)>0
and
n=KN
, we get

\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}=\mathrm{i}\Tr(A^{-1})=\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{KN}}[Dx][\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(H\Psi\Psi^{*})+\mathrm{i}z\Str(\Psi^{*}\Psi)}x^{*}x\,.

(3.6)

Note that when defining
A
we include the factor
\mathrm{i}
to ensure that the condition
\Re\spec(A)=\Im(z)>0
holds.

3.2
Evaluation of the Expectation

The expectation value

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(H\Psi\Psi^{*})}}\right]}}=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(HY)}}\right]}},\qquad Y:=\Psi\Psi^{*}=xx^{*}+\xi\xi^{*}

is the Fourier-transform of the matrix probability distribution.
Since
H
contains only centred Gaussian random variables, the expectation satisfies

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(HY)}}\right]}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(\Tr HY)^{2}}\right]}}}\right)}}.

(3.7)

Since
H
is Hermitian, the entries
h_{\alpha\beta}
and
h_{\gamma\delta}
are independent except for the index pairs
(\alpha,\beta)=(\gamma,\delta)
and
(\alpha,\beta)=(\delta,\gamma)
.
Hence

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(\Tr HY)^{2}}\right]}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta=1}^{KN}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{h_{\alpha\beta}Y_{\beta\alpha}h_{\gamma\delta}Y_{\delta\gamma}}\right]}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta=1}^{KN}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{h_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}\right]}}+\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{\alpha\beta}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}}\right]}}}\right)}}Y_{\beta\alpha}Y_{\delta\gamma}

(3.8)

The blocks
X_{ij}
in our matrix model (
2.1
) are drawn from the complex Ginibre ensemble and therefore we have
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}h_{\alpha\beta}^{2}=0
.
The remaining expectation
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{\alpha\beta}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}
is equal to
\frac{1}{N}s_{ij}
when
\alpha
belongs to the
i
-th and
\beta
to the
j
-th batch of indices, i.e. when
N(i-1)<\alpha\leq Ni
and
N(j-1)<\beta\leq Nj
.
To keep the formulas short, we introduce
E_{N}
as the
N\times N
-matrix that contains
1
in every entry and write
\mathbb{E}\mspace{1mu}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{\alpha\beta}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{2}=(\frac{1}{N}S\otimes E_{N})_{\alpha\beta}
.
Thus

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(H\Psi\Psi^{*})}}\right]}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{KN}(S\otimes E_{N})_{\alpha\beta}Y_{\beta\alpha}Y_{\alpha\beta}}\right)}},\qquad\text{where }Y_{\alpha\beta}=\Psi_{\alpha}\Psi_{\beta}^{*}=(x_{\alpha},\xi_{\alpha})\begin{pmatrix}\overline{x_{\beta}}\!\,\\
\overline{\xi}\!\,_{\beta}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the supertrace is cyclic which allows us to write
Y_{\beta\alpha}Y_{\alpha\beta}=\Psi_{\beta}\Psi_{\alpha}^{*}\Psi_{\alpha}\Psi_{\beta}^{*}=\Str(\Psi_{\alpha}^{*}\Psi_{\alpha}\Psi_{\beta}^{*}\Psi_{\beta})
.
We now replace the Greek index
\alpha=1,\dots,KN
by a composite of two Latin indices
\alpha=(i,l)
, where
i=1,\dots,K
enumerates the blocks in our model (
2.1
) and
l=1,\dots,N
enumerates the entries inside the block:

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Tr(H\Psi\Psi^{*})}}\right]}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}s_{ij}\Str\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{l=1}^{N}\Psi_{(i,l)}^{*}\Psi_{(i,l)}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\Psi_{(j,m)}^{*}\Psi_{(j,m)}}\right]}}}\right)}}.

(3.9)

After we define the supermatrices
Q_{i}:=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\Psi_{(i,l)}^{*}\Psi_{(i,l)}
for
i=1,\dots,K
, we arrive at

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}}\right]}}=\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{KN}}[Dx][\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\overline{\xi}\!\,}]\,\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}s_{ij}\Str(Q_{i}Q_{j})+\mathrm{i}z\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Str(Q_{i})}\right)}}x^{*}x\,.

(3.10)

Observe that
x^{*}x
is the boson-boson block of
Q_{i}
summed over
i=1,\dots,K
.

3.3
Superbosonization

The matrix
Q_{i}
only depends on inner products of
(x_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
,
(\xi_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
and
(\overline{\xi}\!\,_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
.
The remaining degrees of freedom can be integrated out using the
superbosonization formula
. The version from
[
39
]
for
U(n)
-symmetry and
p=q=1
reads

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}[DZ][\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\overline{\zeta}\!\,}]\,F\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{Z}Z&\widetilde{Z}\zeta\\
\widetilde{\zeta}Z&\widetilde{\zeta}\zeta\end{pmatrix}=\int_{D}[DQ]\,\Sdet^{N}(Q)F(Q),\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}a&\sigma\\
\tau&b\end{pmatrix}.

(3.11)

On the left-hand side we have
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\widetilde{Z}=(\overline{Z_{1}}\!\,,\dots,\overline{Z_{n}}\!\,)
,
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n})^{\top}
and
\widetilde{\zeta}=(\overline{\zeta}\!\,_{1},\dots,\overline{\zeta}\!\,_{n})
, where

\zeta_{i}
and
\overline{\zeta}\!\,_{i}
are independent anti-commuting variables.
On the right-hand side the integration domain is
D=(0,\infty)\times U(1)
, i.e.
a
is a real positive number and
b
is a complex number on the unit circle.
The symbols
\sigma
and
\tau
are anti-commuting variables and the integration measure is

[DQ]=\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{a}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b)\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}\underbrace{(a-\sigma b^{-1}\tau)(b-\tau a^{-1}\sigma)}_{=ab-\tau\sigma-\sigma\tau+0=ab}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b)\,b\,\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}.

The measure
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b)
is the Haar measure on
U(1)
.
The integrand on the right-hand side of (
3.11
) also includes the
superdeterminant
of
Q
, which in the case of a
(1|1)\times(1|1)
-supermatrix is simply given by

\Sdet(Q)=\frac{a}{b-\tau a^{-1}\sigma}=\frac{a}{b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1-\frac{\tau\sigma}{ab}}\right)}}^{-1}

and the inverse on the right is defined by the Taylor series of
(1-t)^{-1}
around
t=0
.

We use the superbosonization formula with
n=N
and apply it
K
times, with
Z=(x_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
,
\zeta=(\xi_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
and
\widetilde{\zeta}=(\overline{\xi}\!\,_{(i,l)})_{l=1}^{N}
for
i=1,\dots,K
, and arrive at

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}}\right]}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{(2\pi)^{K}}\int_{(0,\infty)^{K}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{U(1)^{K}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b)\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\prod_{i=1}^{K}b_{i}}\right)}}[\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}]\,\mathrm{e}^{f(Q)}\sum_{i=1}^{K}a_{i},

(3.12)

where
\mathrm{d}a=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\mathrm{d}a_{i}
,
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b)=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b_{i})
,
[\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}]=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\partial_{\sigma_{i}}\partial_{\tau_{i}}
and the exponent is

f(Q)=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}s_{ij}\Str(Q_{i}Q_{j})+\mathrm{i}z\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Str(Q_{i})+N\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\Sdet(Q_{i}),\qquad Q_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&\sigma_{i}\\
\tau_{i}&b_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.13)

3.4
Anti-Commuting Differentiation

From the supertraces we get

\Str(Q_{i})=a_{i}-b_{i},\qquad\Str(Q_{i}Q_{j})=a_{i}a_{j}+\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}-(\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}+b_{i}b_{j})=a_{i}a_{j}-b_{i}b_{j}-\tau_{j}\sigma_{i}-\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}.

For
\log\Sdet(Q_{i})
we use the Taylor expansion
\log(1-x)=1-x+O(x^{2})
to compute

\log\Sdet(Q_{i})=\log(a_{i})-\log(b_{i})-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1-\frac{\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}}{a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}=\log(a_{i})-\log(b_{i})+\frac{\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}}{a_{i}b_{i}}.

Hence the exponent is

\begin{split}f(Q)&=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}a_{j}a_{i}+\mathrm{i}z\sum_{i}a_{i}+N\sum_{i}\log(a_{i})+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}b_{j}b_{i}-\mathrm{i}z\sum_{i}b_{i}-N\sum_{i}\log(b_{i})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}\tau_{j}\sigma_{i}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}+N\sum_{i}\frac{\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}}{a_{i}b_{i}}\\
&=-NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{a}{N}}\right)}}+NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{b}{N}}\right)}}+\sum_{i,j}\tau_{j}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{s_{ji}+s_{ij}}{2N}+\frac{N}{a_{i}b_{i}}\delta_{ij}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\sigma_{i},\end{split}

where the function
I
is defined in (
2.4
).
Note that
S
is symmetric by construction and therefore
\frac{s_{ji}+s_{ij}}{2N}=\frac{1}{N}s_{ij}
.
We now apply the identity (
3.2
) again, with
\xi^{*}
replaced by
\tau^{\top}=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{K})
and
\xi
replaced by
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{K})^{\top}
, to obtain

\begin{split}[\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}]\,\mathrm{e}^{f(Q)}&=\mathrm{e}^{-NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{a}{N}}\right)}}+NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{b}{N}}\right)}}}[\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\tau}]\,\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\tau^{\top}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\frac{1}{N}S+N\diag_{i=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}}\right]}}\sigma}\right)}}\\
&=\mathrm{e}^{-NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{a}{N}}\right)}}+NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{b}{N}}\right)}}}\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\frac{1}{N}S+\diag_{i=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{N}{a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}}\right]}}.\end{split}

To derive (
2.3
), we perform two more steps.
First, we write the integral over the Haar measure
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b_{i})
as a counter-clockwise contour integral over the unit circle around

:
\int_{U(1)}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}(b_{i})\,b_{i}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}}\oint\mathrm{d}b_{i}
.
Second, we perform the coordinate transformation
a_{i}\to Na_{i}
and
b_{i}\to Nb_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-z}}\right)}}}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{K}}\int_{(0,\infty)^{K}}\mathrm{d}a\,N^{K}\oint^{K}\,\mathrm{d}b\,N^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-NI(a)+NI(b)}\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\frac{1}{N}S+\diag_{i=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{N}{N^{2}a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}}\right]}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}Na_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}N^{2K-K+1}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{K}}\int_{(0,\infty)^{K}}\mathrm{d}a\oint^{K}\,\mathrm{d}b\,\mathrm{e}^{-NI(a)+NI(b)}\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag_{i=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{a_{i}b_{i}}}\right)}}}\right]}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}a_{j}.

4

.

4.1

The saddle-points of the

\nabla I(a)=0
, i.e. the coordinates
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
satisfy

1=s_{11}a_{1}^{2}+\frac{s_{12}a_{1}}{s_{12}a_{1}-\mathrm{i}z}-\mathrm{i}za_{1},\qquad a_{2}=\frac{1}{s_{12}a_{1}-\mathrm{i}z}.

(4.1)

The same equations hold for the saddle-points of the
b
-integral, with
a_{j}
replaced by
b_{j}
.
For
z=\eta\zeta
with
\eta\to 0
we see that
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{a_{1}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\asymp\eta^{1/3}
and
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{a_{2}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\asymp\eta^{-1/3}
.
We refer to Section
6.1
for a procedure to determine these rates.

4.1.1
Transformation of the integrals

As
\eta\to 0
the saddle-point has a degenerate limit:
a_{1}\to 0
and
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{a_{2}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\to\infty
.
To correct for this behaviour, we introduce the rescaled coordinates
\widetilde{a}_{1}:=\eta^{-1/3}a_{1}
and
\widetilde{a}_{2}:=\eta^{1/3}a_{2}
and similarly for the
b
coordinates.
Thus, the
I(a)
-term becomes

\begin{split}I(a)&=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\eta^{2/3}s_{11}\widetilde{a}_{1}^{2}+\eta^{0}s_{12}\widetilde{a}_{2}\widetilde{a}_{1}+\eta^{0}s_{21}\widetilde{a}_{1}\widetilde{a}_{2}}\right)}}-\mathrm{i}\eta\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\eta^{1/3}\widetilde{a}_{1}+\eta^{-1/3}\widetilde{a}_{2}}\right)}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log(\eta^{1/3}\widetilde{a}_{1})+\log(\eta^{-1/3}\widetilde{a}_{2})}\right)}}\\
&=s_{12}\widetilde{a}_{1}\widetilde{a}_{2}-\log(\widetilde{a}_{1})-\log(\widetilde{a}_{2})+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\eta^{2/3}}\right)}}\qquad\text{for }\eta\to 0.\end{split}

In the
\eta\to 0
limit, the saddle-points of the rescaled integral satisfy

0=s_{12}\widetilde{a}_{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{a}_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad 0=s_{12}\widetilde{a}_{1}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{a}_{2}}.

However, this system of equations has an infinite number of solutions:
\widetilde{a}_{2}=(s_{12}\widetilde{a}_{1})^{-1}
.
To address this problem, we transform
(\widetilde{a}_{1},\widetilde{a}_{2})
to new variables
(u_{a},v)
such that the saddle-line becomes a saddle-point for
u_{a}
and the action is independent of
v
.
We write
\widetilde{a}_{1}:=u_{a}v
,
\widetilde{a}_{2}:=\frac{u_{a}}{v}
for
u_{a}>0
and
v>0
, then the
u_{a}
-integral has a saddle-point at
u_{a}=(s_{12})^{-1/2}
.
We emphasize that the
v
-integral cannot be approximated by the saddle-point method, we will compute it separately later.
In summary, we transform the
(a_{1},a_{2})
-integral as follows: (
f
= integrand)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a_{2}\,f(a_{1},a_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\,f\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\eta^{1/3}u_{a}v,\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{a}}{v}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}2\frac{u_{a}}{v}.

(4.2)

For the
b
-integral we introduce the new coordinates
u_{b}\in U(1)
and
w\in U(1)
and write
\widetilde{b}_{1}:=u_{b}w
,
\widetilde{b}_{2}:=\frac{u_{b}}{w}
.
Transforming the integrals via
\oint\mathrm{d}b\,f(b)=c\oint\mathrm{d}b\,f(cb)
and

\begin{split}\oint\mathrm{d}\widetilde{b}_{1}\oint\mathrm{d}\widetilde{b}_{2}\,f(\widetilde{b}_{1},\widetilde{b}_{2})&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}t\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}s\,f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t},\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s})\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}\,\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}y\,f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(x+y)},\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(x-y)})\mathrm{i}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}x}2\\
&=\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\oint\mathrm{d}w\,f\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{u_{b}w,\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\frac{u_{b}}{w}\end{split}

shows that

\oint\mathrm{d}b_{1}\oint\mathrm{d}b_{2}\,f(b_{1},b_{2})=\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\oint\mathrm{d}w\,f\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\eta^{1/3}u_{b}w,\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\frac{u_{b}}{w}

(4.3)

The
u_{b}
-integral has two saddle-points:
u_{b}=\pm(s_{12})^{-1/2}
.
In summary, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-\eta\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}&=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}N^{3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\oint\mathrm{d}w\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\eta^{1/3}u_{a}v,\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{a}}{v}}\right)}}+NI\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\eta^{1/3}u_{b}w,\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\right)}}}\\
&\qquad\times\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{\eta^{2/3}u_{a}vu_{b}w},\frac{\eta^{2/3}vw}{u_{a}u_{b}}}\right)}}}\right]}}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\eta^{1/3}u_{a}v+\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{a}}{v}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\frac{u_{a}u_{b}}{vw}.\end{split}

(4.4)

We carry out one more step to simplify this integral representation.
First, we perform the transformations
u_{a}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{12}}}u_{a}
,
u_{b}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{12}}}u_{b}
,
v\to\sqrt{\frac{s_{12}}{s_{11}}}\,v
and
w\to\sqrt{\frac{s_{12}}{s_{11}}}\,w
.
Then we introduce the functions

\displaystyle J(u)

\displaystyle:=u_{a}^{2}-2\log(u_{a})-u_{b}^{2}+2\log(u_{b}),

(4.5)

\displaystyle L(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=N\eta^{2/3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{2}(u_{a}^{2}v^{2}-u_{b}^{2}w^{2})-\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{u_{a}}{v}-\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}}\right)}}+N\eta^{4/3}\frac{s_{12}}{s_{11}}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)(u_{a}v-u_{b}w),

(4.6)

\displaystyle F(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{e}^{-L(u;v,w)}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\eta^{1/3}\frac{s_{12}}{s_{11}}\,u_{a}v+\eta^{-1/3}\frac{u_{a}}{v}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\frac{u_{a}u_{b}}{vw},

(4.7)

\displaystyle D(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{s_{12}^{2}}\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{s_{11}}{\eta^{2/3}u_{a}vu_{b}w},\frac{s_{12}^{2}}{s_{11}}\frac{\eta^{2/3}vw}{u_{a}u_{b}}}\right)}}}\right]}},

(4.8)

where
u:=(u_{a},u_{b})\in(0,\infty)\times U(1)
for brevity.
Now we can write

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-\eta\frac{s_{12}}{\sqrt{s_{11}}}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}&=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}N^{3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\oint\mathrm{d}w\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}\frac{s_{11}^{3/2}}{s_{12}^{3}}F(u;v,w)s_{12}^{2}D(u;v,w)\frac{1}{s_{11}}.\end{split}

(4.9)

As
\eta\to 0
the function
J
contains the leading order terms of
I(a)-I(b)
, while
L
contains the lower order terms times
N
.
In particular, for
\eta\asymp N^{-3/2}
we have
L(u;v,w)=O(1)
.
Therefore, the saddle-points
u^{*}=(u_{a}^{*},u_{b}^{*})
of the
u
-integrals only depend on
J
.
We chose the transformation of
u_{a}
and
u_{b}
such that the saddle-points are given by
u_{a}^{*}=1
and
u_{b}^{*}=\pm 1
.
Then we chose the rescaling of
v
,
w
and
\zeta
such that the leading order of
L
is independent of
s_{11}
and
s_{12}
.
Finally, we defined
D
as the determinant and
F
as the remaining terms in the integrand, choosing appropriate prefactors to make their leading order independent of
s_{11}
and
s_{12}
.
For the
D
-term it will be useful to expand the determinant in
\eta
:

D(u;v,w)=D^{(0)}(u)+\eta^{2/3}D^{(1)}(u;v,w),\qquad\text{where }D^{(0)}(u):=\frac{1}{u_{a}^{2}u_{b}^{2}}-1,\quad D^{(1)}(u;v,w):=\frac{vw}{u_{a}u_{b}}.

(4.10)

Note that the Jacobian of the
(u,v,w)
-transforms is
s_{11}^{-1}
.
The product of the
s_{11}
and
s_{12}
dependent factors leads to the rescaling on the left-hand side of (
2.12
).
In particular, the right-hand side of (
2.12
) is universal, provided that the sub-leading terms of
F
and
L
(which still depend on
s_{11}
and
s_{12}
) do not contribute to the leading order of the integral.
As we will see in the following calculations, the integral over the leading order of
F
and
L
is non-zero, confirming this assumption.

4.1.2
Approximation of the
u
-integral

The critical points and saddle directions of
J(u_{a},u_{b})
are

u^{*}=\begin{cases}(1,+1),\\
(1,-1),\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu=\begin{cases}(1,+\mathrm{i}),\\
(1,-\mathrm{i}),\end{cases}=(1,\mathrm{i}u_{b}^{*})

with second derivatives (note that we substitute
u_{a}^{*}=1
and
(u_{b}^{*})^{2}=1
where possible)

\mu_{a}^{2}(\partial_{u_{a}}^{2}J)(u^{*})=4,\quad\mu_{b}^{2}(\partial_{u_{b}}^{2}J)(u^{*})=4.

We also have
\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u^{*})}=1
for

and the third derivatives

For the functions

and

we will only use the leading terms as
\eta\to 0

\displaystyle F(u_{a},u_{b};v,w)

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1/3}\mathrm{e}^{-L(u_{a},u_{b};v,w)}\frac{u_{a}^{2}u_{b}}{v^{2}w}+O(\eta^{1/3}),

\displaystyle L(u_{a},u_{b};v,w)

\displaystyle=N\eta^{2/3}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{1}{2}(u_{a}^{2}v^{2}-u_{b}^{2}w^{2})-\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{u_{a}}{v}-\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}+O(N\eta^{4/3})

\displaystyle(\partial_{u_{a}}F)(u^{*};v,w)

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1/3}\mathrm{e}^{-L(u^{*};v,w)}\frac{1}{v^{2}w}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{-(\partial_{u_{a}}L)(u^{*};v,w)+2}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}u_{b}^{*}+O(\eta^{1/3}),

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1/3}\mathrm{e}^{-L(u^{*};v,w)}\frac{1}{v^{2}w}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{(\partial_{u_{b}}L)(u^{*};v,w)\,u_{b}^{*}+1}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}u_{b}^{*}+O(\eta^{1/3}),

\displaystyle(\partial_{u_{a}}L)(u^{*};v,w)

\displaystyle=N\eta^{2/3}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{v^{2}-\mathrm{i}\zeta\frac{1}{v}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}+O(N\eta^{4/3}),

For

.
Then for

\displaystyle(\partial_{u_{a}}D^{(0)})(u^{*})=-2,\quad(\partial_{u_{b}}D^{(0)})(u^{*})=\frac{2}{u_{b}^{*}},

\displaystyle(\partial_{u_{a}}^{2}D^{(0)})(u^{*})=6,\quad(\partial_{u_{a}}\partial_{u_{b}}D^{(0)})(u^{*})=\frac{4}{u_{b}^{*}},\quad(\partial_{u_{b}}^{2}D^{(0)})(u^{*})=6.

Using the saddle-point method we obtain the following approximations

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(0)}\\

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(1)}=\sum_{u^{*}}\frac{\mu_{a}\mu_{b}}{\sqrt{N}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}F_{0}D_{0}^{(1)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N}}\right)}}}\right]}}\\

6
Weak Non-Chirality Limit

In this section we prove Theorem
2.7
, i.e. we let
s_{11}\to 0
(for
K=2
) or
s_{12}\to 0
(for
K=3
) as
N\to\infty
and derive the leading order of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr((H-z)^{-1})}\right]}}
as
z\to 0
with
N\to\infty
.
Note that the convergence rate of
z
depends on the rate of
s_{11}
and
s_{12}
, respectively.

6.1
Case
K=2

We start with the assumption that the convergence rates of
s_{11}
and
z
are
s_{11}\asymp N^{-\alpha}
and
z\asymp N^{-\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Later we will choose the rate
\beta
depending on
\alpha
in such a way that we get a non-trivial scaling limit.
From Section
4
we already know that
\beta=\frac{3}{2}
is the appropriate rate when
\alpha=0
.
Furthermore, by comparing with the chiral GUE we see that
\beta=1
for
\alpha\to\infty
.
Let the block variance profile be

S=\begin{pmatrix}N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{11}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{11}\in[0,\infty),\ \alpha\geq 0

and
z=N^{-\beta}\zeta
with
\Im(\zeta)>0
and
\beta\geq 1
.

Analogous to Section
4
, we get the saddle-point equations

\frac{1}{a_{1}}=-\mathrm{i}N^{-\beta}\zeta+N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{11}+a_{2},\qquad\frac{1}{a_{2}}=-\mathrm{i}N^{-\beta}\zeta+a_{1}.

Further assuming that
a_{1}\asymp N^{-c}
for
c<\beta
leads to
a_{2}\asymp N^{c}
via the second equation.
By comparing the first equation times
a_{1}
and the second equation times
a_{2}
we obtain

a_{1}=\frac{-\mathrm{i}N^{-\beta}\zeta a_{2}}{-\mathrm{i}N^{-\beta}\zeta+N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{11}a_{1}}\asymp\frac{N^{-\beta+c}}{N^{-\beta}+N^{-\alpha-c}}\sim N^{-\beta+c-\max(-\beta,-\alpha-c)}.

Since we started with
a_{1}\asymp N^{-c}
, we choose
c=\max(-c,\beta-2c-\alpha)
to get a non-trivial solution for the saddle-point equations.
Further simplification leads to
c=\frac{1}{3}\max(\beta-\alpha,0)
.

As in section
4
we transform the integration variables
(a_{1},a_{2})=(N^{-c}u_{a}v,N^{c}\frac{u_{a}}{v})
for
u_{a},v>0
and
(b_{1},b_{2})=(N^{-c}u_{b}w,N^{c}\frac{u_{b}}{w})
for
u_{b},w\in U(1)
and arrive at

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-N^{-\beta}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}&=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}N^{3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\oint\mathrm{d}w\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u;v,w)D(u;v,w),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle J(u)

\displaystyle:=u_{a}^{2}-2\log(u_{a})-u_{b}^{2}+2\log(u_{b}),

\displaystyle L(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=N^{1-\alpha-2c}\frac{\sigma_{11}}{2}(u_{a}^{2}v^{2}-u_{b}^{2}w^{2})+N^{1-\beta+c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{u_{a}}{v}-\frac{u_{b}}{w}}\right)}}+N^{1-\beta-c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)(u_{a}v-u_{b}w),

\displaystyle F(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=N^{c}\mathrm{e}^{-L(u;v,w)}\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v^{2}w}u_{a}^{2}u_{b},

\displaystyle D(u_{a},u_{b};v,w)

\displaystyle:=\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{N^{2c}}{u_{a}vu_{b}w},\frac{N^{-2c}vw}{u_{a}u_{b}}}\right)}}}\right]}}=D^{(0)}(u)+N^{-\alpha-2c}D^{(1)}(u;v,w)

and

D^{(0)}(u):=\frac{1}{u_{a}^{2}u_{b}^{2}}-1,\qquad D^{(1)}(u;v,w):=\sigma_{11}\frac{vw}{u_{a}u_{b}}.

To get a non-trivial scaling limit, at least one of the powers of
N
in the
L
-function must be zero.
Setting
1-\alpha-2c=0
and
1-\beta+c=0
with
c=\frac{1}{3}\max(\beta-\alpha,0)
gives the solution

\begin{cases}\beta=\frac{1}{2}(3-\alpha),\;c=\frac{1}{3}(\beta-\alpha)&\text{for }\alpha<1,\\
\beta=1,\;c=0&\text{for }\alpha\geq 1.\end{cases}

In particular, when
\alpha>1
the term in
L(u;v,w)
which is proportional to
N^{1-\alpha-2c}
will be subleading and the integral over
N^{-\alpha-2c}D^{(1)}(u;v,w)
will not contribute to the leading order.

We perform a saddle-point approximation to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(0)}&\sim\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{4N^{2}}N^{c}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{11}v^{2}+\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{v}+N^{-2c}v}\right)}}}\sum_{u_{b}^{*}=\pm 1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{11}w^{2}-\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{w}+N^{-2c}w}\right)}}u_{b}^{*}}\\
&\qquad\times\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v^{2}w}\bigg[\sigma_{11}v^{2}+(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v}-1\\
&\hphantom{\qquad\times\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v^{2}w}\bigg[}+\sigma_{11}w^{2}+(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1+N^{-2c}w^{2}}{w}u_{b}^{*}\bigg]\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(1)}&\sim\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2N}N^{c}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{11}v^{2}+\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{v}+N^{-2c}v}\right)}}}\sum_{u_{b}^{*}=\pm 1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{11}w^{2}-\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{w}+N^{-2c}w}\right)}}u_{b}^{*}}\\
&\qquad\times\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v}\sigma_{11}u_{b}^{*}.\end{split}

Next, we want to evaluate the
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v
and
\oint\mathrm{d}w
integrals.
After we expand the integrand, we see that the
v
-integrals are of the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{e}^{-t_{1}v^{2}+t_{2}v+t_{3}\frac{1}{v}}v^{n}\qquad\text{with }t_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{11},\ t_{2}=\mathrm{i}\zeta N^{-2c},\ t_{3}=\mathrm{i}\zeta\text{ and }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

If
t_{1}=0
(i.e. when
\sigma_{11}=0
), we get a result in terms of Bessel-
K
functions, see (
4.18
).
If
t_{2}=0
(i.e. when
c>0
and
N\to\infty
), we get a result in terms of Meijer
G
-functions, see (
4.11
).
But for the general case, we did not find a formula in terms of special functions.
Thus, we leave this integral unevaluated in Theorem
2.7
.
In contrast, the
w
-integral can be performed as in Section
4

\displaystyle\int_{U(1)}\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{e}^{t_{1}w^{2}+t_{2}w+t_{3}\frac{1}{w}}w^{r}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{1}^{k}t_{2}^{l}t_{3}^{2k+l+1+r}}{k!\,l!\,(2k+l+1+r)!},\quad r\in\mathbb{Z}.

Using this formula, it is easy to see that the sum over
u_{b}^{*}=\pm 1
simplifies to
2
times the
u_{b}^{*}=1
case.
If
t_{1}=0
, only the
k=0
terms are non-zero and the sum over
l
gives a Bessel-
I
function, see (
4.19
).
If
t_{2}=0
, only the
l=0
terms are non-zero and the sum over
k
can be written in terms of a generalized hypergeometric function, see (
4.13
).
However, we did not find a formula to express the general double-sum in terms of special functions.

For the possible cases of
\alpha
we arrive at the following results:

\alpha<1

: Then
\beta>\alpha
and
c>0
and the large-
N
limit of the expectation of the resolvent is the same as in Theorem
2.2
with an additional
N^{-\alpha/2}
factor.

\alpha=1

: Then
\beta=1
and
c=0
and we get the formula in Theorem
2.7
.
The coefficients
c_{n,r}
follow from the expansion

\frac{1+v^{2}}{v^{2}w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sigma_{11}v^{2}+(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1+v^{2}}{v}-1+\sigma_{11}w^{2}+(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1+w^{2}}{w}+2\sigma_{11}vw}\right]}}=\sum_{n,r\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n,r}v^{n}w^{r}.

In the special case

we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-N^{-1}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}\sim\frac{1}{4\pi}N\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\zeta(\frac{1}{v}+v)}\oint\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\zeta(\frac{1}{w}+w)}\frac{1+v^{2}}{v^{2}w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{1}{v}+v+\frac{1}{w}+w}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}-1}\right]}}.

The
v
- and
w
-integral can be evaluated via (

) and (
4.19
), respectively.
Finally, we use
[
21
,
(10.29.1)
]
to simplify the resulting combinations of Bessel-

\alpha>1

: Then

and

and
\sigma_{11}=0

Let

with

.
Then the saddle-point equations are

Assuming

from the third equation.

This is also consistent with
\beta=2
for

(from section

) and
\beta=1
for
\alpha\to\infty

We transform the integration variables

\begin{split}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{-c}u_{a1}v,u_{a2},N^{c}\frac{u_{a1}}{v}}\right)}}\qquad\text{for }u_{a1},u_{a2},v>0,\\
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{-c}u_{b1}w,u_{b2},N^{c}\frac{u_{b1}}{w}}\right)}}\qquad\text{for }u_{b1},u_{b2},w\in U(1)\end{split}

and arrive at

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-N^{-\beta}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}&=-\frac{N^{4}}{4\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\int_{U(1)}\mathrm{d}w\int_{(0,\infty)^{2}}\mathrm{d}u_{a}\oint^{2}\mathrm{d}u_{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u;v,w)D(u;v,w),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle J(u)

\displaystyle:=u_{a1}^{2}-2\log(u_{a1})+\frac{1}{2}u_{a2}^{2}-\log(u_{a2})-u_{b1}^{2}+2\log(u_{b1})-\frac{1}{2}u_{b2}^{2}+\log(u_{b2}),

\displaystyle L(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=N^{1-\alpha-c}\sigma_{12}(u_{a1}u_{a2}v-u_{b1}u_{b2}w)+N^{1-\beta+c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{u_{a1}}{v}-\frac{u_{b1}}{w}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}

\displaystyle F(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{e}^{-L(u;v,w)}\frac{N^{-c}u_{a1}v^{2}+u_{a2}v+N^{c}u_{a1}}{v^{2}w}u_{a1}u_{b1},

\displaystyle D(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{S+\diag\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{N^{2c}}{u_{a1}vu_{b1}w},\frac{1}{u_{a2}u_{b2}},\frac{N^{-2c}vw}{u_{a1}u_{b1}}}\right)}}}\right]}}=D^{(0)}(u)+N^{-2\alpha-2c}D^{(1)}(u;v,w).

and

D^{(0)}(u):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{u_{a1}^{2}u_{b1}^{2}}-1}\right)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{u_{a2}u_{b2}}+1}\right)}},\qquad D^{(1)}(u;v,w):=-\sigma_{12}^{2}\frac{vw}{u_{a1}u_{b1}}.

Examining the powers of

\beta=2-\alpha

are the appropriate rates when

and
\beta=1
,

A saddle-point approximation yields

\begin{split}\int\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(0)}&\sim-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2N^{3}}N^{c}\sum_{u_{b1}^{*}=\pm 1}\mathrm{e}^{-L(1,1,u_{b1}^{*},1;v,w)}\\
&\quad\times\Bigg[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{1-\alpha-c}\sigma_{12}v+N^{1-\beta+c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1}{v}+N^{1-\beta-c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)v}\right)}}\frac{1+N^{-c}v+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v^{2}w}\\
&\qquad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{1-\alpha-c}\sigma_{12}w+N^{1-\beta+c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)\frac{1}{w}+N^{1-\beta-c}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)w}\right)}}\frac{1+N^{-c}v+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{v^{2}w}u_{b1}^{*}\\
&\qquad-\frac{1+N^{-2c}v^{2}}{w}\Bigg].\end{split}

Observe that
\int\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ}FD^{(1)}=O(N^{-2+c})
, therefore the
N^{-2\alpha-2c}D^{(1)}
term cannot contribute to the leading order of the final result.

Next, we evaluate the
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v
and
\oint\mathrm{d}w
integrals with (
4.18
) and (
4.19
).
Using
[
21
,
(10.29.1)
]
, we simplify the resulting Bessel-
K
and Bessel-
I
functions and arrive at

\begin{split}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{H-N^{-\beta}\zeta}}\right)}}}\right]}}&\sim 2\mathrm{i}N^{2}\Big[(N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{12}-2N^{-\beta}\mathrm{i}\zeta)\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{K_{0}(x)I_{0}(x)+K_{1}(x)I_{1}(x)}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\\
&\hphantom{=2\mathrm{i}N^{2}\Big[}+\sqrt{N^{-\beta}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)(N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{12}-N^{-\beta}\mathrm{i}\zeta)}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{K_{1}(x)I_{0}(x)+K_{0}(x)I_{1}(x)}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\Big]\end{split}

where
x=2N\sqrt{N^{-\beta}(-\mathrm{i}\zeta)(N^{-\alpha}\sigma_{12}-N^{-\beta}\mathrm{i}\zeta)}
.
Using the formula
[
21
,
(10.28.2)
]
for the Wronskian of
I_{\nu}
and
K_{\nu}
we see that the term on the second line is equal to
\frac{1}{2N}
.

If
\alpha<1
, then
\beta=2-\alpha
and
c=1-\alpha
and for
N\to\infty
we arrive again at the result for the strong non-chirality limit from Theorem
2.2
.
If
\alpha=1
, then
\beta=1
and
c=0
and we obtain the formula for Theorem
2.7
.
The case
\alpha>1
yields the same large-
N
limit as
\alpha=1
with
\sigma_{12}=0
.

Appendix A
Multi-Dimensional Saddle-Point Method

Let us explain the one-dimensional saddle-point method first.
Consider the integral
I_{N}:=\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{NS(u)}f(u)
,
where
\gamma
is a contour in
\mathbb{C}
and
S,f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
are analytic.
We want to find an asymptotic expansion of
I_{N}
as
N\to\infty
.
The idea of the
method of steepest descent
(also known as the
saddle-point method
) is to deform the contour
\gamma
into a contour
\tilde{\gamma}
without changing the endpoints and such that

(i)

\tilde{\gamma}
passes through a critical point
u^{*}
of
S
(i.e.
S^{\prime}(u^{*})=0
),

(ii)

\Im(S(u))=\Im(S(u^{*}))
holds on some neighbourhood of
u^{*}
on
\tilde{\gamma}
,

(iii)

\Re(S(u))<\Re(S(u^{*}))
for
u\neq u^{*}
on
\tilde{\gamma}
(or equivalently,
\max_{u\in\tilde{\gamma}}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\mathrm{e}^{NS(u)}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}
is attained only at
u^{*}
).

Let
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
be a portion of
\tilde{\gamma}
in a neighbourhood of
u^{*}
where (ii) holds.
Then
I_{N}=I_{N,\varepsilon}+I_{N,\varepsilon}^{\prime}
, where
I_{N,\varepsilon}:=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{NS(u)}f(u)
and
I_{N,\varepsilon}^{\prime}:=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}\setminus\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{NS(u)}f(u)
.
We can estimate
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{I_{N,\varepsilon}^{\prime}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\mathrm{e}^{NS(u^{*})}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}
for some constants
C>0
and
\delta>0
(for example,
\delta=-\sup_{u\in\tilde{\gamma}\setminus\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}}\Re(S(u)-S(u^{*}))
).
For
I_{N,\varepsilon}
we first parametrize
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
by
u(x)=u^{*}+\mu x
, where
x\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
and
\mu\in U(1)
is the contour direction.
We extend the integration range from
x\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
to
x\in(-\infty,\infty)
, this introduces only an error of size
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\mathrm{e}^{NS(u^{*})}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}O(\mathrm{e}^{-N\varepsilon})
.
Finally, we use Laplace’s method the derive the asymptotic expansion

I_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{-S^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})}}\mathrm{e}^{NS(u^{*})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({f(u^{*})+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{-1}}\right)}}}\right)}},\qquad\text{where }\arg\sqrt{-S^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)}}.

More generally, if there are several discrete critical points
u^{*}
where
\Re S
attains its maximum and that can be reached by deforming the integration contour, then each of these points contributes to the final result.
A similar asymptotic formula exists when the maximum of
\Re S
is attained at the one of the endpoints of
\gamma
.

For the integrals in Section
4
we need the
d
-dimensional version of the saddle-point method including the lower order terms.
The asymptotic expansion in this case has been derived before by Fedoryuk
[
25
]
(see also
[
26
]
) and reads

\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{NS(u)}f(u)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{2\pi}{N}}\right)}}^{d/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(-S^{\prime\prime}(u^{*}))}}\mathrm{e}^{NS(u^{*})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({f(u^{*})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}N^{-k}}\right)}},

where
c_{k}
depends on
S,f
and their derivatives at
u^{*}
.
The choice of branch for
\sqrt{\det(-S^{\prime\prime}(u^{*}))}
depends on the orientation of the contour.

In the following we present a derivation of the asymptotic expansion for the integral
\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u)D(u)
, where
\gamma
is a
d
-dimensional contour and
J,F,D:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
are analytic in the region where we will deform the contour.
Let
J
have (at least) one non-degenerate critical point
u^{*}
that is not at the boundary of
\gamma
and let
F(u^{*})\neq 0
while
D
and derivatives of
D
can be zero at
u^{*}
.
Furthermore, we assume that
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}J(u^{*})=0
for
i\neq j
(for example, this is the case when
J(u)=\widetilde{J}_{1}(u_{1})+\dots+\widetilde{J}_{d}(u_{d})
).
This last assumption simplifies the parametrization of
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
and helps selecting the correct branch of
\sqrt{\det J^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})}
in the final expression.

Step 1: Critical points of
J

Let
u^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a non-degenerate critical point of
J
, i.e.
\partial_{i}J(u^{*})=0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
and
\det J^{\prime\prime}(u^{*})\neq 0
, where
\partial_{i}
is the derivative with respect to the variable
u_{i}
and
J^{\prime\prime}=(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}J)_{i,j}
is the Hessian.
We deform the contour
\gamma
into
\tilde{\gamma}
so that (i), (ii) and (iii) from above hold and denote by
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
a neighbourhood of
u^{*}
in
\tilde{\gamma}
where (ii) holds.
Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\mu_{i}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}=1
and such that
\mu_{i}^{2}\,\partial_{i}^{2}J(u^{*})>0
.
In practice we use
\mu_{i}=\pm\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\arg(\partial_{i}^{2}J(u^{*}))}
and choose the sign such that
\mu_{i}
has the same orientation as the contour
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
.
Now we parametrize
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
as
u=u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}
, where
y\in(-\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon,\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon)^{d}
and
\mu y:=(\mu_{i}y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,d}
is a shorthand for entrywise multiplication of two vectors.
Then

\begin{split}&\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u)D(u)=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u)D(u)+\int_{\tilde{\gamma}\setminus\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u)D(u)\\
&\quad=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}}\right)}}\\
&\qquad+O(N^{-d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-N\varepsilon-\Re(J(u^{*}))})+O(\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta-\Re(J(u^{*}))})\end{split}

for some
\delta>0
.

Step 2: Taylor expansion

For a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
define
\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\alpha}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
,
\alpha!:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}!
,
(\mu y)^{\alpha}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\mu_{i}y_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\partial^{\alpha}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
.
Then introduce for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the polynomials

J_{k}(y):=\sum_{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\alpha}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}=k}\frac{(\mu y)^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}J(u^{*}),\qquad F_{k}(y):=\sum_{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\alpha}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}=k}\frac{(\mu y)^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}F(u^{*}),\qquad D_{k}(y):=\sum_{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\alpha}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}=k}\frac{(\mu y)^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}D(u^{*}).

Then
J\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N^{-k/2}J_{k}(y)
and similar for
F
and
D
.
Via Taylor-expansion we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}\\
&\quad=\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}\bigg(F_{0}D_{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{F_{1}D_{0}+F_{0}D_{1}-F_{0}D_{0}J_{3}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\\
&\hphantom{\quad=\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}\bigg(}+\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{F_{2}D_{0}+F_{1}D_{1}+F_{0}D_{2}-F_{1}D_{0}J_{3}-F_{0}D_{1}J_{3}+\frac{1}{2}F_{0}D_{0}J_{3}^{2}-F_{0}D_{0}J_{4}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\\
&\hphantom{\quad=\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}\bigg(}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}}\right)}}\bigg),\end{split}

where
J_{k}\equiv J_{k}(y)
,
F_{k}\equiv F_{k}(y)
and
D_{k}\equiv D_{k}(y)
for brevity.

Step 3: Integral over polynomials

For
\Re(a)>0
the Gaussian integral over a monomial is

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}ay^{2}}y^{k}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2\pi}\,(k-1)!!\,a^{-\frac{k+1}{2}},&k\text{ even},\\
0,&k\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

If
J_{2}
contains no mixed derivatives, i.e.
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}J(u^{*})=0
for
i\neq j
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}y^{\alpha}=0\qquad\text{if and only if some entry $\alpha_{i}$ is odd}.

In particular, in the Taylor expansion any term of order
N^{-k/2}
with
k
odd will integrate to 0, e.g.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{F_{1}D_{0}+F_{0}D_{1}-F_{0}D_{0}J_{3}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}=0.

Step 4: Compute the leading order

Assume that
F_{0}\neq 0
and define
h_{i}:=\partial_{i}^{2}J(u^{*})
for brevity.

If
D_{0}\neq 0
, then for
N\to\infty
the leading order is

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}=\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}F_{0}D_{0}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N}}\right)}}}\right)}}\\
&\quad=(2\pi)^{d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u^{*})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{i}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{-1/2}}\right)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({F(u^{*})D(u^{*})+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N}}\right)}}}\right)}}.\end{split}

If
D_{0}=0
, then
F_{2}D_{0}-F_{1}D_{0}J_{3}+\frac{1}{2}F_{0}D_{0}J_{3}^{2}-F_{0}D_{0}J_{4}=0
and thus

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({u^{*}+\frac{\mu y}{\sqrt{N}}}\right)}}\\
&\quad=\mathrm{e}^{-NJ_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{-J_{2}}\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{F_{1}D_{1}+F_{0}D_{2}-F_{0}D_{1}J_{3}}\right]}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N^{2}}}\right)}}}\right)}}\\
&\quad=(2\pi)^{d/2}\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u^{*})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{h_{i}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}^{-1/2}}\right)}}\\
&\qquad\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left[\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{1}{h_{i}}\partial_{i}F(u^{*})\,\partial_{i}D(u^{*})+\frac{1}{2h_{i}}F(u^{*})\,\partial_{i}^{2}D(u^{*})-\frac{1}{2h_{i}^{2}}F(u^{*})\,\partial_{i}D(u^{*})\,\partial_{i}^{3}J(u^{*})}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left]\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{1}{N^{2}}}\right)}}}\right)}}.\end{split}

Summary

As
N\to\infty

\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u)}F(u)D(u)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{2\pi}{N}}\right)}}^{d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-NJ(u^{*})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\lvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\partial_{i}^{2}J(u^{*})}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left\rvert\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}}}}\right)}}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({F(u^{*})D(u^{*})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}N^{-k}}\right)}}.
