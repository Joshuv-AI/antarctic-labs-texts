Enumeration of even dimensional partitions modulo 4
Aditya Khanna

Abstract
. The number of standard Young tableaux possible of shape corresponding to a partition λ \lambda is called the dimension of the partition and is denoted by f λ f^{\lambda} . Partitions with odd dimensions were enumerated by McKay and were further characterized by Macdonald using the theory of 2-core towers. We use the same theory to extend the results to partitions of n n with dimensions congruent to 2 modulo 4 which are enumerated by a 2 ​ ( n ) a_{2}(n) . We provide explicit results for a 2 ​ ( n ) a_{2}(n) when n n has no consecutive 1s in its binary expansion and give a recursive formula to compute a 2 ​ ( n ) a_{2}(n) for all n n .

Introduction

1.1.
Background

We call
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
a
partition of
n
if the entries are weakly decreasing and sum to
n
. The
dimension of the partition
\lambda

f^{\lambda}

2.1
) on shape
\lambda
. Denote by
m_{p}(n)
, the number of partitions of
n
with dimension not divisible by
p\in\mathbb{N}

McKay
[
6
]

m_{2}(n)=2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}}
.
In particular,
m_{2}(n)
enumerates partitions of
n
whose dimensions are odd. We call these
odd partitions
and will denote their count by
a(n)

Recently, there has been some interest in extending Macdonald’s results. In their paper, Amrutha P and T. Geetha
[
1
]
tackle the problem of computing
m_{2^{k}}(n)
. Their equation (6) gives a general solution to this problem but the results are not amenable to enumeration.
They provide explicit results for
m_{4}(n)
when
n=2^{\ell}
which follows from
2

m_{4}(n)
for a general
n
(
1
) and find a special case that lends itself to a pleasing closed form (
2
).
We prove this via the theory of 2-core towers which has been used to enumerate chiral partitions
[
4
]
and other representations of Coxeter groups
[
2
]
. The main motivation for computing values modulo 4 comes from the classification of spinorial representations of the symmetric group
[
3
]

1.2.
Main Results

Let
a_{i}(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
with dimension congruent to
i
modulo 4. Then the number of odd partitions is
a(n)=m_{2}(n)=a_{1}(n)+a_{3}(n)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n=2^{R}+m
such that
m<2^{R}
. Then we have

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\binom{2^{R-1}}{2}\cdot a(m),&\text{ if }m<2^{R-1}\\
2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\left(\binom{2^{R-1}}{3}+2^{R-1}\right)\cdot\displaystyle{\frac{a(m)}{2^{R-1}}},&\text{ if }2^{R-1}\leq m<2^{R}.\end{cases}

2.2.
Hooks and hook-lengths

We call the boxes in Ferrers diagrams
cells
. We label the cell in the

i^{\mathrm{th}}

row from top and

j^{\mathrm{th}}

column from left by
(i,j)
. For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})

(i,j)\in\sh(\lambda)
if
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i}

\sh(\lambda)

if the pair
(i,j),(i,j+1)\in\sh(\lambda)

(i,j),(i+1,j)\in\sh(\lambda)
can be removed and the rows still weakly decrease in size in the new shape. The condition of being able to remove
(i,j),(i,j+1)\in\sh(\lambda)
is equivalent to
\lambda
containing a pair of parts such that
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}+2
. The other condition of being able to remove
(i,j),(i+1,j)\in\sh(\lambda)
is equivalent to
\lambda
containing more than one part of size
j
. A partition
\lambda
that does not contain a removable domino is called a
2-core.

Lemma 6
.

A partition
\lambda
is a 2-core if and only if
\lambda=(n,n-1,\ldots 3,2,1)
for some
n\geq 0

Proof.

A partition which has no consecutive parts that differ by more than 1 in size and contains no repeated parts must have all parts which are consecutive integers ending in 1. The converse is easy to check.

.

A sequence
\mathbf{w}_{k}
corresponds to constructing a 2-core tower of
\lambda
with
v_{2}(f^{\lambda})=1
such that
w_{i}(\lambda)=w^{k}_{i}(n)
, for some
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
. The quantity
\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n))
counts all such partitions.

Proof of
1
.

Fix an
n\geq 1
. By the above discussion as well as
13
, we have that the number of partitions with dimensions congruent to 2 modulo 4 is given by

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n)).

Denote the binary expansion of
n
by
\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}b_{i}2^{i}
as before. If we suppose
n=2^{R}+m
with
m<2^{R}
, then
b_{R}=1
and
b_{i}=0
for
i>R
. For
k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)=\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}
, we have
w^{k}_{i}(n)=w^{k}_{i}(m)
for all
i\neq R
(*). We break the sum up:

a_{2}(n)=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))\right)+\T(\mathbf{w}_{R}(n)).

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\T_{R}(w^{k}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=2^{R}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))\qquad{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\text{(as }w_{R}^{k}(n)=b_{R}=1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{R}}{\T_{R}(0)}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\T_{R}(0)\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(m))\quad{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\text{(by (*))}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{R}}{\T_{R}(0)}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(m))

\displaystyle=2^{R}a_{2}(m).

Now, we deal with the second term:

\displaystyle\T(\mathbf{w}_{R}(n))

\displaystyle=\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R,R-1}\T_{i}(w^{R}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R,R-1}\T_{i}(b_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))}{\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1})\T_{R}(b_{R})}a(n).

The appearance of
a(n)
is the consequence of
12
and the cases
w=0,1
in
15
. We have
b_{R}=1
,
w^{R}_{R}(n)=0
and
w^{R}_{R-1}(n)=b_{R-1}+2
. This simplifies the above expression to

\frac{\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1}+2)}{2^{R}\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1})}a(n).

By
15
and
a(n)=2^{R}a(m)
, we can complete the proof to get

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\binom{2^{R-1}}{2}\cdot a(m),&\text{ if }m<2^{R-1}\\
2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\left(\binom{2^{R-1}}{3}+2^{R-1}\right)\cdot\displaystyle{\frac{a(m)}{2^{R-1}}},&\text{ if }2^{R-1}<m<2^{R}.\end{cases}

The first case corresponds to
b_{R-1}=0
and the second one corresponds to
b_{R-1}=1
.
∎

Recall that a
sparse
number has no consecutive 1s in its binary expansion. In that case, this theorem takes a closed form as evident in
2
. Although we can show the corollary using the recursive relations in we just proved, we choose to do perform direct computations as we believe they are more illuminating.

Proof of
2
.

Let
n
be a sparse number. The summation as before is:

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\T_{k-1}(w^{k}_{k-1}(n))\T_{k}(w^{k}_{k}(n))}{\T_{k-1}(b_{k-1})\T_{k}(b_{k})}a(n).

As
n
is sparse, for
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
, we have
b_{k-1}=0
,
w^{k}_{k}(n)=0
and
w^{k}_{k-1}(n)=b_{k-1}+2=2
.

This gives us the following summation,

a_{2}(n)=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\T_{k-1}(2)}{\T_{k}(1)}a(n).

By using the results from
15
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=a(n)\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\binom{2^{k-1}}{2}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle=a(n)\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{(2^{k-1})(2^{k-1}-1)}{2\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a(n)}{8}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}2^{k}-2.

If
n
is even, then this summation becomes
n-2\nu(n)
. If
n
is odd, the summation becomes
(n-1)+2(\nu(n)-1)=(n-1)+2(\nu(n-1))
. This gives us the final answer as,

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{a(n)}{8}(n-2\nu(n))},&\text{if }n\text{ is even}\\
a_{2}(n-1),&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

∎

Remark 17
.

The theorem above immediately leads us to the value of
m_{4}(n)
. We have
m_{4}(n)=a_{1}(n)+a_{2}(n)+a_{3}(n)=a(n)+a_{2}(n)
by definition. If the number of partitions of
n
is denoted by
p(n)
, then the number of partitions with dimensions divisible by 4 is given by
p(n)-a(n)-a_{2}(n)
.

4.
Closing Remarks and Open Problems

This paper deals with the case of 2 modulo 4 which can be generalized. One may wish to compute even dimensional partitions modulo
2^{k}
for higher values of
k
but the recursions have more cases and too many terms to be of practical use. Another generalization might be to compute partitions with dimensions
p
modulo
p^{2}
, but this endeavor is also racked with a multitude of cases and cumbersome calculations. The recursions obtained here and the specialization in the sparse case make this problem perfectly situated to solve explicitly.
