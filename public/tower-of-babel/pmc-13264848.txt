Road safety policies and motor vehicle fatalities in Ecuador: a geographical and temporal analysis from 1990 to 2022
Authors: Alejandro Rodriguez, Maria Alejandra Ruano, Mercy Julia Borbor, Peter Craig, Jonathan R Olsen, Micaela Calderon, Gervasio Ferreira Santos, Roberto F S Andrade, José Firmino de Sousa Filho, Natalia Cristina Romero-Sandoval, Mauricio Lima Barreto, Philip Cooper, Alastair Leyland, on behalf of the Unit on the Social and Environmental Determinants of Health Inequalities (SEDHI)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEcuador has implemented several road safety policies (RSPs), which may have differentially impacted motor vehicle fatalities (MVFs) across time, geography and population groups. This study aimed to analyse temporal and geographical trends in MVF in Ecuador from 1990 to 2022 and to explore how RSPs are related to these changes.MethodsAn ecological time-series study was conducted using data from death certificates that listed MVF. Crude and age-standardised mortality rates were estimated for the entire population and crude rates for sex, age, geographical region and province. RSPs in Ecuador from 1990 to 2022 were documented, and Joinpoint analyses were used to relate the dates of these policies to changes in temporal and geographical trends.ResultsMVF remained unchanged from 20.17 deaths per 100 000 population in 1990 to 20.31 in 2022. Four significant trends were identified: MVF decreased on average by 3.1% (95% CI −4.5% to 1.9%) per year between 1990 and 2002, increased by 5.6% (95% CI 3.4% to 9.5%) between 2002 and 2010, decreased by 2.8% (95% CI −5.8% to −1.6%) between 2010 and 2020 and increased by 11.3% (95% CI 0.3% to 17.5%) between 2020 and 2022. Between 1990 and 2022, MVF declined by 1.4% (95% CI −2% to −1%) per year for females, 3.6% (95% CI −4.9% to −3.1%) for those aged ≤14 and 2.5% (95% CI −3.1% to −2%) for those aged ≥65. Geographical analyses showed a declining annual trend of 1.8% (95% CI −2.2% to −1.4%) in the Andean region and 0.7% (95% CI −1.3% to −0.3%) in urban areas. Trends of decreasing MVF were seen in only 4 of the 24 provinces.ConclusionsOver the last 30 years, the implementation of multiple policies to improve road safety in Ecuador has been accompanied by fluctuating trends in MVF but no overall decrease. A possible explanation is the impact of economic crises on increasing MVF, which has attenuated the beneficial effects of RSPs.

Introduction

Road traffic accidents are a significant source of deaths and serious injuries worldwide, with economic and social impacts related to loss of productivity, healthcare costs and reduced quality of life.1 The WHO estimated 1.19 million deaths worldwide caused by road traffic accidents in 2021, mainly affecting the male population and young people aged 5–29 years.2 In many low and middle-income countries (LMICs), motor vehicle fatalities (MVFs) are among the top 10 causes of death, representing a significant public health problem.1 2

Fatalities caused by car accidents have now reached epidemic levels in LMICs, with a doubling in the fatality rates compared with high-income countries (HICs), and represent approximately 90% of global deaths from this cause.3 In the case of Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), car accidents are estimated to be responsible for 110 000 deaths and 5 million injuries annually, costing the region between 3% and 5% of its gross domestic product (GDP).4 Additionally, road traffic accidents in LAC are a major cause of premature deaths, the leading cause of death in children aged 5–14 years and the second leading cause of death for young adults.4 Among road users, motorcyclists, pedestrians and cyclists account for most traffic fatalities in the region, with motorcyclists representing 26.5%, followed by pedestrians at 23.5% and cyclists at 7.1%, with slight variations between subregions.5

Several road safety policies (RSPs) have been implemented to reduce deaths from motor vehicle accidents, ranging from public health policies, educational campaigns, laws, road engineering, technological interventions and programmes to modify individual risk behaviours (eg, driving under alcohol or using protective helmets).3 The Global Plan for a Decade of Action for Road Safety aims to halve the number of road traffic fatalities through local, national and global interventions.6 This plan is centred on five pillars: road safety management, safer roads and mobility, safer vehicles, safer road users and postcrash response.5 From 2011 to 2020, the Global Plan saw a 5% reduction in absolute numbers and a 16% reduction in mortality rates by road traffic fatalities worldwide. However, only 10 countries achieved the target of a 50% reduction in deaths, 72 saw a reduction of between 2% to 49% (half of which were HICs), while 66 countries observed an increase in MVFs, of which 20 were in LAC.2

Among LACs, Ecuador did not achieve the targeted 50% reduction in fatalities over the 10 years, and signed up to a second Decade of Action for Road Safety, 2021–2030.7 Reports from the National Institute of Statistics and Sensus (INEC) in 2022 showed that fatalities by car accidents in the country were the seventh leading cause of death nationally, the third cause of death for males and the first cause for children and adolescents between 5 and 17 years.8 While several RSPs have been implemented in the country to reduce vehicle fatalities, these policies have differed in timing and adoption across provinces and localities, leading to uneven progress over time. In this context, we hypothesised that different rates of progress in reducing MVFs may have occurred in different geographical regions and population groups. In the present study, we addressed the following research objectives:

To document RSPs in Ecuador between 1990 and 2022.

Methods

Study design

The study was conducted in two phases. First, a documentary review was conducted to identify and classify RSPs implemented in Ecuador between 1990 and 2022, given the limited scientific literature and reliance on governmental documents, legal frameworks, policy reports and grey literature. This phase aimed only to catalogue RSPs, not to evaluate their effectiveness. Second, an ecological time-series analysis examined how the timing of these RSPs related to national and regional trends in MVF rates from 1990 to 2022, using mortality data derived from national death certificates.

Study setting

Ecuador is an upper-middle-income country, with a GDP per capita of US$6063 in 2021.9 The country covers a total of 283 560 km2 and has 24 provinces, 226 cantons and four different climatic regions. Ecuador had a population of 17 629 765 inhabitants in 2022, of which 77.5% self-identified as mestizos, 7.7% indigenous, 2.2% white and 4.8% Afro-Ecuadorian.10 Quito and Guayaquil are the largest cities, each with approximately 3 million inhabitants. In Ecuador, 64% of the population lives in urban areas and 36% in rural areas, with a life expectancy of 77 years.11

Responsibility for land transportation and road safety is shared by four entities: the ministry of transportation, which oversees regulation and planning; the National Transit Agency (ANT), which regulates, plans and controls these activities nationwide; Municipal Transit Agencies (ATM), which manage traffic control within local governments (GADs); and the Ecuadorian Transit Commission (CTE), which operates on national highways and in delegated territories, where it also investigates traffic accidents and trains enforcement personnel.12

Data collection

The review was conducted across academic databases (Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and SCIELO) and non-academic sources, including university repositories, government websites and Google Search. Search terms were used in both English and Spanish (eg, ‘road safety policy Ecuador’, ‘seguridad vial Ecuador’, ‘ley de tránsito Ecuador’). Documents were included if they described, approved or regulated a national-level RSP, law or programme related to traffic safety. Municipal ordinances and local regulations were excluded unless they had nationwide applicability. The first author reviewed all identified documents and, in cases of uncertainty regarding classification as an RSP, conducted additional verification by examining the legal framework and cross-checking with governmental archives.

For the ecological analysis, data were obtained from INEC, Ecuador’s government statistical agency, which provides annualised, nationwide records derived from death certificates and coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). INEC is the official source of vital statistics in Ecuador and offers complete, standardised coverage across all years of the study period (https://www.ecuadorencifras.gob.ec/defunciones-generales/). In contrast, the ANT compiles on-site crash reports collected by traffic authorities, which are helpful for characterising crash circumstances but are known to under-report fatalities, particularly when deaths occur during transport or later in medical facilities. For these reasons, INEC was selected as the most reliable and consistent source for analysing temporal and geographical trends in MVF between 1990 and 2022.

Annual death certificate databases were obtained from the Health Statistics section.13 To identify death certificates listing MVF between 1990 and 1996, the study used the ICD-9th edition, including codes 802–842. From 1997 to 2022, the ICD 10th edition, including codes V02–V09 (pedestrian); V10–V19 (pedal cyclist); V20–V29 (motorcyclists); V30–V39 (tricyclists); V40–V49 (cars); V50–V59 (pick-up trucks); V60–V69 (heavy transport vehicles); V70–V79 (bus); and V80–V89 (other vehicles). Annual midyear population estimates, total and stratified according to sex, age, region and province, were obtained from INEC.11

Statistical analysis

Annual crude and age-standardised rates (ASRs) per 100 000 population were estimated for the 33-year period. ASRs were calculated using the direct standardisation method with the WHO World Standard Population as the reference. Age-specific mortality rates for 5-year age groups were multiplied by WHO weights and summed to obtain the final rate. The WHO standard was chosen because it is widely used internationally and ensures comparability across long periods and population groups. This fixed reference is particularly appropriate for Ecuador, as it reduces the influence of demographic changes over the 33-year period and allows observed trends to reflect real changes in MVF rather than population ageing.14 Additionally, crude rates by age group (≤14; 15–24; 25–64; >64 years), sex, region (Coast, Andean and Amazon) and province were estimated. Years of life lost (YLL) were calculated to estimate the impact on premature mortality. YLL is a summary measure that illustrates the loss suffered by society through premature death.15 It estimates how many more years a person would have lived if they had not died. This indicator considers the number of deaths from a cause and the distribution of people according to age group. We calculated YLL using the Standard Life Expectancy from the WHO’s Global Health Estimates. Additionally, YLL and ASRs (ASYR) were also estimated. YLL rates were computed using the mid-year population estimates from INEC, and ASYR was calculated using a direct method using the WHO world standard population.16

Joinpoint regression models were used to evaluate trends in MVF rates over the study period.17 18 This method identifies the year(s) when a trend change is produced by connecting several line segments on a log scale at ‘joinpoints’. The Joinpoint method provides the annual percentage change (APC) between trend-change points and estimates the average APC (AAPC) over the entire study period. We conducted a joinpoint analysis for the total population and stratified by age group, sex and region using crude mortality rates. APC and AAPC were calculated with 95% CI. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.19

Crude rates by province were used to build heat maps to evaluate the geographical distribution of mortality using ArcGIS Pro V.3.3 (ESRI, California, USA). Mortality rates and YLL were estimated using SPSS V.28. Joinpoint analyses were computed using the Joinpoint software (V.5.0.2) of the Surveillance Research Programme of the US National Cancer Institute.20

Patient and public involvement

This research was done without patient involvement. The study used secondary information, and therefore, participants were not invited to comment on the study design or consulted to develop patient-relevant outcomes or interpret the results.

Discussion

In the present study, we conducted an ecological time-series analysis to understand better the geographical and temporal trends in MVF in Ecuador and explore how the country’s RSPs could have influenced these trends. While various factors could impact mortality, extensive evidence links the adoption of policies to reduce MVF.3 21 22 However, these policies varied in timing and uptake across provinces and localities, so progress over time could not be constant. We chose a long-term analysis precisely to evaluate the complexity of this landscape, not only describing the problem but also identifying localities and population groups that could be relatively better compared with the national average.

Several studies have examined temporal trends in traffic crashes, injuries, mortality and the causes of car accidents in Ecuador. For example, a study published in 2017, which analysed the period 2000–2015, showed a sustained increase in mortality, associated with the growth of the vehicle fleet and a greater severity of accidents, despite slight improvements in lethality per victim.23 In the same vein, an article published in 2021, which examined the years 2000–2019, found that, although accidents decreased from 2015 onwards due to regulatory reforms, mortality increased in the last years of the period, with an annual growth of 1.8% and a high risk among young men.7 In addition, a 2022 study, covering 1990–2018, revealed that older adults had clearly higher mortality rates and an increasing trend in the years of life potentially lost, which reinforces the persistence of the increase in mortality in vulnerable groups.24

On the other hand, some recent studies show more nuanced trends. The research published in 2019, focusing on 2010–2016, showed a decrease in mortality, from 22 to 18 deaths per 100 000 inhabitants, although high levels of risk persisted among men and young people.25 Similarly, the study published in 2022, which analysed 2010–2019, found a fluctuating trend but with a reduction towards 2016, in addition to identifying distracted driving as the main cause of deaths.26 Finally, the most recent study, published in 2024, examined the years 2011–2022 and showed a slight, non-significant overall trend, with clear decreases among older adults, but a worrying increase in motorcyclist mortality and the aggravation of social inequalities between provinces.27

The present study, which covers the longest period analysed to date (1990–2022), confirms the general patterns described in the literature. In addition, it provides a novel element by relating these changes to the implementation of RSPs and other socio-economic factors, showing that, despite multiple regulations, there has not been an overall reduction in three decades. With this, the present study offers the most complete view, allowing us to reinterpret previous findings within a broader and more explanatory time frame (see figure 3).

The analysis identified four trends with three changing points in 2002, 2010 and 2020. A decreasing trend characterised the first period between 1990 and 2002. This decline could be mainly related to the two National Traffic Safety Laws approved in 1981 and 1996 and the ban on driving under the influence of alcohol established between 1990 and 1994.2830 The 1996 Traffic and Land Transportation Law established organisational norms, planning and control of land traffic and transportation, control and prevention of accidents, control of environmental pollution and noise produced by motor vehicles, and the classification and judgement of traffic violations.31

From 2002 to 2010, an increasing trend was observed in the country (second period). This increase could be explained by the economic and social crisis the country experienced during most of the 2000s, representing Ecuador’s dollarisation crisis following the adoption of the US dollar as the national currency in 2000. This crisis led to prolonged economic stagnation, accompanied by significant increases in poverty, unemployment, income inequality and social unrest. Further, the national government had limited ability to respond to financial shocks due to the lack of monetary policy control, particularly impacting the most vulnerable populations.32 Several studies have pointed out that socio-economic crisis can lead to an increase in mortality rates from car accidents, particularly among lower socio-economic groups, due to factors like increased risky driving behaviours, reduced vehicle maintenance and a higher likelihood of driving older, less safe vehicles, all stemming from financial stress during a crisis.33 34 Furthermore, during a crisis, access to reliable transportation options like public transit can be reduced, forcing people to rely on less safe modes of travel or driving longer distances, increasing exposure to potential accidents.2 Additionally, in a social crisis, public investment in road maintenance and health services is generally cut from the national budgets, two factors that are deeply related to MVFs.1

Several RSPs were reinforced and enacted during the second period of the study, such as regulations on driving under the influence of alcohol and on driving training and licensing. However, these policies do not seem to affect fatalities in the short term. The lack of impact of these policies could be explained by poor implementation and enforcement of regulations, as well as increased informality and corruption, especially in times of socio-economic crisis.35 In addition, during this period, several laws were reformed to reduce penalties and fines for traffic violations in order to alleviate the financial burden caused by the dollarisation crisis.36 This modification in traffic violation penalties could have negatively affected road safety, increasing the number of car accidents and MVF.

The third period, between 2010 and 2020, presented a decreasing trend in fatalities. This decline could be explained by the Organic Law of Land Transportation, Transit and Road Safety of Ecuador (LOTTSV). This law was enacted in 2008 but was implemented in 2010, and from there, it was reformed in 2011, 2014 and 2016. The LOTTSV implemented essential changes in regulations, such as the mandatory use of helmets for motorcycle drivers, recovery of driver’s licence points, having a basic education certificate for non-professional licences and high school diplomas for professional licences, a decrease in the level of alcohol allowed in drivers’ blood and hardening of penalties.7 12 Additionally, the implementation of motor vehicle insurance in 2008 and the creation of the ECU 911 (Integrated Security Service of Ecuador) and the ANT in 2011 could explain this decline. ECU-911 is responsible for dealing with emergencies nationwide through a single number. In the case of a road accident, the ECU-911 provides an immediate and comprehensive response service and mobilises resources.

The year of the COVID-19 pandemic was included in the third period, a year with a low rate of fatalities.37 Although this year affected the trend, the decline started before this event. From 2020 onwards (the fourth period), a new increasing trend was identified, with high annual growth rates. Like the dollarisation crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic brought a severe economic crisis, weakening control institutions, neglecting road network maintenance and affecting emergency services.34 All these socio-economic factors could be related to the increased trend found in the last years of the study.

The study identified a decreasing trend over the study period for the female population, people aged 0–14 and ≥65 years. These declines could be related to the implementation of several RSPs. For example, studies suggest that women respond more quickly to public health policies and technological innovations, such as seatbelts, child restraint seats and measures to reduce alcohol-impaired driving.38 Additionally, female drivers are less likely to engage in risky behaviours, such as speeding and alcohol-impaired driving, that can influence crash risk.39 Regarding people aged 0–14 and older adults, RSPs oriented to protect pedestrians could largely explain the declining trend. Studies have pointed out the high vulnerability of these two groups in car accidents, especially older adults, since most of them die on public roads as pedestrians.4042

The geographical analyses showed that almost all provinces presented an increase in MVF (APC between 1% and 3% per year) over the study period (figure 2), but with some regional disparities. The provinces of the Amazon region showed a marked increase, with the highest rates in recent years. These disparities could be associated with differences in road infrastructure, a lack of highways and deficient emergency medical services, which are common in this region.27 Additionally, in the last decades, the Coast and the Amazon provinces have increased the use of three-wheeled motorcycles, also known as moto-taxis, motorised vehicles used for public transportation.43 These vehicles are intended to meet the demand not met by conventional public transport systems due to their ease of access in urban and rural areas. However, the increase in two to three wheel vehicles has been associated with rising accidents and MVF in LMICs.44

Unlike the provinces of the Coast and Amazon regions, four provinces of the Andean region showed significant decreases over the entire study period. Several factors could explain these trends, including properly implementing RSPs at the provincial or municipal level. For example, in the province of Pichincha, the province with the highest decreasing trend, a local programme intended to reduce emissions from vehicular traffic in the capital city since 2003 could be responsible, in part, for the decline in fatalities in this area. The Municipality of Quito, the most polluted city in Ecuador, designed a strategy to reduce air pollution based on several programmes. One of these programmes was the Vehicular Technical Inspection, which consisted of the annual inspection of gas emissions and the vehicle’s mechanical condition.45 This environmental policy may have impacted the number of car accidents, especially those caused by mechanical vehicle damage, reducing the fatalities in the province.

In Ecuador, as in other LMICs, there is limited causal evidence of the effectiveness of road safety interventions.3 22 46 Indeed, much of the evidence for what works comes from HICs.21 Similar evidence of an intervention’s efficacy may be difficult to obtain in LMICs because crash and other relevant data are often unavailable or poorly reported. This makes rigorous evaluations of interventions in LMICs challenging. In this context, this study is part of a large project, SEDHI (Social and Environmental Determinants of Health Inequalities), which seeks to evaluate the effects of social and environmental policies on health indicators in Ecuador and Brazil. As a first stage, we identified the most relevant RSPs conducted in Ecuador and explored how these policies could relate to trends in MVF. As a second stage, we will plan to evaluate specific RSPs, especially in a defined group and delimited geographical area, using more robust methodologies.

Another key factor that helps explain fluctuations in MVF rates in Ecuador is the rapid rise in motorisation and infrastructure development. For example, the number of vehicles per 1000 inhabitants increased from 52 in 2000 to 175 in 2024,47 with particularly sharp growth after 2010 and 2020, coinciding with several Joinpoint-identified shifts in MVF trends. This expansion was accompanied by notable changes in fleet composition—especially the rapid growth of motorcycles and light vehicles—which increased population exposure to traffic risk and may have reduced the long-term impact of RSPs.44 48 In parallel, Ecuador undertook major road-infrastructure improvements and highway expansions over the past three decades. Although not directly measured in this study, such developments can influence crash risk by affecting travel speeds, mobility patterns and traffic density.49 50 Together, these structural changes likely interacted with policy implementation, enforcement and behavioural factors, helping shape the four temporal trend periods identified.51 Future research incorporating spatial and infrastructural indicators would provide further insight into how these additional determinants contribute to long-term MVF dynamics in Ecuador.

This study has several methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as stated in Objective 1, the component aimed at documenting RSPs implemented in Ecuador was designed as a documentary (narrative) review rather than a systematic or scoping review. This approach was intentionally chosen due to the extremely limited availability of peer-reviewed literature on the topic. Second, the analyses did not include other indicators commonly used in transit analyses. We use only mortality rates because this indicator is more reliable than others, such as the number of car accidents or injuries associated with under-reporting. Previous studies suggest under-reporting in official crash databases. For example, a 2022 study in Ecuador found that crashes involving long-distance buses were substantially under-captured in ANT records compared with a database constructed from mass-media monitoring.52 Third, although we mapped the timing and content of RSPs, our study does not evaluate their effectiveness. As a result, causal inferences regarding policy effects cannot be drawn from these data. Finally, joinpoint analyses present some limitations compared with more robust methodologies, such as impact analyses. However, the method is widely used in trend analyses in traffic studies due to its analytical advantages for surveillance and epidemiology.38 53

Lastly, it is difficult to attribute changes in the trends of MVF to a single cause. The factors influencing the problem are multiple, not static and subject to change. On the one hand, the vehicle fleet has not stopped growing, nor has the number of kilometres travelled. To the extent that accidents occur based on probability, these changes in the vehicle fleet and its use would lead to a secular increase in traffic accidents and the injuries and mortality they cause. While more structured prevention policies have been developed in Ecuador in the last three decades, adopting and implementing the most relevant preventive policies has spread over the years. Some have a precise entry date into force (at least nominally), but others are more gradual. It must be considered that their practical application is not immediate, nor is it homogeneous territorially. Additionally, not all road safety interventions are equally effective. Some provide substantial benefits, while others have little or no positive impacts.

Conclusion

Despite substantial implementation of RSPs, Ecuador has not experienced a significant decrease in MVF over the last three decades. However, population subgroups and specific geographical areas show substantial declines, suggesting the positive effects of some RSPs. The fluctuating trend observed could result from a complex combination of road safety interventions in the case of decreasing tendencies and socio-economic factors in the case of increasing tendencies. More studies need to be conducted using robust methodologies to evaluate specific road safety interventions in delimited areas and populations to avoid variations in policy implementation. To do these evaluations in LMICs, we must improve the data collection system and access to such data.
