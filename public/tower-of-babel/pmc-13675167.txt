Insulin-mediated ciliary remodeling in the choroid plexus epithelium
Authors: Marleen Trapp, L Aurica Ritter, Greta Döking, James Cleland, Anthony D Hill, Annarita Patrizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe choroid plexus (ChP) epithelium plays a crucial role in cerebrospinal fluid production and brain homeostasis. ChP is mainly composed of multiciliated epithelial cells that exhibit a noncanonical ciliary organization whose function is still debated. Insulin signaling is known to be important for central nervous system function, but its role in ChP function and ciliary organization has not been elucidated yet. To investigate how prolonged insulin exposure modulates ChP cells, we treated primary murine choroid plexus epithelial cells (pCPECs) with insulin for up to six days and used a combination of immunohistochemistry, RT-qPCR, RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization, single cell analysis and Western Blot to explore underlying molecular mechanisms. We demonstrated that prolonged insulin treatment significantly increased the percentage of multiciliated clustered pCPECs and their axoneme length. Prolonged insulin treatment selectively induced translocation of IFT20, a key component of the intraflagellar transport system, to the ciliary axoneme by increasing the cytoplasmatic surface area covered by IFT20. To note, the expression of insulin receptors (IRs) in ex vivo ChP epithelial cells started during late embryogenesis and localized predominantly to the apical membrane, but not in the cilium. Our study suggests that insulin signaling may promote ciliary remodeling in choroid plexus epithelial cells, most likely by modulating intraflagellar transport dynamics involving IFT20. These findings reveal a novel role of insulin signaling in ChP cilia organization and function, with potential implications for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) regulation and neurodevelopmental processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06486-6.Keywords: Neurodevelopment, Insulin signaling, Cilia, Intraflagellar transport, Insulin receptors, Metabolism

Introduction

The choroid plexus (ChP) is a specialized membrane within the brain ventricles and consists predominantly of choroid plexus epithelial cells, as well as pericytes, fenestrated endothelial cells, fibroblasts and immune cells [1, 2]. While historically the ChP was regarded primarily as the producer of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and a static barrier, recent studies position the ChP as a communication hub at the interface between the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral environment. In fact, ChP can dynamically interact with the environment and rapidly respond to physiological and pathological stimuli, including neuroinflammation and systemic metabolic cues [3, 4]. For example, ChP expresses glucose transporters and leptin receptors and actively regulates CSF glucose and leptin concentration in response to blood glucose and leptin levels [5–7].

ChP is also known to produce and secrete multiple bioactive factors directly in to the CSF, including insulin [8], epidermal growth factor (EGF) [9] and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) [10] among others [11, 12]. For example, overexpression or downregulation of insulin gene 2 (Ins2), by adeno-associated-virus (AAV) or genetic knockout in the choroid plexus, lead to changes in hippocampal-dependent behaviors [13], demonstrating that ChP is one of the sources of insulin in the brain. It has, therefore, been suggested that ChP-derived-insulin plays a major role in modulating brain insulin signaling, affecting CNS metabolism, food intake and associated pathology’s such as Alzheimer’s [14, 15]. Consistent with this, insulin receptors (IRs) have been detected in the ChP by autoradiography [16] as well as by more recent RNA-scope approaches [17]. However, the precise distribution and developmental dynamics of IRs within the ChP, as well as their downstream signaling effects on the ChP itself, have remained largely unexplored.

ChP epithelial cells commit to a multiciliated fate as early as embryonic stage (E)10.5, shortly after exiting the cell cycle. At this point, the majority of ChP epithelial cells contain only a single cilium (sc); later, multiple scattered and disoriented cilia emerge (mcd stage), and, finally, cilia cluster in one location with parallel orientation (mcc stage) [18]. In addition, ChP cilia have an atypical conformation. In particular, murine ChP cilia are characterized by a 9 + 0 axonemal tubulin configuration with a discontinuous unknown electron-dense component in place of the central pairs, multiple basal feet and outer dynein arms, displaying structural properties of both sensory and motile cilia [18]. It was recently demonstrated that ChP cilia can act as sensory organelles, enabling them to transduce Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) signaling in whole ChP-explants as well as in vivo models. In fact, FoxJ1 ChP epithelial cell knockout mice are characterized by shorter cilia unable to induce Smo-mediated pathway activation leading to changes in CSF production and ventricular enlargement [19]. In addition, Banizes et al. 2007 demonstrated that mice with an insertional mutation of Ift88, Tg737(orpk), lose ChP cilia which is accompanied by elevated cAMP, dysregulated Na⁺/HCO₃⁻ transport, and disturbed intracellular pH. To note, Tg737(orpk) mutants also develop perinatal hydrocephalus [20]. These data suggest that ChP cilia can act as sensory organelles and can drive hydrocephalus for example via altered epithelial ion and fluid transport [21, 22].

Cilia – both primary (sensory) and motile (multiciliated) - are assembled and maintained by intraflagellar transport (IFT), a conserved microtubule-based trafficking system that selects, transports, and positions ciliary components [23]. Two well-studied IFT proteins, IFT20 and IFT88, play distinct but complimentary roles across ciliary subtypes. IFT20 transports ciliary cargo from the Golgi apparatus to the ciliary base and locates predominantly to the Golgi apparatus. Short harpin RNA mediated Ift20 knockdown in human retinal pigmented epithelium cells reduces ciliation by blocking assembly without disrupting the Golgi apparatus [24]. IFT88 mediates anterograde transport along the axoneme and localizes within the ciliary shaft [25]. Conditional Ift88 deletion in primary ciliated neural crest cells (Wnt1-Cre; Ift88fx/−) ablates axonemes while preserving basal bodies [26], while ChP epithelial cell-specific Ift88 deletion reduces both cilia number and axoneme length in multiciliated cells [19].

In this manuscript we explored the consequences of prolonged exposure to growth factors (FGF, EGF, hydrocortisone (HC), insulin (INS) and insulin like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)) on primary murine choroid plexus epithelial cells (pCPEC), focusing on cilia organization and length. Insulin had the strongest effect, altering ChP cilia organization and increasing axoneme length, whereas other growth factors showed mild or no effects. We demonstrated that prolonged insulin treatment modified the localization and surface area of IFT20 but not IFT88. To understand the ChP ability to sense insulin we characterized the developmental expression and localization of the IRs in the ChP using protein and mRNA-based approaches. Our findings demonstrated that IRs specifically localize to the apical CSF-facing side of ChP epithelial cell and their expression peaks during late embryogenesis and remains unchanged until adulthood.

Materials & Methods

Experimental animals

Inbred C57BL/6J and C57BL/6 N mice were purchased from Janvier lab and housed in both the Interdisciplinary Neurobehavioral Core (INBC) at Heidelberg University and the German Cancer Research Center (DKFZ) animal facility. GFP-tagged Otx2 mouse line was kindly provided by Dr. Thomas Lamonerie [27]. Animals were housed in static cages, no environmental enrichment toys were added to the cages, only extra Kimwipes tissue. After weaning, animals were socially housed in groups of two to five animals per cage. Animals were housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled room on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with food and water provided ad libitum. All animal experiments were approved by the German and European Union regulations and the guidelines of the local regional ethics committee (Regierungspräsidium Karlsruhe, Germany, protocol number G-114/18). Animal studies are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were conducted following German Animal Welfare Act regulations.

Primary choroid plexus epithelial cell culture

Preparation of pCPEC was adapted from a published protocol [28]. Shortly, postnatal day (P) 0–2 mice were sacrificed by decapitation. Lateral and fourth ventricle ChP were isolated and pooled together. Due to the small size of the neonatal tissue, samples from all pups coming from one litter (8–12 pups, males and females) were pooled for each experiment. Per condition 2–3 biological replicates were used, the specific numbers are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. ChP tissue was treated with 1 mg/mL Pronase (Sigma Aldrich, #10165921001) for 10 min at 37 °C. To remove fibroblasts, cells were allowed to attach in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #11320033), 10% FBS (Gibco, #333a3164), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, #15070063) for 3 h at 37 °C. For monolayer culture, dissociated cells were plated on laminin (Sigma Aldrich, #L2020; 1 µg/cm2) coated 24-well plates at 0.5 × 105 cells/mL in DMEM/F12, 10% FBS, 2 mM L-Glutamine (Gibco,#25030024), 50 µg/mL gentamicin (Gibco, #15710049), 20 µM AraC (Sigma Aldrich, #C1768) and allowed to reach plate confluence (typically by 5–6 days).

Primary choroid plexus epithelial cell culture drug treatment

pCPEC were treated with either 5 µg/mL human insulin (Sigma Aldrich, #I9278), 5 µg/mL IGF-1 (BioTechne GmbH, #291-G1-200), 10 ng/mL EGF (PeproTech GmbH, #315-09-100), 2 µg/mL HC (Sigma Aldrich, #H4001) or 5 ng/mL FGF (PeproTech GmbH, #450–33 A-10) for 6 consecutive days, since seeding. Alternatively, pCPEC were treated with 5 µg/mL INS for a shorter period: 24 h (INS 24h), 48 h (INS 48h) and immediately processed or they were allowed to recover for up to 5-day (d), 24h+5d and 48h+4d, respectively. For all conditions half of the media was changed every two days to maintain the concentration of the growth factors constant.

Tissue processing for light microscopy

Male P60 mice were either transcardially perfused with 4% PFA (Sigma Aldrich, #P6148) in PBS 0.1 M, pH 7.4 (Gibco, #10010023) or euthanized by cervical dislocation and snap frozen on dry ice. PFA-perfused brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 10, 20 and 30% sucrose (MP Biomedicals, #19401890) in PBS at 4 °C, embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT (Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound, Sakura, #4583) and then frozen. Whole heads from E13.5 to E16.5 mice or isolated brains from E18.5 to P10 mice were rinsed in cold PBS, embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT and directly frozen on dry ice or immersed in 4% PFA for 2 h, followed by freezing. For the embryonic and early timepoints males and females were used.

For the identification of EdU+ cells we used E18.5 tissue generated in Ho et al. 2023 [18].

Immunofluorescence staining and microscopy

pCPEC, plated at 0.2 × 105 cells/mL in 8-well staining chambers (Invitrogen, #155411PK), were washed in PBS, fixed with cold 100% methanol for 5 min or with 4% PFA for 10 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, #T9284) in PBS for 15 min. Fixed pCPEC were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (AppliChem, #A65880100) and 1% milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Antibodies were diluted in the blocking solution. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibody incubation was carried out for 2 h at RT. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Biomol, #Cay15547-25; 1:4000) for 10 min at RT. For proliferation analysis, 10 µM EdU was added to plated pCPEC for 48 h, followed by 72 h without EdU before fixation with 4% PFA. The Click EdU Alexa 594 imaging Kit (Invitrogen, #C10339) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All brains were sliced in 15 μm-thick coronal or sagittal sections using a cryostat (Leica CM1950). Prior to immunostaining, frozen sections were fixed with either cold 4% PFA for 10 min or cold 100% methanol for 5 min on ice, followed by rinsing with PBS 0.01 M. Sections were then blocked and permeabilized with 10% normal donkey serum (Biozol Diagnostica, #LIN-END9010-10) and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by a 2 h incubation with secondary antibodies at RT in antibody solution (3% normal donkey serum and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS). Sections were counterstained with Hoechst for 8 min at RT and mounted using Dako Fluorescent Mounting Medium (Agilent Technologies Inc, #GM304). Primary antibodies can be found in Table 1.

Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM780 or LSM900 confocal microscope with 16 bit and 2x line averaging. ZEN Black software was used for image acquisition and Fiji [29] (NIH) for processing analysis.

Confocal image analysis

pCPEC imaging analysis was performed only on cells organized in rosette-like structures, suggesting a healthy state. 4–5 images were acquired per well.

pCPEC morphology and cilia analysis

For cilia analysis pCPECs were acquired with 40x objective, zoom 1, z-stacks of 0.5 μm interval and 1000 × 1000 fields. For EdU and cytokeratin analysis 20x objective, zoom 1, z-stacks of 1 μm interval and 1024 × 1024 fields were used. All images were acquired for the entire penetration of the antibodies to ensure full reconstruction of the apical surface and cilia apparatus. Analysis was performed on max-intensity projection images with Fiji [29]. The total number of EdU or cytokeratin positive (+) cells was manually quantified; the percentages of EdU+ or cytokeratin+ cells were calculated based on the total number of Hoechst cells; the apical surface cell area was calculated by manually drawing the outline of the apical cell border following zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) staining; the nuclear surface area was calculated by a macro provided by Dr. Damir Krunic, which segmented and measured the outline of Hoechst+ structure; cilia density and number of axonemes were quantified using Cell Counter plug-in with Fiji; axoneme length was measured by drawing a line along ARL13B staining. Unless otherwise indicated, data are shown as fold change, average treated cell values divided by untreated cell (DMEM) or chronic insulin (c-INS) values.

Density Otx2+ cells

For Otx2 cell density, 2048 × 2048 fields of ChP were imaged using a 40x objective. The total number of Otx2+ cells was quantified at each time point and the percentage of Otx2+ cells was calculated based on total number of Hoechst cells.

Density IFT88+ cilia

For IFT88+ cilia analysis, 2048 × 2048 images, z-stack 0.5 μm were acquired using a 40x objective. Number of cilia and IFT88+ cilia where manually counted using Cell Counter plug-in with Fiji. The percentage of IFT88+ cilia was calculated based on the total number of cilia.

Colocalization IFT20 and IFT88 in cilia

For IFT20 and IFT88 cilia colocalization analysis, 2048 × 2048 images, z-stack 0.5 μm were acquired using a 40x objective. The images were processed with the image-analysis program Imaris (release 8.0.2; Bitplane). IFT20 or IFT88 colocalization with glutamylated tubulin (GlutTub+) cilium was estimated on segmented images with the Imaris co-localization module followed by quantification with Fiji.

IFT20 and Golgi apparatus surface area

For IFT20 and Golgi apparatus surface area analysis, 2048 × 2048 images, z-stack 0.5 μm were acquired using a 40x objective. The surface area covered by Golgi apparatus or by IFT20 was calculated employing a dedicated macro developed by Dr. Damir Krunic with Fiji. In particular, the macro segmented the images using a threshold that maximized the selection of immunofluorescent staining over background labeling and then measured the surface area covered by each channel. The average surface area covered by channels was subsequently plotted for each image.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence

Hybridisation chain reaction RNA FISH was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor adjustment [30] and using commercially available reagents, including riboprobes (Molecular Instruments). Cryosections from E18.5 whole heads embryos (males and females) were fixed in pre-chilled 4% PFA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # 28908) for 15 min on ice, dehydrated through a 50–100% ethanol series at RT, rinsed with PBS, incubated in commercial probe hybridization buffer for 15 min at 37˚C, then incubated in probe solution (hybridization buffer supplemented with 4 nM Insr riboprobes) overnight at 37°C. The next day, sections were incubated for 15 min at 37°C in a graded wash buffer series (75% wash buffer/25% 5x SSCT, 50% wash buffer/50% 5x SSCT, 25% wash buffer/75% 5x SSCT, 0% wash buffer/100% 5x SSCT), 5 min at RT in 5x SSCT, 30 min at RT in commercial amplification buffer, then overnight at RT in hairpin solution (amplification buffer supplemented with 60 nM B2-546-h1 and B2-546-h2 hairpins). The next day, sections were incubated 3 × 20 min at RT in 5x SSCT and subjected to post RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization immunofluorescence. Briefly, sections were incubated in blocking solution (1% bovine serum albumin and 5% normal donkey serum diluted in PBST) at RT for 1 h, primary antibody solution (blocking solution supplemented with 1:200 mouse anti-E-cadherin antibody and 1:200 ALEXA Fluor 647 isolectin GS-IB4 conjugate) overnight at 4°C, 2 × 5 min at RT in PBST, secondary antibody solution (blocking solution supplemented with anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 and 1:2000 Hoechst) and 2 × 5 min at RT in PBST. Sections were mounted in Prolong Diamond, cured at RT for at least 24 h then imaged with 1536 × 1536 images, z-stack 0.5 μm using a 40x objective.

Results

Prolonged insulin treatment modulates choroid plexus cilia

To study the impact of prolonged exposure to well-known grown factors, such as FGF, EGF, HC, INS as well as IGF-1 [28, 33], we first established a pCPEC in vitro model of P0-1 mouse ChP (Fig. 1a). As expected, pCPECs were organized in rosette-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and more than 90% of the pCPECs were cytokeratin-positive (cyt+) (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c), an epithelial cell specific marker, confirming an enrichment of epithelial cells. As previously reported, pCPEC showed limited proliferation ability with less than 4% of total pCPEC to be EdU+ (Supplementary Fig. 1 d, e) [28]. pCPEC developed a barrier-like structure [28, 33] within 24 h from seeding (days in vitro, div1), as demonstrated by staining against ZO-1, an epithelial cell-specific tight junction protein. No clear differences was detected in the apical cell area comparing div1 to div6 pCPECs (Fig. 1b, c), suggesting that the composition of the culture media may not encourage the pCPEC to organize as cobblestone-like morphology [34]. When we exposed pCPEC to the individual growth factors we found that most of them (FGF, EGF, HC and INS) reduced the pCPEC apical cell area (0.5 ± 0.2; 0.6 ± 0.2; 0.5 ± 0.5; 0.5 ± 0.3 SD respectively) compared to the untreated control (DMEM, 1 ± 0.6 SD) (Fig. 1d, e), without affecting the total cell density or the nuclei surface area (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g) showing that an enriched culture media can induce apical constriction encouraging a cobblestone-like morphology. On the contrary, IGF-1 did not alter the apical cell area (1.5 ± 0.9 SD) (Fig. 1d, e). Of note, the concentration of the growth factor treatments was selected based on previous protocols [28, 33].

Next, we evaluated the impact of growth factors on the ciliated stage of pCPECs. ChP murine ciliogenesis starts at E10.5 and it is mainly concluded at E18.5, therefore at birth the majority of ChP epithelial cells have completed this process and appear to be mcc cells [18]. Untreated pCPEC showed three types of cilia organization: cells with a single cilium (sc cells) cells with multiple scattered and disorganized cilia (mcd), and cells with multiple clustered cilia oriented towards the CSF (mcc) (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 1 h). Up to 80% of the pCPECs were multiciliated with less than 4% ± 1.5 SEM containing a single cilium. Within the multiciliated cells, only 28% ± 5.6 SEM were mcc whereas the majority were mcd (50% ± 5.8 SEM), suggesting that in untreated conditions pCPEC cilia resemble late embryogenesis developmental stages (Supplementary Fig. 1i) [18]. Because sc accounted for less 5% of the total pCPEC and both sc and mcd represented immature cilia organizational stages [18], we grouped them together and collectively refer to them as non-mcc cells (Fig. 1f). Next, we evaluated whether growth factor treatments influence pCPEC cilia organizational stages. EGF (1.5 ± 0.3 SD), HC (1.4 ± 0.3 SD) and INS (1.7 ± 0.3 SD) significantly increased the percentage of mcc cells compared to untreated cells (1 ± 0.5 SD). This increase was most likely due to the significant reduction of the percentage of non-ciliated cells, suggesting these growth factors modulate cilia organization state. FGF significantly reduced the percentage of non-ciliated cells (Fig. 1g, h), without reaching significance for the mcc cells (1.4 ± 0.4 SD). On the contrary, IGF-1 treatment did not affect the cilia organization when compared to DMEM and the other growth factors (Fig. 1g, h). In addition, except for FGF, the growth factors, did not change the total number of axonemes per cell (Supplementary Fig. 1j, k). Interestingly, insulin treatment was enough to significantly increase the axoneme length in both mcc and non-mcc cilia, with cilia getting 2–6 times longer after insulin treatment (Fig. 1i, j). In contrast, HC treatment significantly decreased the mcc cilia length but significantly increased the non-mcc cilia length (Fig. 1i, j). To determine whether the effect on axoneme length depended on cilia maturation stage, we considered sc and mcd cilia as two independent groups. Surprisingly, only sc cilia showed a significant growth factor-dependent increase in axoneme length, except for IGF-1. Mcd cilia were not affected by any of the growth factor treatments (Supplementary Fig. 1 l). We next asked whether other parameters, such as apical cell area, were similarly influenced by cilia state. Apical cell area surface did not differ between non-ciliated and sc cells, but was significantly reduced in mcd cells upon exposure to most growth factors (Supplementary Fig. 1 m). Altogether, these data indicate that selective growth factors influence key features of pCPEC’s cilia organizational and that cilia state may in turn shape the ability of the cilium to sense specific growth factors. Notably, insulin was the only growth factor capable of modulating multiple key cilia features, ranging from cilia organizational state to axoneme length (Fig. 1k).

Insulin receptors are enriched in choroid plexus epithelial cells from late embryogenesis

To assess whether the developmental timing of ciliogenesis overlaps with the insulin pathway maturation we quantified gene and protein expression of IRs in the ChP starting at E14.5 until P60. Immunohistochemistry showed that IR protein expression was undetectable at E14.5, it peaked at E18.5 coinciding with the completion of ChP ciliogenesis [18] and it stayed stable up to P60 (Fig. 2a). Quantification of IR protein levels, from ChP homogenates, confirmed that IR expression was detectable at E18.5 and it stayed constant until P60 (Fig. 2b, c). Similarly, Insr mRNA expression was reliably expressed starting at E16.5 and its expression remained stable until adulthood (Fig. 2d). In the brain IRs are mainly known to be expressed in the brain capillary endothelial cells of the blood-brain-barrier [35]. To test which cell types express IRs in the ChP, we stained ChP epithelium reporter mouse, Otx2-GFP [27], against Isolectin B4 and found that the majority of Otx2+ cells (85% ± 2.5 SEM) were positive for IRs (Fig. 2e). Taking advantage of the spatial resolution of RNA FISH, we next confirmed that many epithelial cells expressed Insr mRNA (Fig. 2f), however also a subset of Isolectin B4+ cells expressed IRs at E18.5 (Fig. 2g). Similarly, analysis of publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from ChP at multiple developmental timepoints (embryonic and adult) confirmed that Insr mRNA is expressed in both epithelial and endothelial cells, with the highest expression observed in adult endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). We, next, asked whether ChP epithelial cells express Insr mRNA throughout all developmental stages (progenitors, multiciliated cells and differentiated/mature stages). A small but consistent subset of ChP epithelial cells expressed Insr mRNA at every stage examined, with peak expression in mature adult cells (Fig. 2h, i). Finally, we assessed whether actively dividing epithelial cells, identified by EdU incorporation, express IR, and found that sparse proliferative cells were already IR+ (Fig. 2j). All together, these findings suggest that IRs are expressed in both ChP epithelial cells and endothelial cells, starting by late embryogenesis.

Insulin-induced ciliary reorganization requires prolonged exposure

To assess whether shorter insulin treatment was enough to elicit ciliary phenotypes, we treated pCPEC with insulin only for 24–48 h and compared them to pCPEC treated with insulin for 6 days (c-INS) (Fig. 4a, see statistical analysis in MM). Both shorter insulin treatments significantly decreased the percentage of mcc cells compared to c-INS (24h, 0.4 ± 0.2; 48h, 0.6 ± 0.1; c-INS, 1 ± 0.2 SD) (Fig. 4b, c). In the 24 h condition the decrease of mcc cells coincided with a significant increase in the percentage of non-mcc cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and an unchanged percentage of non-ciliated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Whereas in the 48 h condition the decrease of mcc cells corresponded with a maintained percentage of non-mcc cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and the tendency to increase the percentage of non-ciliated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Neither of the shorter treatment conditions affected the number of axonemes in non-mcc and mcc cells compared to c-INS (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Similarly, neither 24–48 h treatment affected axoneme length in non-mcc or mcc cells compared to c-INS (Supplementary Fig. 3e; Fig. 4d). In fact, after 24 h treatment mcc axoneme length was significantly shorter compared to 48 h and c-INS exposure (24h, 0.8 ± 0.4; 48h, 0.9 ± 0.4; c-INS, 1 ± 0.4 SD). Neither 24–48 h insulin treatment was enough to induce apical constriction when compared to c-INS. However, the overall apical cell area was significantly smaller at 48 h compared to 24 h insulin (24h, 2.0 ± 1.3; 48h, 1.6 ± 0.9, c-INS, 1 ± 0.6 SD) (Fig. 4e). Altogether these results revealed that prolonged exposure is necessary to influence pCPEC cilia organization, as 24 h insulin treatment resembled untreated conditions and 48 h represented an intermediate state between untreated and c-INS.

Structural ciliary reorganization may require extended time to respond to a stimulus, we, therefore, modified the treatment protocol by exposing pCPEC to insulin for 24–48 h, followed by a recovery period for up to 5-days (d), 24h+5d and 48h+4d, respectively (Fig. 4f). Neither of these conditions affected the percentage of non-mcc, non-ciliated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g) and the percentage of mcc cells did not reach the level of c-INS (24h+5d, 0.7 ± 1.2; 48h+4d, 0.7 ± 0.1; c-INS, 1 ± 0.2 SD) (Fig. 4g, h). Additionally, neither of the treatment conditions affected the number of axonemes in non-mcc and mcc cells compared to c-INS (Supplementary Fig. 3 h, i). 24h+5d and 48h+4d significantly decreased the axoneme length of the non-mcc cells (Supplementary Fig. 3j) and mcc cells (Fig. 4i) compared to c-INS. Neither treatment was enough to induce apical constriction compared to c-INS (Fig. 4j). All together, these findings suggest that ciliary reorganization is time-dependent, and it requires prolonged exposure to insulin.

Insulin treatment increases IFT20 trafficking between the Golgi apparatus and the cilium axoneme

To investigate the potential molecular machinery underlying how insulin modulates the cilia phenotype in the ChP, we focused on IFT88 and IFT20, proteins well-known to play a crucial role in cilia biogenesis [24, 25] (Fig. 5a). First, we investigated whether ChP expresses Ift88 and Ift20 by quantifying their gene expression levels from ChP homogenates. Both Ift88 and Ift20 mRNA were expressed already at E18.5 and their expression remained stable until P60 (Fig. 5b). Next, we used immunostaining against IFT88 and GlutTub demonstrating that IFT88 was expressed in ChP epithelial cells and that the majority of GlutTub+ axoneme were also IFT88+ (81% ± 3.4 at E18.5; 74% ± 11.6 SEM at P60), indicating that nodal-like ChP cilia contain IFT88 (Fig. 5c). Immunostaining against IFT20 and GlutTub demonstrated that IFT20 was also expressed in ChP epithelial cells, but not in GlutTub+ structures (Fig. 5d). Because it is known that IFT20 controls the transport of ciliary microtubules between the Golgi apparatus and the cilium [24], we next stained against Golgi protein 58K, a marker for the Golgi apparatus, confirming that IFT20 was preferentially enriched in this organelle starting at E18.5 (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

Our findings collectively support a model in which prolonged insulin treatment significantly increases the proportion of primary multiciliated ChP epithelial cells and promotes axonemal elongation. While FGF, EGF, and HC treatments similarly increased the percentage of multiciliated pCPECs, they did not affect ciliary length. In contrast, IGF-1 treatment exerted opposing effects on epithelial characteristics compared to the other growth factors tested. Although most of these growth factors have been detected in the CSF [39], limited data is available regarding their specific role in ChP function. Previous studies have shown that HC administration in chick embryos reduces ChP epithelial cell proliferation [40], whereas EGF, FGF, and IGF-1 promote ChP proliferation [10, 28, 41]. Our findings demonstrate that these growth factors can also influence postmitotic primary ChP epithelial cells, suggesting a role in promoting both cellular and ciliary organization beyond their established effects on proliferation. Notably, insulin has been shown to modulate intracellular Na2+ concentrations, thereby enhancing apical efflux of K+ and Cl− and subsequently driving osmotic water secretion [42], indicating that insulin-mediated effects on ChP may extend to functional aspects of CSF production.

Receptors for insulin, IGF-1, EGF and FGF are known to localize to primary cilia in various cell types [43, 44], and have been implicated in regulating ciliary length through both elongation and resorption mechanisms [45, 46]. Interestingly, insulin has also been shown to act synergistically with IGF-1, EGF, and FGF. Notably, all these growth factors converge on shared downstream pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis, which is known to regulate primary cilia assembly and resorption [43, 47]. However, their individual or combined potential localization and functional role in ChP cilia remain uncharacterized. Our study shows that prolonged insulin treatment uniquely promotes ChP nodal-like cilia elongation, despite the absence of detectable IRs within the ciliary compartment itself. Notably, in pancreatic islets, IRs are recruited to primary cilia upon glucose stimulation [48], where ciliary motility is required for glucose-stimulated calcium influx and insulin release. Pancreatic islet cilia exhibit motility characterized by a combination of planar beating and rotational movement resembling nodal cilia, attributed to their atypical “8 + 1” microtubule configuration [49]. Similarly, ChP cilia possess a non-canonical nodal-like 9 + 0 microtubule architecture and display hybrid characteristics of both primary and motile cilia [18]. The development of purified primary ChP cell culture systems combined with high-resolution multimodal imaging technologies [50], now provides the opportunity to address several critical questions, such as whether IRs translocate to ChP cilia under specific conditions, whether insulin or other growth factors induce ChP ciliary motility, and how insulin influences the three-dimensional ultrastructural organization of ciliary microtubules and motor protein complexes.

Our study reveals that insulin and IGF-1 elicit distinct responses in pCPECs, a finding that is particularly striking given the overlapping receptor specificity of these ligands. While insulin and IGF-1 preferentially bind to their cognate tyrosine kinase receptors (IR and IGF-1R, respectively), each can cross-activate the alternate receptor with lower affinity, and IR and IGF-1R can heterodimerize to form functional hybrid receptors [15, 51]. Despite this receptor promiscuity, insulin predominantly mediates cellular metabolic responses, whereas IGF-1 primarily promotes cellular growth and proliferation [15, 52, 53]. Our data demonstrate that ex vivo IR expression peaks during late embryogenesis, coinciding with the cessation of ChP epithelial cell proliferation and full differentiation [18]. This temporal pattern suggests that insulin signaling may not be essential for ChP proliferation and early development but rather plays a role in post-mitotic cellular maturation. The insulin-dependent ciliary remodeling observed in pCPECs may therefore reflect metabolic adaptation rather than proliferative signaling. Supporting this interpretation, prolonged insulin exposure significantly increased both Golgi apparatus surface area and IFT20 distribution, suggesting enhanced protein processing and production through the secretory pathway [54, 55] and increased trafficking of ciliary components to support axonemal elongation [23]. Collectively, these findings indicate that insulin promotes metabolic adaptation of differentiated ChP epithelial cells, which manifests as enhanced ciliary growth and potentially augmented secretory capacity.

One limitation of this study is that most of our findings are based primarily on immunofluorescence analyses following prolonged insulin treatment, which does not directly establish whether insulin-IR signaling regulates ciliary structural changes or whether these represent secondary downstream effects. While our data demonstrate correlations between insulin treatment and increased multiciliation, axonemal elongation and IFT20 redistribution, the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Future mechanistic validation, through loss-of-function approaches (siRNA/shRNA knockdown or CRISPR-Cas9) knockout of IR and downstream effectors such as PI3K/AKT or MAPK/ERK components [56, 57] and gain-of-function experiments (IR overexpression) will be essential to establish causality.

In the brain, IRs are expressed by capillary endothelial cells of the blood-brain-barrier, where they mediate active insulin transport from the circulation into the brain parenchyma [58]. Recent work from the Egan group demonstrate that ChP epithelial cells represent an additional source of central insulin, with local insulin secretion into the CSF occurring independently of glucose and instead being activated by serotonin [8]. Furthermore, AAV-mediated manipulation of ChP insulin expression has revealed functional consequences for brain physiology: insulin overexpression reduces anxiety-like behavior, while insulin downregulation influences feeding behavior via hypothalamic neuronal circuits [13]. Our findings demonstrate that multiciliated ChP epithelial cells sense and respond to prolonged exposure and elevated insulin concentrations. The insulin-induced ciliary remodeling observed in our study raises the possibility that ChP cilia may function as specialized sensors of local insulin levels. Future studies should determine whether endogenous insulin production engages autocrine or paracrine mechanisms which contribute to the regulation of ciliary homeostasis.

Our experimental approach also warrants some methodological considerations. First, we treated murine cells with human insulin, therefore potential species-specific differences in the binding kinetics between these two species should be considered in the future. However, insulin receptors are highly conserved across mammalian species (> 95% amino acid identity between humans and mice) [59]. Secondly, we used a supraphysiological insulin concentration in our experimental setup. Under physiological condition, CSF insulin levels are markedly lower than the plasma concentration, with reported values of approximately 0.9 ± 0.1 µU/ml in the CSF compared to 12 ± 1.2 µU/ml in circulation in humans [60, 61]. Notably, it has been suggested that the brain protects itself from hyperinsulinemia, as multiple studies have demonstrated reduced CSF insulin levels in diabetic and obese patients due to decreased IR binding affinity and consequent impairment of IR-mediated insulin transport across the blood-brain barrier [62–64]. Interestingly, alterations in primary neuronal cilia have been observed in response to high fat diet and impaired insulin signaling; however, these changes – characterized by ciliary shortening and decreased cilia numbers [65] – are opposite to the insulin induced ciliary elongation observed in our ChP model. These contrasting findings suggest that metabolic dysregulation may influence ciliary remodeling in a cell type specific way, with neurons and ChP epithelial cells responding differently to alterations in insulin signaling. Future studies examining ChP cilia in models of metabolic disease will be critical to understanding the physiological relevance of insulin-mediated ciliary remodeling and its contribution to metabolic dysfunction in the brain barriers.

Conclusion

Beyond its role in CSF production, the ChP can sense and respond to multiple growth factors shaping key differentiation hallmarks, such as cell and cilia organization. Given that insulin receptor resistance in the brain correlates with some brain diseases, our study supports the need to uncover the role of cilia in the insulin signaling pathway in the ChP.
