Improving routine mental health screening for depression and anxiety in a paediatric lupus clinic: a quality improvement initiative for enhanced mental healthcare
Authors: Tala El Tal, Audrea Chen, Stephanie Wong, Asha Jeyanathan, Avery Longmore, Holly Convery, Dinah Finkelstein, Linda Hiraki, Chetana Kulkarni, Neely Lerman, Karen Leslie, Deborah M Levy, Sharon Lorber, Oscar Mwizerwa, Lawrence Ng, Vandana Rawal, Evelyn Smith, Alene Toulany, Andrea M Knight
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health (MH) conditions are prevalent in adolescents with childhood-onset SLE (cSLE). Early identification is crucial in preventing poor patient outcomes; however, MH screening rates remain low.Local problemFrom July 2021–January 2022, only 15% of adolescents in a paediatric tertiary care cSLE clinic were being screened for depression and anxiety. By November 2023, we aimed to increase the percentage of patients with cSLE (≥12–18 years) screened for depression (Patient Health Questionnaire: PHQ-9) and anxiety (Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7: GAD-7) from 15% to 80%.MethodsThis quality improvement project employed the Model for Improvement framework. Stakeholders included the clinic team, patients and families, and MH providers. Statistical process control charts were used to analyse the outcome measure for percentage of screened patients with cSLE. Patient and caregiver satisfaction surveys were conducted at baseline and after screening as a balancing measure.InterventionsMH screening workflow with a referral algorithm was developed with stakeholders. Additional interventions included two MH training workshops for healthcare providers and a preclinic reminder of eligible patients for screening.ResultsOver 21 months, 146 patients with cSLE completed 270 MH screens, increasing the screening rate from 15%, peaking at 100%, to a median of 56%. Sixty-six individuals (45%) reported symptoms of depression and/or anxiety on their initial screen. Of 270 screens, 44 individuals (17%) reported moderate to severe symptoms meeting the screening workflow criteria for referral to a MH service; 10% of patients screened were referred and seen by the MH service within 2–12 weeks. Patients and caregivers reported satisfaction with the MH screening process and quality of MH follow-up.ConclusionDespite not sustainably meeting the target, MH screening rates increased in the cSLE clinic by nearly fourfold, demonstrating feasibility and acceptability. Patients expressed satisfaction with their mental health follow-up, emphasising its importance in their care.

Introduction

SLE is a rare but severe chronic multisystem autoimmune disease associated with significant risk for organ damage and mortality, with up to 20% of all patients diagnosed in childhood.1 2 Mental health (MH) problems are prevalent in youth with childhood-onset SLE (cSLE), with cross-sectional studies estimating a prevalence of 20–60% for depression symptoms, 20–40% for anxiety symptoms and 14% for suicidal ideation.36 These are known to be associated with poor clinical outcomes such as poor medication adherence, poor health-related quality of life and worsened disease activity, pain and disability.3 7 8 Persistent depressive symptoms in adolescents, even if subthreshold for major depressive disorder, infer a greater risk for depression and suicide attempt in adulthood.9 10 Thus, it is critical to screen, identify and treat MH symptoms early on in this vulnerable population, with the potential to minimise the progression of symptoms and improve outcomes.

The American Academic of Pediatrics recommends annual routine MH screening for all youth 10 years and above, and the Canadian Paediatric Society Mental Health Task Force endorses age-appropriate screening at routine clinic visits.11 12 Despite this, rates of routine MH screening for depression and anxiety in youth with cSLE remain low both in the primary and subspecialty setting. In a published 2015 survey, only 2% of 130 surveyed paediatric rheumatologists across the USA and Canada reported that they perform routine depression screening for their patients, despite 85% in favour of this practice.13 The most cited barriers included lack of time, limited MH resources, patient and families’ misconceptions and stigma, and lack of healthcare provider (HCP) training in MH.13 14 The Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) Mental Health Workgroup recently developed and prioritised MH research topics, among which MH screening was rated the most feasible and actionable.15 Work on MH care gaps for youth with rheumatological conditions identified four key strategies for effective rheumatology-based MH intervention: integration of proactive MH care in paediatric rheumatology settings; optimisation of existing resources; improvement of collaboration with local MH providers; and providing access to peer support resources for patients and families.16

A strong body of evidence exists in chronic disease populations that supports the use of MH screening tools with patient referral resources. In one US study, routine formal depression screening was shown to be feasible in a busy subspecialty clinic whereby formal depression screening rates among children with cSLE were raised from an average of 3.3% per month to a sustained monthly rate of more than 80%, with appropriate referral to MH resources.17 Similarly, routine annual depression screening for paediatric diabetes across four clinics was feasibly implemented over 2 years increasing rates from 0% to 75%, and 89% of those positively screened met with a social worker.18 A qualitative study of adolescents with cSLE and their caregivers indicated that they placed equal importance on physical and emotional health, highlighting the growing awareness of MH need.14

In 2018, the ‘4 Mental Health Project’, a pilot aimed at integrating mental and physical health at the hospital, introduced the patient health questionnaire (PHQ-4), a brief self-administered screening tool for depression and anxiety in the cSLE clinic at the tertiary care centre. It consists of four questions derived from a 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), which assesses depression, and a 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), which assesses anxiety. Both questionnaires have been validated in the paediatric population, specifically with adolescents ≥12 years of age.19 20 If the PHQ-4 was positive in the screen, then the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 would be administered. As part of the 2025 Strategic Plan, the hospital adopted a comprehensive MH strategy to provide a more seamless MH experience for patients and families, including emphasis on improving identification of MH concerns.

In July 2021, a multidisciplinary quality improvement (QI) team called the ‘Lupus wellness’ team was developed in the Division of Rheumatology at the tertiary care centre with a primary focus on improving MH screening. Future projects from the Lupus wellness team may focus on understanding patient-reported outcomes such as fatigue and pain. The aim of this QI initiative was to increase the percentage of eligible patients with cSLE (≥12–18 years old with no known prior diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety disorder) who completed routine MH screening for depression and anxiety from 15% to 80% by November 2023.

Methods

Context: setting and local problem

The clinic is a multidisciplinary cSLE outpatient clinic following over 200 children and adolescents with SLE aged less than 18, with more than 60 newly diagnosed patients each year. Weekly clinics review 20–25 patients, and the staff include paediatric rheumatologists, nephrologists, rotating trainees and a nurse. Despite the lack of a dedicated on-site MH provider, the team collaborates closely with adolescent medicine, psychiatry, neuropsychology and social work. Weekly virtual preclinic team meetings are held to review patient care plans.

Key stakeholders in this project included the rheumatology HCPs, psychology, psychiatry, adolescent medicine and social work providers caring for patients with cSLE.

The project was conceived in June 2021 with a projected end date of September 2022. As the project progressed, it became clear that more time was needed to align stakeholders with the proposed change ideas and to conduct more Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) cycles. The project was therefore extended to November 2023.

A 7-month prospective chart audit of 189 eligible patients with cSLE (July 2021–January 2022) revealed a mean baseline PHQ-4 completion rate of 15% (n=28) of patients with cSLE. Of the completed screens, 14% (n=4) were positive. In 2020, a retrospective study conducted locally found that among adolescent patients with cSLE referred to adolescent medicine, 51% exhibited mood concerns, yet only 22% were referred specifically for such issues.21 This indicates a lack of recognition of MH symptoms. In comparison to the study, the baseline rate of PHQ-4 screening and the rate of positive screens were low.

Root cause analysis of the low screening rate was conducted with stakeholders from September to December 2021 using a fishbone diagram (online supplemental appendix A) and 5 Whys, followed by a Pareto Chart (online supplemental appendix B). Stakeholders were asked to place 10 votes on the greatest contributing root causes to poor MH screening rates from their perspective. Out of 14 stakeholders, 9 responded with 90 votes. The most common barriers included limited MH resources, lack of standardised clinic workflow, lack of HCP MH training, uncertainty around management of positive MH screens and patient/family stigma.

From November 2022–May 2023, patients with cSLE (n=28) and their caregivers (n=18) responded to an anonymised survey evaluating their perception of MH care in rheumatology. Baseline attitudes toward MH screening were overall positive with 91% (n=23) of patients and 100% of caregivers (n=18) reporting that they believe emotional health is important, and that they support routine MH screening by their rheumatologist. In addition to accessing MH providers, patients indicated that they were also interested in online MH resources. Patients emphasised privacy and having a follow-up MH plan as essential components to feeling comfortable in the MH screening process.

Interventions

Based on the Pareto diagram, the stakeholders developed a driver diagram (figure 1) to identify the change ideas that mapped to the identified root causes. Over a 21-month period (February 2022–November 2023), four interventions were developed.

GAD-7, 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7; MH, mental health; PHQ-9, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire.

MH screening clinical workflow for HCPs

A clinical workflow (figure 2) was developed with stakeholder input. Multiple developmental PDSAs were conducted to streamline this process to address concerns regarding increased work for HCPs. In one PDSA, HCPs timed their MH screen, and with adjustments to the provider workflow, reduced the time required for screening to 15 min on average. Additionally, in response to patient feedback for more frequent opportunities to discuss MH in clinic, our screening frequency increased from annual screens to include any patient who had not completed screening in the preceding 3 months. To ensure the successful oversight of these workflow adjustments and screening process, the lupus fellow took on the role of the project lead, also known as the ‘MH champion’. The MH champion plays a crucial role in overseeing the clinic workflow, available to answer any questions about the algorithm, and guide in managing results of screening. This role can be filled by any clinic team member—trainee, fellow, staff or nurse—knowledgeable of the change ideas.

Swimlane clinic workflow illustrating the movement of a patient with childhood-onset SLE through the clinic and how the mental health (MH) screen is administered. GAD-7, 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder; PHQ-9, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire.

Virtual MH educational workshops

Aiming to increase HCPs’ awareness and knowledge of MH burden and care through education, two virtual MH workshops (online supplemental appendix C) were conducted in the first 3 months after the new workflow was introduced. The workshops were attended by physicians (staff and trainees), nurses and other allied health practitioners (eg, social workers, physiotherapists, occupational therapists). They featured guest speakers and interactive role-playing scenarios highlighting the introduction of the screening tools, interpretation of screen results and how to conduct suicide risk assessments and safety planning. A pre- and post-assessment was administered to check for knowledge retention (online supplemental appendix D).

Clinical algorithm for MH screening scores

Based on feedback from both HCPs and patients emphasising the importance of having a plan following MH screening, a clinical algorithm (figure 3) to standardise the review and management of the screening results was developed in collaboration with MH stakeholders. In addition, a ‘who should you refer to?’ guide was developed to facilitate referrals to appropriate MH service, incorporating criteria to aid in decision-making. Four symptom severity categories were used based on existing interpretations of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores: none to minimal, mild, moderate and severe. Several developmental and testing PDSAs were conducted with the clinic team to improve clarity, particularly regarding clear criteria for referrals to MH services (online supplemental appendix E) and suicide risk assessment. An MH champion was appointed to oversee clinic workflow, provide education on and recap of the clinic algorithm.

Clinic algorithm for mental health (MH) management based on MH screening results. ED, emergency department; GAD-7, 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7; PHQ-9, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire.

MH resources for patients and caregivers

In response to the patient and caregiver baseline satisfaction survey, an MH handout was developed and approved by the hospital, listing local resources on psychoeducation, crisis hotlines and educational websites.

Study of interventions

Results of the interventions were reviewed and shared monthly with the team as well as with the hospital’s MH strategy committee to ensure synergy with the organisation. Updated control charts were shared periodically with the larger rheumatology division.

Family of measures

The outcome measure was the percentage of eligible patients with cSLE (aged between 12 and 18 years old, and with no prior diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety disorder) who completed PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires, tracked using electronic health record (EHR) flowsheets. The eligibility criteria were later changed to include patients who also had a diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety based on patient feedback.

Process measures used to evaluate and adjust interventions for the PDSA cycles included (1) rate of correct responses on a pre/post MH educational workshop assessment for HCPs; (2) patient/caregiver satisfaction survey responses. Patient satisfaction was measured by an anonymous survey administered at least 8 weeks after the MH screen (online supplemental appendix F). The survey was hosted on a RedCap database and disseminated through clinic visits, email and phone. The survey assessed changes in perspectives regarding MH screening, satisfaction with their MH follow-up and general feedback regarding the MH screening process.

Balancing measures included (1) the rate of referrals to MH services (adolescent medicine, social work, psychiatry) and (2) the number of weeks between referral and MH service receipt by patients, with both measures determined through chart review.

Statistical analysis

This was a QI study using the Method for Improvement framework. Baseline data collection occurred between July 2021 and January 2022. New data were gathered on a weekly basis since implementation on 15 February 2022. Statistical process control (SPC)–p-charts were used to determine whether the series of interventions had a meaningful and sustained impact on the outcome measure. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the primary outcome measure. Qualitative description was used to summarise text responses from patient and caregiver satisfaction surveys.

Ethical considerations

Verbal informed consent was obtained from patients and caregivers who were approached for satisfaction surveys. The clinical algorithm outlined next appropriate steps for HCPs in case of suicidal ideation that required immediate action. This was coupled with education led by MH providers on how to perform suicide risk assessment and safety planning. We used the SQUIRE (Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence) checklist in preparing this report.22

Patient and public involvement

Patient involvement was achieved primarily through anonymous surveys at the start of the project before implementation of the MH screen to gather information about attitudes pertaining to MH, screening modalities and post-screen referral pathways. In response to patient feedback, the project team developed a MH resource sheet to be distributed to all patients who have received MH screening. Anonymous post-implementation survey responses informed PDSA cycles to expand patient recruitment to include all patients irrespective of prior psychiatric diagnoses and to more frequently provide MH screening (to every 3 months).

Results

Outcome measure

During the 21-month intervention period (February 2022–November 2023), 270 MH screens of 146 individuals were completed. Eighty-seven individuals were screened twice, 34 completed three screens and 3 had four screens. The percentage of completed MH screens steadily increased from the baseline of 15% after a series of interventions to optimise the workflow and to provide education to HCPs (figure 4). By October 2022, the target of 80% PHQ-9 and GAD-7 completion was reached, with a peak of 100% in December 2022. However, once the Lupus MH champion left the institution in January 2023, screening rates dropped below the original set target. With each personnel changeover, a brief orientation was conducted to train the new lupus fellow on the workflow, clinical algorithm and data collection. From April 2023–June 2023, screening rates approached the target of 80%, but once again with the fellow MH lead changing over, the screening rates dropped. At the end of the intervention period, the mean screening rate in clinic for adolescents with cSLE was 56%.

Pre- and post-assessments conducted for MH workshops showed an increase in knowledge retention, with respondents scoring an average of 40% correct answers on pre-assessment and 80% on post-assessment. Feedback from HCPs indicated a positive impact: ‘I feel better about handling MH issues’, yet ‘still find suicide anxiety provoking’.

Given the feedback, we changed our MH screening protocol in February 2023 from annual screening of patients without a prior psychiatric diagnosis to screening of all patients without an MH screen in the last 3 months, including patients who have previously had a psychiatric diagnosis. The change in eligibility for screening coincided with the departure of the MH champion, which led to temporarily lowered rates of completed MH screens.

Balancing measures

Over the 21-month course of the intervention, 27 referrals (10%) were made to MH services as per the ‘who should you refer to?’ guide, which represents a rate of 1.6 patients per month. All referred patients with cSLE were seen by an appropriate MH service (social worker, adolescent medicine or psychiatry) within 2–12 weeks of the screening result, depending on the waitlist of the service involved and the urgency of the referral. Eighteen patients were referred to adolescent medicine, seven patients to social work and two patients to psychiatry.

Patient outcomes

Of the 146 unique patients with initial screens, 66 (45%) scored mild or above for one or both screening measures (18 positive for PHQ-9 only, 9 positive for GAD-7 only and 39 positive for both). Less than 15% of patients had moderate to severe symptoms of depression and anxiety on their initial screen (figure 5). Nine patients (6%) indicated suicidal ideation, and their PHQ-9 scores ranged from 7 to 21. On clarification with the nine patients during private interviews, they endorsed passive suicidal ideation and thus did not require immediate presentation to the ED. Safety plans were made with all patients, and urgent referrals were made to psychiatry (n=1), adolescent medicine (n=4) and social work (n=2). One of the patients was seen by social work at their same-day appointment. The two patients not referred were already connected to MH services, and a plan was made with the patient to reconnect with their MH care provider.

Discussion

Summary and interpretation

In July 2021, our multidisciplnary team aimed to enhance the percentage of eligible adolescent patients with cSLE (aged 12–18 years with no known prior diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety disorder) completing routine MH screening for depression and anxiety from 15% to 80% by November 2023. To achieve this, this study established an MH screening pathway, referral algorithm and HCP MH educational workshops. While the initial target of 80% was met but not sustained, the screening rate increased nearly fourfold. The decline in screening rates occurred following the departure of MH champions, highlighting the importance of having an on-site MH champion ensuring continuity.

With regard to fidelity, there were high rates of knowledge retention after the MH educational workshops for HCPs. Patient and caregiver satisfaction surveys revealed positive perceptions regarding the importance of MH discussions during clinic visits and overall satisfaction with the quality of their follow-up care. Patients clearly indicated that MH screening from their rheumatologist was a welcome and positive experience. Early results from the surveys influenced the MH screening protocol such that screening for all adolescent patients with cSLE at an increased frequency (every 3 months) was implemented. These positive findings indicate good uptake of educational interventions and standardised work, suggesting a positive association with the outcome measure.

Concerns regarding overburdening the MH system were allayed by stable rates of referral (10% of screens in 21 months). Despite the initial concerns, there was a consensus among MH providers that referrals became more suitable and timelier with the implementation of clearly defined referral criteria in the clinic algorithm.

Similar to existing literature, the study found that about half of patients with cSLE reported no to minimal symptoms of depression and anxiety (54%, n=82). Forty-six per cent of patients reported mild to severe symptoms and 7% of screens were positive for suicidal ideation (n=18). One American study examining provider education and pre-visit planning to increase cSLE MH screening reported similar rates of patients with at least mild symptoms of depression (48.2%) and thoughts of self-harm (12.5%).17 These high rates of depression and anxiety indicate that MH is important in the cSLE population, and that identification of these issues is paramount to patients receiving appropriate treatment.21

Key challenges

The project encountered several key challenges during implementation. First, reliance on paper-based questionnaires presented a significant obstacle to sustainability. This manual process, involving printing paper questionnaires and inputting answers into the EHR by HCPs, was deemed time-intensive despite the acknowledged importance of MH screening. Additionally, personnel turnover requiring detailed reorientation also affected sustainability. To address this issue, collaboration with hospital’s MH strategy and EPIC groups is underway to explore electronic platforms for patient-reported outcomes, thereby automating the process, reducing waste and improving efficiency.

Second, the high turnover of elective trainees in the cSLE clinic posed difficulties in sustaining the workflow. Many trainees were unfamiliar with the screening tools and clinic workflow already taught to the rheumatology team. To address this challenge, weekly emails and a clinic flow diagram were sent prior to clinic sessions, with processes reiterated during morning huddles. An automated platform may help mitigate this problem as well.

Finally, ensuring appropriate MH follow-up for positive screens, particularly for patients with moderate to severe scores, posed another challenge. Education of providers on next steps after positive screening, providing a clinical decision aid and engagement of MH services helped address this issue. Patient satisfaction surveys highlighted the importance of having a plan for the patient’s MH after screening, thereby emphasising the importance of addressing these challenges for effective patient care. A collaborative care follow-up model is being explored as a change concept moving forward.

Limitations

There are several limitations that merit mention. Primarily, the multidisciplinary clinic was specific to cSLE given the complex nature of the disease and resource availability at this centre, which may constrain generalisability and reproducibility of the project. Resource limitations may include difficulty obtaining multidisciplinary stakeholder engagement or MH resources. For settings with fewer personnel, the MH screening role could be shared among care providers such as nurses, physicians, social work and administrative staff after appropriate training on confidentiality and safety planning.

Second, there were missing data on the reason patients were not referred to an MH provider despite a high screening score. Some patients were not referred as they were already connected to MH providers or preferred to be referred by their family physician.

Third, the absence of demographic data collection precludes commentary on factors such as age, socioeconomic status or educational level that could influence questionnaire uptake. Furthermore, as the clinic does not see patients over the age of 18, the project could not comment on transition-age patients, who are a vulnerable population for which MH is very important.

Fourth, self-reported screening tools for depression and anxiety may underestimate or overestimate the extent of these conditions due to various factors such as the influence of current mental state on reporting, cognitive difficulties stemming from illness and treatments, or apprehension about facing judgemental attitudes from HCPs or caregivers.

Lastly, to simplify the screening process, the project was limited to English-speaking patients, although this did not exclude a significant portion of our patient population. Next steps include integration of a multilingual screening tool into the clinic workflow ensuring a more equitable MH screening.

Conclusions

Depression and anxiety are prevalent among children and adolescents with cSLE, with 45% of patients in this cSLE clinic experiencing at least mild symptoms. Over 21 months, MH screening rates improved from 15%, peaking at 100% and settling to a mean of 56% in the cSLE clinic. Patients received appropriate MH management including psychoeducation and timely referrals to MH services. Stakeholder and patient engagement, alignment with organisational values and MH training for providers were crucial in the success of this project. Strengths of the project include an intuitive clinical algorithm, MH provider buy-in and patient input. Overall, this initiative underlines the importance of timely screening and intervention in optimising patient MH outcomes.

Next steps: future directions

Our innovative multidisciplinary model presents opportunities to enhance MH experience through leveraging existing resources. With the next steps, we would recommend the inclusion of an on-site MH provider, the adoption of a collaborative care model and automation of the screening process.

An on-site MH provider such as a social worker could streamline the screening process by both providing safe MH screening and providing support more efficiently—a concept supported by a similar study conducted in the USA.17

There is a lack of standardised communication and follow-up procedure after screening. Adopting a collaborative care model could address this issue.23 We recommend implementing regular communication with MH services, peer support resources for patients and families, coordinated patient visits with specialists, and standardised documentation and communication processes to enhance follow-up and coordination of care.

Lastly, as demonstrated by the limited sustainability of the project attributed to turnover of both clinical staff and the MH champion, automation of the screening process could significantly reduce unnecessary steps in the clinical workflow, reduce waste by eliminating paper-based questionnaires and reduce errors in transcription of MH screening scores. Automation may make MH screening more feasible in a setting with limited staffing support. Such an intervention would require careful design and usability testing to ensure that patients continue to find the process safe, supportive and seamless.
