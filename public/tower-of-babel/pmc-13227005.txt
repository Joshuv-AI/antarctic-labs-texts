Intraoperative PTH monitoring: does sampling site or assay generation matter?
Authors: Rola Kwayess, Sara Ajjour, Maya Rahme, Mariam Assaad, Marlene Chakhtoura, Omar Kreidieh, Jaber Abbas, Samar Jabbour-Khoury, Rose T Daher, Etienne Cavalier, Ghada El-Hajj Fuleihan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextPrimary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common endocrine disorder for which minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (MIP) is the standard surgical approach. Intraoperative parathyroid hormone (IOPTH) monitoring is commonly performed using second-generation (2G) parathyroid hormone (PTH) assay.ObjectiveTo compare the performance of IOPTH central sampling peripheral sampling, and third-generation (3G) vs 2G assays in patients with PHPT.MethodsThis is a prospective cohort study conducted at American University of Beirut Medical Center, a tertiary hospital in Beirut, Lebanon. The study included 68 subjects with confirmed PHPT who achieved surgical cure, defined as normocalcemia at ≥6 months. IOPTH levels were measured from central and peripheral venous samples using 2G and 3G assays at various time points.ResultsThe median age was 57 years, and 80% were female. One-fourth had osteoporosis or reported kidney stones. Median preoperative calcium was 10.8 mg/dL with PTH 104 pg/mL, and 82% had single-gland disease. IOPTH levels were significantly higher with 2G assay and central sampling vs 3G assay and peripheral sampling. The proportion of patients meeting the Miami criterion did not differ between assays or sampling sites. Median percentage drop in IOPTH was greater with the 3G assay compared to 2G, across all time points, at both sampling sites.ConclusionAlthough both assays similarly identified a ≥50% intraoperative decline, 3G assays demonstrated a steeper and more consistent drop, suggesting that higher assay-specific thresholds may improve decision-making. Central and peripheral sampling showed comparable predictive performance, although central sampling may allow earlier detection of PTH decline.

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common endocrine disorder, with parathyroidectomy being the main treatment. The most recent estimates on prevalence of PHPT in the United States from 2010 suggest an overall rate of 233 per 100 000 in women and 85 per 100 000 in men, with the highest overall prevalence in Black and White women aged 70 to 79 years [1]. Surgical cure of PHPT is defined as normal calcium levels at 6 months postoperatively, which is maintained thereafter [1, 2]. Most cases arise from a single parathyroid adenoma while multi-gland hyperplasia accounts for 10% to 15% of cases [1]. Patients with concordant results of both Sestamibi scanning and ultrasound of the neck represent the easiest to treat, and image-guided minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (MIP) results in cure rates exceeding 95% [3].

Intraoperative parathyroid hormone (IOPTH) monitoring stands as a pivotal adjunct in the surgical management of PHPT since 1996, aiming to ensure the excision of the indexed parathyroid gland, decrease the risk of postoperative hypocalcemia, and accurately predict cure [4]. It enables surgeons to determine the successful removal of hyperfunctioning glands intraoperatively and predict success in the long term with less morbidity [5]. The incorporation of IOPTH facilitated MIP by reducing the need for further unnecessary explorations and decreasing potential costs and operative time [6, 7].

Several criteria have been developed to predict the success of MIP based on the percent drop in PTH levels. The Miami criterion, as defined by Irvin et al, predicts postoperative normocalcemia with an IOPTH drop of ≥50% from the highest of either preoperative baseline or pre-excision level compared to the level at 10 minutes after the indexed parathyroid gland(s) excision [8]. The Miami criterion was shown to have robust performance metrics with the highest accuracy of 97.2% but was based on the second-generation (2G) assay. The 2G assay, also known as “intact” PTH assay, captures both full-length biologically active PTH (1-84) and N-terminal truncated PTH fragments, which can accumulate in patients with chronic kidney disease and lead to overestimation of true active hormone levels. In contrast, the third-generation (3G) or “bio-intact” or “whole” PTH assay does not seem to cross-react with non-(1-84) fragments thus improving analytic specificity. Both assays are available on the market; the 2G assay (with a short turnaround time) is mostly used in clinical practice and the 3G assay is mostly used in research [9, 10]. As for sampling site, some surgeons prefer to draw IOPTH samples from the internal jugular vein during parathyroidectomy, instead of placing an additional peripheral cannula, for more ease of access [11]. However, it is uncertain whether the Miami criterion that was developed using peripheral venous samples can be used for central venous samples to successfully predict cure. It is also unclear how it may differ when using the 3G assay.

Our study aimed to investigate the impact of sampling site selection, central vs peripheral, and of PTH assay type, 2G vs 3G, on PTH levels before and after parathyroidectomy.

Methods

Ethics statement

All participants signed a written consent form, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC).

Study setting

This prospective study was conducted at AUBMC from 2014 till 2024. We recruited patients with primary hyperparathyroidism presenting to the preoperative assessment unit (PAU) for evaluation awaiting parathyroidectomy. Patients with elevated calcium and elevated or inappropriately normal PTH level, ideally with a urinary calcium that is not low, or a Ca/Cr Cl > 0.02, as well as patients with normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism (normal serum calcium and elevated PTH) with evidence of cure by serum calcium level at ≥6 months follow-up were included. Pathology reports were examined to confirm abnormal parathyroid pathology. Several patients were only evaluated by surgeons, without input from endocrinologists; thus, they may be missing certain laboratory values such as urinary calcium collection, x-rays, or densitometry.

Data collection

We recorded data on age, sex, body mass index (preoperatively), presence of medical conditions or thyroid nodules, osteoporosis (confirmed by bone mineral density), hyperparathyroidism symptoms (fatigue, polyuria, polydipsia, history of kidney stones, etc) and family history of PHPT. We also collected information for patient laboratory and radiography tests as available from electronic records or clinic charts. These included preoperative baseline serum calcium, ionized calcium, phosphorus, 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-(OH)D), creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation [12], alkaline phosphatase, PTH concentrations, and 24-hour urine for calcium and creatinine. Additionally, we reported on prior serum calcium and PTH levels (up to 3 levels for each test) measured from previous years. We recorded findings of Sestamibi scan and/or ultrasound done prior to surgery and noted whether patients underwent concomitant thyroidectomy.

We further included data on the number of excised glands and their pathological characteristics as reported in the pathology report. Our pathologists follow the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for proper diagnosis of adenoma vs hyperplasia [13].

PTH and 25-(OH)D assays

The Roche PTH assays used for testing are: Elecsys PTH STAT (2G assay) (Roche Diagnostics Cat# 08928428190, RRID:AB_3750524) and Elecsys PTH (1-84) (3G assay) (Roche Diagnostics Cat# 09005765190, RRID:AB_3750525) electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (ECLIA) on Cobas e immunoassay analyzers (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Sandhofer Strasse 116, D-68305 Mannheim, Germany). Both assays employ a sandwich test principle, where a biotinylated monoclonal antibody reacts with the N-terminal fragment and another monoclonal antibody labeled with a ruthenium complex reacts with the C-terminal fragment. The PTH STAT (2G) assay measures the “Intact” hormone, where the first antibody binds the N-terminal fragment (1-37) and the second antibody binds the C-terminal fragment (38-84), with an assay duration of 9 minutes. However, it cross-reacts up to 96% with large C-terminal fragments like PTH 7-84. On the other hand, the Elecsys PTH (1-84) (3G) assay is more specific and measures the full-length, bio-intact/active PTH (1-84) molecule in human serum and plasma with no cross-reactivity with known other fragments and an assay duration of 18 minutes.

As for traceability, the PTH STAT (2G) assay has been standardized against a commercial PTH test (RIA), while the PTH (1-84) (3G) assay against WHO international standard 95/646 as per the kit inserts. The expected reference range for the PTH STAT (2G) assay as per manufacturer are 17.3-74.1 pg/mL for PTH STAT (2G assay) and 14.9-56.9 pg/mL for PTH (1-84) (3G assay). The intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) for the PTH STAT (2G) assay were 1.3% and 1.0% at means of 43.0 pg/mL and 156.6 pg/mL, respectively. Its inter-assay CVs were 2.3% at 40.0 pg/mL and 2.1% at 150.9 pg/mL, as determined in-house. The intra-assay CVs for the PTH (1-84) (3G) assay were 2.4% at 41.9 pg/mL and 1.7% at 129 pg/mL, while the inter-assay CVs were 4.0% at 40.7 pg/mL and 4.1% at 121.4 pg/mL, as per the manufacturer.

For assay of serum or plasma 25-(OH)D, we used the Roche protein binding assay. Three different versions of the assay were utilized over the study period: Vitamin D total, Elecsys Vitamin D total II, and Elecsys Vitamin D total III with improved specificity and better cross-reactivity for 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 vs D3. The assay's measuring ranges were changed from 3.0-70 ng/mL to 3.0-100.0 ng/mL, to 3.0-120.0 ng/mL, respectively. At AUBMC Clinical Chemistry laboratory, the intra-assay CV was 4.8% to 7.4%, and inter-assay CV was 2.4% to 3.2% for values between 19.6 and 36.7 ng/mL.

The laboratory participates in the quality assurance, evaluation, and accreditation from the College of American Pathologists (www.cap.org) and DEQAS (www.Deqas.org) and has consistently performed against target values.

Sample collection intraoperatively

Surgeons participating in the study withdrew venous samples intraoperatively through the internal jugular vein as per their standard clinical practice, as well as through a peripheral vein. They used angiocath size 18 in the antecubital site opposite the site where the intravenous (IV) line for anesthesia was inserted and then allowed the blood to be drawn by suction into the chemistry tubes. Blood samples were drawn peripherally pre-incision, and from central and peripheral sites at baseline just before gland removal (pre-excision), and at 5 minutes, 10 minutes, and in certain cases, more than 10 minutes after gland removal. Samples from both sites were analyzed at the AUBMC laboratory using 2G and 3G assays.

Follow-up

Follow-up laboratory results were collected 6 months or beyond, mostly at our medical center, but in some cases, due to logistics, at another laboratory. We measured serum calcium, to determine if patients were normocalcemic, as evidence of cure. For patients who had their tests performed at our medical center laboratory, we also measured serum ionized calcium, serum 25-(OH)D, and PTH, using both 2G and 3G assays.

Statistical analysis

Prior to initiating the analysis, we examined the normality of variables. Descriptive baseline values for each variable were represented as median [IQR] for continuous and N (%) for categorical variables, as applicable for baseline characteristics and follow-up laboratory results. We calculated median PTH values and percent decay for peripheral and central venous sampling sites using 2G and 3G assays for patients. For the peripheral site, we calculated the drop from the highest level of either pre-incision or pre-excision baseline value, and from pre-excision level for central site. We used Wilcoxon signed rank test to compare the median levels and percent decline at 5 and 10 minutes or beyond between the different assay/sampling site combinations. We compared the proportions of individuals with elevated 2G PTH (>74.1 pg/mL) and 3G PTH (>56.9 pg/mL) at baseline using the McNemar test. We reported the two-tailed P values of the different comparisons made, and a P value < .05 was considered significant. We produced bar graphs of the proportion of patients with a drop ≥50% according to Miami criteria and compared the proportions using McNemar test for the different assay/sampling site combinations. We rounded age and body mass index to one decimal to the closest integer, and % for categorical variables to integers. We did not correct P values for multiple testing. We also compared the baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 89 study subjects who underwent parathyroidectomy at AUBMC during the study period (2014-2024) consented to participate in the study. For the current analyses, we included subjects who had clear evidence of PHPT, through biochemical profile according to the latest definition [14], and had evidence for achieving normocalcemia at follow-up. We therefore excluded 21 subjects; 16 did not have follow-up results at 6 months or beyond, 1 had an elevated calcium at follow-up, 1 had antibodies against the PTH assay (verified by our laboratory), 1 had secondary hyperparathyroidism, and 2 had normal surgical parathyroid pathology.

The participants’ median age was 57 (51-63) years; the majority were females, with female to male ratio 3.8:1. One-fourth of participants had osteoporosis by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry scans and one-fourth had history of renal stones. The majority (82%) of participants had single-gland disease (Table S1a) [15]. The median preoperative serum calcium level was 10.8 mg/dL and 76% had levels >10.5 mg/dL (Table 1). Despite normal calcium level in around 20% of patients at the time of surgery, their previous medical data confirmed prior hypercalcemia. The median preoperative PTH level was 104 pg/mL and 88% of patients had elevated PTH levels with a median 107 pg/mL and a corresponding calcium level of 10.8 mg/dL. The median for those with inappropriately normal PTH was 58.8 pg/mL, with a median calcium level of 10.7 mg/dL. In addition, 39 subjects had elevated ionized calcium (>1.40 mmol/L) with a median of 1.42 mmol/L (Table 1).

Comparison of 2G vs 3G PTH assays

The proportion of subjects with pre-incision PTH levels above the upper limit of normal was higher with the 3G assay than with the 2G assay (80% vs 73.1%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .2). Of those who had normal pre-incision 2G PTH and elevated Ca (N = 14), 5 had elevated pre-incision 3G PTH levels (35.7%).

We compared median IOPTH levels using both assays at baseline, 5 minutes, 10 minutes, and beyond 10 minutes from central and peripheral sampling sites (Table 3). Regardless of the sampling site and time point (baseline, 5 minutes, 10 minutes, >10 minutes), median PTH values were statistically significantly higher using 2G assay in comparison to 3G assay (Table 3).

Sampling site differences (central vs peripheral)

For our second hypothesis, we assessed the differences in absolute median IOPTH levels and percent drops between peripheral and central sampling sites, using both assays, at various time points. As shown in Table 3, the 2G assay central samples consistently showed significantly higher median PTH levels compared with peripheral samples, at all time points (all P values < .05). The same significant difference was noted using 3G assay at all time points except at 5 minutes.

However, the proportion of patients with IOPTH drop ≥50% within each assay did not show any significant difference between central vs peripheral sites (Fig. 2). When analyzing the proportion of subjects who achieved the Miami criterion, central samples demonstrated a larger % drop at 5 minutes using both assays (2G (P < .001) and 3G (P = .007)), but not at 10 minutes or beyond 10 minutes (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study provides one of the most comprehensive evaluations to date on IOPTH monitoring in PHPT, comparing both assay generations and sampling sites within the same patient cohort.

2G vs 3G assays

Our findings show that although both assays effectively identified patients who achieved the Miami criterion, the 3G assay consistently yielded lower absolute PTH values. These align with previous literature and might be attributable to differences in assay standardization techniques [16]. This finding is expected, since the 3G assay is calibrated against the WHO 95/646 IS whereas the 2G is standardized against the former Allegro intact PTH assay. The 3G assay resulted in significantly greater percent decline across all time points and sampling sites among those who fulfilled the Miami criterion. Notably, all patients had ≥50% drop using the 3G assay >10 minutes after excision, compared to 88% with the 2G assay. This suggests that 3G assay may provide greater reliability and higher sensitivity in confirming biochemical cure, especially when rapid intraoperative decision-making is needed. Our findings are also consistent with the fact that the 2G assay cross-reacts with non-(1-84) PTH in contrast to 3G assay, which showed less cross-reactivity [17-19]

In patients with chronic kidney disease, 3G assays have been shown to predict cure more accurately in comparison to 2G assays [20]. To date, the majority of studies focused on the advantages of using the 3G assay over the 2G assay in secondary hyperparathyroidism, with very few studies in PHPT [21, 22].

Lang et al evaluated patients with PHPT and confirmed that the 3G assay showed a steeper decline and greater normalization of IOPTH at 10 and 20 minutes after excision compared to the 2G assay [23]. Importantly, they noted that while overall predictive accuracy was similar between both assays using the Miami criterion (∼91%), the 3G assay appeared less influenced by renal function [23]. Similarly, a previous study conducted in Lebanon comparing IOPTH decline provided comparable clinical results, albeit with lower absolute values [24]. The differences in drop magnitude could be attributed to the broader detection range of 2G assays and its cross-reactivity with the non-(1-84) PTH fragment [25]. These findings were also reported in a case study by Cavalier et al who noted spurious results with 2G assay; whereby in a cured patient, the Miami criterion was fulfilled using 3G assay and not 2G assay. They hypothesized that the manipulation of the parathyroid gland during surgery leads to the production of non-(1-84) PTH fragment [10].

By integrating comparisons of both assay generations and multiple sampling sites, our study strengthens current evidence supporting the clinical utility of 3G assays in PHPT. The more rapid decline with 3G assays may enhance intraoperative decision-making in equivocal cases and potentially shorten operative time. This is provided 3G assay turnaround time can be made faster to enable its use intraoperatively. Collectively, these findings suggest that the traditional Miami criterion, developed using 2G assays, may warrant re-evaluation when applied to 3G assays to optimize predictive accuracy and surgical efficiency.

Central vs peripheral sampling

Our study demonstrated that central venous sampling consistently yielded higher values than peripheral sampling across most intraoperative time points, regardless of assay type. The difference was statistically significant at all time points using 2G assay and all but the 5-minute point for 3G assay. These findings align with previous reports attributing elevated central levels to the proximity of sampling sites to the parathyroid glands [5]. Indeed, this could raise concerns about the validity of established criteria with such a cutoff.

Despite the higher absolute values, both central and peripheral sites were equivalent in achieving the Miami criterion. Among patients achieving the Miami criterion, the central site showed a significantly steeper early decline at 5 minutes using both assays, suggesting that central sampling could provide earlier intraoperative confirmation of cure, although the absolute time advantage was modest.

These findings align in part with prior studies that evaluated the impact of sample site on IOPTH monitoring. Woodrum et al reported significantly higher central PTH levels but similar rates of ≥50% IOPTH drop at 10 minutes [5]. Although central levels were generally higher at 10 minutes, this discrepancy resolved by 15 minutes; implying that the surgeon utilizing central venous samples may need to extend the sampling period [5].

Similarly, Beyer et al and Abdel-Misih et al evaluated patients with simultaneous sampling from peripheral and central sites and showed higher PTH concentrations with central sampling; yet sampling site did not seem to affect the ≥50% decay [11, 26]. Similar to our observation using 2G assay, Beyer et al reported that the incidence of ≥50% decay decreased in central samples beyond 10 minutes, possibly due to continued manipulation or proximity to remaining parathyroid glands [26].

A study by Edwards et al confirmed the ability of central venous PTH values to independently meet the Miami criterion and predict cure in comparison to peripheral sampling [27]. In contrast to our study, centrally obtained PTH values were significantly higher than peripheral values only at baseline [27]. Additionally, Broome et al showed significantly higher levels of PTH in central samples pre- and post-excision, similar to our results [28].

Our findings support the validity of either site for intraoperative monitoring, with no significant difference in the final predictive outcome. However, our data demonstrated that among patients achieving the Miami criterion, central samples show a steeper decline at an earlier time point (5 minutes) implying that central sampling may offer a time advantage in confirming biochemical cure more rapidly. By integrating dual-site comparisons across both assay generations, our study provides comprehensive evidence that sampling site selection can influence early intraoperative interpretation but does not alter final predictive accuracy.

Strengths and limitations

Our study includes a comprehensive intraoperative sampling strategy involving simultaneous collection from both central and peripheral sites at multiple time points. This allowed for robust within-subject comparison, minimizing inter-individual variability. Moreover, the simultaneous use of 2G and 3G assays on both sampling sites provided valuable and unique data. Indeed, most of the literature reports on assay variability used peripheral sampling. Additionally, the long follow-up duration to confirm biochemical cure further enhances the validity of our findings.

However, the study had several limitations. Ideally, we would have liked to report albumin-corrected serum calcium levels, however an albumin level was not available for all subjects. We, however, report serum ionized calcium levels. Interestingly, a new and large-scale study does not show superiority for albumin-corrected vs unadjusted calcium levels [29]. We had some missing baseline data for several participants, due to the fact that not all of them were evaluated by an endocrinologist. Our study was conducted in a single tertiary center, which may limit generalizability to community hospitals. However, it is recommended that parathyroidectomy be performed by experienced parathyroid surgeons, who usually practice at referral centers [14]. Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients had hyperplasia (38%) than usually reported (15%-20%), even if one includes double adenomas. Moreover, drawing central samples from the internal jugular vein may not be standard practice in all institutions, although surgeons seem to increasingly favor such approach [30]. We only included patients who were cured, to ensure we could assess performance of the 50% drop of IOPTH in relation to cure, but such an approach precludes validation of the predictive accuracy of the Miami criterion.

Clinical implications

Our findings support the reliability of both assays and sampling sites for IOPTH monitoring. However, the steeper declines observed with 3G assays suggest the need for assay-specific criteria, particularly as 3G testing becomes more available.

Conclusion

The Miami criterion is reliable across assay types and sampling sites. The steeper decline observed with 3G assays indicates that a higher cutoff may be appropriate when using 3G assays for intraoperative decision-making. Thus, this could reduce intraoperative decision ambiguity and potentially shorten operative time. While 2G assays remain widely used and are sufficient in most cases, 3G assays may offer advantages in patients with borderline PTH levels, impaired renal function, or when equivocal decrements are documented. In addition, both central and peripheral sampling sites provide reliable intraoperative monitoring and predictive ability with very similar kinetics. However, central sampling yielded consistently higher PTH levels and greater percentage decline, especially at earlier time points. This suggests that central sampling, which was not validated previously for use with Miami criterion, may offer a time advantage for confirming biochemical cure. Future multicenter studies including cured and noncured patients are essential to redefine Miami or alternative thresholds for 3G assays and to establish whether central sampling offers a reproducible time advantage.
