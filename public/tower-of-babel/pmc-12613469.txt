Triglyceride-glucose index and postoperative delirium: a retrospective study exploring a lipid-related marker in neuropsychiatric risk stratification after gastric surgery
Authors: Fengwei Yao, Lei Liu, Xiaolan Chen, Jian Zhang, Zhinan Hao, Zhijun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium is a common neuropsychiatric complication following major gastrointestinal surgery and is associated with poor postoperative outcomes. Patients undergoing gastric surgery often experience significant metabolic stress and insulin resistance, which may contribute to delirium. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a simple surrogate marker of insulin resistance, but its predictive value for postoperative delirium in gastric surgery patients remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective study used data from the MIMIC-IV database and included 819 ICU patients after gastric surgery. Exposure was the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index from first available labs; outcome was ICU delirium assessed by CAM-ICU. Associations were examined with multivariable logistic regression, with standard covariate adjustment.ResultsIncreased ICU delirium was independently linked to a higher TyG index (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.01–1.56). RCS revealed a U-shaped relationship. The association remained stable after PSM and across multiple subgroups. SHAP analysis confirmed TyG as a meaningful predictor.ConclusionA greater TyG index was linked in this retrospective analysis to a higher incidence of postoperative delirium in individuals after gastric surgery. These findings support preoperative risk stratification and targeted monitoring to enhance peri-operative safety and recovery in gastric surgery patients, and prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02794-1.Keywords: Delirium, Postoperative complications, General surgery, Intensive care units, Insulin resistance, Triglycerides

Introduction

Acute brain malfunction known as delirium is typified by diminished awareness and focus [1–3], and it frequently occurs among patients in intensive care units (ICUs), particularly after major surgeries such as gastric operations. These procedures often trigger a biphasic metabolic stress response—from hypometabolism to hypermetabolism—marked by acute metabolic disturbances, excessive inflammatory activation, and increased insulin resistance, all of which contribute to a heightened risk of delirium [4–6]. According to studies, the prevalence of postoperative delirium in patients after stomach surgery varies between 8.2% and 54.4% [7, 8]. This condition significantly prolongs hospitalization and increases healthcare costs, and is closely associated with long-term cognitive decline and elevated mortality, severely affecting patient outcomes and quality of life [9–11].

Although various risk prediction tools for delirium exist, most focus on traditional risk factors such as age, underlying diseases, medication use, and geriatric nutritional indices [12–15]. In recent years, metabolic abnormalities—particularly insulin resistance (IR)—have gained increasing attention for their role in the development of delirium. Through a number of pathways, including as blood-brain barrier disruption, heightened neuroinflammation, and impaired cerebral glucose metabolism, IR is believed to have a role in the pathogenesis of delirium [16]. Notably, a study reported that preoperative dexamethasone administration attenuated inflammation and reduced the incidence of POCD, further highlighting the role of inflammation in perioperative neurocognitive outcomes [17].

As a straightforward and trustworthy surrogate measure for insulin resistance, the TyG index has gained widespread acceptance [18, 19]. Previous studies have confirmed the association between insulin resistance and adverse outcomes in various clinical settings, including patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery, those with acute kidney injury, and those experiencing cardiovascular events [5, 20, 21]. Recent studies have also suggested a possible link between the TyG index and neuropsychiatric complications [22, 23]. Nevertheless, nothing is known about the possible connection between the TyG and postoperative delirium in individuals undergoing stomach surgery. Given the high metabolic stress burden and elevated risk of postoperative delirium in this population, it is clinically significant to investigate the TyG index’s association value in this context.

Therefore, this study hypothesize that the TyG index, as a proxy for insulin resistance, is associated with the occurrence of postoperative delirium in gastric surgery patients and may serve as a valuable predictive biomarker. This study aims to provide a novel metabolic perspective for identifying and managing perioperative neuropsychiatric complications.

Methods

Study participants and eligibility

This study was based on the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1) [24]. Research team was granted authorized access to the database upon completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program on human research protection and certification through PhysioNet (Certificate ID: 65083321). All patients who underwent gastric-related surgical procedures were identified using international diagnostic codes; detailed code listings are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Exclusion criteria: [1] triglyceride levels were measured more than 24 h after ICU admission; [2] patients with missing key laboratory data required for TyG calculation (i.e., triglycerides and fasting glucose); [3] patients who developed delirium on the first day of ICU.

In the end, 819 patients who satisfied the inclusion requirements were included to the research. The total study cohort was first divided into quartiles according to TyG levels (Fig. 1). Subsequently, Patients were classified into high- and low-TyG categories based on the cutoff determined by the Youden index. Patients were then classified into two groups for robustness analysis: T1 group (low TyG, ≤ 9.373054) and T2 group (high TyG, > 9.373054).

Data extraction and preliminary processing

The triglyceride data used in this study were derived from the first venous blood sample collected after hospital admission. Given that strict fasting is often unfeasible in ICU patients, this initial value was adopted as a representative measure. All other covariates were defined using the first recorded value within the first day of ICU.

TyG = ln [triglyceride (mg/dL) ×glucose (mg/dL)/2] [25]. The Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU was used to evaluate delirium [26]. CAM-ICU assessments were performed multiple times daily (typically 2–3 times) by professionally trained ICU nurses and recorded in a structured format within the ICU chart events table. This assessment protocol is aligned with the data structure and quality control procedures of the MIMIC-IV database and is consistent with standard CAM-ICU methodology. In this study, a patient was considered to have experienced delirium if at least one positive CAM-ICU evaluation occurred during their ICU stay.

For variables with a missing rate of less than 20%, this study assumed a missing-at-random (MAR) mechanism and applied multiple imputation [27]. This algorithm utilizes recursive partitioning of observed data via non-parametric regression trees to predict and impute missing values based on the joint distribution patterns of other variables. To enhance the robustness and stability of imputation, five imputed datasets were generated and assessed for consistency in variable distributions. Considering the complex regression and machine learning analyses in the subsequent steps, this study randomly selected the third imputed dataset for primary analysis to avoid overestimation of imputation-related uncertainty, a strategy commonly used in epidemiological research [28].

Applied winsorization to triglyceride and glucose values using the “DescTools” package in R [29, 30]. The impact of this preprocessing was assessed with boxplots, which showed a clear attenuation of outlier influence (Supplementary Figure S1).

Methods of analysis

The main statistical technique employed in this investigation was logistic regression. To enhance model stability and prevent overfitting, this study retained clinically relevant covariates—age, sex, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, benzodiazepine use, and insulin therapy—prior to model construction. Subsequently, the Boruta feature selection algorithm was applied to identify additional candidate variables strongly associated with ICU-acquired delirium. The Boruta algorithm was implemented using default settings. Based on Z-score importance metrics, variables were classified into three categories: “confirmed,” “rejected,” and “tentative.” All selected covariates were evaluated for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF > 5 were excluded. Four models were constructed to assess the association between TyG index and the occurrence of ICU-acquired delirium: Model 1: without adjusting for any covariates; Model 2: adjusted for age at admission, body temperature, sex, SOFA score, and GCS score; Model 3: further adjusted for key laboratory markers including platelet count, international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), and bicarbonate level; Model 4: additionally included ICU treatment- and complication-related variables such as insulin use, benzodiazepine use, cerebrovascular disease, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus, use of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, and acute kidney injury (AKI) to construct a comprehensive model adjusting for potential confounders. The constancy of the TyG–delirium connection across strata of patient was evaluated using subgroup analysis. Interaction effects were tested using likelihood ratio tests.

Lastly, to develop machine learning prediction models, a performance-oriented feature selection strategy was employed. Specifically, only the features selected by the Boruta algorithm were used as input variables across all models to ensure consistency and interpretability. 70% of the dataset was used to train all models, while the remaining 30% was used for validation. Five-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set to prevent overfitting. Hyperparameters for each machine learning model were optimized using grid search combined with five-fold cross-validation to ensure optimal generalization performance. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as the primary performance metric. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was used on the finished model to improve the machine learning prediction model’s interpretability and measure each predictor’s proportional contribution to the likelihood of ICU-acquired delirium [31]. SHAP values were computed as the mean marginal contribution of each feature across all possible combinations, and a global feature importance plot was generated accordingly.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 819 ICU patients included in this study following gastric surgery, 481 patients (58.73%) developed delirium, while 338 patients (41.27%) did not. Compared to the non-delirium group, patients in the delirium group were slightly younger, had higher serum creatinine levels and lower platelet counts, greater body weight, lower systolic blood pressure, higher body temperature, and elevated SOFA and SAPS II scores.

In addition, bicarbonate levels were lower, while blood urea nitrogen and potassium were higher in the delirium group. The TyG index was also significantly higher among patients with delirium. The median Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score also differed between the two groups. Regarding categorical variables, a higher proportion of patients in the delirium group received statins and benzodiazepines during their ICU stay. The prevalence of congestive heart failure and peptic ulcer disease was also greater in this group. Additionally, delirium patients had a greater incidence of acute kidney damage (AKI) and were more likely to undergo continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and vasopressor medication. Other variables, including sex, red blood cell parameters, coagulation markers, electrolyte levels, and certain chronic conditions did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 1).

Boruta algorithm and multicollinearity control

Boruta algorithm identifies variables significantly associated with the outcome by comparing their importance scores against those of artificially generated “shadow features.” In analysis, the Boruta algorithm was run with default parameters and 500 iterations to ensure stability of the results. Ultimately, Boruta categorized all candidate variables into three groups: “Confirmed,” “Tentative,” and “Rejected.” Only the “Confirmed” variables were retained for subsequent multivariable modeling. Figure 2 presents the importance scores of each variable obtained during the Boruta process, along with comparisons against the shadow features. To enhance clinical interpretability, this study supplemented the Boruta-selected variables with additional clinically relevant predictors based on expert judgment, incorporating them into the final multivariable logistic regression model. To assess multicollinearity among covariates, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for each variable (Supplementary Table S2). The final adjusted confounding variables included: mechanical ventilation, sex, insulin use, benzodiazepine use, cerebrovascular disease, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus, vasopressor use, continuous renal replacement therapy, age at admission, platelet count, body temperature, SOFA score, GCS score, international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), and bicarbonate level.

The association between TyG and ICU delirium after gastric surgery is presented in Table 2. In the fully adjusted model, delirium was independently linked to TyG level (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.01–1.56). Tests for trend across Models 1 to 4 were all statistically significant (P for trend < 0.05). Compared to the lowest quartile, delirium was remained substantially related with the top quartile (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.12–3.02).

Figure 3 shows the ROC analysis illustrating the discriminative performance of TyG for predicting delirium. TyG had significantly better predictive performance than triglycerides (AUC = 0.556) and fasting glucose (AUC = 0.552). The ideal cutoff value, as determined by the Youden index, was 9.37. This resulted in AUC of 0.568 (95% CI: 0.53–0.60) and a specificity of 69.8%. The TyG index and delirium showed a U-shaped connection, according to RCS analysis (overall P = 0.0149, P for non-linearity = 0.042). Low TyG values may reflect postoperative malnutrition or energy deficiency, while high TyG values indicate significant insulin resistance and metabolic disturbance. Both extremes of metabolic status may contribute to an elevated risk of neuropsychiatric complications.

Robust analysis

The outcome variable and the original components used to calculate the TyG index were not considered confounders; all other variables were treated as potential confounders. Using the MatchIt package in R, this study carried out 1:1 closest neighbor matching, producing 223 matched pairings. Following propensity score matching, the two groups’ baseline characteristics did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 4).

Covariate balance before and after propensity score matching. Love plot of standardized mean differences (SMDs) for covariates in the high- vs. low-TyG groups, showing improved balance after 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching (caliper 0.2)

Table 3 summarizes the outcomes of logistic regression models both before and after matching. In the fully adjusted Model 4, within the propensity-matched cohort, compared to patients in the low TyG group (TyG < 9.373054), the frequency of delirium was significantly higher in the high TyG group (TyG > 9.373054) [OR = 1.73,95% CI: 1.21–2.47]. Likewise, in the mismatched cohort, the high TyG group continued to have a substantially greater incidence of delirium than the low TyG group [OR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.30–3.15].

Subgroup analyses

As shown in Fig. 5, in the overall population, delirium was positively correlated with every unit rise in the TyG index. In the sex-stratified analysis, both males (OR = 1.38) and females (OR = 1.27) showed similar trends, with no significant interaction detected. The association between TyG and delirium remained consistent across subgroups defined by hypertension, coronary artery disease, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease status, and age stratification, with all P values for interaction > 0.05. In summary, the association between the TyG index and the incidence of ICU-acquired delirium after gastric surgery was robust across most clinical subgroups.

Machine learning-based prediction models

Nine different machine learning models were developed on the training set to predict ICU-acquired delirium and were subsequently evaluated in the testing set. After variable selection using the Boruta algorithm, the final input features included: TyG index, AKI, PT, INR, CRRT, mechanical ventilation, GCS score, SOFA score, vasopressor use, presence of peptic ulcer disease, body temperature, platelet count, and age at admission.

The predictive effectiveness of several machine learning models that included the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index differed across the testing set, as seen in Fig. 6. With an AUC of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.705–0.823), the support vector machine (SVM) outperformed all other models in terms of discriminating. This was followed by GBM (AUC = 0.755), CatBoost (0.750), KNN (0.751), XGBoost (0.732), random forest (0.719), and neural network (0.710). LightGBM (0.666) and AdaBoost (0.642) showed comparatively lower performance. The SVM model with TyG index (AUC = 0.764) was better than the SVM model without TyG index (AUC = 0.752). Since the TyG-integrated model was nearer the diagonal reference line, calibration curves showed improved prediction accuracy. Following TyG integration, decision curve analysis (DCA) also revealed increased net clinical benefit.

Test-set AUCs across nine algorithms (SVM, GBM, CatBoost, KNN, XGBoost, Random Forest, Neural Network, LightGBM, AdaBoost), highlighting SVM with TyG (AUC = 0.764) vs. SVM without TyG (AUC = 0.752). Calibration curves show predictions closer to the 45° reference after adding TyG; decision-curve analysis indicates higher net clinical benefit across clinically relevant thresholds

The significance of distinct characteristics in the SVM model for predicting ICU-acquired delirium is seen in Fig. 7. Internal rankings identified vasopressor use, platelet count, SOFA score, mechanical ventilation, and AKI as the top five predictors, while the TyG index ranked eighth, indicating moderate predictive value. The SHAP plot further confirmed that high TyG values, vasopressor use, AKI, and elevated body temperature were positively associated with increased delirium risk.

Discussion

This research showed a strong correlation between high TyG levels and a higher risk of delirium. Additionally, RCS analysis suggested a nonlinear U-shaped relationship between TyG and delirium. Although TyG alone exhibited only fair discriminatory power (AUC = 0.568), its integration with conventional clinical risk factors and machine learning algorithms significantly improved predictive performance. Therefore, TyG may be best interpreted as an adjunctive biomarker that enhances, rather than replaces, existing risk assessment tools.

Consistent with previous studies, insulin resistance (IR) has been identified as a potential risk factor for postoperative cognitive dysfunction in gastrointestinal surgical patients [5, 32], and some studies have noted an association between TyG and delirium in the elderly [20, 33]. The TyG index may affect delirium via a variety of physiologic pathways. Metabolic disturbances and IR following gastric surgery may increase delirium risk by exacerbating postoperative inflammation, disrupting the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and impairing key neuro-metabolic pathways, such as cerebral glucose utilization and oxidative stress responses. On one hand, IR can amplify systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby compromising BBB integrity [34–36]. On the other hand, IR inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathway and upregulates glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) activity, leading to tau hyperphosphorylation and cognitive impairment [37, 38].

Furthermore, amyloid-beta (Aβ) accumulation has been implicated as a key factor in neuropsychiatric disorders. Insulin normally suppresses Aβ fibril formation and promotes Aβ oligomer clearance via endocytosis, protecting synaptic structure from toxicity [39]. However, under insulin-resistant conditions, impaired Aβ clearance may lead to its cerebral deposition and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, further increasing the risk of delirium [40, 41]. In addition, insulin has been shown to upregulate γ-aminobutyric acid receptor density on the postsynaptic membrane, thereby enhancing inhibitory signaling and stabilizing neuronal activity [42]. The U-shaped relationship observed between TyG and delirium may reflect two distinct metabolic pathways: low TyG values could indicate postoperative malnutrition or energy deficiency, which predisposes patients to neurocognitive vulnerability, while high TyG values reflect severe insulin resistance and metabolic stress, both of which can promote systemic inflammation, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, and neuroinflammation.

Study strengths and limitations

This study leverages a clinically relevant cohort of 819 ICU patients after gastric surgery, with postoperative delirium ascertained using the standardized CAM-ICU, ensuring outcome consistency. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, derived from routine laboratory tests, offers a pragmatic and low-cost marker with immediate bedside feasibility. Robust confounder control (prespecified clinically important variables and data-driven selection with multicollinearity checks), together with complementary analyses—including hierarchical logistic models, trend tests, propensity score matching, and machine-learning evaluation with SHAP—supports the internal validity and interpretability of the association. These features collectively enhance the translational potential of TyG for peri-operative risk stratification in comparable surgical ICU settings.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be noted. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which may introduce selection bias. Multicenter prospective validation is warranted. Second, TyG was measured only once, using the first available triglyceride and glucose values upon ICU admission. This cross-sectional assessment may not capture dynamic perioperative fluctuations in metabolic status that could provide stronger predictive value for delirium. Prospective studies incorporating serial perioperative measurements are needed to better delineate causal relationships. Third, several important perioperative factors—including anesthesia type, postoperative analgesia regimen, sedative exposure, and nutritional support—were not available in the database. These unmeasured confounders may influence delirium risk and introduce residual bias into findings. Additionally, as with all retrospective database studies, variations in data completeness and assessment frequency may affect result accuracy. Finally, although delirium was assessed with the standardized CAM-ICU, inconsistencies in assessment timing and frequency, together with retrospective reliance on documentation, may have led to underdetection of hypoactive, hyperactive, or fluctuating phenotypes, introducing potential misclassification bias.

Conclusion

In summary, a higher triglyceride–glucose index was associated with an increased risk of postoperative delirium after gastric surgery. Because TyG is calculated from routine triglyceride and glucose tests, it can be incorporated into preoperative risk stratification to identify patients at elevated neuropsychiatric risk. Clinically, individuals with higher TyG may merit enhanced delirium surveillance (more frequent CAM-ICU assessments), targeted metabolic optimization (peri-operative glucose–lipid management and avoidance of large glycemic excursions), tailored sedation/analgesia strategies (minimizing deliriogenic exposures when feasible), and proactive resource allocation (early multidisciplinary involvement and discharge planning). Future work should establish actionable thresholds for TyG, assess serial peri-operative dynamics to refine timing of interventions, and evaluate integration of TyG into multivariable risk tools with effects on outcomes and cost-effectiveness.
