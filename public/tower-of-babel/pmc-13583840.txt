Thyroid dysfunction and its associated factors: a cross-sectional study based on Rafsanjan cohort study in the southeast of Iran
Authors: Lida Soltani, Shahin Heidari, Parvin Khalili, Fariba Mazhari, Fatemeh Ayoobi, Mahmood Kahnooji, Zahra Jamali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors with thyroid dysfunction within a large cohort participating in the Rafsanjan Cohort Study (RCS), part of the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN).Study designA cross-sectional study based on RCS.SettingThe study was carried out in Rafsanjan, a region in the southeast of Iran.ParticipantsParticipants were chosen using a cluster sampling technique. A total of 9991 participants between the ages of 35 and 70 years from both genders participated in the initial phase of the RCS.OutcomesThyroid dysfunction was determined based on self-reported diagnosis.MethodsThe association of thyroid dysfunction with gender, age, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, substance use, education and Wealth Score Index (WSI) was assessed using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 9867 participants, thyroid dysfunction prevalence was 8.98%. Women had 6.56 times higher odds compared with men (OR: 6.56, 95%CI 5.03 to 8.55). BMI ≥30 had 22% higher odds compared with BMI <25 (OR: 1.22, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.50). Middle high WSI and high WSI were positively associated with thyroid dysfunction (OR: 1.35, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.65 and OR: 1.61, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.23, respectively). Former smoking was associated with increased odds of thyroid dysfunction (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.14 to 2.38). Age groups 46–55 years and ≥56 years exhibited higher odds of hypothyroidism compared with 35–45 years (OR: 1.47, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.87 and OR: 1.61, 95% CI 1.24 to 2.09, respectively).ConclusionThis study shows the elevated burden of thyroid dysfunction among women and older adults and highlights modifiable associated factors such as BMI and smoking history. These findings emphasise the need for targeted screening and lifestyle interventions in high-risk groups. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate causal pathways.Keywords: Thyroid disease, Prevalence, Risk Factors

Introduction

Hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism are common diseases affecting the entire world population.1 It is reported that subclinical hypothyroidism is likely associated with an increased risk of chronic heart disease mortality and total mortality.2 Prevalence rates of thyroid disorders varied by demographic, genetic and environmental factors.

In Saudi Arabia, 49.77% were found to have a thyroid disorder, with asymptomatic hypothyroidism being the most prevalent (39.25%).3 Another study in Egypt reported a 29.3% prevalence of thyroid dysfunction, with symptomatic hypothyroidism being the most frequent type (44.4%).4 Among diabetic patients in Nepal, a prevalence of 36.02%, with asymptomatic hypothyroidism comprising 26.5% of cases, was reported.5 In Iran, the reported prevalence varies across regions and study designs. Barzegari et al found a 3.64% prevalence among 10 000 participants in western Iran, with hypothyroidism being the most common (3.21%).6 In a cohort study conducted in Isfahan from 2006 to 2021 with 2523 participants, 10.7% had thyroid dysfunction, including overt (2.8%) and subclinical (5.8%) hypothyroidism, and overt (0.8%) and subclinical (1.0%) hyperthyroidism. Higher thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were observed among women and those with a positive family history of thyroid disorders.7 In Tehran’s urban population, clinical thyroid dysfunction was present in 10.3% of participants, with a higher prevalence among females.8

Previous studies have highlighted various contributing factors to thyroid dysfunction, including genetics, autoimmunity, environmental exposures, reproductive history, nutritional status, personal habits and geographic region.3–7 9 10 Across multiple studies, women consistently demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of thyroid disorders than men.3 Other associated risk factors included age, diabetes (particularly type 1), hypertension, smoking and a family history of thyroid disease.4 5 White people showed higher rates of asymptomatic and treated hypothyroidism, while black people exhibited a higher prevalence of asymptomatic hyperthyroidism in the USA. Older age and higher body mass index (BMI) were also associated with increased rates of hypothyroidism, while smoking was linked to lower TSH levels.9 In a study by Kwon et al in Korea, thyroid disorders were more prevalent in women, with thyroid hormone therapy most common among men aged 70–79 years and women aged 60–69 years.11 Garmendia’s meta-analysis on thyroid dysfunction in Europe estimated the mean prevalence of undiagnosed thyroid disorders at 6.71%, and combined diagnosed and undiagnosed cases at 3.82%.12

Understanding the epidemiology of thyroid dysfunction is crucial, as it is associated with increased risks of hypercholesterolaemia, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, arrhythmias and neuropsychiatric conditions.13 14 However, particularly in the context of subclinical thyroid dysfunction, the evidence supporting a clear clinical benefit from treatment or population-based screening remains limited.15 16 Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and in the broader context of established cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors.13 15

Region-specific prevalence data are important for understanding the epidemiology of thyroid dysfunction, as geographical variation in multiple determinants may influence disease patterns. Evidence from studies conducted in Iran suggests that thyroid dysfunction reflects a complex interplay of environmental, nutritional and sociodemographic factors, which vary across provinces.17 The national analyses, based on the Global Burden of Disease data, indicate that iodine deficiency–related burden remains relatively higher in provinces such as Kerman, including Rafsanjan, compared with provinces such as Tehran and Alborz, where iodine sufficiency indicators are generally more favourable.17 These variations may indirectly contribute to regional differences in thyroid dysfunction prevalence. On the other hand, findings from other population-based studies in Iran illustrate notable regional variation in thyroid disorder prevalence, even within iodine-sufficient populations,8 18 which has been attributed to differences in environmental exposures, genetic background, lifestyle factors, age distribution and methodological characteristics.

Beyond nutritional factors, genetic susceptibility, autoimmune mechanisms, environmental exposures and sociodemographic conditions are also likely to influence thyroid disease risk.19 In addition, spatial analyses of thyroid cancer incidence in Iran have demonstrated regional clustering, suggesting broader geographic influences on thyroid-related disorders.20 However, direct comparative data between Kerman province and other regions remain limited, underscoring the need for region-specific epidemiological studies to better characterise local patterns of thyroid dysfunction. The present study investigates the prevalence of thyroid disorders and their associated factors using data from the adult cohort of the Rafsanjan Cohort Study (RCS).

Materials and methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, the data of the RCS were used.21 RCS is a population-based prospective cohort study that was launched in 2015 in Rafsanjan, an area in the southeast of Iran. RCS is a branch of the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN).22 The criteria for inclusion in this study included age ranges 35–70 years, living only in the designated area, Iranian nationality and being a resident of Rafsanjan city for at least 9 months a year.

Based on the sample size estimated by the PERSIAN cohort,22 participants were recruited through a cluster sampling method proportional to the number of households until the target sample size of 10 000 individuals was achieved. The PERSIAN Cohort Central Scientific Committee estimated this sample size due to adequate statistical power. A sample size of 10 000 individuals would detect a relative risk of 2 for an exposure (α level of 0.05, β of 90%, with a 10% prevalence) with 150 incident cases of the outcome during the follow-up phase. A total of 150 incidents for 15 years of follow-up is equivalent to 10 incidents per year, which is very realistic to achieve in RCS.21 A total of 9991 participants between the ages of 35 and 70 years from both genders participated in the baseline phase of the RCS. The baseline phase of RCS ended in 2017. All study participants signed a written consent form.

Associated factors

Demographic characteristics (age, gender and education), socio-economic status, anthropometric measurements (BMI), physical activity, medical history, personal habits (cigarette smoking, alcohol, tobacco and opium consumption) and diet (salt g/day) were assessed using validated questionnaires for all participants.22

Education was recorded as the number of years of education, which was divided into three categories: ≤5, 6–12 and ≥13 years. Socio-economic status (Wealth Score Index (WSI)) was estimated using multiple correspondence analysis of variables, including access to a freezer, washing machine, dishwasher, computer, internet, motorcycle, car and so on. The WSI was classified into four categories, including low, low-middle, middle-high and high groups, based on the 25th, 50th and 90th percentiles, respectively.

Physical activity was calculated using the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) calculated for 24 hours. Then MET was classified into three categories including low, moderate and heavy groups, based on the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively.

Smoking, opium and alcohol consumption were recorded as self-reports. All those who drank approximately 200 mL of beer or 45 mL of spirits at least once a week for at least 6 months were drinking alcohol. Smoking was divided into three categories: non-smoker, current smoker and former smoker. Hookah consumption was divided into two groups: consumers and non-consumers. Reported opium usage at least once a week for 6 months was considered an opium user.

The amount of salt consumed was calculated using the food frequency questionnaire.

The study protocol was designed based on Persian, and the questionnaires were validated by PERSIAN.22 Questionnaires are provided in the online supplemental section.

The ethics committee of Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol (ethical codes: ID: IR.RUMS.REC.1401.203).

Dependent variable

Thyroid dysfunction was determined based on self-reported diagnosis of thyroid disease. The precise question was: Diagnosed with thyroid disease (thyroid disease, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, goitre and thyroid nodules): No/Yes.

Among 9991 participants of the RCS, after excluding people with incomplete information in their medical history and pregnant women, a total of 9867 people were included. Among people who stated that they have a thyroid problem (886), the information on the medication of 499 subjects was available. Subjects who reported consumption of hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism drugs were considered as hypothyroidism (n=481)/hyperthyroidism (n=18).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Statistical analyses

Frequency (%) for categorical variables and mean, along with the SD for the quantitative variables, were calculated. The baseline characteristics of the study groups were evaluated using the χ² test for categorical variables and the t-test for continuous variables. Initially, a set of potential associated factors for thyroid dysfunction was identified based on prior epidemiological evidence and hypothesised relationships (theory-based approach). These theoretically relevant variables (included: age and education)6 9 formed the core of our initial models. As a supplementary step, to ensure a comprehensive analysis and capture other potentially relevant factors, variables with univariable p<0.25 (included: gender, BMI, WSI, physical activity, smoking, alcohol drinking and opium usage), in addition to those deemed to have clinical/theoretical relevance, were also considered as candidates for inclusion in the multivariable model, following purposeful selection strategies.23

Both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to calculate ORs and their corresponding 95% CIs. In the univariable analysis, each selected associated factor was entered individually to assess its preliminary association with thyroid dysfunction. Subsequently, in the multivariable model, all selected factors (both theory-based and those identified by univariable significance) were entered together to examine the independent association of each variable with thyroid dysfunction while controlling for the effects of other variables in the model.

Also, after excluding participants who were diagnosed with hyperthyroidism based on the drugs they took and those who reported having thyroid dysfunction but for whom medication information was unavailable, univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used in hypothyroidism participants.

For regression models, the method of handling missing data, which is complete case analysis (list-wise deletion), was used. This method uses only the cases in a dataset for which there is no missing data on any of the variables. The inconsistency between the frequency of the total number and some of the variables is due to the missing values.

Results

Figure 1 shows the flow chart of the study design of thyroid dysfunction among participants of the RCS. Among the 9867 eligible participants, the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction was 8.98% (n=886). Thyroid dysfunction was significantly greater in women compared with men (84.54% vs 15.46%, p<0.001). Compared with the euthyroid group, participants with thyroid dysfunction were more likely to have a higher mean of BMI (29.34 vs 27.65, p<0.05) and WSI (0.072 vs −0.010, p=0.021) and a lower level of physical activity (37.96 vs 38.88, p<0.001). There were no significant differences regarding age, education and dietary intake of salt among subjects with thyroid dysfunction and the control group (p>0.05; table 1).

Regarding hypothyroidism, in the multivariable regression model, the same associations were observed in terms of gender, education, BMI and WSI and odds of hypothyroidism. A significant association was seen between age and the odds of hypothyroidism. The odds of hypothyroidism in the 46–55 years and ≥56 years age groups were about 1.47 and 1.67 times higher than the 35–45 year age group (OR: 1.47, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.87 and OR: 1.61, 95% CI 1.24 to 2.09, respectively). There was no significant association between physical activity level and odds of thyroid dysfunction or hypothyroidism.

In the univariable regression model, the odds of thyroid dysfunction among subjects with current cigarette smoking and alcohol, tobacco and opium consumption were lower than non-users (OR: 0.27, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.36; OR: 0.31, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.45; OR: 0.48, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.65; OR: 0.38, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.47, respectively) but these associations were lost in the multivariable regression model. Also, the same associations were observed regarding current cigarette smoking and alcohol, tobacco and opium consumption and odds of hypothyroidism.

In the univariable regression model, the odds of thyroid dysfunction among former smokers decreased compared with the non-smoker group (OR: 0.48, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.65), but these odds increased in the multivariable regression model (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.14 to 2.38). There was no significant association between former smokers and the odds of hypothyroidism.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between thyroid dysfunction and its associated factors in the southeastern region of Iran. This study showed that the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction was 8.98%. Female gender, older age, former smokers, subjects with higher BMI, higher WSI and higher education were associated with higher odds of thyroid dysfunction.

Our results showed that women exhibit significantly higher odds of thyroid dysfunction and hypothyroidism (6.56 and 7 times, respectively) compared with men. This profound gender disparity is a consistent and well-documented phenomenon in endocrinology. Prior Iranian studies corroborate this finding; Khosravi et al in Yazd (in the centre of Iran) reported women having a five-fold higher chance of hypothyroidism,18 and Sharafi et al in Fasa (south west of Iran) found women to have 5.94 times higher odds of thyroid dysfunction.24 Globally, studies by Ghaemmaghami et al in Tehran,8 Diab et al in the USA,9 Zaman et al in Iraq,10 Alqahtani et al in Saudi Arabia3 and Khatiwada et al in Nepal5 consistently reported a female preponderance in thyroid disorders. This increased susceptibility in women is primarily attributed to a complex interplay of hormonal, genetic and immunological factors. Oestrogen’s known role in modulating immune responses and thyroid function, coupled with sex-linked genetic predispositions and a generally more robust female immune response, likely contribute to the elevated incidence of autoimmune thyroid diseases in females.25 The profound health and socioeconomic challenges posed by this chronic condition for women, particularly within the Iranian cultural context, underscore the necessity of targeted public health interventions and gender-sensitive clinical management strategies.

The present study found a significant association between higher mean BMI and increased odds of thyroid dysfunction and hypothyroidism. These observations are consistent with emerging evidence linking obesity and central adiposity to thyroid dysfunction.26 The proposed mechanisms involve chronic low-grade inflammation, altered leptin signalling and insulin resistance, all of which can disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis.27 This reinforces the use of BMI as an important indicator in routine thyroid health assessments.

Our findings showed that a higher WSI and education level were associated with increased odds of thyroid dysfunction and hypothyroidism, which is noteworthy and requires further exploration. Traditionally, higher socioeconomic status and education are linked to better health outcomes due to improved access to healthcare, greater health literacy and healthier lifestyles. However, this finding might reflect a greater health awareness and increased access to diagnostic testing among more educated individuals, leading to a higher detection rate of subclinical or asymptomatic thyroid dysfunction.28 Alternatively, it could point to specific lifestyle factors or environmental exposures prevalent in higher educational strata within the study population that are not yet fully understood.

Our study showed a significant association between age and the odds of hypothyroidism, with higher odds observed in the 46–55 years and ≥56 years age groups compared with the 35–45 years age group. This aligns with well-established epidemiological trends.29 Thyroid function naturally changes with advancing age, and the prevalence of hypothyroidism, particularly subclinical hypothyroidism, increases significantly in older populations.29 30 Mirahmad et al also reported an increasing trend with age in the prevalence of total hypothyroidism in older adults. This age-related increase is often attributed to accumulated environmental exposures, a decline in immune function and the natural ageing process of the thyroid gland itself.31

Notably, while current cigarette smoking and alcohol, tobacco and opium consumption were not significantly associated with the odds of hypothyroidism in the multivariable model, the odds of thyroid dysfunction among former smokers increased compared with non-smokers. This finding concerning former smokers, while not observed for hypothyroidism specifically, points to the potential long-term effects of smoking cessation on overall thyroid health, possibly reflecting a rebound effect or underlying inflammatory processes. This area warrants further dedicated research to understand the specific implications of smoking cessation on different aspects of thyroid function.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, data on important risk factors for thyroid disorders, including autoimmune diseases, family history of thyroid disease, iodine nutritional status, medication history and prior radiation exposure to the neck, were not available. The absence of these variables may have resulted in residual confounding, potentially biasing the observed associations between thyroid dysfunction and the factors under study. In particular, iodine status and autoimmune background are major determinants of thyroid disease, and failure to account for them may have led to either overestimation or underestimation of the reported associations. Second, our definition of hypothyroidism included individuals using levothyroxine, which may have introduced misclassification and potentially overestimated the prevalence of hypothyroidism, as levothyroxine may also be prescribed for conditions other than overt hypothyroidism.

Third, the reliance on self-reported diagnosis and medication use represents another important limitation. This may have introduced misclassification bias, potentially attenuating or distorting the observed associations. Fourth, although random sampling was used, the study population may not have fully represented the general population. Differences in demographic characteristics, health-seeking behaviour and participation patterns may have limited the external validity and generalisability of the findings.

Fifth, due to resource constraints, anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibody levels were not measured, and thyroid ultrasonography was not performed. Because autoimmune thyroid disease is a major cause of hypothyroidism, the absence of anti-TPO data limits the aetiological interpretation of the findings. Likewise, the lack of ultrasound data precluded the assessment of structural thyroid abnormalities, such as nodules or goitre, that may be associated with thyroid dysfunction. Sixth, serum levels of thyroid function indicators, particularly TSH and free thyroxine, were not measured, and free triiodothyronine data were also unavailable. Consequently, thyroid dysfunction could not be classified as overt or subclinical, which may have resulted in outcome misclassification and reduced the precision and validity of the estimated associations.

Finally, given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal direction cannot be inferred, and the possibility of reverse causation must be considered. For certain associations, such as those involving age or comorbid conditions, reverse causation may occur because of differences in survival, use of healthcare or behaviour changes caused by disease. In contrast, associations with fixed characteristics such as sex are unlikely to be due to reverse causation and are more likely to reflect underlying biological or hormonal differences. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed thyroid-related risk factors, biochemical thyroid function measurements, anti-TPO antibody assessment and thyroid ultrasonography are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of thyroid dysfunction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction in this study was substantial, with a pronounced female predominance and increased odds among older age groups. The positive associations with higher BMI, higher education and socioeconomic status underscore the complex interplay between metabolic, environmental and social determinants of thyroid health. The higher odds of thyroid dysfunction among former smokers highlight the nuanced effects of lifestyle behaviours on thyroid disease pathogenesis. These findings support the implementation of tailored screening strategies and public health initiatives focusing on modifiable risk factors to reduce the burden of thyroid dysfunction in vulnerable populations. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate causal pathways and inform effective prevention programmes.
