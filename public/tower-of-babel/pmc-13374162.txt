HDAC9 drives intervertebral disc degeneration by potentiating STAT1-mediated senescence and inflammation
Authors: Xingdong Cheng, Jingjing Wang, Yuqing Chen, Rong Xue, Zengxin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntervertebral disc degeneration is a leading cause of chronic low back pain, characterized by cellular senescence, extracellular matrix disintegration, and chronic inflammation. However, the key epigenetic regulators that orchestrate senescence and the resultant inflammatory cascade in disc degeneration remain poorly understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the pathological role and molecular mechanism of HDAC9 in IDD.MethodsWe analyzed HDAC9 expression in aged degenerative discs. Using siRNA-mediated knockdown in primary cells, we assessed senescence, ECM, and inflammation. The mechanism was probed via co-immunoprecipitation, transcriptomics, and ELISA.ResultsHDAC9 expression was significantly upregulated in aged degenerative discs, correlating with enhanced cellular senescence and tissue destruction. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a profound pro-inflammatory and senescent signature in aged discs, with marked activation of the STAT1 signaling pathway. Knockdown of HDAC9 in aged cells attenuated senescence, reduced the expression of senescence markers and matrix catabolic enzyme, rescued proliferation deficits, and mitigated oxidative stress. Mechanistically, HDAC9 physically interacted with STAT1, and its knockdown decreased STAT1 phosphorylation. Both HDAC9 and STAT1 knockdown significantly suppressed the secretion of IL-6, TNF-α. Furthermore, classical STAT1 target genes were upregulated in aged discs.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that HDAC9 is a critical epigenetic driver of age-related IDD. It promotes cellular senescence and a pro-inflammatory microenvironment by interacting with and activating the STAT1 signaling pathway. Targeting the HDAC9-STAT1 axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for mitigating disc degeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06949-3.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a prevalent and debilitating musculoskeletal disorder characterized by the progressive structural breakdown and functional impairment of the intervertebral discs (IVDs) that cushion the vertebrae [1, 2]. Its pathological hallmarks include nucleus pulposus (NP) dehydration, annulus fibrosus (AF) rupture, and calcification of the cartilaginous endplates (CEPs) [1, 2]. As a leading cause of chronic low back pain and radicular symptoms, IDD significantly diminishes patients’ quality of life and imposes a substantial health and socioeconomic burden worldwide [3]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of IDD approaches 80% in the global adult population, a figure that is steadily rising in our aging society [4]. Current therapeutic strategies primarily focus on symptomatic relief, lacking effective interventions that target the underlying pathological mechanisms. Therefore, elucidating the molecular drivers of IDD and developing novel disease-modifying therapies are of paramount clinical importance.

The pathological progression of IDD is driven by a vicious cycle centered on cellular senescence [5]. ECM metabolic imbalance—characterized by aggrecan loss, decreased hydration, and a shift from type II to type I collagen—creates a degenerative microenvironment [6–8]. This environment promotes the accumulation of senescent cells, which are characterized by irreversible cell-cycle arrest and expression of markers such as p16/p21 and SA-β-gal [5, 9]. Crucially, these cells amplify degeneration through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes, which further disrupt ECM homeostasis, promote chronic inflammation via NF-κB signaling, and induce senescence in neighboring cells, thereby creating a self-sustaining loop that accelerates disc breakdown [9–11]. Therefore, targeting the drivers of cellular senescence presents a promising therapeutic strategy for IDD. Recent evidence points to epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant histone modification, as a central upstream controller of aging and senescence [12, 13].

Epigenetic dysregulation, particularly histone modification, is a critical hallmark and driver of aging, directly influencing the transcriptional activity of senescence-associated genes [14, 15]. Among these modifications, histone acetylation, dynamically regulated by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases, serves as a key regulatory mechanism and a promising therapeutic target [16]. Aberrant HDAC expression or activity is implicated in the pathogenesis of various age-related degenerative diseases. In osteoarthritis, HDACs contribute to disease progression by dually regulating inflammation and ECM metabolism: (1) promoting inflammation—HDAC4 activates MAPK signaling to upregulate ADAMTS4/5, COX2, and IL-1β [17–19], while HDAC7 induces MMP3/13 expression [20, 21]; (2) disrupting ECM homeostasis—HDACs inhibit FOXO transcription factors, downregulating PRG4, autophagy-related genes, and the antioxidant protein Sestrin3, leading to oxidative damage and impaired matrix synthesis [22]. Notably, broad-spectrum and isoform-selective HDAC inhibitors have shown efficacy in mitigating cartilage degradation in animal models by suppressing IL-1β-induced MMP and ADAMTS5 expression [23, 24]. These findings suggest that targeting HDAC-mediated epigenetic reprogramming could be a viable strategy for treating IDD.

HDAC9, a class IIa HDAC with nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling capability, fine-tunes tissue-specific gene expression profiles by deacetylating histone and non-histone substrates, thereby regulating cell differentiation, inflammation, and oxidative stress [25, 26]. HDAC9 is upregulated in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells during age-related bone loss, and its siRNA-mediated knockdown attenuates the expression of senescence markers like p53 and p-p53 [27]. HDAC9 is also responsive to inflammatory stimuli; its expression increases upon LPS challenge, and HDAC9 knockout significantly suppresses the secretion of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [28]. This anti-inflammatory effect is confirmed in HDAC9-knockout mice, which exhibit downregulated expression of iNOS, COX-2, and IL-18 [29]. However, the specific regulatory mechanisms of HDAC9 in IDD and its crosstalk with canonical aging pathways remain entirely unknown.

Cumulative evidence underscores cellular senescence and the resultant chronic inflammation as central to IDD pathogenesis. Among epigenetic modifiers, histone deacetylases, particularly HDAC9, have been implicated in aging and inflammatory diseases in other tissues. However, its specific role, regulatory targets, and whether it functions as a molecular nexus integrating senescence and inflammation in the intervertebral disc remain entirely unexplored. Elucidating this mechanism is crucial for developing targeted therapies against age-related IDD.

Methods

Ethics statement

The tissue samples and corresponding experimental protocols used in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Southeast University (Ethical Review Number: 2025ZDKYSB039). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. Written informed consent for publication of relevant clinical data was also obtained from all participants. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

For the Old group, inclusion criteria were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) based on clinical manifestations and imaging findings; (2) availability of complete preoperative imaging data, including lumbar MRI (with CT when necessary), enabling precise identification of the affected segment and assessment of degeneration severity (Pfirrmann grade ≥ III); (3) primary symptoms of chronic low back pain and/or lumbosacral discomfort persisting for ≥ 3 months; and (4) no prior history of surgery or interventional treatment for disc degeneration. Exclusion criteria included: (1) spinal infection, tumor, vertebral fracture, or history of severe trauma; (2) inflammatory spinal diseases (e.g., ankylosing spondylitis) or systemic autoimmune disorders; (3) severe spinal deformities (e.g., significant scoliosis), severe spinal canal stenosis, or high-grade spondylolisthesis (≥ grade II); and (4) prior lumbar surgery or disc-related interventional procedures within the past 3 months.

For the Young group, tissues were obtained from relatively intact regions adjacent to the nucleus pulposus within the same intervertebral disc of IDD patients during surgery, serving as internal controls. Inclusion criteria were: (1) sampling from the same disc level as the degenerated region; (2) preoperative MRI showing relatively homogeneous signal intensity in the control region, without obvious fissures, marked signal loss, or structural disruption; and (3) absence of evident degenerative features on postoperative histological examination (e.g., preserved cell density and matrix integrity), consistent with relatively normal nucleus pulposus tissue. Exclusion criteria included: (1) histological evidence of degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltration in the control region; and (2) insufficient tissue quantity or compromised sample quality due to contamination or damage during collection, transport, or storage.

Healthy male C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. All animals were obtained from a certified laboratory animal center. At enrollment, mice in the Young group were 2 months old (18–22 g), whereas those in the Old group were 24 months old (25–30 g). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 22–25 °C and 50–60% relative humidity, with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to standard chow and water and were acclimated for at least 7 days prior to experimentation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals, and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of our institution (Ethics Approval Number: 20250324003).

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

Following tissue harvesting, intervertebral disc samples were fixed, dehydrated, and cleared, then embedded in preheated paraffin (60 °C). Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 90 °C. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% H2O2. Subsequently, nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min, followed by an overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. The next day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. DAB substrate was then added to visualize positive signals. After completion of the staining, the intensity of the chromogenic signals was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Safranin O/Fast green staining

After fixation in 4% PFA, tissue samples were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Staining was performed using 0.1% Fast Green (with 0.2% acetic acid, 5 min), followed by brief differentiation in 0.1% acetic acid and rinsing. Then, sections were stained with 0.1% Safranin O (with 1% acetic acid, 5 min), differentiated again, and rinsed. After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted with neutral balsam. Safranin O-positive areas were quantified using ImageJ to assess proteoglycan content.

SA-β-Gal staining

After washing with PBS three times, cells were fixed with 1 mL β-galactosidase staining fixative for 15 min at room temperature. The fixative was then removed, and cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, 1 mL of staining working solution was added to each well. The 6-well plate was wrapped with plastic wrap to prevent evaporation and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The next day, images were captured using an inverted microscope, and the number of positive cells as well as the total cell count were analyzed statistically with the ImageJ plugin Cell Counter.

RNA sequencing

After the samples are collected, total RNA is extracted, and purity and quantitative testing are performed to ensure that it meets quality standards. The follow-up was completed by Shanghai Bioprofile Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium to a final concentration of 10 µM. Cells digested with trypsin were collected, centrifuged, and resuspended in the diluted DCFH-DA solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark with gentle mixing every 3 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove extracellular probe. A positive control group was set up by stimulating cells with Rosup diluted 1:1000. Fluorescence intensity was detected using flow cytometry, and data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were measured, and integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. qPCR amplification was performed using SYBR Green master mix with cDNA as the template. The thermal cycling protocol included: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s; followed by melt curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.5 °C per second. siRNA transfection

Cells were seeded at an appropriate density in 6-well plates with antibiotic-free medium containing 10% FBS and cultured for 24 h before transfection. For transfection, siRNA and CRTrans reagent were separately diluted in antibiotic-free medium. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, the diluted solutions were combined and incubated for another 20 min to allow complex formation. The transfection complexes were then added to each well. The plates were gently swirled and incubated for 48 h in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. Gene silencing efficiency was evaluated using qPCR. siRNAsequences are as follows:

siHDAC9 sense: CGAAAGACACUCCAACUAAUG,

siHDAC9 antisense: UUAGUUGGAGUGUCUUUCGUU;

siNC sense: UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT,

siNC antisense: ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat# 04693159001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P5726) for 30 min on ice, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad, Cat# 4561034) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk (BD Difco, Cat# 232100) in TBST buffer (Solarbio, Cat# T1080) for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (p-STAT1, CST, Cat# 9167 ; STAT1, Proteintech, Cat# 10144-2-AP; HDAC9, Proteintech, Cat# 67364-1-lg; P53, Proteintech, Cat# 10442-1-AP; P21, Proteintech, Cat# 10355-1-AP; SMA, Proteintech, Cat# 14395-1-AP; ACAN, Proteintech, Cat# 13880-1-AP; MMP3, Proteintech, Cat# 17873-1-AP; COL2, abcam, Cat# ab34712), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 7074 for anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with TBST, signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 32106).

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

After transfection, NPCs were digested with trypsin to prepare a single-cell suspension. The cell concentration was adjusted based on counts obtained using a hemocytometer, and the cells were seeded into a 96-well plate. A blank control well containing an equal volume of complete medium without cells was included. After cell attachment, the optical density at 450 nm was measured at each time point using a microplate reader (settings: fast mode, 5 s of shaking before reading). Throughout the experiment, cells were maintained in a standard cell culture incubator.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

NPC lysates were incubated with antibody-bound beads targeting HDAC9 or STAT1 overnight at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitated complexes were then collected and subjected to Western blot analysis to detect the interacting proteins.

ELISA (Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)

Add 50 µL of standards or samples to the appropriate wells and incubate at room temperature for 10 min. Then, add 50 µL of anti-TNF-α (Solarbio, SEKH-0047)/IL-6 (Solarbio, SEKH-0013) antibody to each well and incubate for 1 h at room temperature. After washing the plate five times, add 100 µL of the prepared HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB3700866) solution and incubate for another hour at room temperature. Wash the plate five times again. Subsequently, add 100 µL of TMB (Sigma-Aldrich, T0440) one-step substrate to each well and develop at room temperature for 2–10 min. Finally, stop the reaction by adding 100 µL of stop solution and measure the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Isolation of human primary nucleus pulposus cells

Human NP tissues were obtained from discectomy surgeries with informed consent. Tissues were washed in PBS containing 2× antibiotics. Under a dissecting microscope, the gelatinous NP region was meticulously separated from the annulus fibrosus. The isolated NP tissue was minced and digested with 0.2% type II collagenase at 37 °C for 3–6 h. The cell suspension was filtered through 70-µm strainers and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were maintained under hypoxic conditions (2–5% O2) and only early passages (P1–P3) were used for experiments.

Plasmid transfection

Human STAT1 cDNAs were generated in the lab and subcloned into pcDNA3.1. Lipo8000 reagents (Beyotime) were utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Recombinant AAV6 production

Recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 6 (AAV6) vectors expressing shRNA targeting mouse HDAC9 or a non-targeting scramble sequence (shCtrl) were designed and packaged by Corues Biotechnology Co.Ltd., Nanjing, China. Briefly, the shRNA sequence (5‘-gatcGGTTCTCACTCACAGTATATGtcaagagCATATACTGTGAGTGAGAACCttttt-3’) was cloned into an AAV-U6 vector backbone under the control of the human U6 promoter. The recombinant viruses were produced in HEK293T cells via triple-plasmid transfection and purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation. Viral titers were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and diluted to a working concentration of 1 × 1012–5 × 1012 vg/mL in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Mouse AAV6 injection

For in vivo gene delivery, recombinant AAV6 vectors were prepared at a titer of 1 × 1012 vg/mL in sterile PBS. Under anesthesia, a 30G needle was inserted into the target intervertebral disc (IVD) of the mouse tail, and 2 µL of the viral suspension was slowly injected. The needle was held in place for 10–15 s to minimize reflux. Animals were allowed to recover, and transgene expression was assessed 2–4 weeks post-injection.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical analysis, data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-tests were performed when data met the assumptions of normality and equal variance; otherwise, nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test) were applied. For comparisons among three or more groups with single-factor designs, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when assumptions were satisfied, followed by appropriate post hoc multiple comparison tests (Tukey’s test for equal variances or Dunnett’s T3 test for unequal variances). If the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HDAC9 is upregulated in age-associated intervertebral disc degeneration

To investigate the role of cellular senescence in IDD pathogenesis, we first examined senescence markers in human disc tissues. Cells isolated from surgical samples of IDD patients showed a significant increase in SA-β-Gal activity in the old group compared to the young group (Fig. 1A), confirming enhanced cellular senescence with advancing age in human IDD.

We then established a natural aging mouse model using C57BL/6 mice to recapitulate age-related IDD in vivo. Lateral X-ray imaging of mouse tails revealed a notable decrease in intervertebral disc height in old mice relative to young mice (Fig. 1B), indicating progressive structural collapse with aging. Histological analysis of the caudal discs further detailed the degenerative changes. Safranin O/Fast Green staining demonstrated severe age-dependent disc degeneration in old mice, characterized by a marked reduction in disc height, thinning of the cartilaginous endplates, disorganization of the annulus fibrosus, and shrinkage of the NP (Fig. 1C). IHC for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a marker associated with fibrotic remodeling, showed markedly increased expression in the discs of old mice (Fig. 1C). Concurrently, IHC staining revealed a significant upregulation of HDAC9 protein in the degenerative discs of aged mice compared to young controls (Fig. 1C). These findings collectively indicate that HDAC9 expression is elevated during the natural aging process, which is closely associated with cellular senescence, structural deterioration, and fibrotic transformation in the intervertebral disc.

Transcriptomic analysis identifies HDAC9 as a key regulator in aged discs and reveals a pro-inflammatory, senescence-associated signature

Unsupervised clustering of the transcriptomes clearly segregated the samples by age (Fig. 2A), indicating profound and consistent changes in global gene expression during disc aging. Comparative analysis identified a total of 1,006 DEGs between young and old discs, with 516 genes upregulated and 490 genes downregulated (Fig. 2B). Notably, HDAC9 was among the most significantly upregulated genes in the aged group, confirming our initial histological observation and positioning it as a key age-regulated factor. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of all DEGs revealed a broad activation of pathways related to inflammatory responses and immune system processes (Fig. 2C). A symmetrical enrichment plot further delineated the distinct functional shifts: pathways associated with cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NF-kappa B signaling, TNF signaling, and IL-17 signaling were significantly enriched among upregulated genes (Fig. 2D). Conversely, pathways crucial for cellular proliferation and genome maintenance, including DNA replication and cell cycle, were prominently enriched among downregulated genes (Fig. 2D). GSEA confirmed a significant positive enrichment (Enrichment Score > 0) for the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway in old discs. In parallel, a significant negative enrichment (ES < 0) was observed for the DNA replication and cell cycle pathways (Fig. 2E). This global transcriptomic profile delineates a shift in the aged disc microenvironment towards a pro-inflammatory, senescent phenotype concurrent with impaired cellular replicative potential.

HDAC9 knockdown alleviates cellular senescence, extracellular matrix catabolism, and oxidative stress

To investigate the functional role of HDAC9 in age-related disc degeneration, we performed loss-of-function experiments in primary cells. Transfection with HDAC9-specific siRNA efficiently knocked down HDAC9 expression at the protein level compared to the negative control siRNA group (Fig. 3A). Strikingly, western blot analysis revealed that HDAC9 silencing markedly downregulated the protein levels of key senescence markers p53 and p21 and the fibrosis-associated protein α-SMA. Furthermore, the expression of MMP3, a crucial catabolic enzyme in IDD, was also suppressed upon HDAC9 knockdown (Fig. 3A). Concurrently, we observed a significant reduction in the phosphorylation level of STAT1 in the siHDAC9 group, suggesting that HDAC9 may exert its effects, at least in part, through modulating the STAT1 signaling pathway (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this anti-senescence effect, knockdown of HDAC9 significantly reduced the number of SA-β-Gal-positive senescent cells in cultures derived from old mice (Fig. 3B). Functional assays further supported the protective role of HDAC9 inhibition. CCK-8 assays showed that while cells from old group exhibited a significant decline in proliferative capacity at 48 and 72 h compared to young cells, this deficit was partially but significantly rescued by HDAC9 knockdown (Fig. 3C). In line with the senescent phenotype, intracellular ROS levels were significantly elevated in old cells compared to young cells. Importantly, HDAC9 silencing effectively attenuated this age-associated oxidative stress (Fig. 3D, E). To corroborate the aging model at the transcriptional level, qPCR analysis confirmed that cells from old group exhibited a canonical senescence-associated gene signature, characterized by upregulated expression of p16, p21, and p53, and downregulated expression of the proliferation marker PCNA (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these data demonstrate that HDAC9 is a critical driver of cellular senescence, matrix degradation, and oxidative damage in aged cells, and its inhibition can mitigate multiple degenerative phenotypes.

We subsequently validated the above findings in an in vivo model. Compared with the Young group, the Old group exhibited significantly increased expression of ACAN and Col II, accompanied by upregulation of MMP3, P21, P-STAT1, and α-SMA (Fig. 4A). These results suggest that aging is associated with an aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling state, characterized by the simultaneous enhancement of both anabolic and catabolic processes, reflecting a maladaptive compensatory increase in ECM synthesis alongside impaired structural integrity during tissue degeneration. Following HDAC9 knockdown, these alterations were markedly reversed, as evidenced by the restoration of ACAN and Col II expression and the downregulation of HDAC9, MMP3, P21, P-STAT1, and α-SMA (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that HDAC9 silencing suppresses ECM degradation, alleviates cellular senescence and fibrotic phenotypes, and may be associated with inhibition of STAT1 signaling activation. The qRT-PCR results were consistent with the observed changes at the protein level. Furthermore, compared with the Young group, the Old group showed significantly increased expression of P53, P21, P16, and HDAC9, along with decreased PCNA expression (Fig. 4B), indicating activation of senescence-associated gene programs and reduced proliferative capacity. HDAC9 knockdown led to decreased expression of P53, P21, P16, and HDAC9, while PCNA expression was increased (Fig. 4B), suggesting that HDAC9 silencing inhibits the activation of senescence-related transcriptional programs and partially restores cellular proliferative potential.

HDAC9 interacts with STAT1 and synergistically amplifies the senescence-associated inflammatory response

Our transcriptomic data indicated a strong activation of STAT1-related inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2D, E). To validate this at the individual gene level, we analyzed the expression of canonical STAT1 transcriptional targets. The FPKM values from our RNA-seq data confirmed a significant upregulation of PD-L1, IRF1, CXCL8, and CCL2 in old discs compared to young ones (Fig. 5A), providing direct evidence of enhanced STAT1 transcriptional activity during aging. We hypothesized that HDAC9 might regulate STAT1 function. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays revealed a direct physical interaction between endogenous HDAC9 and STAT1 (Fig. 5B). This interaction suggests a potential mechanism whereby HDAC9 could modulate STAT1 activity, possibly facilitating its phosphorylation (as seen in Fig. 3A) or recruiting it to specific genomic loci. ELISA results showed that the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the conditioned medium of cells from old group compared to young group. Crucially, this age-associated inflammatory secretion was markedly attenuated by knocking down either HDAC9 or STAT1 (Fig. 5C). The comparable efficacy of HDAC9 and STAT1 knockdown in suppressing cytokine production underscores the functional importance of their interaction and places STAT1 as a critical downstream mediator of HDAC9’s pro-inflammatory action.

To further investigate the role of HDAC9 and its relationship with STAT1 signaling in cellular senescence, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of HDAC9 with or without STAT1 overexpression. qRT-PCR analysis showed that, compared with the Old group, HDAC9 knockdown significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of senescence-associated markers, including P53, P21, and P16, while markedly increasing the expression of the proliferation marker PCNA (Fig. 6). These findings indicate that HDAC9 silencing effectively alleviates cellular senescence and promotes proliferative capacity. Importantly, overexpression of STAT1 partially reversed the effects induced by HDAC9 knockdown. Specifically, STAT1 overexpression led to increased expression of P53, P21, and P16, along with a reduction in PCNA levels compared with the siHDAC9 group (Fig. 6), suggesting a restoration of the senescence phenotype. In addition, HDAC9 expression was significantly suppressed following siHDAC9 treatment, whereas STAT1 overexpression partially rescued HDAC9 expression levels, indicating a potential regulatory interplay between HDAC9 and STAT1 signaling. Collectively, these results suggest that HDAC9 promotes cellular senescence and inhibits proliferation, at least in part, through activation of the STAT1 signaling pathway.

Discussion

Our study identifies HDAC9 as a novel epigenetic driver of intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD). We demonstrate that HDAC9 expression is elevated in degenerative discs, and its knockdown effectively mitigates cellular senescence, ECM degradation, and inflammatory responses. Furthermore, we mechanistically link these effects to a previously unrecognized partnership with the transcription factor STAT1.

We found that HDAC9 knockdown not only reduced canonical senescence markers and oxidative stress but also rebalanced ECM metabolism by suppressing catabolic enzymes and mitigating fibrotic transition. Concurrently, the secretion of pivotal inflammatory cytokines was profoundly attenuated. This coordinated amelioration of multiple degenerative features suggests that HDAC9 likely operates through an upstream regulatory pathway. Our data identify STAT1 as a critical downstream effector that integrates these HDAC9-mediated effects. We propose a model wherein HDAC9 upregulation, triggered by degenerative stimuli, promotes its interaction with STAT1, as supported by co-immunoprecipitation results. This interaction enhances STAT1 phosphorylation and transcriptional activity, thereby driving downstream pro-inflammatory and catabolic gene expression. Importantly, the upstream triggers responsible for HDAC9 upregulation in IDD remain to be fully elucidated but may include oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β), and mechanical overload—factors known to accumulate during early disc degeneration [30, 31]. These stimuli may converge to activate HDAC9 expression as part of a stress-responsive epigenetic program. In this context, HDAC9 may function at an early-to-intermediate stage of degeneration, amplifying inflammatory and senescence signaling and accelerating disease progression.

Our observation that HDAC9 knockdown suppresses key SASP factors is consistent with reports of its pro-inflammatory role in macrophages and HDAC9-deficient mice [32–34], reinforcing the concept that HDAC9 is a conserved regulator of inflammation across tissues. Our discovery that HDAC9 is upregulated in degenerative discs contributes significantly to advancing our understanding of its role in aging-associated pathologies. This observation aligns with studies demonstrating increased HDAC9 levels in aged bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and in diabetic nephropathy [27, 35, 36], suggesting that HDAC9 may act as a broader regulator of age-related tissue degeneration. However, conflicting findings have been reported in IDD, where reduced HDAC9 expression was observed in aged nucleus pulposus tissue [37]. This discrepancy likely reflects the context-dependent and stage-specific roles of HDAC9, as well as differences in cellular microenvironment and interacting partners. Our identification of STAT1 as a functional partner provides a mechanistic framework to reconcile these differences.Pharmacological targeting of class IIa HDACs has shown promise in other degenerative diseases, including neurodegeneration and cardiovascular disorders [30, 31, 38, 39]. These precedents support the feasibility of developing HDAC9-specific inhibitors or RNA-based therapeutics for IDD. However, given the pleiotropic functions of HDAC9 across tissues, further studies are required to assess potential off-target effects and tissue specificity.

The clinical implications of our findings are noteworthy. First, the strong association between HDAC9 expression and disc degeneration severity suggests that HDAC9 may serve as a potential diagnostic or prognostic biomarker, particularly when combined with imaging-based grading systems such as Pfirrmann classification. Second, targeting HDAC9 offers a novel disease-modifying therapeutic strategy, in contrast to current clinical approaches for IDD, which are largely limited to symptomatic management (e.g., analgesics, physiotherapy, or surgical intervention) rather than targeting underlying molecular mechanisms [40, 41]. Compared with emerging biologic therapies such as growth factor delivery or stem cell transplantation, epigenetic modulation via HDAC9 inhibition may provide a more controllable and reversible intervention strategy.

In addition to molecular regulation, IDD is strongly influenced by biomechanical factors. The intervertebral disc is continuously subjected to complex mechanical loading, including compression, torsion, and shear forces. Notably, differences between quadrupedal animal models and human bipedal posture may influence the translational relevance of experimental findings. Mechanical overload has been shown to induce inflammatory signaling and ECM degradation [42], potentially interacting with the HDAC9–STAT1 axis It is therefore plausible that HDAC9 acts as a mechanosensitive epigenetic regulator, linking biomechanical stress to transcriptional responses in disc cells. Future studies incorporating biomechanical loading models will be critical to validate this hypothesis.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. While we have established the HDAC9–STAT1 interaction, the precise molecular mechanism—such as whether HDAC9 directly deacetylates STAT1—remains to be determined. Additionally, although our data support a pathogenic role for HDAC9 in our model, we cannot exclude context-dependent protective roles under specific conditions. Furthermore, the findings are primarily based on in vitro and animal models, and their applicability to human IDD requires further validation in larger clinical cohorts.

In conclusion, our work establishes HDAC9 as a critical epigenetic checkpoint in IDD pathogenesis. By integrating senescence, ECM catabolism, and inflammation through interaction with STAT1, HDAC9 drives degenerative progression. Targeting the HDAC9/STAT1 signaling axis therefore represents a promising disease-modifying strategy for IDD, although further translational and clinical studies are required to fully realize its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies HDAC9 as a critical epigenetic regulator of intervertebral disc degeneration by promoting cellular senescence, ECM degradation, and inflammation through interaction with STAT1. Mechanistically, the HDAC9–STAT1 axis integrates multiple degenerative signals and accelerates disease progression. These findings highlight HDAC9 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for disease-modifying strategies in IDD.
