Electroencephalographic Normalization as a Biomarker of Clinical Recovery in Down Syndrome Regression Disorder
Authors: Jonathan D. Santoro, Mackenzie Silverman, Maeve C. Lucas, Mariam M. Yousuf, Samuel T. Otey, Stella V. Gray, Brittany Jordan, Madeline D. Kahan, Latanya D. Agurs, Michelle Van Hirtum Das, Deborah Holder, Devin King, Eileen A. Quinn, Ryan Kammeyer, Michael S. Rafii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDown syndrome regression disorder is a syndrome characterized by subacute loss of cognitive, behavioral, and functional abilities in individuals with Down syndrome. Electroencephalography abnormalities are frequently observed during evaluation, but it remains unclear whether these findings represent a dynamic marker of disease activity. We evaluated longitudinal changes in electroencephalography and examined whether normalization is associated with clinical improvement.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective longitudinal cohort study of individuals with Down syndrome regression disorder who underwent electroencephalography within 2 months of symptom onset and repeat studies at six and 12 months. Active epilepsy, antiseizure medication use, or seizures during follow‐up were exclusionary. Changes in electroencephalography were assessed over time. Among participants with abnormal baseline electroencephalography, associations between treatment exposures, electroencephalography normalization, and changes in clinical severity were evaluated.ResultsAmong 589 eligible individuals, 181 met inclusion criteria and 69 (38.1%) had abnormal baseline electroencephalography. Abnormal electroencephalography prevalence decreased to 20.4% at 6 months and 16.6% at 12 months (both p < 0.001 versus baseline). Among participants with abnormal baseline electroencephalography, normalization occurred in 49.3% by 6 months and 56.5% by 12 months. Immunotherapy exposure during the first 6 months was associated with higher odds of normalization (p < 0.001, 95% CI: 20.0–676.1) and faster time to normalization (HR: 15.35, p < 0.001, 95% CI: 4.90–48.09). Normalization was associated with reduced disease severity (all p ≤ 0.01).InterpretationElectroencephalography abnormalities frequently improve over time in Down syndrome regression disorder. Normalization is associated with greater clinical recovery, supporting serial electroencephalography as a potential biomarker of neurophysiologic recovery in this condition.

Introduction

Down Syndrome Regression Disorder (DSRD) is an increasingly recognized neuropsychiatric syndrome in adolescents and young adults with Down syndrome characterized by subacute loss of previously acquired cognitive, behavioral, and functional skills, often accompanied by catatonia, sleep disruption, movement abnormalities, and prominent psychiatric symptoms [1, 2, 3, 4]. Despite growing clinical awareness, DSRD remains etiologically heterogeneous and diagnostically challenging, with variable trajectories and limited objective biomarkers to guide prognosis or treatment selection [3, 5, 6, 7]. Electroencephalography (EEG) is frequently obtained during evaluation for regression to assess for seizures or encephalopathy [6], yet the extent to which EEG abnormalities reflect a modifiable disease signal, rather than nonspecific comorbidity, remains uncertain.

Converging clinical observations have raised the possibility that a subset of DSRD may be driven by immune‐mediated mechanisms, supported by reports of inflammatory markers in some patients and apparent clinical responsiveness to immunomodulatory therapies [5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. In that context, EEG could serve as a pragmatic biomarker of neurophysiologic dysfunction and recovery, particularly if EEG abnormalities improve in parallel with symptom resolution and in association with immunotherapy exposure, akin to data from the autoimmune encephalitis literature [12, 13]. However, longitudinal data linking treatment class to EEG normalization and relating EEG improvement to standardized measures of catatonia and global neuropsychiatric severity are limited. In addition to its diagnostic role, EEG normalization could represent a pragmatic biomarker of treatment response and potentially function as a surrogate endpoint for future interventional trials targeting immune‐mediated mechanisms in DSRD.

To address these gaps, we analyzed a longitudinal DSRD cohort with baseline and follow‐up EEG assessments at 6 and 12 months, detailed treatment exposures across the first year (including immunotherapy and non‐immunotherapy classes), and standardized clinical outcomes (BFCRS, NPI‐Q, and CGI‐S). We tested whether the prevalence of abnormal EEG decreases over time, whether EEG normalization is associated with specific treatment exposures, particularly immunotherapy, and whether EEG normalization tracks with greater clinical improvement. Because objective outcome measures remain limited in DSRD, we additionally explored whether EEG normalization could serve as a candidate treatment‐response biomarker linking neurophysiologic recovery with clinical improvement. We additionally characterized changes in EEG abnormality type over time and examined time to normalization across immunotherapy exposure patterns to better define the temporal relationship between treatment and neurophysiologic recovery in DSRD.

Methods

Approvals and Ethics

This study was approved by the Children's Hospital Los Angeles institutional review board. As this study was retrospective in nature, using data previously obtained from a longitudinal observational database, consent and assent were waived for this study.

Study Design and Participants

This study was retrospective in nature and utilized a longitudinal dataset of individuals diagnosed with DSRD. Individuals were eligible for this study if they met either possible or probable international consensus criteria for DSRD [6]. All participants were required to have completed a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including EEG, brain MRI, and lumbar puncture (LP). Eligible participants had an EEG performed within 2 months of symptom onset, as well as repeat EEGs at 6 months (±4 weeks) and 12 months (±4 weeks) after diagnosis. Clinical and demographic data were primarily extracted from the longitudinal dataset and from the electronic medical record, when necessary. Patients were excluded if they (1) had an active epileptic disorder (defined as seizure in the past year), (2) were on any anti‐seizure pharmacotherapy at baseline (excluding benzodiazepines for the treatment of catatonia), (3) had a seizure within the study period, or (4) were placed on anti‐seizure pharmacotherapy for epilepsy during the study period, except for benzodiazepines prescribed for catatonia.

EEG Assessment and Outcomes

EEG status was recorded at baseline and at 6‐ and 12‐months follow‐up. All EEGs were reviewed on a clinical basis by a board‐certified neurologist with fellowship training in epilepsy and/or neurophysiology. Each study was subsequently independently verified by another board‐certified neurologist who was blinded to the patient's treatment exposure and clinical outcome prior to confirmation of the findings. EEGs were classified as normal vs. abnormal using previously published DSRD criteria [5, 7]. EEG classifications were based on clinical interpretations from board‐certified neurologists; formal quantitative EEG metrics (e.g., spectral power or frequency analysis) were not available in this dataset. In addition, abnormality type was recorded (diffuse slowing, focal slowing, diffuse epileptiform discharges, focal epileptiform discharges). Where available, EEG modality (routine vs. prolonged), state (wake vs. sleep), and sedating medication exposure at the time of acquisition were also recorded. For comparison purposes, an EEG prior to the onset of DSRD symptoms was not required, although data were collected when available.

Treatment Exposures and Clinical Outcomes

Treatments were captured as binary indicators for exposure during 0–6 months and separately during 6–12 months, including specific immunotherapy agents (intravenous immunoglobulin, high‐dose corticosteroids, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, and/or tofacitinib) and non‐immunotherapy classes (SSRI, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, and ECT); “exposed” was defined as receipt of the intervention at any time prior to the corresponding follow‐up EEG within the specified window. Clinical status was evaluated using longitudinal changes in Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS), Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q), and Clinical Global Impression–Severity (CGI‐S). These measures were selected because they were consistently collected across the cohort; formal neuropsychological testing and standardized neurologic examination metrics were not uniformly available in this retrospective dataset. The dataset includes precomputed change variables (baseline→6 months and baseline→12 months), which were used as outcomes for association with EEG normalization.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses evaluated whether EEG abnormalities improved over time, whether EEG normalization differed by treatment exposure (with emphasis on immunotherapy), and whether EEG normalization was associated with changes in BFCRS, NPI‐Q, and CGI‐S. EEG at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months was analyzed as abnormal versus normal per dataset coding; longitudinal prevalence analyses used participants with paired EEG measures, and treatment and outcome‐association analyses were restricted to those with abnormal baseline EEG. EEG normalization was defined as a normal EEG at the relevant follow‐up. Within‐person changes in the prevalence of abnormal EEG were tested using McNemar exact tests for baseline versus 6 months, baseline versus 12 months, and 6 versus 12 months, and figures display percentages with 95% Wilson confidence intervals. Treatment effects were examined using treatment indicators for the 0–6 month and 6–12 month windows; for each treatment class (immunotherapy, SSRI, antipsychotic, ECT, benzodiazepine), EEG normalization at 6 months (0–6 exposures) and at 12 months (6–12 exposures) was compared between exposed and non‐exposed participants using Fisher exact tests, with exposure‐group normalization proportions summarized using 95% Wilson confidence intervals.

Associations between EEG normalization and clinical improvement were assessed by comparing baseline‐to‐follow‐up changes (ΔBFCRS, ΔNPI‐Q, ΔCGI‐S) at 6 and 12 months between those with and without EEG normalization at the corresponding timepoint using two‐sided Mann–Whitney U tests, displayed as boxplots with jittered individual values. A composite clinical improvement score (range 0–1) was derived by averaging the baseline‐referenced proportional change for BFCRS, NPI‐Q, and CGI‐S at each timepoint, calculated as (Baseline − Follow‐up)/Baseline and truncated to the interval [0,1] to limit extreme or worsening values. Time to first EEG normalization was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier methods among participants with abnormal baseline EEG, defining the event at 6 months if the 6‐months EEG was normal, otherwise at 12 months if the 12‐months EEG was normal, censoring those not normal by 12 months; curves were stratified by immunotherapy exposure pattern across 0–6 and 6–12 months (none; 0–6 only; 6–12 only; both when present) and compared using the log‐rank test. Due to quasi‐complete separation in models of 6‐months EEG normalization, we used Firth penalized likelihood logistic regression (with profile penalized‐likelihood 95% CIs) for the logistic regression analyses; Cox models were unchanged. All tests were two‐sided with α = 0.05, analyses used available data without imputation, and figures/statistics were generated in Python.

Results

In total, 589 patients were eligible for the study although only 181 individuals (30.7%) met all inclusion criteria. The most common reason for exclusion was lack of baseline EEG within 8 weeks of symptom onset (230, 56.4%), lack of six‐month EEG (97, 23.8%) and follow up outside of the predefined timeframe at either 6 or 12‐months (81, 19.6%). There were no statistically significant differences between the included and excluded cohorts (Table S1). Clinical and demographic data for included individuals is presented in Table 1. Among 181 participants, 69 (38.1%) had an abnormal baseline EEG. In the normal baseline EEG cohort, 44/112 (39.3%) had a prior EEG, of which 7 (15.9%) were abnormal. In the abnormal baseline EEG cohort, 18/69 (26.1%) had a prior EEG of which 3 (16.7%) were abnormal which was not statistically different from the normal baseline EEG cohort (p = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.22–4.15). Baseline ID severity did not differ between participants with normal versus abnormal baseline EEG (p = 0.32). Similarly, among participants with abnormal baseline EEG, baseline ID severity was not associated with receipt of immunotherapy during the first 6 months (p = 0.11). When modeled as an ordinal variable, baseline ID severity was not associated with abnormal EEG (OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.86–1.71, p = 0.27) or immunotherapy exposure (OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.74–2.08, p = 0.41).

Immunotherapy exposure during 0–6 months was strongly associated with EEG normalization. Among participants with abnormal baseline EEG, normalization at 6 months occurred in 32/36 (88.9%) with immunotherapy versus 2/33 (6.1%) without immunotherapy (Table 3). Using Firth penalized logistic regression to address quasi‐complete separation, immunotherapy exposure during 0–6 months was strongly associated with 6‐month EEG normalization (adjusted OR 89.1, 95% CI 20.0–676.1; p < 0.001), reflecting near‐complete separation between exposed and non‐exposed groups in this cohort. In a sensitivity analysis adjusting for baseline ID severity, immunotherapy exposure remained strongly associated with 6‐month EEG normalization (OR 122.6, 95% CI 20.9–719.3, p < 0.001), while baseline ID severity was not associated with normalization (p = 0.76). In an adjusted Cox proportional hazards model of time to first EEG normalization, immunotherapy use was associated with faster normalization (HR 15.35, 95% CI 4.90–48.09, p < 0.001; Figure 2). The large effect estimates observed in logistic models reflect the marked difference in normalization rates between exposed and unexposed participants and should be interpreted as indicating a strong association rather than a precise estimate of treatment effect size. In contrast, EEG normalization proportions in the SSRI, antipsychotic, ECT, and benzodiazepine exposure groups did not differ significantly from the corresponding non‐exposed participants at either follow‐up (p = 0.33, 0.80, 1.00, and 0.60 at 6 months, respectively; and p = 1.00, 0.76, 0.58, and 1.00, respectively, at 12 months), consistent with no statistically demonstrable EEG improvement attributable to these non‐immunotherapy treatments in this dataset (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this longitudinal DSRD cohort with serial EEG and standardized clinical assessments, we observed a clinically and statistically meaningful reduction in EEG abnormalities over the first year of follow‐up, with normalization occurring more frequently in participants managed with immunotherapy. Abnormal EEG prevalence declined from baseline to 6 months and further to 12 months, and time‐to‐event analyses similarly demonstrated earlier and more frequent normalization among immunotherapy‐treated individuals. In contrast, non‐immunotherapy classes commonly used for symptom management in DSRD (e.g., SSRIs, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, and ECT) were not associated with demonstrable differences in normalization at either follow‐up in exposure‐based comparisons. Importantly, EEG normalization tracked with broader clinical recovery: participants whose EEG normalized had significantly greater improvements in catatonia severity, neuropsychiatric symptom burden, and global illness severity at both 6 and 12 months, supporting EEG normalization as a potentially objective biomarker of treatment response and disease trajectory in DSRD. Notably, the degree of EEG abnormality did not correlate with baseline clinical severity in this cohort, further supporting the distinction between cross‐sectional EEG findings and longitudinal neurophysiologic change. These findings raise the possibility that EEG normalization may function not only as a biomarker of disease activity but also as a candidate surrogate endpoint for treatment response in prospective therapeutic studies. In conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis, normalization of neurophysiologic abnormalities has been used as an adjunct indicator of recovery and treatment effect. If validated in prospective cohorts, serial EEG may therefore offer a practical and scalable outcome measure for clinical trials targeting immune‐mediated mechanisms in DSRD.

A central observation in this dataset is the strong association between immunotherapy exposure and EEG normalization among participants who improved clinically. Across both binary EEG outcomes and time‐to‐normalization analyses, immunotherapy exposure showed a substantially higher observed probability of normalization than non‐immunotherapy approaches. Although baseline MRI abnormalities were associated with higher rates of early EEG normalization in unadjusted analyses, this relationship was no longer significant after accounting for immunotherapy exposure, suggesting that MRI findings may primarily reflect treatment selection rather than an independent predictor of neurophysiologic recovery. Similarly, LP abnormalities were not associated with EEG normalization, suggesting that EEG normalization represents a dynamic neurophysiologic signal temporally associated with treatment exposure and clinical improvement. This aligns closely with prior work supporting an immunotherapy‐responsive subgroup in DSRD/DSDD, including early reports of benefit in selected patients [11] and subsequent larger studies demonstrating neurodiagnostic abnormalities consistent with immune activation and clinical responsiveness to immunomodulatory therapy [3, 5, 7, 9, 14]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that immunotherapy exposure is associated with both clinical response and a measurable neurophysiologic endpoint, serial EEG normalization, while also demonstrating that EEG normalization is associated with larger improvements on validated clinical severity instruments.

These results also sit well within the broader framework proposed by the international consensus criteria for DSRD [6], which emphasize the need for systematic evaluation, including EEG, to characterize potentially reversible contributors to regression. In practice, the DSRD population is heterogeneous, and the diagnostic work‐up often yields nonspecific findings. Our data suggest that serial EEG may provide a pragmatic “signal” of biological recovery, particularly in those receiving immunotherapy, and could be useful as an adjunct outcome measure for prospective observational cohorts and treatment trials. As EEG is widely available, inexpensive, and reproducible across centers, its incorporation into future multicenter trials could facilitate standardized measurement of neurophysiologic recovery alongside clinical endpoints. Non‐epileptiform EEG abnormalities are common in individuals with DS without regression, ranging from 13%–33% of the population [15, 16, 17]. However, available data do not support spontaneous normalization; rather, EEG patterns tend to persist or demonstrate age‐related slowing over time [18]. The degree and rapidity of normalization observed in our cohort, particularly in association with immunotherapy and clinical improvement, would not be expected based on natural history alone and supports a dynamic, treatment‐responsive process.

Notably, the predominance of background slowing and its subsequent resolution is consistent with general neurophysiologic patterns observed in inflammatory encephalopathies, where diffuse slowing is a common EEG feature and can improve with immunotherapy and clinical recovery [19, 20, 21]. Although hypothetical, resolution of diffuse slowing may reflect a reversal of inflammation‐mediated synaptic dysfunction or diffuse network disruption. However, because ‘immunotherapy’ in this cohort encompasses agents with vastly different mechanisms of action (e.g., broad anti‐inflammatory effects of corticosteroids versus B‐cell depletion by rituximab), future prospective studies must delineate which specific immunologic pathways drive this neurophysiologic recovery.

In contrast, we did not detect statistically significant EEG normalization effects associated with non‐immunotherapy categories (e.g., SSRIs, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, and ECT) in this dataset. This pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that these treatments are clinically ineffective in DSRD; rather, our results suggest they may act on symptoms without modifying the underlying disease‐driven encephalopathic signal. Furthermore, we cannot exclude the possibility that ongoing diffuse slowing observed in patients receiving continuous non‐immunotherapy psychotropic or sedating medications (e.g., benzodiazepines, antipsychotics) may be partially iatrogenic. Finally, these findings may be limited by sample size and treatment heterogeneity within categories. Additionally, several exposures may be used preferentially in specific clinical phenotypes (confounding by indication), which can obscure associations in observational designs. Nonetheless, the consistency of the immunotherapy association across analytic approaches supports the hypothesis that, for a subset of patients, EEG abnormalities represent a treatment‐responsive physiological marker rather than a static comorbidity.

The temporal pattern of improvement is also clinically informative. Most normalization occurred by 6 months, with additional gains by 12 months, suggesting a time‐dependent recovery trajectory. This observation is consistent with prior prospective immunotherapy studies in DSRD demonstrating meaningful clinical response with immunotherapy and identifying relapse risk after discontinuation [14]. Our time‐to‐event results further suggest that differentiating immunotherapy exposure windows may be important for understanding treatment timing effects; however, sparse strata in some exposure groups limit definitive inference about delayed initiation. Future work should prospectively capture dosing, duration, and sequencing of immunotherapy to better estimate causal timing effects.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was an observational, retrospectively assembled cohort, creating susceptibility to selection bias (including incomplete availability of serial EEGs and follow‐up assessments) and limiting generalizability to all individuals with DSRD. Compounding this is that patients were evaluated at centers specializing in the diagnosis and management of DSRD, further adding additional selection bias, severity bias, and ascertainment bias to this cohort. Second, treatment exposures were not randomized; therefore, associations between immunotherapy and EEG normalization are vulnerable to confounding by indication. Treatment selection was influenced by the presence of neurodiagnostic abnormalities (specifically MRI and LP) but not phenotypic presentation, sex, age, or baseline disease severity. Accordingly, observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than causal. An additional potential confounder is baseline ID severity, as diffuse EEG slowing may occur in individuals with more severe intellectual disability independent of acute disease processes. However, baseline ID severity did not differ between EEG or treatment groups and was not associated with EEG normalization in adjusted analyses. The dynamic pattern of EEG improvement over time, particularly in association with clinical recovery, argues against these findings representing a static neurodevelopmental trait. Third, treatment variables were captured as binary exposure windows and do not encode medication dose, duration, sequencing, or tapering relative to EEG acquisition. This is particularly relevant for psychotropic and sedating agents, such as benzodiazepines and antipsychotics, which may influence background EEG slowing. Accordingly, some apparent EEG normalization may reflect changes in concomitant medication exposure rather than a direct treatment effect. However, non‐immunotherapy treatment classes, including benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, SSRIs, and ECT, were not associated with EEG normalization in this cohort, suggesting that non‐specific medication or sedation effects alone are unlikely to account for the observed pattern of EEG improvement. Fourth, EEG was analyzed primarily as normal vs. abnormal in the context of having nearly all abnormalities consistent with generalized slowing, and while we additionally examined abnormality type, differences in EEG acquisition conditions and interpretation may introduce measurement variability. Further, formal quantitative EEG measures were not available, and analyses relied on qualitative clinical interpretations of EEG. While this reflects real‐world clinical practice, it may limit sensitivity to more subtle electrophysiologic changes. Fifth, several analyses, particularly those examining treatment exposures, were subject to sparse‐data behavior, with quasi‐complete separation producing very large effect estimates in logistic models. Although penalized regression methods were used to mitigate this issue, the magnitude of these estimates should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as evidence of a strong association rather than a precise estimate of treatment effect. Finally, outcomes were limited to 12 months and were assessed using clinician‐ and caregiver‐reported severity scales. Objective neuropsychological testing, standardized neurologic examination findings, and longer‐term functional endpoints were not uniformly available in this retrospective dataset. While the selected scales capture clinically relevant domains of DSRD, including catatonia, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and global illness severity, they do not fully characterize cognitive recovery and may introduce measurement variability in a population with pre‐existing developmental differences.

In summary, our analysis of abnormality subtypes suggests that improvement is driven primarily by resolution rather than a large redistribution among abnormal subtypes over time. This is relevant for biomarker development: binary EEG status may be sufficient for pragmatic monitoring, but future studies should consider incorporation of quantitative EEG features (e.g., slowing indices, spectral power) to improve sensitivity and allow harmonization across centers. Larger multicenter datasets, ideally with protocolized EEG timepoints and standardized treatment regimens, will also be necessary to mitigate confounding by indication, evaluate effect heterogeneity, and determine whether EEG normalization can serve as a surrogate endpoint for durable functional recovery. Such studies will be critical to determining whether EEG normalization represents merely a correlate of clinical improvement or a robust biomarker capable of informing treatment decisions and trial design in this emerging neuroimmunologic condition.
