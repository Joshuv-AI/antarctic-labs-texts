The silent zone of paediatric asthma: integrating small airway dysfunction assessment into clinical practice
Authors: Cristiana Indolfi, Angela Klain, Giulio Dinardo, Carolina Grella, Salvatore Cascone, Michele Miraglia del Giudice
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySmall airway dysfunction (SAD) is increasingly recognised as a clinically relevant component of paediatric asthma because peripheral airway abnormalities may precede spirometric impairment and contribute to poor disease control despite preserved FEV1. This narrative review summarises current evidence on the assessment and clinical implications of SAD in children, based on studies identified through PubMed/MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library. Spirometry remains the cornerstone of asthma evaluation but has limited sensitivity for early peripheral airway disease. Impulse oscillometry provides the most practical effort-independent assessment of distal airway mechanics, particularly in preschool children, while body plethysmography, multiple-breath nitrogen washout, high-resolution computed tomography, and inflammatory biomarkers including FeNO, alveolar nitric oxide, and volatile organic compounds offer complementary information in selected settings. Current evidence supports a multimodal and clinically integrated approach when symptoms and spirometry are discordant. However, standardised paediatric reference values, cost-effectiveness analyses, and randomised trials demonstrating improved outcomes with SAD-guided management are required before routine implementation in clinical guidelines.

Search strategy and selection criteria.A literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library to identify English-language studies published from 2000 to 2026 investigating small airway dysfunction in paediatric asthma. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to “small airway dysfunction”, “asthma”, “paediatric asthma”, “impulse oscillometry”, “forced oscillation technique”, “spirometry”, “forced expiratory flow”, and “fractional exhaled nitric oxide”. Titles and abstracts of all retrieved records were screened for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Studies evaluating functional or inflammatory markers of peripheral airway involvement in children or adolescents with asthma or recurrent wheezing were eligible for inclusion. Studies addressing the assessment, pathophysiology, prognosis, or clinical implications of small airway dysfunction were also considered when they provided clinically relevant evidence. To ensure comprehensive coverage, additional relevant publications including landmark studies, guideline documents, and key adult studies providing contextual evidence, were identified through manual screening of reference lists from selected articles and reviews. Studies focusing exclusively on non-asthmatic respiratory diseases, animal models, in vitro investigations, or studies not directly addressing small airway dysfunction assessment were excluded. Given the narrative nature of this review, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative meta-analysis was performed. Study selection was intended to provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence rather than an exhaustive systematic review of the literature. Overall, the search yielded 573 records through database searching and five additional records through manual searching. Following screening, 61 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 26 studies were ultimately included in the narrative synthesis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Literature search and study selection process.

Introduction

Small airway dysfunction (SAD) has progressively emerged as a pivotal yet historically under-recognised component of airway disease, particularly in asthma.1 The small airways, generally defined as airways with an internal diameter of less than 2 mm arising from approximately the seventh to eighth generation of bronchial branching, represent a vast anatomical and functional compartment of the lung. When diseased, they contribute disproportionately to airflow resistance.2 The terms “small airways,” “peripheral airways,” and “distal airways” are often used interchangeably to refer to this airway compartment beyond the central bronchi. Although subtle anatomical distinctions exist, they broadly describe the distal conducting airways implicated in asthma pathophysiology.3 Despite their importance, these peripheral airways have long been referred to as the lung’s “silent zone,” as substantial pathological involvement may occur before abnormalities are detected by conventional spirometric indices.4 This concept is especially relevant in paediatric asthma, where symptoms, exacerbations, and impaired control may coexist with apparently preserved forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), masking the presence of distal airway pathology.1,2

Small airway dysfunction in children

In preschool children with wheezing disorders, SAD appears to represent a distinct physiological dimension. Bokov et al. showed that impulse oscillometry-derived markers of peripheral airway dysfunction clustered separately from clinical wheezing phenotypes and asthma probability, suggesting that distal airway mechanics contribute independently to disease expression and may not be fully captured by conventional clinical assessment.5 These observations are supported by evidence from larger paediatric cohorts. Yi et al. studied 851 preschool children with asthma and reported that nearly one-fifth had objective evidence of SAD. Reduced FEF25–75, FEF50, and FEF75 values were associated with more severe airflow obstruction and airway hyperresponsiveness.6 These findings suggest that SAD is not an isolated phenomenon but part of a continuum of airway dysfunction that may precede or amplify central airway abnormalities. In a cohort of children with moderate-to-severe asthma and normal baseline FEV1, Azaldegi et al. found that oscillometric indices of small airway dysfunction were abnormal in a substantial proportion of patients despite preserved spirometric function.7 Although these findings did not immediately translate into changes in routine clinical management, they identified a clinically relevant phenotype characterised by peripheral dysfunction despite normal spirometry.7 This discrepancy between spirometry and peripheral airway mechanics reinforces the concept that reliance on FEV1 alone may underestimate disease burden. Further evidence linking SAD to disease severity comes from studies demonstrating associations with airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness, and symptom burden.8 Liu et al. emphasised that SAD correlates with more pronounced airway hyperresponsiveness and symptom burden, advocating for its integration into paediatric asthma evaluation.8 Taken together, these paediatric data suggest that SAD is neither rare nor incidental but a frequent and clinically relevant component of childhood asthma.

Lessons from adult asthma

Evidence from adult populations provides important complementary insights into the broader clinical relevance of small airway dysfunction across the lifespan. Adult studies consistently show that peripheral airway involvement is not confined to severe disease but represents a common and clinically meaningful feature of asthma, supporting the biological plausibility that similar mechanisms are already active during childhood and may contribute to subsequent disease progression. In the ATLANTIS study, oscillometry-defined small airway dysfunction independently predicted future exacerbations in a large multicentre cohort of adults with asthma, including patients with apparently well-controlled disease.9 Similarly, Cottini et al. demonstrated a remarkably high prevalence of IOS-defined SAD in adults with intermittent asthma treated only with short-acting β2-agonists, with nearly three-quarters of patients showing peripheral abnormalities despite apparently mild disease by conventional clinical criteria. These patients also experienced more frequent exacerbations, nocturnal symptoms, and poorer asthma control.10 Overall, prevalence estimates of approximately 50–60% have been reported for SAD in adult asthmatic populations, whereas a comparable prevalence has not yet been established in children because of methodological heterogeneity across paediatric studies.11 Taken together, these findings reinforce the concept that SAD is a clinically relevant trait throughout life and suggest that the peripheral airway abnormalities identified in childhood may represent the early expression of a disease process that persists into adulthood.

Clinical recognition and prognostic implications of small airway dysfunction

Recent research has attempted to delineate clinical predictors of SAD to facilitate identification in routine practice.12 Elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), a marker of type 2 airway inflammation, has been associated with peripheral involvement, suggesting that inflammation extends beyond central airways in a subset of patients. Female sex, smoking exposure, and increasing age have also been linked to a higher likelihood of SAD, while obesity and nocturnal symptoms further characterise this phenotype.10,12 Among these predictors, exercise-induced asthma (EIA) symptoms appear particularly informative. In classification analyses, overweight asthmatic patients with EIA demonstrated an extremely high probability of SAD, while those with nocturnal symptoms alone also showed a substantial risk.10 Such findings highlight that specific symptom patterns may act as clinical surrogates for peripheral airway disease when advanced physiological testing is unavailable (Fig. 2). In this context, improving the recognition of small airway dysfunction has become an important objective in paediatric asthma research and clinical practice. Overall, paediatric and adult data converge that SAD is common, can be hidden by normal FEV1, and is associated with poorer control, nocturnal symptoms, exercise limitation, and exacerbation risk.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Because forced expiratory flow indices in children aged 0–6 years are strongly influenced by effort, lung volume, expiratory time, and technical quality, these associations should be interpreted as supportive signals rather than definitive proof of distal airway disease. However, this review does not propose SAD as a new disease entity or a replacement for current asthma assessment. It aims to synthesise the evidence into a pragmatic framework for paediatric practice: when symptoms, control, and spirometry are discordant, clinicians may consider complementary assessment with IOS, selected lung-volume measures, and inflammatory biomarkers, while recognising that standardised thresholds and outcome-driven trials are still needed. SAD assessment using functional and structural appraisal tools

Spirometry

Spirometry remains the reference test for the functional evaluation of asthma and is central to current guideline recommendations. According to GINA, asthma diagnosis is confirmed by demonstrating variable airflow limitation, most commonly defined as an increase in FEV1 of 12% or more (and ≥200 mL in older children and adults) after bronchodilator administration, or by significant variability in lung function over time, although recent ATS/ERS technical standards define a positive bronchodilator response as a change of more than 10% of the predicted FEV1 value.13,14 However, spirometric indices such as FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC predominantly reflect large and medium-sized airway function and may remain within normal limits despite clinically relevant involvement of the distal airways.2,11,15 The physiological explanation lies in the structure of the bronchial tree: small airways (<2 mm diameter) are arranged in parallel and contribute minimally to total airway resistance under normal conditions. Consequently, a large proportion of peripheral airways may be affected before spirometric indices become abnormal, a phenomenon that underpins the concept of the small airways as the lung’s “silent zone”.15

This limitation is clinically relevant in asthma. Studies consistently show that spirometry may underestimate disease burden and fail to capture peripheral dysfunction associated with poor control.12,16,17 In adults and children, SAD has been strongly linked to worse asthma control, nocturnal symptoms, and exacerbations, while conventional spirometric indices may still be preserved.11,18 In particular, patients with nocturnal asthma symptoms frequently display marked abnormalities in non-spirometric small airway measures, whereas FEV1 and FEV1/FVC do not correlate with symptom burden, highlighting the dissociation between central airflow and distal lung impairment.18

Attempts to derive small airway information from spirometry rely mainly on mid-expiratory flow indices, especially FEF25–75%, as well as FEF50% and FEF75%. These parameters reflect airflow during the middle and late phases of forced expiration, when lung volumes are lower and the contribution of peripheral airways to flow limitation is greater. Paediatric data support a role for these indices as surrogate markers of small airway involvement. In preschool children with asthma, FEF25–75%, FEF50% and FEF75% correlate strongly with FEV1%, FEV1/FVC%, and peak expiratory flow, and are associated with more severe airflow obstruction and increased airway hyperresponsiveness.6 Notably, small airway flow parameters show a significant relationship with methacholine responsiveness (PC20), in some cases stronger than FEV1, suggesting that distal airway impairment is closely linked to bronchial hyperreactivity in early childhood asthma.6

Similarly, in children with mild-to-moderate asthmatic symptoms, spirometric indices related to the late expiratory phase, particularly FEF25–75, have been associated with small airway abnormalities detected by complementary techniques such as impulse oscillometry, as well as with clinical outcomes including exacerbations and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction.19 These findings indicate that spirometry can provide indirect clues to peripheral dysfunction, especially when mid-expiratory flows are reduced.

Nevertheless, major methodological limitations restrict the reliability of these parameters. Mid-expiratory flows are highly dependent on FVC, lung volumes, and patient effort, and show considerable intra- and inter-individual variability.2,15 Because they are derived from the descending portion of the flow–volume curve, submaximal effort, early termination of expiration, or changes in lung volume can substantially alter FEF values. Standardised cutoffs and clear sensitivity/specificity thresholds for SAD are lacking, and normal FEF25–75% does not exclude peripheral dysfunction.2,15

These limitations are amplified in paediatrics. Preschool children often cannot perform reliable forced expiratory maneuvers meeting ATS/ERS acceptability criteria, including the achievement of an adequate end-expiratory plateau, making spirometry difficult to obtain and even harder to interpret for small airway assessment.2,14,20 Moreover, studies in symptom-controlled paediatric asthma show that children may exhibit persistent abnormal lung function or bronchodilator reversibility despite clinical stability, indicating that spirometry may not fully reflect underlying airway pathology.21 Taken together, these observations reinforce the concept that spirometry, although indispensable for the assessment of global airflow limitation, should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical evaluation and represents a functional “snapshot” at a given time point rather than a comprehensive measure of overall asthma control.11,15

Impulse oscillometry

IOS, derived from the forced oscillation technique, is an effort-independent method that evaluates respiratory system mechanics during quiet tidal breathing. Unlike spirometry, which requires forced expiratory maneuvers to assess airflow limitation, IOS applies pressure oscillations of different frequencies at the mouth and analyses the resulting pressure–flow relationship to characterise respiratory impedance.22 This impedance consists of resistance (Rrs), representing airflow opposition, and reactance (Xrs), reflecting the elastic and inertial properties of the respiratory system. Because low-frequency oscillations penetrate more distally into the bronchial tree, IOS provides a unique window into small airway mechanics and is particularly suited to paediatric populations, including preschool children who are often unable to perform reliable spirometry.23

Physiological meaning of IOS parameters

Among IOS indices, resistance at 5 Hz (R5) reflects total airway resistance (central and peripheral), whereas resistance at 20 Hz (R20) predominantly reflects central airway resistance. The difference between R5 and R20 (R5–R20) is widely used as an index of frequency-dependent resistance and is considered a surrogate marker of peripheral airway involvement. Reactance at 5 Hz (X5) primarily reflects the elastic recoil and peripheral compliance of the respiratory system, particularly those of the distal lung units, becoming more negative with increasing small airway obstruction and peripheral air trapping. The area of reactance (AX), defined as the integrated area of reactance between 5 Hz and the resonant frequency (Fres), summarises the overall burden of peripheral mechanical impairment. Interpretation of IOS parameters in paediatric patients with asthma

Several studies highlight the usefulness of IOS parameters (R5–20, AX, X5, and Fres) as indicators of small airway function and emphasise that the management of SAD is crucial for asthma control.16 IOS is a non-invasive technique that evaluates respiratory mechanics during quiet tidal breathing, without requiring forced respiratory maneuvers. However, there is no clear consensus on which of these parameters best represents a predictor of future uncontrolled asthma. In their study, Zheng et al. aimed to evaluate the use of IOS and FeNO as predictors of asthma control in preschool children aged between 3 and 6 years. They concluded that the optimal AX cutoff value of 37.435 cm H2O/L was able to predict future loss of asthma control compared with the cutoff values of R5 (9.225 cm H2O/(L/s)), R5–20 (6.780 cm H2O/(L/s)), and Z5 (10.935 cm H2O/(L/s)).25 On the other hand, in their study Lin et al. reported R5-20 as a fundamental parameter for defining the diagnosis of asthma as well as the risk of developing uncontrolled asthma.12 Konstantinou et al. in their study evaluated the use of IOS as a tool for objectively detecting airflow limitation in preschool children with viral infection-induced wheezing. In addition to demonstrating that IOS is a simple, highly reproducible, and sensitive technique for detecting this condition, they established that among the parameters commonly used in the assessment of IOS, R5 appears to be the most suitable for evaluating fluctuations in airflow limitation as well as bronchodilator response.26 Finally, Zeng et al. examined preschool children (range 3–6 years), highlighting that IOS parameters most correlated with the functional status of the small airways are R5-20, X5, and AX. Among these, according to their study, X5 is more indicative of the asthma control state.24 In summary, the analysis of the available literature does not provide a unified view regarding which single IOS parameter is the best indicator for the assessment of small airway function and for predicting future asthma episodes. Rather, the group of parameters R5, R5–20, AX and X5 appears to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the condition of the small airways in children.12,24,25

Impulse oscillometry identifies SAD despite normal spirometry

Evidence from both adult and paediatric studies supports the ability of impulse oscillometry to detect small airway dysfunction even in the absence of spirometric abnormalities.27 In a cross-sectional study by Cottini et al., 321 adults with physician-diagnosed asthma and normal baseline spirometry were evaluated using both IOS and conventional spirometric parameters.16 At enrollment, all patients had preserved lung function (FEV1 ≥80% predicted and FEV1/FVC above the lower limit of normal), although they had a clinical diagnosis of asthma and variable respiratory symptoms. Small airway dysfunction defined by IOS (R5–R20 > 0.07 kPa s/L) was detected in 54.1% of participants, whereas spirometry identified peripheral airflow limitation in only 10% of cases using FEF25–75 less than 65% predicted. 46.6% of patients showed abnormalities exclusively detected by IOS, while only 2.5% were identified by both techniques, indicating that conventional spirometry may underestimate distal airway involvement in patients with preserved global lung function.16 Similar findings have been consistently reported in both preschool and school-aged paediatric cohorts, where peripheral mechanical abnormalities may be detected despite preserved FEV1 and otherwise normal conventional spirometry.12,23,24

Practical implementation of oscillometry in the paediatric office

In clinical settings, oscillometry can be considered for implementation in paediatric offices or outpatient clinics when a validated commercial device, trained staff, and appropriate reference equations are available. The test is brief, requires quiet tidal breathing with cheek support, and is generally feasible from preschool age; quality control should include repeated technically acceptable recordings, inspection for leaks or swallowing, and interpretation of resistance and reactance patterns rather than a single parameter. At present, IOS is best used when spirometry is not feasible, when symptoms or exacerbations are disproportionate to FEV1, or when monitoring suspected peripheral airway involvement. Cost, calibration, local reference values, and reimbursement may limit routine adoption, and IOS findings alone should not mandate treatment escalation outside a broader clinical assessment.22

Body plethysmography

Body plethysmography provides a complementary assessment of lung volumes and airway resistance that may help identify air trapping and hyperinflation related to distal airway closure. Unlike spirometry, which primarily describes forced airflow, plethysmography measures static lung volumes such as residual volume (RV), functional residual capacity (FRC), total lung capacity (TLC), and the RV/TLC ratio, as well as airway resistance. In a child with asthma and preserved FEV1, elevated RV or RV/TLC may suggest small airway closure and gas trapping, particularly when symptoms or exercise limitation remain unexplained by conventional spirometry.28,29 Its role in paediatric SAD assessment is therefore supportive rather than diagnostic on its own. Plethysmography requires cooperation with panting maneuvers in an enclosed cabin, trained personnel, and specialised equipment, making it less accessible than spirometry and less practical than IOS for many preschool children. It is most useful in referral or severe-asthma settings, in children with discordant symptoms and spirometry, suspected hyperinflation, or persistent poor control despite apparently normal FEV1. Results should be interpreted together with clinical history, bronchodilator response, IOS when available, and inflammatory biomarkers; Treatment decisions should not rely solely on plethysmographic indices.29

Multiple-breath nitrogen washout

Multiple-breath nitrogen washout (MBNW) is an effort-independent physiological technique that assesses ventilation heterogeneity, a recognised functional manifestation of small airway dysfunction in asthma.30, 31, 32 Unlike conventional spirometry, which relies on forced expiratory manoeuvres, MBNW is performed during tidal breathing while the subject inhales 100% oxygen.32 Its effort-independent nature makes the test particularly suitable for children, including preschool-aged patients, and reduces some of the limitations associated with forced expiratory flow measurements.30,32

The main outcome derived from MBNW is the Lung Clearance Index (LCI), which reflects the number of lung volume turnovers required to reduce the concentration of resident nitrogen to 1/40th of its starting value.31,32 Several studies have shown that LCI may detect abnormalities not identified by conventional spirometric parameters such as FEV1, particularly in the presence of mild or early peripheral airway involvement.30,31,33,34 In addition, Keen et al. demonstrated that indices derived from MBW, particularly Scond and Sacin, were abnormal in a substantial proportion of children with asthma despite only modest abnormalities in conventional spirometry, highlighting the sensitivity of ventilation heterogeneity measurements for detecting peripheral airway dysfunction.33 Cross-sectional paediatric studies suggest that most children with mild-to-moderate asthma have normal or only slightly elevated LCI values compared with healthy controls, whereas abnormal LCI values are more frequently observed in severe asthma and have been associated with lower FEV1 z-scores.31,34 These findings support the concept that ventilation heterogeneity may persist despite preserved spirometry and ongoing anti-inflammatory treatment.30,31 More recently, MBNW has also been applied to children with asthma and obesity, showing that excess body weight may further contribute to abnormalities in ventilation distribution.35

Beyond LCI, analysis of the phase III slope allows the calculation of Scond and Sacin, indices reflecting ventilation heterogeneity arising predominantly from the conductive and acinar airways, respectively.32,36 In asthma, increased Scond has been associated with bronchial hyperresponsiveness independently of airway inflammation,37 suggesting that abnormalities in peripheral airway function may contribute to disease expression through mechanisms that are not fully captured by inflammatory biomarkers alone. Whether these abnormalities represent an early marker of disease progression or a potentially modifiable therapeutic target remains an area of ongoing investigation.

Despite these promising findings, several factors currently limit the widespread use of MBNW in routine clinical practice. Available evidence is largely based on observational studies, while prospective studies evaluating its impact on clinical decision-making and long-term outcomes remain limited.36,38 In addition, prolonged testing times, equipment costs, and incomplete standardisation across devices continue to represent important barriers to broader implementation.32 At present, MBNW is best regarded as a complementary tool alongside spirometry and IOS that may provide additional information on peripheral airway involvement, particularly in specialised or research settings.

Chest high-resolution computed tomography

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) represents the reference imaging technique for evaluating structural abnormalities of the small airways.39 In paediatric asthma, its use is generally limited to selected clinical situations, including severe or difficult-to-treat disease and cases in which alternative diagnoses need to be considered.39,40 This cautious approach reflects the need to balance the potential diagnostic benefit against radiation exposure, which remains a particular concern in children.40

The radiological assessment of SAD in children differs from that in adults because of developmental differences in airway size and lung structure.40 HRCT findings associated with small airway involvement can be broadly divided into direct and indirect signs.

Direct signs include bronchial wall thickening, mucus plugging, and visualisation of peripheral airways that are normally below the resolution threshold of conventional CT imaging.39,40 In cohorts of children with severe asthma, HRCT has also identified structural abnormalities such as bronchiectasis,39 suggesting that airway remodeling may occur in a subset of patients with longstanding or severe disease.

Indirect signs mainly reflect air trapping, which is considered one of the most characteristic imaging manifestations of peripheral airway obstruction.39,40 End-expiratory acquisitions are essential for its detection.40 Affected lung regions fail to empty appropriately during expiration and remain relatively hypodense compared with surrounding lung tissue, which normally increases in attenuation during exhalation.40

Quantitative CT (qCT) approaches allow objective measurement of air trapping through densitometric analysis of expiratory scans. Parameters such as the pixel index, defined as the proportion of lung voxels below predetermined attenuation thresholds, have been used to quantify the extent of peripheral airway involvement.41 Supporting the link between structural and functional assessment of SAD, Fuso et al. reported significant correlations between quantitative CT measures of air trapping and nitrogen washout indices, particularly the phase III slope (ΔN2), suggesting that physiological markers of ventilation heterogeneity may reflect structural abnormalities detected by imaging.42

The association between structural abnormalities and lung function appears less consistent in children than in adults. While adult studies have reported associations between air trapping, airway remodeling, and airflow limitation,40 paediatric studies have not consistently demonstrated a direct correlation between HRCT abnormalities and spirometric impairment.40,41 This discrepancy may indicate that imaging findings in children reflect early airway remodeling, persistent peripheral inflammation, or a combination of both that remains undetected by conventional lung function tests.30,40 These observations also highlight the need for caution when extrapolating evidence from adult populations to paediatric asthma. Given concerns regarding radiation exposure, HRCT is unlikely to play a role in the routine assessment of SAD. Nevertheless, it remains a valuable complementary tool in carefully selected patients, particularly when detailed structural characterisation of the peripheral airways is clinically relevant.39,40

Inflammatory biomarkers of SAD

Because small airway dysfunction arises from the interaction between structural, mechanical, and inflammatory abnormalities, physiological assessment alone may provide an incomplete picture. Inflammatory biomarkers can complement functional testing and help characterise the underlying disease phenotype.

Role of FeNO in SAD

FeNO is a well-established, non-invasive biomarker of Type 2 airway inflammation and reflects eosinophilic inflammatory activity within the bronchial epithelium. A key distinction is needed, however, when FeNO is discussed in relation to SAD. The usual single-flow FeNO used in clinical practice is measured at 50 mL/s and primarily reflects bronchial, relatively large-airway nitric oxide output. Peripheral or alveolar nitric oxide is better represented by the alveolar concentration of nitric oxide (CANO), usually estimated with multiple-flow techniques that include high expiratory flows such as 200 mL/s. Therefore, elevated standard FeNO may suggest a Type 2 inflammatory phenotype that can coexist with distal airway dysfunction, but it should not be described as a direct stand-alone marker of peripheral airway inflammation.43, 44, 45, 46

Paediatric IOS-based studies have further clarified the relationship between FeNO and peripheral airway mechanics. In preschool children, Zeng et al. demonstrated that FeNO levels were significantly higher in uncontrolled asthma and correlated with IOS-derived small airway indices such as X5, AX, and R5–R20.24 Because standard FeNO primarily reflects bronchial nitric oxide, this association should be interpreted as evidence of overlap between Type 2 airway inflammation and peripheral mechanical impairment, not as proof that single-flow FeNO directly measures distal airway inflammation. Similarly, Zheng et al. showed that IOS indices of SAD were predictive of future loss of control and that combining FeNO with IOS improved risk stratification.25 Large-scale population data reinforce these findings. In a cohort of over 5000 school-aged children, Lin et al. demonstrated that uncontrolled asthma was associated with both elevated FeNO and abnormal IOS parameters (R5, R5–R20, X5, AX), and that combining FeNO (>20 ppb) with IOS significantly improved specificity for detecting poor control.12 Notably, R5–R20 emerged as the strongest functional predictor of uncontrolled asthma, whereas FeNO contributed complementary information on Type 2 airway inflammation, supporting the concept that inflammatory activity and peripheral mechanical dysfunction represent distinct but frequently overlapping components of asthma pathophysiology rather than a direct cause-and-effect relationship.12,24

These findings indicate that FeNO should be interpreted as an inflammatory adjunct rather than as a direct diagnostic test for SAD. Standard FeNO alone lacks sufficient sensitivity and specificity to diagnose peripheral airway dysfunction or to be used as a stand-alone screening tool, as highlighted by Baranski et al.47 Its optimal role lies in combination with functional assessment, particularly IOS, and, where available, CANO or other approaches that better separate central from peripheral nitric oxide sources. From a therapeutic perspective, FeNO may provide pharmacodynamic information relevant to Type 2 inflammation, but escalation or treatment decisions should continue to be guided by the overall clinical assessment rather than by FeNO or IOS abnormalities in isolation.48

Exhaled volatile organic compounds

Exhaled volatile organic compounds (VOCs) have emerged as a promising non-invasive approach for assessing airway inflammation and small airway involvement in asthma.49 VOCs are gaseous metabolites generated by inflammatory, oxidative, and metabolic processes occurring within the respiratory tract and can be detected in exhaled breath using technologies such as gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and electronic nose (eNose) systems. Because VOC profiles reflect biochemical activity throughout the bronchial tree, including distal airways, they have attracted increasing interest as potential biomarkers of SAD.50,51 Several studies have demonstrated that children with asthma exhibit distinct VOC signatures compared with healthy controls, suggesting that exhaled breath analysis may provide information on underlying airway inflammation beyond that obtained from conventional lung function testing.52,53 VOC patterns have been associated with eosinophilic inflammation, asthma control, exacerbation risk, and response to inhaled corticosteroid therapy. Importantly, VOC analysis may capture both Type 2 and non-Type 2 inflammatory pathways, potentially offering a broader characterisation of airway disease than FeNO alone.51 The potential relevance of VOCs for small airway assessment derives from their ability to reflect inflammatory and oxidative processes occurring throughout the respiratory tract, including distal airway regions. VOC profiling has shown promise for asthma phenotyping and for identifying distinct inflammatory patterns that may not be captured by conventional biomarkers alone. Consequently, VOC analysis could complement physiological techniques such as IOS and inflammatory biomarkers such as FeNO in the characterisation of airway disease. However, evidence supporting its specific role in assessing small airway dysfunction remains limited, and methodological heterogeneity, lack of standardised sampling procedures, variability in analytical platforms, and limited paediatric reference data currently preclude routine clinical implementation. Discussion

The available evidence suggests that small airway dysfunction is a frequent and clinically relevant component of pediatric asthma, representing an early site of airway involvement with important implications for disease control and prognosis. However, current evidence supports a tiered and clinically integrated approach to its assessment rather than routine implementation of any single diagnostic modality.

The predictive role of small airway dysfunction

SAD is increasingly recognized not merely as a physiological descriptor of peripheral airway involvement but as a clinically relevant and potentially predictive trait in pediatric asthma. A consistent finding across recent paediatric studies is that abnormalities in distal airway mechanics often precede overt spirometric impairment and may anticipate future loss of asthma control. This temporal dissociation between peripheral dysfunction and traditional measures of airflow limitation underscores the need for multidimensional assessment strategies combining functional and inflammatory markers.

IOS plays a central role in this predictive framework. IOS is a promising adjunct in this framework because it is performed during quiet tidal breathing and is less dependent on forced expiratory effort, which is a major advantage in preschool and younger school-aged children.27,54 Paediatric studies have demonstrated that IOS indices reflecting frequency-dependent resistance (R5–R20), distal reactance (X5), and global reactance burden (AX) are significantly associated with asthma control and are able to predict short-term loss of control.12,24,25 In a prospective cohort study by Zheng et al.,25 several IOS-derived indices predicted future loss of asthma control in preschool children, with AX showing the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.884), outperforming R5 (AUC = 0.826), R5–R20 (AUC = 0.842), and Z5 (AUC = 0.879). Similarly, large school-based cohorts have shown that R5–R20 is an independent functional risk factor for uncontrolled asthma, linking peripheral mechanical dysfunction with worse symptom scores and control indices.12 These data support IOS as a marker of risk and peripheral mechanics, but they remain largely observational. To date, randomised paediatric trials demonstrating that IOS-guided treatment changes improve clinical outcomes are lacking; therefore, IOS should inform, not replace, clinical judgment and guideline-based management.22

Spirometry, although less sensitive to early SAD, provides complementary information. Indices such as FEV1 and FEV1/FVC primarily reflect large airway function, while mid-expiratory flows (FEF25–75, FEF50, FEF75) are considered indirect surrogates of peripheral airflow. In paediatric populations, reduced mid-expiratory flows have been associated with more severe airflow obstruction and airway hyperresponsiveness, suggesting that when spirometric abnormalities coexist with IOS-defined SAD, disease involvement likely extends across both central and peripheral compartments.6,19

FeNO adds a crucial inflammatory dimension to this model. Beyond conventional FeNO measurements, emerging evidence suggests that alveolar nitric oxide and cytokine profiles may provide additional insights into distal airway inflammation. Increased CaNO levels have been associated with peripheral airway involvement, while inflammatory mediators including IL-4, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF might contribute to the inflammatory processes underlying small airway dysfunction. Although these biomarkers are not yet routinely incorporated into clinical assessment, they support the concept that SAD reflects both mechanical impairment and complex inflammatory activity within the distal airways. As a marker of type 2 eosinophilic airway inflammation, FeNO reflects the biological processes that may drive airway remodeling, hyperresponsiveness, and peripheral airway narrowing. FeNO adds an inflammatory dimension, but standard clinical FeNO at 50 mL/s mainly reflects bronchial Type 2 inflammation. Its association with IOS-defined SAD suggests overlap between inflammation and peripheral mechanics, while CANO provides a more specific estimate of alveolar or peripheral nitric oxide when multiple-flow testing is available. FeNO alone does not reliably discriminate stable from unstable disease, but combining inflammatory and functional information may improve risk stratification in selected children.12,24,25 Beyond IOS, plethysmography, MBNW, HRCT, and VOC analysis may offer additional insights into the functional, structural, and inflammatory aspects of small airway disease. Nevertheless, their higher costs, limited accessibility, and incomplete standardisation currently restrict their use to selected patients and specialised centers, pending further evidence supporting broader clinical implementation.29,39,40,49,52,53

A practical model is therefore stepwise. Spirometry remains the first-line test for diagnosis, bronchodilator response, and global airflow monitoring. IOS can be added when spirometry is unreliable, normal despite poor control, or discordant with symptoms. FeNO and, when available, CANO help define inflammatory phenotype, while body plethysmography may document air trapping or hyperinflation in referral settings. This framework offers a clinically actionable way to evaluate suspected SAD while preserving an important caveat: treatment escalation should be based on the integrated clinical picture, not on a single abnormal SAD marker. Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that small airway dysfunction is frequently present even in children classified as having well-controlled asthma according to conventional clinical and spirometric criteria. Because SAD has been associated with future loss of asthma control, increased exacerbation risk, and persistent airway abnormalities, its early identification may have important therapeutic implications. Recognising and addressing peripheral airway involvement before overt clinical deterioration occurs may help reduce future exacerbations and potentially limit long-term loss of lung function.

Before oscillometry can be incorporated into future asthma guidelines, several important evidence gaps need to be addressed. These include the development of universally accepted paediatric reference values and standardised diagnostic thresholds across devices, prospective studies confirming its prognostic value in diverse populations, and cost-effectiveness analyses evaluating its implementation in routine clinical practice. Most importantly, randomised clinical trials are needed to determine whether IOS-guided monitoring can improve clinically relevant outcomes by optimising treatment strategies, including the use of extra-fine inhaled corticosteroids or biologic therapies. In settings where lung function testing is unavailable, emerging clinical screening tools such as the Small Airway Dysfunction Tool (SADT) may provide a complementary approach for identifying patients who could benefit from further functional assessment.55 Finally, the practical implementation of multimodal small airway assessment is likely to vary considerably across healthcare systems. While IOS and FeNO are increasingly available in specialised centers, access remains limited in many settings. Therefore, the proposed framework should be considered adaptable according to local expertise, availability of equipment, and healthcare resources.

SAD assessment in preschool children: current opportunities and challenges

In preschool children, feasibility is central. Spirometry is often limited by cooperation, forced expiratory technique, and short expiratory time, making mid-expiratory flows especially variable in children younger than six years.15 IOS is usually more feasible because it requires quiet tidal breathing and can reveal peripheral mechanical abnormalities when spirometry is normal or technically unreliable.17,23, 24, 25, 26 In practice, IOS is most useful for children with recurrent wheeze or asthma symptoms in whom standard spirometry is unavailable, inconsistent, or discordant with clinical status. FeNO may add information on Type 2 inflammation, whereas CANO and plethysmographic lung volumes are generally reserved for centers with the required expertise and equipment. This age group should remain a priority for prospective studies testing whether multimodal assessment improves treatment decisions and outcomes. Despite these advantages, several limitations currently restrict the widespread implementation of IOS in routine clinical practice. Reference values and diagnostic cut-offs are not fully standardised across different populations and devices, and inter-device variability may affect the comparability of measurements between centers. In addition, the clinical interpretation of IOS parameters remains more complex than conventional spirometry, particularly when defining clinically relevant thresholds for small airway dysfunction. Finally, access to IOS equipment is still limited in many healthcare settings, which may restrict its routine use despite its recognised value in paediatric airway assessment.

Conclusions

SAD is a clinically relevant component of paediatric asthma, particularly when symptoms, exacerbations, or poor control occur despite preserved FEV1. The available evidence supports moving beyond a spirometry-only view of asthma assessment, but it does not yet justify treating SAD markers as stand-alone targets.

IOS offers the most practical effort-independent measure of peripheral mechanics in many children, while spirometry remains essential for global airflow assessment and bronchodilator response. Body plethysmography can add lung-volume evidence of gas trapping in selected patients, and FeNO/CANO can help characterise inflammatory phenotype. Together, these tools may improve phenotyping when used selectively and interpreted in clinical context. However, current evidence supporting the combined use of IOS and FeNO is derived predominantly from observational, cross-sectional, and relatively short-term studies, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Before routine implementation can be recommended, paediatric studies must standardise IOS and plethysmographic cut-offs, define age-appropriate reference values, and, crucially, determine whether management strategies guided by SAD assessment improve symptoms, exacerbations, lung-function trajectories, or quality of life. Until such randomised outcome data are available, oscillometry and related small-airway tools should be considered adjunctive assessments that may become part of future guideline-based evaluation rather than mandatory components of routine paediatric asthma care. Overall, the role of SAD assessment in paediatric asthma remains promising but still exploratory, requiring confirmation through larger prospective and interventional studies before firm clinical recommendations can be established.
