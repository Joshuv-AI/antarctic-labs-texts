Corrections to the Smoothness and On-Shell Approximations in Femtoscopy and Coalescence
Isaac G. Smith, Kfir Blum

Abstract
Relativistic heavy-ion collisions produce femtometer-scale sources whose space-time structure can be constrained using two-particle femtoscopic correlations. Standard implementations rely on the smoothness and on-shell approximations, which effectively remove the relative momentum dependence of the particle emission function. We explore the validity of these approximations by deriving model-independent expansions that quantify the leading corrections for femtoscopy and coalescence with arbitrary sources and final-state interactions. The resulting first- and second-order correction terms can be evaluated with essentially the same numerical complexity as the usual Koonin-Pratt expressions; for angle-averaged correlations the first-order contributions vanish by symmetry. We illustrate the framework with explicit calculations in a blast-wave source model. For parameter sets representative of pp and PbPb fits at LHC energies, the corrections are at or below the percent level for pp correlations and deuteron coalescence.



I
Introduction

Relativistic heavy-ion collisions at RHIC and LHC energies produce a high-excitation state (HXS), the properties of which are important for high-energy physics
33
;
17
;
14
and astrophysics
12
.
The asymptotic momenta of particles produced in the collision are correlated by the dynamics of the HXS and by final-state interactions (FSI).
Measuring these correlations allows for the determination of spatial and temporal properties of the HXS, via a method known as femtoscopy
22
;
19
;
35
;
11
. Additionally, comparing the correlations of different species can be used to determine the relative strengths of the FSI. This has been recently attempted for hyperon interactions which are relevant in neutron stars
5
;
6
;
2
;
7
;
34
. The source determined from femtoscopy can also be used to predict bound-state coalescence (see, e.g.,
25
;
32
;
21
;
26

13
;
11
.

Several approximations are often employed in femtoscopy and coalescence analyses. We study two of these approximations which are closely related, termed the smoothness and on-shell approximations, aiming to quantify the error induced by adopting these approximations. We derive model-independent first- and second-order corrections, that can be evaluated with the same level of complexity as the leading-order expressions (though we show that in the case of angle-averaged correlations the first-order corrections vanish due to symmetry considerations).
This provides a practical tool for assessing the validity of the approximations for any given source model.
Others have assessed these approximations’ validity
30
and derived corrections in the specific case of free bosonic correlations
16
, and a less-strict smoothness approximation has also been proposed
10
, however we present a general expansion of the approximations for femtoscopy and coalescence with arbitrary sources and FSI, which we have not found in existing references.

This work is organized as follows. In Sec.

II
Theoretical Background

Consider the number
N_{2,s}
of pairs produced with constituent four-momenta
p_{1}^{\mu}
and
p_{2}^{\mu}
in a given spin channel
s
. By mixing pairs from different events, one can also construct the number
N_{2}^{0}
of these pairs which would be produced if FSI and particle statistics were turned off (up to effects from non-femtoscopic correlations)
22
. The femtoscopic correlation function is then defined as (see, e.g.
11
)

C(p_{1}^{\mu},p_{2}^{\mu})=\frac{\sum_{s}p_{1}^{0}p_{2}^{0}\frac{dN_{2,s}}{d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{1}d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}}{p_{1}^{0}p_{2}^{0}\frac{N_{2}^{0}}{d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{1}d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}}.

(1)

We make the approximation that after some freeze-out time the particles’ dynamics are dominated by their interaction with each other (ignoring all other particles). This is called the sudden approximation. The pair can then be modeled as being produced at freeze-out via a two-particle source function
S(r^{\mu},k^{\mu};p^{\mu})
(the arguments of which will be explained below) that encompasses all interactions prior
11
.

Now, for particles with respective masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
we write

p_{1}^{\mu}=(1+\alpha)P^{\mu}+q^{\mu},\qquad p_{2}^{\mu}=(1-\alpha)P^{\mu}-q^{\mu}

(2)

where

\alpha=\frac{m_{1}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}}{4P^{2}}.

(3)

For equal masses
\alpha=0
so we get
q^{\mu}=\frac{p_{1}^{\mu}-p_{2}^{\mu}}{2}
. Note that there exist other conventions (for example in
16
) where
q^{\mu}
is defined with an extra factor of 2, i.e.
q^{\mu}=p_{1}^{\mu}-p_{2}^{\mu}
in the equal mass case.

For most of our analysis it will be convenient (and conventional) to express the kinematical variable
q^{\mu}
in the pair rest frame (PRF), where it is purely spatial:
q^{\mu}=(0,{\boldsymbol{q}})
. In contrast, the average pair momentum
P^{\mu}
is conveniently expressed in the lab frame (note that in the PRF,
P^{\mu}
is purely temporal).
The correlation function becomes
11

C(\boldsymbol{q};P^{\mu})=\sum_{s}w_{s}\frac{\int d^{4}r\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(r^{\mu},k^{\mu}\right)S(r^{\mu},k^{\mu};P^{\mu})}{\int d^{4}r\,S(r^{\mu},q^{\mu};P^{\mu})\mid_{q^{0}=0}},

(4)

where

D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(r^{\mu},k^{\mu}\right)=\int d^{4}\zeta e^{ik_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(r^{\mu}+\frac{\zeta^{\mu}}{2}\right)\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}^{*}\left(r^{\mu}-\frac{\zeta^{\mu}}{2}\right)

(5)

is the relativistic Wigner density for the scattering Bethe-Salpeter amplitude
\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(r^{\mu})
with asymptotic outgoing momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
; and
w_{s}
is the weight of the spin channel. In Eq. (
4
),
r^{\mu}
and
k^{\mu}
are the relative phase space coordinates for the pair, which we also define in the PRF. Here and in what follows, when PRF quantities (like
\boldsymbol{q}
) appear alongside lab-frame quantities (like
P^{\mu}
) as the arguments of a function (like
C(\boldsymbol{q};P^{\mu})
), we keep the lab-frame argument behind semicolon.

We now invoke the equal-time approximation (ETA)
21
, which states that the Bethe-Salpeter amplitude is approximately independent of time, and reduces to the Schrodinger wavefunction
\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(\boldsymbol{r})
. Then the relativistic Wigner density can be related to the nonrelativistic Wigner density
D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k})
as

D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(r^{\mu},k^{\mu}\right)\approx 2\pi\delta(k^{0})D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k})=2\pi\delta(k^{0})\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{\zeta}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(\boldsymbol{r}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}{2}\right)\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}{2}\right),

(6)

which lets us write the correlation function within the ETA as

C(\boldsymbol{q};P^{\mu})=\sum_{s}w_{s}\frac{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k};P^{\mu})}{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};P^{\mu})}

(7)

using the equal-time source function

S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k};P^{\mu})=\int dr^{0}S(r^{\mu},k^{\mu};P^{\mu})\mid_{k^{0}=0}.

(8)

The traditional next step is to apply two closely-related approximations
35
. Together, they amount to the source function being independent of the relative momentum. First is the on-shell approximation, which arises from a subtlety involved with the average CM momentum
P^{\mu}
, as it is in general
\boldsymbol{q}
-dependent. This also means that the definition of the PRF is dependent on
\boldsymbol{q}
. We define a momentum
p^{\mu}
which is the average CM momentum when
\boldsymbol{q}=0
, i.e.

\boldsymbol{p}\equiv\boldsymbol{P},\qquad p^{2}=\left(\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad p^{\mu}=\left(\sqrt{\left(\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\boldsymbol{P}^{2}},\boldsymbol{P}\right).

(9)

We approximate the PRF as the rest frame of
p^{\mu}
for all
\boldsymbol{q}
(equivalently, we approximate
P^{\mu}\approx p^{\mu}
). Notably,
(1\pm\alpha_{0})p^{\mu}
is on-shell with mass
m_{1}
(
+
) or
m_{2}
(
-
) where

\alpha_{0}=\frac{m_{1}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}}{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}=\frac{m_{1}-m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

(10)

is
\alpha
at
\boldsymbol{q}=0
. This means that the rest frame of
p^{\mu}
is the rest frame of physical particles with momenta
(1\pm\alpha_{0})p^{\mu}
, while the same does not hold for
(1\pm\alpha)P^{\mu}
unless
\boldsymbol{q}=0
(in which case
P^{\mu}=p^{\mu}
and
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
). Thus, our approximation of
P^{\mu}\approx p^{\mu}
is termed the on-shell approximation. For particles with different masses this approximation also involves
\alpha\approx\alpha_{0}
, while for equal masses
\alpha=\alpha_{0}=0
exactly for all
\boldsymbol{q}
. The benefit of this approximation becomes more clear when in conjunction with the next approximation, the smoothness approximation.

The smoothness approximation
9
states

S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})\approx S(\boldsymbol{r},0;p^{\mu})\equiv S(\boldsymbol{r};p^{\mu}).

(11)

If we normalize
S(\boldsymbol{r};p^{\mu})
such that it integrates to 1 over all space, noting the identity
\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q},s}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\left|\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2}
we get the Koonin-Pratt formula
20
;
28

C(\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})\approx\sum_{s}w_{s}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left|\phi_{\boldsymbol{q},s}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2}S(\boldsymbol{r};p^{\mu}).

(12)

Applying the smoothness and on-shell approximations together assures that we do not evaluate the source function at off-shell momenta, which allows for the use of source models which are only defined on-shell (e.g. from classical hydrodynamic or kinetic theory simulations). However, in certain cases the approximations create pathological behavior, such as free bosonic correlation functions dropping below unity
23
;
30
. In this work, we will investigate corrections to both of these approximations.

Before continuing onto our corrections, we also note the analogous formalism for coalescence. The coalescence factor for particles with four-momenta
p_{1}^{\mu}
and
p_{2}^{\mu}
(masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, spins
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
) coalescing into a particle with four-momentum
p_{c}^{\mu}
(mass
m_{c}
, spin
s_{c}
, with
N_{c}
particles produced) is defined as

\mathcal{B}=\frac{p_{c}^{0}\frac{dN_{c}}{d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{c}}}{p_{1}^{0}p_{2}^{0}\frac{N_{2}^{0}}{d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{1}d^{3}\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}},

(13)

where this is evaluated when
p_{c}^{\mu}
is at rest in the PRF, i.e.
P^{\mu}=p_{c}^{\mu}
.
This gives
11

\mathcal{B}=W\frac{\int d^{4}r\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,D\left(r^{\mu},k^{\mu}\right)S(r^{\mu},k^{\mu};P^{\mu})}{\int d^{4}r\,S(r^{\mu},0;P^{\mu})},

(14)

where
D(r^{\mu},k^{\mu})
is now the relativistic Wigner density for the bound state and

W=\frac{(2\pi)^{3}m_{c}(2s_{c}+1)}{m_{1}m_{2}(2s_{1}+1)(2s_{2}+1)}.

(15)

Notice that the denominator contains
S(r^{\mu},0;p^{\mu})
without invoking the smoothness approximation. In fact, both of the event-mixed particles in the denominator and the coalesced nucleus are all assumed to have the same rest frame, i.e.
\boldsymbol{q}=0
for this entire calculation and the on-shell approximation is exact (the mass of the coalesced nucleus is slightly less than the sum of the constituent masses, by the binding energy, but we neglect this here). We can therefore safely replace
P^{\mu}
with
p^{\mu}
.

In the ETA (
14
) becomes

\mathcal{B}=W\frac{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k};p^{\mu})}{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S(\boldsymbol{r},0;p^{\mu})},

(16)

and if we were to apply the smoothness approximation, we would get the analog of the Koonin-Pratt formula for coalescence:

\mathcal{B}\approx W\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left|\phi(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2}S(\boldsymbol{r};p^{\mu}).

(17)

III
The Smoothness Expansion

For the following analysis, we keep the dependence on the average CM momentum
p^{\mu}
implicit, along with the sum over spin channels. Unless noted otherwise, all calculations are performed in the (approximate, via the on-shell approximation) PRF.

III.1
Angle-Dependent Correlations

The smoothness approximation is dependent on the source function varying slowly in
\boldsymbol{q}
. Schematically, every time we take a
\boldsymbol{q}
derivative of the source function at
\boldsymbol{q}=0
we will get some length scale
\varepsilon
(for example in the blast wave model of Sec.
V.1

\varepsilon
is inverse temperature), and thus to investigate the corrections to the smoothness approximation we expand in small
q\varepsilon
:

S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=S(\boldsymbol{r})+q_{i}S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})+q_{i}q_{j}S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(18)

This is normalized such that

\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S(\boldsymbol{r})=1,

(19)

and we also define

\varepsilon^{i}=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r}),\qquad\varepsilon^{ij}=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(20)

Note that
S^{i}
and
\varepsilon^{i}
will be order
\varepsilon
, while
S^{ij}
and
\varepsilon^{ij}
will be order
\varepsilon^{2}
.

Using the expansion (
18
), we see that we can write the numerator of (
7
) as

\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k})\\
=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left[K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\right]

(21)

where we have the kernels

K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\left|\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2},

(22)

K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k_{i}D_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\frac{1}{2i}\left[\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\partial_{i}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\partial_{i}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})\right],

(23)

and

K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k_{i}k_{j}D_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)\\
=\frac{1}{4}\left[\partial_{i}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\partial_{j}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\partial_{i}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\partial_{j}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})-\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r})\right].

(24)

Including the denominator into our calculation, noting that

\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=1+q_{i}\varepsilon^{i}+q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}),

(25)

we get

C(\boldsymbol{q})=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left[K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]\\
-q_{i}\varepsilon^{i}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left[K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{j}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S^{j}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]\\
-q_{i}q_{j}(\varepsilon^{ij}-\varepsilon^{i}\varepsilon^{j})\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\,S(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(26)

This is the smoothness expansion; within the smoothness approximation we only retain

C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\boldsymbol{q})=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})S(\boldsymbol{r}).

(27)

As for the wavefunction with outgoing momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
, noting azimuthal symmetry around the axis of
\boldsymbol{q}
we have

\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\sum_{\ell}i^{\ell}(2\ell+1)R_{q,\ell}(r)P_{\ell}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}{qr}\right),

(28)

where
P_{\ell}
are the Legendre polynomials and the functions
R_{q,\ell}
are obtained by solving the Schrodinger scattering problem, subject to the boundary condition that
u_{q,\ell}(r)=rR_{q,\ell}(r)
is regular at
r\to 0
, and
u_{q,\ell}(r)\to e^{i\delta_{\ell}}\sin(qr-\ell\pi/2+\delta_{\ell})/(qr)
as
r\to\infty
(see e.g.
27
;
31
).
In practical computations, we have found the implementation in the Correlation Analysis Tool using the Schrodinger equation (CATS)
24
very useful
1
1

1

We note that the boundary condition at
r\to\infty
, recalled above in the standard form for the FSI problem, differs from the boundary condition implemented in CATS
24
, where the phase factor
e^{i\delta_{\ell}}
was removed. For angle-dependent correlations this phase can be physically relevant, and we maintain it in the definition and in calculations.
. Since the wavefunction’s directional dependence is contained entirely in
\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}
, we see that
K
,
K_{i}
, and
K_{ij}
are respectively a polar scalar, vector, and tensor. Thus we get the relations
K(-\boldsymbol{r},-\boldsymbol{q})=K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})
,
K_{i}(-\boldsymbol{r},-\boldsymbol{q})=-K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})
, and
K_{ij}(-\boldsymbol{r},-\boldsymbol{q})=K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})
.

In the case of identical particles, we must consider symmetrized and antisymmetrized wavefunctions, and sum over spin channels consistent with Pauli statistics. Specifically, for symmetrized wavefunctions only even
\ell
contribute to (
28
), and for antisymmetrized wavefunctions only odd
\ell
contribute. We also multiply overall by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
for normalization. Additionally, there are symmetry constraints on the source function for identical particles. Though wavefunction statistics are not relevant, the source function itself must be symmetric upon exchange of particles – i.e. at the level of the source function, producing identical particles with separation
\boldsymbol{r}
and relative momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
is the same as producing identical particles with separation
-\boldsymbol{r}
and relative momentum
-\boldsymbol{q}
. Because of this,
S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})
must be odd in
\boldsymbol{r}
since the overall term
q_{i}S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})
is odd in
\boldsymbol{q}
. Thus, for identical particles
\varepsilon^{i}=0
, and the only surviving first-order correction in the correlation function for identical particles is the
K_{i}S^{i}
term (second-order terms involving
\varepsilon^{i}
also vanish, of course).

III.2
Angle-Averaged Correlations

We now consider the angle-averaged correlation

C(q)=\frac{\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,D_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k}\right)S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{k};p^{\mu})}{\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})}

(29)

where
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is the solid angle in
\boldsymbol{q}
-space. This is often the reported quantity in femtoscopic studies, see e.g.
4
;
5
;
6
;
2
. To study this, we look at the angle-averaging of terms appearing in the smoothness expansion. We will use the notation that functions which are denoted as a variation of
\bar{K}
depend only on
r
and
q
(the magnitudes of the PRF three-vectors
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
respectively), and functions which are denoted as a variation of
\bar{S}
depend only on
r
.

First, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=\bar{K}_{0,0},

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,q_{i}K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=q\bar{K}_{0,1}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i},

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,q_{i}q_{j}K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{q^{2}}{3}\bar{K}_{0,0}\delta_{ij}+q^{2}\bar{K}_{0,2}Q_{ij},

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,q_{i}K_{j}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=q\bar{K}_{1,0}\delta_{ij}+q\bar{K}_{1,2}Q_{ij},

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=\bar{K}_{2,0}\delta_{ij}+\bar{K}_{2,2}Q_{ij}.

(34)

Here,
Q_{ij}=3\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j}-\delta_{ij}
is the quadrupole tensor. The first index on
\bar{K}
indicates which order of the expansion the kernel is from, and the second index denotes which degree moment it is attached to. The form of each of these integrals can be determined from symmetry considerations, and the coefficient on
\delta_{ij}
in (
32
) can be determined from the spherical harmonic expansion of
K
. We note that while the LHS of (
33
) is not manifestly symmetric in the tensor indices, the only available non-symmetric term is of the form
\epsilon_{ijk}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{k}
which is forbidden by evenness in
\boldsymbol{r}
(specifically,
q_{i}K_{j}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})
must be even when
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q}\to-\boldsymbol{r},-\boldsymbol{q}
, so its angle average must be even in
\boldsymbol{r}
). Finally, for a real spherically-symmetric FSI potential,

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=0.

(35)

This can be seen as follows. For a spherically-symmetric FSI potential,
V=V(r)
, symmetry considerations force the integral (
35
) to be proportional to
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}
. But
K_{i}
is the probability current, which is divergenceless (assuming a real FSI potential), so the angle-averaged object (
35
) is also divergenceless; this restricts the result to
\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})=A\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}/r^{2}
, with constant
A
. But since the wavefunction is regular at the origin,
A
must be zero.

Once the integrals above are performed, we define the following:

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,S(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{0,0}

(36)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\delta_{ij}S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{2,0}

(37)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,Q_{ij}S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{2,2}

(38)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,Q_{ij}(\varepsilon^{ij}-\varepsilon^{i}\varepsilon^{j})S(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{0,2}

(39)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\varepsilon^{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}S(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{0,1}

(40)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,Q_{ij}\varepsilon^{i}S^{j}(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{1,2}

(41)

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\delta_{ij}\varepsilon^{i}S^{j}(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{S}_{1,0}.

(42)

Using all of this, we arrive at the smoothness expansion for
C(q)
:

C(q)=\int dr\,r^{2}\left[\left(1-\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)\right)\bar{K}_{0,0}\bar{S}_{0,0}+\bar{K}_{2,0}\bar{S}_{2,0}+\bar{K}_{2,2}\bar{S}_{2,2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})

(43)

where
\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)=\delta_{ij}\varepsilon^{ij}
. We see here that the corrections to the correlation function begin at second-order.

We can compare
C(q)
to the isotropic angle average of
C(\boldsymbol{q})
, which has the smoothness expansion

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}C(\boldsymbol{q})=\int dr\,r^{2}\biggl[\left(1-\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)+\frac{q^{2}}{3}|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)\bar{K}_{0,0}\bar{S}_{0,0}+\bar{K}_{2,0}\bar{S}_{2,0}+\bar{K}_{2,2}\bar{S}_{2,2}\\
-q\bar{K}_{0,1}\bar{S}_{0,1}-q\bar{K}_{1,0}\bar{S}_{1,0}-q\bar{K}_{1,2}\bar{S}_{1,2}-q^{2}\bar{K}_{0,2}\bar{S}_{0,2}\biggr]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(44)

where
|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\delta_{ij}\varepsilon^{i}\varepsilon^{j}
, and we have the anisotropic term

C_{\mathrm{aniso}}(q)=C(q)-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}C(\boldsymbol{q})\\
=\int dr\,r^{2}\left[-\frac{q^{2}}{3}|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bar{K}_{0,0}\bar{S}_{0,0}+q\bar{K}_{0,1}\bar{S}_{0,1}+q\bar{K}_{1,0}\bar{S}_{1,0}+q\bar{K}_{1,2}\bar{S}_{1,2}+q^{2}\bar{K}_{0,2}\bar{S}_{0,2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(45)

Eq. (
45
) is the difference between two observable quantities. This difference vanishes in the smoothness approximation, so it provides a method to assess the size of a subset of smoothness corrections in a model-independent way. These anisotropic corrections generally begin at first-order in the smoothness expansion; however for identical particles
\varepsilon^{i}=0
, and thus the first order corrections vanish also here. In fact, the only surviving term of
C_{\mathrm{aniso}}(q)
for identical particles is the
\bar{K}_{0,2}\bar{S}_{0,2}
term.

III.3
Free Particles

The main application we have in mind for the analysis in this paper concerns realistic FSI; however, we first show the smoothness expansion in the context of free particles, in order to demonstrate the significance of each group of terms in the expansion.

III.3.1
Distinguishable Particles

Free distinguishable particles should be uncorrelated, i.e. their correlation function should be identically 1 for all relative momenta. It is good to check that this holds to each order in the smoothness expansion; we have

\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})=e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}},

(46)

and thus

K=1,\qquad K_{i}=q_{i},\qquad K_{ij}=q_{i}q_{j}.

(47)

We then have the correlation function

C(\boldsymbol{q})=1+q_{i}\varepsilon^{i}+q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}-q_{i}\varepsilon^{i}-q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}=1=C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\boldsymbol{q}).

(48)

As expected, the contributions from the numerator and denominator of the correlation function perfectly cancel. The case with angle averaging is similar, and we get

C(q)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}C(\boldsymbol{q})=C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(q)=1

(49)

as required.

III.3.2
Identical Particles

The situation is more interesting for identical particles. The respective symmetrized (
+
) and antisymmetrized (
-
) non-interacting wavefunctions are

\phi^{\pm}_{\boldsymbol{q},\mathrm{free}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\pm e^{-i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\right),

(50)

which lead to the kernels
K=1\pm\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})
,
K_{i}=0
, and
K_{ij}=q_{i}q_{j}
. Additionally, we will always sum over spin channels. For identical particles with spin
s
, symmetric spin channels will have total weight
\frac{s+1}{2s+1}
and antisymmetric spin channels will have total weight
\frac{s}{2s+1}
. Thus, enforcing the appropriate symmetrization conditions for bosons (
+
) and fermions (
-
), the overall kernels for free identical particles will be

K=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}),\qquad K_{i}=0,\qquad K_{ij}=q_{i}q_{j}.

(51)

We then have

C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\boldsymbol{q})=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})S(\boldsymbol{r})

(52)

and the corrected correlation function

C(\boldsymbol{q})=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})S(\boldsymbol{r})+q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}\\
-q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})S(\boldsymbol{r})\right)+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\\
=1\pm\frac{1-q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}}{2s+1}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})S(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\\
=C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\boldsymbol{q})+q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}(1-C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\boldsymbol{q}))+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(53)

Thus, smoothness corrections do occur for non-interacting identical particles.

With angle averaging, we note the integrals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})=j_{0}(2qr),

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}q_{i}q_{j}\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{q^{2}}{3}j_{0}(2qr)\delta_{ij}-\frac{q^{2}}{3}j_{2}(2qr)Q_{ij},

(55)

where
j_{n}
are the spherical Bessel functions, so

C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(q)=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int dr\,r^{2}\,j_{0}(2qr)\bar{S}_{0,0}

(56)

and

C(q)=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int dr\,r^{2}\,j_{0}(2qr)\bar{S}_{0,0}+\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)\\
-\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int dr\,r^{2}\,j_{0}(2qr)\bar{S}_{0,0}\right)+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\\
=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\left(1-\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)\right)\int dr\,r^{2}\,j_{0}(2qr)\bar{S}_{0,0}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\\
=C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(q)+\frac{q^{2}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)(1-C_{\mathrm{smooth}}(q))+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(57)

Finally, the anisotropic correction term is also nonzero for free identical particles:

C_{\mathrm{aniso}}(q)=\mp\frac{q^{2}}{3(2s+1)}\int dr\,r^{2}\,j_{2}(2qr)\bar{S}_{0,2}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3}).

(58)

III.4
Coalescence

Recalling the form of the coalescence factor in (
16
), the smoothness expansion for coalescence only has the numerator contributions,

\mathcal{B}=W\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left[K(\boldsymbol{r})\,S(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{i}(\boldsymbol{r})\,S^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})+K_{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})\,S^{ij}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\right].

(59)

We use a Gaussian wavefunction

\phi(\boldsymbol{r})=\left(\frac{1}{\pi b^{2}}\right)^{3/4}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2b^{2}}}

(60)

to investigate qualitative behavior. The kernels are then conveniently

K=|\phi|^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{\pi b^{2}}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{b^{2}}},\qquad K_{i}=0,\qquad K_{ij}=\frac{|\phi|^{2}}{2b^{2}}\delta_{ij}=\frac{1}{2b^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\pi b^{2}}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{b^{2}}}\delta_{ij}.

(61)

Since we have chosen our wavefunction to be rotationally symmetric,
|\phi|^{2}
can be pulled out of the
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}
integral, and we get

\mathcal{B}=W\int dr\,r^{2}\,|\phi|^{2}\left(\bar{S}_{0,0}+\frac{1}{2b^{2}}\bar{S}_{2,0}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{3})\right).

(62)

The smoothness corrections are greater for a narrower wave function. For wavefunctions with nontrivial angular dependence other
\bar{S}
terms can contribute, however for a central interaction the ground state will have spherical symmetry and the smoothness correction only involves
\bar{S}_{2,0}
, though the kernel
\bar{K}_{2,0}
will not in general be proportional to
|\phi|^{2}
as it is in the Gaussian case.

IV
The On-Shell Expansion

We now look at the corrections to the on-shell approximation. These corrections are purely kinematical; additionally, recalling that the on-shell approximation is exact for coalescence, we need only look at corrections to the correlation function. For the kinematic calculations in the first part of this section, primed quantities are in the PRF frame, and unprimed quantities are in the lab frame; additionally, unbolded momenta will refer to four-vectors as opposed to the magnitude of a three-vector.

Recall the kinematic definitions (
2
) and (
9
), which introduce the true average CM momentum
P^{\mu}=\frac{p_{1}^{\mu}+p_{2}^{\mu}}{2}\equiv(P^{0},\boldsymbol{p})
and the on-shell average pseudo-CM momentum
p^{\mu}=(p^{0},\boldsymbol{p})
with
(p^{0})^{2}=\left(\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}^{2}
. Some algebra gives

P^{0}=p^{0}+\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2p^{0}}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}/m^{2})

(63)

where

\kappa=\frac{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}{4m_{1}m_{2}}.

(64)

In the equal-mass case,
\kappa=1
. Note that here we bold
\boldsymbol{q}^{2}
to avoid confusion with
q^{\mu}q_{\mu}=-\boldsymbol{q}^{2}
.

In the on-shell approximation, we analyze in a pseudo-PRF (we denote this frame with a double prime) such that
p^{\prime\prime\mu}=(m,\boldsymbol{0})
, while without this approximation we would be working in the true PRF where
P^{\prime\mu}=\left(\sqrt{(P^{0})^{2}-\boldsymbol{p}^{2}},\boldsymbol{0}\right)
. The transformation from the pseudo-PRF to the true PRF is a boost with
\beta_{i}=-\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2(p^{0})^{3}}p_{i}
which is of order
q^{2}
, and thus the Lorentz factor is unity up until order
q^{4}
. So, the Lorentz transformation from the pseudo-PRF to the true PRF is

v^{\prime\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2(p^{0})^{3}}\boldsymbol{p}\\
\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2(p^{0})^{3}}\boldsymbol{p}&I\\
\end{pmatrix}v^{\prime\prime\mu}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}/m^{3}).

(65)

A corollary of this is that
\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime\prime}=\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}
(and in the case of
\boldsymbol{k}
in the Wigner function integral
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime\prime}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
) up to third order in relative momentum. This means that to correct the on-shell approximation to second order (leading order) we need only replace
p^{\mu}
with
P^{\mu}
, replace
\alpha_{0}
with
\alpha
(recall the definitions (
3
) and (
10
)), and consider the transformation of
r^{\mu}
in the source function (remembering that this approximation is made only in the calculation of the source function – the Wigner function is already by assumption expressed in the true PRF). For replacing
\alpha_{0}
with
\alpha
, we have

\alpha=\alpha_{0}-\frac{\alpha_{0}\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}/m^{3}).

(66)

This will create corrections to the source function as the two-particle source will be sensitive to
\alpha
, which describes how momentum is divided between the particles. This is irrelevant for equal mass particles, where
\alpha=0
for all
q
.

Considering the above, we also have that the first on-shell corrections will be of order
q^{2}
; specifically, some will be order
q^{2}/m^{2}
, while part of the correction for replacing
P^{\mu}
with
p^{\mu}
will be order
q^{2}\varepsilon/m
. This also means that to order
q^{2}
all of the on-shell corrections are corrections to the smooth correlation function, as all combinations between on-shell and smoothness corrections will be of order
q^{3}
or higher. Now,

S(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime};P^{\mu})=\int dr^{\prime 0}S(r^{\prime\mu},q^{\prime\mu};P^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime 0}=0}\\
=\int dr^{\prime\prime 0}S(r^{\prime\prime\mu},q^{\prime\prime\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime\prime 0}=0}+\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2(p^{0})^{3}}(\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime})\int dr^{\prime\prime 0}\partial_{r^{\prime\prime 0}}S(r^{\prime\prime\mu},q^{\prime\prime\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime\prime 0}=0}\\
+\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2(p^{0})^{3}}p^{i}\int dr^{\prime\prime 0}r^{\prime\prime 0}\partial_{r^{\prime\prime i}}S(r^{\prime\prime\mu},q^{\prime\prime\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime\prime 0}=0}+\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{2p^{0}}\int dr^{\prime\prime 0}\partial_{p^{0}}S(r^{\prime\prime\mu},q^{\prime\prime\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime\prime 0}=0}\\
-\frac{\alpha_{0}\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}\int dr^{\prime\prime 0}\partial_{\alpha}S(r^{\prime\prime\mu},q^{\prime\prime\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{\prime\prime 0}=0}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)\\
\equiv S(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime\prime};p^{\mu})+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime\prime};p^{\mu})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m).

(67)

Note that the first correction term is zero since it is a boundary term, and as mentioned the
\alpha_{0}
term will be zero for particles of equal masses. Since we are not concerned with combining smoothness and on-shell corrections, what will be useful to us is

S(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},0;P^{\mu})=S(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime},0;p^{\mu})+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}\cdot S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime},0;p^{\mu})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)\\
\equiv S(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime};p^{\mu})+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime\prime};p^{\mu})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m).

(68)

We are now done working with frame transformations, so we return to working entirely in the pseudo-PRF and suppressing the
p^{\mu}
argument of
S
and
S^{\prime}
(
p^{0}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
will of course still refer to the components of
p^{\mu}
in the lab frame). We will also return to using
q^{2}=\boldsymbol{q}^{2}
as before. We ignore smoothness corrections as they do not affect this part of the calculation. The correlation function is

C(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})\left(S(\boldsymbol{r})+q^{2}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)}{\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\left(S(\boldsymbol{r})+q^{2}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)\\
=\left(1-q^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})S(\boldsymbol{r})+q^{2}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)\\
=\left(1-q^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)C_{\mathrm{on-shell}}(\boldsymbol{q})+q^{2}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,K(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m),

(69)

where

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(70)

Note that only the
\partial_{p^{0}}
and
\partial_{\alpha}
terms of (
67
) contribute to
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, as the
\partial_{r^{\prime\prime i}}
term integrates to vanishing boundary terms in the direction of
\boldsymbol{p}
.

The angle-averaged correlation is simple,

C(q)=\int dr\,r^{2}\,\bar{K}_{0,0}\left[(1-q^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{S}_{0,0}+q^{2}\bar{S}^{\prime}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)

(71)

where

\bar{S}^{\prime}=\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(72)

The on-shell corrections only have
q
-dependence through
q^{2}
, so they are isotropic in
\boldsymbol{q}
and generate no contribution to
C(q)-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}C(\boldsymbol{q})
.

For free distinguishable particles, analogous calculations to those done in Sec.
III.3
show that all corrections vanish as we would expect. For identical particles, we get

C(\boldsymbol{q})=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\cos(2\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})\left[(1-q^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime})S(\boldsymbol{r})+q^{2}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m)

(73)

and

C(q)=1\pm\frac{1}{2s+1}\int dr\,r^{2}j_{0}(2qr)\left[(1-q^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{S}_{0,0}+q^{2}\bar{S}^{\prime}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/m).

(74)

V
Explicit Calculations in the Blast Wave Model

We show example calculations of the smoothness corrections using the blast wave model for the source function, considering angle-averaged pp correlations with realistic FSI, and deuteron coalescence with a Gaussian deuteron wave function.

V.1
The Blast Wave Model

The blast wave model
32
is a phenomenological relativistic hydrodynamic model for a single-particle source function
S_{1}(x^{\mu},p^{\mu})
, corresponding to the production of a particle at spacetime coordinate
x^{\mu}
and four-momentum
p^{\mu}
. Since it is a single-particle source function, for the pair source we assume that the two particles are produced independently of each other (this is equivalent to density matrix factorization). Then we have, in the case with two different particles
a
and
b
,

S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})=\mathcal{N}\int dr^{0}S(r^{\mu},q^{\mu};p^{\mu})\mid_{q^{0}=0}\\
=\mathcal{N}\int dr^{0}\int d^{4}xS_{1,a}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},(1+\alpha)p^{\mu}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)S_{1,b}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},(1-\alpha)p^{\mu}-\boldsymbol{q}\right),

(75)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant to satisfy the condition (
19
):

\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}=\int d^{4}r\int d^{4}xS_{1,a}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},(1+\alpha)p^{\mu}\right)S_{1,b}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},(1-\alpha)p^{\mu}\right).

(76)

We define the single-particle source function as a function of the lab-frame coordinates, which must be transformed to the PRF for use in (
75
). Letting
z
be the axis of the beam, we use coordinates
\tau=\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}}
,
\rho=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
,
\eta=\mathrm{arctanh}(z/t)
, and the azimuthal angle
\varphi
. Then
32
;
11

\displaystyle S_{1}^{\mathrm{lab}}(x^{\mu},p^{\mu})

\displaystyle=\left(p^{0}\cosh(\eta)-p^{z}\sinh(\eta)\right)J(\tau)\Theta(R_{0}-\rho)e^{-p^{\mu}u_{\mu}/T},

(77)

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(x^{\mu})

\displaystyle=(\cosh\eta\cosh\eta_{t},\,\sinh\eta_{t}\cos\varphi,\,\sinh\eta_{t}\sin\varphi,\,\sinh\eta\cosh\eta_{t}),

(78)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\frac{\rho^{n}}{R_{0}^{n}}\beta_{S}\right),

(79)

\displaystyle J(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Delta\tau}e^{-\frac{(\tau-\tau_{0})^{2}}{2\,\Delta\tau^{2}}},

(80)

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function. This model has 6 parameters:
\tau_{0}
,
\Delta\tau
,
R_{0}
,
\beta_{S}
,
n
, and the temperature
T
. The only difference in the source function for different particle species is the mass which appears in the momentum
p^{\mu}
and the
\alpha
factor. Note that we write the Cooper-Frye (CF) factor
18
here as
p^{0}\cosh(\eta)-p^{z}\sinh(\eta)
which can be applied off-shell
15
. On-shell, where
p^{\mu}
is parameterized as
p^{\mu}=(m_{t}\cosh Y,p_{t}\cos\Phi,p_{t}\sinh\Phi,m_{t}\sinh Y)
with
m_{t}=\sqrt{m^{2}+p_{t}^{2}}
, this factor is more commonly seen as
\frac{m_{t}}{m}\cosh(\eta-Y)
.

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to identical particles.
We choose a coordinate system such that
\Phi=0
(which we can do WLOG) and specialize to midrapidity (
Y=0
), giving us
p^{\mu}=(m_{t},p_{t},0,0)
. We refer to App.
B
for details on the smoothness and on-shell corrections for this model.

The size of the source
R_{0}\approx\tau_{0}
is a primary factor for the magnitude of both of the correlation function itself, as well as the relative corrections to it due to the smoothness expansion. For smaller sources, the correlation function and its relative corrections become larger. Specifically, the corrections become large when the size of the source becomes comparable to the characteristic length of the interaction. This scaling is analyzed further at the level of the source function in App.
B
.
We also found that the smoothness corrections were larger at smaller transverse momenta.

For small sources, a smaller

Overall, we found the smoothness corrections to be dominant compared to the on-shell corrections, and specifically the thermal factor was the main contributor to the smoothness corrections. The on-shell corrections and the CF smoothness corrections were smaller; this can be explained by the former correction being of the form
q^{2}/T^{2}
while the latter are of the forms
q^{2}/(mT)
or
q^{2}/m^{2}
.
For lower-

Finally, we found the anisotropic corrections (
45

VI
Discussion

We have derived corrections to the smoothness and on-shell approximations for both correlation and coalescence calculations. These corrections can be used to test the validity of the smoothness approximation for a given source model and FSI. Additionally, we have presented a combination of observables (
45
) which can be used to empirically test for the presence of a particular subset of smoothness corrections.

In the specific case of the blast wave model with parameters relevant for high-energy pp collisions, the corrections to the angle-averaged correlation can reach about 0.5% (see green curve in the right panels of Fig.
1
), comparable to the nominal experimental and modeling uncertainty for proton-proton correlations reported in
4
(Fig.1(a)
there
). For parameters relevant to PbPb collisions, the corrections are smaller.
Other, potentially more realistic source models can predict similar correlation functions
29
, but could in principle exhibit different smoothness and on-shell corrections which can be tested using our method.

We showed that
in the case of angle-averaged correlations, the leading smoothness corrections are at order
q^{2}
, but for angle-dependent correlations one must also consider order
q
terms. For distinguishable particles, these first-order terms appear in the anisotropic term (
45
); this is one of several terms which are present in the smoothness expansion for distinguishable particles but not for identical particles, and could be significant for, e.g., p
\Lambda
correlations.

Finally, in our calculations we have used the equal-time approximation, which must also be verified in a given model/interaction in order to safely use the Koonin-Pratt formalism. We hope to investigate this approximation in future work.

Appendix A
Technical Details on Calculating Averaged Kernels

We have three objects of the form

I_{ij}=A\delta_{ij}+BQ_{ij}.

(81)

Since there are only two independent terms, it is sufficient to calculate just two elements of
I_{ij}
. Using a cartesian coordinate system where
\boldsymbol{r}
is in the
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
direction, we have that

I_{zz}=A+2B,\qquad I_{xx}=A-B

(82)

so

A=\frac{1}{3}\left(I_{zz}+2I_{xx}\right),\qquad B=\frac{1}{3}\left(I_{zz}-I_{xx}\right).

(83)

We consider a wavefunction
\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}
which is azimuthally symmetric (in the case of a central interaction, one can choose an axis such that this is the case). Let
\theta
be the angle between
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, let
\varphi
be the azimuthal angle, and let
\mu=\cos(\theta)
. Then we have (dropping the subscript for simplicity):

\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}\supset q^{2}\mu^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}+q^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},

(84)

\boldsymbol{q}\nabla\phi\supset q\mu\partial_{r}\phi\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}+q(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\frac{\partial_{\mu}\phi}{r}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},

(85)

\nabla\phi^{*}\nabla\phi\supset|\partial_{r}\phi|^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}+(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\frac{|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}}{r^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},

(86)

and

\nabla\nabla\phi\supset\partial_{r}^{2}\phi\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}+\frac{(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\partial_{\mu}^{2}\phi+r\partial_{r}\phi-\mu\partial_{\mu}\phi}{r^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(87)

Then,

q_{z}q_{z}K=q^{2}\mu^{2}|\phi|^{2},\qquad q_{x}q_{x}K=q^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)|\phi|^{2},

(88)

q_{z}K_{z}=\Im\left[q\mu\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right],\qquad q_{x}K_{x}=\Im\left[q(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\frac{\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}\phi}{r}\right],

(89)

and

K_{zz}=\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{r}\phi|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Re\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{r}^{2}\phi\right],\\
K_{xx}=\frac{(1-\mu^{2})}{2r^{2}}\cos^{2}(\varphi)|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}-\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\Re\left[(1-\mu^{2})\cos^{2}(\varphi)\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}^{2}\phi+r\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi-\phi^{*}\mu\partial_{\mu}\phi\right].

(90)

Integrating over
\varphi
is simple, noting that the
\varphi
-average of
\cos^{2}(\varphi)
is
\frac{1}{2}
, and we arrive at the expressions:

\bar{K}_{0,0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,|\phi|^{2},

(91)

\bar{K}_{0,2}=\frac{1}{12}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,(3\mu^{2}-1)|\phi|^{2}=\frac{1}{6}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,P_{2}(\mu)|\phi|^{2},

(92)

\bar{K}_{1,0}=\frac{1}{6}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\left(\mu\Im\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right]+\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{r}\Im\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}\phi\right]\right),

(93)

\bar{K}_{1,2}=\frac{1}{6}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\left(\mu\Im\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right]-\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{2r}\Im\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}\phi\right]\right),

(94)

\bar{K}_{2,0}=\frac{1}{12}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\Bigg(|\partial_{r}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{r}^{2}\phi\right]+\frac{2}{r^{2}}\Big((1-\mu^{2})|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[r\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right]\Big)\Bigg),

(95)

and

\bar{K}_{2,2}=\frac{1}{12}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\Bigg(|\partial_{r}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[\phi^{*}\partial_{r}^{2}\phi\right]-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big((1-\mu^{2})|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[r\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right]\Big)\Bigg).

(96)

For
\bar{K}_{2,0}
and
\bar{K}_{2,2}
we used integration by parts to note that

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}K_{xx}=\frac{1}{4r^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\left(\frac{(1-\mu^{2})}{2}|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[\frac{(1-\mu^{2})}{2}\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}^{2}\phi+r\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi-\mu\phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}\phi\right]\right)\\
=\frac{1}{4r^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\Big((1-\mu^{2})|\partial_{\mu}\phi|^{2}-\Re\left[r\phi^{*}\partial_{r}\phi\right]\Big).

(97)

Finally, our one remaining kernel is simply

\bar{K}_{0,1}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{q_{z}}{q}K=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,\mu|\phi|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,P_{1}(\mu)|\phi|^{2}.

(98)

Appendix B
Smoothness and On-Shell Corrections to the Blast Wave Model

B.1
Smoothness Corrections

Recall that we take
p^{\mu}=(m_{t},p_{t},0,0)
. Using the usual out-side-long terminology, we write
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{o},q_{s},q_{l})
in the PRF, which gives
q^{\mu,\mathrm{lab}}=\left(\frac{p_{t}}{m}q_{o},\frac{m_{t}}{m}q_{o},q_{s},q_{l}\right)
. Additionally, we will denote the lab frame
\tau
,
\eta
,
\rho
, and
\varphi
coordinates of
x^{\mu}\pm\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}
as
\tau^{\pm}
,
\eta^{\pm}
,
\rho^{\pm}
, and
\varphi^{\pm}
, respectively. Then, we isolate the
\boldsymbol{q}
dependence of (
75
) as follows:

S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})=\mathcal{N}\int dr^{0}\int d^{4}xS_{1}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},p^{\mu}\right)S_{1}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2},p^{\mu}\right)A_{CF}(x^{\mu},r^{\mu},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})A_{T}(x^{\mu},r^{\mu},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})

(99)

where

A^{\mathrm{lab}}_{CF}(x^{\mu},r^{\mu},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})\\
=\frac{\left[\left(m_{t}+\frac{p_{t}q_{o}}{m}\right)\cosh(\eta^{+})-q_{l}\sinh(\eta^{+})\right]\left[\left(m_{t}-\frac{p_{t}q_{o}}{m}\right)\cosh(\eta^{-})+q_{l}\sinh(\eta^{-})\right]}{m_{t}^{2}\cosh(\eta^{+})\cosh(\eta^{-})}\\
=1-\frac{q_{l}}{m_{t}}\left[\tanh(\eta^{+})-\tanh(\eta^{-})\right]-\left(\frac{p_{t}q_{o}}{mm_{t}}\right)^{2}\\
-\frac{q_{l}^{2}}{m_{t}^{2}}\tanh(\eta^{+})\tanh(\eta^{-})+\frac{p_{t}q_{o}q_{l}}{mm_{t}^{2}}\left[\tanh(\eta^{+})+\tanh(\eta^{-})\right]

(100)

and

A^{\mathrm{lab}}_{T}(x^{\mu},r^{\mu},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})=e^{-\frac{1}{T}q^{\mathrm{lab}}_{\mu}\left[u^{\mu}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)-u^{\mu}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)\right]}\\
=1+\frac{1}{T}q_{i}\left[u^{\prime i}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)-u^{\prime i}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)\right]\\
+\frac{1}{2T^{2}}q_{i}q_{j}\left[u^{\prime i}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)-u^{\prime i}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)\right]\left[u^{\prime j}\left(x^{\mu}+\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)-u^{\prime j}\left(x^{\mu}-\frac{r^{\mu}}{2}\right)\right]\\
+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}/T^{3})

(101)

with
u^{\prime\mu}
being
u^{\mu}
where the components are transformed to the PRF (but the arguments remain in the lab frame).

We note that both
A
functions have no dependence on
\tau^{\pm}
. Additionally, all of the
\rho
dependence in the source function and
A
can be written as only a function of
\rho/R_{0}
. When we integrate
S(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q};p^{\mu})
(which gives us
1+q_{i}\varepsilon^{i}+q_{i}q_{j}\varepsilon^{ij}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}/T^{3})
), we can do a change of variables such that we integrate
d^{4}x^{+}d^{4}x^{-}
in the
(\tau^{\pm},\eta^{\pm},\rho^{\pm},\varphi^{\pm})
coordinates. Then the
d\tau^{\pm}
integrals will factor out and cancel with
\mathcal{N}
, and a change of variables to
R^{\pm}=\rho^{\pm}/R_{0}
will remove all
R_{0}
dependence. This shows that the total size of the smoothness corrections as far as the source function is concerned (i.e.
\varepsilon^{i}
and
\varepsilon^{ij}
) is independent of the parameters
R_{0}
,
\tau_{0}
, and
\Delta\tau
. Those parameters solely affect the shape of the corrections
S^{i}
and
S^{ij}
. This is important to keep in mind as we see the large effect of these parameters on the size of the smoothness corrections to the correlation function.

Since we are working with identical particles,
\kappa=1

\varepsilon^{\prime}
