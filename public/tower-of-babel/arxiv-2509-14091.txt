Abstract
Classic reachability games on graphs are zero-sum games, where the goal of one player, 𝖤𝗏𝖾 {\mathsf{Eve}} , is to visit a vertex from a given target set, and that of other player, 𝖠𝖽𝖺𝗆 {\mathsf{Adam}} , is to prevent this. Generalised reachability games, studied by Fijalkow and Horn, are a generalisation of reachability objectives, where instead of a single target set, there is a family of target sets and 𝖤𝗏𝖾 {\mathsf{Eve}} must visit all of them in any order. In this work, we further study the complexity of solving two-player games on graphs with generalised reachability objectives. Our results are twofold: first, we provide an improved complexity picture for generalised reachability games, expanding the known tractable class from games in which all target sets are singleton to additionally allowing a logarithmic number of target sets of arbitrary size. Second, we study optimisation variants of generalised reachability with a focus on the size of the target sets. For these problems, we show intractability for most interesting cases. Particularly, in contrast to the tractability in the classic variant for singleton target sets, the optimisation problem is NP -hard when 𝖤𝗏𝖾 {\mathsf{Eve}} tries to maximise the number of singleton target sets that are visited. Tractability can be recovered in the optimisation setting when all target sets are singleton by requiring that 𝖤𝗏𝖾 {\mathsf{Eve}} pledges a maximum sized subset of target sets that she can guarantee to visit.

Introduction

Two-player zero-sum games played on graphs provide a fundamental framework for modelling decision-making scenarios where two players have opposing objectives. They are extensively used to model reactive systems, where one player (
{\mathsf{Eve}}
) models actions controlled by the system and the other player (
{\mathsf{Adam}}
) models uncontrollable actions of the environment. Analysing such games then provides formal guarantees on the behaviours of the system against all possible behaviours of the environment
[
7
,
17
,
16
,
3
]
.

These games are played on finite graphs, where the vertices are partitioned into ones controlled by
{\mathsf{Eve}}
and
{\mathsf{Adam}}
. Starting from a token placed on a fixed initial vertex, the player controlling the current vertex moves the token along an edge of the graph to jointly form an infinite path. A winning objective specifies the set of acceptable behaviours of the system as a set of infinite paths that are good for
{\mathsf{Eve}}
. In the game,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
attempts to ensure that the path formed is good for
{\mathsf{Eve}}
, while
{\mathsf{Adam}}
tries to obstruct this goal.
Solving games refers to the decision problem of checking which of the two player can win from a given initial vertex.

A key type of objective studied in such settings is
reachability
, where the goal of
{\mathsf{Eve}}
is to reach some vertex among a designated target set of vertices.
Generalised reachability games extend this concept by requiring
{\mathsf{Eve}}
to reach multiple target sets rather than just one. Then,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
is required to visit at least one vertex from each target set in the play.
However, in terms of computational complexity of solving such games, changing from reachability to generalised reachability results in a significant jump. While reachability games are P-complete
[
19
,
12
]
, generalised reachability games are
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete
[
8
]
.
Understanding the complexity of solving special cases of generalised reachability games is crucial, especially when considering the size of target sets as a parameter. For instance, if all target sets consist of a single vertex,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
is required to visit several vertices in the play. Such games can be solved in polynomial time
[
8
]
.

The analysis of generalised winning conditions, i.e., the conjunction of multiple objectives of the same kind has been a significant focus of research
[
6
,
4
,
5
]
.
While generalised reachability games have been well studied, one could also consider the optimisation variant of the problem. This asks
{\mathsf{Eve}}

to visit as many target sets as possible.
This variant can be used to provide strategies where visiting all target sets may not be possible, but
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can still visit a significant number of target sets.
Optimisation variants have been studied beyond generalised reachability objectives
[
15
]
.
In this work, we consider both the case where
{\mathsf{Eve}}
has to name the target sets she visits before the game starts (and visiting other sets does not count)
and
the case where she just wants to maximise the number of sets visited.

Our Contributions.

In this paper, we provide several new complexity results for generalised reachability games:

•

We prove that generalised reachability can be checked in time linear in (1) the size of the game, (2) the number of singleton target sets, and (3) exponential in the number of larger target sets.
It is therefore fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) when considering the number of target sets larger than one as the parameter and polynomial if the number of large target sets is logarithmically bounded.

•

We establish
\mathsf{NL}
-completeness for the single-player case, where only the environment makes decisions.

•

We analyse optimisation variants of the problem, showing that
maximising
the number of singleton target sets (or: target states) visited depends on whether we want to first name the visited target states or to just maximise the number of visited states; they are tractable and NP-complete, respectively.

By investigating these computational aspects, we provide a deeper understanding of the complexity landscape of generalised reachability games and contribute to the broader field of game theory and formal verification.

Related Works.

The starting point of this work is the study of
generalised reachability games
by Fijalkow and Horn
[
8
]
.
Their work revealed the surprising complexity of these games: while reachability games are
P
-complete
[
19
,
12
]
, even modest extensions to standard reachability objectives can lead to significant computational challenges.
This study has influenced the analysis of multi-objective games and strategy synthesis under complex constraints.
This work also provides certain restrictions which make the problem easier, particularly by considering one-player variants, and by parametrising the problem by size of each target set.
They also exploit connections with the true quantified satisfiability (QSAT) problem, one of the standard
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete problems, to provide lower bounds, both in the general case and in some restricted cases.
An open problem stated in their work is the complexity of the problem when target sets have size
2
.

Games with weighted multiple objectives have been studied by Kupferman and Shenwald
[
15
]
, showing how adding different goals and weights makes these games more complex and useful for modelling real systems.
In particular, their results can be used to provide strategies that maximise the number of objectives satisfied, even if not all of the objectives can be jointly satisfied. This yields a
\mathsf{PSPACE}
upper bound for some of the optimisation variants of generalised reachability games we consider. However, they do not study the problem by considering the size of target sets as a parameter.

In the realm of satisfiability, El Halaby
[
10
]
investigated the
computational complexity of MaxSAT
, an optimisation variant of the satisfiability problem.
Prior to this, Kohli et al.
[
14
]
introduced and studied the
Minimum Satisfiability Problem (MinSAT)
, establishing its NP-hardness and discussing its relevance in fields such as fault diagnosis and design verification. However, the optimisation variant of QSAT, a natural
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete problem, is relatively unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, while some algorithms for solving MAX-QSAT are known
[
11
]
, the computational complexity for restricted classes of the problem have not been studied. As several lower bound proofs for generalised reachability games are obtained by reduction from QSAT, we expect that the analysis of optimisation variants of generalised reachability games provides more insight into the optimisation variants of QBF and vice-versa.

is not contained in the minimal attractor set,
or that we have

for all

If the attractors do not form a total preorder, then there exist indices

such that neither

nor
A_{j}\subseteq A_{i}
holds.

Define a strategy for player

as follows. Play arbitrarily until

or

is reached. Assume without loss of generality that we arrive at

first.
Since
t_{i}\notin A_{j}
(otherwise, it would imply

, contradicting our assumption that the attractors do not form a total preorder), player
{\mathsf{Eve}}
cannot attract to
t_{j}

from

. From this point onward, the strategy for player
{\mathsf{Adam}}
is to remain outside of

, and thus, will never reach

. As a result, the reachability player
{\mathsf{Eve}}
fails to achieve her goal.

We consider the remaining case that the attractors form a total preorder, but that

is not contained in the minimal attractor set, or that we have

for all
i
.
In the former case, player
{\mathsf{Eve}}
cannot attract from
s
to
t_{k}
. Thus player
{\mathsf{Adam}}
wins from
s
by simply avoiding
t_{k}
forever.
In the latter case, player
{\mathsf{Adam}}
wins by staying within the attractors

but avoiding the sets

for all

. While this strategy may visit all singleton targets

wins.

∎

Generalised Reachability with Mostly Singleton Targets

Next we consider the case of generalised reachabiliy games with a total of

Theorem 4
.

Let

Proof.

Let

be a generalised reachability game with parameters as stated in the claim.
First, we observe that a necessary condition for player
{\mathsf{Eve}}
to win

is that

{\mathsf{Eve}}
wins
(\mathcal{A},s,\emptyset,T)
as well

8
]
.
If player

wins

,
we hence can follow the proof of Theorem

and
assume without loss of generality a total order on

. Then let

.

Next we transform the game arena of

in order to treat to non-singleton target sets
\mathcal{F}
. To this end,

denote the set of game nodes of

and
consider the game arena

over

, storing the set of target sets from

\mathcal{F}
that have been satisfied so far. States

Next, we inductively define a sequence of sets

We claim that
{\mathsf{Eve}}
wins

if and only if

.

For one direction, let

. Then

can just follow her individual attractor strategies one after another, which results in visits
to all target states

For the converse direction, let

We show that
{\mathsf{Adam}}
wins

We first assume that the attractors of all target sets are different.
Then we construct a winning strategy for

in
\mathcal{G}
as follows.
We note that
{\mathsf{Adam}}
can follow the main objective to force the order in which the individual

are reached.
For this, he first and foremost wins if the first component of

(the original game node in

) falls outside of the attractor for the next target

With this in mind,

can just follow a strategy in

that attempts to prevent that the induced play on

ever reaches

from states of the form

uses his strategy to prevent states of the form

Since

can use this strategy to ensure

We note that if two or more target states have the same attractor, then they are neighbours

Regarding runtime complexity, the proposed solution algorithm computes

∎

This in particular provides fixed parameter tractability.

Corollary 1
.

\mathsf{GenReach}

4
Maximum Generalised Reachability

In this section, we address the complexity of the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
and the
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problems.

4.1
One Player Case with
{\mathsf{Eve}}

We first consider the case where all vertices of the game are controlled by
{\mathsf{Eve}}
. In this case, a strategy of
{\mathsf{Eve}}
produces a unique play
\rho
. Therefore, the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
and
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problems are equivalent if
{\mathsf{Eve}}
pledges the set of targets seen in the play
\rho
. Hence we only state our results for the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
variant.

Theorem 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a game where
{\mathsf{Eve}}
controls all the vertices, i.e,
V=V_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
.

1.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is in
\mathsf{P}
when each target set
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
is of size
1
.

2.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete in general. It is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even when
|F_{i}|=2
for each target set
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

For the case with all target sets of size
1
, we present an algorithm that runs in polynomial time. The algorithm first computes the strongly connected component (SCC) decomposition of the arena. For each SCC, we assign a value equal to the number of target vertices contained in it. Within each SCC,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can visit all the target vertices. Computing the maximum number of target vertices that
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can visit corresponds finding a path in the SCC decomposition that maximises the sum of the value of the SCCs contained in the path.
This can be computed bottom-up using dynamic programming starting from the bottom SCCs.
This is explained in detail in the proof of Theorem
10
, as the algorithm also works for the two-player case.
When target sets are of size
2
, NP-hardness follows from MAX-2-SAT problem, which is known to be NP-hard
[
10
]
. This follows the same reduction as the one used to show PSPACE-hardness in the general case with 2 players and arbitrary target sets
[
8
]
, presented in Figure
2
.

To see membership in NP,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can guess a path of size at most
nk
in length that visits
k
target sets, where
n
is the number of vertices in the arena. This is possible as the length of the path between any two consecutive target vertices not seen before is at most
n
.
∎

4.2
One Player Case with
{\mathsf{Adam}}

Recall that the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
and
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problems can have different solutions even when
{\mathsf{Adam}}
is the sole player, as demonstrated by a game obtained by modifying Figure
1
, where all vertices belong to Adam : in this case, Eve cannot promise to visit any
particular
target vertex, but she can ensure that at least one target vertex is visited.
In this section we deal with the one-player variant of these problems with
{\mathsf{Adam}}
as the only player. First we provide the full complexity picture for the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
problem.

Theorem 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a game where
{\mathsf{Adam}}
controls all the vertices, i.e,
V=V_{{\mathsf{Adam}}}
.

1.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is in
{\mathsf{P}}
when all target sets are singleton.

2.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is
{\mathsf{coNP}}
-complete in general. The
{\mathsf{coNP}}
-hardness holds even when
|F_{i}|=2
for each
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

We begin with a polynomial time algorithm for the case where target sets have size
1
. Note that a strategy of
{\mathsf{Adam}}
is just a path
\rho
. We can assume that
\rho
is a path of the form
s\to t\to t
. Otherwise, one can find a minimal loop in
\rho
and simply repeat it to obtain a path
\rho^{\prime}
of the correct form. Since
\rho^{\prime}
visits a subset of vertices visited by
\rho
,
\rho^{\prime}
should be at least as good as
\rho
for
{\mathsf{Adam}}
. Therefore, we search for paths of the form
s\to t\to t
which visit the least number of target vertices.

We assign weights to edges of the graph
G
using the function
w:E\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

w(u,v)=1
if, and only if,
\{v\}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.
The problem reduces to computing the lightest weighted
s\to t\to t
path in the weighted graph.
This can be done by iterating over all choices of
t
and computing the lightest such path.

For co-NP hardness, we look at the complement problem, i.e. given a game
{\mathcal{G}}
with
V=V_{\mathsf{Adam}}
and
k
, is there a strategy of
{\mathsf{Adam}}
such that on playing this strategy at most
k
targets are visited. We consider the problem MIN-2-SAT, which is known to be NP-hard
[
14
]
.
Following the same reduction as in NP-hardness in proof of Theorem
6
, but giving control of the vertices to
{\mathsf{Adam}}
, we get a game in which
{\mathsf{Adam}}
can visit at most
k
target vertices if and only if there is an assignment which satisfies at most
k
clauses.

For the upper bound, we observe that
{\mathsf{Adam}}
’s strategy can be simplified to a path of length at most
n+1
, because once he sees a vertex twice, he can simply repeat the loop without visiting more target sets. We can thus guess a path of length
n+1
and check if this hits at most
k
targets. This puts the complement problem in NP and thus shows the co-NP membership.
∎

Note that the algorithm to solve
\mathsf{GenReach}
games where all vertices are controlled by
{\mathsf{Adam}}
computes
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
for each target set, and checks if the initial vertex is in the
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
for all target sets or not. If not,
{\mathsf{Adam}}
has a choice to avoid a target set and win. This runs in polynomial time as computing the

\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
can be done in polynomial time. However, this does not work for the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
problem as the initial state
s
might not be in the
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(F_{i})
and
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(F_{j})
, but in the
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(F_{i}\cup F_{j})
. Thus, the play will visit at least
1
of the target sets.
For example, in the game from Figure
1
,
s
and

are neither in
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(\{u_{1}\})
, nor in
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(\{u_{3}\})
, but they are in
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(\{u_{1},u_{3}\})
.

However, the polynomial time algorithm can be adapted to work for the
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problem.

Theorem 8
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a game where
{\mathsf{Adam}}
controls all the vertices, i.e,
V=V_{{\mathsf{Adam}}}
. Then,

\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
is in
\mathsf{P}
.

Proof.

The
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problems asks, if there is a
k
sized subset of target sets such that the play visits a target from each of the
k
chosen target sets.
The algorithm computes the
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(F_{i})
for all target sets
F_{i}
and counts how many of them contain the initial state
s_{0}
. If
s_{0}
is contained in at least
k
of the
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
sets, then any play will visit the
k
target sets. This is because being in the attractor set of a target

guarantees that Eve has a strategy to force the play to reach
F_{i}
, even though Adam controls all the vertices. Therefore,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can promise
k
target sets whose
\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}
contains
s_{0}
. If
s_{0}
is not contained in
k
attractor sets, then no matter which
k

{\mathsf{Eve}}
promises, there will be a promised target set
F^{\prime}
, such that
s_{0}\not\in\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(F)
. Therefore,
{\mathsf{Adam}}
will be able to avoid the promised target sets.
∎

4.3
Two Player Case

Here we discuss the complexity of the two-player case.
We first state the results for the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
problem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a game.

1.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete. The
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness holds even when
|F_{i}|=2
for each
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

2.

\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
is NP-hard when each

Proof.

(1)
The upper bound in the general case, i.e,

, for all
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, follows from
[
15
, Theorem 12]
. In fact, they consider the question of maximizing weighted reachability, where different reachability objectives can have different weight associated to them and
{\mathsf{Eve}}
attempts to maximise the total weight. Our case corresponds to the case where all the reachability objectives have the same weight, which is called the
MaxR
objective in
[
15
]
.

The PSPACE lower bound presented in
[
15
]
follows from the PSPACE-hardness of solving generalised reachability game
[

]
, which works for target sets of size

2

In the reduction from 3-SAT to MAX-2-SAT
[
9

\frac{7}{10}
of the 2-CNF clauses are satisfied if the original 3-CNF clause was satisfied. Otherwise, at most
\frac{6}{10}
of the 2-CNF clauses are satisfied. Since this holds for any assignment, we can transform the matrix of the QBF formula from a 3-CNF to a 2-CNF formula such that exactly
\frac{7}{10}
of the clauses are satisfied by a satisfying assignment. Therefore, this proves that MAX-2-QSAT is PSPACE-hard. Using the construction to translate QSAT to generalised reachability games, we obtain that
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
with target sets of size
2
is PSPACE-hard.

(2) We reduce from the minimum vertex cover problem, which is known to be NP-complete
[

.
Given an undirected graph

and a number

G
has a vertex cover

{\mathsf{Eve}}

and the vertices controlled by
{\mathsf{Adam}}
,

, correspond to the edges of the original graph
G
.
From any vertex in
V_{\mathsf{Eve}}
,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can choose any edge

of the original graph

and move to the vertex corresponding

. From a vertex
(u,v)
in
V_{\mathsf{Adam}}
,
{\mathsf{Adam}}
can move to vertices

, corresponding to the two end points of the edge.
The start vertex is any arbitrary vertex in
V_{\mathsf{Adam}}
.
The target set
{\mathcal{F}}=\{\{v\}~\mid~v\in V_{\mathsf{Eve}}\}
consists of singleton sets containing each vertex of
V
.
We claim that
G
has a vertex cover of size at most
\ell
iff
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can reach at least
\ell
targets.

Consider the following strategy of
{\mathsf{Eve}}
: assuming any ordering on the set
E
,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
chooses the next edge in this order in a round-robin manner. The best response strategy of
{\mathsf{Adam}}
against this is to play a minimum vertex cover of
G
.
Also against this strategy of
{\mathsf{Adam}}
,
{\mathsf{Eve}}
cannot do any better since the number of vertices visited in
V_{\mathsf{Eve}}
is at most the size of the minimum vertex cover. Hence, the value
k
of this game is exactly the size of a minimum vertex cover.
This proves our claim and completes the proof of NP-hardness.
∎

Note that the NP-hardness of the
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
problem with target sets of size
1
is in contrast with the results for
\mathsf{GenReach}
problem which is known to be in P for target sets of size
1
.

Next, we state our results for the two-player case of
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
.

Theorem 10
.

a

1.

The
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problem for the two-player case is in
{\mathsf{P}}
when all target sets are singleton.

2.

\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete in general. It is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard even when all target sets have size
3
.

Proof.

We give a polynomial time algorithm for the case when all target sets are singleton.
The algorithm below is similar to the generalised reachability case and proceeds as follows:

1.

Compute the attractor relation between target vertices forming a preorder graph capturing these relationships. Formally, with
t_{0}=s
, consider the relation
t_{i}\preceq t_{j}
iff
t_{i}\in\mathsf{Attr}_{{\mathsf{Eve}}}(t_{j})
, for
i\in\{0\}\cup[n]
and create a graph with
t_{i}
as vertices and edges from
t_{i}
to
t_{j}
iff
t_{i}\preceq t_{j}
. Call this the preorder graph.

2.

Perform the Strongly Connected Component (SCC) decomposition of this preorder graph.

3.

Assign a weight to each SCC equal to the number of target vertices contained within it.

4.

The resulting SCC decomposition is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). Using bottom-up dynamic programming, find a path in this DAG with the maximum total weight starting from the SCC containing
t_{0}
.

The set of targets on this path from
t_{0}
can be visited because, by construction, the
{\mathsf{Eve}}
-attractors of the targets are ordered by inclusion.

At the same time, any target set
{\mathsf{Eve}}
can promise must have target states whose
{\mathsf{Eve}}
attractors are ordered by inclusion; they are therefore on a path from
t_{0}
in this DAG.
Thus, there cannot be a larger such set, as it would lead to a path with a larger weight.

This shows that our dynamic programming algorithm works.
For the complexity, we note that each step can be performed efficiently:
Attractor computation can be done in
O(|V|+|E|)
. SCC decomposition can also be computed in
O(|V|+|E|)

[
18
]
.
Assigning weights to SCCs and constructing the weighted DAG is linear in the graph size.
Finally, computing the maximum-weight path in a DAG via bottom-up dynamic programming is also linear in the size of the DAG obtained via SCC decomposition. Since each step is polynomial, the overall algorithm solves the problem
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
in polynomial time when each target set is singleton.

For the general case with target sets of arbitrary size, we obtain a
\mathsf{NPSPACE}
algorithm by simply guessing the target sets promised by
{\mathsf{Eve}}
and then applying the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
algorithm for solving generalised reachability games from
[
8
]
. As a consequence of Savitch’s Theorem, this gives a
\mathsf{PSPACE}
upper bound for the general problem. The
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
lower bound holds even for target sets of size
3
as the generalised reachability game with the objective to visit all targets is a special case of the
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problem.
∎

We leave the exact complexity of
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}{}
for the case with target sets of size
2
open; Theorem
6
provides an
\mathsf{NP}
lower bound.

5
Discussion

We have studied variations of games with generalised reachability objectives and maximum generalised reachability objectives, considering the size of target sets as parameter.
We have provided several complexity results for these two problems, showing first
that generalised reachability can be checked in time linear in (1) the size of the game and (2) the number of singleton target sets, and (3) exponential in the number of larger target sets.
This extends the polynomial time complexity from the case where all target sets are singleton
[
8
]
to allow for a logarithmic number of target sets of arbitrary size.
We have then established NL-completeness for the single-player case, where only the environment makes decisions.

Next, we have introduced optimisation variants of the generalised reachability problem, where the goal generalises from visiting all target sets to visiting as many target sets as possible.
The first natural goal here is to just
maximise
the number of target sets visited; we show that this problem is different in that it is
NP
-hard, even when each target set contains only a single element, and
PSPACE
-complete even when the size of target sets is restricted to at most two.
We also show that both single player variants of this problem are tractable if the target sets are singleton, but become intractable already for games with target sets of size two over DAGs with pathwidth two.

We also considered an interesting variant, where
{\mathsf{Eve}}
is asked to pledge – before the game starts – the target sets that are to be visited.
She then has to visit them all, and her goal shifts to pledging a largest set of target sets.
We show that the solution of games with such objectives is tractable for singleton target sets even in the two-player case. Another interesting variant could require
{\mathsf{Eve}}
to specify the order in which the target sets are visited. This variant introduces additional constraints and may not be reducible to the cases considered in this work.

Thus, we clarify the landscape of complexity of several problems in generalised reachability. The major remaining open problem is the complexity of the generalised reachability problem where every target set is of size at most two
[
8
]
.
The promise variant,
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
problem with target sets of size
2
, is shown to be NP-hard (even when
{\mathsf{Eve}}
is the only player). Since
\mathsf{GenReach}
can be seen as a special case of
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
, this has consequences for the exact complexity of
\mathsf{GenReach}
with target sets of size
2
. In this case, a polynomial time algorithm for
\mathsf{GenReach}
would imply
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
is harder (unless P=NP). On the other hand, an upper bound better than PSPACE for
\mathsf{MaxGenReachPromise}
would also imply an improved upper bound for
\mathsf{GenReach}
in the case of target sets of size
2
.

Regarding the optimisation variant
\mathsf{MaxGenReach}
, the exact complexity of the case with singleton sets remains open as we have shown it to be NP-hard and in PSPACE.
