The median effective dose of ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block around the hip capsule in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures
Authors: Shengyu Jin, Chenyu Wang, Qinghua Wu, Pinhui Ke, Chunlan Lin, Pusheng Ren, Zui Zou, Yaohua Yu, Liangqing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to establish the median effective dose (ED50) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.25% ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block for facilitating spinal anesthesia positioning in elderly patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures.MethodsThe elderly patients received elective proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA) surgery under spinal anesthesia. By adjusting the needle bevel orientation toward the superior pubic ramus, an ultrasound-guided PENG block was administered five minutes before positioning. The dose of 0.25% ropivacaine was titrated according to the highest pain level reported on the visual analog scale (VAS) during positioning, starting from 10 ml with subsequent adjustments in 1 ml increments. The modified Dixon Up-and-Down sequential method was used to determine the effective dose, and the median ED50 along with its 95% CI for 0.25% ropivacaine were calculated using Probit analysis. VAS scores were recorded at baseline, 5 min after block administration, and during positioning. Postoperative sufentanil consumption and block-related complications—including bleeding, hematoma, quadriceps weakness, and local anesthetic toxicity—were also documented.ResultsA total of thirty elderly patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures were enrolled. The ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine for a PENG block to facilitate spinal anesthesia positioning was determined to be 3.674 ml (95% CI: 1.879–4.842 ml). At five minutes post-block, both the VAS score during passive movement and the highest VAS score recorded during positioning were significantly reduced compared to pre-block values. However, no significant differences were observed in postoperative VAS scores, postoperative sufentanil consumption, or the incidence of complications.ConclusionFor elderly intertrochanteric fracture patients, ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine PENG block was 3.674 ml. By adjusting the needle bevel orientation, a low-dose local anaesthetic can effectively relieve pain without affecting quadriceps strength.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03806-y.

Introduction

Intertrochanteric femoral fractures, common in the elderly, are primarily managed with Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation (PFNA) [1]. A significant challenge is the severe pain experienced during positioning for intrathecal anesthesia, increasing risks of cardiovascular instability, cerebrovascular events, and delirium [2, 3]. Consequently, effective pre-positioning analgesia is essential.

Currently, the fascia iliaca compartment block is primarily used for perioperative analgesia in patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures [4]. However, this technique has several limitations. Firstly, it requires a large dose of local anesthetic, which carries a relatively high risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) [5]. Secondly, analgesic efficacy may be inadequate due to inconsistent blockade of the obturator nerve [6]. Additionally, it often leads to quadriceps muscle weakness [7].

In recent years, the pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block introduced by Girón-Arango et al. has been increasingly adopted in clinical practice as a novel analgesic technique [8]. In view of its selectively targets the articular branches of the obturator nerve (ON), femoral nerve (FN), and accessory obturator nerve (AON), it has been increasingly adopted in patients with hip fractures. However, studies indicated that 20 ml of ropivacaine for pericapsular hip nerve blockade can spread through and medial to the psoas major muscle, potentially blocking the femoral nerve and compromising quadriceps strength [9, 10]. This indicates that an excessive dosage of local anesthetic injection may lead to an imprecise analgesic effect as it diffuses to an inappropriate area.

Given the age-related decline in physiological reserve and reduced tolerance to local anesthetics in elderly patients, it is meaningful to determine the ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine for PENG block prior to spinal anesthesia positioning. Establishing this dose will help provide scientifically grounded and precise analgesic guidance, minimize adverse effects, enhance anesthesia safety and efficacy, and ultimately improve perioperative outcomes in geriatric patients with intertrochanteric fractures. Clinically, adjusting the needle bevel direction during puncture has been shown to enhance the diffusion of injectate into the myofascial plane [11]. However, it remains unclear whether this maneuver will affect the efficacy of the PENG block. This study aims to investigate the dose-effect relationship of ropivacaine in ultrasound-guided PENG block by adjusting the needle bevel direction for facilitating spinal anesthesia positioning in elderly patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures.

PENG block and anesthesia

Upon arrival in the operating room, all patients received standard monitoring, including electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), and saturation of pulse oxygen (SpO₂). Venous access was established and supplemental oxygen was administered. Ultrasound-guided arterial catheterization was performed for continuous arterial blood pressure (ABP) monitoring.

Before surgery, patients were taught how to use the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) to score the pain intensity. For the PENG block, each patient was placed in the supine position with the affected inguinal area exposed. Following routine disinfection and draping, the anterior superior iliac spine was identified preliminary by palpation. A 2–5 MHz convex array ultrasound probe (Huasheng, model: Labat SP) was placed parallel to the inguinal crease to accurately locate the anterior superior iliac spine. The probe was then shifted caudally in a parallel orientation to visualize the anterior inferior iliac spine. The proximal end of the probe remains fixed while the distal end is rotated 45 degrees counterclockwise to identify iliopsoas muscle, femoral artery and vein, psoas tendon, pubic ramus, and iliopubic eminence. Using an in-plane approach, a block needle (Hakko, model: USG-Type CCR, 22-gauge, 80-mm) was advanced from lateral to medial, to the lateral aspect of the psoas major tendon, with its bevel facing superiorly (facing the probe). Once the needle tip was positioned within the fascial plane between the psoas tendon and the pubic ramus, the bevel was rotated 180° to face dorsally. After confirming negative aspiration, a single bolus of 0.25% ropivacaine (AstraZeneca, batch: NBPA) was injected, as illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2.

Based on published data, the initial ropivacaine volume administered for the PENG block was 10 ml, with an adjacent volume gradient of 1 ml [8, 15]. Five minutes after injection, the orthopedic surgeon applied traction to patient’s affected limb and positioned the patient for spinal anesthesia, with unaffected hip and knee flexed. The patient was placed in lateral position with the affected side on top. The affected limb was extended upward and supported by a blanket placed beneath (between the healthy and affected limbs) to prevent pressure-related complications, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Throughout the trial cohort, the traction and positioning was carried out by the same surgeon.

Analgesic efficacy was assessed using the highest VAS pain score obtained during the positioning. A score < 4 indicated effective analgesia, leading to a 1 ml dose reduction for the next patient. A score ≥ 4 indicated ineffective analgesia, leading to a 1 ml dose increment for the next patient. This up-down sequential allocation continued until 7 transitions between effective and ineffective analgesia occurred.

All PENG blocks were performed under ultrasound guidance by the same anesthesiologist (experienced in over 100 PENG blocks). Pain scores (pre-block, post-block, and during positioning) and quadriceps femoris muscle strength were assessed by physicians blinded to the block procedure. Spinal anesthesia was then performed at the L3-L4 interspace with 2–3 ml of 0.5% ropivacaine. The target dermatomal level was set at T8-T10.

Perioperative analgesic strategy

Patients received no other regional analgesic techniques before surgery. Preoperative analgesia was achieved through the rectal administration of 50 mg diclofenac sodium suppositories twice daily.

For postoperative pain management, a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump containing sufentanil 1 µg·ml− 1 and dolasetron 0.25 mg·ml− 1 was employed. The pump parameters were set as following: a background infusion rate of 2 ml·h− 1, a bolus dose of 1 ml, and a lockout time of 15 min.

Patients were instructed to self-administer a bolus if their VAS at movement reached or exceeded 4. In case of insufficient pain relief, rescue analgesia with either an intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg or a repeated rectal diclofenac sodium suppository 50 mg was given.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ED50 of ropivacaine, calculated by using a Probit regression model based on recorded dosage and corresponding analgesic effect of each case.

Resting pain scores and passive leg raising test scores were recorded at the following time points: prior to nerve block (T0), 5 min after block (T1), immediately following positioning (T2), and at 6, 12, and 24 h post-surgery (T3, T4, T5), respectively (where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst pain imaginable). At each time point, the patient first reported the static VAS score. Then the surgeon elevated the affected leg by 30° and returned it to the original position gently, while preventing knee flexion. The dynamic score was defined as the highest VAS score during this maneuver.

The quadriceps strength was assessed using the Lovett scale (0–5), where grade 0 represents complete paralysis and grade 5 represents normal strength with full movement against gravity and resistance. With supine position, the examiner supported the knee under the popliteal fossa while applying resistance to the anterior calf with the other hand; the patient was then instructed to extend the knee against this resistance. Bilateral assessments (surgical and non-surgical sides) were performed at T0-T5.

MAP and HR were recorded at T0-T2. The total consumption of sufentanil, the number of demands on the PCIA pump, and the frequency of rescue analgesic administration within the first 24 h postoperatively were documented. The occurrence of paresthesia or aspiration of blood during the puncture procedure as well as any complications, including hematoma or infection at the puncture site, nerve injury, or LAST, were recorded.

Muscle strength assessment and complication documentation were collaboratively conducted by two anesthesiologists who were blind to the patient’s medication dosage. In the event of a discrepancy, the decision was made by one more experienced anesthesiologist. The other outcomes were self-reported by patients or recorded after direct reading from the device.

Statistics

Sample size determination

The ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine was determined using the up-and-down method. In accordance with the design requirements of this method, a minimum of seven crossover points between positive and negative responses were targeted. Furthermore, literature suggested that a sample size of 20 to 40 patients would be necessary to obtain a stable estimate of the target dose under realistic conditions [8].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. Normally distributed data were presented as mean with standard deviation (mean ± SD) and were compared using one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median with quartiles (M[Q1,Q3]), and comparisons were made using non-parametric rank-sum tests. The ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine and its 95% CI were calculated by Probit regression analysis [15].

Discussion

This is the first dosage study of PENG block with a specified needle bevel orientation fixed toward the superior pubic ramus. We determined that the ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine for PENG block for facilitating spinal anesthesia positioning in elderly patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures was 3.674 ml (95% CI: 1.879–4.842 ml). We also proved that this dose can achieve effective postoperative analgesia. Importantly, regardless of block efficacy, the muscle strength was preserved without affecting the main trunk of the femoral nerve [16].

The conventional methods of nerve blocks for patients with acute hip fractures, such as femoral nerve block, lumbar plexus block and fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB), have been widely used and recommended in guidelines [17] However, all the aforementioned block techniques target the main trunks of nerves, inevitably affecting muscle strength. Although lumbar plexus block provides adequate analgesia, it is relatively difficult to perform and requires repositioning of the patient, which may increase discomfort. The FICB was proved to be superior to the femoral nerve block, however, anatomical studies suggested that local anesthetic injected into the fascia iliaca compartment can hardly cover the obturator nerve [9].

Sensory innervation of the hip joint is predominantly located within the myofascial plane of the anterior capsule [18] Although the innervation of the hip joint is complex, anatomical studies have confirmed that the anterior capsule has high density of nociceptors [19], which is primarily innervated by articular branches from the FN, ON, and AON [20] PENG block selectively targets the articular branches of these nerves, offering benefits such as technical simplicity, rapid onset, motor-free, and satisfactory analgesic efficacy.

Several researches have concentrated on the dosage of PENG. The original study by Girón-Arango et al. employed 20 ml of 0.25% ropivacaine, a volume and a concentration that were adopted in numerous subsequent studies [8] In some other studies, a higher does was even chosen [21, 22] Cadaver studies indicated that high doses of ropivacaine used in PENG block may spread along the medial and internal pathways of the iliopsoas muscle, potentially leading to femoral nerve involvement and quadriceps weakness [20] A clinical study found that using 20 ml of 0.75% ropivacaine for PENG block in elderly patients with hip fracture, quadriceps strength reduction was observed in 8 out of 28 cases, with 2 patients experiencing complete motor block [23] Recent studies therefore explored the ED50 of ropivacaine for PENG block in the elderly. Zheng et al. reported an ED50 of 8.84 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine for PENG block in geriatric femoral neck fractures [21] An anatomical study further suggested that the maximum injectate volume preserving femoral nerve function during PENG block was 13.2 ml [24]. Consistently, Shang-Ru Yeoh et al. recommended a ropivacaine dose of 5–10 ml to minimize the risk of nerve injury [25].

Our study established an ED50 of only 3.674 ml for 0.25% ropivacaine—markedly lower than previously reported volumes [21] This may be due to the needle tip-down technique during injection, which enables precise injection into the targeted region between the psoas major tendon and the superior pubic ramus. Orienting the needle tip with its bevel directed towards the iliac bone promotes the establishment of a thin layer of local anesthetic diffusion between the iliopsoas muscle and the superior pubic ramus. This allows for targeted and concentrated deposition, which precisely covers the dense sensory nerve branches of the hip joint, instead of depending on passive volume diffusion [26]. A more precise blockade of the target nerve minimizes the risk of intramuscular injection into the iliopsoas muscle or diffusion along its medial and lateral aspects towards the femoral nerve [27]. In ultrasound images, it was observed that the low-volume injected drug was restricted to the pericapsular region, and no extensive diffusion was detected at the ilio-lumbar muscle plane. Consequently, this reduces quadriceps muscle weakness and decreases the injection volume required to achieve satisfactory analgesic effects.

Such a low-dose strategy minimized the spreading of local anesthetic to the adjacent nerves, preserved the motor function, and could support early functional recovery for elderly patients. This dose can also reduce the risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity and leaves a “dose room” for alternative nerve block techniques in cases of failed spinal anesthesia. Future researches could focus on the impact of needle tip bevel orientation on the analgesic effects of PENG block.

Regarding analgesic strategies and combined nerve block techniques, several existing studies have offered valuable insights for clinical practice. The PENG block has been demonstrated to enhance patients’ preoperative comfort, but a number of investigations have reported no significant differences in postoperative analgesic consumption or pain scores between patients receiving a PENG block and those without any block [22, 23, 28–30]. This may be attributed to the generally low anesthetic dosage of PENG and the limited duration of roperidone’s effect. A study indicated that, in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty, the combination of PENG and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) block demonstrated greater efficacy than FICB in terms of muscle strength and opioid dose [31]. Further study may concentrate on the perioperative impact of combination of diverse peripheral nerve blocks on elderly patients undergoing PFNA.

This study has several limitations. Methodologically, although the Dixon up-and-down sequential method and the Probit regression are efficient for small-sample studies, the limited sample size inherently may introduce potential bias and thus affect the generalizability of the results. In terms of participants, this study only included patients aged 60–90 years with a BMI between 18 and 28 kg·m−², it remains unclear whether the results of this study are applicable to patients of other ages or the obesity. Furthermore, the assessment of the VAS pain scores was subjective, which may affect the accuracy of the result. Since analgesic efficacy was assessed only at 5 min post-injection, and the complete onset of ropivacaine typically takes 15–20 min, this study may have underestimated the actual efficacy of low volumes. Future studies should aim to enroll larger and more diverse cohorts, including patients of varying ages and body compositions, and incorporate more objective pain assessment tools—such as functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to monitor hemodynamic changes in pain-related brain regions—to improve the precision and reliability of evidence.

Lastly, it is important to emphasize that ED50 denotes a median effective volume within a controlled research context. The acquired ED50 value should be interpreted as the half-effective dosage for early onset, rather than the recommended effective dosage for a complete block of sensory nerve. In clinical practice, administering an extremely low volume may heighten the risk of an incomplete block, attributed to individual anatomical variances, tissue compliance, and pathological alterations. It is recommended that clinicians refrain from simply adopting this ED50 value as a standard practice. Instead, they should utilize it as a reference for optimizing volume adjustment, and consider employing higher volumes (e.g., ED95). Based on our estimated ED50 values and observed variability, we recommend that the initial dose of 0.25% ropivacaine for intertrochanteric fracture PENG block in clinical practice should not be less than 5.0 mL.

Conclusion

For elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures, the ED50 of 0.25% ropivacaine for PENG block was 3.674 ml. By adjusting the needle bevel orientation, a low dose of anaesthetic can provide rapid and effective analgesia for positioning during neuraxial anesthesia without compromising quadriceps muscle strength.
