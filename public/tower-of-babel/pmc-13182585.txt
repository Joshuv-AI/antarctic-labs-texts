ABSTRACT

There is mounting clinical and biochemical evidence to suggest that intracranial vascular shunting lesions can develop de novo, via a different pathophysiological mechanism than conventional arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). A conventional AVM is a nodular collection of arteries with fistulous connection to venous drainage, creating a nidus of hyperdynamic vascular circulation that is prone to rupture. Conventional AVMs are widely accepted to be congenital lesions that develop secondary to embryological genetic mutations, although a growing body of literature suggests that the pathogenesis of these lesions extends beyond in utero development.1,2

This report describes the development of intracranial arteriovenous shunting lesions in a patient with cirrhosis. We call these lesions cirrhosis-associated intracranial shunting lesions (CAISLs) and hypothesize that they develop due to a disruption in angiogenic homeostasis secondary to liver dysfunction. This is a dynamic disease entity that can both develop and regress in response to the vascular environment in which they form. Additionally, we have performed a literature review on de novo cerebral shunting lesions in cirrhotic patients and propose a mechanism for CAISL development. Our findings expand the differential of intracranial shunting lesions and imply the need to tailor treatment paradigms based on these differences.

Illustrative Case

Methods

A literature review of the Scopus and PubMed databases was performed independently (J.H., M.M.). Search modifiers included “hepatic,” “failure,” “cirrhosis,” “arteriovenous,” “malformation,” “angiography,” “shunt,” “brain,” and “intracerebral.” Articles were included for literature review based on the strength of the evidence, language, format, and neuroimaging techniques. The authors (J.H., M.M.) reached consensus on inclusion and exclusion criteria. The data quality and number of cases were unsuitable for application of a formal systematic review. Case details were collected retrospectively from a secure electronic medical record.

Case Description

A 59-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with worsening confusion. Her past medical history was significant for alcoholic cirrhosis and hepatic encephalopathy. Coagulation parameters were within normal limits. CT and CT angiography (CTA) of the head revealed an acute left parietal intraparenchymal hemorrhage with mild surrounding edema and a prominent draining structure adjacent to the hematoma, as well as a venous anomaly posterior to the right lateral ventricle. MRI confirmed the findings and revealed a right temporoparietal venous anomaly. MRI completed 15 years earlier was reviewed and did not demonstrate the dilated venous structures (Fig. 1). Digital cerebral angiography (DCA) revealed a right-sided shunting lesion measuring 2.17 × 1.32 cm, with a prominent vein draining into the vein of Galen complex (Fig. 2A) and associated with branches of the right middle cerebral artery (Fig. 2B). DCA also revealed a smaller left-sided shunting lesion, 0.49 × 0.54 cm in volume, with a prominent vein draining into the superior sagittal sinus and associated with the distal branches of the left middle cerebral artery. The patient was managed with referrals for stereotactic radiosurgery and potential liver transplantation and has maintained clinical stability in the interim.

Shimoda et al. described a 35-year-old male with alcoholic cirrhosis, found to have a new shunting lesion fed by the paramedian mesencephalic arteries and drained via the vein of Galen.3 Two years later, following a living donor liver transplantation, MRI and angiography revealed that the lesion spontaneously disappeared.3

Morales-Valero et al. reported on a 35-year-old female with cirrhosis of unspecified etiology, who suffered a parenchymal bleed from a large parieto-occipital vascular lesion.4 An MRI study done 4 years earlier, during evaluation of confusion due to hepatic encephalopathy, had not shown any abnormal vascularity.4

Gondar et al. reported on a 57-year-old female with cirrhosis who had a known small developmental venous anomaly with no nidus or arterial feeder identified during a workup for head trauma secondary to acute alcohol poisoning. On a scan obtained 2 years after the injury, during a workup for hemiparesis and dysarthria, the same lesion was shown to be markedly larger with significant arteriovenous shunting between the vein of Trolard and branches of the anterior cerebral and external carotid arteries.5

Kumar et al. reported on a 28-year-old male with cirrhosis that had a Spetzler-Martin grade II AVM in the right frontal lobe that resolved on repeat imaging 1 year after receiving a liver transplantation.6

Do et al. reported on a 33-year-old female with cirrhosis who developed multiple new venous anomalies.7 The patient had a left parieto-occipital lesion with a nidus that drained via the vein of Galen and was fed by the left posterior cerebral artery and middle cerebral artery. Additionally, she had right insular and parietal AVMs that drained into the superior sagittal sinus via the cortical veins. The CAISLs were not present on brain MRI studies performed 7 years prior.7

Aljeradat et al. reported on a 54-year-old male with cirrhosis who was found to have multiple bilateral shunting lesions during a workup for progressive confusion and headaches.8 Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed bilateral, curvilinear, irregular pial vessels with early high-flow arteriovenous shunting. Treatment and outcomes for this patient were not described.8

Yakubu et al. reported on a 38-year-old female with cirrhosis who presented to the emergency department after tonic-clonic seizures.9 A CT study showed a small acute intraparenchymal hemorrhage in the posterior left basal ganglia. MRI/DSA demonstrated an approximately 4.5 × 2–cm left perisylvian pial AVM with a diffuse nidus, predominantly fed by the middle cerebral artery, with perinidal angiogenesis/gliosis and moderately slow flow. This lesion was not observed on a prior MRI study the patient received, suggesting de novo etiology. Notably, this patient was treated conservatively until she continued to suffer multiple bleeding events, for which she then was stabilized by treatment with SRS.9

Pervez et al. reported on a 48-year-old male with cirrhosis who presented with status epilepticus and altered mental status, and imaging showed a right temporal intracerebral hemorrhage with a prominent adjacent vein.10 On readmission in August 2022 for confusion and visual distortion, imaging suggested an approximately 1.4 × 0.8–cm right parietotemporal “tangle-of-vessels” plus a new right cerebellar contrast blush. Catheter angiography revealed bilateral, multiterritory arteriovenous shunting lesions with absent capillary beds, with some lesions lacking a classic nidus and others resembling a loose nidus/cerebral proliferative angiopathy pattern.10

Discussion

Observations

We present the case of a middle-aged female with liver cirrhosis who was found to have new intracranial shunting lesions during workup of a spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage. Based on the presented case and review of the literature, we suggest naming these vascular entities cirrhosis-associated intracranial shunting lesions.

CAISLs appear to develop following the onset of advanced liver dysfunction with portal hypertension and have demonstrated the ability to resolve after liver transplantation. In the 2 cases published by Shimoda et al. and Kumar et al., in which liver transplantation resolved the CAISL, it likely did so by halting the angiogenic and inflammatory impetus we outlined above.3,6 These cases imply an important cause-and-effect relationship that allows us to infer a mechanism for CAISL development.

Lessons

We hypothesize that CAISLs may form due to a biochemical cascade triggered by multiple concurrent cirrhosis-induced conditions. Namely, portal hypertension causes elevated cerebral shear stress that activates endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition through mechanosensitive pathways like the MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and TGF-β/Smad pathways.11–16 Simultaneously, sequelae of cirrhosis such as hepatopulmonary syndrome can lead to hypoxemia, stabilizing HIF-1α and increasing VEGF, angiopoietins, and matrix metalloproteinases, while inflammatory molecules like IL-6 and TNF-α create a permissive environment for neovascularization and the formation of weak, high-flow nodular lesions (Fig. 3).7–19

Cirrhosis induces multiple concurrent physiological changes that lead to the development of CAISLs. Cirrhosis increases circulating inflammatory and angiogenic molecules, while lacking the catabolic properties of a healthy liver to remove them from circulation. Simultaneously, hepatopulmonary syndrome and portal hypertension can cause hemodynamic alterations and vascular remodeling through shear stress mechanical transduction, hypoxia, and nitric oxide production. EndMT = endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition.

Our findings suggest we rework our current understanding of the natural history of AVMs and engage in a discussion of nomenclature and classification. It is reasonable to suggest that CAISLs and conventional AVMs are independent disease entities that belong to the broader category of shunting lesions. Because of the presumed mechanistic differences in the pathogenesis of these lesions, we suggest CAISLs be considered a distinct entity from the conventional AVM—at least until their pathogenic origins are better understood.

Future studies on CAISLs should evaluate whether a circulating biomarker panel can aid in CAISL risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring. A pragmatic approach could pair widely available inflammatory indices used in cirrhosis, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, with angiogenic mediators implicated in vascular remodeling (VEGF, angiopoietins, and matrix metalloproteinases). This strategy may help identify cirrhotic patients at higher risk for developing clinically significant intracranial shunting lesions and guide surveillance intensity.20

From a clinical perspective, several important points bear emphasis. First, the natural history of CAISLs is unknown. While some present as spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages, the vast majority likely remain asymptomatic. Second, it seems plausible that CAISLs and conventional AVMs require different therapeutic approaches. If direct lesion-targeted therapy is considered for a suspected CAISL, the perioperative risks common in advanced cirrhosis may influence treatment selection. In selected cases, SRS may represent a more favorable modality than open microsurgical resection, particularly when coagulopathy or other hepatic complications increase operative risk. We learn a valuable lesson from the case of Yakubu et al., in which we see the fragility of this condition and propensity for bleeding events with conservative management only, highlighting a likely benefit of offering SRS.9 However, if CAISLs are indeed acquired lesions and develop via our proposed mechanism, then halting the disease process through liver transplantation is the most conceptually direct treatment approach. In the absence of liver transplantation, treatment modalities used for conventional AVMs may fail given that the source of the angiogenic stimuli remains. In a proof-of-concept trial, Muster et al. demonstrated that systemic anti-VEGF treatment with bevacizumab did not change AVM volume in 2 patients with conventional cerebral AVMs.21 Given our hypothesis on the differing pathogenic mechanism, we are curious how this same therapy would affect the size and progression of a CAISL. It is reasonable to say that CAISLs are a significantly underrecognized disease process, especially since advanced cerebral imaging studies in patients with cirrhosis are not routinely performed.

A key limitation of our case is that prior imaging was MRI rather than angiography; therefore, the absence of a visible lesion on the prior MRI study cannot definitively exclude an occult preexisting low-flow abnormality. Nonetheless, in the context of the growing literature describing de novo intracranial shunting lesions in cirrhosis, as well as sound pathophysiological rationale, we favor the interpretation that these findings represent acquired lesions. Limited follow-up in this patient precludes firm conclusions regarding interval size or flow change, underscoring the need for larger series with standardized imaging surveillance. An unresolved question is whether targeted cerebrovascular screening should be considered in select cirrhotic populations. At present, evidence is insufficient to recommend routine CTA or MRI-based screening in asymptomatic patients. However, a reasonable hypothesis for future evaluation is targeted noninvasive screening in patients with advanced cirrhosis who develop new focal neurological deficits, unexplained seizures, or atypical intracranial hemorrhage patterns, or who are being evaluated for liver transplantation.

We propose the classification of a disease entity belonging to the category of intracranial shunting lesions, known as CAISLs. They differ in pathogenesis than conventional AVMs and develop secondary to cirrhosis-mediated angiogenic cascades and have been demonstrated to resolve when the angiogenic signal is terminated by liver transplantation. We propose speculative pathogenetic mechanisms for their development, and potential avenues for medical therapy, including evidence for a new exception for liver transplantation. More research is necessary to guide this unique and informative disease entity.
