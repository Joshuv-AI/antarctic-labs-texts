Determining ICU Staff’s Conceptions, Opinions, Views, Experiences, and Reflection (DISCOVER) of Brain Death—Results From a Swiss Multicenter Survey
Authors: Pascale Grzonka, Sebastian Berger, Kai Tisljar, Sabina Hunziker, Urs Fisch, Regina Schumann, Mathias Nebiker, Luca Cioccari, Raoul Sutter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:To assess perceptions, beliefs, and conceptual understanding of brain death among ICU professionals at three Swiss hospitals and to examine their associations with personal, educational, and professional experiences.DESIGN:Cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey.SETTING:Multicenter survey at three Swiss care centers.SUBJECTS:ICU healthcare professionals (i.e., physicians and nurses).INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:Questionnaires assessed demographics, religious beliefs, professional experience, exposure to brain death cases, knowledge of diagnostic criteria, and emotional and ethical attitudes using closed-ended and semi-open questions, Likert scales (1 = “very unsure,” “absolutely not” to 10 = “very sure,” “absolutely”), and checklists. Endpoints were (primary) perceptions, beliefs, conceptual understanding of brain death, and (secondary) interprofessional differences and associations/correlations with personal, educational, social, and professional factors. Among 338 ICU professionals (78.1% nurses, 74% women), key diagnostic brain death criteria were well recognized, although misconceptions about nonessential tests persisted. Self-perceived understanding and approval were high (both median Likert scores, 9; interquartile range [IQR], 8–10), while agreement that a brain-dead patient is not a patient but a corpse was much lower (median, 1; IQR, 1–4). Physicians showed greater approval than nurses (p = 0.003) and were more likely to equate brain death with circulatory death (p < 0.001). Understanding increased with ICU experience (ρ = 0.194) and age (ρ = 0.213; both p < 0.001) and was higher among those with prior exposure to brain death or its diagnostics (both p < 0.001). No significant associations were found for sex, religious beliefs, parenthood, or bereavement.CONCLUSIONS:Although Swiss ICU professionals generally endorse and understand the brain death concept, our data indicate specific areas for improvement in conceptual clarity, particularly among nurses and less experienced professionals. In contrast to personal/philosophical influences, clinical exposure increases understanding and alignment with definitions, underscoring the need for targeted interdisciplinary education.

KEY POINTS

Question: To assess perceptions, beliefs, and conceptual understanding of brain death among ICU professionals at three Swiss hospitals and their associations with personal, educational, and professional experiences.

Findings: Diagnostic criteria of brain death were well-known, although misconceptions were evident. While self-perceived understanding and approval were high, agreement that brain-dead patients are dead bodies was low. Physicians showed significantly higher approval than nurses. Knowledge increased with age, experience, and prior exposure, regardless of sex or religion.

Meaning: Swiss ICU professionals largely accept the brain death concept, yet conceptual uncertainties persist. Clinical exposure increases understanding and alignment with definitions.

According to the World Brain Death Project—a consensus effort endorsed by 27 medical societies to guide professional societies in the development of protocols and procedures for determination of death—brain death is defined as the complete and permanent loss of brain function (1). In Switzerland, as in many countries, brain death is legally recognized as death (www.samw.ch/en). The diagnosis is established through the absence of brainstem reflexes, unresponsiveness to both endogenous and exogenous stimuli, apnea, and the exclusion of clinical confounding factors (2). In brain-dead patients, other organs may still function, which enables organ donation and raises clinical and ethical considerations regarding end-of-life care and transplantation.

With advancements in care enabling the maintenance of organ function beyond brain death, ICUs professionals are faced with complex scenarios, considerations, and challenges related to the brain death concept and ethical and legal aspects with organ donation. Legal and regulatory frameworks vary internationally (3) and requests for support after brain death may collide with religious, cultural, or personal beliefs. A survey of U.S. neurologists found that nearly half had encountered such requests, and many continued care out of fear of litigation, highlighting the need for clearer medicolegal guidelines and public education, as requested by the American Academy of Neurology (4). Despite these challenges, studies investigating the conceptual understanding among ICU professionals, along with their experiences and opinions regarding that concept, remain scarce (5–10).

This survey aimed to investigate the individual perceptions, opinions, beliefs, and conceptual understanding of brain death of ICU professionals at three Swiss medical centers. Additionally, the study sought to determine associations between individual beliefs, personal, educational, and professional experiences, and specific perceptions, opinions, and conceptual understanding related to brain death.

METHODS

Setting, Design, and Ethics

This Swiss cross-sectional questionnaire-based multicenter survey was performed at the University Hospital of Basel, a tertiary academic medical center (operating approximately 30–40 ICU beds), and the two cantonal hospitals of Aarau and Liestal (operating 24 and 8–10 ICU beds, respectively).

They survey followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) (11) and best practices for survey research guidelines (12–14). The study was registered before initiation (NCT06640088; ClinicalTrials.gov, October 10, 2024). The ethics committee (Ethikkommission Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz) approved the survey (entitled “Determining ICU Staffs Conceptions, Opinions, Views, Experiences and Reflection of Brain Death and Organ Donation [DISCOVER]”) on August 27, 2024, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and amendments (EKNZ No. Req-2024-01116).

Questionnaire and Data Collection

Between October 2024 and April 2025, healthcare professionals (i.e., all physicians at the time working on the ICU, as well as all nursing staff including certified ICU nurses, registered nurses and nurses in training), were asked to anonymously answer a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire, implemented in a digital and printed version (Supplemental Material, page 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H991), assessed participants’ demographics and aspects related to personal background, religion, beliefs about life after death, the conceptual understanding of brain death, as well as brain death-related experiences and opinions. A series of Likert-scale questions (graded between 1 expressing “very unsure” or “no, absolutely not” and 10 expressing “very sure” or “yes, absolutely”) explored understanding, agreement, and personal views on the definition and implications of brain death and comfort with continuing care of brain-dead patients, as performed before planned organ donation in patients willing to donate their organs. Empathy and emotional responses were assessed with additional 1–10 Likert-scale items examining emotional and ethical attitudes and views on the treatment of brain-dead patients. A checklist tested the respondents’ knowledge of clinical components involved in brain death diagnostics, such as brainstem reflexes and the apnea test.

Questionnaires with more than 50% unanswered questions were a priori excluded.

Study data were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture tools hosted at the University Hospital Basel (15, 16).

Outcomes

The primary endpoints were individual perceptions, opinions, beliefs, and conceptual understanding of healthcare professionals in the ICU regarding brain death. Secondary endpoints were associations of demographics, beliefs, and personal, educational, social and professional experiences with specific perceptions, opinions, and conceptual understanding of brain death.

Statistics

For descriptive analyses, categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of distributions. Group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using Student t test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. The number of participants not providing answers to specific questions were separately presented.

Univariable comparisons involving both categorical and continuous variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons. Analyses for correlations between two continuous variables were performed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. All univariable comparisons were performed after excluding the few participants who did not provide answers to the specific questions.

To account for multiple comparisons, the significance threshold was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. For each of the two aspects “self-perceived clear understanding of brain death” and “Approval of brain death concept” five comparisons were performed regarding the variables “sex,” “profession, experienced losses of loved ones,” “confronted with brain death,” “confronted with diagnostics regarding brain death,” “religion,” and five analyses for correlations using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. We therefore corrected for 12 tests that set the level of significance at a two-sided p value of less than or equal to 0.004.

All analyses were performed with STATA, Version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Three hundred fifty four healthcare professionals working in one of the three Swiss ICUs participated (response rate of 65.6%). Of those, 16 participants (4.5%) were a priori excluded from our analyses due to missing answers in more than 50% (Fig. 1). Demographics, Personal Background, and Professional Experience

Demographics, personal background, professional experience, and knowledge of the professionals are presented in Table 1. The majority were women (74%) and nurses (78.1%) with a median ICU experience of 19 years (IQR, 10–27 yr), compared with 8 years among the 19.2% physicians (IQR, 5–15; p < 0.001). The median age was 39 years (IQR, 31–47 yr) with nurses being older than physicians (median age, 40; IQR, 31–49, vs. 35, IQR, 31–42; p = 0.025). Most reported exposure to brain death cases (96.4% with ≥ one exposure) and diagnostics (81.4% with ≥ one exposure). Nearly half of the respondents had children, whereas the majority had experienced loss of a loved one (88.8%). Most identified as Christian (52.4%) or reported no religious affiliation (43.3%). Belief in life after death was more diverse (median Likert score, 5; IQR, 1–7), with 40.5% expressing belief, 13.9% disbelief, and 44.1% undecided. Belief in reincarnation was less common (median, 2; IQR, 1–5), with only 20.7% indicating they rather believed in reincarnation, while 63.9% rejected it (Table 1).

In contrast, respondents demonstrated a high level of self-perceived understanding of brain death (median Likert score, 9; IQR, 8–10) and a high degree of agreement with its definition (median Likert score, 9; IQR, 8–10). Participants showed low agreement with the statement that brain-dead patients become corpses or movement is compatible with brain death (both with a median Likert score of 1; IQR, 1–4). However, the notion that brain death equals coma was strongly rejected. Most did not perceive care for brain-dead patients as fundamentally different from other patients (median Likert score, 1; IQR, 1–2), and opinions on the need for analgesia after death were mixed (median Likert score, 4; IQR, 1–5). In contrast, agreement was higher regarding the equivalence of brain death and circulatory death in terms of irreversibility (median, 8; IQR, 5–10).

The participants’ profession played another key role: physicians demonstrated significantly higher approval than nurses (Table 3). Individuals with exposure to brain death and its diagnostics also expressed higher approval but not reaching significance after correction for multiple comparisons. Approval was not affected by age, ICU experience, personal losses, religion, or spiritual beliefs. In contrast, variables such as sex, specific ICU profession, belief in life after death or reincarnation, and personal experiences did not demonstrate associations with self-perceived understanding or approval of the brain death concept.

Divergence Between Biomedical and Intuitive Perceptions

Despite high level of agreement with the concept according to the median Likert scores, the median agreement with the statement that a brain-dead patient is a corpse was low (Likert score, 1; IQR, 1–4; Table 2). A majority expressed ambivalence regarding the need for analgesia after brain death (median Likert score, 4; IQR, 1–5; Table 2). Analyses regarding participants’ characteristics and associated emotional and ethical attitudes of ICU professionals are presented in Supplemental Tables 1–3 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H991). Univariable analyses revealed that despite correction for multiple comparisons, significantly more physicians than nurses approved the concept (p = 0.003) and endorsed that brain death equates to the patient becoming a corpse (p < 0.001). However, overall Likert-scale ratings indicated a high level of approval of the concept, whereas approval of the notion that a patient with brain death should be regarded as a corpse was lower.

Similarly, nurses demonstrated greater uncertainty regarding the brain death concept compared with ICU physicians. This was evidenced by higher Likert-scale ratings among nurses for statements suggesting that any motor activity is incompatible with brain death and that coma is equivalent, as shown in Supplemental Tables 1–3 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H991).

Ethical and Emotional Responses

The statement that care for brain-dead patients differs from that of living patients was largely rejected (median Likert score, 1, IQR, 1–2) with variation across subgroups. Physicians and male participants were more likely than nurses and female participants to perceive such differences (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively; Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H991).

Religious and metaphysical beliefs were not associated with interpretative divergence after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H991).

DISCUSSION

This multicenter survey provides insights into how Swiss ICU professionals perceive, understand, and emotionally respond to the brain death concept. While the self-perceived understanding and approval was high, the findings reveal conceptual ambiguities, role-specific differences and associations between professional experiences and attitudes, all of which carry implications for clinical practice.

ICU professionals had a high level of self-perceived understanding of the concept and a strong agreement with its definition particularly among the more experienced—both in terms of age and years working in ICUs—as well as among those who had direct exposure to brain death. This finding supports the emphasis of the World Brain Death Project that clinicians diagnosing brain death/death should be exposed and trained in the respective clinical evaluation (1). Our findings are also in line with evidence that experienced ICU physicians who underwent targeted training or simulation-based curricula, demonstrate higher knowledge, greater self-confidence, and improved communication skills regarding brain death determination and family counseling (5, 17, 18). However, despite high self-perceived understanding, there remains variability in knowledge and guideline adherence, underscoring the need for ongoing education and exposure (17, 19–21). The American Academy of Neurology and the World Brain Death Project both stress that clinical exposure and education are essential in this context (1, 22).

In contrast, belief in life after death, reincarnation, and personal background characteristics were not associated with self-rated understanding or approval of brain death.

Despite most respondents reporting clinical exposure to brain death, some misconceptions persisted. While most identified core components of brain death diagnostics, many incorrectly included nonessential assessments, revealing a gap between perceived and actual knowledge.

The study further found that approval of the concept and acceptance of irrevocability was influenced by professional role. Physicians demonstrated greater agreement with the concept than nurses, despite nurses having more experience. Nurses, on the other hand, exhibited more conceptual uncertainty, as reflected in their higher agreement that motor responses are incompatible with brain death and that coma equals brain death. This difference is supported by the literature and is attributed to the depth and specificity of medical training received by physicians (1). Physicians, especially those with ICU experience and involvement in brain death protocols, have greater exposure to the clinical, ethical, and legal dimensions of brain death, reinforcing their alignment with established biomedical definitions (5, 17).

In contrast, nurses’ understanding is often shaped by their bedside experiences, which may lead to greater reliance on intuitive perceptions, particularly when observable signs (e.g., motor responses) persist (23). Results from a single-center, interview-based ICU survey to identify different experiences of nurses and physicians regarding brain death suggest that education, exposure, and training are determinants of conceptual clarity and acceptance of brain death among all, but these factors are more developed in physicians (23).

Uncertainty about the need for analgesia after brain death reflects the tension between ethical concerns, emotional conflict and lingering beliefs about their possible awareness. Research and guidelines suggest this uncertainty arises not merely from lack of knowledge but from the experience of caring for patients who still show “signs of life” (e.g., heartbeat, warmth, or movement) and from the emotional difficulty of withdrawing care from someone who appears physically intact (1, 22, 24, 25).

Participants’ low agreement with the statement that a brain-dead patient is a “corpse” should be interpreted cautiously. The term corpse carries cultural, emotional, and clinical connotations, referring to a cold, inert body without physiologic function. In contrast, brain-dead individuals retain somatic functions through medical support and are routinely referred to in clinical practice as “patients” or “deceased patients,” particularly with ongoing care. Disagreement with this survey item may reflect discomfort with the language used rather than reluctance to accept brain death as death.

Physicians and male participants were more likely than nurses and female participants to perceive care for brain-dead patients as distinct from care for living patients, suggesting that gender and professional identity may influence ethical perceptions and caregiving frameworks (9, 23, 26–28). Nevertheless, responses indicated a strong professional consensus that brain-dead patients should be treated with the same respect and diligence. Despite most participants reporting on religious affiliation or belief in life after death, this did not affect attitudes or conceptual clarity possibly reflecting a professional boundary between personal beliefs and clinical reasoning. However, this remains a hypothesis as—to the best of our knowledge—studies in this context are lacking and not planned according to ClinicalTrials.gov.

The systematically assessed conceptual understanding, emotional responses, and ethical attitudes toward brain death among ICU healthcare professionals using a comprehensive questionnaire is a strength of this survey. The multicenter design, the large sample size and high response rate across three centers, including both nurses and physicians, improves generalizability within the Swiss healthcare system. Adherence to STROBE guidelines, preregistration, the use of predesigned questionnaires including Likert scales for individual quantification, and the application of appropriate analyses with corrections for multiple comparisons, further strengthens credibility. Detailed assessments of knowledge, beliefs, experiential, emotional, and ethical dimensions offer a multidimensional understanding of how brain death is perceived by those involved in end-of-life care and organ donation.

The study has limitations. The cross-sectional survey captures perceptions and associations at a single point in time, precluding any conclusions about causality or temporal dynamics in belief development. The reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, including social desirability bias, given the sensitive and ethically charged topic. While the anonymity of the survey likely mitigated some concerns, misinterpretation of questions cannot be excluded. The high proportion of nursing staff may limit the extent to which comparisons across professions can be generalized. The study was conducted exclusively in Switzerland, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other healthcare systems.

Although data were collected across three Swiss hospitals, interinstitutional comparisons were not conducted. The study’s primary objective was to explore individual-level perceptions, beliefs, and conceptual understanding of brain death, rather than to assess institutional differences. All participating hospitals operate under the same national legal and ethical framework for the determination of brain death, reducing the likelihood of systematic institutional variation. Furthermore, the study was neither designed nor statistically powered to detect such differences. Finally, reporting interinstitutional comparisons could inadvertently lead to reputational or interpretive bias, as institutions are often perceived or evaluated based on comparative results; thus, excluding these analyses also served to preserve institutional neutrality and confidentiality.

Despite a diverse professional cohort, this study did not assess interpersonal conflicts, role-based tensions, or the psychologic strain inherent in brain death determination. Additionally, the influence of media exposure and misinformation on the conceptual understanding of ICU staff remained unexamined. Finally, the emotional burden resulting from colleague or family resistance following a brain death diagnosis was not explored. These variables represent critical dimensions for future investigation.

CONCLUSIONS

Although Swiss ICU professionals generally endorse and understand the brain death concept, our data indicate specific areas for improvement in conceptual clarity, particularly among nurses and less experienced professionals. This could be met by professional training programs to improve the alignment with biomedical definitions. Ethical and emotional responses to brain death remain complex and are influenced by professional identity more than by personal beliefs.
