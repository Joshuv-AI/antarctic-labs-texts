Altered gut mycobiome and cross-kingdom microbial interactions in systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Zheng Wang, Yida Xing, Mingxi Xu, Changming Chen, Qing Zhu, Huixiang Chen, Yidi Zhang, Wei Chen, Jianhua Feng, Aiqin Zhang, Renzhen Ma, Xiaohua Liu, Shenghui Li, Qiulong Yan, Guorui Xing, Xueming Yao, Xiaodan Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disorder shaped by host genetics and environmental exposures, including the gut microbiota. While bacterial dysbiosis in SLE is well characterized, the role of the gut mycobiome and its cross-kingdom interactions remains largely unexplored.MethodsUsing fecal metagenomic sequencing from 117 SLE patients and 115 healthy controls (HCs), we established a non-redundant fungal genome catalog and revealed significant alterations in fungal composition, function, and cross-kingdom ecology.ResultsFungal diversity was increased in SLE, with enrichment of potentially pathogenic taxa such as Candida, Malassezia, and Trichophyton, and depletion of commensal genera such as Pichia. Functional analysis showed expanded biosynthetic and redox capacities in SLE-associated fungi, including enrichment of RiPP- and terpene-related biosynthetic gene clusters and oxidative stress–related Pfam domains. Several predicted metabolites—such as kynurenine, phenylacetic acid, secondary bile acids, and acylcarnitines—were linked to immune activation and inflammation, suggesting that fungal metabolism may contribute to immune dysregulation. Network analysis revealed sparser and less centralized fungal–bacterial interactions in SLE, indicating disrupted ecological stability and the emergence of fungal taxa as key structural drivers. Integrating fungal and bacterial profiles markedly improved diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.934), underscoring the complementary predictive value of the gut mycobiome. In contrast, post-treatment samples showed reduced fungal richness but no major compositional shifts.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive, multi-dimensional view of the gut mycobiome in SLE, demonstrating its taxonomic, functional, and ecological remodeling. Our findings highlight the potential contribution of fungal metabolic and redox activities to SLE pathogenesis and support the inclusion of fungi in multi-kingdom microbiome frameworks for disease diagnosis and therapeutic development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07423-0.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Gut microbiome, Cross-kingdom interactions, Co-occurrence networks, Deep metagenomic profiling

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease with diverse clinical manifestations, most commonly affecting young women. It is driven by a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors and is characterized by immune-mediated inflammation across multiple organ systems, including the skin, joints, blood, and kidneys [1, 2]. Over the past 25 years, treatment goals have shifted from ensuring survival to reducing treatment-related side effects, limiting irreversible organ damage, and improving quality of life [3]. This evolving therapeutic landscape highlights the pressing need for deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying SLE pathogenesis.

Among environmental contributors, the gut microbiota, particularly bacterial communities, has been increasingly recognized for its role in regulating host immunity and systemic inflammation [4]. Accumulating evidence from clinical and preclinical studies indicates that alterations in gut bacterial composition are associated with SLE pathogenesis [5, 6]. Several studies have identified disease-specific microbial signatures, with alterations such as decreased Faecalibacterium and increased Enterococcus being associated with disease activity and progression [7–9]. Emerging evidence further indicates that fungal communities are not passive part but actively shape bacterial ecology and host immunity through cross-kingdom interactions such as metabolic cooperation, competition, and biofilm formation. These interactions have been implicated in inflammatory diseases including inflammatory bowel disease and familial Crohn’s diseases [10–13]. In contrast, the gut mycobiome remains largely understudied in SLE. Although recent research has reported signs of fungal dysbiosis in SLE patients [14], most findings are based on internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing. Comprehensive characterization using whole-metagenome sequencing, which enables species-level resolution and functional annotation, remains limited [15, 16]. Building on these insights into bacterial dysbiosis, attention is now turning toward the fungal microbiota as a potential contributor to immune imbalance in SLE.

Building upon these observations, our study provides a comprehensive characterization of the gut mycobiome in SLE using shotgun metagenomic sequencing. We established a curated fungal genome catalog to achieve species-level resolution and functional annotation, enabling in-depth exploration of taxonomic, metabolic, and ecological alterations. Functional analyses were conducted to uncover the biosynthetic and redox capacities of SLE-associated fungi, while cross-kingdom (fungi–bacteria) network modeling was applied to assess changes in ecological connectivity. Furthermore, we examined post-treatment samples to evaluate the responsiveness of the gut mycobiome to clinical interventions. Through this integrative multi-dimensional approach, we identified disease-associated fungal signatures, revealed functional and metabolic reprogramming, and demonstrated that integrating fungal features with bacterial profiles enhances diagnostic accuracy. Collectively, this work provides new insights into the potential role of the gut mycobiome in immune dysregulation, inflammation, and therapeutic response in SLE.

Materials and methods

Subjects and sample collection

Fecal metagenomic datasets comprising 284 samples, including 115 HC, 117 untreated SLE patients, and 52 follow-up samples from treated patients (SLE_post), were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (accession: PRJNA532888).

All participants had abstained from antibiotics or probiotics for at least three months prior to sample collection and had no history of other autoimmune or autoinflammatory conditions, active infections, malignancies, or organ failure. Notably, the majority of SLE patients were female(91.4%, 107/117)with a mean age of 30.8 ± 10.9 years and a mean BMI of 20.8 ± 3.1 kg/m², consistent with the known sex bias in SLE prevalence. The HC cohort included 84.3% females (97/115) with a mean age of 32.4 ± 11.3 years and a mean BMI of 22.2 ± 3.0 kg/m².

Fecal samples were collected in a sterile stool container, frozen at − 80 °C within 2 h of sample collection, and then transported in dry ice prior to extraction (Novogene Bioinformatics Technology) using a TIANGEN kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Agarose gel electro phoresis was used to monitor DNA purity and integrity. Samples with an optical density at 260/280 nm of between 1.8 and 2.0 and a DNA level of > 1 µg were used for library construction.

Construction of gut fungi genome catalog

In June 2025, we retrieved 16,634 fungal genomes from the NCBI database. To ensure high data quality, 1,384 genomes were excluded due to poor assembly quality (N50 < 2,000 bp or scaffold count >10,000) or evidence of multi-genome contamination. The remaining 15,250 high-quality genomes constituted the reference dataset. To further improve fungal identification accuracy, we aligned all genomes against bacterial sequences in the NT database and removed genome fragments with significant bacterial homology. Following protocols adapted from previous studies [14], we updated the reference database and identified 1,490 fungal species associated with the human host. These species were clustered into 317 non-redundant groups based on a 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) threshold.

Processing of metagenomic sequencing data

To ensure data quality, we employed fastp v0.20 [17] to perform quality control on raw metagenomic reads. The filtering steps included polyG tail trimming and removal of reads that met any of the following criteria: (i) length < 90 bp, (ii) mean Phred score < 20, (iii) >30% of bases with a Phred score < 20, (iv) sequence complexity < 30% (e.g., repetitive or homopolymeric sequences), and (v) unpaired reads.

To reduce potential false-positive mapping to fungal genomes, filtered reads were first aligned against the human reference genome (GRCh38), the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) catalog, and the SILVA rRNA database [18]. Reads mapping to human or prokaryotic origins were discarded. The remaining reads were mapped to our curated gut fungal genome catalog using Bowtie2 [18], and fungal read counts were computed per genome.

To construct the mycobiome composition profiles, a two-step normalization was applied. First, raw read counts were normalized by genome length to correct for genome size differences. Then, transcript-per-million (TPM) normalization was performed by dividing each genome’s normalized count by the total normalized counts in the sample and multiplying by one million. This approach ensured inter-sample comparability by standardizing the total abundance to one million per sample. Taxonomic abundances were derived by summing the relative abundances of all genomes assigned to a given taxon.

For the prokaryotic gut microbiome, we used MetaPhlAn4 [19] to profile species-level compositions. Relative abundances were then aggregated to the phylum and genus levels as needed.

Prediction and quantification of fungal biosynthetic gene clusters

To explore the secondary metabolic potential of the gut fungal catalog, we first predicted biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) from all non-redundant fungal groups. For each representative genome, gene structure annotation was performed using AUGUSTUS v 3.5.0 [20] with the Aspergillus nidulans model, generating GFF3 files for downstream analysis. The command used was “augustus --species = aspergillus_nidulans --gff3 = on --stopCodonExcludedFromCDS = false $fasta >$gff3”. Subsequently, antiSMASH v 7.1.0 [21] was applied to identify and classify BGCs using the following parameters:“antismash $fasta -t fungi --genefinding-gff3 $gff3 --cpus 10 --output-dir $outdir --tigrfam --asf --cc-mibig --cb-general --cb-knownclusters --cb-subclusters --pfam2go”.

All results under the KnownClusterBlast/ directory were extracted and integrated into a comprehensive table, summarizing gene cluster annotations and their matched entries in the MIBiG database. Using the abundance profiles of the non-redundant fungal groups, we calculated the abundance of each MIBiG_Cluster by summing the relative abundances of species harboring homologous clusters. Differential abundance of BGCs between groups was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with significance thresholds of fold change > 2 and q-value < 0.05.

To investigate the functional potential of SLE-associated fungi, we performed protein prediction and domain annotation for all non-redundant fungal genomes. First, Prodigal v2.6.3 was used to predict protein-coding sequences (CDSs) from each fungal genome under default parameters with the -p meta mode, which is optimized for metagenomic data. The resulting amino acid sequences were then subjected to Pfam domain identification using HMMER v3.3.2 against the Pfam-A database (release 35.0). Domain hits with an E-value < 1e–5 were retained for downstream analysis to ensure high confidence in functional assignments.

In addition, the secondary metabolites associated with these fungi were determined based on previously published data and reference analyses from earlier studies, linking each fungal species to its reported metabolite profile [14].

Microbial co-occurrence network analysis

To investigate the differential interaction patterns between gut fungi and bacteria in HC and SLE patients, we constructed co-occurrence networks using a random matrix theory (RMT)-based approach available at http://ieg2.ou.edu/MENA [22–25]. This method ensures that correlation thresholds are determined by statistical principles, effectively minimizing spurious associations and focusing on biologically meaningful direct interactions. To improve robustness, only taxa present in at least 10% of samples per group were retained. Network visualization and comparative structural analysis were conducted using the igraph and ggraph packages in R [26].

Statistical analyses and visualization

The number of observed species was defined as those with non-zero relative abundance in each sample. Shannon, Simpson, and observed species indices were calculated using the diversity function from the vegan package [27]. A Bray–Curtis distance matrix was generated based on square root–transformed species-level profiles using the vegdist function in vegan. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was subsequently performed on the distance matrix using the pcoa function in the ape package. To assess variance across groups, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted with the adonis function in vegan. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was implemented via the wilcox.test function.

Fold change (FC) was calculated as the ratio of species abundance between conditions. Species with FC >1.2 were retained to ensure accurate detection of significant microbial differences and to enhance result reliability. Taxonomic hierarchies were visualized using sunburst diagrams via the plot_ly function in the plotly package. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with PhyloPhlAn [28] and visualized in iTOL [29]. All additional plots were generated using the ggplot function in the ggplot2 package.

The random forest (RF) classifier based on gut mycobiome data was constructed using the randomForest function, followed by ten repetitions of ten-fold cross-validation. In each iteration, patient samples were partitioned such that each fold contained one-tenth of the total samples while maintaining class distribution. Classifier performance was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calculated using the roc function. Feature importance was ranked using the importance function. In addition, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) models were developed using the LASSO function, followed by ten repetitions of ten-fold cross-validation. Their predictive performance was also assessed using AUC, calculated with the roc function.

Result

Gut mycobiome characteristics and comparative analysis

To characterize the gut mycobiome in SLE, we analyzed fecal metagenomic data from 117 SLE patients and 115 HC. A customized fungal reference database was constructed by filtering 1,490 human-associated fungal genomes, yielding 317 non-redundant species based on a 95% ANI threshold (Table S1). High-quality reads were mapped to this reference, resulting in fungal profiles comprising 166 taxa across 5 subphyla, 14 classes, 26 orders, 47 families, and 84 genera (Fig. S1).

Alpha diversity analysis revealed a significantly higher number of observed species in SLE patients compared to HCs (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.001), while Shannon and Simpson indices showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. 1A). Beta diversity assessed via Bray–Curtis-based PCoA explained 16.5% (PCoA1) and 14.2% (PCoA2) of the total variance. However, PERMANOVA indicated no statistically significant difference in overall community structure between the two groups (Adonis R² = 0.007, p = 0.097) (Fig. 1B).

Taxonomic profiling revealed Saccharomycotina as the dominant fungal subphylum, followed by Pezizomycotina, Mucoromycota, and Basidiomycota (Fig. 1C). At the genus level, Pichia was the most abundant taxon, alongside Rhizopus, Aspergillus, and Aureobasidium (Fig. 1D). Differential abundance analysis identified 15 genera with significant changes (FC > 1.2, q-value < 0.05), including 13 genera enriched in SLE and 2 enriched in HCs (Table S2).

Identification of fungal biomarkers in SLE

At the class level, Saccharomycetes, Sordariomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Mucoromycetes, Tremellomycetes, and Malasseziomycetes were significantly enriched in the SLE group. Genus-level analysis identified 13 taxa with higher abundance in SLE patients, including Candida, Cryptococcus, Cunninghamella, Cyberlindnera, Lodderomyces, Malassezia, Penicillium, Saccharomyces, Talaromyces, Torulaspora, Trichoderma, Trichophyton, and o__Microascales_g, while Pichia and Fusarium were significantly enriched in HCs (Table S2). Based on stringent criteria (FC > 1.2 and q < 0.05), we identified 21 significantly altered fungal species spanning six classes and 14 genera as potential SLE-associated biomarkers (Fig. 2, Table S3), of which 19 were elevated in SLE patients and only two were more abundant in HCs.

Functional and metabolic potential of SLE-associated fungi

To evaluate the biosynthetic potential of secondary metabolites in SLE and HCs, we employed the antiSMASH pipeline to analyze all non-redundant fungal genomes. A total of 2,343 biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were predicted across these genomes. Based on Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (FC > 2 and q < 0.05), 116 BGCs exhibited significant differences in abundance between groups, of which 114 were enriched in SLE and 2 were enriched in HCs. Among these differential BGCs, other/unclassified clusters accounted for the largest proportion (43.0%), followed by post-translationally modified peptide products (RiPPs) (28.9%), terpenes (9.6%), alkaloids (7.9%), polyketides (5.3%), saccharides (4.4%), and non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPs) (0.9%) (Fig. 3A, Table S4). Although the “other” category represented the largest fraction overall, the top 30 BGCs ranked by fold change were predominantly RiPPs and terpenes (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that SLE-associated fungi may possess an expanded repertoire of RiPP- and terpene-related biosynthetic capacities, potentially contributing to altered host–microbe interactions through bioactive secondary metabolites.

Further metabolite profiling indicated that SLE-enriched fungi were involved in producing indole- and kynurenine-pathway derivatives, aromatic phenolic acids, secondary bile acids, acylcarnitines/dicarboxylic acids, and oxidative or nitrative stress-related molecules. Several of these—such as kynurenine, phenylacetic acid, secondary bile acids, and acylcarnitines—are known to promote immune activation, oxidative stress, and inflammation, potentially contributing to mucosal injury in SLE (Table S5).

Pfam domain analysis revealed decreased Carn_acyltransf and Methyltransf_11 but enrichment of diverse catalytic and redox-related domains (Peptidase_S9, Methyltransf_23, FAD-binding, 2OG-FeII_Oxy, DIOX_N, etc.) (Table S6, Fig. 3C). These functions are linked to oxidation–reduction reactions, amino-acid metabolism, and secondary-metabolite synthesis, indicating enhanced metabolic and redox potential in the SLE mycobiome. Collectively, these results suggest that SLE-associated fungi possess increased functional versatility and oxidative activity that may fuel immune dysregulation and inflammatory damage within the gut.

Altered fungi–bacteria interaction networks in SLE

To investigate the potential role of fungi–bacteria interactions in SLE pathogenesis, we first profiled the gut bacterial communities across cohorts. We then explored the differential taxa at the bacterial species level using uniform screening criteria (FC > 1.2 and q-value < 0.05) and identified 48 bacterial species that were significantly enriched in SLE patients and 64 species that were depleted in SLE patients compared to HC (Table S7).

Subsequently, co-occurrence networks were constructed for SLE patients and HC group using a random matrix theory (RMT)-based approach via the MENA platform. The SLE network exhibited 130 significant fungi–bacteria correlations (Fig. 4A, p < 0.05, |r| >0.40), whereas the HC network, under the same threshold, showed only 97 associations (Fig. 4B, Table S8). Moreover, the SLE network displayed a slightly lower average degree (3.09 vs. 3.18) and a longer average path length (3.74 vs. 2.89), indicating a sparser topological structure and reduced metabolic connectivity in SLE patients (Table S9). In addition, SLE networks displayed larger diameter (8 vs. 5) and lower density (0.037 vs. 0.053), reflecting decreased overall compactness. Meanwhile, network centralization (0.135 vs. 0.359) and heterogeneity (0.913 vs. 1.438) were reduced, whereas modularity (0.274 vs. 0.193) increased, indicating a shift from a highly centralized to a more modular network architecture. These changes imply that the SLE gut ecosystem exhibits greater complexity but lower cohesiveness, characterized by fragmented subcommunities and weakened keystone dominance. Collectively, these topological alterations suggest ecological instability and disrupted cross-kingdom communication between fungi and bacteria in the SLE gut microbiome.

In the SLE group, Penicillium sumatraense c21, Cryptococcus neoformans c143, and Pichia fermentans c90 exhibited the highest degrees, indicating their central roles within the microbial network (Fig. 4C). In contrast, Cunninghamella bertholletiae c121 and Torulaspora delbrueckii c85 occupied similar central positions in the HC network (Fig. 4D). Notably, the top-ranking nodes in the SLE network were predominantly fungal species, whereas in HC, both fungal and bacterial taxa contributed to central connectivity. Analysis of degree distribution further revealed that the HC network was characterized by a few highly connected hub taxa, while in SLE, the degree distribution was more even, suggesting a loss of dominant keystone species and a shift toward a more homogeneous, less hierarchical interaction pattern (Fig. 4E-F). Collectively, these findings indicate a potential reorganization of microbial interaction architecture in SLE, with fungal taxa emerging as key structural drivers of the gut ecosystem.

Enhanced diagnostic performance by integrating fungal and bacterial features

To identify potential diagnostic biomarkers for SLE, species-level prediction models were constructed using both random forest and LASSO algorithms. Feature importance was evaluated using average accuracy decline for the top 30 fungi and bacteria. In the random forest analysis, the combined bacterial-fungal model achieved the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.919, 95% CI: 0.900–0.929), outperforming the bacterial-only (AUC = 0.894, 95% CI: 0.881–0.907) and fungal-only (AUC = 0.835, 95% CI: 0.819–0.852) models. Similarly, in the LASSO analysis, the bacteria and fungi combined model showed the best performance (AUC = 0.934, 95% CI: 0.921–0.947), slightly exceeding the bacterial-only (AUC = 0.932, 95% CI: 0.923–0.941) and fungal-only (AUC = 0.826, 95% CI: 0.809–0.842) models. These findings suggest that integrating bacterial and fungal features enhances diagnostic accuracy, with LASSO yielding marginally better results than random forest.

In the random forest model (Fig. 5E), the top 30 predictive species included bacterial taxa such as Streptococcus anginosus, Roseburia faecis, and Ligilactobacillus salivarius, alongside fungal taxa like Trichophyton rubrum c61, Cyberlindnera jadinii c124, Fusarium sp. C181, and Aspergillus terreus c60. By contrast, the LASSO model (Fig. 5F) prioritized bacterial species such as Actinomyces dentalis, Mogibacterium diversum, Clostridiaceae_unclassified_SGB4769, and Scardovia wiggsiae, along with fungal species like Malassezia furfur c28 and Aspergillus fumigatus c47. Notably, certain taxa, including Actinomyces graevenitzii, Actinomyces oris, Aspergillus sp. C56, and Trichophyton indotineae c62, were consistently identified across both models.

Changes of gut mycobiome communities in SLE patients after treatment

To evaluate the impact of treatment on the gut mycobiome in SLE patients, fecal samples from 52 individuals were collected after treatment. Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences in Shannon and Simpson indices among HC, SLE, and SLE_post groups, indicating similar fungal evenness (Fig. 6. However, the observed species number was significantly lower in the SLE_post group compared to both HC (p < 0.001) and untreated SLE patients (p < 0.001), suggesting that treatment reduced fungal diversity in terms of species count. Beta diversity analysis using Bray–Curtis distances revealed limited separation between the SLE and SLE_post groups, with PcoA1 and PcoA2 explaining 15.8% and 11.2% of the total variance, respectively. PERMANOVA (R² = 0.0066, p = 0.376) indicated no significant difference in overall community structure. In addition, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests revealed no significantly different fungal species (q < 0.05) between the SLE and SLE_post groups, except for a marginally significant change observed in Aspergillus terreus c60 (p = 0.04, Table S10). These results indicate that treatment did not markedly alter the overall gut mycobiome composition at the species level.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive gut fungal genome reference database tailored to human-associated fungi, enabling high-resolution profiling of the mycobiome in SLE. Our analysis of 284 fecal metagenomes, including 52 tracked samples post-treatment, offers a large-scale metagenomic perspective that addresses the limitations of previous ITS-based studies with small cohorts [15].

We observed that SLE patients harbored significantly more observed fungal species than HC, although no significant differences were found in Shannon or Simpson indices, nor in β-diversity as assessed by PcoA and PERMANOVA. This suggests an increase in the number of fungal species, while overall community diversity and evenness remain comparable, a trend consistent with Yang et al. [15]. Such alterations may reflect the greater heterogeneity of fungal colonization in disease states, potentially driven by immune dysregulation.

Saccharomycotina, Pezizomycotina, and Mucoromycota dominated the mycobiome, with a significantly higher abundance of Candida in SLE patients compare to the HC group, in line with prior findings [15]. Candida species are known human pathogens, with over 20 species implicated in mucosal or systemic infections [30], suggesting a plausible role in disease exacerbation.

Importantly, several fungal taxa were significantly enriched in SLE patients. For instance, Trichophyton rubrum c61 showed a more than 15-fold increase. Its secreted proteases (Sub3, Sub4, Mep4) can degrade structural proteins such as keratin and collagen [31], potentially disrupting epithelial barriers and releasing autoantigens, which in turn may fuel autoantibody production, a hallmark of SLE pathogenesis [32]. Likewise, Malassezia furfur c28, another enriched taxon, has been shown to induce inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) [33, 34] especially in lipid-deficient environments, a condition often associated with barrier dysfunction in SLE. Moreover, M. furfur c28 may contribute to local immunosuppression by promoting IL-10 expression, a cytokine whose elevation is linked to aberrant B cell activation and autoantibody production in SLE [35, 36].

Next, our functional analysis of fungal secondary metabolite pathways by antiSMASH revealed additional mechanisms through which enriched taxa may exacerbate SLE pathogenesis. Notably, Cryptococcus neoformans c143 and Aspergillus fumigatus c47, both significantly enriched in SLE patients, relate thioredoxin reductase—a key component of the thioredoxin system that protects fungal cells against oxidative stress [37, 38]. Given the established role of enhanced oxidative stress in driving immune dysregulation, aberrant cell death signaling, and autoantibody production in SLE [39], fungal thioredoxin systems may facilitate pathogen persistence within the oxidative microenvironment characteristic of SLE tissues. Furthermore, we identified geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase (GGDPS) enrichment in SLE-associated fungi, an enzyme linked to amplified inflammatory responses in acute lung injury models [40] that could potentially contribute to the pulmonary complications frequently observed in SLE patients. RiPPs and terpene biosynthetic pathways are known to generate bioactive molecules with antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and immunomodulatory properties [41, 42], implying that fungal metabolites may influence host immune balance. In particular, the accumulation of indole- and kynurenine-pathway metabolites could modulate mucosal T cell differentiation and promote Th17/IL-17 axis activation, a pathway strongly implicated in SLE pathogenesis [43]. L-kynurenine, detected in several SLE-enriched fungi, indicates activation of the kynurenine pathway (KP) in SLE. Proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α induce IDO-1, accelerating KP flux and kynurenine accumulation. This pathway has been linked to inflammation, fatigue, and mTOR activation in lymphocytes, suggesting its potential role in immune and metabolic dysregulation in lupus [44–46].

The concurrent enrichment of oxidoreductases (e.g., FAD-binding proteins, Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases) indicates an enhanced fungal contribution to redox imbalance, which may intensify the oxidative and nitrative stress observed in SLE [39]. Such shifts in fungal metabolism could lead to increased production of reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), impairing epithelial homeostasis and amplifying systemic inflammation. Collectively, these results support a model in which the SLE gut mycobiome exhibits heightened oxidative metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, fostering an inflammatory microenvironment that may exacerbate autoimmune activity through metabolic–immune cross-talk.

Cross-kingdom network analysis revealed altered microbial interactions in SLE. The co-occurrence network of SLE patients exhibited more fungi–bacteria associations than that of HCs, with fungal taxa occupying more central positions. Notably, Penicillium sumatraense c21, Cryptococcus neoformans c143, and Pichia fermentans c90 were the top hub nodes in the SLE network. C. neoformans secretes glucuronoxylomannan (GXM), a capsule component that impairs dendritic cell maturation, MHC-II expression, and Th1 responses [47], while prostaglandins produced by GXM or Candida albicans [48] may further suppress antifungal immunity, suggesting a potential mechanism by which these fungi contribute to immune evasion or modulation in SLE.

The Integration of fungal and bacterial features in classification models yielded higher diagnostic performance than single-kingdom models, suggesting that fungi provide complementary information absent from bacterial models. Key biomarkers identified by both random forest and LASSO included Streptococcus anginosus, Actinomyces oris, and Trichophyton indotineae c62. Notably, T. indotineae c62 is a dermatophyte that typically causes facial ringworm, body ringworm, or jock itch, and is easily transmitted between individuals. While it has been reported globally [49], its association with SLE remains to be elucidated.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between gut mycobiome alterations and SLE pathogenesis. Second, although our cohort size is relatively large compared with previous studies, it may still be insufficient to detect low-abundance taxa, and geographic or dietary factors could have influenced the observed patterns. Third, the treatment-related analyses were based on a limited subset of patients and did not account for variations in medication type, dosage, or disease activity, which may confound interpretation. Moreover, detailed information on participants’ diet, smoking status, and pregnancy or lactation history was unavailable in the public dataset and could act as unmeasured confounders. Finally, host-derived data such as transcriptomic profiles, cytokine levels, and clinical indices (e.g., SLEDAI) were not accessible, limiting our ability to integrate mycobiome alterations with host immune responses. Future studies integrating multi-omics data with comprehensive clinical metadata are warranted to provide deeper mechanistic insights into host–microbiome interactions in SLE.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated characterization of gut mycobiome alterations in SLE across taxonomic, functional, and ecological levels. We identified distinct compositional shifts marked by the enrichment of potentially pathogenic fungi such as Candida, Malassezia, and Trichophyton, alongside depletion of commensal taxa observed in healthy controls. Functionally, SLE-associated fungi exhibited enhanced biosynthetic and redox capacities, including increased RiPP- and terpene-related biosynthetic gene clusters and enrichment of oxidative stress–related Pfam domains. Several predicted metabolites, such as kynurenine, phenylacetic acid, and secondary bile acids, were linked to immune activation and inflammation, suggesting a potential role of fungal metabolism in immune dysregulation. Network analyses further revealed sparser and less centralized fungal–bacterial interactions in SLE, indicating disrupted cross-kingdom ecological stability. Integrating fungal and bacterial features significantly improved disease classification performance, highlighting the complementary diagnostic value of the gut mycobiome. Collectively, these findings suggest that the SLE gut mycobiome not only undergoes compositional and functional remodeling but may also actively modulate immune and metabolic pathways, underscoring its potential as a target for future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
