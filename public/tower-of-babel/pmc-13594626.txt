Transduction efficiency in clinical CAR T cell products: A retrospective study at a single center
Authors: Lipei Shao, Michaela Prochazkova, Yihua Cai, Kyu Lee Han, Kathryn Martin, Rongye Shi, Ruyan Yang, Jinxia Ma, Yanyan Zheng, Pamela Garces, Sue-Ellen Frodigh, Victoria Pham, Nan Zhang, Robert P Somerville, David F Stroncek, Ping Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransduction efficiency (TE) is a key measure of CAR T cell manufacturing quality; however, its variability and relationship to the final product phenotype remain incompletely understood. In this retrospective study, we evaluated TE across 204 GMP-manufactured CAR T cell products, integrating manufacturing parameters, starting material composition, and immunophenotypic and transcriptomic profiles of final products. Multivariable linear regression and protocol-specific correlation analyses were performed to account for manufacturing-related confounding. TE variability was primarily associated with integrated manufacturing workflows and starting material composition. The CD4CD8-Prodigy-TransAct workflow was associated with higher TE compared with the CD4CD8-Bag-Dynabeads workflow, although contribution of individual manufacturing parameters could not be independently determined due to protocol-specific interdependencies. Within PBMC-based manufacturing, monocyte abundance was negatively associated with TE, suggesting protocol-dependent contributions of starting material composition. Higher TE was associated with a less differentiated T cell phenotype, including lower effector memory T cell (TEM) frequencies, particularly within CD4+CAR+ compartment. Transcriptomic analyses further identified associations between TE and proliferation- and oxidative phosphorylation-associated gene-expression signatures; however, these findings were exploratory and may reflect protocol-related differences. Collectively, TE variability was associated primarily with integrated manufacturing workflows, providing a real-world framework for understanding CAR T cell manufacturing performance.Keywords: CAR T cell, transduction efficiency, GMP manufacturing, cellular therapy, manufacturing optimization, retrospective analysis

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has achieved remarkable clinical success in treating hematologic malignancies, leading to multiple FDA-approved products targeting CD19 and BCMA.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 The effectiveness of these treatments heavily relies on the efficient manufacturing of the CAR T cell products, particularly in generating sufficient numbers of CAR+ T cells in vitro. Notably, some products have not met the required cell numbers specified in clinical trial dosing criteria,8,9,10,11 which has led to extended culture durations. This extension can potentially result in an increased exhausted T cell phenotype in the final products.12,13,14 Transduction efficiency (TE), which reflects the proportion of T cells successfully modified to express the CAR construct, is a central critical quality attribute (CQA) included for all good manufacturing practice (GMP) CAR T cell lot-release criteria.

Despite the knowledge that TE is influenced by multiple factors, such as cell quality, the type of viral vectors, and process parameters,15,16,17,18 most studies have reported findings from a single clinical trial or non-GMP manufacturing conditions. Large-scale, real-world comparative analyses of TE across multiple GMP-grade CAR T cells manufacturing protocols within a single manufacturing center remain limited. Moreover, many manufacturing parameters are interconnected within protocol-specific workflows, making it challenging to distinguish the contribution of individual process variables to TE. This lack of comprehensive data presents a significant challenge for early-stage therapy developers and small-scale manufacturers, who often lack the resources or infrastructure necessary for extensive empirical optimization.

To date, our center has successfully facilitated a wide range of phase 1/2 clinical trials dedicated to the manufacturing of therapeutic CAR T cell products. All products were generated under full GMP conditions following standardized workflows, with some protocol-specific variations. This comprehensive dataset provides an opportunity to evaluate TE across multiple manufacturing protocols in a controlled, real-world setting.

In this retrospective study, we analyzed a total of 204 CAR T cell products. We compiled detailed data on TE, manufacturing parameters, patient demographics, and cellular phenotypes from both the starting materials and final products. Through multivariable regression analysis and additional within-protocol analyses, we sought to define the range and sources of TE variability during routine GMP manufacturing and to characterize how TE relates to the product phenotype, while accounting for the interdependence and potential confounding of protocol-specific manufacturing variables. Ultimately, our study provides a data-driven framework to guide process optimization and support evidence-based manufacturing decisions for next-generation academic and point-of-care CAR T cell programs.

Results

Overview of the study

We collected data from 204 CAR T cell products across 10 protocols, including manufacturing parameters, quality control (QC) indices, and basic patient information. Details regarding the protocols and number of patients enrolled into the protocols are provided in Table 1. All data collection was completed by April 2025. Each CAR T cell product was manufactured from patient-derived autologous cells collected through leukapheresis and processed under GMP-compliant conditions at our center. The generalized manufacturing workflow outlined in Figure 1A, includes T cell enrichment and activation, viral transduction, T cell expansion, and culture in defined media supplemented with cytokines. Our analysis focused on TE and its variability across different protocols. TE was determined at the harvest day (final product) based on CAR expression (indicated by markers, such as Protein-L, EGFR, and other specific markers, Table 1), with Figure S1 illustrating the representative gating strategy. Overall, TE demonstrated substantial variability, ranging from 9.9% to 89.7%, with a median value of 55.1% (Figure 1B). All protocols required a TE ≥ 15% to proceed with infusion. Only one product, from protocol P_001 with a TE of 9.9%, fell below this threshold and was not infused into the patient.

TE and manufacturing protocols

TE exhibited substantial variability across manufacturing protocols, with considerable differences observed both between protocols and among individual products within the same protocol (Figure 2A). To better understand the sources of this variability, we next examined the manufacturing and transduction-related parameters used in each protocol. As summarized in Tables 2 and S1, many manufacturing variables were highly interdependent across protocols. For example, protocols initiated with CD4/CD8-enriched T cells and expanded using the bag system were activated with Dynabeads and cultured in AIM-V medium, whereas protocols using the Prodigy platform were activated with TransAct and cultured in TexMACS medium. In contrast, peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-based protocols were consistently activated using anti-CD3 and expanded in culture bags. Additional transduction-related parameters, including multiplicity of infection (MOI), spinoculation, transduction duration, retronectin usage, and cell input at transduction, were also protocol specific. Given these interdependencies and the potential for confounding bias, we applied a multivariable linear regression analysis to evaluate factors associated with TE while accounting for the effects of multiple manufacturing variables simultaneously. After data preprocessing and variable harmonization (Table S2), the final multivariable regression model was defined as: TE ∼ Vector + Process_Type + MOI_Category + Culture_Duration. Detailed definitions and harmonization procedures for each variable are described in the materials and methods section.

As shown in Figure 2B, our model identified both vector type and manufacturing process type as significant factors associated with TE. Specifically, γ-retroviral products demonstrated significantly higher TE compared with lentiviral products (p = 0.024). Traditionally, γ-retroviral vectors are assumed to achieve higher TE due to their strong integration capability.19 Consistently, vector copy number (VCN) was also significantly higher in γ-retroviral products than in lentiviral products (Figure S2A).

In addition, protocols using the CD4CD8-Prodigy-TransAct workflow showed significantly higher TE than the reference CD4CD8-Bag-Dynabeads workflow (p < 0.001). In contrast, the PBMC-Bag-antiCD3 workflow was not significantly different from the reference workflow after adjustment (p = 0.34). To further evaluate the robustness of these associations, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding protocol P_008, a small γ-retroviral protocol that differed from the other γ-retroviral protocols in its manufacturing workflow and provided additional separation between vector type and Process_Type (Figure S2B). The association between the CD4CD8-Prodigy-TransAct workflow and higher TE remained essentially unchanged. The γ-retroviral term also remained associated with higher TE; however, after exclusion of P_008, γ-retroviral vector type was perfectly associated with the PBMC-Bag-antiCD3 workflow in the analysis dataset, resulting in singularity and preventing the PBMC-Bag-antiCD3 coefficient from being estimated separately. This sensitivity analysis further demonstrates the strong interdependence between vector type and manufacturing workflow and limits interpretation of the γ-retroviral coefficient as an independent vector effect.

No significant association between MOI category and TE was detected using the available protocol-level information (p = 0.43). Notably, MOI_Category represents an approximate protocol-level indicator of vector exposure, with lentiviral MOI values and γ-retroviral vector dilutions harmonized into low/high categories despite representing different measures of vector exposure. Therefore, this result should not be interpreted as evidence that MOI has no effect on TE. Similarly, no significant association between approximate culture duration and TE was detected using the available protocol-level information (p = 0.29). Culture_Duration was derived from the midpoint of each protocol-defined culture range rather than the actual culture duration of individual products and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these findings demonstrate associations between TE and integrated manufacturing workflows within the available dataset, while the contributions of individual manufacturing parameters cannot be fully separated because of their protocol-specific interdependencies.

TE and starting material

In addition to manufacturing parameters, we evaluated variability in the starting material used for production, with a particular focus on cell viability and cellular composition. As shown in Figure S3A, cell viability was consistently high across most products, generally around 90% (ranging from 84% to 100%), with the exception of one product under protocol P_009, which showed markedly low viability (16.7%). Despite the overall initial high quality of cells, the TE varied widely, strongly suggesting that high cell viability alone does not guarantee high TE.

Previous studies have reported that the presence of monocytes in the starting material could significantly reduce the percentage of CAR+ T cells.8,15,18,20 In this study, we also evaluated whether the cellular composition of the starting material is beneficial, detrimental or irrelevant for TE. To better control for manufacturing related confounding, correlation analyses between starting material composition and TE were performed separately within individual protocols. The percentage of CD3+ T cells varied across protocols and was mainly driven by whether T cell enrichment was performed. As expected, the six protocols incorporating CD4/CD8 T cell enrichment exhibited substantially higher CD3 proportions than protocols using PBMC as starting material (Figure 3A). Among the six protocols that used CD4/CD8-enriched T cells, the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells showed variable associations with TE across individual protocols (Figure 3B). A negative association between CD8+ abundance and TE was observed in protocol P_001 (r = −0.39, p_adj = 0.019), whereas the other associations were weak or inconsistent across protocols. Similarly, the CD4/CD8 ratio did not show consistent associations with TE across protocols (Figure S3B). Furthermore, multivariable analysis within these six protocols also indicated that the CD4/CD8 ratio was not significantly associated with TE (p = 0.32; Figure S3C).

For the four protocols using PBMC as starting material, positive associations between CD3+ T cell abundance and TE were observed in protocols P_007 and P_009 (Figure 3C; P_007: r = 0.89, p_adj = 0.014; P_009: r = 0.63, p_adj = 0.0021), whereas no clear association was observed in P_006 or P_010. In contrast, monocyte abundance (CD14+) showed a negative association with TE in protocol P_009 (r = −0.67, p_adj = 0.0011). Associations involving NK cells (CD56+) and neutrophils (CD15+) varied across protocols and were not consistently significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Furthermore, multivariable analysis within these four protocols also identified monocyte abundance (CD14+) as negatively associated with TE (p = 0.025; Figure S3D). Because both CD4/CD8-enriched and PBMC-based manufacturing workflows originate from leukapheresis products prior to T cell enrichment (Figure 1A), we next evaluated whether the CD4/CD8 ratio in these apheresis products was associated with TE across individual protocols (Figure S3E). Positive associations were observed in protocols P_001 and P_006 (P_001: r = 0.46, p_adj <0.001; P_006: r = 0.76, p_adj <0.001), whereas no clear association was identified in the remaining protocols.

We further examined patient-related variables, including age at apheresis and sex, for potential associations with TE. Correlation analyses between age at apheresis and TE showed variable trends across protocols, with no consistent association observed (Figure S3F). Similarly, TE distributions between male and female donors varied among protocols without a reproducible pattern (Figure S3G).

Overall, our data suggest that the relationship between starting material composition and TE is highly protocol dependent. Within protocols using CD4/CD8-enriched T cells, variation in CD4/CD8 composition showed minimal association with TE. In contrast, within PBMC-based manufacturing, specific immune cell populations, particularly monocytes, may contribute to TE variability under certain manufacturing conditions. These findings indicate that the impact of starting material composition on TE should be interpreted within the context of individual manufacturing workflows rather than as a universal determinant across all CAR T cell production platforms.

TE and final product

We next evaluated the immunophenotypic profiles of the final products and examined whether any features were associated with TE. The overall T cell purity, measured by percentage of CD3+ T cell, was uniformly high across all final products, averaging 98.6% (range: 82.7%–100%, Figure 4A, top row). This high purity was maintained regardless of the T cell enrichment strategy used (whether CD4/CD8-enriched or derived from PBMC). The CD4+ and CD8+ composition of the final products varied across products. On average, CD4+ T cells accounted for 59.2% of the product (range: 3%–94.2%), while CD8+ T cells represented 37.9% (range: 5%–78.7%) (Figure 4A, middle/bottom rows). Correlation analyses between TE and final product composition were then performed separately within individual protocols (Figures S4A–S4C). Variable associations between TE and CD4+ T cell abundance, CD8+ T cell abundance, and the CD4/CD8 ratio were observed across protocols. In protocol P_001, higher TE was associated with increased CD4+ abundance (r = 0.41, p_adj = 0.0027), decreased CD8+ abundance (r = −0.39, p_adj = 0.0052), and a higher CD4/CD8 ratio (r = 0.40, p_adj = 0.0042). Similar trends were also observed in protocol P_006. However, these associations were not consistently observed in the remaining protocols.

The immunophenotypic differentiation of the CAR T cell products was further evaluated via flow cytometry using the differentiation panel markers: naive and stem-like memory (TN/SCM, CD45RA+CCR7+), central memory (TCM, CD45RA−CCR7+), effector memory (TEM, CD45RA−CCR7-), and CD45RA+ effector memory (TEMRA, CD45RA+CCR7-). Each subpopulation was quantified within three CAR+ populations: CD3+CAR+, CD4+CAR+, and CD8+CAR+. As shown in Figure 4B, TEM cells were predominant subsets among CD3+CAR+ and CD4+CAR+ cells, whereas TN/SCM were the dominant subset among CD8+CAR+ T cells. Across the three CAR+ populations, higher TE was generally associated with lower frequencies of TEM cells, although the strength of this relationship varied among subsets (Figure 4C, 3rd column). The strongest association was observed within the CD4+CAR+ compartment, where higher TE was significantly associated with reduced TEM abundance (r = −0.42, p_adj = 0.0041). A similar negative association was observed in the overall CD3+CAR+ population (r = −0.36, p_adj = 0.023), whereas the association in CD8+CAR+ cells were not significant (r = −0.22, p_adj = 0.37). Higher TE also showed a positive trend with naive/SCM-like cells within the CD4+CAR+ compartment (r = 0.29, p_adj = 0.054). Together, these findings suggest an association between higher TE and a less differentiated T cell phenotype, particularly within the CD4+CAR+ compartment.

TE and transcriptional signatures

In addition to immunophenotypic profiling, we analyzed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 80 CAR T cell final products to identify transcriptomic features associated with TE. Our correlation analysis revealed a set of genes whose expression levels were either positively or negatively associated with TE (Figure 5A; Table S3). Notably, the expression of the early memory T cell gene CCR7 showed a positive correlation with TE (Figure S5A), further supporting our immunophenotypic findings (Figure 4C) that higher TE is associated with a less differentiated T cell phenotype. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis revealed that TE-associated genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to DNA replication and the cell cycle progression (Figure 5B). These findings suggest that TE is associated with proliferative or activated cellular states. However, the observed enrichment patterns may not only reflect biological processes that influence TE but also intrinsic T cell properties, such as activation status and proliferative capacity. Thus, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship with TE. As proliferative capacity, cytotoxicity, and metabolic programs are key hallmarks of T cell differentiation, we performed hierarchical clustering based on their corresponding gene-expression signatures (Table S4). This analysis identified four distinct transcriptional clusters with different average TE values (Figure 5C). Cluster 1 exhibited the highest average TE (58%) and was characterized by a strong proliferation-associated gene-expression signature, relatively low cytotoxicity-associated signature, and a strong oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-associated signature. In contrast, cluster 4 exhibited a substantially lower average TE (36.4%) and showed weaker proliferation- and OXPHOS-associated gene-expression signatures. Cluster 3 demonstrated moderately high TE (49.9%) and retained proliferation- and OXPHOS-associated signatures but showed a stronger cytotoxicity-associated signature relative to cluster 1. Cluster 2 exhibited a similarly low average TE (38.3%) as cluster 4, with intermediate proliferation-associated and relatively low cytotoxicity- and OXPHOS-associated gene-expression signatures. Given that these 80 RNA-seq samples were derived from multiple clinical protocols, we further examined the protocol distribution across the four transcriptional clusters. Protocol composition differed substantially among clusters (Figure S5B), and cluster membership was significantly associated with protocol (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001). Notably, cluster 4 consisted entirely of P_001 samples, whereas the other clusters contained varying proportions of samples from multiple protocols. These findings indicate that protocol-related differences may contribute to the observed transcriptional clustering. Therefore, although differences in average TE were observed among transcriptional clusters, these exploratory associations cannot be attributed specifically to TE, and potential contributions from protocol-related factors cannot be excluded.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated TE across 204 GMP-manufactured CAR T cell products generated from 10 clinical protocols. By incorporating multivariable regression analysis and protocol-specific exploratory analyses, we found that TE variability was primarily associated with integrated manufacturing workflows rather than single manufacturing parameter alone. These workflows reflect a series of interconnected manufacturing decisions, and the individual parameters cannot be interpreted independently. For example, starting CAR T cell manufacturing with PBMC requires only a CD3 stimulus (signal 1), while initiating the process with CD4/CD8-enriched T cells necessitates both signal 1 and a costimulatory signal (signal 2, CD28),21,22,23 which can be provided by either Dynabeads or TransAct. In addition, practical workflow considerations impact the choice of activation and expansion methods. For example, the CliniMACS Prodigy system utilizes only TransAct, a soluble polymer conjugates coated with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 antibodies that does not require physical removal in a closed automated system. Consequently, manufacturing variables such as starting material, activation method, expansion platform, and culture medium are closely linked within protocol-specific workflows, making it difficult to attribute TE variability to any single parameter in isolation. After harmonizing manufacturing variables and implementing a multivariable linear regression model, the CD4CD8-Prodigy-TransAct workflow was significantly associated with higher TE compared with the CD4CD8-Bag-Dynabeads workflow. However, because these manufacturing characteristics were highly interdependent, this association should be interpreted as a difference between integrated manufacturing workflows rather than as evidence for an independent effect of activation method, expansion platform, or another individual process parameter. Although the individual contributions of activation method and expansion platform cannot be fully separated in this retrospective dataset, the observed difference may partially reflect the characteristics of Dynabeads, as phagocytosis8,18 may disrupt sustained activation by Dynabeads and bead-based binding can lead to uneven stimulation compared with soluble antibodies (TransAct), both of which could potentially compromise optimal viral transduction. Furthermore, MOI_Category and Culture_Duration were derived from approximate protocol-level information rather than directly measured product-level exposures. No associations with TE were detected for these variables using the available protocol-level information; however, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that MOI or culture duration has no biological effect on TE.

Beyond manufacturing parameters, the cellular composition of the starting material also appeared to contribute to TE variability in a protocol-dependent manner. In our study, two main types of starting materials were used: PBMC and CD4/CD8-enriched T cells. The primary difference between these materials lies in the T cell enrichment methods used for preparation. Our previous research demonstrated that T cell expansion in PBMC-based protocols, whether using density gradient separation or CD3/CD28 Dynabeads, depended on the number of monocytes present in the starting material.8,20 In the current study, we further explored this relationship by evaluating TE-associated variability within individual protocols. Among the PBMC-based protocols, monocyte abundance showed a negative association with TE in protocol P_009. Consistent with this observation, the multivariable analysis restricted to PBMC-derived products also identified monocyte abundance as negatively associated with TE. As density gradient separation does not completely eliminate monocytes, and given the known inhibitory role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) on T cells,24,25,26,27 MDSCs with a monocytic phenotype may impair T cell activation and, consequently, reduce viral TE. Additionally, our findings indicate that the proportion of T cells within the starting material may also contribute to TE variability. Therefore, the observed associations between TE and immune cell composition likely reflect both differences in the abundance of T cells available for transduction and potential biological effects of these cell populations, such as inhibitory signaling or competition during activation and transduction. Because absolute T cell input at the transduction step was not uniformly measured across protocols, the relative contributions of these mechanisms cannot be fully distinguished in the current study.

Interestingly, we found that TE was associated with specific immunophenotypic and transcriptomic features of the CAR T cell final products. TE demonstrated a strong negative correlation with TEM, while showing a positive association, to a certain extent, with increased proliferation-, and OXPHS-associated gene-expression signatures. However, due to the lack of patient response data, we can’t directly evaluate whether these TE associated phenotypes were related to clinical responses or toxicities. Nonetheless, these observations offer new valuable insights. Products with lower TE inherently contain a higher proportion of CAR-negative T cells, as TE directly reflects the fraction of CAR-expressing cells within final products. Consistent with this definition, lower TE products (e.g., ∼30%) are associated with a higher proportion of CAR-negative T cells compared to higher TE products (e.g., ∼60%). However, this relationship is mathematical rather than independent and therefore does not represent a separate biological correlation. The impact of these CAR-negative T cells on therapeutic outcomes, immune modulation, or potential toxicity remains unclear. Our ongoing research work, utilizing a single-cell atlas of CAR T cell products, aims to clarify the functional roles of these CAR-negative cells and their contribution to patient responses.

Several limitations of this work should be acknowledged. First, despite implementing multivariable regression analysis, this remains a retrospective study in which many manufacturing variables were protocol specific and highly interdependent. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, this was a single-center, retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, detailed patient-level clinical information, including disease burden, prior treatment history, lymphodepletion regimens, bridging therapies, and clinical outcomes, was not systematically available to our manufacturing center. Such factors are known to influence T cell phenotype28,29,30,31 and may contribute to the variability in TE. The lack of these variables limits our ability to fully account for patient-intrinsic contributions to the observed heterogeneity. Finally, absolute T cell input at the time of transduction was not uniformly measured across all protocols because of differences in manufacturing workflows and data collection practices. As a result, we were unable to harmonize this variable across the dataset and incorporate it into the multivariable analysis.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that TE variability in clinical CAR T cell manufacturing is primarily associated with integrated manufacturing workflows and, to a lesser extent, with protocol-dependent differences in starting material composition. Given the strong interdependence among manufacturing variables, these findings do not establish independent effects of individual factors such as vector type, activation method, or expansion platform. Higher TE was associated with a less differentiated T cell phenotype and selected proliferation- and OXPHOS-associated gene-expression signatures, although these associations likely reflect broader biological states rather than direct mechanisms regulating transduction. These results provide a real-world framework for understanding TE variability and highlight the importance of accounting for manufacturing and protocol-related confounding when interpreting factors associated with CAR T cell production.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Data was collected retrospectively from CAR T cell products manufactured under full GMP conditions at the Center for Cellular Engineering (CCE), Clinical Center, NIH. A total of 204 CAR T cell products across 10 clinical trials (10 protocols) were included in the analysis. The respective clinical trials were conducted with institutional review board (IRB) or equivalent ethics oversight in accordance with the requirements applicable to each protocol. All data were derived from standardized manufacturing records, QC documentation, and associated databases. For each protocol, detailed information on manufacturing parameters and cellular composition of both starting materials and final products was compiled. TE was obtained from lot-release testing of final products. All data were collected by April 2025.

CAR T cell manufacturing overview

All CAR T cell products were manufactured according to specific protocols. Briefly, autologous mononuclear cell leukapheresis products were collected from enrolled patients. Depending on the manufacturing protocol, these leukapheresis products underwent T cell enrichment using either magnetic bead-based methods such as anti-CD3/anti-CD28 T cell enrichment or CD4/CD8 selection beads to generate highly purified T cell populations, or density gradient separation using the COBE 2991 or Sepax C-Pro system (Cytiva). Density gradient separation generated a less purified T cell populations that retained additional immune cell subsets, including monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and neutrophils. Following this process, cells were activated using protocol-defined stimulation reagents (TransAct, Dynabeads, and anti-CD3), and subsequently transduced with clinical-grade viral vectors (lentivirus or γ-retrovirus) carrying the CAR construct. After transduction, CAR T cells were expanded ex vivo using protocol-specific culture systems, cytokine supplementation, and defined expansion durations. Throughout manufacturing, in-process monitoring was performed to assess cell growth and viability. At the end of the cultural period, the final products underwent comprehensive QC testing, including assessment of TE, identity, viability, cellular composition, VCN, and safety parameters (replication competent lentivirus or replication competent retrovirus), all in accordance with lot-release criteria.

Multivariable linear regression analysis

Multiple manufacturing variables were collected in this study, including viral vector type, T cell enrichment, activation, and expansion platform, culture media, cytokine supplementation, MOI, and culture duration (days). Prior to performing the multivariable linear regression analysis, data preprocessing and variable harmonization were conducted as follows.

1.Variable redundance. In our dataset, culture media and expansion platform were closely linked in practice, with AIM-V consistently used in bag-based expansion and TexMACS used with the Prodigy system. To simplify the model and avoid redundancy between variables describing similar aspects of the manufacturing process, the expansion platform was retained, and the media variable was excluded from the analysis. A similar situation was observed for vector type and retronectin usage, as retronectin was used exclusively in γ-retroviral protocols. In addition, spinoculation and transduction duration were highly associated with Process_Type, as they were implemented within particular manufacturing workflows. Therefore, they were excluded from the analysis to avoid redundancy and collinearity with other manufacturing variables.

Sample number bias. Two cytokine conditions were present in this dataset: IL-2 and IL-7/IL-15. Due to substantial imbalance in sample size between groups (IL-2: n = 188; IL-7/IL-15: n = 16), the cytokine variable was excluded from the regression analysis to avoid instability in model estimation.

After data preprocessing and variable harmonization, a multivariable linear regression model was constructed with TE as the dependent variable and manufacturing parameters as independent variables (TE ∼ Vector + Process_Type + MOI_Category + Culture_Duration). The variables “Process_Type”, “MOI_Category”, and “Culture_Duration” represent the processed and categorized forms of the original manufacturing parameters described above. The variables MOI_Category and Culture_Duration should therefore be interpreted as approximate protocol-level variables derived from the available manufacturing records. Associations involving these variables were interpreted cautiously and were not used to establish independent causal effects of MOI or culture duration on TE.

Flow cytometry assays

All flow cytometry related data were obtained from QC records archived at our center, including measurements of cell viability, TE, cellular composition, and T cell differentiation status in both starting materials and final products. Briefly, samples were processed according to standardized procedures and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human antibodies. Cell viability was determined using 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) staining. TE was defined as the proportion of viable CD3+ T cells expressing the CAR, as assessed by flow cytometry using protocol-specific CAR detection reagents, including Protein-L, EGFR-specific antibody, BCMA-Fc, and SLAMF7-Fc. Cellular composition was assessed by staining for lineage markers, including CD3, CD4, CD8, CD14, CD16, CD15, CD45, and CD56. T cell differentiation status was defined using phenotypic markers32: CD45RA+CCR7+ for TN/SCM, CD45RA−CCR7+ for TCM, CD45RA−CCR7- for TEM, and CD45RA+CCR7- for TEMRA. Data acquisition was conducted on a BD FACSCantoTM II flow cytometer or BD FACSLyric (BD Biosciences), and flow cytometry data were analyzed using BD FACSDivaTM/BD FACSuite and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

VCN measurement

VCN in CAR T cell final products was measured using a Bio-Rad Auto DG QX200 droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). Detailed experimental procedures have been described previously.33 VCN data was available for a total of 131 final products across nine manufacturing protocols.

Transcriptomic analysis

This study did not generate new bulk RNA-seq data. All transcriptomic data from 80 final products were reanalyzed from our previously published studies, which were originally conducted for different objectives.19,32,34,35 In brief, total RNA was isolated from CAR T cell final products using the miRNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using the NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer and 2100 Bioanalyzer, respectively. DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA kit and sequenced on the Illumina Nextseq550 platform. Raw fastq files underwent QC using FastQC and were further processed with Trimmomatic to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. After filtering, the reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GENCODE hg38) using the STAR aligner. Gene expression levels were quantified using subread (featureCounts), and TPM values were utilized for downstream analysis. Gene set enrichment analysis using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was performed with the gseKEGG function from the legendary clusterProfiler package.36

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.4.2). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Multivariable linear regression analysis was used to evaluate factors associated with TE. Exploratory correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Additional statistical tests are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
