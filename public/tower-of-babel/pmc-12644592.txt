Dose-dependent effects of wheat germ oil and vitamin E on apoptosis and gene expression in breast and cervical cancer cell lines with single-cell RNA-seq profiling
Authors: Navabeh Zamani, Roya Bisheh Kolaei, Farkhondeh Nemati, Abbasali Dehpour Jouybari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for natural sources has raised hopes for a natural metabolite with fewer side effects in the treatment of cancers. Therefore, this study investigated the therapeutic effects of different doses of wheat germ oil (WGO) and α-tocopherol (vitamin E) on MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines through the expression of apoptosis genes and Single-cell RNA seq analysis (scRNA-seq). Cells were treated with WGO (15 µg/ml, 7.5 µg/ml and 3.75 µg/ml) and α-tocopherol concentrations for 14 days after plating in cell culture plates. After that, apoptotic cells were examined using flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC and PI staining. Cell cycle analysis was performed by flow cytometry, expression analysis of BAX, BCL-2 and P53 genes by real-time PCR (RT-PCR), and scRNA-seq of cervical cancer cells were evaluated. Concentrations of 15 µg/ml and 7.5 µg/ml WGO and α-tocopherol led to an increase in necrotic and apoptotic cells and a decrease in viable cells in MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines (p < 0.05). While the concentration of 3.75 µg/ml did not show a significant effect (p > 0.05). In addition, gene expression analysis showed that treatment with different concentrations of WGO and α-tocopherol significantly reduced BCL-2 gene expression (p < 0.05) and increased P53 and BAX gene expression in both cell lines (p < 0.05). The study highlights the beneficial effects of high doses of WGO (15 and 7.5 µg/mL) and α-tocopherol in MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines by examining cell viability, apoptosis and necrosis, and apoptotic pathway genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25408-3.Keywords: Breast cancer, Cervical cancer, MCF-7, HeLa, Wheat germ oil, Α-Tocopherol, Apoptosis, Single-cell RNA-seqSubject terms: Cancer, Cancer genetics

Introduction

Cancer is known as the second leading cause of death worldwide. In this disease, abnormal cells grow uncontrollably in any organ or tissue of the body and exceed their natural boundaries and invade adjacent tissues or spread to other organs1. Among women’s cancers, breast and cervical cancer are the most common types of cancer in developed and developing countries, and in recent years, statistical studies have shown a relatively rapid increase in the incidence of these diseases in the world. Common methods of treating breast and cervical cancer include surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Despite significant advances in cancer research, this disease remains one of the greatest health challenges2. Chemotherapy drugs, in addition to side effects, may also lead to drug resistance. For this reason, investigating plant compounds with fewer harmful effects has become one of the main goals of cancer treatment research. Consumption of some foods with antioxidant properties is also effective in preventing cancer. Plants have gained global importance as primary sources of biologically active compounds in cancer treatment, and their anticancer effects are thought to act by inhibiting cancer-promoting enzymes and aiding DNA repair3.

Wheat germ, a valuable by-product of the wheat milling process, is one of the richest and most unique sources of nutrition. It contains 10–15% fat along with other bioactive compounds. Antioxidant compounds in wheat germ include tocopherols, phenolic compounds, and carotenoids. Phenolic compounds in wheat germ oil (WGO) include free ferulic acid and vanillic acid, as well as glycoflavones. Lutein, zeaxanthin, and beta-carotene are the most common carotenoids in this oil. Linoleic acid, the most abundant fatty acid in wheat germ oil, accounts for 42–59% of the total triglycerides, followed by palmitic acid (16:0) and oleic acid (18:1). This oil is particularly rich in tocopherols, especially vitamin E, and contains α-tocopherol and β-tocopherol, which have many health benefits. Wheat germ has many applications in the medical, cosmetic, agricultural, and food industries. Some of these applications include the production of vitamins and dietary supplements, animal feed, and biological control of insects, as well as the treatment of circulatory and cardiac disorders4,5. A study reported that wheat germ was more effective as an anti-apoptotic and antioxidant than vitamin E and was able to effectively protect against the direct and indirect toxic effects of gentamicin on the kidneys by regulating BAX-BCL-2 gene expression and further inhibiting the transfer of mitochondrial cytochrome c to the cytosol6. Research has also shown that changes in the expression levels of BAX and BCL-2 can affect the response of cancer cells to various treatments. Treatments that cause DNA damage or cellular stress can lead to upregulation of BAX and downregulation of BCL-2, thereby promoting apoptosis in cancer cells. Therefore, the balance between BAX and BCL-2 expression is crucial in regulating apoptosis, and the BAX/BCL-2 ratio serves as a key indicator of a cell’s propensity for programmed cell death7,8. Understanding these mechanisms will help us identify potential therapeutic strategies to restore apoptosis sensitivity in cancer treatment.

Vitamin E, particularly its most active form α-tocopherol, has garnered considerable attention for its anticancer properties due to its potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-apoptotic activities9. It plays a crucial role in neutralizing free radicals and protecting cellular components from oxidative damage, thereby preventing mutagenesis and carcinogenesis10. Mechanistically, vitamin E modulates the expression of key apoptotic regulators by upregulating pro-apoptotic genes such as BAX and P53, while downregulating anti-apoptotic genes like BCL-2, ultimately promoting mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis11,12. It also induces cell cycle arrest at critical checkpoints (G0/G1 or G2/M), interferes with angiogenic signaling (e.g., VEGF suppression), and inhibits pathways such as NF-κB that are often implicated in tumor progression13. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated the ability of vitamin E to reduce tumor growth and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents across multiple cancer types, including breast and cervical cancers14,15. While some clinical trials have produced inconclusive results likely due to variations in dosage, formulation, and patient populations the preclinical evidence strongly supports vitamin E as a promising adjunct in anticancer strategies16.

Techniques like high-throughput sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have become essential for examining the diverse cell populations within tissues, understanding immune evasion mechanisms, and assessing tumor responses to therapy in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The TME is composed of various cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells (ECs), and immune cells, as well as extracellular components such as cytokines, hormones, the extracellular matrix, and growth factors, all of which create a supportive vascular network around tumor cells17. The TME not only plays a fundamental role in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis, but also has profound effects on therapeutic efficacy18. For example, single-cell sequencing analysis has been widely used in breast cancer research to analyze intercellular communication in the TME and uncover key factors influencing tumor heterogeneity and scRNA-seq has been widely used to investigate breast cancer19,20. scRNA-seq is a novel tool that helps to identify the heterogeneity of tumor cells and their TME. Since gene expression studies for cervical dysplasia are scarce, our research focuses on malignant epithelial cells in cervical cancer. The lack of treatment options for cervical cancer that is advanced, recurrent, or metastatic highlights the critical need for further research into the tumor microenvironment to identify novel therapeutic targets.

Given the nutritional and pharmacological profile of wheat germ oil (WGO) and the uncertainty over whether its anticancer effects arise from α-tocopherol alone or from the broader lipid/phytosterol matrix, this study was designed to evaluate WGO and vitamin E (α-tocopherol) as separate interventions and directly compare their dose-dependent effects in breast (MCF-7) and cervical (HeLa) cancer cells. Specifically, we assessed metabolic viability (MTT), apoptosis (Annexin V–FITC/PI), cell-cycle distribution (PI/RNase), and mRNA expression of TP53, BAX, and BCL2 following 14-day exposures to two concentrations of each agent. In parallel, we contextualized apoptosis-related gene expression using publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from cervical cancer to relate cell-intrinsic findings to tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

We obtained the scRNA-seq (Single-cell RNA sequencing) data for three cervical cancer biopsies, which include one normal adjacent sample and two cervical cancer samples, from the Array Express database at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress/studies/E-MTAB-11948. This data, associated with accession ID E-MTAB-11,948, was generated using the 10x Genomics platform.

Quality control and integrate data

In accordance with the standardized pipeline, the 10xGenomics scRNA-seq data was processed using R software with the “Seurat” package21. Quality control (QC) was performed on the raw matrix to remove low-quality cells and generate a high-quality scRNA-seq expression matrix. The following criteria were applied: (1) Only genes expressed in at least three cells and cells expressing more than 200 genes were selected. (2) Only cells expressing between 200 and 3000 genes were included. (3) The proportion of genes associated with mitochondrial or ribosomal functions was calculated per cell, and cells with more than 55% expression of mitochondrial genes were considered low-quality and excluded from further analyses. In this method, the “LogNormalize” method in the “NormalizeData” function was used to normalize the scRNA-seq data. Then, the “FindVariableFeatures” and “VariableFeaturePlot” functions were used to filter and display 2,000 genes with high changes after quality control. All Seurat objects were integrated using the CCA algorithm and the “SelectIntegrationFeatures”, “FindIntegrationAnchors” and “IntegrateData” functions. Finally, the “ScaleData” function was used to center and scale the expression data of each gene so that the mean expression in cells was 0 and the variance was 1.

NonLinear dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering

The “RunPCA” function in the “Seurat” package was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) using 2,000 genes and select the first 20 principal components for cell clustering analysis. The “VizDimLoadings”, “DimPlot” and “DimHeatmap” functions were used to visualize gene expression in PCA. To determine the optimal number of dimensions, the “JackStraw” function with 100 iterations and “ScoreJackStraw” for dimensions 1 to 20 were used and the results were visualized with “JackStrawPlot” and “ElbowPlot”. Additionary, to identify cell clusters, the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions were used with a resolution parameter of 0.5 and the “RunUMAP” function was used to reduce dimensions and identify clusters.

Find markers for each cell types

The “FindAllMarkers” function was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cluster. Marked genes were defined with a minimum percentage of 0.25 and a log-change threshold of 1. To visualize the expression patterns of the two marked genes in different clusters and cell types, the “FeaturePlot” and “DimPlot” functions were used with the “umap” reduction method in the “Seurat” package. Finally, the R packages “SingleR”, “celldex”22, and “SingleCellExperiment”23 were utilized for automatic cluster annotation, referencing the “HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData” function to identify cell types. The “clusterProfler” package in the R programming language was used to enhance the marker genes of essential cells for GO and KEGG characteristics24.

Cell line and cell culture

HeLa and MCF-7 cell lines were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran Cell Bank and initially expanded in tissue-culture–treated T25 flasks (25 cm²). Cells were then seeded into eight 6-well plates and maintained in RPMI-1640 (Bioidea, BI-1006) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (Gibco, A5256701), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Bioidea, BI-1203) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator for 14 days according to the experimental timeline. At the designated endpoints, cells were detached with Trypsin-EDTA (Bioidea, BI-1602) under sterile conditions and processed for downstream assays.

Study population

Dose selection and time-points. A prior broad WGO screen (14.65–3000 µg/mL) from our laboratory (Zamani et al., 2024)25 motivated the focus on low-to-mid µg/mL exposures. In the current study, we generated acute MTT dose–response curves at 24, 48, and 72 h across 0.0293–30 µg/mL to select working doses for mechanistic assays. IC₅₀ values were estimated at 72 h using four-parameter logistic fits. Guided by these data, we used 3.75, 7.5, and 15 µg/mL WGO for downstream assays. α-Tocopherol (vitamin E; single fixed dose) was included as a comparator to benchmark the tocopherol contribution within WGO. Subsequent apoptosis, cell-cycle, and qRT-PCR measurements were performed after 14-day repeated exposure with scheduled medium/re-dosing, as detailed below.

The treatment groups were: vehicle control, α-tocopherol (single dose), WGO 3.75 µg/mL, WGO 7.5 µg/mL, and WGO 15 µg/mL. WGO was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (CAS 68917-73-7); the lot-specific certificate of analysis reported approximately 49.8–57% linoleic acid, ~ 16% palmitic acid, ~ 0.2–0.4% total tocopherols (predominantly α-tocopherol), and unsaponifiables including phytosterols. To transparently justify dose selection, we provide our MTT dose–response line graphs for WGO in MCF-7 and HeLa (mean ± SD, n = 3), with four-parameter logistic fits and IC₅₀ estimates with 95% CIs (Supplementary Fig. S2A–B); the α-tocopherol result is overlaid as a single data point on the same axes to contextualize its effect relative to the WGO curves.

α-tocopherol Preparation and dose selection

The α-tocopherol (vitamin E; Merck, 5008621000, ≥ 96% purity) was prepared per the manufacturer’s protocol. A single fixed dose (15 µg/mL; ~34.8 µM) was used as a comparator, guided by prior reports demonstrating apoptosis without nonspecific toxicity in hormone-sensitive lines. Specifically, a dose of 15 µg/mL (equivalent to ~ 34.8 µM) was chosen based on previous in vitro experiments where α-tocopherol induced significant apoptosis in MCF-7 and HeLa cells without causing nonspecific toxicity to surrounding non-malignant cells9,26,27. Studies have shown that α-tocopherol at concentrations ranging from 10 to 50 µM effectively induces apoptosis, downregulates BCL-2, and upregulates BAX and P53, supporting its role as a modulator of intrinsic apoptotic pathways in breast and cervical cancer models28,29. The 14-day exposure period for both WGO and α-tocopherol was chosen to capture both early and late apoptotic events, as well as sustained changes in gene expression, consistent with prior long-term in vitro treatment protocols.

Flow cytometry for evaluation of cell survival, apoptosis and necrosis

After 14 days of culture of the respective MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines under appropriate conditions, cells were harvested after treatment with different concentrations of WGO and alpha-tocopherol. Cells were detached from the culture plates using trypsin-EDTA and collected in centrifuge tubes, followed by washing with PBS to remove residual media and treatment compounds. Fluorescent dyes such as Annexin V-FITC and PI were used to evaluate viability and apoptosis. Annexin V binds to phosphatidylserine, which is translocated to the outer cell membrane in the early stages of apoptosis, while PI penetrates cells with damaged membranes, indicating necrosis or late-stage apoptosis. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V–FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Pharmingen, Cat. 556547). Cells were stained per the manufacturer’s protocol and incubated for 15–30 min at room temperature in the dark, and data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The stained cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer, which detects the fluorescence emitted by Annexin V and PI. The cell populations of interest (viable cells, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells) were identified based on their fluorescence characteristics: Viable cells: Annexin V negative and PI negative. Early apoptotic cells: Annexin V positive and PI negative. Late apoptotic/necrotic cells: Annexin V positive and PI positive. Data interpretation involved quantification of the percentage of viable, apoptotic, and necrotic cells from the collected data.

Cell cycle assessment by flow cytometry

Collected cells were suspended in a 70% ethanol fixative solution and incubated at −20 °C for 4 h to permeabilize the cell membrane and maintain cell integrity for analysis. After fixation, cells were washed with PBS to remove ethanol and resuspended in a staining solution containing a DNA-binding dye, such as PI or DAPI, along with RNase to digest RNA. Cells for cell-cycle analysis were stained with PI/RNase Staining Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. P4170) and acquired on a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Cells were incubated in the staining solution for 60 min at room temperature in the dark to ensure adequate dye binding to DNA. The stained cells were then analyzed using a flow cytometer, where fluorescence intensity data were collected, which correlated with the DNA content of the cells and allowed determination of cellular distribution in different phases of the cell cycle (Sub-G1, G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the Sinapure One RNA Extraction Kit (SinaClon Co., Cat. EX6051) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed with the cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yekta Tajhiz, Cat. YT4500) using the supplied reagents and primers, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (incubation at 42 °C followed by enzyme inactivation). Quantitative PCR reactions were prepared with RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green High ROX (Ampliqon, Cat. A325402) and gene-specific primers (Table 1). Reactions were run on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), and data were acquired with ROX normalization; melt-curve analysis was performed to verify amplicon specificity. After the qPCR run, fluorescence data were analyzed to determine the threshold cycle (Ct) value for each sample, normalizing the Ct values of target genes against those of the reference gene (housekeeping gene: βActin) to quantify expression levels using the ΔΔCt method for relative quantification, resulting in fold change expression compared to control cells for each gene.

Statistical analysis

Data are mean ± SD. For MTT time-course, we used two-way ANOVA (dose × time, fixed effects) with Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests to verify assumptions; when significant interactions/effects were detected, Dunnett’s post-hoc (vs. control) or Tukey’s HSD (all-pairs) was applied with multiplicity adjustment (α = 0.05, two-sided). For single-time-point endpoints (Annexin V/PI, cell-cycle, qRT-PCR 2–ΔΔCt), one-way ANOVA with the same assumption checks and Dunnett/Tukey post-hoc tests was used; two-group contrasts employed two-sided t-tests (Welch correction if needed). IC₅₀ values (72 h) were obtained from four-parameter logistic fits. scRNA-seq analyses were performed in R (v4.0.3) with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control.

Result

Cell subgroups in cervical cancer vs. normal tissue

Using the FindCluster function, 21 distinct cell clusters were identified in cervical tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples (Fig. 1A). These were automatically annotated into 10 major cell types using the Human Primary Cell Atlas via the SingleR package: epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, T cells, monocytes, CD34 + hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), chondrocytes, tissue stem cells, astrocytes, and hepatocytes (Fig. 1B). Marker gene analysis revealed that epithelial cells, T cells, B cells, fibroblasts, and tissue stem cells were the predominant cell types in tumor samples. Clusters C0, C2, C4, and C15 were identified as epithelial cells; clusters C13, C7, and C20 as B cells; clusters C12, C17, and C1 as T cells; clusters C11, C6, and C10 as fibroblasts; and clusters C3 and C18 as tissue stem cells. Additional clusters were annotated as macrophages (C8), dendritic cells (C19, C16), monocytes (C14), neutrophils (C9), and natural killer (NK) cells (C5). Gene expression analysis showed increased BAX expression in epithelial cells, T cells, tissue stem cells, and fibroblasts (Fig. 1C), while BCL-2 expression was elevated in B cells, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells (Fig. 1D). The top three marker genes for each cell type are listed in Fig. 1E. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analysis of marker genes revealed that immune-related pathways (e.g., immunoglobulin-mediated responses and cytokine signaling) were enriched in NK and neutrophil markers. Fibroblast and macrophage markers were significantly associated with extracellular matrix and PI3K-Akt signaling (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5, Supplementary Table S1).

Effect of WGO on cell viability (MTT assay)

The cytotoxic effects of WGO were quantified by MTT time-course at 24, 48, and 72 h over 0.0293–30 µg/mL (Supplementary Figs. S1–S2). IC₅₀ values were derived at 72 h and used to select 3.75, 7.5, and 15 µg/mL for subsequent long-term experiments. In MCF-7, viability declined in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, with a transient plateau at 48 h where 15 vs. 30 µg/mL yielded similar reductions; by 72 h, the curves further diverged, consistent with delayed cell-cycle–dependent killing and/or MTT kinetic saturation. HeLa showed a comparable pattern with slightly greater high-dose sensitivity at 72 h. Significance indicators are shown on each curve and defined in the legends.

Cell viability, apoptosis, and necrosis (flow cytometry)

Flow cytometry revealed that treatment with 15 µg/mL and 7.5 µg/mL of WGO or α-tocopherol significantly reduced the viability of MCF-7 and HeLa cells (p < 0.001), whereas 3.75 µg/mL had no significant effect (Tables 2 and 3). For example, viability in MCF-7 cells was reduced to 43.6% with 15 µg/mL WGO and to 66.9% with α-tocopherol compared to 79.0% in controls (p < 0.001). Early apoptosis in MCF-7 cells increased significantly with 15 µg/mL (25.6%) and 7.5 µg/mL (23.4%) WGO, as well as α-tocopherol (18.2%) (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6). In HeLa cells, only 15 µg/mL WGO (25.2%) and α-tocopherol (23.2%) significantly increased early apoptosis (p < 0.001). Late apoptosis showed no significant differences across groups. Necrotic cell populations also increased with higher WGO concentrations, particularly in MCF-7 (15.4% at 15 µg/mL; p < 0.001) and HeLa (16.5% at 15 µg/mL; p < 0.01), suggesting dose-dependent cytotoxic effects (Fig. 7).

Cell cycle analysis

Flow cytometric analysis of PI-stained cells showed that both WGO and α-tocopherol induced accumulation in the sub-G1 phase, indicating DNA fragmentation. In MCF-7 cells, WGO at 15 µg/mL increased the sub-G1 population to 9.33% (p < 0.01) and in the G0/G1 phase, in α-tocopherol to 68.59% (p < 0.05). Similar trends were observed in HeLa cells, with WGO producing a more marked G0/G1 arrest at intermediate doses. Detailed phase distributions are provided in Tables 4 and 5.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the anticancer effects of WGO and α-tocopherol on breast (MCF-7) and cervical (HeLa) cancer cell lines. Our findings demonstrate that higher concentrations of both agents (15 and 7.5 µg/mL) significantly reduce cell viability and increase early apoptosis and necrosis, with effects being dose-dependent. HeLa and MCF-7 exhibited non-identical sensitivity to WGO/α-tocopherol. HeLa, an HPV-positive cervical line with E6-mediated p53 suppression, often relies on non-canonical stress responses and shows pronounced shifts in BAX/BCL-2 at lower thresholds. MCF-7 retains functional p53 and an ER-positive phenotype, where TP53 induction dominated and BAX/BCL-2 changes were smaller at matched doses. Differences in membrane lipid composition, antioxidant capacity/ROS handling, and compound uptake/metabolism likely contribute to these lineage-specific responses and to the 48 h response plateau observed in MCF-7 prior to further decline by 72 h.

WGO known for its antiproliferative effects and potential antitumor properties against various human cancers including ovarian cancer is associated with fewer side effects. Research conducted in vitro and in animal models has demonstrated that WGO can induce cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest in various tumor cell types. The synergistic effects of certain plant extracts and metabolites combined with anticancer drugs are known to enhance toxicity in human cancer cells30. In our study, flow cytometry analysis was used to assess the level of apoptosis. Viability assays revealed a significant decrease in the viability of MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines treated with 7.5 µg/mL and 15 µg/mL of WGO and alpha-tocopherol, compared to the control group. However, treatment with 3.75 µg/mL of WGO did not result in a statistically significant decrease in viability. Apoptosis analysis showed that concentrations of 15 and 7.5 µg/mL of WGO and α-tocopherol induced early apoptosis in the MCF-7 cell line, and 15 µg/mL of WGO and α-tocopherol induced early apoptosis in the HeLa cell line. Although late apoptosis was observed at 15 and 7.5 µg/mL in both cell lines, the results were not statistically significant. Interestingly, treatment with 3.75 µg/mL of WGO reduced apoptosis in both cell lines, which may be due to the ineffectiveness of WGO at lower concentrations. Şeleci et al. reported that WGO concentrations below 0.1 mg/mL did not reduce cell viability in any of the tested cell lines, which is consistent with our findings15. Furthermore, evaluation of necrotic cells in MCF-7 and HeLa cell lines showed an increase in necrosis at concentrations of 15 µg/mL and 7.5 µg/mL of WGO in MCF-7 cells, and at 15 µg/mL in HeLa cells. In contrast, treatment with α-tocopherol and 3.75 µg/mL of WGO reduced necrosis, with no significant difference compared to the control group. In line with our study, Ergül et al. reported that wheat germ oil exhibited significant antiproliferative activity in the MCF-7 breast cancer cell line at concentrations above 0.5 mg/mL in a dose-dependent manner. Annexin V binding assay results also showed that IC50 concentrations of wheat germ oil increased both early and late apoptotic populations31. The cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic properties of WGO have been demonstrated in several tumor types, including breast and ovarian cancers, through mechanisms involving cell cycle arrest and mitochondrial dysfunction32.

Cell cycle analysis revealed an accumulation of cells in the sub-G1 and G0/G1 phases, confirming DNA fragmentation and proliferation arrest. Interestingly, HeLa cells exhibited greater sensitivity to WGO-induced necrosis, suggesting a dose threshold beyond which cytotoxicity may extend beyond apoptosis. Furthermore, treatment with various concentrations of WGO and α-tocopherol resulted in a significant increase in cell accumulation in the G0/G1 phase, indicating inhibition of mRNA and protein synthesis. This impairment of cellular growth and survival consequently inhibits cell cycle progression33. Notably, no significant increase in G2/M phase arrest was observed in WGO-treated MCF-7 cells. However, statistically significant decreases were detected in the G2/M and S phases of HeLa cells, suggesting that certain factors may induce G2/M phase arrest and disrupt mitosis independently. In line with this study, Victor Alves de Oliveira and colleagues reported that α-tocopherol exhibits antimutagenic activity that protects cells from DNA-damaging agent34. Additionally, WGO is a potent dietary supplement with impressive antioxidant properties35. These findings indicate that treatment with WGO and α-tocopherol not only inhibits mRNA and protein synthesis but also disrupts mitotic cell division.

qRT-PCR analysis revealed that treating MCF-7 and HeLa cells with wheat germ oil (WGO) and α-tocopherol significantly increased BAX and P53 expression while decreasing BCL-2 levels. This shift elevated the BAX/BCL-2 ratio, promoting apoptosis through mitochondrial membrane disruption, cytochrome c release, and caspase activation. These findings align with previous studies showing similar apoptotic responses to anticancer agents36,37.

Omics approaches such as bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq technology has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing cell populations and biomarkers in cancer tissues38. Li et al. (2021) used scRNA-seq to investigate intertumoral heterogeneity of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) based on tumor tissue and adjacent normal tissue from a patient39. Using scRNA-seq, we profiled cervical cancer tissue and identified 10 distinct cell types, with epithelial cells being the most prevalent. Although P53 was not detected, BAX and BCL-2 were expressed in various cell types. Notably, BAX was upregulated in epithelial cells, T cells, tissue stem cells, and fibroblasts, while BCL-2 was elevated in B cells, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells. In line with our study, Bo Sheng et al. also investigated the multicellular ecosystem of the cervix in cervical carcinogenesis and identified potential biomarkers for early diagnosis40. These results suggest that WGO and α-tocopherol may exert anticancer effects by modulating apoptotic gene expression and enhancing cell death pathways. Our scRNA-seq data also provide new insights into tumor heterogeneity and gene expression in cervical cancer. However, limitations include the absence of non-cancerous control lines, lack of protein-level validation, and missing cytotoxicity curves—all of which will be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, WGO and α-tocopherol exert potent anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects on breast and cervical cancer cells by modulating apoptosis-related genes and disrupting cell cycle progression. Additionally, our scRNA-seq findings provide a detailed view of cervical tumor heterogeneity and gene expression at the cellular level. These insights support further exploration of natural products like WGO and vitamin E in anticancer therapy.
