Explicit Spectral Analysis for Operators Representing the unitary group 𝕌 ⁡ ( d ) \mathbb{U}(d) and its Lie algebra 𝔲 ⁡ ( d ) \mathfrak{u}(d) through the Metaplectic Representation and Weyl Quantization
F. Belmonte G. de Nittis

Abstract
In this article we compute and analyze the spectrum of operators defined by the metaplectic representation μ \mu on the unitary group 𝕌 ⁡ ( d ) \mathbb{U}(d) and operators defined by the corresponding induced representation d ​ μ d\mu of the Lie algebra 𝔲 ⁡ ( d ) \mathfrak{u}(d) . We will show that the point spectrum of both types of operators can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues of the corresponding matrices. For each A ∈ 𝔲 ⁡ ( d ) A\in\mathfrak{u}(d) , we will give conditions to guarantee that H A = − i ​ d ​ μ ​ ( A ) H_{A}=-id\mu(A) has discrete spectrum. Under these conditions, using a known result in combinatorics, we show that the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of H A H_{A} is (up to some explicit translation and scalar multiplication) a quasi-polynomial of degree d − 1 d-1 . Moreover, we show that the counting of eigenvalues function behaves as an Ehrhart polynomial. Using the latter result, we prove Weyl’s law for the operators H A H_{A} .

Let

Theorem 1.5

(Weyl’s law)
.

Let

be the eigenvalues of

where

is the measure corresponding to the canonical volume form on

[

It is mentioned there that, for the Laplace operator over a rectangular box (with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions), Weyl’s law is obtained by counting the non-negative integer lattice points inside certain inflated ellipsoid. (see for instance,
[
9
,
4
]

In relation to the Harmonic Oscillator, we recommend

,
16

Preliminaries

2.1
Weyl Quantization and the Metaplectic Representation.

The operators that we will study in this article come either from Weyl quantization or the metaplectic representation, so we decided to include a brief introduction of these objects in this subsection, including the features of them that we will need later.

Weyl quantization
[
14
,
8
]
(or Weyl calculus or canonical quantization) is a map meant to transform real functions on the canonical phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
(classical observables) into selfadjoint operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(quantum observables) in a physically meaningful manner. Formally, for
\hslash>0
and
f\in S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we define
\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f):S(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

[\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f)u](x)=(2\pi\hslash)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)e^{\frac{i}{\hslash}(x-y)\cdot\xi}u(y)\mathrm{d}\xi\mathrm{d}y,

(1)

where
S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
denotes the topological dual of the Schwartz space
S(\mathbb{R}^{m})
(i.e. the space of tempered distributions). Notice that the integral above in general makes sense only as a tempered distribution. Indeed,
\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f)
has a kernel
K_{f}
defined by composing a partial Fourier transform and a linear change of variable over
f
, thus
K_{f}
belongs to
S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f)
is defined by
[\mathfrak{Op}(f)u](v)=K_{f}(v\otimes u)
, for every
u,v\in S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

There are several approaches to introduce and study Weyl quantization (for instance, see
[
16
,
13
,
11
]
), but for the purposes of this article we will mainly follow
[
7
]
, where a more group theoretic approach is used. However, there is a minor difference with our definition. Indeed, if we denote by
\widehat{\mathfrak{Op}}^{\hslash}
the quantization defined in
[
7
]
, then
\widehat{\mathfrak{Op}}^{2\pi\hslash}=\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}
. This lead to some superficial changes in some formulas, but whenever this need to be considered we will recall it.

One of the main properties of Weyl quantization is its relation with the so called metaplectic representation. All the results mentioned below concerning this topic can be found in
[
7
, Chapter 4]
. Let
Sp(d)
be the real symplectic group, i.e. the group formed by all the linear and symplectic maps
S:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Let
\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
denotes the group of unitary operators over
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The metaplectic representation is a map
\mu_{\hslash}:Sp(d)\to\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that

\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f\circ S^{*})=\mu_{\hslash}(S)\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(f)\mu_{\hslash}(S)^{-1},

(2)

for every
S\in Sp(d)
and
f\in S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Observe that
[
7
, Theorem 2.15]
concerns the case
\hslash=(2\pi)^{-1}
, and the general case follows using the same proof.

Once again, we have a minor difference between the way the metaplectic representation is defined in
[
7
]
and ours. Since
\widehat{\mathfrak{Op}}^{2\pi\hslash}=\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}
, if one denotes by
\hat{\mu}_{\hslash}
the metaplectic representation defined in
[
7
]
, then
\mu_{\hslash}=\hat{\mu}_{2\pi\hslash}
.

Notice that equation (
2
) makes sense because
\mu_{\hslash}(S)
is an isomorphism from
S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself, and it extends continuously to an isomorphism from
S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself
[
7
, Proposition 4.27]
.

Some authors call equation (
2
) the exact Egorov theorem because in a certain sense Egorov theorem asserts a similar claim for arbitrary symplectomorphisms (not necessarily linear) but only in the semiclassical limit, i.e. when
\hslash\to 0

[
16

Usually, we consider the operators coming from Weyl quantization and the metaplectic representation for the fixed value
\hslash=1
. In this case, we define

and

.

The unitary representations

are all unitary equivalent. Indeed,

. Furthermore,

- dilation operator, i.e.

Let us consider the symplectic Lie algebra

corresponding to the Lie group

(4)

Moreover, if

\displaystyle 2\pi[\mathfrak{Op}^{\frac{1}{2\pi}}(p_{A}\circ D_{\hat{\hslash}})u](x)

\displaystyle 2\pi(\hat{\hslash})^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{A}\left(\frac{\hat{\hslash}^{-1/2}(x+y)}{2},\hat{\hslash}^{-1/2}\xi\right)e

{}^{i\hslash^{-1}(x-y)\xi}u(y)\,d\xi dy=\hslash^{-1}\mathfrak{Op}^{\hslash}(p_{A})

and this finishes the proof.
∎

Computing explicitly
\mu_{\hslash}(g)
might be difficult in general. One way to do it is, up to a sign, to use the known values of
\mu_{(2\pi)^{-1}}
on the generators of
Sp(d)
given by
[
7
, eq. (4.24), (4.25) & (4.26)]
. Another way is to give another representation
\nu
explicitly, and then show it is unitary equivalent with
\mu_{\hslash}
. Let us begin with the case
\hslash=(2\pi)^{-1}
described in
[
7
]
and references therein. Let
\mathfrak{F}^{d}
be the Segal-Bargmann space (some authors call it the Fock space), i.e.
\mathfrak{F}^{d}
is the Hilbert space formed by all the holomorphic functions
F:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\int|F(z)|^{2}e^{-\pi|z|^{2}}\,dz<\infty
. Also, let
\hat{B}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathfrak{F}^{d}
be the Bargmann transform
[
7
, Chapter I, Section 6]
. It turns out that
\nu(g):=\hat{B}\mu_{(2\pi)^{-1}}(g)\hat{B}^{*}
admits an explicit expression as an operator with kernel (see
[
7
, Theorem 4.37]
). Moreover,
[
7
, Proposition 4.39]
asserts that, if
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
then

[\nu(g)q](z)=\det(g)^{-1/2}q(g^{-1}z).

(7)

From (
3
), it is clear that every
\mu_{\hslash}
is unitary equivalent with
\nu
. Indeed, if we define
B_{\hslash}=\hat{B}\mathcal{D}_{(2\pi\hslash)^{-1}}
, then

B_{\hslash}\mu_{\hslash}(g)B_{\hslash}^{*}=\nu(g).

2.2
The Harmonic Oscillator.

This section is meant to summarize some well-known facts concerning the
harmonic oscillator
and some results from
[
1
]
that we are going to need later.

The classical harmonic oscillator is the classical Hamiltonian defined by
h_{0}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}(\|x\|^{2}+\|\xi\|^{2})
. It is easy to check that the classical flow of
h_{0}
is given by

\varphi_{t}(x,\xi)=\begin{bmatrix}(\cos t)I&(\sin t)I\\
(-\sin t)I&(\cos t)I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
\xi\end{bmatrix}.

Under the identification of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}\ni(x,\xi)\mapsto x+i\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the flow admits the representation

\varphi_{t}(x+i\xi)=e^{-it}(x+i\xi).

In particular,
\varphi_{t}
belongs to the center of
\mathbb{U}(d)
i.e. it commutes with every element of
\mathbb{U}(d)
, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

We say that
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
is a classical constant of motion for
h_{0}
if
\{h_{0},f\}=0
, where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the Poisson bracket corresponding to the canonical symplectic structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Leibniz’s rule and Jacobi identity show that the set
{\mathcal{A}}
of all constants of motion is a Poisson subalgebra of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. It is easy to show that
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{A}}
if and only if
f\circ\varphi_{t}=f
, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\varphi_{t}
is linear and preserves volume, if
f\in S(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
then
f\circ\varphi_{t}\in S(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and this composition can be extended to
S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, as in the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

For each
T\in S^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we define the distribution
\varphi^{*}_{t}T
by

(\varphi^{*}_{t}T)(f)=T(f\circ\varphi_{t}),

where
f
is any rapidly decreasing function on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. If
\varphi^{*}_{t}T=T
, we say that
T
is tempered constant of motion of the Harmonic Oscillator.

The
d
-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator is the self-adjoint operator
H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(-\Delta+\|x\|^{2})
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The spectrum of
H_{0}
is discrete and equal to
\mathbb{N}_{0}+\frac{d}{2}
. Each eigenvalue
k+\frac{d}{2}
has multiplicity
d_{k}=\binom{d+k-1}{k}
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
the corresponding eigenspace and we denote by
P_{k}
the corresponding orthogonal projection. It turns out that the
d
-dimensional Hermite functions
\{\phi_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}\}
form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, where

\phi_{\alpha}(x)=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\pi^{-d/4}(2^{|\alpha|}\alpha!)^{-1/2}e^{\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}}\left(D^{\alpha}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}\right),

with
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots\alpha_{d}
and
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!\cdots\alpha_{d}!
. Moreover,
\{\phi_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0},|\alpha|=k\}
is a basis of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Thus,

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{H}_{k}.

Notice that
\mathfrak{Op}(h_{0})=H_{0}
i.e. Weyl quantization maps the classical to the quantum harmonic oscillator. The following result is the combination of
[
1
, Theorems 1, 2, 3 and Corollary 3]
and, in a certain sense it asserts that Weyl quantization maps classical to quantum constants of motion of the harmonic oscillator.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f
be a real tempered constant of motion of the classical harmonic oscillator. Then
\mathfrak{Op}(f)[S(\mathbb{R}^{d})]\subseteq S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{Op}(f)
with domain
S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an essentially selfadjoint operator and we also denote by
\mathfrak{Op}(f)
its closure. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{Op}(f)

strongly commutes with
H_{0}
. In particular, if we define
\mathfrak{Op}_{k}(f):=\mathfrak{Op}(f)|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, then

\sigma_{p}\left(\mathfrak{Op}(f)\right)=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sigma_{p}\left(\mathfrak{Op}_{k}(f)\right)

and
\overline{\sigma_{p}\left(\mathfrak{Op}(f)\right)}=\sigma\left(\mathfrak{Op}(f)\right)
, where
\sigma
and
\sigma_{p}
denote the spectrum and the point spectrum respectively.

We shall not provide details of the proof of the previous theorem here, but we would like to mention that a key ingredient to do so is to use that

\mu(\varphi_{t})=e^{itH_{0}}.

(8)

Indeed, this identity and equation (
2
) imply that if
f
is a tempered constant of motion, then
\mathfrak{Op}(f)=e^{itH_{0}}\mathfrak{Op}(f)e^{-itH_{0}}
, which formally says that
\mathfrak{Op}(f)
commutes with
H_{0}
.

It is straightforward to show that, if
A\in\mathfrak{u}(d)\subset\mathfrak{sp}(d)
, then
p_{A}
is a tempered constant of motion. In particular, the spectral decomposition given in theorem
2.3
holds for
H_{A}=\mathfrak{Op}(p_{A})
, and this will allow us to compute the spectra of those operators.

Restricting the metaplectic representation to
\mathbb{U}(d)
also leads to operators strongly commuting with
H_{0}
. Indeed, as we mentioned before
\varphi_{t}
commutes with every
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
. Applying the metaplectic representation and (
8
) we obtain that
\mu(g)e^{itH_{0}}=e^{itH_{0}}\mu(g)
( corollary 4 in
[
1
]
). Thus,

\langle\mu(g)\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle=\langle e^{itH_{0}}\mu(g)e^{-itH_{0}}\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle=e^{it(|\alpha|-|\beta|)}\langle\mu(g)\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle.

Then
\langle\mu(g)\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle=0
, unless
|\alpha|=|\beta|
. Hence, each
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is invariant by
\mu(g)
. Therefore, if we denote by
\mu_{k}(g):=\mu(g)|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, we obtain

\mu(g)=\bigoplus_{k}\mu_{k}(g),

(9)

for every
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
. Derivating the previous identity and applying Theorem
2.3
we obtain the following result
[
1
, Corollary 5]
.

Corollary 2.4
.

For each
A\in\mathfrak{u}(d)
, the operator
H_{A}=\mathfrak{Op}(p_{A})=-id\mu(A)
with domain
S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is essentially selfadjoint and it strongly commutes with
H_{0}
. Moreover, the map
\mathrm{d}\mu
admits the decomposition

\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigoplus_{k}\mathrm{d}\mu_{k},

where
\mu_{k}(g):=\mu(g)|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, for each
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

3
Computing Spectra

Abusing of the notation, we will denote by
\mu
the restriction of the metaplectic representation to the complex unitary group
\mathbb{U}(d)
. According to identity (
9
), in order to compute the (point) spectrum of
\mu(g)
, it is enough to compute the spectrum of
\mu_{k}(g)
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
. Equivalently, we might compute the spectrum of
\nu_{k}(g)=B\mu_{k}(g)B^{*}=\nu(g)|_{\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{k}}
, where
\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{k}:=B(\mathcal{H}_{k})
. Besides
\nu_{k}
, we will need another unitary equivalent representation of
\mu_{k}
. It is well known that
\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{k}
is the subspace generated by the monomials of total degree
k
. Let
S^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
denotes the
k
-th symmetric power of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\{e_{j}\}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For each
j_{1},\cdots j_{k}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
, let
q_{j_{1},\cdots j_{k}}(z)=z_{j_{1}}\cdots z_{j_{k}}
. It is well know that the map
T_{k}:S^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{k}
given by

T_{k}(e_{j_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot e_{j_{k}})=q_{j_{1},\cdots j_{k}}

extends to an isomorphism of vector spaces, where
\odot
denotes the symmetric tensor product. It is straightforward to check that

T_{k}(v_{1}\odot\cdots\odot v_{k})=T_{1}(v_{1})\cdots T_{1}(v_{k}).

(10)

Moreover,
T_{1}(v)=T_{1}(\sum\langle e_{j},v\rangle e_{j})=\sum\langle e_{j},v\rangle z_{j}
, thus

[T_{1}(v)](z)=\langle\overline{v},z\rangle.

The following result is apparently well known, but we could not find it stated, nor proved, in the literature. It will be the main tool to prove theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\eta_{k}(g):=T_{k}^{-1}\nu_{k}(g)T_{k}:S^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to S^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. Then

\eta_{k}(g)(v_{1}\odot v_{2}\odot\cdots\odot v_{k})=\det(g)^{-1/2}(\overline{g}v_{1})\odot(\overline{g}v_{2})\odot\cdots\odot(\overline{g}v_{k}),

(11)

for any
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
and
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots v_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Proof.

Clearly we have that

[T_{1}(\overline{g}v)](z)=\langle\overline{\overline{g}v},z\rangle=\langle g\overline{v},z\rangle=\langle\overline{v},g^{-1}z\rangle=\det(g)^{1/2}[\nu_{1}(g)T_{1}(v)](z)

and this equivalent to equation (
11
) for
k=1
. The general case follows from equation (
10
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
v_{1},\cdots v_{d}
be a basis of eigenectors of
g
corresponding to
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\eta_{k}(g)(\overline{v_{j_{1}}}\odot\cdots\odot\overline{v_{j_{k}}})

\displaystyle=\det(g)^{-1/2}(\overline{gv_{j_{1}}})\odot\cdots\odot(\overline{gv_{j_{k}}})

(12)

\displaystyle=\det(g)^{-1/2}\cdot\overline{\theta}_{j_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta}_{j_{k}}(\overline{v_{j_{1}}}\odot\cdots\odot\overline{v_{j_{k}}})

(13)

This together with equation (
10
) shows that the polynomial
q
given in theorem
1.2
is an eigenvector of
\nu(g)
with eigenvalue
\theta=\det(g)^{-1/2}\cdot\overline{\theta_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{d}}^{n_{d}}
, and therefore
\hat{B}^{*}q
is an eigenvector of
\mu(g)
with the same eigenvalue.

Since the collection of vectors

with

forms a basis of

\sigma(\mu_{k}(g))=\sigma(\eta_{k}(g))=\det(g)^{-1/2}\{\overline{\theta}_{j_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta}_{j_{k}}\mid j_{i}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}\}.

Hence, the decomposition of

\det(g)^{-1/2}\cdot\{\overline{\theta}_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta}_{d}^{n_{d}}\mid(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}\subseteq\sigma_{p}(\mu(g)).

Conversely, if

, for some

and

for every

for some

.

For the second claim, it is well known that every irrational rotation generates a dense orbit in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The converse will follow from proving the last claim of our result (concerning the case of rational rotations eigenvalues). In that case, let
Z(\theta)
be the additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
generated by
\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}},\cdots,\frac{p_{d}}{q_{d}}
. Then multiplying by

maps

into the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}
generated by

. It is well known that such subgroup is generated by the greatest common divisor of

and this implies our result.

∎

Taking

4
Multiplicity, spectral distribution and Weyl’s law

In this section, we will look for conditions to ensure that the spectrum of the operators studied in the previous section is discrete.

As we mentioned in the introduction, the operator
\mu(g)
does not have discrete spectrum, for any
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
. Indeed, if this was not the case then the eigenvalues of
g
must be rational rotations, otherwise theorem
1.2
would imply that the eigenvalues of
\mu(g)
would not be isolated. Assume that the eigenvalues of
g\in\mathbb{U}(d)
are
\theta_{1}=\text{exp}(2\pi i\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}),\cdots,\theta_{d}=\text{exp}(2\pi i\frac{p_{d}}{q_{d}})
, with
p_{1},\cdots p_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q_{1},\cdots q_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
. Equation (
12
) implies that for each
(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
there are at least
\binom{M_{1}+n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\cdots\binom{M_{d}+n_{d}-1}{n_{d}}
linearly independent eigenvectors of
\mu(g)
with eigenvalue
\lambda=\overline{\theta_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{d}}^{n_{d}}
, where
M_{j}
is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\theta_{j}
. Fix
(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. Then we have that
\overline{\theta_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{j}}^{n_{j}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{d}}^{n_{d}}=\overline{\theta_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{j}}^{n_{j}+tq_{j}}\cdots\overline{\theta_{d}}^{n_{d}}
, for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, the multiplicity of every eigenvalue of
\mu(g)
is infinite.

Let us calculate the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of
H_{A}
.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

For each
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(H_{A})
, let

M_{\lambda}=\left\{(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}\;\left|\;-\sum n_{j}s_{j}+\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A)=\lambda\right\}\right.

We claim that
m_{A}(\lambda)=\#(M_{\lambda})
. Indeed, since
H_{A}
strongly commutes with the Harmonic Oscillator, the eigenspace of
H_{A}
corresponding to
\lambda
is the orthogonal sum over
k
of the eigenspaces of
H_{A}|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
corresponding to
\lambda
. Furthermore, since each
T_{k}
is an isomorphism, if we apply
T_{k}
to the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda
given by equation (
14
) we obtain a basis of the eigenspace of
BH_{A}B^{*}|_{\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{k}}
corresponding to
\lambda
, and this implies our claim. In particular,
\lambda
has finite multiplicity if and only if
M_{\lambda}
is finite.

Let us assume that
s_{j}=p_{j}x
, with
p_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and suppose that there are
j_{0}
and
j_{1}
such that
s_{j_{0}}<0<s_{j_{1}}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
p_{j_{0}}<0<p_{j_{1}}
. Fix
n\in M_{\lambda}
. Clearly, if we define
m_{j_{0}}=n_{j_{0}}+tp_{j_{1}}
,
m_{j_{1}}=n_{j_{1}}-tp_{j_{0}}
and
m_{j}=n_{j}
for
j\neq j_{0},j_{1}
, then
m\in M_{\lambda}
for every

. Therefore, if
s_{1},\cdots,s_{d}
do not have the same sign, then
M_{\lambda}
is an infinite set. As we mentioned in the introduction, when
p_{1},\cdots p_{d}
have the same sign, the expression of the multiplicity function
m_{A}
follows from

or references therein.
∎

A function admitting the type of expression described in proposition
1.3
for the multiplicity function
m_{A}
is called a semi (or quasi)-polynomial of degree
d-1
relative to modulus
d!
(see
[

]
). Notice that in the case
A=\mathfrak{J}
this property is trivially satisfied; indeed, if
\lambda=k+d/2
then
m(\lambda)=\binom{d+k-1}{k}
.

For simplicity, in what follows we will assume that
s_{j}=p_{j}x<0
, and we will choose
x>0
.

Before going into the proof of theorem
1.4
, let us recall the definition of an Ehrhart polynomial. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a polyhedra having vertexes with integer coordinates and define

i(\mathcal{P},k):=\#\left(k\mathcal{P}\bigcap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right).

(15)

E. Ehrhart showed that the map
i(\mathcal{P},k)
is a polynomial in
k
(for instance, see
[
5
,
12
,
3
]
), and this was the starting point of an important theory in combinatorics.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

From the proof of Proposition
1.3
, it is clear that the counting of eigenvalues function is given by

N_{A}(r)=\#\{\lambda\in\sigma(H_{A})\mid\lambda\leq r\}=\#\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}\;\left|\;-\sum n_{j}s_{j}+\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A)\leq r\right\}\right.

Let
q
be the minimal common multiple of
-p_{1},-p_{2},\cdots-p_{d}
and let
\mathcal{P}
be the polyhedra defined in the statement of Theorem
1.4
. Then the vertexes of
\mathcal{P}
are
V=\{-qp_{j}^{-1}\mid 1\leq j\leq d\}\cup\{0\}
. It is straightforward to show that

i(\mathcal{P},k)=N_{A}\left(kqx+\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A)\right).

Taking
k=[q^{-1}x^{-1}(r-i\text{tr}(A)/2)]
, we obtain that
kqx+\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A)\leq r
and the inequality in the statement of Theorem
1.4
follows. The value of the coefficients
c_{d},c_{d-1}
and
c_{0}
is a well known fact from the theory of Ehrhart polynomials (for instance, see subsection 1.3 in
[
3
]
).
∎

Finally, let us prove the Weyl’s law claimed in Theorem

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
f(x)=\sum|s_{j}|x_{j}
and
g(x,\xi)=2^{-1}\sum|s_{j}|(x_{j}^{2}+\xi_{j}^{2})
respectively. For each
t>0
, define
\mathcal{P}(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid x\geq 0,f(x)\leq t\}
,
F(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid x\geq 0,f(x)=t\}
and
\mathcal{E}(t)=\{(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\mid g(x,\xi)\leq t\}
. Then,

|\mathcal{P}(t)|=(2\pi)^{-d}|\mathcal{E}(t)|=\frac{t^{d}}{d!|s_{1}|\cdot|s_{2}|\cdots|s_{d}|},

|F(t)|=|F(1)|t^{d-1}=\frac{\|s\|t^{d-1}}{(d-1)!s_{1}\cdots s_{d}}

(16)

and

|F(t)|=(2\pi)^{-d}\|s\|\int_{\partial\mathcal{E}(t)}\|\nabla g\|^{-1}d\mu_{t},

where
\mu_{t}
is the measure corresponding to the canonical volume form on
\partial\mathcal{E}(t)
.

Proof.

The first identity regarding the volume of the polyhedra
\mathcal{P}(t)
and the ellipsoids
\mathcal{E}(t)
are well known (and trivial). Clearly
\nabla f(x)=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
. Then coarea formula (for instance, see theorem 3.2.12 in
[
6
]
) implies that

|\mathcal{P}(t)|=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{F(r)}\|\nabla f\|^{-1}d\eta_{r}\right)dr=\|s\|^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}|F(r)|dr,

where
\eta_{r}
is the measure corresponding to the canonical volume form on the
(d-1)
-face
F(r)
. Derivating the previous equality with respect to
t
leads to equation (
16
). Similarly, we have that

|\mathcal{E}(t)|=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\partial\mathcal{E}(r)}\|\nabla g\|^{-1}d\mu_{r}\right)dr

and derivating with respect to
t
we obtain the second identity.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The inequality established in Theorem
1.4
implies that in order to obtain the asymptotic growth claimed in Theorem
1.5
, we can assume that
(qx)^{-1}(\frac{r}{\hslash}-\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A))
belongs to
\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\displaystyle N^{\hslash}_{A}(r)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}\left[(qx)^{-1}(\frac{r}{\hslash}-\frac{i}{2}\text{tr}(A))\right]^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{d}(qx)^{-d}(\hslash^{-1}r)^{d}+(c_{d-1}(qx)^{-d+1}-\frac{i}{2}dc_{d}(qx)^{-d}\text{tr}(A))(\hslash^{-1}r)^{d-1}

\displaystyle+O\left((\hslash^{-1}r)^{d-2}\right)

Since
c_{d}=|\mathcal{P}(qx)|
,
i\text{tr}(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|s_{j}|
and

c_{d-1}=\frac{1}{2}|\partial\mathcal{P}(qx)|=\frac{(qx)^{d-1}}{2(d-1)!}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{|s_{j}|}{|s_{1}|\cdot|s_{2}|\cdots|s_{d}|}+\frac{1}{2}|F(qx)|,

the previous lemma implies that

\displaystyle N^{\hslash}_{A}(r)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\hslash)^{-d}|\mathcal{E}(r)|+\frac{\hslash^{-d+1}}{2}|F(r)|+O\left((\hslash^{-1}r)^{d-2}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\hslash^{-d}|\mathcal{E}(r)|+\frac{\|s\|}{2}(2\pi)^{-d}\hslash^{-d+1}\int_{\partial\mathcal{E}(t)}\|\nabla g\|^{-1}d\mu_{t}+O\left((\hslash^{-1}r)^{d-2}\right)

Since
A\in\mathfrak{u}(d)
, there is
U\in\mathbb{U}(d)
such that
UAU^{*}=D
, where
D
is the diagonal matrix with entrances
is_{1},is_{2},\cdots is_{d}
. Recall that, under the identification of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the Lie group
\mathbb{U}(d)
corresponds with
\mathbb{O}(2d)\cap Sp(d)
. Thus,

p_{A}(w)=-\frac{1}{2}\langle w,A\mathfrak{J}w\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle w,U^{*}DU\mathfrak{J}w\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle Uw,\mathfrak{J}DUw\rangle=g(Uw).

Since
U
preserves volume, we have that

|\mathcal{E}(t)|=|\{(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\mid p_{A}(x,\xi)\leq t\}|.

Applying coarea formula, we obtain that

\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\partial\mathcal{E}(r)}\|\nabla g\|^{-1}d\mu_{r}\right)dr=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\{(x,\xi)\mid p_{A}(x,\xi)=r\}}\|\nabla p_{A}\|^{-1}d\mu_{r}^{A}\right)dr,

where
\mu_{t}^{A}
is the measure corresponding to the canonical volume form on
\{(x,\xi)\mid p_{A}(x,\xi)=r\}
. Derivating both sides of the previous identity, we have that

\int_{\partial\mathcal{E}(r)}\|\nabla g\|^{-1}=\int_{\{(x,\xi)\mid p_{A}(x,\xi)=r\}}\|\nabla p_{A}\|^{-1}

and this finishes the proof.
∎
