Photo Quiz: Globose Yeast‐Like Forms With Multiple Peripheral Buds in Urine and Cervical Cytology
Authors: Adil Menon, Rachel Koch, Caroline Abramovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 26‐year‐old immunocompetent woman with a history of noninvasive, low‐grade papillary urothelial carcinoma presented with persistent upper abdominal pain. Urine cytology, performed for bladder tumor surveillance, revealed globose, yeast‐like forms (10–30 μm) with thick walls and multiple narrowly attached peripheral buds in a “ship wheel” configuration—morphology classically associated with Paracoccidioides brasiliensis. Three months later, morphologically identical organisms were identified on routine cervical Papanicolaou (Pap) test, confirmed by PAS and GMS stains on cell block material. Neither specimen showed significant inflammation or epithelial abnormality. Clinical and epidemiologic features were inconsistent with paracoccidioidomycosis: the patient was a young woman of reproductive age (male‐to‐female ratio 13–20:1 in chronic paracoccidioidomycosis), had no travel to an endemic region, and lacked pulmonary, mucocutaneous, or systemic disease. The morphologic findings were attributed to Cokeromyces recurvatus, a rare dimorphic mucormycete whose sporangiola are virtually indistinguishable from the multiple‐budding yeast cells of P. brasiliensis on cytologic preparations. This case is novel in two respects: (1) it represents the first reported simultaneous detection of C. recurvatus in both urine and cervical cytology from the same patient, demonstrating multisite genitourinary colonization and (2) identification via urine cytology performed for bladder tumor surveillance extends awareness of this organism beyond the Pap test setting to urinary cytopathology. With fewer than 15 published human cases, this report reinforces that morphology alone is insufficient for definitive fungal identification when an endemic dimorphic fungus is suspected, and that culture or molecular confirmation (broad‐range fungal PCR with DNA sequencing) is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary prolonged antifungal therapy.

Case Presentation

A 26‐year‐old woman with a history of noninvasive, low‐grade papillary urothelial carcinoma presented with persistent upper abdominal pain of several months' duration. The pain was localized to the upper abdomen near the inner rib margin and left of the umbilicus, described as a “pinching” and “sore” sensation, not relieved by acetaminophen and not associated with nausea, vomiting, weight loss, or night sweats.

Urine cytology, performed as part of bladder tumor surveillance, revealed multiple rounded, globose, yeast‐like forms measuring 10–30 μm in diameter with thick walls and multiple narrowly attached peripheral buds arranged in a “ship wheel” configuration (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4). No significant inflammatory background was present. No epithelial atypia was identified.

Discussion

The morphologic appearance of globose, yeast‐like forms (10–30 μm) with multiple narrowly attached peripheral buds in a “ship wheel” or “mariner's wheel” pattern is classically associated with P. brasiliensis, the agent of paracoccidioidomycosis [1, 2]. However, this case illustrates a well‐documented but under‐recognized diagnostic pitfall: C. recurvatus, a rare dimorphic mucormycete (order Mucorales), produces sporangiola—small, spherical reproductive structures borne on recurved sporangiophores—that are morphologically virtually indistinguishable from the multiple‐budding yeast cells of P. brasiliensis in cytologic preparations [3, 4].

This pitfall has been documented in multiple prior reports. Odronic et al. described two cases of C. recurvatus in liquid‐based Pap tests from healthy, asymptomatic women (ages 26 and 47) in which the morphology was initially interpreted as consistent with P. brasiliensis ; broad‐range fungal PCR and DNA sequencing showed an exact match for C. recurvatus [7]. Paquette et al. reported C. recurvatus in a Pap test from an asymptomatic pregnant patient, noting that “rare fungi were considered including Paracoccidioides brasiliensis due to the ‘mariner's wheel’ morphology” before culture confirmed C. recurvatus [8]. Nielsen et al. reported C. recurvatus from peritoneal fluid in a cat, initially misidentified as Coccidioides immitis, and noted that the tissue form of C. recurvatus is “morphologically similar to those of Coccidioides immitis and Paracoccidioides brasiliensis and may be misdiagnosed as one of these organisms on the basis of cytologic or histopathologic specimens” [4]. Kemna et al. described C. recurvatus isolated from endocervical specimens in a diabetic patient, with fungus‐like structures observed in Papanicolaou‐stained cervical smears prepared 1 year apart [9].

Notably, Odronic et al. suggested that two prior case reports describing P. brasiliensis in cervical Pap tests—including the report by Alsharif et al. of “ship's‐wheel” morphology in a liquid‐based Pap test from a healthy 29‐year‐old pregnant woman diagnosed as “consistent with Paracoccidioides” without culture or molecular confirmation—may have been, in fact, misdiagnoses of C. recurvatus [7]. This hypothesis underscores the critical importance of molecular or culture‐based confirmation when morphology alone suggests an endemic dimorphic fungus in the female genital tract.

The present case adds to this literature in two respects. First, it represents the first reported instance of C. recurvatus detected simultaneously in both urine and cervical cytology from the same patient, suggesting colonization of the genitourinary tract at multiple sites. Second, identification through urine cytology performed for bladder tumor surveillance represents a new clinical context for this organism, extending awareness beyond the Pap test setting to urinary cytopathology. As of the most recent published review, only 14 human cases of C. recurvatus had been reported, with 8 (57%) involving the female genital tract [3].

Clinical Significance of C. recurvatus

In the female genital tract, C. recurvatus has consistently been reported as a nonpathogenic colonizer in immunocompetent patients, with no associated inflammation or epithelial abnormality [3, 4]. However, invasive disease has been reported in immunocompromised patients: severe diarrhea in a bone marrow transplant recipient and isolation from pleural and peritoneal fluid in a patient with ruptured duodenal ulcer [10, 11]. No antifungal treatment is indicated for genital tract colonization in immunocompetent patients.

Consequences of Misdiagnosis

Misidentification as P. brasiliensis could lead to unnecessary, prolonged antifungal therapy with associated hepatotoxicity, drug interactions, cost, and patient anxiety [12].
