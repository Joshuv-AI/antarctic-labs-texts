Ascending aortic versus axillary artery cannulation in Stanford type A aortic dissection repair: a propensity score-weighted cohort study
Authors: Yutian Sun, Hao Chen, Xiangyou Sun, Wei Yuan, Jing Zhang, Jun Zhou, Jiamin Yu, Yongjia Pei, Haibo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAxillary artery cannulation is widely regarded as the preferred arterial inflow strategy for surgical repair of Stanford type A aortic dissection (TAAD). Ascending aortic cannulation has emerged as a practical alternative, yet comparative evidence on early outcomes and long-term survival remains limited. This study aimed to compare the early outcomes and long-term prognosis between these two arterial cannulation strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 160 patients who underwent surgery for TAAD at a single center between February 2017 and January 2025. Of these, 64 patients received axillary artery cannulation and 96 underwent ascending aortic cannulation. Early postoperative outcomes and long-term survival were assessed using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and Kaplan-Meier survival curves.ResultsBefore adjustment, 30-day mortality was higher in the axillary than the ascending aortic cannulation group (9.4% vs. 1.0%, P=0.02). After IPTW, mortality remained numerically higher with axillary cannulation but did not reach statistical significance (10.7% vs. 2.9%, P=0.19). After weighting, axillary cannulation was associated with longer operation time (P=0.03), cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time (P=0.001), and aortic cross-clamp time (P<0.001). Axillary cannulation was associated with longer mechanical ventilation and intensive care unit (ICU) stay and higher rates of postoperative cerebral infarction and acute kidney injury (AKI) both before and after IPTW, while reintubation was numerically higher after weighting without reaching statistical significance (P=0.08). Unweighted Kaplan-Meier analysis favored ascending aortic cannulation (P=0.04), whereas the difference was attenuated after IPTW (P=0.11).ConclusionsIn this single-center propensity score-weighted cohort, ascending aortic cannulation was associated with lower short-term mortality and fewer postoperative complications compared with axillary cannulation. Mortality and long-term survival differences favored ascending aortic cannulation numerically but were attenuated after IPTW, underscoring the need for larger prospective studies to confirm these findings.Keywords: Stanford type A aortic dissection (TAAD), cannulation strategy, propensity score, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 160 Stanford type A aortic dissection (TAAD) patients, ascending aortic cannulation was associated with lower short-term mortality and fewer early postoperative complications than axillary cannulation.

What is known and what is new?

• Axillary cannulation is widely considered the preferred strategy for TAAD repair due to antegrade systemic and cerebral perfusion, whereas ascending aortic cannulation is a practical alternative but is often questioned for safety, particularly regarding neurological events.

• Using an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)-adjusted cohort, ascending aortic cannulation demonstrated favorable early outcomes and numerically better late survival, supporting its use as a safe and effective alternative in appropriately selected patients when performed with contemporary techniques.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Ascending aortic cannulation can be considered a viable alternative to axillary cannulation for TAAD repair, with potential perioperative benefits.

• Larger multicenter studies and prospective trials are warranted to confirm these findings and refine patient selection.

Introduction

Stanford type A aortic dissection (TAAD) remains one of the most lethal cardiovascular emergencies and typically requires urgent surgical repair with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) (1). Establishing reliable arterial inflow is a pivotal step that can influence intraoperative perfusion, cerebral protection, and postoperative recovery (2). Among available strategies, axillary artery cannulation has been widely adopted because it may facilitate antegrade systemic and cerebral perfusion and reduce the risk of retrograde embolization (3). However, axillary cannulation can be time-consuming in unstable patients and may be limited by vessel size, local dissection, or technical complexity (4,5). In contrast, ascending aortic cannulation offers rapid establishment of CPB through central access and may improve operative efficiency, but concerns persist regarding cannulation in a dissected aorta and the potential risks of malperfusion and neurological complications (6,7). In this context, ascending aortic cannulation has emerged as a practical alternative in selected patients, but robust evidence comparing early and long-term outcomes with axillary cannulation is still limited.

Therefore, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult TAAD patients undergoing surgical repair to compare axillary artery versus ascending aortic cannulation. To mitigate confounding due to non-random cannulation selection, we applied propensity score-based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE), and we further assessed long-term overall survival using Kaplan-Meier (KM) methods. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0591/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (Rapid Review No. 2023-130), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

This single-center retrospective observational study consecutively enrolled all adult patients who underwent surgical repair for TAAD at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University between February 2017 and January 2025. The diagnosis of TAAD was confirmed by computed tomography angiography (CTA). During the study period, 200 consecutive patients underwent emergent or elective operative repair. Arterial cannulation strategies for establishing CPB included femoral artery cannulation (n=35), axillary artery cannulation (n=64), and ascending aortic cannulation (n=96). In addition, one patient underwent additional cannulation with combined axillary and femoral arterial inflow due to inadequate perfusion. Two patients required intraoperative cannulation conversion: one patient underwent conversion from axillary to ascending aortic cannulation because the axillary artery was small in caliber and involved by the dissection, and one patient underwent conversion from ascending aortic to femoral cannulation because of inadequate perfusion pressure after ascending aortic cannulation.

For the present comparative analysis, we focused on patients whose primary arterial inflow site was either the axillary artery or the ascending aorta. Inclusion criteria for the comparative cohort were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) CTA-confirmed TAAD; and (III) CPB initiated with either axillary or ascending aortic cannulation as the primary arterial cannulation strategy. Exclusion criteria were: (I) femoral or other non-study arterial cannulation strategies used as the primary inflow site at CPB initiation; (II) intraoperative additional cannulation (defined as placement of a second arterial cannula for dual arterial cannulation) or cannulation conversion (defined as switching from one cannulation site to another) due to inadequate perfusion flow/pressure; and (III) incomplete medical records for key study variables. Specifically, among the 200 surgical patients, one patient was excluded for additional cannulation (axillary plus femoral), two patients were excluded for cannulation conversion (axillary-to-ascending aorta, n=1; ascending aorta-to-femoral, n=1), and two patients were excluded because of incomplete medical records. After applying these criteria, 160 patients (axillary cannulation, n=64; ascending aortic cannulation, n=96) constituted the final study cohort. Clinical follow-up was available through January 2026.

Arterial cannulation technique

All operations were performed by a single surgeon. Operative strategies, including the arterial cannulation site, were selected according to the patient’s hemodynamic status, dissection anatomy, and intraoperative imaging assessment (epiaortic ultrasound and transesophageal echocardiography). In general, ascending aortic cannulation was considered when the ascending aortic true lumen could be clearly identified and safely accessed under imaging guidance and when rapid establishment of antegrade systemic flow was desirable. Axillary artery cannulation was favored in patients with significant preoperative cerebral malperfusion or when proximal aortic conditions were considered less suitable for central cannulation (e.g., extensive ascending aortic involvement or concern for insecure true-lumen access). In critically ill patients requiring expedited CPB or when intraoperative perfusion was inadequate with the initial strategy, conversion to an alternative cannulation site or additional cannulation was performed to ensure adequate systemic perfusion.

After induction of general anesthesia and standard sterile preparation, a median sternotomy was performed and CPB was established. For ascending aortic cannulation, the cannulation site was determined using epiaortic ultrasound. Two concentric purse-string sutures with 4-0 Prolene were placed around the puncture site. Under epiaortic ultrasound guidance, an ascending aortic true-lumen puncture was performed using a modified Seldinger technique. After confirming true-lumen entry, a guidewire was advanced; transesophageal echocardiography was used to verify that the guidewire was positioned in the descending aorta. The tract was then serially dilated with vascular dilators, followed by insertion of the arterial cannula. The purse-string sutures were tightened and secured to fix the cannula. Venous drainage was achieved via bicaval cannulation or atriocaval cannulation according to the subsequent operative plan. Systemic cooling was initiated after the onset of bypass, and subsequent aortic repair proceeded per institutional protocol.

Definition

Preoperative malperfusion was assessed based on a combination of clinical manifestations and CTA findings indicating dissection-related involvement of the corresponding arterial territories: cerebral malperfusion was defined as neurological symptoms/signs consistent with cerebral ischemia and CTA evidence of dissection involvement compromising supra-aortic branch perfusion; coronary malperfusion was defined as clinical evidence of myocardial ischemia (ischemic symptoms, electrocardiographic changes, or biomarker elevation as documented) together with CTA evidence of dissection extension affecting coronary perfusion; mesenteric malperfusion was defined as gastrointestinal ischemic manifestations and CTA evidence of dissection involvement of mesenteric vessels. The 30-day all-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring within 30 days after surgery, including both in-hospital deaths and deaths after discharge within 30 days. Postoperative cerebral infarction was defined as a new focal neurological deficit occurring after surgery with radiographic confirmation by brain MRI. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria (8).

Outcomes

The primary early outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Secondary early outcomes included postoperative complications and resource utilization measures: mechanical ventilation duration (hours), intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (days), total hospital length of stay (days), and binary complications including reintubation, tracheostomy, postoperative cerebral infarction, postoperative mesenteric malperfusion, surgical site infection, AKI, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and re-exploration for bleeding.

For long-term outcomes, overall survival was assessed from the date of surgery to death from any cause or the last follow-up, whichever occurred first. Follow-up was conducted through outpatient records and telephone contact in accordance with institutional ethics approval.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed and as median with interquartile range (IQR) otherwise. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. For unadjusted between-group comparisons (ascending aortic vs. axillary cannulation), Student’s t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate (Fisher’s exact test was applied when any expected cell count was <5). All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To account for potential confounding due to non-random treatment assignment, propensity score-based IPTW was performed to estimate the ATE. Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model with cannulation strategy (ascending aortic vs. axillary) as the dependent variable and preoperative baseline covariates as predictors, including age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, drinking history, prior stroke, coronary artery disease, renal insufficiency, Marfan syndrome, cardiac tamponade, and cerebral/coronary/mesenteric malperfusion, cardiac arrest, shock, coma, and the extent of arch branch involvement. Preoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was not included because no patients received ECMO preoperatively in this cohort. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD <0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance.

KM curves were generated for unweighted analyses and compared using the log-rank test. In the IPTW-weighted analysis, weighted survival curves were estimated using weights derived from IPTW, and the between-group difference was evaluated using a weighted Cox proportional hazards model fitted with a survey design object (robust variance estimation).

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 160 patients are shown in Table 1. Among all patients, the median age was 60.0 (IQR, 50.75–68.00) years, and 107 patients (66.9%) were male. Pre-existing comorbidities were common, with hypertension present in 119 patients (74.4%). A history of stroke was observed in 8 patients (5.0%), renal insufficiency in 31 patients (19.4%), and Marfan syndrome in 8 patients (5.0%). Regarding preoperative clinical status, cardiac tamponade occurred in 83 patients (51.9%). Malperfusion was frequently encountered: cerebral malperfusion was documented in 57 patients (35.6%), coronary malperfusion in 34 patients (21.2%), and mesenteric malperfusion in 27 patients (16.9%).

Before IPTW, baseline characteristics were generally comparable between the axillary artery (n=64) and ascending aorta (n=96) groups, except for a significantly higher rate of cerebral malperfusion in the axillary artery group (50.0% vs. 26.0%, P=0.003). Notably, the axillary group also had a greater extent of arch branch involvement (P=0.008), with a higher proportion of patients exhibiting involvement of 2 branches (17.2% vs. 7.3%) and 3 branches (23.4% vs. 9.4%) compared with the ascending aorta group. After IPTW, the two groups were well balanced with no statistically significant differences across preoperative baseline variables (all P>0.05), and SMD were reduced to <0.1, indicating adequate covariate balance (Figure 1).

Inoperative characteristics

Intraoperative variables are summarized in Table 2. Before IPTW, the axillary artery group had significantly longer operative duration than the ascending aorta group (7.46±1.29 vs. 6.43±1.38 h; P<0.001). Consistently, CPB time was longer in the axillary artery group (210.46±38.48 vs. 180.98±33.84 min; P<0.001), and aortic cross-clamp time was also prolonged [median 113.95 (IQR, 98.58–136.44) vs. 95.40 (IQR, 82.01–109.06) min; P<0.001). After IPTW, these differences persisted, with longer operation time (7.34±1.28 vs. 6.63 ± 1.49 h; P=0.03), CPB time (210.85±41.02 vs. 183.01±33.28 min; P=0.001), and cross-clamp time [median 114.03 (IQR, 98.51–133.92) vs. 94.34 (IQR, 81.14–106.54) min; P<0.001] in the axillary artery group compared with the ascending aorta group. Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest duration did not differ significantly before weighting but was slightly longer in the axillary group after IPTW (P=0.04), while cerebral perfusion time remained comparable between groups both before and after weighting. Notably, the lowest intraoperative temperature was lower in the axillary group both before IPTW [median 23.90 (IQR, 23.17–24.30) vs. 24.40 (IQR, 23.90–25.00) ℃; P<0.001] and after IPTW [23.87 (IQR, 23.30–24.30) vs. 24.40 (IQR, 23.90–24.80) ℃; P<0.001]. With respect to surgical techniques, for aortic root management, ascending aortic replacement was the predominant strategy (overall 60.6% before IPTW and 62.9% after IPTW), whereas Wheat and Bentall procedures were performed in selected patients. Arch management was mainly based on total arch replacement (76.9% before IPTW; 76.5% after IPTW). In terms of cerebral protection, antegrade cerebral perfusion was used in nearly all patients (96.9% before IPTW; 97.2% after IPTW). In addition, the cerebral perfusion mode (unilateral/bilateral) did not differ significantly between groups before or after IPTW; bilateral perfusion was used in the majority of patients in both groups (88.8% before IPTW and 88.9% after IPTW overall), indicating broadly consistent cerebral perfusion practices across the two cannulation strategies.

Early outcomes

Short-term postoperative outcomes are presented in Table 3. Before IPTW, in-hospital all-cause mortality was significantly higher in the axillary artery group than in the ascending aorta group (9.4% vs. 1.0%, P=0.02). Patients in the axillary artery group also required longer postoperative mechanical ventilation [median 16.83 (IQR, 12.00–25.92) vs. 13.17 (IQR, 9.00–17.84) h, P=0.007] and had a longer ICU length of stay [4.00 (IQR, 3.00–6.25) vs. 3.00 (IQR, 3.00–5.00) days, P=0.001], whereas total hospital length of stay was comparable between groups (P=0.47). In addition, the axillary artery group had higher rates of reintubation (10.9% vs. 2.1%, P=0.03), postoperative cerebral infarction (17.2% vs. 2.1%, P=0.002), and AKI (40.6% vs. 18.8%, P=0.004). By contrast, the use of CRRT did not differ between groups (7.8% vs. 5.2%, P=0.52).

After IPTW, the between-group differences in several key outcomes persisted. The axillary artery group continued to show longer mechanical ventilation time [17.25 (IQR, 12.00–30.15) vs. 12.95 (IQR, 7.56–17.85) h, P=0.004] and longer ICU stay [4.00 (IQR, 3.00–6.09) vs. 3.00 (IQR, 3.00–5.00) days, P=0.004], with no difference in total hospital length of stay (P=0.60). Reintubation was numerically higher in the axillary artery group (15.4% vs. 3.7%, P=0.08), whereas postoperative cerebral infarction (18.4% vs. 3.0%, P=0.007) and AKI (41.9% vs. 23.5%, P=0.03) remained more frequent; CRRT utilization was comparable between groups (6.4% vs. 7.3%, P=0.84). Although the weighted analysis showed a numerically higher mortality in the axillary artery group (10.7% vs. 2.9%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.19).

Late mortality

Late mortality was evaluated using KM analyses stratified by arterial cannulation site (Figure 1). The overall median follow-up time was 3.28 (IQR, 2.02–4.91) years. In the unweighted cohort, overall survival was significantly better in the ascending aorta group than in the axillary artery group (log-rank P=0.04), with an early separation of the survival curves that persisted over the follow-up period (Figure 2). After applying IPTW, the between-group difference in late survival was attenuated and no longer reached conventional statistical significance (weighted P=0.106), although the survival curve for the ascending aorta group remained numerically higher throughout follow-up (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of patients undergoing surgical repair for TAAD, ascending aortic cannulation was associated with more favorable early postoperative outcomes compared with axillary cannulation. Specifically, the axillary group experienced longer mechanical ventilation and ICU stay, as well as higher rates of postoperative cerebral infarction and AKI, and these differences persisted after IPTW adjustment. Although unadjusted 30-day mortality was higher in the axillary group, the mortality difference became non-significant after IPTW, highlighting both the impact of confounding and the limited number of events. Long-term survival favored ascending aortic cannulation in the unweighted analysis, but the difference was attenuated after weighting.

A plausible mechanistic link between the observed early postoperative advantages and intraoperative management is operative efficiency. In our cohort, axillary cannulation was consistently associated with longer total operative duration, CPB time, and aortic cross-clamp time-both before and after IPTW. Prolonged surgery and CPB time are well-recognized contributors to systemic inflammatory response, pulmonary dysfunction, and renal injury, which may translate into longer ventilatory support, prolonged ICU stay, and increased AKI (9-11). In our study, the axillary group reached a significantly lower nadir temperature than the ascending aorta group both before and after weighting, suggesting a more conservative cooling strategy that would be expected to prolong cooling and rewarming phases and thereby extend CPB duration. This more conservative temperature management may be partly attributable to the higher prevalence of preoperative cerebral malperfusion and the greater extent of arch branch involvement observed in the axillary group prior to weighting, both of which could prompt heightened concern for neurological vulnerability and motivate deeper hypothermia for cerebral protection. The difference in cross-clamp time, which is less intuitively linked to the cannulation strategy itself, may reflect downstream workflow effects once CPB is initiated. In addition to longer CPB runs, a longer cross-clamp time is more likely related to a more cautious operative tempo during circulatory arrest and arch reconstruction, increased time required for key anastomoses and hemostasis, and adjustments in myocardial protection strategies, even when the overall repair strategy appears broadly similar between groups. Importantly, cerebral perfusion practices were largely consistent across cannulation strategies in our center: antegrade cerebral perfusion was used in nearly all patients, and the distribution of cerebral perfusion mode (unilateral/bilateral) did not differ significantly between groups, with bilateral perfusion used in the vast majority. Therefore, the observed differences in operative and CPB durations are more plausibly explained by differences in temperature management and overall operative workflow rather than systematic differences in cerebral perfusion strategy.

Arterial cannulation strategy in TAAD balances speed, safety, and the quality of systemic and cerebral perfusion (12). Axillary cannulation has been widely adopted and is often recommended as a preferred approach because it provides antegrade systemic flow and facilitates antegrade cerebral perfusion, potentially reducing retrograde embolization and malperfusion concerns associated with femoral inflow (13). Nevertheless, femoral cannulation remains commonly used in hemodynamically unstable patients because it is rapidly accessible and can expedite CPB establishment in salvage scenarios, despite concerns regarding retrograde flow, embolization, and malperfusion in selected anatomies (14). Central (ascending) aortic cannulation has gained traction as a pragmatic alternative that can provide rapid antegrade flow through central access (15). However, a key concern with ascending aortic cannulation in TAAD is the potential for embolization, particularly if the cannula or guidewire enters the false lumen or dislodges thrombus, raising a theoretical risk of stroke (7,16). In our study, postoperative cerebral infarction was substantially lower in the ascending aortic cannulation group (2.1% before IPTW) than in the axillary group (17.2%). Several factors may explain this finding: first, our ascending cannulation technique incorporated epiaortic ultrasound to select the puncture site and to guide true-lumen access, with transesophageal echocardiography used to confirm guidewire position in the descending aorta before dilation and cannula insertion. This workflow is consistent with published experience suggesting that echocardiography/epiaortic ultrasound-guided Seldinger cannulation can improve the safety profile of central cannulation in TAAD by minimizing false-lumen cannulation and reducing embolic risk (17-19). Second, cerebral protection was highly standardized, with antegrade cerebral perfusion used in nearly all patients, which may further mitigate neurological risk. Third, the axillary group had a higher prevalence of preoperative cerebral malperfusion before weighting, and patients with pre-existing cerebral hypoperfusion may be more susceptible to postoperative infarction due to reperfusion injury, fluctuating perfusion pressures, and perioperative hemodynamic vulnerability-even when cerebral perfusion strategies are similar. Collectively, these considerations support the interpretation that, when performed with strict imaging guidance and true-lumen confirmation, ascending aortic cannulation can be neurologically safe and may not inherently increase stroke risk.

The unweighted KM analysis suggested improved long-term survival with ascending aortic cannulation, whereas the IPTW-weighted difference was attenuated. This pattern is consistent with residual confounding and limited statistical power, particularly given the relatively small cohort size and low event counts. Nonetheless, the sustained numerical separation of survival curves after weighting is hypothesis-generating and suggests that early perioperative advantages, such as reduced respiratory and renal complications, may translate into downstream survival benefits in larger cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective, non-randomized design, the results remain vulnerable to confounding by indication, as the cannulation strategy was selected clinically rather than assigned by protocol. Notably, before weighting, the axillary group had a higher prevalence of preoperative cerebral malperfusion and a greater extent of arch branch involvement, suggesting that higher-risk anatomy and clinical presentation may have influenced cannulation choice. Although we applied IPTW and incorporated additional markers of preoperative severity (including shock, cardiac arrest, coma, and arch branch involvement extent) to improve covariate balance, IPTW can only adjust for measured variables; therefore, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., granularity of hemodynamic instability, anatomical complexity beyond the coded variables, and other intraoperative decision-making considerations) may persist and could partly account for the observed between-group differences. Second, this was a single-center experience with a modest sample size, which limits generalizability; the observed associations, particularly for infrequent endpoints such as mortality and stroke, should be interpreted cautiously. Larger multicenter studies and ideally prospective randomized trials are warranted to validate whether ascending aortic cannulation achieves outcomes comparable to, or better than, axillary cannulation in contemporary TAAD repair.

Conclusions

In this single-center propensity score-weighted cohort, ascending aortic cannulation was associated with lower short-term mortality and fewer postoperative complications compared with axillary cannulation. Mortality and long-term survival differences favored ascending aortic cannulation numerically but were attenuated after IPTW, underscoring the need for larger prospective studies to confirm these findings.
