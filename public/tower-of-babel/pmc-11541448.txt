Thermodynamic investigation of the efficiency of ammonia-powered marine solid oxide fuel cells with gas turbine
Authors: Serhiy Serbin, Nikolay Washchilenko, Xianrui Zhao, Marek Dzida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImprovement of marine power plants includes increasing their efficiency and drastically reducing emissions of pollutants, which involves transitioning to carbon-free fuels. This article discusses the evolution of a marine power system designed for decarbonization, utilizing ammonia and comprising solid oxide fuel cells with a gas turbine. To enhance efficiency, the system incorporates a steam supply into the fuel burning device of a gas turbine. By adding superheated steam, the system's performance improves significantly. Thermodynamic calculations identified optimal parameters for the gas turbine, which enhances efficiency by utilizing off-gases from the fuel cells. Key parameters include compressor and exhauster pressure ratios, which can be used to increase the specific power. A combined energy system with an overexpansion turbine achieves a total efficiency of 60.62 % at a fuel cell temperature of 1190 K during operation, with the SOFC efficiency being 43.76 %. These findings highlight potential improvements for marine power systems using ammonia and provide insights into the energy conversion processes within such hybrid systems.Keywords: Hybrid power system, ammonia, Solid oxide fuel cell, Optimization, Gas turbine

Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are employed across diverse fields, from portable energy devices to stationary power stations. Their significant advantage is the efficient direct transformation of fuel energy into electricity. Interest in incorporating fuel cells into marine power systems, which additionally feature gas turbines (GTs), is increasing. These are known as SOFC-GT energy systems, where the gas turbine utilizes the heat of exhaust gases from the fuel cells. Typically, an afterburner (combustion chamber or burning device) is installed after the fuel cell, in which the remnants of the fuel leaving the fuel cells react in the oxidant stream (the fuel utilization coefficient in cells is 0.8–0.85). While hydrogen is the most common fuel, other hydrocarbons could also be used, with oxygen or air as the oxidizer. Recently, an increasing number of researchers have been considering ammonia NH3 (both gaseous and liquid) as a promising fuel for various energy systems, including maritime applications.

Ammonia is increasingly recognized as a promising alternative to conventional hydrocarbons due to its potential for low-carbon energy. Lee et al. [1] and Tornatore et al. [2] emphasized that over the coming decades, ammonia can provide an economical and technological pathway to clean energy storage and utilization globally. Ito et al. [3] argued that it can be used as an efficient carrier before electricity generation and combustion, e.g., in fuel cells and gas turbine systems. This approach is supported by numerous studies and industrial trials conducted by companies like Mitsubishi Power [4], for example.

Currently, in the context of solving the decarbonization challenges of energy systems, the assessment of ammonia usage potential for SOFC-GT hybrid systems is relevant. This task is important for both stationary and marine energy systems. Ammonia should be considered a promising fuel as it has the potential to replace hydrocarbons as the primary energy resource for marine power systems. Fuerte et al. [5] demonstrated that ammonia is a feasible fuel option for SOFCs, with the performance of tubular SOFCs operating using ammonia also investigated. In SOFCs, ammonia undergoes a two-step oxidation process: initially, ammonia decomposes into nitrogen and hydrogen at the inlet, and subsequently, hydrogen is oxidized to produce water. The high efficiency of the fuel cell stack has been demonstrated under various conditions.

Afif et al. [6] considered energy systems with direct ammonia-fed fuel cells, showing promise as energy sources for next-generation fuel cell technologies, although they are not yet commercially viable and require additional research. Cheliotis et al. [7] concluded that ammonia can be used as a universal marine fuel within existing infrastructure, as there are no sulfur oxide or carbon dioxide emissions. However, several challenges need to be addressed for ammonia to be used in decarbonization applications in shipping, including choosing a suitable ammonia-powered generator, selecting an appropriate system safety assessment tool, and implementing measures to mitigate potential hazards.Micco1 et al. [8] carried out a techno-economic analysis for the integration of SOFCs fueled by ammonia as an onboard energy system for a commercial vessel. Due to the additional weight of the energy system in the engine room, implementing the proposed system on the vessel will require a cargo reduction of about 2.88 %. Barelli et al. [9] proposed a design for an SOFC system operating on ammonia developing a thermodynamic model to evaluate its efficiency. Experimental results are compared with calculations based on the developed model, achieving an electrical efficiency of 52.1 % for the system operating on ammonia.

Analysis of electrochemical impedance, and quantitative assessment of ammonia conversion, conducted by Oh et al. [10], have shown that reduced ammonia conversion at reduced temperatures is the primary reason for the decreased efficiency of SOFCs. The use of more active ammonia decomposition catalysts can significantly optimize the performance of systems using direct ammonia feed. Lee et al. [11] proposed a cohesive design of an ammonia fuel delivery system for an ocean vessel. A large container ship with a capacity of 14,000 TEU is chosen as the target vessel. The ammonia fuel delivery system with re-liquefaction was developed to deliver liquid ammonia at 40 °C and a pressure of 80 bar.

Yuan et al. [12] showed that еhe SOFC-GT technology is attracting attention due to its potential for clean and efficient electricity generation. In general, SOFC-GT hybrid systems can utilize various alternative fuel types. In recent years, many studies have been conducted on the use of biomass, coal, and ammonia as fuels. As a clean fuel with substantial energy density, ammonia holds promise as a leading fuel for SOFC-GT systems. Yet, for ammonia to realize its extensive potential in the energy sector, challenges related to safety, production expenses, and supporting infrastructure must be overcome. With ongoing improvements in technology and decreasing costs, ammonia could play a crucial role in the transition to sustainable energy.

To assess the quantitative variations in thermodynamic characteristics and economic performance of the SOFC-GT system with ammonia fuel, Kim et al. [13] carried out modeling, including energy, exergy, and economic evaluations. The incorporation of ammonia resulted in a 2–6% gain in the efficiency of the combined system, although it also led to a 14–24 % rise in installation costs. Various approaches are used to increase the effectiveness of the combined system, one of which is the cycle with steam supply into the gas turbine combustor (also known as the contact-type gas turbine cycle). By supplying superheated steam to the combustion chamber, improvements in the effective performance of marine systems can be achieved, as demonstrated in this work.

Ryu et al. [14] highlighted that ammonia is a viable and promising alternative to hydrogen as a marine fuel, particularly for fuel cell propulsion systems. Wehrle et al. [15] presented the results of studies on a multiscale modeling platform for investigating components and stacks operating on ammonia. It was noted that the distributed charge transfer model shows excellent agreement with experimental data.

Sun et al. [16] developed process models for SOFC systems operating on ammonia and determined the impact of exhaust gas recirculation on system performance using energy flow diagrams. Dhawale et al. [17] reviewed the technological challenges associated with SOFCs, the latest advancements in modern technologies, and new anode materials for these systems. They also provided recommendations for transitioning to ammonia as a power generation source. Zhao et al. [18] conducted a sensitivity analysis of the combined system with steam reinjection to investigate how steam mass flow affects the enhancement of the system's electrical efficiency. An increase in the efficiency of the hybrid system with steam reinjection was noted.

Serbin et al. [19], Matveev et al. [20], and Serbin et al. [21] showed that improvements in the energy performance of the gas turbine section can be realized by complicating its thermal schemes. Furthermore, Serbin et al. [22] and Cherednichenko et al. [23] confirmed the importance of enhancing the efficiency of residual fuel afterburning in a special combustion chamber. Serbin et al. [24] and Matveev et al. [25] suggested using the method of plasma-assisted combustion intensification for this, which leads to increased efficiency of the entire combined power system.

Despite the large number of studies of marine hybrid systems, there is practically no research on the efficiency of the promising, in our opinion, hybrid system with an overexpansion turbine and an exhauster, which operates on ammonia. Although several works have analyzed the efficiency of thermal schemes, they do not conduct a comprehensive optimization of defining characteristics of gas turbine units of the so-called contact-type, implying the supply of superheated steam into the burning device to increase the specific power of the gas turbine section.

The hybrid marine system proposed in this work, containing solid oxide fuel cell stacks, a gas turbine, and an afterburner for the fuel cell exhaust gases, operates on liquid ammonia. The novelty and significant difference from analogs lie in the use of an overexpansion turbine and an exhauster in the gas turbine section of the system, which allows for significantly lowering the optimal pressure in the gas turbine cycle and thereby increasing the reliability of the fuel cells themselves. Furthermore, employing a contact-type gas turbine with a superheated steam supply into the burning device facilitates greater recovery of waste heat from the fuel cell off-gases and enhances the power output of the gas turbine component.

This study aims to conduct a thermodynamic analysis of the efficiency of a marine power and heat generation system fueled by ammonia, which includes solid oxide fuel cells and a gas turbine unit with overexpansion and steam injection into the afterburner, and to identify the optimal parameters for the gas turbine cycle.

Materials and methods

Marine energy system operating on ammonia

Fig. 1 depicts a schematic of a marine power plant fueled by ammonia, equipped with SOFCs and a contact-type gas turbine unit (CGTU) that includes overexpansion. Unlike previously considered schemes with regenerative gas turbines [19] and contact-type gas turbine units [26] operating on natural gas, this novel scheme adds several new elements for the supply, vaporization, and reforming of ammonia during operation. Such a scheme allows for a reduction in pressure in the stacks of SOFCs and a rise in the power plant's specific output.

Schematic diagram of a marine energy system operating on ammonia and utilizing SOFC stacks and CGTU: 1—cooling water pump; 2—ammonia supply ejector; 3—built-in stack ammonia heater; 4(1), 4(2)—DC to AC converters; 5—AC to DC converter; 6—condensate pump; 7—feed water tank; 8—feed water pump of the HRSG; 9—circulation pump of the HRSG; 10—HRSG separator; 11—ammonia evaporator; 12(1), 12(2) —control valves; A—air; G—gases; F—fuel (gaseous ammonia); S—steam; W—water.

Compressed atmospheric air passes through the Compressor, where it reaches a certain pressure and then enters the First-stage air preheater. Here it is heated by exhaust gases from the Turbine, which drives the Compressor and Exhauster, with any excess energy transferred to the Generator. After this, the cyclic air passes into the Second-stage air preheater, where it is heated by exhaust gases from the Combustion chamber (CC). The fuel for the CC consists of a blend of gases from the anodes of the SOFC stacks, which are combusted in air that is deficient in oxygen and sourced from the cathodes of the SOFC cells. Since the combustion chamber is supplied with fuel that has a very low calorific value and a high content of water vapor, certain modifications to the fuel burning device are required to ensure the stable operation of the chamber across a wide range of operating conditions. In some cases, the use of catalysts and combustion intensifiers is also necessary.

Following their cooling in the Second-stage air preheater, the combustion products enter the Turbine and expand to a pressure lower than atmospheric. The pressure is regulated based on the calculated value from Exhauster. The exhaust gases from the Turbine, after moving through the First-stage air preheater's gas side channels, are fed into the HRSG. In this system, they generate superheated steam, which is later sent to the CC after being processed by the fuel cells.

The introduction of steam into the combustion chamber reduces the maximum combustion temperature but provides effective cooling of the fuel combustion device's walls. The turbine operates on a gas-steam mixture, so its design includes larger cross-sectional areas to accommodate the working fluid and ensures conditions for its efficient utilization in the proposed contact-type gas turbine system.

After passing through the HRSG, the gas-steam mixture enters the Cooler-condenser, which is circulated with onboard water, where water cools it to a designated temperature. During this process, some of the steam condenses, is pumped by Pump (6) into the Feed Water Tank (7), and then directed to the HRSG Separator (10). The resulting cooled and dehumidified combustion products are compressed in Exhauster and expelled into the atmosphere.

Liquid ammonia stored in the Cryogenic Tanks with a temperature of 20 °C and with a pressure of 8.8 bar enters the Evaporator (11). After evaporation, the ammonia is heated by exhaust gases after the HRSG and supplied to the Ejector (2). After the Ejector (2), ammonia passes through the built-in Heater (3) and then enters the anodes of the SOFC stack.

The proposed scheme enhances the efficiency of heat recovery from the exhaust gases of the fuel cells by using a contact-type gas turbine system. Additionally, the presence of an exhauster reduces the pressure of the working fluid, thereby increasing the operational reliability of the system.

Mathematical approach

For the calculation of the fuel cell stack of the power station, a tubular SOFC stack from Siemens-Westinghouse [26] is selected. Given that this research concentrates on a gas turbine engine capable of delivering up to 700 kW of power, the efficiencies of the turbine components are suggested to be consistent with existing microturbine technology. Referring to the data for the Capstone C200 engine [26] and accounting for a higher compressor pressure ratio, the efficiencies of the centrifugal compressor, the exhauster, and the turbine are taken as 0.80, 0.85, and 0.90, respectively.

According to experimental data [5], the characteristics of the SOFC stack are similar when operating with either ammonia or hydrogen. This similarity holds regardless of the current density. However, the specific power is slightly lower when using ammonia. For instance, at 900 °C and a current density of 159 mA/cm2, the specific powers are 88 and 104 mW/сm2 for ammonia and hydrogen. This corresponds to a 15 % reduction in specific power for the ammonia-operated element. Therefore, in the conducted calculations, the power of the SOFC stack was also reduced by 15 %, while keeping other parameters constant.

Similarly, to obtain comparable results, the mass flow rates of ammonia and hydrogen are chosen such that the amount of heat released during their combustion is the same, assuming 100 % methane conversion in the stack.

Results and discussion

In thermodynamic analysis of gas turbine cycles, the compressor pressure ratio is often chosen as the parameter to be optimized, as this indicator significantly affects the efficiency of the power plant and its design configuration. In our case, it is necessary to add the exhauster pressure ratio to this parameter, as it determines the pressure of the working fluid after the last turbine stage and the overall efficiency of the gas turbine section.

Fig. 2 shows how the exhauster pressure ratio (πеx) affects the efficiency of the combined system when using ammonia and methane as fuels. This analysis examines compressor pressure ratios (πc) varying between 5 and 10, similar to the study on power station operation with methane presented in Ref. [26].

Factors impacting the effectiveness of the SOFC-GT system when operating on ammonia and methane for πеx = 1.2–1.5.

The graph in Fig. 2 shows that using ammonia leads to a rise in the efficiency of the entire power system across the entire range of parameters considered, even with some decreases observed in the efficiency of the fuel cell. The greatest difference is observed for the smallest values of the compressor pressure ratio. For instance, at πc = 5, the efficiency improvement of the system reaches 1.7%–1.9 % (absolute). The thermophysical properties of ammonia and its decomposition products, particularly the specific heat capacities of the components, have the greatest impact on the processes in the turbine section of the energy installation. The graphs in Fig. 2 demonstrate the potential for increased efficiency when utilizing ammonia as a fuel in hybrid systems.

The influence of the πеx on the efficiency of the power system is very noticeable when πc ≤ 7. As the πc increases, the rate of efficiency improvement slows, and the influence of the πеx diminishes. Therefore, for optimal performance with ammonia, the exhaust pressure ratio should be set at πеx = 1.3–1.4 for πc = 7–8.

Graphical dependencies of the parametric analysis of key parameters on the system characteristics during operation are also presented. Fig. 3 demonstrates the variation in the power output of the gas turbine within the thermal configuration in the specified range of πc, obtained under the same conditions as the dependencies in Fig. 2.

Variation in the power output of the gas turbine part for πеx = 1.2–1.5.

Fig. 3 shows that the exhauster pressure ratio notably impacts the power output of the gas turbine part, especially when the compressor pressure ratio is low. The power of the gas turbine part of the marine power station can vary depending on the magnitude of the calculated compressor pressure ratio within the range of 640–673 kW, slightly increasing with the increment in the calculated exhauster pressure ratio but only up to values of πc ≤ 7. Thus, there is an optimal compressor pressure ratio that is specific to the thermal scheme of the contact-type gas turbine unit with an overexpansion turbine.

Fig. 4 shows the variation in temperature of the gas-steam blend preceding the turbine for the hybrid SOFC-GT system, while Fig. 5 presents the change in the temperature of the gas-steam blend at the exit of the combustion chamber for πеx = 1.2–1.5.

Change in blend temperature before reaching the turbine of the SOFC-GT hybrid system.

Temperature variance of the blend upon exiting the burning device of the SOFC-GT hybrid system.

The calculations indicate an increase in gas temperature before the gas turbine across the analyzed parameter range of 876–948 K as πc rises from 5.0 to 10.0. This phenomenon can be explained by the concurrent rise in the air temperature before the second-stage air preheater. Note that the exhauster pressure ratio has little effect on the temperature of the working fluid before the turbine.

Fig. 5 illustrates the functioning of the burning device, where the residual combustion products of ammonia that reform upon exiting from the anodes of the SOFCs react with steam-enriched air with a reduced oxygen content exiting the cathodes of the SOFCs. It should be noted that there is a slight decrease in the temperature at the outlet of the burning device as the compressor pressure ratio increases from 5 to 10. Moreover, an increase in the value of πеx within the specified range of 1.2–1.5 also slightly reduces this temperature. The observed pattern can be ascribed to how the airflow rate supplied by the gas turbine compressor to the cathodes changes with the escalation in the compressor pressure ratio, as presented in Fig. 6).

Variation in airflow through the compressor per one stack in the SOFC-GT hybrid scheme.

Fig. 6 demonstrates that when the calculated compressor pressure ratio grows in the range between 5 and 10, the air mass flow rate per SOFC stack through the gas turbine compressor also rises. Additionally, during the increase in πеx from 1.2 to 1.5, the airflow rate increases by approximately 1.1 % for each 0.1 increment.

Fig. 7 depicts the relationship between the relative steam content in the blended stream before the gas turbine compressor and the calculated compressor pressure ratio while varying the exhauster pressure ratio between 1.20 and 1.50.

Change in the relative steam content in the flow when πеx is changed.

As shown in Fig. 7, the gas-steam blend contains a significantly high percentage of steam before reaching the compressor, with values ranging from 6.2 % to 10 %. Moreover, the steam content in the mixture increases with a decrease in both the calculated compressor pressure ratio and the exhauster pressure ratio. Under nominal operating conditions of the power system, the steam content is approximately 7.64 %.

Fig. 8 presents the dependencies of the change in the airflow rate through the compressor. This airflow supplies air to all 15 SOFC stacks of the 2500 kW power station, following the SOFC-GT scheme. The dependencies are shown across the range of considered changes in the key parameters.

Change in airflow for a 2500 kW energy system.

As depicted in Fig. 8, the airflow rate through the gas turbine compressor fluctuates between 6.25 and 6.74 kg/s, increasing with the calculated pressure ratio. Notably, the mass flow rate of air also increases with the exhauster pressure ratio.

The results of optimization calculations for the hybrid power system operating on ammonia compared to the closest thermal circuit, but operating on methane [26], indicate changes in several system parameters as a result of variations in the thermodynamic properties of the fluid in use. When using ammonia fuel instead of methane, the compressor air flow increased significantly (6.54 kg/s instead of 3.21 kg/s), the temperature of the steam-air mixture at the turbine exit decreased (559 K instead of 619 K), the gas turbine section power increased (669.3 kW instead of 524.2 kW), the power of the fuel cells decreased (1923.8 kW instead of 2112.5 kW), and the overall system efficiency slightly increased (60.62 % instead of 59.4 %). When compared with the system [14], operating on ammonia, it can be noted that due to the more efficient utilization of heat from fuel cells in the proposed contact-type gas turbine unit, despite the lower efficiency of the SOFC itself (43.76 instead of 50.49), the overall efficiency of the hybrid system remained at the same level (60.62 instead of 60.96).

Conclusion

Research has been conducted on a hybrid energy system that operates on ammonia, featuring solid oxide fuel cells coupled with a contact-type gas turbine unit with steam supply into the burning device. As a result, a schematic design of a prospective marine power system SOFC-GT has been developed, which operates on ammonia and utilizes solid oxide fuel cell stacks, as well as a contact-type gas turbine unit. The efficiency of the gas turbine part of the plant is increased by injecting steam into the burner device and expanding the resulting gas-steam mixture inside the turbine. Integration of the contact gas turbine plant into the power system ensures its overall efficiency of 60.62 % at an operating temperature of 1190 K and the efficiency of the solid oxide stack of 43.76 %. It is advised that the exhauster pressure ratio of the power system should be in the range of 1.3–1.4 with a compressor pressure ratio of 7–8. Further investigation is needed to determine rational characteristics for the elements of the thermal scheme SOFC-GT, as well as the use of the exergy analysis method to minimize losses both in individual parts of the system and in the entire system as a whole. In addition, constructive schemes for the afterburners of exhaust gases from fuel cells when operating on ammonia should be designed.
