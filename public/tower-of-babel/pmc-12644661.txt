Correlation between perioperative glycemic variability and early postoperative functional recovery in elderly patients with lumbar degenerative disease: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Mingyang Qian, Rui Li, Wei Jiang, Li Hua, Qingsong Zou, Chen Jin, Jiangming Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerioperative glucose variability (GV) is one of the key factors of patient prognosis. This Prospective Cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between perioperative GV and early postoperative functional recovery in elderly patients undergoing surgery for lumbar degenerative diseases. Elderly patients who underwent lumbar spine surgery from October 2024 to April 2025 were included. The coefficient of variation (CV) of glucose was calculated as an indicator of GV. We categorized the CV into three groups based on tertiles. Regression models evaluated GV’s impact on ODI and JOA. Subgroup analyses validated findings. A total of 171 patients (mean age 72.09 years; 44.44% male) were included in this study. GV showed a significant negative correlation with 3-month postoperative JOA (r=-0.209, P < 0.01) and a significant positive correlation with ODI (r = 0.339, P < 0.01). Multivariate regression analysis further identified GV as an independent predictor of decreased JOA scores (P = 0.035) and increased ODI scores (P < 0.001) in the early postoperative period. Subgroup analyses confirmed the consistency of the associations between GV and postoperative ODI and JOA across various subgroups. GV is closely related to early postoperative functional recovery in elderly patients with lumbar degenerative disease. Monitoring GV may be a valuable approach to predict the early postoperative functional outcomes in this patient population, potentially guiding more personalized treatment strategies and postoperative management to improve their early postoperative functional status and quality of life.Keywords: Elderly, Glycemic variability, Lumbar spine surgery, Early postoperative functional recovery, Surgical outcomeSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Lumbar Degenerative Disease (LDD) is one of the most common disorders in spinal surgery, affecting approximately 266 million individuals globally each year, accounting for 3.63% of the population1. It often causes low back pain and neurological dysfunction due to nerve compression. It has been reported2 that 50% of elderly patients have LDD. Surgical intervention becomes necessary when conservative treatments fail. Although surgery can reduce the patient’s pain, studies have shown that nearly 40% of patients still report postoperative dysfunction, with their ODI dysfunction index improvement at 3 months postoperatively falling short of expectations3. Thus, identifying the potential risk factors that influence functional recovery after LDD surgery is imperative.

Diabetes is recognized as one of the most prevalent age-related chronic systemic diseases. It was found that the prevalence of diabetes among elderly patients with LDD amounted to 12.1%4. In recent years, diabetes mellitus has been shown to be associated with several adverse outcomes after lumbar spine surgery, including increased incidence of infection5, had a prolonged hospital length of stay6, increased cost of care7, and increased reoperation rates8. It has also been shown that diabetic status affects the overall recovery rate and lower extremity sensory/motor function in patients9,10. Dokai et al.11 reported that patients with cervical spondylosis and comorbid diabetes had poorer postoperative sensory recovery function in the lower extremities. Similarly, Cinotti et al.12 noted that 80% of diabetic patients undergoing lumbar decompression surgery had delayed nerve conduction velocities in the lower extremities. A prospective study showed that diabetic patients who underwent spinal surgery had higher ODI scores at 2 years postoperatively compared to nondiabetic patients (32.1 vs. 26.8)13. Tanishima et al.14 found that poor glycemic control may prevent postoperative functional recovery in patients with cervical spondylosis, with no significant improvement in JOACMEQ scores for upper and lower extremity function at 1-year postoperatively in patients with poorly controlled HbA1c levels. The causes of these results may be that diabetes-induced peripheral neuropathy and autonomic neuropathy affect sensory and motor functions, leading to postoperative pain and poor prognosis for patients15. However, it is not the sole determinant of postoperative recovery from lumbar degenerative diseases. In fact, postoperative recovery is influenced by multiple factors, including patients’ blood glucose control, surgical methods, and drug factors16. Therefore, it may not be sufficient to assess postoperative functional recovery based solely on the presence or absence of diabetes or average blood glucose indicators such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), because the acute metabolic state during the perioperative period - especially glucose variability (GV) - may be another modifiable risk factor that can impair recovery through mechanisms such as increased oxidative stress and inflammation, affecting both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

Glycemic Variability (GV) describes the degree of fluctuation in blood glucose over time due to physiological endocrine self-regulation disorders. It encompasses a range of indicators reflecting both short-term and long-term variations in blood glucose levels. As a important critical marker of glycemic control, GV is frequently highlighted in diabetes-related diseases17–19. Emerging evidence suggests that sudden glucose fluctuations can exacerbate inflammatory response, induce oxidative stress, and damage the vascular endothelium, with strong associations with increaseding reoperation rates20, higher readmission rates21, surgical site infections21, and mortality22. One study reported that lower PROMIS scores were associated with higher GV, with measured by the coefficient of variation, which is a significant predictor of perceived cognitive function23. Additionally, elevated GV has been found to be associated with poor 30-day functional outcome in patients with acute cerebral hemorrhage24. However, the relationship between perioperative GV and early postoperative functional recovery in lumbar spine surgery remains unexplored and the impact of perioperative GV on postoperative outcones have yet to be systematically investigated.

We believe that perioperative glycemic variability (GV), rather than diabetes status alone, may serve as a more sensitive marker of metabolic stress and a significant predictor of functional recovery following lumbar spine surgery.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective Cohort Study.

Participants

Patients who underwent lumbar spine surgery in the Department of Orthopedics at our hospital between October 2024 and April 2025 were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) diagnosis of lumbar degenerative diseases, including lumbar disc herniation and lumbar spinal stenosis, confirmed by both imaging studies and ICD-10 diagnostic codes (e.g., M51.2, M48.0); (3) scheduled for elective surgical treatment; and (4) avaliability of complete medical records. Exclusion criteria included: (1) preoperative combined infection in other body regions; (2) patients undergoing revision surgery; (3) concurrent psychiatric or cognitive disorders; and (4) missing or incomplete clinical data.

Assessment of GV

Perioperative glucose levels were continuously monitored using an ambulatory glucose monitoring system. A sensor was placed on the lateral upper arm one day before surgery and remained in place until postoperative day 3. The device measured interstitial glucose every 15 min, yielding 96 readings per day and a total of approximately 288 glycemic data points per patient over the monitoring period.

The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean of all collected interstitial glucose values during the monitoring period. It was based on all available continuous glucose measurements. Based on the coefficient of variation (CV), patients were stratified into tertil groups: tertile 1 (low variability), tertile 2 (moderate variability), and tertile 3 (high variability). See Fig. 1.

Flow chart of patient selection process.

Data collection

Perioperative data were obtained from electronic medical systems and administrative records. Covariates were selected based on a comprehensive review of relevant literature and export consultation. These covariates included age, sex, education level, body mass index (BMI), smoking, alcohol consumption, and the presence of comorbid hypertension and diabetes. Blood samples were collected on the second day of admission to assess biochemical markers, including total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), creatinine, total bilirubin, and hemoglobin. The factors related to the surgery include: the type of surgery (divided into “simple decompression” or “fusion plus decompression”), the number of surgical segments, the duration of the surgery (from the skin incision to suturing, measured in minutes), and the intraoperative blood loss (EBL, measured in milliliters). Additionaly, mean preoperative blood glucose, perioperative MAGE, and postoperative steroid therapy (typically a short, tapering course of dexamethasone administered at the surgeon’s discretion primarily to reduce postoperative edema and radicular pain) were also included in the data collection.

Outcomes assessment

Postoperative functional recovery was evaluated at the 3-month follow-up visit using two validated instruments: the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI).

Both assessments were performed by trained orthopedic clinicians who were blinded to the patient’s glycemic variability grouping, in order to minimize assessment bias. The evaluations were conducted during routine outpatient follow-up approximately 12 weeks (± 7 days) after surgery.

Data analyses

The data analyses was performed by SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality assumptions were tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and histogram test. Summary statistics are presented as the number of patients (percentage) for categorical variables and as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for continuous variables. Group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-squared test and the ANOVA test or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Person’s correlation analyses were conducted to fathom the correlation between the independent variables and the results. Multiple linear regression was used to examine the association between GV and early postoperative functional recovery. Variables with a P-value ≤ 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate regression analysis. Finally, subgroup analyses were further conducted uesing stratified linear regression to evaluate the stability of the association between GV and early postoperative functional recovery across different clinical settings.

Stratified comparisons by blood glucose coefficient of variation (CV)

Patients were stratified into three tertiles based on CV: low (CV = 16.17%), middle (CV = 22.25%), and high (CV = 29.78%). A total of 58 patients were assigned to the first tertile (low variability), 56 to the second tertile (intermediate variability), and 57 to the third tertile (high variability).

Comparison of postoperative outcomes of patients in different CV subgroups

Figure 2 illustrates the comparison of postoperative function and LOS among the three CV groups. The results indicate that ODI scores in the high CV group were significantly higher than those in the middle CV group (P < 0.05) and the low CV group (P < 0.001), whereas the difference between the middle and low CV groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that greater glycemic variability is associated with more pronounced postoperative dysfunction. JOA scores in the low CV group were significantly higher than those in the middle CV group (P < 0.05) and the high CV group (P < 0.01), indicating better neurological recovery in patients with lower glucose variability. The difference in JOA scores between the middle and high CV groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Regarding LOS(refers to the period from admission to discharge), the high CV group was significantly longer than the middle CV group (P < 0.05) and the low CV group (P < 0.001), and the middle CV group was also significantly longer than the low CV group (P < 0.05), indicating that the higher the blood glucose variability, the longer the postoperative hospital stay.

Subgroup analysis

To further explore the relationship between perioperative GV and early postoperative functional recovery, subgroup analyses were conducted. Age was divided into two groups using 75 years as the cut-off, and additional subgroup analyses were performed based sex, hypertension, diabetes according to gender, hypertension, diabetes status and postoperative steroids therapy. Covariates included age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption, steroid therapy, creatinine, total bilirubin, hemoglobin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C. Interaction tests were performed using age, sex, hypertension, diabetes and postoperative steroids therapy as effect modifiers. In the ODI analysis, only the postoperative steroids therapy subgroup showed a statistically significant interaction P-value (P for interaction = 0.004). All other subgroups (age, gender, hypertension, diabetes) demonstrated no statistically significant interactions (P > 0.05), and the postoperative steroid therapy also showed no significant interaction in the JOA analysis, as shown in Table 9. This suggests that these factors do not significantly moderate the relationship between CV, ODI, or JOA.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the influence of perioperative glycemic variability (GV) on short-term functional outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for lumbar degenerative disease (LDD). Our findings revealed that higher GV levels were significantly associated with increased Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores and decreased Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores at three months postoperatively, reflecting impaired functional recovery. These results support our initial hypothesis that elevated GV adversely affects postoperative rehabilitation. Importantly, while diabetes has been reported to affect neurological recovery and patient-reported outcomes, we acknowledge that diabetes is not a single entity compromising clinical outcomes in lumbar degenerative disease. To address this, our multivariate analyses adjusted for diabetes status and other comorbidities, yet perioperative GV remained an independent predictor of early functional recovery. And subgroup analyses indicated that the associations between the coefficient of variation (CV) and postoperative ODI and JOA scores remained consistent across different strata, including age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes status. These findings indicate that metabolic instability reflected by GV plays a role distinct from the presence of diabetes perse and not significantly modified by these common clinical factors. However, in terms of ODI, it was found that the effect of CV on ODI was more significant in patients who did not use steroids after surgery (interacting P = 0.004), suggesting that steroids may alleviate the impact of functional impairment caused by blood glucose fluctuations to some extent through anti-inflammatory effects.

Elevated GV was significantly associated with delayed postoperative functional recovery. A large-scale study involving 7,139 patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty identified diabetes as a risk factor for moderate-to-severe activity limitations at both 2- and 5-year follow-up periods25. Similarly, Mohammed et al.26 reported limited improvement in ODI scores among diabetic patients requiring insulin therapy, further supporting the detrimental effect of hyperglycemic states on postoperative recovery. In the field of spine surgery, Furlan27 and Nori28 observed that hyperglycemia was associated with impaired neurological recovery following spinal cord injury and cervical spondylotic myelopathy, respectively. Additionally, Udby et al.29 found that patients with diabetes undergoing surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis experienced significantly worse disability at 2-year follow-up, with Oswestry Disability Index scores of 38 compared to 29 in non-diabetic patients. Consistent with these findings, our study showed that patients in the high GV group had ODI scores approximately 5 points higher and JOA scores lower (22.64 vs. 25.03) compared to those with low GV, with statistically significant differences.

However, not all studies align with these findings. Bakes et al.30 in a cohort of patients undergoing surgery for traumatic spinal cord injury, found no significant association between elevated glucose levels and neurological recovery. Similarly, Byron et al.31 reported that diabetes did not significantly influence 12-month JOA scores in patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy. These discrepancies may be partly explained by population heterogeneity. Notably, the aforementioned studies primarily focused on traumatic spinal cord injuries, in which neurological deficits result from acute mechanical disruption. In such cases, the cascade of secondary injury mechanisms, including ischemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation, peaks within 48 h post-injury32, potentially masking the additional effects of chronic metabolic dysregulation associated with diabetes. Moreover, the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) due to concomitant multi-organ trauma may confound the independent role of hyperglycemia, further complicating interpretation of glucose-related effects on recovery outcomes33.

Distinct from prior studies that primarily focused on diabetes as a single disease entity, our study evaluated the independent impact of perioperative glycemic variability (GV) on postoperative recovery, irrespective of baseline diabetic status. The inclusion of non-diabetic patients was crucial to isolate the effect of GV itself, highlighting that stress-induced glycemic instability is a concern for all patients undergoing major surgery, not just those with diabetes. To our knowledge, this is the first study to clearly demonstrate that GV serves as an independent predictor of functional outcomes in patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery. By comparing ODI and JOA scores across tertiles of the coefficient of variation (CV), we found that patients in the highest CV group (CV > 25.23%) exhibited the poorest postoperative recovery, a finding that remained significant even after adjusting for potential confounders such as age.

Moreover, multivariate analysis incorporating both preoperative mean blood glucose and CV showed that high CV remained significantly associated with worse ODI and JOA scores, whereas preoperative mean glucose was not statistically significant. These results suggest that GV may be a more sensitive indicator than average glucose levels in reflecting perioperative glycemic control and its effect on surgical recovery.

Although subgroup analysis revealed that the associations between CV and postoperative ODI/JOA scores were not significantly modified by diabetes status, univariate ANOVA indicated a significant difference in GV between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. The inclusion of both diabetic and non-diabetic patients was intentional to reflect the real-world surgical population and to disentangle the effect of glycemic variability (GV) from baseline diabetic status. Stress-induced hyperglycemia has been frequently reported in non-diabetic individuals during the perioperative period, and such transient metabolic disturbances have been associated with adverse outcomes in major surgeries. Therefore, assessing GV across the entire surgical cohort allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of perioperative metabolic instability. These considerations suggest that perioperative GV is a clinically relevant parameter not only in diabetic but also in non-diabetic patients. This finding provides new insights into the physiological mechanisms underlying glycemic fluctuations and highlights the need to further explore GV as a dynamic metabolic parameter in perioperative care.

Previous studies have shown that high blood sugar can impair nerve recovery after surgery34,35. However, the mechanisms underlying the effect of GV on delayed postoperative functional recovery are unclear. May be closely associated with oxidative stress, inflammatory response and microcirculation disturbances36. GV can promote the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) by activating the NF-KB signaling pathway. These inflammatory factors cause degeneration of intervertebral disc tissue and inflammatory reactions in the intervertebral joints. They increase muscle catabolism, leading to postoperative muscle strength decline and thereby delaying functional recovery37–40. At the same time, hyperglycemia activates RAGE receptors by increasing AGE accumulation, which promotes inflammatory responses and fibrosis and hinders nerve regeneration41. Additionaly, evidence suggests that rapid glucose swings induce even more potent oxidative stress by repeatedly triggering and suppressing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production42. This “glycemic variability-induced oxidative stress” is a key mediator of subsequent pathological processes. It directly contributes to endothelial dysfunction by impairing the function and survival of vascular endothelial cells43[,44. The compromised microvasculature and oxidative environment further promote the disruption of the blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB)45, a critical structure for maintaining the homeostasis of the spinal cord milieu. Once the BSCB is disrupted, it facilitates the infiltration of pro-inflammatory factors and cells into the neural tissue, thereby aggravating neuroinflammation46. Oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, barrier breakdown, and neuroinflammation can directly impede neural recovery and musculoskeletal regeneration. This mechanism may explain why GV was a more sensitive predictor than mean glucose levels in our multivariate model.

The results of this study provide new insights into the future treatment of lumbar spine surgery. GV is more time-sensitive and intervenable, a critical step forward in quantifying the effect of GV on patient prognosis. Translating glycemic variability (GV) into actionable clinical indicators, by determining the GV threshold—for instance, GV > 25.23% being associated with the poorest postoperative functional recovery—these findings provide objective criteria for personalized assessment. It could serve as a critical therapeutic target in future perioperative management, enabling the design of personalized monitoring protocols centered around these parameters to advance precision medicine and ensure interventions are tailored to individual patients’ risk profiles. Firstly preoperative GV assessment should be incorporated into the routine perioperative risk assessment process, especially in patients of advanced age or in the presence of basal metabolic abnormalities. There is also a need to improve the identification and management of GV in non-diabetic patients. In addition, it is essential to construct intervention strategies centered on glycemic variability (GV) control as the primary objective. Examples include dynamic adjustment protocols based on real-time feedback from continuous glucose monitoring systems (CGMS) or multimodal intervention pathways integrating nutritional, pharmacological, and exercise therapies. These approaches enable precise perioperative glycemic management tailored to individual metabolic profiles. Furthermore, clinical intervention programs should be adapted to the characteristics of patients with high GV. Third, the incorporation of GV parameters into the assessment system for perioperative metabolic management, such as coefficient of variation (CV) and time in target range (TIR) for glucose, can help guide the adjustment of glucose control strategies. These findings are important guidance for optimizing the perioperative metabolic management pathway, especially for the elderly patient population who are vulnerable to postoperative functional outcomes. Therefore, in the future, perioperative glycemic management needs to shift from an “average glycemic control” paradigm to a “dynamic glycemic control” paradigm, which can more accurately tailor to different patients and thus achieve better clinical recovery benefits.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, this study was a single-center design with a limited sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, this study did not collect preoperative ODI and JOA scores. Therefore, we were unable to calculate functional improvement rates or adjust for baseline functional status in our analysis. While the balance of other key baseline characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities) across GV groups mitigates this concern to some extent, the absence of preoperative patient-reported outcomes remains a limitation. Future studies should include comprehensive preoperative assessment to confirm the impact of GV on the net functional gain after surgery. Third, the follow-up of this study was only 3 months, which is too short to effectively verify whether GV has any correlation with long-term prognosis. A longer follow-up period is needed in the future to assess the impact of GV on mid- and long-term recovery. In addition, the ODI and JOA scores were used in this study as the main evaluation indexes of functional recovery, which are still subjective scales, although they have good clinical correlation. The explanatory power of the results can be further enhanced in the future by combining imaging parameters (e.g., MRI recovery score) or functional tests.

Conclusion

Among elderly patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery, glycemic variability (GV) is correlated with delayed postoperative functional recovery. Patients with a coefficient of variation (CV) exceeding 25.23% have poorer recovery outcomes. These findings suggest that optimizing glycemic control strategies to reduce variability may be a modifiable target for improving postoperative recovery in patients with lumbar degenerative disc disease (LDD). However, this study is only an observational study. Future prospective studies and intervention trials are needed to validate these findings and explore the optimal strategy for glycemic variability control in the spinal surgery population.
