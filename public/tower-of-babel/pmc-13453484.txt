Unveiling the contribution of histidine-end-terminal pBAEs to endosomal escape using super-resCLEM imaging
Authors: María Navalón-López, Teodora Andrian, Lorenzo Albertazzi, Salvador Borrós, Cristina Fornaguera, Silvia Pujals
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The success of gene therapy focuses on the development of efficient and safe carriers capable of overcoming cellular barriers to deliver genetic material to the nucleus. Poly(beta-amino ester) nanoparticles (pβAE NPs) are promising cationic polymers that can complex nucleic acids to form polyplexes, facilitating intracellular delivery. Among the key intracellular hurdles, endosomal escape remains one of the most critical and debated steps. While the incorporation of histidine into polyplex formulations has been proposed to enhance endosomal escape via proton buffering, definitive evidence of this process has remained elusive. To address this gap, a super-resolution correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) approach was employed to decipher the intracellular trafficking of pβAE NPs. Specifically, we compared two pβAE formulations, one incorporating a histidine-arginine end-capping structure (RH) and a control containing only arginine end-capping (R). Our findings revealed that RH polyplexes efficiently escaped endosomes at early timepoints and achieved cytosolic localization, whereas R polyplexes predominantly remained trapped within endo-lysosomal compartments, ultimately undergoing exocytosis or degradation, as confirmed by lysosome-specific staining. This study provides, for the first time, direct high-resolution evidence of histidine-mediated endosomal escape in gene therapy, underscoring the role of histidine in overcoming intracellular barriers. Moreover, it highlights the power of super-resolution correlative imaging techniques in elucidating the nanoscale trafficking of gene delivery systems, enabling more rational design of next generation nanocarriers.

Introduction

Gene therapy offers transformative potential for treating genetic disorders, cancer and infectious diseases by introducing functional nucleic acids into target cells. However, its clinical success remains largely dependent on the ability of delivery systems to overcome intracellular barriers that arise after cellular uptake [[1], [2], [3]]. Once internalized, these nanocarriers become enclosed within membrane-bound endosomal vesicles that traffic the nanocarriers through the endo-lysosomal system. Most nanocarriers fail to escape from these vesicles and are instead directed towards lysosomal compartments, where acidic pH and enzymatic degradation compromise the integrity of the therapeutic cargo. This failure to achieve cytosolic release results in a substantial loss of therapeutic efficacy [4]. As a result, endosomal escape has been recognised as one of the most critical and challenging barriers in gene delivery systems [[5], [6], [7]]. Although numerous strategies have been proposed ranging from pH-responsive materials to buffering agents, the exact mechanisms remain under debate.

Nevertheless, several delivery systems have successfully overcome intracellular barriers. Such nanocarriers include lipidic nanoparticles, viral vectors, extracellular vesicles or polymeric nanoparticles (NP). Among polymeric NP, poly(β-amino ester) (pβAEs) polymers stand out as one of the most promising candidates due to their versatile building blocks that confer high transfection efficacy, low toxicity and excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. The properties of pβAEs can be precisely tailored by modifying their backbone (Fig. 1A) with tripeptides, such as arginine (Arg or R) or histidine (His or H), via a cysteine (Cys or C) linker [11] as shown in Fig. 1B. These modifications were previously reported to enhance specific functionalities: R-containing polymer provide the cationic charge required for efficient nucleic acid condensation and cellular internalization, whereas histidine-containing polymers contribute buffering capacity in acidic endosomal environments, promoting endosomal escape and ultimately improving transfection efficiency [[4], [5], [6]]. These properties are obtained with the indicated polymer proportions in Fig. 1C, and an N/P ratio of 25:1 resulting in two distinct NP formulations, referred to as R and RH. Both R and RH formulations generate homogeneous and positively charged polyplexes with comparable physicochemical characteristics. R and RH NPs exhibited similar hydrodynamic diameters (188.8 ± 4.8 nm and 189.3 ± 2.9 nm, respectively) and low polydispersity indices (0.18 ± 0.01 and 0.16 ± 0.04, respectively), while maintaining positive ζ-potentials of 36.0 ± 4.0 mV and 27.3 ± 0.8 mV, respectively (Supporting Information Table S1) These properties are consistent with efficient pDNA condensation and cellular interaction. Furthermore, both formulations demonstrated efficient cellular uptake and successful transfection in A549 model lung cancer cells, with RH NPs displaying enhanced transfection performance compared with R formulations, in agreement with previous reports from our group (Supporting Information Figure S1) [[13], [14], [15]]. Although pβAEs show promising transfection efficiency, the mechanisms governing the intracellular release of genetic material remain poorly understood and vary depending on the chemical structure of the polymer. In this context, understanding whether the buffering capacity conferred by histidine plays a decisive role in promoting endosomal escape is critical for the development of effective delivery systems. However, the lack of high-resolution tools capable of directly visualizing these nanoscale trafficking events has prevented validation and understanding of this intracellular mechanism. A deeper understanding of how pßAE-based NPs interact with and navigate these barriers is essential to optimize their design and unlock their full clinical potential [16].

Advanced imaging techniques are needed to track NPs in cells and show their precise nanoscale interactions with intracellular structures. Traditional fluorescence microscopy has been a key technique for tracking NPs within cellular environments due to its high sensitivity and ability to specifically label NP components [17]. However, its spatial resolution is insufficient to resolve the detailed interactions between polyplexes and cellular ultrastructure[[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Conversely, electron microscopy (EM), particularly transmission electron microscopy (TEM), offers the ultrastructural resolution necessary to visualize NP localization within endosomal and cytosolic compartments [24]. However, the lack of molecular specificity in EM limits its ability to track labelled NPs effectively.

Correlative Light and Electron Microscopy (CLEM) bridges this gap by combining the molecular specificity of fluorescence microscopy with the nanoscale resolution of EM. Recent advancements in super-resolution techniques, such as two-color direct Stochastic Optical Reconstruction Microscopy (dSTORM), further enhances the capabilities of CLEM by achieving single-molecule resolution [25]. The correlation of dSTORM with TEM, enable the detailed tracking of pβAE polyplexes as they navigate intracellular barriers, providing insights into their interactions with endosomal membranes and mechanisms of endosomal escape. Thus, in this study, we employed a two-color super-resolution CLEM approach (super-resCLEM) to investigate the endosomal escape mechanisms of histidine-containing pβAE polyplexes. The aim is to evaluate whether histidine's buffering capacity plays a critical role in facilitating the endosomal escape of NPs during intracellular trafficking in delivery systems. By employing a two-colour imaging strategy, we were able to independently track both the carrier and the cargo. This approach enabled us to distinguish the various states of the NPs: when they are complexed, when the cargo is released and when the cargo reaches its intended intracellular destination. Additionally, we studied the trafficking of polyplexes within lysosomal compartments. This complementary analysis provided robust evidence of the intracellular journey of pβAE polyplexes, including their interactions with lysosomes, a critical step of their fate and therapeutic success. Using super-resCLEM, we gained a comprehensive understanding of the intracellular dynamics of pβAE polyplexes, contributing to the advancement of NP-based delivery systems for gene therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sucrose, sodium acetate (AcONa), 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich®. Cyanine dyes used for polymer labelling were obtained from Lumiprobe, whereas plasmid labelling was performed using the Label IT® Tracker™ Intracellular Nucleic Acid Localization Kit from Mirus. 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) was also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich®. Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), L-glutamine, penicillin, and streptomycin were obtained from Biowest. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and culture plates were purchased from Corning. The GFP plasmid (3486 bp) was produced and purified from Escherichia coli. Cell culture was performed in 92 × 17 mm dishes (Nunc™, Fisher Scientific) using a pGFP plasmid encoding green fluorescent protein.

For sample preparation, Fixative 1 consisted of 4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1% glutaraldehyde, and 0.1 M PHEM buffer (240 mM PIPES, 100 mM HEPES, 8 mM MgCl2, and 40 mM EGTA; pH 6.9). Fixative 2 consisted of 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PHEM buffer. Samples were processed using an Eppendorf 5804 R centrifuge. Dextran was used at 20% in PBS, and planchettes with a depth of 200 μm were employed for high-pressure freezing, performed using a Leica HPM100 system. Freeze substitution was carried out using 0.1% (w/v) uranyl acetate in acetone. Samples were embedded in Lowicryl HM20 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and sectioned using a Leica Ultracut UC7 ultramicrotome. Additional reagents included methylene blue and Hoechst stain (20 μg/mL in PBS, Sigma-Aldrich). DAPI was also used at 20 μg/μL in PBS. MilliQ water was used throughout.

Fluorescence calibration beads (Tetraspeck, 0.1 μm diameter; ∼1.8 × 1011 particles/mL in 2 mM sodium azide, Invitrogen) were used for imaging calibration. Imaging was performed in GLOX buffer consisting of 100 mM mercaptoethylamine (MEA), an oxygen scavenger system containing 5% (w/v) glucose, 0.5 mg/mL glucose oxidase (Sigma-Aldrich), and 40 mg/mL catalase (Sigma), supplemented with 15% glycerol in PBS at pH 8.5. Samples were mounted on 22 × 22 mm glass coverslips (Corning Cover Glass, thickness 1½) and sealed with transparent nail polish.

Microscopy was performed using a Nikon N-STORM system equipped with a Hamamatsu ORCA Flash 4.0 camera. Electron microscopy grids consisted of nickel 200-mesh formvar film-coated hexagonal grids. Post-staining was performed using 2% uranyl acetate in MilliQ water and Reynolds’ solution (3% lead citrate). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets were used where indicated. Samples were stored in a desiccator when required.

Image processing and analysis were performed using Adobe Photoshop CS9, Fiji software, and MATLAB. Co-localization analysis was carried out using the JACoP plugin, and Pearson/Manders' co-localization coefficients (including Manders’ coefficient, MC) were calculated.

LysoTracker green was used at a final concentration of 100 nM in HBSS for live-cell staining. Cell culture

A549 (ATCC CRM-CCL-185TM) cell line was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin G, 100 μg/mL streptomycin and 2 mmol/l L-glutamine. All cells were cultured at 37°C, under 5% CO2/95% air atmosphere, to reach 70%-90% confluence. Afterwards, the maintenance cell passages were done in 100 mm × 20 mm petri dish (Corning).

Methods

Polymer synthesis and characterization

Poly(β-amino ester) (pβAES) polymers end-modified with arginine and histidine were synthesized by the Group of Materials Engineering following a two-step procedure previously described in the literature [26].

Briefly, C6 pBAE polymer was synthesized by addition polymerization of 5-aminopentanol, hexylamine to 1′4-butendiol diacrylate using a slight excess of diacrylate (0.5:0.5:1.2 M ratio). Continuous stirring 20h at 90°C. Oligopeptide end-modified pBAE were obtained by end-modification of acrylate-terminated C6 polymer with thiol-terminated oligopeptide at a molar ration of 1:2.5 in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. The resulting polymer was precipitated in a mixture 7:3 v/v of diethyl ether/acetone. Then it was dried overnight and dissolved in DMSO in order to obtain a solution of 100 mg/ml. The polymers were modified with arginine (C6CR3) and histidine (C6CH3).

Synthesized structures were confirmed by 1H NMR and spectra was recorded in a 400 MHz Varian (Varian NMR Instruments, Claredon Hills, IL) and methanol-d4 was used as solvent:

NP synthesis

The NPs were prepared at a N/P molar ratio of 25/1 (OM-pBAE/Nucleic acid), by mixing equal volumes of pGFP 0.5 mg/ml with the different polymer combinations at 12.5 mg/ml in a solution of sodium acetate 12.5 mM at a pH of 5.2. RH NPs were formulated using a 60:40(v/v) ratio of R:H polymers, whereas R NPs were prepared using 100% R polymer. The nucleic acid was added to the polymer solution, mixed by pipetting, followed by 30 min of incubation at room temperature (V1). For the formation of discrete structures after incubation, the mixture was nanoprecipitate in a same volume of milliQ water. Thereafter, the same initial volume of hepes 20 mM + 4 wt% sucrose (pH 7.4) solution was also added as cryo and lyoprotectors ending with sample 1/3 diluted compared with the first incubation.

Polymer and plasmid labelling

OM-pBAE polymers were labelled with Cyanine 5 (Cy5) fluorophores by reaction with Cy5 NHS ester at a 1:1 M ratio in the presence of excess triethylamine. The reaction was carried out overnight at room temperature, allowing covalent attachment of the fluorophores to the amine groups of the oligopeptide-modified polymer. The labelled polymer was purified by precipitation in a 7:3 (v/v) diethyl ether/acetone mixture, washed twice, dried overnight, and dissolved in DMSO (100 mg/mL).

Plasmid, particularly pGFP, (1 mg/ml) was labelled with Cy3 following the Label IT® Tracker™ Intracellular Nucleic Acid Localization Kit protocol. For imaging experiments, a DNA labelling reagent:pGFP ratio of 0.5:10 (v/w) was used and the reaction time was extended to 2 h to achieve the desired labelling density.

Labelling densities of OM-pBAE polymers and pDNA were determined using a Tecan Infinite 200 Pro instrument (Tecan, Barcelona, Spain). For dSTORM imaging in CLEM studies, polyplexes were prepared using 12% Cy5-labelled OM-pBAE and 25% Cy3-labelled pGFP. To achieve these final labelling percentages, the labelled polymer and labelled pDNA preparations were diluted with their corresponding non-labelled counterparts. Mixing was performed using stock solutions of 100 mg/mL for OM-pBAE and 1 mg/mL for pDNA prior to polyplex formulation.

Cell seeding, polyplex transfection and fixation for dSTORM-TEM correlative imaging

A549 cells were seeded on a 92 × 17 mm cell culture dish and incubated for 24h in full DMEM at 37°C and 5% CO2, to full confluency. The medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS, then the polyplexes were incubated at a concentration of 1.9 ng pGFP/μL in DMEM (2 mL per well). The cells were incubated with polyplexes during the indicated timepoints (2h, 4h, 6h and 24h). Next, cell media was removed and cells were washed with PBS twice. Next, the cells were first fixed for 30 min at RT by adding 1 mL volume of Fixative 1, in 1 mL of DMEM in the culture dish. Then, fixative solution was discarded and replaced with 5 mL Fixative 1 for another 30 min at RT. Then, the solution was removed and replaced with 1.5 mL of Fixative 2 for 5 min at RT., ensuring that the cell dish is evenly covered. The cells were then scraped off (whilst in Fixative 2) using a cell scraper and transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf. These were then pelleted using a centrifuge at 2500 rpm (1200 rcf) for 10 min at RT. The supernatant was replaced with 1 mL Fixative 2, then centrifuged again. The pellet was kept in Fixative 2 at 4°C and covered from light until next steps.

dSTORM imaging

First, single NPs were imaged to set up the labelling concentrations for optimum localizations signaling (Supporting information Figure S3). Then, for cells and NPs visualization several reference images were taken. The epifluorescence lamp on DAPI filter was used to take a 13x13 stitch (1024x1024 pixels) of the distribution of the thin sections on the grid, using the NIS-Elements stitch function. The second reference image was taken using the same lamp and filter at 1024x1024 pixels inside the grid hexagon of interest, showing the cell nuclei, then one image was acquired at 256x256 pixels, which gives the exact field of view (FOV) that will be imaged by dSTORM. Then, using the Nikon N-STORM system configured for HiLo (highly inclined and laminated optical sheet) imaging, a low-resolution image of pDNA (Cy3 labelled) was taken using the 561 nm (80 mW) laser, and a low-resolution image at 2% laser power and 20 ms. These latter two images (DAPI and Cy3 channel) were used for correlation of low-resCLEM images. Then sample needed to be imaged through the 647 nm channels for the polymer, 561 nm channel for the pDNA and 488 nm for the beads in the stated order) samples were imaged by dSTORM by illuminating 647 nm (160 mW) and 561 nm (80 mW) channels with laser at 100% power and 10% laser power for the 488 nm (80 mW) last channel. Fluorescence was collected using a Nikon 100x, 1.49 NA oil immersion objective and passed through a quadband pass dichroic filter (97335 Nikon). Images were acquired onto a 256 x 256-pixel region (pixel size 0.16 mm) of a Hamamatsu ORCA Flash 4.0 camera at 20 ms integration time. 10,000 frames were acquired in each channel. dSTORM images were reconstructed using the NIS Elements software in Nikon using a 2D Gaussian fitting and a threshold of 180 for both channels. The threshold is the difference between the photon number in the peak pixels and the background pixels. The trace length parameter was set to 5 to avoid different molecules blinking consecutively, meaning that molecules identified in 5 consecutive frames would be counted as 1, or removed if more than 5 frames.

Preparation for TEM imaging

To remove the grid from the microscopy chamber without ripping/damaging the grid, it is important to be as gentle as possible whilst doing the following steps. First, a paper tissue soaked in acetone was used to gently remove the nail polish around the edges of the coverslip, trying to not move the coverslip whilst doing this. Then, as seen in Fig. 3, the microscopy chamber was placed in a petri dish filled with milliQ water, and the coverslip was gently pushed with water from the pipet tip in between the coverslip and the slide, until detached from the microscopy slide, then with a tweezer, gently lifted. Next, the grid was picked up using a tweezer and washed to remove buffer salts on 5 x 40 μL milliQ water drops for 1 min each. The grid was dried for at least 15 min, and then stained. For staining of the ultrathin sections, the grid was first placed on a 20 μL drop of 2% uranyl acetate for 30 min at RT. Then, the grid was removed using a tweezer and rinsed thoroughly in 200 mL milliQ water using a jet of water. If the grid dropped in the beaker, it was gently removed and washed with another 200 mL of milliQ water. The grid was dried for at least 15 min.Fig. 3Grid retrieval and contrasting steps for TEM imaging.

Grid retrieval and contrasting steps for TEM imaging.

To prevent precipitation of lead carbonate in Reynolds’ solution due to atmospheric CO2, the grids were placed in a closed Petri dish. A NaOH pellet was placed inside the same sealed environment (but not in contact with the sample), to exclude atmospheric CO2.

Then, a 20 μL drop of 3% lead citrate (Reynolds’) was placed quickly underneath the petri dish, next to the NaOH pellet. TEM imaging

Grids were examined using a JEOL 1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan) at the Electron Cryomicroscopy Unit of the CCiTUB, equipped with a tungsten filament as the electron source. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Micrographs were acquired using a 1k × 1k MegaView CCD camera in combination with Analysis 3.2 software.

The regions of interest (ROI) were initially identified at low magnification with the assistance of previously acquired reference images. Once located, serial images of the ROI were captured at a magnification of ×15,000. These images were subsequently assembled into composite micrographs using the Photomerge function in Adobe Photoshop CS9. Higher magnification images (e.g., ×30,000) were also obtained to provide enhanced resolution of specific structural features.

Image correlation

For the overlay of low-resolution/dSTORM and TEM images, initial alignment was performed manually using Adobe Photoshop CS9. The dSTORM image was rotated and scaled to match the TEM image, ensuring approximate spatial correspondence. Subsequently, ICY software was employed to refine the correlation using its dedicated plugin. This plugin enabled particle matching between the two imaging modalities by identifying and aligning fiducial markers (beads) present in both images, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the overlay.

Lysosomal pβAE degradation pathway

A549 cells were incubated with pβAE NPs for defined time-points (2h,4h,6h,12h,18h,24 and 48h). The sample was then washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular NPs and kept in HBSS during image acquisition. One sample was imaged immediately after 24-h incubation, while two other replicates were placed back in the incubator for the different time points (after replacing the PBS for cell culture medium). Prior to imaging, the samples were washed with HBSS and the lysosomes were stained with LysoTracker Green (100 nM final concentration in HBSS) for 15 min. After washing three times with PBS, the samples were imaged in HBSS. The fluorescence images acquired were processed and analyzed using Fiji and the built-in, co-localization plugin JACoP.57 Within this plugin, Manders co-localization was found as an appropriate analysis strategy as this method measures the fraction of co-occurrence of the signal in two channels rather than their correlation. After manual selection of the cell area with the region of interest (ROI) manager, the Manders’ coefficient (MC) was calculated for 10-20 biological replicates for each time point (using three technical replicates). More specifically, MC indicates the fraction of pixels of the NPs ROI that overlap with the pixels of the LysoTracker ROI, resulting in a value between 0 and 1. One means that 100% of the pixels of the NPs ROI overlap with the pixels of the LysoTracker channel, with 0 being a 0% pixel overlap.

Statistical analysis

For the quantification of dSTORM localizations, a MATLAB code developed in our group by Feiner Gracia et al. [28] was used. Briefly, a XYT-coordinate file of the dSTORM image is imported in MATLAB, from which the number of localizations are extracted in function of the specified settings. Bandwidth of 100 nm, Min Pts in a cluster of 60 nm, Max particle diameter of 400 nm, Min Particle Diameter of 80 nm and Distance Aggregates of 20 nm.

Results

Super-resolution microscopy reveals divergent internalization pathways

To understand the pβAE NPs cellular trafficking in greater detail, CLEM was chosen as the technique that enables both the examination of cellular structures through EM and the precise detection of NPs using fluorescence microscopy. TEM was selected due to its ability to provide high-resolution localization of NPs and detailed insights into cellular ultrastructure [22,25]. For accurate NP detection, dSTORM was employed, as it offers the advantages of using conventional fluorophores with nanoscale resolution and allows for multi-colour imaging [16,29].

To investigate the internalization and intracellular distribution of RH and R NPs over time, we selected key time points based on previously published data [16,30]. These results demonstrated distinct differences in their behaviours at 2 and 4 h. RH polyplexes demonstrated rapid internalization and colocalization with early endosomes (EE) within 2h, progressing toward the nuclei by the 4h mark while still colocalizing with EE. In contrast, R polyplexes, which lack histidine, exhibited slower internalization. At 2 h, they were predominantly localized on the membrane, and by 4 h, limited internalization was observed, with low colocalization with EE. This may be attributed to their strong cationic charge anchoring them to the cell surface.

Building on previous findings, we selected specific time points to optimize two-colour dSTORM for analysing pβAE NP trafficking. For RH polyplexes, the 4h point was shifted to 6h to capture potential endosomal escape occurring later than previously observed. For R polyplexes, we kept the 2h and 4h time points to confirm whether their cationic charge keeps them associated with the plasma membrane. A 24h point was added for both formulations to assess nuclear delivery and long-term intracellular fate. From a transfection perspective, such premature plasmid release outside the cytoplasm is unfavourable, as it reduces the fraction of intact, protected cargo available for successful internalization, endosomal escape and ultimately, nuclear delivery [32,33]. In contrast, maintaining NP integrity until endosomal escape, as observed with RH NPs, is critical for maximizing transfection efficiency.

This behaviour is consistent with previous reports describing that polymer stability and intracellular trafficking of pBAE-based NPs strongly depend on surface charge and endosomal interaction dynamics [10,11]. In particular, highly cationic systems have been associated with increased membrane interactions and altered intracellular stability profiles. However, these measurements provide information on NP structural integrity over time, but do not reveal the specific stage of the endolysosomal pathway in which the particles reside. To address this, we complemented the STORM analysis with electron microscopy (EM).

Mapping the endo-lysosomal trafficking routes of RH vs. R NPs with super-resCLEM

The combination of fluorescence data with EM enables high-resolution mapping of NPs within endolysosomal compartments, providing detailed insight into their intracellular localization. Using super-resolution CLEM, we tracked polymer- and plasmid-labelled NPs and directly identified the specific subcellular compartments involved in their trafficking.

As endosomes mature, they undergo acidification, which correlates with increased electron density in EM images [23,34] producing a visualization of darker vesicles. Therefore, for RH NPs (Fig. 6), at ST incubation, they are predominantly found within clear vesicles, thus corresponding to early endosomes. At MT, however, the NPs are no longer enclosed within any membrane-bound vesicles, indicating successful escape from the endosomal compartment. In addition, a significant amount of free plasmid can be observed dispersed in the cytoplasm. At LT, the NPs have finally been internalized, however, they are now found within electron-dense vesicles, consistent with late endosomes or lysosomes, indicating that they have failed to escape the endolysosomal pathway. It is worth noting that small fraction of free plasmid can reach the nuclei (highlighted in a white circle), in comparison with the amount observed for RH NPs at LT of Fig. 6. These observations are consistent with the transfection efficiencies reported in the Supporting Information Figure S1.

In contrast, RH NPs successfully escape before reaching degradative compartments. The difference can be attributed to histidine's buffering capacity. In fact, this result is in accordance with our previous publications, where we already hypothesized the histidine effect on endosomal scaping [10,[35], [36], [37]]. This interpretation is further supported by recent studies on amino acid-functionalized PBAEs, including the work of Kaplan et al., [38] who identified histidine-containing PBAEs as promising candidates for enhanced intracellular delivery and highlighted the importance of histidine-mediated endosomal buffering in transfection performance. With a pKa around 6, histidine residues retain protons in the acidic endosomal environment, leading to continued proton influx. This process contributes to osmotic swelling and ultimately membrane disruption facilitating endosomal escape via a proton sponge-like effect [10,39].

Endolysosomal colocalization over time

Using CLEM technique, we were able to resolve the different stages of the endosomal escape process mediated by PBAE NPs and confirm the critical role of histidine in enabling this mechanism. However, due to the inherently low-throughput nature of this method, a complementary endolysosomal staining assay was performed, using confocal microscopy and allowing time-lapse imaging and monitor NPs behaviour over multiple incubation time points. Although this approach helps identify general trends, it lacks the spatial resolution and ultrastructural information provided by CLEM. Moreover, the detected fluorescent signal cannot be unequivocally assigned to intact NPs limiting its suitability as a standalone method for mechanistic studies. Nevertheless, it provides valuable dynamic information on NP localization and complements the high-resolution observations obtained by CLEM.

This endolysosomal staining assay corroborates our earlier findings in a simpler and faster manner through the fluorescent staining of NPs and lysosomes. In the case of RH NPs (Fig. 9), we observed that at early time points (up to 6 h), the acidified compartments of the endolysosomal pathway (green) do not colocalize with the NPs, as evidenced by the absence of a yellow signal typically indicating colocalization. This is consistent with the NPs being located in early endosomes that have not yet undergone acidification. At later time points (12 and 18 h), some yellow signal is detected, indicating partial colocalization with more acidified endosomal compartments. Conclusions

The results presented in this paper provide novel insights into how histidine peptide modification of pBAE polymers influences the intracellular fate of pBAE NPs. By directly comparing R and RH NPs and combining super-resCLEM together with lysosomal staining, we were able to track and visualize NP localization in detail, uncovering differences in their trafficking dynamics and endosomal escape efficiency.

For the first time, we provide direct high-resolution evidence of histidine-mediated endosomal escape, demonstrating the critical role of histidine in enhancing gene delivery. Our CLEM analysis allowed visualization of the entire endosomal escape process, from NP interaction with membranes to cargo release or degradation. RH NPs efficiently escaped endosomes by MT post-incubation, dispersing into the cytoplasm and ultimately localizing in the nucleus at LT. In contrast, R NPs exhibited delayed internalization, remaining primarily membrane-bound or within electron-dense vesicles, indicating poor endosomal escape at LT incubation.

The integrated use of time-lapse confocal microscopy and lysosomal staining further validated the endosomal escape process, allowing dynamic tracking of NP trafficking. These findings underscore the utility of this imaging approach for studying the behaviour of NPs in gene therapy applications.

In conclusion, this highlights the importance of histidine in enhancing endosomal escape and demonstrates, for the first time, the uability of super-resCLEM to investigate the intracellular trafficking and endolysosomal processing of NPs. This methodology provides a robust framework for studying NP trafficking and informs the rational design of next-generation nanocarriers, ultimately advancing more efficient gene delivery strategies.
