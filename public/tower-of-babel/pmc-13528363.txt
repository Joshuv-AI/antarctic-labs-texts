“Trying to Do No Harm”: exploring clinician concerns towards the use of AI for risk prediction in psychiatry
Authors: Justine Chang, Valentina Tamayo Velasquez, Andrea Waddell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare is expanding, including in psychiatry. However, its successful adoption depends on clinician acceptance and trust. Despite the growing interest, there remains a knowledge gap in understanding the clinician perspectives and concerns, towards AI in psychiatry.ObjectiveThis qualitative, pre-implementation study explored clinician concerns and perceived barriers towards the application of predictive AI for clinical outcomes in a large mental health hospital.Methods and AnalysisFour virtual focus groups were conducted with 16 clinicians who provided care at a large mental health hospital in Ontario, Canada. Two focus groups (n=9) included physicians, and two (n=7) included allied clinicians. Participants discussed their awareness and concerns with predictive AI for clinical outcomes. Transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSix themes emerged regarding clinician willingness to use and implement AI for clinical outcome prediction in mental healthcare: AI model performance, quality of data sources, system issues, end-user behaviours, patient outcomes and clinician well-being. Subthemes included the absence of technical infrastructure, quality data to support AI development, the ‘black box phenomenon’ of AI algorithms, loss of critical thinking, medicolegal concerns and the potential harms from over-intervening.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study that uses focus groups to explore a full range of clinician attitudes towards machine learning-based prediction tools in mental healthcare settings. It highlights major areas of concern for emerging AI technology. Understanding clinicians’ perspectives is critical to identifying barriers to the introduction of AI in psychiatry.Keywords: Machine Learning, Artificial intelligence, Delivery of Health Care, Electronic Health Records, Patient Care

Introduction

In recent decades, digital technologies like electronic medical records (EMR), telemedicine and mobile phone applications have transformed the way healthcare is delivered.1 2 However, psychiatry and mental health services often lag behind in adoption due to heightened concerns and hesitations around how the technology will fit in these unique settings.3–5 The progress in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) offers promising potential to address health system challenges and revolutionise the delivery of care.6 Understanding the concerns regarding AI from clinicians is essential for successful adoption.

Psychiatric settings pose unique challenges to consider that set them apart from other areas of healthcare. Psychiatric disorders involve subjective and complex human perceptions, thoughts and behaviours that are inherently difficult to measure and interpret.7 Even with the aid of mental status assessments and occasionally laboratory evidence, clinicians frequently encounter varying degrees of diagnostic uncertainty and variability.8 Clinical deterioration and resulting adverse events can look quite different in psychiatric settings and can include the use of restraint, self-harm, suicidal behaviour and patient violence or aggression.9 10

AI encompasses machines that are designed to exhibit human intelligence, possessing abilities such as learning, pattern recognition, reasoning and decision-making.11 A subset of AI technology is ML, which uses algorithms and statistical models to learn from large datasets to identify new information, detect patterns and predict outcomes.12–14 To date, ML has already been successfully deployed in many areas of healthcare, such as assisting with image recognition and categorisation, diagnostic support, transcription of patient interactions and more.14 The promising potential for ML algorithms to handle complex data for outcome prediction may help identify high-risk psychiatric patients.

A recent review identified nine predictive models aimed at identifying patients at risk of a mental health-specific adverse event.15 While the models had moderate to good performances, more validation and pilot studies are needed to establish the clinical performance and efficacy of these models. Despite the expansion of ML-based predictive models in psychiatry, such tools are not yet prevalent in routine clinical practice. Model accuracy is only one factor for successful AI deployment in real life; clinicians play an important role as the key users of these tools.

Importantly, ML-based tools can only be effectively implemented if the key users find them useful, feasible and have trust and confidence in them.16 17 To ensure ML-based outcome prediction tools meet clinician needs and integrate smoothly into the clinical workflow, it is important to engage clinicians through each step of the development and deployment process. To date, the literature on the attitudes and perspectives of a range of clinicians towards predictive ML tools is sparse. Successful integration of AI requires a pre-implementation phase and careful consideration of clinician and patient perspectives.

Currently, our institution is considering the adoption of ML-based tools to support risk prediction and overall safety in psychiatric settings. The objective of this study is to explore the perceptions, specifically the perceived worries and concerns, towards the use and implementation of predictive ML tools among a range of clinicians in a large psychiatric hospital. As a pre-implementation study, we aim to capture these perspectives and subsequently identify key considerations to support the ongoing development and integration of AI in psychiatry.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study using focus groups to gather insights on attitudes toward AI from clinicians from Waypoint Centre for Mental Health Care, a large inpatient mental health hospital in Ontario, Canada.

Data collection

Due to the scarcity of literature addressing our research question and the lack of an existing conceptual framework in psychiatry, we decided to use focus group discussions to explore views and thoughts among a heterogeneous participant group. The focus group method also aligned with our research goals and timeline as pre-implementation research.

Participant recruitment and sample selection

As a pre-implementation study for the development and piloting of an ML predictive tool, participants were recruited locally from the Waypoint, an inpatient mental health hospital providing specialty mental health and addictions services as well as geriatric and forensic mental health services. Recruitment occurred via email invitations distributed in Waypoint’s internal network between 31 July 2023 and 21 August 2023 (online supplemental file 1).

We used maximum variation sampling, a type of purposive sampling, to identify common themes that emerge from heterogeneous samples. Clinicians were eligible if they were providing clinical care to patients at Waypoint. There were no exclusion criteria, as we intended to maximise the variation in age, gender, type of clinical work and experience with AI. We did stratify participants into a physician (MD) group and an allied health clinicians (AHC) group to allow for any comparison but also to mitigate any potential power hierarchy within the group that may hinder a free flow of ideas.18

Patient and public involvement

Given the focus of this study on clinician perspectives on prospective ML predictive technology in their practice, we did not involve any patients or the public in the design or conduct. We further elaborate on the implications and future directions in the discussion section.

Procedure

We developed a list of focus group questions based on existing literature on the use and perception of AI in medicine, which have mostly originated from other medical specialties.1 2 19–23

The focus group guide underwent two rounds of revisions following a discussion with the research team and included nine key final questions (see online supplemental file 2). The questions covered general awareness of AI, potential applications of AI in psychiatry, perceived risks, worries and concerns, the impact of AI on clinician roles, disagreements with AI and perceived willingness to implement among colleagues.

The research team included AW, a physician-scientist and the principal investigator, JC, a medical student, and VTV, a PhD student. The first session was led mainly by AW with the presence of JC and VTV. The remaining sessions were led by JC and VTV. Each focus group lasted about 60 min.

All focus group sessions were conducted virtually through Zoom, and the transcripts retrieved from Zoom’s closed captioning function were manually transcribed verbatim by VTV and JC.

Data analysis

We followed Braun and Clarke’s reflexive approach to thematic analysis, which considers and acknowledges the researcher’s active role in the interpretive analysis of the data.24 25 The analysis is a result of the intersection of the dataset, the theoretical assumptions of the analysis and the analytical skills and resources of the researcher. Within reflexive thematic analysis, we used an inductive approach to coding. While interpretations were primarily based on respondent and data-based meanings, we acknowledge and embrace the subjectivity and creativity inherent in reflexive thematic analysis.24

Data analysis was performed by one researcher, JC, who has a general knowledge of psychiatry, EMR use and familiarity with the various roles of physicians and AHC.

NVivo V.12.7.0 was used for coding and annotating. Coding occurred progressively as the four transcripts were analysed in sequence. Codes generated in the earlier stages of analysis were frequently revisited and refined as subsequent transcripts were analysed. Coding stopped once all transcripts were analysed and iterative analysis revealed no new codes. The team reviewed all the final themes.

Results

Participants included

A total of four focus groups were conducted with clinicians who provided clinical care in psychiatry. Two focus groups included physicians only (MD group) (n=9) (67% females). Six physicians were psychiatrists by training with various subspecialised focuses (geriatric psychiatry, forensic psychiatry, dual diagnosis); the remaining three physicians had a background in family medicine or internal medicine with additional training in mental healthcare. The other two focus groups included various types of AHC such as social work, nursing, pharmacy, behavioural specialist and clinical manager (n=7) (57% females).

A total of 16 participants were included, and each focus group ranged from 3 to 6 participants (see table 1).

Reflexivity

Following Braun and Clarke’s approach to reflexive thematic analysis, we reflect on the potential influences the researchers had on the data collected and interpretation. The first MD group was led by AW, the principal investigator. We noted that the views expressed by this group were more optimistic in contrast to the other MD group, where there was markedly more hesitation and caution. Regarding data analysis, we recognise that interpretations of the data are a result of the intersection between the role and experiences of the data analyst, JC, the research question and the dataset. For example, when coding data, concepts that were more clinically oriented or familiar to the medical student analyst may have been ascribed more meaning and relevance. We attempted to stay close to the data to generate codes reflective of the participants’ sentiments while embracing some subjectivity in the interpretation, as afforded by the unique experiences of the data analyst.

Focus group interviews: themes

We underwent three iterations of coding to arrive at 102 codes that were organised into themes around four central organising concepts: ‘Potential of AI’, ‘Worries and Concerns’, ‘Relationship with AI’ and ‘Promoting AI Acceptability’ (online supplemental file 3). Many of the themes and subthemes identified under ‘Promoting Acceptability of AI’ are directly related and correspond to themes and subthemes that emerged from ‘Worries and Concerns’. In this paper, we focus on ‘Worries and Concerns’, which captures the hesitation present in the focus group discussions around various aspects of the technology and its influence on clinicians’ willingness to implement ML-based predictive tools. Quotes are identified as either from the MD group or the AHC group.

Worries and concerns

Upon exploring clinicians’ worries and concerns about AI, six themes, indicated below, emerged (see figure 1). The more specific subthemes are detailed under each theme in quotation marks.

Map representing the six themes as well as the various subthemes under ‘Worries and Concerns’. AI, artificial intelligence.

AI model

AI model

Concerns relating to the AI predictive model include ‘Accuracy’ and ‘Reliability’, the ‘Lack of Transparency’ of the AI algorithm and the question of who is responsible for ensuring the AI model is maintained and up to date with best practices (‘Model Maintenance’).

I reference the black box kind of thing earlier…Like if I have a calculator, I am going to trust the calculator not second guessing its calculations. But, I did at one point learn how to do arithmetic and if I really did want to, I feel like I could go step by step… and understand how to do those calculations and get to that end result. And if I get to a different end result, I’m going to second guess myself more than the calculator, but I could at least understand kind of the steps it took. And that’s something I don’t think we can do with AI. AHC

Data source

Data source

Participants worried that existing data in healthcare may not be representative, equitable and of high quality. Poor quality of input data affects model validity and may perpetuate existing biases (‘Quality’). There were also concerns about data privacy (‘Privacy’).

Thoughts that I have is garbage in garbage out. MD

I worry that like as we’re developing AI we’re gonna perpetuate sort of biases… that we’ll further embed sort of some of the things that we’re already facing with in terms of like equity and diversity. MD

End user characteristics and behaviours

End user characteristics and behaviours

Many participants shared concerns about who is using it. Participants worried that individuals may vary in their skillset to properly interact with AI tools (‘User Skillset’) and subconsciously prompt for a response (‘Biased Prompting’).

We have a nursing shortage in our province. We have agency staff. There is a steep, steep learning curve, and many of them are new to mental health, new to our setting, new to our patients. They’re going to be very skewed in their capacity to use these instruments. MD

One participant worried that there is already an ‘Over-reliance’ from nursing staff on the EMR to provide care as ordered by the clock, and more AI-enabled tools may lead to deterioration in ‘Critical Thinking’:

Critical thinking is gone. “Well, you know what, I’m seeing this patient having this and this but there’s no clock.” Nobody is thinking outside of that box to go, well, you know what, I’m going to initiate and do this assessment myself. AHC

System (infrastructure and institutional preparedness)

System (infrastructure and institutional preparedness)

There were concerns pertaining to infrastructural and organisational readiness for AI. Participants feared that the current technical infrastructure in healthcare may not be robust enough to support AI development (‘Technical Infrastructure’). They also worried that there is a lack of clarity on who is leading the development and a lack of guidance and support from organisations (‘Institutional Leadership’). The ‘Cost-savings Driven’ mindset of institutions also concerned some participants about their job security at the threat of efficient and cost-effective AI.

I hear a lot about … what’s the physician’s readiness, but I would also argue it’s what’s the system’s readiness to support physicians in doing that, i.e., things like CPSO (College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario), kind of all of those other parts. MD

Infrastructure is actually a rate-limiting step. And that includes both like physical plant infrastructure but also like electronic infrastructure and so I think that it’s hard to talk about opportunities without talking about the things that are practically constraining to those opportunities. MD

I think the most important thing is transparency with the staff … those that are building this model and essentially what the plan is going forward… sharing kind of what’s in the works, what we’re working on, kind of what the goals are. AHC

Clinician wellness

Clinician wellness

Threats to clinician wellness were another critical area of concern for the participants. The physician group frequently mentioned a sense of burden around an anticipated increase in ‘Responsibility and Expectations’ as well as ‘Medico-legal Liability’ as the most responsible provider. When a new decision support tool is introduced, any disagreements with the tool will need to be resolved with proper and lengthy documentation.

I think people need to understand their responsibility vis à vis sort of what’s being recommended and their responsibility for determining the accuracy … An algorithm is recommending a treatment plan or something that’s different than what you would naturally do. Then agreeing with it or not agreeing with it both could have potential consequences … And if we didn’t agree with something that was coming out … we will spend a lot of time documenting to cover ourselves from the medical-legal risk of … why we didn’t comply with it. MD

My concerns and hesitation are … how much more additional work that adds to our burden of non-patient facing time. MD

While AI applications may help offload tasks for straightforward patient cases, clinicians may face an increased volume of complex cases.

I think a lot of people in senior leadership see tools like this as building more capacity … [which] means that there’s higher expectations. So my concern would be… that we’re not given the right amount of time or overloaded with the client load that we can’t really do a good job. AHC

Clinician ‘Autonomy’ can be threatened when AI predictive models or other decision support tools are introduced without considering the clinician perspective. Moreover, AI alerts that ‘instruct’ clinicians also reduce a sense of autonomy.

Ask people what they need, rather than forcing things on them that they don’t need… assess people for their needs as opposed to other priorities that are not directly patient-related. MD

I’d like [it to] try to word it in such a way that there’s no conclusion drawn… like… a prompt to do a more thorough assessment of the patient… as opposed to like pushing people in one direction or another. MD

Additionally, some participants worried that clinician judgement would be less valued with the increasing presence of AI (‘Value for Clinician Expertise’). Concerns around ‘Job Security’ were more frequently mentioned by the AHC group.

Patient outcomes

Patient outcomes

Finally, participants worried that predictive models for patient deterioration or aggression in psychiatry may lead to over-intervening (eg, over-secluding) that may actually harm patients (‘Patient Harm’).

I really do worry that… if there is uncertainty about a patient and the algorithm is saying they might be getting more violent that … the sense will be that the safer thing to do would be to overreact rather than underreact. And that from the staff perspective that’s true but from a patient harm perspective … potentially could be the opposite. MD

In addition, some worried that the predictive model may not be appropriate for a smaller subset of complex and high-risk patients—for which contextual awareness and appropriate clinical judgement become important:

If you took everything and actually like put nothing else into the equation, they should not be coming out of their room. Every time they come out of their room within 5 hours, they punch someone. But we have staff members that push to have them out of their room … we have ways that we can intervene … [and]mitigate those risks. But as an AI, they’re looking at the straightforward facts. That person’s too dangerous to come out of the room. AHC

Another concern unique to psychiatry is whether the integration of AI into patient care may complicate the ‘Illness Process’ of psychosis and certain delusions.

Like my heart kind of breaks for people with, like, paranoid personalities and schizophrenia and stuff who then start realizing and looking behind the desk and seeing that we’re communicating with AI to … develop their interventions and stuff. AHC

Discussion

Primary findings

Our study identified six major themes of concern surrounding AI in psychiatry, ranging from the development, properties and implementation of the technology to how the technology and people may interact and exert influence on each other. These themes, which are derived from clinicians’ feedback and concerns, shape their personal willingness and the systems within which they work, readiness to implement predictive AI tools in their practice. The six themes are AI model, data source, end user behaviours, system preparedness (infrastructural and institutional), clinical well-being and patient outcomes.

An AI-based decision support tool requires a supportive computing infrastructure and power. Furthermore, in situations where the AI tool performs its own independent assessment and generates a clinical decision that the clinician either agrees or disagrees with, participants worried about not being able to understand how the algorithm arrived at the conclusion, expressing apprehension about the ‘black box phenomenon’. Matters are complicated further since the AI can be ‘wrong’. This concern touches on the accountability and explainability of clinical decisions. Participants highlighted a need for as much transparency as possible regarding the model development process to assist clinicians in critically appraising the validity of the model and interpreting the AI output for a given clinical scenario. At the same time, there were concerns around the potential decline in critical thinking in the face of automated alerts and an over-reliance on AI technology.

Furthermore, participants did not have trust in all users of AI technology and worried that some may not have the skillset to interact with AI. In a similar study investigating general practitioners’ attitudes towards AI-enabled systems, it was found that physicians may vary in their ‘affinity with technology’, which can, in turn, influence their engagement and use of AI more than characteristics like age.26 Attitudes also vary depending on the intended use of AI. Scott et al conducted a review of 27 studies, of which 23 were surveys, to examine stakeholder attitudes toward AI and found that AI applications intended for automated data interpretation and synthesis were favoured over those that directly influenced clinical decisions or potentially impacted clinician–patient relationships.27 This is consistent with our finding that participants had favourable attitudes towards AI applications that ‘inform’ and not ‘instruct’. In the same review study, they also observed that users with more direct experiences with AI had more positive attitudes.

Ethical considerations

There are unique ethical considerations in the field of psychiatry due to patient characteristics as well as the unique types of adverse events. Predictive models shift care from reactive to pre-emptive, which may look different in psychiatry. For example, while a high-risk ML prediction in the general internal medicine ward may trigger an urgent reassessment or medical intervention, in psychiatry, it could lead to control interventions such as restraints, which carry their own risk.28 Participants in our study highlighted a unique intersection between predictive AI and the illness process of psychotic disorders. Similarly, there is previous literature surrounding the concerns of AI biases and the further amplification of certain inequities.29

Strengths and limitations

While our sample provided valuable insights for the development of our in-house ML prediction model, our study was limited to one hospital, which limits the generalisability of our findings to other mental health settings. At the same time, a strength of our study lies in the heterogeneity of our sample, where our physicians and allied clinicians all had various training backgrounds and were providing different aspects of mental healthcare. In our physician group, our psychiatrists had various subspecialised focuses and we also included non-psychiatrists who were providing care at a large mental health hospital. Our allied clinician groups included care providers that were traditionally less represented in this type of research, such as pharmacy, social workers and behavioural support specialists.27

Nevertheless, we recognise that given participants were recruited from one institution located in rural Ontario, the sample may have reflected a more conservative group of clinicians. Future studies would benefit from recruiting participants from multiple hospital sites and care settings to investigate whether other factors like geographical location, practice type, as well as individual tolerance for novelty and adoption of new practices can influence receptiveness towards AI technology. Another limitation to note is that this study did not include patients or members of the public. While the project was intentionally clinician-focused to inform the internal key targets for implementation and planning, we recognise that patients and the public contribute unique perspectives that should be captured, through appropriate patient and public involvement in future work. Therefore, in future studies, we plan to engage patients and people with lived experience in more structured discussions around specific use cases of predictive tools in mental healthcare. Lastly, this study did not return the results to the participants for validation. We suggest that future studies may benefit from the use of measurement-based tools such as surveys for participant validation.

Clinical implications

With similarities to the revolution from paper to computerised medicine in the early 2000s, we are now discovering the potential of AI in transforming healthcare delivery and practices. While some individuals have more optimism than others, we found that clinicians in psychiatry generally do not think AI is ready to take a place in patient care due to concerns about model validity and accuracy, system readiness and technical infrastructure, variability in skill to use AI, uncertain influence on patient outcomes and the burdens on clinicians. Based on our findings, we put forth the following recommendations for clinicians, vendors and developers and healthcare institutions to promote successful and effective integration of predictive AI tools in clinical psychiatry: (1) tailored education and training for clinicians along with the implementation of AI tools; (2) regular and transparent communication with clinicians throughout the model development process to allow for critical appraisal and trust-building; (3) ongoing and standardised process of evaluating model performance; (4) early engagement with clinicians post-implementation to gather feedback and enhance user experience; (5) clear messaging from institutions on the intent of AI as a support tool to complement clinical decision-making and improve workflow.

We find that our recommendations align with the ‘Good Machine Learning Practice for Medical Device Development’ (GMLP) developed jointly by the US Food and Drug Administration, Health Canada and the UK’s Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency in 2021.30 Of the 10 guiding principles listed as GMLP, there is a similar focus on providing users with clear and essential information, such as model performance across subgroups and characteristics of the data source used for model training and testing. Furthermore, the GMLP places an emphasis on evaluating the performance of the ‘human-AI team’, recognising human factors considerations and human interpretability of the model outputs. Some of these factors, we believe, would have been similar to the themes we identified under ‘end-user related’ worries and concerns like biased prompting, over-reliance, user skill set and user accountability. Our recommendation for ongoing evaluation and re-evaluation of model performance informed by clinician concerns was also supported by GMLP, where they additionally outline the risks of overfitting and degradation of the model over time that would require monitoring and re-training.30 The UK’s National Health Service, through their ‘AI Lab’, further suggests quasi-experimental and mixed-method approaches to evaluating AI tools and a duration of at least 2 years for evaluating multisite deployment.31

The Canadian Medical Protection Association (CMPA) recently published a paper titled ‘The Medico-legal Lens on AI Use by Canadian Physicians’ outlining a proposed framework of responsibilities for various stakeholders involved in the integration of AI.32 For vendors and developers, the CMPA similarly highlights the importance of transparency and continuous monitoring, although our findings suggest that transparency goes beyond communicating model performance to clinicians and requires clear communication regarding the whole model development process. A key message from the CMPA advocates for the involvement of stakeholders including clinicians, patients and management throughout the design, development, validation, deployment and monitoring stages of AI products.

To our knowledge, there are currently no psychiatry-specific guidelines on the use of predictive AI from the various psychiatric associations. From the American Psychological Association (APA), however, several mental health-related AI applications, although not ML-based outcome prediction tools, were mentioned, including digital therapeutics facilitating cognitive behavioural therapy and even AI-powered patient cases to train clinicians on how to provide therapy.33 The APA lists 13 steps to guide clinicians on appraising and selecting the most appropriate AI tool to augment their practice. One of the checklists includes knowing if mental and behavioural health clinicians were involved or represented on the leadership team of vendor or AI development companies.34 The checklist assists clinicians in doing their due diligence on selecting an AI tool and can be applied to psychiatry settings as well.

Our study offers important insight into some clinicians’ perspectives and concerns for AI in mental health and serves as a reference point in time as the technology continues to evolve. As clinicians gain more experience with AI in their practice, their perspectives and receptiveness may change and new concerns may arise. These formative stage insights should be taken into consideration prior to the implementation of any AI-based tools, particularly predictive tools. Ultimately, continuous engagement and partnership with clinicians and key knowledge users will be critical for identifying emerging challenges and ensuring the successful adoption and integration of AI in psychiatric settings.
