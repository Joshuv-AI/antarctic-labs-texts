Effect of TMEM18 rs7561317 on metabolome in context of obesity
Authors: Sabiha Farooq, Sobia Rana, Amna Jabbar Siddiqui, Ayesha Iqbal, Syed Ghulam Musharraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTMEM18 has been indicated by genome-wide association studies as a key gene linked to obesity, yet its exact function and connections with major metabolic networks are unknown.ObjectivesThe current study is aimed to determine whether the TMEM18 rs7561317 polymorphism linked with anthropometric indicators of obesity is accompanied with alterations in serum metabolites.MethodsThis was a case–control study in which a total of 542 participants (Pakistani nationals) were involved including overweight or obese cases and those having normal body mass index (BMI). All participants provided blood samples which were utilized to extract serum and genomic DNA. The genomic DNA of all participants was genotyped for the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant while their serum samples were subjected to untargeted gas chromatography–mass spectrometry based metabolomics.ResultsA total of 42 putatively annotated metabolites were selected for further statistical analyses. Analyses revealed that the TMEM18 gene variant (rs7561317) exhibited statistically significant association with five metabolites namely urea, eicosane, geraniol, pentadecanoic acid and porphine as well as with BMI, percent body fat, waist circumference and weight. The G allele of this variant appears to increase the risk of developing overweight or obesity and may be associated with metabolite alterations.ConclusionThe findings highlight the role of metabolite alterations in the manifestation of obese phenotype among individuals carrying the GG genotype of the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02466-w.

Introduction

Obesity is an intricate and multifactorial metabolic malady representing a major global public health concern (Hruby & Hu, 2015a). It is manifested by abnormal accretion of fat mass owing to increase in size and number of adipocytes. This increase in fat mass triggers various pathological pathways detrimental to health (Landgraf et al., 2020). The alarming increase has been seen in global prevalence of obesity in last few decades (Hruby & Hu, 2015b). While environmental and behavioral factors like unhealthy eating habits and low/lack of physical activity play important roles in expression of obese phenotype; it also has a considerable hereditary component as genetic factors contribute substantially to its onset and progression (Locke et al., 2015; Yengo et al., 2018). Therefore, comprehending the genetic architecture of obesity is crucial to reveal its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms (Willer et al., 2009).

Numerous genetic loci associated with increased obesity risk have been identified via genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (Locke et al., 2015). Among these, the transmembrane protein 18 or TMEM18 has appeared as a key putative gene involved in the expression and progression of obese phenotype (Fall & Ingelsson, 2014). This gene is expressed in key brain regions including the hypothalamus that controls feeding behavior (Almén et al., 2010; Willer et al., 2009), and has been reported to regulate body weight by means of transcriptional repression mechanisms (Larder et al., 2017b; Speakman, 2013). Loss of TMEM18 function in rodent models when exposed to high fat diet led to augmented body weight owing to overeating. On the contrary, TMEM18 overexpression in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus led to reduced body weight via increased energy expenditure and decreased food intake in wild-type mice. These outcomes imply that genetic variations affecting TMEM18 expression or function might contribute to the development of obesity. In particular, the rs7561317 variant (A > G) positioned nearly 22 kilobases downstream from the gene encoding transmembrane protein 18, is potentially associated with higher body mass index (BMI) (Larder et al., 2017a). Though the evidence that TMEM18 is involved in neural stem cell motility corroborated its role in central nervous system, the precise connection between its expression, feeding behavior, and obesity phenotypes remains to be fully explicated.

Metabolite genome-wide association studies (mGWAS) have been employed to investigate the regulation of metabolic profiles at genetic level and their relations to complex phenotypes like obesity (Adamski & Suhre, 2013). Such strategies aid in identifying biological networks that link genetic variations to alterations in metabolites. The several metabolites that are part of lipid, branched-chain amino acid, purine/pyrimidine metabolism, aromatic amino acid catabolism, beta-oxidation, and the urea cycle have been related to obesity phenotypes (Kim et al., 2019). Many studies rather than carrying out whole-genome analyses focus on already known candidate genes, hence, provide targeted comprehensions about connection between genotype and phenotype. Pakistan being the fifth most populous country of the world, encounters an alarmingly growing obesity epidemic as substantial proportion of its population has been categorized as overweight or obese (Marrachelli et al., 2014; Ried et al., 2013). Despite this, research on the genetic and metabolic aspects of obesity in this region remains limited.

The findings of our previous study (Rana & Sultana, 2020) underscores the relevance of the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant in population-specific obesity risk but do not explicate the downstream metabolic perturbations connected with it. To bridge this gap, studying the metabolite profile in relation to the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant may provide novel insights into the metabolomic alterations underlying its association with obesity and help elucidate its functional role. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the association between the TMEM18 rs7561317 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and anthropometric indicators of obesity in relation to serum metabolite profiles, to enhance comprehension regarding the biochemical mechanisms that drive obesity in this population.

Materials and methods

Participants’ recruitment and ethical approval

A case–control study design was employed to assess the genetic and metabolic factors associated with obesity. The study cohort comprised 542 participants, including cases (n = 246) and controls (n = 296), aged between 12 and 63 years, with a mean age of 28.89 ± 8.85 years. Participants were recruited from Karachi (majority from the University of Karachi) using a simple random sampling method without replacement. The study sample comprised participants from diverse ethnic backgrounds, predominantly Urdu-speaking (> 50%), followed by Punjabi, Pashtun, Sindhi, Balochi, and others.

According to standard BMI classifications, the participants of the study were divided into two categories. The individuals having normal BMI (18.5-24.9 kg/m2) were categorized as controls while those having higher BMI than normal (≥ 25 kg/m2) were categorized as overweight or obese cases. All subjects gave written informed consent for their participation in the study. A parent or legal guardian provided consent in case of individuals under 18 years of age.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the IEC (Independent Ethics Committee) of the institute (International Center for Chemical and Biological Sciences abbreviated as ICCBS), University of Karachi, Pakistan. All research procedures adhered to the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 1989.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric data were collected using standardized protocols to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Measurements included height, weight, BMI, waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), and skinfold thickness at multiple anatomical sites, including the triceps, thighs, abdomen, and supra-iliac region. From these measurements, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as WC divided by HC, while waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was derived by dividing WC by height. Percent body fat (%BF) was estimated using validated skinfold-based equations.

Blood sample collection and serum preparation

Approximately 5 mL of peripheral blood was collected from each participant following an overnight fast of 8–12 h, using standard venipuncture procedures. Sterile, single-use needles (BD, NJ, USA) were employed to ensure aseptic conditions. For genomic DNA extraction, 2 mL of blood was transferred into EDTA-coated vacutainer tubes (Cat. No. 367841, BD, USA). The remaining 3 mL was collected in serum separator tubes containing clot activator and gel (Cat. No. 367983, BD, USA) for serum isolation.

Samples designated for serum separation were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (serum) was carefully aliquoted into labeled microtubes and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Genomic DNA isolation and genotyping

Whole blood samples were processed to separate genomic DNA employing the EZ-10 kit designed for genomic DNA extraction via spin column (Catalog No. BS684, Bio Basic, Ontario, Canada) in line with the provided protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed prior to downstream applications to ensure suitability for genotyping.

The TMEM18 rs7561317 SNP was genotyped using a TaqMan™ allelic discrimination assay (Assay ID: C__11804554_10, Cat. No. 4351379, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reaction mixture included TaqMan® Genotyping Master Mix (Cat. No. 4381656, Applied Biosystems), along with appropriate primers and probes.

Each genotyping run included quality control measures, comprising two reactions without template (NTCs) and a positive template control (PTC) representing each genotype (GG, AG, and AA). To further validate the results, about 15% of the samples were selected on random basis and re-analyzed for genotype verification, demonstrating high reproducibility and accuracy.

Solvents and reagents

Analytical-grade solvents and reagents were utilized throughout the study to ensure the accuracy and consistency of experimental results. The chemicals used included N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA), methanol (Tedia, Tediaway, Fairfield, USA), pyridine (Lab-Scan, Bangkok, Thailand), methoxylamine hydrochloride, hexane, and myristic-d27 acid (Acros Organics, New Jersey, USA), which served as an internal standard. Deionized water was obtained from a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). All serum samples were subsequently analyzed using a high-resolution gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) platform.

Sample preparation and metabolomics analysis

The protocol for serum sample preparation and metabolite profiling followed previously published methods and adhered to relevant experimental standards and guidelines (Musharraf et al., 2015). In brief, protein precipitation was performed using chilled methanol containing myristic-d27 acid as an internal standard. The resulting supernatant was transferred onto a 96-well C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) plate and subjected to vacuum filtration. The solid phase was sequentially washed with deionized water and methanol, and metabolites were eluted using methanol into a 96-well collection plate.

Methanolic eluates were dried at room temperature using a vacuum concentrator and stored at 4 °C until further processing. For derivatization, the dried extracts were treated with methoxylamine hydrochloride followed by conversion into trimethylsilyl (TMS) derivatives. After derivatization, the samples were centrifuged and analyzed using GC-MS as previously described (Musharraf et al., 2013). Agilent MassHunter software was employed for processing the initial data. Detected spectral peaks were matched against the NIST mass spectral database (Wiley Registry NIST 11) with compound identities assigned based on a similarity index ≥ 70%. The resulting GC-MS data were imported into Mass Profiler Professional (MPP) software for further preprocessing, including filtering and normalization.

A total of 2295 spectral features were detected. Following statistical filtering (p < 0.05), 142 features showed significant associations. Of these, 42 were putatively annotated (level 2 of Metabolomics Standards Initiative), while 100 remained unannotated. Subsequent statistical analyses were conducted exclusively on the annotated metabolites, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 21, Chicago, IL, USA) to detect serum metabolites that exhibit a strong correlation with obese phenotypes in relation to the TMEM18 rs7561317 gene variant. Quantitative variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All variables with non-normal distributions were normalized prior to regression to meet linearity assumptions.

Participants were stratified into three genotype groups based on the TMEM18 rs7561317 polymorphism: AA (wild-type) was coded as 1, AG (heterozygous) as 2, and GG (homozygous risk allele) as 3. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was assessed independently for control and case groups using an online HWE calculator. To explore the relationship between the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant and obesity risk, logistic regression analyses were performed under recessive (GG vs. AG + AA), dominant (GG + AG vs. AA), over-dominant (AG vs. GG + AA), and additive (GG vs. AG vs. AA: genotype as a linear dosage of risk alleles) genetic models with the G-allele of TMEM18 rs7561317 considered the risk allele for defining genotype categories. The inheritance pattern was assessed via the h-index scoring approach. After determining the best-fitting inheritance pattern via h-index, all subsequent regression analyses involving genotype were performed under a recessive model framework.

Linear regression was subsequently employed to assess the association between genotype (rs7561317), putatively annotated metabolites, and anthropometric traits (BMI, weight, WC, WHR, HC, WHtR, and %BF). For associations between metabolites and anthropometric traits, metabolites were treated as independent variables and anthropometric traits as dependent variables. Effect sizes (β coefficients) along with p-values (< 0.05 threshold for statistical significance) were reported for all correlation analyses.

All analyses were adjusted for age and gender to minimize their potential confounding effects on the outcome. To control for false discovery rates due to multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied.

Association between TMEM18 rs7561317 variant and overweight/obesity risk

To assess the relationship between the TMEM18 rs7561317 polymorphism and obesity susceptibility, a binary logistic regression analysis was carried out using various genetic inheritance models, including recessive, dominant, overdominant, and additive models (Table 1). The G allele of the variant demonstrated a statistically significant association with increased risk of overweight/obesity under the additive and recessive models. To determine the most appropriate inheritance pattern for this variant, the h-index was calculated. The resulting h-index value of − 0.49 suggests a recessive pattern of inheritance for the obesity-related trait linked to rs7561317 (Table 1).

Discussion

TMEM18 has appeared as a potential obesity-associated gene identified through GWAS. The SNP rs7561317 downstream from the TMEM18 gene has exhibited a strong correlation with increased BMI. The experimentation in animal models indicate that the expression of TMEM18 in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) is controlled via nutritional status emphasizing its potential role in central energy balance. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying association between TMEM18 variants and obesity remain partially known (Larder et al., 2017b). Extending our previous findings (Rana & Sultana, 2020) the current study identifies metabolomic perturbations connected with the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant, thereby providing functional insight into metabolic pathways potentially mediating its role in obesity.

In the current study, TMEM18 rs7561317 variant was found to be significantly associated with obese phenotype in recessive genetic model. However, it is worth mentioning that slightly higher raw counts of the risk genotype, GG, observed in the control group (189) as compared to the case group (199) is attributable to the larger number of control participants (cases: 246, controls: 296). While comparing groups of different sizes, the relevant comparison is based on proportional frequencies of genotype or allele within each group rather than absolute counts. Accordingly, the proportional frequency of the GG genotype is higher in obese cases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(189\div 246=0.768\right)$$\end{document} than in controls \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(199\div 296=0.672\right)$$\end{document}, indicating that GG genotype is more prevalent among cases. This difference in distribution of these relative frequencies is consistent with the observed significant association with obesity risk (Table 1: OR = 1.616, p = 0.014). In addition, linear regression analysis using continuous phenotypes such as BMI, weight, WC, WHtR, and %BF demonstrated a significant positive β coefficient for the GG genotype of recessive model, highlights that carriers of this genotype have higher obesity-related measures (Table 2), independently supporting for the risk effect of the GG genotype. Therefore, both the higher proportional frequency of GG in cases and its positive association with obesity-related traits consistently support its role as a risk genotype. Moreover, apart from the current study, such apparent discrepancies can occur in complex traits due to sampling variability, covariate effects, or gene–environment interactions. The regression model accounts for confounding factors and provides a more accurate estimate of the genotype–phenotype association, whereas simple allele counts do not. Therefore, such observations do not always contradict the identified risk effect of the GG genotype and underscores the multifactorial nature of obesity.

Following the observed association of the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant with anthropometric measures (weight, BMI, WC, and %BF), a total of 16 metabolites were also found to be correlated with the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant. We further examined the relationship between these anthropometric measures and the metabolites connected to the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant in relation to obesity. Among the twelve metabolites analyzed (porphine, n-pentadecanoic acid, urea, geraniol, n-eicosane, glycerol, phenylethanolamine, heptadecanoic acid, phosphoric acid, 2-penten-2,4-diol, 2-methyl-9,10-dihydroanthracene, and stearic acid), nine showed significant associations with one or more of the anthropometric traits linked to rs7561317. Notably, five putatively annotated metabolites porphine, geraniol, n-pentadecanoic acid, urea, and n-eicosane demonstrated significant correlations across all four TMEM18 rs7561317-associated anthropometric variables (weight, BMI, WC, and %BF).

An important metabolite putatively annotated in the current study is porphine, which represent the parent structure of porphyrins and ultimately are the key parts of hemoproteins. Former studies have reported that the genes involved in heme biosynthesis namely HMBS, ALAS1, and ALAD show sustained expression in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) as well as in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), with a pronounced upregulated expression in SAT. Remarkably, individuals having obese phenotype display a decreased expression of these genes implying a possible link between excessive build-up of adipose tissue and compromised biosynthesis of heme (Yap et al., 2015). However, these observations should be interpreted with caution, as the tentative annotation of porphine may reflect a related porphyrin-derived fragment rather than the intact compound, and therefore requires further validation.

The second significant metabolite namely pentadecanoic acid (C15:0) is an odd-chain saturated fatty acid (OCFA) present in minute quantities in dairy, fish, ruminant meat, and some plants (Jenkins et al., 2015). C15:0 has been associated with desirable outcomes in cardiometabolic and inflammatory conditions (Mathieu et al., 2010; Venn-Watson & Butterworth, 2022). In obese individuals, C15:0 appears to serve as a biomarker for oxidative stress and inflammation (Wang et al., 2011). Several large-scale population studies have consistently reported an inverse association between circulating OCFA levels (C15:0 and C17:0) and the risk of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and mortality (Aglago et al., 2017; Forouhi et al., 2014; Kurotani et al., 2017). However, inconsistencies remain, possibly because of cohort differences. In the current study, C15:0 demonstrated a positive association with anthropometric indices (BMI, weight, %BF, WC) and the TMEM18 rs7561317 risk allele. This highlights a possible connection between reduced dietary OCFA intake and increased susceptibility to obese phenotypes.

A significant positive correlation of geraniol (a naturally occurring monoterpenoid alcohol) with anthropometric indicators of obesity and the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant was also found in the current study. Many studies have reported about the Geraniol’s anti-lipid peroxidative (Chen & Viljoen, 2022) and anti-hyperglycemic properties (Babukumar et al., 2017). Its diphosphate form, geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP), is a key intermediate in the mevalonate pathway that is involved in adipocyte turnover and cholesterol biosynthesis (Jayachandran et al., 2015; Yeh et al., 2018). Elevated geraniol levels in individuals carrying the risk allele of TMEM18 rs7561317 suggest its possible role in adipogenic and metabolic processes, even though further validation in larger genotype datasets is needed.

Eicosane (a saturated hydrocarbon) was another metabolite found to be positively associated with anthropometric obesity traits and the TMEM18 variant. Similar hydrocarbons have been identified as markers of oxidative stress in different anomalies, including infectious, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases in some previous studies (Bobak et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2022). In the context of obesity, the presence of such metabolites should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect oxidative stress or possible exogenous contributions, and their biological relevance remains to be established.

Finally, urea was also positively associated with the TMEM18 rs7561317 risk allele and obesity-related parameters. Urea is a principal nitrogenous waste product that reflects the balance between protein metabolism and renal function. Elevated urea levels in individuals with obesity may be attributed to altered nitrogen metabolism, increased protein catabolism, and potential renal dysfunction (Khan et al., 2017). Former studies have shown a positive correlation between BMI and serum urea and uric acid levels, suggesting that obesity may undesirably impact renal function and amino acid metabolism (Dikker et al., 2023). The findings suggest that elevated urea may serve as a metabolic indicator of impaired renal function and increased insulin resistance in obese state.

The current study has certain limitations. First, the sample size was relatively modest (n = 542). Second, genetic structure of the sample population was not assessed. Although the sample population reflects the known ethnic composition of Karachi (predominantly Urdu-speaking (> 50%), followed by Punjabi, Pashtun, Sindhi, Balochi, and others), subtle population stratification cannot be completely excluded. Metabolite annotations were performed through spectral matching against the NIST Mass Spectral Library using reverse match scores without retention index (RI) matching or validation with authentic reference standards; therefore, all annotations should be considered putative and interpreted with caution. Additionally, the moderate sample size and exploratory nature of the study limit biological inference, and the findings should be regarded as preliminary and require further validation.

Conclusion

This study highlights the metabolic associations of the TMEM18 rs7561317 polymorphism with overweight/obesity. The findings underscore the role of metabolite alterations in the obesity among individuals carrying the GG genotype of the TMEM18 rs7561317 variant. These observations may provide preliminary insights into metabolic patterns associated with this polymorphism, however, further studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these associations and dissect the underlying biological mechanisms.
