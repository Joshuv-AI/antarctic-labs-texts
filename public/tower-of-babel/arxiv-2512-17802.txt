Systematic search of laser and phase modulation noise coupling in heterodyne interferometry
Kohei Yamamoto, Olaf Hartwig, Lennart Wissel, Holly Leopardi, Kenji Numata, Ryan Derosa

Abstract
Heterodyne interferometry for precision science often comes with an optical phase modulation, for example, for intersatellite clock noise transfer for gravitational wave (GW) detectors in space, exemplified by the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA). The phase modulation potentially causes various noise couplings to the final phase extraction of heterodyne beatnotes by a phasemeter. In this paper, in the format of space-based GW detectors, we establish an analytical framework to systematically search for the coupling of various noises from the heterodyne and modulation frequency bands, which are relatively unexplored so far. In addition to the noise caused by the phase modulation, the high-frequency laser phase noise is also discussed in the same framework. The analytical result is also compared with a numerical experiment to confirm that our framework successfully captures the major noise couplings. We also demonstrate a use case of this study by taking the LISA-like parameters as an example, which enables us to derive requirements on the level of the laser and phase modulation noises in the high frequency regimes.



I
Introduction

Heterodyne interferometry is an optical technique widely used in precision science
2
;
1
;
14
;
11
;
10
.
Essential ingredients are two electromagnetic fields with a frequency offset, the so-called heterodyne frequency, a beam combiner to generate their optical beatnote at the heterodyne frequency, a photoreceiver (PR) to convert the optical power to an electronic voltage signal, and a phasemeter to measure the phase of the heterodyne beatnote.
The interferometer used as a transducer reports the observable of interest in an experimental setup, often spatial displacement, via the phase signal with very high precision and/or accuracy.

This technique is often combined with an optical modulation to transfer or acquire additional information, for example, intersatellite clock synchronization or absolute ranging for spaceborne gravitational-wave detectors like the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA)
5
.
LISA aims to observe gravitational waves (GWs) in the millihertz regime by configuring interspacecraft heterodyne interferometers with three spacecraft separated by around 2.5 million kilometer.
For the purpose of differential clock noise reduction, LISA phase-modulates an outgoing beam with an onboard system clock around
2.4\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
and extracts the phase of the heterodyne beatnotes not only between beam carriers that have the information of GWs but also between the clock sidebands in which the onboard clock difference is encoded
8
;
30
.
All heterodyne frequencies in the constellation are adjusted within
5\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}30\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
via laser offset lock, so-called frequency planning
9
.

Since we modulate the phase of the primary laser carrier, this could naturally cause additional noise couplings in the metrology chain.
From the perspective of the clock noise transfer, the coupling of the noise in the observation band, namely
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{mHz}
to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
, has been studied in great depth
3
;
12
;
31
;
26
.
However, the working principle of the phasemeter is known to also cause the unavoidable coupling of noises at the frequencies related to heterodyne beatnotes, e.g., the integer multiple of the heterodyne frequency
24
;
23
;
4
.
The combination of the multiple heterodyne beatnotes and the phasemeter architecture in LISA could generate numerous noise couplings from the heterodyne band.

In this paper, we systematically search for the coupling of modulation noises above the GW observation band to the final phase extraction of individual heterodyne beatnotes by the phasemeter.
In addition to the noise caused by phase modulation, the coupling of the laser phase noise is also discussed in the same analytical framework.
The noise frequency band of interest is not only the heterodyne band but also the modulation band (the MHz and GHz regime for LISA, respectively).
The noise couplings found analytically are compared with those found by numerical simulation to confirm that our analytical framework successfully captures major noise contributions.
Finally, we demonstrate a use case of this study by applying it to the LISA-like set of requirements, which enables us to derive new requirements on the high-frequency noise sources in the system.

II.1
Modulation signal

We consider the case of modulating a beam phase with a sinusoidal signal.
Let us formulate the modulation signal
V_{i}
in units of voltage that drives an electro-optic modulator (EOM) as

\displaystyle V_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{0}(1+a_{i}(\tau))\cos(\omega_{\mrm{m},i}\tau+ \phi^\mrm{sb}_{\mrm{tar},i}(\tau) + \theta_i(\tau))+v_{i}(\tau),

(1)

where
a_{0}
is a nominal modulation amplitude;

\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}
is an angular frequency of the sinusoidal modulation signal, related to the frequency
f_{\mathrm{m},i}
by
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}=2\pi f_{\mathrm{m},i}
;

\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}
is the target phase to encode onto modulation sidebands;

a_{i}
is a modulation relative amplitude noise;

\theta_{i}
is a modulation additive phase noise;

v_{i}
is a modulation additive voltage noise.

i
is an index to label a modulation signal and an associated laser beam (
i\in\{i,j\}
).

II.2
Laser beam

A non-modulated electromagnetic field representing a laser beam can be formulated as

\displaystyle E_{i}=A_{i}\cdot\exp(j\omega_i\tau+ j\phi^\mrm{car}_{\mrm{tar},i}(\tau) + jp_i(\tau)),

(2)

where
A_{i}
is a nominal amplitude;

\omega_{i}
is a laser angular frequency, related to the frequency
f_{i}
by
\omega_{i}=2\pi f_{i}
;

\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}
is the target phase that we aim to measure with a phasemeter at the end;

p_{i}
is a laser phase noise in radian.
In this paper, we assume that
A_{i}
is constant.
The noise coupling of the beam amplitude, caused by the relative intensity noise (RIN), can be found in
24
;
23
.

Next, we phase-modulate the beam in
eq.

2
using the modulation signal in
eq.

1
.
We consider the EOM as a pure linear converter of an input voltage to a phase with a conversion factor of
\pi/V_{\mathrm{\pi}}

where
V_{\mathrm{\pi}}
is determined by the EOM specification.
Therefore, the actual modulation phase becomes

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{V_{\mathrm{\pi}}}V_{i}(\tau).

(3)

As a result, the phase-modulated laser beam reads as follows:

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i}\cdot\exp(j\omega_i\tau+ j\phi^\mrm{car}_{\mrm{tar},i} + jp_i + j\phi_i)

\displaystyle=A_{i}\cdot\exp\left(j\omega_{i}\tau+j\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}+j(p_{i}+n_{i})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\left.+jm_{i}\cos(\omega_{\mrm{m},i}\tau+ \phi^\mrm{sb}_{\mrm{tar},i} + \theta_i)\right),

(4)

since we define

\displaystyle m_{i}

\displaystyle=m_{0}(1+a_{i}),

(5)

\displaystyle m_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{V_{\mathrm{\pi}}}a_{0},

(6)

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{V_{\mathrm{\pi}}}v_{i}.

(7)

where
m_{0}
is a modulation depth in radian.
Here and throughout the rest of the paper, we omit the time arguments for simplicity.

We apply the Jacobi-Anger expansion to
eq.

4
, to first-order modulation sidebands,

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i}\cdot\exp\left(j\omega_{i}\tau+j\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}+j(p_{i}+n_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\cdot\left(J_{0}(m_{i})+jJ_{1}(m_{i})\mathrm{e}^{j(\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}+\theta_{i})}+jJ_{1}(m_{i})\mathrm{e}^{-j(\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}+\theta_{i})}\right),

(8)

where
J_{n}(x)
is the Bessel function of the first kind.
Noise couplings due to the higher-order modulation sidebands would not be dominant; however, we particularly discuss the noise coupling caused by the second-order modulation sidebands in
section

IV
assuming that the nominal modulation depth
m_{0}
is not so small.

It is obvious that the laser phase noise
p_{i}
and the modulation additive voltage noise
n_{i}
are indistinguishable.
Hence, for the rest of this paper, we only consider the laser phase noise
p_{i}
.
However, it is important to note that any result of
p_{i}
also applies to
n_{i}
.

II.3
Beatnote signal

We finally formulate the interference between two laser beams
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
.
The PR output signal
s_{\mathrm{pr}}
is expressed below in units of W with some constant factors omitted, such as a responsivity (A/W) and a transimpedance-amplifier gain (V/A):

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{pr}}

\displaystyle=|E_{i}+E_{j}|^{2}/2

\displaystyle=(|E_{i}|^{2}+|E_{j}|^{2})/2+\real[E_{i}E^{*}_{j}]

\displaystyle=(P_{i}+P_{j})/2+A_{i}A_{j}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\cdot\real\left[\exp(j\omega_\mrm{het}\tau+ \phi^\mrm{car}_\mrm{tar} + jp_\Delta)\cdot\left(J_{0}(m_{i})J_{0}(m_{j})+J_{1}(m_{i})J_{1}(m_{j})\mathrm{e}^{j(\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+\theta_{\Delta})}+J_{1}(m_{i})J_{1}(m_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-j(\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+\theta_{\Delta})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(P_{i}+P_{j})/2

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+A_{i}A_{j}J_{0}(m_{i})J_{0}(m_{j})\cos\left(\omega_{\mathrm{het}}\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+p_{\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+A_{i}A_{j}J_{1}(m_{i})J_{1}(m_{j})\cos\left((\omega_{\mathrm{het}}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau+\phi^{\mathrm{usb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+p_{\Delta}+\theta_{\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+A_{i}A_{j}J_{1}(m_{i})J_{1}(m_{j})\cos\left((\omega_{\mathrm{het}}-\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau-\phi^{\mathrm{lsb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+p_{\Delta}-\theta_{\Delta}\right),

(9)

where the target phase differences to measure are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\phi^{x}_{\mathrm{tar}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{x}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\phi^{x}_{\mathrm{tar},j}\hskip 14.22636ptx\in\{\mathrm{car},\mathrm{sb}\},

(10)

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{usb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}+\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar}},

(11)

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{lsb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar}}-\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{tar}},

(12)

eq.

9
plays a pivotal role throughout this paper.
For simplicity, the same physical quantities from individual beams are bundled as its difference as follows:

P_{i}=A^{2}_{i}
: single beam power;

\omega_{\mathrm{het}}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}
: heterodyne frequency;

\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}}=\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}-\omega_{\mathrm{m},j}
: modulation frequency offset (e.g., nominally around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
in LISA);

p_{\Delta}=p_{i}-p_{j}
: laser phase noise difference;

\theta_{\Delta}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
: modulation phase noise difference.
Note that throughout the paper, we assume
\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{m}}>0
:
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}>\omega_{\mathrm{m},j}
.

The PR signal in
eq.

9
has four constituents: non-interfering components, a carrier-carrier beatnote, a usb-usb beatnote, and a lsb-lsb beatnote.
As we model an EOM as a pure phase modulator, neglecting residual amplitude modulation, any modulation noise never changes the laser total power, and correspondingly also not the power of any non-interfering components as well.
Therefore, we discuss only the interfering components for the rest of the paper.

II.4
Strategy

Let us wrap up this section by providing a general description of our strategy to analyze the noise coupling.

First, following
24
, we model a given noise
x_{i}
as a monotonic tone at the angular frequency of
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
as

\displaystyle x_{i}(\tau;\omega_{\mathrm{n}})

\displaystyle=m_{x}\cos(\omega_\mrm{n} \tau+ \rho),

(13)

where
m_{x}
is the noise (long-term average) amplitude and
\rho
is a given random phase.
We will only consider the first-order coupling of
m_{x}
.
As discussed later, some noises are expected to have coherence; therefore, it is important to base different noises on the consistent form.
In contrast to the time-domain analysis, the noises have to be evaluated in amplitude spectral density (ASD) at the end to project it onto a setup sensitivity, which is usually expressed in the frequency domain.
As presented in
appendix

B
, we can easily translate the time-domain coupling factor to that in ASD by multiplying by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
; therefore, we will stick to the time-domain analysis.

Second,
appendix

A
derives the phasemeter readout noise for the input signal in
eq.

85
, which we cite here:

\displaystyle s=k\cos(\omega\tau+ \phi_k)+l\cos\left((\omega+\epsilon)\tau+\phi_{l}\right),

(14)

where
\epsilon\ll\omega
and
l\ll k
.
According to
eqs.

87
and
88
, when the phasemeter extracts the target phase
\phi_{k}
, the actual phasemeter readout
\phi_{\mathrm{read}}
is affected by the second monotonic noise term as:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{read}}

\displaystyle\approx\phi_{k}+l/k\cdot\sin(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_l-\phi_k).

(15)

The noise appears as the tone at
\epsilon
in the observation band.
Hence, to analyze individual noise sources and their coupling to the phasemeter readout, we first transform equations to the same algebraic structure as
eq.

14
, second derive the condition on the noise frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
that gives the noise term the frequency of
\omega+\epsilon\approx\omega
with
\epsilon
neglected for simplicity, and third apply
eq.

15
to determine the coupled noise.

Third, the whole noise analysis will be categorized into two parts in terms of frequency regime: heterodyne-band noise in
section

III
and modulation-band noise in
section

IV
.
The former is a noise around the heterodyne frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{het}}
(namely, the MHz regime for LISA), while the latter is a noise around the modulation frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}
(namely, the GHz regime for LISA).
We will show, in
section

IV
, that the modulation-band noise can be virtually down-converted to the heterodyne frequency band, with which the result of the heterodyne-band noise in
section

III
can be combined.
In
fig.

1
, we illustrate the relation between the frequency bands.

Illustration of the frequency bands discussed in this paper.
The respective frequency regimes in the LISA case are written in brackets.
Throughout the paper,
\epsilon
represents a (angular) frequency in the observation band, phase in which is to be extracted by a phasemeter.
Note that noises are not shown here.

Finally, a certain type of noise (
p_{i}
,
a_{i}
,
\theta_{i}
, or
n_{i}
) has incoherent contributions from individual laser beams in an indistinguishable way, as shown in
eq.

. Hence, we can consider only one of the laser contributions without the loss of generality. To estimate the total coupling factor, we can simply multiply the resulting noise coupling factor by
\sqrt{2}
representing the incoherent sum between the two beams.

Heterodyne-band noises

In addition to the heterodyne-band noise, noise in the observation band will also be incorporated in the same framework.

III.1
Self noise coupling

We first provide a general description and then apply it to the individual beatnotes.

General description:

The self noise coupling considers only a single beatnote with amplitude and phase noise.
Hence, the signal form in the scope is

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=A_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\omega_\mrm{A}\tau+\phi_\mrm{tar}+\phi_\mrm{n}),

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle=A_{0}(1+a_{\mathrm{n}})

III.2
Mutual noise coupling

Similarly to

III.1

General description:

If a signal of interest contains two sinusoidal tones at different frequencies, we can generally formulate the signal as

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=s_{\mathrm{A}}+s_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=A_{0}\cos(\omega_\mrm{A}\tau+\phi_\mrm{tar})+B_{n}\cos(\omega_\mrm{B}\tau+\phi_\mrm{B}+\phi_\mrm{n}),

(34)

where
\omega_{\mathrm{A}}\neq\omega_{\mathrm{B}}
, and a given phase
\phi_{\mathrm{B}}
of the noise sinusoid is considered.
The first term
s_{\mathrm{A}}
is the primary, whose phase
\phi_{\mathrm{tar}}
has to be measured, and the second term
s_{\mathrm{B}}
is the secondary considered as a noise source.

b_{n}
in
B_{n}(=B_{0}(1+b_{n}))
and
\phi_{\mathrm{n}}
are the relative amplitude noise and the phase noise, respectively.
In the mutual noise coupling, the question to answer is how the noises in
s_{\mathrm{B}}
couples to the measurement of
\phi_{\mathrm{tar}}
in
s_{\mathrm{A}}
.

First, regarding the relative amplitude noise
b_{n}
(
=m_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\omega_\mrm{n}\tau+\rho)
) in
B_{n}
, the secondary noise tone can be written as

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=B_{0}(1+m_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\omega_\mrm{n}\tau+\rho))\cos(\omega_\mrm{B}\tau+ \phi_\mrm{B})

\displaystyle=B_{0}\cos(\omega_\mrm{B}\tau+ \phi_\mrm{B})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{B_{0}m_{\mathrm{n}}}{2}\cos\left((\omega_{\mathrm{n}}-\omega_{\mathrm{B}})\tau+\rho-\phi_{\mathrm{B}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{B_{0}m_{\mathrm{n}}}{2}\cos\left((\omega_{\mathrm{n}}+\omega_{\mathrm{B}})\tau+\rho+\phi_{\mathrm{B}}\right).

(35)

Our interest is the noise term at
\omega_{\mathrm{A}}
; therefore, there are multiple solutions to
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{n}}-\omega_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=\pm\omega_{\mathrm{A}}\Rightarrow\omega_{\mathrm{n}}=\pm\omega_{\mathrm{A}}+\omega_{\mathrm{B}},

(36)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{n}}+\omega_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=\pm\omega_{\mathrm{A}}\Rightarrow\omega_{\mathrm{n}}=\omega_{\mathrm{A}}-\omega_{\mathrm{B}},

\omega>$0\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$

\displaystyle s_{\omega_{\mathrm{A}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A_{0}\cos(\omega_\mrm{A}\tau+\phi_\mrm{tar})+\frac{B_{0}m_{\mathrm{n}}}{2}\cos\left(\omega_{\mathrm{A}}\tau+\rho-\phi_{\mathrm{B}}\right)&\text{ if $\omega_{\mathrm{n}}=\omega_{\mathrm{A}}+\omega_{\mathrm{B}}$,}\\

Cascaded analysis:

The analysis above suggests that the laser phase noise
p_{i}
is virtually down-converted by
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}
to the modulation amplitude and phase noises (
a_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
), and vice versa.
This implies that the down-conversions occur in a cascaded manner.
To describe this functionally, let us write a given noise
x_{i}
in
eq.

13
as a function of the angular frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
, parameterized by the noise amplitude
m_{x}
and the random phase
\rho
as:

\displaystyle x_{i}(\omega_{\mathrm{n}};m_{x},\rho)

\displaystyle=m_{x}\cos(\omega_\mrm{n} \tau+ \rho).

(60)

Based on this, the process of the frequency downconversion of the laser phase noise
p_{i}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle p_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};m_{\mathrm{p}},\rho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\begin{cases}a_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right)&\\
\theta_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right).&\end{cases}

(61)

The right arrow represents the virtual frequency conversion process, and the terms listed on the right-hand side are the resulting noises.
On the other hand, the modulation amplitude and phase noises at
n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{a}}
and
n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{\theta}}
turn into the laser noise as,

\displaystyle a_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{a}};m_{\mathrm{a}},\rho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow p_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{a}};\frac{m_{0}m_{\mathrm{a}}}{2},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+p_{i}\left((n+1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{a}};\frac{m_{0}m_{\mathrm{a}}}{2},\rho+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right),

(62)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{\theta}};m_{\mathrm{\theta}},\rho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow p_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{\theta}};\frac{m_{0}m_{\mathrm{\theta}}}{2},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+p_{i}\left((n+1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{a}};\frac{m_{0}m_{\mathrm{\theta}}}{2},\rho+\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}+\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

(63)

If we combine
eqs.

61
and
62
for the coupling path
p_{i}\rightarrow a_{i}\rightarrow p_{i}
and
eqs.

61
and
63
for the coupling path
p_{i}\rightarrow\theta_{i}\rightarrow p_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle p_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};m_{\mathrm{p}},\rho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\begin{cases}a_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right)&\\
\theta_{i}\left((n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right),&\end{cases}

\displaystyle\rightarrow p_{i}\left((n-2)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{2},\rho-2\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right)+p_{i}\left(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{2},\rho\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+p_{i}\left((n-2)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{2},\rho-2\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\pi\right)+p_{i}\left(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{2},\rho\right)

\displaystyle=0+p_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}};m_{\mathrm{p}},\rho)

(64)

The resulting
p_{i}
at
(n-2)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
via
a_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
at
(n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
have a relative
\pi
phase shift; therefore, they cancel and result in 0 in the last line.
The up-converted parts resulting from
a_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
just recovers the original laser phase noise at
n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Hence, in this framework, the laser phase noise

can be down-converted only once, and if we try to downconvert it twice, the original laser phase noise is reproduced.

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{\theta}};m_{\mathrm{\theta}},\rho)

IV.3
Coherence

As shown in
fig.

3
, a single modulation-band noise has various coupling paths.
Therefore, it is important to properly track individual coupling paths and consider the coherence between them to evaluate the impact on the final measured phase.
In this section, we discuss the noise coherence taking specific noises as examples: noises at
n\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\epsilon
where
n\in\{1,2\}
and
\epsilon
is a tiny angular frequency offset in the observation band as used in
eqs.

14
and
15
.

First, the laser phase noise
p_{i}
around the modulation frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}
is, according to
eq.

61
, down-converted to the modulation amplitude and phase noises in the observation band as,

\displaystyle p_{i}(\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}+\epsilon;m_{\mathrm{p}},\rho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\begin{cases}a_{i}\left(\epsilon;\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}\right)\\
\theta_{i}\left(\epsilon;\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}},\rho-\phi^{\mathrm{sb}}_{\mathrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\end{cases}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\text{extraction}]{\text{phase}}\begin{cases}0+0=0&\text{for $\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{read}}$}\\
0+\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}}\cdot\cos(\rho-\phi^\mrm{sb}_{\mrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2})=\frac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{0}}\cdot\cos(\rho-\phi^\mrm{sb}_{\mrm{tar},i}-\frac{\pi}{2})&\text{for $\phi^{\mathrm{usb}}_{\mathrm{read}}$}\\

(77)

where the first arrow represents the virtual down-conversion, and the second arrow represents phase extraction by the phasemeter with the right-hand side as the coupled noises.
There is no influence on the measured carrier phase
\phi^{\mathrm{car}}_{\mathrm{read}}

eq.

31
.

Verification

Figure 5:

Diagram highlighting the processing steps of the numerical experiment.
All plots are amplitude spectra, instead of ASDs, to properly assess the height of monotonic tones.
The noise coupling factor for an individual beatnote is computed by the ratio of the coupled noise in its measured phase (bottom right) to the injected noise (top left).
Regarding the plots of the power spectrum, the results of the modulation amplitude noise
a_{i}

are taken as an example.

We so far derived various noise couplings analytically based on some approximations like the linear approximation and the sideband expression of the phase modulation.
In order to confirm that our analytical framework really covers the main noise couplings, we report on a numerical experiment in this section.
The numerical simulation was implemented, as written below, so that it does not impose any linear approximation.
Therefore, the agreement between the analysis and the numerical simulation guaranties that the framework for the systematic search in
sections

III
and
IV
maintains sufficient precision for the primary noise couplings.
The setup of the numerical experiment is described below and is also shown in
fig.

5
.

Contrary to the PR signal with the first-order modulation sidebands in
eq.

9
, we generate a time series according to

\tilde{s}_{\mathrm{pr}}(\tau)=\cos(\omega_\mrm{het} t + \tilde\phi_1(t) - \tilde\phi_2(t)),

(81)

where

\tilde{\phi}_{i}(t)=p_{i}(t)+m_{0}(1+a_{i}(t))\cos(\omega_{\mrm{m},i}t + \theta_i(t)).

(82)

We use LISA-like parameters:
The modulation frequencies
\omega_{\mathrm{m},i}
are assumed at
\omega_{\mathrm{m},1}/(2\pi)=3\times 5\times 19\times 2^{23}=$2.390\,753\,28\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
,
\omega_{\mathrm{m},2}/(2\pi)=19\times 17\times 113\times 2^{16}=$2.391\,998\,464\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
.
The nominal modulation depth
m_{0}
was set at
0.584\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}
, which invests
16\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the optical power for the sidebands.
The heterodyne frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{het}}/(2\pi)
was set at
10\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
.

Since we do not perform any further expansion, the highest frequency components of
eq.

81
will be in the GHz range.
To avoid these being aliased into our measurement band, we initially use a relatively high sampling rate of
1.28\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
for the heterodyne-band noises and
12.8\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
for the modulation-band noises to produce a first instance of the signal which is then immediately filtered and downsampled to a final rate of
80\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
.
We then perform a standard I/Q demodulation on these downsampled signals at three frequencies:
\omega_{\mathrm{het}}
for the carrier-carrier beatnote,
\omega_{\mathrm{het}}-\omega_{\mathrm{m},1}+\omega_{\mathrm{m},2}
for the usb-usb beatnote, and
\omega_{\mathrm{het}}+\omega_{\mathrm{m},1}-\omega_{\mathrm{m},2}
for the lsb-lsb beatnote.
The low-pass filter in the I/Q demodulation is designed from a Kaiser window, with a transition band from
500\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
and a suppression of
240\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
.
Hence,
500\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
defines the bandwidth of the phasemeter output.

Due to the high initial sampling rate, it is infeasible in this setup to produce sufficiently long data streams to reach the actual LISA measurement band of
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{mHz}
to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
.
For each noise source, we generate a synthetic data stream with the sample size of
10^{7}
.
Considering the sampling rates mentioned above, the lowest frequency bin we can reach is
128\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
for the heterodyne-band noise and
1.28\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
for the modulation-band noise.

To verify individual noise couplings, we inject a monotonic noise at the target noise frequency with an offset frequency of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
and a noise amplitude of
10^{-4}
with the units of
\mathrm{rad}
for
p_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
and no unit for
a_{i}
.
In this way, the coupled noise appears at
30\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
in a phase measured by the I/Q demodulation, as shown on the bottom right in
fig.

5
.
Finally, comparing the height of the coupled noise peak at
30\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
in the amplitude spectrum with the injected amplitude of
10^{-4}
, we can derive the magnitude of a noise coupling factor.
To compare the simulation result with analysis in the time domain, the coupling factors derived in simulation is multiplied by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
because the height of the coupled noise peak in the amplitude spectrum corresponds to the root-mean-square value, which needs to be converted to the amplitude.

fig.

6
shows the result for the heterodyne-band noise.
The coupling factors (black horizontal lines), derived from the analysis in
section

III
, all agree with the numerical simulation shown in the colored markers.
The simulation and the analysis are consistent also in terms of the sideband combination in
eq.

31
: the colored stars (simulation) always coincide with the vertically-capped black lines (analysis), while the colored stars do not appear when the black lines are not shown.
This confirms that our analysis properly calculates not only the magnitude of the coupled noises but also their phases, in other words, coherence.
There are some colored markers in the gray area below
3\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}
.
Our analytical framework does not predict those couplings found in simulation; hence, they are expected to arise from the outside of our framework, e.g., couplings from the second- or higher-order modulation sidebands.
From these results, we conclude that we were able to capture all major couplings of the three types of noise in the heterodyne frequency band using the analytical framework in
section

III
.

VI
Application

To demonstrate the application of this study, we consider the case where one defines noise requirements from a target sensitivity of phase extraction in ASD.
We particularly focus on heterodyne-band noise here to concisely show a use case.

As presented in
appendix

B
, the time-domain coupling factors in
table

3
can be converted to those in ASD by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.
In addition, as mentioned at the end of
section

II.4
, considering incoherent noise contributions with the same noise amplitude from the two interfering beams, a coupling factor gains another factor of
\sqrt{2}
.
Hence, a total phase noise caused by a noise source
x
(
\in\{p,v,a,\theta\}
) in phase extraction of a beatnote
y
(
\in\{\mathrm{car},\mathrm{usb},\mathrm{lsb},\mathrm{usb}-\mathrm{lsb}\}

\displaystyle\tilde{X}^{y}=\sqrt{\sum_{\omega}\left(2|C^{y}_{\tau,\omega}|\tilde{x}_{\omega}\right)^{2}}=2\sqrt{\sum_{\omega}(|C^{y}_{\tau,\omega}|\tilde{x}_{\omega})^{2}},

(83)

where
\tilde{x}_{\omega}
is the ASD of the noise
x
at a relevant frequency
\omega
in one of the two beams, and
C^{y}_{\tau,\omega}
is its time-domain coupling factor to the beatnote
y
.

In the case like LISA where the whole system comprises multiple heterodyne interferometers with different time-dependent heterodyne frequencies, a noise requirement should be defined over a wide frequency band, instead of at specific frequencies.
For this purpose,
\tilde{x}_{\omega}
in
eq.

83
is replaced by a common broadband noise representation
\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{band}}
.
If a requirement for
\tilde{X}^{y}
is written by
\tilde{X}^{y}_{\mathrm{req}}
, one way to formulate the requirement of the individual noise source would be the following:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{band,req}}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\{\mathrm{car},\mathrm{usb},\mathrm{lsb},\mathrm{usb}-\mathrm{lsb}\}}\tilde{x}^{y}_{\mathrm{band,req}}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\{\mathrm{car},\mathrm{usb},\mathrm{lsb},\mathrm{usb}-\mathrm{lsb}\}}\frac{\tilde{X}^{y}_{\mathrm{req}}}{2\sqrt{\sum_{\omega}|C^{y}_{\tau,\omega}|^{2}}}.

(84)

VII
Conclusion

Heterodyne interferometry is a versatile tool used in many fields of precision science.
The technology is often combined with a laser phase modulation to extract additional information of an experimental setup, e.g., intersatellite clock noise transfer for GW detection in space.

In this paper, in the format of spaceborne GW detectors, we study additional noise couplings caused by the phase modulation especially in the high frequency regimes like heterodyne or modulation bands, which were not well explored before.
In addition to the modulation noise, the laser noise is also discussed in the same framework.
In
section

III
, we systematically derive all possible noise couplings from the heterodyne band based on interactions between heterodyne beatnotes in a PR signal in
eq.

9
.
All couplings found are listed in
table

3
.
In
section

IV
, we shift the frequency band of interest to the even higher frequency regime, i.e., the modulation band.
We show that in this case, we can first rewrite the noise in a different form via trigonometric analysis or sideband analysis, focusing on a single electromagnetic field.
This enables us to transform the noise in the modulation band to the noise in the heterodyne band with amplitude scaling factors, which then can be combined with the discussion in
section

III
to compute the total coupling factor.
In
section

V
, we verify the couplings derived analytically by comparison with the numerical simulation.
Contrary to the analysis, the simulation does not rely on any approximation, e.g., the first-order approximation.
Therefore, the agreement between the analysis and the simulation confirms that our analytical framework successfully captures primary noise couplings.
Finally, in
section

VI
, we demonstrate a use case of this study taking the LISA-like setup as an example.
This show how we could derive requirements on each noise source (
p_{i},v_{i},a_{i},
and
\theta_{i}
) based on the target precision of phase extraction by a phasemeter and the noise coupling factors derived in this paper.

It should be noted that unlike the aliased laser frequency noise

This means that the only possible treatment is to mitigate the noise sources themselves or fine-tune a few parameters like

Appendix A
Additive readout noise

Let us derive a general formula for the coupling of additive readout noise in phase extraction with a phasemeter based on its working principle
13
.
The phase-locked loop (PLL) on a phasemeter extracts the phase of the input sinusoidal signal with a band-pass effect around the input signal frequency.
As an input to the phasemeter, we consider the following signal:

\displaystyle s=k\cos(\omega\tau+ \phi_k)+l\cos((\omega+ \epsilon)\tau+ \phi_l),

(85)

where the first term is a primary signal and
\phi_{k}
is a target phase to extract, and the second term is the monotonic representation of the additive noise at
\omega+\epsilon
where
\epsilon\ll\omega
.
We assume that the noise amplitude
l
is much smaller than the nominal signal amplitude
k
:
l\ll k
.
The scope is how the second term disturbs the phase extraction of the first term.

We model the phase extraction by a simple I/Q demodulation.
The I and Q values are formulated by

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=s\cdot\cos(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle=k\cos(\omega\tau+ \phi_k)\cdot\cos(\omega\tau)+l\cos((\omega+ \epsilon)\tau+ \phi_l)\cdot\cos(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{LPF}}(k\cos\phi_{k}+l\cos(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_{l}))/2,

(86a)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=s\cdot\sin(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle=k\cos(\omega\tau+ \phi_k)\cdot\sin(\omega\tau)+l\cos((\omega+ \epsilon)\tau+ \phi_l)\cdot\sin(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{LPF}}(k\sin\phi_{k}+l\sin(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_{l}))/2,

(86b)

where “LPF” represents the low-pass filtering to filter out the second harmonic of the input signal.
The phase extraction is achieved by
\arctan
of their ratio as

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{read}}

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(Q/I\right)

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{k\sin\phi_{k}+l\sin(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_{l})}{k\cos\phi_{k}+l\cos(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_{l})}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\phi_{k}+\phi_{\mathrm{err}},

(87)

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{err}}

\displaystyle=l/k\cdot\sin(\epsilon\tau+ \phi_l-\phi_k),

(88)

where
l\ll k
is used in the last line in
eq.

87
.
This suggests that the phase noise amplitude is determined by the ratio of the noise amplitude
l
to the signal amplitude
k
in the input signal in
eq.

85
.
Also, the resulting angular frequency of the noise is
\epsilon
; therefore, if the angular frequency offset
\epsilon
is in the science observation band (namely
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{mHz}
to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
for LISA), the noise appears in the final phase readout in the band.
Although we model phase extraction with I/Q demodulation in this section, the noise coupling does not change in the PLL-based phasemeter as long as the tracking bandwidth is sufficiently higher than the frequencies of the signal of interest.

Appendix B
Coupling factor conversion

In this paper, we use a monotonic noise representation and analyze its coupling to a measured phase in the time domain.
However, in practice, the noise to deal with is stochastic, and we are interested in its coupling to the measured phase in terms of the amplitude spectral density (ASD) in the frequency domain.
In this section, the conversion of the coupling factor between the time and frequency domains is discussed.

Following
eq.

13
, let us define the monotonic noise
n
in the time domain,

\displaystyle n(\tau)=m_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\omega_\mrm{n}\tau+\rho),

(89)

where
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
is an angular frequency.
As discussed in
appendix

A
, of our interest is the sum of the signal frequency
\omega_{s}
(or a frequency that results in
\omega_{s}
after some down- or up-conversions) and a small offset
\epsilon
in the observation band:
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}=\omega_{s}+\epsilon
.

\rho
is a given stochastic phase.

is also random; however, considering it as a long-term rms value, it can be replaced by

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2n_{0}b},

(90)

as discussed around Eq. (26) in
24
.
Assuming a white noise,
n_{0}
is the noise floor in power spectral density (PSD), and
b=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
, which is the white noise bandwidth we consider.
The factor of
\sqrt{2}
in
eq.

90
reflects the fact that the mixing process in PLL of a phasemeter wraps the uncorrelated noise powers below and above the primary signal frequency
\omega_{s}
(namely, at
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}=\omega_{s}\pm\epsilon

f=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
, which result in the phase noise at
\epsilon
with the PSD noise floor increased by
2
in the phasemeter output.
Therefore, to represent the total coupling of the noise at
\omega_{\mathrm{n}}
by a single monotonic tone in
eq.

\sqrt{2}
to
m_{\mathrm{n}}
in
eq.

90
.

An important question is how to relate a noise coupling factor of
n(\tau)
to the one of the PSD (or ASD) noise floor
n_{0}
(or
\sqrt{n_{0}}
).
In other words, we need to drive the relation between the coupling factors of
C_{\tau}
in the time domain and
C_{\mathrm{asd}}
in units of ASD in the following expressions of the phasemeter output phases,

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{read}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\phi(\tau)+C_{\tau}\cdot m_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\epsilon_n\tau+ \rho-\phi),

(91)

\displaystyle S_{\phi_{\mathrm{read}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S_{\phi}(\omega)+C^{2}_{\mathrm{asd}}\cdot n_{0}.

(92)

is in units of PSD.

\sin(\epsilon_n\tau+ \rho-\phi)

89

This happens in the mixing process on a phasemeter.

\phi

We can now derive the noise coupling in PSD from the time-domain expression in

\displaystyle S_{n}\approx E\left[\left(C_{\tau}\cdot m_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\epsilon_n\tau+ \rho-\phi)\right)^{2}\right]/b=2C^{2}_{\tau}n_{0}.

By equating it to the noise term in

Appendix C
Single sideband

We recall the formulation of a single sideband here.
Let us consider an electromagnetic field at
\omega
with a single sideband at
\Delta\omega

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=A(1+a\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t})\mathrm{e}^{j\omega t},

(95)

where we assume
\Delta\omega>0
; hence, this is an upper single sideband.

eq.

95
suggests that the single sideband modulates both the phase and the amplitude of the field.
If we decompose this into amplitude and phase, we get

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=A\left(1+\frac{a}{2}\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}+\frac{a}{2}\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}+\frac{a}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t}-\frac{a}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t}\right)\mathrm{e}^{j\omega t}

\displaystyle=A\left(1+\frac{a}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}+\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t})+\frac{a}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}-\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t})\right)\mathrm{e}^{j\omega t}

\displaystyle\approx A\left(1+\frac{a}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}+\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t})\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{a}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{j\Delta\omega t}-\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t})\right)\mathrm{e}^{j\omega t}

\displaystyle\approx A\left(1+a\cos(\Delta\omega t)\right)\mathrm{e}^{j(\omega t+a\cos(\Delta\omega t-\pi/2))}.

(96)

Therefore, the upper single sideband can be interpreted as the combination of an amplitude modulation and a coherent phase modulation with the phase delay of
\pi/2
.
The lower single sideband can be easily derived with the replacement as
\Delta\omega\rightarrow-\Delta\omega
,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=A(1+a\mathrm{e}^{-j\Delta\omega t})\mathrm{e}^{j\omega t}

\displaystyle\approx A\left(1+a\cos(-\Delta\omega t)\right)\mathrm{e}^{j(\omega t+a\sin(-\Delta\omega t))}

\displaystyle=A\left(1+a\cos(\Delta\omega t)\right)\mathrm{e}^{j(\omega t+a\cos(\Delta\omega t+\pi/2))}.

(97)

Hence, the lower single sideband can be interpreted as the combination of the amplitude modulation and the coherent phase modulation with the phase advancement of
\pi/2
.
Note that the amplitude of the single sideband
a
directly becomes the amplitude of the amplitude and phase modulations.
