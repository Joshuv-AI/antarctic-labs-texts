Biogenic silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles synthesized using Foeniculum vulgare seed extract: Antimicrobial activity and in vivo toxicological evaluation
Authors: Saman Muhsin Abdulkareem, Sabir Ahmed Ismael, Youns Saleh Mustafa, Sarwar Nawzad Jafar, Rebwar Muhammad Hamasalih, Muhsin Ali Ahmed, Zirak Faqe Ahmed Abdulrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiogenic synthesis of metal nanoparticles using medicinal plants has emerged as a sustainable and environmentally friendly strategy for producing multifunctional nanomaterials with promising biomedical applications. Here, an aqueous seed extract of Foeniculum vulgare was utilized as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent for the synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs). The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized by UV–Visible spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). UV–Visible analysis confirmed nanoparticle formation through characteristic surface plasmon resonance and absorption peaks, while FTIR spectra indicated the involvement of plant-derived phytochemicals in nanoparticle reduction and stabilization. XRD analysis verified the crystalline structure of both nanomaterials, with an average crystallite size of approximately 37 nm, whereas SEM revealed predominantly spherical nanoparticles with diameters ranging from 22 to 128 nm. EDX analysis further confirmed the elemental composition of the synthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs.The antimicrobial performance of the biogenic nanoparticles was investigated against Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans using agar well diffusion, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), and minimum bactericidal/fungicidal concentration (MBC/MFC) assays. Both AgNPs and ZnONPs exhibited concentration-dependent antimicrobial activity; however, AgNPs produced larger inhibition zones and lower MIC and MBC/MFC values than ZnONPs. Among the tested microorganisms, C. albicans was the most susceptible, whereas P. aeruginosa exhibited the highest level of resistance.The in vivo toxicological profile was evaluated following oral administration of 10 mg/kg/day for 14 consecutive days. AgNP-treated animals showed only minor biochemical and histopathological alterations. In contrast, ZnONP treatment significantly increased serum ALT, AST, and urea levels and significantly decreased ALP activity and the hepato-somatic index compared with the control group (P < 0.05). ZnONP-treated animals also exhibited more pronounced hepatic and renal histopathological alterations than AgNP-treated animals. These findings indicate that F. vulgare-mediated AgNPs provide superior antimicrobial performance with a more favorable short-term safety profile than ZnONPs under the experimental conditions. Collectively, the results highlight the potential of biogenic AgNPs synthesized from F. vulgare seed extract as promising antimicrobial nanomaterials for future biomedical and pharmaceutical applications.Keywords: Foeniculum vulgare, Green synthesis, Silver nanoparticles, Zinc oxide nanoparticles, Antimicrobial activity, MIC, Biocompatibility, Histopathology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most serious threats to global public health, compromising the effectiveness of conventional antimicrobial therapies and increasing the burden of infectious diseases worldwide. The rapid emergence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) microorganisms have resulted in prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, treatment failure, and higher mortality rates. Extensive use and misuse of antimicrobial agents in human medicine, veterinary practice, and agriculture have further accelerated the evolution of resistant pathogens, while the development of new antibiotics has slowed considerably. Consequently, the search for alternative antimicrobial strategies capable of overcoming microbial resistance has become a major research priority.1, 2, 3, 4

Nanotechnology has attracted considerable attention as a promising approach for developing next-generation antimicrobial agents. Among various nanomaterials, metallic nanoparticles possess unique physicochemical characteristics, including nanoscale dimensions, high surface-area-to-volume ratio, and tunable surface properties, which contribute to their broad-spectrum biological activities. These distinctive properties have promoted extensive investigations into their potential applications in medicine, pharmacy, food preservation, environmental remediation, and biomedical engineering.5, 6, 7, 8

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) are among the most extensively investigated metal-based nanomaterials because of their remarkable antimicrobial properties and versatile biomedical applications. In addition to their antimicrobial potential, these nanoparticles have been explored for wound healing, tissue engineering, drug delivery, biosensing, antimicrobial coatings, and food-packaging systems. Nevertheless, their biological performance depends largely on particle size, morphology, crystallinity, surface chemistry, and synthesis approach, emphasizing the importance of developing reproducible and environmentally sustainable fabrication methods.6, 9, 10, 11, 12

Conventional physical and chemical methods for nanoparticle synthesis frequently require toxic reducing agents, hazardous organic solvents, high energy consumption, and sophisticated instrumentation, limiting their environmental sustainability and biomedical applicability. To address these limitations, green synthesis using plant-derived extracts has emerged as an environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and sustainable alternative. Plant metabolites simultaneously function as natural reducing and stabilizing agents, enabling nanoparticle production under mild reaction conditions while minimizing the use of hazardous chemicals.13, 14, 15, 16

Foeniculum vulgare Mill. (fennel) is an aromatic medicinal plant widely recognized for its high content of bioactive phytochemicals, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, coumarins, tannins, and essential oil constituents. These naturally occurring metabolites provide excellent reducing and stabilizing capacity for nanoparticle biosynthesis. Moreover, F. vulgare has been extensively reported to possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and anticancer activities, making it an attractive candidate for the green production of biologically active nanoparticles.17, 18, 19, 20

Although numerous studies have described the green synthesis of either AgNPs or ZnONPs using different medicinal plants, direct comparative investigations remain scarce because of differences in plant species, synthesis protocols, characterization methods, and biological evaluation models. Furthermore, previous studies have primarily focused on nanoparticle synthesis and antimicrobial activity, whereas comparatively few have simultaneously evaluated their in vivo biological safety. Since nanoparticles intended for biomedical applications inevitably interact with living tissues, comprehensive assessment of both antimicrobial efficacy and systemic biocompatibility is essential for their successful clinical translation.14, 18, 21, 22, 23, 24

Rationale for Selection of Nanomaterials, Plant Species, and Plant Part.

The selection of the nanomaterials, plant species, and plant part in the present study was based on their reported biological relevance and suitability for green nanoparticle synthesis. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) were selected because both are extensively investigated metal-based nanomaterials with well-documented antimicrobial properties and potential biomedical applications. Their inclusion also enabled a direct comparison of two chemically distinct nanomaterials synthesized under comparable plant-mediated conditions.6, 9, 10, 11, 12

Foeniculum vulgare Mill. (fennel) was selected as the plant source because it is a medicinal plant rich in bioactive phytochemicals, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, coumarins, tannins, and essential-oil constituents. These metabolites can participate in the reduction of metal ions and stabilization of the resulting nanoparticles. In addition, the reported antimicrobial and other biological activities of F. vulgare provided a relevant basis for investigating its use in the preparation of biologically active nanoparticles.17, 19, 20

The seed was selected as the plant part because fennel seeds are rich in bioactive phytoconstituents and have previously been successfully employed for the green synthesis of both silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles. Previous studies have specifically reported the rapid green synthesis of AgNPs using F. vulgare seed extract and the biosynthesis of ZnONPs using fennel seed extract, providing a literature-supported basis for selecting seeds in the present comparative study.9, 19, 20

In addition to the selection of the plant and plant part, the aqueous extraction method was selected because it is consistent with the green and environmentally sustainable objective of the present study. Water provides an environmentally compatible medium for plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis and avoids the use of potentially hazardous organic solvents. Moreover, aqueous plant extracts contain phytochemicals and other biomolecules that can participate in the reduction of metal ions and subsequently contribute to the stabilization and capping of the synthesized nanoparticles.13, 14, 15, 16 The use of an aqueous extract also allowed direct compatibility with the aqueous silver nitrate and zinc nitrate precursor solutions employed in the present study. Although non-polar extracts may contain lipophilic bioactive constituents, their preparation generally requires organic solvents and additional processing steps. Therefore, the aqueous seed extract was selected as a simple, environmentally compatible, and practical system for obtaining plant-derived constituents capable of contributing to the green synthesis and stabilization of AgNPs and ZnONPs.13, 14, 15, 16

Therefore, the present study aimed to biosynthesize AgNPs and ZnONPs using Foeniculum vulgare seed extract through a green synthesis approach, characterize their physicochemical properties, compare their antimicrobial activities against clinically relevant bacterial and fungal pathogens, and evaluate their in vivo safety using biochemical and histopathological analyses in an experimental rat model. By integrating physicochemical characterization with antimicrobial and toxicological assessments, this study provides a comprehensive comparison of the efficacy and safety profiles of plant-mediated AgNPs and ZnONPs and contributes to the development of environmentally sustainable nanomaterials for future biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Foeniculum vulgare seed extract

Mature seeds of Foeniculum vulgare were purchased from a local market in Halabja, Iraq. The seeds were thoroughly washed three times with deionized water to remove surface contaminants and air-dried overnight. Five grams of dried seeds were refluxed in 150 mL of deionized water at 80 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the extract was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper and stored at 4 °C until further use.7, 25

Phytochemical profiling of Foeniculum vulgare seed extract

To identify the bioactive phytochemicals responsible for the reduction, stabilization, and capping of the biosynthesized nanoparticles, the aqueous seed extract was subjected to comprehensive phytochemical analysis using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and standard qualitative/quantitative biochemical assays.25, 26

The presence of these diverse phytochemicals explains why the same extract successfully mediated the synthesis of two chemically distinct nanoparticles (AgNPs and ZnONPs) under identical conditions, despite the different reduction mechanisms required for silver and zinc ions.

Green synthesis of silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles

Green synthesis of AgNPs and ZnONPs was carried out using the aqueous F. vulgare seed extract as both reducing and stabilizing agent.

For AgNP synthesis, 5 mL of the extract was mixed with 45 mL of 10 mM aqueous silver nitrate (AgNO₃) solution. The reaction mixture was heated at 65 °C for 20 min. The pH of the AgNP reaction mixture was adjusted to 10 using 1 N NaOH, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 72 h to complete nanoparticle formation.14, 15, 19

For ZnONP synthesis, 5 mL of the extract was mixed with 45 mL of 10 mM aqueous zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) solution. The reaction mixture was heated at 65 °C for 20 min and the pH was adjusted to 10 using 1 N NaOH, followed by incubation at room temperature for 72 h.9, 11, 20

Rationale for standardized temperature and pH conditions

The temperature of 65 °C was selected as an optimal balance between accelerating the reduction kinetics of both Ag+ and Zn2+ ions and preserving the structural integrity of heat-labile phytochemicals (e.g., flavonoids and phenolic acids) present in the F. vulgare extract. Higher temperatures may degrade these bioactive compounds, while lower temperatures prolong reaction times and reduce reduction efficiency.13, 14, 15, 16

The pH was adjusted to 10 using NaOH for both syntheses to create a strongly alkaline environment, which serves multiple essential roles:

•For AgNPs, alkaline pH enhances the reducing power of phenolic hydroxyl groups and flavonoids, facilitating the rapid reduction of Ag+ to Ag0. Additionally, it promotes the deprotonation of functional groups, improving the adsorption of phytochemicals onto the nanoparticle surface, which acts as a stabilizing and capping layer.14, 15, 19

For AgNPs, alkaline pH enhances the reducing power of phenolic hydroxyl groups and flavonoids, facilitating the rapid reduction of Ag+ to Ag0. Additionally, it promotes the deprotonation of functional groups, improving the adsorption of phytochemicals onto the nanoparticle surface, which acts as a stabilizing and capping layer.14, 15, 19

•For ZnONPs, alkaline pH is critical for the hydrolysis of zinc nitrate precursors and the subsequent condensation reaction to form ZnO nuclei. At pH 10, the formation of Zn(OH)₂ intermediates is favored, which upon dehydration yield crystalline ZnO. This pH also ensures complete precipitation and minimizes the formation of undesirable by-products such as zinc hydroxide carbonates.9, 11, 20

For ZnONPs, alkaline pH is critical for the hydrolysis of zinc nitrate precursors and the subsequent condensation reaction to form ZnO nuclei. At pH 10, the formation of Zn(OH)₂ intermediates is favored, which upon dehydration yield crystalline ZnO. This pH also ensures complete precipitation and minimizes the formation of undesirable by-products such as zinc hydroxide carbonates.9, 11, 20

Although the same temperature and pH were applied to both syntheses, the specific phytochemical–metal interactions differ: Ag+ reduction is primarily driven by electron donation from phenolic compounds, whereas Zn2+ precipitation and oxidation require hydroxyl ion-mediated hydrolysis. The chosen conditions were therefore optimized to accommodate both mechanisms, ensuring reproducible synthesis of phase-pure, crystalline nanoparticles with controlled size and morphology.

Control solutions containing the corresponding metal precursor and sterile deionized water without plant extract were prepared to verify that nanoparticle formation resulted from phytochemical-mediated reduction. Formation of nanoparticles was initially monitored by visual color change. The reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the collected pellets were washed three times with deionized water to remove unreacted compounds. Finally, the purified nanoparticles were calcined at 500 °C for 2 h and preserved for subsequent physicochemical characterization and biological analyses.

Physicochemical characterization

The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized using ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). UV–Vis spectroscopy was performed to confirm nanoparticle formation through characteristic surface plasmon resonance absorption26. FTIR analysis was used to identify functional groups involved in nanoparticle reduction and stabilization. XRD analysis was conducted to determine crystalline structure and estimate crystallite size using the Debye–Scherrer equation, whereas SEM and EDX analyses were employed to evaluate particle morphology, size distribution, and elemental composition.

Antimicrobial activity assay

The antimicrobial activity of the synthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs was evaluated against selected Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and pathogenic fungi using the agar well diffusion method. Fresh microbial suspensions were prepared and adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard before inoculation onto Mueller–Hinton agar for bacteria and Sabouraud dextrose agar for fungi. Wells of uniform diameter were punched into the agar plates and filled with different concentrations of nanoparticle suspensions. Sterile distilled water served as the negative control. No positive controls were included in the agar well diffusion assay, as the study focused on evaluating the intrinsic antimicrobial activity of the biosynthesized nanoparticles. Following incubation under appropriate conditions, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal/fungicidal concentration (MBC/MFC)

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs was determined using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines with minor modifications. Briefly, serial two-fold dilutions of each nanoparticle suspension were prepared in sterile Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB) for bacterial isolates and RPMI-1640 medium for Candida albicans, yielding final concentrations ranging from 2 to 512 μg/mL.

Fresh overnight cultures of Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 19606, and Candida albicans ATCC 10231 were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL) and subsequently diluted to obtain a final inoculum of approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL in each well. Aliquots of 100 μL of the microbial suspension were added to each well of sterile 96-well microtiter plates containing 100 μL of nanoparticle suspensions at different concentrations. Growth control wells containing culture medium and inoculum without nanoparticles and sterility control wells containing only culture medium were included in each experiment. F. vulgare extract was used as a positive control for bacterial isolates. No additional positive control was used for C. albicans in the MIC/MFC assays, as the study aimed to evaluate the intrinsic antifungal activity of the nanoparticles.

The microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for bacterial isolates and 30 °C for 24–48 h for C. albicans. Following incubation, microbial growth was assessed visually, and the MIC was defined as the lowest nanoparticle concentration showing complete inhibition of visible microbial growth.

To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC), 10 μL aliquots were aseptically withdrawn from wells showing no visible growth and spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates for bacterial isolates or Sabouraud dextrose agar plates for C. albicans. The inoculated plates were incubated under the same conditions as described above. The MBC or MFC was defined as the lowest nanoparticle concentration that produced no visible colony growth, corresponding to ≥99.9% killing of the initial microbial inoculum. All antimicrobial experiments were performed in triplicate, and the obtained MIC and MBC/MFC values were expressed as the modal values from three independent experiments.

Experimental animals

Healthy adult male albino rats weighing approximately 200–250 g were obtained from the Animal House Facility of the College of Veterinary Medicine. Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle, and free access to standard pellet diet and water) for one week prior to the experiment to allow acclimatization.

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the international guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the College of Science, Salahaddin University-Erbil, Erbil, Kurdistan Region, Iraq (Ethics Approval No. 23034). The study protocol complied with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for reporting animal research. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Experimental design

Experimental design

Animals were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n = 5 per group). The control group received normal saline, whereas the remaining groups received F. vulgare extract (150 mg/kg), AgNPs (10 mg/kg), or ZnONPs (10 mg/kg), respectively, by oral gavage for 14 consecutive days. At the end of the treatment, blood samples were collected for biochemical analysis, and liver and kidney tissues were excised for histopathological examination.

Biochemical analysis

Serum samples were separated by centrifugation and analyzed using commercially available diagnostic kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. Liver function was assessed by measuring alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Kidney function was evaluated by determining serum urea and creatinine concentrations. All biochemical measurements were performed following standardized laboratory procedures.

Histopathological examination

Liver and kidney specimens were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24–48 h. Following routine tissue processing, samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned at approximately 5 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological sections were examined under a light microscope, and representative photomicrographs were recorded for pathological evaluation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (version XX; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons among experimental groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Green synthesis and visual observation of nanoparticles

The successful biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) using Foeniculum vulgare seed extract was initially confirmed by visual observation. The reaction mixture containing silver nitrate exhibited a distinct color change from pale yellow to dark brown, indicating the reduction of Ag+ ions and the formation of AgNPs. Likewise, the zinc precursor solution gradually changed to a milky white suspension, suggesting the successful formation of ZnONPs. No visible color change was observed in the control solutions lacking the plant extract, confirming the essential role of the phytochemicals in nanoparticle synthesis. These observations provided preliminary evidence for the successful green synthesis of both nanoparticles.

UV–visible spectroscopic analysis

The formation of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) using Foeniculum vulgare seed extract was initially confirmed by UV–Visible spectroscopy (Fig. 1). The UV–Vis spectrum of the synthesized AgNPs exhibited a distinct surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption band centered at 423 nm, which is characteristic of metallic silver nanoparticles. In contrast, the synthesized ZnONPs displayed a prominent absorption peak at 386 nm, confirming the successful formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles. No comparable absorption bands were observed in the spectrum of the F. vulgare seed extract, indicating that the observed peaks originated exclusively from nanoparticle formation.

UV–Visible absorption spectra of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs), and Foeniculum vulgare seed extract.

The appearance of sharp and well-defined absorption bands suggests efficient reduction of silver and zinc ions by the phytochemicals present in the plant extract and indicates the successful formation of stable nanoparticles with relatively uniform size distribution. Furthermore, the absence of additional absorption peaks within the scanned wavelength range suggests that the synthesized nanoparticles were free from detectable impurities or undesirable by-products. The characteristic SPR peak of AgNPs observed in the present study agrees well with previously reported absorption wavelengths for biologically synthesized silver nanoparticles, while the absorption peak recorded for ZnONPs is consistent with the optical properties of crystalline zinc oxide nanoparticles (Fig. 1).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed to identify the phytochemical functional groups responsible for the reduction, stabilization, and capping of the biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs (Fig. 2). The FTIR spectrum of the Foeniculum vulgare seed extract exhibited several characteristic absorption bands corresponding to hydroxyl, carbonyl, amine, and ether functional groups, confirming the presence of bioactive phytochemicals involved in nanoparticle synthesis.

Additional absorption bands within the range of 1380–1450 cm−1 were associated with C—N stretching and CH bending vibrations, while peaks between 1030 and 1100 cm−1 indicated C—O stretching vibrations of alcohols, ethers, and polysaccharides.

Following nanoparticle synthesis, noticeable shifts in the position and intensity of several absorption bands were observed compared with the crude plant extract. These spectral changes indicate that phenolic compounds, flavonoids, terpenoids, and proteins present in F. vulgare actively participated in the bioreduction of silver and zinc ions and subsequently adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface as stabilizing and capping agents. Such phytochemical-mediated stabilization is considered essential for preventing particle aggregation and improving colloidal stability.

In the low-wavenumber region, characteristic absorption bands observed below 700 cm−1 were assigned to metal–oxygen vibrations, confirming the successful formation of Ag—O and Zn—O bonds. The presence of these bands further supports the synthesis of crystalline metal oxide nanoparticles through the green synthesis approach. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to investigate the crystalline structure, phase purity, and crystallite size of the biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs. The diffraction patterns confirmed that both nanoparticles possessed a well-defined crystalline nature without evidence of significant amorphous phases. The sharp and intense diffraction peaks indicated successful crystallization during the green synthesis process.

For AgNPs, the characteristic diffraction peaks were assigned to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes of face-centered cubic (fcc) metallic silver, which are in good agreement with the standard Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) data for crystalline silver. The predominance of the (111) reflection suggested preferential crystal growth along this plane, a feature commonly reported for biosynthesized silver nanoparticles due to their relatively low surface energy and enhanced thermodynamic stability.

Similarly, the XRD pattern of ZnONPs exhibited the characteristic reflections corresponding to the hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure of ZnO. The diffraction peaks matched well with the standard ZnO diffraction pattern, confirming the successful formation of crystalline zinc oxide nanoparticles. The absence of additional diffraction peaks attributed to secondary phases or precursor residues indicated that the biosynthesized ZnONPs possessed high phase purity without detectable crystalline impurities (Fig. 3).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs.

The diffraction profile obtained in the present study also demonstrated relatively narrow peak widths, indicating good crystallinity of both nanomaterials. High crystallinity is considered an important characteristic because it enhances the structural stability of nanoparticles and significantly influences their optical, catalytic, and antimicrobial properties. Well-crystallized nanoparticles generally exhibit improved electron mobility, enhanced surface activity, and greater physicochemical stability compared with poorly crystalline materials.

According to the XRD results, the average crystallite size was estimated to be approximately 37 nm, which is consistent with the nanoscale morphology observed by electron microscopy.

The close agreement between the crystallite size calculated from XRD and the particle dimensions observed by SEM suggests that the synthesized nanoparticles consist predominantly of single crystalline or weakly aggregated nanocrystals. Minor differences between crystallite size and particle size may arise from particle aggregation or the formation of polycrystalline assemblies during the biosynthesis process, which is commonly observed in plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis.

Overall, the XRD results confirmed the successful green synthesis of highly crystalline and phase-pure AgNPs and ZnONPs. These structural characteristics are expected to contribute positively to their biological performance, since nanoparticle crystallinity and crystal orientation have been reported to influence antimicrobial activity, reactive oxygen species generation, surface reactivity, and long-term physicochemical stability.

Scanning Electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to investigate the morphology, particle size, and surface characteristics of the biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs. The SEM micrographs (Fig. 4a–c) revealed that the AgNPs exhibited mixed morphology, consisting predominantly of spherical particles along with occasional cuboidal and irregularly shaped nanoparticles. Similarly, the ZnONPs (Fig. 4d–f) displayed a heterogeneous size distribution, with nanoparticles ranging from approximately 22 to 128 nm in diameter. This broad size distribution and morphological diversity are commonly observed in plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis and are attributed to variations in nucleation and crystal growth kinetics during the biosynthesis process. The phytochemical composition of the F. vulgare extract, which contains multiple bioactive compounds with different reducing capacities and metal-chelating affinities, may simultaneously influence nucleation rates, crystal growth, and aggregation behavior, resulting in the observed heterogeneity. Furthermore, the presence of various phytochemicals (e.g., phenolic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and proteins) can selectively adsorb onto specific crystallographic planes, thereby influencing particle shape and size during the growth phase.

(d–f) ZnONPs at 2.0 μm, 5.0 μm, and 10 μm magnifications, respectively, revealing heterogeneous size distribution (ranging from 22 to 128 nm) and slight aggregation. Scale bars are indicated on each micrograph.

The particle size observed by SEM ranged approximately from 22 to 128 nm, confirming the successful synthesis of nanoparticles within the nanoscale regime. The wide variation in particle size may be associated with differences in nucleation and crystal growth rates during the biosynthesis process. In green synthesis, bioactive compounds including phenolic compounds, flavonoids, proteins, and polysaccharides act simultaneously as reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents, thereby influencing nanoparticle morphology, growth kinetics, and final particle dimensions. These phytochemicals effectively limit excessive particle growth and improve colloidal stability by preventing severe agglomeration.

The average crystallite size estimated from XRD analysis was approximately 37 nm, which aligns with the smaller nanoparticles observed in the SEM images; however, the broader SEM size distribution suggests the presence of polycrystalline aggregates or the formation of multiple crystallites within a single particle. This phenomenon is commonly observed when aggregation occurs during sample preparation for SEM analysis, where nanoparticles may cluster together during the drying process, resulting in the appearance of larger particles that are actually composed of several smaller crystallites.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed to determine the elemental composition and purity of the synthesized nanoparticles (Fig. 5). The EDX spectra confirmed the successful formation of both nanomaterials by exhibiting strong characteristic signals corresponding to silver in AgNPs (Fig. 5a) and zinc together with oxygen in ZnONPs (Fig. 5b), verifying the formation of crystalline ZnO. Characteristic elemental peaks appeared at approximately 1.5, 4.7, 11.2, and 12.5 keV, which are consistent with the presence of Ag and Zn in the synthesized nanoparticles.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spectra confirming the elemental composition of biosynthesized nanoparticles. (a) EDX spectrum of AgNPs showing characteristic silver (Ag) peaks at approximately 3.0, 3.3, 22.2, and 25.2 keV, along with weak carbon (C) and oxygen (O) signals from phytochemical capping agents. (b) EDX spectrum of ZnONPs displaying characteristic zinc (Zn) peaks at approximately 1.0, 8.6, and 9.6 keV and an oxygen (O) peak at 0.5 keV, confirming the formation of zinc oxide. The absence of additional elemental peaks indicates high purity of both nanomaterials.

In addition to the principal metallic elements, weak carbon signals were detected in the EDX spectra. These carbon signals are attributed to the adsorption of phytochemical molecules originating from the F. vulgare extract that remained attached to the nanoparticle surface as natural capping agents after synthesis. Such organic surface coatings are advantageous because they enhance nanoparticle stability, reduce particle agglomeration, and improve biocompatibility. Furthermore, small amounts of oxygen and sodium detected in the spectra are likely associated with residual plant-derived constituents and oxygen-containing functional groups adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface rather than crystalline impurities.

The absence of unexpected elemental peaks in the EDX spectra indicates that the biosynthesized nanoparticles possessed high elemental purity with minimal contamination from precursor compounds or undesirable by-products. The combined SEM and EDX analyses therefore confirmed that the green synthesis approach successfully produced nanosized and chemically pure AgNPs and ZnONPs, albeit with heterogeneous size distribution and mixed morphology. These structural and compositional characteristics are considered essential for enhancing the physicochemical stability and biological performance of nanoparticles, particularly their antimicrobial activity, catalytic efficiency, and surface reactivity.

The enhanced antimicrobial performance of AgNPs can be attributed to several complementary mechanisms. Due to their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, AgNPs readily interact with microbial cell membranes, causing membrane disruption, increased permeability, and leakage of intracellular components. Furthermore, silver nanoparticles release Ag+ ions, which interact with sulfhydryl-containing proteins, interfere with respiratory enzymes, induce oxidative stress through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and ultimately damage nucleic acids and essential cellular biomolecules. These combined mechanisms lead to irreversible microbial cell death and explain the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of AgNPs reported in numerous studies.

ZnONPs also demonstrated considerable antimicrobial activity, although their inhibitory effect was generally lower than that of AgNPs. The antimicrobial mechanism of ZnONPs is mainly associated with ROS generation under aerobic conditions, dissolution of Zn2+ ions, and direct interaction of nanoparticles with microbial membranes. These processes induce oxidative damage to membrane lipids, proteins, and DNA, ultimately suppressing microbial growth. The relatively lower antibacterial activity of ZnONPs observed in this study may be related to their lower ion release rate and reduced surface reactivity compared with AgNPs.

The susceptibility of microorganisms varied depending on the microbial species. Candida albicans exhibited the highest susceptibility to AgNPs, whereas P. aeruginosa showed comparatively greater resistance, particularly at lower nanoparticle concentrations. The increased resistance of P. aeruginosa may be attributed to its complex outer membrane, efficient efflux pumps, and intrinsic defense mechanisms that restrict nanoparticle penetration. In contrast, the thick peptidoglycan layer of the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus did not prevent effective interaction with the nanoparticles, resulting in considerable growth inhibition.

Consequently, the green-synthesized nanoparticles exhibited greater antimicrobial activity than the crude plant extract, suggesting that nanoparticle formation substantially improves the biological effectiveness of the bioactive phytochemicals.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive comparison of Foeniculum vulgare-mediated AgNPs and ZnONPs by integrating physicochemical characterization, antimicrobial evaluation, and in vivo toxicity assessment. Although both nanomaterials were successfully synthesized through a green approach and demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, AgNPs consistently exhibited greater antimicrobial efficacy together with better biocompatibility than ZnONPs. These findings highlight the importance of nanoparticle composition in determining both biological performance and biological safety.

Although the present study successfully utilized F. vulgare seed extract for the green synthesis of both AgNPs and ZnONPs, the specific phytochemical constituents responsible for the reduction and stabilization processes were not fully elucidated through advanced analytical techniques such as LC-MS/MS. This represents a limitation of the current investigation. Nevertheless, FTIR analysis provided indirect evidence for the involvement of hydroxyl (–OH), carbonyl (C=O), amine (N—H), and ether (C–O–C) functional groups, which are characteristic of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, proteins, and polysaccharides. These phytochemical classes are well-documented for their strong reducing capacities and metal-chelating properties. Phenolic acids (e.g., gallic, caffeic, and rosmarinic acids) and flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, kaempferol, and apigenin) possess ortho-dihydroxy groups that readily donate electrons to reduce Ag+ and Zn2+ ions, while their carboxyl and hydroxyl moieties facilitate strong adsorption onto nanoparticle surfaces, providing electrostatic and steric stabilization.13, 14, 15, 16, 27 The high total phenolic and flavonoid content of fennel seeds, as previously reported in the literature,17, 19, 20 further supports the role of these phytochemicals in mediating nanoparticle formation. The phytochemical-rich capping layer not only enhances colloidal stability but may also contribute synergistically to the observed antimicrobial activity, as many of these compounds possess intrinsic antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.13, 14, 15, 16

The successful synthesis of AgNPs and ZnONPs was confirmed by complementary physicochemical analyses. UV–Visible spectroscopy verified nanoparticle formation through characteristic surface plasmon resonance and absorption bands, whereas FTIR spectra indicated that plant-derived phytochemicals participated in nanoparticle reduction and stabilization. XRD analysis confirmed the crystalline structure of both nanomaterials, and SEM observations demonstrated predominantly spherical nanoparticles with slight aggregation. These findings are in good agreement with previous studies reporting that phytochemical-rich medicinal plants provide effective reducing and capping agents for the green synthesis of metallic nanoparticles with desirable physicochemical properties.10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16 Similar comparative observations have been reported for green-synthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs using other medicinal plants. For instance, AgNPs synthesized from Thespesia populnea leaf extract exhibited superior antioxidant activity compared to ZnONPs derived from the same plant.27 Likewise, studies using Lantana camara and Borassus flabellifer extracts have demonstrated that AgNPs generally possess stronger antibacterial potency, whereas ZnONPs offer greater biocompatibility.28, 29 These findings are consistent with the present results, reinforcing the general trend that plant-mediated AgNPs exhibit superior antimicrobial efficacy, while the choice of plant matrix influences the magnitude of this difference and the overall safety profile of the synthesized nanomaterials.

A major finding of the present investigation is the significantly greater antimicrobial activity of AgNPs than ZnONPs against all tested microorganisms. The superior antimicrobial performance of AgNPs can be explained by the simultaneous action of several complementary mechanisms. Silver nanoparticles readily interact with microbial cell surfaces, increasing membrane permeability and causing leakage of intracellular constituents. In addition, the gradual release of Ag+ ions promotes intracellular oxidative stress through excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to protein denaturation, inhibition of respiratory enzymes, lipid peroxidation, and structural damage to nucleic acids. The simultaneous disruption of multiple essential cellular functions considerably decreases the probability of resistance development compared with conventional antibiotics that generally target a single metabolic pathway.5, 6, 7, 8

By comparison, ZnONPs exhibited lower antimicrobial activity despite demonstrating clear inhibitory effects against all tested microorganisms. Their antimicrobial action is mainly associated with ROS generation, Zn2+ ion release, and oxidative damage to microbial membranes. However, the efficiency of these mechanisms is highly influenced by nanoparticle size, crystallinity, surface chemistry, surrounding environmental conditions, and microbial species. Similar observations have been reported for biosynthesized ZnONPs prepared from medicinal plants, where antimicrobial efficacy was generally lower than that of AgNPs under comparable experimental conditions.11, 13

Compared with previous studies on plant-mediated nanoparticles, the present investigation offers several important advantages. Previous work using Foeniculum vulgare seed extract has primarily focused on the synthesis and antimicrobial or cytotoxic properties of either AgNPs or ZnONPs separately. For example, fennel seed-derived ZnONPs have previously demonstrated antimicrobial activity and cytotoxic effects against cancer cells, but the study did not provide a direct head-to-head comparison with AgNPs synthesized from the same plant matrix or an in vivo comparative assessment of systemic toxicity.9 Similarly, an earlier study reported the green synthesis of AgNPs using F. vulgare seed extract and demonstrated antibacterial activity, but its evaluation was limited to antimicrobial screening without comparative assessment of ZnONPs or in vivo toxicological profiling.19 In contrast, the present study simultaneously synthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs using the same F. vulgare seed extract and evaluated them under comparable experimental conditions, thereby minimizing variability related to the plant source and synthesis strategy. Importantly, the study integrates physicochemical characterization, broad-spectrum antimicrobial testing, MIC/MBC/MFC determination, serum biochemical analysis, and liver and kidney histopathology within a single experimental framework. This integrated design demonstrated that AgNPs achieved consistently lower MIC and MBC/MFC values than ZnONPs while producing substantially fewer biochemical and histopathological alterations after repeated oral administration. Thus, the principal advantage of the present study is not simply higher antimicrobial activity, but the identification of a more favorable efficacy–safety profile for F. vulgare-mediated AgNPs under the experimental conditions investigated. Such a direct comparative assessment provides information that cannot be obtained from studies evaluating either nanoparticle type independently and may facilitate more rational selection of biogenic nanomaterials for future biomedical applications.

Among the tested microorganisms, Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed the highest resistance to both nanoparticles. This observation is consistent with the intrinsic characteristics of this opportunistic pathogen, including reduced outer membrane permeability, multidrug efflux pumps, biofilm formation, and efficient stress-response systems that collectively reduce susceptibility to antimicrobial agents.30, 31 In contrast, Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans exhibited greater susceptibility, indicating that nanoparticle–cell interactions vary according to microbial cell envelope composition and physiological characteristics.

The enhanced antimicrobial activity of AgNPs is particularly important in the context of antimicrobial resistance. Khaledi et al. demonstrated that overexpression of multidrug efflux pumps contributes substantially to antimicrobial resistance in clinically important bacterial pathogens, while subsequent evidence has highlighted the contribution of biofilm formation to persistent multidrug resistance.32, 33 Because nanoparticle-mediated antimicrobial activity relies on simultaneous physicochemical interactions rather than a single molecular target, metallic nanoparticles may represent promising alternatives for combating multidrug-resistant microorganisms.

The comparison of the biosynthesized nanoparticles with standard antibiotics (Ciprofloxacin and Fluconazole) revealed that AgNPs exhibited significantly higher antimicrobial activity than the positive controls. At 512 μg/mL, AgNPs produced inhibition zones of 20.0 mm and 18.0 mm against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, respectively, compared to 8.0 mm and 7.0 mm for Ciprofloxacin. This superior activity can be attributed to the multiple mechanisms of action of AgNPs, including membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and intracellular interaction with proteins and DNA (Liao et al., 2019). In contrast, conventional antibiotics typically target a single metabolic pathway, making them more susceptible to resistance development (Ventola, 2015). The enhanced activity of AgNPs against C. albicans (28.0 mm) compared to Fluconazole (no inhibition) is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that AgNPs may be effective against azole-resistant fungal strains, which are increasingly prevalent in clinical settings (Pfaller et al., 2010). These findings underscore the potential of plant-mediated AgNPs as novel antimicrobial agents that can complement or replace conventional antibiotics in the fight against multidrug-resistant microorganisms.

The MIC and MBC assays further confirmed the superior antimicrobial potency of AgNPs compared with ZnONPs. The lower MIC and MBC values obtained for AgNPs indicate that smaller nanoparticle concentrations were sufficient not only to inhibit microbial growth but also to eliminate viable microorganisms. The close agreement between inhibition zone diameters, MIC, and MBC results strengthens the reliability of the antimicrobial evaluation and demonstrates that the observed activity was not merely associated with nanoparticle diffusion but reflected genuine antimicrobial efficacy. Furthermore, the relatively narrow difference between MIC and MBC values suggests that AgNPs exert predominantly bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic effects, which agrees with previous investigations on biosynthesized silver nanoparticles.14, 15

Although both nanoparticles exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, their susceptibility patterns varied among the tested microorganisms. The comparatively greater resistance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa may be explained by its intrinsic resistance mechanisms, including low outer membrane permeability, efficient multidrug efflux systems, biofilm formation, and adaptive stress-response pathways that collectively reduce intracellular accumulation of antimicrobial agents.30, 31 In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria and Candida albicans were more susceptible to nanoparticle exposure, possibly because of differences in cell wall architecture and membrane composition that facilitate nanoparticle interaction and cellular penetration.

Evaluation of biological safety is an essential requirement for the biomedical application of engineered nanoparticles. In the present study, animals received a dose of 10 mg/kg of either AgNPs or ZnONPs via oral gavage for 14 consecutive days, based on previously reported sub-acute oral toxicity studies in rodents where this dose level has been shown to induce measurable biochemical and histopathological alterations without causing severe systemic toxicity.34 The 14-day treatment period is consistent with standard protocols for sub-acute toxicity assessment, allowing sufficient time for nanoparticle accumulation and tissue response while minimizing confounding factors associated with prolonged exposure. Biochemical analyses demonstrated that animals treated with AgNPs exhibited only slight alterations in serum liver and kidney function markers, whereas ZnONP-treated animals showed significantly greater elevations in ALT, AST, and urea concentrations together with a reduction in ALP activity. These biochemical findings indicate that ZnONPs produced a greater degree of hepatic and renal stress than AgNPs under the experimental conditions employed.

The higher toxicity associated with ZnONPs is most likely related to their greater capacity for intracellular dissolution and Zn2+ ion release. Excessive intracellular zinc accumulation may disrupt cellular homeostasis, impair mitochondrial function, stimulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and activate inflammatory and apoptotic signaling pathways. Mechanistically, ZnONPs induce oxidative stress through the generation of ROS, leading to lipid peroxidation, depletion of intracellular antioxidants such as reduced glutathione (GSH), and oxidative DNA damage. This oxidative burden subsequently triggers mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis via upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (BAX, caspase-3) and downregulation of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2.35 Persistent oxidative stress promotes lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, DNA damage, and cellular degeneration within hepatic and renal tissues.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 In the present study, although biochemical and histopathological alterations were observed in ZnONP-treated animals, oxidative stress biomarkers such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and GSH were not measured. This constitutes a limitation of the present investigation, and future studies should incorporate these parameters to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying ZnONP-induced toxicity. Conversely, the comparatively mild biochemical alterations observed following AgNP administration suggest that the phytochemical coating derived from Foeniculum vulgare may improve nanoparticle stability and partially attenuate oxidative injury by limiting uncontrolled silver ion release and nanoparticle aggregation. Similar improvements in biocompatibility have previously been reported for plant-mediated metallic nanoparticles synthesized from phenolic-rich medicinal plants.13, 14, 15, 16

Histopathological observations were fully consistent with the biochemical findings. Liver sections from AgNP-treated animals largely preserved normal hepatic architecture with only mild cellular alterations, whereas ZnONP-treated animals exhibited more evident hepatocellular degeneration, sinusoidal dilatation, vascular congestion, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Likewise, renal tissues from the ZnONP group demonstrated mild tubular degeneration together with glomerular alterations, whereas kidneys from AgNP-treated animals remained largely comparable to those of the control group. The close correlation between biochemical markers and microscopic tissue evaluation provides additional evidence supporting the relatively favorable safety profile of biosynthesized AgNPs.

It is noteworthy that although ZnONPs induced measurable hepatic and renal alterations, the observed lesions remained relatively mild, indicating that the administered dose (10 mg/kg) did not produce severe systemic toxicity during the 14-day experimental period. Previous investigations have demonstrated that nanoparticle toxicity is strongly influenced by particle size, administered dose, exposure duration, surface chemistry, and synthesis strategy. Green-synthesized nanoparticles generally exhibit improved biocompatibility compared with chemically synthesized counterparts because naturally occurring phytochemicals act as stabilizing agents, reduce nanoparticle aggregation, and moderate nanoparticle–cell interactions.13, 14, 15, 16, 36, 37, 38, 39

A major strength of the present study is the simultaneous comparison of AgNPs and ZnONPs synthesized from the same plant extract using identical experimental conditions. This integrated design minimized methodological variability and enabled a direct comparison of their physicochemical characteristics, antimicrobial efficacy, and biological safety. Such comprehensive comparative studies remain relatively limited in the current literature and provide valuable information for selecting appropriate nanomaterials for future biomedical applications.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The antimicrobial experiments were performed using a limited number of reference bacterial and fungal strains, whereas multidrug-resistant clinical isolates and mature biofilms were not investigated. Moreover, molecular biomarkers associated with oxidative stress (such as MDA and GSH), inflammatory responses, apoptosis, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetics were not evaluated. Future studies should incorporate these parameters together with long-term toxicity assessments and efficacy studies using clinically relevant multidrug-resistant pathogens to further clarify the therapeutic potential and biosafety of plant-mediated metallic nanoparticles.

Overall, the present findings demonstrate that Foeniculum vulgare-mediated AgNPs possess a more favorable balance between antimicrobial efficacy and biological safety than ZnONPs. The combination of potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, relatively low systemic toxicity, and environmentally sustainable synthesis supports their potential application as promising antimicrobial nanomaterials for future biomedical and pharmaceutical development.

A notable limitation of the present study is the absence of comprehensive phytochemical profiling (e.g., LC-MS/MS) of the F. vulgare seed extract. Such analysis would have allowed precise identification of the specific phenolic acids, flavonoids, and other bioactive metabolites responsible for the differential reduction and stabilization of AgNPs versus ZnONPs. Furthermore, quantitative correlation between specific phytochemicals and the observed antimicrobial or toxicological outcomes could provide deeper mechanistic insights. Future studies should therefore employ advanced metabolomic approaches to elucidate the precise role of individual phytoconstituents in nanoparticle biosynthesis, surface functionalization, and biological activity. Additionally, investigating the phytochemical composition of the final nanoparticle surface (after washing and calcination) would help distinguish between compounds that are merely adsorbed versus those that are covalently bound or strongly complexed with the metal surface.

A further limitation of the present study is the absence of hemolysis assays, which would have provided additional valuable information regarding the blood compatibility of the biosynthesized AgNPs and ZnONPs. Hemolysis experiments are considered a standard in vitro method for evaluating the potential of nanomaterials to damage red blood cell membranes and release hemoglobin, and they would have helped clarify the discrepancies observed in the animal experimental data.5, 11, 15 Previous investigations have consistently reported that plant-mediated AgNPs and ZnONPs exhibit low hemolytic activity (<5%) due to the presence of phytochemical capping agents that reduce direct interaction with RBC membranes and minimize oxidative damage.14, 15 For instance, Rónavári et al. (2017) demonstrated that green-synthesized AgNPs using various plants extracts showed hemolysis percentages below 3% at concentrations up to 500 μg/mL, which is well within the acceptable range for biomedical applications.14 Similarly, Andleeb et al. (2025) reported that AgNPs synthesized using Viola odorata and Onosma hispidum extracts exhibited no pronounced hemolysis even at higher concentrations (300 μg/mL), demonstrating their biocompatibility and hemolytic safety profile.11 Additionally, studies on ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using Mentha piperita extract showed minimal hemolysis, with plant-based capping agents ensuring safer blood interactions compared to chemically synthesized counterparts.8, 13 Based on these literature reports and our in vivo biochemical and histopathological findings (which showed that AgNPs induced significantly fewer alterations than ZnONPs), we anticipate that AgNPs would exhibit lower hemolytic activity than ZnONPs. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that direct hemolysis experiments are required to confirm this hypothesis. Future studies should incorporate hemolysis assays together with other hematological parameters (e.g., complete blood count, platelet aggregation, and coagulation assays) to establish a more complete safety profile for these nanomaterials prior to clinical translation.5, 11, 15

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that Foeniculum vulgare extract is an effective and sustainable biogenic resource for the green synthesis of silver (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs). Comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirmed the successful formation of crystalline, predominantly spherical nanoparticles with favorable structural and compositional properties. Both nanomaterials exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and fungal pathogens in a concentration-dependent manner. However, AgNPs consistently showed superior antimicrobial performance, as evidenced by larger inhibition zones and lower MIC and MBC/MFC values compared with ZnONPs.

The in vivo evaluation further revealed that biosynthesized AgNPs induced only minor alterations in liver and kidney function biomarkers and caused minimal histopathological changes following 14 days of oral administration at 10 mg/kg, whereas ZnONPs produced comparatively greater hepatic and renal tissue alterations. These findings indicate that AgNPs synthesized using F. vulgare possess a more favorable balance between antimicrobial efficacy and short-term biocompatibility than ZnONPs under the experimental conditions employed.

Overall, the integration of green synthesis, potent antimicrobial activity, and acceptable short-term biological safety highlights the potential of F. vulgare-derived AgNPs as promising candidates for biomedical and pharmaceutical applications, particularly in the development of alternative antimicrobial agents to address the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that the present findings are based on a 14-day sub-acute toxicity study, and definitive conclusions regarding long-term safety and chronic toxicity cannot be drawn at this stage. Therefore, further investigations involving multidrug-resistant clinical isolates, molecular mechanisms of antimicrobial action, long-term toxicity, pharmacokinetics, and in vivo therapeutic efficacy are required before clinical translation can be considered.
