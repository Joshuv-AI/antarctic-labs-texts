Diaphragmatic pacing system from the point of view of the patients: 14 years of clinical experience and follow-up
Authors: Giuliana Stagni, Francesco Curto, Matteo Giacomini, Federico Pozzi, Arturo Chieregato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh cervical spinal diseases (C1–C4), resulting in paralysis of the respiratory muscles, makes the person dependent on invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) for life support. Although effective from a ventilatory point of view, IMV is associated with increased morbidity and mortality; ventilated patients have higher rates of respiratory complications, a significant reduction in life expectancy, and above all, a serious psychological repercussion with estrangement from the world. Diaphragmatic pacing system (DPS) is a well-established technique capable of restoring paraphysiological ventilation.Aim of the studyA narrative review of the case history and their follow-up of 13 patients dependent on life support ventilation who have been implanted since 2011 in the Neurocritical Care Unit of the Niguarda Hospital, Milan.ResultsDiaphragmatic pacing system was successful implanted in all the patients: of them 9 were affected by traumatic cervical SCI, 2 by Arnold Chiari sequelae, 1 due to a spontaneous ischemia. One patient with Pompe disease was included although his pathophysiology primarly affects the diaphragm instead the high cervical spine tract.During the 14 years follow-up, 92% of patients (12/13) used DPS and in 42 % of them (5/12 patients) we were able to be disconnected from IMV for at least 12 h. One of the patients (7.7%) was completely weaned from IMV and the tracheostomy was finally removed. One patient needed hospitalization for a respiratory complication. All the patients perceived their quality of life to be excellent or good.DiscussionThe long-term follow-up of 13 patients with DPS suggests that the implant could be a potential long-term alternative to IMV, particularly in terms of quality of life. The diaphragmatic stimulator protects from IMV associated respiratory complications, therefore reducing hospitalization. Allowing spontaneous breathing for a variable number of hours enables the caregiver to support the individual more effectively, facilitating their reintegration into daily social and work activities. Diaphragmatic pacing system does not eliminate the disability but allows for more independent years of life with a greater possibility of social reintegration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44158-025-00321-3.Keywords: Cervical spine injury, Diaphragm pacing, Invasive Mechanical ventilation, Quality of life, Long term outcome, Follow up, Respiratory complications, Neurocritical care, Tracheostomy, Weaning from ventilation

Background

In cervical spinal cord injury (cSCI) patients (C1–C4), invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) is essential for survival, yet long-term IMV dependency is linked with increased respiratory complications such as posterior lobe atelectasis, pneumonia, barotrauma, tracheomalacia, and reduced life expectancy [1, 2]. IMV also affects daily life quality, limiting speech, reducing smell, increasing secretion requiring regular suctioning, and generating disruptive ventilator noise.

The diaphragmatic pacing system (DPS), a technique that simulates natural ventilation through diaphragm-targeted electrical stimulation, is a recognized approach in cSCI patients. By facilitating IMV disconnection for varied periods, DPS mitigates IMV’s adverse effects. While DPS has shown potential benefits, evidence on long-term outcomes is limited. This review aims to address this gap by presenting our 14-year single-center experience with DPS in cSCI patients.

Material and methods

Setting

Niguarda hospital hosts a SCI unit (SU) and a Neurocritical Care Unit (NCC). Critical care physicians and respiratory therapists work together to achieve the best results with SCI patients, with aggressive respiratory and physioterapic approach including extensive use of NIV and mechanical in-exsufflation (MIE). Since 2011 to 2024, 132 patients with traumatic cervical spine injury were primarily referred to our NCC: Of them none received DPS and only 16 tracheostomy (12.1%). This approach is largely inspired by the teachings of Bach [3–5]: the results of this activity had been reported in 2021 [6]. In this setting, NeuRx DPS (Synapse Biomedical Inc, Oberlin, OH, USA ) was introduced in 2011 as a consequence of the Hub function of our Hospital for this kind of patients. Since 2011, twenty patients were referred to our NCC outpatient clinic by various Italian SU, ICU but more frequently after a spontaneous requests of patients cared at home by they next of kin. All the patients, except one. presented to our ICU in controlled IMV and tracheostomy. After patients selection, 13 patients received DPS implantation. We retrospectively analyzed their medical records to unveil not only clinical characteristics but also their personal histories.

Patients’ selection and implantation

The inclusion criteria for DPS implantation and the patient’s evaluation flowchart in our institution was described in Fig. S1. The team involved in the evaluation before DPS implantation involve critical care physicians, respiratory therapists, surgeons, neurologists, psychologists. The electrodes are implanted directly into the diaphragm through laparoscopic access [7, 8], preserving the nerves from surgery-related damage. After the surgery, an adapting and training period (called “conditioning”) took place with the involvement of the patient and the caregiver (Fig. S2). Patients were hospitalized in NCC or in SCI unit to monitor the initial conditioning phase and caregivers’ education.

Follow-up

Over the years, we developed an ambulatory system that integrates acute and chronic phases in a territorial environment and aims at comprehensive patient care. Due to the high inter-individual variability in clinical and social background, we built an outpatient system tailored to every single patient composed of monthly telephone interviews, involvement of local resources (general doctors, integrated home assistance, teachers, emergency system), continuous training for caregivers (periodic meetings, flow charts), 24 h/24 h telephone availability and interventions at home in selected cases. The patients were scheduled for contact approximately every 3 months. At each monthly meeting, patients were asked about their perceived quality of life according to the EQ-VAS visual analogue scale (EQ-5D-L, version 2018) (https://euroqol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/EQ-5D-3LUserguide-23-07.pdf). The maximum EQ-VAS scores stable after DPS implantation were recorded and dichotomized. The EQ VAS scores were classified into four sub-groups: < 65 (bad), 65–79 (fair), 80–89 (good), and 90–100 (excellent) [9].

Patients submitted to DPS implantation

The description of the 13 cases is reported in Tables 2 and 3. The median age of the patients at the time of implantation was 20 years with one patient over 60 years and 3 children (≤ 8 years). The median delay from spinal deficit and DPS implantation was 4 years. All the 13 patients except one presented before our center with tracheostomy and controlled IMV. The unique patient presenting without tracheostomy was a patient affected by surgical complication after surgery for Arnold Chiari syndrome (see case 13). One patient on 13 never used the DPS. In 4 patients, DPS was used for more than 12 h a day without IMV, and in 2 patients, use was between 8 and 12 h without IMV. Therefore in 5 patients on 13 (38.5%) the DPS use was associated with at least 12 h without IMV. In the remaining 5 patients, in 3 of them the DPS was used for its ability to activate assisted ventilation. The median duration of use during the day of the DPS was 16 h; of them the use of DPS without any ventilatory support was in median of 11 h.

The follow-up is still ongoing for the surviving patients (9/13, 69%) and the median observation period was 9 years.

In 11 patients (85%), there was an improvement in quality of life but only in 7 patients (54%) this was maintained. Their best quality of life was rated by themselves as excellent in 6 (46.2%) cases while good in the remaining 7 (54.8%) cases. The patients did not undergo hospitalization for ventilatory complications during their follow-up and the causes of death were independent of the DPS.

Case 1: male, traumatic SCI, cervical C0, AIS A, 10-year follow-up, now 12 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: the patient experienced a cardio-circulatory arrest (CCA) at the scene of a road accident when he was 2 years old. However, he did not suffer any appreciable cognitive deficits during his recovery. He underwent a tracheostomy 24 days after the injury and was transferred to our NCC 87 days later. Due to dysautonomic spinal syndrome caused by the tracheostomy cannula, we considered a DPS placement to restore paraphysiological ventilation and reduce the trigger effect of the cannula. The plan was supported by the patient’s long life expectancy, absence of cognitive defects, and no traumatic or acquired lung injury. The implantation was carried out 1 year after the injury. Plateau phase: during the plateau phase, which occurred after 6 months, the patient achieved 24-h ventilator liberation, normal feeding by oral route, and verbal communication that was adequate for his developmental age. Up-to-date features: currently, at 12 years old, he currently uses DPS 24 h a day and attends grade 1 at a public school. The ability to maintain spontaneous, stimulated breathing has allowed the patient to resume activities previously prevented by the ventilator. Indeed, both practical and collectively imaginative reasons dictate that a person on IMV is necessarily unfit for social reintegration. In a child, these factors can lead to further deprivation of stimulation, already maximal in severe tetraplegia, slowing cognitive and emotional development. In this patient, DPS attenuates these phenomena at least partially.

Case 2: female, traumatic SCI, cervical C2, AIS A, 16-year follow-up, now 20 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: the patient experienced a road accident at the age of 4 in October 2008, resulting in CCA at the scene. However, she recovered without any significant cognitive deficits. She underwent a tracheostomy 14 days after the injury and was later transferred to the SU of Niguarda Hospital for rehabilitation. After 11 months, she was discharged and received an implant 3 years after the injury. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient was able to use DPS for one hour. After 3 years, the patient was liberated from the ventilator for 24 h using the DPS for 12 h a day. During this long, positive period, the patient was able to cross the Puna de Atacama desert in northern Argentina thanks to the active DPS and closed tracheostomy tube. The patient had good phonation and no fatigue. The issues were related to the patient’s age and the parents’ difficulty in accepting the child’s fatigue during reconditioning. In the next few years, there was a worsening of scoliosis, resulting in left lung compression and this induced a gradual return to the use of the ventilator, until the patient underwent scoliosis correction surgery, which had an excellent outcome. Up-to-date features: she is currently 20 years old and attends university. She uses DPS in association with the pressure-assisted ventilation 24 h a day. We are planning to recondition the diaphragm again. In this case, the resumption and subsequent cessation of stimulated spontaneous breathing highlighted the limitations associated with the ventilator, particularly during the developmental age. The DPS allowed experiences such as helicopter riding, assisted cycling, and extended travel without burdensome bureaucracy (air flight), but above all, the gradual recovery of independence as the patient grew, without the constraints of potential disconnection from the ventilator.

Case 3: male, Arnold Chiari I syndrome, 12-year follow-up, died at 26 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: in April 2007, at the age of 14, he underwent neurosurgery due to Arnold Chiari I syndrome. Unfortunately, post-operative complications resulted in incomplete spastic quadriplegia, which caused him to have no respiratory drive and become dependent on a mechanical ventilator for 24 h. After 39 days, he was tracheostomized and transferred to our SU, where he underwent rehabilitation until he was discharged at home after 79 days. The implant was carried out 4 years after the initial surgery. The progressive bulbar involvement due to the worsening of the Arnold Chiari syndrome and the consequent dysphagia can difficult remove tracheostomy. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient achieved 16 h of ventilator liberation per day without experiencing fatigue. Five years after the DPS placement, the patient experienced one episode of atelectasis with pleural effusion, and 1 year later, seizures worsened the lung condition. Following these events, the patient used DPS triggered assisted pressure ventilation for 12 h per day. Up-to-date features: the patient passed away in March 2023 at the age of 30, due to multiorgan failure in recidivant sepsis. The injury at age 14 brought an abrupt end to ongoing plans and future expectations for both the boy and his family, disrupting their daily lives and reconstructing them around the needs induced by their disability. The fear of accidental disconnection from the ventilator shifted their attention to the device, leading to avoidance of the outside world. After the implant, school, social, and sports activities were resumed, reconnecting the boy with horseback riding—in essence, with his previous life—and creating a bridge between his disability and his previous aspirations.

Case 4: female, traumatic SCI, cervical C0, AIS A, 19 year follow-up, now 39 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: the patient was a female who experienced a road accident at the age of 19. She suffered from CCA and recovered without any noticeable cognitive deficits. Two days after the injury, she underwent a tracheostomy. After 52 days, she was transferred to the Niguarda SU where she underwent rehabilitation until she was discharged to her home 16 months after the injury. Due to the integrity of her phrenic nerve, she is a suitable candidate for DPS implantation, but not for family management. Considering the patient’s young age and cognitive and clinical state, a collegial examination determined that the implant should be done 6 years after the injury. The procedure was complicated by a hypertensive pneumothorax, which subsequently resolved completely. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained 5 h of DPS use per day. However, along the first 3 years no further improvement occurred, probably because the patient lived in a long stay residence without dedicated caregivers. Therefore, the reconditioning was stopped and she returned to use ventilator until 2016, when she began to use DPS intensively. Up-to-date features: now she is 39 years old and uses DPS 18 h a day with closed cannula. She regained a good speech and no fatigue both supine or in the wheelchair. She lives in the long stay residence and she wrote to us a small text about her life (Fig. 1). She is actively involved to endorse road traffic safety project in schools. The recent breakage of an electrode, resulting in a few days’ interruption in the use of the DPS, has highlighted how returning to dependence on the ventilator is perceived as a return to a disability that had been defeated, taking away what represented the achievement of autonomy beyond the injury: the smells and flavors, the voice and breathing not tied to a detachable tube, the gaze of others not focused on the neck.

A patient’s thought: on the figure the original text, here the translation “Due to Covid, being impossible for me to wear a mask while in the wheel chair, I stopped doing diaphragmatic stimulation because I couldn’t protect myself by breathing through my mouth and nose. A few days ago I started again, a fantastic thing that excites me every time is my being able to smell the scents… Even when I’m on the street, everything takes me back to the past: the scent of someone who is cooking that escapes from the windows, the plants in flower, the smell that I can’t explain that you smell when passing through the alleys, the freshly cut grass that makes my nose itch and also bad smells here and there… I could continue as if all that were new but instead it’s simply something I’ve forgotten because when I use the respirator it’s a bit like living in a bubble…I’m enjoing this excited moment with my eyes shining happily like a little girl…”

Case 5: male, traumatic SCI, cervical C2, AIS A, 14-year follow-up, now 44 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: male, sport accident at 17 years in a foreing state. He was tracheostomized after a few days and then he was transported to an Italian SU to carry out the rehabilitation process until discharge to home. After 13 years the DPS was implanted. Despite the lacking family support; the determination of the patient and his cognitive level led the team to carry out the procedure. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained 4 h of use of DPS and after 3 years the use reduced to 1 h. The reconditioning was inconsistent during to the first 3 years, until 2014, when thanks to a regular training with DPS again he reached 5 h use in the wheelchair and 5 h supine. The patient obtained a satisfactory phonation, no fatigue supine but use of accessory respiratory muscles on the wheelchair with shoulders’ pain and contraction. The problem was overcome by setting the stimulator used while seated in a different and implemented way. Up-to-date features: now he is 44 years old and uses DPS 8 h a day and he perceive a good social life.

From the blog created by the patient, the chapter is titled “Listen to your breathing”: “It has improved my life 100% by giving me more freedom in my travels. Now when I leave the house I no longer have the burden of tubes and the respirator, and it has also made my life much easier for my parents.”

Case 6: female,traumatic SCI, cervical C2, AIS A, 8-year follow-up, died at 12 years

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: female, road accident at 4 years (January 2007). The patients suffered CCA and traumatic sub-arachnoid hemorrhage with severe cognitive deficit. She was tracheostomized a few days post-injury and after 73 days she was transferred to the Niguarda SU where she carried out the rehabilitation process until discharge to home 13 months post-injury. Suitable candidate for DPS implant due to phrenic nerve integrity and family management but she did not meet the requirement of cognitive adequacy and cranial nerve integrity. In light of the young age and the presence of an excellent care giver, the team decided for the implant that was carried out 4 years later not withstanding a dysphagia due to bulbar involvement, cognitive deficit, inconstancy in the ambulatory clinic visit. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained 4 h of use of DPS, and she maintained 4 h during the first 3 years, with open cannula. The main problem was dysautonomia with increase in blood pressure and heart rate which relieved by the supine position. Eleven years post-injury, she used permanently the DPS associating, to the previous 6 h/day, even further 18 h in which the DPS triggered pressure assisted ventilation. Up-to-date features: on August 2019 she died, due to accidental disconnection from ventilator while DPS was unfortunately not being used. Cultural and language barriers have prevented the adequate transmission of some fundamental concepts, yet it is clear that failure to use the DPS simultaneously with the ventilator has led to death; even when this is not the case, it is an example that even if spontaneous stimulated breathing is not achieved for a long period of time, the DPS still ensures respiratory activity in the event of accidental disconnection from the MV.

Case 7: male, traumatic SCI, cervical C2, AIS A, 6-year follow-up, died once 40 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: male, during a sport related trauma at 34 years (October 2014) suffered of a CCA without residual cognitive deficit. He was tracheostomized after 8 days. He carried out rehabilitation at an Italian SU along 8 months and therefore discharged to home. He was implanted 2 years after trauma. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained 15 h of use of DPS, after 3 years 18 h and after 5 years we reached 24/24 use of DPS. Thus, he underwent NIV adaptation in order to make the potentially decannulation feasible. However, after family difficulties and the loss of the principal caregiver, he left work and social reintegration projects. Up-to-date features: once at 41 years old he used DPS 18 h a day with closed cannula, good speech and no fatigue both supine and on the wheelchair. He did not use DPS during the night for fear as no one is able to guarantee a correct global management and this becomes more relevant when the person is asleep. After a period of secondary depression caused by the difficulty in finding a new way of life on the wheelchair, he began to recover interest in life but unfortunately, he died of CCA at home not related to DPS malfunctions. From his blog: “I started breathing on my own for thirty seconds, then a minute, then two, then three, until within a month I was breathing on my own for 10 h straight. Now, three months later, I can breathe without the aid of a ventilator all day. I feel extremely free. I'm breathing better than with the ventilator, and most importantly, I can start thinking about doing activities I couldn't even begin to do before. Now I'd like to experience the thrill of sports again, like skiing, paragliding, or something else. I have a never-ending to-do list, and little by little, I'll tick them off. Every day I wake up feeling more motivated because every day is another breath, a breath full of life.”

Case 8: female, traumatic SCI, cervical C0, AIS A, 10-year follow-up, now 70 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: female, road accident at 44 years associated with a CCA without cognitive deficit; she was admitted to our NCC and she was tracheostomized 7 days post-injury. Thirty-6 days later she was transferred to the SU where she carried out the rehabilitation process until discharge at home 14 months later. Suitable candidate for DPS implant due to phrenic nerve integrity but not for family management, as the only care giver was the daughter. In consideration of the cognitive and clinical state and of the determination of the patient, collegial examination decided for implant, carried out 34 months post-injury. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained only 40 min of use of DPS, and 60 min after 1 year. After 3 years, 2 h. Since she had no family support, she did not perform reconditioning and despite of an excellent intraoperative response of the diaphragm to the stimulus, she was unable to use the stimulator. Since 4 years after the implant she stopped use of DPS, while after 2 further years she was hospitalized for cognitive impairment where the team could re-start DPS again with good volume despite the deconditioning. Up-to-date features: now she does not use DPS as she has no organized caregivers and she is not more accompanied to the ambulatory for the follow-up. In this case, too, the cultural and social gap determined failure from the patient’s perspective, though not from a biological/clinical perspective. Indeed, even though deconditioned, the patient’s diaphragm was able to contract effectively. Experience in this case led to the inclusion of the lack of motivated caregivers and an adequate social and cultural background among the exclusion criteria.

Case 9: male, traumatic SCI, cervical C3, AIS A, 8-year follow-up, now 24 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: male, victim of a sport accident in Greece, at 14 years-old (May 2016). He was transported to local hospital, where he was tracheostomized after 1 day. He returned to Italy after 1 month admitted to Niguarda SU in January 2017. The patient had numerous bedsores, multi-resistance infections and urinary stones which led to a long hospitalization and delay in positioning the stimulator until December 2017, with no complications. Plateau phase: after 3 months, the patient gained 16 h of DPS use, he was adapted to NIV and removed tracheal cannula. After 2 years, he used DPS 16 h but he did not use it during the night, not for clinical causes but for a personal choice. Family problems and the absence of a care giver able to manage the patient in all aspects, led to difficulties in monitoring the respiratory function. Up-to-date features: now he is 24 years old and uses DPS 16 h a day with good speech. He has no adverse events related to the stimulator but respiratory problems due to family management difficulties. He regularly attends university. Despite the management challenges posed by the decision to return to Greece, the DPS has allowed him to breathe independently even without regular checkups and assessments. This indicates that, once reconditioning is complete, the consistent use of the pacing system is adequate to ensure proper lung ventilation without respiratory complications.

Case 10: female, traumatic SCI, cervical C2, AIS A, 7-year follow-up, now 69 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: female who fell at the age of 62 years (July 2018); she was tracheostomized 16 days post-injury. She was therefore evaluated at our NCC for the implant which was carried out 3 month post-injury. The implant was associated with a pneumothorax with no need for drainage. She was back transferred to her initial ICU after 9 days with a 12 h ventilator liberation, closed tracheostomal cannula, good speech and no ventilatory fatigue. Plateau phase: after 1 year, the patient gained 24/24 h of use of DPS with closed cannula also during the night. Since she had no family support, she was admitted to a long stay care unit. She was scheduled for decannulation but the level of care was inadequate for NIV. Up-to-date features: now she is 69 years old and uses DPS 24 h. Unfortunatley, the long stay residence where she is cared for is unable to guarantee the necessary maneuvers to prevent derecruitment and a gradual loss of tidal volume has been observed. Despite that, a satisfactory compromise was reached: use of the DPS with speaking valve during periods spent in the wheelchair in order to allow maximum autonomy by reducing the risk of respiratory complications and use in DPS assisted pressure ventilation for the rest of the day, protecting against deconditioning and ensuring safety.

Case 11: male, bulbar lesion and medulla oblungata ischemia, 6-year follow-up, now 24 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: male, who at 17 years old developed neck pain, upper limb paresthesia, headache, respiratory arrest; early tracheostomy, no respiratory drive and 24 h dependence on IMV. He was first discharged to a Rehabilitation Unit and then to home. Thirteen months later the implant took place. Intra and peri-operative complications: pneumothorax with the need for drainage and subsequent complete resolution. After 18 days, he went back home. Intraoperative stimulation showed a very poor response. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient gained 1 h with DPS, with open cannula. After 1 year, the patients obtained 2 h of DPS use, with difficult in reconditioning, low tidal volumes during stimulation and fatigue. It was decided to use DPS triggered assisted pressure ventilation 24/24 h to increase the diaphragmatic force and endurance. Up-to-date features: now he is 24 years, he uses DPS with ventilator 24/24 h, plus trials with only DPS have been introduced. In both settings no desaturations, no abdominal—thoracic paradox. In DPS triggered assisted pressure ventilation a good speech, no fatigue, good synchrony DPS - ventilator. Our future’s plan would have been to increase periods with only DPS reducing the pressure support during the use in triggered assisted pressure ventilation. Unfortunately, the family’s severe psychological problems and extreme cultural poverty led to the family's estrangement and the interruption of all contact. In this case, the family’s critical situation overlapped with the clinical difficulties, given the unknown etiology of the cSCI and the severe bulbar and cognitive impairment.

Case 12: male, Pompe disease, 7-year follow-up, died once 7 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: he was a child with diagnosis of Pompe disease when he was 3 months years old, tracheostomy (3 years old) and gradually dependent on ventilator 24/24 h. He was proposed for DPS and the implant took place in 4 years later without intra and post-operative complications. The program was based on the protocol for children with Pompe Disease of the University of Florida [10, 11] with the aim of preserving diaphragm from total deconditioning during enzyme therapy. Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient used DPS in triggered assisted pressure ventilation 24/24 h with different levels of pressure support and he used DPS with CPAP in two trials/day. After 3 years, the follow-up showed the maintenance of the diaphragmatic functionality without further deterioration. Up-to-date features: he died in 2022 due to an acute cardiac dysfunction. In this patients, the DPS system allowed the family to manage a very serious illness with a poor prognosis more safely and with greater peace of mind. Caregiving was also easier for the secondary caregivers, allowing them to dedicate more time to recreational and social activities.

Case 13: male, Arnold Chiari I syndrome, 9-year follow-up, now 52 years old

Acute phase and DPS evaluation: male with Arnold Chiari Syndrome. At 36 years old, he underwent a surgery for cervical decompression with subsequent motor deterioration up to quadriplegia. Three years later, he became more and more dependent on NIV and on MIE, therefore 7 years post-surgery he was evaluated for the implant of DPS. Although the conduction of the phrenic nerves was poor as well as the diaphragm response, the wills of the patient to avoid tracheostomy brought to the implant (August 2016). Plateau phase: after 6 months, the patient experienced an increase of 2 h using DPS while in the wheelchair, NIV during the night, and a mouthpiece during the day. After 2 years, the patient was able to use DPS for 4 h and in the remaining 20 h he was managed with NIV and mouthpiece [3–5]. This increased autonomy allowed the patient to continue working as a lawyer, both at home and in court. However, after further 3 years, the patient’s condition worsened, resulting in weight loss, dysphagia, volume reduction with stimulator, and a new diagnosis of motor neuron disease. The patient’s disease progression necessitated a tracheostomy, due to dysphagia, and percutaneous endoscopic gastrotomy. Up-to-date features: despite being unable to communicate verbally, the patient continues to work as a lawyer from home using a computer. He uses DPS without a ventilator only for short periods. The onset of upper motor neuron disease interrupted the process of achieving maximum autonomy with DPS. This also adds to the safety in the event of disconnection from the MV and facilitates periodic replacement of the tracheal cannula, which is performed at home.

Discussion

The 14 years follow-up study describes the histories of 13 patients who underwent DPS for a median of 9 years. Five patients have benefited of at least 12 h of ventilator liberation and no history of respiratory events such as pneumonia or acute respiratory failure. Eleven patients on 13 (85%) (including the patient in who the DPS was never used) appreciated an improvement of the quality of life. In seven of them (7/13, 53.8%) the improvement never declined along the several years, while the perceived quality of life of the thirteen patients was, at its best level, reported as excellent or good.

Among the limitations of this study is the lack of comparison in the same center and in the same period of patients who continued on MV because they had a similar lesion or because they were evaluated but excluded from DPS implantation. In our case series, the use of DPS seems to be associated with a disappearance of atelectasis and pulmonary infectious complications in accordance with the physiological findings described by Wijkstra et al. [12] on a large sample 46 patients 13 of them patients were implanted in Niguarda and are described in this paper. In other studies, DPS, compared to MV, seems to be associated with a reduction in the amount of diaphragmatic atrophy, secretion volume, barotrauma, posterior atelectasis, tracheomalacia and infections [12–15].

Our study also confirm the long-term functioning of the electronic system over the years, consistent with Monden’s work [13]. Concerning the patients quality of life, while here each individual trajectory was reported in a narrative way, previous studies analyzed in detail the reason comparing the quality of life of patients with DPS in respect to those with MV. In such studies [14, 16], a greater possibility of moving and traveling, avoidance of fan noise, less use of caregivers during daily activities, a restoration of the sense of smell and above all an improvement in the image of themselves. In the present case series, the best quality of life level achieved seems highly comforting, placing itself between excellent and good. In patients narratives the use of DPS seem to allow for the safe resumption of everyday activities and experiences, offering a new way of experiencing their disability, extending the period during which the person with SCI can reintegrate into their social context. For a defined period, the patient can take an active role in managing their own disability. This empowerment provides the opportunity for individuals to regain partial control over a body that may no longer respond to commands and, in some cases, may no longer be perceived as their own. Inevitably, due to the long-term follow-up observation, some patients experienced, as expected, a decline in the quality of life they had gained thanks to DPS positioning, often after an initial improvement. The overall implications of the results could be of interest for clinicians and policymakers.

Conclusions

The long-term follow-up of 13 patients with DPS suggests that the implant could be a potential long-term alternative to IMV, particularly in terms of quality of life. Patient stories demonstrate that there are many factors that contribute to improving the quality of life, and DPS is one of them. In conclusion, DPS does not eliminate the disability but allows for more independent years of life with a greater possibility of social reintegration.
