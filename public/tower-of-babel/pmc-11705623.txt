Incidence of infective endocarditis in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Somyata Somendra, Saurabh Mehrotra, Parag Barwad, Himanshu Gupta, Ajay Bahl
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on the incidence of infective endocarditis (IE) in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is sparse. Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is an uncommon complication of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Data on the incidence, risk factors and precipitating factor is sparse especially from India. A multicentre study reported the incidence to be 1.4 per 1000 patient years.1 However, IE is associated with high morbidity and mortality in HCM patients.2,3 We have a cohort of HCM patients over 20 years with long-term follow up data. This study evaluates this HCM cohort with the aim to study the incidence, clinical profile and outcomes of IE in these patients (see Fig. 1).

Methods

All patients entering the HCM cohort from May 2003 to June 2022 of a tertiary care hospital and had least one follow-up visit were included and followed up till June 2023. This was a retrospective cohort analysis. The protocol was in accordance with the Helsinki Convention and was approved by the institute ethics committee. Individuals were diagnosed with HCM when echocardiography identified unexplained left ventricle hypertrophy ≥13 mm or >2 standard deviations corrected for body surface area for children and adolescents. Patients were sub-grouped on presence or absence of left ventricular outflow tract or mid-cavity gradients. Patients with obstructive HCM were those with a left ventricular outflow tract or mid-cavity Doppler gradient or both ≥30 mm Hg on echocardiography at rest or on provocation. Others formed the non-obstructive group.

Only individuals who were diagnosed with IE after entry into the cohort were included. Patients with IE diagnosed prior to recruitment into the cohort were excluded from the analysis. Diagnosis of IE was confirmed from the records. IE was diagnosed on the basis of clinical criteria with transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiographic evidence of vegetation, in the presence of clinical features consistent with IE. Risk factors, clinical profile and outcomes of these patients were studied.

Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Incidence of IE was estimated as the number of patients who developed IE during follow-up divided by the total number of person-years of observation accumulated during follow-up in the study population or in each patient subgroup. The incidence of IE in different patient subgroups was compared by use of the chi-square test. All tests were 2-tailed. The 95 % confidence limits of rates were calculated using the Poisson distribution. Follow-up times were calculated from the date of the first patient enrolment in the cohort to the time of the most recent evaluation.

Results

The study cohort consisted of 529 HCM patients with a total follow up duration of 3244.6 years. The mean follow-up duration was 6.1 ± 4.7 years. The median follow-up duration was 5.3 (range 31 days to 20.1) years The mean age at entry into the HCM cohort was 43.8 ± 16.0 years (range 9 months–82 years). This was largely an adult cohort with only 38 (7.2 %) patients being <18 years of age. Of these only 4 (0.76 %) were <10 years of age at entry into the cohort. 147 (27.8 %) were females. Baseline characteristics are mentioned in Table 1. 229 patients had significant obstruction at rest or on provocation. Of these 175 had left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) while 54 only had mid-cavity gradient.

Characteristics of the three patients with infective endocarditis.

None of the patients underwent a procedure that may have warranted IE prophylaxis. Two patients had vegetations on the mitral valve (one had LVOTO while 1 was nonobstuctive) and one on the aortic valve (had LVOTO). Only one patient had a positive blood culture which grew alpha hemolytic Streptococcus.

Both patients with mitral valve endocarditis were left with severe mitral regurgitation and did not undergo mitral valve surgery. One died of heart failure and shock 12 days after completing the antibiotic course. The second patient had central retinal artery occlusion prior to starting antibiotics. Subsequently she had progressive exertional breathlessness, recurrent transient ischemic attacks, ischemic stroke and multi-infarct dementia. She was on oral anticoagulant therapy and died of intracerebral bleed 6 years later. The third patient had an uncomplicated course and is alive 12 years after IE. He only had a mild residual aortic regurgitation.

In addition to the 3 patients who developed IE during follow up as part of the cohort, 2 additional patients had history of IE prior to entry into the cohort. The first patient had nonobstructive HCM with an infected pacemaker pocket. The single chamber pacemaker was explanted but the lead was left in situ and a new pacemaker implanted using an epicardial lead. 5 years later at the age of 44 years, he developed prolonged fever and vegetations on the lead left in situ. The patient was managed with antibiotics and lead extraction. He had residual moderate tricuspid regurgitation on follow up. The second patient was a 20 year old male who developed Klebsiella pneumoniae endocarditis with vegetations on mitral valve. He had not undergone any recent bacteremia procuring procedure. He developed severe mitral regurgitation and heart failure and died of progressive heart failure around 1 year later.

Discussion

Data on the incidence of IE in HCM patients is sparse with only one previous study which reported an incidence to be 1.4 per 1000 patient years.1 The incidence of IE in our study was only 0.92/1000 patient years. Thus, IE is a rare complication punctuating the course of HCM. Selection bias was eliminated since consecutive patients in the cohort meeting the inclusion criteria were included. Some patients were lost to follow up with 64 (12.1 %) patients having a follow up duration of less than 1 year. Being a tertiary care hospital our HCM patient cohort is likely to include more symptomatic patients than in the community.

However, when it occurs, IE is associated with serious sequelae. Two out of 3 patients developed severe valvular regurgitation, one of whom died of progressive heart failure and shock in the same hospitalization.4 If the current indications of valve replacement are considered, both would have been recommended mitral valve replacement. Long term complications included valvular regurgitation, embolic phenomenon and heart failure.

Vegetations have been reported in mitral as well as aortic valves and the left ventricular outflow tract in HCM patients.5,6 In our study, mitral valve was involved in two and aortic valve in 1 patient.

IE prophylaxis is no longer recommended for dental or other medical procures in HCM patients.7 None of the patients who developed IE had undergone a procedure that may have warranted consideration for IE prophylaxis.

Less than 10 % of our cohort had implanted cardioverter defibrillators (ICD)/pacemakers. The low use of device use was most commonly due to patient choice and economic factors. Only one patient had developed IE related to lead infection prior to entry into the cohort. HCM patients are often young and need multiple generator replacements. With higher ICD implantation rates, IE related to lead infections are likely to increase. This patient also highlights the risk of leaving behind leads in cases of pacemaker pocket infections. The patient had intermittent discharge from pocket site and developed IE 5 years later.

To conclude IE is a rare complication in HCM patients with an incidence of 0.92/1000 patient years. However, when it occurs, IE is associated with high morbidity and mortality.
