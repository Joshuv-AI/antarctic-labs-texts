Introduction

1.1
Main results

In a talk at the July 2024 Italy-France Analytic Number Theory Workshop in Genoa, Ramaré asked the following question: is the prime exponential sum

\pi(x;\alpha):=\sum_{p\leq x}\mathrm{e}(\alpha p)

(where we use the standard notation
\mathrm{e}(\beta):=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\pi\beta}
and the sum is on primes
p
) unbounded for all
\alpha
?

The study of this exponential sum (or the similar
\psi(x;\alpha):=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\mathrm{e}(\alpha n)
) dates back to Vinogradov
[
9
, §9]
, who proved the first non-trivial
upper bound
on
\psi(x;\alpha).

Theorem 1

(Vinogradov
[
9
]
, §9, Theorem 3)
.

Define for
\alpha\in\mathds{R}/\mathds{Z}
and
x\geq 2,

R(x,\alpha):=\min\left\{R>0:\exists\,1\leq q\leq R,(a,q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{R}{qx}\right\}.

(1)

Then when
\varepsilon>0
is fixed, we have uniformly in
\alpha\in\mathds{R}/\mathds{Z}
and
x\geq 2,

\psi(x;\alpha)\ll\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{R(x,\alpha)^{1/2}}+x^{4/5+\varepsilon}.

Remark
.

By Dirichlet’s theorem, we always have
R(x,\alpha)\leq x^{1/2}
and, for algebraic irrational
\alpha,
we have
R(x,\alpha)\gg x^{1/2-\varepsilon}.

When
R(x,\alpha)
is very small, we say
\alpha
is in a major arc and the study of
\psi(x;\alpha)
reduces to the study of primes in arithmetic progressions mod
q
(where
q
comes from (
1
)). When
R(x,\alpha)
is large (close to its typical value
\asymp x^{1/2}
), Vinogradov’s theorem gives an upper bound
\psi(x;\alpha)\ll x^{4/5+\varepsilon}.

However, much less is known regarding
lower bounds
for
\psi(x;\alpha)
when
R(x;\alpha)
is large. The most significant lower bound result is that of Vaughan
[
8
]
, who showed the
L^{1}
average lower bound

\int_{0}^{1}\big|\psi(x;\alpha)\big|\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\gg x^{1/2}

(note that we trivially have the
L^{2}
average
\int_{0}^{1}\big|\psi(x;\alpha)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\asymp x\log x
developing the square and using the prime number theorem). However, no non-trivial lower bound is known for fixed
\alpha.
A closely related result was proven in a recent preprint of Pylaiev and Tyrrell
[
7
]
, who showed the exponential sum
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\mathrm{e}(\alpha n)
is unbounded for a large class of multiplicative functions
f,
without providing an explicit lower bound.

Our main result proves the prime exponential sum has no better than square root cancellation on short intervals.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
and
\varepsilon>0.
Then for
y\leq x^{1/3-\varepsilon},
we have

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\gg y\log x,

where the implied constant depends only on
\varepsilon.

The following corollary then answers Ramaré’s question.

Corollary 3
.

For all
\varepsilon>0,
we have uniformly in
\alpha\in\mathds{R},

\sup_{n\leq x}\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|\gg x^{1/6-\varepsilon}

and

\sup_{n\leq x}\big|\pi(n;\alpha)\big|\gg x^{1/6-\varepsilon}.

It is well known Diophantine approximations of
\alpha
play a key role in the behaviour of
\psi(x;\alpha),
as can be seen by the role of
R(x,\alpha)
in Theorem
1
. Similarly, when we have a Diophantine approximation of suitable height (which happens when
R(y,\alpha)\asymp y^{1/2}
) and assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) for all Dirichlet
L
-functions, we can improve on Theorem
2
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume GRH. Fix
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
and
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0.
Then for
y\leq x^{5/9-\varepsilon}
such that we have a rational approximation
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
for some irreducible fraction
\frac{u}{s}
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}s^{2}\leq y\leq s^{2}/12,
we have

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\gg y\log x,

where the implied constant depends only on
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Corollary 5
.

Assume GRH. Then for all irrational
\alpha,
we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{-5/18+\varepsilon}\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|>0.

We note that the assumption on
\alpha
in Theorem
4
plays a key role in going beyond the
x^{1/3}
barrier of Theorem
2
, as discussed in Section
2.2
. However, when this condition is verified, we do not need an assumption as strong as GRH to get beyond
y=x^{1/3},
even though the best known character sum estimates barely fail to improve the range of
y.

We conclude this section by noting we expect our method to be able to give lower bounds on exponential sums
\sum_{n<m\leq n+y}f(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)
for a wide class of arithmetic functions
f.
We showcase here the case of the divisor function
\tau,
where we are able to reach very large values of
y.
We prove general results (infra Theorem
8
and Proposition
10
) developing a new method to prove a lower bound of exponential sums. In this article we apply them for
\Lambda
and
\tau
. In following papers we will apply them to the case of products of
k
primes (
f=\mathbb{1}_{\Omega(n)=k}
), as well as the case of
f=\mu^{2}
, where the exponential sum does have better than square-root cancellation.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
and
\varepsilon>0.
Then there is an absolute constant
C
such that for
y\leq Y:=x\left(\frac{\log\log x}{C\log x}\right)^{2},
whenever we have a rational approximation
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
for some irreducible fraction
\frac{u}{s}
with
\varepsilon s^{2}\leq y\leq s^{2}/12,
we have

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\tau(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\gg y\big(\log(x/y)\big)^{2}\log(Y/y),

where the implied constant depends only on
\varepsilon.
In particular, for all irrational
\alpha,
we have

\limsup_{x\to\infty}x^{-1/2}(\log x)(\log\log x)^{-2}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)\mathrm{e}(\alpha n)\right|>0.

For comparison, Goldston and Pandey
[
2
]
have shown the
L^{1}
lower bound

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)\mathrm{e}(\alpha n)\right|\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\gg x^{1/2},

but no non-trivial lower bound for fixed irrational
\alpha
is known (note however than the exponential sum is trivially unbounded as
\tau
is unbounded).

1.2
Notation

We fix
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
and
f
an arithmetic function, which will later be taken to be
\Lambda
or
\tau.
We define the exponential sum

F(x;\alpha):=\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\mathrm{e}(\alpha n),

as well as
F(x):=F(x;0)=\sum_{n\leq x}f(n).
For
f=1,
we note

E_{x}(\alpha):=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\mathrm{e}(\alpha n).

We then define the sums on short intervals,

F(n,n+y;\alpha):=F(n+y;\alpha)-F(n;\alpha)=\sum_{n<m\leq n+y}\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)f(m).

When
f=\Lambda,
we note
\psi
instead of
F.

Finally, when
Q,y<x
are fixed, we define

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(Q,y):=\left\{\frac{a}{q}:q\leq Q,(a,q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{6y}\right\}

(2)

the set of rational approximations of
\alpha
of height
Q
with precision
1/y,
which plays a key role in our estimates.

Throughout the paper, the implicit constants from the
\ll
and
O()
notations may depend on constants
\varepsilon
and
C
when relevant, but not on
\alpha.

1.3
Outline of the proofs

Our core idea is to look at the behaviour of the exponential sum in a random short interval. Indeed, fixing
f
supported on
[1,x],
we expect
F(n,n+y;\alpha)
to behave like
F(n,n+y;a/q)
whenever
\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\ll 1/y,
and are able to control the error terms on average as soon as
q
is small in terms of
x.
Thus, instead of requiring
R(x,\alpha)
to be small, we only need
R(y,\alpha)
to be small (in terms of
x
), which can be ensured by taking
y
small enough.

Our tool to estimate the average
\sum_{n}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}
is the large sieve (reminded below), which when applied to
n\mapsto F(n,n+y;\alpha)
gives us a lower bound on this average based on the value of its Fourier transform at chosen points.

Theorem 7

(Montgomery-Vaughan large sieve,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
M,N\in\mathds{Z},N\geq 0,

f
supported on
[M+1,M+N],
and
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathds{R}/\mathds{Z}
a finite set, with

\delta:=\min_{\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}|\beta-\beta^{\prime}|.

Then

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\big|F(M,M+N;\beta)\big|^{2}\leq\big(N+\delta^{-1}\big)\sum_{n=M+1}^{M+N}\big|f(n)\big|^{2}.

(3)

We can now state and prove our main inequality from which we derive Theorems
2
,
4
and
6
. It will also be used in forthcoming papers to obtain lower bounds on the exponential sums over products of
k
primes and squarefree integers.

Theorem 8
.

Let
f
supported on
[1,x].
Then for
y\leq x
and
Q\leq x^{1/2},
we have

\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}\gg\frac{y^{2}}{x}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\big|F(x;a/q)\big|^{2},

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(Q,y)
is defined by (
2
).

Proof.

We fix
g:n\mapsto F(n,n+y;\alpha),
which is supported on
[-y+1,x-1]
and whose Fourier transform is given by

G(-y,x-1;\beta):=F(x;\alpha+\beta)E_{y}(-\beta).

Applying Theorem
7
to
g
and

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{\frac{a}{q}-\alpha:q\leq Q,(a,q)=1\right\}

(which verifies
\delta\geq Q^{-2}
) thus gives

\displaystyle\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+Q^{2}}\sum_{q\leq Q}\sum_{(a,q)=1}\big|F(x;a/q)\big|^{2}\left|E_{y}(\alpha-a/q)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{y^{2}}{x}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\big|F(x;a/q)\big|^{2}

as
y\leq x,Q\leq x^{1/2}
and
E_{y}(\beta)\gg y\mathbb{1}_{|\beta|\leq 1/6y}.

∎

We now need to decide the relation between
y
and
Q.
When
y=Q^{1-\varepsilon},
we can find major arcs
\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}
for every denominator
6y\leq q\leq Q.
In this case we are able to estimate the right-hand side of (
4
) using a Bombieri-Vinogradov type estimate for exponential sums. In the case of primes (Theorem
2
), we use the following estimate by Liu and Zhan (note that we actually only need the case
\lambda=0
).

Theorem 9

(Liu and Zhan
[
4
, Theorem 3]
)
.

For all
A>0,
there is a constant
E=E(A)>0
such that for all
x\geq 3
and

Q\leq x^{1/3}(\log x)^{-E},\,\delta\leq Q^{-3}(\log x)^{-E},

we have

\sum_{q\leq Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}\max_{|\lambda|<\delta}\left|\psi\left(x;a/q+\lambda\right)-\frac{\mu(q)}{\phi(q)}E_{x}(\lambda)\right|\ll x(\log x)^{-A}.

Since Theorem
9
requires
Q\ll x^{1/3},
and only provides an accurate estimate of the right-hand side of (
4
) when
y\ll Q,
we need the bound
y\ll x^{1/3}
in Theorem
2
.

However, when
Q\ll y\ll Q^{2},
we still expect to find some fractions
\frac{a}{q}
with
q\leq Q
and
\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\ll\frac{1}{6y}.
This is why when we assume information on
all

F(x;a/q)
(given by GRH in Theorem
4
), we are able to obtain results for larger
y
such as Theorem
4
.

2
Major arcs in short intervals

2.1
The setting
y=Q^{1-\varepsilon}

When we consider major arcs of denominator up to
Q=y^{1+\varepsilon},
we get the following result, from which we derive Theorem
2
in Section
3
.

Proposition 10
.

Assume
f
is supported on
[1,x],
fix
Q\leq x^{1/2}
and assume there is a constant
C
and functions
g
and
\eta
(which may depend on
x
) with
\eta(q)\ll(\log q)^{C}
such that

\sum_{q\leq Q}|g(q)|\max_{(a,q)=1}\left|F(x;a/q)-x\frac{g(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{C}}

(5)

and for all
\varepsilon>0,

\sum_{Q^{1-\varepsilon}<q\leq Q}\frac{|g(q)|^{2}}{\phi(q)}\gg\frac{\log Q}{\eta(Q)}.

(6)

Then for
\varepsilon>0
and
y\leq Q^{1-\varepsilon},
we have

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}\gg\frac{y\log Q}{\eta(Q)}.

Remark
.

When
f=\Lambda,
we have (
5
) with
g=\mu
from Theorem
9
, so that (
6
) holds for
\eta=1.
When
f
is multiplicative, we expect
g\approx f*\mu
and (
6
) might only hold true for a larger
\eta.
An extreme case is
f=1,
for which
g=\mathbb{1}_{q=1}
and (
6
) does not hold. This is consistent with the fact the exponential sum
\sum_{n\leq x}\mathrm{e}(\alpha n)
is bounded in this case.

Proof of Proposition
10
.

For technical reasons, it will be useful to only consider major arcs with denominators
\gg y,
so we fix
Q_{0}:=Q^{1-\varepsilon/2}\geq y^{1+\varepsilon/2}
and define

\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q_{0}<q\leq Q,(a,q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{6y}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}.

(7)

Since
Q\leq x^{1/2},
we have by Theorem
8

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}x^{2}\frac{|g(q)|^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}-2x\frac{|g(q)|}{\phi(q)}\left|F(x;a/q)-x\frac{g(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|

\displaystyle=y^{2}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\frac{|g(q)|^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}-E,

(8)

with an error term

E:=\frac{2y^{2}}{x}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\frac{|g(q)|}{\phi(q)}\left|F(x;a/q)-x\frac{g(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|.

Now, since
Q_{0}\geq y^{1+\varepsilon/2},
we have for any
Q_{0}<q\leq Q,

\#\left\{a:(a,q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{6y}\right\}=\frac{\phi(q)}{3y}+O\big(2^{\omega(q)}\big)\asymp\frac{\phi(q)}{y},

(9)

so that the error term is bounded by

E\ll\frac{y^{2}}{x}\sum_{Q_{0}<q\leq Q}\frac{\phi(q)}{y}\frac{|g(q)|}{\phi(q)}\max_{(a,q)=1}\left|F(x;a/q)-x\frac{g(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|\ll\frac{y}{(\log Q)^{C}}

(10)

using (
5
).

Moreover, by (
9
) and (
6
), the main term of (
8
) verifies

\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\frac{|g(q)|^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}=\sum_{Q_{0}<q\leq Q}\frac{|g(q)|^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}\frac{\phi(q)}{y}\gg\frac{1}{y\eta(Q)},

(11)

so that it follows from (
8
) and (
10
)

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\big|F(n,n+y;\alpha)\big|^{2}\gg\frac{y\log Q}{\eta(Q)}+O\left(\frac{y}{(\log Q)^{C}}\right)

as desired. The condition
\eta(Q)\ll(\log Q)^{C}
ensures the error term is small enough compared to the main term.
∎

2.2
The setting
y\gg Q

We now assume we are able to estimate
F(x;a/q)
for all
q
(rather than on average over
q
) and want to take
y\gg Q,
in order to prove Theorem
4
which allows for
y\gg x^{1/3}.
The first problem is that, in that case, there might actually be no major arc in the interval
\left[\alpha-\frac{1}{6y},\alpha+\frac{1}{6y}\right]
. Indeed, consider
\alpha=\frac{u}{s}+\frac{1}{2y},
with
s<\frac{y}{Q}.
Then, any fraction with denominator less than
Q
(other than
\frac{u}{s}
itself) is at least
\frac{1}{Qs}>\frac{1}{y}
away from
\frac{u}{s},
and thus at least
\frac{1}{2y}
away from
\alpha,
which means the set
\mathcal{A}
of major arcs defined in (
2
) is empty. This is the reason why we are not able to go beyond Theorem
2
without extra information on
\alpha.

However, for most choices of
\alpha
and
y,
the set
\mathcal{A}
is of the expected cardinality
\asymp Q^{2}/y
when
Q\gg y^{1/2+\varepsilon}.
Indeed, it follows from the lemma below that the set
\mathcal{A}
is of the expected size for
y
up to
O(Q^{2})
under the assumption of Theorem
4
, which occurs for some arbitrarily large values of
y
for all
\alpha.

Lemma 11
.

Assume
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
for some irreducible fraction
\frac{u}{s}.
Then there is an absolute constant
C
such that for
Q\geq Cs,
we have

\#\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q<q\leq 2Q,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{2}{s^{2}}\right\}\asymp\frac{Q^{2}}{s^{2}}.

Proof.

The upper bound is trivial from the fact distinct fractions
\frac{a}{q},\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
with
Q<q,q^{\prime}\leq 2Q
are always at least
\frac{1}{qq^{\prime}}\geq\frac{1}{4Q^{2}}
apart.
Now, since
(u,s)=1,
we can consider a Bézout relation
vs-tu=1
with
s\leq t<2s
(this is possible as
t
is defined modulo
s
). We then have
\left|\frac{u}{s}-\frac{v}{t}\right|=\frac{1}{st}\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}},
so that both
\frac{u}{s}
and
\frac{v}{t}
are less than
\frac{2}{s^{2}}
away from
\alpha.

We now consider

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{(a;b):\frac{Q}{s+t}<a,b\leq\frac{2Q}{s+t},(a,b)=1\right\}.

First, note that
(au+bv,as+bt)
divides both
(as+bt)v-(au+bv)t=a
and
(au+bv)s-(as+bt)u=b,
so that
(au+bv,as+bt)=1
when
(a,b)=1.

Moreover,
\frac{au+bv}{as+bt}
is always between
\frac{u}{s}
and
\frac{v}{t},
so that

\left\{\frac{au+bv}{as+bt}:(a;b)\in\mathcal{B}\right\}\subset\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q<q\leq 2Q,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{2}{s^{2}}\right\}.

The conclusion then follows from noting
|\mathcal{B}|\asymp\left(\frac{Q}{s+t}\right)^{2}\asymp Q^{2}/s^{2}
when
Q/s\geq C.

∎

Showing
\mathcal{A}
is of the right size is however not sufficient to obtain the main term of Theorem
4
. Indeed, when
f=\Lambda,
only the major arcs with
squarefree
denominators yield a significant contribution to the right-hand side of (
4
) since the expected contribution of a major arc
\frac{a}{q}
is proportional to
\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi^{2}(q)}.
We thus use in the proof of Theorem
4
a result of Heath-Brown, which shows we can find such major arcs with squarefree denominators in the desired interval when
y\ll Q^{5/3-\varepsilon}.
This explains the bound
y\ll x^{5/9-\varepsilon}
appearing in Theorem
4
, since we need
Q\ll x^{1/3-\varepsilon}.

Lemma 12

(Heath-Brown,
[
3
]
)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0.
Then for all irrational
\alpha,
there exists infinitely many rationals
\frac{a}{q}
such that
\mu^{2}(q)=1
and
\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq q^{-5/3+\varepsilon}.

More precisely, when we consider a Diophantine approximation
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
for some irreducible fraction
\frac{u}{s},
we have for any
Q\gg s^{6/5+\varepsilon},

\#\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q<q\leq 2Q,\mu^{2}(q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{2}{s^{2}}\right\}\asymp\frac{Q^{2}}{s^{2}}.

Finally, it turns out information on average on the error term such as (
5
) is too weak to deduce Theorem
4
, since
\mathcal{A}
is too sparse when
y\gg Q.
This is why we instead assume GRH in Theorem
4
, which allows us to estimate
\psi(x;a/q)
for all major arcs
a/q.

Proofs of the main results

In this section, we derive Theorem
2
from Proposition
10
, as well as Theorems
4
and
6
using Theorem
8
. We then derive Corollaries
3
and
5
from Theorems
2
and

.

3.1
The case of primes

We first prove Theorem
2
using Proposition
10
and Theorem
9
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Since Proposition
10
requires a function supported on
[1,x],
we consider
f=\Lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}_{[1,x]}.
Theorem
9
then tells us the bound (
5
) is verified for
Q=x^{1/3-\varepsilon/2}\geq y^{1+\varepsilon/2}
with
g:=\mu
(since in that case
|g(q)|\leq 1
), and the bound (
6
) is then verified with
\eta=1.
Then, by Proposition
10
, we obtain

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\gg y\log Q

as desired (the extra condition
1\leq m\leq x
comes from the fact we are considering
f=\Lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}_{[1,x]}
rather than
\Lambda.
)
∎

We now prove Theorem
4
. We use the following lemma, which gives us information on exponential sums in major arcs assuming GRH.

Lemma 13
.

Assume GRH. Then for all
q\leq x
and
(a,q)=1,
we have

\left|\psi(x;a/q)-x\frac{\mu(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|\ll(xq)^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}.

Proof.

We decompose the exponential sum into character sums. Defining the Gauss sum

G(\chi):=\sum_{(b,q)=1}\mathrm{e}(b/q)\chi(b),

we have when
n
is coprime with
q,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}(an/q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{(b,q)=1}\mathrm{e}(b/q)\sum_{\chi\,\mathrm{mod}\,q}\overline{\chi}(b)\chi(an)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\mathrm{mod}\,q}\chi(a)G\left(\overline{\chi}\right)\chi(n),

so that summing on
n
and noting the contribution of primes that are not coprime with
q
to
\psi(x;a/q)
is
\ll\omega(q)\cdot\log x\ll(\log x)^{2},
we deduce

\psi(x;a/q)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,\mathrm{mod}\,q}\chi(a)G\left(\overline{\chi}\right)\psi(x;\chi)+O\big((\log x)^{2}\big)

(12)

Now, we have
G(\chi)\ll q^{1/2}
for all
\chi
(
[
1
, chapter 9]
) and, under GRH, we know (
[
5
, Theorem 13.7]
)

\psi(x,\chi)=x\mathbb{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}+O\left(x^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}\right).

Injecting these estimates in equation (
12
), we get

\psi(x;a/q)=x\frac{G(\chi_{0})}{\phi(q)}+O\big(q^{1/2}x^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}\big)

as desired, as

G(\chi_{0})=\sum_{(b,q)=1}\mathrm{e}(b/q)=\mu(q).

∎

We can now prove Theorem
4
using Theorem
8
and Lemma
12
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We again consider
f=\Lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}_{[1,x]},
and fix
Q:=x^{1/3-\varepsilon/6}
in order to define
\mathcal{A}
as in (
2
). By Theorem
8
, we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}x^{2}\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi^{2}(q)}-2x\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi(q)}\left|\psi(x;a/q)-x\frac{\mu(q)}{\phi(q)}\right|

\displaystyle\gg y^{2}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi^{2}(q)}+O\left(\frac{y^{2}}{x}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{(xq)^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}}{\phi(q)}\right)

(13)

where the error term is bounded by Lemma
13
.

Now, since we assumed
\varepsilon^{\prime}s^{2}\leq y\leq s^{2}/12
for some rational approximation
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}},
we have by Lemma
12
for all
y^{3/5+\varepsilon/6}\leq Q^{\prime}\leq Q/2,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}\\
Q^{\prime}<q\leq 2Q^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi^{2}(q)}\geq\frac{1}{4Q^{\prime 2}}\#\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q^{\prime}<q\leq 2Q^{\prime},\mu^{2}(q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{2}{s^{2}}\right\}\gg\frac{1}{s^{2}}\asymp\frac{1}{y},

so that summing on dyadic intervals (as
y^{3/5+\varepsilon/6}\leq Qx^{-\varepsilon/6}
when
y\leq x^{5/9-\varepsilon}
) we obtain the main term of (
13
) satisfies

y^{2}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mu^{2}(q)}{\phi^{2}(q)}\gg y\log x.

Moreover, since the elements of
\mathcal{A}
are rationals of denominator
\leq Q,
the intervals
\left[\frac{a}{q}-\frac{1}{2qQ},\frac{a}{q}+\frac{1}{2qQ}\right]
do not overlap so that

\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{qQ}\leq\frac{1}{3y}.

Injecting this into the error term of (
13
), we find that the error term is
\ll yx^{-1/2+\varepsilon/6}Q^{3/2}
which is smaller than the main term with our choice of
Q:=x^{1/3-\varepsilon/6}.

∎

3.2
The case of the number of divisors

We now prove Theorem
6
. The reason why we are able to reach
y=x^{1-\varepsilon}
in this setting is because the exponential sum at rationals can be very well understood, as shown by the lemma below.

Lemma 14
.

For
1\leq q\leq x^{1/2}
and
a
with
(a,q)=1,
we have

\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}n\right)=\frac{x}{q}\Big(\log(x/q^{2})+2\gamma-1\Big)+O\left(x^{1/2}(1+\log q)\right).

Proof.

Writing
\tau(n)=\sum_{uv=n}1,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}n\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{mn\leq x}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}mn\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m\leq x^{1/2}}\sum_{m<n\leq x/m}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{am}{q}n\right)+\sum_{m\leq x^{1/2}}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}m^{2}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle:=2T+E.

The sum over squares
E
is trivially bounded by
O(x^{1/2})
(better bounds exist, but this one is sufficient for our purpose). Now, if
am\equiv b\,\mathrm{mod}\,q
with
-q/2<b\leq q/2,
the inner sum
\sum_{m<n\leq x/m}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{am}{q}n\right)
is
O(q/|b|)
unless
b=0
in which case it is
x/m-m+O(1).
Thus, since
a
is coprime with
q,
noting
\bar{a}
its inverse modulo
q
we have

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x^{1/2}\\
q|m\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{x}{m}-m+O(1)\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-q/2<b\leq q/2\\
b\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x^{1/2}\\
m\equiv\bar{a}b\,\mathrm{mod}\,q\end{subarray}}O\left(\frac{q}{|b|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{q}\Big(\log(x^{1/2}/q)+\gamma+O(q/x^{1/2})\Big)-\frac{q}{2}\left(\frac{x^{1/2}}{q}+O(1)\right)^{2}+O(x^{1/2}/q)+O\left(q\log q\cdot\frac{x^{1/2}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{q}\Big(\log(x^{1/2}/q)+\gamma-1/2\Big)+O\left(x^{1/2}(1+\log q)\right)

when
1\leq q\leq x^{1/2}.
Combining with (
14
) gives the desired result.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
6
using Theorem
8
and Lemma
11
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We fix
Q:=x^{1/2}\frac{\log\log x}{C_{1}\log x}
with
C_{1}
large enough so that Lemma
14
yields

\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}n\right)\asymp\frac{x}{q}\log(x/q^{2})

for
q\leq Q.
We then have by Theorem
8
applied to
f=\tau\cdot\mathbb{1}_{[1,x]}
and Lemma
14
,

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\tau(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\gg\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{x\log(x/q^{2})}{q}\right)^{2},

(15)

where
\mathcal{A}
is defined by (
2
).

We now cut the sum into dyadic intervals based on the value of
q.
Since we have a rational approximation
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
with
\frac{2}{s^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{6y}
and
s^{2}\asymp y,
we have by Lemma
11
an absolute constant
C_{2}
such that for
C_{2}y^{1/2}\leq Q^{\prime}\leq Q/2,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{q}\in\mathcal{A}\\
Q^{\prime}<q\leq 2Q^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{x\log(x/q^{2})}{q}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\gg\left(\frac{x\log(x/Q^{\prime 2})}{Q^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\#\left\{\frac{a}{q}:Q^{\prime}<q\leq 2Q^{\prime},(a,q)=1,\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{6y}\right\}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{x^{2}}{y}\big(\log(x/Q^{\prime 2})\big)^{2}.

The conclusion then follows from (
15
) when
y\leq Y:=(Q/4C_{2})^{2}
as the number of dyadic intervals verifying
C_{2}y^{1/2}\leq Q^{\prime}\leq Q/2
is then
\gg\log(Y/y).

∎

3.3
Proofs of Corollaries

We now prove Corollary
3
from Theorem
2
and Corollary
5
from Theorem
4
.

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Fix
y=x^{1/3-\varepsilon}.
We have for all
-y<n\leq x,

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|=\big|\psi\big(\min\{n+y,x\};\alpha\big)-\psi(n;\alpha)\big|\leq 2\sup_{n\leq x}\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|.

(16)

Taking the
L^{2}
norm, we get

\sup_{n\leq x}\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|\gg\left(\frac{1}{x}\sum_{-y<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\gg(y\log x)^{1/2}\gg x^{1/6-\varepsilon}

(17)

by Theorem
2
as desired.

To move on to
\pi(n,\alpha),
we note that we have for all
n\geq y^{2},

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<p\leq n+y\\
2\leq p\leq x\end{subarray}}(\log p)\mathrm{e}(\alpha p)+O\big((\log n)^{2}\big)=(\log n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<p\leq n+y\\
2\leq p\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}(\alpha p)+O\big((\log n)^{2}\big)

since there is at most one
p^{k}
in
(n,n+y]
for each
k\geq 2,
and
\log p=\log n+O(y/n)=\log n+O(1/y)
for
n<p\leq n+y.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sup_{n\leq x}\big|\pi(n;\alpha)\big|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{y^{2}\leq n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<p\leq n+y\\
1\leq p\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}(\alpha p)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\log x}\sup_{y^{2}\leq n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|+O\left(\log x\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\log x}\left(\frac{1}{x}\sum_{y^{2}<n\leq x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<m\leq n+y\\
1\leq m\leq x\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mathrm{e}(\alpha m)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+O\left(\log x\right)

\displaystyle\gg\frac{1}{\log x}\big(y\log x+O(y^{4}/x)\big)^{1/2}+O\left(\log x\right)

\displaystyle\gg x^{1/6-\varepsilon}

by Theorem
2
with
y=x^{1/3-\varepsilon}.

∎

We now prove Corollary
5
, where we assumed
\alpha
is irrational and GRH in order to use Theorem
4
.

Proof of Corollary
5
.

Since
\alpha
is irrational, we can find arbitrarily large
s
such that
\left|\alpha-\frac{u}{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}
for some irreducible fraction
\frac{u}{s}.
For such an
s,
fix
y=s^{2}/12
and
x=y^{9/5+\varepsilon}.
It then follows from (
16
) and Theorem
4
that for such
x,
we have
n\leq x
such that

\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|\gg(y\log x)^{1/2}\gg n^{5/18-\varepsilon}.

Moreover, we must have

n\geq\big|\psi(n;\alpha)\big|\gg(y\log x)^{1/2}\gg s,

which ensures
n
grows to infinity with
s.

∎
