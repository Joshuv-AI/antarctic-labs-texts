ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCancer metabolism has emerged as a critical area of study in cancer research. Dysregulated lipid metabolism has been observed in various cancer types, and this metabolic rewiring facilitates tumor growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance. Additionally, aberrant lipid metabolism is associated with poor patient prognosis.MethodsThis review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of cancer metabolism, with a specific focus on lipid metabolism/lipogenesis and its association with resistance to third‐generation epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR‐TKIs).ResultsLipid metabolism/lipogenesis provides cancer cells with energy substrates and building blocks for membrane biogenesis. In parallel, resistance to third‐generation EGFR‐TKIs has emerged as a major challenge in the treatment of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) driven by EGFR mutations.ConclusionUnderstanding the intricate connections between lipid metabolism/lipogenesis and resistance to third‐generation EGFR‐TKIs is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Targeting lipid metabolism pathways holds promise as a novel therapeutic approach to overcome treatment resistance and improve patient prognosis. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms and identify potential drug targets in lipid metabolism.

Introduction

In the past time, the scientific community has witnessed an unprecedented surge of interest and investigation into the intricate realm of cancer metabolism [1, 2, 3, 4]. This burgeoning field has unveiled a profound understanding of how cancer cells undergo a process called metabolic reprogramming, which constitutes a pivotal hallmark of malignancy [5, 6, 7, 8]. Among the myriad alterations that take place within cancer cell metabolism, special attention has been directed towards the transformations occurring in lipid metabolism and lipogenesis—the complex biochemical processes that govern the synthesis, storage, and utilization of fats within cells [9, 10, 11].

Lipid metabolism, a multifaceted orchestration of molecular events, encompasses the intricate pathways responsible for the breakdown, transport, and synthesis of lipids [12, 13, 14, 15]. These lipids serve as crucial components for cellular structure, energy storage, and signaling. In the context of cancer, aberrations in lipid metabolism have been identified as pivotal drivers in the relentless growth and survival of malignant cells [16, 17, 18]. The dysregulation of lipid metabolism not only fuels the uncontrolled proliferation of cancer cells but also bestows upon them a heightened adaptability and resilience, enabling them to thrive in adverse microenvironments.

Intriguingly, lipogenesis, a subset of lipid metabolism, refers to the de novo synthesis of fatty acids within cells. This process assumes paramount importance in cancer biology, as it provides cancer cells with the raw materials required for the construction of membranes, as well as the generation of signaling molecules crucial for their survival and proliferation. The perturbations in lipogenesis represent a pivotal target for therapeutic interventions aimed at disrupting the sustenance of cancer cells [19, 20, 21].

In the past years, people realized that the metabolic processes within cancer cells undergo significant changes, a phenomenon known as metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming is now considered a defining characteristic of cancer. One of the key aspects of this metabolic reprogramming involves alterations in lipid metabolism and lipogenesis. Lipid metabolism refers to the process by which lipids (fats) are broken down or synthesized for energy and other cellular functions [22, 23]. Lipogenesis, on the other hand, is the process of synthesizing fatty acids and triglycerides, which are crucial for cell growth and division. In cancer cells, these processes are often upregulated, providing the cells with the necessary energy and materials to proliferate rapidly.

This comprehensive review endeavors to shed light on a specific facet of cancer metabolism—the resistance that cancer cells develop against third‐generation EGFR‐TKIs. These innovative inhibitors, designed to target a specific molecular pathway implicated in cancer progression, have exhibited remarkable efficacy in certain contexts. However, the emergence of resistance poses a formidable challenge in clinical practice, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of the intricate interplay between cancer metabolism and therapeutic interventions. These drugs are designed to inhibit the activity of the EGFR, a protein that is often overexpressed in cancer cells and contributes to their uncontrolled growth. However, many cancer cells develop resistance to these drugs, rendering them ineffective. Understanding the mechanisms behind this resistance is crucial for the development of more effective cancer treatments and provides a comprehensive perspective on the evolving landscape of cancer research and therapeutic strategies.

Mechanisms of Lipid Metabolism in Cancer

Cancer Metabolism

Cancer cells manifest a distinct metabolic profile that sets them apart from normal cells [24, 25, 26]. This specialized phenotype is marked by a heightened affinity for glucose and an elevated rate of lactate production, even when ample oxygen is available—a phenomenon widely recognized in the scientific community as the Warburg effect [27, 28, 29, 30]. This intriguing metabolic shift represents a fundamental adaptation that confers a significant advantage to cancer cells in their unrelenting quest for growth and expansion.

The Warburg effect, named after the renowned scientist Otto Warburg who first observed it, entails a deviation from the typical metabolic behavior observed in healthy cells [31]. In ordinary circumstances, cells derive their energy predominantly through a process known as oxidative phosphorylation, which occurs within the mitochondria and relies on the efficient consumption of oxygen. However, in stark contrast, cancer cells exhibit a preference for a less efficient but expedited pathway, which involves glycolysis—a process that converts glucose into pyruvate, producing a modest amount of energy and, critically, leading to the generation of lactate as a byproduct [32, 33, 34].

What makes this metabolic reprogramming particularly intriguing is that it occurs even when oxygen levels are sufficient for oxidative phosphorylation to proceed optimally. This suggests that cancer cells deliberately opt for a less energetically efficient pathway. The underlying rationale for this metabolic shift lies in the urgent demand of cancer cells for rapid and sustained growth. By favoring glycolysis, cancer cells can quickly generate the essential energy and molecular building blocks needed for the synthesis of macromolecules such as nucleotides, proteins, and lipids—crucial components required for cellular replication and division [35].

Furthermore, this metabolic adaptation provides cancer cells with a strategic advantage in the competitive microenvironment of a tumor. By relying on glycolysis cancer cells can outcompete neighboring normal cells for limited resources, securing a metabolic niche that supports their relentless proliferation [36, 37, 38].

Tumor Metabolic Pathway

Tumor metabolism refers to the unique set of biochemical processes that occur within cancer cells to support their rapid growth and proliferation. Compared to normal cells, tumor cells exhibit altered metabolism, characterized by increased glucose uptake, aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), and upregulation of pathways like the pentose phosphate pathway and lipogenesis [27]. These metabolic changes enable cancer cells to meet the high energy demands required for sustained proliferation and survival. Understanding tumor metabolism is crucial for developing targeted therapies that disrupt cancer cell growth while minimizing harm to normal cells.

In summary, the Warburg effect epitomizes a striking metabolic alteration that sets cancer cells on a distinct trajectory. By exhibiting an elevated reliance on glycolysis, even in the presence of oxygen, cancer cells ensure a rapid and abundant supply of energy and biosynthetic precursors—vital resources that fuel their exponential growth. This metabolic reprogramming not only distinguishes cancer cells from their normal counterparts but also underscores the critical role of metabolism in the intricate biology of cancer.

Cancer cells are known to display a distinct metabolic phenotype, which is characterized by an increased uptake of glucose and the production of lactate. This occurs even when oxygen is present, a phenomenon that is referred to as the Warburg effect, named after the Nobel laureate Otto Warburg who first observed this in the early 20th century [31].

Under normal physiological conditions, cells generate energy through a process known as oxidative phosphorylation, a highly efficient process that occurs in the mitochondria when oxygen is present. However, Warburg observed that cancer cells prefer to generate energy through a less efficient process known as glycolysis, which involves the conversion of glucose into lactate [39]. This is surprising because this process is typically a feature of cells in low‐oxygen conditions.

Despite being less efficient, this shift to glycolysis provides several advantages to cancer cells. Firstly, glycolysis provides a rapid source of energy, which is crucial for the fast proliferation of cancer cells. Secondly, the by‐products of glycolysis can be used as building blocks for the synthesis of macromolecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids. This is essential for the production of new cells during cell division.

This metabolic reprogramming, therefore, equips cancer cells with the necessary energy and biosynthetic precursors to support their rapid proliferation. Understanding this unique metabolic phenotype of cancer cells is crucial as it provides potential targets for cancer therapy. For instance, drugs that can inhibit glucose uptake or lactate production could potentially slow down the growth of cancer cells [40, 41].

Lipid Metabolism and Lipogenesis in Cancer

Lipid metabolism in cancer refers to the altered regulation and dysregulation of lipid‐related processes in cancer cells [42]. Lipid metabolism encompasses the synthesis, breakdown, and transport of lipids in the body [43, 44, 45, 46]. In cancer, there are significant changes in lipid metabolism that contribute to the growth, survival, and progression of cancer cells [47, 48, 49].

Some key aspects of lipid metabolism in cancer include: Lipogenesis: Lipogenesis is the process of synthesizing lipids, including fatty acids and triglycerides [50, 51]. In cancer, there is often an upregulation of lipogenesis, leading to increased synthesis of lipids. This increased lipogenesis is driven by enhanced expression and activity of lipogenic enzymes, such as fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC), and ATP citrate lyase (ACLY) [52, 53]. Lipogenesis provides cancer cells with the necessary building blocks for membrane synthesis, energy production, and signaling molecules [54, 55, 56]. Lipid droplet accumulation: Cancer cells often exhibit an accumulation of lipid droplets (LDs), which are storage organelles for lipids. LDs serve as a reservoir of energy‐rich triglycerides that can be utilized during periods of nutrient deprivation or increased energy demand. The accumulation of LDs in cancer cells is associated with altered lipid metabolism and lipogenesis. Lipid signaling molecules: Lipids, such as phospholipids and sphingolipids, act as signaling molecules involved in various cellular processes. Dysregulated lipid metabolism in cancer can lead to altered production and signaling of these lipid molecules, affecting cell growth, survival, and migration [57, 58]. Lipid oxidation: While lipogenesis is often upregulated in cancer, there can also be alterations in lipid oxidation, the breakdown of lipids for energy production. Some cancer cells exhibit increased fatty acid oxidation to meet their energy demands. However, the balance between lipogenesis and lipid oxidation can vary depending on the specific cancer type and metabolic context.

The dysregulation of lipid metabolism in cancer is driven by various factors, including oncogenic signaling pathways, altered nutrient availability, and metabolic reprogramming [59, 60, 61]. Understanding the specific alterations in lipid metabolism in different types of cancer can provide insights into potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment. Targeting lipid metabolism in cancer is an active area of research, with the aim of developing novel therapeutic strategies to inhibit cancer cell growth and survival.

Lipogenesis in cancer refers to the increased synthesis of lipids (fatty acids and triglycerides) by cancer cells [62]. Cancer cells have altered metabolic pathways compared to normal cells, and one of the metabolic adaptations is increased lipogenesis lipogenesis. This increased lipogenesis provides cancer cells with the necessary building blocks for membrane synthesis, energy production, and signaling molecules.

Dysregulated signaling pathways: Oncogenic signaling pathways, such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR and AMPK, play a role in regulating lipogenesis in cancer [63, 64, 65]. Activation of these pathways can stimulate lipogenic enzyme expression and activity, promoting lipid synthesis. Altered nutrient availability: Cancer cells often experience nutrient deprivation due to the rapid proliferation and high metabolic demands. In response, cancer cells reprogram their metabolism to utilize alternative carbon sources, such as glutamine and acetate, for lipogenesis. Targeting lipogenesis in cancer has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy [66, 67, 68]. Inhibiting lipogenic enzymes or disrupting lipogenic signaling pathways can impair cancer cell growth and survival. However, further research is needed to fully understand the role of lipogenesis in cancer and develop effective therapeutic interventions.

This pathway is often upregulated in many cancer types to meet the high demand for lipids required for cell membrane formation, energy storage, and signaling molecules.

Fatty acid synthesis pathway: Cancer cells also acquire fatty acids from exogenous sources, such as the bloodstream or surrounding tissues. These fatty acids can be processed through the fatty acid synthesis pathway, where they are incorporated into LDs or cell membranes to support cell growth and survival [70].

Pathways of Resistance to EGFR Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors

The Discovery History of TKI Drugs

The discovery and evolution of TKI drugs for lung cancer treatment have greatly transformed the landscape of targeted therapy in oncology. Here is a brief overview of the history of TKIs in the context of lung cancer.

First Generation TKIs

The first generation of TKIs for lung cancer treatment, such as erlotinib (Tarceva) and gefitinib (Iressa), was developed in the early 2000s. These drugs target the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a key driver of lung cancer progression. EGFR is a member of the ERBB family, a classic cell surface signaling protein, and the most common mutated oncogene. It consists of an extracellular ligand binding domain, a hydrophobic transmembrane region anchored to the cell membrane, and a tyrosine kinase domain located within the cell. EGFR and EGF, TGF ligands combine to form homomorphic or heteromorphic dimers, activating the tyrosine kinase domain. ATP binds tightly to these domains and transmits signals to downstream signaling cascades. There are four downstream signaling pathways, including PLC/PKC, RAS/RAF/MEK/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT, respectively, as demonstrated in Figure 2. EGFR plays important roles in promoting cell survival, proliferation, differentiation, and inhibiting apoptosis by triggering the signal transductions above.

Overview of the EGFR structure, signaling, and functions. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) and transforming growth factor (TGF), the two ligands for the EGFR, cause the activation of dormant receptors through homo‐ or hetero‐dimerization. Once EGFR is activated, it will cause multiple downstream signaling cascades activation such as PLC/PKC pathway, RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway, PI3K/AKT signal pathway, and JAK/STAT pathway, thus promoting transcriptional activation, cell survival, proliferation, mitosis, differentiation, metastasis, and anti‐apoptosis.

The initial success of these drugs in a subset of patients with EGFR‐mutant lung cancer revolutionized the treatment of the disease [82].

Second Generation TKIs

With the rise of resistance mechanisms to first‐generation TKIs, second‐generation TKIs like afatinib (Gilotrif) and dacomitinib (Vizimpro) were developed to overcome these resistance mechanisms and improve treatment outcomes for patients [83]. Second‐generation TKIs have enhanced potency and broader activity against different EGFR mutations, making them valuable options in the management of lung cancer.

Third Generation TKIs

The most recent developments in TKI therapy for lung cancer involve third‐generation inhibitors, such as osimertinib (Tagrisso), which target specific EGFR mutations, including the T790M resistance mutation [84]. Third‐generation TKIs have shown remarkable efficacy in overcoming resistance to earlier generation inhibitors and improving outcomes in patients with advanced lung cancer.

The continuous evolution of TKIs in lung cancer therapy highlights the importance of targeted treatments in improving patient outcomes and quality of life. Ongoing research and clinical trials are focused on further optimizing TKI therapy, identifying new resistance mechanisms, and developing novel treatment strategies to address the challenges posed by lung cancer (Figure 3).

Resistance to Third Generation EGFR‐TKIs

EGFR‐TKIs are a class of targeted therapies that inhibit the activity of the EGFR, a protein that is often overexpressed or mutated in various types of cancer [85, 86, 87]. Despite their initial efficacy, the development of resistance to EGFR‐TKIs is a major clinical challenge. The mechanisms of resistance are multifaceted and include secondary EGFR mutations, activation of alternative signaling pathways, and alterations in cancer metabolism [88].

EGFR‐TKIs represent a class of precision therapies designed to specifically target and inhibit the activity of the EGFR. This receptor is a crucial protein implicated in various types of cancer, frequently exhibiting anomalies such as overexpression or mutations that drive aberrant cellular signaling pathways [89, 90, 91]. The development of EGFR‐TKIs marked a significant milestone in cancer treatment, offering a promising strategy to halt the uncontrolled growth and proliferation driven by aberrant EGFR activity.

Initially, EGFR‐TKIs exhibit commendable effectiveness in suppressing the oncogenic potential of EGFR, leading to considerable clinical benefits for patients [92, 93, 94]. However, a formidable challenge arises in the form of acquired resistance, a phenomenon that limits the sustained efficacy of these targeted therapies. This development of resistance represents a critical hurdle in clinical practice, prompting intensive research to unravel the underlying mechanisms.

The mechanisms governing resistance to EGFR‐TKIs are intricate and multifaceted, reflecting the complex nature of cancer biology. One prominent avenue through which resistance emerges is the acquisition of secondary mutations within the EGFR gene itself [95, 96, 97]. These secondary mutations, distinct from the primary alterations that initially drove cancer progression, confer a structural change in the EGFR protein, rendering it less susceptible to the inhibitory effects of TKIs. This evolutionary adaptation equips cancer cells with a molecular workaround, enabling them to circumvent the therapeutic blockade.

For example, the research by Zhen Pan, Kai Wang, and others shows that Lovastatin effectively overcomes the resistance of EGFR TKIs by reducing cholesterol levels and XCT790 by inhibiting ERR α expression [98]. In addition to secondary mutations within EGFR, an alternative strategy employed by cancer cells to evade the effects of EGFR‐TKIs involves the activation of alternative signaling pathways [99]. These alternative pathways bypass the inhibited EGFR pathway, allowing cancer cells to sustain their growth and survival even in the presence of the targeted therapy. This phenomenon underscores the remarkable adaptability of cancer cells and emphasizes the need for a comprehensive understanding of the intricate network of signaling cascades within the cell.

Furthermore, alterations in cancer metabolism have emerged as a significant contributor to resistance against EGFR‐TKIs [100]. The metabolic reprogramming observed in cancer cells, including changes in glucose metabolism and the utilization of alternative energy sources, can confer a survival advantage. These metabolic adaptations may provide cancer cells with the necessary resources and energy to withstand the inhibitory effects of EGFR‐TKIs, further complicating therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, while EGFR‐TKIs have revolutionized cancer treatment by targeting the aberrant EGFR signaling pathway, the emergence of resistance poses a formidable challenge. The mechanisms underlying this resistance are diverse, involving secondary mutations in EGFR, activation of alternative signaling pathways, and alterations in cancer metabolism. Understanding these intricate mechanisms is crucial for devising innovative therapeutic strategies to overcome resistance and improve outcomes for cancer patients.

Despite the initial effectiveness of EGFR‐TKIs in treating cancers with overexpressed or mutated EGFR, a major clinical challenge is the development of resistance to these drugs. These are additional mutations in the EGFR that prevent the EGFR‐TKIs from effectively binding to and inhibiting the receptor. Another mechanism of resistance is the activation of alternative signaling pathways. These are other growth‐promoting pathways in the cell that can compensate for the inhibited EGFR activity and continue to drive the growth and survival of the cancer cells.

Alterations in cancer metabolism also contribute to the resistance to EGFR‐TKIs [104, 105, 106]. For instance, cancer cells may increase their reliance on other metabolic pathways for energy and biosynthesis, reducing their dependence on the EGFR pathway and thereby diminishing the effectiveness of EGFR‐TKIs.

EGFR‐TKIs belong to a specific category of cancer treatments known as targeted therapies [114, 115, 116]. Unlike traditional chemotherapy, which indiscriminately kills rapidly dividing cells, targeted therapies are designed to interfere with specific molecules that contribute to the growth and survival of cancer cells. EGFR‐TKIs, as their name suggests, are designed to inhibit the activity of the EGFR. EGFR is a protein that resides on the cell surface and is involved in signaling pathways that regulate cell growth, survival, and differentiation [117, 118]. When a molecule known as a growth factor binds to EGFR, it activates the receptor, triggering a cascade of events inside the cell that ultimately leads to cell proliferation and survival [119, 120].

In many types of cancer, the EGFR is often overexpressed or mutated [121]. Overexpression refers to the condition where the EGFR gene is amplified, leading to the production of abnormally high amounts of the EGFR protein [122]. On the other hand, EGFR mutations often result in a receptor that is constitutively active, meaning it is always turned on, even in the absence of growth factors. Both overexpression and mutation of EGFR can lead to uncontrolled cell growth, a hallmark of cancer.

EGFR‐TKIs work by binding to the EGFR and blocking its activation, thereby inhibiting the downstream signaling pathways that promote cell growth and survival [123, 124]. By doing so, these drugs can slow down or even halt the growth of cancer cells. This makes EGFR‐TKIs a valuable tool in the treatment of cancers that are driven by overexpressed or mutated EGFR. However, the development of resistance to these drugs remains a significant challenge in cancer treatment, necessitating ongoing research to improve their efficacy.

Abnormal lipid metabolism and lipid production can play a dual role in the process of TKI (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) resistance, which may be the cause of resistance or one of the side effects after resistance, which has sparked widespread discussion in the academic community.

On one hand, studies have shown that abnormal lipid metabolism and fat production may be closely related to TKI resistance mechanisms. Some studies suggest that drug‐resistant tumor cells generally have higher lipid metabolism activity, which may promote tumor cell tolerance to TKIs [141]. Abnormal lipid metabolism can alter intracellular lipid content and synthesis pathways, thereby affecting cellular signaling pathways, including signal transduction of TKI targets, leading to drug failure.

On the other hand, TKI treatment itself may also cause abnormal lipid metabolism and fat production, which may be one of the side effects of the drug. Some TKI drugs may interfere with the metabolic pathways of normal cells, including lipid synthesis and metabolism. This drug‐induced lipid metabolism abnormality may lead to a series of adverse reactions, including abnormal fat production, high cholesterol, high triglycerides, etc., which in turn affect the quality of life and drug resistance risk of patients [142, 143].

Therefore, abnormal lipid metabolism and fat production in TKI treatment may be both the cause of drug resistance and one of the side effects of drug therapy itself. The current research exploration is not clear, and further research is needed to explore. It is very important for tumor patients to monitor lipid metabolism and fat metabolism during TKI treatment, and adjust treatment plans in a timely manner.

These findings highlight the complex interplay between lipid metabolism and drug resistance in cancer. They suggest that targeting lipid metabolism, in addition to EGFR, could potentially enhance the effectiveness of EGFR‐TKIs and overcome drug resistance [144]. However, more research is needed to fully understand these mechanisms and to develop safe and effective strategies for targeting lipid metabolism in cancer.

Conclusion

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells serves as a critical linchpin in cancer progression and its therapeutic resistance. The orchestration of these metabolic alterations, particularly in lipid metabolism and lipogenesis, exerts a profound impact on the behavior and adaptability of cancer cells, thereby supporting their uncontrolled proliferation and survival—key hallmarks of cancer biology.

Recognition of these metabolic shifts not only deepens our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying cancer development but also holds great promise for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. By identifying specific vulnerabilities within cancer cell metabolic pathways, researchers may uncover a wealth of potential targets for innovative treatments, which could herald a new era in cancer therapeutics and revolutionize the management of this devastating disease.

In addition, in the context of developing resistance to EGFR‐TKI, the study of metabolic changes is of greater significance, which is also a huge challenge faced by clinical doctors and researchers at present. Addressing this challenge requires a comprehensive understanding of the subtle metabolic differences underlying drug resistance, as well as the exploration of deeper mechanisms, as well as the exploration of deeper molecular mechanisms, which may facilitate the prevention or overcoming of drug resistance. This, in turn, could enhance the efficacy of EGFR‐TKIs and improve the prognosis of patients with drug‐resistant tumors.

Looking forward, future research endeavors should focus on unraveling the intricate interplay between cancer metabolism and therapeutic resistance. This complex relationship is likely to yield further insights into the vulnerabilities of cancer cells and potential avenues for therapeutic intervention. It represents an exciting frontier in oncology research, offering hope for breakthroughs that could reshape the landscape of cancer treatment and ultimately improve the lives of countless individuals affected by this formidable disease.

In conclusion, cancer cell metabolic reprogramming characterized by significant alterations in cellular metabolic processes plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and the development of therapeutic resistance. This reprogramming includes alterations in lipid metabolism and lipogenesis, the process of synthesizing lipids.

Lipids serve multiple functions in cells, acting as energy sources, signaling molecules, and building blocks for cellular structures. In cancer cells, the processes of breaking down and synthesizing lipids are often upregulated, providing the cells with the necessary energy and materials to proliferate rapidly. Moreover, certain lipids can activate signaling pathways that promote cell survival, contributing to the resistance to therapies such as EGFR‐TKIs.

Understanding these metabolic alterations in cancer cells could potentially open up new avenues for cancer treatment. For instance, drugs could be developed to target key enzymes in lipid metabolism or lipogenesis, thereby disrupting the growth and survival of cancer cells. Alternatively, strategies could be devised to counteract the survival signaling pathways activated by certain lipids, enhancing the effectiveness of existing therapies like EGFR‐TKIs.

Future research in this field should aim to further elucidate the complex interplay between cancer metabolism and therapeutic resistance. This involves not only a deeper understanding of the metabolic processes within cancer cells but also how these processes interact with various signaling pathways and therapeutic agents. Such research could potentially lead to the development of more effective strategies for overcoming resistance to cancer therapies, ultimately improving the prognosis for cancer patients.
