Lipids and Lipid Metabolites in the Diagnosis, Risk Prediction and Treatment of Chronic Kidney Disease and Acute Kidney Injury: A Narrative Review
Authors: Stefan Erfurt, Johanna Gruzla, Karsten-Henrich Weylandt, Oliver Ritter, Meike Hoffmeister, Daniel Patschan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) represent the principal nephrological conditions encountered globally. Despite the diverse etiologies and pathogeneses associated with these disorders, both are characterized by disruptions in lipid metabolism, largely due to the frequent involvement of inflammatory mechanisms in the onset and progression of acute and chronic renal dysfunction. This review will examine two key areas: the diagnostic and prognostic relevance of lipids and their metabolites, particularly fatty acids (FAs), in AKI and CKD; and the potential therapeutic application or external modulation of lipid components in the management of these diseases. The following databases were utilized for reference identification: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus. The search period spanned from 2010 to 2025. The search terms employed in various combinations included “CKD,” “AKI,” “CRP,” “inflammation,” “serum fatty acids,” “lipids,” “lipid profile.”SummaryA total of 28 studies were selected for further analysis. Twelve studies focused on diagnostics and prognostics in CKD, while nine addressed treatment strategies for CKD. The number of studies performed in AKI 5 (diagnostics/prognostics) and 2 (therapy). In CKD, free FAs may help predict three clinical outcomes: mortality, progression of CKD, and cardiovascular risk. However, therapy-related references remain inconsistent, anti-proteinuric effects of omega-3 FAs have been reported sporadically. The current data on lipids in AKI diagnostics, prognostics, and therapy are still too limited to draw any definitive conclusions.Key MessagesFree FAs enable the prediction of significant outcomes associated with CKD. Omega-3 FAs may exhibit anti-proteinuric properties. Evidence regarding FA-based diagnostics and therapies in acute kidney injury remain limited.

Introduction

Diagnostics and Risk Prediction in Chronic Kidney Disease and Acute Kidney Injury

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant problem in the field of nephrological medicine on a global scale. In 2024, the KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease was updated [1]. The diagnosis of CKD must be made if surrogate markers of impaired renal excretory function and/or structural kidney damage are detectable for more than 3 months. The etiology of CKD has evolved over time, with diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension emerging as the predominant causes [2]. Accordingly, the prevalence of CKD rises with advancing age. As kidney function deteriorates, there is an elevated risk of significant cardiovascular and renal outcomes, including myocardial ischemia, strokes, atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and the need for kidney replacement therapy (KRT). These associations have been clearly illustrated in the current KDIGO guideline [1]. Various factors or comorbidities exist that accelerate the loss of renal reserve. These are usually defined as progression factors. Some factors, such as arterial hypertension and diabetes mellitus, are better known, others, such as renal metabolic acidosis [3–6] or exposure to lead [7], are probably less well known. For some years now, there has been increasing evidence that the progression of CKD and thus the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and death are significantly promoted or increased by inflammatory processes [8–16]. In the complex interplay between inflammation initiation and inhibition, various lipid compounds such as fatty acids (FAs), their metabolites, and others play decisive roles.

Acute kidney injury (AKI), the second major nephrological syndrome, is a frequent and potentially serious complication of a variety of diseases under inpatient conditions [17]. For over 3 decades, the mortality rate of affected individuals has remained relatively stable [18], which indicates the need for reliable measures to detect AKI faster and to treat it more efficiently. There is a well-established relationship between CKD and AKI. CKD has been shown to increase the risk of AKI, and in turn, each instance of AKI increases the likelihood of developing CKD over an individual's lifetime [19]. Inflammatory conditions, whether of infectious or other origin, also increase the risk of AKI [18, 20, 21]. Numerous experimental findings point to the central role of FA metabolism in the pathogenesis of AKI, especially ischemic and toxic AKI. The proximal tubule of the kidney depends critically on mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) to meet its high ATP demands for active solute transport, maintenance of epithelial polarity, and metabolic functions such as gluconeogenesis and ammoniagenesis [22]. FAO provides a dense and efficient source of reducing equivalents and ATP necessary for Na+/K+-ATPase activity and the operation of numerous apical and basolateral transporters, thereby sustaining tubular reabsorption and overall renal homeostasis. Impairment of FAO – through downregulation of key regulators such as PPARα [23], CPT1, or upstream energy sensors like AMPK – leads to lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced ATP production, cellular dedifferentiation, and increased susceptibility to injury, processes that promote interstitial fibrosis and progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) [24]. Therapeutic strategies that preserve or enhance proximal tubule FAO and mitochondrial health have shown promise in experimental models for protecting against acute and chronic tubular injury, highlighting FAO as a central metabolic hub for renal tubular integrity and function.

In this article, two main topics will be addressed: (I) the diagnostic/prognostic significance of lipid FAs and their metabolites in CKD and AKI and (II) possible therapeutic application options of lipid compounds (FAs, their metabolites, corresponding modulators, and others) in both syndromes. The objective of this study was to demonstrate that substrates of lipid metabolism in both syndromes have the potential to be employed for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

CKD and AKI: Epidemiology and Outcomes

Estimates indicate that CKD affects 10–15% of adults in Central Europe [25], with prevalence rising with age. This dynamic is largely attributable to the increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension among the elderly. In Germany, the number of patients requiring dialysis is expected to increase by up to 23% by 2040 compared to 2017 [26]. However, the terminal stage of CKD (CKD stage 5D) is frequently not reached, as cardiovascular morbidity and the associated risk of death increase disproportionately with lowering excretory kidney function and/or progressive albuminuria. This dramatic association has been demonstrated in the latest version of the 2024 KDIGO guideline for CKD [1]. Ultimately, this is also the fundamental medical significance of CKD: the syndrome is a highly potent cardiovascular risk factor.

The epidemiological and prognostic situation for acute kidney injury is no less dramatic. In a comprehensive epidemiological survey by Susantitaphong et al. from 2013 [27], an average AKI incidence of almost 22% of all hospitalized adults was reported. Under intensive care conditions, the incidence increases dramatically in some cases (up to 60%) [28]. The same applies to the risk of death associated with AKI. Individuals treated with intensive care have a 50% probability of dying if AKI has manifested itself [28]. In addition to the acute increase in the risk of death, the lifetime risk of death increases after each individual episode of AKI [19], and increases with the severity of the dysfunction according to established criteria (RIFLE criteria [29]. In the meantime, there is no longer any doubt that AKI also increases the lifetime risk of CKD, as both experimental and clinical data are too consistent [30–32].

It is evident that novel strategies are imperative for the diagnosis, risk profiling, and management of CKD and AKI. In CKD, gliflozines have been identified as significant progression slowing agents in both diabetic and nondiabetic conditions [33–35]. This advance is de facto the first of its kind in nearly 30 years. To date, there have been no comparable developments or discoveries in AKI, either in terms of early detection or treatment.

Methods

We searched the literature between March and September 2025. The following databases were utilized for reference identification: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus. The search period spanned from 2010 to 2025. The search terms employed in various combinations included “chronic kidney disease” and “acute kidney injury,” “CRP,” “inflammation,” “serum fatty acids,” “lipids,” “lipid profile.” Articles were included that reported on clinical trials in humans, as well as systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in the specific time period. These included observational studies as well as intervention studies and randomized, placebo-controlled studies in the defined clinical syndromes of CKD and AKI. Studies published before 2010 or those that did not meet the criteria of being clinical studies, clinical trials, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), meta-analyses, or systematic reviews were excluded. In addition, in vitro studies, animal studies, articles not related to AKI or CKD, case reports and preprint articles were not considered. Stefan Erfurt, Johanna Gruzla, and Daniel Patschan carried out the literature search independently of each other. The final inclusion was made by consensus between the three authors. Results

In this presentation, studies on polyunsaturated FAs are discussed first, followed by research on monounsaturated and saturated FAs. These sections are then followed by studies of other lipid components – such as triglycerides (TGs), apolipoproteins, and related molecules. As a result, the references are not listed in strict chronological order.

Diagnostic and Prognostic Role of Fatty Acids and Their Metabolites in CKD and AKI

Chronic Kidney Disease

The first selected study was published in 2012 by Shearer et al. [36]. The authors investigated the demographics and FA profiles of patients with CKD, specifically comparing stages 3–4 and stage 5 CKD to control subjects. The findings revealed significant differences in renal function and nutritional markers, with elevated TGs in the stage 3–4 CKD group. Polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs), particularly those involved in arachidonate synthesis, were found to be less abundant in stage 5 CKD patients compared to controls, while eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) levels were similar to controls but higher than in stage 5 CKD. Analysis of plasma FA concentrations showed variability, with only EPA showing significant differences. The study also examined the correlation between estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and FA abundance, finding that lower eGFR was associated with decreased levels of certain PUFAs, particularly eicosadienoic and eicosatrienoic acids. In contrast, monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) exhibited mixed relationships with renal function, with palmitate decreasing and oleic acid increasing as renal function declined. Additionally, a decrease in stearic acid was linked to declining kidney function. In terms of mortality risk prediction for patients with stage 5 CKD, nervonic acid was identified as a significant predictor, with a notable association between its abundance and increased age-adjusted mortality risk. For every 0.15% increase in nervonic acid, the risk of mortality increased by 2.1-fold.

Gollasch et al. [37] examined the clinical and demographic characteristics of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients compared to healthy control (HC) subjects, noting no significant differences in age or body mass index (BMI). The ESRD patients had various underlying conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, and were predominantly Caucasian. The analysis of red blood cell (RBC) FA status revealed that ESRD patients had decreased levels of several FAs, including EPA and various other n-6 FAs, while levels of certain FAs like C22:1 n-9 and C24:1 n-9 were increased. Despite these changes, the total RBC FA levels remained unchanged. In plasma, ESRD patients exhibited lower levels of specific FAs such as C18:2 n-6 and EPA, while most free FAs (FFA) were found to be elevated. The omega-3 quotient in RBCs did not differ significantly between the two groups, although free FA levels of EPA and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) were higher in ESRD patients. The effects of hemodialysis on FA levels were also investigated. In RBCs, only a slight decrease in C18:2 was observed, while plasma showed significant changes in several saturated and n-6 FAs. However, hemodialysis did not alter the overall RBC FA profile or omega-3 quotient. The findings suggest that ESRD is associated with a distinct RBC FA status that is not significantly modified by hemodialysis treatment, although certain FAs in plasma were affected.

Sikorska-Wisniewska et al. [38] analzyed clinical and biochemical characteristics of the following patient groups: controls, CKD patients not on dialysis (CKD-ND), and kidney transplant recipients (RTx). It focused on serum levels of omega-3 (n-3) and omega-6 (n-6) PUFAs. Results showed that serum levels of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), EPA, and DHA were significantly lower in the RTx group compared to controls. For n-6 PUFAs, linoleic acid (LA) levels did not show significant differences, but arachidonic acid (AA) levels were also lowest in the RTx group. Despite the observed differences in PUFA levels, no significant associations were found between glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and FA levels. Dietary analysis indicated that RTx recipients had lower intakes of oily fish, a primary source of EPA and DHA, although this did not reach statistical significance. Positive correlations between fish intake and serum levels of DHA and EPA were noted, but significant associations were limited to specific groups. Further analysis revealed negative correlations between EPA and TGs, and between DHA and TGs, both significant in the control group. LA showed an inverse correlation with TGs across all groups, while in CKD-ND patients, LA positively correlated with total and high density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (CHO). AA also negatively correlated with TGs in all groups. No significant correlations were found between FA levels and markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, or BMI. Additionally, an analysis of PUFA in subcutaneous adipose tissue of CKD patients at the time of transplantation showed no significant differences compared to controls. Finally, the authors presented the reduction of potentially cardiovascular protective PUFA in RTx patients as the decisive finding of their study. Nonetheless, the researchers were unable to furnish a definitive explanation for the findings. Furthermore, it was (understandably) impossible to reach a conclusion regarding the implications for cardiovascular prognosis.

A 2016 published [39] study evaluated patients aged 18 years and older with a baseline eGFR of at least 30 mL/min, identifying 79 individuals who progressed to end-stage kidney disease over 6 years. These progressors were frequency matched with 121 nonprogressors based on age, sex, race, and diabetes status. The cohort had a mean age of 59 years, with a balanced representation of males and females and equal numbers of African American and Caucasian participants. The study aimed to identify independent lipid predictors of CKD progression. The cohort was divided into training and test sets, leading to the identification of 382 lipids after filtering. From these, 49 lipids showed significant differences between progressors and nonprogressors. Various classification methods (PLS-DA: partial least squares discriminant analysis, RF, and logistic regression) were employed to narrow down the independent predictors, revealing consistent lipids across methods, particularly diacylglycerols (DAGs) and monoacylglycerols (MAGs). Notably, higher levels of DAG 36:0 and DAG 32:0 were associated with a reduced risk of progression, while higher levels of MAG 16:0 indicated an increased risk. The study also conducted internal validation and found that the multimarker lipid panel had a comparable predictive ability in both original and validated models. Additionally, lipid class analysis showed lower levels of certain lipid classes in progressors, such as CHO esters and ceramides, while some classes like MAG showed higher levels. The findings were consistent across patients with and without diabetes, highlighting trends in lipid abundance related to chain length and the number of double bonds in different lipid classes. Overall, the study identified specific lipids that could serve as independent predictors of CKD progression, contributing to the understanding of lipid metabolism's role in kidney disease.

Mika et al. [40] examined the FA profiles of kidney transplant (Tx) patients compared to HCs. Tx patients exhibited hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL CHO, with lower levels of long-chain FAs (LCFA) and very long-chain FAs (VLCFAs), except for elevated palmitic acid (16:0). Odd-chain FAs (OCFA) and branched-chain FAs were more prevalent in Tx patients, while PUFA were generally lower, except for DHA and adrenic acid (AdA). To minimize the impact of recent surgery and inflammation, patients within 3 months post-transplant were excluded from further analysis, revealing smaller differences in FA profiles between Tx patients and HC. Principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed distinct separation based on FA profiles, particularly highlighting elevated oleic acid (18:1) and decreased saturated FAs (SFAs) in Tx patients. Longitudinal analysis indicated that the most significant disturbances in FA profiles occurred shortly after transplantation, with gradual stabilization over time, although Tx patients still differed from HC. Patients with CVD showed notable differences in FA profiles, with increased pro-inflammatory medium and long-chain SFAs and decreased beneficial FAs. Grafted kidney function, as indicated by glomerular filtration rate (GFR), also influenced FA profiles, with impaired function correlating with lower PUFA levels and higher MUFA levels. Additionally, inflammation and statin use were linked to alterations in FA profiles, highlighting the complex interplay between time posttransplant, CVD, inflammation, and renal function on FA metabolism in kidney RTx. Other factors like BMI and nutritional status did not show significant effects on FA profiles. The findings’ inherent complexity rendered it challenging to reach a definitive conclusion. Nevertheless, the authors hypothesized that conspicuous deviations in the FA profile in post-transplant patients could result in an elevated cardiovascular risk.

A 2021 published analysis by Pinto and colleagues [41] aimed to explore the relationships between dialysis, sleep, 24-h heart rate variability (HRV), and long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated FA (LC n-3 PUFA) status in patients who have recently started hemodialysis. The investigation involved 49 patients, of whom 45 completed the first visit, and 31 completed two visits. Among those, 39 provided high-quality (HRV) recordings during a 2-h dialysis period, 40 during sleep, and 35 for 24 h. The majority of participants had hypertension (90.8%) and type 2 diabetes (38.6%). Significant correlations were found between HRV parameters and LC n-3 PUFA levels across study visits, with erythrocyte and plasma EPA and DHA showing moderate to strong correlations. However, the median levels of erythrocyte omega-3 index (O3I) were low, with only two individuals exceeding 5%. Compared to a healthy reference cohort, patients undergoing hemodialysis exhibited higher heart rates, lower HRV, and higher levels of inflammatory markers, along with lower proportions of LC n-3 PUFAs. No significant associations were observed between erythrocyte O3I, EPA, or DHA and HRV parameters. However, higher EPA:DHA ratios were inversely associated with several HRV indicators. Plasma DHA was positively associated with HRV during dialysis, while DPA(n-3) and EPA showed negative associations with short-term HRV metrics during sleep. Notably, plasma EPA was positively associated with longer-term HRV measures over 24 h, indicating a complex relationship between LC n-3 PUFA levels and HRV in hemodialysis patients.

CKD is characterized by an increase in serum MUFAs and a decrease in n-3 PUFAs. A 2022 published study by Śledziński et al., 2022 [42] aimed at comparing the FA profiles of the same patients before and after kidney transplantation. Three months post-transplant, patients showed improvements in renal parameters and a reduction in CRP levels. However, certain even-chain saturated FAs decreased, while palmitic acid increased. The total amount of ultra-long-chain FAs (ULCFAs) significantly decreased, and while the total MUFAs remained unchanged, there was a notable increase in the desaturation index. Additionally, some n-6 PUFAs decreased after transplantation, and the total n-3 PUFA content also declined. This study highlights the complex interplay of kidney function and FA profiles in transplant patients. Ultimately, the alterations in the FA profile subsequent to transplantation were hypothesized to function as potential promoters of cardiovascular risk.

Ong et al. [43] analyzed data from 25,570 participants across 19 cohorts in 12 countries to investigate the relationship between seafood-derived n-3 PUFAs and the risk of incident CKD. The participants, aged between 49 and 77 years, were followed for a median of 11.3 years. Among them, 19.3% developed incident CKD during the follow-up period. The findings revealed that higher levels of total seafood-derived n-3 PUFAs were associated with an 8% lower risk of incident CKD per interquartile range, with consistent results across different cohorts and lipid compartments. When assessed categorically, individuals in the highest fifth of seafood n-3 PUFA levels had a 13% lower risk compared to those in the lowest fifth. Sensitivity analyses confirmed these associations, even with stricter definitions of CKD. In secondary outcomes, higher seafood n-3 PUFA levels were linked to a reduced risk of a ≥40% decrease in eGFR and a slower annual decline in eGFR, particularly for DHA. Conversely, ALA showed no significant association with incident CKD or changes in eGFR. Effect modification analyses indicated that the association between n-3 PUFAs and CKD was not influenced by demographics or clinical characteristics, such as age, race, or existing health conditions. Overall, the study suggests that seafood-derived n-3 PUFAs may provide protective benefits against the development of CKD.

Koh et al. [44] evaluated baseline characteristics and health outcomes related to plasma levels of polyunsaturated FAs (PUFA) in two cohorts: participants without CKD and those with pre-existing CKD. In cohort 1, consisting of 3,289 individuals without CKD, the median age was 57 years, with a median eGFR of 97.5 mL/min/1.73 m2. Participants with higher PUFA levels tended to be younger, more likely to be female, non-white, and nonsmokers, and exhibited better health profiles, including lower blood pressure (BP) and improved lipid profiles. The study found a significant inverse relationship between PUFA levels and the risk of developing CKD, with hazard ratios (HRs) decreasing across higher quartiles of PUFA. Specifically, higher levels of omega-3 and its components, such as DHA, were associated with a lower risk of CKD and all-cause mortality. In cohort 2, which included 6,735 participants with CKD, the association between total PUFA levels and kidney failure risk treatment was not significant, although DHA showed a notable inverse relationship. The study also highlighted that higher PUFA levels were linked to reduced all-cause mortality in this cohort. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that DHA levels were consistently associated with lower risks of CKD and kidney failure risk treatment. Subgroup analyses indicated that the protective effects of PUFA were more pronounced in younger individuals and those without a history of CVD. Overall, the findings suggest that higher PUFA levels, particularly omega-3 FAs, are beneficial for kidney health and mortality outcomes, although the effects vary between individuals with and without CKD.

In 2024, Śledziński and colleagues [45] examined a group of 25 kidney transplant (KTx) patients, detailing their characteristics and outcomes posttransplantation. Among the patients, 19 underwent their first transplant, four had their second, and two had their third. Pretransplant, 20 patients had a panel of reactive antibodies of 0%, while others had low panel of reactive antibodies levels. The mean number of mismatches for six antigens was 3.04. Nineteen patients had no donor-specific antibodies (DSAs), and six had low levels of DSA. Delayed graft function (DGF) was observed in seven patients, but none experienced rejection or needed changes in immunosuppressive medication during the first year. Laboratory results showed significant improvements in kidney function parameters post-transplant, including decreased creatinine levels and increased eGFR. Hemoglobin and sodium levels rose, while potassium levels decreased. CRP, an inflammatory marker, also significantly decreased. Fatty acid analysis revealed no significant changes in total SFAs or total MUFAs shortly after KTx. However, odd-chain saturated FAs (OCFAs) increased significantly after 6–12 months. Specific even-chain FAs with 14, 20, 22, and 24 carbon atoms showed increased levels, while palmitic acid (C16:0) decreased. Very long-chain FAFAs (VLCFAs) increased significantly after 6–12 months, while ULCFAs decreased over the same period. Regarding polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs), total n-3 PUFAs increased significantly after 6–12 months, following an initial decrease. Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) levels showed a similar trend, with a decrease at 3 months and an increase thereafter. Some n-6 PUFAs, like arachidonic acid (ARA) and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA), decreased at 3 months but increased at 6–12 months, while linoleic acid (LA) showed a significant increase at 3 months but only a trend toward increase after 12 months. Overall, total n-6 PUFAs did not change significantly over the study periods. In summary, it was concluded that transplantation-associated changes in circulating FAs manifest in a complex pattern; however, the potential consequences for adjustments to diet or nutrition remain to be further elucidated.

Mantovani et al. [46] investigated kidney-function decline in 85 elderly post-menopausal women with non-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes mellitus. Over a median follow-up of 4.6 years, 15.3% of participants experienced a decline in kidney function, defined as a ≥30% decrease in eGFR. At baseline, the average eGFR was 83 mL/min/1.73 m2, with only 8.2% of participants having microalbuminuria. By follow-up, the mean eGFR dropped to 76 mL/min/1.73 m2, and some patients developed microalbuminuria or macroalbuminuria. Patients who experienced kidney-function decline were generally older, had higher serum nonesterified FA (NEFA) levels, and were more likely to be treated with sulfonylureas compared to those who did not decline. Other clinical characteristics, including diabetes duration and the use of various glucose-lowering drugs, did not significantly differ between the two groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that those in the highest tertile of baseline serum NEFA levels had a significantly increased risk of kidney-function decline. Cox proportional hazards models indicated that higher serum NEFA levels were associated with a 2.3-fold increased risk of eGFR decline in unadjusted models, which remained significant even after adjusting for various renal risk factors, yielding an adjusted HR of 3.79. Other independent risk factors included the presence of hypertension and microalbuminuria at baseline, as well as lower baseline eGFR. The study concluded that elevated serum NEFA levels are a significant predictor of kidney-function decline in elderly women with type 2 diabetes mellitus, independent of other established risk factors.

Lanzon et al. [47] analyzed the metabolomic and lipidomic profiles of severely obese individuals with CKD, comparing those with CKD (OD) to obese individuals without CKD (O). The cohort included 11 OD patients and 14 O patients, with significant findings regarding body weight, biochemical markers, and lipid profiles. OD patients exhibited higher glucose levels, increased TGs, and lower HDL levels compared to O patients. A notable percentage of OD patients were diabetic and hypertensive, with higher proteinuria and lower eGFR. Lipidomic analysis revealed 71 significant lipid compounds, primarily increased in OD patients, including various TGs, diglycerides, and phosphatidylcholines. Notably, short-chain TGs showed a negative correlation with eGFR in OD patients. Additionally, essential amino acids were found to be elevated in OD patients, with specific changes in polar metabolites observed. Following bariatric surgery, OD patients exhibited improved serum lipid profiles and metabolic markers, including significant weight loss and reduced proteinuria. The lipidomic analysis post-surgery indicated a decrease in many lipid species, with 46 significant lipids annotated. Correlations between lipid species and renal function markers shifted after surgery, with medium-chain TGs and diglycerides showing a positive correlation with uric acid instead of the previously noted short-chain TGs. The polar metabolomic profile post-surgery showed a decrease in certain essential amino acids, while alanine increased. Urinary analysis revealed significant decreases in valine and glutamine levels after surgery, with higher levels of hippuric acid. Overall, bariatric surgery led to substantial metabolic and lipidomic improvements in obese patients with CKD, aligning their profiles more closely with those of non-CKD patients.

Acute Kidney Injury

In a study from september 2025 [48], thirty-four adults undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass cardiac surgery were studied; 17 (50%) developed cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury (CSA-AKI) by KDIGO criteria [49]. All AKI cases appeared within 48 h postoperatively (82% within 24 h). Most were stage 1 (11 patients); three patients had stage 2–3 AKI, and one required ∼36 h of KRT. Lipidomic profiling annotated 4,053 lipid species across 48 classes. Major subclasses included TGs, phosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylethanolamines, sphingomyelins, and DAGs. CSA-AKI sera showed increased levels of acylcarnitines, cholesteryl esters, certain hexosyl/trihexosyl ceramides, lysophosphatidic acids, lysosphingomyelins, PG, and phosphorylated phosphatidylinositols (PIP/PIP2/PIP3), among others. Multivariate models (PLS-DA, OPLS-DA) separated groups robustly, and 65 lipid species differed significantly (64 upregulated, 1 downregulated). Hierarchical clustering showed most differentially expressed lipids were upregulated in CSA-AKI. Correlation/network analyses identified PE (14:0/22:2) and PE (39:1) as early-increased candidates and pointed to glycerophospholipid metabolism as a key affected pathway. Certain lipids (e.g., PI (18:0/18:1)) were negatively correlated with many others.

A 2020 published investigation by Wang et al. [50] investigated the role of serum apolipoprotein A5 levels in pediatric patients with sepsis, revealing significant findings related to its diagnostic and prognostic value. In a mouse model, serum ApoA5 levels increased after LPS treatment, and similar trends were observed in pediatric patients, where ApoA5 levels at PICU (pediatric intensive care unit) admission were significantly higher in healthy children compared to those with sepsis. The study enrolled 101 pediatric sepsis patients with a median age of 19 months, noting a mortality rate of 10.9%. Key differences were found between survivors and nonsurvivors in terms of PRISM III scores, respiratory failure, shock, and the need for mechanical ventilation and vasoactive agents. Notably, serum ApoA5 levels were significantly lower in nonsurvivors compared to survivors, with a correlation to various organ dysfunctions such as multiorgan dysfunction syndrome and AKI. Univariate logistic regression indicated low ApoA5 levels as an independent risk factor for PICU mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses demonstrated that serum ApoA5 could serve as a diagnostic biomarker for sepsis, achieving an AUC of 0.753, and as a prognostic predictor for PICU mortality with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.789. Additionally, serum ApoA5 levels were positively correlated with serum calcium levels but did not show significant correlations with other organ function indicators. These findings highlight the potential of ApoA5 as a valuable biomarker in managing pediatric sepsis.

A study published in 2021 by Sun and colleagues [51] again used metabolomic profiling in an effort to identify predictive marker panels in AKI requiring dialysis. The study analyzed serum samples from patients undergoing KRT as part of the Acute Renal Failure Trial Network (ATN) study [52], focusing on identifying metabolites associated with mortality and kidney function recovery. A total of 97 samples were collected on day 1 (51 from patients who died before day 8 and 46 from those who survived beyond 8 days) and 105 samples on day 8 (25 from patients who died before day 28 and 80 from those who survived beyond 28 days). Baseline characteristics showed no significant differences in age, gender, or cardiovascular sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores between the groups on day 1, but a significant difference in CV-SOFA scores was noted on day 8. Metabolomic analysis revealed 11,520 ion features in negative ionization and 15,955 in positive ionization modes. A total of 51 metabolites with low variability were analyzed, with significant changes observed in specific metabolites linked to mortality. On day 1, 1-arachidonoyl-lysoPC was notably altered, while on day 8, several metabolites, including creatine and citrulline, were dominant in differentiating between the groups. Box plots indicated higher levels of certain metabolites in patients who died compared to survivors. Finally, the study developed biomarker panels for mortality prediction using ROC curves, showing varying degrees of performance across different models. The so-called m4 (4 metabolites) model from day 1 demonstrated the best performance, while the m11 (11 metabolites) model from day 8 showed balanced results with high specificity and acceptable sensitivity. For kidney function recovery, significant metabolite changes were noted in patients off KRT compared to those still dependent on it, with the m3 model yielding a favorable AUC for predicting recovery. Overall, the study highlights the potential of metabolomic profiling in understanding mortality and renal recovery in critically ill patients.

Zhang et al. [53] reported on 1,028 patients aged 18 years and older with primary nephrotic syndrome, of whom 81 (7.9%) patients presented with AKI. All AKI patients underwent renal biopsy, confirming a definitive pathological diagnosis. The prematch analysis revealed that AKI patients were older, had a higher percentage of males, and exhibited greater rates of infection, higher systolic blood pressure (SBP), and increased BMI compared to non-AKI patients. Additionally, the AKI group had significantly higher levels of 24-h urine proteinuria and FFA, alongside lower albumin and HDL levels. After propensity score matching (PSM), 77 patients were matched for analysis. The multivariate logistic regression indicated that higher rates of infection, elevated SBP, increased serum FFA, and greater proteinuria were associated with a tendency toward AKI. The analysis showed that a FFA level of 1 mmol/L corresponded to an 82.18% risk of AKI, with a notable increase in AKI incidence with rising FFA levels. Among the AKI cases, 46% were classified as stage 1, 30% as stage 2, and 25% as stage 3, with no significant differences in FFA levels across the stages. The incidence of AKI varied by pathological type, being highest in patients with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) (22.95%) and lowest in those with minimal change disease (12.5%). The analysis revealed that the AKI group had a higher proportion of minimal change disease, FSGS, immunoglobulin A nephropathy, and mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (MesPGN) compared to the non-AKI group, which predominantly consisted of patients with membranous nephropathy (MN). No significant differences in FFA levels were found among different pathological types of AKI or non-AKI patients, indicating that serum FFA levels were unrelated to the pathological type. The study concluded that serum FFA has a weak diagnostic value for distinguishing between AKI and non-AKI patients in primary nephrotic syndrome (area under the ROC curve of 0.63).

The next selected study [54] investigated the clinical and metabolic characteristics of patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI). A total of 67 patients were analyzed, with 14 classified as AKI I-II and 53 as AKI III. The results indicated that SA-AKI primarily affected males. Through plasma oxidative lipidomics profiling, 127 metabolites were identified, demonstrating stable analytical conditions. Unsupervised principal component analysis and supervised OPLS-DA revealed a distinct separation between SA-AKI patients and HCs, identifying 21 oxidized lipids as significant biomarkers, with 2 upregulated and 19 downregulated in SA-AKI. Machine learning techniques, including Random Forest (RF) and LASSO regression, were employed to reduce the dataset complexity and identify key biomarkers. Five oxidized lipids were highlighted, with 9,10-DiHOME and 5,6-DiHETrE showing excellent performance in distinguishing SA-AKI from HCs. A diagnostic model was developed, achieving an AUC of 0.907, indicating high sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, the study explored potential pathophysiological implications, revealing significant enrichment in the pathways of vascular smooth muscle contraction and serotonergic synapse, with a noted downregulation of vascular smooth muscle contraction in SA-AKI patients. This suggests that altered lipid metabolism may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of SA-AKI.

Fatty Acids, Their Metabolites, and Modulators of Fatty Acid Metabolism in CKD and AKI Management

Chronic Kidney Disease

A study published b Barden et al. in 2015 [55] investigated the effects of n-3 FA supplementation on arachidonic acid metabolism in patients with CKD. In total 85 patients were randomized, baseline characteristics showed a mean age of 56.5 years, a BMI of 27.3 kg/m2, and an eGFR of 35.8 mL/min/1.73 m2. A total of 74 patients completed the study, and most were on antihypertensive medications, including ACE inhibitors. Compliance was confirmed through elevated levels of platelet n-3 FAs and plasma coenzyme Q (CoQ) in treatment groups. The study found that n-3 FA supplementation for 8 weeks significantly reduced plasma levels of 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE) and F2-isoprostanes, while urinary levels of these compounds remained unchanged. Additionally, n-3 FAs did not significantly affect urinary nitrite or nitrate excretion. Regression analyses revealed that post-intervention plasma 20-HETE was inversely related to platelet EPA and positively related to platelet arachidonic acid (AA), accounting for 28.4% of the variation in plasma 20-HETE. Importantly, n-3 FAs led to significant reductions in BP, with decreases in both SBP and diastolic BP (DBP) observed during awake and asleep hours. At baseline, plasma 20-HETE was negatively associated with awake SBP, but post-intervention changes in 20-HETE did not correlate with changes in BP. However, post-intervention plasma 20-HETE was positively associated with both 24-h SBP and diastolic BP after adjusting for baseline measurements, indicating a significant relationship primarily influenced by daytime BP readings. The inclusion of factors like BMI, eGFR, and ACE inhibitor use did not significantly alter these relationships. Overall, the study highlights the effects of n-3 FAs on plasma markers and BP regulation, with specific interactions observed between plasma 20-HETE and BP.

In a 2017 published meta-analysis [56], 171 articles were selected, from which 137 were excluded due to duplicates and irrelevance, leaving 34 potentially eligible studies. Ultimately, 9 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, focusing on omega-3 FA supplementation for CKD patients. Among these, 8 trials examined the effects of omega-3 compared to no supplementation, while 1 trial compared high-dose to low-dose supplementation. The studies involved 444 patients with follow-up periods ranging from 2 to 76.8 months, conducted across the USA, Japan, Europe, and Australia. Quality assessment using the Jadad score classified 3 studies as high quality and the rest as moderate. The analysis revealed that omega-3 supplementation significantly reduced the risk of proteinuria (standardized mean difference: −0.31; p = 0.004) without heterogeneity among trials. However, it did not significantly affect the creatinine clearance rate (CCR) or eGFR. Notably, omega-3 supplementation significantly lowered the risk of ESRD (relative risk: 0.49; p = 0.047) based on data from 207 patients. Subgroup analyses indicated that certain factors, such as location, age, and study quality, influenced the reduction in proteinuria, but no significant differences were found for CCR and eGFR. The study also noted potential publication bias for proteinuria and CCR, although no evidence of bias was detected in the statistical tests performed.

In 2019, De Boer and colleagues [57] investigated whether supplementation with vitamin D3 or omega-3 FAs can prevent the development or progression of CKD in individuals with type 2 diabetes. The study enrolled participants with a mean age of 67.6 years and a median diabetes duration of 6–10 years. Among the 1,312 randomized participants, 46% were women, and 31% were from racial or ethnic minorities. Common glucose-lowering medications included biguanides and sulfonylureas, with insulin use at 20%. Antihypertensive medications were utilized by 80% of participants, and 13% had an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Retention rates were high, with 83% providing at least one follow-up blood or urine sample after 5 years. Adherence to study medications was reported at 92% and 88% for vitamin D and 91% and 89% for omega-3 FAs at two and 5 years, respectively. The primary outcome, change in eGFR, showed a mean decline of 12.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 from baseline to year 5. There were no significant differences in eGFR changes between treatment groups (vitamin D vs. placebo and omega-3 vs. placebo). Secondary outcomes, including a composite of kidney failure or death, also did not differ significantly by treatment assignment. Urine albumin-creatinine ratio increased from baseline to year 5, but similarly, no significant differences were observed between treatment groups. Adverse events were comparable between the supplementation and placebo groups, with instances of kidney stones and gastrointestinal bleeding reported. Overall, the study found no significant effects of vitamin D or omega-3 FA supplementation on kidney function or related outcomes over the 5-year period.

A 2020 published study by Valle Flores et al. [58] addressed the potential benefits of omega-3 FA supplementation for treating inflammation-related conditions in patients with CKD, particularly those undergoing hemodialysis. Despite its promise, omega-3 supplementation is not commonly used in CKD patients, possibly due to a lack of awareness about its therapeutic effects. Several clinical trials examined the impact of omega-3 FAs on inflammation markers such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP. Some studies reported a reduction in these markers with omega-3 supplementation, while others have found no significant effects. The current trial was designed as prospective, randomized, double-blind study conducted with CKD patients on regular hemodialysis. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the study group (group A; 46 patients) received four capsules (2.4 g) of omega-3 FAs daily over a 12-week intervention period, while the control group (group B; 47 patients) received four capsules of paraffin oil. Finally, 102 individuals were deemed eligible. After randomization, 19 patients were excluded due to various reasons, leaving 46 patients in the omega-3 group (group A) and 47 in the control group (group B) for final analysis. The mean age was similar between groups, with no significant differences in sex distribution, BMI, duration of hemodialysis, or medical history (p ≥ 0.05). After 12 weeks of treatment, no significant changes were noted in functional indicators or disease markers for either group (p ≥ 0.05). However, group A exhibited a significant decrease in inflammatory markers: CRP (−26.08%), IL-6 (−25.06%), and TNF- (−13.13%) (p ≤ 0.001). The IL-10 concentration increased (+12.21%), though not significantly (p = 0.27), while the IL-10/IL-6 ratio rose significantly (+41.65%; p ≤ 0.001). In contrast, group B showed no significant changes in inflammation markers (p ≥ 0.05). Overall, significant differences in inflammatory markers and interleukin ratios were observed between the two groups after the treatment period.

Bunout et al. [59] conducted a double-blind clinical trial to investigate the effects of U-3 PUFA on albuminuria in patients with CKD. The study addressed certain limitations by using a highly deodorized oil containing n-3 PUFA, which resembled corn oil in taste and odor. In total, 50 participants were randomized into two groups: a control group receiving corn oil and an active group receiving n-3 PUFA. Five participants from the corn-oil group and four from the n-3 PUFA group were lost to follow-up. Demographic data, BMI, and baseline albumin excretion showed no significant differences between groups. Participants in the n-3 PUFA group experienced significant increases in eicosapentaenoic and docosahexaenoic FA levels, as well as a rise in the omega-3 index from 3.1% to 5.5%. A 20% reduction in urine albumin excretion was noted in both groups, but the difference was not statistically significant. Notably, participants receiving n-3 PUFA maintained stable pulse wave velocity, while controls showed an increase. The n-3 PUFA group also had a significant decrease in triacylglycerol levels. Glycosylated hemoglobin increased in both groups without differences. No effects on inflammatory parameters were observed. A secondary analysis of 28 participants in the n-3 PUFA group who had increased EPA levels showed a significant reduction in SBP. Sensitivity analysis based on baseline glomerular filtration rate (GFR) indicated that results remained consistent, except for a nonsignificant reduction in serum TGs in participants with GFR above 60 mL/min/m2.

A prospective cohort study, published in 2020 [60] involved 27 hemodialysis patients (13 males and 14 females) with an average age of 67.33 years and an average of 3.98 years on hemodialysis. Participants were supplemented with 1,000 mg of fish oil daily, and various health parameters were assessed at baseline and over 3 months. Results showed no significant changes in BMI, serum calcium, phosphorus, or intact parathyroid hormone levels. However, fish oil supplementation significantly reduced pruritus scores, with average scores decreasing from 22.85 at baseline to 14.11 by the third month (p < 0.001). The severity of pruritus also improved, with 40.7% of patients experiencing severe pruritus at baseline compared to a reduction in severity over time. Inflammatory markers, including CRP and IL-6, showed significant improvement, with CRP levels decreasing from 0.40 mg/dL at baseline to 0.29 mg/dL by the third month (p < 0.05), and IL-6 levels dropping from 21.81 pg/mL to 3.72 pg/mL (p < 0.001). Additionally, renal function improved, indicated by a decrease in plasma creatinine levels. Hematological factors also showed positive changes; total CHO decreased significantly, while HDL levels increased over time (p < 0.001). Triglycerides (TGs) levels improved as well, and low density lipoprotein levels decreased initially but showed a slight increase by the third month. Skin hydration, measured by tissue dielectric constant values, significantly improved with fish oil supplementation, indicating better skin condition. Overall, fish oil supplementation was effective in ameliorating pruritus and improving various health parameters in hemodialysis patients.

A 2024 published study by Ao and colleagues [61] examined the baseline characteristics and long-term outcomes of 24,497 participants, aged 59.1 years on average, regarding their use of fish oil supplements. Among them, 7,122 individuals regularly used fish oil. Compared to nonusers, fish oil users were generally older, more physically active, and had higher rates of hypertension and high CHO. They also had lower levels of CRP, blood glucose, and HbA1c, alongside higher HDL CHO levels. Over an average follow-up of 11.3 years, 3,533 cases of CKD were reported, with 1,024 occurring in fish oil users. Adjusted models indicated that fish oil users had a 12% lower risk of CKD events in diabetic patients (HR 0.88), a finding that remained significant after further adjustments for various factors. Notably, those using fish oil experienced a delayed risk of CKD events by approximately 2.79 years. The protective effects of fish oil were particularly pronounced in certain subgroups, including men, older adults, nonsmokers, and those with a healthier diet. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the association, even after accounting for dietary factors and medication use. Additionally, fish oil intake was inversely related to serum levels of HbA1c and CRP, while positively associated with HDL-C, suggesting that these factors mediate the relationship between fish oil use and CKD risk. The authors ultimately endorsed the positive impact of fish oil supplementation in preventing CKD among patients with diabetes.

A meta-analysis conducted by Fei and colleagues in 2024 [62] aimed to evaluate the efficacy of omega-3 FAs in reducing proteinuria. The exploration led to the identification of 562 records, from which 241 duplicates were removed, resulting in 321 records for evaluation. After applying predetermined criteria, 152 papers were excluded, leaving 71 studies for full-text assessment. Ultimately, nine studies were included in the meta-analysis, which focused on the impact of omega-3 FAs on kidney diseases such as IgA nephropathy, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, CKD, and diabetic nephropathy. These studies, conducted between 1989 and 2015 across various countries (Greece, Italy, Japan, USA, Sweden, Australia), involved a total of 347 participants with sample sizes ranging from 19 to 54. The interventions varied, utilizing EPA and DHA at doses between 1.8 g/day and 12 g/day, sometimes combined with other medications. Control groups received placebos or other treatments. The studies had JADAD scores ranging from 2 to 4, indicating moderate to high methodological quality. The meta-analysis employed both fixed- and random-effects models, revealing a nonsignificant overall effect size (standardized mean difference) of 0.0599 and −0.1600, respectively. Substantial heterogeneity was noted among the studies, with an I2 statistic of 91.1%, indicating significant variation. Publication bias was assessed but the analysis was limited due to the small number of studies. A funnel plot analysis did not indicate potential publication bias, with a p value of 0.4012. Overall, the findings suggest that omega-3 FAs may not have a significant impact on kidney diseases, despite the considerable heterogeneity among the studies.

Finally, Lok and colleagues [63] published a double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled trial conducted at 26 sites in Canada and Australia. It enrolled 1,228 adults on maintenance hemodialysis to test whether daily n-3 polyunsaturated FA supplementation (4 g fish oil containing 1.6 g EPA and 0.8 g DHA) reduced serious cardiovascular events compared with a corn-oil placebo. Over a median follow-up of 3.5 years, the primary composite outcome of serious cardiovascular events occurred less often in the fish-oil group (0.31 vs. 0.61 events per 1,000 patient-days), yielding a HR of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.47–0.70; p < 0.001). An extended endpoint that included noncardiac deaths also favored fish oil (HR 0.77; 95% CI, 0.65–0.90). Individual outcomes were reduced with fish oil: cardiac death (HR 0.55), fatal/nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI; HR 0.56), peripheral vascular disease leading to amputation (HR 0.57), and fatal/nonfatal stroke (HR 0.37). The composite of first cardiovascular event or death from any cause was lower with fish oil (HR 0.73; 95% CI, 0.61–0.87). Adherence and adverse-event rates were similar between groups. The investigators conclude that daily n-3 FA supplementation lowered the rate of serious cardiovascular events in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.

Acute Kidney Injury

Hoogeveen et al. [64] analyzed the effect of marine and plant-derived n-3 FAs on kidney function in patients after MI. The study involved 2,344 patients with a history of MI, who were well-balanced across four treatment groups. The mean age was 69 years, with 81% being men. Key baseline characteristics included 19% with diabetes, 23% who were obese, and 15% who smoked. Most patients (98%) were on antithrombotic agents, and the median daily fish intake was 14.7 grams, with 21% consuming less than 5 grams per day. Only 5.2% of participants used fish oil supplements during the trial. Patients participated for a median of 41.3 months, with the EPA-DHA group receiving an average of 239 mg of EPA and 159 mg of DHA daily, while the ALA group received 1.99 g of ALA. After 40 months, the placebo group experienced a decline in eGFR of 6.9 mL/min per 1.73 m2. In contrast, the EPA-DHA group showed a smaller decline of 2.1 mL/min per 1.73 m2, representing a 30% reduction compared to placebo. No significant effects were observed in the ALA group. The treatment effect of EPA-DHA was more pronounced in patients with CKD, showing a difference of 4.9 mL/min per 1.73 m2. The incidence of CKD and rapid decline in kidney function after 40 months was 15% and 34%, respectively, with odds ratios indicating no significant benefit from EPA-DHA compared to placebo. Overall, while EPA-DHA supplementation appeared to slow kidney-function decline, the effects were modest and varied based on baseline CKD status.

A prospective randomized controlled trial [65] assessed the effects of oral omega-3 FA supplementation in patients with chronic renal insufficiency undergoing coronary angiography, based on the premise that reactive oxygen species (ROS) contribute to the development of contrast-induced nephropathy. The study compared the baseline characteristics and outcomes of participants either receiving N-acetylcysteine (NAC) or receiving omega-3 FAs, all of whom had CKD. Participants in the NAC group were significantly older (66.18 years) than those in the omega-3 group (60.65 years; p = 0.006). Both groups had similar average eGFRs and comparable rates of diabetes, hypertension, and previous heart conditions. However, hyperlipidemia was more prevalent in the NAC group (38.8% vs. 17.5%; p = 0.007). More patients in the omega-3 group were treated with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (54.0% vs. 34.3%; p = 0.024). Contrast-induced nephropathy rates were similar between the two groups, with 7.7% overall incidence (p = 0.919). Kidney replacement therapy was needed for 8 patients, with a higher proportion in the omega-3 group (9.5% vs. 3.0%; p = 0.121). There were six in-hospital deaths, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.361). The rates of in-hospital MI and ischemic stroke were also similar, and the length of hospital stay was comparable (6.7 days for NAC vs. 6.16 days for omega-3; p = 0.714). Overall, the study found no significant differences in outcomes between the two treatment groups. Discussion

The literature discussed on the diagnostic and prognostic value of lipid compounds in CKD provides numerous findings of potentially high clinical benefit that are certainly still little known. Nervonic acid, for example, has been identified as a predictor of mortality in CKD stage 5D, with a linear association between increased acid levels and risk of death [36]. Afshinnia et al. [39] identified a number of lipid compounds that facilitate the differentiation between CKD patients who do and do not experience disease progression. Finally, it has been posited that deviations in FA metabolism may be a contributing factor to the heightened cardiovascular risk observed in transplant patients [42]. Therefore, three studies alone have yielded findings that are highly relevant for clinicians. Specifically, these findings support the assessment of three key risks: (I) the risk of death, (II) the risk of progression, and (III) cardiovascular risk in CKD. It may be suggested that new biomarkers are not necessary for CKD management, as the integrative analysis of eGFR and albuminuria already provides a sufficient assessment of the risk for significant clinical endpoints [1]. Nonetheless, the advent of novel biomarkers has the potential to unveil novel perspectives for more precise prognostication of specific clinical endpoints. The data on acute kidney injury previously discussed illustrate the possibilities that may arise with the availability of new biomarkers. The study by Sun et al. [51] clearly led to the identification of panels of metabolites that can predict mortality in cases of AKI. The aspect of mortality prediction has so far received scant attention. Nevertheless, the prediction of death or the approximate estimation of the mortality risk in individuals with AKI is of significant relevance. The evaluation of novel AKI biomarkers is typically contingent upon their capacity to facilitate the timely detection of the syndrome.

Regarding the therapeutic use of specific lipid components in CKD, no definitive recommendation can currently be made for omega-3 FAs, despite documented anti-proteinuric effects. At present, the data are not sufficiently consistent. Research indicates that fish oil has the potential to serve as an adjuvant therapeutic agent for CKD-associated pruritus and to improve cardiovascular morbidity in dialysis patients. Recent experimental data [66] in diabetic (db/db) mice – an established model of diabetes-induced CKD – show that therapeutic modulation of lipid metabolism can be beneficial. Empagliflozin markedly reduced intrarenal lipid accumulation in these animals, but this effect was antagonized by overactivation of Complement C1q C chain (C1QC). The study thus highlights a link between intrarenal inflammation and disrupted lipid homeostasis and demonstrates that these processes can be therapeutically targeted, in this case by sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition. Modulation of intrarenal lipid accumulation in diabetic CKD has also been demonstrated with the use of other therapeutics (statins, fibrates) [67]. In conclusion, these data underscore the significant role of disturbances in lipid homeostasis in the progression of CKD.

In contrast, the role of lipid components in treating AKI has received limited attention, as this article clearly demonstrates. After a thorough review of the available literature, only two studies were identified. In both cases, patient collectives were analyzed that are characterized by a disproportionately high risk of AKI, on the one hand individuals with administration of iodinated contrast medium, on the other hand individuals with MI. The latter are already at risk, even without coronary intervention, as MI often has hemodynamic consequences. Figure 2 summarizes the most important findings from the literature to date.

This review has various limitations. The studies identified were highly heterogeneous. There were substantial differences in study design and methodology, patient characteristics, lipid metabolites evaluated, and clinical endpoints. Important factors influencing lipid metabolism, such as diet and drug therapies, were insufficiently considered. Consequently, more specific statistical analyses and comparisons could not be performed. Nevertheless, the included studies yielded highly noteworthy and prospective findings.

In conclusion, there is an urgent need for studies on patients with AKI in general. In particular, for example, the common prerenal etiology is caused by infectious diseases including sepsis. According to current knowledge, the acute renal dysfunction in these patients is essentially the result of a deregulated immune response with intrarenal inflammation. Especially with the regulation of inflammatory processes, there is a broad consensus that lipid metabolites such as free FAs have a modulating influence. This relationship has hardly been investigated for renal processes to date and should be approached in more detail in future studies.
