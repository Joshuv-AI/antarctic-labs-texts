Commercial determinants of health: case study of ultra-processed food companies in Thailand
Authors: Nongnuch Jindarattanaporn, Weerapak Samsiripong, Salakjit Chuenchom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltra-processed food companies in Thailand have rapidly expanded, and they play an important role in the domestic economy and public policy. These companies influence policy-making by building networks with political authorities and using various strategies to pursue their interests. Increased consumption of ultra-processed foods has been shown to be strongly associated with obesity and other non-communicable diseases (NCDs), which are a growing public health concern in Thailand. This study investigated the commercial determinants of health (CDOH) strategies of the major ultra-processed food companies in Thailand.MethodsThis study employed document review to classify CDOH’s strategies of ultra-processed food companies in Thailand. The authors searched publicly available information by 15 ultra-processed food companies, including both national and transnational companies, between January 2019 and December 2024. In addition, 30 semi-structured interviews were conducted with field experts to provide additional insights to support the findings from the document review. Thematic analysis was used to interpret the data.ResultsNational ultra-processed food companies in Thailand employ all seven strategies of CDOH, namely: (1) Political, (2) Scientific, (3) Marketing, (4) Supply chain and waste management, (5) Labour and employment, (6) Financial, and (7) Reputation management strategies. Transnational companies employed all but one of the seven strategies (labour and employment).ConclusionsThe wide array of CDOH strategies used to shape public policy and opinion may hinder efforts to protect the health of Thais from obesity and diet-related NCDs. To counter this, the authors recommend systematic monitoring of CDOH strategies, stronger accountability for the ultra-processed food companies, mandatory lobbyist registration, stricter management of conflict of interest between government officials and food companies, and strict enforcement of relevant legislation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12992-025-01174-9.Keywords: Commercial determinants of health, Strategy, Ultra-processed food company, Thailand

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are responsible for 41 million deaths each year, accounting for 74% of all global deaths [1]. A significant 77% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The leading cause of NCD-related deaths is cardiovascular disease followed by cancer, chronic respiratory disease, and diabetes [1]. The growing burden of NCDs poses a major barrier to socio-economic development, and presents a serious threat to health security at both the national and global level [2].

Commercial determinants of health (CDOH) are a key driver of many health risk factors, including poor nutrition, air pollution, and physical inactivity. Beyond these risk factors, commercial determinants also influence health outcomes such as NCDs, infectious disease, epidemics, road- and weapon-related injuries, violence, and mental health conditions [3, 4]. These determinants can be understood across seven domains of commercial influence: (1) Political engagement—efforts to shape policy and regulation; (2) Scientific influence—funding or producing research to advance corporate interests; (3) Marketing strategies—promoting products through traditional and digital channels; (4) Supply chain and waste management—decisions on sourcing, production, environmental impact; (5) Labour and employment practices—working conditions, workforce strategies; (6) Financial management—tax practices, investments, financial flows; and (7) Reputation management—corporate social responsibility (CSR) and image-building activities [5–7]. The strategic practices of CDOH are not confined to a single country. Rather, they may represent transnational patterns employed by multinational corporations, with strategies often exchanged, adapted, and applied across borders [8]. The actions of private companies therefore have significant impacts on public health in all countries [9, 10]. Thai, East Asian, and international research highlights those commercial determinants which shape health behaviors and outcomes in various ways [11]. Certain manufacturing processes have posed serious health risk to workers e.g., high injury rates, disease transmission, etc. [12]. These activities have also caused environmental damage to nearby communities, who often receive insufficient compensation [7]. These broader impacts underscore the need to examine commercial determinants within a wide health framework that includes occupational and environmental health.

The ultra-processed food companies in Thailand have been found to strategically manage information to benefit their own interests at the expense of the consumer public [13]. For instance, soft drink companies have funded research led by government officials, but only in ways that supported their business agenda [13]. Major Thai ultra-processed food corporations have also provided financial support to media outlets to positively bias news coverage, build credibility, and gain public acceptance [13]. The impact of the expansion and aggressive marketing of large multinational food companies is clearly evident in Thailand. From January to December 2024, food and beverage companies in Thailand invested a staggering US$ 445 million in advertising, particularly through television and internet media—more than any other product sector [14]. The top three food companies with the highest advertising spending were all multinational corporations: Unilever, Nestlé, and Coca-Cola [14].

Such massive investment in advertising has significantly increased consumer exposure to ultra-processed food promotions [15]. Thai research has shown that the operations of ultra-processed food businesses in the country influence children’s health through increased exposure to unhealthy products and marketing [16]. These companies frequently market products high in sugar, fat, and sodium by using cartoon characters on packaging, celebrity endorsements (including actors, singers, athletes, influencers, etc.), discount signs, promotional giveaways, and lucky draw contests. As a result, four out of ten Thai children age six to 18 report liking, purchasing, and consuming foods they encounter through these marketing tactics [16].

There are some studies related to Thailand regarding the commercial determinants affecting health within ultra-processed food industry. However, these studies have primarily focused on marketing activities such as advertising and marketing communications [17, 18], as well as political activities, including the political operations of 12 ultra-processed food companies [13]. Thus, there remains a critical gap in understanding how ultra-processed food companies operate across all seven domains of commercial, with the ultimate goal of maximizing profit, wealth, and power in the global food system [5–7]. Access to information about these business strategies and practices is essential, as it enables relevant authorities to develop strategies for regulating or managing corporate activities. Such oversight can contribute to improved public health outcomes within the country [4]. The lack of comprehensive understanding across these seven strategic domains has resulted in fragmented and insufficient policy responses to counter the influence of the ultra-processed food industry. Therefore, our study aims to explore the business strategies employed by ultra-processed food companies operating in Thailand, with a particular focus on how these strategies reflect and reinforce the globalised nature of CDOH.

Methods

Qualitative research methodology was applied to gain in-depth insights and a better understanding of complex business strategies from a variety of key informants. The study drew upon four approaches which identify CDOH in the food industry, as proposed by Gómez (2025), Gilmore et al. (2023), Friel et al. (2023), Mialon (2020), and Jaichuen et al. (2018) [6, 7, 13, 19, 20]. Two complementary qualitative methods were employed: Document review and semi-structured interviews. For the document review, information was defined as any publicly-available material related to the operations, strategies, or policy engagement of ultra-processed food companies. This included company websites, press releases, CSR reports, media coverage, government records, and materials from non-governmental organisations. The approaches commonly classify CDOH [6, 7, 13, 19, 20] into the following strategies: 1) Political; 2) Science; 3) Marketing; 4) Supply chain and waste; 5) Labour and employment; 6) Financial; and 7) Reputation management strategies (Additional File 1).

Selection of companies

In this study, the selection of ultra-processed food companies for data collection involved a two-step process. Firstly, a list of the most prominent companies (national and transnational) in each product category was compiled using 2023 Thai market share data from the Euromonitor Passport database [21]. Secondly, a Thai Expert Advisory Group (TEAG) was established to choose companies from this list based on the following criteria: Representation across diverse food categories; inclusion of companies with the highest overall and category-specific market shares; variation in corporate nutrition/health policies and practices; and the potential for access to their strategies related to the CDOH. The involvement of these experts was included to enhance the study’s contextual understanding of Thailand.

Members of the TEAG consisted of five health and non-health experts from the governmental and non-governmental sectors: One senior government official, three university professors and one leader of non-governmental organisations (NGO). Of the initial list of 20 food and beverage companies across all categories, 15 were selected by the TEAG to be included in the study. Inclusion criteria included companies with a significant market presence, influence on national food policies, and visibility in public and policy discourse. Both national and transnational companies were included to capture a comprehensive picture of corporate strategies and their CDOH impacts. To avoid duplication and ensure balanced representation, exclusion criteria were applied to five companies from the initial list because they were subsidiaries of selected companies. This purposive selection ensured that the study focused on companies most likely to shape the food environment and public health outcomes in Thailand. Eight of the companies were Thai-owned and the remaining companies were transnational companies (Table 1).

Document review

Company documents and information related to a company’s strategy to influence policy, promote products, manage research and scientific outputs, organise labour practices, oversee supply chains and waste, handle financial management, and maintain corporate reputation were systematically collected from various publicly-available data sources, as recommended by the CDOH framework [6, 7, 13, 19, 20] and with guidance from TEAG. Various databases and other related data sources were selected based on their coverage of Thai academic and grey literature relevant to food, nutrition, and public health policy. These include company websites, domestic and international social media, websites of other relevant private sector organisations (e.g., Federation of Thai Industry). Databases include library databases at major universities in Thailand, including ThaiLIS, ThaiJO, and the News Center X (NCX) database (the largest and real-time newspaper database in Thailand). Websites and published materials from relevant ministries and departments were also reviewed as well as those of other entities, including political parties, various NGO, and the Thai Health Promotion Foundation. Documents were retrieved systematically using keyword searches such as “nutrition policy,” “corporate social responsibility,” “tax,” “marketing to children” in both English and Thai across company websites, and major news outlets, supplemented by snowballing through references found in initial documents. Initial searches retrieved 2577 documents which were screened for relevance according to pre-specified inclusion criteria. The data covers the period between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2024. Information was obtained in either Thai or English, or both, depending on the source.

Semi-structured interviews of key informants

After completing the document review, semi-structured interviews with key informants were conducted to supplement evidence from the document review. Key informants were those who were involved in policy processes on obesity prevention and diet-related NCDs, or experts who had more than five years’ experience and significant expertise in monitoring or researching private sector organisation policies and practices across different public health areas such as food and nutrition, alcohol, tobacco, breastfeeding, labour, agriculture, and other public health related fields, or experts who could provide insight and first-hand experience on the CDOH strategies and tactics used by food companies. A list of key informants was initially drawn from secondary data sources, including government websites and documents, NGO publications/websites, major Thai newspapers and internet searches, as well the recommendations from TEAG. Snowball sampling was then applied to identify other relevant key informants until information became saturated. Data saturation was determined when subsequent interviews yielded no new themes or insights.

A list of 39 multi-sectoral key informants were selected and invited to participate in the study; only 30 agreed. Of these, ten were former or current senior officials from the governmental sector (GO) related to commercial or public health interests; seven were from not-for-profit civil society organisations (CSO); four were from academia (AC); seven were from food-industry-related private sector organisations (FI) including food companies and industry associations; and two were from media companies (ME). The nine invitees who declined to participate may have felt they had only limited perspectives on CDOH strategies and tactics. Nevertheless, participants from all key stakeholder groups were included, ensuring adequate representation. To support robustness of the findings, monthly meetings were held among authors to triangulate the findings and mitigate potential bias. Key informant interviews took place between December 9, 2024 and March 12, 2025.

Pilot interview questions (Additional File 2) were tested with individuals who had similar characteristics to the key informants of this study, and revised if necessary prior to data collection. Written informed consent was sought before face-to-face interviews. Interviews were recorded and lasted approximately one hour, and were transcribed verbatim. Confidentiality was fully maintained.

Data analysis

The strategies of CDOH data from the document review and key informant interviews were analysed using a qualitative, thematic analysis with reference to the CDOH framework [6, 7, 13, 19, 20]. The CDOH framework was applied from the outset to guide the coding process. Codes and themes were mapped directly to the seven domains of commercial influence: political engagement, scientific influence, marketing strategies, supply chain and waste management, labour and employment practices, financial management, and reputation management. This approach allowed new themes to emerge while ensuring that the findings could be situated within the established framework. Content of the interviews was analysed, and emerging themes were identified. Relevant quotations in Thai which supported a theme were translated to English, and no attributes of the key informants were identified except for their group affiliation.

A preliminary codebook was developed iteratively, and data were coded using NVivo version 12.2. Two researchers (NJ, SC) independently coded the document review and interview data, then compared and discussed their codes and themes. Discrepancies were arbitrated by a third researcher (WS) and resolved through consensus among all three researchers.

Triangulation was conducted by systematically comparing themes and findings from the key informant interviews and document review using a comparison matrix. Significant differences were defined as discrepancies in the presence, emphasis, or interpretation of themes across the two data sources. When differences were identified, the research team discussed whether they reflected complementary insights, data gaps, or conflicting evidence, and the resolution was documented in the analysis notes.

All data were drawn from publicly-available records. While the study identifies specific companies and individuals, this was done solely to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Interpretations focus on institutional strategies rather than personal motives. For clarity in presentation, results are shown separately for Thai and transnational companies. This division is intended solely for transparency and ease of reading, and does not imply that the study specifically aimed to compare the two groups.

Results

The companies were classified into two groups: Thai and transnational. The study found that Thai companies engaged in all several strategic domains, multinational companies operating in Thailand were found to implement only six strategies. Despite the differences in the strategies employed between Thai and transnational companies, the study found no substantial differences in the content of such strategies across company classification.

Thai ultra-processed food companies engaged in all seven strategic domains, while multinational companies operating in Thailand were found to implement only six strategies, with no observable evidence of labour and employment strategies in the available data. Discussion

This study explored the CDOH strategies applied by 15 major ultra-processed food companies in Thailand, using qualitative research methodology, publicly-available information, and interviews with key informants. The study revealed that companies applied several practices, which could potentially influence public policy, programs, and Thai society. The ultra-processed food companies in Thailand have played an active role in shaping public policy by seeking inclusion in working groups, advisory committees, and technical panels at both national and international levels. Their participation has extended beyond providing information to directly advancing or protecting business interests. This pattern aligns with findings from a study of alcohol control which documented similar involvement by alcohol companies in countries such as Australia, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Botswana, Canada, Colombia, Ireland, Lao PDR, Thailand, and Vietnam [24]. The study noted that industry representatives were often allowed to participate in government task forces responsible for the development, implementation, or enforcement of policy. For example, in Vietnam, alcohol industry actors were officially invited to engage in policy consultations [24]. Our findings are also consistent with a previous study on the political activities of food corporations in Thailand, which reported that food companies sought involvement in government working groups to influence policy formulation [13]. Such participation by food industry actors in policy processes has persisted up to the time of this research, reflecting an ongoing strategy of institutional engagement to shape regulatory environments. Drawing lessons from global experience, policy options should include adopting clear conflict-of-interest frameworks, such as those recommended by the WHO Framework of Engagement with Non-State Actors (FENSA) and its guidelines, establishing independent advisory bodies free from commercial influence, and ensuring mandatory disclosure of all committee members’ industry affiliations [25, 26]. Additionally, mechanisms for civil society participation in policymaking (e.g., in Sierra Leone and in alcohol control contexts) could be expanded in Thailand to counterbalance corporate influence and strengthen accountability [27, 28]. Such measures would help align Thai food policy governance with international best practices, and safeguard public health objectives.

In Thailand, the food industry’s influence over policymakers is facilitated by the “revolving door,” with former industry employees entering government agencies and ex-public officials taking roles in food corporations. These connections can influence policy decisions and may create potential conflicts of interest, shaping regulatory environments in ways that bolster industry interests. Our findings are consistent with the tobacco industry in Australia and food industry in Brazil, where tobacco and food company personnel were found to have previously held positions and be representative in government agencies at the state, territory, and federal levels [29, 30]. Whereas Article 126 of the Organic Act on Counter Corruption B.E. 2561(2018), prohibits public officials from engaging in activities that conflict with the public interest, Article 127 (4) allows them to work with private sector organisations at least two years after leaving office [31]. This limited cooling-off period may be insufficient to prevent conflicts of interest, particularly in sectors with significant commercial influence such as food. Strengthening post-employment restrictions, requiring prior disclosure of private sector roles, and establishing independent monitoring mechanisms could mitigate undue industry influence and support public health governance.

The ultra-processed food companies in Thailand use legal action or the threat thereof against public policies or opponents. This practice is similar to findings from focus groups and in-depth interviews with 29 international tobacco control researchers and policy advocates, who reported a wide range of industry-led intimidation strategies. These include media attacks, online harassment, legal threats, non-legal threats such as death threats, freedom of information requests, surveillance, complaints to institutions, and even theft—such as the burglary of offices and vehicles, with stolen computers and no identified perpetrators [32]. Similarly, in Brazil, researchers have documented cases where public health experts were intimidated by the ultra-processed food industry, including threats of legal action, leading to self-censorship and, ultimately, undermining public policy efforts [30]. Therefore, the following strategies are proposed to address intimidation and harassment by ultra-processed food companies: (1) Documenting and monitoring incidents of intimidation and harassment; (2) Building individual preparedness through awareness-raising and targeted training (e.g., in IT security and legal literacy); and (3) Providing support for affected individuals, including legal advice and access to peer support networks [32]. Importantly, international experience demonstrates that legal mechanisms such as anti-SLAPP (Strategic Lawsuits against Public Participation) legislation can provide safeguards against industry-led intimidation. For example, Canada and several EU-member states have adopted anti-SLAPP frameworks to protect public participation and prevent the misuse of litigation to silence critics [33–35]. Lessons from these contexts suggest that similar protective measures could be adapted for Thailand to safeguard public health advocates and researchers from intimidation while ensuring policy processes remain transparent and evidence-based.

Ultra-processed food industries have engaged in lobbying on policies related to labour, food, nutrition and health, and the environment. These efforts also include the use of third-party actors or organisations to lobby on their behalf. This pattern is consistent with studies on food companies in Brazil, as well as research on the alcohol and tobacco industries in the United States (US), which similarly highlight lobbying as a key strategy to influence policy-making. In response to such practices, Action on Smoking and Health (ASH) launched the U.S. Tobacco Lobbyist and Lobbying Firm Registration Tracker, which discloses the identities of lobbyists and lobbying firms representing the tobacco industry in every US state and the District of Columbia [36]. Findings from this study also indicate that food corporations in Thailand have used lobbying tactics to delay the implementation of new laws and regulations. Our results are consistent with developments in the United Kingdom, where despite legislation banning the advertisement of unhealthy foods to children – originally scheduled to take effect on 3 December 2024 - the ultra-processed food industry has continued to lobby for delays. As of 2025, the law has yet to be fully enforced [37].

However, many high-income countries have adopted transparency-oriented lobbying policies rather than imposing strict limitations on stakeholder engagement. For instance, Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, and Norway have implemented measures that require government officials and political office holders to publicly disclose records of meetings or interactions with representatives from the alcohol industry. These disclosures typically include the date, time, and purpose of the meeting, the names of participants from both government and industry, a summary of topics discussed or any potential agreements made, and the publication of this information on official government websites within a specified timeframe. Additional mechanisms include mandatory lobbyist registries and ministerial diaries that further support transparency and public accountability [24]. For Thailand, these lessons suggest several policy options. The Thai government could establish a lobbying transparency framework, including a mandatory lobbyist registry and ministerial diary system that records all interactions between policymakers and industry representatives. Regular public disclosure of these records, with summaries of meeting objectives and outcomes, would strengthen accountability and allow civil society and media oversight. Additionally, guidelines could be introduced to manage conflicts of interest in policy committees, limiting direct influence of industry actors on the development and implementation of public health regulations. Implementing these measures would help mitigate undue corporate influence, and align Thai lobbying practices with international best practices, thereby safeguarding public policy integrity and promoting a healthier food environment.

The ultra-processed food companies in Thailand actively promote brand awareness and visibility among consumers through a wide range of marketing strategies. These practices are similar to findings from a systematic review conducted by WHO, which revealed that food and non-alcoholic beverage companies in both high-income and middle-income countries employ diverse and creative marketing strategies [8]. These include endorsements by celebrities or athletes, the use of cartoon characters and mascots, promotional campaigns involving giveaways and branded games, co-branding with other popular products, as well as visually engaging designs using vivid colours, animation, and persuasive messaging. The review and accompanying meta-analysis found that such food marketing strategies are positively associated with increased consumption, preferences, choices, and purchase requests among children and adolescents. International experience provides important lessons for Thailand. For example, Chile implemented front-of-pack warning labels, comprehensive restrictions on child-directed marketing, and bans on sales of unhealthy products in schools, which collectively reduced children’s exposure to unhealthy food advertising [38]. Similarly, the UK has introduced restrictions on advertising high fat, sugar, and salt (HFSS) products on television and online platforms [39]. In Thailand, the Bureau of Nutrition (BON), in the Ministry of Public Health, has developed a draft Act to regulate unhealthy food marketing that affects children’s health [40]. The draft Act controls unhealthy food and beverage advertising in all channels, sales promotions, and any statement to indicate the value for money of unhealthy foods and beverages (e.g., sales tag). The draft Act precludes the sale of unhealthy foods at educational establishments and children’s centres, and includes provisions for labelling to signpost unhealthy foods [40]. Hence, BON should propose the draft Act to the Minister of Public Health and Thai Cabinet.

Some ultra-processed food companies in Thailand employ Sub-Area Licensing, a business arrangement in which the master franchisor delegates management authority over a specific geographical area to a local business entity or investment group, known as the Sub-Area Licensee. This model grants the local licensee the right to operate the franchise within a designated territory - typically outside major urban centers - and to further expand operations, including the authority to sub-franchise to smaller local operators under its supervision. Our findings are consistent with franchising theory, which identifies Sub-Area Licensing as a key strategy for regional, national, or international franchise expansion. It enables the parent company to reduce administrative and operational burdens while enhancing local market responsiveness and accelerating growth by empowering local partners with partial control over business development [41]. This strategy may directly affect the availability and accessibility of healthy food [42]. Importantly, such arrangements may influence the availability and accessibility of healthier food options [43], thereby raising public health concerns. To address this influence, policy measures could include stricter regulation of sub-franchise operations, incentives for offering healthier products, and monitoring mechanisms to ensure compliance with nutrition and health standards approaches that have been implemented in other countries to promote a healthier food environment [44, 45]. These strategies may be particularly relevant for Thailand and similar contexts to mitigate the impact of large food companies on dietary patterns and public health.

National ultra-processed food corporations in Thailand have engaged in labour and employment practices that undermine labour rights, including preventing union formation, dismissing employees who attempt to organise, and acquiring businesses in ways that dismantle existing unions. Additionally, some companies impose excessive working hours without adequate rest, fail to pay legally-mandated overtime, and deny regular days off. Our findings are consistent with those of the study in the European fast-food industry which found that a fast-food company has systematically resisted unionisation efforts in several European countries. The tactics included legal and administrative strategies aimed at preventing unions from gaining a presence within restaurant premises, thereby impeding workers’ right to organise [46]. These practices reflect broader patterns of wage violations and exploitative working conditions that are prevalent in the global food industry. For example, in the US, food production workers frequently face long hours with insufficient compensation, while many fast-food employees work under conditions of low wages and unpaid overtime, in violation of labour law [47]. Several European countries have implemented strong labour inspection regimes, mandatory reporting of working conditions, and legal protections for union activities, which have improved compliance and strengthened workers’ rights [48]. The US has also used targeted enforcement actions and labour advocacy programs to ensure compliance with overtime and minimum wage law [49]. Therefore, the Department of Labour Protection and Welfare, Ministry of Labour in Thailand can draw on international best practices in labour rights protection, including strengthening labour law enforcement, safeguarding and promoting unionisation, ensuring transparency in corporate labour practices, and engaging civil society and labour organisations in monitoring compliance. Implementing these measures could mitigate the influence of large food corporations on workers’ rights and foster fair and safe working conditions in the food industry.

The strength of this study is the integration of multiple sources of publicly-available data. In cases where publicly-available information was limited, the study was complemented by key informant interviews. These interviews helped provide additional context and validate information from documentary sources. The analysis was grounded in verifiable evidence, and conducted using a systematic and rigorous approach, with the aim of generating reliable academic findings that can inform further research and policy response to the conduct of food corporations. Nevertheless, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, particularly given the complexity and political sensitivity of the issues involved.

There are several limitations of our study. Firstly, publicly-available documents are limited in both scope and depth. Gathering information on certain strategies - particularly those involving political parties, high-ranking government officials, academics funded by food corporations, policy lobbying, product research and development, employment contracts, and workplace conditions - remains a significant challenge. The absence of such data may lead to an underestimation of the extent of food industry influence on public policy. These data gaps may be due to the lack of disclosure by government agencies and food companies, especially private limited companies, which are not legally obligated to publish detailed operational information. Another contributing factor is the limited transparency regulations in Thailand. Unlike several other countries, Thailand does not have policies requiring government bodies to disclose interactions or partnerships with food corporations. As a result, monitoring the political strategies and activities of food companies remains difficult, and the findings presented in this study may not fully capture the breadth and depth of corporate influence.

Secondly, our study limited its document review to 2019–24, meaning some data reflect past events while others are more recent. This was due to limited public disclosure. The frequency of each of the seven corporate strategies was not counted; any occurrence - single or repeated - was recorded as ‘present.’ As such, comparing the frequency of strategies is not possible and may not be accurate. Finally, in-depth interviews may be subject to respondent bias or social-desirability bias. For example, some participants appeared to offer responses that aligned with socially-desirable norms, potentially influencing the authenticity of the data [50], especially on sensitive issues such as politics, business practices, or government operations [51]. Some key informants may withhold information or cite information that is difficult to access and verify. To address these limitations, the study also relied on publicly-available documents to enhance data credibility because the use of document analysis reduces the likelihood of response bias stemming from participants’ tendency to conform to socially-desirable norms [52]. Although company and individual names are reported, these references are limited to information already in the public domain. Accordingly, the analysis was directed at institutional and structural strategies, not personal intentions, thereby minimising potential reputational concerns.

Conclusions

Amid growing concerns about the role of ultra-processed food companies in promoting an unhealthy food environment leading to NCDs and influencing public policy, this study provides evidence on the strategies they use. These include a range of CDOH approaches similar to those used by the food, tobacco and alcohol companies in other countries. A CDOH monitoring system is recommended to identify entry points for action, alongside regulation and enforcement to ensure transparency and accountability across all sectors, including the ultra-processed food companies, academia, media organisations, and Thai government.
