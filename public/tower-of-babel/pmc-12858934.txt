A novel deep learning approach for intrusion detection in maritime radar networks
Authors: Md Mostofa Nurannabi Shakil, Md. Alamgir Hossain, Md. Samiul Islam, Nidadavolu Venkat Durga Sai Siva Vara Prasad Raju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In recent years, maritime radar networks have become essential for ensuring the safety and security of maritime operations. However, with the increased interconnectivity of these systems, they have also become vulnerable to cyber-attacks, posing significant risks to critical infrastructure. Traditional intrusion detection systems (IDS) often struggle to detect sophisticated and evolving attacks in real-time due to their reliance on manual feature extraction and shallow machine learning techniques. This research addresses this gap by introducing MARINERNet, a deep learning-based intrusion detection system designed specifically for maritime radar networks. The proposed system uses a novel architecture that integrates 1D convolutional layers, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and residual connections to automatically extract relevant features from raw radar network data, enhancing detection accuracy without manual intervention. MARINERNet is evaluated on both binary and multiclass classification tasks, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance. Specifically, the model achieves 98.52% accuracy for multiclass classification and perfect accuracy for anomaly detection (binary classification). The approach is scalable, capable of handling large datasets, and adaptable to real-time intrusion detection, making it suitable for deployment in dynamic radar environments. This research not only provides an effective solution for detecting intrusions in maritime radar networks but also contributes to the broader field of cybersecurity by offering a robust, deep learning-based approach that can be applied to other network systems.

Introduction

This section provides an overview of the growing cybersecurity challenges in maritime radar networks and introduces the motivation, scope, and contributions of this research. It also contextualizes the study within existing intrusion detection systems and highlights the novel aspects of MARINERNet.

Background and motivation

Maritime radar signal processing involves several critical operations that convert raw electromagnetic reflections into meaningful navigational insights. Radar systems emit radio frequency pulses and then process the reflected signals to detect objects, determine their range, velocity, and direction1. A fundamental technique in radar processing is Doppler shift analysis, which helps differentiate between moving and stationary targets by measuring frequency changes in the returned signals. This is crucial for identifying other vessels, buoys, or obstacles in real-time2.

Another important process is clutter suppression, which mitigates unwanted echoes caused by sea waves, rain, or atmospheric interference. Techniques such as Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) and adaptive filtering are widely employed to enhance signal clarity. Additionally, pulse compression techniques, using matched filtering, allow radar systems to achieve high resolution without increasing transmitted power, improving the detection of small or distant targets3,4.

These signal processing techniques form the foundation of modern maritime radar systems and are essential for reliable navigation and situational awareness, especially in dynamic marine environments where both safety and security depend on accurate and timely radar interpretation5.

Maritime intrusion refers to any unauthorized access, manipulation, or disruption of maritime navigation systems, particularly radar and communication networks, which compromises the safety, security, and operational integrity of maritime operations6,7. With the increasing interconnectivity of modern maritime radar networks, vessels and port facilities are becoming more reliant on real-time radar and sensor data to ensure safe navigation, collision avoidance, and monitoring of surrounding environments. While these networks provide critical situational awareness, their growing complexity and connectivity have also introduced significant cybersecurity vulnerabilities, making them susceptible to sophisticated cyberattacks that can manipulate radar data, disrupt operations, or mask the presence of vessels8,9.

The motivation for this research stems from the recognition that traditional intrusion detection systems (IDS) and conventional machine learning approaches are often inadequate for modern maritime radar networks10,11. These approaches typically rely on manual feature engineering and shallow models, which struggle to detect novel or evolving attack strategies in real-time. Additionally, the operational constraints of maritime environments such as high traffic volume, dynamic network conditions, and imbalanced data distributions, demand intrusion detection systems that are not only highly accurate but also scalable and capable of real-time deployment12,13.

Recent studies have highlighted the increasing frequency and sophistication of maritime cyberattacks, including Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks that disrupt radar communications, object manipulation attacks that add, remove, or relocate vessels on radar displays, and transformation attacks such as scaling, rotation, and translation of radar signals14,15. These threats underscore the urgent need for advanced detection mechanisms capable of automatically learning discriminative features from raw radar data without extensive manual intervention.

Maritime radar systems play a crucial role in ensuring the safety and security of vessels at sea by providing real-time situational awareness. These systems operate by emitting electromagnetic waves (radio waves) and analyzing the reflected signals from objects within their range, such as other vessels, landmasses, and environmental obstacles. The radar signals are processed to detect and classify targets based on their distance, speed, and direction. In maritime radar signal processing, raw radar returns are typically filtered, transformed, and analyzed using various techniques to ensure effective target detection and reduce interference from the environment6,16.

One key technique in maritime radar signal processing is Doppler processing, which allows for the measurement of the velocity of objects relative to the radar7. By analyzing the Doppler shifts in the reflected signals, the radar system can distinguish moving targets from stationary ones, which is particularly important for detecting moving vessels or obstacles in the vicinity of the ship. This processing is crucial for collision avoidance and navigation in congested waters. Additionally, clutter suppression is vital to improving radar performance. Radar systems are often affected by environmental noise such as sea waves, rain, or other non-target reflections (clutter). Advanced techniques like adaptive filtering and CFAR (Constant False Alarm Rate) algorithms are employed to reduce this clutter, ensuring that only relevant targets, such as other ships or obstacles, are detected and tracked4,17.

Another essential aspect of maritime radar signal processing is target detection and tracking. After noise and clutter have been filtered out, radar systems use detection algorithms such as matched filtering and moving target indication (MTI) to identify potential targets18. These algorithms assess the strength, position, and movement of detected objects. Following detection, tracking filters like Kalman filters or particle filters are employed to predict the future trajectory of targets based on their current movement. These tracking filters are essential for continuous monitoring and avoiding false positives in dynamic maritime environments. In addition, signal modulation techniques like pulse compression are used to enhance the radar’s resolution without compromising its detection range, allowing for better identification of smaller or distant objects19,20.

However, the integration of maritime radar systems into broader communication networks has increased their vulnerability to cyber threats. As radar systems become more connected, the risk of cyberattacks that could manipulate radar data or disrupt operations grows. For example, attackers exploit vulnerabilities in the communication protocols, causing disruption in radar operations or falsifying radar data. The need for robust and adaptive intrusion detection systems (IDS).

Challenges in maritime radar intrusion detection

Intrusion detection in maritime radar networks faces several key challenges. First, the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of radar traffic, including real-time signals and control messages across multiple protocols, creates complex and noisy data that is difficult to analyze accurately. Second, there is a class imbalance, as benign traffic dominates while attack instances such as DoS, object manipulation, and transformation attacks are rare, making detection of critical threats challenging. Third, traditional IDS and shallow machine learning approaches rely heavily on manual feature engineering, which is time-consuming and often fails to capture evolving or sophisticated attacks. Finally, ensuring real-time detection and scalability across diverse radar scenarios adds further complexity21–23. These challenges necessitate an adaptive, automated, and high-performance solution capable of handling high-dimensional, imbalanced, and dynamic radar data, which is the primary motivation behind MARINERNet.

Objectives of the research and major contributions

The primary objective of this research is to develop MARINERNet, a deep learning-based intrusion detection system for maritime radar networks. The system aims to enhance detection accuracy by automatically identifying both known and novel cyberattacks, such as DoS, scaling, and object manipulation, while minimizing false positives and negatives. It automates feature extraction using 1D convolutional layers and squeeze-and-excitation blocks, ensuring real-time performance and scalability for large datasets. Additionally, MARINERNet supports both binary and multiclass classification tasks, making it adaptable for various operational requirements and providing a robust solution for securing maritime infrastructure against cyber threats.

Related works

The security of maritime radar networks has become a critical area of research due to the increasing reliance on digital radar systems for navigation, collision avoidance, and situational awareness. Traditional approaches to intrusion detection in these networks largely relied on signature-based methods and rule-based systems, which are inherently limited in their ability to detect novel or sophisticated cyberattacks. For example, conventional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) often monitor for known attack signatures such as Denial-of-Service (DoS) or simple data manipulations, but fail when attackers deploy novel transformations like scaling, rotation, or translation of radar objects. These limitations have motivated researchers to explore machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques, which can automatically learn patterns from raw network traffic and identify anomalies without predefined rules.

Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of shallow machine learning methods for radar network security. Techniques such as Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) have been applied to network traffic features extracted from radar systems, achieving moderate success in detecting DoS attacks and simple anomalies. However, these methods typically require manual feature engineering, which is both labor-intensive and domain-specific. Moreover, they struggle with high-dimensional radar data and imbalanced datasets, common in realistic maritime environments where benign traffic dominates and malicious activity is sparse19,24,25. Hossain et al.23 proposed a semi-supervised learning method for marine radar security using a Self-Training Classifier with Random Forest, achieving 99.99% accuracy on the RadarPWN dataset. While effective in controlled settings, the method has key limitations: it overfit to the dataset, lacks testing under diverse maritime conditions, and faces challenges in real-time deployment and integration with existing radar systems.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to deep learning architectures, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), which can automatically extract hierarchical features from sequential or spatial data. For instance, CNNs have been effectively applied to classify radar signals and detect adversarial manipulations in network traffic without manual intervention. Similarly, RNNs and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have shown promise in capturing temporal dependencies in radar traffic, improving the detection of stealthy or time-dependent attacks26–28. These studies underscore the potential of deep learning to address both feature engineering challenges and complex attack patterns in maritime radar networks.

In addition to deep learning models, recent works have explored attention mechanisms and residual learning to enhance detection performance. Attention mechanisms allow models to focus on the most informative features, improving detection accuracy for subtle attacks, while residual connections help train deeper networks without suffering from vanishing gradients. Integrating these techniques has been shown to significantly improve both multiclass and binary classification performance in intrusion detection tasks29,30. However, most existing approaches have focused either on small-scale datasets or specific attack types, limiting their applicability to realistic maritime radar environments, which include a mixture of benign traffic and diverse attack scenarios.

Despite these advances, gaps remain in developing scalable, real-time intrusion detection systems capable of handling high-dimensional radar data while maintaining high detection accuracy across multiple attack types. Current models often lack adaptability to new or evolving attacks, and few provide both binary and multiclass detection capabilities in a single framework. This research addresses these gaps by introducing MARINERNet, a deep learning-based IDS that combines 1D convolutions, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and residual connections. MARINERNet is designed to automatically extract features from raw radar traffic, handle large datasets efficiently, and perform robust detection across both binary and multiclass classification tasks, making it suitable for deployment in operational maritime radar networks.

Dataset Preparation

The dataset used in this study is the RadarPWN dataset31, which is a specialized collection designed to evaluate the resilience of maritime radar systems against various cyberattack techniques. The dataset is derived from simulated maritime environments, including scenarios such as Rostock, Simple Estuary, and Santa Catalina. These environments represent different radar operating conditions captured during simulated maritime operations using tools like Bridge Command, OpenCPN, and the Radar Attack Toolkit (RAT).

To provide an intuitive understanding of the input data used for training and evaluation, we include visual examples of each attack class from the RadarPWN dataset. As shown in Fig. 2, the dataset includes seven distinct attack types: Denial of Service, Scaling, Rotation, Translation, Object Addition, Object Removal, and Object Relocation. Each attack alters the radar display in a specific way, simulating realistic and potentially hazardous adversarial scenarios.

For instance, DoS attacks (Fig. 2b) obscure the radar signal using random or blank noise, while scaling (Fig. 2c) and rotation (Fig. 2d) affect the perceived distance and orientation of targets. More advanced attacks such as object addition, removal, or relocation (Fig. 2f–h) manipulate surrounding objects in a context-aware manner, mimicking deceptive tactics against navigation systems.

To ensure that the class distribution was maintained across both sets, stratified sampling was employed. Stratified sampling ensures that the proportions of each class in the original dataset are preserved in the training and test sets. Model architecture

MARINERNet is a deep learning-based intrusion detection system designed to automatically learn relevant features from raw radar network traffic data. Unlike traditional intrusion detection systems (IDS) that rely on manual feature engineering, MARINERNet utilizes a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) to perform feature extraction and classification directly from the raw input data. The model leverages a 1D convolutional layer for extracting sequential patterns from the data, squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks for recalibrating channel-wise feature responses, and residual connections to improve gradient flow and enable deeper architectures. Conv1D layers and feature extraction

Convolutional layers are pivotal in MARINERNet for automatically extracting hierarchical features from the radar network traffic data. Specifically, 1D convolutional layers (Conv1D) are used, which are well-suited for sequential data such as time-series or radar signal data. The Conv1D operation slides a kernel/filter across the data, computing the dot product between the filter and a local region of the input, effectively capturing local dependencies.

Squeeze-and-excitation block

The squeeze-and-excitation (SE) block is a key component introduced to enhance the representational power of the network by adaptively recalibrating the feature map channels. It works by focusing on the most relevant features in the feature map, suppressing the less informative ones. This is achieved through two main steps: squeeze and excitation.

i. Squeeze: Global average pooling is applied to the output of the convolutional layers to summarize the feature map into a channel descriptor. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X\in\:{R}^{H\times\:W\times\:C}$$\end{document} be the output from the convolutional layers, where H is the height (number of samples), is the width (number of features), and is the number of channels (feature maps). The squeeze operation computes the channel-wise global average:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{c}=\frac{1}{H\:\times\:W}\sum\:_{i=1}^{H}\sum\:_{j=1}^{W}{X}_{i,\:j,\:c}$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{c}$$\end{document} is the average of channel across all spatial locations.

ii. Excitation: The channel descriptors are passed through two fully connected layers to learn a set of attention weights that indicate the importance of each channel. The excitation step applies the learned weights to recalibrate the original feature map:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{c}={X}_{c}\cdot\:\sigma\:({W}_{2}\cdot\:ReLU({W}_{1}\cdot\:{s}_{c}\left)\right)$$\end{document}

This recalibration allows the network to focus on more informative channels and suppress less useful ones, improving the detection capability of the model.

Residual connections

Residual connections, introduced in ResNet, are used to facilitate the training of deep networks by mitigating the vanishing gradient problem. These connections allow the network to learn the residual mapping instead of the direct mapping, which simplifies the learning process. In MARINERNet, residual connections are applied after certain convolutional layers to enhance gradient flow and improve convergence.

Given an input and an output () from a convolutional block, the residual connection creates an identity shortcut:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y=F\left(X\right)+X$$\end{document}

Where, F(X) is the output of the convolutional block, is the input to the block, and is the output after adding the identity shortcut.

This simple addition ensures that the network can learn the residual mapping \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left(X\right)$$\end{document} instead of the full transformation, which helps in training deeper networks. By adding the shortcut connection, the model is encouraged to focus on learning the difference between the input and output, rather than the entire transformation, making it easier to optimize the weights.

The residual connection helps the MARINERNet model to remain efficient and avoid degradation in performance as the number of layers increases, especially when dealing with complex radar network data.

The MARINERNet architecture is a sophisticated design that leverages powerful techniques such as 1D convolutional layers, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and residual connections to build an effective and efficient intrusion detection system. These components work together to automatically learn hierarchical features, focus on the most relevant features through attention mechanisms, and enable the training of deeper models without encountering optimization issues. This combination of techniques ensures that MARINERNet delivers state-of-the-art performance in detecting intrusions in maritime radar networks.

Model training and optimization

Model training and optimization are critical stages in developing any deep learning model, as they ensure that the model not only learns from the data but also generalizes well to unseen instances. In this research, the training process for the MARINERNet model involves the use of modern optimization techniques and hyperparameter tuning to maximize its performance for the task of intrusion detection in maritime radar networks. This section provides a detailed description of the training process, hyperparameter tuning, loss function and metrics, and model evaluation strategy.

Training process

The training process for MARINERNet involves the iterative optimization of the model’s parameters (weights and biases) based on the input data. The dataset is divided into training and validation sets, with the training set used to update the model parameters, and the validation set used to monitor the model’s performance during training. The goal is to minimize the loss function while improving the model’s accuracy in classifying both benign and attack traffic.

Data Preparation

The training data is preprocessed, including scaling of numerical features and encoding of categorical variables as described in Sect. 3.2. This ensures that all input data is in a suitable form for the neural network. The training and validation sets are split in a stratified manner, ensuring that the class distribution is preserved across both sets.

Model training

The MARINERNet model is trained using backpropagation and the Nadam optimizer. Backpropagation involves calculating the gradients of the loss function with respect to each model parameter and adjusting the parameters to reduce the error between predicted and true labels. The optimizer uses an adaptive learning rate to adjust the model’s parameters efficiently. Specifically, Nadam is a combination of Adam and Nesterov momentum and helps the model converge faster. The general training update rule is.

Batch size and epochs

A batch size of 32 samples was chosen for training, allowing the model to update the weights frequently and speed up convergence. The model was trained for up to 200 epochs to ensure sufficient training. Early stopping is employed to monitor the model’s validation loss and stop the training once the validation performance stops improving, preventing overfitting.

Callbacks

The EarlyStopping callback halts the training if the validation loss does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs. The ReduceLROnPlateau callback is used to decrease the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 if the validation loss stagnates.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameter tuning is essential for optimizing the model’s architecture and training process. It involves searching for the best combination of hyperparameters that lead to the highest model performance. Key hyperparameters tuned in this study include:

Learning rate

The learning rate controls the step size taken during the optimization process. A higher learning rate might lead to faster convergence but could overshoot the minimum, while a lower learning rate ensures gradual progress but take more epochs to converge.

Number of Filters and Kernel Size: In the convolutional layers, the number of filters and the kernel size were fine-tuned. The number of filters determines how many features maps the network will learn at each convolutional layer. The kernel size controls the size of the local region the filter will examine, affecting how much local context is considered when extracting features.

Dropout rate

Dropout is a regularization technique used to prevent overfitting. The dropout rate specifies the proportion of neurons to be randomly deactivated during training. Experimenting with different dropout rates allows the model to generalize better to unseen data.

Batch size

The batch size determines how many samples are processed before the model’s internal parameters are updated. In this study, a batch size of 32 was used, but this was also fine-tuned during the optimization process.

The tuning was performed through grid search or random search over predefined parameter spaces. Once the best hyperparameters were identified, the model was retrained using those optimal settings.

Although MARINERNet achieved perfect accuracy (100%) in the binary classification (anomaly) task, several factors within the model design and training process help ensure that this result is not a symptom of overfitting but rather reflects genuine generalization.

First, the model incorporates regularization techniques, including dropout layers and batch normalization, which prevent the network from memorizing training examples and instead encourage it to learn generalizable patterns. Dropout randomly deactivates neurons during training, reducing interdependency among features, while batch normalization stabilizes the learning process by normalizing intermediate activations.

Second, early stopping and learning rate reduction on plateau callbacks were employed. Early stopping monitors the validation loss and halts training once no improvement is observed for a fixed number of epochs, thus preventing the model from continuing to train beyond its generalization point. The adaptive learning rate adjustment further refines convergence by lowering the step size when improvement stagnates. Third, stratified data splitting ensured that both the training and test sets contained representative distributions of benign and attack classes, maintaining data diversity and minimizing data leakage. The model’s validation accuracy consistently mirrored training accuracy during the process, confirming that the observed perfect performance extended to unseen samples rather than being restricted to the training data.

Finally, the relatively compact model architecture and 1D convolutional structure contribute to a lower risk of overfitting by limiting model complexity while maintaining high expressive power through residual and squeeze-and-excitation connections. These design decisions collectively enhance MARINERNet’s robustness and ensure that its exceptional binary accuracy reflects a well-generalized model rather than overfitting.

These complementary approaches illuminate how MARINERNet distinguishes between benign radar operations and diverse intrusion scenarios, thereby enhancing transparency, accountability, and user confidence in real-world deployments.

Discussion of results and insights

The results obtained from the MARINERNet model’s performance in both multiclass and binary classification tasks demonstrate its remarkable efficacy in detecting intrusions and benign activities in maritime radar networks. The model achieved outstanding accuracy, with both multiclass and binary classification tasks reaching high precision, recall, and F1-scores. These metrics indicate that MARINERNet not only classifies intrusions accurately but also minimizes both false positives and false negatives, making it highly reliable for real-time intrusion detection.

In the multiclass classification task, MARINERNet displayed an impressive accuracy of 98.52%, with near-perfect performance across most of the attack types, including Benign, DoS, and Addition. The confusion matrix highlighted the model’s strong ability to differentiate between various types of intrusions, with only a small number of misclassifications. However, certain attack types, like Scaling and Translation, showed slightly lower performance, with recall and precision scores dipping marginally compared to other classes. This suggests that while the model is highly effective, there is potential for improving its detection capabilities, particularly in recognizing more subtle attack types.

When the task was simplified to a binary classification (Benign vs. Attack), the model’s performance was flawless, achieving perfect precision, recall, and F1-scores of 1.0000 for both classes. The confusion matrix confirmed that the model correctly classified all instances without any false positives or false negatives, demonstrating its robustness and reliability when distinguishing between benign traffic and attack activities. The ROC curves for the binary classification task also highlighted MARINERNet’s strong discriminatory power, with AUC scores nearing 1.0 for both classes, further confirming the model’s effectiveness in detecting attacks with minimal error.

The training process for MARINERNet showed efficient convergence, with both training and validation accuracy quickly reaching near-perfect values and training loss dropping significantly in the early epochs. This rapid convergence suggests that the model is able to learn the underlying patterns in the data efficiently, even for complex, high-dimensional radar network traffic.

One notable insight from the results is the model’s scalability and its ability to handle large datasets. The parameter analysis indicated that the number of trainable parameters remained manageable despite the model’s complexity. The model’s efficiency in training and performance on large datasets highlights its suitability for deployment in real-time systems where both speed and accuracy are critical.

In terms of model optimization, the use of squeeze-and-excitation blocks and residual connections has contributed significantly to enhancing feature selection and improving gradient flow, which likely played a key role in the model’s ability to achieve high performance with relatively fewer parameters compared to other deep learning approaches. Additionally, the Nadam optimizer played an important role in speeding up convergence and minimizing the loss function effectively during training.

The time complexity of the proposed MARINERNet model is primarily dominated by the computations within the Conv1D layers, particularly the dilated convolution with 64 filters, which process each input flow of radar features sequentially. While the squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks and residual connections enhance feature representation and gradient flow, they contribute minimally to the overall computational cost. The compact architecture of MARINERNet contributes to its scalability. With approximately 66,000 total parameters (about 260 KB), the model size is significantly smaller compared to typical deep learning-based intrusion detection systems that have millions of parameters. This small footprint ensures that MARINERNet can be deployed on systems with limited computational resources, such as CPU-only embedded devices in maritime radar systems. Despite its modest size, the model efficiently processes large datasets, supporting high-volume radar traffic scenarios without exceeding memory or processing limits.

The core convolutional operations, combined with residual blocks and squeeze-and-excitation layers, have a linear relationship with input size. This scalability enables the model to be adapted for real-time intrusion detection in high-traffic environments, such as maritime radar networks, without compromising its performance.

Empirical evidence further supports the scalability claim. When tested on a standard CPU accelerator in Google Colab, the binary classification model reached convergence in approximately 4,763 s, while the multiclass version took 5,606 s with around 180,000 samples for training and validation. This demonstrates the model’s ability to train efficiently even with large datasets. Inference time per sample remains under a few milliseconds, allowing the model to handle real-time traffic in operational environments.

Moreover, MARINERNet is adaptable to increasing data volumes and new classes. The modular architecture of the proposed model, including 1D convolutional layers, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and residual connections, allows for easy adjustments when adding new attack types or operational conditions. For instance, adding new classes only requires modifications to the final dense layer, with a negligible increase in the number of parameters (about 600 extra parameters). This demonstrates functional scalability, where new attack types can be incorporated without requiring a full re-architecture or significant computational overhead.

The architecture of the model is designed with deployment in mind, particularly in resource-constrained maritime radar environments. Given the constraints of radar systems, such as limited memory, real-time processing needs, and tight power budgets- MARINERNet’s small size, efficient resource usage, and linear scaling behaviour make it ideal for large-scale deployment. Whether applied to a single radar station or distributed across multiple vessels, the model can scale with minimal additional resource burden, ensuring consistent and efficient performance across different environments.

Overall, MARINERNet offers a robust and efficient solution for intrusion detection in maritime radar networks. While the model performs exceptionally well across most classes, further fine-tuning could improve its performance in detecting rarer or more sophisticated attack types, such as Scaling and Translation. Additionally, the model’s performance in real-time environments could be further optimized for low-latency detection, making it highly adaptable for deployment in dynamic and critical radar network environments.

Conclusion

In this research, we introduced MARINERNet, a novel deep learning-based intrusion detection system designed specifically for maritime radar networks. The study addressed the growing concern of cybersecurity within maritime operations by leveraging deep learning to detect both benign traffic and various cyberattacks such as Denial-of-Service (DoS), scaling, rotation, and addition attacks, among others. Through the use of the RadarPWN dataset, which includes realistic radar network traffic under both normal and adversarial conditions, we demonstrated MARINERNet’s exceptional performance in both multiclass and binary classification tasks, achieving near-perfect accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores. The results showcase the model’s robustness, particularly its ability to differentiate between benign and malicious network behavior with minimal misclassification, even under imbalanced data conditions. The significance of these findings lies in MARINERNet’s high potential for real-time deployment in dynamic radar environments, offering a scalable, reliable, and efficient solution to detect intrusions in maritime radar networks. This research underscores the importance of applying deep learning to cybersecurity in critical infrastructure systems, ensuring the safety and resilience of modern maritime operations against increasingly sophisticated cyber threats.

Future research in this domain could explore the integration of reinforcement learning (RL) to enable MARINERNet to adapt and evolve with emerging attack strategies in real-time, enhancing its detection capabilities. Additionally, incorporating edge computing technologies could facilitate low-latency, on-site intrusion detection, improving the system’s performance in resource-constrained maritime environments while ensuring faster response times for anomaly mitigation.
