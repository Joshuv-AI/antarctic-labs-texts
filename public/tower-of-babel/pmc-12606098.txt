Enabling multirotor UAVs to perch, land and detach with standard propeller guards
Authors: Yuying Zou, Haotian Li, Yunfan Ren, Fanze Kong, Wei Xu, Yihang Li, Yixi Cai, Fu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread adoption of multirotor uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) is hindered by limited flight time and degraded stability to wind disturbances. Perching, inspired by birds, offers a solution by saving energy and maintaining stability. However, current perching methods often rely on heavy mechanisms, have limitations on surface materials, and obstruct vision sensors. In this study, we propose a process to enable multirotors to perch, land, and detach, all with standard propeller guards. This approach eliminates additional mechanisms, avoids sensor interference, and significantly reduces energy consumption by leveraging the ceiling effect. In over 150 perching and detaching tests with various surface orientations and materials, the approach achieved a 100% success rate and saved 25% - 100% energy consumption compared to hovering. It also improved positioning accuracy by 50 times under strong winds by ensuring stable environmental contact. We demonstrated in real-world environments, showcasing the autonomous perching and detaching ability using only onboard sensing and computation.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering, Energy conservation

Introduction

The endurance and stability of uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) are crucial for various applications, such as inspection, scanning, and monitoring. Long flight times enable extended missions, allowing UAVs to cover larger areas and perform more comprehensive tasks without the need for frequent recharging or battery replacement. Stability ensures accurate sensing and enhanced safety in adversarial weather conditions, which is essential for obtaining reliable data and preventing hazards to the UAV and its surroundings. These factors are particularly important in environments where UAVs must operate autonomously or close to human habitats.

Obtaining force support from environmental structures can effectively conserve UAVs’ energy and stabilize their position, mimicking the behavior of birds or insects resting on branches or other elements in nature. This behavior, known as “perching”, reduces the required propeller thrusts due to the force contribution from the environmental structure, hence saving energy. Moreover, perching reduces the degree of freedom of UAV motion by making contact with the environment, which enhances stability even under disturbances.

One common approach to realizing UAV perching is integrating grippers that resemble the claws of animals, facilitating perching by gripping objects1–15. The grippers usually consist of actuators to control grasping (for perching) or releasing (for detaching). Such designs often bring extra weight to the UAV, as well as introduce complexities in production and control. Furthermore, although energy consumption is reduced during perching, the increased weight leads to more power consumption in normal flights.

Surface-attaching is another way to realize UAV perching, encompassing methods utilizing adhesive materials16–23, suction cups24–27, micro-spines28–30, magnets31–36, and VELCRO37–39. These approaches are more lightweight than using grippers, but they each impose specific constraints on the surface. For example, using adhesive materials16–23 requires particular surface materials to produce the appropriate adhesion force. Suction cups24–27 are limited to smooth surfaces, micro-spines28–30 necessitates rough surfaces, magnets31–36 need metal surfaces, and VELCRO37–39 demands prior modification (i.e., attaching VELCRO) of the surface. Moreover, repeated perching and detaching can wear out the adhesive materials over time17–21. Some methods also involve additional components, such as trusses21, servos25, pumps26, and springs30, which further increase the design complexity.

Another critical drawback of both the gripper-based and surface-attaching-based approaches is their severe interference with the UAV mission. The grippers1–11,13,14 or attaching materials17–21,23,26,28,32,34–39 are usually placed at the bottom of UAVs, which will interfere with the mission sensors (e.g., cameras) that are also placed at the bottom on most existing UAVs40–42. Moreover, these approaches often require the UAVs to perch with their ventral side attached to a structure, which will obstruct the field of view (FoV) of the mission sensor at perching. The blockage of the sensor FoV will cause the mission to be interrupted, which is disadvantageous to the overall task efficiency.

We propose to perch multirotors on surfaces using solely their propeller guards as the supporting structure (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Video 1). The benefits of our method include using existing parts (i.e., propeller guards) to perch, eliminating the need for extra mechanisms that cause increased weight and power consumption to the UAV’s normal flights (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the perching surfaces can be any flat surfaces with arbitrary orientations, including the level ground (Fig. 1c). The composition is further simplified by removing the landing gear. Additionally, our method enables the multirotor to perch on surfaces with less limitation on materials and roughness, and provides the flexibility for the multirotor to detach freely at any time (Fig. 1d). Besides, the ventral side consistently faces outward, ensuring no view obstructions on mission sensors caused by environmental structures or UAV components such as perching mechanisms and landing gear. The entire FoV of the onboard sensor is preserved. Furthermore, during perching, the propellers remain in rotation, providing thrust to secure firm contact with the surface, thereby enhancing stability and positioning accuracy.

Our approach leads to significant energy saving during perching in contrast to hovering. At perching, the propeller is very close to the surface where the “ceiling effect” arises (Fig. 1b). The ceiling effect is an aerodynamic phenomenon43–50 that establishes a low-pressure area between the propeller and the surface right above, attracting the rotors of the UAVs more towards the surface and reducing power consumption when generating the same amount of thrust. Data indicates a power saving of 1.8 to 3 times due to the ceiling effect44–48, which contributes to the main power reduction in our approach. Moreover, when perching on walls or slopes with our approach, friction between propeller guards and the surface reduces the required propeller thrusts for counteracting gravity, which further improves energy efficiency.

We conducted extensive analysis and experiments to verify our approach compared to our previous conference paper51. In more than 150 tests with various surface orientations and materials, the approach achieved a 100% success rate in both perching and detaching with a coherent planning and control strategy. We also accomplished 25–100% power saving compared to hovering through an automatic energy minimization method. Meanwhile, the quadrotor at perching improved the positioning accuracy by 50 times than normal hovering when in the presence of strong wind disturbances. Real-world demonstrations were carried out, showing the ability of our approach to accomplish autonomous perching and detaching based solely on onboard sensing and computing devices.

Results

Optimal thrust and energy

We analyzed the optimal thrust and energy of our perching approach (see Methods). To hold the multirotor position during perching, the propeller thrusts, surface normal forces, and the friction between propeller guards and the surface must interact to counteract the gravity (Fig. 3a), leading to a lower bound on the propeller thrust T. Subject to the lower bound, we minimize the power consumption P, which is related to the propeller thrust by P∝TT from the momentum theory52. This leads to a convex optimization problem that can be solved by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions53. The optimal solutions break into three cases, with each further breaking into more sub-cases (Fig. 3b).

As shown in Fig. 3b–d, the optimal solutions, including the collective thrusts produced by upper propellers Tu* normalized by the gravity mg, the normalized lower propellers collective thrust Tl*mg, and the normalized total power PN=Tu*mgTu*mg+Tl*mgTl*mg, are influenced by both the environmental factors (i.e., the surface orientation angle θ and the coefficient of static friction μs) and the quadrotor’s geometry (i.e., the ratio lhlv). For a given quadrotor (with fixed lhlv), the normalized total power PN monotonically decreases with the friction coefficient μs (Fig. 3d), while with θ, the power initially increases and then decreases (Fig. 3d). Inspecting the effect of quadrotor geometry, it is seen that the power PN decreases with the lhlv (Fig. 3c).

To determine which configurations of surface orientation θ and friction coefficient μs that lead to power saving with our perching approach, we compared the total power PN and that required for hovering, calculated as 0.50.5+0.50.5=0.707. Given that the power model P∝TT did not incorporate the ceiling effect, the area where PN ≤ 0.707 signifies the power-saving area without considering the ceiling effect (see Fig. 3b). Upon considering the ceiling effect, which has been reported to increase thrust by a factor of 1.5 leading to more than 1.8 times power-saving44–48, the power-saving area with the ceiling effect corresponds to the region with PN/1.8 ≤ 0.707 (also depicted in Fig. 3b). Through comparing these two areas, it becomes evident that the ceiling effect further improves the power efficiency.

Quadrotor platform

We designed quadrotor prototypes to verify our perching approach. The quadrotors (Fig. 4a) follow a common quadrotor configuration54,55, featuring propeller guards for safety measures. The camera can be mounted at the bottom of the quadrotor. To simplify the design, the propeller guards together with motor supports and the main frame, are all integrated. The propeller guards are further enforced with soft rubbers, which serve as dampers and increase friction during perching. The distance between the propellers and the upper surface is minimized to 4 mm to enhance the ceiling effect (Fig. 4b). Our prototypes used off-the-shelf motors (T-MOTOR F60 pro), an electronic speed controller (ESC) (T-MOTOR F60A), and propellers (GEMFAN 513D propellers). All motors are linked to the ESC with DShot1200 communication protocol, which enables the bidirectional rotation of motors in flight, commonly referred to as 3D mode. Thus, the quadrotor can produce bidirectional thrust, which is essential for executing agile aerobatic maneuvers during perching and detaching maneuvers.

We constructed three different variants of the perching quadrotor (Fig. 4c). The “DEFAULT” weighs 1.05 kg and has a 35-mm lever arm lv. The “HEAVY”, equipped with a metal payload, weighs 1.27 kg with the same lever arm lv. The “TALL”, designed with an extended frame, maintains the 1.27 kg weight but a longer lever arm lv of 65 mm. The detailed comparison of these variants’ parameters is shown in Supplementary Table 2. In sum, the “DEFAULT” and “HEAVY” have equal lengths of lever arms but differ in weight, while “TALL” and “HEAVY” have different lengths of lever arms but identical weights.

Perching and detaching

To verify the perching and detaching ability of our approach, we test the three variants of UAV prototypes (“DEFAULT”, “HEAVY”, and “TALL”) on surfaces with different orientation angles. The surface is a transparent acrylic board, measuring 120 cm in length and 90 cm in width, and is positioned at orientation angles of 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180° (Fig. 5). For each combination of the surface orientation and UAV prototype, ten repetitive perching and detaching experiments were conducted, leading to a total number of 150 tests. During the experiments, the surface and the quadrotor’s poses were measured by a VICON motion capture system56.

Perching and detaching process on an acrylic board.

a Composite images and the trajectories of one perching experiment. b Composite images and the trajectories of one detaching experiment. c Actual perching locations of all ten repetitive experiments of the three quadrotor prototypes. The expected perching location is the center of the board. Images (i)–(v) in each group are experiments at surface orientations of 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°, respectively.

Experimental results show that we achieved 100% success in both perching and detaching operations in the 150 tests. Fig. 5a, b showcase one successful perching and detaching maneuvers of the “DEFAULT” quadrotor on each surface orientation angle, affirming the quadrotor’s capability to perch to and detach from a surface without the need for additional mechanisms or human intervention (Supplementary Video 2). These results validate the effectiveness and reliability of our approach. Fig. 5c depicts the perching locations of the 150 tests, where the expected perching location is the center of the acrylic board. The mean distance from the center is 4.7 cm, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean distance of (4.1, 5.2). The standard deviations of the perching location were measured as 3.4, 3.4, 9.8, 4.5, and 4.3 cm for surface orientation angles of 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°, respectively. The slightly looser distribution at 90° perching is attributed to the impact of gravitational forces when the quadrotor is tilted to 90° to align with the surface. Overall, the high concentration near the target center of all 150 perching locations demonstrates a high degree of repeatability of our approach.

Power consumption

We verified the energy efficiency of our perching approach by investigating the power consumption of three quadrotor configurations and three surface materials. The quadrotor configurations were the “DEFAULT”, “HEAVY”, and “TALL” mentioned above (Fig. 4c). For the surface materials, we used cotton material, acrylic board, and anti-slip tape, whose fiction coefficients were measured as 0.89, 2.07, and 2.49, respectively (Fig. 4d). For each quadrotor configuration, we measured the power consumption at surface orientations of 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°. For each surface material, we did the orientations of 45°, 90°, and 135°. The orientations 0° and 180° were not measured for different surface materials, because the friction variation caused by surface materials does not change the power consumption in these situations (see Fig. 3d).

Attaining the minimum power of the quadrotor perching on a surface requires the knowledge of the surface friction coefficient in principle (Fig. 3d). However, such information is not known to the UAV in practice. To address this issue, we proposed a minimum thrust searching method (see Methods). The method progressively reduces the propeller thrusts, following the pattern of the optimum thrusts, until a detachment from the surface is detected (Fig. 6a, b). The method enables the quadrotor to attain the minimum power consumption at perching without any prior knowledge of the surface parameters.

The power consumption of different quadrotor configurations perching on an acrylic board is depicted in Fig. 6c. In normal hovering, the average power required for the “DEFAULT”, “HEAVY”, and “TALL” quadrotors was reported as 352, 483, and 488 W, respectively. In contrast, when perching on a 0° surface, the power consumption was measured as 214, 272, and 273 W for the respective quadrotors, saving ~39, 44, and 44% power consumption compared to hovering. Since perching at 0° surfaces leverages no friction for power-saving, these power savings were all attributed to the ceiling effect, demonstrating the effectiveness of perching with the ceiling effect in improving efficiency while maintaining simplicity without the need for complicated mechanisms.

When perching at 90°, the power consumption for the three quadrotors is 134, 161, and 192 W, respectively, with energy savings of 62, 67, and 61%. The more energy saving at the perching surface of 90° than that of 0° is because of the further power reduction due to the exploitation of friction (see Fig. 3d). When the quadrotor perches on the acrylic board inclined at 135° or 180°, the quadrotor was completely held by friction and surface normal forces, and all the motors were turned off, resulting in negligible power consumption due to the onboard electronics. When perching at 45° on the acrylic board, the power consumption for the three quadrotors is 223, 286, and 290 W, respectively, with energy savings of 37, 41, and 41%. The power saving in this case is lower than that of 0° and 90° perching due to the force distribution factors, such as the thrusts, surface normal forces, and friction, which result in higher thrusts demanded in this particular orientation (see Fig. 3b, d). However, the power saving is still significant due to the leveraging of the ceiling effect. Comparing the different quadrotor configurations, we notice that the “HEAVY” consumes more power than “DEFAULT” because of the increased weight, and that the “TALL” consumes more power than “HEAVY” due to the decreased ratio lhlv. Both phenomena agree with our previous theoretical analysis (Fig. 3c).

Next, we investigate the power saving at three different surface materials shown in Fig. 4d. Figure 6d shows the power consumption of the “TALL” quadrotor perching on the three types of surfaces with orientations of 45°, 90°, and 135°. It is evident that lower friction (μs = 0.89) leads to higher power consumption at perching. When perching at 45°, power consumption on the three surfaces (with friction coefficients μs of 0.89, 2.07, and 2.49) amounts to 366, 290, and 288 W, respectively, leading to energy savings of 25, 41, and 41% in comparison to the hovering state. Similarly, in the case of 90° perching, the power consumption is 274, 192, and 155 W, respectively, resulting in energy savings of 43, 61, and 68%. It is noteworthy that even though perching at 90° with a small friction coefficient (μs < 1) requires a larger thrust compared to hovering (see Fig. 3b, d), the power consumption is still lower due to the ceiling effect. When perching at 135° on the low-friction surface, the motors need to remain operational to maintain a stable perch, consuming 40 W of power.

In sum, we examined a total number of 21 perching configurations, with three surface friction coefficients (μs = 0.89, 2.07, 2.49) and three quadrotor configurations (i.e., the “DEFAULT”, “HEAVY”, and “TALL”). In all the cases, the quadrotor consumed lower power than hovering, with power savings of 25–100%. The lowest power saving was attained at 45° perching with μs = 0.89, while the highest power saving was attained at 135° and 180° perching. The results demonstrate that the exploitation of the ceiling effect and contact friction leads to effective power reductions at perching. We finally compiled a comprehensive list of the theoretical minimum thrust for all these cases (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). The actual throttles and power consumption in experiments are compared to re-scaled Tu*, Tl*, and PN in Supplementary Fig. 1. The theoretical results are consistent with the experimental results, affirming the reliability of our minimum thrust searching process.

Stability under disturbance

Our approach enables the UAV to establish firm contact with the surface, improving stability in the presence of disturbances. To verify this ability, we conducted disturbance tests using a fan (Fig. 7). The fan was positioned 1-m away from the quadrotor and capable of generating a wind speed of 5 m/s (Fig. 7a). Both hovering and perching tests were performed, with the fan being turned on for a duration of 15 s. When the wind was applied, the quadrotor at hovering showed obvious displacement and attitude changes, with a root-mean-square position error of 0.055 m. In contrast, during perching, the quadrotor remained stable with a significantly reduced position error of 0.001 m, improving the positioning accuracy by 50 times. We extended the test to surface orientation angles of 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°, where the positioning errors were also reduced by at least 50 times in all cases (Fig. 7d). These results show that our approach effectively improves the UAV’s stability and positioning accuracy when perching.

Real-world demonstration

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in real-world scenarios (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Video 3). The UAV prototype “DEFAULT” is equipped with a T265 camera at its bottom, which has a 163° diagonal FoV57. The camera captures monitoring videos of the environment, as well as providing visual odometry. The visual odometry is then combined with inertial measurements from the flight controller for positioning58. The demonstrations took place in three places, a roadside, a building entrance, and a construction site. Prior to executing perching maneuvers, the quadrotor was manually piloted to a position ~1 m away from the designated perching targets. Subsequently, the quadrotor planned perching trajectories toward 90° walls situated to its left at the specified distance. Minor position errors within a range of a few centimeters existed during the manual piloting process. The quadrotor successfully perched and detached in all three scenes, with energy savings of 65, 62, and 50%, respectively, compared to hovering. Moreover, in all the tested cases, the onboard camera maintains perfect clear visibility without any occlusion caused by any parts of the UAV. These results validate the ability of our approach to perch and detach on differential materials with onboard sensing and computation, improving energy efficiency and preserving the entire sensor FoV.

Discussion

In this section, our primary focus is on stating the major contributions compared to existing studies, particularly our previous conference paper51. We also discuss limitations and outline potential future works here. We provide a thorough discussion on additional mechanisms weight on UAV, different perching and detaching methods, and the adaptability of our approach in Supplementary Notes. Due to space limitations, readers may refer to the Supplementary Notes for a more in-depth discussion.

In our previous conference paper51, we have conducted preliminary experimental verification, related to controlling the quadrotor to achieve and maintain abnormal attitudes and reaching the target positions with the ventral side outward. The perching was achieved with ducted propellers, and the UAV could not detach from the surface. In this current work, we showed the ability to both perch and detach by standard propeller guards, with multiple UAV configurations on different surface orientation angles and materials. Furthermore, we have comprehensively addressed and unified the trajectory planning problems for both perching and detaching. The inclusion of detaching trajectory planning is essential for real-world applications. We further conducted optimal thrust and energy analyses and developed an automatic energy minimization method without any prior knowledge of the contact surface friction or external sensor. In addition to indoor experiments, real-world demonstrations are carried out, where the quadrotor completes perching and detaching based solely on onboard sensing and computing devices.

Another method comparable to ours is presented in ref. 22, in which the quadrotor also perches with the upper side towards ceilings and walls, thereby also exploiting the ceiling effect for energy saving. However, the perching method differentiates substantially. First, the custom mechanism in ref. 22, comprising adhesive pads and a revolute joint in between pads, was not available on existing quadrotors, limiting its adaptability and bringing extra weight and complexity to the quadrotor. Besides, the quadrotor in ref. 22 was demonstrated for perching on ceilings and walls only, the ability to perch on inclined surfaces at other angles, which was reliably achieved by our method, was not demonstrated. Finally, the perching process in ref. 22 involves pressing and exerting preload to the adhesive pads. The perching process takes more than 10 s while our approach exploits the reversible propeller thrust to perch the UAV agilely, taking less than 2 s for perching.

While our perching method demonstrates effectiveness, certain limitations persist with our method. For instance, our method does not warrant a 100% energy saving when compared to methods using grippers1–15, adhesive materials16–21, suction cups24–27, micro-spines28–30, magnets31–36, and VELCRO37–39. In some perching scenarios (for instance, perching at a surface with less than 90° orientation angles), the motors have to remain operating during perching to maintain firm contact with the surface, requiring continuous power consumption and potentially producing noise. The efficiency of energy savings can vary depending on the geometry of the UAV and the friction of the perching surface material. Another limitation is that a flat surface area larger than the multirotor size is essential for successful perching, as curved or uneven surfaces may impede the process. Moreover, the rubber on propeller guards could be worn after long-term usage and repeated perching, resulting in reduced friction and weakened cushioning.

In the future, experiments could be expanded to more complex or unstructured environments, including scenarios with moving surfaces and diverse outdoor conditions. More real-world tasks are expected to be achieved, such as automatic surface detection and surveying during perching.

Methods

Multirotor UAV model

The UAV is modeled based on the standard multirotor dynamics59, except that the thrust direction can be reversed. The ESC, T-MOTOR F60A, is able to interpret both positive and negative commands, enabling the motors to rotate in either direction through the DShot1200 communication protocol. Consequently, the propellers can generate normal thrust or reverse thrust. When reversing the direction, the motor often suffers from a delay, which is much larger than the response delay when rotating in a single direction60–64. If part of the motors on a multirotor are reversing directions while the rest are not, the drastically different motor response could cause unbalanced moments, severely degrading the multirotor control performance. To avoid such an issue, we limit the four propellers to produce thrusts in the same direction in practice, either all pointing upward or all pointing downward at the same time with respect to the UAV body.

Perching and detaching process

The target perching surfaces can vary in their orientation angles. Surfaces like ceilings and inclined roofs typically have zero to small orientation angles as depicted in Fig. 5a(i and ii). Walls have large orientation angles near 90° as shown in Fig. 5a(iii), where the UAV mainly relies on friction to overcome gravity. Slopes and grounds possess greater orientation angles relative to the UAV’s hovering attitude, as illustrated in Fig. 5a(iv and v).

In general, the perching process consists of three phases (Fig. 9a, b): an initial approach phase using forward thrust, a subsequent decelerate phase employing reversed thrust, and a final contact-establishing phase with high forward thrust again. The forward thrust assists the UAV to quickly approach the surface, while the reverse thrust slows down the UAV, which is critical to preventing impacts with the surface. To minimize the response delay when switching motor directions, low throttle values are enforced before and after the switching. The second phase terminates when the UAV is within 5 cm of the target, where the contact-establishing phase is triggered. In the contact-establishing phase, the UAV specifies high forward thrust (60%) to make firm contact with the surface without bouncing off.

When perching at 0° and 45° (shown in Fig. 5a(i–ii)) with short perching distance, the first phase can approach the surface with appropriate terminal speed for perching, making the second phase unnecessary in practice.

Flipping trajectory planning

In some situations, it becomes necessary for the UAV to perform a 180° flip without actually perching to the ground. As depicted in Fig. 9c, the trajectory for flipping has two phases with a phase of forward thrust and a phase of reverse thrust. The switching of thrusts occurs at the top of the arc. We adopt the same optimization formulation in Eq. (1a) for the flipping trajectory. In the first phase, the UAV flips from a normal hovering state sstart and progresses towards an intermediate state with position pmid, velocity vmid, minimum positive thrust τmin, and an upright posture (zb,mid is aligned horizontally), as illustrated in Fig. 9c. The boundary conditions for the first phase are therefore identical to those in Eq. (2). In the second phase, the UAV completes the maneuvers with reverse thrust, ending in a reversed hovering state send as presented in Fig. 9c. Therefore, the initial conditions for the second phase are the same as the initial conditions in Eq. (3), while the terminal state is direct send. This trajectory is applicable for both forward and backward flips, assisting transitions between two hover attitudes.

Trajectory tracking control

The trajectory planning provides the desired position, velocity, and acceleration in the world frame. The trajectories can then be mapped to the UAV system state (p, v, R) and control input (τ, ω) by differential flatness transform, in which R is the orientation of the UAV. Since we are using bidirectional thrust, for each desired acceleration from the trajectory, there are two combinations of desired thrust and attitude: one involving positive thrust and a normal attitude, and the other involving negative thrust and a reversed attitude. The UAV is forced to choose the desired attitude that aligns closer to its current attitude as the reference, avoiding unnecessary flips. The obtained trajectory of full state (p, v, R) and control input (τ, ω) is then tracked by an on-manifold model predictive controller (MPC)66. This particular MPC design has been specifically chosen for its suitability in handling large attitude changes and executing highly aggressive trajectories. Notably, the thrust constraint in the MPC is extended to include negative thrust such that we could have bidirectional thrust in the control input. This allows for the implementation of a unified controller capable of trajectory tracking with bidirectional thrust, ensuring coherent control of the UAV throughout its flight.

Automatic minimum thrust searching process

The optimal thrust and energy (Eqs. (12–14)) require to know the surface friction coefficient μs precisely. However, the surface friction is often unknown in practical UAV applications. Being able to search for the minimal thrust, hence power, without any prior knowledge of the surface friction becomes crucial. In response to this necessity, we propose an active minimal thrust searching method based on the observations in the previous section that, in all cases, the optimal thrusts Tu* and Tl* are either identical or Tu*>Tl*. This process starts with the same values of Tu and Tl that ensures the multirotor to perch. Then, it gradually decreases Tu and Tl at the same rate, keeping Tu = Tl, until the contact loss is about to occur. Finally, it freezes Tu and continues to reduce Tl until a contact loss is about to occur again. As shown in Fig. 6a, the presented searching process will lead to the optimum solutions across all cases. The minimal thrust searching process outlined in ref. 22 shares similarities with our approach, involving gradually reducing thrusts until contact loss is encountered. However, our strategy implements several steps to ensure achieving optimal thrusts and energy, based on our mentioned theoretical analysis.

To implement the above minimum thrust searching process, we directly specify the motor throttles, denoted as Tup and Tlow for the upper and lower motor throttles, respectively. After establishing contact with high motor throttles, both Tup and Tlow were gradually decreased in small decrements of 1% per 0.5 s (Period A, Fig. 10b). When contact loss occurred, the current throttle value was marked as Tup,min, the minimum throttle for upper motors, and both Tup and Tlow were increased by 10% to regain contact. Then, Tup and Tlow reduced again until reaching Tup,min+3% (Period B). The addition of 3% in throttle is implemented as a safety measure to ensure secure contact even in the presence of disturbances. Subsequently, the upper throttle Tup was frozen at Tup,min+3%, and Tlow was further reduced with 1% decrements until the second contact loss occurred (Period C). When contact loss happened again, the current throttle value of the lower motors was marked as Tlow,min, the minimum throttle for lower motors, and Tup and Tlow were increased to Tup,min+10% and Tlow,min+10% for recovering the contact. Finally, Tup and Tlow reduced to Tup,min+3% and Tlow,min+3%, respectively (Period D). In certain cases, contact loss did not occur, and the throttle is reduced to zero without the 3% margin.

A key requirement in the minimum thrust searching process is the detection of contact loss. To do so, we rely on the angular rate data obtained from the inertial measurement unit (IMU) onboard the UAV. The IMU is part of the flight controller and provides accurate and sensitive measurements of the UAV’s state. In particular, the angular rate data from the IMU has a pronounced fluctuation when the UAV loses its contact with the surface in the minimum thrust searching process, as presented in Fig. 10b, c. When the UAV is in firm contact with the surface, the angular rate measurements fluctuate within a small range. On the other hand, unstable contact with the perching surface leads the UAV to vibrate, resulting in large angular rate fluctuations that can be easily detected by a pre-defined threshold. As shown in Fig. 10c, when perching at 0° surface, the contact loss can be detected within a few millimeters. In this example, the contact loss is detected at t1 = 1.423 s within a negligible 1.1 mm displacement of the UAV. The maximum displacement is 1.5 mm, and contact is reestablished at t3 = 1.487 s. This detecting method guarantees precise detection and rapid recovery. Another example of minimum thrust searching process at a 90° perching is presented in Supplementary Video 4. Figure 10b presents the thrust searching process of the “TALL” quadrotor perching at 0° and 90°. Figure 10b(i) presents the data obtained during the perching experiment with the final throttle values recorded as Tup = Tlow = 36%. On the other hand, Fig. 10b(ii) displays the final throttle of Tup = 28% and Tlow = 22%. The equality or inequality of Tup and Tlow, as well as their final outcomes, are dependent on various factors such as the orientation angle θ, surface friction coefficient μs, and the lever arms lv and lh, as discussed earlier. The throttles of the upper and lower motors throughout the searching process are also presented in Fig. 6b. The throttle reduction patterns align with the proposed method demonstrated in Fig. 6a, showcasing the coherence of the search process and its outcomes.
