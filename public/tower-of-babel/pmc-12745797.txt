MLKL/retromer axis controls PD-L1 recycling to compromise antitumor immunity during VCP inhibition–induced necroptosis
Authors: Yang Wang, Zi-Jia Huang, Nan-Nan Yu, Jun-Ze Liang, Yong-Hui Zeng, Qian Zhao, Yi-Ping Zhou, Yu-Jie Zeng, Zheng-Hua Sun, Shuixing Zhang, Qing-Yu He, Jing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTumor cells exploit programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) for immune evasion. The dynamic trafficking of PD-L1 within intracellular compartments can be regulated by cell stress or death signals, which impacts the therapeutic benefits. However, the mechanisms underlying this process remain largely undetermined. Here, we find that valosin-containing protein inhibition (VCPi) induces necroptosis in colorectal cancer (CRC) by delaying the degradation of mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL). The accumulated MLKL recruits the retromer complex and TBC1D5 to inactivate Rab7A, which redirects PD-L1-loaded vesicles to the cell surface, thereby promoting immune evasion. Dual inhibition of VCP and the retromer complex potentiates immunotherapy efficacy in CRC, offering a promising combinatorial therapeutic strategy.

Membrane trafficking is essential for spatiotemporal distribution of protein in eukaryotic cells by coordinating the sorting and transport of membrane/cytosolic proteins to destinations for recycling, degradation, or secretion (1). This intricate network—spanning endocytosis, autophagy, and exocytic pathways—is frequently dysregulated in cancer. Critical immune regulators such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) undergo continuous vesicular trafficking through endosomal compartments, where they are sorted toward either i) plasma membrane recycling for surface display, ii) lysosomal degradation, or iii) extracellular vesicle (EV) encapsulation for immunosuppressive secretion (2). Notably, cellular stress responses—including necroptosis—have been shown to exploit these pathways, enhancing vesicular trafficking to confer survival advantages and therapy resistance (3, 4). Recent evidence indicates that accelerated endosomal recycling enhances PD-L1 surface expression, thereby driving immunosuppression (5, 6); meanwhile, EV-mediated cargo release provides an alternative escape mechanism during death-inducing stimuli (3). These observations suggest that certain cell death stresses may be inadvertently accompanied by PD-L1-mediated immune evasion as an adaptive response.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health issue, ranking second in cancer-related mortality worldwide (7). Only a subset of CRC patients currently benefits from immunotherapy. While inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) can enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockers (ICBs) and promote antitumor T cell responses, it may paradoxically activate cell-intrinsic PD-L1 signaling (8), leading to ICB resistance through unknown mechanisms. Deeper understanding of subcellular PD-L1 signaling is thus essential for designing effective combination therapies.

Valosin-containing protein (VCP/p97), a AAA+ ATPase chaperone, governs cellular proteolysis through endoplasmic reticulum–associated degradation and ubiquitin–proteasome system coordination (9, 10). Our previous work established its prometastatic role in lung cancer via NF-κB/MMP2 signaling (11), and recent studies link VCP inhibition to paraptosis (12) and ICD induction. This study reveals that VCP inhibition induces CRC necroptosis but concurrently triggers vesicular trafficking—accelerating EV release and retromer-mediated PD-L1 recycling to promote immune evasion. We establish mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) as a VCP substrate whose accumulation upon VCP inhibition recruits TBC1D5 to inactivate Rab7A, thereby suppressing lysosomal delivery and redirecting PD-L1 to the cell surface. Importantly, dual targeting of the retromer complex and VCP potently enhances anti-PD-L1 efficacy, offering a combinatorial strategy for CRC immunotherapy.

Results

VCP Plays an Oncogenic Role in CRC.

To investigate the clinical significance of VCP in various cancer types, we compared disease-specific survival for 31 cancer types and found that CRC exhibited the highest hazard ratio (HR) for VCP (HR = 3.32, P = 0.04, SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Indeed, CRC patients with high VCP levels had worse overall survival (HR = 1.87; P = 6.2e−6; SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Moreover, we determined the expression of VCP in 80 pairs of CRC tumor tissues and matched adjacent noncancer tissues by immunohistochemistry. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1C, 71.25% (57/80) of tumor tissues demonstrated high VCP expression, while 28.75% (23/80) of nontumor tissues exhibited high VCP expression. Collectively, these data illustrate that VCP plays an oncogenic role in CRC and that inhibition of VCP may be a potential therapeutic strategy for clinical trials.

Inhibition of VCP Suppresses CRC Viability But Improves Intracellular Vesicle Trafficking.

Indeed, knockdown of VCP by shRNA suppressed the proliferation of both HCT116 and HT29 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C). Moreover, we employed a specific VCP inhibitor (VCPi), CB5083 (13) and found that CB5083 dramatically decreased the cell viability of both HCT116 and HT29 cells in dose- and time-dependent manners (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E). We established the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model by using HCT116 cells and found that oral administration of CB5083 (30 or 60 mg kg−1) suppressed tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F and G), as indicated by the tumor growth curve (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H) and Ki-67 staining (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). Taken together, these data indicate that CB5083 effectively inhibits CRC growth both in vitro and in vivo.

Transmission electron microscopy images of ultrastructure of HCT116 cells demonstrated that CB5083 treatment induced the formation of giant vacuoles, especially after 24 h (Fig. 1A). We visualized the organelles including ER (mCherry-KDEL) and mitochondria (COXIV-TagBFP2) in HEK293 cells and found that CB5083 treatment induced significant dilation of the ER but tiny effect on mitochondria (Fig. 1B), suggesting that prolonged treatment of VCPi results in cytoplasmic vacuolization, a typical feature of nonapoptotic cell death. In HCT116 cells with short-term exposure of CB5083 (3 h, a time point without cell death), we observed an increased number of vacuoles containing intraluminal vesicle-like structure in the CB5083-treated cells (Fig. 1C), suggesting that VCPi promotes the generation of multivesicular endosomes (MVEs) before cell death. Cargo in the endolysosomal network can either be degraded in lysosomes or exported to the cell surface or extracellular space (14). CB5083 treatment reduced the colocalization of LAMP2 and p62 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), suggesting that VCPi reduces the cargo transport to lysosome. Regarding the critical role of MVEs in EV biogenesis (15), we thus tested the potential impact of VCPi on EV production. First, we knocked down VCP in HCT116 and HT29 cells and purified the secreted EVs from their culture supernatants. NanoSight particle tracking analysis (NTA) showed an increase in the number of particles (~200 nm) secreted from CRC cells after VCP knockdown (VCP-KD) (Fig. 1D). This increase in EV release was further confirmed by canonical exosome markers CD63 and TSG101 (Fig. 1E). Similarly, CB5083 induced a dose-dependent increase in EVs from CRC culture supernatants (Fig. 1F), which was corroborated by the upregulation of CD63 and TSG101 (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that VCPi induces EV release from CRC.

Time-Resolved Proteomics Profiles the Protein Alteration by CB5083.

To uncover how VCPi regulates vesicular trafficking and subsequent cell death, we performed DIA-based quantitative proteomics in CB5083-treated HCT116 cells at different time points (Fig. 2A), which were chosen based on the established kinetics of CB5083-induced protein accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and MBV formation (Fig. 1C). As a result, a total of 4,317 proteins across all samples were identified (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Principal component analysis and cluster analysis revealed that samples with 0, 1, and 3 h groups were clearly separated (Fig. 2B), indicating high reproducibility and consistent protein distribution across the three biological replicates within each group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Based on volcano plots analysis, a total of 730 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified between 1 h and 0 h groups, and 745 DEPs were identified between 3 h and 0 h groups (Fig. 2C). Among these overlapping DEPs, we focused on 200 DEPs that were upregulated by CB5083 in a stepwise manner (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Table S1). These proteins were significantly enriched in processes related to cell death, vesicle trafficking, and nucleic acid processing (Fig. 2E). Within the protein–protein interaction network associated with necroptotic cell death, we particularly focused on MLKL, a primary executor of necroptosis (16) that was recently implicated in vesicle trafficking (3) (Fig. 2F). To validate whether VCPi induces MLKL accumulation, we performed western blotting and found that MLKL protein levels were increased with CB5083 treatment in time and dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2 G and H), this effect was confirmed by other VCP inhibitors DBeQ and NMS-879 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). We noted that CB5083 did not affect MLKL mRNA levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Consistent with these findings, VCP-KD significantly increased MLKL protein level in both HCT116 and HT29 cells (Fig. 2I), confirming that VCP negatively regulates MLKL expression at the protein level.

VCPi Induces Necroptosis by Delaying MLKL Degradation.

VCP is a pivotal component of the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation system that is known for its ability to capture and accelerate its substrate for degradation (8); we thus speculated that VCP can interact with MLKL as substrate to facilitate its proteasomal degradation. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) demonstrated that VCP bound to MLKL in HCT116 cells (Fig. 3A). To map the binding region of VCP and MLKL in detail, we generated two truncated mutants of VCP, including N-terminal deletion mutant (ΔN, with a deletion of amino acids 1 to 187) and C-terminal deletion mutant (ΔC, with a deletion of amino acids 761 to 806). Co-IP assays demonstrated that C-terminal deletion of VCP weakens its interaction with MLKL (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the C-terminal domain of VCP is crucial for its interaction with MLKL. Indeed, treatment of CB5083 and MG132, not Baf-A1, displayed a comparable effect on MLKL accumulation (Fig. 3C), suggesting that VCP-associated ubiquitin–proteasome degradation, rather than the lysosome pathway, is accountable for endogenous MLKL degradation. The protein stability of MLKL was increased in CRC cells with CB5083 treatment (Fig. 3D) or with VCP-knockdown (Fig. 3E). As MLKL is initially recognized as necroptosis executor (17), we thus tested the effect of VCP inhibitor on necroptosis induction. Inhibition of VCP by CB5083 promoted the formation of necrosome (the MLKL–RIPK1–RIPK3 complexes) in HCT116 cells (Fig. 3F), as well as the phosphorylation (Ser358) of MLKL (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). Furthermore, CB5083 treatment showed significant induction of the aggregated MLKL-GFP foci on the cell membrane of both HEK293 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 3G), which display comparable effects with TZ (TNF-α+zVAD-fmk, the known necroptosis inducer). Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining assay showed that most of the cell death induced by CB5083 in CRC cells can be classified as necrosis (PI+/Annexin V- population), and the number of necrotic cells (PI+ population) was significantly elevated in a dose-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). It is reported that necroptosis-induced cell damage leads to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including ROS outburst and high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1) release (16). Here, we observed CB5083 triggered ROS burst (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). It also induced the upregulation of HMGB1 in CRC cells, supernatants (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D), and tumor tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). We further found that CB5083-induced necrotic cell death and proliferation inhibition were rescued by MLKL inhibitor necrosulfonamide (NSA) and MLKL-knockdown (Fig. 3 H–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S5F), confirming that MLKL accumulation is required for CB5083-mediated necroptosis. In addition to NSA, z-VAD-FMK (apoptosis inhibitor), Baf-A1 (autophagy inhibitor), and ferrostatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor) had no significant effects on restoring cell viability in HCT116 cells inhibited by CB5083 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). Indeed, CB5083 treatment and VCP-knockdown significantly enhanced the TZ-induced cell death and proliferation inhibition, as compared to single treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 H–J). Collectively, these results indicate that VCP inhibitor triggers MLKL-mediated necroptosis in CRC.

VCPi Triggers Necroptosis But Induces CRC Immune Escape by Promoting PD-L1 Trafficking to the Cell Surface.

The induction of necroptotic cell death leads to the release of DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1), which attracts immune cells into the tumor microenvironment and elicits antitumor immune response (18). We thus determined the antitumor immune response in mice treated with CB5083. CB5083 treatment alone or combination with anti-PD-L1 antibody only exhibited modest antitumor effect in immune-competent mice bearing MC38 tumors (Fig. 4 A–C). Treatment with CB5083 alone, or CB5083 in combination with anti-PD-L1 increased the proportion of CD86+CD11c+ immune cell populations (Fig. 4D), while did not promote the activation of intratumoral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and (Fig. 4 E and F). We observed an obvious increase of PD-L1 on the surface of cancer cells in CB5083-treated mice (Fig. 4G), suggesting that the antitumor immune response induced by CB5083 is impeded due to the unexpected upregulation of PD-L1. Indeed, our flow cytometry results showed that different VCP inhibitor (CB5083, DBeQ, and NMS-879) treatment and VCP knockdown significantly improved the expression of surface PD-L1 in CRC cells (Fig. 4 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), with only a minor increase in the total abundance of PD-L1 but no change in its mRNA level (Fig. 4 J and K and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). We noted that PD-L1 was low in EV fractions from both HCT116 and HT29 cells, as compared to another EV factor HMGB1 (Fig. 4 J and K). These findings demonstrate that CB5083 induces necroptosis in CRC; however, VCPi-induced PD-L1 upregulation confers the ability for immune evasion on CRC tumors, blunting the response to immunotherapy in vivo.

PD-L1 is a transmembrane protein known to be targeted to MVEs for lysosomal degradation, exosomal secretion, or recycling to the plasma membrane (19). We found that CB5083 treatment improved PD-L1-mCherry localization to the CD63-positive MVEs (Fig. 4L). To investigate the effect of VCPi on MVEs, we employed the constitutively active Rab5-Q79L mutant, which induces formation of large endosomes with a mixed morphology between early endosome and MVEs. We observed that CB5083 treatment increased the transport of PD-L1 to the limiting membrane of MVEs (Fig. 4M), indicating that VCPi accelerates PD-L1 trafficking by directing it toward the MVE membrane. Strikingly, upon CB5083 treatment, MLKL was recruited to the membrane and colocalized with CD63 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), suggesting a potential link between CB5083-induced MLKL accumulation and vesicle trafficking. Knockdown of MLKL in HCT116 and HT29 cells reduced the number of secreted EVs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). In these MLKL-KD cells, CB5083 failed to induce EV secretion and membrane PD-L1 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F), indicating that MLKL is required for VCPi-induced EV release and PD-L1 trafficking.

CB5083 Induces TBC1D5 to Inactivate Rab7A and Reroutes PD-L1 to the Cell Surface via the Retromer Complex.

We next explored whether the VCP/MLKL pathway interrupts the vesicle trafficking toward lysosome and observed that the colocalization of PD-L1 and lysosome was decreased after CB5083 treatment or MLKL overexpression (Fig. 5A). Lysosomal inhibition by Baf-A1 restored EV secretion and membrane PD-L1 expression that were reduced by the MLKL-KD (Fig. 5 B–D); moreover, it augmented EVs and HMGB1 secretion in CRC cells with CB5083 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). Since Rab7A activity is required for the MVE-lysosome fusion (20), we thus tested whether CB5083 inactivates Rab7A. Notably, we found that MLKL was bound with Rab7A, and the interaction was boosted by CB5083 (Fig. 5E). GTP-agarose pull-down experiments revealed that the active Rab7A was decreased in cells treated with either CB5083 or MLKL overexpression (Fig. 5F). Using the dominant-negative Rab7A mutant (T22N) rescued the reduction in cell surface PD-L1 caused by MLKL-KD (Fig. 5G), suggesting VCPi-induced MLKL accumulation enhances EV secretion and membrane PD-L1 expression by inactivating Rab7A. Notably, we confirmed that CB5083 enhanced the recruitment of TBC1D5, but not other reported Rab7A GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), TBC1D2A, TBC1D2B, and TBC1D15 (21), to Rab7A and MLKL (Fig. 5H and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). TBC1D5 knockdown was able to reverse Rab7A inactivation under CB5083 treatment (Fig. 5I), suggesting that TBC1D5 is required for CB5083-induced Rab7A inactivation. Moreover, the CB5083-induced recruitment of TBC1D5 to Rab7A was restored by MLKL-KD (Fig. 5J), but not by knockdown of TBC1D2A, TBC1D2B, and TBC1D15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B), suggesting that MLKL is required for CB5083-induced recruitment of TBC1D5 to Rab7A, a process independent of other reported GAPs.

MLKL Regulates the Retromer Complex to Mediate PD-L1 Trafficking to the Cell Surface.

The membrane receptors can be transported to the cell surface by recycling endosomes (22) or the retromer complex (VPS35–VPS26–VPS29) (23). Here, we found that CB5083 increased the colocalization of PD-L1 with VPS35 (a retromer complex component), while this effect was restored by MLKL knockdown (Fig. 6A). Moreover, VPS35-KD abolished the expression of PD-L1 on cell surface increased by CB5083 (Fig. 6 B and C), while knockdown of Rab11A (a key regulator of endosomal recycling) could not achieve a similar effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B), suggesting that CB5083 increases PD-L1 trafficking via the retromer complex, but not recycling endosomes. Regarding the regulatory role of the retromer–TBC1D5 complex in Rab7A activity (24), we tested the binding possibility of MLKL with the retromer complex, indeed, that MLKL bound to Rab7A, as well as VPS35, VPS29, and VPS26 (Fig. 6D). The retromer complex was reported to control Rab7A activity through the VPS29–TBC1D5 interaction (24), here, CB5083 induces the recruitment of VPS29 and TBC1D5 to Rab7A, while MLKL-KD abolished this effect (Fig. 6E). Taken together, our findings indicate that VCPi regulates PD-L1 to the cell surface by the retromer complex, and MLKL is required for this progress by recruitment of the retromer–TBC1D5 complex to inactive Rab7A, preventing PD-L1 for lysosomal delivery. We also determined the clinical relevance of MLKL and VPS35 in CRC patients stratified by tumor membrane PD-L1 expression. CRC patients with PD-L1-high exhibited higher levels of MLKL and VPS35 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D), suggesting a positive correlation between MLKL/VPS35 and membrane PD-L1 in CRC.

VPS35 Suppression Blocks Membrane PD-L1 Expression and Enhances Immunotherapy with VCP Inhibitor.

We next determined the effect of VCPi on MLKL accumulation and membrane PD-L1 expression on 5 cases of CRC patient–derived organoids (PDOs). Indeed, CB5083 treatment increased the fluorescence intensity of MLKL, as well as the membrane PD-L1 (Fig. 7 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S10), supporting that PD-L1 cell surface expression is an effector response of CB5083-induced MLKL-associated necroptosis. Regarding the unsatisfactory immunotherapeutic effect with CB5083, we speculated that targeting the retromer complex to block membrane PD-L1 trafficking will improve the therapeutic effect. We thus examined the effect of Vps35 ablation in combination with or without CB5083 (i.g. 30 mg/kg) based on the MC38 model with anti-PD-L1 therapy (i.v. 100 μg/injection) (Fig. 7C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). The shVps35+CB5083+anti-PD-L1 group (triple-combination) demonstrated superior anticancer effects than the CB5083+anti-PD-L1 group (Fig. 7 D and E). In line with the inhibitory effect on tumor growth, triple-combination treatment significantly increased the proportions of intratumoral DC cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7 F–H), as well as prolonged survival compared to other groups (Fig. 7I). Overall, these results suggest the therapeutic potential of combining retromer complex inhibitor with CB5083 in improving the immunotherapeutic effect of CRC.

Discussion

Necroptosis inducers hold significant promise for triggering ICD to enhance immunotherapy efficacy, primarily through triggering tumor necroptosis and the release of DAMPs to recruit antitumor immune cells (25, 26). Our work demonstrates that VCPi induces robust necroptosis in CRC, accompanied by ROS outburst and HMGB1 release—a definitive ICD signature. However, despite its inherent tumoricidal effect, VCPi exhibited modest antitumor effect in combination with anti-PD-L1 in vivo due to concomitant EV secretion and PD-L1 surface trafficking. Through DIA-based quantitative proteomics, we identified MLKL as a substrate of VCP, inhibition of VCP delays MLKL proteasomal degradation. The accumulated MLKL recruits GTPase-activating protein TBC1D5 to inactivate Rab7A, thereby redirecting PD-L1-containing vesicles to retromer-mediated recycling and plasma membrane delivery. This molecular hijacking explains the paradoxical immunosuppression observed during VCPi-induced necroptosis (Fig. 7J).

Although MLKL is well established as the terminal executor of necroptosis via RIPK1-RIPK3-mediated phosphorylation and membrane disruption (27), our findings reveal its noncanonical role in endosomal trafficking. While MLKL-driven necroptosis can theoretically establish an inflammatory tumor microenvironment (28), CB5083 treatment unexpectedly triggered MLKL-dependent vesicle trafficking redirection, as MLKL-KD abrogated both EV release and PD-L1 surface translocation. The accumulated MLKL recruits TBC1D5 to inactivate Rab7A—a master regulator of endolysosomal fusion—thus redirecting MVEs toward plasma membrane docking rather than lysosomal degradation. Notably, PD-L1 was predominantly sorted to CD63+ limiting membranes instead of ILV interiors, suggesting selective cargo loading that warrants further investigation.

The continuous recycling of PD-L1 and its subsequent repopulation on the cell surface may underlie cancer immune escape (29) or resistance to ICB therapy (30). Although PD-L1 was reported to distribute on tumor cell surface by Rab11A-mediated recycling (31), while upon VCP inhibition, retromer complex (VPS35–VPS26–VPS29), but not Rab11A, plays a major role in rerouting PD-L1-loaded vesicles to the cell surface—a process dependent on MLKL. Given the regulatory role of VPS29–TBC1D5 interaction in Rab7A activity (26), we proposed MLKL binds with the retromer ternary complex and recruits TBC1D5 via subunit VPS29, forming a complex that inactivates Rab7A and overrides Rab11-dependent recycling. Our findings reveal how pathological MLKL accumulation co-opts this machinery for immune evasion. It appears that the MLKL–retromer–TBC1D5 complex forms upon VCP inhibitor–mediated cell stress, but whether or not this also occurs in other contexts warrants further investigation.

Regarding the limited therapeutic efficacy of combination therapy using VCP inhibitor and anti-PD-L1 (32), the addition of VPS35 deletion dramatically improved the anticancer effect. This was achieved by blocking CB5083-induced PD-L1 recycling, displaying enhanced intratumoral DC, CD4+/CD8+ T cell infiltration. Although high levels of PD-L1 in tumors predicts response to ICB, stress-induced PD-L1 upregulation frequently compromises its therapeutic efficacy. Our work proposes that targeting both VPS35 and VCP could enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy, providing a promising avenue for CRC treatment.
