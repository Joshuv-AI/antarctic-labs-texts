Use of STOPPFrail Medications Following Receipt of the Danish Terminal Grant: A Nationwide Descriptive Study
Authors: Daniel Pilsgaard Henriksen, Thomas Øhlenschlæger, Ann‐Cathrine Dalgaard Dunvald
Published: 2026

Introduction

Patients with limited life expectancy represent a population where care needs change over time. Therapeutic goals shift, and the rationale for many medications changes, especially for long‐term preventive therapies, where time‐to‐benefit exceeds the remaining years of life [1]. The STOPPFrail criteria provide a validated, explicit list of medication classes considered potentially inappropriate in frail older adults with limited life expectancy, intended as a practical aid to deprescribing [1].

Recent Danish data from care home residents demonstrate that use of medications listed in STOPPFrail is widespread, with 88% using at least one such medication [2]. Population‐level data in patients with a formally documented short life expectancy are sparse. The Danish Terminal Grant (TG) scheme identifies such a cohort nationwide.

Healthcare utilisation trajectories at the end of life are largely comparable between cancer and non‐cancer terminal conditions in Denmark [3], yet palliative care has traditionally been more developed for patients with cancer.

We aimed to describe the use of STOPPFrail medications among TG recipients stratified by cancer history.

Methods

This nationwide register‐based study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [4].

The TG is granted administratively by the municipality upon application from the patient's physician, who certifies short life expectancy (weeks to months) and cessation of curative treatment [5]. The grant runs for 1 year and is available to patients in both home and care home settings. We used nationwide prescription data from a source population of all Danish adults (≥ 18 years) with at least one redeemed prescription for chronic disease medication between 1995 and 2022, defined by an ATC code list adapted from the Rx‐Risk Comorbidity Index [6]. From this population, we identified an incident cohort of all adults registered under the TG scheme (patient type code 99) between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2022. The index date was defined as the date of the first TG redemption.

Individual‐level data were obtained by linking the Danish National Prescription Registry [7], the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) [8], the Civil Registration System and the Danish Education Register using the unique Danish personal identification number.

Baseline covariates were assessed at index date. Education was categorised according to Statistics Denmark definitions. Multimorbidity burden was assessed using the Danish Multimorbidity Index [9], a weighted score based on 39 chronic conditions. Cancer status was defined as any cancer diagnosis (ICD‐10: C00‐C97, D37‐D48) recorded in the DNPR within 5 years before the index date. Total medication count and STOPPFrail classes were calculated from redemptions in the 6 months before index.

We assessed redemption of drugs belonging to the STOPPFrail version 2 criteria [1], using the ATC‐based registry operationalisation published by Mose et al. [2]. This operationalisation covers the drug‐specific criteria identifiable from dispensed prescriptions and yields the 17 medication classes reported here; criteria that cannot be operationalised in prescription data, such as the general criteria on indication and tolerability, are not included.

Demographic characteristics were presented as total numbers and percentages for categorical values and medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. We presented demographic characteristics in total and grouped by registered cancer and non‐cancer history.

Our primary outcome was the proportion of patients redeeming at least one STOPPFrail medication after the index date. Secondary outcomes were prevalence proportions for each of the 17 medication classes, the proportions redeeming ≥ 2 and ≥ 3 classes, and the median number of classes. No established cut‐off exists for the number of concurrent STOPPFrail classes; ≥ 2 and ≥ 3 were chosen pragmatically to indicate how many patients redeemed drugs from more than one class.

Follow‐up ran from index until death, migration or end of study; observation periods therefore varied with survival. Median survival was calculated from index to death.

Analyses were performed in Stata Version 18.

According to Danish law, ethical approval is not required for register‐based studies using anonymised data.

Results

The cohort comprised 84 819 individuals with a recorded TG between 2018 and 2022. After excluding 5668 non‐incident individuals and 1359 who did not reside in Denmark at least 5 years up to their index date, we included 78 676. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Median age was 78 years (IQR 70–86), and 50.4% were female. A cancer diagnosis within 5 years before index was registered in 64.9% (n = 51 027) of patients. The median Danish Multimorbidity Index score was 5 (IQR 4–7). Median survival from the index date was 30 days (IQR 6–102), with 43 days (IQR 13–114) for patients with a cancer history and 9 days (IQR 2–64) for patients without.

Overall, 69.0% of patients redeemed at least one STOPPFrail medication after receiving a TG (Table 2). The median number of STOPPFrail classes redeemed post‐index was 1 (IQR 0–3), 45.7% redeemed ≥ 2 classes, and 28.4% ≥ 3 classes. The most frequently redeemed classes were proton‐pump inhibitors (PPI) (34.9%), antipsychotics (34.4%) and oral corticosteroids (OCS) (32.3%). When stratified by cancer history, 78.4% redeemed at least one STOPPFrail medication (cancer) compared to 51.8% (non‐cancer).

Discussion

In this nationwide study, 69% redeemed at least one STOPPFrail medication after receiving a TG. However, the medication profile reflects limited life expectancy, dominated by symptom control rather than disease prevention.

Our findings complement the recent study by Mose et al. on Danish care home residents [2]. While they found that preventive medications like antihypertensives and lipid‐lowering agents were dominant at the time of care home admission, they observed a shift towards symptom‐relieving drugs in the last 4 months of life. Our study, with a median survival of 30 days, confirms this. The most frequently redeemed classes in our cohort were PPIs, antipsychotics and OCS.

The high prevalence likely reflects a limitation of the STOPPFrail criteria in the last weeks of life. As noted by Mose et al., antipsychotics and PPIs are frequently initiated at the end of life for palliative indications such as delirium, nausea and gastroprotection [2]. Our cohort had substantially higher OCS use than the care home population [2], likely attributable to both the high proportion of patients with a cancer history and the much shorter remaining survival in our cohort compared with care home residents at admission. OCS are frequently used in the last weeks of life to improve wellbeing, most often described in cancer but likely used for similar indications in non‐cancer conditions. A significant portion of ‘inappropriate’ use is therefore likely clinically justified. This questions the usefulness of STOPPFrail as a quality indicator in the last weeks of life. Applied mechanically, the list risks becoming a tick‐box exercise rather than a prompt for clinical judgement.

The lower use of STOPPFrail medications in the non‐cancer group may reflect a markedly shorter median survival from index (9 days), which could have limited the observation window for further redemptions. This may in turn reflect later recognition of the terminal phase, and thus later application for a TG, in non‐cancer conditions, combined with less developed palliative pathways [3]. These hypotheses warrant further investigation.

However, therapeutic inertia regarding purely preventive medication remains a potential concern. Despite a median survival of 1 month from index, 24.8% of patients redeemed antihypertensives, and 6.6% redeemed lipid‐lowering drugs. Continuation may be reasonable in patients with longer survival, and abrupt discontinuation immediately after receipt of a TG may not be acceptable to patients. We cannot separate these from patients close to death, in whom preventive benefit is absent. Version 2 of STOPPFrail explicitly frames deprescribing as a shared decision with the patient [1], and the TG may serve as a trigger for that conversation rather than for discontinuation as such.

A key limitation is the lack of clinical indication. Without knowing prescribing intent (palliative vs. preventive), we likely overestimate ‘inappropriate’ use for drugs with dual indications such as OCS and antipsychotics. The index date was the first TG‐redemption rather than administrative approval, excluding patients who died before redemption; our cohort therefore represents terminally ill patients actively engaging with pharmacotherapy. Cancer status from DNPR rather than cause of death may misclassify patients whose terminal condition differs from their cancer history. Observation periods varied with survival, limiting direct subgroup comparisons. Also, TG recipients represent a selected subset of all terminally ill patients in Denmark, and findings may not generalise to patients who do not receive a TG. Lastly, the TG code has not been validated in a validation study.

STOPPFrail medications are widely used among terminally ill patients, but the dominance of OCS, antipsychotics, and PPIs suggests palliative intent. Continued use of purely preventive drugs nevertheless warrants systematic medication review. The TG may serve as a useful trigger for structured medication review, and earlier identification of patients needing palliative‐oriented prescribing remains important for future work.
