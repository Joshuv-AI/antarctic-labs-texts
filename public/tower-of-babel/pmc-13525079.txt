The regulatory role of post-translational modifications in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yuan Ji, Bajin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disease characterized by mucosal inflammation limited to the colon, which may progress to pancolitis in severe cases. This debilitating condition significantly impairs patients’ quality of life and may result in long-term disability, representing a growing global public health concern. Despite extensive research, the precise pathogenesis of UC remains incompletely understood. Current evidence implicates a multifactorial etiology involving genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and gut microbiota imbalance. Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs), including phosphorylation, glycosylation, acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, citrullination, and palmitoylation, have emerged as key regulatory mechanisms in cellular biology. These modifications play pivotal roles in modulating protein activity, structural stability, protein–protein interactions, and intracellular signaling pathways. Furthermore, PTMs are involved in immune regulation and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the roles and mechanisms of various PTMs in UC pathogenesis, with an emphasis on their involvement in regulating immune function, epithelial integrity, and inflammatory signaling. Elucidating the contribution of PTMs to UC may offer novel insights into disease mechanisms and provide promising therapeutic targets for future treatment strategies.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, post-translational modifications, immune dysregulation, epithelial barrier dysfunction

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by relapsing inflammation localized to the colonic mucosa. Patients with UC may experience structural and functional changes in the colon due to the extension of disease towards the proximal regions, sometimes progressing to pancolitis, which severely impacts the patient’s quality of life and can result in long-term disability [1]. UC has emerged as a global public health concern, with an overall increasing incidence trend [2].

The pathogenesis of UC is intricately linked to intestinal inflammatory responses and is driven by multiple complex factors, primarily including immune dysregulation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, gut microbiota alterations, and genetic predisposition [3]. Immune dysregulation can be considered the key driving force behind intestinal inflammatory responses in UC pathogenesis. In UC, the balance between immune tolerance and immune defense maintained by innate immune cells is disrupted. Innate immune cells, such as macrophages, are activated by various external stimuli and produce a wide range of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-23 (IL-23) etc., triggering inflammatory responses [4–6]. Subsequently, adaptive immune cells, such as T cells, differentiate into distinct effector subsets through immune responses and secrete various pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) and interleukin-21 (IL-21) etc., sustaining inflammation (see Fig. 1) [7–10]. Another hallmark of UC is epithelial barrier dysfunction. When the gatekeeping function of the intestinal epithelium, including tight junctions and the mucus barrier, is compromised, bacteria, bacterial components (like lipopolysaccharide [LPS] and flagellin), and luminal antigens can penetrate the mucosal barrier (see Fig. 2), thus initiating and perpetuating inflammation. This triggers a vicious cycle in which luminal antigens activate mucosal immune cells, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which further damage the epithelium and compromise tight junction integrity [4, 11, 12]. Moreover, the progression of intestinal inflammatory responses is closely fueled by alterations in the gut microbiota [13–15]. The influence of genetic predisposition should not be underestimated [16, 17]. Although research on the pathogenesis of UC has made considerable progress, many questions remain unresolved. Specifically, the molecular mechanisms underlying immune dysregulation and epithelial barrier dysfunction have not yet been fully elucidated.

It is well established that post-translational modifications (PTMs) play crucial roles in maintaining protein stability, facilitating specific ligand receptor interactions, regulating molecular signaling pathways, and modulating a wide range of cellular functions [18]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that diverse forms of PTMs play pivotal roles in modulating the immune responses and contributing to the development of inflammatory diseases [19–22]. Classical post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination are well recognized for their critical roles in intestinal inflammatory diseases, particularly in modulating immune responses and contributing to epithelial barrier dysfunction [23–26]. However, the roles of PTMs in the pathogenesis of UC remain incompletely understood. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in elucidating the molecular mechanisms of PTMs in UC pathogenesis and explores their potential as novel therapeutic targets for UC.

The role of phosphorylation in the pathogenesis of UC

To regulate essential cellular processes and key signaling pathways, certain proteins undergo phosphorylation, a post-translational modification in which a phosphate group is added to serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues [18]. Phosphorylation is dynamically regulated by various kinases and phosphatases and represents the most well-characterized PTM involved in inflammatory responses associated with UC.

TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), a pivotal regulator of inflammatory signaling, can activate the downstream NF-κB pathway via autophosphorylation, thereby initiating inflammatory responses in the intestine [27]. Researchers have demonstrated that TAK1 and its downstream signaling intermediates involved in mediating inflammation are markedly activated in both male and female patients with UC [27]. In vivo experiments using Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS)-induced colitis models have shown that pharmacological inhibition of TAK1 significantly attenuates disease pathogenesis [27]. A subsequent investigation revealed that TAK1 undergoes autophosphorylation at threonine 187 (Thr187), a process dependent on its interaction with Raf kinase inhibitor protein (RKIP) [28]. However, the proinflammatory role of TAK1 in UC remains controversial. Some researchers suggest that TAK1 may exert a protective effect against colitis via phosphorylation at serine 412 (Ser412) [29]. Thus, more precise and comprehensive evidence is needed to delineate the exact role of TAK1 in UC pathogenesis.

Phosphorylation also plays a critical role in the dysregulation of the intestinal barrier. Some researchers have highlighted the role of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) in maintaining tight junction integrity [30]. The activation of FAK, which depends not only on its autophosphorylation but also on phosphorylation at Tyr576/577 by Src, promotes the recovery of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) recovery in Caco-2 cell monolayer [30]. Following treatment with the FAK inhibitor PF-228, FAK activity was significantly suppressed, resulting in increased epithelial barrier permeability and the disassembly of tight junctions [30]. These findings further underscore the critical role of FAK in maintaining and restoring intestinal epithelial barrier integrity through the regulation of tight junction protein assembly and distribution. However, it is unfortunate that data on FAK expression and phosphorylation levels in patients with UC are currently unavailable.

In addition to phosphorylation, dephosphorylation also plays a vital role in regulating the integrity and function of the intestinal epithelial barrier. Protein tyrosine phosphatase σ (PTPσ) is considered a positive regulator of epithelial barrier integrity, as its deficiency in PTPσ−/− mice leads to notable barrier defects [23]. The authors have demonstrated that during DSS-induced colitis in mice, the apical junction complex-associated protein ezrin displays abnormal localization at the plasma membrane, resulting in impaired intestinal barrier function. The altered localization is attributed to tyrosine dephosphorylation of ezrin at the Y353 or Y145 residues mediated by PTPσ [23]. As with FAK, data on changes in PTPσ expression levels in patients with UC are currently lacking.

The role of acetylation in the pathogenesis of UC

Acetylation, primarily regulated by acetyltransferases and deacetylases, can modulate protein-protein interactions by transferring acetyl groups from acetyl-coenzyme A to the ε-amino groups of lysine residues on target proteins [18]. A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that acetylation plays a pivotal regulatory role in modulating immune responses and the development of inflammatory diseases [31–34]. Notably, acetylation also contributes to the regulation of immune responses involved in the pathogenesis of UC.

Researchers have found that the expression level of lysine acetyltransferase 2B (KAT2B) is significantly downregulated in colonic epithelial cells within the inflamed regions of patients with UC [24]. This downregulation has been shown to impair the anti-inflammatory function of intestinal epithelial cells, notably by suppressing IL-10 production [24]. This effect is closely linked to KAT2B’s role in regulating the transcription of cell signaling pathway through histone acetylation [35]. Although lysine 9 and 14 on histone 3 (H3K9 and H3K14) are among the most reported acetylation targets of KAT2B [36], in vitro experiments involving KAT2B inhibition revealed no changes in the acetylation levels at these sites. Interestingly, researchers identified a novel acetylation target lysine 5 on histone 4 (H4K5), whose acetylation level strongly correlated with KAT2B expression following its suppression [24]. This observation led to the hypothesis that KAT2B-mediated acetylation of H4K5 may represent a histone modification specific to intestinal epithelial cells. Moreover, in vitro knockdown of KAT2B resulted in a significant decrease in H4K5 acetylation at the IL-10 promoter region, providing further support for this hypothesis [24]. Nevertheless, the current evidence remains insufficient to definitively establish H4K5 as the primary histone acetylation site dysregulated by KAT2B in the pathogenesis of UC. Additional studies are required to validate this proposed mechanism.

Deacetylation has also been implicated in the development of UC. Immunohistochemical analyses have revealed that the expression of Silent Information Regulator 1 (SIRT1), a well-established deacetylase, is significantly downregulated in the colonic epithelium of patients with UC [37]. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that reduced SIRT1 expression leads to hyperacetylation of heat shock factor protein 1 (HSF1) and suppression of some key molecular chaperones required for maintaining intestinal homeostasis [37]. This disruption contributes to endoplasmic reticulum stress and promotes the development of UC. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which SIRT1 exerts its deacetylase activity and regulates HSF1 acetylation remain poorly understood. Further research is warranted to clarify the specific pathways through which SIRT1 contributes to UC pathogenesis.

Current research on the role of acetylation in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis has primarily focused on inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation, while studies addressing intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction remain rare.

The role of methylation in the pathogenesis of UC

Methylation essentially refers to the covalent attachment of methyl groups to lysine and arginine residues on histones. This modification is tightly regulated by various methyltransferases and demethylases [18]. Common methylation sites are primarily located on histones H3 and H4 [38]. By altering the methylation status of these histones, gene transcription can be modulated, subsequently influencing the activation of downstream signaling pathways and other related biological processes. Unfortunately, studies on the impact of methylation modifications on the pathogenesis of UC remain relatively limited.

Some researchers have shown that histone lysine methyltransferase G9A suppresses the differentiation of Th17 and regulatory T cells (Treg cells) in the gut by mediating the dimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me2) [39]. The dynamic balance between IL-17–producing Th17 cells and Foxp3⁺ Treg cells within the intestinal immune microenvironment is essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis [40]. Consequently, G9A activation is considered a key trigger of intestinal inflammation and a major contributor to adaptive immune dysregulation in the gut. Notably, G9A does not appear to influence the differentiation of Th1 and Th2 cells [41]. Both in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that treatment with small-molecule G9A inhibitors significantly attenuates the development of colitis [39]. However, clinical data regarding G9A remain scarce.

Moreover, enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), a major histone methyltransferase, can suppress proinflammatory cytokines expression in experimental colitis by inhibiting NF-κB activation [42]. This effect is mediated through the upregulation of histone H3 at lysine 27 trimethylating (H3K27me3) [42]. However, one study have shown that EZH2 can also exert its effects through H3K27me3 independent mechanisms [43]. Whether EZH2 regulates the pathogenesis of UC by methylating other molecular targets remains to be determined and will require further evidence in future research.

Similar to acetylation, current research on the role of methylation in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis remains primarily focused on immune dysregulation.

The role of glycosylation in the pathogenesis of UC

Glycosylation is a classical form of post-translational modification that modifies protein properties through the addition of single glycans or complex polysaccharide chains, thereby influencing protein stability, functional activity, and subcellular localization [44]. A variety of glycosyltransferases and glycosidases are critically involved in regulating T cell activation [45, 46], and glycosylation has also been shown to play a crucial role in modulating T cell function during the pathogenesis of UC [47].

A study has shown that in both colitis model mice and patients with UC, the levels of α1–6 fucosyltransferase (FUT8) dependent core fucosylation in the inflamed intestinal mucosa are consistently elevated. FUT8-deficient mice exhibit a protective phenotype against T cell mediated intestinal inflammation [47]. Further investigations suggest that this effect may be due to the loss of core fucose, which increases the hydrophilicity of TCR-associated glycoproteins, thereby disrupting their accumulation in lipid rafts, membrane microdomains that preferentially recruit hydrophobic molecules [47]. Moreover, the absence of core fucose may impair the antigen-binding capacity of TCRs, further contributing to reduced T cell activation [47]. Collectively, these findings indicate that diminished core fucosylation in CD4⁺ T cells plays a protective role in the pathogenesis of T cell mediated colitis.

In addition to its role in immune dysregulation, glycosylation also plays a regulatory role in modulating intestinal epithelial barrier function. It is well recognized that the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier critically depends on the regulation of adherens junctions, with E-cadherin serving as a central driver in the assembly of adherens junction protein complexes [48]. Disruption in E-cadherin expression directly impairs adherens junctions, resulting in compromised epithelial barrier function [48]. Emerging evidence indicates that both the expression and stability of E-cadherin are regulated through remodeling of its glycan structures [49]. Although E-cadherin can undergo both O-glycosylation and N-glycosylation, current studies have established that N-glycans located in its extracellular domain are primarily responsible for mediating cell–cell adhesion [50, 51]. Further research has identified that the glycosyltransferase GnT-III modulates E-cadherin N-glycosylation and alters glycan structures to enhance E-cadherin–dependent adhesion [52]. Asparagine residues within the extracellular domain of E-cadherin have been reported as critical sites for N-glycosylation [53]; however, definitive evidence pinpointing the specific glycosylation sites regulated by GnT-III remains lacking. Thus, more detailed investigations are required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulatory process.

The role of ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and Neddylation in the pathogenesis of UC

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is a highly significant form of post-translational modification. Ubiquitin, a highly conserved protein, is covalently attached to the lysine residues of target proteins through a cascade of enzymatic reactions, ultimately marking them for degradation.

Extensive research has explored the role of ubiquitination in the pathogenesis of UC, with multiple studies highlighting the involvement of various E3 ubiquitin ligases [54–58]. One study comparing colonic tissue samples from patients with UC and healthy controls found that the expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase ring finger protein 183 (RNF183) was significantly upregulated in UC patients [54]. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed that RNF183 IOD values were strongly correlated with Mayo scores, indicating a link to disease severity. In contrast, ELISA analysis of plasma samples revealed no significant difference in serum RNF183 levels between the UC group and healthy controls [54]. These findings suggest that RNF183 is more closely associated with local mucosal inflammation in the intestinal tissue rather than systemic disease activity. Further studies have revealed that RNF183 promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of IκBα, reducing its protein levels. This reduction facilitates the nuclear translocation of NF-κB dimers and enhances their transcriptional activity, leading to activation of the NF-κB-p65 signaling pathway in intestinal epithelial cells and ultimately initiating a cascade of inflammatory responses within the gut [54].

Interestingly, another research group uncovered a potential alternative pathway by which RNF183 contributes to the development of UC in a DSS-induced colitis mouse model [55]. Their in vivo experiments demonstrated that RNF183, expressed in colonic epithelial cells, promotes the ubiquitination of its substrate DR5, a cell surface receptor of the TNF receptor superfamily that binds to TRAIL and activates caspase-8 [59]. This modification facilitates the lysosomal trafficking and degradation of DR5, ultimately triggering downstream apoptotic responses [55].

In addition to RNF183, another member of the same family, ring finger protein 186 (RNF186), has also been implicated in the regulation of UC pathogenesis [57]. Specifically, RNF186 mediates the ubiquitination of its substrate EPH receptor B2 (EPHB2) at lysine residue 892 (Lys892), triggering its degradation and subsequently activating downstream autophagy pathways [57]. This process facilitates the clearance of intracellular pathogens and contributes to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. Disruption of this pathway, leading to impaired autophagy, has been associated with increased susceptibility to UC.

Moreover, ring finger protein 5 (RNF5) has been identified as a key regulator of inflammatory responses in the pathogenesis of UC. Comparative analyses of colonic tissue sections from healthy individuals and UC patients, including both inflamed and non-inflamed regions, revealed that RNF5 is highly expressed in healthy controls and in the non-inflamed areas of UC patients, whereas its expression is markedly reduced in inflamed regions [56]. This suggests a role for RNF5 in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Further in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrated that RNF5 mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of S100A8, one of damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), released from the mucosa following injury, thereby effectively suppressing downstream inflammatory responses [56]. Supporting these findings, previous immunohistochemical analyses have confirmed elevated S100A8 expression in the inflamed colonic epithelium of UC patients. Notably, transcriptional analysis revealed lower RNF5 expression levels in UC patients who did not respond to anti-TNF therapy, further highlighting the importance of the RNF5–S100A8 regulatory axis in the inflammatory processes underlying UC pathogenesis [56].

Deubiquitination also plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of UC. In the intestinal mucosa of UC patients, the expression of receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), a key mediator of necroptosis, is markedly elevated compared to healthy individuals, while its expression remains low in non-inflamed regions of the colon [58]. In vitro studies have demonstrated that a deubiquitinating enzyme tumor necrosis factor α-induced protein 3 (A20) can suppress RIPK3 activation by reducing its K63-linked ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting necroptosis and preserving intestinal barrier integrity. Notably, A20 expression is aberrantly regulated in inflamed colonic tissues of UC patients, underscoring its critical role in modulating persistent inflammatory responses during disease progression [58]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that A20’s deubiquitinating function is phosphorylation-dependent [58]; however, the upstream signals that mediate A20 activation remain to be fully elucidated.

SUMOylation

SUMOylation, akin to ubiquitination, involves the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO) to lysine residues on target proteins [60]. This post-translational modification plays a crucial role in regulating protein stability, subcellular localization, and protein–protein interactions [61]. Unlike ubiquitination, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 9 (UBC9) is the sole E2 enzyme responsible for SUMO conjugation in mammalian cells [61].

Regarding UBC9, a study has demonstrated that its expression is significantly downregulated in both DSS-induced colitis mouse models and samples from patients with UC, with this reduction showing a positive correlation with disease severity [62]. Further analysis revealed that pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 were markedly elevated in UC patients [62]. Given these findings, it can be inferred that patients with impaired UBC9-mediated SUMOylation machinery exhibit a more pronounced inflammatory response. Notably, a significant downregulation of protein kinase B alpha (Akt1) expression was also observed in UC patients. To investigate the relationship between UBC9 and Akt1, researchers employed the DSS mouse model and found that reduced UBC9 expression was accompanied by a marked decrease in Akt1 activity within cells [62]. This observation is consistent with previous reports suggesting that SUMOylation enhances the stability of Akt1 [63, 64]. Additionally, other studies have shown that deletion of Akt1 exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice [65]. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of the UBC9–Akt1 pathway in regulating inflammatory responses during the pathogenesis of UC. However, the precise molecular details, such as the specific SUMOylation sites through which UBC9 regulates Akt1, remain unclear, warranting further investigation to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

In addition to UBC9, researchers have found that protein inhibitor of activated STAT1 (PIAS1), an E3 SUMO ligase known to suppress NF-κB and STAT1 activity and thereby attenuate inflammation [66], is also significantly downregulated in both UC patients and colitis mouse models [67]. Previous studies have demonstrated that PIAS1 can effectively inhibit NF-κB activation through the SUMOylation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) [68, 69]. Building on these findings, subsequent studies using DSS-induced colitis mouse models demonstrated that protein inhibitor of activated STAT1 (PIAS1) attenuates intestinal inflammation by mediating the SUMOylation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ), which suppresses the phosphorylation of RelA, a key component of the NF-κB signaling pathway, as well as the transcriptional activity of NF-κB [67]. Moreover, researchers developed a novel nanomaterial-based PIAS1 complex that enhanced SUMO1-modified protein levels in the colon, resulting in significant alleviation of colitis in mice [67].

DeSUMOylation, mediated by specific deSUMOylases, also plays a pivotal role in regulating the pathogenesis of UC. A recent study reported that the expression of the deSUMOylase sentrin/SUMO-specific protease 7 (SENP7) is significantly upregulated in endoscopic tissue samples from UC patients [70]. Concurrently, these samples exhibited a marked reduction in global SUMO2/3 conjugation and a notable increase in the levels of unmodified (free) SUMO2/3 proteins [70]. Consistent with these findings, DSS-induced colitis mouse models also showed a significant upregulation of SENP7 expression in intestinal tissues. Interestingly, the research group that made these discoveries is the same team that previously demonstrated how UBC9-mediated SUMOylation of Akt1 contributes to intestinal inflammation in UC [63]. Building on that work, they conducted a proteomic interactome analysis of colonic tissues and uncovered strong associations between SENP7 and several immune signaling pathways known to be involved in UC pathogenesis [70]. Moreover, mice with epithelial-specific knockdown of SENP7 exhibited robust anti-inflammatory responses, further highlighting the critical role of deSUMOylation in UC development. However, the specific substrates targeted by SENP7 and the downstream molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated, warranting further investigation.

Neddylation

Like SUMOylation, Neddylation is considered a specialized form of ubiquitin-like post-translational modification. It is primarily mediated by the neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated protein 8 (NEDD8) [71].

A recent study on DSS-induced colitis in mice revealed that, during the alleviation of intestinal inflammation using small-molecule interventions, the binding of NEDD8 to its substrate Cullin 1 was effectively disrupted [72]. This was accompanied by a reduction in the expression levels of the E1, E2, and E3 enzymes involved in the Neddylation process, ultimately leading to the inhibition of NF-κB signaling activation and the exertion of potent anti-inflammatory effects [72]. However, as this study primarily aimed to assess the anti-inflammatory efficacy of small-molecule agents, it did not conduct an in-depth exploration of the underlying Neddylation mechanisms. Moreover, to date, no studies have reported on the correlation between Neddylation and clinical manifestations in patients with UC.

Overall, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and neddylation play indispensable regulatory roles throughout UC pathogenesis. Notably, these post-translational modifications exhibit distinct and stage-specific functions during different phases of disease progression. The role of citrullination in the pathogenesis of UC

Unlike the mentioned post-translational modifications, citrullination is generally regarded as an irreversible protein modification, as no enzymes capable of reversing this process have been identified in human tissues [73]. Citrullination is catalyzed by a family of calcium-dependent enzymes known as peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs), which convert positively charged arginine residues into neutral citrulline through deimination or demethylimination [74]. Among the five known PAD family members, PAD4 has been specifically implicated in the pathogenesis of UC [75].

Immunohistochemical analysis of biopsy samples from patients with UC revealed strong PAD4-positive staining in inflammatory cells and the lamina propria [75]. In contrast, no distinct difference in citrullination levels was observed between non-inflamed and inflamed tissues from the same individuals with Crohn’s disease (CD) [76].

A recent study investigating the mechanisms of rectal bleeding and mucosal hemostasis in UC revealed that, beyond its classical role in promoting neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, PAD4 also contributes to immunothrombosis by converting arginine residues on histones and other peptides into citrulline [77]. This citrullination process alters chromatin structure and modulates the function of various proteases, including serine proteases, metalloproteinases, and calpains [78–80]. Notably, PAD4 stabilizes immunothrombi by inhibiting anticoagulant proteins such as antithrombin and promoting thrombin-mediated activation of transglutaminase XIIIa [77]. Immunothrombi serve as emergency protective barriers at sites of epithelial injury. These findings suggest that PAD4-dependent immunothrombosis plays a key role in promoting mucosal wound healing and preventing ulcerative bleeding in severe UC.

Further research is needed to elucidate the role of citrullination in intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction, and it is also hoped that future studies will clarify whether citrullination contributes to immune dysregulation in the pathogenesis of UC.

The role of palmitoylation in the pathogenesis of UC

Unlike conventional post-translational modifications (PTMs), palmitoylation can be regarded as a relatively “young member” of the PTM family. Although it was first discovered in the 1980s [81], proteomics studies have since identified more than 3,000 human palmitoylated proteins [82]. Like most PTMs, palmitoylation is a reversible modification catalyzed by a family of 23 zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyltransferases (ZDHHCs) [83]. Specifically, these enzymes are thought to transfer the palmitoyl group from palmitoyl-CoA to a conserved cysteine residue within their own DHHC motif, and then subsequently to a cysteine residue on the substrate protein to complete the modification process (see Fig. 3) [84]. As the primary enzymes responsible for depalmitoylation, several depalmitoylases have been well characterized. These enzymes, all belonging to the serine hydrolase family, catalyze the removal of S-palmitoylation modifications from proteins. The main depalmitoylases include APT1 (LYPLA1), APT2 (also known as LYPLA2), ABHD17A/B/C, and ABHD10 [85, 86]. Interestingly, a recent study revealed that the mRNA levels of both ZDHHC7 and LYPLA2 were upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with UC [26]. As these genes encode enzymes with opposing functions, ZDHHC7 catalyzing palmitoylation and LYPLA2 catalyzing depalmitoylation, their simultaneous elevation raises a compelling question: could both processes be actively and coordinately involved in UC pathogenesis? To explore this possibility, researchers uncovered a dynamic palmitoylation–depalmitoylation cycle that plays a critical role in regulating inflammation in UC [26]. Previous studies have established that STAT3, a key transcription factor in inflammatory signaling, is recruited to the plasma membrane under specific stimuli and phosphorylated by JAK2 [87]. Once phosphorylated (p-STAT3), it translocates to the nucleus to activate the transcription of downstream target genes [88]. Notably, in naïve CD4⁺ T cells, p-STAT3 promotes the expression of IL-17 A and other genes involved in Th17 cell differentiation, a process known to be central to UC pathogenesis [40]. Recent findings demonstrate that STAT3 palmitoylation at Cys108, mediated by DHHC7, is essential for its membrane localization and subsequent phosphorylation [26]. Before nuclear translocation, p-STAT3 must be depalmitoylated, a step selectively carried out by APT2 [26]. This sequence of modifications defines a regulatory palmitoylation-depalmitoylation cycle that is crucial for STAT3 activation. Furthermore, in UC patients, the expression levels of STAT3 target genes like IL-17 A show strong positive correlations with the expression of ZDHHC7 and LYPLA2, providing further evidence that this palmitoylation cycle plays an important regulatory role in disease development [26]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the STAT3 palmitoylation–depalmitoylation cycle is not only a key mechanism in the activation of proinflammatory signaling but also a potential therapeutic target in UC.

The role of gut microbial metabolites in the pathogenesis of UC

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is now widely recognized as a key contributor to UC pathogenesis. Among the diverse products of the gut microbiota, microbial metabolites have emerged as pivotal mediators of intestinal immune responses and epithelial barrier integrity. Perturbations in their production or signaling are increasingly implicated in disease progression, highlighting their potential as both therapeutic targets and biomarkers [89]. Notably, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, together with microbiota-derived acyl-CoA derivatives, are among the best-characterized bioactive metabolites [90]. They not only directly or indirectly regulate a broad spectrum of biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and gene expression, but also serve as ligands for G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), thereby activating multiple anti-inflammatory signaling pathways [91]. A study demonstrated that butyrate, a well-established HDAC inhibitor, suppresses NF-κB activation by enhancing histone H3 acetylation, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ultimately ameliorating DSS-induced colitis [92]. More recently, pharmacologically induced enrichment of propionate and butyrate was shown to enhance Sp1 phosphorylation via the GPR43/GPR109A/ERK1/2 signaling pathway while suppressing HDAC1 activity by disrupting its direct interaction with HIF-1α, thereby promoting HIF-1α acetylation and ultimately improving intestinal mucosal barrier function [93].

However, evidence supporting the involvement of short-chain fatty acids in regulating acetylation during UC pathogenesis remains limited. To date, no direct evidence has linked acetate to post-translational modifications in the context of UC. Moreover, although propionate and butyrate have been reported to regulate other forms of post-translational modifications, experimental evidence demonstrating their involvement in UC is still lacking. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the roles of SCFA-mediated post-translational modifications in UC pathogenesis.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC) is highly complex, involving a range of contributing factors such as immune dysregulation, epithelial barrier dysfunction and alterations in the gut microbiota. This review focuses on post-translational modifications (PTMs) as a molecular lens to explore their roles in UC development. PTMs represent critical regulatory mechanisms in molecular biology, typically catalyzed by specific enzymes. Therefore, in discussing how PTMs influence UC pathogenesis, we emphasize the roles of these corresponding enzymes, how they interact with their substrates, which amino acid residues are modified, and what downstream effects these modifications elicit. Notably, several of the modifying enzymes discussed in this review have been identified in clinical samples from patients with UC and are associated with disease onset and severity (see Table 2).

Post-translational modifications in UC pathogenesis

The evidence summarized in this review highlights several important issues that deserve further investigation. Although phosphorylation is one of the most prevalent and fundamental post-translational modifications, studies investigating its regulatory role in the pathogenesis of UC have declined markedly in recent years. This raises an important question: has phosphorylation ceased to be a key regulatory mechanism in UC, or has it simply received less attention due to a shift in research priorities? Given its central role in cellular signal transduction, this issue deserves further investigation. A similar situation applies to acetylation, another classical and essential regulatory mechanism. Moreover, existing studies on acetylation have predominantly focused on its roles in inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation, while its contribution to other key pathogenic processes, including intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction and gut microbiota homeostasis, has received comparatively little attention. Further mechanistic studies in these areas are therefore needed. In addition, increasing evidence from other disease models indicates that ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and neddylation rarely function independently. Instead, these PTMs frequently act in a coordinated or sequential manner within the same signaling pathway, with two or even all three modifications cooperatively regulating protein activity, stability, and downstream signaling. However, despite their well-recognized interplay in other pathological contexts, direct evidence for such crosstalk in UC is still lacking. Investigating the coordinated regulation among ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and neddylation may therefore represent a promising direction for future studies aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms of UC pathogenesis.

Based on the major pathogenic mechanisms in UC, this review primarily summarizes the regulatory roles of PTMs in two key areas: immune dysregulation and impairment of intestinal barrier function (see Fig. 4). This focus does not imply that the impact of genetic alterations or microbial dysbiosis is of lesser importance; rather, it reflects the relative lack of studies investigating the roles of PTMs in these areas. Some evidence has suggested a potential link, for example, intestinal epithelial glycosylation is known to significantly influence gut microbiota composition in UC [94], we also discussed examples of microbiota-derived metabolites regulating UC pathogenesis through PTMs, but the specific modifying enzymes and molecular pathways involved have not yet been clearly elucidated and require further investigation.

The figure illustrates well-established post-translational modification (PTM) pathways supported by relative research, including phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and palmitoylation. These modifications are implicated in signaling pathways relevant to immune dysregulation and intestinal barrier dysfunction, encompassing the regulation of inflammation-associated transcription factors, immune cell differentiation, and the maintenance of tight junction integrity

Our review of the current literature revealed that numerous studies on post-translational modifications (PTMs) converge on one of the most critical signaling pathways involved in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC): the NF-κB signaling pathway. For example, phosphorylation of TAK1 promotes downstream NF-κB activation [27], and RNF183-mediated ubiquitination of IκBα likewise enhances NF-κB signaling [54]. In contrast, several PTMs exert inhibitory effects on NF-κB signaling. EZH2-mediated histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) [42], PIAS1-mediated SUMOylation of PPARγ [67], and NEDD8-mediated neddylation of Cullin1 [72] have all been reported to suppress NF-κB activation. Thus, regardless of whether PTMs function as positive or negative regulators, NF-κB consistently occupies a central position within the complex network of PTM-mediated regulation. These findings further underscore the indispensable role of the NF-κB pathway in UC pathogenesis, particularly during the initiation of inflammation and the subsequent amplification of immune responses. Therefore, components of the NF-κB signaling pathway, together with PTM regulators that modulate its activity, represent promising therapeutic targets for the future treatment of UC.

We also observed that activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway is the result of crosstalk among different PTMs. It is important to note that different types of PTMs not only function independently but can also act synergistically or interact with one another, a phenomenon observed in the pathogenesis of many diseases, including UC. For instance, as mentioned above, the palmitoylation–depalmitoylation cycle has been shown to play a significant regulatory role in UC inflammatory activity [26]. Phosphorylation–ubiquitination crosstalk represents the most classical example of NF-κB regulation in the pathogenesis of UC. In addition, acetylation–phosphorylation crosstalk was clearly demonstrated in a recent study showing that propionate and butyrate improve intestinal barrier function through HIF-1α [93]. Specifically, these metabolites not only enhanced Sp1 phosphorylation but also promoted HIF-1α acetylation, and the coordinated action of these two pathways contributed to the restoration of intestinal barrier function [93]. However, other classical forms of PTM crosstalk, such as phosphorylation–SUMOylation, glycosylation–phosphorylation, and ubiquitination–SUMOylation, still lack direct evidence in the context of UC. In fact, more biologically relevant PTM crosstalk often occurs at the level of PTM-regulating enzymes or their substrates. As discussed above, the activity of RIPK3, a key mediator of necroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells, is coordinately regulated by ubiquitination and phosphorylation [58]. To suppress RIPK3 activation, the deubiquitinase A20 must first undergo phosphorylation to enhance its deubiquitinating activity and is subsequently recruited by ABIN3 to inhibit RIPK3 activation [58]. This example clearly illustrates the functional crosstalk between phosphorylation and ubiquitination. However, reports describing PTM crosstalk involving specific PTM-regulating enzymes or their substrates in UC remain extremely limited. We anticipate that future studies will further elucidate the mechanisms by which these PTM-regulating enzymes contribute to UC pathogenesis and provide a foundation for their future development as potential therapeutic targets.

Regarding future clinical applications, we believe that several challenges currently limit the therapeutic potential of PTM-targeted strategies in UC. First, most PTM-regulating enzymes lack tissue specificity and are widely expressed across different tissues, making them potential therapeutic targets but also increasing the risk of toxicity to normal tissues. Second, many PTM-regulating enzymes act on multiple substrates rather than a single target, which further complicates the development of selective therapeutic agents. Furthermore, as discussed above, PTM crosstalk represents another major challenge, as activation of a given signaling pathway is often mediated by the coordinated action of multiple PTMs rather than a single modification. Nevertheless, a recent pharmacological study has provided encouraging evidence for the clinical translation of PTM-targeted therapies. TAK-981 (subasumstat), a SUMO-activating enzyme (SAE) inhibitor that has already entered phase I/II clinical trials for cancer therapy, has been reported to exert significant therapeutic effects in multiple experimental colitis models, including DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis [95]. These findings suggest that targeting SUMOylation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for UC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a systematic and comprehensive investigation of both established and yet to be discovered PTMs is essential for a full understanding of the regulatory networks underpinning UC. Such studies will not only expand our basic scientific knowledge but also pave the way for the identification of new therapeutic targets and the development of more effective treatment strategies. Future therapeutic strategies targeting PTMs may focus on the development of PTM-regulating enzyme inhibitors with greater substrate or tissue specificity, as well as on targeting key regulatory nodes involved in PTM crosstalk. Furthermore, integrating multi-omics approaches with precision medicine strategies may facilitate the development of more effective PTM-based therapeutics for UC.
