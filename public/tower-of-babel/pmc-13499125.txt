Predominantly cystic glioblastoma mimicking benign cystic lesions: A radiological diagnostic challenge
Authors: Riya Mercy Jacob, Geena Benjamin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common and most aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults. Although it typically presents as a heterogeneously enhancing solid mass with central necrosis, predominantly cystic glioblastoma is an uncommon morphological variant that may mimic other cystic intracranial lesions and create diagnostic uncertainty. We report the case of a 58-year-old man who presented with headache and vomiting for 1 week's duration. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, and total white blood cell count, were within normal limits. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a large predominantly cystic intra-axial lesion in the left frontal lobe with thick irregular peripheral enhancement, extensive surrounding vasogenic edema, and marked mass effect. Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated facilitated diffusion within the cystic component, favoring a neoplastic process over abscess. The patient underwent surgical excision, and histopathological examination confirmed glioblastoma (WHO grade 4). This case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical cystic presentations of glioblastoma and emphasizes the role of diffusion-weighted imaging in differentiating cystic neoplasms from other ring-enhancing intracranial lesions.Keywords: Glioblastoma multiforme, Cystic glioblastoma, Magnetic resonance imaging, Diffusion-weighted imaging, Ring-enhancing lesion, Differential diagnosis

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults and represents the highest-grade diffuse astrocytic neoplasm in the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of central nervous system tumors [1]. Despite advances in surgical and adjuvant therapies, prognosis remains poor, with reported median survival ranging from 12 to 18 months [2]. Radiologically, glioblastoma classically appears as a heterogeneously enhancing infiltrative intra-axial mass with central necrosis, extensive vasogenic edema, and significant mass effect.

Predominantly cystic glioblastoma is an uncommon morphological variant, reported in approximately 5%-10% of cases [3]. Such lesions may closely resemble other cystic intracranial pathologies, including brain abscess, cystic metastasis, and low-grade glioma. Accurate differentiation is essential because prognosis, management, and therapeutic urgency differ substantially among these entities.

We report a case of histopathologically confirmed predominantly cystic glioblastoma presenting as a large cystic frontal lobe lesion. The case highlights the imaging features that aid in differentiating cystic GBM from other ring-enhancing intracranial lesions and emphasizes the importance of clinicoradiological-pathological correlation.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old male presented with headache and recurrent episodes of vomiting for 1 week. There was no history of fever, seizures, altered sensorium, focal neurological deficit, recent infection, trauma, or known malignancy. The patient had no significant past medical history, including hypertension or diabetes mellitus. There was no relevant family history of neurological disease or malignancy.

Neurological examination at presentation was unremarkable. Routine hematological and inflammatory investigations, including complete blood count, total leukocyte count, ESR, and CRP, were within normal limits.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain demonstrated a large, well-defined, mildly lobulated predominantly cystic intra-axial lesion in the left frontal lobe measuring approximately 6.8 × 5.3 × 5.1 cm. The lesion appeared hypointense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR images.

Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated facilitated diffusion within the cystic component without central diffusion restriction. Corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps showed elevated ADC values, supporting nonrestrictive cystic contents.

Postcontrast T1-weighted images demonstrated thick irregular nodular peripheral enhancement with focal enhancing solid components. Extensive surrounding vasogenic edema was present, resulting in significant mass effect, including compression of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle, approximately 1.6 cm midline shift, and subfalcine herniation (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Based on the imaging findings, differential diagnoses included cystic glioblastoma, brain abscess, cystic metastasis, and other cystic glial neoplasms. Brain abscess remained a differential consideration; however, the absence of central diffusion restriction and the presence of thick irregular nodular peripheral enhancement favored a neoplastic lesion rather than an infective process. The patient subsequently underwent left frontal craniotomy and gross excision of the lesion.

Histopathological examination revealed a highly cellular infiltrative astrocytic neoplasm composed of pleomorphic tumor cells, including gemistocytic forms and multinucleated giant cells within a fibrillary background. Prominent microvascular proliferation with glomeruloid vascular structures and areas of necrosis were identified. These findings were diagnostic of glioblastoma (WHO grade 4).

The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient remained neurologically stable without new deficits. At 2-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated clinical improvement and was receiving adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy at another institution.

Discussion

Glioblastoma accounts for approximately 60%-70% of malignant primary brain tumors in adults and remains one of the most aggressive central nervous system neoplasms [4]. While the typical imaging appearance of GBM is well recognized, predominantly cystic glioblastoma is considerably less common and may present a significant diagnostic challenge. Similar diagnostic difficulties have been reported in previous cases of cystic glioblastoma, where atypical cystic morphology initially suggested alternative diagnoses such as abscess or cystic metastasis [3].

The pathogenesis of cyst formation in glioblastoma remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include liquefactive necrosis secondary to rapid tumor growth and hypoxia, increased vascular permeability resulting in fluid accumulation, and cystic degeneration of tumor tissue [5,6]. These mechanisms contribute to the formation of large cystic components that may dominate the radiological appearance of the lesion.

The primary diagnostic challenge in the present case was differentiation from brain abscess and cystic metastasis. Brain abscesses characteristically demonstrate smooth thin peripheral enhancement and marked central diffusion restriction owing to viscous purulent contents [7]. In contrast, our case demonstrated facilitated diffusion within the cystic component and thick irregular nodular peripheral enhancement, findings that strongly favored a neoplastic process. Additionally, the absence of systemic symptoms and normal inflammatory markers provided supportive clinical evidence against infection.

Cystic metastases may also present as ring-enhancing lesions [8]. However, metastatic lesions are frequently multiple, often located at the gray-white matter junction, and may demonstrate different enhancement characteristics depending on the primary malignancy. The solitary frontal lobe location, irregular enhancing rim, extensive surrounding edema, and histopathological findings favored a primary glial neoplasm [7].

Several studies have suggested that patients with cystic glioblastoma may experience slightly improved survival compared with those with conventional noncystic glioblastoma. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent, and treatment recommendations remain unchanged. Maximal safe surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide-based chemotherapy continues to represent the standard of care [9].

The educational value of this case lies in the recognition that glioblastoma may occasionally present as a predominantly cystic lesion. Failure to consider cystic GBM in the differential diagnosis may delay appropriate treatment, particularly when imaging findings overlap with those of brain abscess or metastatic disease.

Conclusion

Predominantly cystic glioblastoma is an uncommon but important radiological variant of glioblastoma that may mimic other cystic intracranial lesions. Recognition of imaging features such as irregular nodular peripheral enhancement, extensive surrounding edema, significant mass effect, and absence of central diffusion restriction can aid in narrowing the differential diagnosis. Histopathological examination remains essential for definitive diagnosis. Awareness of this atypical presentation may facilitate earlier diagnosis and timely management.
