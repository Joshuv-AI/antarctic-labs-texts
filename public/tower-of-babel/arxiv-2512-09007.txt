An ETH-ansatz-based environmental-branch approach to master equation
Wen-ge Wang

Abstract
In this paper, a method for deriving master equation is developed for a generic small quantum system, which is locally coupled to an environment as a many-body quantum chaotic system that satisfies the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis ansatz, resorting to neither the Born approximation nor the Markov approximation. The total system undergoes Schrödinger evolution, under an initial condition in which the environmental branches possess no correlation with the interaction Hamiltonian. Derivation of the master equation is based on piecewise usage of a second-order expansion of a formal expression, which is derived for the evolution of the environmental branches. Approximations used in the derivation are mainly based on dynamic properties of the environment.



I
Introduction

I.1
Motivations

Open quantum system is one of the most important and widely studied topics in modern physics.
Measurable properties of such systems, characterized by expectation values of their observables,
are given by their reduced density matrices (RDM).
Due to entanglement of an open system with its environment,
its RDM is usually not sufficient to determine its time evolution.
However, as is known, under an environment as a thermal bath satisfying certain conditions,
evolution of the RDM approximately follows a so-called master equation
breuer2002
.
Typically, derivation of a master equation, particularly, that of a Lindblad form, is based on
certain approximations such as Born approximation and Markov approximation.

Within the present analytical framework, it is usually difficult to
make quantitatively clear dynamic prerequisites that lie behind the Born and Markov approximations.
From the dynamic perspective, even the concept of quantum thermal bath is hypothetical,
since it neglects dynamical correlations that may exist in physical models of practical interest.
Indeed, in recent years, growing interest has been found for various non-Markovian effects,
for example, back-action of the central system on environment
and its application in controlling coherence
(see, e.g., Refs.
GongJB12
;
addis2014coherence
;
roszak2015decoherence
;
zhang2015role
;
cakmak2017
;
guarnieri2018steady
).

Over the past few decades, it has been found that quantum chaotic systems possess many
of the properties typically ascribed to quantum thermal baths

RBC06
;
Rigol-AiP16
;
Deutch-RPP18
,
especially for systems that satisfy the so-called eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) ansatz

Rigol-AiP16
;
Deutch-RPP18
;
Deutch91
;
srednicki1994ET
;
srednicki-JPA96
;
srednicki1999ETH
.
For the purpose of studing dynamics of open quantum systems,
it is reasonable to expect that taking an environment as a dynamical system
that satisfies ETH — particularly,
a many-body quantum chaotic system, rather than a hypothetical thermal bath —
may be more beneficial.
Indeed, recent studies have shown that, in the derivation of master equation,
ETH ansatz is helpful for arguing validity of the Markov approximation
and useful in the calculation of correlation functions
DonvSMGM-PRB25
.

In the study of dynamic effects of environment,
the most direct method is to decompose the total system’s state
|\Psi\rangle
as

|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}|\alpha\rangle|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}\rangle
,
where
|\alpha\rangle
are the energy eigenstates of the central system
and
|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}\rangle
are the corresponding environmental branches.
In fact, inner products of the environmental branches yield elements of the RDM, namely,

\langle\phi_{\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle=\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)
.
One question of interest is whether and how chaoticity of an environment,
particularly the properties assumed in the ETH ansztz,
could be useful in the study of dynamic properties of the environmental branches
and hence of the RDM.

As is known, under nondissipative (pure-dephasing) interactions,

I.2
Purpose and basic strategy of this paper

In this paper, we study a generic open quantum system,
which is locally coupled to an environment as a generic many-body quantum
chaotic system that satisfies the ETH ansatz.
The central system is much smaller than the environment.
The purpose is to study the possibility of making approximations,
each of which is introduced due to certain specific dynamic property of the environment,
such that a master equation of the Lindblad form can be obtained finally for the RDM.

To achieve this goal,
a main difficulty comes from computation of the time evolution of the environmental branches under
dissipative interactions, which may induce correlations among the environmental branches.
To overcome this difficulty, we are to derive
a formal expression for the evolution of the environmental branches
and, then, adopt the following strategy for studying the possibility of deriving a master equation.

Firstly, we divide a finite time period of interest into a series of short time intervals
and, within each
short
interval, expand the above mentioned formal expression
up to the second-order terms.
Secondly, making use of the ETH ansatz, we are to show that most of the expansion terms obtained
give negligible contributions, with only a few of them left.
Thirdly, by comparing summations of the left terms over all the short intervals,
a dynamic condition is to be derived under which only two of them give considerable contributions.
Finally, we discuss the possibility of deriving a master equation of the Lindblad form.

The paper is organized as follows.
The setup is given in Sec.
II
.
The basic framework of the environmental-branch approach to
RDM evolution, as well as preliminary analysis based on the ETH ansatz,
are discussed in Sec.
III
.
In Sec.
IV
, a formal expression for the time evolution of
environmental branches is derived
and some of its predictions are discussed with the help of the ETH ansatz.
In Sec.
V
, based on results obtained above
and following the strategy discussed in the above paragraph, a master equation is derived.
In Sec.
VI
,
as an application of the derived master equation, decoherence rate in the case of pure dephasing
is discussed and compared with previously known results.
Finally, conclusions and discussions are given in Sec.
VII
.

Environmental branch formulation of RDM

In this section, we discuss the basic form of the environmental-branch approach to

III.1
).
We also give a preliminary analysis from the perspective of

III.1
Environmental-branch expression of RDM

To get an expression of the RDM in terms of environmental branches,
let us consider an arbitrary initial state
|\Psi(t_{0})\rangle
, expanded as
|\Psi(t_{0})\rangle=\sum_{n}\Psi^{0}_{n}|n\rangle
.
The Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(t)\rangle=H|\Psi(t)\rangle

(7)

with
\hbar=1
, predicts the time evolution of

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{n}\Psi^{0}_{n}e^{-iE_{n}t}|n\rangle
.
In the branch formulation, it is written as follows,

|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)},

(8)

where the
environmental branch

|\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle
, as a vector in the
space of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{E}}
, is given by

\displaystyle|\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle=\langle\alpha|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{n}\Psi^{0}_{n}e^{-iE_{n}t}\langle\alpha|n\rangle.

(9)

A direct computation from Eq.(
7
) gives that

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}}=(e^{S}_{\alpha}+H^{\mathcal{E}})\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}}+\sum_{\gamma}H^{I}_{\alpha\gamma}\ket{\phi_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}},

(10)

where
H^{I}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\langle\alpha|H^{I}|\beta\rangle
, as an environmental operator, is written as

\displaystyle H^{I}_{\alpha\beta}=H^{IS}_{\alpha\beta}\,H^{I\mathcal{E}}.

(11)

Taking the partial trace of
\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}
over the environmental degree of freedom
yields the RDM,
\rho^{S}(t)=\tr_{\mathcal{E}}(\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)})
.
It is straightforward to verify that

\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\langle\phi_{\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle,

(12)

that is, the RDM elements can be simply written as inner products of the environmental branches.
Making use of Eq.(
10
), it is straightforward to get the following
evolution equation of the elements,

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}}{dt}=i(e^{S}_{\beta}-e^{S}_{\alpha})\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+i\sum_{\gamma}(H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\gamma}-H^{IS}_{\alpha\gamma}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\gamma\beta}),

(13)

where
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}
is a c-number defined by

\displaystyle H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}:=\langle\phi_{\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|H^{I\mathcal{E}}|\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle.

(14)

Expanding the environmental branches in the environmental eigenstates, i.e.,

\displaystyle|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=\sum_{i}C_{\alpha i}(t)|i\rangle,

(15)

one gets that

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\sum_{i}C^{*}_{\beta i}(t)C_{\alpha i}(t).

(16)

From Eq.(
13
), one sees that quantities like
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}

play a key role in the evolution of the RDM.
Inserting Eq.(
15
) into Eq.(
14
), one gets that

\displaystyle H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}=\sum_{i,j}C^{*}_{\beta j}(t)C_{\alpha i}(t)H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}.

(17)

This quantity behaves in a complicated way with time, particularly due to
correlations between the branch-expansion components (
C_{\alpha i}(t)
and
C_{\beta j}(t)
)
and the matrix elements
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}
.
Such correlations originate from the role played by
H^{I\mathcal{E}}

in the time evolution of the environmental branches,
as a part of the interaction Hamiltonian.

2

2

This point is seen clearly in Eq.(
40
) to be derived later.

are quite small,
one has

\displaystyle H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}\simeq H^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}.

(29)

Below, we argue that
\lambda
should be equal to
1.5
in the limit of large
N
,
if the RDM undergoes a nonnegligible evolution (apart from change in the phase).
In such a case, the second part on the rhs of Eq.(
13
)
should differ notably from zero, which implies that
at least some of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\gamma}
or
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\gamma\beta}

should be notably different from zero.
Then, since contributions from
H^{I\mathcal{E}(l)}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}
of
l=1,2,3
are negligible,
at least some of
H^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}_{\phi,\alpha\gamma}
or
H^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}_{\phi,\gamma\beta}

should be finite and notably different from zero.
According to the scaling behavior in Eq.(
28
), this requires that

\lambda=1.5
in the limit of
N\to\infty
with
\rho_{\rm dos}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\infty
.

The above result of
\lambda=1.5
excludes the possibility of
no phase correlation among the three quantities of
C^{*}_{\beta j}(t)
,
C_{\alpha i}(t)
, and
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}
,
which would imply
\lambda=1
.
Indeed, as mentioned previously,
these three quantities are correlated due to impact of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
on the branch evolution.
Besides, it also excludes the possibility of
\lambda=2
, which corresponds to
full compensation of phases of the three quantities.

An exception is for the initial time
t_{0}
, at which the system-environment interaction has not taken place.
Indeed, it is possible for the environment’s initial state to possess no correlation with
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
.
For example, no correlation exists at
t_{0}
, if the environment
\mathcal{E}
initially lies in a thermal state,
which implies that
\lambda=1
at
t_{0}
.

In order to get a further understanding for the value of
\lambda=1.5
,
we note that the number of coefficients of each environmental branch scales as
M_{\Gamma}
,
while,
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
has
M_{\Gamma}^{2}
elements within the considered energy shell,
half of which possess random phases according to the ETH ansatz.
Clearly, it is impossible for the
2M_{\Gamma}
phases of
C^{*}_{\beta j}(t)C_{\alpha i}(t)

to fully compensate those of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}
, the latter of which scales of
M_{\Gamma}^{2}
.
Let us consider a possible ultimate compensation,
in which phases of
\big(\sum_{i}C_{\alpha i}(t)H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}\big)

may match those of
C^{*}_{\beta j}(t)
for all
j
.
This usually excludes the possibility for phases of
C_{\alpha i}(t)

to match well those of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}
for each
j
.
In this case, the sum over
j
scales as
M_{\Gamma}
,
while, the sum over
i
may scale as
M_{\Gamma}^{1/2}
;
and hence finally one may have
\lambda=1.5
.

Even with the result in Eq.(
29
),
it is still not an easy task to derive a master equation from Eq.(
13
).
This is due to a two-fold feature of the situation:
On one hand,
H^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}
may show certain fluctuation feature with mean zero.
On the other hand, the time evolution may induce correlations between branch coefficients
and the operator
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
, which appear on the rhs of Eq.(
27
),
and as a consequence
H^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}

can not be treated as a random number.

5
5

5

In fact, this type of correlation is crucial for understanding decoherence and thermalization behaviors.

IV
Evolution of environmental branches

In this section, we first derive an evolution equation for environmental branches (Sec.
IV.1
),
then, making use of a formal solution to the equation,
study properties of a finite time evolution under the ETH ansatz (Sec.
IV.2
).

IV.1
Formal expression for branch evolution

It is easy to see that, under nondissipative interactions
for which
H^{IS}_{\alpha\gamma}=0
for all
\alpha\neq\gamma
,
Eq.(
10
) reduces to a Schrödinger-type equation under certain effective Hamiltonian
and the environmental branches
|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
undergo a Schrödinger-type evolution.
Below, we show that this property is generalizable to a generic dissipative interaction.

To study a generic branch evolution, let us write Eq. (
10
) in the following form:

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}}=\sum_{\gamma}{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha\gamma}\ket{\phi_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}},

(30)

where
{\mathcal{M}}
is a
d_{S}\times d_{S}
matrix, defined by

\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}})_{\alpha\gamma}=(e^{S}_{\alpha}+H^{\mathcal{E}})\delta_{\alpha\gamma}+H^{I\mathcal{E}}H^{IS}_{\alpha\gamma}.

(31)

This formulation suggests
that one may introduce an auxiliary degree of freedom related to the label
\alpha

and write kets (bras) in a matrix form, which is to be refereed to as
\alpha
-matrix
.
Specifically, kets of the
d_{S}
branches are written in the following matrix form,

\displaystyle[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle]:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}|\phi_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle\\
|\phi_{2}^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle\\
\vdots\\
|\phi_{d_{S}}^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle\\
\end{array}\right),

and, similarly, the bras are written as

\displaystyle[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}|]\equiv\left(\langle\phi_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}|,\;\langle\phi_{2}^{\mathcal{E}}|,\;\cdots,\;\langle\phi_{d_{S}}^{\mathcal{E}}|\right).

(36)

In the notation of
\alpha
-matrix, it is easy to check that the RDM has the following matrix expression,

\displaystyle[\rho^{S}]=\tr_{\mathcal{E}}\Big([|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle][\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}|]\Big),

(37a)

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\big]_{\beta}\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle\big]_{\alpha},

(37b)

where
\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle\big]_{\alpha}
represents the
\alpha
th row of
\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle\big]

and
\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\big]_{\beta}
the
\beta
th column of
\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\big]
.

Then, Eq.(
30
) is written in a compact form,

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle]={{\mathcal{M}}}[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}\rangle],

(38)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}=[H^{S}]+H^{\mathcal{E}}[I]+H^{I\mathcal{E}}[H^{IS}].

(39)

Here,
[H^{S}]
and
[H^{IS}]
, as the
\alpha
-matrices for
H^{S}
and
H^{IS}
, respectively,
are equal to the matrices of
H^{S}_{\alpha\beta}
and
H^{IS}_{\alpha\beta}
,
and
[I]
indicates the
d_{S}\times d_{S}
unit matrix.
Equation (
38
) gives the following formal solution to the branches’ evolution,

\displaystyle[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle]=e^{-i{\mathcal{M}}(t-t_{0})}[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle].

(40)

Furthermore, in the
\alpha
-matrix notation, the evolution equation of the
RDM in Eq.(
13
) is written in a concise way,

\displaystyle i\frac{d[\rho^{S}]}{dt}=\Big[[H^{S}],[\rho^{S}]\Big]+\Big[[H^{IS}],[H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi}]\Big],

(41)

where
[H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi}]
indicates the matrix with elements of

[H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi}]_{\alpha\beta}=H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}
.

6
6

6

Note that the
\alpha
-matrix notation is not equivalent
to the eigenbasis-representation of the system
S
.
For example,
[H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi}]
is meaningless in the latter case.

Equation (
40
) determines the dynamics of the subspace,
which is spanned by the environmental branches, denoted by
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\{\phi\}}
,
namely,
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\{\phi\}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
.
Due to the environmental evolution governed by
H^{\mathcal{E}}
, usually, the subspace

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\{\phi\}}
changes fast with time.
However, for the purpose of determining evolution of the RDM,
what is of relevance is relative motion of the branches within the subspace,
particularly, overlaps of the branches [Eq.(
37b
)].

IV.2
Finite-time evolution under ETH

In this section, based on the above obtained formal solution to the environmental-branch evolution [Eq.(
40
)], with the help of the ETH ansatz,
we discuss some properties of the evolution of RDM within a finite time period.

To get an explicit expression for the RDM elements from Eq.(
37b
),
let us write explicitly the component
\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle\big]_{\alpha}

according to Eq.(
40
),

\displaystyle\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)\rangle\big]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(e^{-i{\mathcal{M}}(t-t_{0})}\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle\big])_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i(t-t_{0}))^{n}}{n!}\left({\mathcal{M}}^{n}\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle\big]\right)_{\alpha}.

(42)

Similarly, the bra component is written as

\displaystyle\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t)|\big]_{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|\big]e^{i{\mathcal{M}}(t-t_{0})})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i(t-t_{0}))^{n}}{n!}\left(\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|\big]{\mathcal{M}}^{n}\right)_{\beta}.

(43)

Substituting the above expansions into Eq.(
37b
), one gets

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{(i(t-t_{0}))^{n+m}}{n!m!}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|\big]{\mathcal{M}}^{n}\Big)_{\beta}\Big({\mathcal{M}}^{m}\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle\big]\Big)_{\alpha}.

(44)

With
k=n+m
, the above expansion is rewritten as

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)-\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})(t-t_{0})^{k},

(45)

where Eq.(
12
) has been used for
k=0
and

G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
of
k\geq 1
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=i^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-n}}{n!(k-n)!}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\big[\langle\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|\big]{\mathcal{M}}^{n}\Big)_{\beta}\Big({\mathcal{M}}^{k-n}\big[|\phi^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle\big]\Big)_{\alpha}.

(46)

Substituting Eq.(
39
) into the rhs of Eq.(
46
)
and write the
\alpha
-matrices in their component forms,
one gets an expression of
G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
, which takes the form of

\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\langle\phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|\cdots|\phi_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle
.
Here, the dots represent a sum of environmental operators (with appropriate coefficients),
which include
I^{\mathcal{E}}
( the environmental identity operator),
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
, and so on.
We use
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
with a label
\eta
to indicate these operators
and use
x_{\alpha\beta\eta}^{(k)\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
to indicate their coefficients.

7
7

7

See Eq.(
V.2
) to be given in Sec.
V.2
for some examples of the operators
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}

and Appendix
A
for a method of computing the operators
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
.

Dividing
G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
according to the label
\eta
, one writes that

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})=\sum_{\eta}G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{0}),

(47)

where

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{0})=\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}x_{\alpha\beta\eta}^{(k)\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\langle\phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|Y^{(k)}_{\eta}|\phi_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle.

(48)

It can be shown that, due to locality of the interaction,

Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
of
k
not large is either the environmental identity operator
I^{\mathcal{E}}
or a local operator
(see Appendix
A
).
Note that the identity operator
I^{\mathcal{E}}
can be regarded as satisfying the ETH ansatz in Eq.(
6
),
with vanishing fluctuation function of
f(e^{0},\omega)=0
;
meanwhile, as mentioned previously, local operators are usually believed to satisfy the ETH ansatz.
We use
k_{\rm ETH}
to indicate an upper bound of
k
,
such that all the operators
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
with
k\leq k_{\rm ETH}
obey the ETH ansatz.
Following arguments given in Appendix
A
,
one finds that
k_{\rm ETH}
may go to infinity in the thermodynamic limit of
N\to\infty
.

For
k\leq k_{\rm ETH}
with
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
satisfying the ETH ansatz,
the quantity
\langle\phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})|Y^{(k)}_{\eta}|\phi_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}(t_{0})\rangle

may be treated in a way similar to that
given previously for
H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{\phi,\alpha\beta}
in Sec.
III.2
.
We also divide both
G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
and
G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{0})

into four parts labelled with
l
, writing that

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta(\eta)}(t_{0})=\sum_{l=1}^{4}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta(\eta)}(t_{0}),

(49)

where the meaning of
l
is similar to that given in Eq.(
19
),
but with
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
replaced by
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
.
We use
y^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
to indicate the value of the diagonal function for
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}

in the ETH ansatz at the center of the window
\Gamma
.

For
l=1
, similar to Eq.(
20
),
making use of Eq.(
47
)-(
48
), one gets that

\displaystyle G^{(k,1)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})=\sum_{\eta,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}y^{(k)}_{\eta 0}x_{\alpha\beta\eta}^{(k)\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\rho^{S}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(t_{0}).

(50)

For
l=3
, following arguments similar to those given previously around Eq.(
26
),
one sees that
G^{(k,3)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
with
l=3
coming from fluctuations of diagonal elements
should be negligible, too.

For
l=2
, following arguments similar to those given previously for
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
,
one sees that
G^{(k,2)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
, which come from deviation of
the diagonal function
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
from
y^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
,
is written in a form like the rhs of Eq.(
25
), but with
{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{I\mathcal{E}}
replaced by
a slope
{y^{\prime}}^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
of the diagonal function for
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
.
If assuming that
{y^{\prime}}^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
are sufficiently small,
then, the quantity
G^{(k,2)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
would be negligible.

For
l=4
,
G^{(k,4)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})
from offdiagonal elements
should have the same scaling behavior
as that in Eq.(
28
), namely,
M_{\Gamma}^{\lambda-1.5}
.
As discussed in Sec.
III.2
,
under an initial condition (at
t_{0}
) in which there is no correlation
between the initial state and the operator
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
,
e.g., with
\mathcal{E}
initially lying a thermal state,
one has
\lambda=1
.
As a result,
G^{(k,4)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})\sim M_{\Gamma}^{-1/2}
is also negligible.
To summarize, under the conditions discussed above, one has

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})\simeq G^{(k,1)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})\quad\text{for $k\leq k_{\rm ETH}$}.

(51)

For a given time
t
, we use
k_{\rm tru}(t)
to indicate a value of
k
,
at which the rhs of Eq.(
45
) may be effectively truncated;
and below, for brevity, we usually do not write explicitly the dependence of
k_{\rm tru}
on
t
.
Suppose that
(t-t_{0})
is not very large, such that
k_{\rm tru}\leq k_{\rm ETH}
.
Then, substituting Eq.(
50
) with Eq.(
51
) into Eq.(
45
), one gets that

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)\simeq\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})(t-t_{0})^{k}

\displaystyle=\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}\sum_{\eta,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}y^{(k)}_{\eta 0}x_{\alpha\beta\eta}^{(k)\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\rho^{S}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(t_{0})(t-t_{0})^{k}.

(52)

Thus, the RDM
\rho^{S}
at a time
t

is approximately computable from the initial RDM
\rho^{S}(t_{0})
,
if the following conditions are fulfilled.
That is, (i) the environment satisfies the ETH ansatz,
(ii) the environment’s initial state possesses no correlation with the interaction Hamiltonian,
and (iii) all the slopes
{y^{\prime}}^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
of
k\leq k_{\rm tru}(t)
are sufficiently small.

However, practical computation of
\rho^{S}(t)
by making use of Eq.(
52
)
usually becomes more and more difficult with increasing
(t-t_{0})
.
Indeed, this would require larger and larger values of
k_{\rm tru}(t)
, for which
the summed terms on the rhs of Eq.(
52
) become quite complicated.
In addition, even if the operator
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
has a very small slope
{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{I\mathcal{E}}
,
it is unnecessary for other operators
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
of
k\leq k_{\rm tru}(t)
to possess
very small slopes
{y^{\prime}}^{(k)}_{\eta 0}
.

8

8

See, e.g., properties of
(H^{I\mathcal{E}})^{2}
discussed later around Eq.(
81
).

V
Derivation of a master equation

In this section, we discuss a method of deriving master equation for RDM.
We first discuss the strategy to be employed (Sec.
V.1
),
then, compute some quantities needed for the derivation (Sec.
V.2
),
and finally derive a master equation (Sec.
V.3
).
Condition for validity of the method is discussed in Sec.
V.4
.

V.1
Basic strategy of deriving master equation

Before discussing the basic strategy to be employed below,
we recall some features of an ordinary derivation of master equation,
which starts from a differential-integral equation in which the integrand contains
the total density of state
\rho^{S+\mathcal{E}}
.
By Born approximation, which assumes that
\rho^{S+\mathcal{E}}(t)\simeq\rho^{S}(t)\otimes\rho^{\mathcal{E}}_{0}

for all times
t
, the complexity of
mutual influence of the central system
S
and
the environment
\mathcal{E}
in the time evolution is circumvented.
By Markov approximation, which assumes that the RDM in the above-mentioned time integral may be
replaced by the RDM at the considered time, the complexity induced by the evolution
of the RDM is circumvented.
Then, with technical approximations such as rotating wave approximation,
a differential equation is derived for the RDM.

As mentioned previously, one purpose here is to give a derivation of master equation,
which is based on properties of the chaotic dynamics of the environment,
without directly making use of the Born and Markov approximations
whose dynamic prerequisites have not been quantitatively justified.
One useful observation is that,
making use of the formal solution to the evolution of the environmental branches [Eq.(
40
)],
at least formally, one may deal with the mutual influence of
S
and
\mathcal{E}
in the time evolution.
This makes it unnecessary to employ the Born approximation.

To study evolution within a period of
[0,T]
, we are to divide the period into a series of
M
short time intervals,
within each of which the above mentioned formal solution is used for computing the time evolution.
We use
\tau
to indicate the length of the short intervals,
which are separated by instants
t_{m}=m\tau
of
m=0,\ldots,M
, with
T=M\tau
.
Due to smallness of
\tau
,
k_{\rm tru}
is small and, hence, one has

k_{\rm tru}\leq k_{\rm ETH}
for a large environment.
Then, similar to the first equality in Eq.(
52
),
variation of the RDM within the
m
th interval is written as

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m+1})-\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})\simeq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})\tau^{k},

(53)

where
G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
has the same expression
as the rhs of Eq.(
46
) but with
t_{0}
replaced by
t_{m}
.

Further, similar to Eq.(
49
),

G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
is divided into four parts labelled by
l
, that is,

\displaystyle G^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})=\sum_{l=1}^{4}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m}).

(54)

And, for the same reasons as those discussed previously,

G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
of
l=3
are negligible.
However, it is unnecessary for all of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
of
l=2
or
4
to be small,
though we assume smallness of
{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{I\mathcal{E}}
as mentioned previously.
Then, with further division of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}
into
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}

(labelled by
\eta
) written explicitly, one has

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m+1})-\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})\simeq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}\sum_{l\neq 3}\sum_{\eta}\tau^{k}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m}),

(55)

and, as a result,

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(T)-\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(0)\simeq\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}\sum_{l\neq 3}\sum_{\eta}\tau^{k}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m}).

(56)

To proceed, one needs to compute explicit expressions for the terms
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m})

on the rhs of Eq.(
56
) and find out major contributions.
We use
{\mathcal{L}}^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
to indicate the sum of the major
contributions (divided by
\tau
); more exactly,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m}):=

\displaystyle{\sum_{l,\eta}}^{\prime}\tau^{k-1}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m}),

(57)

where the prime means that only nonnegligible terms of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m})
are included.
Furthermore, the sum of
{\mathcal{L}}^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
over
k
, indicated as

{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})
, is written as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}{\mathcal{L}}^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\rm tru}}{\sum_{l,\eta}}^{\prime}\tau^{k-1}G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m}).

(58)

Then, Eq.(
56
) is written as

\displaystyle\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(T)\simeq\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(0)+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\tau{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m}).

(59)

Suppose that dependence of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})

on the labels
\alpha
and
\beta
could turn out to be similar to that for an observable of
S
,
namely,
{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})=\langle\alpha|{\mathcal{L}}(t_{m})|\beta\rangle
, where
{\mathcal{L}}(t_{m})
is some operator.
Then, the change of the RDM is written in an operator form,

\displaystyle\rho^{S}(T)-\rho^{S}(0)\simeq\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\tau{\mathcal{L}}(t_{m}).

(60)

If further
{\mathcal{L}}(t_{m})
takes the form of
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho^{S}(t_{m}))
, then, due to smallness of
\tau
,
approximately
(\rho^{S}(T)-\rho^{S}(0))
can be gotten by solving the following master equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho^{S}(t)}{dt}={\mathcal{L}}(\rho^{S}).

(61)

As an application of the generic framework discussed above, below,
we discuss the simplest nontrivial case of
k_{\rm tru}=2
,
which may happen for a sufficiently small interval length
\tau
.
In this case, one needs to compute contributions from
k=1\,\&\,2
only.
In principle, the framework may work for larger values of
k_{\rm tru}
as well,
though the computation would be much more complicated.

V.2
Explicit expressions of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m})
of
k=1,2

In this section, we derive explicit expressions of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta}(t_{m})

and of
G^{(k,l)}_{\alpha\beta\eta}(t_{m})
for
k=1\,\&\,2
and
l=1,2,4
.
It turns out that only the following four operators
Y^{(k)}_{\eta}
of
\eta=1,2,3,4

V.3
Master equation

In this section, we compare contributions of the four
G
-terms listed above
to the evolution within the whole period of
[0,T]

and show that two of them can be neglected under certain conditions.
This enables derivation of a master equation,
with an explicit form of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
in Eq.(
58
).

Firstly, we compare
G^{(1,4)}_{\alpha\beta 2}(t_{m})
and

\tau G^{(2,1)}_{\alpha\beta 4}(t_{m})
, which are given by
Eq.(
70c
) and Eq.(
85
), respectively.

9
9

9

From Eq.(
70c
), one sees that
G^{(1,4)}_{\alpha\beta 2}
can not be
reduced to a function of elements of the RDM.
Hence, when this term is nonnegligible, the RDM
does not satisfy any simple form of master equation.

From the two equations, one sees that the two terms are qualitatively different.
Hence, although the latter contains
\tau
,
one can not directly conclude that its final contribution to
\rho^{S}(T)
, as a sum over all the short time intervals,
should be smaller than that the former.
Below, we give analysis in their final contributions.

Let us first discuss
G^{(1,4)}_{\alpha\beta 2}(t_{m})
.
Its final contribution is written as

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}G^{(1,4)}_{\alpha\beta 2}(t_{m})\simeq\sigma M^{\xi},

where
\sigma^{2}
represents the variance of
G^{(1,4)}_{\alpha\beta 2}(t_{m})

and
\xi
is some positive parameter, satisfying
\xi\leq 1
.
The value of
\xi
depends on the dynamics of the environment
\mathcal{E}
, as well as on
M
and
\tau
.
For example, at a fixed value of
M
and a very small
\tau
such that
M\tau\ll 1
,
one has

since there is only tiny evolution within the period of
M\tau

What is of interest here is finite
\tau

To have an understanding for the value of
\xi
at finite
\tau

let us analyze factors that control the dynamic evolution of the environmental branches.
From Eqs.(
38

39

the
\alpha

[H^{S}]

H^{I\mathcal{E}}[H^{IS}]

H^{\mathcal{E}}[I]

The first one, namely
[H^{S}]

The second one,

among

\lambda=1.5
in Eq.(
28

These correlations imply time correlations of
H_{\phi,\alpha\gamma}^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}(t)
.

One key observation is a role played by the third factor — the environmental Hamiltonian
H^{\mathcal{E}}
.
It generates chaotic motion of the environment,
which should be able to suppress the above discussed time correlations
of
H_{\phi,\alpha\gamma}^{I\mathcal{E}(4)}(t)
at least for far-separated times.
In other words, the environmental chaotic dynamics should be able to cause decay of the correlations.
As a consequence,
the parameter
\xi
in Eq.(
89
) may be notably smaller than
1
,
even possibly close to
0.5
for sufficiently large
\tau
.

VII
Summary and discussions

In this paper, a method is proposed for studying the RDM of a generic small quantum system,
which is locally coupled to an environment as
a huge many-body quantum chaotic system that satisfies the ETH ansatz.
Initially, the environmental state possesses no correlation with the
interaction-Hamiltonian-matrix elements on the environment’s eigenbasis.
The framework mainly consists of three parts.

The first part is a formal expression for the time evolution of environmental branches,
the inner products of which give the RDM.
Making use of this formal expression and with the help of the ETH ansatz,
it has been shown that under certain condition
the RDM is approximately computable from the initial RDM for times not very long.

The second part is the division of a time period of interest into a series of short
time intervals with length
\tau
.
Within each interval, the above mentioned expression
for the time evolution of environmental branches is expanded up to the second-order terms.
Then, the ETH ansatz is used to make a series of approximations, which much simplifies the result.

And, the third part is a further simplification, which is due to the chaotic dynamics of the environment.
More exactly, chaotic motion of the environment may suppresses correlations among the environmental branches,
which are generated by the interaction along the evolution.
This simplification, finally, gives rise to a Lindblad-form master equation for the RDM.

As an application of the master equation obtained,
decoherence rate under a generic nondissipative system-environment interaction
has been computed.
The obtained result is in consistency with what is known by the RMT,
under an appropriate choice of the value of
\tau
.

The proposed method makes use of neither Born approximation nor Markov approximation.
Instead, it makes use of a series of approximations, each of which has a clear dynamic background.
Below is a list of the main approximations that have been made in the derivation of the master equation.

•

Contributions related to the case of
l=2
in Eqs.(
70b
) and (
78b
)
are negligible, due to the assumed slow variation of the diagonal function
of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
in the ETH ansatz.

•

All contributions related to the case of
l=3
in Eq.(
19
) are negligible,
which come from fluctuations of diagonal elements in the ETH ansatz.
These contributions have been shown negligible by direct computation.

•

The formal solution to the environmental-branch evolution within each short time interval
is truncated at the second order expansion, with
k_{\rm tru}=2
, due to smallness of
\tau
.

•

Contributions that appear on the rhs of Eqs.(
87
)-(
88
) are negligible,
mainly due to smallness of
\tau
.

•

The contribution on the rhs of Eq.(
92
) is negligible,
due to the chaotic dynamics of the environment
and by an appropriate choice of the value of
\tau
which is not very small.

•

The contribution on the rhs of Eq.(
95
) is negligible,
due to the chaotic dynamics of the environment.

Two final remarks:

(i) In principle, discussions that lead to Eq.(
92
) should be generalizable
to the cases related to Eqs.(
70b
) and (
78b
),
which would loose the requirement on slow variation of the diagonal function of
H^{I\mathcal{E}}
.
But, discussion on conditions for this possibility may be much more complicated.
And, (ii) A topic of future interest is to study impacts of
breakdown of some of the above discussed approximations.
In particular, it seems reasonable to expect effects that are usually under the name of nonMarkovian.
