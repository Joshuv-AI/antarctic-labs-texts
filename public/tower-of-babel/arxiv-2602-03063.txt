The Small-Dispersion Limit of the Intermediate Long Wave Equation via Semiclassical Soliton Ensembles
Matthew Dominique Mitchell

Abstract
We study the small-dispersion limit of the intermediate long wave (ILW) equation, specifically on a class of well-behaved initial conditions u 0 u_{0} where the number of solitons in the solution increases without bound. First, we conduct a formal WKB-style analysis on the ILW direct scattering problem, generating approximate eigenvalues and norming constants. We then use this to define a modified set of scattering data and rigorously analyze the associated inverse scattering problem. The main results include demonstrating L 2 L^{2} -convergence of the solution at t = 0 t=0 to the original initial condition u 0 u_{0} and for 0 < t < t c 0<t<t_{\mathrm{c}} to the associated solution of invicid Burgers’ equation, where t c t_{\mathrm{c}} is the time of gradient catastrophe.



Keywords:
integrable systems, WKB analysis, logarithmic potential, equilibrium measure

The term “semiclassical” comes from quantum mechanics. The small-dispersion limit of the KdV equation corresponds under the scattering transform exactly to a semiclassical analysis of the Schrödinger equation. So in this work, “small dispersion” and “semiclassical” mean the same thing on the PDE and spectral sides of the IST, respectively.

be the associated semiclassical soliton ensemble according to Definition

.
Furthermore, let

denote the solution to invicid Burgers’ equation (

.
Recall that this is only globally well-defined up until the time of gradient catastrophe

Then as

To conclude, we discuss in Section

2
Formal WKB for the ILW Scattering Equation

A. A. Minzoni and T. Miloh were the first to analyze the semiclassical asymptotics of the eigenvalues of the ILW scattering equation (
1.8
) for Klause-Shaw potentials where the number of eigenvalues increases as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}

[
27
]
.
Our goal in this section is to present their arguments with several key modifications.

1.

Their analysis reveals an infinite number of asymptotic solutions to the scattering equation of which they throw away all but two, whereas we categorize these solutions into those that can form normalizable wavefunctions (scattering solutions) and those that cannot (extraneous solutions).

2.

They expand the wavefunction along the lower boundary of the strip and neglect information about the solution interior to in the strip or on the upper boundary, whereas we expand the wavefunction around the center of the strip, account for the
\epsilon
-sized imaginary component of the strip, and give an expansion formally valid for the whole strip.

3.

Their asymptotic solutions for the wavefunction are given in terms of implicit solutions to transcendental equations, whereas we write our solutions explicitly in terms of classical functions.

4.

They only identify and make use of one solution to the proposed model turning point equation, whereas we provide asymptotics of the infinitely many solutions and demonstrate via a matching argument that indeed the only one which was identified in
[
27
]
participates in the construction of normalizable wavefunctions.

Additionally, we also provide the entirely new result of a formal asymptotic formula for the norming constants associated to the eigenvalues in the ILW scattering data.

2.1
Asymptotic Solutions Away from the Turning Points

We first make the substitution in the ILW scattering equation (
1.16
)

\phi(z)=\Phi(z)\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\zeta}{\epsilon}z\right)

(2.1)

to put (
1.16
) into what we will call “canonical form,”

\mathrm{i}\epsilon\Phi^{+}_{x}(x)+u(x)\Phi^{+}(x)=\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\Phi^{-}(x)\,.

(2.2)

In this section, we take the potential in the ILW scattering equation
u
to be an admissible initial condition, satisfying Definition
1.1
.
We wish to learn something about the solutions to (
2.2
) as we take the semiclassical limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.
This was done in
[
27
]
by conducting a WKB-style approximation similar to that for semiclassical limit of the time-independent Schrödinger equation.
Supposing that
\Phi(z)
does not vanish in the strip, so that we can make the exponential substitution

\Phi(z)=\exp\left(S(z)\right)

(2.3)

and, moving all the exponentials to one side, we obtain the new form of the scattering equation

\mathrm{i}\epsilon S_{x}^{+}+u=\zeta\exp\left(-2\delta\zeta+S^{-}-S^{+}\right)\,.

(2.4)

If we begin looking for a dominant balance between the two terms on the left, this suggests a formal expansion in integer powers of
\epsilon
with leading coefficient
1/\epsilon
,

S(z)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon}\theta(z)+\alpha(z)+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\beta(z)+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\,.

(2.5)

Note though, that because the vertical width of the strip
\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon}
is shrinking as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, the real and imaginary parts of
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
should contribute at different orders to this series, i.e. since
-\delta\epsilon\leq y\leq\delta\epsilon
,
y
is itself
O\left(\epsilon\right)
.
If the functions are assumed analytic in the expansion, then we should Taylor expand them away from the center of the strip to be able to accurately order every term in the series:

S(z)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon}\theta(x)+\left(\alpha(x)+\frac{y}{\epsilon}\theta_{x}(x)\right)+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\left(\beta(x)+\frac{y}{\epsilon}\alpha_{x}(x)+\frac{y^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\theta_{xx}(x)\right)+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\,.

(2.6)

Plugging this formal expansion into (
2.4
), we note that this also brings the right side into the dominant balance,

\displaystyle\theta_{x}+u+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\alpha_{x}-\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon\theta_{xx}+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta\exp\left(-2\delta\zeta+2\delta\theta_{x}+\mathrm{i}2\delta\epsilon\alpha_{x}+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\right)

(2.7)

\displaystyle=\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\theta_{x})}\left(1+\mathrm{i}2\delta\epsilon\alpha_{x}+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\right)\,.

(2.8)

Equating order-by-order in
\epsilon
, we have the relations determining the formal functions
\theta
and
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\theta_{x}+u

\displaystyle=\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\theta_{x})}\,,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{x}-\delta\theta_{xx}

\displaystyle=2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\theta_{x})}\alpha_{x}\,.

(2.10)

We will define a function for
\eta,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
by

G(\eta;\zeta):=-\eta+\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\eta)}\,,

(2.11)

then the relationship (
2.9
) determines
\theta_{x}
by the equation

G(\theta_{x}(x);\zeta)=u(x)

(2.12)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is clear that this relationship is transcendental with infinitely many solutions due to the exponential function in
G
.
However, with some simple manipulations, we can solve this equation using the Lambert
W
-function
[
29
, Section 4.13]
, also known as the product-logarithm (See Section
2.1.1
).
We will let
U\in\mathbb{R}
stand for the output of
G
to define the branched inverse function for
G
on its first variable:

G^{-1}_{n}(U;\zeta):=-U-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{n}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+U)})\,.

(2.13)

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
denotes which of the standard branches of the Lambert
W
-function we use.
This means for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, there is a leading-order phase which is given by

\theta_{x}(x)=G^{-1}_{n}(u(x);\zeta)\,.

(2.14)

Once we have a solution for
\theta_{x}
, now we look at the next-to-leading-order term, determined by (
2.10
), and rearranging gives

\alpha_{x}(x)=\frac{\delta\ \theta_{xx}(x)}{\displaystyle 1-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\theta_{x}(x))}}=\partial_{x}\int^{\theta_{x}(x)}\frac{\delta\,\mathrm{d}\eta}{\displaystyle 1-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\eta)}}\,.

(2.15)

This integral can be evaluated with a change of variables
u=2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\eta)}
and then a partial-fraction decomposition. Using the implicit definition of
\theta_{x}
(
2.12
) as well as its explicit equality (
2.14
) we find the result:

\displaystyle\alpha(x)=\int^{\theta_{x}(x)}\frac{\delta\,\mathrm{d}\eta}{1-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-\eta)}}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\displaystyle-W_{n}\left(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-u(x))}\right)}{\displaystyle 1+W_{n}\left(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta-u(x))}\right)}\right)+C

(2.16)

where
C\in\mathbb{C}
is an integration constant.
The presence of the logarithm in (
2.16
) and the exponential in (
2.3
) motivates the definition of the amplitude functions for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}

A_{n}(U;\zeta):=\left(\frac{\displaystyle-W_{n}\left(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+U}\right)}{\displaystyle 1+W_{n}\left(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+U)}\right)}\right)^{1/2}

(2.17)

where we take the principal square-root for
U>0
near zero and then use the continuous branch from then on.
Should we run into a zero or singularity for
U\in\mathbb{R}
inside of the square-root, we can always arbitrarily choose to extend the function around this by taking the branch that is continuous for
U
with a small positive imaginary part.
Then, choosing a branch
n\in\mathbb{Z}
of the Lambert
W
-function and taking the integration constant to be zero (a freedom we have because the scattering equation is linear), we have from (
2.16
)
\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(x)}=A_{n}(u(x);\zeta)
.
Integrating
\theta_{x}
from (
2.14
) in
x
, and assembling the remaining terms in (
2.6
) to constant order in
\epsilon
, we can define the formal expansions for the solutions to the scattering equation for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, any spectral parameter value
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
and any initial point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{0})

\displaystyle:=A_{n}\big(u(x);\zeta\big)\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon}\int^{x}_{x_{0}}G^{-1}_{n}(u(x^{\prime});\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\frac{y}{\epsilon}G^{-1}_{n}(u(x);\zeta)\Bigg)\big(1+O\left(\epsilon\right)\big)\,.

(2.18)

We note that this formal asymptotic solution (
2.18
) shares many characteristics with the solution generated from WKB analysis of the time-independent Schrödinger equation. The fast instantaneous phase coefficient
\theta_{x}/\epsilon
and the slowly varying amplitude
A=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}
are both locally determined as functions of the potential.
There also appear to be quite a few differences, like the fact that there are infinitely many formal asymptotic solutions.
We also note that this limit appears to be singular, in the sense that we lose all information about the wavefunction’s analyticity inside the strip: if
u
is not analytic,
G^{-1}_{n}(u(x);\zeta)
, its integral, or
A_{n}\big(u(x);\zeta\big)
are not either.
Of course, this was to be expected as the strip’s vertical width shrinks to zero, and to keep terms well-aligned in our formal
\epsilon
-series, we had to break the analyticity at the outset.

2.1.1
Explanation of the Standard Branches of the Lambert
W
-function and Identifying Extraneous and Scattering Solutions

To understand the behavior of the solutions, we give a brief overview of the standard branch definitions of the Lambert
W
-function.
The Lambert
W
-function is the branched inverse of the complex analytic function
w\mapsto w\mathrm{e}^{w}
, where the range of each branch in the
w
-plane is a maximal domain of univalence of the inverse.
The standard branch definitions have as the boundaries of their ranges the curves defined by
w\mathrm{e}^{w}<0
, excluding the line segment from
w=-1
to
w=0
.
One of the connected curves of these branch range boundaries is exactly the negative quadratrix
-\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\{-z\,|\,z\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}\}
.
There are an infinite number of branches, due to the exponential in the mapping
w\mapsto w\mathrm{e}^{w}
, falling into three qualitative categories:

1.

\mathbf{n\neq\pm 1,0}
. These branches
W_{n}(z)
all have one branch point at
z=0
with cuts taken along the negative real axis.
The output on the cut axis are taken to be continuous with the values above the cut.
For
n>0
,
W_{n}(z)
for
z
just above the cut limit to a smooth curve from
z=+\infty+\mathrm{i}(2n+3)\pi
to
z=-\infty+\mathrm{i}(2n+1)\pi
. From below, they limit to another smooth curve from
z=+\infty+\mathrm{i}(2n+1)\pi
to
z=-\infty+\mathrm{i}2n\pi
. In this sense, these branch ranges behave very similar to the branch ranges of the logarithm, but right sides of the ranges have been sheared vertically by
\pi
. For
n<0
, the right sides are sheared vertically by
-\pi
.

2.

\mathbf{n=\pm 1}
.
W_{\pm 1}(z)
both have two branch points, one at
z=0
which is logarithmic in nature, and one at
z=-\mathrm{e}^{-1}
which has a square-root behavior.
Again, the outputs along the branch cuts along the negative reals are taken to be continuous with those just above.
W_{1}(z)
for
z
just above the cut maps to a smooth curve from
z=+\infty+\mathrm{i}3\pi
to
z=-\infty+\mathrm{i}2\pi
.
For
z
just below the cut, the behavior is different and depends on which side of the branch point
z=-\mathrm{e}^{-1}
we limit to: for
\mathrm{Re}\left[z\right]<-\mathrm{e}^{-1}
, this approaches the upper half of
-\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, and if
-\mathrm{e}^{-1}<\mathrm{Re}\left[z\right]<0
, then this approaches the half line
(-\infty,-1)
.

W_{-1}(z)
is similar but the behavior is flipped between the directions approaching the cut and its now the lower half of
-\mathcal{Q}_{1}
.

3.

\mathbf{n=0}
.
W_{0}(z)
has only one branch point at
z=-\mathrm{e}^{-1}
of a square-root nature and the cut extends out along the negative real axis.
This branch maps the entire plane minus the cut to the interior of
-\mathcal{Q}_{1}
in a one-to-one manner.

W_{0}(z)
for
z
just above the cut approach the upper half of
-\mathcal{Q}_{1}
while those below map to the lower half.

We now note that the argument of the Lambert
W
terms in the asymptotic solution
\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
(

) are always
-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))}
.
Clearly, we should avoid
\zeta=0
since this makes all Lambert
W
arguments zero for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
which is a logarithmic branch point for all branches
n\neq 0
and
W_{0}(0)=0
.
Thus, for any
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}/\{0\}
, the portion
-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}
is always nonzero.
The multiplying term
0<\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta u(x)}<1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
so changing
x\in\mathbb{R}
just amounts to moving the argument of the Lambert
W
-function along a single complex ray, with a minimum and maximum possible magnitude since
u(x)
is bounded,

\left|-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\right|\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta u_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq\left|-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))}\right|\leq\left|-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\right|\,.

(2.19)

As
W_{n}(z)
is continuous for any branch
n\in\mathbb{Z}
along any ray bounded away from the origin, the only way the formal asymptotic solution
\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
(
2.18
) can be discontinuous is if the term in the denominator of the amplitude
A_{n}(x;\zeta)
(
2.17
),

1+W_{n}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))})\,,

(2.20)

is ever zero.
There are only two branches of the Lambert
W
-function which contain
-1
in their ranges:
n=-1,0
.
This motivates us to group the formal asymptotic solutions into two categories.

Definition 2.1
.

We refer to
\Phi_{-1}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
and
\Phi_{0}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
as the scattering solutions while
\Phi_{n}(x;\zeta,x_{0})
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\neq-1,0
will be referred to as the extraneous solutions.

The names designate which solutions we expect to contribute to the WKB analysis and which ones should not.
The idea is that a blowup in an asymptotic solution for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
indicates a turning point where a matching procedure must be completed in order to construct a normalizable wavefunction.
If a solution does not blowup for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then this solution should not interact with any others through a matching procedure.
The conclusion drawn is that either the solutions corresponding to
n\neq-1,0
are truly extraneous to the ILW scattering problem, or contribute beyond all orders in
\epsilon
.

In order for the amplitude term
A_{n}(u(x);\zeta)
with
n=-1,0
to blow up at some point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, by (

) its required that

-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\leq-e^{-1}\leq-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta u_{\mathrm{max}}}\,.

(2.21)

That is,
-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}\in[-\mathrm{e}^{2\delta u_{\mathrm{max}}-1},-\mathrm{e}^{-1}]
.
Miraculously,
-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta\zeta}
is exactly the inverse of the Lambert

-function applied to
-2\delta\zeta
.
Thus,
-2\delta\zeta
needs to be in any of the image sets

.
To avoid redundancies, we can fix a branch range where

Then we can have blowups in the scattering solutions if and only if

\zeta\in-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{-1}([-\mathrm{e}^{2\delta u_{\mathrm{max}}-1},-\mathrm{e}^{-1}])\subset-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{-1}([-\infty,-\mathrm{e}^{-1}])=\mathcal{Q}_{\delta\,+}\,,

where we are using the quadratrix interval notation (

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{-1}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))})\in\left(\frac{1}{2\delta},\zeta\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}},

(2.28)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{0}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))})\in\left(\zeta^{*},\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}\,,

(2.29)

Roughly, this means the formal asymptotic solutions
\Phi_{-1}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
and
\Phi_{0}(z;\zeta,x_{0})

(2.30)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{-1}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))})\in\left(\frac{1}{2\delta},+\infty\right),

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\delta}W_{0}(-2\delta\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x))})\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)\,.

Roughly, this means that the formal asymptotic solutions

2.2
The Model Turning Point Equation

The interpretation of the amplitude term of
\Phi_{-1}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
and
\Phi_{0}(z;\zeta,x_{0})
(
2.18
) blowing up at a turning point is that the dominant balance which we considered in (
2.4
) is only appropriate for
u(x)
away from the level
E(\zeta)
.
In the neighborhood of their equality, some terms in the scattering equation that were previously leading order, cancel, and a new dominant balance is formed.

We derive the model equation for a transition across a turning point by fixing
\zeta\in\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}
and considering an
\epsilon
-shrinking interval in
x\in[a(\epsilon),b(\epsilon)]\subset\mathbb{R}
where there is one and only one point
x_{0}\in[a(\epsilon),b(\epsilon)]
such that (
2.25
), or equivalently (
2.26
), is satisfied. Then, by substituting

\Phi(z)=\Psi(z)\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{u(x_{0})}{\epsilon}z\right)

(2.33)

into the canonical scattering equation (
2.2
), we have after clearing exponential factors

\mathrm{i}\epsilon\Psi_{x}^{+}+(u(x)-u(x_{0}))\Psi^{+}=\zeta\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta(\zeta+u(x_{0}))}\Psi^{-}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{2\delta}\Psi^{-}\,.

(2.34)

If the rate at which the interval shrinks around the turning point is chosen appropriately, we expect that we may expand
u(x)-u(x_{0})=u^{\prime}(x_{0})\ (x-x_{0})+O\left((x-x_{0})^{2}\right)
and, as long as we have
u^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
, then we may neglect the sub-leading terms in the Taylor expansion. Thus, we now have

\mathrm{i}\epsilon\Psi_{x}^{+}+u^{\prime}(x_{0})\ (x-x_{0})\Psi^{+}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{2\delta}\Psi^{-}\,.

(2.35)

We may now want to remove as much dependence of the equation on the values of
u^{\prime}(x_{0}),\epsilon,\delta
and
x_{0}
as we can via a shift and re-scaling of the independent variable.
For this, let
\psi(Z):=\Psi(z)
where
Z:=a(z-x_{0})
for some yet undetermined
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\neq 0
.
Then the equation becomes for
X=\mathrm{Re}\left[Z\right]
,

\mathrm{i}\epsilon a\ \psi_{X}^{+}+\frac{u^{\prime}(x_{0})}{a}X\psi^{+}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{2\delta}\psi^{-}\,.

(2.36)

Additionally, due to the scaling
\psi
is analytic on the strip
\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon a}
. It is now apparent that
\epsilon
can not be removed entirely from the equation: trying to equate any two terms, in an attempt to factor out an overall scale, will always leave the third term
\epsilon
-dependent. This simply indicates that the solutions to the scattering equation depend nontrivially on the semiclassical parameter
\epsilon
. Instead, we use this scaling to reduce the number of parameters in the equation from two,
u^{\prime}(x_{0})
and
\delta
, to one. First, we will consider
u^{\prime}(x_{0})>0
, then define
a
and the new height parameter
h>0
by

a=\frac{1}{2\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad h=(2\delta)^{2}u^{\prime}(x_{0})\,.

(2.37)

Then, after multiplying through by
2\delta
and bringing all of the terms to the same side, we have the model equation for the behavior near a turning point as

0=\mathrm{i}\epsilon\psi_{X}^{+}+hX\psi^{+}-\mathrm{e}^{-1}\psi^{-}

(2.38)

where
\psi
is analytic on the strip
\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon/2}
.

For
u^{\prime}(x)<0
we instead make the substitution
\psi(Z):=\Psi(-z^{*})^{*}
where
Z:=(z-x_{0})/2\delta
. Then

\psi^{\pm}(X)=\psi\left(X\mp\mathrm{i}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)=\Psi(-2\delta X\mp\mathrm{i}\delta)^{*}=\Psi^{\pm}(-2\delta X)^{*}\,.

(2.39)

Taking the conjugate of (
2.36
), using these boundary value identities and evaluating at
x=-2\delta X+x_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\epsilon\Psi^{+}_{x}(-2\delta X)^{*}-2\delta u^{\prime}(x_{0})\,X\,\Psi^{+}(-2\delta X)^{*}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{2\delta}\Psi^{-}(-2\delta X)^{*}

(2.40)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\epsilon}{2\delta}\psi^{+}_{X}(X)-2\delta u^{\prime}(x_{0})\,X\,\psi^{+}(X)-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{2\delta}\psi^{-}(X)\,.

(2.41)

After multiplying through by
2\delta
and defining

h=-(2\delta)^{2}u^{\prime}(x_{0})>0

(2.42)

we have the same form as (
2.38
). Thus, we can handle the analysis at both kinds of turning points in a uniform manner.

2.2.1
Solving the Model Turning Point Equation by Laplace Transform

Suppose there is an analytic function
\hat{\psi}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
such that a solution to the model turning point equation (
2.38
) could be formulated as

\psi(Z)=\int_{C}\hat{\psi}(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kZ/\epsilon}\mathrm{d}k

(2.43)

where
C
is a contour on which the integral above converges rapidly enough for any
Z\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon/2}
so that we may differentiate under the integral,

\psi_{X}^{+}(X)=\int_{C}\hat{\psi}(k)\partial_{X}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\mathrm{d}k=\int_{C}\mathrm{i}\frac{k}{\epsilon}\ \hat{\psi}(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\mathrm{d}k\,;

(2.44)

that we may integrate-by-parts with zero boundary term,

\displaystyle X\psi^{+}(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{C}\hat{\psi}(k)\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}-\mathrm{i}\epsilon\partial_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\right)\mathrm{d}k

(2.45)

\displaystyle=\int_{C}\mathrm{i}\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{\psi}(k)+\hat{\psi}^{\prime}(k)\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\mathrm{d}k;

(2.46)

and that evaluating at both the upper and lower boundaries are simultaneously well-defined

\psi^{-}(X)=\int_{C}\hat{\psi}(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon+\mathrm{i}/2)}\,\mathrm{d}k=\int_{C}\hat{\psi}(k)\,\mathrm{e}^{-k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\,\mathrm{d}k\,.

(2.47)

Plugging this form into the model turning point equation (
2.38
),

0=\int_{C}\left(-k\hat{\psi}(k)+\mathrm{i}h^{2}\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{\psi}(k)+\hat{\psi}^{\prime}(k)\right)-\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}\hat{\psi}(k)\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(X/\epsilon-\mathrm{i}/2)}\,\mathrm{d}k\,,

(2.48)

and simply requiring the integrand be zero identically, we get a first-order differential equation for
\hat{\psi}
. This has the solution

\hat{\psi}(k)=A\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{h\epsilon}\left[\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}\right]-\frac{k}{2}\right)

(2.49)

for any
A\in\mathbb{C}
independent of the variables
Z
and
k
. This gives our solution as

\psi(Z)=A\int_{C}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{h\epsilon}\left[\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}\right]+\mathrm{i}k\left(\frac{Z}{\epsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}k\,.

(2.50)

For large
k
and fixed
Z\in\mathbb{C}
, the real part of the exponent is dominated by the behavior of the imaginary part of the function

f(k):=\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}\,.

(2.51)

The landscape of
\mathrm{Im}\left[f(k)\right]=\mathrm{Im}\left[k\right]\mathrm{Re}\left[k\right]+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{Re}\left[k\right]-1}\sin(\mathrm{Im}\left[k\right])
determines where the contour
C
may end in order to force rapid convergence of the integral.
In analogy with geographical topography, we’ll refer to unbounded regions where
\mathrm{Im}\left[f\right]\to-\infty
as
k\to\infty
“valleys” and unbounded regions where
\mathrm{Im}\left[f\right]\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
“mountains.”
Then finding a suitable integration contour
C
becomes the problem of connecting two valleys.
In the west (
\mathrm{Re}\left[k\right]<0
), the exponential term
\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}
dominates
\mathrm{Im}\left[f\right]
and so we see there is an infinite alternating series of mountains and valleys spreading out in the north-south direction.
The valleys lie around the lines
\mathrm{Im}\left[k\right]=\pi\nu
where
\nu\in 2\mathbb{Z}-1/2
.
The mountains occur in between, around the lines
\mathrm{Im}\left[k\right]=\pi\nu
where
\nu\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1/2
.
In the east (
\mathrm{Re}\left[k\right]>0
), the quadratic term
k^{2}/2
dominates, and we get just one large valley in the south around the argument
-\pi/4
and one large mountain in the north around the argument
\pi/4
.
These features are easily seen in the landscape of Figure
7
.

2.2.2
A Basis of Solutions to the Model Turning Point Equation

Its clear that there are infinitely many homotopy classes of integration contours
C
which connect valleys in the landscape of
\mathrm{Im}\left[f\right]
.
Towards constructing a basis of solutions, we define a basis of contours.
Let
C_{\nu}
for
\nu\in 2\mathbb{Z}-1/2
be any piecewise-smooth, simple contour such that

C_{\nu}\quad\text{begins at}\quad k=-\infty+\mathrm{i}\pi\nu\quad\text{and ends at}\quad k=-\infty+\mathrm{i}\pi\left(\nu+2\right)\,.

(2.52)

These are contours which connect pairs of nearest-neighbor valleys in the west. Additionally, we define
C_{-\infty}
to be any piecewise-smooth, simple contour such that

C_{-\infty}\quad\text{begins at}\quad k=-\infty-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\text{and ends at}\quad k=\infty\ \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\,.

(2.53)

An arbitrary contour
C
which connects any valley to any other is Cauchy-equivalent to a finite sum of these basis contours
\{C_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\{-\infty\}\cup(2\mathbb{Z}-1/2)}

Hence, the solution (
2.50
) associated to
C
can be written as the sum of the fundamental solutions

\displaystyle\psi_{\nu}(Z;\epsilon,h)

\displaystyle:=\int_{C_{\nu}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{h\epsilon}f(k)+\mathrm{i}k\left(\frac{Z}{\epsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}k\quad\text{for all}\ \nu\in\{-\infty\}\cup\left(2\mathbb{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(2.54)

:
The landscape of
\mathrm{Im}\left[f\right]
. Yellow shading corresponds to a positive value while blue shading corresponds to a negative value.
Additionally, the fine black lines are some particular level contours.
The alternating mountain and valley structure can be clearly seen on the left of the image with the valleys in blue and the mountains in yellow.
On the right, we also see the northern mountain (yellow) and the southern valley (blue) in the first and fourth quadrants respectively.

as defined above are depicted in red with their labels.

As
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, the asymptotics of these fundamental solutions can be computed using the method of steepest descent.
For this reason, we need to understand the dependence of the saddle points of the function in the exponent of (
2.54
) on
Z
and
h
.

2.2.3
The Behavior of the Saddle Points

Consider
Z=X+\mathrm{i}\epsilon(\tilde{Y}-1/2)\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon/2}
with
\tilde{Y}\in[0,1]
, then the contribution of
\tilde{Y}
to the exponent in the integrand of (
2.54
) is next-too-leading-order in
\epsilon
,

\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{h\epsilon}f(k)+\mathrm{i}k\left(\frac{Z}{\epsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{h\epsilon}f(k;\tilde{X})-k\tilde{Y}\right)

(2.55)

where we’ve introduced

f(k;\tilde{X}):=f(k)-k\tilde{X}=\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}-k\tilde{X}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{X}=hX

(2.56)

to simplify notation. This means the saddle points that we are interested in are defined by

0=\partial_{k}f(k;\tilde{X})=k+\mathrm{e}^{-k-1}-\tilde{X}\,,

(2.57)

which can be expressed in terms of the Lambert
W
-function

k_{n}(\tilde{X})=\tilde{X}+W_{n}(-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{X}-1})\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.58)

There are three qualitatively distinct configurations of these saddle points as
X\in\mathbb{R}
is allowed to vary:

1.

For
\tilde{X}<0
large, we see from the asymptotics of the Lambert-W function
[
29
, Equation 4.13.1_1]
,

\displaystyle k_{n}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}+\big(-\tilde{X}-1-\log\big|\tilde{X}+1\big|+\mathrm{i}(2n+1)\pi+o(1)\big)

(2.59)

\displaystyle=-\big(\log\big|\tilde{X}+1\big|+1\big)+\mathrm{i}(2n+1)\pi+o(1)\,.

(2.60)

That is, they are about evenly spaced in the imaginary direction and with real part going like a negative log of
|\tilde{X}|
. The saddle points
k_{n}(\tilde{X})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
are all simple.

2.

As
\tilde{X}
increases towards zero, the saddle points below the real-line move upwards and the saddle points above the real-line move down. All of the saddle points maintain their distance from each other except for
k_{0}(\tilde{X})
and
k_{-1}(\tilde{X})
, which coincide at
\tilde{X}=0
. As
\tilde{X}
increases past zero, the saddle points
k_{0}(\tilde{X})
and
k_{-1}(\tilde{X})
move apart again but now on the real line, in the character of a fold coalescing of saddle points.

3.

For
\tilde{X}>0
large, we note the asymptotics of the Lambert-W function are distinct between the 0-branch and the others.

k_{0}(\tilde{X})=\tilde{X}+O\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{X}-1}\right)

(2.61)

while

\displaystyle k_{n}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}+\big(-\tilde{X}-1-\log\big|\tilde{X}+1\big|+\mathrm{i}2n_{+}\pi+o(1)\big)\quad\text{for all}\quad n\neq 0

(2.62)

\displaystyle=-\big(\log\big|\tilde{X}+1\big|+1\big)+\mathrm{i}2n_{+}\pi+o(1)\,.

(2.63)

with
n_{+}=n
if
n>0
and
n_{+}=n+1
if
n<0
. So there is again a vertical tower of about evenly spaced saddle points in the imaginary direction and with real part going like a negative
\log|\tilde{X}|
. However, this tower of saddle points is now centered on the real line instead of symmetric about it and there is one saddle point which has split off and is at about
\tilde{X}
on the real-line. Once again, the saddle points
k_{n}(\tilde{X})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
are all simple.

Matching at the Turning Points

O\left((x-x_{0})^{2}\right)

This is reasonable only if we are working in a shrinking

By the transformation
\tilde{X}=h(x-x_{0})/2\delta

\tilde{X}=0

We now heuristically justify the construction of normalizable eigenfunctions
\psi
of the ILW scattering equation (
2.2
) by matching the formal asymptotic solutions (
2.18
) across turning points by way of the rigorous asymptotics of our basis of solutions to the model turning point equation (
2.38
).
Assuming the potential
u_{0}
is an admissible initial condition satisfying Definition
1.1
, then for a particular level
\zeta\in(1/2\delta,\zeta_{\mathrm{max}})_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}
there are two distinct turning points
x_{\pm}(\zeta)
satisfying (
2.26
) and ordered
x_{-}(\zeta)<x_{+}(\zeta)
.
These split
\mathbb{R}
into three disjoint regions, the “left classically forbidden” region
F_{-}(\zeta)
, the “classically allowed” region
A(\zeta)
and the “right classically forbidden” region
F_{+}(\zeta)
define by

\displaystyle F_{-}(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\big(-\infty,x_{-}(\zeta)\big)\,,

(2.68)

\displaystyle A(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\big(x_{-}(\zeta),x_{+}(\zeta)\big)\,,

(2.69)

\displaystyle F_{+}(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\big(x_{+}(\zeta),+\infty\big)\,.

(2.70)

In each of these three regions, we consider an infinite linear combination of asymptotic solutions (
2.18
).

\displaystyle\Phi_{F-}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{(n)}_{-}\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{-}(\zeta))\text{ for }x\in F_{-}(\zeta),

(2.71)

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{(n)}_{-}\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{-}(\zeta))=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{(n)}_{+}\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{+}(\zeta))\text{ for }x\in A(\zeta),

(2.72)

\displaystyle\Phi_{F+}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{(n)}_{+}\Phi_{n}(z;\zeta,x_{+}(\zeta))\text{ for }x\in F_{+}(\zeta)\,,

(2.73)

with
\{f^{(n)}_{\pm},a^{(n)}_{\pm}\}_{n\in Z}\subset\mathbb{C}
are some possibly
\epsilon
-dependent but definitely
z
-independent constants.
Additionally, we wrote the same expansion in the classically allowed region two different ways, one integrating in from the left turning point and one from the right turning point which are equivalent when the coefficients satisfy the relationship

\frac{a^{(n)}_{+}}{a^{(n)}_{-}}=\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon}\int_{x_{-}(\zeta)}^{x_{+}(\zeta)}G^{-1}_{n}(u(x^{\prime});\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)\,.

(2.74)

which can be required as this is a
z
-independent constant.

Now, we also introduce the
\epsilon
-shrinking transition regions around the turning points

T_{\pm}(\zeta,\epsilon)=\left(x_{\pm}(\zeta)-\epsilon^{\alpha}2\delta M^{\chi}_{\pm},\ x_{\pm}(\zeta)+\epsilon^{\alpha}2\delta M^{\chi}_{\pm}\right)\,,

(2.75)

for some
M^{\chi}_{\pm}>0
and
1/2<\alpha<2/3
.
We write
x\in T_{\pm}(\zeta,\epsilon)
as
x=x_{\pm}(\zeta)\mp\epsilon^{\alpha}2\delta\chi_{\pm}

(2.76)

\chi_{\pm}>0

\chi_{\pm}<0

x\in F_{-}(\zeta)

G^{-1}_{n}(u_{0}(x);\zeta)

n=-1

In the other cases of

\Phi_{n}(x;\zeta,x_{-}(\zeta))

\Phi_{F-}(x,\zeta)

n\leq-1

in the definition of our modified scattering data. Because the matching argument did not handle errors in a rigorous way, we cannot make any statements about the small-dispersion limit for an exact initial condition. Instead, we content ourselves to study the inverse problem for this modified scattering data, which we expect to be close to the true scattering data as we take the small-dispersion limit.
The sense in which we mean that the semiclassical soliton ensemble is “close” to the original initial condition is that the initial condition is recovered as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
for
t=0
, as will be demonstrated in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3
Inverse Scattering for the Semiclassical Soliton Ensemble

We now turn our attention to the process of constructing a solution to the ILW equation using inverse scattering.
Since the scattering data that we derived in the previous section through the direct scattering problem is not rigorous, we resign ourselves to study an inverse scattering problem which we expect to be asymptotically close as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
to the exact inverse scattering problem associated to a given initial condition
u_{0}
.

Definition 3.1

(The ILW semiclassical soliton ensemble)
.

For an admissible initial condition
u_{0}
satisfying Definition
1.1
, we define the associated semiclassical soliton ensemble for the ILW equation to be the sequence for
N\in\mathbb{N}
of
N
-soliton solutions (
1.21
) where we take
\epsilon=\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
and plug in the modified scattering data from Definition
2.3
.
It is convenient to express the
N
-soliton solution for the semiclassical soliton ensemble as

u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)=\partial_{x}D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t)\,,

(3.1)

where
D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
is the symmetric, finite-difference operator

D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t):=\frac{F_{N}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)-F_{N}(x-\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)}{\mathrm{i}2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle Z_{N}(z,t)

\displaystyle:=\det\left(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)\,,

(3.3)

\displaystyle F_{N}(z,t)

\displaystyle:=2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\log Z_{N}(z,t)\,.

(3.4)

We remind the reader that the
N
-soliton formula is one of only a few aspects of the ILW IST that rests on rigorous foundation since (see
[
24
]
[Chapter 5] for a proof)
Analyzing the semiclassical soliton ensemble (
3.1
) amounts to studying the behavior of the determinant in (
3.3
) as the matrix size grows without bound.

3.1
Expanding the Determinant

We begin our analysis by conducting a “Fredholm-type expansion” of the determinant that appears in (
3.3
).
To demonstrate this expansion, let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathbb{C}_{M\times M}
and consider

\det\left(I_{M}+A\right)=\begin{vmatrix}1+A_{1\,1}&A_{1\,2}&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
A_{2\,1}&1+A_{2\,2}&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{M\,1}&A_{M\,2}&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}\,.

(3.5)

We use linearity of the determinant in the first column to expand our determinant into a sum of two: one with the contribution from
I_{M}
in the first column and another with the contribution from
A
in the first column.

\det\left(I_{M}+A\right)=\begin{vmatrix}1&A_{1\,2}&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
0&1+A_{2\,2}&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&A_{M\,2}&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}A_{1\,1}&A_{1\,2}&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
A_{2\,1}&1+A_{2\,2}&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{M\,1}&A_{M\,2}&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}\,.

(3.6)

Now we expand on the second column of each of these determinants yielding a sum of four matrix determinants, one for each possible ways to select the first two columns independently from either
I_{M}
or
A
.

\displaystyle\det\left(I_{M}+A\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
0&1&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}1&A_{1\,2}&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
0&A_{2\,2}&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&A_{M\,2}&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{vmatrix}A_{1\,1}&0&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
A_{2\,1}&1&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{M\,1}&0&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}A_{1\,1}&A_{1\,2}&\cdots&A_{1\,M}\\
A_{2\,1}&A_{2\,2}&\cdots&A_{2\,M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{M\,1}&A_{M\,2}&\cdots&1+A_{M\,M}\end{vmatrix}\,.

(3.8)

Continuing this procedure by expanding each resulting matrix determinant on each next column, it is clear that the result is a sum over all matrices formed by selecting every column independently from either
I_{M}
or
A
.
Each of these matrices can be associated with a unique subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
which is the set of indices of the columns which are inherited from the matrix
A
.
The sum is then conducted over the power set of
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
,

\det\left(I_{M}+A\right)=\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{M}}\det(A^{S})

(3.9)

where
A^{S}
is the matrix that has for each index in
S
, the associated column of
A
and all other columns from
I_{M}
.

Now, we simplify some of these determinants once more by noting that performing a Laplace expansion of
\det(A^{S})
on a column which comes from
I_{M}
, only one principle minor contributes, which is the one where the column that we expanded on and the associated row are removed.
Iteratively Laplace expanding on the columns of
A^{S}
which come from
I_{M}
, the only principle minor that survives is the minor which only contains the index elements of the set
S
, which we’ll notate
\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(A\right)
.
Note that
\mathrm{Minor}_{\mathbb{Z}_{M}}\left(A\right)=A
and that
\mathrm{Minor}_{\emptyset}\left(A\right)
would be empty, but this minor would result from expanding the matrix determinant
\det(A^{\emptyset})=\det(I_{M})=1
, so we set
\mathrm{Minor}_{\emptyset}\left(A\right)=[\,1\,]
to write our final formula simply as:

\det\left(I_{M}+A\right)=\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{M}}\det\left(\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(A\right)\right)\,.

(3.10)

We may now apply this formula to
\det(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}))
from (
3.1
) for
z=x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We begin by noting that the decomposition of (
1.23
) extends to the principal minors
\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)
since the left and right matrices in the decomposition are diagonal.
For any
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}
,

\displaystyle\det\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)

\displaystyle=\det\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(D_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)^{2}\det\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(C_{N}\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{n\in S}c_{n}\exp\left(-2\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{n}\right]\frac{z}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}-2\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta_{n}-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{t}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\ignorespaces\times\left(\prod_{n\in S}\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{n}\right]}\prod_{n<\ell\in S}\frac{\left|\zeta_{\ell}-\zeta_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left|\zeta_{\ell}-\zeta_{n}^{*}\right|^{2}}\right)

(3.12)

where it is understood that a product over
S=\emptyset
is one to be consistent with
\det(\mathrm{Minor}_{\emptyset}\left(\diamond\right))=1
.
Bringing the positive definite terms from the Cauchy determinant into the exponential and plugging in the modified norming constants (
2.127
), we get

\displaystyle\det\mathrm{Minor}_{S}\left(\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-2\sum_{n\in S}\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{n}\right]\frac{z}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}+\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta_{n}-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{t}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\ignorespaces\left.+2\sum_{n\in S}\frac{\theta_{+}(\zeta_{n})}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}-\sum_{n,\ell\in S}g(\zeta_{\ell},\zeta_{n})\right)\,,

(3.13)

where
g:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the “regulated” Green’s function for the Laplacian on the upper half-plane

g(\zeta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{\zeta-\lambda^{*}}{\zeta-\lambda\phantom{{}^{*}}}\right|&\text{if }\zeta\neq\lambda\\
\displaystyle\log\left|\zeta-\lambda^{*}\right|&\text{if }\zeta=\lambda\end{cases}\,.

(3.14)

Again, we note that sums over
S=\emptyset
should be taken to be zero.
The full determinant
\det(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}(z/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}))
is a sum of terms (
3.13
) over over all possible
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.
We search for a dominant balance in
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
in the exponent of the summand (
3.13
) and find it by collecting a factor of
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
with every sum over
S
(i.e.
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}
with the double sum).
As we take
N\to\infty
, we may then expect that these sums over
S
behave like Riemann sums, and limit to integrals.
Towards this end, we define for
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, the associated point measures in one variable, which we parameterize using the imaginary part along the quadratrix,

\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)=\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\pi\sum_{n\in S}\delta(\kappa-\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{n}\right])\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.15)

for
0\leq\kappa\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}<\pi/2\delta
where

\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}:=\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}\right]\,,

(3.16)

\delta
here is the Dirac-delta unit point measure and
\mathrm{d}\kappa
is the Lebesgue measure on the line.
Now, for ease of notation, we will also define several quantities, splitting the real from imaginary contributions. For
z\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle V(\zeta;z,t)

\displaystyle:=z\ \mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta\right]+t\ \mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]-\theta_{+}(\zeta)\,,

(3.17)

\displaystyle E_{N,S}(z,t)

\displaystyle:=\int V(\zeta(\kappa);x,t)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint g\big(\zeta(\kappa),\zeta(\eta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\eta)\,,

(3.18)

\displaystyle P_{S}

\displaystyle:=\int\kappa\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)\,,

(3.19)

\displaystyle Q_{S}

\displaystyle:=\int\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)\,.

(3.20)

Thus, using the notation (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (
3.19
) to simplify the result (
3.13
) and then plug this into (
3.10
), we get a new expression for the full determinant and define a new compact notation for the result:

\displaystyle Z_{N}(z,t)=\det\left(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}\left(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\pi}E_{N,S}(z,t)\right)\,.

(3.21)

Looking at (
3.3
) and briefly setting
z\in\mathbb{R}
, you may notice some similarities with the standard real exponential integral with large parameter
\lambda:=\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\pi}>0
,
I(\lambda):=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x
where
f
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function on
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
.
In the case where
f
has a unique global minimum
x_{0}\in(a,b)
, it is well-known that in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
,
\log I(\lambda)\approx-\lambda f(x_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0})}\right)
by Laplace’s method.
Our sum (
3.3
) for fixed
N
is, first off, a discrete sum over particular sets of sample measures.
As
N\to\infty
, the size of the set of sample measures grows as
2^{N}
, and therefore we can think of the sum in (
3.3
) as a Riemann sum-like discretization of a functional integral which is integrated over some set of admissible densities
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{I}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\mathpzc{E}[\rho]}\mathcal{D}\rho
.
It is then reasonable to expected that if there is a unique minimizer
\rho_{0}
of the resulting functional
E
over
\mathcal{A}
, that
\log\mathcal{I}(\lambda)\approx-\lambda\mathpzc{E}[\rho_{0}]+\cdots
by a functional integral generalization of Laplace’s method.
In effect, this is exactly what P. D. Lax and C. D. Levermore proved for the KdV equation in
[
21
]
, and what we will show in section
3.2
.

In the case of the ILW equation, we have the addition complication that the exponentials are complex, since in (
3.1
) we need to plug
z=x\pm\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
into (
3.3
).
However, since the imaginary part of the exponent in (
3.3
) is
O\left(\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\right)
, the upshot is that this does not present too much difficulty.
In preparation for the main result, we establish the following bounds on the magnitude and argument of
Z_{N}(z,t)\in\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
z=x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define the minimum value

E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t):=\min_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}}E_{S}(x,t)\,.

(3.22)

Then,
Z_{N}(z,t)
has the bound in magnitude

0<\left[d\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\right]^{N}\leq\left|Z_{N}(z,t)\right|\leq 2^{N}\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\pi}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)\right)\,,

(3.23)

where

d(\theta):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle 1&0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\\
\displaystyle\sin\theta&\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta\leq\pi\end{array}\right.,

(3.24)

and the bound in argument

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq-\sgn(y)\arg\left(Z_{N}(z,t)\right)\leq 2N\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\,,

(3.25)

where the argument here is defined to be zero when
z\in\mathbb{R}
and continuous for the rest of
z\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\delta}
.

Proof.

The right side of (
3.23
) follows from an application of the triangle inequality to (
3.3
) and the fact that
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)
bounds all of the
E_{N,S}(x,t)
from below.
The left side of (
3.23
) and the argument bounds (
3.25
) are quite a bit more involved and require an a analysis of the eigenvalues of the matrix
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
.

We begin by using the factorization (
1.23
) to peel off the matrix multiplicative contributions dependent on the imaginary part
y
,

\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})=\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}Ky\right)\ \Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ \exp\left(-\mathrm{i}Ky\right)\,,

(3.26)

where
K
is the
N\times N
diagonal matrix with
K_{nn}:=\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{n}\right]=\kappa_{n}
for all
1\leq n\leq N
.
Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
, then it follows by matrix similarity that it is also an eigenvalue of the matrix

A:=\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\ \Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ \exp\left(\mathrm{i}K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)=\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\ \Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\,.

(3.27)

It is clear that the matrix
\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)
is unitary.
Now we want to show
\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
is positive-definite.
Looking back at (
3.12
) with
z=x\in\mathbb{R}
, we see that the principle minors
\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
are easily seen to be manifestly positive, so positive-definiteness follows immediately by Sylvester’s criterion.

For each distinct eigenvalue
\lambda
, we have at least one eigenvector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
of the matrix
A
.
By the calculation,

\displaystyle|\lambda|\,\left|\left|v\right|\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left|A\ v\right|\right|

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\ \Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ v\right|\right|

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ v\right|\right|\,,

(3.30)

we have that

|\lambda|=\frac{\left|\left|\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ v\right|\right|}{\left|\left|v\right|\right|}>0

(3.31)

since
\left|\left|v\right|\right|\neq 0
.
So all of the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
are nonzero and their arguments are well-defined.

Now, for simplicity of calculation, let
u=\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\,v
and consider

\displaystyle\lambda\,u^{\dagger}v

\displaystyle=u^{\dagger}\,A\,v

(3.32)

\displaystyle=u^{\dagger}\,\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\,\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ v

(3.33)

\displaystyle=u^{\dagger}\,\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\,u\,.

(3.34)

Since
\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
is, again, positive-definite,

u^{\dagger}v=v^{\dagger}\ \Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\ v>0\,.

(3.35)

Thus, the principal argument of
\lambda
must coincide with the principal argument of the right side of (
3.34
).
We divide by the positive
\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}
and then can simplify this using the diagonal form of
K
and the components of
u
,
u_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
for
1\leq n\leq N
,

\arg(\lambda)=\arg\left(\frac{\displaystyle u^{\dagger}\ \exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2K\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\ u}{\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}}\right)=\arg\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}2\kappa_{n}\frac{y}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\frac{|u_{n}|^{2}}{\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}}\right)\,.

(3.36)

We note from the conjugate symmetry of the phases across
y=0
, we have

\arg(\lambda)=-\sgn(y)\arg\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}2\kappa_{n}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\frac{|u_{n}|^{2}}{\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}}\right)\,.

(3.37)

From the definition of the modified scattering data
2.3
,
\kappa_{n}\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}
for all
1\leq n\leq N
,

0\leq 2\kappa_{n}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\leq 2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\leq 2\delta\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}<\pi\,.

(3.38)

This confines all of the phases of the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
to a sector of angle less than
\pi
.
Since the sum in (
3.37
) is a weighted average of these phases, by convexity of sectors with angle less than
\pi
, it follows that the weighted average of the phases is also contained in the sector and therefore shares the principal argument bounds.
Finally, relating this to the principal argument of
\lambda
by (
3.37
), we have arrived at the uniform bound for any eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
,

0\leq-\sgn(y)\arg(\lambda)\leq 2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}<\pi\,.

(3.39)

Now we are ready to resolve the lower bound for
\left|Z_{N}(z,t)\right|
in (
3.23
).
Noting that this is the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
evaluated at one, we can let
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be the set of eigenvalues (not necessarily distinct, duplicates accounting for algebraic multiplicity of the root) and we have

|Z_{N}(z,t)|=\left|\det\left(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)\right|=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left|1+\lambda_{n}\right|\,.

(3.40)

For each
1\leq n\leq N
,

\displaystyle\left|1+\lambda_{n}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|1+|\lambda_{n}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\arg(\lambda_{n})}\Big|^{2}=1+2|\lambda_{n}|\cos\left(\arg(\lambda_{n})\right)+|\lambda_{n}|^{2}.

(3.41)

Subject to (
3.39
), the expression is always minimized for fixed
|\lambda_{n}|\geq 0
when
|\arg(\lambda_{n})|
is greatest:

\displaystyle\left|1+\lambda_{n}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1+2|\lambda_{n}|\cos\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)+|\lambda_{n}|^{2}

(3.42)

\displaystyle=\left(|\lambda_{n}|+\cos\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\right)^{2}+\sin^{2}\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\,.

(3.43)

If
2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}|y|/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\leq\pi/2
, then the cosine term is non-negative and the quadratic is strictly increasing for
|\lambda_{n}|\geq 0
, thus the minimum is obtained at
|\lambda_{n}|=0
, which has the value one.
If instead
\pi/2<2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}|y|/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}<\pi
, then the cosine term is negative and the quadratic has its minimum at for some
|\lambda_{n}|>0
, which has the value
\sin^{2}\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}|y|/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\right)
.
Putting these cases together and taking the positive square-root, we have from the definition (
3.24
)

\left|1+\lambda_{n}\right|\geq d\left(2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\right)\,.

(3.44)

Using this bound for each factor in (
3.40
), we get the desired lower bound in (
3.23
).
The positivity of this lower bound (left-most inequality in (
3.23
)) follows from the fact that
d(\theta)>0
for all
0\leq\theta<\pi
and
2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}|y|/\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\leq 2\delta\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}<\pi
.

Lastly, because of the positivity of this lower bound argument of
Z_{N}(z,t)
can be defined to be continuous throughout the strip
\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
.
Moreover, since
\Delta_{N}(x,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
is positive definite,
Z_{N}(x,t)>0
, and so the argument can be taken to be zero on the real line.
Because the elements of
\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
are continuous functions of
z
and
t
, there exists an indexing where each of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
are continuous in
z
and
t
.
With this enumeration, we can compute the continuous argument of
Z_{N}(z,t)
according to

\arg\big(Z_{N}(z,t)\big)=\arg\left(\det\left(I_{N}+\Delta_{N}(z,t;\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\right)\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\arg(1+\lambda_{n})\,.

(3.45)

From a simple geometric argument involving sector where the phases of each
\lambda_{n}
are restricted to (
3.39
), we can demonstrate that

0\leq-\sgn(y)\arg(1+\lambda_{n})\leq-\sgn(y)\arg(\lambda_{n})\leq 2\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{|y|}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\,,

(3.46)

which, with (
3.45
), gives the bound (
3.25
).
∎

3.2
Characterizing the Minimizer

We’re now going to focus on characterizing the minimal measure which we expect to dominate the determinant (
3.3
).
This will be done by relating
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)
, the minimizer over the discrete point-mass measures
\{\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}\}_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}}
, to a minimizer over a larger set of admissible measure densities
\mathcal{A}
.
The set
\mathcal{A}
is exactly the set of densities associate to measures that are well-approximated by
\{\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}\}_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}}
as
N\to\infty
in the weak sense.
We will use the very same mathematical machinery that was developed by P. D. Lax and C. D. Levermore for working out the limit of the the KdV equation
[
21
, Section 2]
with a few small modifications, for the ILW equation’s small-dispersion limit.

Theorem 3.2
.

The measures
\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)
for all
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
have uniform bounded variation. Moreover
\{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}(\kappa)\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
, i.e. the measures will all indices included, have the weak limit

\mathrm{w-}\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}(\kappa)=\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.47)

where
\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}
is defined by the ILW Weyl law (
2.105
).

Proof.

First, the measures
\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)
are nonnegative, and we have the clear bound

\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}(\kappa)\leq\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}(\kappa)=\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\pi N\leq R\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)<\infty\,,

(3.48)

where we used the definition of the modified scattering data (
2.126
) and the properties of the Weyl law in Lemma
2.2
.
Now let
f:[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function.
We integrate
f
against
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}
and change variables with
r=R^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\zeta(\kappa))
, letting
R^{-1}(r)=\kappa
denote the inverse.

\displaystyle\int f(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\pi\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(\kappa_{n})

(3.49)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\pi\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(R^{-1}(\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\pi n))\,.

(3.50)

This is exactly the right endpoint Riemann sum with uniform
N^{\textrm{th}}
partition approximate to the integral

\int_{0}^{R^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(1/2\delta)}f(R^{-1}(r))\mathrm{d}r=-\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}f(\kappa)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\kappa}R^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}f(\kappa)\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.51)

Because this is integrable and the integrand is continuous, we know that the right endpoint Riemann sum limits to the integral, and thus,

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\int f(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{Z}_{N}}(\kappa)=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}f(\kappa)\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.52)

This demonstrates (
3.47
).
∎

Knowing that the discrete measures
\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}
have lower and upper bounds which have weak limits as
N\to+\infty
, we expect the measures
\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}
to well-approximate in the limit measures with densities belonging to the admissible set of densities

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\ \rho\ \left|\ \rho\text{ is a real, measurable function on $[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]$ and }0\leq\rho\leq\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}\ \right.\right\}\,.

(3.53)

Since
\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa
as a measure has trivially bounded variation

\left|\left|\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}\right|\right|_{L^{1}}=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=R\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)<\infty\,,

(3.54)

each
\rho\in\mathcal{A}
has an associated measure
\mathrm{d}\mu=\rho\,\mathrm{d}\kappa
which have uniformly bounded variation.
We can define now functionals which are versions of the quantities
E_{N,S}(x,t)
(
3.18
),
P_{S}
(
3.19
), and
Q_{S}
(
3.20
) but which can be computed using the densities
\rho\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathpzc{E}[\rho](x,t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\left(V(\zeta(\kappa);x,t)\ \rho(\kappa)+\frac{1}{2}\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))\right)\ \rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,,

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\log\left|\frac{\lambda-\zeta(\eta)^{*}}{\lambda-\zeta(\eta)\phantom{{}^{*}}}\right|\rho(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,,

(3.56)

\displaystyle\mathpzc{P}[\rho]

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\kappa\ \rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,,

(3.57)

\displaystyle\mathpzc{Q}[\rho]

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]\ \rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,,

(3.58)

and where
V
is still the same as in (
3.17
).

We note that for the discrete version (
3.14
) of the logarithmic kernel of
\mathpzc{L}
(
3.56
) had to have a modified diagonal, i.e. when
\kappa=\eta
, for finiteness.
For the functional version, the logarithmic kernel of
\mathpzc{L}
(
3.56
) is positive and has no worse than a logarithmic singularity on the diagonal
\kappa=\eta
and that

Theorem 3.3
.

\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}]
is continuous and takes the value

\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}](\zeta(\kappa))=\theta_{+}(\kappa)+\theta_{-}(\kappa)

(3.59)

for
\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
, i.e. on the interval along the quadratrix
[1/2\delta,\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}]_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}
.
Consequently,
\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
is continuous and satisfies

0\leq\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta)\leq\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}](\zeta)

(3.60)

for all
\rho\in\mathcal{A}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

The proof of the first statement is presented with the proof of Corollary
3.9.1
.
The bounds in the second statement follow trivially from noticing that the kernel of
\mathpzc{L}
is positive on the upper half-plane and using the bounds from the definition of
\rho\in\mathcal{A}
.
Continuity of
\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
follows from considering a sequence
\{\zeta_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
in the upper half-plane with a limit and applying the generalized dominated convergence theorem to the integrands of
\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta_{m})
and
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}](\zeta_{m})
.
The continuity of
\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
then extends to the whole plane by odd symmetry.
From these properties, making the appropriate adjustments to the proof of Theorem 2.2 from
[
21
]
, we have

Theorem 3.4

(The limit of the discrete minimizers
E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}
.)
.

For all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a minimum of the functional
\mathpzc{E}[\diamond](x,t)
(
3.55
) attained on the admissible set
\mathcal{A}
,

E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t):=\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}}\mathpzc{E}[\rho](x,t)\,.

(3.61)

Furthermore

\lim_{N\to+\infty}E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}(x,t)=E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)

(3.62)

uniformly on compact subsets of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

That is to say, the limit as
N\to+\infty
of the discrete minima
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)
is equal to the minimum of the functional
\mathpzc{E}[\diamond](x,t)
(
3.55
) over the admissible set of densities
\mathcal{A}
.
Should the reader be interested in the content of these proofs, we direct their attention first to
[

]
and then, for a more in-depth explanation, to C. D. Levermore’s dissertation
[
23
, Chapter 2]
.

3.3
The Distributional Limit of the Semiclassical Soliton Ensemble Solution

Theorem 3.5
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the distributional limit of (
3.1
) is given by

\mathrm{d}-\lim_{N\to\infty}u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(\diamond,t)=-\frac{4\delta}{\pi}\partial_{x}^{2}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(\diamond,t)

(3.63)

where
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}
is defined in (
3.61
).

Proof.

We will prove the distributional limit from definition.
Let
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and let
K\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact support of
\chi
, and consider the solution
u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)
integrated against
\chi
in
x
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(x)\ u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(x)\ \partial_{x}D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(3.64)

Moving the derivative onto
\chi
is a standard invoking of integration-by-parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(x)\ u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{x}(x)\ D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(3.65)

where the boundary term disappears due to the compact support of
\chi
.
Dealing with the finite-difference operator
D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
is much trickier.
We will exploit the fact that the difference is shrinking with
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
and using a near-analytic extension of
\chi
to get our result.

First, define
\phi_{+}:\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\phi_{+}(z):=\chi_{x}(x)+\mathrm{i}(y-\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\chi_{xx}(x)\,,

(3.66)

where again we always identify
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
.
Then, from the
\overline{\partial}
-generalization of Cauchy’s theorem,

\int_{C}\phi_{+}(z)\ F_{N}(z,t)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{0}^{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\overline{\partial}_{z}\big(\phi_{+}(z)\ F_{N}(z,t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.67)

with
a<b
such that
K\subset[a,b]
and
C
is the boundary of the rectangle
(x,y)\in[a,b]\times[0,\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}]
, i.e. the integration region on the left-hand side, oriented positively.
Since
F_{N}
is analytic, which follows from Theorem
3.1
where the determinant was shown to be nonzero for all
|y|\leq\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, we have that
\overline{\partial}_{z}F_{N}(z,t)=0
, so product rule gives

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{z}\big(\phi_{+}(z)\,F_{N}(z,t)\big)

\displaystyle=F_{N}(z,t)\ \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)\phi_{+}(x+\mathrm{i}y)

(3.68)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(y-\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\chi_{xxx}(x)\,F_{N}(z,t)\,.

(3.69)

Since
\chi
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, there is a maximum magnitude of
\chi_{xxx}
on
[a,b]
, call it
M_{\chi,3}\geq 0
.
Let us also introduce notation for the maximum modulus of
F_{N}
on a
y
-symmetric rectangular region

M_{F_{N}}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[a,b]\\
y\in[-\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}]\end{subarray}}|F_{N}(z,t)|\,.

(3.70)

Thus, inserting (
3.69
) into the integrand of the double integral in (
3.67
), we have the bound

\left|\int_{a}^{b}\int_{0}^{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\overline{\partial}_{z}\big(\phi_{+}(z)\ F_{N}(z,t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\frac{\delta^{2}\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}}{2}(b-a)M_{\chi,3}M_{F_{N}}\,.

(3.71)

Now let us consider the contour integral on the left side of (
3.67
) by parameterization:

\displaystyle\int_{C}\phi_{+}(z)\,F_{N}(z,t)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\phi_{+}(x)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\phi_{+}(b+\mathrm{i}y)\,F_{N}(b+\mathrm{i}y,t)\,\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{b}^{a}\phi_{+}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\,F_{N}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}^{0}\phi_{+}(a+\mathrm{i}y)\,F_{N}(a+\mathrm{i}y,t)\,\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.72)

We plug in the form of
\phi_{+}
(
3.66
) and simplify,

\displaystyle\int_{C}\phi_{+}(z)\,F_{N}(z,t)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\big(\chi_{x}(x)-\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\chi_{xx}(x)\big)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int^{0}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}(y-\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\chi_{xx}(b)\ F_{N}(b+\mathrm{i}y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{x}(x)\,F_{N}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}(y-\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\chi_{xx}(a)\,F_{N}(a+\mathrm{i}y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.73)

where the
\chi_{x}
terms disappeared in the vertical segments due the endpoints being outside of its support:
\chi_{x}(a,t)=\chi_{x}(b,t)=0
.
Because of this vanishing, the integrals over the vertical segments can be bounded easily by
\frac{\delta^{2}\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}}{2}M_{\chi,2}M_{F_{N}}
,
where
M_{\chi,2}
is a uniform bound on
|\chi_{xx}|
over
x\in[a,b]
.
We have succeeded in bounding the difference between the integrals along the horizontal segments of
C
which involve
F_{N}(x,t)
and
F_{N}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)
.
This forms half of the finite-difference operator (
3.2
) on
F_{N}
.
For the other half, we do the exact same exercise but for a rectangle in the lower half-plane and for a function
\phi_{-}:\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\phi_{-}(z):=\chi_{x}(x)+\mathrm{i}(y+\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N})\chi_{xx}(x)\,.

(3.74)

This gives identical bounds since the previous bounds on
\chi
were
y
-independent and the bound on
F_{N}
was chosen to be on all of
\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
.

Subtracting the resulting lower half-plane horizontal integrals from the those of the upper half-plane in (
3.73
) and dividing by
\mathrm{i}2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
lets us assemble the finite-difference in (
3.65
) along with a term integrated on the real axis:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}\left(\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{x}(x)\,F_{N}(x+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\chi_{x}(x)-\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\chi_{xx}(x)\Big)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad-\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{x}(x)\,F_{N}(x-\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N},t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\chi_{x}(x)+\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\chi_{xx}(x)\Big)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{x}(x)\,D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(3.75)

The bounds on the double integral (
3.71
) and those of the vertical components along with the rearranging the equalities (
3.67
) and (
3.73
) lets us assemble a bound on the left-hand side of equality (
3.75
) by naïve triangle identity.
Nearly there, we combine our first integration-by-parts result (
3.65
) with (
3.75
) and its bound to get our first approximation to the integral of
u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(\diamond,t)
against
\chi
:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}\chi(x)\,u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt\ignorespaces=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{x}(x)\,D_{\mathrm{i}\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

(3.76)

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt\ignorespaces\leq\frac{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}{2}\Big((b-a)M_{\chi,3}+M_{\chi,2}\Big)M_{F_{N}}\,.

(3.77)

To demonstrate the limit, we need to show that the right-hand side of (
3.76
) indeed goes to zero.
For this, we split
F_{N}
into real and imaginary parts

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left[F_{N}(z,t)\right]

\displaystyle=2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\ln\left|Z_{N}(z,t)\right|\,,

(3.78)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\left[F_{N}(z,t)\right]

\displaystyle=2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\arg\left(Z_{N}(z,t)\right)\,,

(3.79)

and then applying Theorem
3.1
to the right sides of the above equalities, we have the bounds on the real and imaginary parts of
F_{N}

\displaystyle 2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N\ln d(2\delta\kappa_{\mathrm{max}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Re}\left[F_{N}(z,t)\right]\leq-\frac{4\delta}{\pi}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)+2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N\ln 2\,,

(3.80)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathrm{Im}\left[F_{N}(z,t)\right]\right|\leq 4\delta^{2}\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\,.

(3.81)

As
N\to+\infty
, all of terms in these bounds limit to zero independently of
x,t
except for the term

-4\delta/\pi\,E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)
.
Indeed, the terms
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N
tend to zero, by the construction of
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
(
2.125
).
Additionally,
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)
, due to Theorem
3.4
, has a uniform limit on
[a,b]
.
Since its real and imaginary parts have uniform bounds, so too does
|F_{N}|
and hence
M_{F_{N}}
.
This shows that the bound in (
3.77
) will tend to zero as
N\to+\infty
.

Lastly, since we now only have
F_{N}
evaluated at
x\in\mathbb{R}
at the beginning of (
3.77
), we can establish a tighter lower bound than (
3.80
), relying directly on the fact that
Z_{N}(x,t)
(
3.3
) is a sum of positive terms, which is greater than any one of its terms:

\displaystyle F_{N}(x,t)

\displaystyle=2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\log\big(Z_{N}(x,t)\big)

(3.82)

\displaystyle=2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\log\left[\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\pi}E_{S}(x,t)\right)\right]

(3.83)

\displaystyle\geq 2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\log\left[\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}\pi}E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}(x,t)\right)\right]

(3.84)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{4\delta}{\pi}E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}(x,t)\,.

(3.85)

This with the upper bound from (
3.80
) yields the three-sided inequality

\displaystyle 0\leq F_{N}(x,t)+\frac{4\delta}{\pi}E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N\ln 2\,.

(3.86)

By the sandwich theorem, we have that
F_{N}(x,t)\to-4\delta/\pi\ E_{N}^{\mathrm{min}}(x,t)
uniformly in
x
and
t
as
N\to+\infty
. Considering

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}\chi(x)\,u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,\frac{4\delta}{\pi}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left|\int_{a}^{b}\chi(x)\,u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,F_{N}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x)\,\left(F_{N}(x,t)+\frac{4\delta}{\pi}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left|\frac{4\delta}{\pi}\int_{a}^{b}\chi_{xx}(x,t)\ \big(E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)-E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

(3.87)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}{2}\Big(RM_{\chi,3}+M_{\chi,2}\Big)M_{F_{N}}+(b-a)M_{\chi,2}2\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{2}N\ln 2

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(b-a)M_{\chi,2}\frac{4\delta}{\pi}\max_{x\in[a,b]}\big|E^{\mathrm{min}}_{N}(x,t)-E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)\big|\,,

(3.88)

where in the first step we added and subtracted terms and split using the triangle identity, then in the second step we bounded the first term on the right in (
3.87
) using (
3.77
) and the second two by the naïve maximum on the integration range.
Lastly, we have that the first and second term in (
3.88
) tends to zero from the arguments above while the third term tends to zero by the uniformity of this limit in Theorem
3.4
.
Since
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}
was arbitrary, we have proved that the limit of
u^{\mathrm{SSE}}_{N}(x,t)
in a distributional sense coincides with
-4\delta/\pi\ \partial_{x}^{2}E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)
.
∎

Theorem
3.5
demonstrates two important qualities of the ILW small dispersion solution.
First, that the finite difference in the
N
-soliton solution between the two edges of the strip
\mathcal{S}_{\delta\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}
can be approximated in a distributional sense by
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
times a derivative on the center of the strip as
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\to 0^{+}
, as one might expect.
And second, the limit of
F_{N}(x,t)
as a large exponential sum is behaving exactly as described after (
3.3
), that is that thinking of this as a Riemann-sum discretizations of a functional integral, we have just demonstrated a generalization of Laplace’s method for functional exponential integrals.
As it turns out, this minimal value
E^{\mathrm{min}}_{\infty}(x,t)
directly determines the solution of the ILW semiclassical soliton ensemble.

3.4
Characterizing the Minimizing Density

Theorem
3.4
guarantees that the that there exists a minimizer of
\mathpzc{E}[\diamond](x,t)
in
\mathcal{A}
, now we wish to characterize and construct it.
Towards this end, we will show that this minimizer is uniquely determined by a set of variational conditions, the result of Theorem
3.9
. This will follow from

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
let
\rho=\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
, then

\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))\,\rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\geq 0\,,

(3.89)

with equality iff
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be as in the hypothesis, then
\rho\in L^{1}[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
, so we can define the two-dimensional Fourier transform for measures restricted along the quadratrix by

\hat{\rho}(\ell,k):=\int_{K}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\ell\xi(\kappa)-\mathrm{i}k\kappa}\ \rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.90)

where
K=[-\kappa_{\mathrm{max}},\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
and we extend
\rho
to
[-\kappa_{\mathrm{max}},0)
with odd symmetry.
Then we define the Gaussian-mollified sequence of two-dimensional densities
\{\rho_{n}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
,

\rho_{n}(\zeta):=\int_{K}G_{n}(\zeta-\zeta(\kappa^{\prime}))\,\rho(\kappa^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\prime}\,,\text{ where }G_{n}(\zeta):=\frac{n^{2}}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{2}\left|\zeta\right|^{2}/2}

(3.91)

The net result of this mollification is that the charge density
\rho
, which can be thought of as a distribution supported on the quadratrix, is spread out into the plane by convolution with a radially symmetric Gaussian of width
1/n
.

We exploit the fact that
\rho_{n}
are Schwartz class and
\rho_{n}(\zeta)=-\rho_{n}(\zeta^{*})
to establish an equality between the “spatial” integral (
3.89
) and a Fourier integral using Parseval’s theorem and using the tempered distribution for the Fourier transform of the
\log
-kernel

\displaystyle\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}:=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\left|\zeta-\lambda\right|\rho_{n}(\zeta)\rho_{n}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda

(3.92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\left|\hat{\rho}_{n}(\ell,k)\right|^{2}}{\ell^{2}+k^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell\,\mathrm{d}k=:\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Four}}\,,

(3.93)

where we have the shorthand for the real two-dimensional Lebesgue measure on the complex plane
\mathrm{d}^{2}z=\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Re}\left[z\right]\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Im}\left[z\right]
and
\hat{\rho}_{n}
is the standard two dimensional Fourier transform of
\rho_{n}
.
Because of the form of (
3.93
), the lemma statement applied instead to
\rho_{n}
is now apparent.
We now wish to show that as
n\to\infty
, that is as the width of the Gaussian in (
3.91
),
\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}
tends to the integral in the lemma statement.

Towards this end, we note that the entire integrand of (
3.92
) with (
3.91
) plugged in is absolutely integrable, use
\rho\in L^{1}[-\kappa_{\mathrm{max}},\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
and
\displaystyle\max_{\kappa^{\prime}\in[-\kappa_{\mathrm{max}},\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]}G_{n}(\zeta-\zeta(\kappa^{\prime}))
is
C^{2}(\mathbb{C})
which decays beyond all orders.
From Fubini, we rearrange the order of integration and isolate the terms which constitute pure convolutions on the plane.
Then we use the commutation of convolutions and conduct the convolutions of the two Gaussians explicitly:

\displaystyle\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{K^{2}}\rho(\kappa)\rho(\kappa^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}G_{n}(\zeta(\kappa^{\prime})-\zeta)\log\left|\zeta-\lambda\right|G_{n}(\lambda-\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.94)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{K^{2}}\rho(\kappa)\rho(\kappa^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\left|\zeta(\kappa^{\prime})-\lambda\right|G_{n}(\lambda-\zeta^{\prime})G_{n}(\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\zeta^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.95)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{K^{2}}\rho(\kappa)\rho(\kappa^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left|\zeta(\kappa^{\prime})-\lambda\right|G_{n/\sqrt{2}}(\lambda-\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.96)

Now we split the
\kappa^{\prime}
integral over zero and use the oddness of
\rho
to write the integral over just
[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
,

\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{K}\rho(\kappa)\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left|\frac{\zeta(\kappa^{\prime})^{*}-\lambda}{\zeta(\kappa^{\prime})^{\phantom{*}}-\lambda}\right|G_{n/\sqrt{2}}(\lambda-\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.97)

where we combined the inner-most integral with
\kappa^{\prime}\to-\kappa^{\prime}
and an overall sign difference between them.
Fubini’s theorem again let’s us reorder the inner two integrals since the integrand is dominated by an integrable function: use
|\rho(\kappa^{\prime})|\leq\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa^{\prime})
and that the new
\log
-kernel is positive everywhere on the integration domain.
The integral over
\kappa^{\prime}
is then exactly the integral computing
\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\lambda)
, so we choose to compute this one first,

\displaystyle\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{K}\rho(\kappa)\int_{\mathbb{C}}G_{n/\sqrt{2}}(\zeta(\kappa)-\lambda)\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.98)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa)(G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho])(\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,,

(3.99)

where we introduced a notation for the convolution and used the even symmetry of the integrand in
\kappa
so as to show that we have almost recovered exactly the integral (
3.89
).
The hope is that as the width of the Gaussian in the convolution goes to zero (
n\to\infty
), we recover just
\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
, and thus the integral in (
3.89
).
We set this up by computing the norm of the difference

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa)\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}\kappa-\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Spat}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa)\Big(\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))-(G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho])(\zeta(\kappa))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\right|

(3.100)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\rho\right|\right|_{L^{1}[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]}\left|\left|\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\diamond))-(G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho])(\zeta(\diamond))\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]}

(3.101)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}\right|\right|_{L^{1}}\left|\left|\mathpzc{L}[\rho]-(G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho])\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})}\,.

(3.102)

It suffices to show that
G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho]\to\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
uniformly as
n\to\infty
.
For this, we need a partition of unity
\chi:\mathbb{C}\to[0,1]

\chi(\zeta):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|\zeta|\leq\frac{3}{2}|\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}|\\
0&\text{if }|\zeta|\geq 2|\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}|\end{cases}

(3.103)

and which is
C^{2}(\mathbb{C})
with bounded second partial derivatives.
Employing the partition and linearity of the the convolution operation

G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\mathpzc{L}[\rho]=G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*(\chi\mathpzc{L}[\rho])+G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\big((1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho])\big)\,.

(3.104)

In the first term of (
3.104
),
\chi\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
is continuous by Theorem
3.3
and compactly supported, by Heine-Cantor it is uniformly continuous.
A simple argument using the definitions of uniform continuity and the convolution gives that
\{G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*(\chi\mathpzc{L}[\rho])\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a uniformly equi-continuous sequence.
By the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, there is a subsequence
\{n_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
for which
G_{n_{m}/\sqrt{2}}*(\chi\mathpzc{L}[\rho])\to\chi\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
uniformly as
m\to\infty
.

Now we work on the second term of (
3.104
).
Noticing that there is a non-zero distance between the support of
1-\chi
and
[\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}^{*},\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}]_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}
, we see immediately from the differentiability of the log-kernel that the second partials of
(1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
are uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{C}
, say by the constant
C>0
.
Using an two dimensional extension of Taylor’s remainder theorem, it follows that for any
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\left|\big[G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\big((1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]\big)\big](\zeta)-(1-\chi(\zeta))\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta)\right|

(3.105)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\mathbb{C}}G_{n/\sqrt{2}}(\zeta-\lambda)\Big|\big((1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]\big)(\lambda)-\big((1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]\big)(\zeta)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

(3.106)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\mathbb{C}}G_{n/\sqrt{2}}(\zeta-\lambda)C|\lambda-\zeta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

(3.107)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2\sqrt{2}C}{n}\,.

(3.108)

Thus, we have uniform convergence of
G_{n/\sqrt{2}}*\big((1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]\big)\to(1-\chi)\mathpzc{L}[\rho]
as
n\to\infty
.

In light of (
3.102
), using the partition of unity and the triangle identity for the
L^{\infty}
-norm, passing to the subsequence we have shown

\lim_{m\to\infty}\ell_{n_{m}}^{\textrm{Spat}}=\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa)\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.109)

Now we work on the Fourier side (
3.93
).
An application of Fubini demonstrates that

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}(\ell,k)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\mathrm{Re}\left[\zeta\right]-\mathrm{i}\ell\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta\right]}\rho_{n}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\zeta

(3.110)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\int_{K}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k(\mathrm{Re}\left[\zeta\right]-\xi(\kappa))-\mathrm{i}\ell(\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta\right]-\kappa)}G_{n}(\zeta-\zeta(\kappa))\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\xi(\kappa)-\mathrm{i}\ell\kappa}\rho(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\zeta

(3.111)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-(\ell^{2}+k^{2})/2n^{2}}\hat{\rho}(\ell,k)\,.

(3.112)

We see that the integrand of

\ell_{n}^{\textrm{Four}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-(\ell^{2}+k^{2})/n^{2}}\frac{\left|\hat{\rho}(\ell,k)\right|^{2}}{\ell^{2}+k^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell\,\mathrm{d}k

(3.113)

is, due to the Gaussian term, a monotonically increasing sequence of functions which have a clear point-wise limit almost everywhere and whose integrals have a limit on the subsequence
\{n_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
from our work on the spatial side.
So by monotone convergence theorem, we have the result

\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\rho(\kappa)\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta(\kappa))\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=\lim_{m\to\infty}\ell_{n_{m}}^{\textrm{Spat}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\ell_{n_{m}}^{\textrm{Four}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\left|\hat{\rho}(\ell,k)\right|^{2}}{\ell^{2}+k^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\ell\,\mathrm{d}k\,.

(3.114)

The Fourier integral in (
3.114
) is clearly always non-negative and zero if
\rho
is almost everywhere zero.
For the strict positivity, we rely on the fact that
\hat{\rho}
is
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
since
\rho
is compactly supported.
Thus, the Fourier integral in (
3.114
) is zero, iff
\hat{\rho}
is zero everywhere.
Additionally, we realize that if
\hat{\rho}
is identically zero then

0=\mathrm{i}^{n}\frac{\partial^{n}\hat{\rho}}{\partial\ell^{n}}(0,0)=\int_{K}\kappa^{n}\rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.115)

Determining
\rho
from these moments can be recast in the form of the Hausdorff moment problem, for which the criteria of a unique solution for the measure
\rho(\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa
from these moments is satisfied.
And, we already know
\rho=0
has these moments, so we’re done.
∎

Now we are ready to characterize the minimizing density.

Theorem 3.7
.

For each
(x,t)
, the minimizing density
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)=\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa;x,t)\in\mathcal{A}
is uniquely determined by the variational conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{clc}\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)=0&\mathrm{if}\ \kappa\in I_{+},&\textrm{(voids)}\\
0\leq\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\leq\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)&\mathrm{if}\ \kappa\in I_{0},&\textrm{(bands)}\\
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)=\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)&\mathrm{if}\ \kappa\in I_{-},&\textrm{(saturations)}\end{array}\right.\,,

(3.116)

where the sets
I_{-},I_{0},
amd
I_{+}
partition
[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]
according to

\displaystyle I_{-}=I_{-}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]\ \Bigg|\ \frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}\big(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\big)<0\ \right\}\,,

(3.117)

\displaystyle I_{0}=I_{0}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]\ \Bigg|\ \frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}\big(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\big)=0\ \right\}\,,

(3.118)

\displaystyle I_{+}=I_{+}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}]\ \Bigg|\ \frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}\big(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\big)>0\ \right\}\,.

(3.119)

and the Fréchet derivative of the functional
E
is given by

\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho\}(\zeta)=\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho\}(\zeta;x,t):=V(\zeta;x,t)+\mathpzc{L}[\rho](\zeta)\,.

(3.120)

Proof.

Uniqueness of the minimizer
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
follows from the convexity of the admissible set of charge densities
\mathcal{A}
and the strict convexity of the functional
\mathpzc{E}[\diamond](x,t)
, which in turn follows trivially from the quadratic form of
E
and the positive-definiteness of the quadratic term from Lemma
3.6
.
The variational conditions can then be derived using these properties.
Take
\rho\in\mathcal{A}
and let
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
be a minimizer of consider
\rho_{\theta}=(1-\theta)\rho^{\mathrm{min}}+\theta\rho\in\mathcal{A}
for
0\leq\theta\leq 1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathpzc{E}[\rho_{\theta}](x,t)-\mathpzc{E}[\rho^{\mathrm{min}}](x,t)

\displaystyle=\theta\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\Big[V\left(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\right)+\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{min}}](\zeta(\kappa))\Big]\left(\rho(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa

(3.121)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathpzc{L}[\rho-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}](\zeta(\kappa))\left(\rho(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.122)

Since
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
is the unique minimizer, this difference is nonnegative for all
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, and thus, the coefficient
\theta
must be nonnegative.
The integrand of the linear term without the difference
\rho-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
we can identify as the Fréchet derivative (
3.120
).

Now we’ll show that the variational conditions do indeed characterize the unique minimizer
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
.
We begin by constructing the particular
\rho_{-}\in\mathcal{A}
which is

\rho_{-}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)&\text{if }s\in I_{-}(x,t)\\
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa;x,t)&\text{if }s\notin I_{-}(x,t)\end{array}\right.\,.

(3.123)

We then have for the coefficient of
\theta
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}(\zeta(\kappa))\left(\rho_{-}(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa=\int_{I_{-}}\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}(\zeta(\kappa))\left(\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,.

(3.124)

So if
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
differs from the upper bound
\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}
on
I_{-}
then
\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
is positive while the Fréchet derivative is negative yielding a negative total value if the difference is supported on a nonzero measure set.
Thus, we conclude that for
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
to truly be the minimizer, it can differ from the upper bound
\rho^{\mathrm{Wyl}}
by no more than a set of measure zero on
I_{-}
.
Of course, sets of measurable functions are only distinguished up to sets of measure zero, and thus we may choose the set the output on the potentially deviating set to the upper bound and get the third condition in
3.116
.
By an identical argument with the lower bound
0
on
I_{+}
, we arrive at the first condition in
3.116
.
And by exhaustion of cases, we have the second condition in
3.116
.
We have shown that the unique minimizer
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
satisfies the variational conditions.

Now suppose
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies the variational conditions.
Since
\rho^{*}
satisfies the variational conditions and the fact that
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
is the minimizer, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{*}\}\left(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\right)\left(\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)-\rho^{*}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\geq 0\,,

(3.125)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\frac{\delta E}{\delta\rho}\{\rho^{\mathrm{min}}\}\left(\zeta(\kappa);x,t\right)\left(\rho^{*}(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\geq 0\,.

(3.126)

Adding these two expressions together, the
V
-terms cancel and we can combine the
\mathpzc{L}
-terms, accounting for an overall negative sign, to get

\int_{0}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathpzc{L}[\rho-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}](\kappa)\left(\rho^{*}(\kappa)-\rho^{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\leq 0.

(3.127)

By lemma
3.6
, it must be that this integral is zero and
\rho^{*}=\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
.
This shows that the variational conditions determine the minimizer uniquely.
∎

3.5
Solving the Variational Conditions

Instead of attempting to solve the variational conditions directly to find an explicit form for the minimizing density
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
, we will assume first that we may differentiate the minimizing density with respect to
x
and
t
and that the sets
I_{-}(x,t)
,
I_{0}(x,t)
and
I_{+}(x,t)
change continuously as
x
and
t
do. Then, we can differentiate the variational conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{min}}_{x}](\kappa)=-\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta(\kappa)\right]&\text{if }s\in I_{0}(x,t),\\
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}_{x}(\kappa)=0&\text{if }s\notin I_{0}(x,t).\end{array}\right.\,,

(3.128)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{min}}_{t}](\kappa)=-\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(\zeta(\kappa)-\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\right]&\text{if }s\in I_{0}^{\mathrm{o}}(x,t),\\
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}_{t}(\kappa)=0&\text{if }s\notin I_{0}^{\mathrm{o}}(x,t).\end{array}\right.\,.

(3.129)

Solving these differentiated variational conditions, (
3.128
) and (
3.129
), then integrating in
x
and
t
consistently (mixed derivatives are equal) and verifying the result satisfies the full variational conditions (
3.116
), then by Theorem
3.7
, we have constructed the unique minimizing density
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
.
Once we have
\rho^{\mathrm{min}}
, then the distributional limit of the ILW semiclassical soliton ensemble can be given expressed explicitly using Theorem
3.5
.

Looking at the differentiated variational conditions (
3.128
) and (
3.129
) and the form of
\mathpzc{L}
(
3.56
), we realize these are two independent problems in logarithm potential theory for a measure with density supported on a Schwartz symmetric subset of
[\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}^{*},\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}]_{\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}
and which has odd symmetry across the real axis.
On its support, we have a prescribed value for the logarithmic potential
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\mathrm{min}}]
.

To continue, we will make one more assumption, which is that

consists of a single interval:
I_{0}=[0,\beta]
where

.
This is consistent with the KdV small-dispersion limit
[
21
]
and, as it will turn out, it will constrain us to the simplest case possible, that of “small time” before the formation of the DSW (see Figures

,
2(c)
and

3.5.1
The One Band Ansatz

As it turns out, this problem can be solved by inspection and then verifying that our ansatz has all of the properties required to establish it as the unique solution.
This is because the logarithmic potential for the solution to the differentiated variational conditions with a prescribed symmetric interval
I
is determined uniquely by the conditions enumerated in the following theorem.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\rho^{\prime}
is Hölder continuous with power
-1/2
and has logarithmic potential
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]
which satisfies the differentiated variational conditions (
3.128
) or (
3.129
), then the logarithmic potential
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]
is uniquely identified by the properties:

1.

harmonic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus I
and continuous on
\mathbb{C}
,

2.

the value on
I
is dictated by the differentiated variational conditions (
3.128
) or (
3.129
),

3.

odd symmetry under reflection across the real axis:
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}](\zeta)=-\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}](\zeta^{*})
,

4.

decay at infinity
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}](\zeta)=O\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\zeta}}\right)
as
\zeta\to\infty
.

Additionally, the density
\rho^{\prime}
can be extracted by the formula

\rho^{\prime}(\kappa)=\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta^{\prime}(\kappa)\Big(\partial_{\zeta}\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]^{-}(\zeta(\kappa))-\partial_{\zeta}\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]^{+}(\zeta(\kappa))\Big)\right]

(3.130)

where the
+
and
-
indicate the left and right boundary values to the quadratrix respectively and
\partial_{\zeta}=(\partial_{\mathrm{Re}\left[\zeta\right]}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta\right]})/2
is the holomorphic derivative in
\zeta
.

Proof.

First, it is clear from the formula for
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]
(
3.56
) that it satisfies these properties.
Now, if we subtract some other logarithmic potential with these properties, the result is a function of
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
which is harmonic away from the quadratrix interval, zero on it and decays to zero as
\zeta\to\infty
.
By the extremum principal of harmonic functions, any maximum or minima of this difference of functions must occur on the interval, or at infinity, or the difference of functions is constant.
In all cases, it must be identically zero.
Thus,
\mathpzc{L}[\rho^{\prime}]
is uniquely determined by the conditions. Lastly, from
[
31
, Theorem 1.3]
, the density
\rho^{\prime}
can be recovered from the log-potential by (
3.130
).
∎

Inspecting the solution process of
[
21
,
22
]
to the analogous problem for the KdV equation, we see that underlying their solution to the

-differentiated variational condition for a single symmetric band is an extension of the function which determines the derivative of the WKB phases for the Shrödinger equation, one that is analytic away from the band in the complex plane.
The properties of this analytic function are exactly what leads to it playing a direct role in satisfying the variational conditions due to the asymptotic scattering data being derived from the same function.
Thus, to look for the solution to the ILW differentiated variational conditions, we should try to determine the analytic continuation of the derivative of the fast phase.

Since the Lambert

(3.131)

Furthermore, we consider only

Follow along the steps in Figure

The present work leaves several questions open. In Section

A natural question is can this work be made rigorous?
