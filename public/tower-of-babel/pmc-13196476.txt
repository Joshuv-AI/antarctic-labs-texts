Metabolomics and Artificial Intelligence (AI) assisted metabolomics in the diagnosis of non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections: progress and challenges
Authors: Madhan Kumar Murthy, Vivek Kumar Gupta, Anand Prakash Maurya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections are caused in individuals who are immunocompromised or with certain lung conditions. The NTM disease in the lungs, also referred to as NTM-pulmonary disease (NTM-PD), can lead to nodular bronchiectasis or cavitary disease is serious as it can cause respiratory failure or complications like fibrosis or scarring. Thus, NTM infection diagnosis at an earlier stage is important for efficient disease management.Aim of reviewThis review summarizes the role played by metabolomics and artificial intelligence (AI) assisted metabolomics in enhancing the efficiency of NTM diagnosis.Key scientific concepts of reviewNTM infections deserve importance as the non-tuberculous mycobacteria are ubiquitous and can cause infections in immunocompromised and those with abnormal lung conditions. Thus, this review elaborates about the current diagnostic methods for NTM infections and their demerits. The potential of metabolomics in providing solutions to the lacunae in current diagnostic methods is also addressed along with the challenges associated with this diagnostic platform. Also, the pivotal role played by the AI based technologies in bolstering the current metabolomic methodologies is also discussed and finally the way forward for harnessing this technology to enhance NTM diagnosis is also explained.

Introduction

Non tuberculous mycobacterial infections are those caused by non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) which are found widely distributed in the environment in different soil and water sources. They cause infections in individuals with compromised immune systems or those with lung conditions like patients with bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (the risk is increased in COPD when the patients take inhaled corticosteroids) [1]. The NTM enters the body via wound or by inhalation into the lungs (in the case of pulmonary disease (PD) caused by NTM or NTM-PD) that causes infection. NTM infections can manifest as lung infections which can be nodular bronchiectasis or cavitary disease which are more serious as it leads to fibrosis, lung scarring in the former and cavities in the latter which may lead to respiratory failure [2]. It can also cause infections in skin tissues and lymph nodes and in those with weak immune systems; it can cause disseminated disease which is fatal [3]. On the other hand, NTM infections that occur outside the lungs are referred to as NTM-extra pulmonary disease (NTM-EPD) that is not so common. They affect body parts like soft tissues, skin, lymph nodes, musculoskeletal system and they manifest many clinical symptoms in both immunocompromised as well as immunocompetent individuals. In individuals with underlying lung disease, those who underwent certain surgeries are also susceptible to NTM-EPD infections. Diagnosis of NTM-EPD infections involves clinical, radiological and microbiological investigations for identifying the NTM species [4]. Among the NTM species, the common pathogens Mycobacterium avium complex and M. abscessus cause chronic, difficult-to-treat lung disease that leads to significant morbidity with case-fatality reaching 10.8% for pulmonary and 50% for disseminated infections [5]. Apart from these species, M. kansasii is also a virulent NTM species that causes pulmonary disease which resembles TB [6] and M. chimaera is associated with health-care related infections particularly after cardiac surgery with the involvement of contaminated heater cooler units [7]. While M. marinum is associated with ‘fish tank granuloma’, a skin infection [8], M. ulcerans is responsible for Buruli ulcers, a severe necrotizing skin disease [9]. The NTM species like M. chelonae and M. fortuitum are associated with skin, soft-tissue and medical device related infections [10].

Since NTM infections can prove to be really life-threatening in some cases, timely diagnosis and treatment is advocated for proper management of this disease. The diagnosis of NTM infections involves a combination of clinical, radiological, microbiological and/or molecular methods and the treatment of NTM infections involves a combination of antibiotics for many months [4] and in some cases surgery [11] may be done as a part of the treatment procedure. Treatment of NTM infections is considered very essential for preventing the progression to chronic disease, improving the quality of life and for reducing the mortality which is more relevant in individuals with underlying health conditions, as said earlier. Also, the treatment regimen is designed based on the infected NTM species and the location of infection. A systematic review by Dahl et al. (2022) incorporating 47 studies on NTM infections has shown that many studies reported increased pulmonary infection (82.1%) and disease (66.7%) trends. Increasing trend was observed for Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infection and disease and for M. abscessus infection but not disease by the study [12]. Different NTM species are distributed in different geographic regions based on environmental conditions and other factors. A review highlights the varied geographic distribution of NTM species based on the studies from different geographic locations [13]. To cite a few, MAC was found to be predominant in United Kingdom and in the United States along with it were M. kansasii and M. abscessus which contributed to NTM infections. M. xenopi was reported to be the common NTM species in Europe. M. intracellulare and M. abscessus were reported to be common in Australia. Thus, the varied distribution of different NTM species across the globe suggests specialized local surveillance and tailoring of clinical management accordingly. Because of their distinct pathogenic behaviours and susceptibilities, a uniform treatment strategy doesn't work for NTM species, hence a personalized, species-specific and expert-guided approach would be required. The fact that NTM microorganisms can be challenging to isolate adds even more complexity. Patients may find it difficult to tolerate the drugs used for the treatment of NTM infections, and extensive regimens of anti-mycobacterial medication are frequently necessary for sufficient suppression or eradication of NTM [4,14,15].

Although the currently used diagnostic modalities help in identification of the NTM species, the time taken for diagnosis, lack of sensitivity, cost involved in some methods has made exploration of alternative methods including the ‘omics’ platform for promising solutions. Along these lines, this review explores the advancements in metabolomics for the diagnosis of NTM and the demerits associated with it and the way forward for harnessing this methodology for efficient NTM diagnosis.

Diagnostic modalities for NTM infections

The clinical diagnosis of NTM infections is done by a combination of symptomatology, radiographic findings and by microbiological confirmation. The clinical specimen obtained is cultured to identify the NTM species causing the infection [4]. In the case of diagnosis by symptoms, the clinician can consider the risk factors precipitating the NTM infections like lung diseases or immunocompromised immune systems. The non-specific symptoms that may be present in NTM infections are chronic cough (with or without production of sputum), fatigue, fever, loss of appetite, weight loss, hemoptysis. Chest X-ray or computed tomography (CT) scans for NTM diagnosis should focus on the presence of nodules, cavities, bronchiectasis (multifocal), small nodules in the radiograph [16]. High resolution computed tomography (HRCT) scans are more efficient as they can provide an in-depth view of multifocal bronchiectasis and small nodules in the lungs which are characteristic of NTM infections [17].

Microbiologically, sputum smear (but it cannot differentiate between M. tuberculosis and NTM) and culture are used for the diagnosis. It takes around 8 weeks to identify the NTM species after the culture. Molecular techniques like PCR and genome sequencing methods like targeted next generation sequencing (tNGS), metagenome sequencing (mNGS), help in accurate identification of the NTM species. In contrast, metabolomics provides a fundamentally different advantage by reflecting real-time biochemical activity associated with active NTM disease [18]. Metabolic signatures integrate both pathogen-derived metabolites and host metabolic responses, enabling discrimination between clinically relevant states such as active infection versus colonization, assessment of disease burden, and monitoring of treatment response—areas where nucleic acid–based assays often fall short despite high sensitivity [19,20].

Available evidence also indicate that different NTM species and disease phenotypes generate distinct and reproducible metabolic patterns [18]. Although mycobacterial species-specific diagnosis is possible by metabolomics, not many studies have explored it owing to the technical and analytical challenges, biological factors like intra-individual variability (based on diet, medications, gut microbiota), economic factors (cost of instrumentation), practical factors (large-scale validation).

Integration of metabolomics into clinical workflows in future will need rigorous validation, standardization and cost-effectiveness when compared to the other standard methods. Pairing metabolomics with AI technologies may help in providing superior accuracy, speed, specificity and the ability to differentiate complex but closely related NTM infections [21].

The type and frequency of testing of specimens are crucial for diagnosis of NTM species. In the case of NTM-PD, sputum specimens are primarily used for disease diagnosis. The sputum induction is considered if there is high degree of clinical suspicion and the tests are non-diagnostic. If there is difficulty in producing sputum by the patient, it is induced by making the patient breathe in a mist of saline which aids in loosening the mucus and promotes coughing. In cases where this induction fails, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage is performed which involves collection of fluid from the lungs through a bronchoscope which has more sensitivity than culture for NTM-PD diagnosis [22,23]. Bronchiectasis patients when infected by NTM manifest symptoms that are mild and also excretion of NTM is intermittent hence sputum may not be a reliable specimen for diagnosis and lung biopsy or bronchoscopy may be valuable for diagnosis [24]. For diagnostic purposes, as per the leading international infectious disease and respiratory medicine societies (ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA) guidelines, more than one positive culture is recommended for and also the same NTM species should be isolated in two or more sputum cultures for confirmation. But, in the case of specimens from sterile sites, (eg., fluids like cerebrospinal fluid/pericardial fluid) or when biopsies are used for diagnosis, the guidelines recommend that one specimen is sufficient for diagnosis [25].

If the NTM infection involves organs or tissues other than the lungs (NTM-EPD), a biopsy is taken for smear and acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining, culture and histopathological evaluation. The tissue is examined to identify granulomas or for other characteristics indicating NTM infection. The type of tissue required depends on the organ affected which may include lymph nodes, skin and soft tissues, bone, blood. In the case of lymph nodes, biopsy is taken or fine needle aspiration of the fluid of lymph nodes is taken and then analyzed for the pathology. Bone biopsy may be needed if bone will be involved and blood cultures should be performed if the NTM causes disseminated infections. Since there are no specific guidelines for diagnosis of NTM-EPD, it is done based on expert opinion and knowledge from NTM advisory groups when NTM-EPD is suspected.

Many diagnostic modalities listed above are widely used for NTM diagnosis, but still the challenges faced include low sensitivity, difficulty in distinguishing between colonization and infection, slow growth which often leads to delayed or missed diagnosis. Thus, alternate high through put technologies is sought for to provide solutions to this problem.

Mycobacterial species identification by metabolomics

Species level identification of mycobacterial species has also been shown by earlier studies using metabolomics. An earlier study using metabolomics was able to differentiate mycobacterial species (M. genavense, M. fortuitum, M. simiae, M. tuberculosis) based on their fatty acid and mycolic acid cleavage products using gas chromatography. In this study, M. fortuitum was differentiated from M. genavense by the specific marker 2-methyl octadecenoic acid found in M. fortuitum and by the ratio of tetracosanoic acid to hexacosanoic acid. M. genavense, M. simiae, M. tuberculosis exhibited similar Gas liquid chromatography (GLC) profiles but were discerned by the presence of either cis-10-hexadecenoic acid or cis-11-hexadecenoic acid and by their content of tetradecanoic acid [26]. Another study was also able to differentiate four mycobacterial species (M. tuberculosis, M. avium, M. bovis, M. kansasii) using metabolic patterns obtained from gas chromatography. In this study, 12 compounds were observed to be the potential metabolic markers which described the variation between the sample groups [27]. The study by Beccaria et al. (2021) and Kuntzel et al. (2018) explored volatilome profiles by GC-MS in culture head space of seven mycobacterial species and thirteen mycobacterial species respectively and found that this technique was very efficient in discriminating mycobacterial species [28,29]. Thus, although there are few studies highlighting the ability of metabolomics in NTM species identification, showing the potential of this technique in NTM diagnosis, further progress has been hindered by technical, biological and logistic challenges. Although it has high sensitivity and ability to differentiate closely related NTM species, slow growth of NTM, the complexity of clinical samples, instrumentation cost, limited funding for NTM research have been the caveats for advancements in this arena. But the field is overcoming these barriers and moving forward with advanced data bases, high resolution techniques and integration with AI for meaningful and better outputs.

Integrating metabolomics in clinical workflows can help in rapid and highly accurate differentiation of NTM infections from tuberculosis. Urine samples can be utilized in targeted or untargeted metabolomics for a rapid, non-invasive triage test. Specific urine biosignatures like altered levels of tryptophan, methionine, valine, 3-hydroxyanthranilate have earlier been shown to differentiate NTM from TB. If machine learning models like random forest will be integrated into workflow, it can enhance the accuracy of classification between NTM and TB. Integration of the metabolomic approaches in workflows may pave way for ‘personalized medicine’ approach. This will help clinicians to monitor treatment responses and also for predicting relapses when compared to other traditional methods which may be more time consuming and may not be very sensitive.

Advantages of metabolomics over ‘omics’ and other technologies in the diagnosis of NTM infections

When compared to other omics technologies, metabolomics is very unique in the sense that it reflects the physiological state of the individual and their interaction with the environment. Thus, the key information provided by metabolomics cannot be provided by other ‘omics’ technologies (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics). Metabolomics captures low–molecular weight metabolites that reflect the final downstream output of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic regulation. As a result, metabolic profiles provide a more immediate snapshot of disease activity, host immune responses, and pathogen–host interactions than upstream omics layers [30]. This is especially relevant for NTM infection, which is characterized by chronic, heterogeneous host responses and variable bacterial burden [[30], [31], [32]]. The unique advantages which metabolomics offer when compared to other ‘omics’ technologies are as follows.i)Ideal representation of the host-pathogen interaction and disease phenotype and reflection of physiological status

Technologies like genomics and proteomics indicates disease potential which may not correlate with actual disease state because of environmental factors and post-translational modifications (PTM) (found in proteins which are reversible and transient, non-specific and the presence of biological variability in PTM). The end products of cellular processes (i.e., ‘metabolites’) reflect the disease state as well as the clinical phenotype of an individual thus it reflects the immediate biochemical status of the individual. Metabolic perturbations often occur earlier than detectable changes at the transcript or protein level. Metabolites can also occur in biofluids (urine, saliva, blood) much before clinical symptoms are visible which are usually diagnosed at a very late stage by traditional diagnostic methods (like imaging). This makes metabolomics particularly valuable for early diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and distinguishing active NTM disease from colonization or latent infection—an ongoing diagnostic challenge in clinical practice [18,33].

Since metabolite levels change in response to lifestyle, medication, diet, disease, genetics, gut flora, they can help in providing a sensitive and dynamic health status. Thus, it provides a holistic view of disease process (by inclusion of external and internal factors) which other ‘omics’ technologies cannot capture.ii)High suitability for non-invasive and non-sputum samples

Metabolic analysis can be performed in non-invasive and easily accessible body fluids like urine, saliva, blood, breath condensate thus it is advantageous over methods that use invasive procedures (like bronchoscopy, tissue biopsy methods). Hence it is very useful in individuals who cannot produce sputum and also in individuals with paucibacillary disease [[34], [35], [36]].iii)Lesser data complexity and dataset suitability for machine-learning

Data analysis is less complex in metabolomics due to the smaller number of metabolites known in the human metabolome (around 2000-3000) when compared to thousands of genes (genomics) or millions of proteins (proteomics) and more than million transcripts (transcriptomics) found in the human cell. This can also help in simplifying data analysis for biomarker identification. Also, Metabolomics generates high-dimensional but relatively compact datasets that are well suited for machine-learning and AI-based classification models. These approaches can identify diagnostic metabolic signatures even when individual biomarkers show modest effect sizes, improving robustness across heterogeneous patient populations [[37], [38], [39]].iv)Ability to capture both host- and pathogen-derived signatures

Ability to capture both host- and pathogen-derived signatures

Metabolomic profiles integrate signals from host metabolism (e.g., immune activation, lipid remodelling, oxidative stress) and pathogen-specific metabolic pathways, such as mycobacterial lipid and energy metabolism [40]. This dual perspective is more difficult to achieve with single-organism–focused genomic approaches and is particularly relevant for NTM species with diverse metabolic adaptations [41].

Thus, metabolomics offers several advantages over the conventional diagnostic methods for NTM and omics technologies and it has the potential for aiding in early diagnosis as it is pivotal in providing the functional state of an organism in real time.v)Ability of metabolomics in distinguishing environmental contamination/colonization from actual NTM infections/disease

Ability of metabolomics in distinguishing environmental contamination/colonization from actual NTM infections/disease

Metabolomics can differentiate environmental contamination of NTM species from true infection/disease by identifying unique metabolic fingerprints. They help in specific discrimination of host immune response metabolites (infection) from exogenous non-host derived metabolites (contamination). Also, true infection leads to dynamic metabolic changes in amino acid metabolism or lipid pathways demonstrating the response of the host to the pathogen aiding in differentiation. Such altered metabolic profiles are reflected in biofluids like urine, blood, sputum wherein specific markers like valine, methionine, tryptophan associated pathways act as discriminators [21,42].

Metabolomics may also help distinguish colonization from active disease by identifying metabolic patterns linked to bacterial burden, inflammation, and clinical severity, which are expected to be less pronounced in asymptomatic colonization [34]. Since colonization involves bacterial presence without tissue invasion, the robust metabolic host-response signature of an active infection is absent. In cystic fibrosis patients, it has been shown that sputum metabolomics had the ability to distinguish NTM-infected individuals from those with colonization identifying distinct and elevated metabolites like 2-methyl citrate/homocitrate and selected ceramides [42].

If metabolomics is coupled with machine learning, it can increase the discriminatory ability between infection and colonization in a very efficient way. Importantly, integrating metabolomic data with clinical, radiologic, and microbiologic findings, as well as using standardized sampling and appropriate controls, will aid in accurate interpretation [43].

Metabolomic technologies for the diagnosis of NTM infections

The study of metabolites referred to as ‘metabolomics’ has the potential in diagnosing NTM infections by identifying metabolic signatures of the host and the pathogen and helping in diagnosis of different NTM infections as well as in the discovery of biomarkers. It may be defined as a cutting-edge research platform for methodical investigations of the small-molecular metabolite profiles of a cell, tissue, or organ—the by-products of cellular processes [32]. Metabolomic studies can be of two types: targeted and untargeted, wherein the former explores specific metabolites in a profound way whereas the latter investigates the entire metabolome which may unravel the metabolic changes that are unforeseen. It is possible to distinguish between various biological systems and find putative metabolite markers unique to these systems by comparing the metabolic profiles of various cells or systems using statistical analyses [30,44]. The method has been used to diagnose several illnesses or pathogens, including MTB [[45], [46], [47]]. This method has also been used to distinguish healthy individuals from patients suffering from infections like urinary tract infections and pneumococcal disease using metabolomic information derived from urine samples [32,44].

Species/strain identification by metabolomics

However, detection from culture and species/strain identification through intracellular metabolite analyses were the primary focus of earlier metabolomic studies on MTB isolates [48]. The potential of MTB's extracellular metabolites as particular biomarkers is not well understood [34]. Metabolomic studies, for instance, have been carried out to compare the hyper- and hypo-virulent strains, identify different species of Mycobacterium, and examine how MTB strains use carbon [49]. Potential signature metabolites have been identified in a few studies utilizing samples from infected humans or animals, but their applicability for routine diagnostic purposes has not yet been established [49]. The authors of a study speculate that there may be novel extracellular metabolites that are specifically produced by MTB that can be found in bodily fluids for non-invasive tuberculosis diagnosis because MTB can produce stearic acid and volatile organic compounds (VOC) that can be found in the sputum and urine of infected patients, respectively [34].

Technologies

The metabolomes of culture supernatants of MTB (nine strains) and NTM species (nine strains) were characterized using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization–quadrupole time of flight–mass spectrometry (UHPLC–ESI–Q TOF–MS) to find possible biomarkers for tuberculosis diagnosis [48]. The metabolome data was subjected to both univariate and multivariate statistical analyses to identify particular metabolites that are exclusively present in the culture supernatants of MTB species but not NTM species [48]. Further, the authors reported that twenty-four metabolites with noticeably higher levels in MTB were found using multivariate and univariate analysis, and seven metabolites were found using tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) [48].

Further, Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) provides limited sensitivity; however, dynamic nuclear polarization, cryo- and microprobes, and stronger fields can improve this [50]. It offers very high reproducibility, permits non-destructive analysis, and requires very little sample preparation. In a single measurement, NMR can identify every metabolite with an NMR concentration level [51]. It is also appropriate for in vivo research, particularly 1H magnetic resonance spectroscopy [52].

In a study by Breen et al., 2022, the sputum metabolites were studied in cystic fibrosis patients with NTM. The investigators observed that many metabolites differed significantly in NTM compared to the controls like tryptophan associated and branched chain amino acid metabolites but the compounds involved in metabolism of phospholipids showed elevated levels [42]. The microbiome and metabolome data of the study was integrated by ‘sparse Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) method’, a machine learning technique and it showed differences between the control and NTM samples [42].

A study investigated the metabolites in the exhaled breath of active NTM patients (who were patients with cystic fibrosis) and indolent patients. The technique of two-dimensional gas chromatography time of flight mass spectrometer (GC x GC-TOFMS) was employed for exploring the compounds from the breath samples. It was observed that the molecule C13H28 (putatively identified as tridecane) was found in the breath samples of NTM patients and those with indolent disease when compared to those from whom NTM was not cultured (from their samples). Also, it was observed that the metabolite, C12H26 (putatively identified as 2,2,4,6,6-penta methyl heptanes) was highly abundant in the exhaled breath samples of NTM and indolent patients compared to those patients from whom NTM was not cultured. The higher abundance of 14 out of 17 biomarkers in their study in active-NTM patients but not in NTM never positive group made the investigators propound that these markers were mycobacteria associated breath signatures. The study also emphasizes that a large study for validation should incorporate other confounding factors (drugs, history of treatment, nutritional status) to get a clear picture [53].

Interestingly, another study by Beccaria et al. (2021) evaluated the bacterial volatilome for classifying and identifying mycobacterial species using Head space solid phase microextraction gas chromatography Mass spectrometry (HS SPME GC-MS) and machine learning. The study investigated the VOC in the culture head space of seven species of mycobacteria for defining the volatilome profiles to know those that discriminate between the species. They also used machine learning approach for selecting thirteen discriminatory features which they postulate to serve as bacterial biomarkers. It was observed that FT1559 (Furan, 2-methyl-3-(methylthio)-) and FT0867 (Furan, 2-butyl-) were discriminant among each complex of mycobacteria viz., M. abscessus complex, MAC and M. tuberculosis. FT0792 (Phenylacetaldehyde), FT1866 (unknown) and FT1521 (unknown) were discriminant either within M. abscessus complex or the MAC complex thus distinguishing mycobacterial classes belonging to the complexes in an unique manner [29].

Another study on bacterial volatilome explored the head space of 13 mycobacterial species for the VOC. The samples were analyzed by Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC-MS). From the study, it was identified that the VOC profiles were species-specific for eleven mycobacterial species which also manifested a visual bacterial growth. Compared to controls, the study found that Mycobacteriaceae had a core-profile with 8 increasing and 9 decreasing compounds. The study concludes that the presence of 13 mycobacterial species can be proven by analyzing VOC profiles. To conclude, volatilome studies may help in accelerating diagnosis and also simplify the diagnostic methods for NTM [28].

The laboratory investigation by Oppermann et al. (2025) showed that the metabolite amounts in the NTM-inoculated and control mycobacteria growth indicator tubes (MGITs) did not change significantly [54]. The study also recorded that, only broth components were visible in the spectra, indicating either low analyte concentrations or higher media signals overpowered them [54]. Thus the authors suggest optimization of sample preparation methods for improving NTM diagnosis by NMR [54].

Kim et al. (2024) reported that, 11 metabolites associated with elevated oxidative stress were markedly different in NTM-PD compared to healthy controls [18]. Results of this study contributed to a more thorough understanding of the pathophysiology of NTM-PD and offer suggestions for innovative therapeutic strategies [18]. Cho et al. (2020) reported that, compared to Latent TB infected (LTBI) subjects or healthy controls, metabolic profiles showed that active TB patients had lower serum levels of glutamine, methionine, and asparagine and higher levels of glutamate, sulfoxy methionine, and aspartate [55]. The group with active tuberculosis also had higher ratios between metabolically related partners, such as sulfoxy methionine/methionine, glutamate/glutamine, and aspartate/asparagine. In patients with active TB, there was no discernible variation in the serum concentration of these metabolites based on the severity of the disease or the chance of recurrence. Glutamate, aspartate, methionine, glutamine, sulfoxy methionine, and asparagine are examples of novel serum biomarkers that may be helpful for the quick, non-invasive, and adjunctive diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis [55].

In another study, it was observed that E-Flux-MFC is an improvement on the original E-Flux technique that makes it possible to predict how changes in internal and external metabolite production will correlate with measurements of gene expression [56]. The method's accuracy in forecasting alterations in MTB lipids after the deletion and induction of two extensively researched transcription factors (TFs) was also confirmed by the authors [56].

Wang et al. (2019), reported that the inhibition of MTB's ATP synthase by bedaquiline (BDQ) results in a complex metabolic response that is suggestive of a particular hierarchy of ATP-dependent interactions. The enzyme whose activity responds most to variations in ATP levels is glutamine synthetase (GS) [57]. The antimycobacterial activity of BDQ was unaffected by chemical supplementation with extracellular glutamine. Further suppression of MTB's glutamine synthetase, however, advanced the initiation of BDQ-mediated destruction and worked in concert with it. This suggests that MTB's glutamine synthetase is a metabolic vulnerability of BDQ rather than an antimycobacterial agent [57].

In a study by Anh et al. (2024), untargeted metabolomics of urine of TB and NTM patients was performed to know the usefulness of this method in differential diagnosis. The study found that NTM patients expressed high levels of methionine, glutarate, valine, 3-hydroxyanthralinate, corticosterone and indole-3-carboxyaldehyde when compared to TB patients. Thus, the authors propound that metabolomics may aid in discovering biomarkers for the non-invasive diagnosis of NTM infections [21].

Advantages of metabolomics in the diagnosis of NTM

Metabolomics can help in NTM diagnosis by detecting the disease in an earlier stage, can help in identifying novel biomarkers and also may pave way for non-invasive diagnostics. The metabolomic changes may aid in differentiating NTM from tuberculosis infection and may help in disease prognosis (marked by metabolomic changes in stages of progression of NTM) as well as in monitoring the efficiency of the administered treatment and thus help in monitoring treatment outcome.

Metabolic profiling may aid in NTM diagnosis early even before the clinical symptoms may appear which may help in timely diagnosis as well as initiation of treatment. Metabolomic changes due to NTM infections may lead to a unique profile with upregulated/downregulated metabolites which may be used as biomarkers for NTM diagnosis. The use of urine or blood or other easily accessible samples for metabolomic profiling and diagnosis may help in devising diagnostics based on non-invasive methods. Since NTM and tuberculosis infections have unique metabolomic profiles, it can be used for differential diagnosis of NTM from TB disease [21]. Studying metabolomic profiles over time during the disease may help in monitoring disease progression and analyzing metabolomic profiles before and after treatment, metabolomics may help in evaluating the efficiency of treatment.

Challenges in metabolomics technology for the diagnosis of NTM

Although metabolomics has several advantages, it has many challenges like the complexity of metabolome, requirement of potent analytical methods and interpreting the huge data generated in metabolomic analysis. Other challenges include the type of platform used for metabolomic analysis, different types of sample preparation methods and inter-individual variation which can lead to variation in results.

The metabolome is influenced by genetic, environmental, gut microflora, as well as by lifestyle changes which can impact metabolic profile thus making it difficult to observe NTM specific metabolic changes. Moreover, the identification and quantification of enormous metabolites is a daunting task. The use of different platforms for metabolite identification like liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), NMR with different sensitivities can yield biased results. Proper sample extraction and preparation are very important for obtaining reliable data. The metabolomics technology generates huge datasets which will require extensive statistical analysis as well as interpretation which is a challenge.

Advent of AI-technologies in the advancement of metabolomic studies on NTM

The efficiency of NTM diagnosis by metabolomics has been increased with the advent of AI technologies specifically machine learning. Complex metabolic profiles of NTM infections can be analyzed by AI algorithms for identification of metabolic patterns of the infections. The identification of specific metabolic patterns in NTM infections may help in improving the accuracy of diagnosis, aid in differential diagnosis and also for identifying biomarkers for early diagnosis and monitoring of treatment [58]. Further, researchers are also developing non-invasive diagnostic techniques to increase speed, accuracy, and discrimination between NTM and TB by utilizing metabolomics, which is enhanced by AI and machine learning (ML).

The AI technologies can analyze huge amount of data and observe the patterns between metabolite levels in NTM infections. Thus, they may help in metabolite identification for differentiating between TB and other lung infections from NTM infections [21]. The AI can also help in developing predictive models for estimating the risk of developing NTM infections based on the metabolic profile of individuals. This deserves importance in individuals at high risk of developing NTM infection wherein it can help in targeting prevention and implementation of surveillance methods. The AI technology can help in identifying biomarkers by metabolomic data analysis and detecting metabolites showing consistent changes in samples positive for NTM. These biomarkers are useful for early and accurate diagnosis of NTM infections so as to manage the disease efficiently.

Combining AI based metabolomic analysis for diagnosis along with AI based imaging techniques/molecular methods will improve the overall accuracy of NTM diagnosis. By considering an individual's unique metabolic profile, effective treatment strategies can be implemented and this will pave way for targeted therapies tailored to individual's specific metabolic requirements highlighting the potential of AI technologies in personalized medicine [59]. Analysis of metabolic changes associated with NTM may help in finding drug targets which will help in developing effective treatments for NTM infections. AI tools and analysis of metabolomic data of NTM

The metabolomic data derived from NTM if analyzed with the help of AI tools, can automate it and enhance the tasks like preprocessing of data, metabolite annotation, biomarker discovery, predictive modelling efficiently. AI tools will be efficient in handling vast metabolomic datasets, performing accurate spectral matching and predicting of molecular structures leading to faster diagnosis which may aid in improving the understanding of NTM pathogenesis as well as for identifying new therapeutic targets [60].

For analyzing metabolomic data of NTM with the aid of machine learning, training of machine learning algorithms like supervised or unsupervised learning models needs to be done. The supervised models include Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). These supervised models will be useful for classifying patients into different groups, identifying of biomarkers and for the prediction of treatment response based on specific metabolomic profiles. The unsupervised learning models include Principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction (reducing the number of variables for easily comprehending the structure of metabolomic data) and clustering algorithms (e.g., K-means) used for grouping samples with similar metabolic profiles to identify the metabolic subtypes of diseases. Deep learning (DL) algorithms like convolutional neural networks (CNN) are being used for recognizing complex patterns in high dimensional metabolomic datasets [61,62].

The selection of a specific algorithm depends on the specific research goal and the characteristics of the metabolomic dataset. For classifying samples or predicting outcomes, supervised methods like RF, SVM, ANN are used for building predictive models. For reducing data complexity and identifying key metabolites, unsupervised techniques like PCA may be employed. In the case of complex datasets obtained especially from spectral analysis, DL may exhibit superior performance for pattern recognition. AI and platforms used for analyzing metabolomic data of NTM

AI tools like machine learning algorithms and deep learning models (described in the previous section) are integrated into platforms like MetaboAnalyst [67], MeltDB 2.0 [68] and Galaxy [69] via programming libraries like Scikit-learn in Python and Caret, mixOmics, Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA) in R. MetaboAnalyst is a comprehensive platform for statistical analysis and metabolomic data visualization including the pathway analysis. Galaxy is an open-source platform which can integrate and analyze ‘omics’ data. Other software platforms include Data Independent Acquisition in Liquid chromatography coupled to Mass Spectrometry (MS-DIAL) [70], MetaCore [71], InCroMAP [72], 3Omics [73]. The pre-processed data from platforms like MetaboAnalyst can be exported to and analyzed using various deep learning (DL) frameworks like PyTorch, TensorFlow which perform tasks like pattern recognition and classification. These frameworks perform tasks like metabolite annotation, pathway analysis and integration of multiomics data for identifying biological patterns and biomarkers [74].

Open-source data mining and machine learning platforms like Waikato environment for knowledge analysis (WEKA) [75], Konstanz information miner (KNIME) [76] and ‘Orange’ [77] are also utilized for metabolomic data analysis. While WEKA is a collection of ML algorithms which are useful for classifying samples based on metabolomic profiles, identifying biomarkers and predicting phenotypes or disease outcomes from metabolomic data, it requires users to have an understanding of ML concepts. The KNIME and ‘Orange’ platforms are visual work-flow based platforms and it allows users to build data analysis through its drag-drop functionality. Because of this user-friendly interface, it can be used by users without extensive programming knowledge. KNIME finds utility in pre-processing and processing of raw metabolomic data, integration of data from different sources (Mass spectrometry, NMR, clinical data) and in visualizing metabolomic data and results. ‘Orange’ is used for visualizing metabolomic datasets, classifying and clustering samples based on metabolomic profiles, building and comparing of different predictive models.

Cost of AI-driven metabolomic data analysis platforms

The cost associated with AI-assisted metabolomic data analysis platforms varies significantly but many are open-source platforms (MetaboAnalyst, Galaxy) and are free of cost. The costs are associated with computational infrastructure (computers with high performance and huge data storage) and software packages like ‘soft independent modelling for class analogy’ (SIMCA) for multivariate analysis. SIMCA employs techniques like PCA and PLS-DA for identifying patterns, correlations and key variables within datasets. Also, the cost associated with human expertise for analysis and data interpretation should also be taken into account. Data volume and complexity add to the cost as it requires greater computational resources and processing power.

The use of pre-built, software–as-a-service (SaaS) solutions for metabolomic data analysis is cost-effective as it leverages existing AI models and the cloud infrastructure, which can be useful for scaling biomarkers for disease management at a lower cost. In the case of specialized metabolomic analysis, custom models and software need to be developed from the scratch which involves significantly higher costs with software development reaching $60,000 to $200,000 or more. Also, the costs associated with support and maintenance of AI software and models can be up to $20,000 per year. Platforms like ‘biostate.ai’ aims in reducing the cost in multiomics analysis by automated processing and streamlining of workflows which can bring down to the sample cost to around $80. Thus, costs may range from utilizing free tools with significant expertise to hiring dedicated teams and paying for advanced, commercial AI-powered analytical platforms.

Combining machine learning with urinary metabolomics

In a study, urine samples from patients with TB and NTM were subjected to untargeted LC-MS metabolomics, followed by rigorous statistical modeling and machine learning classifiers [21]. Significant changes in amino acid metabolism were found in the study, particularly in the tryptophan pathway. Glutarate, tryptophan, valine and 3-hydroxyanthranilate were all lower in NTM instances, whereas methionine was higher [21]. Among the several models, random forest had the best performance, with an AUC >0.8. Corticosterone, methionine, valine, glutarate, indole-3-carboxyaldehyde and 3-hydroxyanthranilate, were identified as six possible indicators. This study demonstrates the robust prioritization of biomarkers from small and complex datasets using machine learning [21].

Comparing NTM, TB, and other predictive models with multiomics

A study used ML in conjunction with multiomics (lipidomics and proteomics) to distinguish TB from NTM, other lung illnesses, LTBI [78]. The study showed the viability and power of ML-based diagnosis in complicated mycobacterial situations, considering the fact that the lipid PC (14:0_22:6) was identified as a crucial marker in differentiating NTM from active TB [78].

Machine learning, microbiome, and BALF metabolomics

Combining metagenomic and metabolomic sequencing with ML analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) identified unique microbial signatures in NTM patients, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa enrichment and impaired tryptophan metabolism indicated by elevated kynurenine, that contributed to immunosuppression and disease persistence [79,80]. These AI/ML-guided integrated multi-omics examinations have potential for both diagnostic and mechanistic understanding [79,80].

Challenges in Statistics and Validation

Limited sample numbers in several metabolomic studies raise questions about the generalizability of the models [87]. Rigorous statistical analysis is necessary to avoid over-fitting in the large and complex metabolomics datasets [88]. The validation of biomarkers necessitates thorough external cohort confirmation and internal cross-validation, addressing robustness, selectivity, and reproducibility.

Although AI has the potential in diagnosis of NTM by metabolomics, it suffers from many limitations. In order to circumvent these problems, accurate and robust AI algorithms are required along with the usage of large and diverse datasets for training. Thus, AI with its ability to analyze complex metabolic profiles, can identify metabolic signatures related to NTM, enhance the diagnostic accuracy and can lead to timely and more effective treatments.

Future perspectives

Although metabolomics is a high throughput technique which can be used for NTM diagnosis by analyzing biomarkers in biological fluids like blood and urine, future directions for harnessing its potential would involve: (i) Advancements in efficient analytical techniques for detecting and quantifying metabolites. (ii) Effective machine learning algorithms for interpreting complex metabolomic data and identifying biomarkers (iii) Development of cost-effective, non-invasive diagnostic tests based on metabolomic studies like urine based assays, breathome analysis etc. (iv) Study of metabolic profile changes after NTM infection may lead to identification of therapeutic targets (eg., tryptophan–kynurenine axis observed in BALF in NTM patient studies, may have diagnostic and therapeutic implications) (v) Identifying and validating metabolic targets for differentiating NTM-PD from pulmonary TB and other lung diseases (vi) larger scale validation studies involving multiple centres for verifying consistency of biomarkers in different groups (vii) development of efficient AI-driven analytical platforms for ML inference, mass spectrometry and sample preparation (viii) development of targeted assays which can help convert non-specific results into quick, tailored tests (eg., panels assessing important metabolites like 3-hydroxyanthranilate or methionine) (vi) Integration of other ‘omics’ technologies like genomics, proteomics along with metabolomics to get a better understanding of host-pathogen interactions and changes in metabolic patterns for devising efficient diagnostic tests for NTM.

Thus, in order to distinguish NTM infections from TB and other lung conditions and also for diagnosing NTM infections, AI-assisted metabolomics may ease the way for a quick, non-invasive, and precise diagnosis. The combination of metabolomic profiling with machine learning classifiers, supported by strong biomarkers such as lipid species and amino acids, has the potential in efficient diagnosis. However, strict validation, standardization, and translational frameworks are necessary to achieve clinical integration. With its distinct combination of biological knowledge, scalability, and precision, AI-assisted metabolomics has the potential to revolutionize NTM disease diagnosis.
