Promoting radiation safety in endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography through dose recording and diagnostic reference level benchmarking
Authors: Tsutomu Nishida, Kengo Matsumoto, Shiro Hayashi
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the article by de Latour et al,1 “Impact of an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography radiation safety initiative on pregnant and nonpregnant staff dose exposures: a quality improvement initiative.”1 This quality improvement initiative showed that measures can reduce staff exposure by shortening fluoroscopy time (FT) from 12.1 to 6.3 minutes (48%) and lowering the monthly shallow-dose equivalent by 70%. The undetectable fetal badge readings suggest that endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography during pregnancy may be feasible with protection.1

Notably, although the initiative included a fluoroscopy bed and additional shielding, the same C-arm unit and fluoroscopy settings were maintained, including a high pulse rate of 30 pulses per second.1 Education and monthly badge monitoring likely increased awareness, although badge reports were delayed. In addition, reviewing and optimizing the pulse rate may further reduce staff dose.

In our published 8-year single-center time trend study,2 FT remained unchanged, whereas air kerma at the patient entrance reference point (Ka,r) and dose–area product (DAP) decreased by approximately 71% and 60%, largely after equipment upgrades, respectively. Dose metrics declined further with ongoing tracking and discussion; by contrast, in the present quality improvement study, reductions seem mainly because of shorter FT. Per-case reporting of FT, Ka,r, and DAP enables near–real-time feedback and team sharing.

A national survey of Japanese gastroenterologists found that only 17% had adequate knowledge of diagnostic reference levels (DRLs).3 In our multicenter radiation exposure from gastrointestinal fluoroscopic procedures (REX-GI) study, local DRLs varied widely across Japan, and benchmarking against national DRLs provided each center with an opportunity to reflect on its own practice and identify modifiable drivers of higher doses.4,5 We also observed a strong correlation between ocular lens exposure and DRL quantities,6 supporting the routine recording and visualization of these data to help manage occupational exposure. Accordingly, we encourage the routine recording of DRL quantities (FT, Ka,r, and DAP).7
