Mind The Gap: Deep Learning Doesn’t Learn Deeply
Lucas Saldyt, Subbarao Kambhampati

Abstract
This paper aims to understand how neural networks learn algorithmic reasoning by addressing two questions: How faithful are learned algorithms when they are effective, and why do neural networks fail to learn effective algorithms otherwise? To answer these questions, we use neural compilation , a technique that directly encodes a source algorithm into neural network parameters, enabling the network to compute the algorithm exactly. This enables comparison between compiled and conventionally learned parameters, intermediate vectors, and behaviors. This investigation is crucial for developing neural networks that robustly learn complex algorithms from data. Our analysis focuses on graph neural networks (GNNs), which are naturally aligned with algorithmic reasoning tasks, specifically our choices of BFS, DFS, and Bellman-Ford, which cover the spectrum of effective, faithful, and ineffective learned algorithms. Commonly, learning algorithmic reasoning is framed as induction over synthetic data, where a parameterized model is trained on inputs, traces, and outputs produced by an underlying ground truth algorithm. In contrast, we introduce a neural compilation method for GNNs, which sets network parameters analytically, bypassing training. Focusing on GNNs leverages their alignment with algorithmic reasoning, extensive algorithmic induction literature, and the novel application of neural compilation to GNNs. Overall, this paper aims to characterize expressability-trainability gaps – a fundamental shortcoming in learning algorithmic reasoning. We hypothesize that inductive learning is most effective for parallel algorithms contained within the computational class NC .

1.1
Expressivity-Trainability Gaps

Gaps between expressivity and trainability are a fundamental problem with neural networks.
For many architectures, the upper-bound expressivity is well-established, and techniques like neural compilation establish constructive proofs of high-performing optima
[
shaw2024alta
]
. Despite this, conventional neural network training fails to find these programs, even for relatively simple problems
[
shaw2024alta
,
velivckovic2022clrs
]
.
Fundamentally, this is an issue with optimization – gradient descent methods are not guaranteed to converge.
However, it is also an issue with representation, which clearly affects the optimization problem gradient descent attempts to solve. Even when an architecture is universally expressive, certain solutions are easier to express than others.
Because of the shortcomings of optimization and representation, there are gaps between the types of solutions that gradient descent finds and the solutions produced by neural compilation.
Compiled solutions are sparse, and can easily represent sequential algorithms.
In contrast, learned solutions are often dense
[
zhong2023pizza_clock
]
, and as this paper will show, it is easier to learn shallow parallel algorithms than it is to learn deep sequential ones.

Effectiveness and Faithfulness

An Expressivity-Trainability Gap occurs when there is a difference between a learned and ground-truth algorithm, either in effectiveness or faithfulness.

Faithfulness can be more than a binary question of if a learned algorithm matches a perfect ground-truth version.
A stronger version involves proposing alternative, simpler but imperfect programs that are consistent with the behavior of the learned model.
For example, ALTA
[
shaw2024alta
]
proposes multiple parity programs, such as sequential-relative and sum-and-modulo, and shows that learned transformer behavior is consistent with sum-and-modulo even though sequential-relative is the perfect and length-general solution. Importantly, sum-and-modulo is in
NC
, while sequential-relative is in
IS
.

Neural Compilation

Neural Compilation is a technique for transforming conventional computer programs into neural network parameters that compute the input algorithm. Fundamentally, neural compilation constructs an injective function that maps program space to parameter space:

\mathcal{C}:\Gamma\mapsto\Theta
so that the behaviors of
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
f(\theta),\theta\in\Theta
are consistent on all inputs. Effectively, compiled programs act as constructive expressivity proofs for particular algorithms and architectures.
The earliest results in neural compilation stem from
[
siegelmann1992computational
]
and
[
gruau1995neural
]
.
Decades later,
[
bunel2016adaptive
]
developed
adaptive
neural compilation, for initializing networks with compiled solutions and then further training them.
After the invention of the transformer architecture, there became significant interest in characterizing its internal mechanisms through circuits and programs.
From this came RASP, TRACR, and ALTA
[
Weiss2021ThinkingTransformers
,
lindner2024tracr
,
shaw2024alta
]
, which compile a domain-specific language into transformer parameters.
Our work is the first to propose and evaluate direct neural compilation for graph neural networks.

Graph Neural Networks and Architecture Parallelism

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been used to model learning algorithmic reasoning.
There is strong evidence that graph neural networks have
algorithmic alignment
, e.g. that they are more trainable than their counterparts when applied to appropriate tasks
[
xu2019algorithmic_alignment
]
.
Many of the most important algorithms in computer science can be expressed as graph-based algorithms. The CLRS30 benchmark dataset contains the most common and important algorithms from an introductory computer science curriculum
[
velivckovic2022clrs
]
.

Within GNNs, we primarily focus on graph attention networks (GATs)
[
velivckovic2017graph
,
brody2021attentive
]
.
Fundamentally, graph attention networks update every node representation at once.
For a graph attention network to represent a sequential algorithm, it must leave the majority of memory unaltered at each timestep.
While we do not formally analyze the computational model of graph attention networks in this work, their computation is much more consistent with
NC
than
IS
(See
[
li2022expressive_graph
,
xu2019algorithmic_alignment
]
for formal results).

Architecture Parallelism: Transformers

The success of the transformer architecture is largely attributable to its wide, parallel, and shallow nature, which mitigates the issues with long and unstable gradient paths that were common in RNN-based models
[
vaswani2017attention
]
.
In a single pass, a transformer model can at best express algorithms within
TC-0

[
merrill2022saturated
]
, but when iterated, transformers can, in theory, express universal computation
[
giannou2023looped
,
Strobl2023TransformersExpressivity
]
.
Despite this, transformers still experience wide expressivity-trainability gaps, and still tend towards learning parallel and shallow algorithms
[
shaw2024alta
]
.

There is a precise mathematical relationship between graph attention networks and transformers: The attention mechanism of a GAT is filtered by graph topology, while a transformer operates on a fully-connected graph. See Equation
. A casually-masked transformer can be seen as a graph attention network with an adjacency matrix where the upper-triangular portion is all ones.

Differentiable Computing

Previous work has considered differentiable models of computation, such as LSTMS or other RNNs
[
hochreiter1997long
]
.
This was expanded by Neural Turing Machines and Hybrid Differentiable Computers
[
graves2014neural
,
graves2016hybrid
]
.
However, sequential models of computation are often exceptionally difficult to train, which was a big factor in the invention of transformers
[
pascanu2013difficulty
,
vaswani2017attention
,
Kaiser2016
]
.
We view the history of differentiable computing as further evidence supporting the
NC
-Learnability hypothesis.

The Scalar Bottleneck Hypothesis

To the best of our knowledge, direct neural compilation has never been considered for graph attention networks. The closest work creates differentiable “black-box” algorithm implementations that closely resemble graph attention networks
[
ong2024parallelising
]
.
Critically, this explores the
scalar bottleneck hypothesis
– which states that to faithfully model algorithmic behavior, models must convert dense latent states into individual scalars.
To mitigate this
[
ong2024parallelising
]
proposes running a parallel ensemble of algorithms.
The scalar bottleneck appears in neurally compiled algorithms, which often produce sparse weights.
However, it is possible to represent compiled algorithms using denser states, but this possibility has simply not been directly considered in previous neural compilation work.
The scalar bottleneck hypothesis offers another explanation of expressivity-trainability gaps.
However, it does not fully explain why algorithms like BFS are easier to learn than DFS. Partially, it may be that state-tracking in DFS is more taxing at a scalar representation level, because the model must learn to represent a global stack.

Algorithmic Phase Space

A large body of work has aimed to characterize the internal mechanisms learned by neural networks, in an effort to understand them and improve them
[
rai2024practical
,
nanda2023progress
]
.
For example
[
zhong2023pizza_clock
]
proposes the idea of
algorithmic phase space
, and classifies solutions within this space as “pizza” and “clock” algorithms, but primarily argues for surprising diversity of potential learned algorithms.
In view of this, we note that neurally compiled solutions represent a small part of algorithmic phase space, and a more informed neural compilation strategy would aim to characterize algorithmic phase space in terms of compiled solutions.
Characterizing the difference between compiled and learned algorithms in phase space is an important factor in understanding the expressivity-trainability gap.

Background: Graph Attention Networks

Graph neural networks work by iteratively refining vector representations at each node in a graph by exchanging information between nodes according to the graph topology. For a graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
nodes, the model receives input vectors
v_{i}
of dimension
f
for each node, and edge information
\mathcal{E}
, which is an
n\times n\times f
tensor, where
f
is a feature dimension.
We define the graph topology with an adjacency matrix
A
, which is an
n\times n
matrix with binary entries. Also, the graph contains metadata in a vector
g
, which is also dimension
f
.

At each time step
t
, the model produces new hidden representations
h_{i}
for each node, which become inputs to the next step.
