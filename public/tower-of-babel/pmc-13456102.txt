Interactions between sulfonamide antibiotics and metal ions: does toxicity increase or decrease?
Authors: Fangxi Cui, Shengkai Cao, Yuanrong Zhu, Huixian Li, Guanghui Guo, Juan Jiang, Xiaojie Bing, Linke Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the increasing occurrence of multiple pollutants in aquatic environments, interactions among coexisting contaminants and their combined environmental effects have attracted considerable attention. Among these interactions, the coexistence of antibiotics and metal ions is widespread; however, the effects of antibiotic–metal interactions on photochemical behavior and biological toxicity remain insufficiently understood. In this study, two representative sulfonamide antibiotics, sulfadiazine (SDZ) and sulfamethoxazole (SMX), together with three environmentally relevant metal ions (Fe(iii), Al(iii), and Cu(ii)), were selected to investigate changes in spectroscopic characteristics, physicochemical properties, and toxicity following metal binding. Toxicity was evaluated using the Vibrio fischeri bioluminescence inhibition assay. FTIR analysis suggested that the pyrimidine nitrogen atom of SDZ and the sulfonamide nitrogen and sulfonyl oxygen atoms of SMX may participate in metal binding. UV-vis spectra showed pH-dependent red or blue shifts and changes in absorption intensity, indicating that metal interaction altered the electronic environment of sulfonamides. SDZ showed a greater tendency to interact with metal ions under acidic conditions, whereas SMX favored interaction under neutral conditions. Among the investigated metal ions, the apparent interaction strength with sulfonamides followed the order Fe(iii) > Cu(ii) > Al(iii). Metal binding was associated with enhanced fluorescence intensity, which may be attributed to increased molecular rigidity and reduced non-radiative energy dissipation. Toxicity assays revealed that the toxicity of sulfonamides generally decreased after interaction with metal ions. Especially, when the intrinsic toxicity of a metal ion exceeded that of the corresponding antibiotic, the observed toxicity of the equilibrated metal–antibiotic system was lower than the sum of the individual toxicities. Overall, interactions between sulfonamide antibiotics and metal ions significantly influence their spectroscopic behavior and toxicity, with important implications for contaminant fate, bioavailability, environmental risk assessment, and remediation strategies in aquatic environments.

Introduction

The extensive use and misuse of antibiotics have raised growing environmental concerns worldwide.1 Due to limited metabolic efficiency, more than 90% of administered antibiotics may be excreted into the environment in unmetabolized form,2 thereby contributing to the dissemination of antibiotic resistance and associated ecological and human health risks. Among various antibiotic classes, sulfonamides (SAs) are one of the most frequently detected antibiotics in environmental matrices, including soils, water bodies, and sediments.3–7 Their usage in aquaculture ranks second only to tetracyclines,8 and they are also among the most widely produced and consumed antibiotics globally.9 SAs are frequently detected in various surface waters at relatively high concentrations,10 typically ranging from ng L−1 to µg L−1. (ref. 9) For example, SMX concentrations reached 91.2 ng L−1 in Lake Itasca within the Mississippi River Basin, USA,11 while SDZ and SMX were identified as dominant antibiotics in groundwater from the Hutuo River recharge area of the North China Plain, with average concentrations of 29 and 9.2 ng L−1, respectively.12 Substantial spatial variability in sulfonamide contamination has also been reported, with concentrations ranging from sub-ng L−1(0.86 ng L−1) levels to over 1500 ng L−1 in some aquatic environments, such as Baiyangdian Lake, China.13 Due to their high occurrence frequency and relatively elevated environmental concentrations, sulfonamides are considered a dominant class of antibiotic contaminants in aquatic ecosystems.

However, in addition to freely dissolved antibiotics, aquatic environments also contain various coexisting substances, such as inorganic anions and cations, natural organic matter, and microplastics derived from human activities.1 These coexisting constituents can interact with antibiotics, thereby complicating their environmental transport, transformation, and toxic effects. Structurally, most antibiotics consist of a nonpolar core and multiple polar functional groups, such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino groups, which contain heteroatoms including O, N, and S. This structural feature enables antibiotics to act as ligands and coordinate with metal ions, forming antibiotic–metal complexes. In addition, antibiotics often contain aromatic rings, which confer aromaticity and form conjugated six-membered electron systems, making them susceptible to electrophilic substitution reactions with metal ions.1 Polar functional groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino, and amide groups also allow antibiotics to undergo protonation and deprotonation under different conditions such as pH, which further enhance their hydrophilicity and solubility in aquatic environments.1

Metal ions (e.g., Cu(ii), Zn(ii), Cd(ii), and Al(iii)) are widely present in various aquatic environments, primarily introduced through anthropogenic activities14 such as industrial and agricultural processes.1 For example, relatively high concentrations of Fe(iii), Al(iii), and Cu(ii) have been reported in Taihu Lake, reaching 0.3871, 0.0050, and 1.0195 mg L−1, respectively.15 Previous studies have demonstrated that the coexistence of metal ions and antibiotics in aquatic environments represents a typical combined pollution system. These metal ions tend to coordinate with antibiotics containing strong binding functional groups, such as carboxyl, amino, and phenolic hydroxyl groups, leading to the formation of metal-associated antibiotic species.16–19 The number and type of functional groups in antibiotic molecules can help infer the possible coordination mode of the resulting complexes, including monodentate, multidentate, and bridging ligands. Generally, such complexes preferentially form stable chelate structures with five- or six-membered rings.20,21 These coordination forms also have been widely detected in environmental waters, particularly in aquaculture systems and their adjacent areas.22,23 Compared with alkaline earth metals, transition metals tend to form more stable complexes with antibiotics.1 Given the widespread coexistence of sulfonamides and metal ions such as Fe(ii), Al(iii), and Cu(ii) in aquatic environments, significant interactions between these components are likely to occur. Major background ions (such as Ca(ii) and Na(i)) are abundant in natural aquatic systems and may influence antibiotic speciation, but they generally exhibit relatively low acute toxicity under environmentally relevant conditions. Since this study focused on pollution characteristics and toxicity changes before and after sulfonamide–metal interactions, Fe(iii), Cu(ii), and Al(iii) were selected as representative metal ions associated with anthropogenic inputs and higher toxicological relevance.24,25

Compared with freely dissolved antibiotics, metal-associated antibiotic species altered physicochemical properties, thereby influencing processes such as environmental transport and biodegradation.26,27 These species often show enhanced persistence, modified reactivity, and increased toxicity, resulting in more complex environmental behavior.28 In the presence of metal ions, the migration and transformation pathways of antibiotics-including oxidation,29 adsorption,30 and photodegradation22-can be significantly altered, which in turn affects their removal efficiency in wastewater and natural waters.1 Conversely, the removal of certain metal ions may also be influenced by their interaction with antibiotics.31 The formation of such metal-associated antibiotic species is governed by multiple factors, including the functional group composition of organic pollutants, the type of metal ion, the relative concentrations of organic matter and metal ions, and environmental conditions.31 To date, research on metal-associated antibiotic species has primarily focused on their environmental occurrence, removal technologies, environmental remediation, as well as applications in pharmaceutical design and antimicrobial materials. However, limited attention has been paid to the coordination mechanisms and binding sites of antibiotic–metal interactions.31 Moreover, the photochemical properties and toxicity variations of antibiotic–metal complexes remain poorly understood. For example, Federica Carnamucio32 determined the stability constant of the norfloxacin (NAL)-As(iii) complex using potentiometric titration and confirmed the formation of a stable bidentate ligand complex. However, this study mainly demonstrated the existence and coordination mechanism of the complex, without further exploring its physicochemical properties.

Therefore, it is hypothesized that interactions between metal ions and antibiotics may alter the original molecular environment of antibiotics, and then affecting their physicochemical properties, biological toxicity, and environmental behavior. Thus, in this study, representative sulfonamide antibiotics were selected to systematically investigate changes in spectroscopic characteristics, photochemical properties, and biological toxicity following interaction with environmentally relevant metal ions. The specific objectives of this study were: (i) to explore the potential metal-binding sites involved in antibiotic–metal interactions based on spectroscopic evidence; (ii) to evaluate changes in photochemical properties associated with metal binding; and (iii) to compare the interaction characteristics and structural responses of different sulfonamide antibiotics toward metal ions. On this basis, the study further aims to improve the understanding of how metal ion–antibiotic interactions influence contaminant fate, transformation processes, and ecological effects in aquatic environments.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Sulfadiazine (SDZ, purity > 98.0%) and sulfamethoxazole (SMX, purity > 98.0%) were purchased from Beijing Bailingwei Technology Co., Ltd. FeCl3·6H2O, CuCl2·2H2O, and AlCl3·2H2O were all of analytical grade and were used without further purification. Freeze-dried luminescent bacteria (Vibrio fischeri) were supplied by Zhejiang Tox Biotech Co., Ltd. Ultrapure water used in all experiments was produced using a purification system from Millipore SAS.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

Solid complex samples were prepared according to the method of Sciscenko et al. (2021),33 with slight modifications. Briefly, 40 µL of CuCl2 solution (2.5 mol L−1) was added to 50 mL of antibiotic solution (1.0 mmol L−1), followed by magnetic stirring for 10 h. The supernatant containing the interactions was subsequently frozen at −20 °C until solidification and then subjected to vacuum freeze-drying to obtain solid interaction samples. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the prepared complexes and antibiotic standards were recorded using a vacuum FTIR spectrometer (VECTOR-22, Bruker, Germany). Measurements were performed using the KBr pellet method, with potassium bromide used as the background.

UV-visible and fluorescence spectroscopic analysis

For the two sulfonamides (SDZ and SMX) and three metal ions (Fe(iii), Cu(ii), and Al(iii)), three types of solutions were prepared. The pH of the solutions were adjusted to 3.0, 7.0, and 11.0 using HCl or NaOH prior to UV-visible and fluorescence spectroscopic analyses.

The three solution types were defined as follows:

(i) SAs solution (10 µmol L−1) without metal ions;

(ii) SAs solution (10 µmol L−1) with metal ions (50 µmol L−1) added and measured immediately (denoted as M + SAs);

(iii) SAs solution (10 µmol L−1) with metal ions (50 µmol L−1), followed by magnetic stirring in the dark for 10 h and equilibration for 1 h to ensure promote interact formation (denoted as M-SAs in the spectra).

UV-visible absorption spectra of the above solutions were recorded using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (TU1909, Beijing Purkinje General Instrument Co., Ltd, China). Fluorescence spectra were measured using an integrated steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence spectrometer (FS5, Edinburgh Instruments, UK).

Luminescent bacteria toxicity assay

The luminescent bacteria toxicity assay was conducted according to the standard method ISO 11348-3-2007, with appropriate modifications to optimize the experimental conditions. Before the experiment, freeze-dried Vibrio fischeri was reactivated at 4 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of pre-cooled reconstitution solution (1% (m V−1) NaCl, 4 °C) was added to the freeze-dried bacteria to prepare a hydrated reagent. The mixture was thoroughly shaken and then activated at 15 °C for 15 min. The bacterial suspension was used within 3 h. NaCl solution (20 g L−1) and ZnSO4·7H2O solution (19.34 mg L−1) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively, to calibrate the luminescent bacteria toxicity analyzer. In the single acute toxicity test, 100 µL of bacterial suspension was added to 900 µL of each sample solutions. Pollutants concentrations were adjusted to ensure that the relative luminescence intensity ranged between 40% and 60%, allowing comparison with the combined toxicity groups. The biological toxicity of metal ions in aqueous solutions largely depends on their chemical speciation. For example, iron ions predominantly exist as Fe(iii) hydroxides under neutral conditions, which exhibit significantly lower toxicity than their ionic forms. To minimize the independent toxicity of metal ions and standardize exposure conditions, the pH of all samples was adjusted to 7.0 using HCl or NaOH before testing. Acute toxicity experiments were performed for 15 min under dark conditions using the luminescent bacteria toxicity testing mode in a HACH Eclox rapid water quality analyzer (Hach, USA), with blank controls and parallel experiments included. Samples toxicity was expressed as the average relative inhibition rate (I) of luminescence, as shown in eqn (1).1In eqn (1), I0 represents the luminescence intensity at 0 min, measured immediately after the addition of the bacterial suspension and thorough mixing, and is expressed as relative luminescence intensity. I15 represents the luminescence intensity after 15 min of incubation. All toxicity measurements were performed in triplicate. For combined toxicity tests, mixed solutions were prepared while maintaining constant concentrations of each component, followed by the same experimental procedure described above.

Results

FTIR spectral characteristics of sulfonamides before and after interaction with Cu(ii)

The FTIR spectra of the two sulfonamide antibiotics, SMX and SDZ, exhibited several similarities (Fig. 1). The FTIR spectrum of SDZ showed characteristic bands at approximately 1320–1300 cm−1 and 1160–1130 cm−1, which were assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the sulfonyl group, respectively. After incorporation with Cu(ii), these bands showed noticeable shifts and intensity changes, suggesting possible involvement of sulfonyl oxygen atoms in metal binding. In addition, variations in the C–N stretching region around 1250 cm−1 indicated that pyrimidine nitrogen may also participate in the interaction with Cu(ii) (Fig. 1a). For SMX (Fig. 1b), characteristic absorption bands observed in the regions of approximately 1330–1300 cm−1 and 1160–1140 cm−1 were assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the sulfonyl group, suggesting possible involvement of sulfonyl oxygen atoms in metal binding. Furthermore, the medium-intensity doublet located at 3500–3300 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of the amino group (–NH2), showed a clear red shift in the spectrum of the SMX–Cu sample, indicating that amino nitrogen may also participate in the interaction with Cu(ii). Nevertheless, FTIR evidence alone is insufficient to definitively determine the binding sites and interaction mode of the resulting species.

Comparison of FTIR spectra of sulfonamide antibiotics (SAs) before and after interaction with Cu(ii): (a) SDZ and (b) SMX.

Differences in UV-visible absorption properties of sulfonamides and their metal-associated species

Considering that the dissociation states of sulfonamides (SAs) vary under different pH conditions, the UV-visible spectra of SDZ and SMX were first analyzed at three pH (3.0, 7.0, and 11.0), as shown in Fig. 2. SAs possess relatively large conjugated structures and multiple chromophoric groups, resulting in strong UV absorption. In aqueous solutions, their characteristic absorption bands are mainly located in the range of 225–275 nm, which are primarily attributed to π → π* electronic transitions of the aromatic rings. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the UV-visible spectra of SDZ showed noticeable variations under different pH conditions. At pH 7.0, the maximum absorption peak of SDZ was observed at 270 nm. Under alkaline conditions (pH 11.0), the maximum absorption peak showed a blue shift, whereas under acidic conditions (pH 3.0), a red shift was observed. In addition, the absorbance intensity of SDZ increased under both acidic and alkaline conditions, with a more pronounced enhancement under alkaline conditions. In contrast, SMX exhibited different spectral behavior under varying pH conditions. Compared with pH 7.0, the maximum absorption peak of SMX showed a red shift accompanied by decreased absorbance intensity at both pH 3.0 and 11.0, indicating more significant spectral changes.

Comparison of UV-visible absorption spectra of sulfonamide antibiotics (SAs) under different pH conditions.

A systematic comparison was further conducted on the UV-visible spectra of SAs, metal ion solutions, and their mixtures before and after interaction under three pH conditions (Fig. 3). Significant differences in the absorption spectra were observed before and after equilibration, suggesting the occurrence of interactions between metal ions and sulfonamide antibiotics. These interactions were accompanied by changes in the electronic environment of the molecules, resulting in altered spectroscopic characteristics. For SDZ, under acidic conditions (pH 3.0), the interaction with the three metal ions resulted in a pronounced hyperchromic effect accompanied by a slight red shift in the absorption peaks. This spectral behavior is consistent with a metal binding process between SDZ and the metal ions, which may enhance conjugation and lead to increased absorbance and a red shift. At neutral pH (pH 7.0), the maximum absorption peaks of SDZ exhibited blue shifts after interaction with all three metal ions. This maybe due to the formation of putative coordination species with increased molecular rigidity arising from electron pair sharing, which enhances structural planarity and results in a blue shift. In addition, the secondary absorption peak increased in intensity after interaction with Fe(iii), whereas it decreased after interaction with Cu(ii) and Al(iii). Under alkaline conditions (pH 11.0), the maximum absorption intensity increased after interaction with Fe(iii), while both the primary and secondary absorption peaks decreased in intensity after interaction with Cu(ii) and Al(iii), with no obvious red or blue shifts. This may be attributed to relatively weak interactions between SDZ and these metal ions or to minimal changes in the electronic distribution of the system after interaction with metals. Notably, the secondary absorption peak disappeared after interaction with Cu(ii), whereas both the primary and secondary peaks showed significant hyperchromic effects in the presence of Fe(iii). Based on the extent of spectral changes, Fe(iii) appeared to exhibit a stronger interaction with SDZ than Cu(ii) and Al(iii). The observed order of apparent interaction strength, Fe(iii) > Cu(ii) > Al(iii), maybe closely related to differences in the interaction characteristics and their electron-accepting capacity of these metal ions. Although Fe(iii) and Al(iii) are theoretically expected to form hydroxide species under neutral and alkaline conditions, no precipitation was observed in this study. This result is consistent with previous findings reported for tetracycline systems.34

UV-visible absorption spectra of SDZ before and after interaction with different metal ions at various pH conditions (pH 3.0, 7.0, and 11.0).

The changes observed in the UV-visible spectra (Fig. 4) further confirmed the formation of SMX–metal interaction product. Compared with SDZ, the spectral variations of SMX were more pronounced under neutral conditions (pH 7.0), suggesting that SMX is more prone to interaction at neutral pH. Consistent with the results for SDZ, the spectral changes of SMX before and after interaction with different metal ions indicated that Fe(iii) exhibits a stronger interaction with SMX than Cu(ii) and Al(iii). Under acidic conditions (pH 3.0), a significant hypochromic effect was observed after interaction between SMX and Fe(iii), while no obvious red or blue shift in the maximum absorption wavelength was detected. In contrast, no noticeable spectral changes were observed for the Cu(ii) and Al(iii) systems before and after interaction. At pH 7.0, the spectral changes for the Fe(iii) system were negligible, whereas a hypochromic effect was observed for both Cu(ii) and Al(iii) after interaction. Under alkaline conditions (pH 11.0), no significant spectral changes were observed for the Al(iii) system. However, peak intensity decreased in the Cu(ii) system, while the absorption peak disappeared entirely in the Fe(iii) system, indicating the absence of a distinct absorption feature.

UV-visible absorption spectra of SMX before and after interaction with different metal ions at various pH conditions (pH 3.0, 7.0, and 11.0).

Differences in fluorescence properties of sulfonamides and their metal-associated species

Under different pH conditions and at the same antibiotic concentration, sulfonamides (SAs) exhibited relatively weak fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5). However, the addition of metal ions (Cu(ii), Fe(iii), and Al(iii)) enhanced the fluorescence intensity of SAs, including both SDZ and SMX, under all pH conditions. This enhancement can be attributed to the formation of luminescent metal-associated species, in which SAs act as multidentate ligands that interact with metal ions such as Al(iii) and Cu(ii). The increased fluorescence intensity suggests that interaction leads to greater molecular rigidity and planarity. Moreover, the absence of fluorescence quenching indicates that the metal-binding sites are not directly involved in the intrinsic fluorescence centers of the antibiotics. For SDZ, the fluorescence intensity remained relatively weak before and after interaction under neutral and alkaline conditions, whereas under acidic conditions, fluorescence intensity increased significantly after interaction with all three metal ions. Among them, Cu(ii) induced a more pronounced enhancement than Fe(iii) and Al(iii), suggesting that the SDZ–Cu(ii) interaction product possesses a more planar structure and stronger luminescent properties. A similar trend was observed for SMX; however, fluorescence enhancement was more prominent in the SDZ–Cu(ii) system. In contrast, among the three metal ions, Al(iii) induced a more significant fluorescence enhancement in the SMX system than Fe(iii) and Cu(ii). This phenomenon may be attributed to charge delocalization within the interaction species framework during possible coordination, where the positive charge of Al(iii) and the negative charge of the SMX ligand are distributed over the entire structure.35,36 Such charge transfer can reduce the polarity of the metal center, stabilize the chelate system, and lower the excited-state energy, thereby enhancing fluorescence emission. In addition, previous studies have shown that trivalent metal ions generally exhibit higher complex stability constants than divalent metal ions.1 The smaller ionic radius and higher charge density of Al(iii) further contribute to the formation of more stable SMX–Al(iii) interaction products.

Fluorescence emission spectra of sulfonamides (SDZ and SMX) after interaction with three metal ions (Fe(iii), Cu(ii), and Al(iii)) under different pH conditions.

Analysis of individual and combined toxicity of sulfonamides and metal ions

Based on the luminescent bacteria assay using Vibrio fischeri, the toxicity of SAs showed noticeable changes before and after interaction (Fig. 6). For SDZ, the observed toxicity of the mixtures with the three metal ions exceeded the expected additive effect based on the individual components, suggesting a synergistic interaction. For SDZ, the combined toxicity in the presence of the three metal ions was greater than the sum of the individual toxicities, suggesting a synergistic effect between SDZ and the metal ions. For SMX, the combined toxicity with Fe(iii) and Al(iii) was also higher than the sum of their individual toxicities, suggesting synergistic interactions. In contrast, the combined toxicity of SMX with Cu(ii) was lower than the sum of their individual toxicities, indicating a potential antagonistic effect. Furthermore, the toxicity of the metal-associated species formed by SDZ and SMX was lower than the combined toxicity observed before interaction. This suggests that the toxicity of the antibiotic–metal interaction species is lower than that of either the free metal ions or antibiotics, and that the latter dominate the overall toxic effects in the mixed systems.

Inhibition of luminescence in Vibrio fischeri induced by sulfonamides (SDZ and SMX) and three metal ions (Fe(iii), Cu(ii), and Al(iii)) before and after interaction (data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3)).

Discussion

Possible coordination mechanism and structure of metal-associated sulfonamide species

Among the metal ions considered in this study, Cu(ii) can readily undergo single-electron redox reactions within the biologically relevant redox range, generating electron-accepting species. It is therefore regarded as the most effective divalent ion for binding with organic molecules.37 Accordingly, Cu(ii)- associated SAs species were selected as the model system for investigating metal-binding sites. Based on structural analysis and spectroscopic evidence, sulfonamide antibiotics may coordinate with metal ions mainly through donor atoms in the sulfonamide moiety (–SO2–NH–), including sulfonyl oxygen and sulfonamide nitrogen atoms, as well as heterocyclic nitrogen atoms in aromatic rings. These interactions may lead to relatively rigid coordination structures with localized binding sites. Because SAs contain multiple potential coordination sites, their actual coordination modes may vary depending on the type and coordination preference of the metal ion.38 Previous studies have reported electrostatic repulsion between the aniline amino group and Cu(ii), suggesting that the –NH2 group is unfavorable for coordination.39 Consistently, in this study, FTIR analysis did not show the disappearance of absorption peaks associated with the aniline amino group after SDZ and SMX interacted with Cu(ii) (Fig. 1), indicating that this group does not participate in coordination. Although the aniline amino group theoretically has coordination capability, it is unlikely to coordinate with Cu(ii) under the conditions investigated here. Furthermore, earlier studies have shown that under mildly alkaline conditions (pH 8–9), the asymmetric stretching vibration of the sulfonamide group (SO2–NH2) disappears upon coordination with metals such as cobalt and nickel acetate,38 indicating the involvement of nitrogen atoms in coordination. In agreement with these findings, the FTIR spectra in this study revealed that the characteristic absorption peak corresponding to the nitrogen atom in the pyrimidine ring of SDZ disappeared after interaction, while the absorption peak associated with the sulfonamide group of SMX also vanished. Based on these results, both the sulfonamide group and the attached pyrimidine ring are inferred to participate in the coordination of SDZ, leading to changes in its conjugated structure. In contrast, the coordination of SMX with metal ions mainly involves the sulfonamide group. Therefore, the primary coordination sites of sulfonamides may include the nitrogen atom in the pyrimidine ring, as well as the sulfonamide nitrogen and sulfonyl oxygen atoms. Previous studies have suggested that both SDZ and SMX complexes adopt an octahedral geometry, with a metal-to-ligand stoichiometric ratio (M : L) of 1 : 2.40,41 In addition, only approximately 1.55% of Cu(ii) exists in coordinated form relative to the total metal concentration.42 Although the absolute concentration of antibiotic–metal interactions in natural waters remains uncertain, the formation of such species, even at trace levels, may alter chemical speciation and thereby influence environmental behavior and risk assessment. For instance, assuming the coexistence of SDZ (10 ng L−1) and Cu(ii) (1 mg L−1), the concentration of the resulting complex is estimated to be approximately 4.29 × 10−7 mg L−1 in 1 L of water. Although present at trace levels, such complexes may exhibit distinct environmental risks because of differences in chemical speciation. Their potential synergistic toxicity and altered environmental behavior highlight the limitations of traditional single-pollutant risk assessment frameworks, and provide scientific support for more accurate evaluation of ecological risks in complex pollution systems.

The results of this study (Fig. 6) further demonstrate that both the magnitude and direction of spectral shifts, as well as toxicity variations before and after interaction, differ among antibiotic–metal interactions. Sulfonamides are polar molecules with relatively high water solubility and possess two primary acid–base dissociation sites: the nitrogen atom in the pyrimidine ring and the sulfonamide nitrogen.43 For instance, under acidic conditions (pH = 3.0), more pronounced changes were observed in the FTIR spectra of SDZ before and after interaction, indicating that coordination is more favorable under acidic conditions. This may be related to pH-dependent changes in protonation state, which alter electron density distribution and thus affect coordination behavior. Such electron-donating characteristics facilitate coordination with metal ions and promote complex formation. In contrast, SMX tends to interact more readily under neutral conditions. This is likely due to the weak acidity of the sulfonamide group (–SO2NH–), which can dissociate into –SO2N−– under neutral or slightly alkaline conditions. The resulting anionic form enhances its affinity toward metal cations (e.g., Cu(ii) and Fe(iii)), leading to the formation of more stable complexes.44 These findings indicate that pH plays a critical role in the coordination process, primarily by regulating the protonation state of antibiotics and the speciation of metal ions. Notably, no optimal coordination of sulfonamides was observed under strongly alkaline conditions, suggesting that metal ions preferentially form hydroxide precipitates with OH− at high pH, thereby inhibiting coordination. Similar phenomena have also been reported for tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics. Furthermore, differences in coordination sites between SDZ and SMX result in variations in complex structures, which ultimately lead to distinct trends in UV-visible absorption and fluorescence spectra. Although many metal ions, particularly paramagnetic Cu(ii), commonly induce fluorescence quenching in fluorescence-sensing systems, fluorescence enhancement was observed in the present Cu(ii)–sulfonamide systems. This suggests that chelation-induced structural rigidification and increased molecular planarity may dominate over the intrinsic quenching effect of Cu(ii) under the tested conditions. It is also noteworthy that SMX behaves as a relatively weak chelating agent toward Cu(ii), and the formation of SMX–Cu(ii) complexes is highly pH-dependent, with significant interaction occurring near neutral conditions (e.g., pH ≈ 6).44 Under ideal conditions, the stability of antibiotic–metal complexes is commonly described by the stability constant (K). Compared with tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides generally exhibit lower stability constants, typically in the range of 3–4. Empirically, complexes within this range are more susceptible to dissociation under varying environmental conditions, which is consistent with the findings of this study.

It should also be noted that the coordination interactions proposed in this study are inferred primarily from spectroscopic evidence. Although the results suggest the possible involvement of specific functional groups in metal binding, additional structural characterization techniques are required to unambiguously confirm the coordination mode and molecular structure of the resulting species further.

Differential toxicity of metal-associated sulfonamide antibiotics species

The interaction of metal ions can alter the physicochemical properties (e.g., octanol–water partition coefficient), migration and transformation processes (e.g., adsorption and degradation), and ecological effects (e.g., uptake and toxicity) of antibiotics.45,46 Based on comparative analysis of the luminescent bacteria toxicity results for the two sulfonamide antibiotics, the reduced toxicity observed after sulfonamide–metal interaction may be attributed to complexation-induced changes in chemical speciation and bioavailability. Free metal-ions are more toxic than their metallic complexes.47,48 Metal binding can decrease the freely dissolved fractions of both antibiotics and metal ions, thereby reducing their direct interaction with bacterial cells. In studies of inorganic antimicrobial agents, bacterial inactivation is generally attributed to the adsorption of metal ions onto the bacterial surface, followed by disruption of the cell membrane, penetration into the cell, and subsequent interactions with intracellular components, ultimately leading to cell death.49 When SMX and Cu(ii) coexist in solution, SMX may preferentially and competitively occupy specific binding sites on the bacterial surface. As a result, the concentration of free Cu(ii) ions capable of directly interacting with bacterial cells and exerting toxic effects is reduced, leading to lower overall toxicity of the mixed system. Previous studies have shown that, based on EC50 values derived from luminescent bacteria assays, Cu(ii) is more than 250 times more toxic than SMX.50 In addition, the present study found that the toxicity of metal-associated sulfonamide antibiotics species was lower than the combined toxicity observed before interaction. Previous research has also demonstrated that only approximately 1.55% of Cu(ii) exists in coordinated form relative to the total metal concentration.42 These findings indicate that the toxicity of Cu(ii) dominates in the mixed system. Cu(ii) concentration may be an important factor controlling the overall toxicity of antibiotic–metal mixtures in aquatic environments. Therefore, the decreased toxicity observed in the present sulfonamide–metal systems may be explained by reduced bioavailability of the active toxic species after interaction, although the magnitude of this effect depends on the antibiotic structure, metal type, pH, and stability of the interaction.

Comparative analysis of the individual and combined toxicities of sulfonamide antibiotics and metal ions revealed that the SMX–Cu(ii) system exhibited a distinct pattern compared with the other systems: the sum of the individual toxicities was greater than their combined toxicity in the mixed solution (assume for a condition that no interaction occur in the mixed systems). Specifically, in the SMX–Cu(ii) system, the expected additive toxicity based on individual components exceeded the observed toxicity of the mixture, suggesting an antagonistic interaction. In contrast, for the other antibiotic–metal combinations, the observed mixture toxicity exceeded the expected additive effect, indicating potential synergistic interactions. Further comparison of the individual toxicities of the two antibiotics and three metal ions revealed that the toxicity of sulfonamides generally exceeded that of the metal ions. However, an exception was observed in the SMX–Cu(ii) system, where the toxicity of Cu(ii) was higher than that of SMX. Based on these observations, a general pattern can be proposed for the toxicity variation of metal-associated sulfonamide antibiotics system: when the toxicity of an individual metal ion exceeds that of the corresponding antibiotic, the sum of the individual toxicities tends to be greater than the combined toxicity of the mixture. Notably, the toxicity results obtained in this study reflect the acute response of Vibrio fischeri under the tested conditions. Further validation using additional test organisms would be helpful for assessing the broader ecological relevance of these findings.

Compared with sulfonamides, tetracycline antibiotics, such as tetracycline (TC) and oxytetracycline (OTC), exhibit distinct toxicity patterns in the presence of metal ions. For TC, the combined toxicity with Fe(iii) and Cu(ii) was lower than the sum of the individual toxicities before interaction, indicating an antagonistic effect. In contrast, the combined toxicity of TC with Al(iii) exceeded the sum of the individual toxicities, suggesting a synergistic effect. For OTC, the combined toxicity with all three metal ions was greater than the sum of individual toxicities, indicating synergistic interactions. Except for the TC-Al(iii) system, the toxicity of species formed after interaction was generally higher than the combined toxicity before interaction in the other systems. This trend is opposite to that observed for sulfonamides and may be attributed to differences in possible coordination sites and structures between tetracyclines and sulfonamides. In tetracycline antibiotics, interaction typically occurs at oxygen-containing functional groups such as ketone and hydroxyl groups, leading to the formation of stable chelate ring structures with metal ions.51 These species are generally more stable than those formed by sulfonamides, as reflected by their higher stability constants (log K). For example, the log K values for Fe(iii), Al(iii), and Cu(ii) complexes with tetracyclines are reported to be as high as 13.4, 12.5, and 8.41,52–54 respectively, whereas those for sulfonamide–metal complexes are typically around 3. Previous studies have suggested that the stable molecular configuration of tetracycline complexes enhances their binding affinity to bacterial ribosomes.55 Moreover, interactions between antibiotics and specific metal ions may act as a redox-active center, inducing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).29 This process can synergize with the intrinsic antibacterial activity of antibiotics, leading to a significant enhancement in overall toxicity.

Environmental implications

Although information on the occurrence and concentrations of antibiotic–metal complexes in wastewater and sludge matrices remains limited, these complexes are generally recognized to exhibit physicochemical properties distinct from those of the parent antibiotics, particularly in terms of photodegradation, adsorption, bioavailability, and solubility (Fig. 7).56 Upon interaction between antibiotics and metal ions, the positive charge on the metal center and the negative charge on the ligand are delocalized throughout the coordination framework, resulting in reduced overall polarity of the chelate system. Many metal ions, particularly paramagnetic Cu(ii), commonly induce fluorescence quenching in fluorescence-sensing systems,25,57–59 fluorescence enhancement was observed in the present Cu(ii)–sulfonamide systems. This suggests that chelation-induced molecular rigidification and increased planarity may outweigh the paramagnetic fluorescence-quenching effect of Cu(ii) under the tested conditions. Therefore, the observed fluorescence enhancement may serve as indirect spectroscopic evidence of structural changes, which could further influence the environmental fate and ecological risks of metal-associated antibiotic species. Based on the principle of “like dissolves like”, antibiotic–metal interactions generally show low solubility, or even insolubility, in aqueous media.60 The decreased solubility affects the environmental behavior of these complexes, including their migration and sedimentation processes, making them more likely to accumulate in bottom sediments and reducing their exposure to sunlight, thereby lowering photodegradation efficiency. Photodegradation is one of the primary pathways for antibiotics removal from aquatic environments,61 occurring through both direct photolysis and indirect processes involving reactive intermediates that lead to bond cleavage. The UV-vis spectra revealed that, at pH 2–3, the pyrimidine-ring N atoms of sulfonamide antibiotics (SAs) were readily protonated, leading to the formation of cationic species. Under these acidic conditions, the absorption maxima exhibited a red shift accompanied by decreased absorbance, with the absorption band showing its lowest intensity. In contrast, over the pH range of 4.5–11,61 the sulfonamide N atoms were more susceptible to deprotonation, resulting in the formation of anionic species. Accordingly, the maximum absorption peaks at pH 7.0 and 11.0 showed varying degrees of blue shift. In this study, the altered photochemical properties of the interaction products were evidenced by spectral shifts observed in UV-visible spectra. A red shift after interaction suggests the introduction of additional photoactive sites, enabling more efficient utilization of the visible-light fraction of natural sunlight and potentially accelerating degradation, thereby reducing environmental persistence. In contrast, a blue shift indicates increased molecular stability after interaction, resulting in greater resistance to photodegradation. Consequently, such interaction products may persist longer in aquatic environments and pose an increased risk of long-range transport. These findings highlight the importance of considering secondary pollution arising from metal-associated antibiotic species in environmental risk assessment.

In conventional environmental monitoring and risk assessment frameworks, the effects of single pollutants are typically considered independently, assuming that their environmental behavior and toxicity follow a simple additive pattern. Under this assumption, the overall toxicity of pollutant mixtures is often evaluated as the sum of individual toxicities. However, the results of this study demonstrate that the toxicity of metal-associated sulfonamide antibiotic species differs from that of their mixtures, with the toxicity of the interaction products being lower than the combined toxicity of the corresponding antibiotic–metal systems before equilibration. Previous studies have often conflated the combined toxicity of antibiotic–metal mixtures with the toxicity of the corresponding complexes. In contrast, this study experimentally distinguishes between these two effects and provides a comparative analysis, thereby challenging the conventional assumption that interaction necessarily enhances toxicity. These findings suggest that antibiotic–metal complexes should be regarded as a distinct class of emerging contaminants. This perspective provides a scientific basis for re-evaluating the environmental behavior and ecological risks of combined pollution systems. Furthermore, the results offer new insights into water treatment strategies for systems contaminated with both sulfonamides and metal ions. For example, in photo–Fenton processes used to treat such complex pollution systems,62 free iron ions may preferentially coordinate with antibiotics, forming complexes that inhibit the overall efficiency of the process. In addition, complex formation can influence the generation and distribution of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), singlet oxygen (1O2), and superoxide radicals (˙O2−), thereby altering degradation pathways and efficiencies during photochemical reactions.63

In addition, antibiotic–metal interactions may exert significant impacts on microbial communities, including bacteria and fungi. Previous studies have suggested that electron delocalization within such complexes can enhance their lipophilicity, thereby promoting stronger interactions with bacterial cell membranes. The formation of antibiotic–metal complexes may also facilitate the selection and proliferation of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes, posing potential risks to ecological and human health.64–66 Overall, this study provides fundamental insights into changes in photochemical properties and toxicity of sulfonamide antibiotics upon possible coordination with representative metal ions. These findings have important implications for future applications in photocatalytic degradation technologies and for controlling by-product formation during water treatment processes.

Conclusion

In this study, two representative sulfonamide antibiotics and three metal ions were selected to systematically investigate the changes in chemical structure, physicochemical properties, and biological toxicity before and after interaction under different pH conditions. The results confirmed that possible coordination interactions occur between sulfonamides and metal ions. The metal-binding site of SDZ was identified as the nitrogen atom of the pyrimidine ring, whereas for SMX, the interaction primarily involved the sulfonamide nitrogen and sulfonyl oxygen atoms. The addition of metal ions significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity, suggesting the formation of luminescent chelate complexes that increase molecular rigidity and planarity. SDZ exhibited a greater tendency to interact under acidic conditions, whereas SMX favored the formation of metal-associated species under neutral conditions. The apparent coordination ability of the three metal ions toward sulfonamides followed the order: Fe(iii) > Cu(ii) > Al(iii). After interaction, the toxicity of sulfonamides in combination with metal ions was generally reduced compared with that of the pre-interaction mixtures. Except for the SMX–Cu(ii) system, the observed toxicity of the mixtures exceeded the expected additive toxicity based on the individual components, suggesting more-than-additive toxic effects. A general pattern can be proposed for toxicity variation in sulfonamide–metal interaction systems: when the toxicity of an individual metal ion exceeds that of the corresponding antibiotic, the sum of individual toxicities tends to be greater than the combined toxicity.

Currently, antibiotics and metal ions are widely used worldwide and are frequently detected in aquatic environments, leading to increasingly serious concerns regarding combined pollution. However, studies on the environmental behavior of metal-associated antibiotics species, including their migration, transformation, toxicity, and ecological effects, remain limited. In particular, key aspects such as the influence of pH and concentration on antibiotic–metal ratios, coordination configurations, and the proportion of putative complexes among total dissolved species are still poorly understood. In this study, experiments were designed to investigate the characteristic spectral properties and biological toxicity before and after interaction with metal ions. The observed blue and red shifts in UV-visible absorption spectra indicate that interaction significantly influences the photodegradation and transformation behavior of antibiotics in aquatic environments. In addition, the formation of metal-associated species may also affect microbial communities, including bacteria and fungi. These findings help address existing knowledge gaps in the photochemical properties and biological toxicity of sulfonamide antibiotics. Future studies should extend laboratory-scale experiments to real aquatic environments and systematically evaluate how natural organic matter, coexisting pollutants, and abundant background ions affect antibiotic–metal interactions under environmentally relevant conditions. Long-term monitoring and tracking studies are also needed to better understand the environmental fate of these putative complexes. Such efforts will contribute to the development of a more comprehensive theoretical framework for evaluating the interactions, toxicity, and ecological risks of metal-associated antibiotics species in aquatic systems.
