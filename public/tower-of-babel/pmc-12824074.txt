Developing and validating a nomogram of suicide risk in lung cancer patients based on the SEER database
Authors: Wenhui Li, Hao Lu, Heyuan Tang, Chenze Ma, Ziyu Wei, Rui Jian, Hongyang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study sought to determine independent predictors of suicide among patients with lung cancer and to establish a dynamic nomogram to predict suicide risk, with the ultimate goal of enabling early recognition and prevention in high-risk populations.MethodsWe analyzed 188,147 lung cancer cases from the most recent Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2004–2015) to identify predictors of suicide risk and develop a predictive model. The cohort was randomized into a training and validation group. Validation consisted of assessing the consistency index (C-index), subject-operating characteristic curves (ROC), and calibration curves.ResultsA total of 188,147 eligible lung cancer patients were enrolled and randomized into the training and validation groups. Multifactorial Cox regression analysis showed that age, race, sex, grade, and marital were independent predictors of suicide in lung cancer patients. The accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated by C-index and ROC curves, which showed acceptable performance on both training and validation sets. In addition, calibration plots showed that nomograms containing all these factors showed strong predictive accuracy.ConclusionThis study identified key risk factors for suicide in lung cancer patients and developed a dynamic nomogram that provided individualized risk predictions based on multifactorial clinical and pathological parameters. The model offered a superior alternative to traditional static risk scales, enabling early identification of high-risk individuals to support timely prevention and intervention. Furthermore, it has been implemented as a web-based visual tool, allowing convenient, real-time, and personalized suicide risk assessment. By facilitating early detection and intervention, this approach can help clinicians improve decision-making efficiency and provide more precise, patient-centered care, ultimately contributing to better psychological outcomes and quality of life for lung cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04249-6.Keywords: Lung cancer, Suicide risk, Diagnosis, SEER, Nomogram

Introduction

Suicide has developed into a serious global public health crisis and has become a major mortality factor for cancer patients, leading not only to the tragic end of an individual’s life, but also to far-reaching consequences for families and society [1, 2]. Cancer patients have a higher risk of suicide, and some studies have shown that the suicide mortality rate of cancer survivors is at least two to four times higher than that of the general population [3].

Lung cancer is one of the malignant tumors with the highest morbidity and mortality rates worldwide, early screening for lung cancer is necessary [4, 5]. According to research reports, non-tumor related deaths account for 10% to 35% of all causes of lung cancer deaths based on different diagnostic years and tumor stages, and the proportion of non-tumor related deaths has steadily increased in recent years [6, 7]. Considering the global suicide rate, 800,000 people die each year [8, 9]. Studies have shown that the incidence of suicidal ideation in lung cancer patients is high and is associated with a variety of factors, including physiological (e.g., high symptom burden) [10, 11], psychological (e.g., poor state of mind, frustration with belonging, self-exhaustion, and stigma) [12], and social (e.g., high economic stress, negative life events) [13], which together make cancer patients a high-risk group for suicidal risk [14].

The psychological state of patients plays a crucial role in cancer treatment and rehabilitation, influencing therapeutic outcomes and prognosis, while also representing an often overlooked burden [15–17]. Suicide is the culmination of uncontrollable suffering; due to the differences in the factors influencing suicidal ideation in oncology patients compared to the rest of the population, and the lack of a comprehensive evaluation system in clinical decision-making to predict the risk of suicide in oncology patients. This lack makes it difficult for healthcare professionals to identify and intervene with high-risk patients in a timely manner, thus missing the best opportunity to prevent suicidal behavior [18].

Previous studies have provided valuable insights into the risk factors of suicide among lung cancer patients. For instance, Ma et al. (2021) and Urban et al. (2018) analyzed SEER-based data to identify demographic and clinical determinants of suicide risk in this population [19, 20]. However, these studies primarily focused on descriptive or static analyses and lacked individualized, real-time predictive tools that could be applied in clinical settings. Moreover, few investigations have incorporated user-friendly platforms, such as web-based calculators or nomograms, to facilitate the translation of predictive findings into practical clinical decision-making [21, 22]. Consequently, despite growing awareness of psychological distress and suicide risk in oncology, clinicians still lack accessible instruments for timely identification and intervention.

To address the above limitations, we developed a dynamic nomogram that provides individualized suicide risk probabilities for lung cancer patients based on multifactorial clinical and pathological parameters. This model offers a superior alternative to traditional static risk scales, enabling early identification of high-risk individuals to support timely prevention and intervention. Furthermore, we implemented the model as a web-based visual tool, allowing convenient, real-time, and personalized suicide risk assessment to assist clinicians in improving decision-making efficiency and providing more precise, patient-centered care.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We retrieved data from the SEER database on 582,441 initial cases of lung cancer, with the primary study endpoint of “suicide and self-inflicted injury” deaths. The inclusion criteria are as follows: Patients diagnosed with lung cancer from 2004 to 2015. According to the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, we searched for lung cancer patients using morphology codes 8012/3, 8046/3, 8070/3, 8140/3, 8240/3, 8250/3, 8560/3, 8041/3, 8042/3, 8043/3, 8044/3, and 8045/3 and site codes C33.9-C34.9. Exclusion criteria: Individuals lacking clinical pathological features. Figure 1 details the specific criteria and data selection process. Data extracted from each patient included age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, marital status, AJCC 6th edition T, N, and M staging, histologic type, surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, vital signs, months of survival, and cause of death.

Flowchart for lung cancer patient selection and study design

The Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to identify independent risk factors associated with suicide risk. Although suicide occurrence was analyzed as a binary event, the Cox model was considered appropriate because the SEER database provides follow-up time information, enabling estimation of hazard ratios that account for both the occurrence and timing of events during the observation period. This approach has been widely applied in previous SEER-based studies investigating suicide risk among cancer patients. Deaths from other causes were classified as non-suicide events. The final study group consisted of 188,147 lung cancer-diagnosed cases. To develop and validate the nomogram, we randomly assigned 56,444 patients to the validation group and 131,703 patients to the training group from the SEER database.

Ethical statement

This study utilized data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program of the National Cancer Institute. The SEER data are de-identified and publicly accessible, and their use strictly adheres to the SEER Research Data Use Agreement (https://seer.cancer.gov/data/access.html). Therefore, this study was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval and patient informed consent.

Statistical analysis

SEER*Stat version 8.3.9.0 software was used to extract patient data. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using SPSS version 23.0 and R software (version 4.3.3). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables, as it has been shown to provide superior power for detecting non-normality in medical datasets with moderate sample sizes [23]. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Patients were randomly assigned to the training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. This proportion was selected following common practice in clinical prediction modeling, as it provides a balance between robust model development and reliable internal validation, ensuring representativeness and reducing potential sampling bias. Cumulative survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Variables with significance in univariate analyses (P < 0.05) were entered into multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors and calculate odds ratios (ORs).

A nomogram was developed based on significant predictors from the multivariate model. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and calibration plots with 1,000 bootstrap resamples to reduce overfitting bias and assess internal validity [24, 25]. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) reflected model discrimination, and calibration curves compared predicted versus observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves were applied to evaluate the model’s net clinical benefit. A dynamic nomogram was constructed using the R package “DynNom.” A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristic

This study analyzed 188,147 lung cancer patients (training cohort: 131,703; validation cohort: 56,444 patients) from a database between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2015, who were eligible for inclusion. The study population included male (52.0%, n = 97851) and female (48.0%, n = 90296), with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:1, and more likely to be 60–79 years old (123,494, 65.6%), white (157,364, 83.6%), and married (108,070, 57.4%). Consistent with the study [26], lung cancer was highly prevalent in the age group of 60–80 years.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of the training and validation cohorts. As mentioned in Statistical analysis section, continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared with independent-samples t-tests, while non-normally distributed variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Survival outcomes were compared using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test. In terms of demographic and clinical characteristics, there was no significant difference between the training and validation cohorts (all P > 0.05). To examine suicide-related characteristics, we divided the cohort into a suicide group (264 individuals) and a non-suicide group (187,883 individuals), with an overall median survival of 46.8 ± 50.0 months in the non-suicide group and 39.7 ± 42.7 months in the suicide group. Prognostic factor analysis

The analysis of prognostic factors for the training cohort is detailed in Table 2, and univariate analyses showed that the risk of suicide was associated with age, race, sex, SEER historic stage, grade, tumor size, T, M1, node dissection, marital, surgery, and household income. Multivariable analyses identified as 80–99 year olds HR = 2.30 (1.30–4.05), White HR = 3.55 (1.56–8.08), Males HR = 5.69 (3.79–8.54), Grade II HR = 2.01 (1.09–3.73), Grade III HR = 1.99 (1.07–3.71), Divorced HR = 2.09 (1.36–3.22), and Single HR = 2.62 (1.77–3.87) as significant impact factors. A nomogram was constructed based on these factors (Fig. 2), and by summing the scores of the selected predictors, the total risk score for each patient could be determined. The Kaplan-Meier curves for the relevant variables are shown in Fig. 6.

Development and validations of predicting nomograms

To assess the accuracy of predicting suicide risk, the C-index and ROC curves were compared. The C-index for the training set and validation set nomogram survival month were 0.766 and 0.725, respectively. The nomogram ROC curves for the training and validation sets were estimated to have an acceptable accuracy, with the training set nomogram ROC curves having an AUC of 0.796 for 1 year, an AUC of 0.825 for 2 years, and an AUC of 0.778 for 3 years, (Fig. 3A - C). The validation set nomogram ROC curves have an AUC of 0.733 for 1 year, 0.743 for 3 years and 0.75 for 5 years (Fig. 3D - F). The calibration plots in Fig. 4 depict the probability of suicide for the training and validation sets at 1, 3 and 5 years. Overall, the developed model exhibits relatively accurate predictive performance.

Discussion

The suicide of cancer survivors is an important public health issue. Among various types of tumors, the suicide rate of lung cancer patients after diagnosis is significantly higher than that of the general population, and shows an increasing trend year by year [14]. Research shows that most suicide victims have had contact with the healthcare system before taking action. A recent systematic review reported that up to 80% of suicide victims had contact with primary healthcare providers within the year preceding death, and 44% had such contact within the final month [27]. This suggests that clinical doctors play a crucial role in identifying and intervening in high-risk suicide populations. However, there is currently a lack of clinical tools or predictive models that can accurately assess the suicide risk of lung cancer patients [28, 29].

To fill this gap, this study constructed a column chart model for predicting suicide risk based on the SEER database and clinical pathological parameters of lung cancer patients. Although previous studies have developed predictive tools based on 112,372 lung cancer patients and identified predictive factors such as gender, race, marital status, tumor grade, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [30]. This study included a larger sample diagnosed between 2004 and 2015, identified different independent prognostic factors, and further established a dynamic predictive model suitable for clinical survival analysis based on Kaplan-Meier marginal regression and Cox proportional hazards model.

Beyond differing in predictor selection, our model introduces a significant methodological advance as the first dynamic nomogram developed for suicide risk prediction in lung cancer patients. Unlike static models, which provide fixed estimates, our tool allows for real-time, updated risk assessment, enhancing its clinical utility in evolving patient contexts.

In the final multivariate model, age, tumor grade, marital status, race, and gender became independent prognostic factors for suicide risk in lung cancer patients, while radiotherapy did not retain independent predictive value. These findings are consistent with previous studies that also emphasize the role of demographic and clinical factors in suicide prediction [31], strengthening the importance of incorporating psychosocial and structural variables into risk stratification. Since the Cox regression model included nineteen predictors, the calculated events-per-variable (EPV) was 13.9, exceeding the recommended minimum threshold of 10 and demonstrating satisfactory model stability. Furthermore, internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples confirmed the model’s calibration and discrimination performance, suggesting minimal overfitting and reliable predictive capacity.

Among lung cancer patients, advanced age is an important factor in the higher suicide rate, reflecting a broader trend in the cancer population [32]. Physical weakness caused by lung cancer treatments, including surgery and chemotherapy, can lead to impairment of lung function and increased risk of psychological distress and suicide (Fig. 6A), especially among older patients, who have poor adherence to treatment [33]. Consistent with previous findings, adherence declines with age (p = 0.001), as older patients are less likely than younger patients to receive surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and combination therapy [34]. These factors negatively affect quality of life and treatment outcomes, making older patients particularly vulnerable.

We also identified several demographic predictors associated with suicide risk in lung cancer patients, and our findings are generally consistent with the literature. We identified male gender as an independent predictor of suicide death in lung cancer patients, which is consistent with previous reports of suicides in lung cancer patients and in other cancer patients [35, 36]. Meanwhile, male lung cancer patients showed a higher risk of suicide on the survival curve (Fig. 6C). Consistent with previous reports, marital status was associated with suicide deaths in lung cancer patients (Fig. 6E). Previous studies have found that single, divorced lung cancer patients have a higher risk of suicide compared to married patients [37]. In addition, there is an effect of race on the presence of suicide risk in cancer survivors. Some studies show higher suicide risk for whites among bladder cancer patients [38, 39].

Consistent with our findings, our study found that race increases the likelihood of suicide in lung cancer, showing that the survival curve for whites was lower than that of other races (Fig. 6B); grade was also a risk factor for suicide in lung cancer patients (Fig. 6D), and the higher the grade of the tumor, the worse the prognosis, the psychological burden, and too much worry about the prognosis [40]. The analysis in this study did not include variables such as smoking status and depression status, mainly due to the structural limitations of the SEER database: the database did not regularly collect patients’ smoking history and mental health status (such as depression). Regarding the impact of tumor TNM staging on suicide, studies have shown that the higher the stage of lung cancer, the higher the incidence of suicidal ideation [41, 42].However, due to the long-term and unpredictable nature of the disease, there has been a significant increase in suicide cases among patients with advanced lung cancer [43]. Patients with advanced lung cancer suffer greater pain due to physical symptoms and inability to take care of themselves and control their daily lives. Although these factors are theoretically associated with suicide risk, this study constructed a predictive model with good discriminative ability within the existing data framework by incorporating alternative indicators such as SEER staging, tumor grading, and demographic characteristics (C-index = 0.725). If future research can integrate mental health data from electronic health records, it is expected to further improve the accuracy of predictions.

Previous studies have shown that economic difficulties lead to a higher risk of suicide, and timely intervention is necessary. Economic difficulties can lead to reduced treatment opportunities [44] (Fig. 6F). However, our research results indicate that household income is not a risk factor for suicide risk. This finding may be partly explained by the income distribution bias inherent in the SEER database. Specifically, the proportion of low-income individuals (less than $50,000) accounted for only 9.5% of the total population, whereas middle-income ($50,000–$75,000) and high-income (greater than $75,000) groups comprised 45.5% and 45.0%, respectively. Such an underrepresentation of low-income patients may reduce the statistical power to detect the impact of economic hardship on suicide risk. Furthermore, among middle- and high-income groups, income differences might exert less influence on psychological distress or healthcare accessibility, leading to a nonsignificant association in this cohort. Therefore, the apparent lack of association between household income and suicide risk in our study should be interpreted with caution, and future research incorporating more balanced socioeconomic data is warranted.

This study is developed based on Western populations, and potential differences need to be considered when applied to Asian populations. There are differences in epidemiological characteristics (such as a higher proportion of non-smokers) [4], and cultural background (different awareness and reporting rates of mental health issues) [45, 46] between the Asian lung cancer population and the Western population, which may affect the expression and prediction of suicide risk.

In summary, predicting the suicide risk of lung cancer patients has important clinical significance. We suggest that healthcare providers pay more attention to the psychological state of patients, invest more assessment time in high-risk individuals, and refer them to mental health professionals, in order to further reduce the risk of suicide through effective intervention.

Clinical implications

Patients diagnosed with lung cancer face a higher risk of suicide. Therefore, prioritizing early identification work is crucial. we can identify individuals at risk and take appropriate preventive measures in a timely manner, effectively reducing the risk of suicide.

Study limitations

Although this study innovatively introduced clinically available predictive factors and constructed a dynamic nomogram for suicide risk in lung cancer patients, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was retrospective and based on the SEER database, which lacks randomization and detailed information such as specific treatment regimens, history of mental disorders, or prior suicide attempts, making it susceptible to potential confounding factors. Nevertheless, the calibration test showed that the predicted results were highly consistent with observed values, indicating good robustness within the current dataset. To further mitigate bias, we employed multivariate regression and internal validation using bootstrap resampling; however, external validation in independent cohorts is still needed. Future studies should prospectively evaluate the model in diverse populations and incorporate additional psychosocial and treatment-related variables to enhance its predictive accuracy and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

This study developed a dynamic nomogram to predict individualized suicide risk in lung cancer patients by integrating commonly available clinical and pathological indicators. The model not only offers a superior alternative to traditional static risk scales but also enables early identification of high-risk patients, facilitating timely intervention. Implemented as a web-based visual tool, it allows clinicians to perform convenient, real-time, and personalized risk assessment. By providing a preliminary reference for systematically monitoring patients’ psychological state, the model can assist healthcare professionals in identifying high-risk individuals who may benefit from referral to mental health services, thereby supporting more precise, patient-centered care and improving the efficiency of clinical decision-making.
