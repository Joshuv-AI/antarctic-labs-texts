Evaluation and prediction of the physical properties and quality of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds processed and stored in different conditions using machine learning models
Authors: Daniel Fernando Figueiredo Spengler, Paulo Carteri Coradi, Dágila Melo Rodrigues, Izabela Cristina de Oliveira, Dalmo Paim de Oliveira, Paulo Eduardo Teodoro, Larissa Pereira Ribeiro Teodoro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The conservation of seed quality throughout storage depends on established conditions, monitoring, sampling and laboratory analysis, which are subject to errors and require technical and financial resources. Thus, machine learning techniques can help optimize processes and obtain more accurate results for decision-making regarding the processing and conservation of stored seeds. Therefore, the aim was to assess and predict the physical properties (moisture content, seed mass, length, thickness, width, volume, apparent specific mass, projected area, sphericity, mean diameter, circular area, circularity, drag coefficient), and physicochemical quality (crude protein, ash content, and acidity index) of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds under different processing conditions with pulp, without pulp (scarification), without pulp (fermented), and storage conditions at 10 and 23 °C over six months. Data were analyzed on Weka software (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis) version 3.9.5. testing the following models: Pearson correlation, Artificial Neural Networks, decision tree algorithms RepTree and M5P, Random Forest, and Linear Regression. Processing cerrado jatobá seeds by fermentation and storage at 10 °C minimized physical changes and preserved the physicochemical quality of the seeds in polyethylene plastic, glass container, tetrapack, and polyethylene container, over six months. The combination of processing, temperature, and packaging variables for Artificial Neural Networks, RepTree, Random Forest, and M5P algorithms outperformed linear regression, providing higher accuracy rates. Artificial Neural Network and Random Forest models were the best predicting the effects of treatments on changes in physical properties and physicochemical quality of jatobá seed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81260-x.

Introduction

Jatobá-do-cerrado is a characteristic legume of the Brazilian cerrado. The plant reaches heights of 4–6 m and produces fruits measuring 6 to 18 cm in length and 3–6 cm in diameter. The fruits are utilized in the food industry, while the leaves and seeds find application in the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries. The plant’s pods are palatable and possess high nutritional value with a low content of antinutritional compounds. Its starchy fruits are edible and highly valued by the regional population, often consumed or used as ingredients in the preparation of cakes, bread, porridges, biscuits, and snacks with high fiber content1,2.

Post-harvest techniques may be utilized in preserving seed quality. Thus, it is important to collect seeds promptly upon dispersal and store them until the soil reaches adequate moisture levels during the rainy season3. This practice facilitates enhanced seed germination rates and promotes the establishment of uniform seedlings4,5. Such methodologies are of paramount importance, particularly in the restoration of open areas, where seeds and seedlings are exposed to desiccation. Furthermore, delaying seed dispersal offers the added advantage of minimizing the exposure to predation, a significant impediment to seed germination and, consequently, forest regeneration6.

Thus, adequate post-harvesting of seeds allows the maintenance of physicochemical properties7. The conservation and prediction of the quality of stored seeds can inform timelines for seed sowing that provide conditions for better germination. Therefore, it is essential to know the physical characteristics and post-harvest behavior of seeds. Understanding the shape and size of the seeds is essential for designing processing machinery or for analyzing seed quality during pre-processing and storage stages8,9.

Considering the potential that Jatobá seeds hold and the need for the fruits and their respective seeds to go through post-harvest processes to be preserved, it is necessary to study and evaluate which technologies and controls must be carried out to preserve the seed quality and reduce losses. The storage stage contributes significantly to the ability to obtain seeds during conventional sowing periods and achieve quality and homogeneity standards. To achieve this, it is necessary to anticipate the ideal storage conditions, type of packaging, and maximum storage time for each crop4. Some species can be dehydrated and subsequently stored at low temperatures to achieve longevity. For conventional germplasm bank systems, seed stocks are stored under controlled conditions, with a temperature of 10 °C and 40% relative humidity5. Although the seeds in these databases are maintained for a period of one year, this does not prevent the seed deterioration, as seed metabolism is completely halted only at temperatures below − 130°C6.

As there is still no advanced research on post-harvest seeds of the jatobá species, we proposed to evaluate the conservation of seeds with different post-harvest processing and storage temperatures to evaluate options for appropriate seed quality during and storage time. To fill gaps where conventional statistics fail to generate satisfactory prediction results, data modeling using Machine Learning techniques can become a viable alternative to assess the quality of stored Jatobá seeds, instead of carrying out time-consuming and expensive tests in laboratories10. Appropriately modeled, ML techniques can produce results in shorter time frames compared to traditional statistical regression models. In seed processing and storage units, the use of ML can be an auxiliary tool in decision-making within the seed storage environment, thus contributing to the optimization of the process and reduction of losses, contributing to the formation of a more sustainable post-harvest system11. In this context, the utilization of ML algorithms on Weka software (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis), testing the models of Pearson correlation, Artificial Neural Networks, decision tree algorithms RepTree and M5P, Random Forest, and Linear Regression can enhance data processing and analysis capabilities10. With this, we were able to determine the appropriate conditions for seed conversion and the best models that can be applied to predict quality, that is, the time that seeds can remain stored. Thus, the objective of this study was to evaluate and predict the physical and qualitative properties of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds processed by different methods and stored under different temperature and packaging conditions over a period of six months.

Materials and methods

Experimental description

Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds were manually harvested from farms located in Chapadão do Sul/MS and air-dried. The experimental design employed was completely randomized (5 × 3 × 2), comprising five different packaging materials (polyethylene plastic, kraft paper, glass container, tetrapack, polyethylene container), three processing methods (with pulp, pulp-free-scarified, pulp-free-fermented), and two storage conditions (10 and 23 °C) (Fig. 1). Kraft paper packaging from Canadian Kraft Paper Industries Ltda. (Manitoba, Canada) was chosen because it has high permeability and gas exchange with the storage environment (weight of 0.8 g and 80% permeability), representing the closest condition to natural storage, with temperatures of 10 and 23 °C. Polyethylene packaging, from Premium Pack Company (Guarulhos, São Paulo, Brazil) with a density of 0.93 g cm−3 represented the storage of seeds in intermediate conditions with gas exchange, with temperatures of 10 and 23 °C. Storage of seeds in glass container packaging, with a density of 2.200 g cm−3, Glass Storage Container Company (Arkansas, United State), tetrapac packaging, Tetrapack Company (São Paulo, Brazil), with around 70% cardboard, 25% plastic and 5% aluminum, and polyethylene container, Harch Portugal Company (Carnaxide, Portugal) with a density of 1.400 g cm−3 were chosen as they reduce the oxygen rate inside the container ensuring a semi-hermetic environment.

A portion of the seeds (100 seeds) underwent processing to remove the pulp. Two different processing methods were employed: manual scarification and fermentation. Manual scarification involved the use of a cutting blade for manual pulp removal, whereas fermentation processing entailed submerging the dried seeds in water for 24 h to facilitate pulp removal. Seeds with and without pulp were stored at 10 and 23 °C in polyethylene plastic bags, kraft paper bags, glass containers, tetrapacks, and polyethylene containers. Evaluation of the physical properties, moisture content, crude protein, ash content, and acidity index of the seeds was conducted at zero time, three, and six months of storage.

Analysis of the physical and physicochemical properties of jatobá-do-cerrado seeds

The moisture content was determined by the standard method in an oven at 105 °C ± 1 °C for 24 h with three replicates as recommended12. Seed size was determined by measuring the length, width, and thickness of each seed using a caliper with a resolution of 0.01 mm after drying at different temperatures. The apparent bulk density of the Jatobá seeds was determined by the supplemental volume method, using volumetric flasks (10 ± 0.04 mL) and an analytical balance (with an accuracy of 0.0001 g). Soybean oil was used as additional liquid volume. For the determination, five samples were used for each storage temperature, each containing thirty seeds. The density of the liquid (soybean oil) was obtained by the ratio of the mass to the volume of the fluid from the flask. The empty volumetric flask was initially weighed and weighed again with oil up to the 10 mL mark. The apparent density of the Jatobá seeds was determined by placing each sample inside a container of known volume and mass. The volume inside the flask was then supplemented with soybean oil to a volume of 35.10 mL, and the flask was weighed again. The knowledge of the mass of the liquid used in the supplemental volume (obtained by subtraction) and its density allowed for the calculation of the volume occupied by the seeds. The amount of crude protein was determined for each sample using the Kjeldahl digestion method, as described in AOAC12, in three replicates. This method, developed in 1983, is based on three stages: digestion, distillation, and titration. The process involves digesting the organic matter of the sample and the protein into ammonium sulfate (NH3SO4) by digesting the mixture with sulfuric acid and heat. The organic material of the sample was digested with sulfuric acid and a catalyst, and the nitrogen was transformed into ammonium salt. To determine the protein content, 1 g of sample was weighed and placed in a digestion tube, to which 1 pellet of copper catalyst (Cu) and 15 mL of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) were added. The tubes were heated in the digestion apparatus at a temperature of 420 °C and removed when a light green color developed (after about 1 h). After cooling, the sample was added to 70 mL of distilled water and 30 mL of boric acid in an Erlenmeyer flask. In the distillation step, ammonia was released from the ammonium salt by reacting with hydroxide. This occurred through the capture of nitrogen process, which was titrated and quantified. This procedure was performed using a preheated tubular distiller and a digester. With the aid of a lever contained in a retort, NaOH (40%) was added to the tube, and the mixture was distilled for about 4 min. After distillation, titration was performed with 0.1 N H2SO4. The ash analysis was performed on a 2 g sample of ground Jatobá seeds, which was placed in a tarred porcelain crucible and heated at 100 °C in an oven and then incinerated at 600 °C in a muffle furnace for 4 h at 600 °C. The sample was then cooled in a desiccator to room temperature and weighed12. After incineration, ash determination was obtained by the difference between the mass of the empty crucible, previously incinerated, and the mass of the crucible and calcite residue, considering the mass of the fresh sample.

Statistical analyses

A normality test was performed on the dependent variables. An analysis of variance (using Tukey’s test for post-hoc tests) was carried out for the dependent variables: moisture content (MC), seed mass (Ma), length (L), thickness (T), width (W), volume (V), apparent specific mass (ASM), projected area (PA), sphericity (SP), mean diameter (AD), circular area (CA), circularity (C), drag coefficient (DC), crude protein (CP), ash content (AS), and acidity index (IA), depending on the processing and storage conditions, to compare which were the best conditions for seed conservation. Next, the Pearson correlation network was constructed among the predicted variables MC, Ma, L, T, W, V, ASM, PA, SP, AD, CA, C, DC, CP, AS, and IA, to verify the correlations between the predicted variables and understand the effects of seed processing and storage conditions. These analyses were conducted using the Rbio software (Biometric and Statistical Analysis) version 2.0, following the procedures recommended by Bhering et al. (2017).

Machine learning

Seed quality was predicted (dependent variables) depending on processing and storage conditions, using the Weka software (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis) version 3.9.5, employing machine learning models compared to the traditional linear multiple regression model, to define which could achieve a better fit: Artificial Neural Network (ANNs), decision tree algorithms (REPTree, RandTree, and M5P), Random Forest (RF), and Multiple Linear Regression (MLR). Weka (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis) aims to aggregate algorithms from different approaches in artificial intelligence dedicated to the study of machine learning. This sub-area intends to develop algorithms that allow a computer to “learn” either inductively or deductively. Weka (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis) performs computational and statistical analysis of the data provided, resorting to data mining techniques, inductively trying to generate hypotheses for solutions from the patterns found and, at the extremes, even theories about the data in question.

The ANNs model tested consists of a single hidden layer formed by a number of neurons equal to the number of attributes plus the number of classes, all divided by 214. The RepTree model is an adaptation of the C4.5 classifier that can be used in regression problems with an additional pruning step based on an error reduction strategy15. The RandTree model consists of a class for constructing a tree that considers K attributes chosen randomly at each node, and it does not perform pruning. Additionally, it has an option to allow the estimation of class probabilities (or target mean in the case of regression) based on a holdout set (backfitting). The M5P model is a reconstruction of Quinlan’s M5 algorithm based on the conventional decision tree with the addition of a linear regression function to the leaf nodes16. The RF model can produce multiple prediction trees for the same dataset and use a voting scheme among all learned trees to predict new values17 (Belgiu and Dr’agu 2016). The MLR model was used as a control model, as it serves to predict relationships between variables that have a good correlation, being widely used in common statistics (Fig. 2).

Through the machine learning models, the following variables were predicted: moisture content (MC), seed mass (Ma), length (L), thickness (T), width (W), volume (V), apparent specific mass (ASM), projected area (PA), sphericity (SP), mean diameter (AD), circular area (CA), circularity (C), drag coefficient (DC), crude protein (CP), ash content (AS), and acidity index (IA). A standard software configuration was used for all tested models18 on an Intel® CoreTM i5-3317U CPU with 4 GB of RAM. All analyses were applied using stratified random cross-validation with 10 folds and 10 repetitions (80% training) and (20% validation). The following input parameters were considered in the models: time (zero, three, and six months), Processing (with pulp, without fermented pulp, and mechanically scarified without pulp), Storage Condition (10 °C and 23 °C), and Packaging (polyethylene container, tetrapak, glass container, polyethylene plastic bag, and kraft paper bag) with three repetitions for each treatment, totalizing 270 samples.

The statistics used to verify the quality of the fit were the pearson correlation coefficient (r) between the observed and predicted values by each model. Additionally, the mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted values in relation to the observed values was calculated according to the methodology proposed by Bhering et al.19. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) is the most common way of measuring a linear correlation. It is a number between − 1 and 1 that measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables. When one variable changes, the other variable changes in the same direction. The mean absolute error (MAE) is a measure of errors between paired observations expressing the same phenomenon. These analyses were conducted using the Rbio software (Biometric and Statistical Analysis) version 2.0, and the R software (statistical computing), version 2.0. Furthermore, the ggplot2 and gridExtra packages were utilized for visualization and analysis.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds

The results of the physical analyses of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds were significant at a 5% probability level for different processing and storage conditions and non-significant for different types of packaging. The length, width, and thickness of the seeds varied from 2.38 to 2.93 cm, 1.97 to 2.34 cm, and 1.39 to 1.74 cm, respectively. The width of the seeds varied depending on the type of packaging used for storage. There was a greater increase in seed width when stored in glass containers and tetrapacks, and a reduction in seed width in polyethylene plastic bags and paper packaging. Similar results were observed for seed volume (Fig. 3). The volume of the seeds increased after storage in tetrapacks and PET containers and decreased in seeds stored in kraft paper bags. The axes of the seeds increased with the increase in storage time in glass containers, tetra packs, and polyethylene plastic bags.

Evaluation of physical properties: (A) length, (B) width, (C) thickness, and (D) volume of processed and stored jatobá-do-cerrado seeds in different forms.

The thickness of the seeds increased when stored in PET containers after three months of storage at 10 °C, while after six months of storage, the thickness of the seeds decreased in most packaging treatments, especially kraft paper bags. The seed diameter remained unchanged due to processing and storage. The moisture content of the seeds increased from 9.71 to 13.43% (w.b.) at a storage temperature of 10 °C and from 9.71 to 13.18% (w.b.) at 23 °C, also altering the seed mass and specific mass (Fig. 4). The values for physical properties were higher than those found by Andrade et al.7 for Hymenaea courbaril L species. Seeds stored in glass containers and tetrapacks showed the highest moisture contents, regardless of the storage temperature20. The moisture content increased in the seeds from the third month of storage in all types of packaging. Moisture content directly influenced the metabolic processes of the seeds, as an increase in moisture content elevated the respiratory rate of the seeds.

The circularity and sphericity of the seeds were influenced by mechanical scarification depulping. Sphericity ranged from 0.73 (seeds with pulp) to 0.84 (scarified seeds without pulp), while circularity ranged from 0.66 (seeds with pulp) to 0.86 (scarified seeds without pulp). The circular areas of the seeds were larger for seeds with pulp (5.40 cm2) and smaller in scarified seeds (3.77 cm2). The same trend was observed in the projected area of the seeds, although the lowest values were obtained in seeds depulped by fermentation (Fig. 5). Therefore, it is essential to know the physical characteristics and post-harvest behavior of seeds to establish design criteria for the industry to develop technologies. Understanding seed shape and size is essential for designing processing machines or for analyzing seed quality during the pre-processing and storage stages8,9.

Evaluation of physical properties: (A) sphericity, (B) circularity, (C) circular area, (D) diameter, and (E)projected area of processed and stored jatobá-do-cerrado seeds in different forms.

Differences were observed in the projected area, circular area, sphericity, circularity, and diameter among the different types of packaging, regardless of storage time and temperature. Kraft paper bag packaging had a greater effect on the projected circular area, circularity, and diameter of the seeds, while tetrapack, glass, and PET container packaging influenced the size of the seeds. Both at low and high storage temperatures (10–23 °C), seeds lost mass when stored in permeable packaging (kraft paper bags and tetrapacks). Physical changes can influence seed germination rates4,5.

The physical changes influenced the quality of the seeds21. Significant alterations (P < 0.05) were observed in the chemical quality of the Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds subjected to different processing and storage conditions, while no significant differences were found based on the type of storage packaging. The best results for crude protein were observed under storage conditions at 10 °C for the pulp-free seeds with fermentation (11.41%) in the third month of storage. Whereas the lowest crude protein percentages were found in the seeded pulp (9.32%) from the third month of storage onwards. Additionally, a reduction in the ash percentage (Fig. 6) was observed with increasing storage time. Matuda and Maria Neto2 studied the chemical composition of jatobá-do-cerrado seeds and obtained the following results for moisture, ash, protein, and lipids (10.18%, 1.80%, 9.05%, and 5.3%, respectively). The seeds depulped by fermentation showed the lowest ash percentages (1.34%), while the highest percentages were found in the seeded pulp (2.53%). The acidity index in the seeds increased from the third month of storage (Fig. 6). The highest acidity index was observed in the seeded pulp (9.73 mL of 1 N NaOH), and the lowest in the pulp-free seeds after fermentation (4.71 mL of 1 N NaOH). The percentage of crude protein in the seeds stored at 10 °C decreased after the third month of storage in tetrapack packaging and glass containers, and increased after storage in kraft paper bags, polyethylene plastic bags, and PET containers. At the storage temperature of 23 °C, the highest percentage of crude protein was obtained in seeds stored in glass containers, tetrapacks, or paper, while seeds stored in PET containers and polyethylene plastic bags obtained the lowest percentage of crude protein. On the other hand, over the storage period, the ash percentage decreased for all types of packaging tested, with the greatest effects observed for kraft paper packaging, regardless of the storage conditions. Thus, the fermentation process to remove the pulp favored the conservation of the seeds throughout storage, regardless of the packaging used. Storage at 10 °C minimized physical changes and preserved the physicochemcial quality of the seeds for storage in kraft and polyethylene packaging.

Linear models tended to be used as a simpler class of algorithms and worked to generate a line of best fit. While machine learning has offered unparalleled capabilities in handling complex, high-dimensional data, traditional statistical models have provided clarity, simplicity, and reliability, especially on smaller, well-defined datasets. In prediction using Machine Learning models, there was no difference among the tested models for the physical properties, such as width, thickness, mean diameter, projected area, circular area, sphericity, circularity, and apparent specific mass of the seeds (Table 1; Figs. 8A-D, 9A-D, 10A-D, 11A-D, 12A-D and 13A-B, and 14A-D). However, for volume, drag coefficient, and seed water content, the ANNs, RepTree, M5P, RadTree, and RF models showed the lowest MAE values compared to MLR. For these variables, the r values did not show significant differences among the models. However, for seed mass analysis (Table 1; Fig. 12A-B), the r values were below 0.5, with the highest for the MLR model and the lowest for ANNs, while the MAE values were higher for ANNs and lower for MLR. Machine Learning methods have been used to predict many crop yields22,23, nitrogen application rates in soils24, leaf nitrogen concentration25, seed classification26, and crude protein percentage in stored grains27. The Random Forest algorithm, for instance, has been successfully employed in crop yield prediction28. Compared to Multiple Linear Regression models, this technique is effective and easier to use in yield forecasting analyses for maize, soybean, and potato29,30. Another example is Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), which are algorithms that can be trained to analyze and interpret complex food safety data31, as well as physical and chemical predictions32,33.

ANNs have the capability to predict the outcomes of new independent input data, making them superior for modeling nonlinear data34. This characteristic has made computational models more attractive due to the incomplete understanding of the problem to be solved with sufficient and readily accessible training data35.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the processing of Jatobá-do-Cerrado seeds by fermentation and storage at 10 °C minimized physical changes and preserved the physicochemical quality of the seeds in polyethylene plastic, glass container, tetrapack, and polyethylene container, over six months. In addition, the Artificial Neural Networks and Random Forest models were the most effective and can be used to predict the effects of treatments on changes in physical properties and physicochemical quality of Jatobá seeds, and with this, anticipate the storage time for each condition, in order to preserve the quality of the seeds.
