Triple-reaction-center catalysis drives simultaneous oxidation-reduction towards diverse emerging contaminants under oxidant-free conditions
Authors: Dengzheng Zhang, Qingbai Tian, Yuanfang Wang, Yanbiao Liu, Binrong Li, Yang Zhang, Menglin Zhang, Cong Lyu, Xing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecisely designed self-driven single-atom catalysts (SACs) can achieve efficient degradation of emerging contaminants (ECs) without oxidants addition, but such systems often exhibited selectivity limited to electron-donating ECs, lacking broad-spectrum degradation capability. To address this limitation, a triple-reaction-center catalyst (Co0/CoN2-SAC) is designed in this study, integrating an electron-deficient region based on carbon framework and an electron-enriched region featuring both metallic cobalt (Co0) and atomically dispersed CoN2 sites. The electron-deficient region facilitates ECs adsorption through π–π interactions, and the CoN2 sites in the electron-enriched region serve as electron acceptors, extracting electrons from electron-donating ECs. While the Co0 sites in the electron-deficient region serve as the electron donor for donating the electron towards electron-withdrawing ECs accompanying with reactive atomic hydrogen (H*) and •OH attacking, enabling efficient reductive degradation of electron-withdrawing ECs. As a result, the Co0/CoN2-SAC system exhibits broad-spectrum degradation capability for both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ECs, whereas the acid-leached counterpart lacking Co0 shows activity solely toward electron-donating species. Elucidation of this simultaneous oxidation-reduction mechanism provides critical insights for the rational design of adaptive catalytic systems capable of treating complex wastewater matrices, ensuring robust contaminant removal across diverse industrial and municipal water treatment applications.Subject terms: Environmental chemistry, Pollution remediation, Heterogeneous catalysis

Introduction

In recent years, the detection of complex emerging contaminants (ECs) in various water and wastewater samples has become increasingly prevalent1–4. These persistent ECs pose significant challenges to water pollution remediation and the sustainable management of water resources5. Traditional heterogeneous Fenton-like systems often necessitate the use of various oxidants, such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), peroxymonosulfate (PMS), peroxodisulfate (PDS), ozone (O3) and peracetic acid (PAA), to generate versatile free radicals or nonradical species through catalytic processes, enabling efficient removal of a wide range of ECs (Fig. 1a)6–14. However, this approach not only consumes significant amounts of oxidants but also poses risks of secondary pollution due to excess oxidants, necessitating additional treatment processes11,15–18. Specifically, the sulfate ions generated during the reaction process by the most widely studied sulfur-containing oxidants, such as PMS and PDS, represent one of the key bottlenecks limiting their large-scale application11,16–19.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), peroxymonosulfate (PMS), peroxodisulfate (PDS), ozone (O3) and peracetic acid (PAA).

To address these challenges, numerous scholars have developed a series of dual-reaction-center (DRC) catalysts that incorporate electron-deficient and electron-rich active sites, enabling significant reductions in oxidant consumption or even achieving high catalytic efficiency without the addition of external oxidants (Fig. 1b)20,21. In such systems, the catalytic mechanism proceeds through two sequential steps: first, electron-donating pollutants are adsorbed onto the electron-deficient regions of the DRC catalysts and act as electron donors to the catalyst; second, the transferred electrons migrate via C–O–metal bond bridges to the electron-rich regions, where they are accepted by metal sites (e.g. single metal atoms) or dissolved oxygen, thereby sustaining the catalytic cycle and facilitating efficient pollutant degradation with minimal or no external oxidants11,22–27. For instance, our previous study demonstrated that DRC-based iron single-atom catalysts (Fe-SACs) could effectively extract electrons from electron-donating pollutants such as paracetamol (PCM) and bisphenol A (BPA), concurrently achieving pollutant degradation and reduction of iron valence states via an auto-catalytic process (Fig. 1b)20. Tian et al. proposed that the synergistic effect between atomic coordination engineering and pollutants could collectively govern oxidant-free single-atom catalysis, thereby advancing sustainable water purification12. Nonetheless, these oxidant-free systems are primarily effective for the degradation towards electron-donating pollutants and exhibit limited efficacy for electron-withdrawing pollutants with poor electron-donating capability, making their removal a persistent challenge via oxidant-free system18,20,28.

Studies indicate that zero-valent metals (such as zero-valent cobalt (Co0), zero-valent iron (Fe0), and zero-valent manganese (Mn0)) can produce reactive atomic hydrogen in catalytic systems, allowing for efficient reductive degradation of various electron-withdrawing ECs29–32. Consequently, the hypothesis of constructing a triple-reaction-center (TRC) system compared with previous DRC systems on the catalyst surface, comprising an electron-deficient carbon-based region, electron-rich regions including both single-atom sites and dispersed zero-valent metals, would enable simultaneous oxidation and reduction pathways for the degradation of both electron-donating or electron-withdrawing ECs (Fig. 1c). In this configuration, electron-donating ECs can be oxidised through the electron-transfer pathway at the single-atom/carbon interface, whereas the adsorbed electron-withdrawing ECs can be efficiently reduced via atomic hydrogen generated in situ by zero-valent metals anchored on the TRC-based catalyst. This hypothesized process would also be operated without additional oxidants, achieving broad-spectrum ECs removal and significantly broadening the applicability of oxidant-free catalytic systems.

To unravel the underlying mechanism of this catalytic process, we developed a triple-reaction-center (TRC) catalyst featuring electron-rich zero-valent cobalt (Co0) coexisting with cobalt single atoms within an electron-deficient carbon framework (Co0/CoN2-SAC). This catalyst was applied in an auto-catalytic system that operated without external oxidants and demonstrated superior degradation performance toward a wide range of ECs exhibiting diverse electron-donating or electron-withdrawing capabilities (Supplementary Table 1). We observed that after selective removal of Co0, the degradation capacity of the resulting catalyst toward electron-withdrawing ECs was significantly reduced, whereas its activity toward electron-donating ECs were largely retained, confirming the critical role of coupled oxidation and reduction processes within the TRC architecture of Co0/CoN2-SAC. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and electrochemical analyses further validated the synergistic oxidation and reduction pathways enabled by the TRC-based catalyst, facilitating broad-spectrum ECs degradation. This work therefore provides some insights into the rational design of TRC-based catalysts for coupled oxidation-reduction of both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ECs under oxidant-free systems.

Results

Characterizations of the TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC

The synthesis schematic of TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC, featuring both Co0 and cobalt single atoms anchored on the electron-deficient carbon framework, was illustrated in Fig. 2a. The cobalt content in Co0/CoN2-SAC was determined to be approximately 3.1 wt% (negligible residual Zn of 0.03 wt%), and it was decreased to 1.3 wt% after acid pickling (Supplementary Table 2). The XRD pattern of the resulting Co0/CoN2-SAC displayed characteristic peaks corresponding to the Co0 (Fig. 2b); this was confirmed by the Co2p XPS spectrum, which showed the formation of zero-valent cobalt within the synthesised DRCs catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 1). XPS analysis of the C1s spectrum revealed distinct peaks assigned to C–C/C=C, C–N and N=C–N bonds, consistent with a carbon-nitrogen framework (Supplementary Fig. 2)33,34. A noticeable shift in the pyridinic nitrogen peak was observed in the N1s spectrum of Co0/CoN2-SAC, suggesting uniform coordination between cobalt single atom and pyridinic nitrogen in the support matrix (Supplementary Fig. 3). The morphological features of Co0/CoN2-SAC, as revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) exhibited an irregular, clumpy structure (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of Co0/CoN2-SAC showed nanoparticle with a diameter of 10 nm. In contrast, this crystal plane was disappeared in the Co0-free sample after acid pickling of Co0/CoN2-SAC (Fig. 2c); this was also confirmed by the transmission electron energy spectrum (EDS) images of Co0/CoN2-SAC and Co0-free sample (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 6). Carbon lattice surrounding the vacancy of Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid pickling indicated that zero-valent Co was anchored to the carbon framework through Co–C bonds (Supplementary Fig. 7). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) images showed that the nanoparticles co-existed with individual atoms in the carbon framework. Furthermore, the AC-HAADF-STEM image also showed the removal of Co0 species after the acid pickling of TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC, leaving only single-atom cobalt in the carbon framework (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, the spots in AC-HAADF-STEM displayed three different distances (Fig. 2f), corresponding to zero-valent cobalt (4.21 nm) and cobalt single atom in the TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC system before and after acid washing (0.97 and 0.73 nm). Raman spectroscopic analysis revealed that the ID/IG ratio (1.023) of Co0/CoN2-SAC was nearly identical to that (1.025) of acid-treated Co0/CoN2-SAC, indicating that acid pickling exhibited no discernible effect on the relative abundance of graphitic carbon versus defective carbon (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Furthermore, structural insights into the coordination environment were obtained through X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis. The Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectrum indicated an average oxidation state of +0.136 for cobalt. The presence of such cobalt species in a near-zero valence state in Co0/CoN2-SAC confirmed the co-existence of Co0 as the dominant cobalt species and cobalt single atoms (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 10). Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis revealed both the strong Co-Co bond and Co-N bond (with the Co/N ratio calculated as 1:1.92), strongly suggesting the co-existence of Co-N2 configuration and Co0 (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Figs. 11–16 and Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, the 3D wavelet-transform (WT) contour map of Co0/CoN2-SAC exhibited two well-separated intensity maxima with partial overlap, with dispersion characteristics clearly distinct from reference materials such as Co foil, Co2O3, CoO and Co3O4 (Fig. 2j)35–37. This WT profile aligned well with the XRD, XPS and EXAFS analysis, further corroborating the co-existence of Co0 nanoparticles and CoN2-SAC.

Catalytic performances based on TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC in oxidant-free system

Paracetamol (PCM) removal by the TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst showed that 0.1 g/L of Co0/CoN2-SAC could removal approximately 39.2% of PCM, and its removal efficiency could be increased to 94.9% with the removal rate (kobs) of 0.236 min−1 at 0.5 g/L of Co0/CoN2-SAC (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 17). In addition, the Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid leaching exhibited a comparable kobs result for PCM removal to that of the pristine Co0/CoN2-SAC (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 18). In contrast, the Co-NPs as well as the CN counterparts showed inferior removal capacity towards PCM. The homogeneous oxidant-free system also proved completely ineffective for PCM removal (Supplementary Fig. 19). These results indicated that the single-atom active sites (CoN2) were primarily responsible for the catalytic degradation of PCM. The removal of PCM by Co0/CoN2-SAC in an oxidant-free system demonstrated anti-interference capability under various anion species and pH conditions (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 20, 21), attributed to its unique electronic structure and surface properties that prevent active site blocking or passivation by salt ions, consistent with the typical DRC-based catalytic mechanism2,16,20,38–40.

The removal performances of Co0/CoN2-SAC towards different ECs were presented in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 22. The Co0/CoN2-SAC could achieve superior removal capacities towards different ECs, even those of refractory electron-withdrawing ECs, such as p-nitrophenol (NP) and nithbenzoic acid (NBA). However, the stripping of Co0 from Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid pickling exhibited two significantly different removal trends for versatile ECs: (i) the removal of electron-donating ECs was almost unaffected or only slightly influenced, while (ii) the kobs values electron-withdrawing ECs by the Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid pickling was significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig. 23). For example, the kobs value of NBA by Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid pickling was only approximately 5% compared with that of bare Co0/CoN2-SAC (Supplementary Fig. 24). These results indicated that the Co0 species were responsible for the electron-withdrawing ECs, while CoN2 sites played a crucial role in removing electron-donating ECs. Furthermore, non-selective adsorption can be ruled out as the dominant removal pathway for these ECs, given the low uptake of electron-withdrawing ECs observed with the acid-treated Co0/CoN2-SAC. For example, only 25% of NBA removal could be achieved by the Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling), and the adsorption of NBA by NC was less than 5% (Supplementary Fig. 24), which indicated that the solo adsorption of NBA by the resulting catalyst was between 5 and 25% (considering that the auto-catalytic process was a synchronous surface adsorption and in situ self-degradation process, it was difficult to accurately distinguish the proportion of adsorption).

The electrophilic/nucleophilic indexes of different ECs represented their electron-donating capacities (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 25)41–44, and the results showed that both the electrophilic index and nucleophilic index of ECs were inconsistent with their kobs values achieved by Co0/CoN2-SAC (Supplementary Fig. 26). However, the electrophilicity index of ECs exhibited a very good correlation (R2 = 0.936) with their kobs values after Co0 stripping from Co0/CoN2-SAC (Fig. 3f). These results further substantiated the essential contribution of CoN2 sites in Co0/CoN2-SAC to the removal of electron-donating ECs. Comparison of normalised kobs values in the TRC-based Co0Co/N2-SAC system with reported catalytic systems exhibited the superior removal capacities towards both the electron-withdrawing/electron-donating ECs under oxidant-free conditions (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Tables 4, 5), which partially alleviated the bottleneck in most reported DRC-based systems with high selectivity towards electron-donating ECs18,20,21,24,25,45.

Total organic carbon (TOC) of PCM and NBA after removal by the Co0/CoN2-SAC showed that mineralisation of PCM and NBA was about 41–55% (Supplementary Fig. 28). To assess the practical applicability of the Co0/CoN2-SAC systems in real wastewater, degradation experiments were conducted using actual wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluent spiked with 5 mg/L each of PCM and NBA. The removal efficiencies for both contaminants remained nearly unchanged over the designated reaction period, performing comparably to those observed in ultrapure water (Supplementary Fig. 29). This result indicated robust catalytic activity and minimal interference from the complex matrix of coexisting ions, organic matter and suspended solids in the effluent. As for the stability capacity, the used Co0/CoN2-SAC and Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling) kept more than 99% of removal capacity for PCM after several cycles of regeneration (Supplementary Fig. 30).

Auto-catalysis mechanism of TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC in oxidant-free system

Quenching experiments were conducted to elucidate the involvement of radical and nonradical pathways in the Co0/CoN2-SAC systems. The quenching effects of EtOH (100 mM) and DMPO (5 mM) on PCM degradation were found to be negligible in both the Co0/CoN2-SAC and acid-pickled Co0/CoN2-SAC systems. In contrast, a pronounced quenching effect was observed for NBA upon addition of the same scavengers. Furthermore, quenching tests revealed minimal inhibitory effects towards both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ECs (e.g. PCM and BPA) in the acid-pickled Co0/CoN2-SAC system, suggesting a dominant nonradical mechanism in this Co0-free configuration (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 31–34). Furthermore, the apparent rate constant (kobs, min−1) for PCM degradation remained largely unchanged in an atmosphere of N2, while that for NBA decreased to one-quarter of its initial value (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 35), consistent with a radical-dependent pathway for NBA transformation. In addition, the removal of PCM and NBA by Co0/CoN2-SAC between dark and control conditions showed no statistically difference (Supplementary Fig. 36). This result confirmed that pollutant removal was driven by the auto-catalytic process, rather than by direct photolysis or photo-assisted pathways. The contribution of radical and nonradical pathways in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system, as revealed by quenching experiments, indicated that radical oxidation plays a significant role in the degradation of NBA, whereas its involvement was negligible in the case of PCM. In contrast, for the Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling) system, radical oxidation exhibited minimal contribution toward both PCM and NBA degradation, suggesting the absence of active radical species generation in this system (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38). In the Co0/CoN2-SAC system, distinct •O2−, •OH and H* signals were observed in the EPR spectra, whereas these signals were nearly undetectable in the acid-pickled Co0/CoN2-SAC system (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 39a). These results suggested that metallic Co0 would facilitate the generation of •O2−, •OH and H* for the degradation of electron-withdrawing ECs. In addition, the •O2− signals were disappeared in the N2-purged system, providing direct experimental evidence that the •O2− was derived from DO (Supplementary Fig. 39b). However, the degradation of PCM in the N2-purged Co0/CoN2-SAC system was also unchanged, which indicated that the role of •O2− was negligible in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system. The potential formation of high-valent cobalt-oxo intermediates was further probed using trans-stilbene oxidation assays, with no significant epoxidation observed (Supplementary Fig. 40), suggesting a negligible contribution of high-valent cobalt-oxo species under the tested conditions.

The XANES analysis of spent Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst revealed a notable shift in cobalt oxidation state by decreasing from +0.11 to +0.035 following PCM degradation and increasing to +0.15 after NBA degradation (Fig. 4d, e). These results also corresponded well to the distinct different degradation pathways towards electron-donating/electron-withdrawing ECs based on the Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst. Given that the removal of NBA was predominantly attributed to metallic Co0 in the Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst, this result indicated that metallic Co0 would function as an electron donor in the electron-rich domains and facilitated the electron transferring to NBA. In contrast, the CoN2 sites in the Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst served as the electron acceptors in electron-rich domains, thereby accepting electrons from PCM molecules. Moreover, XAFS results confirmed the preservation of local coordination structures throughout the catalytic process (Supplementary Figs. S41–S44), indicating structural stability under reaction conditions.

The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of Co0/CoN2-SAC showed a relative lower charge transfer resistance compared with that of acid-treated Co0/CoN2-SAC (Supplementary Figs. 45 and 46). This improved charge transfer capability in the Co0/CoN2-SAC was attributed to the anchoring of both metallic Co0 and atomically dispersed CoN2 sites, facilitating efficient electron conduction. Open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements were performed to monitor the redox behavior of the Co0/CoN2-SAC and Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling) systems under different conditions (Supplementary Fig. 47). Upon addition of PCM (△E = − 0.0889 V) or NBA (△E = + 0.0411 V), a measurable shift in potential was observed in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system and the opposite trend confirmed the different electron transfer direction towards different ECs. In contrast, for the acid-pickled system where metallic Co0 was selectively removed, the potential response upon PCM addition remained nearly unchanged (△E = − 0.0878 V), while the response to NBA with △E = − 0.0009 V became significantly attenuated (Fig. 4f, g). The markedly reduced potential change in the absence of Co0 demonstrated that efficient electron transfer to NBA was dependent on the existence of Co0, highlighting its role as an essential electron donor in the catalytic process43,46–48. The radar plot analysis showed no significant correlation between △E and kobs (min−1) in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system (Supplementary Fig. 48a). This was attributed to the simultaneous oxidation and reduction capabilities of the Co0/CoN2-SAC system toward a broad range of ECs. In contrast, after selective removal of metallic Co0 by acid pickling, a strong positive correlation between △E and kobs (min−1) was observed (Supplementary Fig. 48b), reflecting the predominant oxidation pathway involving electron transfer from electron-donating ECs to the CoN2 sites in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system. These findings confirmed that electron-transfer pathways dominated the oxidation mechanism in the Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling) system49. The role of Co0 in modulating △E and kobs was further validated by the OCP analysis and degradation performances achieved by a series of acid-treated Co0/CoN2-SAC samples prepared with varying acid pickling times (3–9 h). OCP results revealed a progressive decrease in the potential response to NBA as acid pickling time increased from 3 to 9 h, whereas the response to PCM remained nearly unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 49). This trend aligned quantitatively with the observed degradation kinetics, which showed that NBA removal efficiency declined markedly with prolonged acid treatment, while PCM removal remained virtually unaffected (Supplementary Figs. 50 and 51). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that Co0 sites were primarily responsible for NBA degradation, whereas the CoN2-SAC moiety dominated PCM removal.

These findings supported a TRC-based degradation routine towards both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ECs as: (i) these ECs were first adsorbed onto the electron-deficient regions of Co0/CoN2-SAC; (ii) the auto-catalytic mechanism in the Co0/CoN2-SAC system would occur with coupled oxidation-reduction degradation wherein the CoN2 sites moiety acted as the primary electron acceptor for the oxidation of electron-donating ECs while Co0 served as the electron donor for donating the electron towards electron-withdrawing ECs accompanying with H* and •OH attacking (Fig. 4h). The Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) was further employed to investigate the surface potentials of pristine and used Co0/CoN2-SAC samples as well as their electron transfer processes towards different pollutants (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 52). The pristine Co0/CoN2-SAC and the acid-washed Co0/CoN2-SAC exhibited surface potentials of 26.97 and 10.63 mV, respectively. These results indicated that the coexistence of Co0 and CoN2 induced a strong built-in electric field, which was attenuated upon the removal of Co0. After soaking the Co0/CoN2-SAC in an electron-deficient NBA solution, its surface potential dropped to 19.10 mV due to the targeted interaction between Co0 and NBA. Similarly, the surface potential of the acid-washed Co0/CoN2-SAC for Co0 stripping significantly decreased to near zero following the degradation of electron-rich PCM. Collectively, these results demonstrated that Co0 and CoN2 selectively facilitated electron transfer processes with electron-deficient and electron-rich pollutants, respectively. Furthermore, the in situ synchrotron-based Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (SR-FTIR) exhibited characteristic C−O stretching vibration bands at 1223 and 1321 cm−1 upon exposure of NBA with Co0/CoN2-SAC (acid pickling), which was assigned to intact NBA molecules since the acid-washed Co0/CoN2-SAC was inert to the NBA degradation (Fig. 4j, k and Supplementary Fig. 53)50,51. In contrast, when NBA was introduced to the Co0/CoN2-SAC system, the corresponding SR-FTIR spectra revealed a marked increase in the intensity of the 1223 cm−1 band and a concurrent decrease in that of the 1321 cm−1 band. These results suggested that the electron-withdrawing NBA molecule was degraded on the catalyst surface, a process in which the Co0 sites played a critical role.

DFT calculations of TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC in oxidant-free system

DFT calculations were further performed to elucidate the origins of the superior catalytic performance33,52,53. We first analyzed the electronic structure using the electron localisation function (ELF), a topological descriptor ranging from 0 to 1 that quantifies the degree of localisation of electrons. As shown in Fig. 5a, electrons in the CoN2 moiety were highly localised on the carbon support, creating a kinetic barrier for electron transfer. In contrast, the Co0 clusters in both Co0-C and Co0/CoN2-SAC systems exhibited distinct electron delocalization characteristics. This metallicity transformed the Co0 region into an electron reservoir, facilitating efficient electron injection into adsorbed H2O molecules to trigger the generation of reactive atomic hydrogen (H*)54. Supplementary Fig. 54 also exhibited that the Co0 clusters leading to the decreased work function value than other samples, which indicated that stronger electron-donating ability. This unique charge distribution validated the TRC design, where the electron-rich Co0 clusters served as active centers for reductive H* production, synergising with the oxidative carbon support to enable the coupled degradation mechanism without external oxidants. The thermodynamics and kinetics of the H* evolution pathway was also investigated, and this process was initiated with water adsorption (Fig. 5b, c)55. The Co0/CoN2-SAC (−0.016 eV) and Co0-C (−0.021 eV) systems exhibited significantly stronger H2O affinity of Co metallic clusters compared to CoN2 (+0.39 eV). The subsequent H2O activation step revealed that H* generation on the CoN2 site was hindered by a high energy barrier of 2.270 eV (rate-determining step: *H2O → *OH + H). Conversely, the introduction of Co0 altered the reaction pathway, shifting the rate-determining step to the step of *OH + H → *H. Notably, the Co0/CoN2-SAC system exhibited the lowest energy barrier (0.409 eV), surpassing both Co0-C (0.458 eV) and CoN2 (2.270 eV), thus confirming it as the most thermodynamically favorable architecture for H* production.

To understand the interactions between the active sites and the generated H*, we calculated the adsorption energies (Fig. 5d) and the crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Figs. 55, 56). While CoN2 showed negligible affinity for H* (−0.1 eV), the Co0-based systems showed significantly enhanced adsorption. In the Co0/CoN2-SAC system, the active H* adsorption energy at different sites was similar to that in the Co0-C system, but slightly lower overall (Supplementary Data 1). Specifically, the integrated COHP (ICOHP) analysis provided insight into the bonding nature, which showed a more negative value typically indicates stronger bonding56–58. After H adsorption onto the CoN2-SAC system, it exhibited nearly overlapping α and β spins of the Co-H bonds in the COHP (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Data 2), suggesting the quenching of the Co magnetic moment and consequent restoration of spin symmetry. The Co0/CoN2-SAC system displayed relatively high ICOHP values (−0.3989 for α spin and −0.4290 for β spin) compared to CoN2-SAC (−1.2104/−1.2105) and Co0-C (−0.4089/−0.4758). This result, combined with the moderate adsorption energy, suggested that the Co-H bonding in the TRC architecture was moderately strong to activate H* yet labile enough to facilitate its desorption and subsequent utilisation for ECs reduction, verifying a synergistic optimisation of H* turnover frequency. Furthermore, electrostatic potential (ESP) mapping provided a more detailed explanation of the active H* generation sites from the perspective of the electronic configuration (Fig. 5f). The Co0 clusters and single-atom Co sites manifested as electron-rich regions (blue), while the surrounding carbon framework appeared electron-deficient (red), indicating that Co0 in the TRC system played the main role in activate H* production. These results suggested that the Co-H bonding between Co0 and H* in the TRC architecture was moderately strong to activate H* yet labile enough to facilitate its desorption and subsequent utilisation for ECs reduction, verifying a synergistic optimisation of H* turnover frequency.

To comprehensively understanding the behavior of H* utilisation, we integrated analyses of orbital interactions, differential charge density, density of states (DOS) and the d-band center (Fig. 5g–i and Supplementary Figs. 57, 58). In the CoN2 system (Fig. 5g), the projected density of states revealed a significant spatial overlap between the H 1s orbital and the Co 3dz2 orbital. This configuration induced strong “head-on” orbital repulsion (Pauli repulsion), which severely restricted electron transfer from the metal center to the adsorbate (Δq = 0.0427 e), rendering the generation of reactive H* unfavourable. Conversely, the Co0-C system exhibited broad resonance between the H 1s orbital and the multiple d-orbitals of Co (Fig. 5h), indicating enhanced d-s hybridization. This strong coupling facilitated substantial electron injection (Δq = 0.3191 e). Most notably, the Co0/CoN2-SAC system demonstrated a unique electronic synergy (Fig. 5i). While maintaining a high magnitude of electron transfer (Δq = 0.3100 e) comparable to the Co0-C system—essential for H* formation—the PDOS highlighted a distinctly weakened d-s orbital overlap. This attenuation in bonding strength was further quantified by the downshift of the d-band center relative to the Fermi level. Consequently, the Co0/CoN2-SAC system achieved an optimal catalytic balance: it ensured sufficient electron injection to trigger H* generation while preventing over-binding to facilitate subsequent desorption. This 'formation-desorption' equilibrium maximised the utilisation efficiency of H* species for pollutant reduction.

To delineate the directionality of interfacial electron transfer between the specific active sites and target contaminants, we conducted frontier molecular orbital analysis (Fig. 5j, k)27,28,59. For the CoN2 moiety, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is located at −3.540 eV. When aligned with the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels of ten representative pollutants (Fig. 5j), a favorable energetic gradient was observed20,60,61. This energy gap facilitated spontaneous electron flow from the contaminants to the CoN2 sites, corroborating the role of CoN2 as the primary electron acceptor for the oxidative degradation of electron-donating pollutants. In contrast, the metallic Co0 cluster exhibited a high-lying HOMO level at −3.790 eV. As illustrated in Fig. 5k, this orbital energy was well-matched with the LUMO levels of the selected contaminants. The relatively high energy of the Co0 HOMO provided a strong thermodynamic driving force for electron injection into the unoccupied orbitals of the organic molecules. This driving force was particularly pronounced for electron-deficient contaminants (which typically possess lower LUMO levels), thereby validating the enhanced reductive capability of the Co0 domains. Collectively, these orbital interactions provided a solid quantum chemical basis for the distinct selectivity of the TRC catalyst: CoN2 sites extracted electrons for oxidation, while Co0 sites donated electrons for reduction.

Catalytic devices as well as life cycle assessment (LCA) for the Co0/CoN2-SAC system

Based on the degradation mechanisms of auto-catalytic systems, we designed specific devices tailored and performed comprehensive experimental validation. In the auto-catalytic Co0/CoN2-SAC system, the catalyst was immobilized on a polyurethane foam (PUF) filter core, a highly porous and mechanically robust support material and served as reactive sites that facilitated internal electron transfer between pollutants and catalytic active centers, enabling contaminant removal without requiring additional energy input. A schematic representation of this device was shown in Fig. 6a. The SEM image confirmed uniform deposition of the catalyst on the PUF fiber (Fig. 6b). To assess its practical applicability, the PCM solution (50 μg/L) was continuously circulated through the PUF felt filtration column at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Over a 16-h continuous operation period, the PCM removal efficiency consistently exceeded 90% (Fig. 6c). In contrast, the contribution of the PUF felt alone accounted for 30–60% of PCM removal (Fig. 6c). A certain cobalt leaching (<0.12 mg/L) was observed at the first 4 h, and it was almost negligible after 16 h (Supplementary Fig. 59). Overall, the system exhibited exceptional operational feasibility and maintained high removal efficiency throughout the 16-h test. The slight performance dynamics, possibly linked to the initial leaching phase or the immobilization approach, point to areas for further optimisation to enhance long-term stability. As a result, this oxidant-free system could be used in the advanced treatment stage of water treatment (Fig. 6d). To further verify the removal of the target pollutants by the catalyst in the column experiments, numerical simulation technology based on the Groundwater Modeling System (GMS) software was employed to visualise the migration and transformation behaviors of pollutants during the column experiments. The results indicated that the pollutants underwent rapid homogeneous diffusion with limited attenuation in the catalyst-free column (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Table 6). In contrast, the diffusion rate of pollutants was significantly reduced in the catalyst-packed column. Notably, a distinct retardation effect on pollutant migration was observed at the catalyst-packed layers, which evidenced the concentration reduction and diffusion inhibition effects of the catalyst.

In addition, degradation intermediates of pollutants in the Co0/CoN2-SAC systems were also assessed. A characteristic set of polymerisation and degradation intermediates was observed for all different pollutants (Supplementary Figs. 60–65). Specifically, the highly stable nitro and carboxyl functional groups in NBA could also be degraded, providing strong evidence that the catalytic mechanism governing NBA transformation was fundamentally different from that of PCM. The degradation intermediates of PCM in WWTP effluent differed from those in ultrapure water, indicating that coexisting substances in the wastewater matrix significantly influenced their formation and transformation pathways (Supplementary Figs. 66 and 67). This degradation process regulated by electron transfer has certain selectivity, for instance, emphasizing the attack of electron-donating functional groups such as hydroxyl and amino groups. However, it can also easily lead to more subsequent changes in the products, which made it more likely for multiple forms of product changes to occur in complex actual wastewater62. We further assess the ecological safety of the Co0/CoN2-SAC system based on the wheat growth experiment (Supplementary Figs. 68–70). Results showed that root and leaf development in wheat seedlings exposed to PCM or NBA solutions after the treatment of the Co0/CoN2-SAC system was comparable to that observed in the deionized water control.

A comprehensive system boundary and LCA was further conducted to quantitatively evaluate the environmental footprint and economic feasibility of three Co0/CoN2-SAC catalytic systems (PMS-addition Co0/CoN2-SAC, PMS-free Co0/CoN2-SAC, PMS-free Co0/CoN2-SAC after acid pickling) and three conventional systems (Fenton/H2O2, Fenton/PMS and Fe3O4/PMS systems) for treating the PCM wastewaters57,63. The Ecoinvent 3.12 database was combined with the ReCiPe 2016 V1.03 Midpoint for collecting the specific datasets, which were shown in Fig. 6f and Supplementary Tables 7–1464. The three Co0/CoN2-SAC catalytic systems for the water treatment could significantly reduce the chemical usage and energy consumption via building the endogenous energy-driven system without the need of external oxidants, thereby contributing to a substantially lower environmental footprint compared to conventional catalytic systems (Fig. 6g). In addition, these systems could also achieve relatively lower carbon emission compared with the Fenton/H2O2 and Fenton/PMS systems (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 71). Overall, these Co0/CoN2-SAC-based systems demonstrated consistently lower environmental burden than conventional Fenton/PMS, Fenton/H2O2 and Fe3O4/PMS systems across the majority of midpoint impact categories (Fig. 6i), underscoring their improved sustainability profile. Future research should focus on reducing both the catalyst loading and the material inputs required for catalyst synthesis in the auto-catalytic system, thereby further enhancing its environmental performance and practical feasibility.

Discussion

Precisely designed self-driven SACs enable efficient oxidant-free removal of electron-donating ECs; however, achieving the concurrent removal of both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ECs within a single system remains a formidable challenge. In this work, a TRC catalyst (Co0/CoN2-SAC) was synthesised, featuring the coexistence of Co0 nanoclusters and atomically dispersed CoN2 sites. Results demonstrated that Co0/CoN2-SAC exhibited superior degradation performance for both ECs types, significantly outperforming conventional single-atom and nanoparticle catalysts. In contrast, after acid leaching to remove Co0 species, the resulting catalyst containing only isolated Co single atoms maintained high activity toward electron-donating ECs but showed drastically reduced efficiency for electron-accepting species. The electron-deficient region facilitated ECs adsorption through π–π interactions, and the CoN2 sites in the electron-enriched region served as electron acceptors, extracting electrons from electron-donating ECs. While the Co0 sites in the electron-deficient region served as the electron donor for donating the electron towards electron-withdrawing ECs accompanying with H* and •OH attacking, enabling efficient reductive degradation of various electron-withdrawing ECs. This dual-pathway mechanism is further supported by XANES analysis, which revealed a decrease in cobalt oxidation state from +0.11 to +0.035 after degradation of PCM, and an increase to +0.15 following NBA degradation, consistent with reductive and oxidative processes, respectively. Furthermore, the Co0/CoN2-SAC system has been successfully implemented in a dedicated catalytic module for long-term operation in actual water sample. The environmental sustainability of both configurations was quantitatively assessed using LCA. Future research should focus on optimising synthesis protocols and minimising catalyst loading to further reduce potential environmental impacts.

Methods

Chemicals

Various chemicals, such as Co(NO3)2·6H2O (≥98.5%), Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (≥99%), 2-methylimidazole (≥99%), EtOH (≥99.7%), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, ≥99%), NaOH (≥99%), H2SO4 (95–98%), NaCl (≥99.5%), NaNO3 (≥99%), Na2HPO4 (≥99%), Na2SO4 (≥99%), NaHCO3 (≥99%), trans-stilbene (≥98%) were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. The selected pollutants, such as paracetamol (PCM, ≥99.9%), bisphenol A (BPA, ≥99.5%), p-chlorophenol (CP, ≥99%), ciprofloxacin (CIP, ≥99.5%), tetracycline (TC, ≥98%), sulfamethoxazole (SMZ, ≥99%), hydroxbenzoic acid (HBA, ≥99.5%), naproxen (NPX, ≥99.5%), p-nitrophenol (NP, ≥99%) and nithbenzoic acid (NBA, ≥99.5%) were obtained from Aladdin Chemical Reagents Co., LTD.

Fabrication of Co0/CoN2-SAC

The preparation of the TRC-based Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst involves four sequential steps as follows: (i) mixing 9.75 g of 2-methylimidazole and 6.75 g of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.3 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O in 180 ml of methanol solution and stirring the mixture for 24 h; the solution was centrifuged three times at 7150 × g for 4 min, and the solids were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h and grinded; (ii) the dried sample was then put on the tube furnace and pyrolyzed with under N2 atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C/min to 1000 °C, and then burn for 2 h; (iii) the primary sample was acid washed by the 1 mol/L of sulfuric acid at 60 °C for 6 h, and washed with deionized water until pH reaching neutral. The final product (Co0/CoN2-SAC) was then dried and collected for subsequent use. The Co0-free sample was prepared via the acid pickling of Co0/CoN2-SAC, which was conducted by hot-acid washing and stirring for 24 h (under a water bath at 80 °C) to remove zero-valent cobalt. For comparison, the CN catalyst was prepared following the same procedure but without the addition of Co(NO3)2·6H2O. Co NPs/N-C was prepared by following the same procedure but without the addition of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O.

The continuous Co0/CoN2-SAC operation system was fabricated by uniformly coating 0.4 g of the Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst is onto a 3.3 g polyurethane foam (PUF) filter core, a highly porous and mechanically robust support material that ensures both high catalyst loading and minimal flow resistance. The catalyst-coated PUF was then packed into a cylindrical catalytic column (length 250 mm, diameter 25 mm). Degradation tests of the Co0/CoN2-SAC system, as well as electrochemical measurement, DFT calculation and LCA, were presented in the Supplementary file.

Experiment procedures

The degradation experiment by the oxidant-free Co0/CoN2-SAC system was implemented by introducing 0.5 g/L of Co0/CoN2-SAC catalyst into the pollutant solution. Degradation experiments were carried out over a period of 1 h, with samples collected at ten discrete time intervals. At each sampling point, 1 mL of aqueous solution was withdrawn and immediately filtered through a nylon membrane (0.22 µm). The filtered sample was promptly mixed with 0.5 ml of methanol solution to effectively quench any ongoing reaction. Degradation performance evaluations for other catalysts were conducted following identical experimental procedures. All experiments were conducted in triplicate within 100 mL conical flasks to ensure data reproducibility.

Characterization

Morphologies of the samples were determined by a scanning electron microscope (TESCAN MIRA LMS, SEM) and a transmission electron microscope (FEI Talos F200X G2, TEM). The components and structure of the samples were determined by X-ray diffraction (Rigaku SmartLab SE, XRD) equipped with Cu Kα radiation. The chemical states of different elements of these samples were detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi, XPS). The XAFS spectra of Co0/CoN2-SAC were measured by the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), China, which were performed with electron energy of 3 GeV. The radiation was monochromatized by a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. XANES and EXAFS data reduction and analysis were processed by Athena software. The HAADF-STEM images were recorded with an FEI Themis Z scanning transmission electron microscope with double aberration correctors in STEM mode. Metal contents in the Fe/N-SAC were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (Agilent 720ES). The obtained XAFS data was processed in Athena (version 0.9.26) for background, pre-edge line and post-edge line calibrations. Then Fourier transformed fitting was carried out in Artemis (version 0.9.26). The k3 weighting, k-range of 3 to ~12.5 Å−1 and R range of 1–3 Å were used for the fitting of Fe-Sample. For wavelet transform analysis, the χ(k) exported from Athena was imported into the Hama Fortran code.

Detection of pollutants and radicals

The concentration of target pollutants was determined by a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, UltiMate 3000) with a C18 column (Waters XTerra MS C18, 3.5 μm 4.6 × 250 mm) and a diode-array detector. The flow rate, wavelength and the mobile phase of different compounds are summarised in Supplementary Table 1. Total organic carbon was determined on a TOC-VCPH analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan). HPLC-MS analyses were performed on a UHR QTOF system (maXis, Bruker Co., USA), equipped with an electrospray ionization interface. Separation was conducted on an Agilent-ZORBAX SB-C18 COLUMN (150 × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 μm granulometry). The mobile phase utilised methanol and water (0.1% formic acid) with a linear gradient and a flow rate of 200 μL/min. Detection was performed in negative mode. The ionization voltage was 4.0 kV, and the capillary temperature was 335 °C.

Electrochemical analysis

The open circuit potential of Co0/CoN2-SAC coated glassy carbon electrode (Co0/CoN2-SAC/GCE) was measured by using the saturated silver chloride electrode as a reference electrode. The Co0/CoN2-SAC/GCE was prepared by dispersing 10 mg of Co0/CoN2-SAC in a N,N-dimethylformamide solution (1 ml) containing 5 wt% Nafion perfluorinated resin, and then dropping the solution onto the GCE for three times.

DFT calculation

All the calculations are performed in the framework of the density functional theory with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package. The generalized gradient approximation proposed by Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof is selected for the exchange-correlation potential. The long-range van der Waals interaction is described by the DFT-D3 approach. The cut-off energy for plane wave is set to 400 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10−5 eV in the iterative solution of the Kohn–Sham equation. A vacuum layer of 20 Å is added perpendicular to the sheet to avoid artificial interaction between periodic images. The Brillouin zone integration is performed using a 2 × 2 × 1 k-mesh. All the structures are relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.03 eV/Å.

Theoretical calculations for the highest-occupied molecular orbital energies (EHOMO, eV) and the lowest-unoccupied molecular orbital energies (ELUMO, eV) of pollutants were calculated using DFT. The calculations of possible reaction pathways were performed using the Gaussian 16 program package. Full geometry optimisations were conducted to locate all the stationary points, using the B3LYP with the Def2-SVP basis.

Fluid dynamics numerical simulation

To quantitatively characterize the coupled hydrogeological and chemical processes governing contaminant migration in porous media amended with polyurethane sponge, we integrated GMS (Groundwater Modeling System) software with its MODFLOW and RT3D modules based on laboratory column experiments. This hybrid experimental-numerical framework enabled us to upscale 1D observations to a 2D reactive transport model. First, relevant parameters are determined with reference to actual experimental conditions and the properties of ordinary polyurethane sponge at 25 °C. Then, a conceptual model is established by generalizing the column into a homogeneous confined aquifer, setting the bottom boundary as a constant head and constant concentration boundary (for contaminant input) and the top boundary as a constant head boundary (to maintain steady flow). Next, numerical models are built, where the MODFLOW module is used to simulate the groundwater flow field, obtain head distribution and flow velocity, while the RT3D module is applied to simulate contaminant advection, dispersion and chemical reactions. Through the two-way coupling of the groundwater movement equation and the advection-dispersion equation, a migration numerical model is established, and the column experiment data is used to simulate and verify the constructed model.

LCA analyze

In this study, we used Simapro 9.5 and data Ecoinvent 3.12 for modeling, and quantified the model through ReCiPe 2016 V1.03 Midpoint. The life cycle environmental effects of treating the wastewater by different processes were obtained. Through Simapro Monte Carlo simulation (1000 times), the uncertainty analysis results were obtained, including the mean value, median value, confidence interval and other statistical parameters.

Wheat seed germination experiment

The effect of treated water on the germination and growth of wheat was evaluated through a hydroponic system. First, uniformly shaped and intact wheat seeds were carefully selected and pre-treated via soaking in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min, and then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water for disinfection. Subsequently, 300 seeds were evenly distributed on three filter papers, and the filter papers were immersed in 10 mL of solution. The experimental setup was then transferred to a constant-temperature incubator. The cultivation conditions were maintained at 25 °C, with a light/dark cycle of 12 h/12 h. To compensate for evaporation, the solution was replaced every 12 h. Three treatment groups were used, with one control group (Group 1) only receiving deionized water rinsing, and the other two groups (Groups 2 and 3) irrigated with the effluent from the PCM or NBA solution treated by the Co0/CoN2-SAC system.
