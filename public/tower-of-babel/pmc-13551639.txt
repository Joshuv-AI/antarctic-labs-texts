When CO2 sensing fails: Protective effects of sex hormones and potential mechanisms
Authors: Tara A Janes, Silvia Cardani, Silvia Pagliardini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreathing movements driven by the brainstem rhythmogenic neural networks rely on chemosensory inputs to produce a motor output that is homeostatically adjusted to the prevailing metabolic demand. The central CO2 chemoreflex is a critical component of respiratory neural circuitry, as defects in these sensors cause hypoventilation syndromes, which are typically difficult to manage pharmacologically. Progesterone has long been known to stimulate breathing in both sexes, and remarkably, a potent progestin drug, etonogestrel (ETO), has been shown to enhance CO2‐chemosensitivity in animal models and in a subset of female patients affected by congenital central hypoventilation syndrome. Our recent work shows that systemic chronic ETO treatment recovered the CO2 chemoreflex in female rats in which <80% of neurons of the key CO2 chemosensory structure of the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN) were eliminated, whereas male rats with similar lesions and ETO serum levels did not show recovery of the CO2 chemoreflex. Interestingly, female respiratory recovery was associated with increased expression of the pH sensors Task2 and Gpr4 mRNA in the surviving RTN neurons, suggesting that progestin drugs may recover the CO2 chemoreflex responses in a sex‐specific fashion.Keywords: CO2 chemoreflex, etonogestrel, progesterone, respiration, sex differences

INTRODUCTION

In mammals, the maintenance of arterial blood gases—the partial pressures of oxygen (pO2) and carbon dioxide (pCO2)—is a fundamental homeostatic requirement for sustaining cellular metabolism and maintaining systemic pH, both of which are critical for optimal cellular function. 1 , 2 Unlike slower‐acting regulatory systems, such as renal compensation, the respiratory system provides rapid, real‐time adjustments to match ventilation with metabolic demands. This review examines central CO2 chemoreception—the primary driver of breathing under most physiological conditions—and the modulatory influence of sex hormones on this process. Given that even minor fluctuations in pCO2 elicit robust changes in ventilatory output, understanding the nuances of this system is critical for addressing the mechanistic basis of respiratory failure and autonomic instability.

CO2 sensing in the brain

The high metabolic rate characteristic of mammals necessitates high‐fidelity sensory feedback to detect rapid deviations in blood chemistry. This feedback is provided by specialized chemoreceptors located within both the peripheral and the central nervous system. While the cellular mechanisms of signal transduction remain a subject of active investigation, the conserved nature of CO2 sensors in particular underscores the evolutionary importance of respiratory stability function. 1

Central CO2 chemoreception involves an integrated network of CO2/H+‐sensitive sites within the brain, with varying contributions to the overall ventilatory response. While several structures have been proposed as chemosensitive, current evidence highlights some key nuclei: (a) the Retrotrapezoid Nucleus (RTN), widely considered as a master integrator of central CO2 drive, 1 (b) the Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (NTS), a primary relay station for peripheral afferents that also possesses intrinsic chemosensitivity, 3 , 4 , 5 (c) the medullary Raphe containing serotonergic neurons that provide a tonic drive to the respiratory network and enhance the ventilatory response to CO2, 6 , 7 (d) the locus coeruleus (LC), containing CO2 chemosensitive neurons that enhance ventilation and coordinate arousal and panic responses, 8 (e) the hypothalamus, a homeostatic structure containing orexinergic neurons that enhance sympathetic outflow and breathing 9 , 10 that may function as state‐dependent CO2 chemoreceptors, 11 and finally (f) specialized glia surrounding the chemosensitive and rhythmogenic neurons that release signaling molecules (e.g., ATP) in response to pH changes. 12 , 13 Additionally, while the carotid bodies (CB) primarily function as oxygen sensors, 2 their ability to detect rapid changes in pCO2 allows them to contribute to the ventilatory response to hypercapnia. 14 , 15 , 16 Although overwhelming evidence establishes the RTN as the main center for central CO2 chemoreception, the other structures mentioned above likely serve a supplementary function, making modest contributions in coordination with the RTN. 17

This integrated system operates continuously under basal conditions to support eupneic breathing. However, its high sensitivity also renders it vulnerable to dysfunction. Pathological perturbations in chemosensory control are implicated in several clinical conditions, most notably sleep‐disordered breathing (such as central sleep apnea or Cheyne‐Stokes ventilation), hypoventilation syndromes (e.g., Congenital Central Hypoventilation Syndrome, CCHS), and Sudden Infant Death Syndrome.

CENTRAL CO2 ‐CHEMORECEPTION AND BREATHING

Ventilatory responses to pCO2 during wake and sleep

The respiratory neural networks described above exhibit an exquisite sensitivity to fluctuations in arterial pCO2 and pH, whereby even minute deviations can trigger robust homeostatic adjustments in ventilatory output. The physiological “gain” of this system—the magnitude of the ventilatory response relative to the change in pCO2—is a critical determinant of respiratory stability during wake and sleep. On one hand, impaired sensory input or defects in its central integration can lead to blunted chemosensitivity, contributing to chronic hypopnea, recurrent apnea and hypercarbia/hypoxemia as observed in some hypoventilation syndromes. 18 Conversely, pathologically high chemoreflex responsiveness for a given stimulus (i.e., increased loop gain) is hypothesized to underlie sleep apneas, Cheyne‐Stokes breathing as well as panic disorder in which mild hyperventilation and hypocarbia under resting conditions are prominent symptoms. 19 , 20 , 21

During sleep, excitatory inputs to central respiratory networks are diminished, body metabolism decreases, and muscle tone is reduced, favoring slower and more variable breathing, particularly during rapid‐eye movement (REM) sleep. Ventilatory responses to pCO2 are also blunted during non‐rapid‐eye movement (NREM) and REM sleep. 22 , 23 This creates a state of “vulnerability” during which respiratory pathology is most likely to manifest, and indeed many hypoventilation syndromes present during sleep. High loop gain is particularly relevant during sleep when the apneic threshold (the level of pCO2 below which the metabolic drive to breathe ceases) is increased. Ventilatory overcompensation for small rises in pCO2 leads to hypocapnia, subsequently crossing the apneic threshold and triggering central apneas that may resolve in arousal. This cycle of arousal, hyperpnea, and subsequent apnea underscores the importance of a fully functional chemoreflex during sleep.

Phox2b‐expressing neurons in the CO2 ‐chemoreflex network

In mammals, a classic sensory‐integration arc for CO2 chemoreception is modeled as the pathway spanning the CB, the NTS, and the ventral respiratory column, with a major role for the RTN. 24 While this “simplified” view of CO2‐sensing admittedly excludes other putative CO2‐sensing regions such as the hypothalamus, LC, and medullary raphe, research suggests that the CB‐NTS‐RTN pathway contributes collectively to ~70% of the ventilatory response to CO2. 16 A defining genetic feature of this central CO2‐chemoreflex arc is the adult expression of the transcription factor paired‐like homeobox 2b (Phox2b), which is essential for the development of several autonomic structures 25 , 26 , 27 and persists in discrete populations of adult neurons. 28 , 29 The notable absence of Phox2b in many rhythmogenic and premotor respiratory neurons of the medulla suggests that this factor may play a specialized role in supporting the chemoreceptive phenotype in mature neural networks. 30

Primary contributions within the Phox2b‐expressing CO2‐chemoreflex arc arise mainly from the NTS and the RTN neurons, with modest inputs from the CBs. A large proportion of adult neurons in the caudal NTS express Phox2b (NTSPhox2b), although not all are chemoreceptive or respiratory‐related. 5 Broadly inhibiting caudal NTS neurons with muscimol blunted the hypercapnic ventilatory response in wakefulness and NREM sleep in adult rats. 31 In mice, chemogenic stimulation of NTSPhox2b neurons potentiated the hypercapnic ventilatory response, while chemical or genetic ablation of the same population blunted ventilatory responses to CO2 (~20% impairment during 8% CO2 inhalation). 4 , 32 This effect appears to arise from a fraction of chemoreceptive NTSPhox2b neurons in the caudal NTS (~40%) that increased firing rate during bath acidification, 4 likely mediated by the expression of transient receptor potential channel 5 (TRPC5). 5

Phox2b‐expressing neurons in the RTN (RTNPhox2b) play a pivotal role in central chemoreception, being directly activated by changes in pH as well as being a major integrator of stimuli originating from other CO2 activated regions (NTS, hypothalamus, LC, and medullary raphe). In fact, large‐scale lesions (chemical or genetic induction), 33 , 34 , 35 or acute optogenetic inhibition 36 of the RTN can severely blunt or virtually abolish ventilatory responses to hypercapnia, implicating the RTN as the critical output hub that not only contributes to CO2‐sensing but also integrates chemosensory inputs from other regions of the brain.

The RTN has been defined in rodents by a complex molecular profile: these neurons originate from the cellular lineage that expresses early growth response 2 (Egr2), Atonal bHLH transcription factor 1 (Atoh1), Ladybird homeobox 1 (Lbx1), and Phox2b. 35 , 37 , 38 , 39 In adulthood they are characterized by the expression of neuromedin B (Nmb), the vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (Slc17a6), neurokinin receptor 1 (NK1R), galanin, and the pituitary adenylate cyclase‐activating polypeptide (PACAP). 40 , 41 The intrinsic CO2 chemosensory properties of the RTN are attributed to the partially overlapping expression in Nmb/Phox2b‐expressing neurons of the proton‐activated G protein‐coupled receptor 4 (GPR4) and the pH‐sensitive background K+ channel TWIK‐related acid‐sensitive K+ channel‐2 (TASK‐2, Kcnk5). Evidence from knockout models indicates that these sensors work in a summative manner. 42 , 43 However, it should be noted that RTN neurons are molecularly heterogeneous, with a small subset in mice lacking GPR4 or TASK‐2 (10%–20%). 40 Similar to rodent data, putative RTN neurons expressing vesicular glutamate transporter 2, galanin, NK1R, PACAP and PHOX2B have been localized in the same anatomical region in macaques 44 and humans, but the details of co‐expression vary between studies. 45 , 46 , 47

HYPOVENTILATION SYNDROMES—“WHEN THE CO2 ‐CHEMOREFLEX FAILS”

Hypoventilation syndromes: A broad definition

Hypoventilation syndromes represent a heterogeneous group of disorders defined by an inadequate ventilatory response to metabolic demands, which leads to elevated arterial pCO2 levels (hypercapnia) and, frequently, concomitant hypoxemia. These conditions stem from structural or functional dysfunctions across the respiratory control system that manifest as a severely blunted automatic “drive to breathe,” a phenomenon that is particularly pronounced during sleep when the wakefulness stimulus is absent and the system relies exclusively on metabolic feedback. The International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD‐3) categorizes hypoventilation syndromes by their underlying pathophysiology. These disorders span central syndromes involving primary brainstem or chemosensory failure, such as CCHS and rapid‐onset obesity with hypothalamic dysregulation, hypoventilation, and autonomic dysregulation (ROHHAD), obesity hypoventilation syndrome, defined by chronic daytime hypercapnia in obese individuals, and mechanical “respiratory pump” failures resulting from restrictive lung diseases, chest wall deformities, or neuromuscular disorders. Additionally, acute respiratory failure can be induced by the central nervous system‐depressing effects of pharmacological agents like opioids and benzodiazepines. 18 , 48 In the next sections we will focus on the respiratory dysfunction associated with the RTN impairment that has been proposed to be the main pathological feature of CCHS patients.

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome as a model of RTN dysfunction

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome is a genetic disorder primarily caused by mutations in the PHOX2B gene, a master regulator transcription factor essential for the development of the entire visceral reflex circuitry and the autonomic nervous system. 49 , 50 Genetically, the disorder is heterogeneous, which results in clinical presentation and severity that differ significantly between individuals, underscoring the need for individualized patient care. The majority of cases (approximately 90%) are caused by heterozygous polyalanine repeat expansion mutations (PARMs) in the third exon, where the normal 20‐alanine tract expands by +5 to +13 repeats, resulting in genotypes ranging from 20/25 to 20/33. The severity of multi‐organ autonomic dysfunction has been correlated with the expansion mutation length 50 , 51 with no apparent sex preference, and survival of CCHS patients is highly influenced by the concomitant presence of Hirschsprung disease. 52

In patients with shorter PARMs, alveolar hypoventilation and apnea, hallmarks of the disease, present mostly as nocturnal hypoventilation/apnea while patients with longer PARMs experience ventilatory disturbance during sleep and wake as well as chronic hypercarbia. 53 Critically, impaired ventilatory sensitivity to pCO2/pH and hypoxemia likely contribute, at least in part, to hypoventilation and apnea. The remaining cases are caused by non‐polyalanine repeat expansion mutations (NPARMs), such as missense, nonsense, or frameshift mutations, which often (but not always) result in more severe clinical phenotypes. 50 , 54 , 55

Even before the discovery of PHOX2B mutations in CCHS patients, 49 extensive studies showed that during embryonic development, PHOX2B is indispensable for the specification of the noradrenergic phenotype and the formation of key nodes in the central respiratory network. 25 , 26 , 27 Interestingly, the introduction of the polyalanine expansion mutations in this gene, to replicate the CCHS disease in animal models, disrupts the differentiation and migration of the RTN neurons, leading to complete RTN agenesis and profound functional impairment of the CO2‐chemoreflex. 38 , 56 , 57 , 58 Although direct clinical confirmation of RTN agenesis in CCHS patients remains challenging, imaging studies have identified significant structural anomalies in the brainstem. Given that PHOX2B is required for the development of many autonomic nuclei that underlie behaviors that are dysfunctional in CCHS (respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, neurologic, ophthalmologic, thermoregulatory and endocrine), it is likely that the PHOX2B mutation results in a multi‐nodal disruption of autonomic networks rather than a localized lesion alone, as supported by evidence in the imaging studies. 59 , 60 For example, changes in axial diffusivity in diffusion tensor imaging studies, a measure of axonal damage, highlighted some defects not only in the ventral medulla close to the RTN location, but also in the periaqueductal gray, the raphe nuclei and the superior cerebellar decussation. 60

The cellular mechanisms by which mutant PHOX2B drives the pathophysiology of CCHS remain uncertain. One leading theory is haploinsufficiency, 61 though respiratory dysfunction might also be caused by toxic “gain‐of‐function” of the mutated protein. 62 , 63 , 64 Because PHOX2B is a fundamental transcriptional regulator, it is highly probable that transcriptional dysregulation linked to reduced levels of PHOX2B or ectopic expression of the mutant protein results in the abnormal expression of target genes and plays a major role in the disease. Furthermore, in vitro research has also identified the formation of protein aggregates in the presence of the mutant PHOX2B within the neural cytoplasm, 65 suggesting another potential mechanism by which transcriptional activity might be impaired. Regarding the role of PHOX2B in CO2‐chemoreception, recent work from our laboratory has shown that in vivo knockdown of PHOX2B in adult RTN neurons of rats impaired the CO2 chemoreflex and reduced the expression of acid‐sensing Gpr4 and Task2 mRNA in RTN neurons (Figure 1). 30 The link between PHOX2B and regulation of ion channel expression has been further investigated in neuroblastoma cell lines where PHOX2B knockout induced transcriptional repression of several ion channels that altered the electrical properties of cells in vitro, 66 suggesting a key role for PHOX2B in maintaining neuronal function and excitability.

Given the heterogeneity in the underlying genetic mutations, the diverse autonomic pathology and the uncertainty in cellular mechanisms underlying these dysfunctions, CCHS patients remain intractable. Currently, standard care involves assisted ventilation during sleep (and in some cases during wakefulness) or diaphragmatic pacing, both of which have drawbacks and associated health risks. Therefore, there has been a push to develop pharmacological interventions aimed primarily at stabilizing and stimulating breathing.

PROGESTERONE AS A MODULATOR OF THE CO2 ‐CHEMOREFLEX AND THERAPEUTIC POTENTIAL FOR HYPOVENTILATION SYNDROMES

Expression of sex‐hormone receptors in the brain and local synthesis

While classically defined by their peripheral reproductive functions, sex hormones—more accurately termed neurosteroids in this context—function as potent neuromodulators within the central nervous system (CNS). These lipophilic molecules readily cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), but they are also synthesized de novo by neurons and astrocytes. 67 The local synthesis of sex‐hormones, particularly within the hippocampus, hypothalamus and pons 68 , 69 offers a distinct homeostatic advantage. By producing neurosteroids locally, the CNS can achieve rapid, efficient signaling within specific neural circuits without inducing systemic hormonal shifts that would affect broader reproductive or somatic functions. Within the brain, these neurosteroids serve as critical mediators for neural health by modulating diverse functions such as neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, glucose metabolism, myelin repair and neuroprotection. 70 , 71 , 72 Progesterone also plays a particularly important role in tuning the excitatory‐inhibitory balance of the brain by acting as a positive allosteric modulator on GABAergic receptors, increasing glutamatergic uptake and upregulating AMPA receptors. 73

Nuclear progesterone receptors (PRs), which act as transcriptional regulators, are widely expressed in the brain of both sexes, 70 with the specific expression of the “A” and “B” isoforms and splice variants differing by region, developmental age and sex. Importantly, PR expression is abundant within the primary respiratory control centers, the hypothalamus, the chemosensitive nuclei of the NTS, the LC and the respiratory‐related motoneurons 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 and within the peripheral carotid bodies. However, molecular data from our laboratory previously suggested that PR expression within the RTN may be low. 74 Nonetheless, PR receptor distribution through respiratory control networks suggests that progesterone is well‐positioned to act as an intrinsic neuromodulator of the ventilatory drive. In addition to its actions through classic nuclear receptors, PG can also influence neural activity on shorter timescales (several minutes) via membrane receptor (mPR) activation of G‐protein intracellular signaling pathways. 78 There is indeed evidence that mPRs are expressed in brainstem respiratory control areas (NTS), hypoglossal motonucleus and dorsal vagal complex 78 ; and their knockdown impairs hypercapnic ventilation in male and female mice. 79

Modulation of the CO2 ‐chemoreflex by female sex‐hormones

An early association between progesterone and breathing was first established in pregnant women as a potential mechanism to explain modest hyperventilation and concomitant hypocapnia observed during late‐stage pregnancy. 80 Building on these observations, a growing body of clinical research has identified progesterone as stimulatory for restful breathing and CO2‐chemoreflexes 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 and sex differences in ventilation, CO2 chemosensitivity, as well as occurrence of sleep disordered breathing were observed. 85 , 86 , 87 In animal models, the case for progesterone's ability to enhance ventilation has been generally affirmative, 84 , 88 , 89 , 90 but not without some ambiguity given the fact that stimulatory effects in many preclinical studies are either modest or mostly observed in ovariectomized rats. Recent data obtained from our laboratory indicates that in naturally cycling female Sprague–Dawley rats, hormonal level fluctuations occurring with each estrus stage had no significant influence on restful breathing or the hypercapnic ventilatory response (Figure 2A,B; unpublished data), and are in agreement with previous studies. 91 Similarly, recent meta‐analysis determined that while restful ventilation was higher in women during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, serum progesterone only accounted for a small part of the ventilatory effect. 92

These data therefore suggest that the natural, cyclic fluctuations of progesterone may have only modest effects on breathing. However, progesterone supplementation well‐above normal circulating levels, or the use of synthetic progestins (which have higher affinity for PRs) may have more potent effects on ventilation. The use of progesterone as a potential therapy for alveolar hypoventilation was tested in patients (male and female) with obesity hypoventilation syndrome 93 and resulted in progressive enhancement of minute ventilation and reduction of pCO2 over 3 weeks. Following these promising results, synthetic progestins were later tested as a method to improve ventilation in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and obstructive sleep apnea, with variable results. 83 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 Further considerations such as age, sex, hormonal levels, region‐specific receptor expression (including isoform expression), agonist binding affinity and potential androgenic effects should be considered when evaluating the efficacy of progesterone and its synthetic derivatives to stimulate ventilation.

Clinical management of CCHS and the “progestin breakthrough”

The clinical management of respiratory dysfunction in CCHS patients remains primarily supportive, relying on lifelong mechanical ventilation during sleep or, in some cases, diaphragm pacing, depending on the severity of the mutation and the consequent autonomic dysfunction. For decades, pharmacological interventions to stimulate breathing in these patients yielded disappointing results. However, a major advancement occurred with the serendipitous discovery that the progestin desogestrel, used for contraception, showed a dramatic and unexpected partial recovery of the CO2 chemoreflex response in CCHS patients. 99 This initial observation was subsequently supported by further evidence that desogestrel increased baseline respiratory frequency and reduced end‐tidal CO2 (PETCO2). 100 Despite these promising results, ventilatory responsiveness to progestin treatment was complicated by significant inter‐individual variability, which appears closely linked to the patient's genetic profile. Evidence gathered since 2010 suggests that the recovery of chemosensitivity is more frequent in patients with shorter PARM expansions, such as the 20/25 and 20/26 genotypes, who typically retain a less severe respiratory impairment. 99 , 100 Conversely, patients with non‐PARM mutations or longer PARM expansions (20/27 or higher), which present with more severe respiratory symptoms, failed to incur significant benefits with desogestrel. 101

MECHANISMS OF PROGESTIN ACTION

Despite the paucity of clinical data supporting the effectiveness of desogestrel (and its active metabolite etonogestrel, ETO) treatment in CCHS patients, a few laboratories started investigating the potential effects associated with hormonal administration on respiration in order to better understand mechanisms and sites of action responsible for ventilatory recovery. Studies in rodent models demonstrated that acute administration of ETO increased breathing rate in newborn rats, increased phrenic nerve activity in in vitro preparations under basal and acidosis conditions, and increased minute ventilation in a CCHS mouse model. 100 , 102 , 103 These effects were attributed to the action of ETO on the activity of the serotonergic and orexinergic systems. 100 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105

In our laboratory we first established a model for the chronic delivery of ETO in female rats using size‐adjusted medical grade Nexplanon® implants (Organon), in order to reproduce the long‐term administration of progestin‐only contraception in rats at ETO serum concentrations comparable to humans. 74 In that experimental setting, we showed that chronic ETO administration showed modest results on ventilation in healthy female rats, accompanied by a reduction in oxygen consumption (V̇O2) and carbon dioxide production (V̇CO2) compared to sham‐operated controls, suggesting that in a physiologically intact system, chronic progestin exposure acts as a subtle metabolic modulator rather than a potent primary ventilatory stimulant (Figure 2C). Changes in metabolic activity were also supported by the modest weight gain observed in our rats, similar to what was observed following long‐term contraceptive use in women. 106 , 107 Interestingly, we also observed a reduction in the expression of PHOX2B and its target genes at the level of the NTS (Figure 2D), 74 confirming previous in vitro studies that established a link between desogestrel administration, PHOX2B expression levels and its transcriptional activity. 108

The therapeutic potential of ETO, however, became evident when the central CO2 chemoreflex was compromised in female rats. 33 Using a model of targeted RTN impairment 34 —where chemosensitive neurons are eliminated via saporin‐conjugated toxins targeting NK1R‐expressing neurons in the RTN—we demonstrated that chronic (but not acute) ETO treatment effectively rescued the hypercapnic ventilatory response in female rats after 3 weeks of treatment (Figure 3A). 33 , 109 A critical mechanistic insight derived from this lesion model is the existence of a threshold effect for functional recovery. In fact, we observed that CO2 chemoreflex rescue was strictly contingent upon the extent of the neuronal loss: functional recovery was exclusively observed with RTN lesions that extended up to 80% of the RTN population. Conversely, the treatment failed to restore the chemoreflex when >80% of RTN neurons were eliminated. 33 The finding that less severe lesion phenotypes in rats were responsive to ETO treatment agrees with anecdotal clinical reports that less severe CCHS phenotypes with a shorter polyalanine expansion also benefitted more from ETO as compared to more severe genotypes. 101

Possible explanations for these results are that progestins (and ETO in particular) are effective in stimulating the activity of the surviving RTN neurons or recruiting the supportive role of other chemosensitive nuclei in the brain to promote recovery of the CO2‐chemoreflex response. Interestingly, a combination of these two mechanisms could account for this remarkable recovery.

Sex‐specific effects of ETO on ventilatory recovery

We initially performed experiments of ETO supplementation in female rats with RTN lesions; however, we questioned whether the ETO‐driven CO2‐chemoreflex recovery could apply to both sexes. 109 Interestingly, we observed a striking sex‐dimorphism in ventilatory responsiveness to ETO treatment: despite exhibiting similar RTN lesions, comparable PGR expression levels in several CO2 chemosensitive structures, and achieving identical plasma ETO levels to their female counterparts, male rats failed to demonstrate any respiratory recovery (Figure 3B). 109

At the molecular level, this sex difference may be explained by data showing that ETO‐mediated recovery in females correlated with a significant transcriptional upregulation of the key pH sensors GPR4 and Task2 in the RTN region (Figure 3D). Conversely, males failed to upregulate these receptors, which are critical for CO2 chemoreception. Although it is not known if the pH sensors in the RTN are differentially regulated between sexes, our recent results indicate that in naïve rats the levels of Gpr4 mRNA in both the RTN and the NTS in males were elevated compared to female rats, while Task2 mRNA levels were not different. 109 Whether this observation translates into different levels of protein expression, or in a different ability to respond to CO2 remains to be established. Nonetheless, the results obtained in RTN lesioned rats suggest that ETO, a progestin with strong PR affinity and little androgenic effects, drives a female‐specific transcriptional program that is effectively “locked” or inaccessible in males.

While the correlation between ETO administration and upregulation of pH sensors is robust in female rats, 109 it remains to be determined whether this is a direct genomic effect of ETO on PRs—via binding to progesterone response elements (PREs) on the Gpr4 and Task2 promoters—or an indirect consequence of activation of other PR signaling pathways within the surviving RTN neurons. Such effects could also be mediated by intermediary transcription factors, secondary signaling cascades, such as MAPK/ERK pathways, 70 or even post‐transcriptional mechanisms that stabilize mRNA levels. Several non‐exclusive mechanisms may contribute to the molecular changes at the level of the RTN that support sex‐dependent CO2‐chemoreflex recovery. These include the presence of a sex‐specific PR isoform ratio (PR‐A vs. PR‐B) in RTN neurons, where the two isoforms may exhibit opposite transcriptional activities, 110 , 111 , 112 or differences in the recruitment of nuclear co‐activators like SRC‐1, as observed with estrogen. 113 Additionally, epigenetic constraints or sex‐specific secondary signaling cascades, such as the MAPK/ERK pathways, 70 might prevent the upregulation of chemosensory markers in male rats.

While the likely potentiation of surviving RTN neurons through enhanced expression of pH sensors is the primary outcome of our functional recovery studies, a broader network perspective is essential, as contributions from other putative CO2‐chemosensitive structures—such as the NTS, the raphe, or the hypothalamus—cannot be excluded. Upon ETO treatment, these structures could undergo functional plasticity and enhance their CO2 sensitivity or could provide additional stimulation to the surviving RTN neurons. Still, the lack of response in male rats points toward a fundamental divergence in intracellular signaling across multiple chemosensory gateways in a sex‐dependent manner.

Implications for treatment and future directions

Both preclinical and clinical studies show promising results with ETO treatment that are both dependent on sex and severity of the disease. 99 , 100 , 102 Although translating these findings into clinical practice that commences at birth presents significant challenges (e.g., potential disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, altered infant growth), it is important to reflect on the potential benefit that a stimulatory approach may have on the chemoreflex response of CCHS patients. Given the heterogeneity of the genetics and underlying phenotypes, individualized care and treatment would be beneficial.

A deeper understanding of the molecular pathways underlying CCHS pathogenesis will facilitate the identification of specific cellular and molecular targets disrupted by the PHOX2B mutation. Recent studies in cell lines point to dysregulation of ion channel expression as key factors that may compromise neuronal excitability or function resulting from the expression of mutated PHOX2B. 66 It will now be critical to determine whether these alterations also occur in CCHS animal models and, most importantly, in CCHS patients.

Looking ahead, identifying the cellular and network pathways that systemic hormonal administration stimulates to enhance ventilation will be critical for future drug development aimed at promoting functional recovery and bypassing PHOX2B dysfunction. Targeting the downstream molecular environment affected by ETO treatment could bypass the need for hormonal therapy entirely, offering a viable respiratory therapeutic strategy for patients of both sexes while avoiding systemic hormonal side effects.
