Empathy training in healthcare: an umbrella review
Authors: Aniela Edwards, Krista-Gaie Grant, Nicole C Hammer, Miad Alfaqih, Kyi Phyu Nyein, Alice Abia-Okon, Rachel Grossman, Megan E Gregory
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpathy among healthcare professionals is declining due to burnout and demands of clinical practice. By honing empathetic skills, clinicians can improve patient-centered care, communication, trust, and healthcare outcomes. While prior reviews on empathy training in healthcare exist, many focus on narrow inclusion criteria, resulting in a lack of a comprehensive understanding of empathy training across the broad spectrum of healthcare. We conducted an umbrella review of these reviews to assess the impacts of various training, educational, or experiential learning methods on empathy among healthcare providers, staff, and students.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using terms such as “empathy,” “compassion,” and “training,” to identify systematic, scoping, and narrative reviews on empathy training for healthcare workers and students. Two coders screened and coded the reviews, including their inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., sample, training methods), number of studies included, and results of each review. Study quality for each review was assessed using AMSTAR.ResultsAcross 42 reviews, 71% conclude that empathy training is associated with positive outcomes for students, staff, providers, or patients. Empathy training appears to be effective for all health-related professions, including both students and practicing healthcare workers. Training that encourages active participation (e.g., experiential learning, reflective exercises) is generally associated with better outcomes compared to more passive methods. Training that integrates self-reflection and incorporates patients into the training also appears more impactful.ConclusionsLearner engagement and training methods significantly influence empathy training outcomes. There is a need to standardize empathy assessment tools and increase long-term follow-up to assess sustainability of outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08219-y.Keywords: Empathy, Education, Medical, Communication, Health personnel, Training

Introduction

Empathy, defined as the ability to perceive and resonate with the emotions of others, is an important skill for healthcare professionals to improve patient care [1, 2]. Specifically, empathy has been associated with increased patient satisfaction, improved patient trust, and enhanced physician attitude, which can lead to better clinical outcomes [3]. Furthermore, empathy is also beneficial for healthcare professionals. There is evidence that physicians who receive empathy training report higher levels of compassion satisfaction, reduced burnout, and increased resilience [4].

Empathy training plays a pivotal role in fostering effective communication between doctors and patients, leading to the establishment of trust-based relationships [2, 3, 5]. Patient-centered communication, which includes empathy as a core component, has similarly been positively associated with patient satisfaction [5]. By honing their empathetic skills, doctors can navigate difficult conversations, address patient concerns, and foster a sense of partnership, which is essential for shared decision-making and collaborative care. Heightened communication and trust-building not only contribute to better patient outcomes, but also create a more positive and therapeutic healthcare experience for patients. Additionally, positive healthcare experiences boost the morale of workers, leading to higher job satisfaction and decreased burnout [6]. On the other hand, empathy training can expose healthcare professionals to emotional exhaustion and burnout. The demands of empathetic care, coupled with the challenges of managing patients’ emotions, can contribute to burnout among healthcare professionals and lead to compassion fatigue [7]. Therefore, there are some conflicting results regarding empathy training and outcomes for healthcare professionals.

Moreover, many healthcare professionals often face pressures that lead to a decline in empathy [4, 6, 8]. The emphasis on productivity and patient outcomes, with little focus on fostering a doctor-patient relationship, may cause emotional exhaustion and burnout. This reduced emphasis on emotional vulnerability can negatively affect the empathy of healthcare professionals [4, 7, 8]. Interventions targeting senior leadership to emphasize the importance of the doctor-patient relationship and fostering a compassionate work environment can be a significant step toward reversing this trend [8].

One of the reasons for such conflicting results is that empathy represents a multifaceted construct (e.g., empathy as an affective state or behavior). Empathy comprises three main components: behavioral, cognitive, and affective [1, 3, 5]. Behavioral empathy is demonstrated through actions that convey understanding of another person’s feelings [1, 5]. Cognitive empathy refers to the ability to understand and take on another’s perspective of someone else [2, 3, 5]. Affective empathy is the ability to share and resonate with the emotions of others [1, 3, 4]. Each aspect contributes uniquely to interactions between medical professionals and patients: affective empathy helps healthcare professionals to connect emotionally with patients, cognitive empathy enables understanding of the patient experience, and behavioral empathy allows this understanding to be communicated through supportive actions [1, 3, 5]. Further contributing to this is the fact that existing measurement tools for assessing empathy only capture certain facets of empathy (e.g., Questionnaire of Cognitive and Affective Empathy measuring cognitive and affective states), and there is a lack of standardized approach to measuring empathy. Moreover, it can be influenced by various factors, such as dispositions, culture, ethnicity, gender, and specialty disparities [9]. There is also evidence that empathy is not immutable and can be nurtured and molded [10]. In studies stratifying participants based on their baseline empathy, individuals with overall higher empathy have less pronounced longitudinal declines over time. Furthermore, several types of empathetic interventions have shown promise in augmenting empathy among receptive individuals.

While there are several prior reviews on empathy training in healthcare, many of these have focused on fairly narrow inclusion criteria. For example, Winter et al. included only randomized controlled trials in their 2020 review and only qualitative studies in their 2022 review [11]. Meneze et al. (2021) focused only on medical students in their review, and Nembhard et al. (2022) only included quantitative studies in their review [12, 13]. This results in the lack of a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of empathy training in healthcare, despite the prevalence of reviews on the topic. Therefore, to better understand the multifaceted nature of empathy and its training, we aimed to conduct an umbrella review to integrate all of these prior reviews to assess the impacts of various training, educational, or experiential learning methods or interventions on empathy among healthcare providers, staff, and students. Specifically, we sought to understand: (1) who is participating in empathy training in healthcare?, (2) what types of educational methods are most commonly utilized in healthcare empathy training?, (3) whether healthcare empathy training is associated with positive or negative outcomes, and (4) whether the effectiveness of empathy training differs based on learner type (student, healthcare professional), profession (nurse, physician, other), or educational method (didactic, simulation, reflective writing, etc.).

Search process

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, published from inception to June 2023. We structured the search using specific MeSH terms such as “empathy,” “compassion,” “healthcare workers,” “training,” and “patience,” utilizing the operators such as “AND” and “OR” to refine our search. We used the Covidence platform for a systematic screening and tracking process. Two coders conducted the title and abstract screening. If the title appeared ambiguous, we proceeded to read the entire article, as necessary, to determine if the article met our predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, the coders conducted the full-text screening, in which two coders screened full-text articles and came to a consensus.

Data extraction

For each article, we coded: first author name, year, title, journal, abstract content, aim of review/gap statement, review type, databases searched by the article, search terms used, search start and end date of the review, a full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria (including population, intervention type, study design outcomes), total number of studies included in review, and overall findings. Given the variety of educational intervention types included in the reviews, we subsequently categorized similar educational methods into common groupings based on the descriptions provided by the reviews related to their inclusion criteria for educational method. For example, reviews that aimed to include studies on role-play, simulation, or other practice-based methods were categorized as “experiential learning” methods.

Two coders used a standardized data extraction form on Covidence to code all articles that were deemed relevant upon full text review. To establish consensus, coders first independently coded 3 of the same articles and discussed discrepancies. This process was iterative, with 3 rounds of coding 3 of the same articles until inter-coder agreement was close to 100%. Subsequently, the coders divided up data extraction for the remaining articles.

Quality and bias review

The included reviews were assessed using the AMSTAR tool, A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews, which is designed to evaluate the methodological quality and risk of bias of systematic reviews, including those that contain randomized and/or non-randomized studies [14]. AMSTAR is a validated and widely used instrument in evidence-based healthcare [14], developed to help users differentiate between reviews based on methodological rigor and to support the development of high-quality systematic reviews [14]. The tool covers 16 critical criteria, such as protocol registration, clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, thorough explanation of results, and assessment of risk of bias. The full list of questions is in Appendix A. Each included review was independently coded using the AMSTAR tool by two members of the research team. Following individual assessments, the two reviewers scheduled consensus meetings to compare their results. Any discrepancies in coding were discussed and resolved during these meetings to ensure consistency and agreement across all domains. We selected AMSTAR for its established reliability and its specific focus on evidence-based healthcare literature.

Reflexivity

Team members involved in screening and coding articles were AE and KG. Team members involved in quality assessment coding were NCH and MA. Team members involved in data interpretation, synthesis, and writing included all of these team members, plus KPN, AAO, RG, and MEG. AE and KG are medical students, MA is a graduate student and NCH is a staff member in a college of medicine, RG is an undergraduate student, AAO was an undergraduate student at the time this project began and now is a research data associate in a school of medicine, KPN is a non-clinical researcher fellow in a medical university, and MEG is a non-clinical faculty member in a college of medicine. These professions and current training may have played a role in how the data were coded or findings were interpreted. We attempted to mitigate this by ensuring > 1 individual screened and coded each article, and by using standardized data extraction templates. We acknowledge that the potential for biases remains.

Learner types and professions

Empathy interventions have proven effective at improving empathy among all types of healthcare professionals and throughout the varied stages of training. Results of the review studies have consistently indicated that empathy interventions improve empathy measures in medical students, nursing students, nurses, doctors, and other healthcare providers. However, the extent of improvement differs depending on the specific intervention used, the population of the healthcare professionals being studied, and the stage of training of the professionals and students [11, 12, 38, 44]. Other factors that influence the degree of change in empathy from interventions studied include baseline empathy levels, the level of engagement elicited from the population being studied, and other responsibilities the professionals may have which may impact their focus on the program [11, 12, 44, 48].

Medical students (n = 12, 28.6%) and nursing students (n = 7, 16.7%) were very frequently represented in review studies regarding empathy training. Interventions for these groups prioritized establishing a strong foundation of empathy before independent clinical practice [12, 19, 49]. Experiential learning, role-playing, and reflective writing interventions were shown to be distinctly beneficial in preventing a decline in empathy throughout medical training for these groups [19, 46, 53]. Studies have also suggested that the greatest improvement in empathy may be seen when interventions are integrated into practice earlier in training and when interventions are continuously adjusted based on the particular stage of training of the learner. The remaining review studies for healthcare professionals focused on physicians, nurses, podiatrists, and other allied healthcare workers, emphasizing the reduction of burnout while increasing the quality of patient-centered care [11, 36, 42]. The interventions for these individuals focused on mindfulness, enhancing communication skills, and developing leadership skills to establish a collaborative healthcare environment [41]. The results of review studies for these individuals suggested that interventions with a longer duration and those involving actual patients were superior in improving empathy as compared to didactic-only interventions [43, 44]. The studies that integrated both students and practicing healthcare professionals suggested that interventions that were modified for the needs of the group were most effective [29, 50]. Nurses benefited from storytelling and reflective interventions, likely due to the extent of direct patient care [27, 30, 51]. Physicians and other healthcare professionals in more focused roles benefited significantly from empathy training combined with methods to account for emotional exhaustion and burnout [36].

Educational methods

Various educational methods have been employed to enhance empathy among healthcare workers (HCWs) and students. While some interventions demonstrate greater effectiveness, the following approaches yield unique results and insights into empathy training.

Mindfulness and empathy

Mindfulness-based and empathy-specific interventions (k = 2, 4.8%) have shown promise in enhancing empathy within medical education [15, 32]. Participation in mindfulness training has been associated with increased empathy scores among medical and premedical students. One widely utilized approach is the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program, which incorporates meditation, body scans, journaling, and stress management techniques to cultivate compassionate self-awareness and acceptance of others [15, 32]. Additionally, films portraying patient experiences, such as Wit (adapted from Margaret Edson’s Pulitzer Prize winning play), have been shown to enhance empathy by providing insight into patient suffering and emotional states [32]. Overall, studies indicate that mindfulness-based interventions improve empathy scores, heighten awareness of patient experiences, and enhance emotional adaptability [15, 32, 36].

Experiential learning

Experiential learning (k = 23, 50%), including simulations, role-playing, and hands-on experiences, has been extensively studied and consistently associated with improved empathy [12, 16, 17, 19, 21, 23, 29–31, 35, 38, 41, 43, 44, 46, 49–53]. These interventions generate deep learning and behavior modification, rather than superficial knowledge acquisition, with studies emphasizing the importance of debriefing sessions to reinforce empathetic behaviors [38]. The clinical relevance of experiential learning activities enhances their effectiveness, allowing participants to engage with real-world scenarios. The optimal duration of these interventions remains debated, with conflicting findings regarding whether shorter or longer interventions yield superior results [38, 53]. Mixed-model approaches, integrating various pedagogical techniques, have shown promising outcomes, particularly when implemented in later stages of medical training [11, 28, 34, 44].

Communication skills training

Communication-focused interventions (k = 3, 7.1%) have demonstrated significant improvements in empathy by enhancing patient-provider interactions [17, 22, 32, 49]. These interventions incorporate active listening, empathy validation, open-ended questioning, non-verbal communication, shared decision-making, and cultural sensitivity training [17, 22]. Studies suggest that structured communication training enhances patient satisfaction, strengthens provider-patient relationships, and fosters more patient-centered care [22, 32, 49].

Various educational methods

Various educational approaches (k = 11, 26.2%)—including problem-based learning, self-directed learning, seminars, workshops, and structured curricula—have been explored as empathy interventions [10, 12, 21, 23, 28, 36, 38, 49, 52]. These strategies have demonstrated positive effects on knowledge acquisition, attitudes, and clinical behaviors related to respectful maternity care, reducing instances of disrespect and abuse and mitigating obstetric violence [28]. Studies indicate that interactive learning formats, such as role-playing, discussions, and case-based learning, are particularly effective in fostering empathy [12, 28, 36]. Notably, problem-based learning and workshops have been linked to increased patient respect and reduced provider biases [21, 28, 31, 38, 46].

Creative arts interventions

Creative arts interventions, which use expressive modalities (k = 7, 16.7%)—including humanities, drama training, narrative medicine, reflective writing, and digital storytelling—have been widely studied as tools to enhance empathy [15, 17, 39, 44, 46, 50, 52]. Though specific methodologies vary, reflective writing as a creative arts tool has consistently emerged as an effective intervention, primarily serving as artistic and narrative expression. Empathy outcomes in these studies have been assessed through self-perception surveys, patient feedback, and third-party evaluations [15, 17, 38, 39, 46]. Despite limitations in study design and limited quantitative data, all reviews we examined in this category reported positive effects on empathy development. Integrating reflective writing into medical curricula has been encouraged, but further research is needed to define measurable and reproducible empathy outcomes [34, 38, 41, 44, 48, 51]. Narrative medicine, storytelling, and drama-based interventions have enhanced empathy and emotional intelligence, particularly among medical students [15, 17, 39, 40, 44].

Reflective learning

Reflective learning (k = 5, 11.9%) has emerged as a crucial component in empathy development for healthcare students and professionals [23, 30, 49, 52, 53]. In contrast to creative arts interventions, reflective learning is rooted in experiential learning theory and serves as a structured educational process. As opposed to emphasizing creativity or narrative expression, reflective writing as a reflective learning tool is rooted in introspection, and encourages individuals to analyze their emotions, patient interactions, and clinical experiences. Common interventions in this category include journaling, structured debriefing sessions, simulation-based encounters, and mindfulness exercises.

Reflective writing—encompassing journaling, essays, and narrative reflections—has been consistently linked to improved empathy levels by fostering self-awareness and emotional processing [53]. Simulation-based education has demonstrated significant benefits in empathy development when combined with structured reflection [19]. Debriefing sessions following simulations enable participants to process their experiences, identify emotional responses, and translate lessons into clinical practice. Additionally, mindfulness-based reflection (e.g., MBSR) has been linked to improved empathy scores, particularly in nursing and medical students [34]. Longitudinal studies indicate that narrative-based interventions contribute to long-term retention of empathetic skills, especially in high-stress healthcare environments [23, 49, 53].

Structured empathy programs

Several structured training programs (k = 12, 28.6%) have successfully enhanced empathy among healthcare students and professionals. These programs integrate theoretical knowledge with experiential and reflective learning to improve empathetic communication and patient interactions.

Patient-as-educator models which involve direct patient participation through storytelling, interviews, and shadowing have been shown to enhance medical students’ communication skills [54]. Similarly for surgical education, empathy-focused curricula mitigate the decline in empathy observed during training and the structured curriculum integrates role-modeling, standardized patient interactions, and mentorship which improve communication skills and patient satisfaction [36]. Structured programs aimed at improving oral health literacy and patient-centered communication demonstrate positive outcomes with focused training sessions (ranging from 20 min to half a day) [10]. Interprofessional education programs, as highlighted by Fossey et al., have been particularly beneficial, fostering collaboration and mutual respect among healthcare teams [9].

Summary of educational methods

Across various educational methods, our umbrella review indicates that active engagement and experiential learning may be the most effective strategies for fostering empathy among healthcare professionals. While didactic methods provide foundational knowledge, interventions integrating patient interactions, simulation-based learning, and reflective practices appear to produce more substantial and long-lasting improvements in empathy. Future empathy training programs should prioritize interdisciplinary, longitudinal, and patient-centered approaches to enhance both short-term and sustained empathetic behaviors in healthcare settings.

Outcomes of empathy training

Patterns and themes in empathy training outcomes

Overall, 30 of 42 reviews (71.4%) concluded that empathy training in healthcare leads to positive outcomes, while 11 reviews (26.2%) presented mixed or inconclusive findings, and two reviews (2.4%) found negative outcomes associated with empathy training (See Table 2). Positive outcomes included increased patient satisfaction, improved provider-patient communication, and enhanced provider empathy levels.

Findings suggest that the effectiveness of empathy training varies based on the type of intervention, duration, and the target audience. Reviews focusing on experiential learning methods (66.7% positive outcomes) and other structured educational approaches (71.9% positive outcomes) noted greater success than interventions relying primarily on didactic learning.

A key theme across successful interventions was the use of mindfulness and reflective practices. For instance, it was found that loving-kindness meditation improved neural plasticity, suggesting that meditation can facilitate structural brain changes that enhance empathetic skills [38]. Similarly, arts and literature-based interventions, such as creative and reflective writing, were found to promote self-awareness and encourage deeper patient-provider connections [51, 52]. It was further argued that these interventions may be particularly effective due to their dual benefits—enhancing empathy while improving healthcare professionals’ well-being, which may encourage sustained engagement [50].

The format of empathy interventions also appeared to influence effectiveness. Sinclair (2016) highlighted experiential and role-playing interventions as effective in fostering empathy [46]. However, another review article emphasized that arts- and literature-based interventions may have advantages due to their impact on self-reflection and emotional processing, suggesting that the optimal intervention may depend on participant preferences and desired outcomes [50].

Duration and timing of interventions

Findings regarding the optimal duration and frequency of empathy training were mixed. Both Levett-Jones (2019) and Martin (2020) found no significant correlation between the length of an intervention and its effectiveness, suggesting that emphasis should be placed on the quality and design of training rather than duration alone [38, 53]. Conversely, Samarasekera (2023) reported that empathy scores increased when interventions were delivered over extended periods, indicating that longer exposure to empathy-focused training may yield more sustained benefits [44]. These discrepancies suggest that while duration may play a role, other factors—such as participant engagement and the type of intervention—may be equally or more influential in determining effectiveness.

Empathy in clinical training

The long-term impact of empathy interventions remains a complex and evolving area of research, particularly in medical education. Menezes (2021) found that training with virtual and standardized patients resulted in lasting improvements in empathy among third-year medical students, suggesting that this type of empathy training could potentially serve as protection against the typical trajectory of decline in empathy in medical students as they progress through clinical training [8, 12].

However, Pakkonen (2021) reported that didactic methods alone were insufficient to improve empathy, reinforcing the idea that interactive, experiential methods may be more effective [41]. Additionally, research by Spatoula (2019) and Zhou (2021) highlights variability in how empathy is measured, noting that while the Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) often detects a decline in empathy during clinical training, the Questionnaire of Cognitive and Affective Empathy (QCAE) suggests potential improvement in certain domains [48, 52]. These findings highlight the importance of using comprehensive, multidimensional measures of empathy to assess changes over time accurately.

Leadership in empathy development

An emerging theme in empathy training research is the critical role of leadership in fostering an empathetic healthcare culture. Samarasekera and Pakkonen emphasized the need for leadership-driven initiatives, arguing that senior administrators play a pivotal role in sustaining empathy-based interventions across healthcare teams [41, 44]. Leadership involvement may create a ripple effect, promoting organizational shifts toward a more empathetic and patient-centered environment.

Despite its potential impact, current empathy training programs often overlook leadership development as a key intervention target. Samarasekera (2023) identified a significant gap in existing programs, noting that most interventions focus on frontline healthcare providers and trainees rather than leadership teams [44]. Addressing this gap by integrating empathy-focused leadership training could amplify the effectiveness of broader empathy initiatives and foster a more compassionate, system-wide approach to patient care.

Review Types

While literature reviews all share a common goal of summarizing what is known and has been studied in a particular topic area, they can vary in their methods and approach to synthesis. Reviews can be comprehensive and follow a prescribed, reproducible approach to searching, screening, and coding (e.g., systematic), or may be more general, seeking only to provide a big picture of a topic without concern for obtaining all available evidence on a topic nor producing a reproducible methodology (e.g., narrative). Reviews may also vary in their approach to synthesizing findings, wherein they may seek to provide a broad sense of what has been studied in a topic area and corresponding gaps (e.g., scoping reviews), may seek to answer one or more specific research questions (e.g., meta-analysis), or may attempt to analyze a topic area in depth, place it into context, and develop a conceptual model (e.g., critical review). With the exception of meta-analysis, which can only be done with quantitative studies, reviews can include papers that are quantitative, qualitative, or mixed (include both quantitative and qualitative studies). Quality assessment

Table 4 indicates the results of the AMSTAR assessment for each article. Of the 42 included reviews, the majority (83%) clearly articulated their research questions using the PICO framework (See Tables 4 and 5). More than half conducted their study selection (74%) and data extraction (64%) in duplicate. Fewer reviews (45%) adequately addressed heterogeneity, and a smaller number (33%) explained their rationale for including specific study designs or described the included studies in sufficient detail. Less than a quarter (24%) employed a comprehensive literature search strategy, and only 19% reported establishing their methods in advance or registering a protocol. Very few reviews included a list of excluded studies with justifications (2%) or reported on funding sources for the included studies (2%). A limited number of reviews (31%) used satisfactory techniques to assess the risk of bias in individual studies. Among the reviews that conducted meta-analyses, only 14% used appropriate statistical combination methods, and a same proportion (14%) assessed the potential impact of risk of bias on the results of their meta-analyses. Less than half of the reviews (40%) accounted for risk of bias when interpreting results, and very few (10%) investigated sources of bias. Lastly, the majority of reviews disclosed sources of funding or potential conflicts of interest for the review itself. Table 5 summarizes these findings.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Empathy is widely recognized as a cornerstone of effective healthcare, with strong evidence linking it to improved patient outcomes, higher satisfaction rates, and greater adherence to treatment plans [27, 42, 44]. Numerous interventions, including simulation, reflective writing, and experiential learning, have consistently demonstrated their ability to cultivate empathetic behaviors among healthcare professionals [12, 23, 43]. In particular, educational interventions that require active engagement—such as role-playing and immersive exercises—are highly effective, especially for students early in their training [26, 34].

Effective strategies for empathy training

Empathy training interventions that simulate real-life patient interactions have successfully strengthened patient-provider communication and fostered meaningful clinical relationships [19, 26]. For example, role reversals—where trainees alternate between being a patient and a provider—have been shown to deepen understanding of patient perspectives and emotional experiences [25]. Additionally, debriefing sessions following simulations allow participants to reflect on their experiences, process emotions, and translate these insights into empathetic clinical behaviors [42, 44].

Conversely, passive learning approaches, such as didactic lectures, have demonstrated limited effectiveness in fostering empathy [23, 44]. Instead, multimodal approaches that integrate mindfulness, reflective practices, and experiential learning have been identified as the most impactful [27, 43]. Evidence also suggests that longer and repeated exposure to empathy training leads to more sustained improvements, reinforcing the importance of longitudinal interventions in medical education [26, 34, 44].

Challenges in measuring empathy

Despite these advancements, the lack of standardized, universally applicable measurement tools for assessing empathy remains a significant challenge. Existing instruments, such as the JSE and the QCAE, capture distinct facets of empathy, making direct comparisons across studies difficult [19, 42]. This illustrates the need for comprehensive, validated tools that assess empathy’s cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions [12, 22]. Developing such tools would enhance comparability across studies, improve intervention evaluations, and guide curriculum development in healthcare education.

The role of culture in empathy development

Cultural context plays a crucial role in shaping empathy levels and determining the effectiveness of training interventions. Research indicates that gender, ethnicity, and cultural background influence how individuals perceive and express empathy, emphasizing the importance of tailoring interventions to diverse populations [33, 35]. Culturally adapted training programs enhance inclusivity and maximize the impact of empathy-building strategies, ensuring they are relevant to the diverse patient populations healthcare providers serve [26, 44].

Leadership as a catalyst for empathy in healthcare

A growing body of research highlights the critical role of leadership in fostering a culture of empathy within healthcare organizations. Senior administrators influence organizational priorities and workplace culture, making their engagement in empathy training a potential ripple effect mechanism for change [33, 41]. Leadership-focused interventions could reinforce the importance of compassionate care across all levels of healthcare, amplifying the effects of empathy training initiatives [26]. However, current training programs often overlook leadership as a key target for empathy interventions, representing an area for future improvement [44].

Practice implications

Embedding empathy training into medical school admissions and curricula represents a promising strategy for developing empathetic healthcare providers from the outset. Research suggests that selecting candidates with higher baseline empathy levels may lead to better patient-centered care outcomes [21, 28]. Additionally, medical curricula should prioritize simulation-based learning, direct patient interactions, and reflective practices to ensure immediate and long-term empathy development [10, 12, 53].

The evidence highlights the effectiveness of multimodal, active-learning approaches in empathy training while highlighting key gaps in measurement tools, cultural adaptation, and leadership engagement. Addressing these challenges will enable healthcare professionals to cultivate and sustain empathy throughout their careers, ultimately enhancing patient care and fostering a more compassionate healthcare workforce.

Limitations and future research

The lack of consensus on definitions and measurement tools for empathy and patient-centeredness remains a widely recognized challenge in evaluating the effectiveness of interventions [22, 28, 55]. Existing literature highlights that this inconsistency hampers comparisons across studies and limits the generalizability of findings [19, 25, 26]. While many interventions demonstrate short-term improvements in empathy among healthcare students, the long-term retention of these skills post-training is rarely monitored. This emphasizes the need for longitudinal research examining the sustained impact of empathy interventions, particularly given the well-documented decline in empathy during clinical training and professional practice [19, 25, 26].

Advancing empathy research in healthcare education

To ensure the sustained development of empathy among healthcare professionals, future research must adopt a comprehensive, multi-faceted approach that builds upon identified gaps in past studies. Effective strategies should integrate direct patient interactions, simulation-based learning, reflective practices, and robust long-term follow-up assessments [26, 27, 53]. Addressing the steady decline of empathy observed throughout healthcare education requires optimizing intervention timeframes and tailoring training to individuals’ baseline empathy levels, potentially enhancing both short- and long-term program efficacy [21, 26, 54, 55]. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies assessing the durability of empathy-related improvements over time. Current literature largely focuses on immediate post-intervention outcomes, despite the ultimate goal being to sustain empathy throughout clinical practice and into a physician’s career [26, 27, 55]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—valued for their replicability and reliability—should be employed more frequently in empathy training studies to improve the generalizability of findings [24, 26]. Another critical research gap is the limited focus on empathy training for actively practicing physicians. Maintaining empathy throughout a healthcare career is essential for enhancing patient care and mitigating professional burnout, yet few studies examine which interventions are most effective for experienced clinicians [27, 53]. To this end, empathy training could potentially be embedded into ongoing professional development programs such as continuing medical education (CME) and interprofessional education sessions, as well as incorporated into leadership initiatives that foster empathic healthcare systems [55, 56]. These programs can provide clinicians with strategies to manage emotionally demanding patient interactions while maintaining appropriate professional boundaries [55, 57]. In doing so, empathy training may help reduce burnout and staff sickness by supporting emotional resilience and preventing the internalization of patients’ distress [55, 57].

Developing Standardized Empathy Measurement Tools

Beyond research applications, standardized empathy assessments could also be used in medical school admissions to identify candidates with strong baseline empathy levels, potentially contributing to more patient-centered healthcare professionals. Integrating quantitative and qualitative assessment methods—such as validated self-reports, observational assessments, and patient feedback—would provide a more holistic evaluation of empathy development and clinical application [53].

Cultural considerations in empathy training

Cultural context plays a significant role in shaping empathy levels and responses to interventions. Research indicates that gender, ethnicity, and cultural background influence how individuals perceive and express empathy, reinforcing the need for culturally sensitive training programs [26, 33]. Future research should explore how cultural differences impact empathy development and assess whether existing interventions are equally effective across diverse populations. Comparative analyses of empathy training outcomes across different cultural settings may offer insights to design more inclusive and impactful interventions [33, 53].

Comparative analyses of educational approaches

Further research is also needed to compare the effectiveness of various educational strategies across different healthcare disciplines and training stages. While methods such as mindfulness-based strategies, simulation exercises, and reflective writing have demonstrated efficacy in specific contexts, a comprehensive comparison of their impact across professions remains limited [19, 26, 55]. Understanding different training approaches’ unique benefits and limitations would allow educators to tailor strategies to specific learner populations, ultimately improving empathy training outcomes and patient care [27].

Conclusion

Empathy training interventions in healthcare have been widely successful in enhancing empathetic behaviors and improving patient care. Active engagement, experiential learning, effective communication skills, and diverse educational methods all contribute to fostering empathy among healthcare professionals. Moving forward, addressing measurement inconsistencies, developing culturally inclusive interventions, and incorporating leadership-focused empathy training will be critical in advancing the field. By implementing comprehensive and evidence-based training strategies, healthcare organizations can cultivate a more compassionate, patient-centered healthcare environment and ensure that empathy remains a fundamental pillar of medical practice.
