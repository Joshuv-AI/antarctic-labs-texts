Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of dentists in Erbil City, Iraq, towards tele-dentistry: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Heran Ibrahim Hassan, Fighan Jalal Hussein, Vian Omer Majeed, Kochr Ali Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesTeledentistry has emerged as a promising digital health strategy; however, its implementation depends largely on clinicians’ preparedness. This study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of dentists in Erbil City, Iraq, toward teledentistry and identified factors associated with its adoption.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 426 licensed dentists. KAP levels were classified using modified Bloom’s cut-off criteria, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of good knowledge, positive attitude, and good practice.ResultsOverall, poor knowledge was observed in 62.9% of participants, moderate knowledge in 9.2%, and high knowledge in 27.9%. Attitudes were predominantly neutral (49.1%), while only 8.7% demonstrated positive attitudes. Poor practice was reported by 63.1%, and only 16.2% showed good teledentistry practice. Knowledge level was significantly associated with professional qualification (p < 0.001), while positive attitude was associated with female gender (AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.08–6.52, p = 0.034) and good knowledge (AOR = 7.17, 95% CI: 3.28–15.66, p < 0.001). Good practice was significantly associated with good knowledge (AOR = 23.29, 95% CI: 10.70–50.70, p < 0.001) and increasing age (AOR = 1.12 per year, 95% CI: 1.07–1.17, p < 0.001). The most frequently reported barriers were concerns regarding patient acceptance (84.7%) and lack of formal training (83.8%). These findings likely reflect perceived rather than objectively measured barriers and may be influenced by limited clinical experience with teledentistry.ConclusionsTeledentistry adoption among dentists in Erbil remains limited and appears primarily constrained by gaps in knowledge and practical competence rather than negative perceptions alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08823-4.Keywords: Teledentistry; Knowledge, attitudes and practices; Dentists; Digital health; Telemedicine; Iraq

Introduction

Digital health technologies have rapidly transformed healthcare delivery, enabling remote consultation, diagnosis, monitoring, and professional collaboration through telemedicine platforms [1–3]. Within oral healthcare, teledentistry represents a specialized application of telemedicine that utilizes information and communication technologies to support clinical care, patient follow-up, and professional education [4]. Evidence suggests that teledentistry can improve access to dental services, reduce geographical barriers, and enhance communication between patients and providers [5, 6]. Despite these advantages, its integration into routine dental practice remains inconsistent, particularly in resource-variable settings.

Teledentistry has demonstrated applicability across multiple dental disciplines, including preventive care, orthodontics, and oral diagnosis, with the potential to facilitate early disease detection and interprofessional collaboration [7, 8]. The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated its global adoption by enabling continuity of care during periods of restricted access to clinical services [9, 10]. However, increased exposure to teledentistry has not consistently translated into sustained clinical utilization, indicating a gap between awareness and actual practice.

In low- and middle-income countries, including Iraq, the implementation of teledentistry remains limited. This is attributed to a combination of structural and behavioral barriers, including inadequate digital infrastructure, limited professional training, and the absence of comprehensive regulatory frameworks [11]. In the Kurdistan Region specifically, these challenges are compounded by variability in access to digital technologies and limited integration of digital health into dental education. Moreover, existing evidence suggests that barriers extend beyond technological constraints, encompassing uncertainties related to medico-legal responsibilities, clinician competence, and patient acceptance.

Previous studies in similar contexts have predominantly focused on descriptive assessments of awareness or perceptions, with limited exploration of how knowledge, attitudes, and practices interact to influence real-world adoption [12, 13]. Furthermore, many studies rely on descriptive analyses without applying multivariable approaches to identify independent predictors of adoption behavior, limiting the ability to understand the mechanisms underlying implementation gaps. This gap is particularly relevant in Iraq, where empirical evidence on teledentistry adoption remains scarce.

Erbil City, a major center for dental education and clinical practice in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, provides a relevant setting to examine these dynamics. Understanding dentists’ preparedness and behavioral responses toward teledentistry is essential for identifying barriers to implementation and informing targeted capacity-building strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of dentists in Erbil City toward teledentistry and to identify factors associated with its adoption. It was hypothesized that higher levels of knowledge would be associated with improved practice, while attitudes may influence this relationship within the framework of behavioral adoption models. By providing context-specific evidence, this study seeks to support the development of targeted training initiatives, policy frameworks, and implementation strategies to enhance the integration of teledentistry into routine dental care in Iraq.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted to assess dentists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward teledentistry in Erbil City, Kurdistan Region of Iraq. Data were collected using an online, self-administered structured questionnaire distributed through widely used professional and social media platforms, including Facebook, Telegram, and WhatsApp. The survey was conducted over an eight-week period, from 18 August 2025 to 11 October 2025. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional research.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethical Committee of the College of Dentistry, Hawler Medical University (Reference No.: HMUD/2526017; Date of approval: 19/10/2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [14]. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to questionnaire completion. Participant anonymity was ensured, and no personal identifiable information was collected.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised licensed dentists registered with the Kurdistan Dental Association – Erbil Branch. Both general dental practitioners and dental specialists working in public and/or private dental clinics within Erbil City were eligible for participation. No restrictions were placed on age or years of clinical experience to allow inclusion of a wide range of practicing dentists.

Dentists were eligible if they held at least a Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) or Doctor of Dental Surgery (DDS) degree, were actively practicing dentistry during the study period, and provided electronic informed consent. Dentists who were not engaged in clinical practice were excluded, those who declined participation, and those who submitted incomplete questionnaires. Questionnaires with more than 10% missing responses were considered invalid and excluded from analysis.

Sample size and sampling technique

The minimum sample size was calculated using Epi Info™ version 7.2 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, USA), assuming an expected prevalence of 50%, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level, based on the standard single population proportion formula:

The 50% expected prevalence was selected to maximize sample size in the absence of reliable prior regional estimates, as recommended in methodological literature [15]. To account for potential non-response, a 10% increment was applied, resulting in a target sample size of 361 participants.

A convenience sampling technique was employed due to the absence of a comprehensive and up-to-date sampling frame of dentists actively reachable through digital platforms in Erbil City. Participants were recruited through online professional dental networks and social media groups. This approach may introduce selection bias by preferentially including dentists who are more familiar with digital tools, potentially leading to overestimation of teledentistry awareness and practice.

Given the open online distribution of the survey and the absence of a defined sampling frame, the total number of dentists who received the questionnaire could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated. However, findings should be interpreted in light of the non-probability sampling approach.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured electronic questionnaire developed via Google Forms and adapted from previously validated instruments assessing teledentistry-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices [16–18]. The questionnaire was reviewed by two experts in dental public health and digital health to assess content and face validity prior to pilot testing.

The questionnaire consisted of four sections:

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics.

It should be noted that the knowledge items primarily assessed awareness and recognition of teledentistry concepts rather than in-depth clinical or procedural knowledge, and therefore should be interpreted accordingly. As the data were self-reported, responses may also be subject to recall and social desirability bias.

Scoring and classification of KAP domains

Each correct or positive response was assigned one point, and total scores were converted into percentages. KAP levels were categorized using modified Bloom’s cut-off criteria: low (< 60%), moderate (60–75%), and high (> 75%).

The use of modified Bloom’s criteria was based on its widespread application in KAP studies, as supported by previous literature [19–21], although this approach may reduce granularity. For regression analysis, moderate and low categories were combined to avoid sparse data bias, improve model stability, and ensure adequate cell counts for reliable estimation.

Pilot testing and reliability

A pilot study was conducted among 15 dentists who were excluded from the final analysis to assess the clarity, relevance, and feasibility of the questionnaire. Minor revisions were made based on participant feedback. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

Data management and confidentiality

All data were collected anonymously and stored electronically in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. No personally identifiable information was collected. Data were anonymized and coded prior to analysis. Secure, encrypted backups were maintained in accordance with institutional data management policies, and only aggregated results were reported.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize participant characteristics and KAP levels.

Associations between variables were examined using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Effect size was assessed using Cramer’s V.

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of good knowledge, positive attitude, and good practice. Variables were selected based on theoretical relevance and bivariate analysis (p < 0.20) and were entered into the model using a backward stepwise approach.

For regression analysis, outcome variables were dichotomized, with high KAP scores (> 75%) coded as the outcome of interest. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Additional diagnostics included assessment of influential observations and residual analysis to ensure model robustness.

All tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.

Multivariable analysis demonstrated that good knowledge of teledentistry was significantly associated with working in private practice, fewer years of clinical experience, and increasing age, while dentists with a DDS/BDS qualification were less likely to demonstrate good knowledge compared with those holding postgraduate degrees. Female gender and good knowledge were significantly associated with a positive attitude toward teledentistry, whereas other factors did not show independent associations after adjustment. Good knowledge emerged as the strongest predictor of effective teledentistry practice, and older age also significantly correlated with improved practice. In contrast, positive attitude did not remain independently associated with practice after adjustment. No statistically significant associations were observed for gender, type of practice, clinical experience, or qualification in relation to practice. Discussion

Main findings

In this cross-sectional survey of dentists in Erbil City, teledentistry readiness was characterized by low levels of knowledge and practice, alongside predominantly neutral attitudes. Nearly two-thirds of participants demonstrated poor knowledge and a similar proportion reported poor practice, indicating limited integration of teledentistry into routine clinical care.

Importantly, these findings reflect a context of limited awareness and mixed perceptions rather than a simple gap between acceptance and implementation, as only a small proportion of participants expressed clearly positive attitudes toward teledentistry.

Multivariable analyses demonstrated a strong association between good knowledge and good practice. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the large effect size (AOR = 23.29) may partly reflect the dichotomization of variables, which can inflate effect sizes, as well as potential residual confounding.

Interpretation within theoretical frameworks

The findings can be interpreted using the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the COM-B framework. Within these models, knowledge contributes to perceived ease-of-use and capability, which in turn facilitate behavioral adoption.

In the present study, the strong association between knowledge and practice may reflect increased familiarity with teledentistry tools, improved confidence in their use, and reduced perceived complexity of digital workflows.

However, the strong association between knowledge and practice should be interpreted as consistent with a TAM-based explanation rather than evidence of a direct causal relationship.

It is also important to note that the knowledge domain assessed in this study primarily reflects awareness and recognition rather than in-depth clinical or procedural knowledge, which may partially explain the observed associations.

Notably, attitude did not independently predict practice in the adjusted model, which contrasts with the traditional KAP framework where attitude is expected to mediate the relationship between knowledge and practice. This may indicate that awareness can drive initial adoption behaviors, while attitudinal change may require sustained exposure, clinical experience, and system-level support.

Comparison with other studies

Our findings are broadly consistent with international evidence suggesting that the perceived potential of teledentistry may exceed its actual use in settings where training, workflow integration, and governance structures are still developing.

For example, recent surveys from the region—such as Jordan—describe generally favorable perceptions of teledentistry’s potential while simultaneously emphasizing the practical constraints that limit routine implementation [22].

Similarly, studies from South Asia continue to report that awareness can be high, yet day-to-day utilization remains low, and training deficits are repeatedly identified as a central barrier [23, 24]. A recent umbrella review synthesizing multiple systematic reviews concluded that although teledentistry shows promise in improving access, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness, evidence quality remains variable and implementation challenges persist, particularly related to training and integration into routine care [25].

Importantly, European data illustrate that mature systems still encounter adoption barriers, but these are more likely to revolve around implementation details (e.g., interoperability, workflow, regulatory clarity) rather than basic awareness—highlighting how system readiness shifts the nature of barriers over time [26].

The observed barrier pattern in this study—particularly the prominence of training gaps and concerns regarding patient acceptance—is also consistent with theory-driven implementation frameworks (e.g., COM-B / TDF), which emphasize that capability and opportunity constraints often dominate early stages of adoption.

Patient acceptance as a key barrier

A major finding of this study was the high proportion of dentists reporting concerns regarding patient acceptance of teledentistry.

This perception likely reflects uncertainty regarding patient trust, communication quality, and the perceived limitations of remote clinical assessment, particularly for procedures requiring direct examination or intervention.

Such concerns may act as a significant psychological and professional barrier, even when basic awareness exists, and highlight the importance of patient-centered implementation strategies, including education, clear consent processes, and reassurance regarding privacy and clinical safety.

These findings are consistent with established adoption theories such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the COM-B framework, which emphasize the role of capability and perceived ease-of-use in shaping behavioral uptake [27, 28]. In this context, the observed association between knowledge and practice may reflect increased confidence and familiarity with teledentistry workflows.

Clinical and practical implications

From a clinical perspective, the findings suggest that teledentistry adoption should be approached through staged implementation.

Initial integration may focus on low-risk, high-yield applications such as follow-up consultations, triage of urgent conditions, orthodontic monitoring, and pre-visit screening.

These applications are particularly relevant in high-volume outpatient dental settings, where time efficiency and accessibility are critical.

Policy and system-level implications

The findings also emphasize the necessity of structured policy frameworks to support teledentistry implementation.

Key considerations include the development of clear guidelines on licensing, data privacy, cross-border consultation policies, and reimbursement mechanisms for teleconsultations, as well as clarification of medico-legal responsibilities.

Without these structural supports, adoption is likely to remain limited despite increasing awareness.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the relatively large sample size (N = 426), which exceeded the minimum calculated requirement and enhanced statistical precision. The use of multivariable models further strengthens the findings by allowing identification of independent predictors.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and observed associations should not be interpreted as directional relationships. Second, the online recruitment strategy using digital platforms may have led to over-representation of dentists who are more digitally engaged, potentially resulting in overestimation of teledentistry awareness and practice levels. Third, the use of a convenience sampling approach further limits the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, the inability to calculate a response rate restricts assessment of potential non-response bias. Fifth, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability bias. Finally, the measurement of knowledge was limited to awareness-based items, and unmeasured contextual factors—such as regulatory environment and institutional policies—may also influence adoption patterns.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study, teledentistry use among dentists in Erbil City was low and was largely constrained by limited knowledge and practical competence, primarily reflecting awareness rather than advanced clinical competence, and cannot be explained by negative perceptions alone.

Knowledge was strongly associated with teledentistry practice, highlighting that competency development, rather than attitudinal change alone, appears to play an important role in effective implementation. Strengthening undergraduate curricula and continuing professional education, alongside clearer professional and regulatory guidance, represents a strategic pathway to promote sustainable and equitable integration of teledentistry into routine dental care.

Policy initiatives aiming to expand teledentistry in resource-variable settings should prioritize competency-based training, regulatory clarity, and patient-centered implementation strategies to ensure that digital health innovations translate into sustained clinical practice.
