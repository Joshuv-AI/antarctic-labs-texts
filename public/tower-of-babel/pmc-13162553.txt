Treatment with low-dose bicistronic immunotherapy mitigates parasite persistence and improves serological and cardiac outcomes in adult mice infected with Trypanosoma cruzi: comparative analysis with benznidazole
Authors: Subhadip Choudhuri, Allison Wyrick, Nandadeva Lokugamage, Siddhartha De, Upendra Marathi, Nisha Jain Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChagas disease (CD), caused by Trypanosoma cruzi (Tc), remains difficult to treat in adults, as benznidazole (BZN) has limited curative efficacy and notable adverse effects. This study evaluated a newly-designed immunotherapy without (BCV) and with RIG-I adjuvant (BCVR). Adult C57BL/6 mice were infected and treated with BCV/BCVR (0.5 mg/kg, two doses, 21-day interval) or BZN (50 mg/kg for 6-weeks). Outcomes included parasite burden, biomarkers of inflammation, fibrosis, and heart disease, and left ventricular (LV) function. Infected/untreated mice developed high acute parasite burden, and persistence of proinflammatory, tissue damage, and clinical disease-related biomarkers in circulation and cardiac tissue. Treatment with BCV, BCVR or BZN achieved >95% control of parasite burden. Tissue inflammatory infiltrate and peripheral biomarkers of inflammation, necrosis, and heart involvement were significantly reduced throughout the infection and disease by BCV, while BCVR- and BZN-mediated protection was pronounced in the chronic phase. Pro-fibrotic gene expression and cardiac hypertrophy were blocked in CD mice post-treatment (BCV > BCVR = BZN). Morphological changes in the LV walls’ thickness associated with compromised systolic and diastolic performance of the heart were abated by BCV/BCVR, while BZN moderately controlled the LV dysfunction in CD mice. We surmise that BCV/BCVR immunotherapies outperformed BZN in controlling the parasite persistence and ameliorating cardiac pathology and LV dysfunction that contribute to heart failure and death in CD. Overall, BCV and BCVR are promising alternatives to prevent and control CD morbidities that have not been uniformly feasible because of the adverse effects of BZN in adults.KEYWORDS: Trypanosoma cruzi, immunotherapy, Chagas disease, cardiomyopathy, left ventricular function

Introduction

Trypanosoma cruzi (Tc) is transmitted by triatomines, and it is endemic in vast areas ranging from the southern USA to Argentina in the Americas [1]. Tc disseminates and replicates in diverse tissues during the acute phase, after which it is controlled, but not eliminated, by the immune system. Ultimately, parasite persistence and continuing inflammation contribute to chronic cardiomyopathy, heart failure and death [2]. Other morbidities, including coagulopathy and gastroenterological and neurological disorders, are also noted in Chagas disease (CD) patients [3–5].

Treatment options for CD, especially for adults, present a discouraging scenario. Benznidazole (BZN) and nifurtimox are most effective in achieving parasitological cure in acutely infected children (<15-years-old) [6]. Reduced or no contact with the infected triatomine vector is considered the safest strategy to prevent infection among those travelling to endemic countries. This is because the efficacy of anti-parasitic drugs is diminished in adults, and these drugs induce significant adverse events, leading to a high rate of treatment discontinuation in infected adults [7]. Thus, safe and effective treatment options for achieving the parasitological cure in infected adults are highly limited [8]. These persistent challenges underscore the urgent need for novel and improved therapeutic solutions for CD.

Some recent efforts have focused on developing nanotechnology-based systems that encapsulate and precisely release BZN to enhance the therapeutic potency of lower doses and improve patient safety [9]. Others have conceptualized (but not developed) compounds to leverage the ubiquitin–proteasome system for selectively degrading the parasite proteins [10]. Immunotherapies, e.g. immune checkpoint inhibitors, target-specific monoclonal antibodies, and recombinant cytokines, that empower the host defence, have also emerged as promising therapeutic intervention(s) against some cancers and autoimmune diseases [11], though these have not been tested for modulating the host response to infectious agents.

Undeniably, immunotherapies that can specifically enhance the pathogen-specific host immune defence offer an innovative approach to decrease the burden of microbial infections, including CD. It is envisioned that an immunotherapy constituted of one or more parasite antigens and complemented with adjuvants will have a higher safety profile and will be equally effective or better than BZN in arresting the parasite persistence and cardiac complications. In this context, we employed an unbiased, carefully designed computational/biological screening approach for the selection of candidate antigens for immunotherapy development against CD. Of the several antigens screened, short, antigenic peptides TcG2 and TcG4, were selected as candidate antigens because these were conserved in the genome of clinically relevant parasite isolates of all lineages (I-VI), expressed in infective/intracellular stages of Tc, and recognized by IgG antibodies and type 1 CD8+T cells in multiple infected hosts [12,13]. In mice, TcG2/TcG4 induced type 1, T cell (CD8 > CD4) activation and trypanolytic antibody response leading to >85% control of Tc infection and chronic CD pathology [14–16]. When tested in dogs, TcG2/TcG4 provided up to 80% protection from challenge infection and 70–85% reduction in cardiomegaly and necrosis, leading to none-to-mild cardiac compromise, while severe abnormalities with changes in long QT interval, J-point, and ST segment elevations were noted in untreated/infected dogs [17,18]. These studies offered a proof-of-principle for further development, optimization, and evaluation of immunotherapies against the ongoing threat of Tc infection and CD, and to reduce their impact on economic stability and global health.

For this study, we have engineered next-generation therapy, incorporating the TcG2/TcG4 immunogenicity. Briefly, mouse-codon optimized TcG2-TcG4-encoding sequences were synthesized (cistrons separated by P2A linker) and cloned into a new generation of plasmid to produce bicistronic (BCV) immunotherapy. The retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) is a cytoplasmic double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) pattern receptor that, when activated, leads to type 1 interferon (IFN) response [19]. RIG-I agonists are shown to elicit quantitatively improved adaptive immune responses even at low antigen doses [20]. We, therefore, made BCVR incorporating RIG-I agonist along with the TcG2-TcG4 bicistronic construct. Both BCV and BCVR were designed in compliance with the HHS/FDA regulatory guidance for immunotherapies against infectious agents. These immunogens: (a) lack nonessential sequences for plasmid replication and target gene expression, (b) contain synthetic non-human mRNA leaders and terminators to minimize genomic integration, (c) apply advanced technology for antibiotic-free plasmid amplification, and (d) contain R6K-derived mini-origin and SV40-CMV-HTLV-1R promoter to enhance target gene expression, thereby offering efficient, secure, and cost-effective gene delivery [21].

We examined the efficacy of BCV and BCVR as therapeutic agents and determined if these immunotherapies offer comparable or better efficacy than BZN in controlling parasite replication and persistence, systemic and tissue inflammation, profibrotic response, chronic cardiomyopathy, and left ventricle (LV) dysfunction in mice infected with Tc. We also monitored a select panel of clinical disease biomarkers with an aim to identify those that were predictive of amelioration of tissue damage and heart disease in infected mice.

Methods

Key resources and reagents are listed in Supplementary Table S7.

Mice, parasites, and immunotherapies

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and housed in micro-isolation cages (max: 5 mice/cage) at 12 h day/night cycle with ad libitum access to water and standard chow. National Institutes of Health guidelines were followed for the care and housing of laboratory animals. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Welfare Office (protocol# 0805029) and conducted by BSL2/ABSL2-trained personnel in a BSL2-approved laboratory.

Tc SylvioX10 strain (ATCC 50823) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Parasites were passaged every 2–3-months in mice to maintain the virulence. To propagate trypomastigotes for challenge infection, C2C12 myoblasts (ATCC CRL-1772) were cultured in complete RPMI1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). At 70% confluency, cells were infected with Tc trypomastigotes (1:3, cell: parasite ratio), previously collected from mice. Cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 5–7 days. Freshly released trypomastigotes were centrifuged at 3000xg for 8 min and re-suspended in 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS: 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4 and 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4).

T. cruzi (CL/Brenner) sequences for TcG2 (AY727915, 220aa) and TcG4 (AY727917, 92aa) are available in GenBank. Mouse codon-optimized TcG2 and TcG4, separated by P2A linker, were synthesized (GenScript Biotech). P2A peptide (GSGATNFSLLKQAGDVEENPGP) cleaves at penultimate G during translation via ribosome skipping, thus facilitating the expression of multiple genes from a single vector to reduce the cost of production [22]. Synthetic TcG2-P2A-TcG4 sequence was cloned in NTC9385R (1753bp) and NTC9385R-eRNA41H (2539 bp) (Aldevron, Fargo, ND) to generate BCV and BCVR, respectively (Figure 1(a) and 1(b)). Recombinant constructs were confirmed by sequencing, and 50 mg of each was purified using the HyperGRO fermentation process [23].

(a,b) Bicistronic immunotherapies. Mouse codon-optimized TcG2 (666 bp) and TcG4 (276 bp) separated by a P2A linker (66 bp) were cloned into NTC9385R (lacks RIG-I agonist) or NTC9385R-eRNA41H (co-expresses RIG-I agonist eRNA41H) to generate BCV (a) and BCVR (b), respectively. (c) Confirmation of immunogens expression. Mice were injected with BCV or BCVR (10 μg = 0.5 mg/kg each dosage, 2-doses, 21-day intervals, intramuscular). Sera samples were obtained 21-days after the second dose, and analyzed for TcG2-, TcG4-, and Tc-specific IgGs by an ELISA (n = 6 mice/group). (d) Experimental model. C57BL/6 adult, female mice (8-week old) were challenged with T. cruzi (SylvioX10, 10,000-trypomastigotes/mouse, intraperitoneal). Beginning from 21-days post-infection, mice were treated with BCV or BCVR (as above) or benznidazole (BZN; 50 mg/kg, drinking water, 6-weeks). Mice were euthanized at 65-days (acute parasitemic phase) and 210-day (chronic disease phase) post-infection (controls: mock-infected, infected/untreated). All experiments were repeated in at least two independent studies (n = 5 mice/group/study). (e–h) Parasite burden. Total DNA was isolated from the heart and skeletal muscle (SkM) tissues of acutely (e,f) and chronically (g,h) infected mice and analyzed by qPCR for Tc18SrDNA level (n = 8–12-mice per group). Data were normalized to murine Gapdh. In bar graphs, individual datapoints (average of duplicate analysis per mouse) and mean values ± standard error mean (SEM) are plotted. For the control group, no effects of vehicle or empty vector were observed on the experimental outcomes, and the pooled data are shown. Significance was calculated by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc test, and p-values are annotated as ≤0.05*,^, ≤0.01**,^^, and ≤0.001***,^^^ (*control vs. any of the infected groups, ^infected vs. infected/treated).

BCV/BCVR expression

The cDNAs for TcG2 and TcG4 were cloned in-frame with a C-terminal His-tag into a pET-22b plasmid (Novagen, Gibbstown, NJ). Plasmids were transformed in BL21 (DE3) pLysS-competent cells, and recombinant proteins were purified using the poly-histidine fusion, peptide–metal chelation chromatography system.

Tc trypomastigotes were suspended in 1XPBS (1 × 1010/2 mL) and subjected to three freeze (−80°C, 20 min) and thaw (room temperature, 10 min) cycles. Next, RIPA buffer (1X composition: 25 mM Tris-HCL pH7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) containing protease inhibitors cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) was added, and lysates were sonicated on ice at maximum speed for 5 min in an Ultrasonic bath sonicator (Emerson-Branson). After centrifugation at 13,500 rpm for 15 min, protein concentration in the soluble trypomastigote lysate (TcL) was measured by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad).

Mice were immunized with BCV or BCVR (10 μg, intramuscular, 2 doses at 21-day intervals) and serum samples were collected at 21 days after booster. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) response was monitored by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Flat-bottom, 96-well plates (Becton Dickinson, Oxnard, CA) were coated with recombinant TcG2 and TcG4 proteins or Tc lysate (5 μg/100 μL/well) for 12 h. Plates were washed with PBS-0.01% Tween-20 (PBST) and PBS and incubated at 37 C at each step. Plates were blocked with 1% BSA-PBST for 1 h and incubated with sera samples from mice immunized with empty plasmid, BCV, or BCVR (1:50 dilution in 1% BSA-PBST, 100 μL/well) for 2 h and goat anti-mouse IgG–horseradish peroxidase conjugate (1:1,000 dilution in 1% BSA-PBST) for 1 h. Colour was developed with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) chromogenic reagent (100 μL/well, Kirkegaard & Perry Labs, Gaithersburg, MD) and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm by using a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Experimental model

C3H/HeJ, C57BL/6, and BALB/c mice show different susceptibilities to Tc infection and CD progression. In our experience, when infected with a similar strain and dose of Tc, C3H mice exhibited persistence of a high load of parasites and inflammation, and BALB/c mice exhibited severe acute effects with a mixed immune response. In comparison, C57BL/6 displayed control of acute parasitaemia, survived the challenge infection, and developed chronic cardiomyopathy, thus offering an opportunity to study the effect of immunotherapies against chronic CD. Thus, all studies were conducted in C57BL/6 mice.

Eight-week-old, adult female mice (C57BL/6) were challenged with Tc (10,000-trypomastigotes/mouse, intraperitoneal). From 21-day post-challenge, mice were treated with BCV or BCVR. We have previously found that at least two doses of pCDNA3.TcG2 and pCDNA3.TcG4 (25 μg each) delivered at 21 days interval were required for eliciting significant control of Tc infection in mice [24]. NTC9385R is >50% lesser in size (1753bp vs. 5546 bp), and more efficacious in gene expression than pCDNA3. We, therefore, chose to deliver low doses of BCV and BCVR at 10 μg/mouse, which equates to 0.5 mg/kg. Following the standardized conditions [24], two doses were delivered intramuscularly at 21-day intervals to achieve antigen-specific immune activation. To compare BCV/BCVR efficacy against the current standard therapy, a group of mice was treated with BZN. BZN treatment at 30–50 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks or 100 mg/kg/day for 3 weeks was found to be effective in controlling the parasite burden in mice [25]. Considering the potential adverse effects of BZN, we chose to treat mice with 50 mg/kg BZN for 6 weeks in drinking water. Mice with and without Tc infection and treated with vehicle or empty vector were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

C57BL/6 mice infected with SylvioX10 trypomastigotes exhibit acute parasitaemia for approximately 2 months. Subsequently, persistence of low-grade parasites, inflammatory infiltrate, fibrosis, and oxidative stress leads to chronic cardiomyopathy and LV dysfunction by 6–8-month post-infection [26]. Therefore, for all experiments, mice were euthanized at 65- and 210-day post-infection to evaluate treatment efficacy against the acute parasitemic phase and chronic disease phase, respectively.

After each treatment, mice were monitored daily for 1 week and on alternate days for 2 weeks and physical/clinical well-being was scored as (1) healthy, (2): ruffled-fur, lethargic, (3): hunched, orbital-tightening, >15% weight-loss, and (4): >20% weight loss along with other features of score 3. Lesion size (<10, 11–15, >16 mm), erythaema, induration, swelling (<20, 21–50, >51 mm), and local reactogenicity lasting for <3, 4–7, >8 days were scored as 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Blood samples were kept for 1 h at room temperature in EDTA vacutainers, centrifuged at 2000xg for 30 min, and plasma stored at 4°C and −20°C, respectively. Tissue sections were stored at −80°C for DNA/RNA purification or fixed in 10% formalin for histology. Mice were monitored for LV function in the chronic phase. The schematic timeline of infection, treatment, and assessments is presented in Figure 1(d).

Anti-cardiac IgGs

Normal mouse heart tissues (100 mg) were homogenized for 2 cycles (30 s each) using the Tissue-Tearor (BioSpec Products, Bartlesville, OK), sonicated on ice for 5 min (10 s ON, 10 s OFF), centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and soluble lysates were evaluated for protein concentration by Bradford assay. Corning flat-bottom 96-well plates were coated with heart lysate (5 μg/100 μL/well) for 2 h, and blocked with 1% BSA-PBST for 1 h, and incubated with sera samples from acutely infected mice treated with BCV, BCVR, or BZN (1:100–1:400 dilutions, 50 μL/well) for 2 h and HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:1000 dilution) for 30 min. Plates were washed with PBST and PBS and incubated at 37°C at each step. Finally, colour was developed with TMB substrate (100 μL/well) and recorded at 450 nm.

Parasite burden by qPCR

Heart and skeletal muscle (SkM) tissues (25 mg) were homogenized on ice (2 cycles, 30 s each), and total DNA was isolated using a Qiagen DNeasy kit, and assessed for quality (OD260nm/OD280nm = 1.8–2.0) and quantity (OD260nm of 1 = 50 μg/mL) using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Reaction mix (10 μL) containing 12.5 ng DNA, 300nM of Tc18SrDNA-specific, murine Gapdh-specific oligonucleotides, and SYBR-Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) were subjected to 40 cycles of qPCR amplification (95°C/10 s, 63°C/30 s) on CFX384 Touch Real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate. The PCR baseline subtracted curve fit mode was applied to calculate threshold cycle (Ct) values, and ΔCt (Ct target – Ct reference) was calculated to normalize the values for target to mean Ct values of the Gapdh housekeeping gene. The relative change in target was calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method [2−(ΔCt of sample) ÷ 2−(ΔCt of control)].

Immunofluorescence assay for tissue parasites

Opal IHC kit (NEL810001KT, Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) was used for the detection of Tc lipophosphoglycan (LPG) using Tcr-7D3+ antibody (LS-Bio, Vector Labs). Briefly, formalin-fixed 5-micron heart tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and graded alcohol, immersed in the citrate buffer (10 mM citric acid, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 6.0), and microwaved (100% power for 45 s, 20% power for 15 min) for antigen retrieval. Slides were rinsed with TBST (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20), and tissue sections on slides were marked with an ImmEdge hydrophobic barrier pen (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA). Slides were sequentially incubated at room temperature with PerkinElmer blocking/diluent buffer for 10 min, primary antibody for 6 h, Opal polymer anti-mouse/rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 10 min, and Opal fluorophore (Opal-520, diluted in the amplification buffer) for 10 min. Opal fluorophore noncovalently binds with opal HRP-IgG, allowing signal amplification. At each step, slides were washed with TBST (3 times, 2 min each). Slides were counter-stained with 1X DAPI and mounted using anti-fade fluorescence mounting medium (ab104135, Abcam, Waltham, MA). The Tcr-7D3+ antibody to detect TcLPG was titrated to obtain a maximal signal-to-noise ratio. Slides were imaged at 10X magnification by using an Olympus IX51 fluorescence microscope equipped with an XM10 camera and CellSens software (v.4.2). Images were collected using DAPI and FITC (Opal 520) filters and analyzed using QuPath (v.0.5.1) software. Percentages of Tcr-7D3+ were calculated from the total number of DAPI+ cells present in a microscopic field. Average positive cell (%) was calculated by analyzing tissue slides from five mice per group (2 slides per tissue, 9 microscopic fields per tissue section).

Histology

Heart and SkM tissue sections were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, dehydrated in graded ethyl alcohol, and cleared in xylene. Paraffin-embedded, five-micron tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E, nuclei: blue, extracellular matrix and cytoplasm: pink) or Masson’s trichrome (nuclei: dark-red, fibres, and cytoplasm: pink-red, collagen: blue).

Images were acquired and analyzed using an Olympus IX51 microscope equipped with a digital camera and CellSens v4.3 software. Briefly, each tissue-section was scanned in >5-microscopic fields and images captured as.png files. The input.png files were converted to output.h5 files using FIJI/ImageJ (v.1.54f). The ilastik (v.1.4.0.post1) was trained on cumulative images (5–7 per group) to segment original image pixels for background, tissue, nuclei, or collagen according to the colour and shape of the stain. Each image was then pseudo-colored (red, green, and blue) and analyzed for different colouration pixels using Pixel classification/ImageJ. From H&E-stained tissues, the inflammation score was calculated as per cent nuclei = red (nuclei) pixels / (red + green (ECM/cytoplasm) total tissue pixels) * 100. From Masson’s Trichrome-stained tissues, the fibrosis score was calculated as per cent fibrotic tissue = red (collagen) pixels / (red + green total tissue pixels) * 100.

RT-qPCR

Frozen heart and SkM tissue sections were homogenized in Trizol reagent (tissue: buffer ratio, 1:10, v/v), and total RNA was isolated by the chloroform/isopropanol/ethanol method. Total RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I (AM2222, Ambion, Austin, TX) and analyzed for quality and quantity as above. RNA (1 μg) was reverse-transcribed using an iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad), and the reaction mixture (20 μL) containing 50 ng cDNA, 300–500 nM target-specific oligonucleotides, and SYBR-Green Supermix was subjected to real-time qPCR for 40-cycles (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30 s). Relative expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, as above.

Biomarkers of inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiac disease

A custom-designed Mouse Luminex Discovery 16-plex Panel (LXSAMSM-16, Bio-Techne/R&D Systems) was used to examine the expression levels of CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, CCL7, CXCL1, CXCL10, ICAM1, IFNγ, IL1β, IL6, MMP3, FGF, TIMP1, TNFα, TNFRI, and TNFRII. The kit included analyte-specific antibodies pre-coated onto magnetic microparticles embedded with fluorophores at set ratios. Microparticles, standards, and plasma samples were added into wells to allow immobilized antibodies to bind to sample analytes. Then, a cocktail of biotin-conjugated analyte-specific antibodies and streptavidin-phycoerythrin conjugate was added. Plates were washed at each step to remove unbound materials. Finally, microparticles were resuspended and analyzed using a Bio-Plex 200 System (Bio-Rad), which uses one laser to excite the dyes in each microparticle to identify the microparticle region and a second laser to excite phycoerythrin to measure the amount of analyte bound to the microparticle. The concentration of the unknown sample was calculated by fitting a 5-parameter logistic curve to a cocktail-mix standard curve.

An ELISA was performed to assess plasma levels of copeptin, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), myostatin, endostatin, galectin-3, and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), following manufacturers’ instructions. Standard curves were developed using the recombinant proteins provided with the ELISA kits. The intra- and inter-assay variability values were 3.0–15.0% and 2.0–15%, respectively, for various assays. Absorbances for endpoint colour were measured at 450 nm.

Echocardiography

Mice were sedated with 1.5% isoflurane/100% O2 on a 37°C heating pad. ECG electrodes of Vevo2100 ultrasound (VisualSonics) were connected to the mouse paws to monitor heart rate and respiratory physiology. Ultrasound gel was applied to the shaved mouse chest, and an MS400 (18–38 MHz) transducer was used for high-resolution echocardiography. The heart was imaged in B-mode and M-mode to examine the structural and functional parameters of the left ventricle in diastole (-d) and systole (-s), respectively. Pulse wave Doppler imaging was performed to measure heart diastolic function. All measurements were obtained in duplicate, and data were analyzed using the Vevo2100 software.

Statistical analysis

Using preliminary mean and standard deviation values on inflammatory events in infected vs. control mice, we calculated that enrollment of a minimum of 5-mice/group will provide 95% confidence interval and achieve a statistical power of 80% with a probability of type 1 error (α) at the 0.05 level. We, therefore, enrolled 5-mice/group, and conducted all studies in at least two sets of independent experiments to ensure reproducibility.

In graphs, individual datapoints show the average of technical replicates per mouse. The mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) values show the average of biological replicates from two or more independent experiments. No effects of vehicle or empty vector were observed on the experimental outcomes, and the pooled data are shown in the negative control group. ComBat was applied to identify/adjust batch effects due to lab protocols. In general, we did not observe batch effects as the biological variables (e.g. age, gender, genetic background) were controlled, and immunogens, parasites, and other reagents used in the study were bulk produced (or purchased), controlling the potential batch-to-batch variations throughout the study.

Statistical analysis was performed on biological replicates. The Smirnov–Kolmogorov test was used to check data normality. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post-hoc test (normally distributed data) or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc test (non-normally distributed data) was performed to evaluate significance comparing control, infected, and infected/treated groups. For the control group, no effects of vehicle or empty vector were observed on the experimental outcomes, and pooled data were used for statistical analysis. The Pearson correlation test was performed, and the coefficient (r) was used to find a linear relationship between two continuous, normally distributed variables. Nonparametric Spearman’s rank correlation was performed, and the coefficient (ρ) was used to determine a monotonic relationship between ranked and non-normally distributed variables. All datasets were managed in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Verification of BCV/BCVR expression and immunotherapy safety

Schematics of BCV and BCVR design are presented in Figure 1(a) and 1(b)). To verify that BCV and BCVR express the encoded antigens, mice were immunized with BCV or BCVR (10 μg each, 2 doses at 21 days interval), and sera samples obtained 21 days after the second immunization were analyzed by an ELISA using recombinant Tc2 and TcG4 proteins or Tc antigenic lysate. We noted 150% to 387% increase in anti-TcG2, anti-TcG4, and anti-Tc IgG levels (BCVR > BCV) in immunized (vs. control) mice (Figure 1(c)). These data confirmed that BCV and BCVR encoded antigens were expressed in vivo.

Schematics of experimental design are presented in Figure 1(d). Briefly, adult mice were challenged with Tc, treated with BCV, BCVR or BZN, and euthanized at 65 days (acute phase) and 210 days (chronic phase) post-infection. Our intent was to determine if 2-dose BCV or BCVR immunotherapies exhibit comparable (or better) effectiveness than long-term BZN treatment for 6-weeks in protecting adult mice from Tc infection and CD.

Mice challenged with Tc exhibited no fur changes, and no lesions, swelling, or reactogenicity at the injection site. Mild erythema and local reactogenicity lasting for <3 days were noted after intramuscular delivery of BCV and BCVR, though it had no adverse effects on the physical activity of mice. Lethargic behaviour, evidenced by ruffled fur, lasted for >2 weeks in mice during the BZN treatment. Serum levels of anti-cardiac IgGs (OD: 0.35–0.44 vs. 0.13, 1: 100 dilution) were slightly elevated post-challenge and not worsened by any of the treatments. These findings verify the in vivo safety of BCV and BCVR immunogens.

Tissue distribution of T. cruzi is decreased post-treatment with immunotherapy

Tissue parasites were evaluated by Tc18SrDNA-specific qPCR (Figure 1(e–h), supplementary Table S1). Parasites were detected at very high levels in the heart and skeletal muscle (SkM) of acutely infected mice. Treatment with BCV, BCVR, or BZN resulted in >98.5% reduction in myocardial and SkM levels of acute parasite burden (Figure 1(e) and 1(f)). Chronic parasite persistence at 210 days remained detectable (albeit at lower levels than the acute phase) in the heart tissue of infected mice, and it was further reduced by 66% and 39% with BCVR and BZN treatments, respectively (Figure 1(g)). Tc level remained significantly elevated in SkM tissue of chronically infected mice, and it was decreased by 96.8–97.7% after BCV, BCVR, or BZN treatment (Figure 1(h)).

To verify the therapeutic efficacy of the immunogens, heart tissues of acutely and chronically infected mice (without and with BCV or BCVR treatment) were analyzed by immunofluorescence microscopy for TcLPG antigen. Representative images along with percentages of TcLPG+/DAPI+ cells in myocardial tissues of acutely and chronically infected mice are shown (supplementary Figure S1). As above, we noted an extensive increase in Tc antigen in heart tissue of acutely infected (vs. control) mice, and it was decreased by 76.3–87.5% in immunogen-treated groups (supplementary Figure S1(a–e)). Likewise, TcLPG antigenic signal was persistent in heart tissue of chronically infected mice, and it was decreased by 76.2–91.4% by immunotherapies (supplementary Figure S1(f–j)). The maximal decline in Tc antigenic signal was noted in the BCVR-treated mice.

These results showed that 2-dose immunotherapies were highly effective and can replace long-term BZN treatment for controlling the acute and chronic tissue parasites in adult mice.

Systemic inflammation in Tc-infected mice is controlled by BCV/BCVR treatment

Systemic inflammation is characterized by widespread tissue infiltration of inflammatory infiltrate and release of inflammatory and reactive mediators in circulation. We first performed H&E staining to evaluate tissue inflammation. Representative images along with semi-quantitative inflammation scores of myocardial and SkM tissues of acutely and chronically infected mice are shown (Figure 2, supplementary Table S2). Widespread inflammation with disseminated inflammatory infiltrate or large inflammatory foci resulting in loss of tissue integrity was predominant in the heart and SkM tissues of acutely infected mice (inflammation score: 25.7 and 33.8, respectively, Figure 2(b,h,f,l)). Tc-induced inflammatory infiltrate was decreased by 47.4–50.5% (Tc.BCV = Tc.BCVR = Tc.BZN) and 38.3–63.2% (Tc.BCV > Tc. BCVR > Tc.BZN) in heart and SkM tissues, respectively, of acutely infected/treated groups (Figure 2(a–l)), and coalescing inflammatory foci were still noted in SkM of the Tc.BZN group (Figure 2(k)). In the chronic phase, disseminated or focal inflammatory sheets persisted in the heart and SkM tissues of infected/untreated mice (inflammation score: 26.2–27.3). In comparison, inflammatory infiltrate was decreased by 31.1–46.2% (Tc.BCVR > Tc.BCV = Tc.BZN) and 22–63% (Tc.BCV > Tc. BZN > Tc.BCVR), respectively, in the heart and SkM tissues of chronically infected/treated mice (Figure 2(m–x)). Mock-infected mice exhibited no detectable Tc DNA and antigens, and an inflammatory infiltrate in their tissues. These results revealed that BCV and BCVR immunotherapies were similarly or more effective than BZN in controlling the tissue infiltration of inflammatory infiltrate after enhancing the host’s ability to arrest replication and persistence of parasites. Circulatory biomarkers of inflammation, cell death, and cell migration were evaluated by a 16-plex immunoassay (Figure 3, supplementary Table S3). No detectable levels of TNFα, FGF, ICAM1, and CCL3 were noted in the plasma of all enrolled groups. Proinflammatory cytokines (IL1β, IL6, and IFNγ) and chemokines (CCL2, CCL5, CCL7, CXCL1, and CXCL10) were increased by 1.4–3.6-fold and 0.5–10.3-fold, respectively, in plasma of acutely infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 3(a–h)). Chronic persistence of secreted cytokines, chemokines, and other biomarkers was decreased by 42.1–89.2%, 49.3–90.3% (except CXCL10), and 75.3–92.7%, respectively, in infected mice after either of the treatments (Figure 3(m–x)). These results provide evidence that proinflammatory/cell death response persists and remains aggravated during the acute-to-chronic progression of CD. BCV immunotherapy was effective in arresting the tissue (Figure 2) and circulatory (Figure 3) inflammatory response throughout the infection and disease development, while BCVR-, and BZN-mediated protection from inflammation was more evident in the chronic phase.

Cardiac and peripheral biomarkers of fibrosis and disease progression in CD (± treatment)

Next, we determined if immunotherapies were efficacious against cardiac pathology in CD. We first performed Masson's Trichrome staining to visualize and score the tissue fibrosis in the chronic phase of CD in infected mice (Figure 4(a–l), supplementary Table S4a). These data showed an intense increase in myofibroblasts’ permeation and collagen deposition in the heart (fibrosis score: 22.83) and SkM (fibrosis score: 17.94) of the infected/untreated (vs. control) group (Figure 4(b,h)). Chronic infiltration of myofibroblasts and collagen deposition were normalized to control level in the heart and decreased by 53.1–84.5% in SkM fibres of infected/BCV-treated (vs. infected/untreated) mice (Figure 4(c,i)). Collagen deposition was reduced by 36.3–78% in the cardiac (but not SkM) tissues of Tc.BCVR and Tc.BZN (vs. infected/untreated) groups (Figure 4(f,l)).

Masson’s Trichrome method is limited in distinguishing type I and type III collagens that provide structural strength and elasticity, respectively. An increase in muscle volume with an increase in type III/type I ratio often indicates flexible remodelling, while an increase in type I/type III ratio is associated with stiffer, less functional muscle tissues. We, therefore, performed RTqPCR to corroborate the histological findings (Figure 4(m–t), supplementary Table S4(b)). These data revealed that mRNAs encoding for COL1A1, COL3A1, and COL5A1 collagens were increased by 4.9–13.3-fold in the myocardium of chronically infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 4(m–o)). Similarly, mRNAs for TGFβ that signals hypertrophic growth and metalloproteinases (MMP2, MMP9, MMP12, and MMP13) that break down extracellular matrix during hypertrophic remodelling were increased by 9.8-fold and 4.7–52.2-fold, respectively, in the myocardium of chronically infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 4(p–t)). Treatment with BCV, BCVR, or BZN led to 3.5–16.9-fold, 1.7–6.7-fold, and 1–21.8-fold decline in the myocardial levels of mRNAs encoding collagens, TGFββ), and metalloproteinases, respectively, in infected/treated (vs. infected/untreated) CD mice. In general, BCV was slightly more effective than BCVR and BZN in blocking the pro-fibrotic/hypertrophic gene expression in chronically infected mice.

There is a lack of validated prognostic biomarkers for diagnosing the risk of heart disease development in Tc-infected individuals. In a recent study, we screened several potential biomarkers in human patients presenting a spectrum of CD symptoms and found that copeptin, endostatin and 8-OHdG could be useful in diagnosing the clinical disease progression [27]. We, therefore, evaluated if these and other biomarkers of heart disease were altered in infected/untreated (vs. infected/treated) mice (Figure 5, supplementary Table S5). Copeptin (breakdown product of vasopressin, stable biomarker of myocardial infarction), galectin (biomarker of inflammatory heart disease), and myostatin (biomarker of cardiac muscle wasting) are being developed for predicting heart failure, and these biomarkers were elevated by 132–212% (Figure 5(a–c)) and 99–400% (Figure 5(g–i)) in acutely and chronically infected (vs. control) mice, respectively. Treatment of infected mice with BCV, BCVR, or BZN decreased these circulatory biomarkers of cardiac involvement by 12–55% and 30–76%, respectively, in acute and chronic phases. Endostatin (inhibits angiogenesis, biomarker of increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events) was moderately elevated in acutely infected mice, and it did not change post-treatment (Figure 5(d)). 4-HNE may contribute to heart failure by causing oxidative damage, and 8-OHdG is a biomarker of oxidative DNA damage. We noted none-to-moderate changes in the 4-HNE levels in the studied groups (Figure 5(e,k)). Plasma levels of 8-OHdG were increased by 192% and 262% in acutely and chronically infected (vs. control) mice, respectively, and treatment with BCV, BCVR, and BZN resulted in 31.9–54.6% and 61.8–73.8% decline in circulatory 8-OHdG levels, in acute and chronic phase, respectively (Figure 5(f,l)).

Together, the results in Figures 3–5 showed that evolution from acute to chronic Tc infection promotes proinflammatory response with tissue damage, tissue fibrosis, and remodelling in the myocardium. Increased cardiac expression of collagens, MMPs, and TGFβ in chronically infected mice indicates that the heart is actively undergoing pathological remodelling, where the elevated infiltration of inflammatory infiltrate plays a key role in transitioning into fibrosis and structural damage. Moreover, such detrimental alterations in cardiac tissue were also evident by the detection of several proinflammatory, tissue damage, and cardiovascular disease-related biomarkers in the circulation of infected mice. Our data revealed the strong therapeutic efficacy of BCV in arresting cardiac proinflammatory response and fibrosis in acute and chronic phases of CD progression. BCVR and BZN therapy were more effective in controlling chronic cardiac tissue damage.

Left ventricular function is preserved by immunotherapy treatment in CD mice

Echocardiography imaging was performed to determine the functional efficacy of the immunotherapies in CD (Figure 6, supplementary Table S6). LV mass was increased by 66.9%, while LV posterior wall (LVPW) was thinned by 40.7–45.8% during systole and diastole in chagasic (vs. control) mice (Figure 6(a–c)). Significant changes in LV inner diameter (LVID-s, 73.9% increase), and LV volume at systole (LVVS, 74.7% increase) and diastole (LVVD, 28.5% decline) indicated increased stiffness of the heart, which adversely affected its ability to pump blood in chronically infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 6(d–f)). These structural features (LV mass, LVPW-s, LVPW-d) were improved towards normal levels in the order of Tc.BCVR ≥ Tc.BCV > Tc.BZN in chronically infected/treated groups (Figure 6(a–c)), and improvements in LV systolic and diastolic volumes were maximally noted in the Tc.BCV group (Figure 6(e–f)). The indices of LV systolic function, i.e. stroke volume (SV), cardiac output (CO), and ejection fraction (EF), were decreased by 43.3%, 43.1%, and 34.3%, respectively, in chronically infected (vs. control) mice (Figure 6(g–i)). Fractional shortening (FS) is directly correlated with changes in SV, CO, and EF at systole, and it was decreased by 51.5% in chronically infected mice (Figure 6(j)). In comparison, a partial-to-full recovery of systolic/diastolic function, and myocardial contraction and relaxation indices was noted in infected/treated groups in the order of Tc.BCV = Tc.BCVR > Tc.BZN.

Correlation analysis was conducted to determine if changes in clinical biomarkers exhibit a direct relationship with changes in heart function in immunogen-treated and non-treated infected groups (supplementary Figure S2). In chronically infected, untreated mice, an increase in plasma levels of 8-OHdG, copeptin and galectin was negatively correlated with several parameters of LV function, including EF, CO, SV, and LVVD, and positively correlated with LVVS, indicating that clinical biomarkers increased with loss of LV function in CD mice. When infected mice were treated with BCV, correlations of 8-OHdG with CO and LVVS, copeptin with LVVS, and galectin with EF, CO, and LVVS were nullified. When infected mice were treated with BCVR, the correlation of copeptin with SV and LVVD, and galectin with CO, SV, and LVVD was neutralized. Further, infected mice treated with BZN exhibited that correlations of 8-OHdG, copeptin, and galectin with decline in CO, LVVD, and LVVS were abolished.

Together, these results suggest that changes in the LV walls’ thickness (and thereby contractile capacity) contributed to compromised systolic and diastolic performance of the heart in CD mice, and the benefits of the immunotherapies in preserving LV hemodynamics and myocardial performance were delivered via control of collagenosis and stiffness of the left ventricle in CD mice. Long-term treatment with anti-parasite chemical therapy (benznidazole) moderately improved the LV function in chronically infected mice. Furthermore, the correlation analysis showed that circulatory levels of clinical biomarkers (8-OHdG, copeptin, and galectin) were inversely correlated with LV function-related parameters in CD. In chronically infected/untreated mice, an increase in clinical biomarkers indicated a decline in heart function. Treatment of infected mice with immunogens resulted in a general decline in clinical biomarkers and normalization of the heart function.

Discussion

Trypanosoma cruzi infection continues to be a global health concern, as it leads to chronic heart disease associated with high morbidity and mortality. Recent estimates indicate that more than 10.5 million people are living with T. cruzi infection and over 75-million individuals are at risk of Tc infection in the endemic regions of the Americas [28–30]. Recent analyses of the global burden of diseases suggest that Tc infection resulted in approximately 308,000–398,000 new cases of Chagas disease worldwide in 2023 [30]. It is estimated that 10–40% of infected individuals will develop symptomatic heart disease and CD-related cardiac complications, causing approximately 8,500–12,000 deaths annually [30]. In addition, non-endemic regions have experienced notable increases in disease prevalence due to the migration of infected individuals from endemic countries [31]. In the US alone, approximately 0.5-million Latin American-born individuals infected with Tc are living, of whom about 20% will progress to chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy, contributing an economic burden of $4.96 billion (2019 USD) in healthcare costs [32]. Furthermore, changes in vectorial distribution and the persistence of active transmission cycles in wild and domestic animal reservoirs have led to the detection of autochthonous cases of Tc infection in at least eight U.S. states [33]. Overall, the global burden of CD is estimated to be 1.9-, 8.8-, and 35.4-times greater than that of HIV, malaria, and tuberculosis, respectively [32]. These findings underscore the urgent need for increased investment in the development of new and effective treatments for Chagas disease.

Early detection of Tc infection and treatment with currently approved drug therapies – benznidazole and nifurtimox – are critical for preventing chronic complications of CD. These drugs primarily target actively replicating parasites and are, therefore, most effective during the acute parasitemic phase [6]. However, Tc infection is frequently asymptomatic, leading to underdiagnosis and missed opportunities for early treatment. Once the acute parasitemic phase is controlled by the immune system and the host enters the indeterminate-to-chronic phase, the parasite acquires a dormant, non-replicative form that persists in various tissues and is less susceptible to drug treatment [34]. In addition, treatment with anti-parasitic drugs in chronically infected adults is often limited by significant adverse effects, including rash, gastrointestinal problems, and neuropathy, which can result in treatment discontinuation [35]. Evidence supporting the efficacy of BZN in chronic infection remains inconclusive; for example, a systematic review identified 4666 reports testing the efficacy of BZN in Tc-infected adults. Of these, only 23 studies met the pre-defined inclusion criteria, thus highlighting the low-to-moderate quality of available data for conclusively addressing the impact of BZN treatment in chronically ill adults [36]. Consequently, many adults with established Tc infection do not opt for anti-parasitic therapy, and instead seek clinical care focused on managing cardiac function once symptomatic heart complications have developed [37]. Overall, the limited effectiveness of current therapies in chronic disease reflects multiple challenges, including drug toxicity, limited availability, lack of optimized treatment regimens for chronic infection, and the absence of reliable biomarkers or rapid tests to confirm treatment successes [38].

Studies conducted in genetically modified mice and other experimental models, as well as in humans, have established that an immunotherapy will need to enhance multiple immune effector mechanisms for reducing/eliminating parasite persistence and be effective against CD. CD4+T lymphocytes producing IFNγ and cytolytic activity of CD8+T lymphocytes kill the intracellular parasites [39]. The Th1-polarized T cells also contribute to stimulation of Tc-specific antibodies, facilitating phagocytosis by opsonization or direct parasite killing by complement-mediated lysis [39]. Yet, an unbalanced, long-term immune activation can allow the development of a dormant form of the parasite that can hide in tissues and escape immune detection, and also cause a direct deleterious effect on the host tissue, contributing to the immunopathology of CD [40]. Keeping these observations at the upfront, studies examining the potential utility of immunodominant epitopes as immunotherapies against CD obtained mixed results. For example, Tc24, Tc52, and trypomastigote surface antigen (TSA1) were found to enhance parasite-directed Th1 immunity in mice [41,42]. Consequently, therapeutic administration of Tc24 and TSA1 enhanced the survival from an otherwise lethal, acute Tc infection in mice [43] and elicited no adverse effects in infected macaques [44]. Yet, Tc24 and TSA1 failed to arrest cardiomyopathy in chronically infected dogs [45]. The immunodominant repeat region of trans-sialidase (TS) offered significant protection from lethal challenge infection despite eliciting mixed Th1/Th2 T cell response in the Balb/c mice [46]. Others found that ASP2- and TS-based immunotherapies were not efficacious in limiting parasitaemia or mortality [47], and TSA1 enhanced myocarditis in infected mice [48]. Treatment with SSP4 and TS immunogens reduced the magnitude of cardiac lesions and LV dysfunction in experimentally infected dogs [49]. Recently, a chimeric immunogen, Traspain incorporating the immunodominant epitopes of cruzipain, TS, and ASP2, was designed to elicit a broad, polyfunctional response. Traspain induced humoral and cell-mediated immunity that provided cross-protection from infection with different Tc strains, and chronic inflammation and heart damage in mice [50]. In alignment with these studies, we engineered BCV immunotherapy, incorporating two antigens with or without an adjuvant and used a refined delivery framework to enhance the expression of cloned antigens in adherence to the established regulatory compliance guidelines. Both immunogens enhanced the splenic frequencies of Tc-specific, mature effector, and effector/effector memory T cells; CD4+T subsets exhibiting increased production of cytolytic molecules for parasite killing, and CD8+T subsets displaying protective function to limit tissue damage in immunized/infected mice. Further, BCV and BCVR immunogens enhanced the Tc-specific antibodies that were functionally active and capable of controlling the parasite motility and survivability [51]. Our findings in this study demonstrate that treatment with two-dose BCV and BCVR was equally or more effective than the long-term BZN treatment in controlling acute replication and chronic persistence of parasites. Subsequently, BCV or BCVR treatment of infected mice impeded the tissue inflammatory infiltrate and cardiac fibrosis that otherwise were pronounced in chronically infected, untreated adult mice. Both BCV and BCVR elicited no cardiac toxicity, as is commonly observed with BZN. Importantly, BCV and BCVR immunotherapies provided superior improvement of cardiac function in chronically infected mice than was observed by BZN treatment of infected mice. These findings underscore the importance of BCV and BCVR immunogen-based therapy over long-term treatment with benznidazole in preserving the myocardial contraction and relaxation-related functions during chronic CD.

Some studies have shown that BZN treatment in infected adult humans reduces the circulating parasites but does not significantly prevent cardiac deterioration over five years of follow-up in CD patients [52]. Our findings in this study similarly demonstrate that, although BZN and immunogens provide comparable control of acute parasitaemia, BZN is least effective at reducing parasite persistence and associated inflammation, and offers minimal-to-moderate protection of cardiac function in chronically infected mice. Previous work from our group showed that supplementing BZN with phenyl-alpha-tert-butyl nitrone, an antioxidant, was necessary to control chronic inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as improve LV contractile activity in CD rats [53]. Building on these observations and our current data, it can be surmised that while BZN effectively eliminates circulating parasites during the acute phase, it fails to modulate protective immunity. This limitation allows parasite persistence and sustained inflammation, both of which contribute to progressive cardiac damage in CD. Indeed, a combination therapy incorporating BZN with Tc24 or TSA1 immunogens was found to offer both trypanocidal and immunologic benefits in mice infected with Tc [54,55]. Thus, adjunctive strategies that pair BZN with immune-modulating therapies represent a promising approach for controlling disease progression in CD.

Increasing innate immune signalling to potentiate adaptive immunity represents a promising strategy for effective control of intracellular pathogens. For example, adjuvanting with a dominant negative isoform of Src homology region 2 domain-containing tyrosine phosphatase 1 (SHP1) prevented the wild-type SHP1-dependent inhibitory “off switch,” thereby enhancing the frequency of Tc24- and TSA1-specific CD8+ IFNγ+ T cells in BALB/c mice [56]. Similarly, codelivery of the TLR4 agonist E6020 with Tc24 immunogen augmented protective CD8+T cell responses against acute lethal infection in mice [41,42]. In addition, codelivery of c-di-AMP, an activator of the STING pathway, enhanced Traspain chimeric antigen-dependent priming of functional humoral response as well as pathogen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, resulting in improved control of acute infection in mice [50]. Notably, these studies primarily evaluated prophylactic efficacy during acute parasitaemia and did not assess the therapeutic potential of adjuvanted immunogens against chronic CD. In the present study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of BCVR comprising TcG2 and TcG4 antigens in combination with a RIG-I agonist in an experimental model of chronic CD. RIG-I is a cytosolic pattern recognition receptor that detects short, double-stranded RNA and initiates signalling cascades culminating in IRF3/7- and NFκB-dependent transcriptional programs [19]. RIG-I agonists have been extensively investigated in cancer and antiviral settings due to their capacity to induce type I interferons, proinflammatory cytokines, and immunogenic cell death [57,58]. Herein, RIG-adjuvanted BCVR was highly effective in controlling the parasite burden. However, BCVR-treated mice exhibited peripheral and tissue inflammation and tissue fibrosis that was lower than that noted in untreated/infected mice but higher than the BCV-treated mice. Others have noted that stem-loop RNA (SLR) RIG-I agonists, combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g. anti-PD1), can elicit durable immune memory and improved long-term outcomes [59,60]. Moreover, targeted, localized delivery approaches, such as nanoparticle-based systems, have been demonstrated to minimize toxicity while enhancing both local and systemic immunostimulatory effects of RIG-I agonist(s) as cancer immunotherapy [61]. Nevertheless, although RIG-I agonists are consistently effective in prophylactic or early therapeutic settings, their therapeutic efficacy appears diminished in advanced stages of chronic infections, as observed in late-stage SARS-CoV-2 models [60]. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for further investigation to optimize the structural design, timing, and delivery strategies of RIG-I agonists to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing immune-mediated toxicity in chronic infectious diseases.

In the present study, CXCL1 level was not increased significantly during the acute infection phase in all experimental groups. CXCL1 is a part of the CXCR2-binding chemokines’ network that together promote neutrophil recruitment. Immune response to acute Tc infection is primarily driven by macrophages and CD8+ T cells [39]. Further, acute Tc infection blunts the CXCL1-mediated neutrophil recruitment by undermining the IL-17 signalling pathway; absence of IL-17RA signalling leads to reduced expression of CXCL1, weakening the neutrophil-dependent immune response [62]. Thus, it can be surmised that CXCL1-driven neutrophil recruitment and activation through CXCR2 receptor is not a dominant immune signalling network in the acute phase of Chagas disease.

8-OHdG and 4-HNE are widely recognized as specific biomarkers of oxidative DNA damage and lipid peroxidation, respectively, in biological tissues. In this study, 8-OHdG levels were significantly elevated in infected mice and were markedly reduced following control of parasite burden and infection-associated inflammation in BCV/BCVR- or BZN-treated mice. In contrast, no significant differences in 4-HNE levels were observed among the experimental groups. This lack of change may be attributed to the rapid metabolism of 4-HNE, which limits its detectability in biological samples. In comparison, 4-HNE–protein adducts are more stable and represent a more reliable indicator of lipid peroxidation; therefore, their assessment should be prioritized in future studies.

In summary, we surmise that control of cardiac fibrosis and dysregulated cardiac events in chronic disease states is an achievable goal with the use of bicistronic BCV/BCVR immunogens, and our treatment-based findings provide promising future therapies for the prevention of Chagas cardiomyopathy. We found low-dose administration of BCV/BCVR immunogens (0.5 mg/kg, 2-doses at 21-day intervals) was well tolerated and elicited no adverse effects in adult mice, while BZN treatment (50 mg/kg) for 6 weeks was required to achieve similar efficacy as BCV/BCVR. Further, BCV and BCVR are simple in design and delivery and offer low-cost alternatives to prevent and control CD morbidities in adults that have not been uniformly feasible with benznidazole treatment.

Limitations of the study

The present study did not evaluate potential sex-based differences, as all experiments were conducted exclusively in female mice. It is also possible that BCV/BCVR immunotherapies delayed, rather than reversed, the progression of CD. Moreover, the use of young adult mice does not fully recapitulate the complexity of human CD, which is characterized by a prolonged latent/indeterminate phase spanning decades, accompanied by immunosenescence and chronic low-grade inflammation following the acute parasitemic phase, ultimately progressing to chronic cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Future studies should assess the long-term efficacy of BCV/BCVR in preventing cardiac pathology across the life span in both male and female mice of diverse genetic backgrounds, as well as in outbred rodent models and other relevant hosts (e.g. dogs) that are either experimentally or naturally infected with T. cruzi. Additionally, further investigation is warranted to establish the long-term safety profile of BCV/BCVR immunotherapies and to confirm the absence of adverse outcomes, including potential autoimmune responses, in treated animals during ageing.
