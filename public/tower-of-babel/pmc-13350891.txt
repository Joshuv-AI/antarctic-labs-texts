Towards explainable language reasoning via multi-modal knowledge graphs
Authors: Chunyu Lu, Jun Luo, Kang Yu, Tianran Chen, Xueli Wang, Feng Qian, Chen Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing complexity of natural language reasoning in artificial intelligence necessitates a shift from opaque, black-box processing to transparent, interpretable decision-making. While large language models (LLMs) demonstrate remarkable generative capabilities, their reasoning pathways often remain implicit, which limits auditability in high-stakes settings. To address this issue, we propose a framework for explainable language reasoning grounded in Multi-Modal Knowledge Graphs (MMKGs). Our approach unifies textual, visual, and structural knowledge in a shared graph representation, enabling the system to anchor inference in explicit semantic relations rather than in latent correlations alone. We introduce a planner-executor design in which an LLM generates constrained symbolic plans and a deterministic graph engine executes them to return both an answer and a replayable explanation subgraph. Empirically, the framework is competitive on six textual and multimodal benchmarks, reaching 79.8% Hits@1 on WebQSP and 49.3 accuracy on OK-VQA under the protocol used in this study. We deliberately report the main quantitative comparisons as point estimates and interpret small margins conservatively. The principal contribution of the work is therefore the MMKG-grounded reasoning architecture, its operationally auditable explanation mechanism, and the accompanying clarification of what can and cannot be claimed theoretically about explanation faithfulness.Keywords: Explainable AI, Multi-modal knowledge graphs, Natural language processing, Interpretable machine learning, Knowledge representationSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed how humans interact with technology, particularly through advances in natural language processing (NLP) and reasoning. Modern systems can answer complex questions, generate fluent dialogue, and integrate knowledge across multiple sources. Large language models (LLMs) have powered this progress by learning from vast corpora and encoding subtle semantic regularities. Yet their most celebrated strength-the ability to generate human-like responses-coexists with their most pressing weakness: a lack of interpretability. In high-stakes settings such as healthcare, finance, law, and scientific discovery, decision-makers cannot rely on answers that are correct most of the time but opaque all of the time. They need transparent systems that can explain how an answer was derived, cite evidence, and allow human auditors to inspect the reasoning process. As a result, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has become an urgent research priority1,2.

Although various explanation techniques exist, attention heatmaps, saliency visualizations, post-hoc rationales, many have limited faithfulness. They often approximate what looks like reasoning rather than exposing what caused a model’s decision. For example, chain-of-thought prompting allows LLMs to produce intermediate steps, but recent studies show these rationales can be unfaithful: models may generate plausible reasoning chains that are disconnected from the underlying computation3,4. This gap between generated explanations and actual decision pathways undermines trust, as end-users cannot determine whether a rationale is faithful or fabricated. In domains where accountability is critical, such as clinical decision support or judicial assistance, the risk of misleading explanations is unacceptable.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) provide a way forward. A KG encodes entities and their relationships in explicit triples, creating a semantic network that is both machine-readable and human-interpretable. When combined with machine learning, KGs can serve as external memory, constrain reasoning, and produce explicit inference paths. Methods such as KG-BERT for knowledge graph completion5, multi-hop graph reasoning networks6, and explainable QA pipelines7 illustrate the potential of KGs to add structure and interpretability. Moreover, graph neural networks (GNNs) and their explanation modules such as GNNExplainer8 provide ways to highlight subgraphs most responsible for predictions. By anchoring reasoning in a graph, explanations can emerge as traversable paths rather than abstract probabilities.

Human reasoning, however, is not purely textual. People draw inferences from visual perception, contextual cues, diagrams, and structured knowledge. The last five years have seen an explosion of multimodal AI research, particularly in vision-language pretraining. Contrastive learning approaches such as CLIP9 and ALIGN10 align images and text in shared embedding spaces, enabling zero-shot classification and retrieval. Fusion-based approaches like ALBEF11, BLIP12, and BLIP-213 integrate representations to support both understanding and generation. Large multimodal language models demonstrate few-shot learning across interleaved text and image inputs. These advances underscore the importance of multimodal learning, but they remain largely correlation-based. They lack explicit relational structure and thus cannot easily expose interpretable reasoning chains.

To achieve both performance and transparency, multimodal knowledge graphs (MMKGs) have emerged as a promising paradigm. MMKGs extend traditional KGs by incorporating multimodal data, images, audio, video, alongside text and structured triples. Nodes may carry multimodal representations, and edges may encode relations enriched with perceptual features. By grounding reasoning in MMKGs, systems can align evidence across modalities and trace explicit inference paths that include visual and textual elements. For example, a QA system might detect an object in an image, map it to a KG entity, and traverse relations to textual evidence, producing an answer accompanied by a multimodal explanation. Surveys by Chen et al.14,15 provide comprehensive overviews of MMKG construction, completion, and applications, highlighting their growing role in AI.

The literature already provides essential building blocks. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)16 combines parametric models with external memories to produce factual, citable outputs. Commonsense induction frameworks like COMET17 enrich KGs with plausible relations. Vision-language models demonstrate large-scale cross-modal associations. Explainable QA over graphs7 shows how reasoning paths can be returned with answers. Yet, no existing framework integrates all three objectives simultaneously: multimodal coverage, reasoning accuracy, and faithful explanation. Current multimodal models excel at correlation but not transparency. KG-based systems provide transparency but are limited in multimodal coverage. And XAI methods provide explanations but not always faithfully. This fragmentation leaves a gap for approaches that unify these elements.

The central problem this paper addresses is the lack of a unified framework that grounds language reasoning in multimodal knowledge graphs, enabling explanations that are both faithful and interpretable. Existing methods either generate plausible rationales without guarantees of faithfulness, or they rely on unimodal KGs that omit crucial perceptual evidence. Without multimodal grounding, explanations risk incompleteness; without structured graphs, explanations risk unfaithfulness; without careful reasoning design, explanations risk being inefficient or opaque. Bridging these challenges requires a framework that integrates MMKGs with explicit reasoning mechanisms capable of producing verifiable inference paths.

We argue that MMKGs offer three distinctive advantages. First, they provide faithfulness at the execution level: explanations are tied to explicit graph traversals rather than post-hoc approximations. Second, they provide auditability: nodes and edges can store provenance, allowing explanations to cite their evidence sources. Third, they provide adaptability: graphs can be incrementally updated without retraining, ensuring that reasoning remains current as knowledge evolves. By coupling LLM-based reasoning with MMKG-grounded execution, we aim to balance performance with interpretability, offering explanations that are useful, verifiable, and trustworthy.

Related work

Explainable AI for language reasoning. The need for interpretable AI has driven a surge of research in natural language explanations, post-hoc rationales, and visualization methods. Surveys such as Danilevsky et al.1 and Rajabi et al.2 provide comprehensive overviews of explanation approaches in NLP. Saliency-based and attention-based techniques attempt to reveal influential tokens but often lack causal faithfulness. Recent interest in chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting has further popularized natural language rationales, yet studies show that these explanations can be unfaithful to the actual decision process3. Lyu et al.4 propose constraints to enforce faithful CoT reasoning, but these methods still operate largely in a text-only setting. Overall, while XAI for language has advanced interpretability, it remains challenged by the faithfulness gap and the absence of structured, multimodal grounding.

Knowledge graph–based reasoning. Knowledge graphs (KGs) encode entities and relations in explicit triples, providing a natural substrate for interpretable reasoning. Early efforts such as KG-BERT5 adapted pretrained language models for knowledge graph completion, while Feng et al.6 developed multi-hop relational reasoning networks to enable scalable inference for QA. Explaining reasoning paths has been studied through symbolic query generation and graph traversal methods7. GNNExplainer8 and related work introduced explanation methods for graph neural networks, highlighting subgraphs responsible for predictions. Commonsense induction frameworks such as COMET17 demonstrate how LMs can expand KGs with new relations, enriching graph coverage. Despite these advances, most KG-based approaches focus on structured or textual data only, with limited integration of multimodal evidence. Recent work has also emphasized the broader role of structured external knowledge in language and multimodal reasoning. In particular, explainable QA pipelines over graphs show how explicit reasoning paths can be returned alongside answers7, while recent surveys on multi-modal knowledge graphs and KG-supported multimodal learning highlight the importance of graph structure for grounding, cross-modal alignment, and interpretability14,18. We position our work in this research line, with a particular focus on separating symbolic planning from deterministic execution so that the explanation corresponds to an auditable execution record.

Multi-modal knowledge graphs. Recent surveys highlight the construction and application of multi-modal knowledge graphs (MMKGs), which extend KGs with textual, visual, and sometimes audio modalities14,15. MMKGs provide richer context for tasks such as entity alignment, cross-modal retrieval, and knowledge completion. Yu et al.19 demonstrated cross-modal reasoning in knowledge-based visual QA, illustrating how integrating vision and graph relations can improve interpretability. At the same time, challenges remain in scalability, cross-modal alignment, and faithful explanation generation. While MMKGs promise explicit and interpretable multimodal reasoning, systematic integration with language reasoning systems is still in its infancy.

Method

We propose a unified framework for explainable language reasoning grounded in Multi-Modal Knowledge Graphs (MMKGs), as illustrated in Fig. 1. The framework converts heterogeneous inputs (text and images in the present experiments, with video treated as an architectural extension rather than an evaluated modality) into a single graph substrate, learns aligned multimodal representations, executes query-specific reasoning over the graph, and returns both an answer and a faithful explanation. The central design choice is to separate planning from execution: an LLM generates a constrained symbolic plan, while the final computation is carried out by deterministic graph operations. This separation ensures that explanations correspond to the executed evidence rather than post-hoc rationales.

Framework overview

Given a query Q (optionally accompanied by multimodal context such as an image or structured table), the system performs (i) grounding to MMKG entities and relations, (ii) subgraph retrieval and path search guided by symbolic constraints and learned scoring, (iii) deterministic execution to produce an answer, and (iv) extraction and verbalization of the executed reasoning subgraph as an explanation. The framework is designed around three principles: (1) structural grounding-all answers must be derived from explicit graph evidence; (2) multimodal integration-entities are represented using aligned features across modalities; and (3) faithful explanation-the explanation is the subgraph traversed during execution, optionally pruned for local minimality.

Formally, the system maintains an MMKG as the shared substrate. For each query, the planner outputs a symbolic plan and the executor returns an answer A and an executed subgraph . Entity and relation extraction. Graph construction begins with modality-specific extraction pipelines. For text corpora, named entity recognition and relation extraction identify candidate triples. For images and videos, object detection and scene graph generation produce visual entities and relationships; for videos, we aggregate detections across frames and optionally create temporal edges. Each extracted entity mention is resolved to a canonical node through entity linking and clustering, which combines string similarity, contextual similarity, and embedding-based matching.

Cross-modal alignment. A major challenge is aligning entities across modalities. We align textual mentions and visual objects by mapping both modalities into a shared embedding space (e.g., CLIP) and maximizing cosine similarity, while enforcing type compatibility constraints from ontologies (e.g., “instrument” vs. “person”). For example, the textual entity “stethoscope” is linked to visual regions whose embeddings are closest in the shared space, then validated by type constraints. The output is a consistent entity inventory in which each node aggregates aligned features from multiple modalities.

Dynamic updates and indexing. Real-world knowledge evolves, so the MMKG supports incremental updates. For scalability, the MMKG is sharded and indexed (entity index, relation index, ANN embedding index) to enable efficient subgraph retrieval at inference time.

Knowledge substrate verification

Graph structure improves interpretability but does not guarantee correctness. Ethics, safety, and societal impact

The proposed framework improves transparency by exposing explicit evidence paths, but explainability does not eliminate ethical and societal risks. We therefore incorporate provenance, uncertainty, and verification signals, and we outline deployment safeguards.

Bias and fairness. MMKGs inherit biases from their sources (coverage imbalance in encyclopedic KBs, demographic skew in vision datasets). Explanations may inadvertently legitimize biased evidence by making it appear authoritative. We mitigate this by attaching provenance and confidence metadata to edges, encouraging source diversity during retrieval, and recommending stratified evaluation of both answers and explanations. Explanations should be interpreted as auditable evidence traces rather than guarantees of fairness.

Privacy and data protection. Multimodal inputs may contain sensitive personal information. Incorporating such content into an MMKG increases re-identification risk due to relational linking. Practical safeguards include data minimization (storing embeddings instead of raw media when feasible), access control for sensitive nodes/edges, automated anonymization and redaction, retention and deletion policies, and audit logs for compliance with applicable regulations.

Trust calibration and over-reliance. Faithful explanations may increase user trust beyond warranted levels. To calibrate trust, we expose edge confidence , verification score , and unverifiable flags when evidence cannot be validated. We recommend user interfaces that distinguish verified from speculative edges and require human review in high-stakes settings.

Robustness and misuse. Adversaries may attempt to manipulate the MMKG (poisoning relations or alignments) to induce plausible but misleading explanations. Mitigations include restricting high-impact relations to curated sources, versioned updates with anomaly detection, cross-checking critical claims against trusted references, and limiting the influence of low-provenance edges during scoring. Governance mechanisms should prevent fully autonomous decision-making in sensitive applications.

Environmental impact and limitations. Training multimodal encoders, GNNs, and using LLM planning increases compute and energy usage. We recommend mixed-precision training, subgraph-limited inference, and caching to reduce overhead. Finally, the framework does not guarantee correctness: its reliability is bounded by MMKG quality, alignment accuracy, and verification coverage. We therefore position it as an assistive, auditable reasoning tool rather than an autonomous decision-maker.

Ablation study

To further understand the contribution of each component in our proposed framework, we conduct an ablation study by systematically disabling individual modules. The tested variants include: (i) removing the Graph Grounding module, (ii) removing the Multimodal Alignment mechanism, (iii) removing the LLM-based Planning component, and (iv) removing the Explanation Module. From Fig. 6, several important insights emerge. First, the removal of Graph Grounding produces the sharpest performance degradation across all datasets. Accuracy on WebQSP falls from 79.8 to 72.3, and ScienceQA accuracy drops from 77.4 to 70.1. This decline demonstrates that explicit symbolic grounding is not simply a supporting feature but a structural necessity. Without graph grounding, the system is forced to rely on loosely coupled embeddings, which fail to capture the compositional semantics needed for robust multi-hop reasoning.

Second, eliminating the Multimodal Alignment module primarily affects multimodal benchmarks. On ScienceQA, accuracy decreases by more than 6 points, confirming that cross-modal consistency is vital for correctly linking textual entities with their corresponding visual evidence. Although performance on WebQSP and ComplexWebQuestions remains relatively stable, the degradation on ScienceQA shows that alignment plays a decisive role when reasoning depends on multimodal integration.

Third, removing the LLM-based Planning module significantly reduces compositional reasoning ability. The F1 score on ComplexWebQuestions falls from 69.1 to 64.2, a drop of nearly 5 points. This suggests that while symbolic graph traversal can capture basic relations, the planning ability of large language models is indispensable for handling complex queries that involve logical conjunctions, negations, or nested compositions.

Finally, the absence of the Explanation Module has minimal effect on predictive accuracy, with less than 1-point difference across datasets. However, this component is essential for generating faithful and human-readable explanations. Without it, the system cannot provide interpretable reasoning chains, thus undermining user trust and transparency even though factual correctness is preserved.

To assess robustness, we evaluate our framework on two challenging benchmarks: ImageNet-A with captions27, which contains adversarially selected images that frequently mislead standard vision models, and OK-VQA25, an open-ended multimodal benchmark requiring reasoning over noisy and incomplete evidence. Second, on OK-VQA, our framework reaches 49.3, again exceeding the cited BLIP-2 and retrieval-augmented baselines in the present comparison. This pattern is consistent with the view that multimodal alignment within the MMKG helps link visual evidence to textual entities under incomplete or ambiguous conditions. Unlike fusion-only models, our approach leverages symbolic constraints to resolve uncertainty and generate explanations grounded in explicit reasoning paths.

Taken together, these results suggest that robustness is not simply a byproduct of scaling language or vision models. Instead, it may emerge from the deliberate integration of symbolic structure, multimodal alignment, and faithful reasoning paths. Because these results are reported here as point estimates rather than fully documented seed-averaged statistics, we interpret the margins conservatively and view the robustness study as supportive rather than definitive.

Discussion

This work presents a unified framework for explainable language reasoning grounded in multi-modal knowledge graphs (MMKGs), with the goal of reconciling strong reasoning performance and faithful interpretability. The experimental results indicate that explicit graph grounding can improve both accuracy and robustness on knowledge-intensive and multimodal benchmarks, while simultaneously producing explanations that are auditable and verifiable.

Interpretability and performance. A key observation is that enforcing structural constraints through MMKGs does not necessarily degrade performance. On the contrary, constraining reasoning to semantically meaningful graph paths often improves multi-hop reasoning by reducing spurious correlations. However, this benefit comes with a trade-off: when the MMKG is incomplete or noisy, strict grounding may limit recall compared to fully neural models that can rely on latent associations. This highlights an inherent tension between faithfulness and coverage, suggesting that future systems should adaptively relax constraints when evidence is insufficient, while clearly signaling uncertainty to users.

Limitations. Despite its advantages, the framework has several limitations. First, it relies on accurate cross-modal entity alignment, which remains imperfect and can propagate errors through the reasoning process. Second, the quality of explanations is bounded by the completeness and correctness of the underlying MMKG; missing or biased knowledge cannot be recovered through reasoning alone. Third, the use of large language models as planners introduces additional computational cost and potential failure modes when generated plans are poorly grounded or ambiguous.

Future work. Several directions for future research emerge from this study. Extending MMKGs to incorporate richer modalities such as video, audio, and temporal information would enable more comprehensive reasoning. In addition, integrating continuous human feedback into the reasoning and explanation pipeline could further align explanations with human judgment over time. Finally, exploring more efficient planning and verification mechanisms will be essential for deploying MMKG-grounded reasoning systems at scale in real-world, high-stakes applications.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a unified framework for explainable language reasoning via Multi-Modal Knowledge Graphs (MMKGs). The proposed architecture integrates structured knowledge representation, multimodal alignment, and language-based reasoning into a coherent pipeline that produces not only accurate answers but also faithful and interpretable explanations. By grounding reasoning in explicit graph structures while leveraging the generative planning capabilities of large language models, our approach bridges the gap between symbolic reasoning and neural perception. Through extensive evaluation across diverse benchmarks-including WebQSP, MetaQA, ComplexWebQuestions, OK-VQA, ScienceQA, and ImageNet-A-we demonstrated that our framework consistently outperforms strong baselines in terms of accuracy, faithfulness, and robustness. The ablation study confirmed that each module of the framework contributes complementary strengths: graph grounding ensures structural fidelity, multimodal alignment provides cross-modal consistency, LLM-based planning enhances compositional reasoning, and the explanation module delivers human-understandable justifications. Moreover, robustness experiments revealed that our method is significantly more resilient to adversarial noise and incomplete evidence compared with fusion-only or retrieval-augmented models. The ability to deliver faithful reasoning chains grounded in multimodal evidence makes the proposed MMKG framework particularly suitable for real-world applications where trust, transparency, and accountability are essential, such as healthcare, scientific discovery, and decision support systems. While the current work primarily focuses on text and images, future research can extend MMKG construction to incorporate additional modalities such as audio, video, and sensor data. Another promising direction is the exploration of continual learning and temporal reasoning over evolving knowledge graphs, enabling the system to adapt dynamically to new information streams.
