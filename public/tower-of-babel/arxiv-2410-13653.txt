Abstract
. We lift the Lefschetz number from an algebraic invariant of maps between spaces to an invariant of morphisms of data over the spaces.



1.
Introduction

Both in Applied and Pure Mathematics, it is becoming more popular to study not only (algebraic) invariants of spaces, but also (algebraic) invariants of data over these spaces. A very insightful instance of this approach shows up in Euler Calculus (see, for example,
[
3
,
2
]
and the references therein) where the Euler integral may be seen as a lift of the classical Euler characteristic
\chi
from an invariant of spaces to an invariant of data over spaces.

The Lefschetz number of a continuous map between topological spaces
f\colon X\to X
is a fundamental invariant in Mathematics. It may be seen as a generalization of the Euler-Poincaré characteristic since the Lefschetz number of the identity map coincides with the Euler-Poincaré characteristic. The importance of the Lefschetz number led to the development of Lefschetz calculus (see
[
7
]
), that is, integration with respect to the Lefschetz number.

In this work we aim to lift the Lefschetz number from an invariant of maps between spaces to an invariant of morphisms of data structures over the spaces. In doing so, we answer positively a question raised in
[
3
]
.

In order to accomplish our goal, the first step is to formalize the idea of data structures over spaces. We do so in two novel different complementary approaches and we show the strong relation between them (Theorems
4.3
,
5.2
). Let
X
be a definable or tame cell complex (for example a finite simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and let
f\colon X\to X
be a homeomorphism. In the first approach, which is a continuation of our previous work (
[
7
]
), we define data over a space as a counting function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{Z}
and the aggregation of information corresponds to integration with respect to the combinatorial Lefschetz number. The second approach encodes data over
X
as a special kind of (constructible) sheaf
\mathcal{F}
over
X
and the algebraic-topological invariant obtained corresponds to computing a sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number of
f
.

The main accomplishments of this work are the following.

First, we define the novel notions of combinatorial Lefschetz number (see Subsection
2.2
) and sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number (see Subsection
2.5
) of a homeomorphism between definable cell complexes and we establish a relation between them (Theorem
3.2
).

Second, we introduce the two novel approaches to lifting the Lefschetz number from maps between spaces to morphisms of data over spaces: the combinatorial one in Subsection
4.1
and the sheaf theoretic one in Subsection
2.5
. The former one has the advantage of computability and simplicity while the latter not only provides an enlightenment of Lefschetz calculus (which we began in
[
7
]
) but also proves invaluable in guaranteeing well-definedness of the combinatorial approach (Corollary
3.3
) and its topological invariance (Corollary
3.4
).

Third, we prove a representation theorem (Theorem
4.3
) which establishes a strong bond between aggregation of data information (integration with respect to the combinatorial Lefschetz number) and the sheaf-theoretic number. Theorem
4.3
may be restated in very simplified terms as follows:

Theorem
4.3
(Representation theorem).

Let
X
be a nice cell complex and let
f\colon X\to X
be a cellular homeomorphism. If
\mathcal{F}
is a nice (constructible) sheaf on
X
compatible with
f\colon X\to X
, then the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number can be computed by aggregating data in a combinatorial way:

(1)

L_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\int_{X}h\mathrm{d}\varLambda f,

where
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is the nice function associated to
\mathcal{F}
.
Conversely, if
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is a nice function, its associated nice sheaf
\mathcal{F}
satisfies Equation (
1
).

Moreover, we obtain Barrow’s rule for integration with respect to Lefschetz number (Theorem
4.5
):

\int_{X}h\mathrm{d}\varLambda f=L_{c}(X,f,\oplus_{j>0}\tilde{\mathbb{R}}^{j}_{h^{-1}(j)})-L_{c}(X,f,\oplus_{j<0}\tilde{\mathbb{R}}^{-j}_{h^{-1}(j)}).

Fourth, we study to what extent the Representation theorem (Theorem
4.3
) characterizes the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number. Let be
\mathcal{C}
the family of triples
(X,f,\mathcal{F})
where
X
is a “nice” cell complex,
\mathcal{F}
is a “nice” (constructible) sheaf on
X
and
f
a cellular homeomorphism compatible with
\mathcal{F}
. We prove Theorem
5.2
, which may be stated in a very simplified way as follows:

Theorem
5.2
.

The sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number is the only map between
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbb{Z}
that satisfies:

(1)

A cofibration axiom to compute it inductively on the skeleta.

(2)

The Representation theorem (Theorem
4.3
) on wedge of spheres and graphs. That is, if
X
is a finite wedge of
n
-spheres or a graph,
\mathcal{F}
is a nice sheaf and
f
is a cellular homeomorphism compatible with
\mathcal{F}
, then

L_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\int_{X}hd\varLambda f,

where
h
is the constructible function associated to the sheaf.

Compare Theorem
5.2
with
[
1
]
, where Lefschetz number between cell complexes is characterized.

2.
Definitions of the Lefschetz number

In this section, we introduce the combinatorial and sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz numbers. In order to do so, we first introduce the notions of definable cell complexes and

-

2.1.
Definable cellular structures

We introduce the definable structures we will work with. For a more detailed account on
o
-minimal topology we refer the reader to
[
9
]
. Recall that an
o
-minimal
structure over
\mathbb{R}
is a collection
\mathscr{A}=\{\mathscr{A}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
so that the following properties hold:

(1)

\mathscr{A}_{n}
is an algebra of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

The family
\mathscr{A}
is closed with respect to cartesian products and canonical projections.

(3)

The subset
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},x<y\}
is in
\mathscr{A}_{2}
.

(4)

The family
\mathscr{A}_{1}
consists of all finite unions of points and open intervals of
\mathbb{R}
.

(5)

Every
\mathscr{A}_{n}
contains all the algebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Given an o-minimal structure, we will say that a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
definable
if
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
.

A
generalized simplicial complex
is a finite collection
K
of open simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfying that given two open simplices in the complex, the intersection of their closures is the empty set or the closure of an open simplex in the complex. By
incomplete subcomplex
of a simplicial complex we mean a union of open simplices (which it is not necessarily closed). We illustrate a generalized simplicial complex in Figure
1
, where, as we will do along the paper, we will abuse of notation and denote by
X
both the geometric realization and the underlying simplicial complex.

.

We will use o-minimal structures that contain the semi-linear sets so that, in this way, the generalised simplicial complexes are definable. Moreover, it holds the following triangulation theorem:

Theorem 2.1

(Definable triangulation theorem
[
9
]
)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a definable set and let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a finite family of definable subsets of
X
. Then there exists a definable triangulation of
X
compatible with the collection of subsets.

Let be
X
a finite (therefore compact) CW complex. It can be embedded in some
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, it makes sense to define a
definable cellular complex

X
as a finite CW complex such that all its open cells are definable. As a consequence, definable cellular complexes have definable skeletons in all dimensions. We define an
incomplete subcomplex
of
X
as a union of open cells of
X
. An incomplete subcomplex of a compact generalized simplicial complex
X
is an example of a
incomplete subcomplex
of
X
. The reader may think in terms of generalized simplicial complexes when reading Section
3
.

2.2.
The combinatorial Lefschetz number

We briefly recall the definition of the combinatorial Lefschetz number for homeomorphisms between simplicial complexes. We refer the reader to
[
7
]
for a detailed treatment. Let
f:X\rightarrow X
be a homeomorphism of a simplicial complex to itself. Let
U\subset X
be a definable
f
-invariant set (
f(U)=U
). Let
\overline{U}
be its closure in
X
. We define the combinatorial Lefschetz number of
f:U\subset X\rightarrow X
(relative to
U
) in several steps:

(1)

We consider a triangulation
(L,\overline{K},K)
of
X
compatible with
(X,\overline{U},U)
(the existence of this triangulation guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
) and a map
\tilde{f}\colon L\to L
induced by
f
.

(2)

We construct a simplicial approximation
\tilde{f}^{\text{\rm simp}}\colon L\to L
of
\tilde{f}
in
L
(with respect to a possibly finer simplicial structure in
L
).

(3)

Let
C_{*}(\tilde{f}^{\text{\rm simp}}):C_{*}(L)\rightarrow C_{*}(L)
be the induced simplicial chain map. In each dimension
p
, we will say that an
incomplete basis of
C_{*}(U)
consists of the
p
-simplices
\sigma^{p}
such that the open simplex corresponding to
\sigma^{p}
is an open simplex of
U
. Thus, we can restrict the matrix of the map
C(\tilde{f}^{\text{\rm simp}})
to the square submatrix of coefficients
(i,j)
such that
\sigma_{i}^{p}
and
\sigma_{j}^{p}
belong to the incomplete basis of
C_{*}(U)
. Then,
\varLambda^{c}(C(\tilde{f}^{\text{\rm simp}}),C_{*}(U))
is defined as the alternating sum of the traces of these submatrices in the different dimensions of the complex
X
.

(4)

Finally, we set
\varLambda_{c}(U,f)_{X}=\varLambda^{c}(C(\tilde{f}^{\text{\rm simp}}),C_{*}(U)).

We extend the definition to cellular homeomorphisms. Let
X
be a definable finite cellular complex. Let
f:X\rightarrow X
be a cellular homeomorphism and consider an incomplete subcomplex
U\subset X
that is
f
-invariant and locally compact. Then, by Theorem
2.1
there exists a definable triangulation of
X
compatible with
U
, call it
(Y,K,g)
(it does exist even if
U
is not locally compact). We define the Lefschetz combinatorial number of
f
in
U
as

\varLambda_{c}(U,f)_{X}=\varLambda_{c}(K,g)_{Y}.

For simplicity of the notation, we will denote
\varLambda_{c}(U,f)
instead of
\varLambda_{c}(U,f)_{X}
.
We will prove later that the combinatorial Lefschetz number of
f
in
U
is well-defined (Corollary
3.3
).

The following lemma follows from the additivity of the combinatorial Lefschetz number for generalized simplicial complexes (see
[
7
]
) and the definable triangulation theorem (Theorem
2.1
):

Lemma 2.2

(Additivity of the combinatorial Lefschetz number)
.

Let
X
be a definable cellular complex and let
U
and
V
be two disjoint definable and
f
-invariant subsets. Then,
\varLambda_{c}(U\cup V,f)_{X}=\varLambda_{c}(U,f)_{X}+\varLambda_{c}(V,f)_{X}
.

2.3.
c
-constructible sheafs

Let be
X
a definable cellular complex. We say that a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
of finite dimensional real vector spaces on

is a

-constructible sheaf
if:

•

there exists a partition
\{X_{i}\}
of definable locally compact incomplete subcomplexes of

such that, for all

,
\mathcal{F}_{X_{i}}
is constant on each connected component (this is the case for example of a constructible sheaf where the connected components of each

where the sheaf is locally constant are simply-connected).

If

is a homeomorphism we say that the
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}
is
f

if the

are
f

Lemma 2.3
.

(A)

If
\mathcal{F}
is a constant sheaf on
X
and
f:X\rightarrow X
is a map, then
f^{\ast}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}
.

(B)

If
X
is a simply-connected space and
\mathcal{F}
is a locally constant sheaf, then
\mathcal{F}
is the constant sheaf.

(C)

Let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a proper map and let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf on

f^{\ast}:H_{c}^{\ast}(Y,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow H_{c}^{\ast}(X,f^{\ast}\mathcal{F})
induced by

in cohomology with compact supports.

(D)

Let be
A
a definable subset of
X
and
\tilde{\mathbb{{R}}}_{A}
the constant sheaf on
A
. Then
H_{c}^{\ast}(A,\tilde{\mathbb{{R}}}_{A})\cong H_{c}^{\ast}(A,\mathbb{R})
.

We are interested in applying Lemma
(C)
(C) to the case of a homeomorphism
f\colon X\to X
and a
f
-

-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}

. We circumvent this problem by defining an

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(i_{1})_{!}i_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(i_{2})_{!}i_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus\ldots\oplus(i_{k})_{!}i_{k}^{\ast}\mathcal{F},

where

2.4.
The associated
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}

The goal of this subsection is to prove that under certain conditions, there is a natural isomorphism between
f^{\ast}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A
be a topological space which equals the disjoint union of two open subsets
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf on
A
. Then there exists a canonical isomorphism between
\mathcal{F}
and
(i_{1})_{!}i_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(i_{2})_{!}i_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}
, where
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
are the inclusions.

Proof.

As
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are also closed, we have
(i_{1})_{!}=(i_{1})_{\ast}
and
(i_{2})_{!}=(i_{2})_{\ast}
. On the other hand, as they are open, we have
i_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{|W_{1}}
and
i_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{|W_{2}}
. In consequence, if we take
V
an open set of
A
we have

\bigl((i_{1})_{!}i_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\bigr)(V)=\bigl((i_{1})_{\ast}\mathcal{F}_{|W_{1}}\bigr)(V)=\mathcal{F}_{|W_{1}}(V\cap W_{1})=\mathcal{F}(V\cap W_{1}).

The same result is obtained for
i_{2}
.
Finally,

\bigl((i_{1})_{!}i_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(i_{2})_{!}i_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\bigr)(V)=\mathcal{F}(V\cap W_{1})\oplus\mathcal{F}(V\cap W_{2})\cong\mathcal{F}(V).\qed

As a consequence of this result we obtain the following corollary.

.

Let
X

and
W_{2}

the inclusions. Then there is a natural isomorphism

j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\cong(j_{1})_{!}j_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(j_{2})_{!}j_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}

Proof.

By Lemma

, we have

j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\cong(i_{1})_{!}i_{1}^{\ast}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(i_{2})_{!}i_{2}^{\ast}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}=(i_{1})_{!}j_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(i_{2})_{!}j_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}

j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\cong j_{!}(i_{1})_{!}j_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus j_{!}(i_{2})_{!}j_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\cong(j_{1})_{!}j_{1}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\oplus(j_{2})_{!}j_{2}^{\ast}\mathcal{F},

Theorem 2.7
.

Let

2.5.
The sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number

Let be
X
a definable cellular complex,
\mathcal{F}
a
c
-constructible sheaf on
X
and
f
a cellular homeomorphism that respects the constant components of
\mathcal{F}
and the partition that we consider to define the associated sheaf. We define the
sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number of

f

asociated to

\mathcal{F}
as

L_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\mathrm{tr}(f^{\ast},H^{n}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})).

We will set the notation
L_{c}(X,f)=L_{c}(X,f,\tilde{\mathbb{R}})
.

Note that the way of assigning
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{F}
could be different if we considered a different partition. Theorem
4.3
will show that the Lefschetz number is independent of this choice and therefore is well-defined. As a consequence of this, in the rest of the paper we will denote by
\mathcal{F}
both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.8
.

We will see later that the sum
\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\mathrm{tr}(f^{\ast},H^{n}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}))
converges since
H^{n}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
vanishes for a sufficiently large
n
.

3.
Well-definedness and topological invariance of the combinatorial Lefschetz number

The purpose of this section is to prove that the combinatorial Lefschetz number in the setting of cellular complexes and incomplete subcomplexes is well-defined. Moreover, we also obtain a topological invariance result as well as other results that will be of interest in the future.

We begin by checking that the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number satisfies a certain additivity rule.

Proposition 3.1

(Dimensional additivity of sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number)
.

Let
X
be a cellular complex and
D
a locally compact subcomplex. Consider a cellular homeomorphism
f:X\rightarrow X
such that
D
is
f
-invariant (this is
f(D)=D
). Then, the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number equals the sum of the Lefschetz numbers on each dimension:

\mathrm{L}_{c}(D,f)=\sum_{i}\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)

Proof.

Suppose
D
has dimension
n
. Then,
D^{(n)}-D^{(n-1)}
, that is, the union of all the open cell of maximal dimension of
D
, is open in
D
. Then, we have the following long exact sequence

\ldots\rightarrow H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n)}-D^{(n-1)})\rightarrow H_{c}^{m}(D)\rightarrow H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n-1)})\rightarrow\ldots.

The naturality of the long exact sequence guarantees that the following diagram is commutative:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n)}-D^{(n-1)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n-1)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n)}-D^{(n-1)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{c}^{m}(D^{(n-1)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}

we obtain

\mathrm{L}_{c}(D,f)=\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(n)}-D^{(n-1)},f)+\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(n-1)},f).

But
D^{(n-1)}
is a locally compact space (it is a closed subspace of a locally compact space) so we can repeat the argument and obtain

\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(n-1)},f)=\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(n-1)}-D^{(n-2)},f)+\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(n-2)},f)

Finally, as
D^{(0)}=D^{(0)}-D^{(-1)}
, we arrive to the desired result.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Equivalence of Lefschetz numbers)
.

Let
X
be a definable cellular complex and let
D
be a definable locally compact incomplete subcomplex. If
f\colon X\to X
is a cellular homeomorphism such that
D
is
f
-invariant, then
\varLambda_{c}(D,f)=\mathrm{L}_{c}(D,f)
.

Proof.

For each dimension
i
, as
f
is a cellular homeomorphism and
D
is
f
-invariant, we have that
X^{(i)}
,
D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)}
and
X^{(i)}-(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)})
are
f
-invariant. As each
X^{(i)}
is a definable cellular complex, by additivity of the combinatorial Lefschetz number (Lemma

), we have

(2)

\varLambda_{c}(X^{(i)},f)=\varLambda_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)+\varLambda_{c}(X^{(i)}-(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)}),f).

Furthermore, by an argument similar to the one of Theorem

, we have:

(3)

\mathrm{L}_{c}(X^{(i)},f)=\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)+\mathrm{L}_{c}(X^{(i)}-(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)}),f).

Now, note that the first and the last term in Equation
2
equal the Lefschetz homological (or cohomological as we work on a field) number since the spaces are triangulated by complete (compact) simplicial complexes. The first and the last terms in Equation
3
are also the Lefschetz cohomological number because of Lemma
2.3
(D) and because the spaces are compact. As a consequence, we obtain that
\varLambda_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)=\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)
.
Finally, from Theorem
3.1
:

\mathrm{L}_{c}(D,f)=\sum_{i}\mathrm{L}_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)

and

\varLambda_{c}(D,f)=\sum_{i}\varLambda_{c}(D^{(i)}-D^{(i-1)},f)

we obtain the result.
∎

4.
The sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number as an integral

In this section we obtain the Representation Theorem for

-
c
-constructible sheaves (Theorem

) which evidences the strong relation between the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number ad integration with respect to the (combinatorial) Lefschetz number.

4.1.
\mathbb{N}
-constructible functions and constructible sheaves

Let
f:X\rightarrow X
be a cellular homeomorphism of a definable cellular complex to itself. A function
h:X\to\mathbb{N}
(
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
) is
f
-
integrable
or
f
-

if there are a finite number of level sets
\{X_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}=\{h^{-1}(j)\}_{j=0}^{n}
and each level set is a locally compact, definable,
f
-invariant incomplete subcomplex. Note that

(where

stands for the characteristic function on

of the form
h=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\mathds{1}_{U_{j}}
, where the
c_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and the sets
U_{j}
are definable and
f
-invariant. We define

\int_{X}(c_{1}\mathds{1}_{U_{1}}+\ldots+c_{n}\mathds{1}_{U_{n}})\,d\varLambda_{c}f=c_{1}\varLambda_{c}(f,U_{1})+\ldots+c_{n}\varLambda_{c}(f,U_{n}).

This is well defined (
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
). For an example of a constructible function and an integral, see
[
7
, Example 7.3]
.

A
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}
in
X
defines the
f
-constructible function

that sends each point

to the dimension of the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{x}
and where the level sets consist of locally compact incomplete subcomplexes. Conversely, given a
f
-constructible function

in the cellular complex
X
, we can define a
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf that encodes
h
. To do so, in each
X_{j}
we define the constant sheaf
\tilde{\mathbb{R}}_{X_{j}}^{j}
as the extension by zero to
X
of the constant sheaf on
X_{j}
(such extension exists because
X_{j}
is locally closed). We define the
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}

associated to h
in
X
given by
\mathcal{F}=\oplus_{j}\tilde{\mathbb{R}}^{j}_{X_{j}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a cellular complex and
U\subset X
a definable locally compact incomplete subcomplex. Let
f:X\rightarrow X
be a cellular homeomorphism such that
U
is
f
-invariant and let
\mathcal{F}
be the constant sheaf of dimension
n
. Then the sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number of
f
associated to
\mathcal{F}
on
U
is equal to
n
times the combinatorial Lefschetz number of
f
on
U
, that is

L_{c}(U,f,\mathcal{F})=n\varLambda_{c}(U,f).

Proof.

First of all, as we deal with finite dimensional vector spaces, we must recall that the sheaf
\mathcal{F}
is nothing more the
n
-sum of the constant sheaf with value
\mathbb{R}
. So, if

0\rightarrow\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{J}^{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{J}^{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{J}^{2}\rightarrow\ldots

is an inyective resolution of the constant sheaf with value
\mathbb{R}
, then

0\rightarrow\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\mathcal{J}^{0}\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\mathcal{J}^{1}\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\mathcal{J}^{2}\rightarrow\ldots

is an inyective resolution of
\mathcal{F}
.

By taking sections with compact supports and removing the first term, we have

0\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\Gamma_{c}(U,\mathcal{J}^{0})\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\Gamma_{c}(U,\mathcal{J}^{1})\rightarrow\oplus_{n}\Gamma_{c}(U,\mathcal{J}^{2})\rightarrow\ldots

and so
H_{c}^{\ast}(U,\mathcal{F})\cong\oplus_{n}H_{c}^{\ast}(U,\mathbb{R})
. Then, by Lemma
(D)
, we conclude that
H_{c}^{\ast}(U,\mathcal{F})\cong\oplus_{n}H_{c}^{\ast}(U;\mathbb{R})
. As a consequence, we have that
\varLambda_{c}(U,f,\mathcal{F})=n\mathrm{L}_{c}(U,f)
, and Theorem
3.2
guarantees that the last term is
n\varLambda_{c}(U,f)
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

We have

for a sufficiently large

.

Proof.

Let

be the open set consisting of the top dimensional cells that are in a constant component

of the sheaf. As it is open and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{m}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F})}}

4.2.
Sheaf-theoretic Lefschetz number and Lefschetz calculus

We establish a relation between the Lefschetz number on sheaves and the integral with respect to the Lefschetz number.

Theorem 4.3

(Representation theorem)
.

Let
X
be a definable cellular complex and let
f\colon X\to X
be a cellular homeomorphism. If
\mathcal{F}
is a
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf on
X
such that
f\colon X\to X
respects the constant components of
\mathcal{F}
, then

(4)

L_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\int_{X}h\mathrm{d}\varLambda f,

where
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is the
f
-constructible function associated to
\mathcal{F}
.
Conversely, if
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is a
f
-constructible function
h=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\mathds{1}_{U_{j}}
, its associated
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}
satisfies Equation (
4
).

Proof.

Let us begin by considering
U_{1,n}
as the open set consisting of the top dimensional cells that are in a constant component
X_{1}
of the sheaf. As it is open and
f
-invariant, we can consider the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(U_{1,n},\mathcal{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(X-U_{1,n},\mathcal{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(U_{1,n},\mathcal{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(X,\mathcal{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{n}_{c}(X-U_{1,n},\mathcal{F})}}
.
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\ast}}

As a consequence, since the three cohomology groups vanish on a sufficiently large dimension
n
, we have

\varLambda_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\varLambda_{c}(U_{1,n},f,\mathcal{F})+\varLambda_{c}(X-U_{1,n},\mathcal{F}).

Now, if we repeat the argument taking
U_{2,n}
instead of
U_{1,n}
and
X-U_{1,n}
instead of
X
we arrive to

\varLambda_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\varLambda_{c}(U_{1,n},f,\mathcal{F})+\varLambda_{c}(U_{2,n},\mathcal{F})+\varLambda_{c}(X-U_{1,n}-U_{2,n},\mathcal{F}).

Then we can repeat this argument until arrive to

\varLambda_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varLambda_{c}(U_{j,n},f,\mathcal{F})+\varLambda_{c}(X^{(n-1)},f,\mathcal{F}),

and then apply the same for the cells in progresive lower dimension.

Finally we obtain

(5)

\varLambda_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varLambda_{c}(U_{j,i},f,\mathcal{F})\right)

We apply Theorem
4.1
to each summand of the right-hand side of the equation. Then,

\varLambda_{c}(X,f,\mathcal{F})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{m}c_{j}\varLambda_{c}(U_{j,i},f)\right),

where
c_{j}
denotes the dimension of the sheaf on
X_{j}
. Regrouping the sum and using the the additivity of the combinatorial Lefschetz number we obtain:

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{m}c_{j}\varLambda_{c}(U_{j,i},f)\right)=\sum_{j=i}^{m}c_{j}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\varLambda_{c}(U_{j,i},f)\right)=\sum_{j=i}^{m}c_{j}\varLambda_{c}(X_{j},f)

where
X_{j}=\sqcup_{i}U_{j,i}
. Observe that
\int_{x}h\mathrm{d}\varLambda f=\sum_{j=i}^{m}c_{j}\varLambda_{c}(X_{j},f)
where
h=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\mathds{1}_{X_{j}}
is the associated
f
-constructible function to the
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}
. For the converse of the theorem, note that the associated associated
f
-constructible function to the associated
f
-
c
-constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}
to
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is
h\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
.
∎
