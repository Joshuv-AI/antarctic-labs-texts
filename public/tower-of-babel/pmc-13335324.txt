Uncovering the metabolomic signatures in adolescent depression using a targeted metabolomics approach
Authors: Kaustubh Kishor Jadhav, Simone Avesani, Aino-Kaisa Piironen, Rosalba Giugno, Timo Lakka, Tommi Tolmunen, Katja M Kanninen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a leading cause of mortality worldwide and remains a growing public health concern. Most research has focused on adults, despite evidence that adolescence is a common period of onset of mental health problems. Metabolomics, which reflects the ongoing bodily biochemical processes through metabolite profiling, has emerged as a promising avenue to identify possible indicators of disease state. To contribute to this growing field, this study aims to identify early occurring metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression using a targeted metabolomics approach.MethodsA total of 204 adolescents diagnosed with clinical depression were compared with 78 healthy peers using targeted nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy using serum samples (Nightingale Health Ltd.). In total, 250 metabolite measures were quantified, including both absolute concentrations and percentage measures. A combination of univariate and multivariate analyses was conducted to identify metabolomic signatures in adolescent depression. Additionally, subgroup analyses for drug-naïve and medicated adolescents with depression were performed using the same analytical approach.ResultsOf the 250 metabolite measures analyzed, 61 were identified as metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression based on statistical significance (adj.p-value < 0.05) and multivariate contribution (VIP > 1.0). These belong to ketone bodies, glycolysis-related metabolites, amino acids, inflammatory markers, fatty acids, lipids, lipoprotein subclasses, and particle sizes. In subgroup analyses, 24 and 74 metabolomic signatures were identified among drug-naïve adolescents with depression and those consuming medications.ConclusionBy applying a targeted metabolomics approach, this study identifies metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression. These early biological signatures offer valuable insight into the metabolic alterations associated with adolescent depression, particularly within the drug-naïve group, where confounding effects of medication were minimized. Continued research on peripheral metabolic markers may help advance early detection strategies and contribute to the development of more individualized and precision-based approaches.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08207-6.Keywords: Metabolomic, Adolescent, Depression, NMR, Targeted, Metabolite

Introduction

Depression is the leading cause of disability and impaired well-being worldwide, posing a major public health concern. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2024 Atlas, more than one billion people live with mental health problems, with anxiety followed by depression being the most prevalent. Most mental disorders often have their first incidence in adolescence, making this a vulnerable phase. It is estimated that approximately one in five adolescents worldwide experiences depression, and this number is expected to continue rising [1]. Diagnosing depression in adolescents is challenging due to the heterogeneity of the disorder and the differences in its clinical presentation compared with adults [2]. These challenges underscore the importance of early identification and intervention, with adolescence representing a critical window of opportunity. Although several biomarkers have shown promise for aiding early diagnosis and providing insights into understanding the biological underpinnings of depression, most existing research has focused primarily on adults.

Over the past decade, considerable progress has been made in the discovery of biomarkers for mental health through high-throughput omics approaches such as transcriptomics [3] and proteomics [4]. Although several candidate biomarkers have been identified, none have yet achieved clinical applicability. This has shifted attention towards the emerging field of metabolomics, which focuses on the identification and quantification of small molecules (<1.5 kDa) in biological samples. Unlike other omics techniques, metabolomics can provide a direct reflection of ongoing biochemical changes in the body [5]. Owing to its simplicity, reproducibility, and quantitative precision, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) based metabolomics has been adopted in targeted metabolomic studies [6, 7]. Metabolomics has been successfully applied to investigate the biological changes occurring in various neurodegenerative disorders [8], and its potential relevance in psychiatric conditions has become a focus of interest for many researchers [9].

Depression is increasingly recognized as a disorder with systemic metabolic alterations, as supported by several meta-analyses [10, 11]. Among its most consistent biological features is inflammation, although not all individuals with depression exhibit an altered inflammatory profile. Numerous studies have reported elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines [12], C-reactive protein [13], and glycoprotein acetyls [14], supporting the role of immuno-metabolic dysregulation in depressive pathology. Core symptoms such as fatigue and anhedonia have been mechanistically linked to disturbances in energy metabolism, both in the central nervous system and at the level of mitochondrial function [15–17]. Alterations in circulating triglycerides, diglycerides, cholesterol, and lipid components of lipoprotein particles, including high-density lipoproteins (HDL) and low-density lipoproteins (LDL), have also been observed [18, 19]. Such lipid disturbances may contribute not only to increased cardiovascular risk but also to cognitive decline, a frequent comorbid feature observed in individuals with depression [20]. Furthermore, growing evidence links depression to gut microbiota alterations, suggesting that gut-brain axis signaling may influence systemic metabolite profiles [21].

Together, previous findings underscore the metabolic underpinnings of depression and highlight the potential of metabolomics to identify early diagnostic indicators and novel therapeutic targets. To advance research in the field of metabolomics in adolescent depression, the present study aims to uncover the altered serum metabolomic profile associated with adolescents with clinical depression in comparison to individuals devoid of mental health problems using a targeted NMR approach.

Materials and methods

Study sample

Depression Cohort (DEP): The samples in this study were drawn from the ongoing SMART (Systemic Metabolomic Alterations Related To different psychiatric disease categories in adolescent outpatients) project, which comprised participants (14–19 yrs) admitted to the adolescent psychiatric outpatient clinic of the Kuopio University Hospital (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06262958). Depression was assessed following the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-IV codes, using a Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders (SCID) [22]. Depression severity was evaluated with the Beck's Depression Inventory (BDI), which assesses cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and physical symptoms [23]. Exclusion criteria involved: inability to provide informed consent, difficulties in understanding the study procedures or questionnaires, and presence of severe psychotic symptoms or intellectual disability. For the metabolomic analyses, 204 adolescents (at baseline) with clinical depression (Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)/dysthymia or both) were recruited from the enrolled participants.

Healthy Controls (HC): Healthy controls were derived from the 8-year follow-up of the ongoing Physical Activity and Nutrition in Children (PANIC) study (Kuopio, Finland) (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01803776). From this cohort, healthy adolescents were selected among 200 individuals who had not received any interventions (physical activity or nutrition) at baseline and during the 8-year follow-up, and who self-reported to be physically healthy and free of mental health problems. HCs were included if they reported no daily pain, had a BDI score ≤12.0, and a Cohen’s stress score ≤ 26.0. For the metabolomics, 78 healthy adolescents were selected from the participants at the 8-year follow-up.

Blood sampling

For the DEP adolescents, venous blood samples were collected in the morning after overnight fasting. Samples were kept at 4°C for 30 min, centrifuged at 2500 g at 4°C, and serum was collected, aliquoted, and stored at -70°C. For the HCs, venous blood samples were collected following a 12-hour fast, kept for 30 min, centrifuged at 2663 g at room temperature, serum was collected, aliquoted, and stored at -80°C until metabolomics analysis. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Targeted metabolomic analyses

Serum metabolites were quantified using a targeted high-throughput proton NMR Core Metabolomics platform (Nightingale Health Ltd., Helsinki, Finland). Detailed descriptions have been published previously [24, 25] and are also available in Supplementary File #10.

Data analysis

All data analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.1). Preprocessing involved imputation of missing values and TAG labels across the dataset to zero. According to the Nightingale Health analysis guide, empty cells indicate that a biomarker was not applicable for the sample type or was not measured. Therefore, these values were imputed as zero during data preprocessing (see Supplementary File #2). DEP samples were included if participants had a diagnosis of MDD (DSM-IV, 296.20-296.34), dysthymia (300.4), or both, while those with other mood disorders were excluded. Of the 250 metabolite measures quantified by Nightingale Health Ltd. metabolomics, 77 were reported as percentages, and most of the remaining 173 were reported primarily as absolute concentrations. Only metabolite measures with at least 80% of all existing sample measurements were retained for analysis. The 77 percentage measures (PERC) were used after dividing the values by 100. The 173 metabolites expressed in absolute concentrations (ABS) were log-transformed before analysis. The ABS and PERC metabolite measures were analyzed separately to account for differences in data scaling, normalization, and avoiding bias due to the redundant information.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize data in a low-dimensional space. Outliers were identified using the Mahalanobis distance [26]. To ensure robust selection of metabolites altered in depression, both univariate and multivariate approaches were applied. Linear regression (ggforestplot package [27]) was used for univariate analysis, while Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) served as the multivariate method (ropls package [28]). Metabolomic signatures were selected based on significant metabolite measures (adj.p-value < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg method) identified in linear regression and their Variable Importance in Projection (VIP > 1.0) from OPLS-DA. The OPLS-DA model used 7-fold cross-validation and was run 500 times to reduce overfitting, and its performance was further evaluated using permuted R² and Q² values (see Supplementary File #13). The metabolite measures with VIP > 1.0 were considered most influential. Log₂ fold changes (log₂_FC), calculated as ratios of means, were estimated using the same linear model implemented in the ggforestplot package, with HCs as the reference group. Within the DEP dataset, additional subgroup analyses were conducted for drug-naïve (DN) and medicated (MED) participants using the same procedures. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were conducted, including sex-stratified analyses along with BMI included as a confounding variable in a subset of samples.

Principal component analysis

In PERC, five metabolite measures with more than 20% missing values were excluded, resulting in 72 metabolite measures. PCA was performed separately for ABS (173 metabolites) and PERC (72 metabolite measures). 10 outliers were removed from ABS and 7 from PERC. The outlier samples identified in all our analyses were primarily from the DEP group. For subsequent analyses, the ABS dataset included 272 samples (HC = 78, DEP = 194), while the PERC dataset included 275 samples (HC = 77, DEP = 198). Age significantly influenced the first principal component in both ABS and PERC, although the effect was limited to 1.5% and 3%, respectively. A visible separation between HCs and DEP was observed when sex was considered as the grouping variable.

Linear regression (Univariate)

Linear regression analyses were performed separately for ABS and PERC, with age and sex included as covariates. Of the 250 metabolite measures, 168 (ABS = 107, PERC = 61) were statistically significant (adj.p-value < 0.05). The outcomes are presented as log2_FC in Fig. 1.

Metabolomic signatures in adolescent depression

By selecting metabolite measures common to both analyses, 61 ABS were identified as the metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression. Since the Q² values for 29 PERC were low, these metabolites were considered less reliable as metabolomic signatures (see Supplementary Table 2). Thus, 61 metabolomic signatures were both statistically significant and contributed to multivariate importance. These belong to ketone bodies, glycolysis-related metabolites, amino acids, inflammatory markers, fatty acids, lipids, lipoprotein subclasses and particle sizes. Detailed results are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 8.

Sensitivity analysis

Due to lower confidence in the PERC, only ABS were used in the sensitivity analyses. Sex-stratified analyses were conducted using linear regression models that included group*sex interaction and adjustment for age. Among females, 159 participants were included in the DEP group and 37 in the HCs group, with mean ages of 16.4 ± 1.54 and 15.6 ± 0.50 years, respectively. In females, 43 metabolomic signatures (see Supplementary Table 3) were identified that were statistically significant (adjp-val < 0.05). Among males, 35 participants were included in the DEP group and 41 in the HCs group, with mean ages of 17.2 ± 1.69 and 15.7 ± 0.57 years, respectively. Linear regression analysis identified 41 metabolomic signatures (see Supplementary Table 4) that were significant (adjp-val < 0.05) in males. Multivariate analyses were not performed using OPLS-DA, as this method does not allow for testing interaction terms or adjusting for covariates in the same way as linear regression.

In addition to age and sex, BMI was considered a confounder for a subset of participants who reported BMI measures. Primary analyses included age and sex as covariates, while subsequent analyses also included BMI to identify metabolomic signatures independent of BMI. After outlier removal, 56 DEP (BMI = 25.0 ± 5.57) samples and 78 HCs (BMI = 20.9 ± 3.63), with mean ages of 16.8 ± 1.55 and 15.6 ± 0.53 years, were included in the analysis. Using age and sex as covariates, 65 metabolite measures were identified from ABS via linear regression (adjusted p < 0.05) and OPLS-DA (Q² = 0.2, R²Y = 0.4, R²X = 0.7, RMSEE = 0.4, pR2Y = 0.002, pQ2 = 0.002). After controlling for age, sex, and BMI, 28 metabolite measures were identified (adjusted p < 0.05; Q² = 0.1, R²Y = 0.3, R²X = 0.6, RMSEE = 0.4, pR2Y = 0.002, pQ2 = 0.002), of which 25 were common to both analyses (see Supplementary Table 5).

Subgroup analysis

Drug-naïve depressed adolescents

This subgroup of the DEP dataset included only participants without any medications at baseline, categorized as the drug-naïve (DN) group, consisting of 69 adolescents with depression (M = 15, F = 54). The mean ages of the HCs and DN samples were 15.6 ± 0.53 and 16.7 ± 1.58 years, respectively. Four outliers were removed, resulting in a final dataset of 65 DN and 78 HC participants. The 250 metabolite measures were divided into ABS and PERC for further analysis.

For ABS, linear regression identified 24 metabolite measures that were significantly altered (adj.p-value < 0.05) (see Fig. 2), while OPLS-DA highlighted 47 as important (VIP > 1.0). Combining these approaches yielded 24 metabolite measures that were both statistically significant and contributed meaningfully to the multivariate analysis (Table 3). In contrast, for PERC data, linear regression identified 34 metabolite percentages with a statistically significant contribution; however, OPLS-DA could not be performed. Thus, 24 metabolomic signatures were identified among the DN group. More information is added in Supplementary Table 9.

Depressed adolescents consuming medications

The remaining depressed adolescents who were consuming psychotropic and/or other medications were classified as the medicated (MED) group. This group consisted of 135 adolescents, with the majority being female (83.7%). The mean ages of the HCs and MED groups were 15.6 ± 0.53 and 16.5 ± 1.60 years, respectively. Using a combination of univariate and multivariate approaches for metabolite selection, 74 metabolite measures from ABS showed statistical significance and multivariate contribution (see Supplementary Tables 6 and 10). The 26 PERC showed lower confidence, given the low Q2 (0.1), and were thus not selected as metabolomic signatures (see Supplementary Table 7). Therefore, 74 metabolomic signatures were identified, differentiating MED from HCs. Additional information on the volcano plots (see Supplementary Fig. 1) and medications is provided in Supplementary File #9.

Common metabolomic signatures

Of the 24 metabolomic signatures identified in the DN set, all were also present in the DEP set, while 22 were shared by both DEP and MED. Among the 61 metabolomic signatures in the DEP set, 56 were likewise detected in the MED set, as seen in Fig. 3. Discussion

This study investigated the emerging field of metabolomics to identify metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression. Of the 250 metabolite measures analyzed, 61 were recognized as metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression. By applying a robust selection approach based on statistical significance and multivariate importance, these metabolomic signatures contribute to the growing field of metabolomics in psychiatry.

The metabolomic signatures identified in this study on adolescents are consistent with those found in adult studies. Of the top 24 metabolites associated with depression in adults [29], 12 were also observed in this study. Similarly, from another study [30], VLDL cholesterol, VLDL and HDL particle diameter, the inflammatory marker glycoprotein acetyls, triglycerides in VLDL and HDL, total fatty acids, total monounsaturated fatty acids, and acetate were also seen in this study. Moreover, the study by Bot et al. suggested that lipid alterations may be both a consequence of depression and part of its underlying pathophysiological pathways, including chronic hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activation and inflammatory processes. This can lead to lipolysis, release of fatty acids, increased VLDL synthesis, hypertriglyceridemia, and reduced HDL cholesterol. Excess cortisol, resulting from dysregulation of the HPA axis, has been shown to inhibit enzymes involved in fatty acid unsaturation. Additionally, experimental studies in mice suggest that administration of fatty acids can, in turn, influence HPA-axis functioning [31]. Additionally, the direction of change in the metabolite profiles, this study concerning adolescents, aligns with findings from meta-analyses of 19 blood-based metabolomics studies, which reported lower concentrations of pyruvate, phenylalanine, and glutamine in adults with depression [32]. Moreover, sensitivity analyses showed that most metabolomic signatures identified in the main analyses were consistently found in sex-stratified analyses among males (67%) and females (69%). Of these metabolites, histidine and phenylalanine were highly significant in both sexes, followed by acetate, citrate, lactate, and some VLDL measures. These findings may reflect disruptions in neurotransmitter metabolism, as histidine is the precursor for histamine, and phenylalanine for dopamine and norepinephrine [33]. Additionally, alterations in citrate and lactate suggest potential dysregulation of glycolysis and energy metabolism [10]. These pathway-related findings were interpreted in comparison with previously published literature, as no pathway analyses were conducted in this study. Therefore, formal pathway analyses are warranted in future studies to confirm these observations. This overlap reinforces the robustness of these findings across sexes, suggesting that many of the metabolomic signatures identified in this analyses are consistent in both males and females. Furthermore, regarding potential confounders, BMI data was limited in the DEP cohort. Some adolescents declined to report their weight due to heightened sensitivity surrounding body image concerns, resulting in missing BMI information. Sensitivity analyses based on the available BMI data suggested that several metabolite measures were unlikely to be influenced by BMI and may instead reflect a potential association with the disease. These included some amino acids, lactate, and a few VLDL measures (especially triglycerides). However, given the sample size and the potential effects of medication use, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Also, several metabolite measures, including HDL, LDL, some VLDL measures, and a few fatty acids, appeared to be influenced by BMI. These consistent alterations across multiple VLDL-related measures suggest a broader dysregulation of triglyceride-rich lipoprotein metabolism rather than independent effects. Therefore, these should be interpreted as representing integrated alterations at the lipoprotein subclass level rather than as isolated metabolomic signatures.

A drug-naïve status is often prioritized to minimize potential confounding effects of treatment, which is especially important in studies involving psychiatric disorders. This study identified 24 metabolomic signatures associated with clinically depressed drug-naïve adolescents. Of these, 22 metabolomic signatures were identified in all the depressed adolescents as well, which may suggest that they are being altered irrespective of the medication status.

Metabolites such as glutamine, pyruvate, lactate, citrate, glycine, and acetoacetate that are key intermediates in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and amino acid metabolism have been reported to show altered levels in the blood of patients with major depressive disorder [10]. In addition to traditional inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, glycoprotein acetyls have gained increasing attention as inflammatory indicators and have been shown to correlate with depression severity and persistence [34]. This study found glycoprotein acetyls to be elevated in depressed adolescents.

Furthermore, serum fatty acids and their compositional patterns have been associated with depression, with these relationships varying according to age, sex, and BMI [35]. Depressed individuals who exhibit alterations in lipid profiles, fatty acids, and other metabolites involved in energy metabolism may potentially benefit from tailored dietary and exercise-based interventions.

In recent years, increasing attention has been given to the immuno-metabolic phenotype of depression, which is characterized by elevated inflammatory activity coupled with metabolic dysregulation. Individuals with immuno-metabolic depression exhibit decreased ratios of omega n-3 to total fatty acids, docosahexaenoic acid to total fatty acids, and polyunsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids, alongside increased ratios of monounsaturated to total fatty acids, and apolipoprotein B to apolipoprotein A1 in the adult-onset trajectory [36]. Although dyslipidemia has been widely discussed in association with depression and depressive symptoms, these changes are likely secondary. Factors such as increased BMI, uncontrolled eating habits, poor dietary choices, and smoking can substantially contribute to elevated lipid profiles [37]. Moreover, certain antidepressant medications have also been shown to increase lipid concentrations in cell culture experiments [38]. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude whether the elevated lipid signatures in this study are direct indicators of depression. The metabolic changes observed in depression are also linked to metabolic syndrome, and evidence suggests that the relationship is bidirectional [39, 40].

Conclusion

This study employed a targeted metabolomics approach to pinpoint the metabolomic signatures associated with adolescent depression and to provide further insights into the underlying metabolic alterations associated with the disorder. Metabolomic signatures were identified using a combination of univariate and multivariate statistical methods. These findings were consistent with previous studies in adults with depression and align with known metabolic features of the disease. These results highlight the potential of metabolomics for advancing our understanding of adolescent depression. In addition, this study adds to the growing evidence supporting the role of peripheral metabolic alterations associated with mental health disorders, which may, in the future, provide signatures to aid disease diagnosis and prediction.

Strengths and limitations

With the focus on adolescents, this study uncovers the early metabolomic signatures in adolescent depression that may precede long-term chronic illness. Having drug-naïve participants is essential to understand the primary metabolic alterations, by minimizing the confounding effects of medications, and having them is a key strength of this study.

One of the limitations of the study was the inability to include confounders such as BMI and medication in the analyses, except in sensitivity analyses. Additionally, differences in storage temperature prior to centrifugation, temperature at initial freezing, and centrifugation temperature were observed between DEP and HCs, which may have influenced the findings. Certain metabolomic signatures, including lactate, pyruvate, and specific lipids, are known to be sensitive to such pre-analytical variations. Additionally, some drugs, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, can subsequently alter lipid metabolism and inflammation. As a result, their levels may be influenced by storage conditions and medications, so these findings should be interpreted with caution. Another limitation of this study is the sex imbalance in the depression group, with a predominance of females. This unequal distribution may limit the generalizability of the findings to males. Some signatures, such as the inflammatory marker glycoprotein acetyls, detected in the DEP dataset, were absent in the DN subgroup, likely reflecting the smaller sample size rather than a true absence of inflammation. The limited sample size and potential multicollinearity among PERC variables may explain the inability of OPLS-DA to construct a robust model, as reflected by the low Q² values. Additionally, NMR has a limitation compared to mass spectrometry-based methods in detecting low-abundant metabolites.
