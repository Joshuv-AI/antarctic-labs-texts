1.
introduction

In this paper, we consider the following polyharmonic equation

(1.1)

(-\Delta)^{n}u=K(x)e^{2nu}\quad\text{in}~~{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},

where
n\geq 1
and
K\neq 0
. This equation naturally arises in conformal geometry.

Let
({\mathcal{M}}^{2},g)
be a Riemannian surface with the Gauss curvature
k_{g}
, consider a metric
g_{\omega}=e^{2\omega}g
conformal to
g
, then the corresponding Gauss curvature
k_{g_{\omega}}
satisfies
-\Delta_{g}\omega+k_{g}=k_{g_{\omega}}e^{2\omega}
. In particular, for
({\mathcal{M}}^{2},g)=(\Omega,|dx|^{2})
with
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, it becomes
-\Delta\omega=k_{g_{\omega}}e^{2\omega}
in
\Omega
, called the prescribed Gauss curvature equation, whose study attracted a lot of attention.

For general Riemannian manifold
({\mathcal{M}}^{2n},g)
, Graham-Jenne-Mason-Sparling
[
15
]
introduced higher order operators
P_{g}
(called the GJMS operators) and the corresponding
Q
-curvature
Q_{g}
, which satisfy

P_{g}\omega+Q_{g}=Q_{g_{\omega}}e^{2n\omega}\quad\mbox{if }g_{\omega}=e^{2\omega}g.

Their work generalized the construction of Branson-Orsted
[
1
]
for
({\mathcal{M}}^{4},g)
, corresponding to the Paneitz operator
[
36
]
. Since then, the study of
Q
-curvature plays an important role in conformal geometry.

One natural question is the prescription of
Q
-curvature, that is, whether we can find a conformal metric that gives the desired
Q
-curvature. Consider the flat model case with
({\mathcal{M}}^{2n},g_{0})=({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},|dx|^{2})
, then
P_{g_{0}}=(-\Delta)^{n}
, the problem is reduced to find solutions to (
1.1
) for a given
Q
-curvature function
Q_{g_{u}}=K
.

Let us begin with the simplest case
n=1
and
K\equiv 1
. In 1850s, Liouville
[
28
,
29
]
showed for the first time that all local real solutions to
-\Delta u=e^{2u}
in a simply connected domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, are given by a locally univalent meromorphic functions
h(z)
with

u(x)=\ln\frac{2|h(z)|}{1+|h^{\prime}(z)|^{2}}.

Chou-Wan
[
12
]
and Brito-Hounie-Leite
[
2
]
then extended Liouville’s formula for non-simply connected domains of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. It means that there is a rich family of entire smooth solutions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, as we can take
h(z)=az+b
or
e^{az+b}
and so on, so people added the finite total curvature condition
1\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},dv_{g_{u}})
or equivalently
e^{2u}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},dx)
, where
dx
means the Lebesgue measure. In 1991, Chen & Li
[
5
]
proved a famous classification result: Any smooth solution to

(1.2)

\displaystyle-\Delta u=e^{2u}\;\;\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{2u}dx<\infty

is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle u_{x_{0},\lambda}(x)=\ln\frac{2\lambda}{1+{\lambda^{2}}|x-x_{0}|^{2}}

with
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\lambda>0
. Geometrically, it means that any metric conformal to
|dx|^{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with the Gauss curvature
k_{g_{u}}\equiv 1
and finite total curvature is provided by a stereographic projection from the standard sphere
S^{2}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

In general, we say that the solution to (
1.1
) has finite total curvature if
Ke^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},dx)
, and we denote by
\Lambda_{u}
the total
Q
-curvature associated to
g_{u}
, that is

\Lambda_{u}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}Ke^{2nu}dx.

Consider now the positive constant
Q
-curvature equation in higher dimensions.

(1.4)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}u=(2n-1)!e^{2nu}\;\;\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\quad\Lambda_{u}=(2n-1)!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}e^{2nu}dx<\infty.

Here
(2n-1)!
is the
Q
-curvature of the Euclidean sphere
S^{2n}
. It is well-known that any stereographic projection from
S^{2n}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
yields a conformal metric with the constant
Q
-curvature equal to
(2n-1)!
. In other words, the formula in (
1.3
) defines a family of solutions to (
1.4
), and their total curvature
\Lambda_{u_{x_{0},\lambda}}\equiv\Lambda(S^{2n})
, the total
Q
-curvature of
S^{2n}
.

However, the set of the conformal metrics with positive constant
Q
-curvature and finite total curvature for
n\geq 2
becomes much more rich than the
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
case.

Using variational method, Chang-Chen
[
3
]
proved that for any
n\geq 2
and any
0<\gamma<\Lambda(S^{2n})
, there exist solutions to (
1.1
) with
K\equiv(2n-1)!
and
\Lambda_{u}=\gamma
. The condition
\Lambda_{u}<\Lambda(S^{2n})
was not only suggested by technical reason, it is also necessary when
n=2
.
Indeed, Lin
[
27
]
showed that any solution to (
1.4
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
verifies
\Lambda_{u}\leq\Lambda(S^{4})
, and
the equality holds if and only if the solution is spherical (see also
[
17
]
for the geometric setting). Furthermore, for any
0<\gamma<\Lambda(S^{4})
, Wei & Ye

[
40
]
provided infinitely many non radial solutions to (
1.4
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
with
\Lambda_{u}=\gamma
. This phenomenon is generalized for all
n\geq 2
by Hyder & Martinazzi
[
23
]
.
When
n\geq 3
, Martinazzi
[
31
]
found a striking new phenomenon, he showed that there exist solutions to (
1.4
) for which the total curvatures are larger than
\Lambda(S^{2n})
.
Later, Huang-Ye
[
18
]
, Hyder
[
21
]
proved that the total curvature
\Lambda_{u}
can take arbitrary positive value, that is, for
n\geq 3
,
\gamma>0
, there exist conformal metrics
g_{u}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
such that
u
resolves (
1.4
) and
\Lambda_{u}=\gamma
.

When
K
is a negative constant, there is no entire smooth solution to
\Delta u=e^{2u}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, see Theorem 1 in
[
35
]
. Again, a completely different situation appears for
n\geq 2
. Hyder & Martinazzi
[
23
,
20
]
showed
that for any
n\geq 2
,
\gamma<0
, and any polynomial
P
with
{\rm deg}(P)\leq(2n-2)
and
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}P(x)=\infty
, there exists
u
such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}u=-(2n-1)!e^{2nu}\quad\text{in}\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\;\;\Lambda_{u}=\gamma

and

(1.6)

\displaystyle u(x)=-P(x)+\frac{2|\gamma|}{\Lambda(S^{2n})}\ln|x|\rightarrow\ell\in{\mathbb{R}}\quad\mbox{as }\;|x|\to\infty.

All these results indicate that the situation is drastically changed in higher dimensional cases (
n\geq 2
), even for conformal metrics with constant
Q
-curvature.

In
[
4
]
, Chang-Qing-Yang used an important notion of
normal metrics
for conformally flat manifolds to prove the formula of the asymptotic isoperimetric ratio. The normal metric was first introduced by Huber
[
19
]
, he proved that in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the conformal metric is always normal if we have finite total Gauss curvature.

Definition 1.1
.

A conformal metric
g_{u}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is said normal if it has finite total
Q
-curvature, and there is
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(1.7)

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\ln\frac{|y|}{|x-y|}Q_{u}(y)e^{2nu}dy+C,\quad\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

where

(1.8)

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\frac{(2n-1)!}{2}|S^{2n}|=2^{2n-1}(n-1)!\pi^{n}.

People are interested to understand the geometric meaning of the normal metrics. Chang-Qing-Yang
[
4
]
showed that for
n=2
, if the scalar curvature
R_{g_{u}}
is nonnegative at infinity, then the metric must be normal; Wang-Wang
[
37
]
generalized this result for general
n
and
R_{g_{u}}^{-}e^{2nu}\in L^{n}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},dx)
; Recently, Li defined in
[
26
]
a volume entropy for conformal metrics and showed that the metric
g_{u}
is normal if and only if its volume entropy
\tau_{u}
is finite.

When
K
is a positive constant, Wei-Xu
[
39
]
showed that all normal solutions to (
1.4
) are given by the stereographic projection for any
n\geq 2
, i.e. by (
1.3
). If
K
is a negative constant, Martinazzi
[
30
]
proved that there is no normal solution to (
1.5
) for any
n\geq 2
.

For general non-constant curvature cases, the prescribed Gauss curvature equation (i.e.
n=1
in (
1.1
))
was intensively studied, see for instance
[
32
]
-
[
34
]
,
[
6
]
-
[
11
]
and the references therein.

In
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, several studies were realized for the existence or nonexistence of normal conformal metrics with special
Q
-curvature; or equivalently normal solution to (
1.1
) with
n=2
.

•

For
K(x)=1-|x|^{p}
, Hyder-Martinazzi
[
24
]
showed that a normal solution exists if and only if
p\in(0,4)
, while for
K(x)=1+|x|^{p}
, it is proved in
[
24
]
that for any
p>0
, there exists normal conformal metric;

•

The nonexistence results of normal conformal metric are established in
[
25
,
38
]
with
K(x)=(1-|x|^{p})|x|^{\beta}
for some
p
,
\beta
and prescribed total curvature
\Lambda
.

As far as we know, there are few results for the existence of normal conformal metrics with general non constant
Q
-curvature and
n\geq 2
. For
K(x)=|x|^{p}
with
p>-2n\geq-2
, Hyder-Mancini-Martinazzi
[
22
]
showed that a radial normal solution exists with
\Lambda_{u}=\Lambda(S^{2n})(1+\frac{p}{2n})
.

Here we are interested to understand normal conformal metrics with general
Q
-curvature, or more generally the solutions to (
1.1
) with logarithmic control at infinity, i.e. satisfying

(1.9)

\displaystyle u(x)=O(\ln|x|)\;\;\mbox{ as }|x|\to\infty,

that we call also normal solution by abuse of notation.

The following results (Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
below) show that under mild polynomial growth control on
K
, any solution verifying (
1.9
) will have a precise logarithmic behavior as
|x|\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
) satisfying (
1.9
). Assume that
K
satisfies the polynomial growth condition

(1.10)

\displaystyle|K(x)|\leq C(|x|^{\lambda}+1)\;\;\mbox{in }\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\quad\mbox{with }\;C>0,\lambda\geq 0.

•

If
K
has constant sign at infinity, then
K(x)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, i.e. the conformal metric
g_{u}=e^{2u}|dx|^{2}
has a finite total curvature
\Lambda_{u}
.

•

If
K(x)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, there holds

(1.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{u(x)}{\ln|x|}=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}K(x)e^{2nu}dx.

A direct consequence is a lower bound for the total curvature of normal metrics
g_{u}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
K
have constant sign at infinity and satisfy (
1.10
), any normal solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

\Lambda_{u}\geq-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}

where

(1.12)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(K):=\sup\left\{\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}\;|\;K(x)(1+|x|)^{2n\beta}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})\right\}.

Hence a necessary condition to have normal conformal metric in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
with such nonpositive
Q
-curvature
K\neq 0
is
\alpha_{1}(K)\geq 0
.

Indeed, let
\alpha=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}
. Using (
1.11
),
K(x)(1+|x|)^{2n(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
for any
\varepsilon>0
, so
\alpha-\varepsilon\leq\alpha_{1}(K)
hence
\alpha\leq\alpha_{1}(K)
.

Moreover we get a refined asymptotic behavior of
u
when
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
K
satisfy (
1.10
). Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
satisfying (
1.9
) and
Ke^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. If
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
, then

(1.13)

\displaystyle u(x)=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}\ln|x|+u(0)+\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\ln|y|K(y)e^{2nu}dy+o(1),\quad\text{as}~|x|\to\infty.

Now we turn to the existence of normal conformal metrics when
K
is nonpositive with
\alpha_{1}(K)>0
. Notice that the quantity
\alpha_{1}(K)
was first introduced by Cheng-Ni in
[
10
]
for the Gauss curvature function
K
to characterize the existence of solutions to problem (
1.1
) with
n=1
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
K\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
be nonpositive and satisfy the polynomial growth control (
1.10
). Assume that
\alpha_{1}(K)>0
, then for any
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{1})
, there exists a solution
u_{\alpha}
to (
1.1
) such that

(1.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\big[u(x)-\alpha\ln|x|\big]=\ell\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Remark 1.6
.

Seeing Corollary
1.3
and
\alpha=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}
, the above existence result is optimal, except for the borderline case
\alpha=\alpha_{1}(K)
, or equivalently
\Lambda_{u}=-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
.

If furthermore
K
is radially symmetric, we can obtain the similar existence result without any growth assumption on
K
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
K
be a nontrivial and nonpositive function in
C_{\rm rad}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. Suppose that
\alpha_{1}(K)>0
, then for any
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{1})
, there exists a radial solution
u_{\alpha}
to (
1.1
) satisfying (
1.14
).

The paper is organized as follows:
In Section 2, we begin with the study of the polyharmonic linear equation
\Delta^{n}u=f
in

, we prove that with mild conditions on

2.
Preliminary: Linear Theory

We begin with the study of linear polyharmonic equation
\Delta^{n}u=f
in the conformal dimension.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
v\in C^{2n}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
\Delta^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
with
f
having constant sign near
\infty
. If
v(x)=O({\ln|x|})
at
\infty
, then
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
.

Proof
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
f
is nonnegative near
\infty
. Assume that
f\not\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, so is
\overline{f}
. Here and in the following,
\overline{g}
stands for the spherical average of a generic function
g\in L^{1}_{loc}
, i.e.

\overline{g}(r)=-\!\!\!\!\!\!\int_{\partial B_{r}}g(x)d\sigma.

Denote
v_{k}:=\Delta^{n-k}\overline{v}
for
0\leq k\leq n
. Then
\Delta v_{1}=\overline{f}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, so that

(2.1)

\displaystyle r^{2n-1}v_{1}^{\prime}(r)=\int^{r}_{0}s^{2n-1}\overline{f}(s)ds\to\infty\quad\mbox{as }r\to\infty.

If
n=1
, clearly
v_{1}=\overline{v}
and
\lim_{r\to\infty}|\overline{v}(r)|/\ln r=\infty
, which is impossible.

From now on, let
n\geq 2
. By (
2.1
),
v_{1}
is nondecreasing near
\infty
and
\lim_{r\to\infty}{v_{1}}(r)=:\ell_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup{\{\infty}\}
exists. If
\ell_{1}\neq 0
, then
\overline{v}(r)\sim\frac{\ell_{1}}{C_{0}}r^{2n-2}
as
r\to\infty
, which contradicts the assumption
v(x)=O(\ln|x|)
at infinity, hence
\ell_{1}=0
.

Using again (
2.1
), given any
M>0
,
v_{1}^{\prime}(r)\geq Mr^{1-2n}
for
r
large, therefore

-v_{1}(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{1}^{\prime}(s)ds\geq\frac{M}{2(n-1)}r^{2-2n}\quad\mbox{for $r$ large}.

By induction, we can claim that for any
1\leq k\leq n-1
,
\lim_{r\to\infty}v_{k}(r)=0
, and there holds
(-1)^{k}v_{k}\geq MC_{k}r^{2(k-n)}
for
r
large enough, where
C_{k}
are positive constants depending only on
k
and
n
.

So we get
(-1)^{n-1}\Delta\overline{v}\geq CMr^{-2}
for
r
large enough, therefore
(-1)^{n-1}\overline{v}^{\prime}\geq-CMr^{-1}
for large
r
. As
M>0
is arbitrary,
\lim_{r\to\infty}|\overline{v}(r)|/\ln r=\infty
, which yields a contradiction. Hence
f
must belong to
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
.
∎

For the existence issue to
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
verifying (
1.9
), we will use a polynomial growth condition

(2.2)

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq C(|x|^{\lambda}+1)\quad\text{with}~\lambda\geq 0.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Suppose that
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
satisfies (
2.2
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Then there exists a continuous function
v
satisfying
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and (
1.9
). Moreover,
v
is unique up to a constant, and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{v(x)}{\ln|x|}=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(x)dx.

Proof
. Denote

(2.4)

\displaystyle v(x)=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\ln\frac{|x-y|}{|y|+1}f(y)dy,\quad\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Clearly
v\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
is well defined and
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{n}v(x)}{\ln|x|}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\frac{-\ln|x-y|+\ln(|y|+1)+\ln|x|}{\ln|x|}f(y)dy

\displaystyle=:\sum_{1\leq i\leq 3}\int_{T_{i}}P(x,y)f(y)dy

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where

\displaystyle T_{1}=B_{1}(x),\quad T_{2}=B_{R},\quad T_{3}={\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus(B_{1}(x)\cup B_{R})

with
R\geq 1
,
|x|\geq R+2
.

First,
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}I_{2}=0
because
\|P(x,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{2})}\to 0
if
|x|\to\infty
. In
T_{1}
, as
|x|\geq 3
,

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq C\int_{B_{1}(x)}|f(y)|dy+\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\int_{B_{1}(x)}-|f(y)|\ln|x-y|dy.

By (
2.2
) and
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}(x)}-|f(y)|\ln|x-y|dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{|x-y|\leq|x|^{-(\lambda+1)}}-|f(y)|\ln|x-y|dy+\int_{|x|^{-(\lambda+1)}<|x-y|<1}-|f(y)|\ln|x-y|dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;C|x|^{\lambda}\int_{|x-y|\leq|x|^{-(\lambda+1)}}-\ln|x-y|dx+(\lambda+1)\ln|x|\int_{|x|^{-(\lambda+1)}<|x-y|<1}|f(y)|dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;C|x|^{(1-2n)\lambda-2n}\ln|x|+o(\ln|x|).

We see that
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}I_{1}=0
. In
T_{3}
,
there holds

\displaystyle|P(x,y)|\leq C,\quad\mbox{since }\frac{1}{|x|}\leq\frac{|y|+1}{|x-y|}\leq\frac{|x-y|+|x|+1}{|x-y|}\leq 2+|x|.

Therefore

\displaystyle|I_{3}|\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus B_{R}}|f(y)|dy\longrightarrow 0\;\;\mbox{as }R\to\infty.

We conclude then

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\left[\frac{\gamma_{n}v(x)}{\ln|x|}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(x)dx\right]=\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3})=0.

If there is another solution
w
satisfying (
1.9
), then
\Delta^{n}(v-w)=0
and
(v-w)=O(\ln|x|)
at
\infty
. Standard elliptic theory yields then
v-w
is
constant in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. ∎

When strengthening a little bit the integrability of
f
, we get a more precise asymptotic behavior for the above solution
v
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Assume that
f
satisfies (
2.2
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and
f(x)\ln(2+|x|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. Then there exists a unique continuous function
v
such that
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\left[v(x)+\frac{\ln|x|}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(y)dy\right]=0.

If moreover
f\in\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, there exists a unique
v\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.

Proof
. As
f(x)\ln(2+|x|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
f
is locally bounded, define now

(2.6)

\displaystyle v(x)=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(y)\ln|x-y|dy,\quad\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Then
v\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
is well defined and
(-\Delta)^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. So

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}v(x)+\ln|x|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\ln\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}f(y)dy

\displaystyle=:\sum_{1\leq i\leq 4}\int_{T_{i}}\ln\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}f(y)dy

\displaystyle=:\sum_{1\leq i\leq 4}I_{i}

where

\displaystyle T_{1}=\left\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},|y-x|\leq|x|^{-(\lambda+1)}\right\}

and

\displaystyle T_{2}=B_{R},\quad T_{3}=B_{\frac{|x|}{2}}(x)\setminus T_{1},\quad T_{4}={\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus(B_{\frac{|x|}{2}}(x)\cup T_{2})

with
R\geq 1
,
|x|\geq 2R+2
.

Obviously,
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}I_{2}=0
since
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. For
I_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{T_{1}}|f(y)|\ln|x|dy-\int_{T_{1}}|f(y)|\ln|x-y|dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{T_{1}}|f(y)|\ln|y|dy-C|x|^{\lambda}\int_{T_{1}}\ln|x-y|dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{T_{1}}|f(y)|\ln|y|dy+C|x|^{(1-2n)\lambda-2n}\ln|x|.

Moreover, there holds

\displaystyle 2\leq\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\leq|x|^{\lambda+2}\leq 2^{\lambda+2}|y|^{\lambda+2}\quad\mbox{in }\;T_{3}

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+|y|}\leq\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\leq 2\quad\mbox{in }\;T_{4},

since
|x-y|\leq|x|+|y|\leq|x|(1+|y|)
in
T_{4}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|I_{i}|\leq C\int_{T_{i}}|f(y)|\ln(|y|+2)dy,\quad\mbox{for }i=3,4.

Combining all these estimates, we deduce that

\displaystyle\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\Big|\gamma_{n}v(x)+\ln|x|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(y)dy\Big|\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus B_{R}}|f(y)|\ln(|y|+2)dy.

Taking
R\to\infty
, (
2.5
) holds true. When
f\in\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, (
2.5
) means just
v\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
.∎

Remark 2.4
.

As we used only the kernel to prove Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
, similar results remain valid for the corresponding linear equation in odd dimensions, i.e. for
(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

The following result is not directly used to study the
Q
-curvature equation (
1.1
), but some ideas of the approach will be useful for us.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that
f
satisfies (
2.2
) and
f(x)(1+|x|)^{\epsilon_{0}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Then there exists a unique function
v\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
satisfying
\Delta^{n}v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and

(2.7)

\displaystyle v(x)=-\frac{\ln|x|}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(x)dx+O(|x|^{-\delta})\quad\text{with $\delta>0$, as $|x|\to\infty$}.

Proof. Let
v
be defined by (
2.6
) and

{\widetilde{w}}(x)=v(x)+\frac{\ln|x|}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}f(x)dx.

Denote by
w
the Kelvin transformation of
{\widetilde{w}}
with the respect to the unite disk, i.e.
w(x)={\widetilde{w}}(x/|x|^{2})
, then

\Delta^{n}w=|x|^{-4n}f\left(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\right)=:h\quad\text{in}~B_{1}\setminus{\{0}\},

By the assumption on
f
, we have
h(x)|x|^{-\epsilon_{0}}\in L^{1}(B_{1})
. Fix
\epsilon>0
such that
(4n+\lambda)\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
, using (
2.2
), there holds

|h(x)|^{1+\epsilon}=|h(x)||x|^{-\epsilon_{0}}\left(|h(x)||x|^{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\epsilon}}\right)^{\epsilon}\leq|h(x)||x|^{-\epsilon_{0}}\left(C|x|^{-4n-\lambda+\epsilon_{0}/\epsilon}\right)^{\epsilon}\leq C_{\epsilon}|h(x)||x|^{-\epsilon_{0}}.

So
h\in L^{1+\epsilon}(B_{1})
. Let
\xi
be the solution of

\Delta^{n}\xi=h\quad\text{in}~B_{1},\quad\quad\Delta^{i}\xi|_{\partial B_{1}}=0,~~0\leq i\leq n-1.

By classical elliptic theory and Sobolev embedding,
\xi\in C^{0,\delta}(B_{1})
for some
\delta>0
. As
w\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
is bounded by Theorem
2.3

-\Delta^{n}\zeta=0\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta\geq 0\quad\text{in}~B_{1}\setminus{\{0}\}.

Applying Theorem 3.1 in
[
13
]
, we get

\zeta(x)=H(x)+\sum_{|\beta|\leq 2n-2}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\Phi(x)\quad\text{in}~B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus{\{0}\},

where
\beta
are multi-index,
a_{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\Phi(x)=-\ln|x|
and
H\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a polyharmonic function. Remark that
\zeta\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we must have
a_{\beta}\equiv 0
for any

. Hence,

w=H+\xi-C_{1}\;\;\in C^{0,\gamma}(B_{\frac{1}{2}}).

Coming back to
{\widetilde{w}}
who tends to
0
at infinity, we get the claimed expansion. ∎

3.
Asymptotic behavior and Pohozaev identity

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
and
1.4

The results are direct consequences of the previous section.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
) verifying (
1.9
) and
K
satisfy (
1.10
). Clearly
K(x)e^{2nu}
verifies also the polynomial growth condition (
1.10
) with a different
\lambda
. Suppose now
K
is of constant sign at infinity, so is
K(x)e^{2nu}
. Lemma
2.1
implies that
K(x)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, then (
1.11
) holds true.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
is completed.

If we assume that the total curvature
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
, using (
1.11
), there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
K(x)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},(1+|x|)^{\epsilon_{0}}dx)
. Let
v
be given by Theorem
2.3
, or more precisely by (
2.6
) with
f=Ke^{2nu}
. As
u-v=O(\ln|x|)
is polyharmonic in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, there holds
u-v\equiv\ell
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, hence

u(x)=u(0)-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(\ln|x-y|-\ln|y|)K(y)e^{2nu}dy,

Applying (
2.5
), Theorem
1.4
holds true.∎

Remark 3.1
.

If
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
in Theorem
1.4
, we could also apply Theorem
2.5
to get more precise behavior for the solution
u
. Moreover, Theorem
1.2
is in fact valid for the
Q
-curvature equation
(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}u=K(x)e^{mu}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for any dimension
m\geq 1
.

3.2.
A Pohozaev Identity

Under more precise growth condition like

(3.1)

\displaystyle C_{1}|x|^{\lambda}\leq|K(x)|\leq C_{2}|x|^{\lambda},\quad\text{for}~|x|~\to\infty,

with
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have the following equality which is a consequence of the Pohozaev identity.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
K\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
satisfy (
3.1
). Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) verifying (
1.9
). Suppose that
K(x)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
. Assume moreover either
x\cdot\nabla K
is of constant sign at infinity or
\alpha_{1}(x\cdot\nabla K)\geq\alpha_{1}(K)
, then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(x\cdot\nabla K)e^{2nu}dx=\frac{n\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}(\Lambda_{u}-2\gamma_{n}).

Proof
. By (
3.1
), obviously
\alpha_{1}(K)=-1-\frac{\lambda}{2n}
. As
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
, by the proof of Theorem
1.4
, there holds

u(x)=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}Ke^{2nu}\ln|x-y|dy+C,\quad\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

For
k\geq 1
, denote

D_{k}w:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta^{\frac{k}{2}}w&\mbox{if $k$ is even};\\
\displaystyle\partial_{r}\big(\Delta^{\frac{k-1}{2}}w\big)=\frac{x}{|x|}\cdot\nabla\big(\Delta^{\frac{k-1}{2}}w\big)&\mbox{if $k$ is odd}.\end{array}\right.

We claim that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\forall\;1\leq k\leq 2n-1,\quad\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}|x|^{k}D_{k}u(x)=A_{k}\alpha\;\;\mbox{with }\alpha=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}

and

A_{k}=(-1)^{\left[\frac{k-1}{2}\right]}2^{k-1}\left[\frac{k-1}{2}\right]!\frac{(n-1)!}{\left(n-1-\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]\right)!}.

Here

[t]
means the integer part of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Indeed,
D_{k}\ln|x|=A_{k}|x|^{-k}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\backslash\{0\}
for any
1\leq k\leq 2n-1
. There holds then

\displaystyle|x|^{k}D_{k}u(x)-A_{k}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}|x|^{k}\Big(D_{k}\ln|x|-D_{k}\ln|x-y|\Big)Ke^{2nu}dy

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{T_{i}}P_{1}(x,y)Ke^{2nu}dy

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}

where

\displaystyle T_{1}=\left\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},|y-x|\leq\frac{|x|}{2}\right\},\;\;T_{2}=B_{R},\;\;T_{3}={\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus(T_{1}\cup T_{2})

with
R\geq 1
,
|x|\geq 2R+2
.

Thanks to the mean value theorem, for any
y\in T_{2}

\Big|D_{k}\ln|x|-D_{k}\ln|x-y|\Big|\leq|y|\times\sup_{B_{R}(x)}\big|\nabla^{k+1}\ln|z|\big|\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{k+1}},

so we get

\displaystyle|I_{2}|\leq\frac{C}{|x|}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}|K|e^{2nu}dx\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }\;|x|\to\infty.

In
T_{3}
, there holds
|Q(x,y)|\leq C
, then

|I_{3}|\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus{B_{R}}}|K|e^{2nu}dx\to 0,\quad\text{as}~R\to\infty.

Finally, for
|x|
large, we have
|K(y)|e^{2nu}\leq C|x|^{2n\alpha+\lambda}
in
T_{1}
. Therefore, let
\alpha=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}
,

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{T_{1}}\left(1+\frac{|x|^{k}}{|x-y|^{k}}\right)|K|e^{2nu}dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{T_{1}}\left(1+\frac{|x|^{k}}{|x-y|^{k}}\right)|x|^{2n\alpha+\lambda}dy

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{2n\alpha+\lambda+2n}.

So
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}I_{1}=0
because
\alpha<\alpha_{1}(K)=-1-\frac{\lambda}{2n}
. The claim (
3.3
) holds true. Similarly, we have also

(3.4)

\displaystyle|x|^{k}D_{k}(x\cdot\nabla u)\to 0\quad\mbox{as $|x|\to\infty$},\quad\forall\;1\leq k\leq 2n-1.

Applying the Pohozaev identity for
(-\Delta)^{n}u=K(x)e^{2nu}
in
B_{R}
for
R>0
, there holds,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2n}\int_{B_{R}}(x\cdot\nabla K)e^{2nu}dx+\int_{B_{R}}Ke^{2nu}dx+\frac{R}{2}\int_{\partial{B_{R}}}(D_{n}u)^{2}d\sigma\\
=&\;\frac{R}{2n}\int_{\partial{B_{R}}}Ke^{2nu}d\sigma-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\int_{\partial{B_{R}}}(-1)^{n+j}(D_{2n-1-j}u)D_{j}(x\cdot\nabla u)d\sigma\\
&\;+(-1)^{n+1}R\int_{\partial{B_{R}}}(D_{2n-1}u)(D_{1}u)d\sigma.\end{split}

For the last term, we used
(x\cdot\nabla u)=R(D_{1}u)
on
\partial B_{R}
. By (
3.3
)-(
3.4
), (
3.1
) and
\alpha<\alpha_{1}(K)
, the right hand side goes to
(-1)^{n+1}A_{2n-1}A_{1}\alpha^{2}|S^{2n-1}|
as
R\to\infty
.

If
(x\cdot\nabla K)
is of constant sign at infinity or
(x\cdot\nabla K)e^{2nu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
(when
\alpha_{1}(x\cdot\nabla K)\geq\alpha_{1}(K)
), tending
R\to\infty
in (
3.5
), using (
3.3
) and
|S^{m}|=(m+1)\pi^{\frac{m+1}{2}}/\Gamma\left(\frac{m+3}{2}\right)
, we deduce that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(x\cdot\nabla K)e^{2nu}dx

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\alpha-\frac{|S^{2n-1}|}{2}A_{n}^{2}\alpha^{2}+(-1)^{n+1}A_{2n-1}A_{1}\alpha^{2}|S^{2n-1}|

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\pi^{n}}{(n-1)!}\left[2^{n-1}(n-1)!\right]^{2}\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\alpha(\alpha+2),

with
\alpha=-\frac{\Lambda_{u}}{\gamma_{n}}
. ∎

Remark 3.3
.

For
K(x)=|x|^{p}
, as the left hand side of (
3.2
) equals to

, we see that any normal solution to (

) satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha=-2-\frac{p}{n},\quad\mbox{or equivalently }\;\Lambda_{u}=\Lambda(S^{2n})\Big(1+\frac{p}{2n}\Big).

This was proved in
[
22
]
for radial normal solutions (see also
[
16
]
with
n=2
).

4.
Normal conformal metrics for negative
Q
-curvature

Here we will prove the existence of normal solutions to (
1.1
) for
K\leq 0
, i.e. Theorem
1.5
. Fix
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
u_{0}(x)=\ln|x|
for any
|x|\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Clearly,
(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}\equiv 0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus B_{\frac{1}{2}}
and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}dx=-\gamma_{n}.

We would like to find a solution
u
to (
1.1
) of the form

u=w+\alpha u_{0}+\frac{1}{2n}c_{w}

with
w\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and a suitable constant
c_{w}
. Indeed, a direct computation shows that to get
u
solution of (
1.1
) is equivalent to find
w\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
c_{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}w=K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}-\alpha(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}\;\;\mbox{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{2n}$}.

To have such
w
, we will use Leray-Schauder’s fixed point argument, see Theorem 11.3 in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T
be a compact mapping of a Banach space
X
into itself, and suppose that there exists a constant
M_{0}
such that
\|z\|_{X}\leq M_{0}

for all
z\in X
satisfying
z=tT(z)
with some
t\in(0,1]
. Then
T
has a fixed point.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
. We will proceed by several steps.

Step 1: The general setting.
Fix
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{1})
. For any
w\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, there exists a unique
c_{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}dx=-\alpha\gamma_{n}.

Indeed,
c_{w}
is given by

(4.9)

\displaystyle c_{w}=\ln(\alpha\gamma_{n})-\ln\left\{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}-K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw}dx\right\}.

Now we can apply Theorem
2.3
to get a solution
v\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}v=K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}-\alpha(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}=:\Psi(w)\quad\mbox{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{2n}$}.

Since
\alpha<\alpha_{1}(K)
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}(1+|x|)^{\varepsilon_{0}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}),

hence
\Psi(w)\in\dot{L^{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})\cap L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\ln(2+|x|)dx)
. Denote
{\mathbb{T}}(w)=v
, so
{\mathbb{T}}
is a mapping from
c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
into itself and it’s not difficult to see that
{\mathbb{T}}
is continuous.

Step 2: Compactness of the operator
{\mathbb{T}}
.
Recall that
c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
is a Banach space with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Consider the ball
\Sigma:=\{\|w\|_{\infty}\leq M\}
in
c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
with
M>0
, we shall prove that
{\mathbb{T}}(\Sigma)
is relatively compact in
c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
.

Using (
4.9
), we see from that
c_{w}
are uniformly bounded for
w\in\Sigma
. Therefore the family
\{\Psi(w),w\in\Sigma\}
is uniformly integrable in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\ln(2+|x|)dx)
and verifies the growth condition (
1.10
) with some common constants
C
and
\lambda
.

By the proof of Theorem
2.3
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
R>0
depending only on
M
and
\varepsilon
, such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}(w)(x)|\leq\varepsilon,\quad\forall\;|x|\geq R,\;w\in\Sigma.

Furthermore, we have the integral representation of
{\mathbb{T}}(w)
,

(4.11)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}(w)=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\ln|x-y|K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}-\alpha u_{0}(x).

There holds then

|\Delta({\mathbb{T}}(w)+\alpha u_{0})(x)|\leq C_{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\frac{|K(y)|}{|x-y|^{2}}e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}dy,

which yields readily that

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}(w)(x)|+|\Delta{\mathbb{T}}(w)(x)|\leq C_{M,R},\quad\forall\;|x|\leq R+1,\;w\in\Sigma.

By elliptic theory,
\{{\mathbb{T}}(w)|_{\overline{B}_{R}},\;w\in\Sigma\}
is relatively compact in
C(\overline{B}_{R})
. Combining with (
4.10
), the family
\{{\mathbb{T}}(w),\;w\in\Sigma\}
is precompact, hence relatively compact in
c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. So the operator
{\mathbb{T}}
is compact.

Step 3: Uniform local estimate.
Now we consider the family of eventual fixed points of
t{\mathbb{T}}(w)
with
t\in(0,1]
. Assume that
w=t{\mathbb{T}}(w)
,
t\in(0,1]
and
w\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, that is

(-\Delta)^{n}w=tK(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}-t\alpha(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}\;\;\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Applying again Theorem
2.3
, there holds

(4.12)

\displaystyle w(x)=-\frac{t}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}K(y)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2nw+c_{w}}\ln|x-y|dy-t\alpha u_{0}(x).

Denote

(4.13)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{w}}:=w+\frac{c_{w}}{2n}+\frac{\ln t}{2n}+t\alpha u_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{w}:={\widetilde{w}}-t\alpha u_{0}=w+\frac{c_{w}}{2n}+\frac{\ln t}{2n}.

Clearly,
(-\Delta)^{n}{\widetilde{w}}={\widetilde{K}}e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
with
{\widetilde{K}}:=Ke^{(1-t)2n\alpha u_{0}}
. As
K\not\equiv 0
, without loss of generality, up to an orthogonal transform of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, we can assume that
K<0
in
B_{3}
, so
|{\widetilde{K}}|\geq C_{0}>0
in
B_{3}
for any
t\in(0,1]
. We deduce then

\displaystyle\int_{B_{3}}e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}dx\leq\frac{1}{C_{0}}\int_{B_{3}}|{\widetilde{K}}|e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{0}}\int_{B_{3}}|K|e^{2nw+c_{w}+2n\alpha u_{0}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}|K|e^{2nw+c_{w}+2n\alpha u_{0}}dx=C_{1}\alpha\gamma_{n}.

On the other hand, thanks to (
4.12
), we have

\Delta{\widetilde{w}}(x)=-\frac{(2n-2)t}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\frac{{\widetilde{K}}e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}}{|x-y|^{2}}dy.

With Fubini’s theorem, as
{\widetilde{K}}\leq 0
and
t\in(0,1]
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{B_{3}}|\Delta{\widetilde{w}}|dx\leq\frac{2n-2}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}{\widetilde{K}}e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}\int_{B_{3}(y)}\frac{dx}{|x-y|^{2}}dy\leq C\alpha.

Using Theorem 4.2 in
[
23
]
, there exists
C>0
(independent of
{\widetilde{w}}
) such that

\sup_{B_{2}}{\widetilde{w}}\leq C.

From the uniform upper bound of
{\widetilde{w}}
,
\overline{w}
is uniformly upper bounded in
B_{2}
.

Moreover, we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|{\widetilde{w}}\|_{C^{2n-1}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|\overline{w}\|_{C^{2n-1}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq C.

Indeed, let
x\in B_{1}
, for any
1\leq k\leq 2n-1
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}{\widetilde{w}}(x)|\leq C_{k}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\frac{|{\widetilde{K}}|e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}}{|x-y|^{k}}dy

\displaystyle=C_{k}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{|{\widetilde{K}}|e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}}{|x-y|^{k}}dy+C_{k}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus{B_{1}(x)}}\frac{|{\widetilde{K}}|e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}}{|x-y|^{k}}dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{k}}+C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}|{\widetilde{K}}|e^{2n{\widetilde{w}}}dy

\displaystyle\leq C+C\alpha\gamma_{n}.

Step 4: Uniform upper bound in exterior domain.
For that, we will use the Kelvin transform, let
{\widetilde{v}}(x):=\overline{w}(x|x|^{-2})
for
0<|x|\leq 2
, then

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}{\widetilde{v}}=\overline{K}(x)e^{2n{\widetilde{v}}}=:\widetilde{f}\;\;\mbox{in }B_{2}\setminus\{0\},\quad\mbox{where }\overline{K}(x)=|x|^{-4n-2n\alpha}K\left(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\right).

Here we used
\Delta^{n}u_{0}(x)\equiv 0
for
|x|\geq\frac{1}{2}
. As
\Lambda_{u}>-\alpha_{1}(K)\gamma_{n}
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\alpha+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2n}<\alpha_{1}
, so
K(x)(1+|x|)^{2n\alpha+\varepsilon_{0}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, hence
\overline{K}(x)|x|^{-\varepsilon_{0}}\in L^{1}(B_{2})
. Fix
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that
(4n+2n\alpha+\lambda)\epsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{0}
where
\lambda
is the constant in (
1.10
), there holds, similarly as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
,

|\overline{K}(x)|^{1+\varepsilon_{1}}\leq C\overline{K}(x)|x|^{-\varepsilon_{0}}\left(|x|^{-4n-2n\alpha-\lambda+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon_{1}}}\right)^{\varepsilon_{1}}=C\overline{K}(x)|x|^{-\varepsilon_{0}}\quad\mbox{in }B_{2}.

Hence
\widetilde{f}\in L^{1+\varepsilon_{1}}(B_{2})
. Let
v_{1}
be the solution to

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}v_{1}=\widetilde{f}\;\;\mbox{in }B_{2},\quad v_{1}=\Delta v_{1}=\ldots=\Delta^{n-1}v_{1}=0\;\;\mbox{on }\partial B_{2}.

Clearly
v_{1}\in W^{2n,1+\varepsilon_{1}}(B_{2})
. Hence
v_{2}={\widetilde{v}}-v_{1}+C_{t}
verifies

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}v_{2}=0\;\;\mbox{in }B_{2}\setminus\{0\}.

Here
C_{t}
is a large enough constant depending on
t
such that
v_{2}\geq 0
. Pay attention that we do not claim any uniform control on
C_{t}
actually, but it does exist for each
t
since
\overline{w}
is bounded at infinity.

Applying Theorem 3.1 in
[
13
]
, we claim that
v_{2}
can be extended as a smooth polyharmonic function in
B_{2}
, therefore
{\widetilde{v}}\in W^{2n,1+\varepsilon_{1}}_{loc}(B_{2})
and
(-\Delta)^{n}{\widetilde{v}}=\widetilde{f}\leq 0
in
B_{2}
.
Denote
{\widetilde{v}}_{k}=(-\Delta)^{k}{\widetilde{v}}
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
. By (
4.14
), there holds

\displaystyle{\widetilde{v}}_{n-1}(x)\leq\sup_{\partial B_{1}}{\widetilde{v}}_{n-1}\leq C_{1}\;\;\mbox{in }B_{1}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)\Big({\widetilde{v}}_{n-2}+\frac{C_{1}}{4n}r^{2}\Big)\leq 0\;\;\mbox{in }B_{1}.

Hence

\displaystyle{\widetilde{v}}_{n-2}(x)\leq\sup_{\partial B_{1}}{\widetilde{v}}_{n-2}+\frac{C_{1}}{4n}\leq C_{2}\;\;\mbox{in }B_{1}.

By induction, we can claim finally that
{\widetilde{v}}\leq C
in
B_{1}
, so we have
\overline{w}(x)\leq C
for any
|x|\geq 1
hence
\overline{w}\leq C
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.

Step 5: Conclusion.
Combining
Steps 3–4
, if
w\in c_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
w=t{\mathbb{T}}(w)
with
t\in(0,1]
, then
2n\overline{w}=2nw+c_{w}+\ln t\leq C
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.
Recall that
w=t{\mathbb{T}}(w)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{n}w=\Psi(w)\;\;\mbox{with }\Psi(w)=K(x)e^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2n\overline{w}}-t\alpha(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}.

Using now the uniform integrability and the uniform polynomial control of
\Psi(w)
, the proof of Theorem
1.4
yields
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq C
. By Leray-Schauder’s fixed point Theorem, there exists a fixed point
w
for
{\mathbb{T}}
, so we get the desired solution as
u=w+\alpha u_{0}+\frac{c_{w}}{2n}
. The proof of Theorem
1.5
is completed. ∎

5.
Normal metrics for radial nonpositive
Q
-curvature

In this section, we consider radial functions

remains true without any growth control on the

-curvature

5.1.
Radial solution to polyharmonic equation

Our proof is based on the understanding of linear equation in the radial setting.

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
n\geq 2
and
\varepsilon>0
,
f\in\dot{L}_{\rm rad}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
f(1+|x|)^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. Then there exists a unique solution
u\in c_{0,{\rm rad}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
to
\Delta^{n}u=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Furthermore, there is
C>0
depending only on
n
and
\varepsilon
such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\infty}\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(1+|x|)^{\varepsilon}|f(x)|dx.

Proof
. As
f\in\dot{L^{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, let

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{1}(r)&=-\frac{1}{2n-2}\int_{r}^{\infty}sf(s)ds-\frac{1}{2n-2}r^{2-2n}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2n-1}f(s)ds\\
&=-\frac{1}{2n-2}\int_{r}^{\infty}sf(s)ds+\frac{1}{2n-2}r^{2-2n}\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{2n-1}f(s)ds.\end{split}

Easily
\Delta v_{1}=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Using the first line of (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|v_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2n-2}\int^{\infty}_{0}s|f(s)|ds.

Moreover, by the second line in (
5.2
), for any
r\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|v_{1}(r)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n-1}r^{2-2n}\int^{\infty}_{r}s^{2n-1}|f(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n-1)r^{2n-2+\varepsilon}}\int^{\infty}_{r}s^{2n-1+\varepsilon}|f(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq CM_{f,\varepsilon}r^{-2n+2-\varepsilon}

where

\displaystyle M_{f,\varepsilon}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(1+|x|)^{\varepsilon}|f(x)|dx.

Therefore, there holds

(5.3)

\displaystyle|v_{1}(r)|\leq C_{1}M_{f,\varepsilon}\Big[\chi_{[0,1)}(r)+\frac{1}{r^{2n-2+\varepsilon}}\chi_{[1,\infty)}(r)\Big],\quad\forall\;r\geq 0.

Now we will apply the following elementary result, which proof is postponed.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

\displaystyle h_{\beta}(r)=\chi_{[0,1)}(r)+\chi_{[1,\infty)}(r)\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}.

Then for any
2<\beta<2n
, there exists a unique solution
w\in c_{0,{\rm rad}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
to
\Delta w=h_{\beta}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and we have
|w(r)|\leq C_{n,\beta}h_{\beta-2}(r)
for all
r\geq 0
.

Remark that (
5.3
) means just
|v_{1}(r)|\leq C_{1}M_{f,\varepsilon}h_{2n-2+\varepsilon}(r)
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. By induction with Lemma
5.2
, we get
v_{i}\in c_{0,{\rm rad}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
\Delta v_{i+1}=v_{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and

\displaystyle|v_{i}(r)|\leq C_{i}M_{f,\varepsilon}h_{2n-2i+\varepsilon}(r)\;\;\mbox{for all }r\geq 0,\;1\leq i\leq n.

Obviously
u=v_{n}
is the desired solution to
\Delta^{n}u=f
. ∎

Remark 5.3
.

The above proof yields a more precise estimate at infinity:
|u(r)|\leq Cr^{-\varepsilon}M_{f,\varepsilon}
for all
r\geq 1
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
. Let

\displaystyle w(r)=-\frac{1}{2n-2}\int_{r}^{\infty}h_{\beta}(s)sds-\frac{1}{2n-2}r^{2-2n}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2n-1}h_{\beta}(s)ds=:-\frac{1}{2n-2}(J_{1}+J_{2}).

We have, for
r\geq 1
,

J_{1}(r)=\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{1-\beta}ds=\frac{1}{\beta-2}r^{2-\beta}

and

\displaystyle J_{2}(r)=r^{2-2n}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2n-1}h_{\beta}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2-2n}}{2n}+r^{2-2n}\int_{1}^{r}s^{2n-1-\beta}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2-2n}}{2n}+\frac{1}{2n-\beta}r^{2-\beta}

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{2-\beta}.

For
r\in[0,1]
, there holds
|w(r)|\leq\|w\|_{\infty}\leq C\|h_{\beta}(s)s\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}.
So we are done.∎

By the second line of (
5.2
), we see that

\displaystyle v_{1}(r)=\frac{1}{2n-2}\int_{r}^{\infty}sf(s)\Big[\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{2n-2}-1\Big]ds

which yields another simpler fact.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that
f\in\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
is radial and
f
has constant sign for
r\geq r_{0}>0
. Then the unique ground state solution to
\Delta v=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
has the same sign of
f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\backslash B_{r_{0}}
.

By working more carefully, we get the following result for
f
having less integrability assumption, which has its own interests.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Assume that
f\in\dot{L}_{\rm rad}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
f(\ln(2+|x|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, and
sf(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. Then there exists a unique
u\in c_{0,{\rm rad}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})

\Delta^{n}u=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, and there is

depending only on

such that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\infty}\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big[s+s^{2n-1}\ln(2+|s|)\Big]|f(s)|ds.

Proof. For any radial function
g\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, we know that if
sg(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, then
\Delta\Psi(g)=g
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
where

(5.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi(g)(r)&:=-\frac{1}{2n-2}\int_{r}^{\infty}sg(s)ds-\frac{1}{2n-2}r^{2-2n}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2n-1}g(s)ds,\quad\forall\;r>0.\end{split}

Let
f
be radial such that
(s+s^{2n-1})f(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. We define for any
1\leq\ell\leq 2n-1
,
k\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
r>0
,

\displaystyle H_{k,\ell}(r)=r^{k}\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{\ell}f(s)ds,\quad\mbox{and}\quad G_{k,\ell}(r)=r^{k}\int_{0}^{r}s^{\ell}f(s)ds.

The rest of proof is based on the following lemma which can be proved by direct calculations.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
(s+s^{2n-1})f(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
.

(i)

If
k>-2n
,
1\leq\ell\leq 2n-1
,
1\leq k+\ell+2\leq 2n-1
, there holds
H_{k,\ell}\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
sH_{k,\ell}(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and

\displaystyle\Psi(H_{k,\ell})\in{\rm Vect}\Big\{H_{0,k+\ell+2};\;H_{k+2,\ell};\;G_{2-2n,k+\ell+2n}\Big\}.

(ii)

If
-2n<k<-2
,
1\leq k+\ell+2\leq 2n-1
, there holds
G_{k,\ell}\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
sG_{k,\ell}(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and

\displaystyle\Psi(G_{k,\ell})\in{\rm Vect}\Big\{H_{0,k+\ell+2};\;G_{k+2,\ell};\;G_{2-2n,k+\ell+2n}\Big\}.

Consider now a continuous radial function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\ln(2+|x|)dx)\cap\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. Define
v_{1}=\Psi(f)
, then

\displaystyle v_{1}=-\frac{1}{2n-2}H_{0,1}-\frac{1}{2n-2}G_{2-2n,2n-1}=-\frac{1}{2n-2}H_{0,1}+\frac{1}{2n-2}H_{2-2n,2n-1}.

Using Lemmas
5.6
(i), we have
v_{2}=\Psi(v_{1})
is well defined if

and

\displaystyle v_{2}\in{\rm Vect}\Big\{H_{0,3};\;H_{2,1};\;H_{4-2n,2n-1};\;G_{2-2n,1+2n}\Big\}.

More generally, let
v_{k}=\Psi(v_{k-1})
for

that they are well defined, and for any

belongs to

\displaystyle{\rm Vect}\Big\{H_{2i,2k-2i-1};\;i\leq k-1\Big\}\oplus{\rm Vect}\Big\{H_{2k-2n,2n-1}\Big\}\oplus{\rm Vect}\Big\{G_{2i-2n,2n+2k-2i-1};\;1\leq i\leq k-1\Big\}.

In particular,

\displaystyle v_{n-1}=a_{n}H_{-2,2n-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i,n}H_{2i,2n-2i-3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}c_{i,n}G_{2i-2n,4n-2i-3}=:a_{n}H_{-2,2n-1}+J.

Here
a_{n}
,
b_{i,n}
and
c_{i,n}
are universal constants depending on
i
and
n
. Applying again Lemma
5.6
,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}s|J(s)|ds\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(s+s^{2n-1}\right)|f(s)|ds.

It remains to study
H_{-2,2n-1}
. Recall that
f\in\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, so
H_{-2,2n-1}=-G_{-2,2n-1}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}s|H_{-2,2n-1}(s)|ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s|G_{-2,2n-1}(s)|ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}s|H_{-2,2n-1}(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{2n-1}|f(\sigma)|d\sigma+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s}\int_{s}^{\infty}\sigma^{2n-1}|f(\sigma)|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma^{2n-1}|f(\sigma)||\ln\sigma|d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\sigma|f(\sigma)|d\sigma+C\int_{1}^{\infty}\sigma^{2n-1}|f(\sigma)||\ln\sigma|d\sigma.

To conclude,
sv_{n-1}(s)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and
v_{n-1}\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, there exists then a unique
u\in c_{0,{\rm rad}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
\Delta u=v_{n-1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Moreover,
\|u\|_{\infty}\leq C\|sv_{n-1}(s)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}
, so we are done. ∎

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

Fix
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{\rm rad}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that

for

. Clearly,

Let

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},(1+|x|)^{\varepsilon}dx)\cap\dot{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}),

\displaystyle r^{2n-1}{\widehat{w}}^{\prime}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}s^{2n-1}\Delta{\widehat{w}}(s)ds\leq C+C\int_{1}^{r}s^{2n-5}ds\leq C+Cr^{2n-3}.

Hence
{\widehat{w}}^{\prime}(r)\leq Cr^{-2}
for
r\geq 1
, which yields
{\widehat{w}}(r)\leq C
for
r\geq 1
.

To conclude,
{\widehat{w}}
is uniformly upper bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, so
e^{2n\widehat{w}}
is uniformly bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. It’s easy to see that
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq C
, as

w=t\Phi(w)={\mathbb{T}}\Big(Ke^{2n\alpha u_{0}+2n{\widehat{w}}}-t\alpha(-\Delta)^{n}u_{0}\Big).

We can apply for example (
5.1
). By Leray-Schauder’s argument, there exists a fixed point
w
for
\Phi
, hence the desired solution. ∎
