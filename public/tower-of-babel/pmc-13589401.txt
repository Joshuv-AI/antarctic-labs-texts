Biallelic Pathogenic Variants in PYGM Impair Retinal Glycogenolysis Causing a Range of Phenotypes

ABSTRACT
PurposeBiallelic pathogenic variants in PYGM (glycogen myophosphorylase) cause glycogen storage disease V (GSDV), a skeletal muscle disorder with reported association with hereditary macular dystrophy (HMD). This study investigated the role of PYGM in HMD and broader retinal disease through clinical and functional analyses.MethodsAn autosomal recessive HMD family underwent phenotyping and genome sequencing. Separately, data from 65 patients with GSDV (mean age = 51.7 years) were analyzed for retinal involvement. Immunohistochemistry, RT-qPCR and translating ribosome affinity purification assays assessed retinal expression of glycogen phosphorylase (GP) isoenzymes. Glycogenolysis was studied in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE-1) cells. Retinal sections from PygmR50*/R50* mice were also examined.ResultsGenome sequencing identified a homozygous pathogenic stop-gain variant in PYGM (NM_005609.4:c.148C > T; p.[Arg50*]) in the proband and affected sibling, and re-phenotyping revealed features of GSDV. Retinal involvement was identified in 30 of 65 GSDV cases (23 bilateral), with higher prevalence in participants >60 years (approximately 2.6-fold) and men (approximately 2-fold). PYGM expression was localized to Müller glia and photoreceptors. Cell-type-specific GP isoenzyme expression was found in mouse retina and RPE-1 cells. RPE-1 cells demonstrated glycogenolysis. PygmR50*/R50* mice lacked glycogen accumulation but exhibited photoreceptor outer segment thinning consistent with Pygm being the predominant isoform expressed in mouse photoreceptors.ConclusionsGSDV is moderately associated with retinopathy, and may present as non-syndromic HMD, warranting retinal monitoring for patients, and the inclusion of PYGM in retinal dystrophy gene panels. Redundancy in retinal glycogenolysis may account for lower penetrance and higher age-related prevalence in patients. Photoreceptor thinning observed in PygmR50*/R50* mice strengthens the association between HMD and GSDV in humans.

Hereditary macular dystrophies (HMDs) are a subgroup of inherited retinal disorders (IRDs) characterized by macular photoreceptor and pigment epithelial atrophy causing irreversible vision loss.1 Common monogenic forms of HMD include Stargardt macular dystrophy, Best disease, pattern dystrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and autosomal dominant drusen.2 HMDs harbor a complex genetic landscape, involving over 30 genes with diverse patterns of inheritance, including autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked, and mitochondrial.3–8 These HMD genes encode proteins expressed in several retinal cell types and are known to facilitate an array of cellular functions, including ciliation, phototransduction, and lipid metabolism.9

HMDs may be restricted to the retina (non-syndromic) or manifest as part of a disorder affecting other systems (syndromic). Pathogenic variants in genes, such as ABCA4, PRPH2, PROM1, BEST1, TIMP3, and RP1L1, are commonly associated with non-syndromic HMD.2,3 However, other genes, such as MFSD810 and MMACHC,11 have been implicated in both syndromic and non-syndromic HMD.12 Nonetheless, missing heritability is high in HMDs; at present, the causative genetic variant can be identified in only approximately 36% to 67% of cases using panel-based testing and/or exome sequencing (ES).13–15 Thus, unsolved cases of HMD may represent diseases resulting from non-coding variants in known genes not detectable by ES or panel-gene testing16,17 or rare/novel gene-disease associations.

Biallelic variants in PYGM (muscle glycogen phosphorylase), a gene responsible for the breakdown of intracellular glycogen stores into glucose monomers, cause glycogen storage disease V (GSDV or McArdle disease), an autosomal recessive myopathy characterized by muscle cramps, elevated serum creatine kinase, and, occasionally, rhabdomyolysis.18,19 In some instances, a reticular form of pattern dystrophy primarily affecting the RPE (representing a syndromic sub-type of HMD) has been associated with GSDV.20–27

This study aimed to characterize the genetic cause of presumed non-syndromic HMD in a family using genome sequencing (GS). Following the identification of a pathogenic homozygous stop gain variant in PYGM (NM_005609.4: c.148C > T; p.[Arg50*]) in two affected family members, clinical re-phenotyping established a diagnosis of GSDV. Hypothesizing that retinal involvement represents an under-recognized manifestation of GSDV, we subsequently investigated 65 individuals with GSDV and identified retinopathy in 30 of them. As prior reports have only studied PYGM expression in the RPE,28 we characterized its expression pattern in other retinal layers to assess if it had a role in glycogenolysis elsewhere in the retina. Additionally, as the mechanism underlying the incomplete retinal penetrance in GSDV is unknown, we studied the relative expression of glycogen phosphorylase (GP) isoenzymes in human RPE-1 cells and wild-type mice retina to explore whether isoenzymes could contribute to this phenomenon. Finally, by assessing glycogenolysis in RPE-1 cells and investigating retinal involvement in a knock-in mouse model (PygmR50*/R50*),29 we propose a mechanistic rationale for PYGM involvement in HMD.

Methods

Analysis of Proband and Family

Study Approval and Participant Recruitment. This project was approved by the Research Ethics Board at the Hospital for Sick Children (HSC), Toronto, Canada (REB 1000005895) and complied with the Tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The proband and available family members (n = 7) were recruited through the ocular genetics program (at HSC) after obtaining informed consent (Fig. 1). DNA was extracted from blood or saliva samples in all participants.

Pedigree structure and segregation results for the PYGM candidate variant. The genotype at the PYGM locus is listed for each recruited family member. Only the affected individuals as well as a sibling who declined an eye examination (II-5) show the candidate variant in a homozygous state.

Clinical Evaluation of Participants. The proband (II-6) and affected sibling (II-3) underwent detailed ocular phenotyping, including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement, fundus photography, fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), full-field electroretinogram30 (ERG), pattern electroretinogram31 (PERG), and electrooculogram32 (EOG).

Preliminary Genetic Testing . Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) based gene-panel testing for IRDs (261 genes; Emory Genetics Laboratory and Fulgent) including deletion duplication analysis (120 genes) was performed in II-6, followed by mitochondrial genome analysis (London Health Sciences Center). Supplementary Table S1 lists all genes tested in the proband.

Genome Sequencing and Genetic Analysis. Genomic DNA from II-6 (proband) and II-3 (affected sibling) underwent paired-end GS using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (40× coverage). Reads were mapped against the NCBI GRCh37 (human b37, hg19/2009) reference genome. Variant calling and quality scores were performed using GATK (version 3.7). Structural variants, indels, and single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were annotated using a filtration pipeline developed at The Centre for Applied Genomics (TCAG, HSC, Toronto, Canada) using ANNOVAR (Supplementary Fig. S1). Copy number variants were called using CNVnator version 1.3.0 and ERDS version 1.1. Structural variants were called by Manta (version 0.29.6). SNVs were annotated using the TCAG Small Variant Annotation pipeline version 27.1. Transposable elements were detected with MELT version 2.2.0.

Variant filtering and analysis of the genomes was performed using an in-house pipeline. Variants in exonic, intronic, and intergenic regions with a frequency below 0.5% in 1000 genomes (version 2015) and gnomAD (version 2.1.1) control databases were prioritized. Variants not shared between II-3 and II-6 were excluded. Given the suspected recessive mode of inheritance, only genes carrying two or more variants were retained. All remaining variants were then manually validated using the original BAM files. Among those validated, only those predicted functionally significant from at least two of six in silico tools were kept (tools and thresholds of significance: Polyphen ≐ 1,33 Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT) ≤0.05,34 Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) phred ≥15,35 phyloP Mammalian and Vertebrate ≥4,36 and spliceAI ≥0.2).37 All candidate variants were then subject to segregation analysis, and noncoding candidate variants were further analyzed for overlap with retinal and RPE-specific cis-regulatory elements using publicly available datasets38 (Supplementary Methods i).

Retinal Evaluation in GSDV Cohort

Data from 65 participants with confirmed GSDV (41 men, 24 women; age range = 26–80 years) from 3 participating institutions based in Canada (Toronto, n = 19) and the United Kingdom (London/Leeds, n = 46) were analyzed, retrospectively. The study conformed with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and with local research ethics board approvals (HSC [REB 1000017804]; Moorfields Eye Hospital [MEH]; and the Northwest London Research Ethics Committee). All patients carried at least two variants in PYGM and/or had a muscle biopsy-proven diagnosis of GSDV and were referred from metabolic clinics to these tertiary eye clinics for ocular assessments. Notably, none of the patients examined in Toronto had any history or diagnosis of retinal involvement. Most participants examined in Toronto had undergone ERG (n = 17) and EOG (n = 16) testing according to International Standards.30,32 Demographic details, BCVA, widefield color fundus and FAF imaging, OCT, and genetic details were collected. Genetic information and limited details on eye examination in a subset of patients from the UK cohort were previously reported.39 None of the 65 patients in the cohort underwent gene-panel testing to exclude any alternate cause for IRDs.

All retinal images were centrally analyzed at the HSC. We stratified retinal involvement on a per-eye basis into four types (types 1–3 = affected and type 4 = unaffected) based on changes seen in fundus photography, FAF, and OCT examinations (Table 1). Macular involvement was defined as any change noted within the supero- and infero-temporal arcades (vertically), and an approximately 9.5-mm diameter horizontally centered at the fovea (twice the distance between fovea and optic disc); this is in line with what clinicians would commonly refer to as the macula rather than an anatomic definition.40 Any other retinal area involvement was categorized as extramacular. Two ophthalmologists (authors A.T. and D.P.) reviewed and graded the images separately; any discordances in classification were resolved by an IRD specialist (author A.V).

Genetic variants were re-interpreted in accordance with the latest American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.41 Furthermore, variants were classified as having predicted severe (stop-gain, frameshift, and splicing) or mild (missense and in-frame deletions) effects to assess correlations between variant severity and retinal involvement.

Exploratory statistical analyses were performed to evaluate clinical, demographic, and genotypic associations within the GSDV cohort. Specifically, we conducted 2-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) to assess the effects of retinal phenotype class and eye on age; retinal phenotype class and eye on BCVA; retinal phenotype class and the presence/absence of retinal involvement on age; and variant severity and eye on BCVA. Post hoc analysis was performed using the Tukey-Kramer test for pairwise comparisons. One-way ANOVAs were performed to compare the variables of age on sex, age on retinal involvement, the number of p.(Arg50*) alleles on retinal phenotype class, and the number of p.(Arg50*) alleles on retinal involvement. Chi square correlations were performed to evaluate retinal phenotypic differences with sex, variant severity, and age, and for variant severity on age; age was categorized by decade for analysis (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80–89 years). Last, point biserial correlations (rpb) were performed to evaluate the relationship between retinal involvement (“yes” or “no”) and age, as well as retinal involvement and the number of p.(Arg50*) alleles for each participant. The threshold for statistical significance was set to ≤ 0.05 for all tests. All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB version 9.11 (R2021b) with the Statistics and Machine Learning and Wavelet toolboxes. A summary of statistical analyses is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Functional Studies

Human Retina Immunohistochemistry. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on a fresh human donor eye, as previously described.42 Retinal cryosections were labeled for cone arrestin, rhodopsin, and PYGM, and imaged at 40× magnification (Supplementary Methods ii). The human tissue experiments complied with the guidelines of the ARVO Best Practices for Using Human Eye Tissue in Research.

Retinal Expression of GP Isoenzymes. GP isoenzyme expression in the RPE was assessed using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and the Translating Ribosome Affinity Purification (TRAP) assay. For RT-qPCR, RNA was isolated from hTERT RPE-1 cells and expression of PYGM, PYGB, and PYGL was quantified. The TRAP assay was conducted using the RiboTag mouse model, which contains a floxed Rpl22 allele encoding a ribosomal protein fused to a triple hemagglutinin (HA) tag, enabling cell-type–specific analysis of actively translated mRNAs. When crossed with a Cre-expressing line, HA-tagged ribosomes are selectively expressed in Cre-positive cells. Immunoprecipitation of polyribosomes with an HA antibody isolates ribosome-associated transcripts from these cells, thereby enriching for mRNAs undergoing active translation. RPE-Cre (Best1), rhodopsin-Cre (rod), human red/green opsin-Cre (cone), and Müller cells-Cre (Rax) mice were crossed with floxed Rpl22 mice43; subsequently, RT-qPCR targeting the three GP isoenzymes from the resulting litters was performed to study cell-specific expression (Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Methods iii). All experiment protocols were conducted in compliance to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Glycogen and Glycogenolysis Assay. Glycogen levels in hTERT RPE-1 cell lines were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (MyBioSource, cat# MBS729453) following the manufacturer's instructions. Glycogenolysis was induced in hTERT RPE-1 cells maintained in glucose-free medium until reaching 80% confluence. Glycogen breakdown marked by the release of glucose into the media was then measured over time. Retinal glycogen accumulation in a knock-in mouse model of GSDV (PygmR50*/R50*) and wild-type control was evaluated using Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) and PAS-diastase staining of retinae, followed by imaging and analysis of outer-segment to inner-segment ratios (Supplementary Methods iv, v).

Results

Index Family

Clinical Phenotype of HMD With Peripheral Reticular Changes

Proband

A 57-year-old man presented with a 10-year history of progressive vision loss. His BCVA was 0.4 and 0.0 LogMAR in the right eye (RE) and the left eye (LE), respectively. Fundus evaluation showed widespread reticular pigmentary changes in the mid and far periphery, patchy macular chorioretinal atrophy, and marked peripapillary atrophy (type 1 retinopathy) in both eyes (Supplementary Fig. S2). FAF showed hypo-autofluorescence corresponding to the regions of retinal atrophy, and the reticular changes had a predominant hyper-autofluorescent aspect (Figs. 2a, 2b). The RE OCT showed subfoveal and parafoveal loss of external limiting membrane (ELM), ellipsoid zone (EZ), and RPE (Fig. 2c). The LE OCT showed mild disruptions of EZ and ELM parafoveally (Fig. 2d).

Widefield fundus autofluorescence (AF) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) of proband (II-6) and elder sibling (II-3). At baseline, the proband (II-6, 57 years) showed bilateral (a, b) macular, peripapillary, and nasal hypo-autofluorescent areas suggestive of chorioretinal atrophy with hyper- and hypo-autofluorescent reticular patterns throughout the fundus. Macular OCT of the right eye (c) showed foveal thinning with loss of the outer retinal layers from outer nuclear layer (ONL) to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), whereas the left eye (d) showed subfoveal preservation of the ONL, ellipsoid zone (EZ), and RPE, with mild parafoveal EZ disruption. At follow-up (II-6, 64 years), AF showed enlargement of hypo-autofluorescent areas in both eyes (e, f), with macular OCT indicative of progressive retino-choroidal thinning, further loss of outer retinal layers (g, h), and pronounced thinning of ONL, EZ, and RPE. The elder sibling (II-3, 70 years) showed macular, peripapillary, and nasal hypo-autofluorescent areas with diffuse reticular hyper- and hypo-autofluorescent changes (i, j). OCT macula of both eyes (k, l) showed prominent foveal thinning with near total loss of the outer retinal layers with mild preservation of the subfoveal EZ in the left eye (l) and bilateral choroidal thinning.

At 7-year follow-up, BCVA declined to 1.1 and 0.18 LogMAR in the RE and LE, respectively. There was a notable increase in scalloped chorioretinal atrophy (RE > LE) which was more evident on FAF imaging (Figs. 2e, 2f). OCT showed progressive outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning and total/near-total loss of ELM, EZ, and RPE (Figs. 2g, 2h). The ERG showed mild to moderate generalized rod and cone photoreceptor dysfunction, and the PERG P50 component to standard and large field stimuli were nondetectable, consistent with widespread macular dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. S3a). The EOG light peak to dark trough ratio was normal, revealing no evidence of generalized RPE dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. S3b).

Proband's Sibling

A 70-year-old woman with BCVA of 1.0 and 0.1 LogMAR, in the RE and LE, respectively, had type 1 retinopathy with reticular changes and scalloped chorioretinal atrophy similar to the proband. FAF showed hypo-autofluorescent atrophic patches and predominantly hyper-autofluorescent reticular changes (Figs. 2i, 2j). OCT showed foveal thinning, reduced ONL thickness, and total loss of ELM and EZ, with some sparing of EZ subfoveally in the LE (Figs. 2k, 2l). The ERG responses were mildly reduced and delayed, and the PERG was undetectable, indicating generalized rod and cone system dysfunction with macular involvement. The EOG light peak to dark trough ratio was within normal limits, revealing no evidence of generalized RPE dysfunction (see Supplementary Figs. S3a, S3b). Retinal images obtained at 60 years of age showed less severe chorioretinal atrophy and a better-preserved retinal lamination (see Supplementary Fig. S2).

Genome Sequencing Analysis Identifies a Homozygous Pathogenic Variant in PYGM

We identified 341 variants shared between II-6 and II-3 through GS analysis. Of these, 75 genes showed at least 2 variants. Following BAM validation and assessment of functional significance, six candidate variants remained in three genes: PYGM, HDAC9, and LRMDA (Supplementary Table S4). Three intronic variants were identified in HDAC9, with segregation analysis showing that one HDAC9 variant was in trans with the remaining two HDAC9 variants (Supplementary Fig. S4). The two LRMDA variants were found in trans (see Supplementary Fig. S4). One LRMDA variant was located near a weak RPE H3K27ac signal; however, this was not accompanied by corresponding ATAC-seq or CTCF peaks. Furthermore, the second LRMDA variant did not overlap any cis-regulatory signal. All three HDAC9 variants lacked supporting retinal/RPE cis-regulatory signal. Therefore, the HDAC9 and LRMDA variants were deprioritized. It is also noted that HDAC9 has not previously been implicated in retinal pathology,44 whereas LRMDA is associated with an unrelated ophthalmological phenotype, oculocutaneous albinism.45

In PYGM, a ClinVar pathogenic stop-gain SNV (NM_005609.4:c.148C > T; p.[Arg50*]; ClinVar ID: 2298) was identified in a homozygous state in both patients. Segregation analysis revealed that participant II-5 (see Fig. 1) also harbored the variant in homozygous state, but he declined an eye examination. This variant has been reported in gnomAD version 4.1.0 (allele frequency of 0.002837, including 12 homozygotes).46 This variant leads to premature termination and loss of function of the encoded protein, glycogen myophosphorylase.

Re-Phenotyping of Affected Sibship Confirms a Diagnosis of GSDV

The proband had a history of muscle cramps and difficulty matching the walking pace of peers from schooling years. His creatine kinase levels were elevated to 611 U/L (Reference range = 44–275 U/L). II-3 had a history suggestive of myoglobinuria and similarly elevated creatine kinase levels (661 U/L). In view of these findings along with retinopathy, their diagnosis was revised to GSDV with ocular involvement due to biallelic variants in PYGM. They were then referred to a metabolic specialist for ongoing care.

GSDV Patients Show Signs of Retinopathy

Sixty-five participants (41 men and 24 women) with GSDV underwent an eye examination. Ages at examination ranged from 26 to 80 years (mean ± SD = 51.69 ± 14.46 years) which was not significantly different between men (range = 26–80 years, mean ± SD = 53.39 ± 15.26) and women (range = 28–75 years, mean ± SD = 48.79 ± 12.72, P = 0.22). Thirty participants (46%) showed evidence of retinopathy, including 23 with bilateral involvement. Among participants with bilateral involvement, the majority had symmetric retinal staging (18/23; 78.3%). Among patients who had symmetrical retinal staging, age did not differ meaningfully across retinal phenotype groups. However, retinal involvement was associated with age category when age was grouped per decade (χ2 = 15.09, P = 0.02), such that participants with retinal involvement (58.23 ± 2.62 years) were older than participants without (46.06 ± 2.05 years, F(63) = 13.82, P < 0.01). Notably, 80% of participants >60 years had retinal involvement in at least 1 eye (16/20); and 15 of them had bilateral involvement. Men (23/41) were more frequently affected than women (7/24, χ2 = 4.418, P = 0.036).

Overall, there was an effect of the type of retinal phenotype on BCVA when analyzed per eye (F(3, 183) = 8.17, P < 0.001; see Supplementary Table S2). Thirty-four eyes had type 1 retinopathy with an average BCVA of 0.18 LogMAR (range = −0.1 to 1.8; Supplementary Table S5). Twelve eyes had type 3 retinopathy and an average BCVA of 0.14 LogMAR (range = −0.1 to 0.6). Seven eyes had type 2 retinopathy (extramacular), whereas 77 eyes had no retinal involvement (type 4); these eyes had an average LogMAR BCVA of 0.033 and 0.029, respectively.

Macular involvement in type 1 retinopathy consisted of yellow deposits and/or reticular pigmentary changes mostly at the RPE (pattern dystrophy). This led to (i) small hypo- or hyper-autofluorescent dots on FAF imaging, more common in the younger affected participants, (ii) predominantly hyper-autofluorescent reticular pattern more evident in older affected participants (the youngest reported case was 59 years, case 43, RE), or (iii) marked scalloped chorioretinal atrophy (only seen in RE of case 64; a 79-year-old subject; Fig. 3). Peripheral involvement in type 1 cases included pigmentary changes in a reticular pattern on fundus photography and FAF imaging (see Fig. 3). Macular involvement in type 3 retinopathy was similar to type 1 cases with reticular hyper-autofluorescent pattern seen in older affected (Fig. 4). Among eyes with type 2 retinopathy, reticular pigmentary patterned (2a) appearance in the extramacular region was seen in 10 of 12. In the two remaining eyes, linear hypopigmented streaks (2b) that hyper-autofluoresced were observed (RE of cases 15 and 46; Fig. 5). ERGs (n = 17) and EOGs (n = 16) were normal in all those tested in our cohort, excluding generalized retinal and RPE dysfunction, respectively.

All except one participant (case 55; MEH 10035) had at least 2 variants identified in PYGM; for case 55, the diagnosis of GSDV was supported by a muscle biopsy. In total, 29 unique alleles were detected and classified based on updated ACMG criteria: 17 pathogenic and 12 likely pathogenic (Table 2; ACMG classification criteria for the pathogenicity of each allele are provided in Supplementary Table S6). One in-frame deletion, 6 stop-gain, 7 frameshift, and 15 missense variants were identified. The p.(Arg50*) variant was the most prevalent: 81 alleles in 49 participants (including 32 homozygous). The next most common variants were p.(Gly205Ser) (9 alleles), p.(Lys754Asnfs*49) (5 alleles), p.(Arg576*) (4 alleles), and p.(Gln567Pro) (4 alleles). The severity of the alleles did not correlate with retinal involvement (χ2 = 1.78, P = 0.41). Of 42 participants who had 2 severe variants, 21 (50%) showed retinal involvement (18 bilateral); mean age of unaffected participants was 46.52 years and 58.76 years for those affected. Seven of the 17 participants (41%) who had one severe and one mild allele, demonstrated retinopathy (mean age of unaffected = 46.9 years; mean age of affected = 53.14 years). Only 1 of the 5 participants with 2 mild variants had retinopathy at the age of 76 years (mean age of unaffected = 41.5 years).

Glycogen Phosphorylases are Expressed in Human and Mouse Retina

PYGM is expressed in the human RPE, but its expression in other retinal cell types remains unknown.28 Thus, we immunostained the retina with the PYGM antibody, which showed immunoreactivity spanning the ELM and nerve fiber layers, suggesting PYGM expression in Müller cells (Fig. 6a, i). As we observed incomplete retinal penetrance in the GSDV cohort, we explored whether GP isoforms could contribute to this phenomenon, by assessing their retinal expression patterns. Glycogen phosphorylation is facilitated by three tissue-enriched isoforms encoded by distinct genes: PYGM (muscle), PYGL (liver), and PYGB (brain).47 Although these isoforms catalyze the same reaction, they differ in their tissue distribution and regulatory properties.48 We found that hTERT RPE-1 cell line showed mRNA expression of all three GP isoenzymes with PYGM being the least abundant (Fig. 6b). We used the TRAP assay to identify and quantify actively translating GP isoforms in murine RPE, rod, cone, and Müller cells (Figs. 6c, 6d). RT-qPCR analysis with cell-specific markers showed enrichment of Rpe65, Rhodopsin, S-cone opsin, and Glutamine synthetase in the mouse RPE, rods, cones, and Müller cells, respectively, suggesting the enrichment of cell-type-specific mRNA. In the RPE, Pygl demonstrated higher expression than Pygm (5-fold) and Pygb (12-fold). However, Pygm was the most abundantly expressed isoform in mouse rods and cones, compared with Pygb and Pygl. Comparison of GP isoforms between RPE and Müller cells shows that Pygb and Pygm are predominant in Müller cells, whereas Pygl is abundant in RPE. However, Pygm expression levels were comparable between RPE and Müller cells.

Glycogen in the hTERT RPE-1 Cell Line Breaks Down to Glucose Under Nutrient Deprivation

Under standard growth conditions (4.5 g/L of glucose), hTERT RPE-1 cells contain approximately 59.4 pg of glycogen per million cells (Fig. 6e). Upon nutrient deprivation, hTERT-RPE-1 cells released glucose over time, indicating the capacity to mobilize intracellular glycogen through glycogenolysis (Fig. 6f). The rate of glucose release increased during the early phase of starvation and plateaued after approximately 6 hours.

Discussion

This large and comprehensive study affirms the role of PYGM in syndromic HMD and retinopathy through clinical, genetic, and functional analyses. We demonstrate that GSDV can occasionally masquerade as non-syndromic HMD, which, in our pedigree, was re-classified as GSDV retinopathy following GS and re-phenotyping. Overall, a substantial proportion (nearly half) of participants within the GSDV cohort exhibited retinopathy, with a higher prevalence (80%) in those older than 60 years. In this retrospective cohort, men were nearly twice as frequently affected with retinopathy compared with women. This study is the first to define the spectrum of retinal phenotypes in GSDV, with concurrent macular and extramacular involvement (type 1) being the most prevalent pattern observed. Most participants with retinopathy (90%) had at least one loss-of-function allele, with p.(Arg50*) being the commonest. Nonetheless, older age rather than variant(s) severity was correlated with higher incidence of retinopathy. Further, we show for the first time that all three GP isoenzymes are present in human and mouse retinae, with their relative abundance differing by cell-type and species, and that human RPE-1 cell lines accumulate and metabolize glycogen to release glucose upon starvation. Last, PygmR50*/R50* mice showed photoreceptor thinning with no evidence of glycogen accumulation, suggesting that retinal damage likely arises from impaired glycogen utilization rather than overload toxicity.

The proband and sibling in our pedigree presented with widespread scalloped chorioretinal atrophy, and peripheral reticular pigmentary changes. Although the standard of care gene panel testing was negative, GS identified a pathogenic stop-gain variant in PYGM. Whereas the macular changes seen in the sibship were severe and atypical compared with previously described cases of GSDV retinopathy, the peripheral reticular changes were consistent with prior reports.20,22,24–26,49 This underscores the growing ability of NGS technologies, like GS, to facilitate the diagnosis and characterization of IRDs even when the gene-disease association is under-recognized.6,50 These platforms have permitted the recharacterization of established genetic mechanisms in IRDs, providing insight into metabolic disease genes previously thought to be exclusively associated with syndromic diseases.51 For example, variants in MFSD8, CLN3, HGSNAT, and SUMF1, previously associated to syndromic disorders with progressive retinal involvement, were recently shown to manifest as non-syndromic HMD or isolated retinal dystrophy.52–59 This report also highlights the importance of re-phenotyping in validating routine and unexpected genetic results,60 which facilitated accurate diagnosis and tailored GSDV management strategies in our sibship. It remains uncertain whether the third sibling who carried biallelic p.(Arg50*) variants and declined evaluation exhibits any ocular or systemic symptoms. Approximately 8% of patients with confirmed GSDV from a Spanish registry remain virtually asymptomatic13 and gnomAD reports homozygous individuals for the p.(Arg50*) allele. In addition, approximately 54% of patients in our cohort did not exhibit retinopathy at their last evaluation. Taken together, it is possible that the non-evaluated sibling has no symptoms or signs that presently require medical attention.

In our combined GSDV cohort, half showed retinal involvement. An earlier study characterizing systemic features reported retinal involvement in 15 of 41 examined participants (36.6%).39 The current study benefits from a larger sample size (n = 65) and also investigates subgroups by retinopathy type. Participants with retinal involvement were older (by an average of 12.2 years). Further, men in our cohort were nearly twice as likely to have retinopathy. Given that women with GSDV have an increased ability to utilize free fatty acids (estradiol dependent) as an energy source in skeletal muscles,61,62 we speculate that a similar mechanism, if present in the retina, could contribute to this effect. To this end, sex-related differences in disease expression have been reported in other HMDs; for instance, female over-representation has been described in milder ABCA4- and EFEMP1-associated diseases, whereas women are under-represented in autosomal dominant Best disease.63,64 Although these observations suggest that biological sex may act as a modifier of the HMD phenotype, the mechanisms underlying such effects remain incompletely understood and require further validation.

In our cohort, participants with macular involvement demonstrated a mild average reduction in visual acuity, with 6 older participants demonstrating a BCVA worse than 0.4 LogMAR (7 eyes; range = 63–79 years). The macular auto-fluorescence changes akin to pattern dystrophy were similar to prior reported cases of GSDV retinopathy.20–26 Further, we describe an early form of macular involvement presenting as isolated depositions that are hyper- or hypo-autofluorescent on imaging (5 eyes). Whereas the pattern of peripheral changes seen in our cohort is similar to prior reports,20–26 our study implicates isolated peripheral retinal changes (including linear hypopigmented streaks) as a possible manifestation of GSDV retinopathy seen in 10.8% of the cohort. In the present study, only the index proband and sibling presented as “presumed” non-syndromic HMD, whereas retinal involvement in the larger cohort was identified in those with an established diagnosis of GSDV; therefore, it remains uncertain how frequently patients with GSDV first present to ophthalmologists with retinopathy or whether biallelic PYGM variants can give rise to isolated retinal disease. Hence, its inclusion in retinal gene panels may facilitate further identification of retinal-first presentations (such as pattern retinal dystrophy or its phenocopies65–70), as many targeted IRD gene panels do not presently test for PYGM (Blueprint Genetics, Fulgent Genetics, and Prevention Genetics).

The two most prevalent alleles identified in this cohort, p.(Arg50*) and p.(Gly205Ser), were also the most frequently found in European GSDV cohorts.19,39 Intriguingly, no age-corrected genotype-phenotype correlation was found with regard to the severity of retinopathy. Earlier studies did not identify any genotype-phenotype correlation with regards to the systemic phenotype.19,39,71 One study reported most patients carrying missense variants in PYGM to not express any PYGM protein72; although that report only had one variant seen in our cohort, p.(Gly205Ser), it is plausible that some of the other missense variants we identified could produce a similar loss-of-function effect, explaining the lack of retinal genotype-phenotype correlation in our cohort.

We show that PYGM is expressed in the human Müller glia and photoreceptors. In RPE-1 cells, differential expression of all three GP isoenzymes was observed. Prior studies have reported PYGB expression in human cone photoreceptors,73 combined PYGB and PYGM expression in human RPE,28 and PYGM expression in ARPE-19 cells.28 Differences in origin and stability may account for the distinct expression pattern of GP isoenzymes between cell lines, with the RPE-1 line being more stable. To determine whether similar organizational patterns exist in vivo, we defined for the first time the expression profile of actively translating GP isoenzymes in the mouse retina using the TRAP assay. Prior rodent studies have only characterized the expression of Pygm protein in cones and Pygm transcript in the RPE.74–76 Our TRAP analysis found that akin to human RPE-1 cells, Pygl is the most abundant isoenzyme in the mouse RPE. In mouse photoreceptors, all GP isoenzymes were found; however, Pygm was the most abundant, with cones having approximately 20-fold more abundance than rods. Additionally, Müller cells expressed both Pygm and Pygb. Publicly available human retina single-nucleus RNA sequencing data and mouse retina single-cell RNA sequencing data also suggest differential expression of the three GP isoenzymes across a variety of retinal cell types77–79 (Supplementary Fig. S6). Taken together, these patterns suggest functional redundancy and cell or tissue-specific isoenzyme preference in human and mouse retinal glycogenolysis. Further, we speculate that such functional redundancy may contribute to the phenotypic variability and incomplete retinal penetrance observed in patients with GSDV. Similar expression patterns of muscle and brain GP isoforms have been reported in rodents and primates, suggesting that this potential redundancy may be preserved across other species as well.73,80 Alternatively, although multiple GP-isoenzymes are present within individual retinal cell types, their relative abundance may suggest specialized roles. The high expression of Pygm observed in mouse photoreceptors may relate to the general requirement for rapid glucose mobilization to sustain phototransduction and outer segment renewal.81 In contrast, enrichment of Pygl in the RPE may reflect distinct regulatory demands associated with its broader metabolic capacity. Müller cell expression of both Pygm and Pygb supports their role in maintaining metabolic support for retinal neurons. Further work is needed to characterize the biological function of the differing cell-specific abundances observed in mice and human retinae. Additional validation is also required to determine whether the proposed functional redundancy causes reduced retinal penetrance, given that modifier alleles, epistasis, and epigenetic changes may also contribute to this phenomenon.82–84

Our study also showed that RPE-1 cells accumulate glycogen and release glucose during nutrient deprivation. Although isoenzyme-specific contributions were not directly examined, these findings indicate that glycogenolysis contributes to a proportion of glucose availability in the RPE. Prior work demonstrating lysosomal glycogen degradation in RPE cells also suggests that cytosolic glycogenolysis may play a significant role.85 Together, these pathways likely provide metabolic resilience during periods of increased demand. Photoreceptors rely heavily upon glucose, whereas the RPE uses greater metabolic flexibility, including the capacity for oxidative phosphorylation and beta-oxidation.86 Under stress conditions, such as aging or disease, the RPE shifts toward increased glycolysis and glucose consumption.87 A similar increase in glucose dependence was also recently observed in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived PYGM-null RPE, limiting their ability to provide glucose to photoreceptors.88 Thus, RPE glycogen stores may serve as an important reserve to support both RPE and photoreceptor metabolism, the disruption of which could compromise the integrity of the outer retina. In patients with GSDV, retinal manifestations almost always begin in the RPE, further implicating that impaired RPE glycogenolysis precedes photoreceptor degeneration. Loss of PYGM may impair glucose availability during metabolic stress, particularly with advancing age, providing a plausible explanation for the increased prevalence of retinopathy in older participants.

Retinae from PygmR50*/R50* mice showed thinning of photoreceptor OS without a corresponding loss of ONL thickness, a described feature of retinal degeneration.89 This is consistent with our TRAP assay (used to quantify actively translating mRNA) that shows Pygm to be the predominant isoenzyme in mouse photoreceptors, the lack of which could potentially lead to cell damage. This pattern of OS thinning rather than photoreceptor loss could be consistent with altered membrane biogenesis, presenting an alternative pathomechanism. Compartmentalization of retinal metabolism supports this interpretation: glucose is largely spared by the RPE for photoreceptor metabolism, whereas lactate produced by photoreceptors is transported to the RPE and Müller cells to fuel mitochondrial metabolism.90 In addition, Müller cells incompletely oxidize glucose and divert it toward biosynthetic pathways, including serine synthesis.91 Serine serves as a precursor for key phospholipids required for membrane biogenesis.92 The expression of GP isoenzymes in the Müller cells and RPE suggests that glycogenolysis could support the serine biosynthetic pathway or the pentose phosphate pathway, important for lipid biosynthesis93 and redox balance, respectively.94 Further studies will be needed to directly test this relationship and define its relevance to retinal physiology and disease.

Although our method was not powered to detect RPE glycogen deposition, the absence of overt accumulation in other retinal layers suggests that remaining GP isoenzymes may partially compensate to prevent glycogen buildup. Nonetheless, chronic absence of Pygm alone leads to photoreceptor damage, thereby suggesting that isoenzymes cannot entirely compensate over time. The outer retinal changes noted in patients with GSDV retinopathy have some similarities to those observed in the mouse model, but differ from other glycogen storage-associated retinopathies, such as Pompe and Lafora diseases, which are characterized by prominent glycogen accumulation.95–98

Whereas our study provides novel clinical and mechanistic insights into GSDV-associated retinopathy, certain limitations warrant consideration. As the GSDV cohort was derived from cases referred to tertiary-eye care centers from metabolic specialists, the reported prevalence of retinal involvement may be influenced by ascertainment bias. We also note the exploratory nature of the statistical analyses performed in the cohort given that multivariable modeling was not conducted. Additionally, inter-eye correlation and confounders, including center-level variability and comorbidities, were not adjusted for. Therefore, the age- and sex-related observations presented in this study may vary across different cohorts. In addition, given that the GSDV cohort did not undergo retinal panel gene testing; although unlikely, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of alternate genetic causes for retinopathy. Last, whereas the use of the human RPE-1 cell line provides a largely faithful model to study relative GP isoenzyme abundance, future work adopting retinal organoids would provide a closer understanding of the relative abundance in photoreceptors and its role in retinopathy.

Conclusions

This study helps establish the spectrum, patterns, and prevalence of GSDV-associated retinopathy in humans. Our findings support the inclusion of PYGM in gene panel testing for HMDs and IRDs. Overall, increasing age and male sex rather than the genotype appeared to be the most important factors driving retinal involvement in this cohort. Central vision is affected when the macula is involved, yet generalized retinal function is typically normal unless there is significant involvement of retinal periphery in some older individuals. Functional redundancy in retinal glycogenolysis may underlie lower prevalence of retinopathy in younger participants; however, the mechanism underlying lower prevalence in women remains unknown. Although GP isoenzyme expression overlaps across retinal cell types, incomplete compensation following PYGM loss likely ultimately renders the outer retina vulnerable. Further studies are needed to clarify isoenzyme-specific functions in the RPE, photoreceptors, and Müller cells, and their contributions to retinal metabolic homeostasis.
