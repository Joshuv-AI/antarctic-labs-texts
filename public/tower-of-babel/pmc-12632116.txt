Analysis of factors influencing high infectivity in patients with tuberculosis using clinical and imaging methods
Authors: Jing Han, Wenlong Ding, Wanjie Yang, Zhiheng Xing, Xue Li, Yonghui Li, Yi Xie, Zhen Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo optimise early screening for hospitalised patients with highly contagious tuberculosis (TB) to allow for effective isolation measures to reduce the risk of TB transmission.MethodsData were collected from 9458 newly diagnosed patients with TB from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2023 at a designated TB hospital in Tianjin. The patients were divided into two groups based on their infection status: highly infectious and less infectious. Data, including clinical characteristics and computerised tomography (CT) examination results, were extracted from the hospital information system. High-resolution chest CT was performed using standardised protocols. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive model.ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed significant differences in sex, age ≥ 60, patient delay, and CT findings (cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, bronchial lesions, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, and pericardial effusion) between the two groups (P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression identified age ≥ 60 (odds ratio (OR) = 1.382), patient delay (OR = 1.830), cavitation (OR = 3.146), consolidation (OR = 2.077), tree-in-bud sign (OR = 2.764), and bronchial lesions (OR = 1.213) as independent risk factors for high infectiousness in patients with TB. The ROC curve analysis showed good discriminative ability, with an area under the curve of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.7–0.72).ConclusionsAge ≥ 60, patient delay, cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, and bronchial lesions can effectively predict the high infectiousness of patients with TB, aiding in early screening and isolation measures to reduce the risk of TB transmission within hospitals.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Infectivity, Clinical characteristics, CT examination, Prediction model

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and is primarily transmitted through airborne droplets. TB has remained a major global public health challenge in recent years, particularly in high-burden countries. World Health Organization data indicate approximately 10.8 million new TB cases globally in 2023, including an estimated 741,000 new cases in China, accounting for 6.8% of the global incidence and ranking third among the 30 high-TB-burden countries [1]. In a hospital environment, due to the large number of patients, high population, and high mobility, the risk of TB transmission is particularly pronounced.

Several factors contribute to the transmission and infectivity of TB within hospitals. First, the long growth cycle of M. tuberculosis and the complexity of diagnostic procedures often result in prolonged screening times, delaying the identification of infectious cases [2, 3]. Second, inadequate ventilation systems, ineffective isolation measures, and low awareness of protective measures among medical staff in hospitals may also exacerbate the spread of TB [4]. Third, the prevalence of multidrug-resistant TB and extensively drug-resistant TB further complicates hospital infection control, as these patients require longer diagnosis and treatment periods, increasing the risk of transmission [1]. Fourth, overcrowding and high patient turnover present additional challenges in allocating space for triage and isolation in low-income, high-TB-burden countries [5–7].

Although hospitals generally implement TB infection control measures—such as negative-pressure isolation rooms and personal protective equipment—these measures are usually initiated only after the diagnosis. Because early symptoms of TB are often non-specific and the diagnostic process is time-consuming, delays in diagnosis may prevent the timely isolation of highly infectious patients, thereby increasing the risk of transmission [8]. Research shows that the time from admission to isolation averaged 3.3 days for exposures, with a mean of 41 staff exposed per patient and approximately 38 person of resource [9]. Medical staff may become a source of hospital transmission after TB infection. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that when medical staff were used as indicator cases, the proportions of active TB in exposed infants, children, and adults were 0.11%, 0.38%, and 0.09%, respectively [10]. Therefore, rapid and reliable identification and isolation of suspected cases, along with timely initiation of treatment, are key measures to prevent the spread of TB [11].

Although some studies have explored imaging in patients with TB [12, 13], a lack of prediction models combining big-data-based clinical features with imaging results remains, hindering the identification of highly infectious patients. Therefore, an in-depth study of this topic holds significant theoretical and practical importance. Optimising early identification of highly contagious patients with TB within hospitals, enhancing the effectiveness of zoning management strategies for highly and less infectious patients, and implementing effective isolation measures can reduce the risk of TB transmission, protect the health and safety of medical staff and other patients, and provide important theoretical and practical insights for TB control.

Methods

Participants

From 1 January 2015–31 December 2023, 10,600 newly diagnosed patients with TB were registered at the outpatient and inpatient departments of the designated TB hospital in Tianjin. These patients were residents of Tianjin and surrounding provinces and cities. TB was diagnosed according to national and World Health Organization standards [14, 15], considering the patient’s medical history, clinical manifestations, and laboratory test results. For patients with confirmed TB, direct reporting, treatment, and management of the infectious disease network were carried out promptly in accordance with the “Law on the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases” and the “Implementation Guidelines for Tuberculosis Prevention and Control in China”.

The inclusion criterion for the study was patients aged ≥ 18 years at consultation. Exclusion criteria included patients < 18 years at admission, absence of computerised tomography (CT) examination results, extrapulmonary TB without lung parenchymal involvement, non-tuberculous mycobacterium infection, and patients who had already started anti-TB treatment (including those treated for less than 1 month) (Fig. 1). The final study included 9,458 patients.

Flow diagram for participant inclusion and exclusion

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Haihe Hospital (28 February, 2024; No. HHKT-002). Due to the study’s retrospective nature, written informed consent was not required.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital information systems. Standardised forms were used to collect the patients’ clinical characteristics, including age, sex, human immunodeficiency virus status, case classification, patients delay, haemoptysis, and CT examination results. The following imaging findings were obtained: cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, bronchiectasis/thickening/narrowing/twisting, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, linear opacity, pericardial effusion, and miliary nodules.

CT examination

The patient who was deemed in need of an imaging examination by the attending physician, underwent a high-resolution chest CT scan. The scans were conducted within 1 month of outpatient consultation or admission, using two spiral CT scanners (BrightSpeed, GE, USA, and Aquilion Prime 128, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan), following the same protocol. To ensure the accuracy of respiratory phase data collection, patients were instructed to hold their breath at the end of deep inhalation before the scan. The patients were positioned supine with their hands above their heads in a head-first, and a full-lung spiral CT scan was performed from the thoracic inlet to the bilateral costophrenic angles at the end of a deep inhalation. The CT scan parameters for the BrightSpeed were: tube voltage 120 kV, automatic tube current, scan speed 0.75 s/r, collimator width 16 × 0.625 mm, matrix 256 × 256, with reconstruction using standard and lung algorithms, reconstruction layer thickness 1.25 mm, and reconstruction interval 1.25 mm. The CT scan parameters for Aquilion Prime 128 were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; automatic tube current; scan speed, 0.5/s; collimator width, 64 × 0.5/mm; matrix, 512 × 512; reconstruction using FC 30 and FC 52 algorithms; reconstruction layer thickness of 1.0 mm, and reconstruction interval 0.8 mm. Two experienced radiologists evaluated the main areas and imaging findings and reached conclusions. If the conclusions were inconsistent, images were reviewed until consistent results were obtained.

Specimen collection

Sputum: After waking up in the morning, patients were instructed to rinse their mouths thoroughly with sterile saline and forcefully cough the second sputum into a sterile cup, or the sputum was collected using a suction catheter. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was collected from patients undergoing bronchoscopy. Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid: After nebulised inhalation of 2% lidocaine anaesthesia, fibreoptic bronchoscopy was performed, and multiple lavages with 0.9% sterile saline were administered to collect the lavage fluid. All the specimens were sent for testing within 1 h of collection.

All parameters were considered dichotomous. Based on the subsequently obtained microbiological results, patients were categorised into two groups according to their final infection status:

Group 1: Highly infectious patientsAccording to national and international recommendations, these patients had at least one microscopy-positive acid-fast bacillus (AFB+) stain in their sputum or bronchial lavage fluid during outpatient or inpatient care. These patients were considered highly infectious due to certain transmissible bacteria in their sputum.

Group 1: Highly infectious patients

According to national and international recommendations, these patients had at least one microscopy-positive acid-fast bacillus (AFB+) stain in their sputum or bronchial lavage fluid during outpatient or inpatient care. These patients were considered highly infectious due to certain transmissible bacteria in their sputum.

Group 2: Low infectious patientsAFB stain-negative patients at the time of admission to outpatient or inpatient care were considered to have low infectiousness, regardless of the culture results obtained weeks later [16].

Group 2: Low infectious patients

AFB stain-negative patients at the time of admission to outpatient or inpatient care were considered to have low infectiousness, regardless of the culture results obtained weeks later [16].

Statistical analysis

The normally distributed numerical variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between the two groups were performed using t-tests. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions are represented by the median (quartile, Q), and non-parametric tests were used to compare the two groups. Count data are expressed as n (%) and were compared using the χ2 test.

Stepwise backward selection of predictors in multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to establish the prediction model for TB contagiousness. Because the prediction model aims to achieve optimal predictive performance, all predictor variables with a significance level below 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were considered in the multivariate analysis. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to assess the risk of highly infectious. To evaluate the discriminative performance of the logistic model, the area under the ROC (AUC) curve was calculated by comparing the actual outcomes with those predicted by the model.

Results

Basic participant information

Among 9458 patients with TB, there were 2892 women (30.58%) and 6566 men (69.42%), aged 18–96 (49.81 ± 19.53) years. Among the hospitalised patients, 5626 (59.48%) initially screened positive and belonged to the highly infectious group. The remaining 3832 (45.52%) patients initially screened negative. All patients underwent CT examination, and the imaging results were analysed based on cavities, consolidations, tree-bud signs, bronchiectasis/thickening/stenosis/distortion, pleural effusion, lymph node enlargement, cord shadows, pericardial effusion, and miliary nodules. The number of patients with one or more positive imaging results was 6954, accounting for 73.53%. Among the patients with positive imaging results, 4629 patients (66.57%) were bacteria-positive, and 2325 (33.43%) were bacteria-negative.

Bivariate analysis results for high infectivity in patients with TB

Patients were grouped based on whether they were highly infectious, and a bivariate analysis was performed. The results showed that gender (P < 0.001), age ≥ 60 (P < 0.001), patient delay (P < 0.001), and CT findings including cavitation (P < 0.001), consolidation (P < 0.001), tree-in-bud sign (P < 0.001), bronchial lesions (P < 0.001), pleural effusion (P < 0.001), lymphadenopathy (P < 0.001), and pericardial effusion (P < 0.001) had statistically significant differences. Multivariate results of predictive indicators for high infectiousness in patients with TB

Using the level of patient infectiousness as the dependent variable (0 = no, 1 = yes), factors with P < 0.2, including sex, age ≥ 60, case classification, patient delay, haemoptysis, and CT findings (cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, bronchiectasis/thickening/narrowing/twisting, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, linear opacities, pericardial effusion), were included as independent variables in a multivariate unconditional logistic regression analysis. The results showed that age ≥ 60 (OR = 1.382), patient delay (OR = 1.830), cavitation (OR = 3.146), consolidation (OR = 2.077), tree-in-bud sign (OR = 2.764), and bronchiectasis/thickening/narrowing/twisting (OR = 1.213) were independent risk factors for high infectiousness in patients with TB (Table 2).

Discussion

Early identification and isolation of patients with highly infectious pulmonary TB can significantly reduce its spread. Combining CT imaging-related indicators may enhance the detection of highly infectious cases. Improving access to medical services and reducing treatment delays are critical steps in effectively managing TB.

This study retrospectively analysed the data of 9,458 patients with TB in Tianjin. The results indicated that age ≥ 60, patient delay, and CT findings of cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, and bronchiectasis/thickening/narrowing/twisting could be used as predictive indicators for high infectiousness in patients with TB. Based on these results, a predictive model was created to help healthcare workers better identify highly infectious patients. A study on TB epidemiology in the Russian Federation highlighted that to maintain a positive trend in reducing TB incidence and mortality, population coverage with screening should be at least 60% while maintaining screening in children using immunodiagnostic tests at a level of at least 90%. The possibility of revising the screening conditions through a more comprehensive examination in high-risk groups and symptomatic patients, including microscopy, molecular genetic methods, and computed tomography (CT) [17]. Although not suitable for early detection, CT plays a critical role in the higher-level diagnosis of existing lung changes, such as cavitation, consolidation, and bronchial abnormalities, which are associated with high infectiousness. Our study collected CT data, which is more sensitive than radiography for detecting parenchymal lesions and lymphadenopathy [18]; therefore, our predictive model may have higher sensitivity than previous predictive models based on X-ray images [19].

The study results indicate that elderly patients (age ≥ 60) are more likely to be highly infectious (AFB+). Due to declining immune system function and increased comorbidities, elderly patients have become a high-risk group for highly infectious TB. As people age, their immune responses weaken, making them more susceptible to TB infection and more likely to develop active TB [20, 21]. Additionally, older individuals often have multiple chronic diseases, such as diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cardiovascular diseases. These comorbidities not only increase the risk of TB infection but also exacerbate the condition, leading to higher infectiousness [22]. Studies have shown that the incidence and infectiousness of TB are significantly higher in older people compared to younger people [23]. Li K et al. [24] found that the incidence of severe pulmonary lesions is significantly higher in elderly patients (aged ≥ 60 years) (20.95%, 97/463). This finding is consistent with Righi’s study [25], which may be due to the prolonged disease course and repeated pathological changes in elderly patients. These factors often lead to the co-existence of new and old lesions, increase the possibility of disseminated TB, and result in more extensive CT findings. Elderly patients often have atypical or non-specific TB symptoms. In China’s fifth national tuberculosis prevalence survey, nearly half (48.8%) of confirmed TB cases are in elderly patients, 39.8% of whom have no symptoms, and 53.2% have not sought any medical care [26]. This atypical manifestation or failure to seek medical care may lead to patient delay [27]. Patients may remain infectious longer, increasing the risk of transmitting to others.

Patient delay is an important predictive indicator of high TB infectiousness. Symptoms that are atypical or masked by comorbidities can delay the diagnosis of TB. Patients with delayed diagnoses remain untreated for longer periods, thereby increasing their infectiousness through the spread of TB bacteria [28]. Patients with delayed treatment usually have more severe conditions [29], higher bacterial loads, and more extensive lesions, and an increased risk of cavitation, which facilitates TB transmission through coughing. Multiple studies have indicated that a patient delay is one of the main risk factors for TB transmission, especially among older adults [30].

Cavitation refers to cavities formed in the lung tissue owing to infection and destruction, usually containing many TB bacteria. The formation and presence of cavities increase the infectiousness of patients because these cavities can directly transmit TB bacteria through the respiratory tract. Studies have shown that cavitation is an independent predictor of high infectiousness in TB, with the number and volume of cavities positively correlated with the sputum smear positivity rate [16, 31, 32]. Consolidation refers to the density of lung tissue due to inflammation or infection, a common imaging finding in active TB. The concentration of TB bacteria was higher in areas of consolidation, making them more likely to spread through the airways. When consolidation involves multiple lung segments or lobes, the sputum smear-positivity rate increases significantly, indicating the presence of extensive and severe lesions [32]. The tree-in-bud sign is a characteristic imaging manifestation of M. tuberculosis spreading through the bronchi and is typical of active pulmonary TB. Markus U. also reported that the tree-in-bud sign best predicts high PTB infectivity (AFB+) [16]. Ko et al. found that as the AFB smear grade increases, the frequency and degree of the tree-in-bud sign also increase, further supporting its role as an infectious indicator [33].

Bronchiectasis, thickening, narrowing, and twisting are important CT manifestations of highly infectious TB. This is consistent with the research results of Jong Hun Kim et al. [13]. Fatih Ors et al. also reported that bronchial lesions may be a predictive factor for smear positivity in patients with PTB [34]. Bronchiectasis is associated with the destruction of the bronchial wall and the accumulation of infectious secretions, which can promote the release of M. tuberculosis into the airway [35]. At the same time, positive sputum cultures reflect a high number of M. tuberculosis and strong disease activity, which, in turn, promote bronchial lesions. Research has shown that patients with active pulmonary TB have a high co-existence rate of bronchial disease and lymphadenopathy [36]. Bronchial stenosis occurs in 10–40% of patients with active TB [37, 38] and can lead to segmental or lobar atelectasis, lobar hyperinflation, mucus impaction, and obstructive pneumonia, further increasing the risk of transmission.

Although this study provides an important basis for optimizing early identification and isolation measures for patients with highly infectious TB in hospitals, some limitations remain. Regardless of the culture results obtained several weeks later, patients with negative AFB upon admission or hospitalisation are considered to have low infectivity. This classification method may underestimate the transmission risk in some patients, especially when culture results are not yet available.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate that age ≥ 60 years, patient delay, cavitation, consolidation, tree-in-bud sign, and bronchial lesions, are independent risk factors for high infectiousness in patients with TB. These factors can potentially help improve the early identification of highly infectious TB patients and enhance patient management in hospitals. Furthermore, identifying specific risk factors, such as advanced age and patient delay, underscores the importance of targeted interventions for high-risk groups.

Although CT is unsuitable for early detection, it plays a critical role in diagnosing lung changes associated with high infectiousness, such as cavitation, consolidation, and bronchial abnormalities. In high-burden and resource-limited settings, our predictive model offers a cost-effective and scalable solution by enabling early risk assessment and targeted isolation of highly infectious patients. This approach reduces the burden on hospital resources and minimizes the risk of TB transmission within healthcare facilities.
