Parent and Caregiver Perspectives of Their Child's Transition From Paediatric to Adult Care: A Meta‐Ethnography
Authors: Anushka Patel, Hannah Rosales, Linda Nguyen, Karen Beattie, Michelle Batthish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe transition from paediatric to adult healthcare for adolescents with chronic conditions is a pivotal yet challenging phase for caregivers, given their crucial role in preparing their child for this transition. This process involves complex adjustments in roles, responsibilities and navigation of healthcare systems. To address these challenges, we synthesized the qualitative experiences of caregivers to better understand their perspectives and readiness during this transition process, highlighting the emotional, structural and systemic factors involved.MethodsA systematic review and meta‐synthesis were conducted using Noblit and Hare's meta‐ethnographic approach. Systematic searches were performed across PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL and PsycINFO. Data analyses involved extracting first‐ and second‐order constructs and synthesizing them into third‐order constructs, generating a novel conceptual framework that reflects experiences and needs of caregivers.ResultsThe database search yielded 9306 studies. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 23 qualitative studies were included. Analyses identified five key themes: (1) Caregiver Empowerment and Advocacy, where caregivers emphasized advocating for their needs within healthcare, employment and education systems; (2) Building Networks and Collaboration, highlighting the importance of collaborative networks for seamless transitions; (3) Emotional and Psychological Preparedness, indicating the need for emotional and psychological resources among caregivers; (4) Structural and Systemic Support, emphasizing the role of structured programs, communication technologies and cross‐sectoral support systems; and (5) Tailored Transition Plans and Professional Integration, where caregivers play a critical role in advocating for individualized plans that build skills and confidence in adolescents as they navigate healthcare and employment opportunities.ConclusionsThis meta‐ethnography emphasizes the multifaceted roles of caregivers during their child's healthcare transition. The findings highlight the need for structured support systems that empower caregivers, provide emotional resources and foster collaborative networks. Tailored transition plans addressing both the needs of adolescents and caregivers are essential. Further research should explore interventions that enhance caregiver readiness—the preparedness, confidence and knowledge caregivers need to support youth during transitions as well as the efficacy of transition support programs. Introduction

Over the past few decades, advancements in medical care have significantly improved the life expectancy and quality of life for adolescents with chronic conditions. However, the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare remains a substantial challenge, particularly for caregivers. The healthcare transition process involves moving from developmentally supportive, family‐centred care to adult healthcare systems that prioritize independence and self‐management. For caregivers, the transition represents not only a logistical shift but also an emotional journey, as they support their child's increasing independence while continuing to provide guidance as they navigate new healthcare systems and responsibilities. The decisions caregivers make during this period—including coordinating care across systems, advocating for resources and fostering adolescents' independence in managing their chronic condition—have significant implications for continuity of care, health outcomes and emotional well‐being.

Systemic barriers further complicate this transition. Fragmented communication between paediatric and adult care providers, the lack of standardized transition protocols and insufficient transition‐specific training for healthcare professionals can exacerbate the challenges caregivers face (Ames et al. 2023; Nguyen et al. 2023). Additionally, caregivers frequently report feeling ill‐prepared to support their child during this phase, citing inadequate guidance from healthcare providers and a lack of tailored resources (Lyons et al. 2014). Caregiver experiences during transition are also shaped by emotional factors. Feelings of anxiety, fear and uncertainty about their child's readiness for adult care and potential lapses in care coordination are common (Garvey et al. 2016). The emotional toll of these concerns is often compounded by the dual responsibility of supporting their child's autonomy while simultaneously ensuring safety and adherence to treatment plans (Garvey et al. 2016; Scal et al. 1999).

Despite the critical role caregivers have during healthcare transition, their perspectives remain comparatively underrepresented in transition care research. Much of the existing literature focuses primarily on adolescent readiness and clinical outcomes, with caregiver experiences often captured indirectly within clinician‐ or youth‐centred frameworks (Bemrich‐Stolz et al. 2015). As a result, caregiver‐specific needs, challenges and perspectives remain less consistently explored within the broader transition care evidence base. This gap highlights the need for a caregiver‐centred approach to transition care that accounts for their emotional, practical and systemic challenges (Bemrich‐Stolz et al. 2015).

Facilitators such as structured programs, transition coordinators and peer support networks have shown promise in easing these challenges (Ames et al. 2021). However, these interventions are not universally available and often fail to comprehensively address the unique needs of caregivers.

Understanding the lived experiences of caregivers is therefore crucial for identifying the specific challenges they face and the support they require. For instance, caregivers often report feeling overwhelmed by the need to navigate complex healthcare systems while also managing their child's emotional and developmental needs during this transition (Transition 2025). The emotional strain can lead to caregiver burnout and reduced quality of life, which may in turn affect their ability to support their child effectively (Hospital, B.C.s 2025). Furthermore, the evolving role of caregivers during this transition necessitates a careful balance between fostering independence in their children and ensuring that they receive the necessary support and guidance (T.H.f.S.C. (SickKids) 2018).

Given these complexities, a deeper understanding of caregiver experiences is needed to inform more effective transition support strategies. By synthesizing qualitative evidence, this meta‐ethnography aims to explore the lived experiences of caregivers supporting adolescents with chronic conditions during the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare. Understanding how the roles of caregivers evolve during this transition is crucial for developing effective strategies and frameworks that support their needs. Ultimately, enhancing caregiver support during healthcare transitions can lead to improved outcomes for both adolescents and their families.

To further explore these dynamics, our research questions were as follows: (1) What are the lived experiences of caregivers during the healthcare transition for adolescents with chronic conditions? (2) What systemic, emotional and practical barriers do caregivers encounter during this process? (3) How do caregivers' roles evolve as they balance fostering independence and maintaining support? (4) What strategies and frameworks can be developed to support caregivers effectively during healthcare transitions?

Methods

Study Design

This review was designed as a meta‐ethnography to synthesize findings of qualitative studies while retaining the richness of their context. Meta‐ethnography aims to develop overarching concepts and theories while preserving the unique contexts of individual studies.

The methodological rigour and interpretive depth of meta‐ethnography suited the aim of this study in synthesizing caregiver experiences during the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare. The methodology enabled us to interpret and integrate diverse caregiver perspectives, facilitating the development of a conceptual framework that reflects the complexity of their experiences (Hirata and Kobayashi 2023).

Eligibility Criteria

This review comprised qualitative studies published in peer‐reviewed journals in English between January 1990 and November 2023. The selection process utilized the ‘Population’, ‘Phenomena of Interest’ and ‘Context’ (PICo) framework, as recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute for the meta‐synthesis of qualitative studies (Munn et al. 2020). The PICo framework is particularly suited for qualitative research, focusing on understanding experiences, perceptions and contexts rather than evaluating intervention effectiveness.

A systematic search was conducted using key terms that captured the focus of this review. Search terms were applied across multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL and PsycINFO, to ensure a comprehensive and rigorous search. PubMed was selected because it provides access to MEDLINE‐indexed records as well as additional in‐process and ahead‐of‐print citations that may not yet be fully indexed in MEDLINE (see Supporting Information S1 for search strategy).

Eligible articles were those that (1) examined the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare, (2) included caregiver perspectives with identifiable caregiver‐derived data (e.g., direct quotes or findings attributable to caregivers) and (3) focused on chronic conditions within gastroenterology, rheumatology, endocrinology or nephrology. These specialties were selected because information from this study will be used to inform the development of a caregiver‐focused transition questionnaire in collaboration with clinicians across these disciplines. Studies that included mixed participant groups (e.g., adolescents, caregivers and clinicians) were eligible if caregiver perspectives could be clearly distinguished within the reported findings; no minimum proportion of caregiver participants was required. Studies focusing solely on adolescents, healthcare professionals or unrelated phenomena were excluded.

Search Strategy and Screening

The database search utilized a combination of targeted keywords and Boolean operators to identify relevant studies. The search terms included the following: Parent OR caregiver AND (paediatric OR child OR young adult OR youth) AND (transition to adult care OR transfer) AND (perspectives OR experiences OR expectations). Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL and PsycINFO. To ensure comprehensive coverage, citation tracking and manual searches were also performed to identify additional relevant studies. References were imported to Covidence, a systematic review software (Innovation, V.H 2025).

Two independent reviewers (A.P. and H.R.) screened titles and abstracts of identified studies. Following initial screening, full‐text articles were reviewed for eligibility based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through consensus.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

This meta‐ethnography followed the seven phases outlined by Noblit and Hare to guide the synthesis of qualitative findings (Noblit and Hare 1988).

The aim of this synthesis was to explore the experiences of caregivers supporting youth through the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare. After screening, two reviewers (A.P. and H.R.) independently reviewed selected articles to develop a thorough understanding of their content and context. The primary reviewer (A.P.) extracted study characteristics including the study title, author(s), year of publication, research objectives, methodology, sample characteristics, country of origin and key findings. The reviewers collaboratively analysed these studies to identify core concepts and recurring patterns.

Through an iterative comparative process, concepts were translated across studies into shared themes, categories and subcategories, forming the basis of our first‐order constructs. These insights were further synthesized into higher‐order interpretations (second‐ and third‐order constructs), offering a cohesive understanding of the caregiver journey. Interpretation discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The final synthesis is expressed as a structured narrative, presenting key themes and interpretive insights supported by illustrative quotations from the original studies.

To enhance the trustworthiness and interpretive rigour of the synthesis, member‐checking was undertaken by a graduate researcher (H.R.) and three faculty members (M.B., K.B., and L.N.) with expertise in qualitative methodology, meta‐ethnography and paediatric care. Their diverse disciplinary perspectives contributed to the refinement of the analytical framework and validation of the synthesized findings.

Results

Search and Study Characteristics

About 11 569 relevant studies were identified by electronic search and 9306 remained after duplicate studies were eliminated (Figure 1). After title and abstract screening, 135 studies were reviewed in full text by two independent reviewers (A.P. and H.R.) and 112 studies were excluded. Finally, 23 studies were included in this meta‐ethnography. Table 1 summarizes characteristics of included studies, such as methodology, sample demographics, subspecialty and transition stage. Although studies included adolescents with chronic conditions across gastroenterology, rheumatology, endocrinology and nephrology, meaningful comparisons of caregiver experiences across condition groups were limited. The qualitative studies varied substantially in clinical populations, study aims and reporting of caregiver perspectives, which limited the ability to identify consistent condition‐specific differences. Across conditions, however, caregivers described broadly similar challenges related to navigating healthcare systems, supporting adolescent independence and managing emotional uncertainty during the transition process.

Themes Identified in Caregiver Experiences

Through the meta‐ethnographic synthesis, five key themes were identified describing the experiences of caregivers during the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare across the included studies. These themes encapsulate both systemic and individual dimensions of the transition process, offering insights into caregivers' roles, challenges and needs: (1) ‘Caregiver Empowerment and Advocacy’, where caregivers emphasized advocating for their needs within healthcare, employment and education systems; (2) ‘Building Networks and Collaboration’, highlighting the importance of collaborative networks to facilitate seamless transitions; (3) ‘Emotional and Psychological Preparedness’, emphasizing caregivers' need for emotional and psychological resources to cope with the transition process; (4) ‘Structural and Systemic Support’, emphasizing the necessity of structured programs, communication technologies and cross‐sectoral support systems; and (5) ‘Tailored Transition Plans and Professional Integration’, where caregivers play a critical role in advocating for and supporting individualized plans that equip adolescents with the skills and confidence to navigate healthcare and employment opportunities.

These five themes collectively illustrate the multifaceted experiences of caregivers, emphasizing the need for caregiver‐inclusive transition frameworks that address both systemic and emotional challenges while supporting skill‐building and collaborative care strategies.

During further analysis, we also identified an overarching dynamic involving caregivers, adolescents and healthcare providers: “Shared Responsibility and Mutual Adjustment Among Caregivers, Adolescents, and Healthcare Providers in Transition Dynamics.” This theme reflects the collaborative nature of the transition process, in which caregivers, adolescents and healthcare providers align their goals, engage in shared decision‐making and adapt to evolving roles and responsibilities as adolescents transition from paediatric to adult care.

By addressing this overarching theme, future policies and interventions can better integrate the diverse needs of all knowledge users, ultimately enhancing the transition experience for families navigating paediatric to adult healthcare systems. This theme emphasizes alignment of goals, shared decision‐making and adaptation among caregivers, adolescents and healthcare providers.

Identified Themes

Caregiver Empowerment and Advocacy

Caregivers consistently described taking proactive roles to navigate complex healthcare systems and advocate for their adolescents' needs. Many parents became the bridge between paediatric and adult services, securing referrals, educational accommodations and workplace support for their children's health needs. This advocacy was often portrayed as crucial to smoothing the transition: effective planning and caregiver involvement helped anticipate challenges and ‘reduce the abruptness’ of moving to adult care (Nicholas et al. 2018). For example, in a qualitative study of diabetes care transitions, parents expressed a desire to remain involved in medical decisions and planning, emphasizing that having “a supportive person, group, or network could positively enhance the transition process” (Butalia et al. 2020). Multiple studies also noted a tension between advocacy and fostering independence. Caregivers struggled with the dual role of championing their child's needs while also letting go to build the youth's self‐reliance. For instance, Crawford et al. reported that mothers often found the transition more challenging than the adolescents did, feeling compelled to stay heavily involved (Crawford et al. 2022). One adolescent also admitted, “I am used to looking at mum, and then she just answers,” illustrating how parents remained the default spokespeople (Crawford et al. 2022). Yet some caregivers recognized the need to step back as their child entered adulthood. Shaw et al. reported that parents were advised to “sit back and let [the youth] talk” to providers, even if it felt like not doing “100% of what I should be doing” (Shaw et al. 2004). Indeed, a mother in one study reflected, “I've done my 17 years, and my son can do his lifetime,” expressing that after guiding her son through adolescence, she wanted him to assume responsibility going forward (Crawford et al. 2022). Not every experience of advocacy was positive: some healthcare providers observed that over‐involved parents could inadvertently hinder youth autonomy. In one case, a clinician noted a young adult was ‘hopeless’ with medication because “his mother … has not given him responsibility”, underscoring the unintended downside when caregivers cannot or will not relinquish control (Crawford et al. 2022). A few caregivers also felt disempowered in adult settings that expected them to step aside entirely. Overall, however, caregiver empowerment and advocacy emerged as a dominant theme across studies, with parents and guardians striving to champion their child's healthcare needs through the transition while delicately balancing support with independence. Negative cases tended to centre on instances where this balance faltered—either the caregiver overstepped, or conversely, was left feeling excluded, highlighting the importance of clear role negotiation during transition.

Building Networks and Collaboration

Our meta‐ethnography revealed that collaborative support networks—spanning family, peers and professionals—are pivotal in a successful transition. Caregivers frequently mentioned the value of strong communication between paediatric and adult care teams to ensure continuity of care. For example, Coyne et al. described how coordinated information‐sharing and joint clinic visits helped families feel more secure moving into the adult system (Coyne et al. 2019). Beyond clinical teams, several studies highlighted the importance of peer and community support for caregivers. Sáez et al. and Yi‐Frazier et al. both noted that support groups and workshops allowed caregivers to exchange advice and coping strategies, alleviating feelings of isolation that many experienced (Saez et al. 2022; Yi‐Frazier et al. 2022). In one study, a parent described how they mobilized an informal network around their teen—informing the child's friends and their significant other about the condition—so that these peers could help “keep [the child] in check” after transfer to adult care (Hilliard et al. 2014). Such collaboration extends the support system beyond the immediate family, spreading caregiving responsibilities across a trusted network. Collaboration with educators and other community professionals was also emphasized. In a synthesis of parent perspectives on chronic illness transitions, participants stressed the need for better coordination “between professionals (including general practitioners and school teachers)” and clearer sign‐posting of information about transition resources (Menon et al. 2020). Some caregivers noted confusion about whom to contact during crises in the adult system until a dedicated coordinator or ‘key worker’ was put in place—suggesting that assigning a point‐person can improve coordination (Shaw et al. 2004). Indeed, caregivers consistently voiced that trust and ongoing communication with all members of the care team (paediatric and adult) was fundamental. Several parents in one study said that feeling comfortable with new adult providers, and seeing the team be attentive with regular follow‐ups, were key indicators of a successful transition (Bihari et al. 2022). By building networks of collaboration, caregivers felt more supported and informed rather than having to navigate the transition alone. Conversely, when networks were absent or weak, families felt adrift and isolated—a negative case noted in multiple studies. For example, families reported feeling ‘considerable confusion’ about accessing help after transfer until better communication channels were established (Shaw et al. 2004). Overall, building networks and collaboration was identified in most studies as crucial for creating a safety net around transitioning youth (Crawford et al. 2022; Shaw et al. 2004; Coyne et al. 2019; Saez et al. 2022). Caregivers benefitted from both peer‐to‐peer connections and stronger inter‐provider collaboration, which together eased transition burden and anxiety. Any diverging perspectives came mainly from instances where support was lacking, reinforcing how much their presence matters to families.

Emotional and Psychological Preparedness

Nearly all studies underscored the intense emotional toll the transition imposes on caregivers. Parents and other carers described high levels of anxiety about their adolescents' readiness to manage their condition independently and how they would fare in a fragmented adult system. For instance, caregivers' feared that their child might struggle with self‐management or fall ill without the close oversight that paediatric care provided (Yi‐Frazier et al. 2022; Ness et al. 2020). This worry often left caregivers feeling unprepared and stressed. For example, participation in a parent support network eased caregivers' anxiety by normalizing their experiences and providing practical coping tips (Saez et al. 2022). Another facet of emotional preparedness was trust in the adult care team. Caregivers felt reassured when new providers took their teens' concerns seriously and established rapport. In one study, a mother noted relief that her daughter “found a physician she is comfortable with” who listens and addresses her worries, which in turn gives the mother peace of mind (Bihari et al. 2022). On the other hand, if caregivers sensed that the adult team was not responsive or the transition was poorly handled, their anxiety spiked. Several participants (both caregivers and providers) observed that an ‘unsuccessful transition’ could negatively affect mental health—raising adolescents' social and general anxiety and even contributing to depression which indirectly multiplies parents' stress (Bihari et al. 2022). A particularly stark negative case came from a study where a parent described receiving no gradual preparation at all: “All we got on her 16th birthday was… a letter which says ‘You're discharged’” (Shaw et al. 2004). This abrupt, impersonal handoff left the family blindsided and distressed, exemplifying how the lack of emotional preparation can devastate caregivers. Indeed, caregivers often reported feeling ‘thrown in the deep end’ when transitions were not well‐supported, leading to feelings of abandonment and fear for their child's well‐being (Nicholas et al. 2018). Across studies, emotional and psychological preparedness was a pervasive theme—caregivers consistently voiced the need for reassurance, information and counselling to mentally prepare both themselves and their children for the upcoming changes. While a minority of caregivers with resilient youth or strong support systems felt more at ease, the dominant narrative was one of anxiety tempered only by the availability of emotional support resources and positive relationships with healthcare providers.

Structural and Systemic Support

Caregivers' experiences also highlighted the critical role of structural and systemic support in easing transition challenges, through structured communication pathways and cross‐sector support systems. Caregivers greatly valued interventions like transition coordinators, joint paediatric‐adult clinics and written care plans that could provide continuity and clarity during the transfer. For example, parents in multiple studies advocated for a named coordinator or ‘key worker’ to guide the family, as confusion about who would manage care in the adult system was a major stressor (Saez et al. 2022). Several caregivers also suggested leveraging technology for systemic support—one parent in a diabetes transition study argued for communication tools familiar to youth (e.g., text messaging) to keep young patients engaged and informed, reflecting a desire for health systems to adapt to adolescents' habits (Butalia et al. 2020). When strong structural supports were present, caregivers reported significant relief. One parent described the paediatric team's proactive approach as a stabilizing force: “[Our pediatric specialist] was just very like, ‘okay, we're gonna fix this’ … Oh, thank God there's a grown‐up in charge,” indicating how reliable providers can serve as structural anchors during transition (Feingold et al. 2021). Likewise, parents praised flexible scheduling and accommodations by adult services—“We can work it around his school schedule”—as examples of system‐level understanding that made the process easier. Such adjustments (e.g., aligning appointments with a youth's college timetable or living situation) illustrate the systemic flexibility families seek (Bihari et al. 2022). However, a number of studies also revealed structural shortcomings that acted as barriers. Parents were often alarmed by the stark contrast between paediatric and adult care environments (Crawford et al. 2022). Caregivers described adult clinics as impersonal and overwhelming—one mother felt her son became “one of a couple of hundred … in this huge, big conglomeration” after transfer, highlighting the loss of a personalized focus in adult care (Crawford et al. 2022). Another parent lamented moving from a ‘world class’ children's hospital to a “rundown [and] badly run” adult facility, which “makes the transition much harder” (Crawford et al. 2022). Such experiences left caregivers nervous about the quality of adult care, with some fearing that adult providers would not be as thorough or attentive (Crawford et al. 2022). Structural gaps in other domains were noted as well: for instance, if a young adult's health crisis occurred while away at college, parents often had to step in directly due to lack of local support, as one parent who spent “seven weekends [at campus]” caring for her daughter attested (Feingold et al. 2021). This indicates that without formal transitional supports bridging paediatric care, post‐secondary health services and adult specialists, families are burdened and strained further. In summary, caregivers widely agreed on the need for robust structural and systemic supports to facilitate a smooth transition. They valued organized, communicative systems—flexible scheduling, cross‐provider coordination, easy access to information and when those were absent, their experiences were markedly more difficult. Divergent perspectives were mainly seen in the contrast between instances where systemic supports functioned well versus where they broke down. The negative cases, such as inadequate adult clinic infrastructure or non‐existent transition planning, served to emphasize caregivers' calls for better system‐level investment in transition programs.

Tailored Transition Plans and Professional Integration

Finally, caregivers highlighted the importance of tailored transition plans and close professional integration during the handover to adult care. Rather than a one‐size‐fits‐all approach, families advocated for individualized planning, which accounts for each adolescent's medical, developmental and situational needs. Several studies reported that parents wanted customized transition timing and preparation: for example, participants in Gray et al. called for clinicians to explicitly discuss and set transition goals with the adolescent—“sit me down and say, ‘What's your goal? What can I do to make this happen?’” instead of transferring care at a preset age (Gray et al. 2015). Caregivers suggested that during clinic visits leading up to transfer, providers should routinely clarify who is responsible for tasks (refilling prescriptions, making appointments, etc.) and progressively shift those responsibilities to the youth. For example, if youth looked to their parents for answers when doctors asked about medications, it indicated insufficient self‐management skills (Yi‐Frazier et al. 2022). Notably, caregivers often voiced opinions on when transition should occur, favouring flexibility in age criteria. Some felt that delaying transfer until after major life milestones—“These four years that they're in college … after college would be a good time to do it”—could improve outcomes (Gray et al. 2015). Such views reflect that optimal timing might differ for each family, and rigid age cut‐offs (e.g., at 18 years old) do not suit everyone. As for professional integration, caregivers stressed the value of paediatric and adult providers working together and including the family during the transition period. Adolescents and parents appreciated when adult‐care providers made them feel that involvement was “my choice and my decision… [I was] always included”, indicating a successful integration where the young person felt empowered and the parent felt reassured (Crawford et al. 2022). Another parent in that study observed that adult clinicians were “skilled at getting information out of [her daughter]” by asking open‐ended questions and constantly checking understanding (Crawford et al. 2022). This not only welcomes the youth into adult care but also gives parents confidence that their child will be cared for. Tailored plans often went hand‐in‐hand with a gradual role adjustment: caregivers, adolescents and providers actively negotiated how and when responsibilities would shift. Several mothers in the studies described intentionally phasing their involvement. One mother noted that around ages 18–21, her child was ‘that little bit more mature’, so she encouraged the doctors to start directing questions to the young adult while she moved into a supportive but less central role (Crawford et al. 2022). Individualized approaches were seen as key to building the adolescent's competence while not cutting the parent out abruptly. Where available, formal written transition plans and joint meetings with paediatric and adult teams helped operationalize these tailored strategies (e.g., setting specific self‐management goals before transfer, arranging overlapping visits). By contrast, negative cases were evident in studies where no personalized planning took place—some families reported that transitions felt sudden or ‘automatic’ based solely on age, with little adaptation to the child's readiness. Others noted that without clear guidance, they were uncertain how to coach their teens for adult care. Diverging experiences reinforced the consensus that tailored transition plans and professional integration are crucial. Studies that touched on this theme concluded that involving caregivers in creating personalized transition roadmaps—with setting timelines, adult care navigation education and defining the evolving role of each person—was associated with positive transition outcomes. Caregivers viewed this individualized, team‐based approach as reassuring for themselves and empowering for the adolescent, whereas absent transition planning was linked to confusion and poorer outcomes.

Discussion

The transition from paediatric to adult care is a critical period for adolescents with chronic health conditions, and caregivers play a pivotal role in this process. This discussion synthesizes findings from the included studies to explore caregiver perspectives across five key domains identified in the meta‐ethnography: caregiver empowerment and advocacy, building networks and collaboration, emotional and psychological preparedness, structural and systemic supports and the development of tailored transition plans supported by interdisciplinary care.

Caregiver Empowerment and Advocacy

Findings from this meta‐ethnography highlight the importance of empowering caregivers to effectively advocate for their adolescents while gradually supporting increasing independence. Caregivers frequently assume responsibility for navigating healthcare systems, coordinating appointments and ensuring continuity of care during the transition process. Healthcare professionals have a role in equipping caregivers with the knowledge and skills necessary to advocate for the needs of their child while facilitating a gradual shift toward adolescent self‐management.

Educational initiatives are an important component of caregiver empowerment (Nicolaidis et al. 2015). Structured transition programs should serve to align educational efforts with specific challenges caregivers face and incorporate targeted supports such as care coordination, improved communication between providers and opportunities for caregiver engagement in transition planning (Nicolaidis et al. 2015).

One potential strategy is the implementation of a standardized caregiver‐focused questionnaire or assessment tool in clinic settings to systematically capture caregiver perspectives, concerns, preparedness and support needs through the transition process. Such a tool could help clinicians identify areas of strain, inform family‐centred interventions tailored to the needs of each caregiver‐adolescent dyad and ensure that caregiver concerns are incorporated into transition planning. In practice, the use of a questionnaire or assessment tool could allow teams to identify families who may benefit from additional care coordination, more frequent follow‐up, psychosocial support or targeted transition counselling.

Building Networks and Collaboration

The findings also show the importance of collaborative networks that connect caregivers, adolescents and healthcare professionals. Beyond educational initiatives, caregivers may benefit from support systems that reduce burden and provide opportunities for shared learning and mentorship to prepare for the transition from paediatric to adult healthcare. These supports may include caregiver support groups, peer support networks and caregiver peer navigator programs in which experienced parents could support families entering the transition process.

Skills‐based courses or workshops may also help ease caregivers' load by offering practical guidance on navigating adult healthcare systems, understanding evolving provider roles, supporting adolescent self‐management and preparing for shifts in responsibility over time. Additional supports such as transition coordinators, written and digital navigation resources, checklists and structured opportunities for caregivers to participate in shared transition planning may further enhance preparedness and reduce uncertainty.

Additionally, fostering collaboration between paediatric and adult healthcare providers can enhance communication and ensure that caregivers are adequately prepared for the transition process (Nadarajah et al. 2021). By strengthening collaborative networks among families and healthcare teams, healthcare systems could promote more coordinated and supportive transition experiences.

Emotional and Psychological Preparedness

The transition process can be a significant emotional experience for caregivers. Many caregivers experience anxiety, uncertainty and concern about their adolescent's readiness for adult healthcare systems (Yi‐Frazier et al. 2022; Ness et al. 2020). Emotional and psychological preparedness is therefore an important component of successful transition planning.

Healthcare professionals should be trained to facilitate effective communication and relationship‐building with families, ensuring that caregivers are equipped to advocate for their children's needs while promoting independence (MacNeill et al. 2022). Training approaches such as simulation‐based modules, triadic role‐plays, reflective case studies and narrative medicine exercises grounded in caregiver experiences may help clinicians better understand caregiver perspectives and improve communication practices.

Additional techniques, such as co‐led sessions with caregivers and youth, narrative medicine exercises and shared decision‐making frameworks, can further strengthen providers' ability to validate caregiver expertise while gradually supporting adolescent autonomy. Longitudinal strategies, including audit and feedback tools, interdisciplinary shadowing and community‐of‐practice case forums, may also promote sustained reflection and system‐wide alignment with family‐centred transitional care principles (MacNeill et al. 2022).

Structural and Systemic Support

The findings also highlight the need for broader systemic changes to support caregivers during healthcare transitions. Healthcare institutions should implement structured transition programs that address the unique challenges faced by adolescents with chronic conditions and their families (MacNeill et al. 2022). These programs could include integrating caregiver‐focused workshops and counselling sessions within transition clinics to provide guidance on navigating healthcare systems while supporting the adolescent to develop independence.

Digital health strategies, such as mobile applications or telehealth consultations, may further support communication and ongoing engagement among caregivers, adolescents and healthcare providers. Mobile applications, telehealth consultations and digital educational resources may provide accessible platforms for communication, information sharing and follow‐up support during the transition period.

By addressing systemic barriers through coordinated care protocols, targeted interventions and supportive policies, healthcare systems can create a more comprehensive and equitable transition experience for all knowledge users involved.

Tailored Transition Plans and Professional Integration

Finally, the synthesis highlights the importance of individualized transition planning supported by interdisciplinary care teams. Several transition interventions illustrate the potential benefits of programs that actively engage caregivers alongside adolescents in preparing for adult healthcare.

For example, the ‘Adulting with IBD’ Workshop (Inflammatory Bowel Disease) (Waschmann et al. 2021) represents a biopsychosocial, multidisciplinary approach aimed at educating families about the transition to adult care. In a pilot study involving 16 adolescent–parent dyads, over 60% of participants reported that the workshop was helpful, and 92% indicated that they would recommend it to others. Participation in the workshop was also associated with improvements in transition readiness, with Transition‐Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ) scores increasing by approximately five points for adolescents and 10 points for parents following the intervention, suggesting enhanced skills and confidence for managing the shift to adult care (Waschmann et al. 2021).

Similarly, parent‐centred education toolkits have demonstrated promise in supporting families during healthcare transitions. The TRansition US Together (TRUST) study evaluated a Parent‐Adolescent Transition Toolkit intervention designed for parents and adolescents in juvenile arthritis and lupus care. The toolkit provided structured guidance and resources to families (Heera et al. 2024). In a cohort of 21 youth (14–18 years) and their parents, transition‐readiness (Transition‐Q scores) steadily improved, with the greatest gains occurring after the toolkit was introduced. Readiness scores increased (β = 7.8, p < 0.05, and β = 15.5, p < 0.05) after toolkit implementation, demonstrating that a parent‐centred educational toolkit can enhance adolescents' preparedness for adult care (Heera et al. 2024). These improvements were achieved without adverse effects, underlining the toolkit's feasibility and benefit in supporting caregiver–youth dyads during transition.

Family‐focused counselling interventions have also been explored as a strategy to support both adolescents and caregivers during transition. One example is Behavioural Family Systems Therapy for Diabetes—Transition (BFST‐DT), a family‐centred intervention designed to address modifiable family dynamics that influence transition readiness among adolescents with Type 1 diabetes (Papadakis et al. 2024). The program is delivered virtually over 6 months and includes four multi‐family group sessions and six individual family therapy sessions. Adapted from an evidence‐based family therapy model, BFST‐DT focuses on strengthening communication, collaborative problem‐solving and shared responsibility for diabetes self‐management as adolescents assume greater autonomy in their care. Early reports note that BFST‐DT represents one of the first family‐centric transition interventions in Type 1 diabetes and is currently being evaluated for its effectiveness (Papadakis et al. 2024).

A key principle underlying effective healthcare transition is the adoption of a team‐based approach in which professionals from paediatric and adult care collaborate to bridge the gap between child‐centred and adult‐oriented healthcare systems. Such models promote continuity of care while addressing the medical, psychosocial and developmental needs of adolescents during the transition period. Several interdisciplinary transition programs illustrate the benefits of this collaborative approach.

For example, a multidisciplinary transition clinic in Tel Aviv for adolescents with inflammatory bowel disease integrates paediatric and adult gastroenterologists, a nurse specialist and a psychologist within a coordinated transition program (Yerushalmy‐Feler et al. 2017). Over the course of three to four joint visits conducted across 7 months, adolescents demonstrated significantly increased self‐efficacy (p < 0.0001) (Yerushalmy‐Feler et al. 2017). This model allowed patients and families to meet adult providers prior to transfer and participate in collaborative care planning, which contributed to higher patient satisfaction and improved readiness for adult care.

Similarly, integrated diabetes transition models have demonstrated promising outcomes. At Westmead Hospital in Australia, a young adult diabetes clinic offers joint paediatric and adult endocrinology care supported by nurses and diabetes educators (Rueter et al. 2021). Compared with standard adult clinics, this model has been associated with improved follow‐up, fewer hospitalizations for diabetic ketoacidosis and improved glycaemic control. Key elements of the program include a transition coordinator, structured self‐management education and interdisciplinary collaboration, all of which support young adults in maintaining engagement with healthcare services following transfer.

In addition to medical coordination, successful transition clinics often incorporate psychosocial and allied health supports. Many programs include social workers, counsellors, psychologists and nurse coordinators who help address the emotional and social challenges associated with transition. These professionals also assist with follow‐up care, provide education and support families in navigating complex healthcare systems. By integrating medical management with psychosocial and practice support, multidisciplinary teams can help adolescents and their caregivers navigate the transition process with greater confidence and preparedness.

Across these models, several shared components emerge as critical to successful transition care. Effective programs typically involve coordinated collaboration between paediatric and adult clinicians, engagement of diverse healthcare professionals—including physicians, nurses, social workers, mental health professionals and educators—and a structured process that gradually shifts care responsibility from caregivers to adolescents. Such interdisciplinary team‐based programs have been implemented across multiple chronic conditions including diabetes, IBD, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, congenital heart disease and cystic fibrosis. Evidence from these initiatives suggests that coordinated multidisciplinary approaches can improve transition outcomes by enhancing disease control, strengthening self‐management skills, sustaining clinic attendance and increasing patient and caregiver satisfaction.

Given these benefits, current transition guidelines increasingly recommend the development of formal interdisciplinary transition clinics or structured transition programs to facilitate a more seamless shift into the adult healthcare system.

Conclusions

This review highlights the urgent need to learn from the experiences of parents and caregivers to ensure that transitions programs include enhanced support systems, collaborative care models and policy interventions that recognize caregivers as essential knowledge users in the transition from paediatric to adult care. Addressing the diverse needs of families is crucial for improving health outcomes and fostering long‐term self‐management among young adults with chronic illnesses. A coordinated, supportive and family‐centred approach—rooted in empowerment, emotional preparedness and tailored planning cannot only improve the transition experience but also equip adolescents to take ownership of their health as they enter adulthood. Future research should continue to explore both caregiver and adolescent experiences, focusing on the design and implementation of evidence‐based transition programs that are adaptable across healthcare contexts and responsive to the complex realities of chronic illness management.
