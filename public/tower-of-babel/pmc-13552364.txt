Repairing the Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Trial Credibility Gap
Authors: Michael Benatar, Jeffrey D. Rothstein, Kelsie Snow, Nadia Sethi, David M. Taylor, Kuldip D. Dave, Christopher J. McDermott, Thomas I. Cochrane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is a long history of early- and mid-phase amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) clinical trial data being used to make claims of clinical benefit that fail to translate into successful phase 3 outcomes. It is suggested that fallacious scientific reasoning is being encouraged by perverse incentives arising from a “clinical trial industrial complex” that greatly influences trial design, data interpretation, and results communication. Recurring fallacies include false premises underlying outcome comparisons, misuse and incorrect interpretation of biomarkers, mismatches between study design and stated objectives, selective reporting of outcomes, over-reliance on post hoc analyses and open-label extension data, and overly optimistic framing of inconclusive data. We argue that these practices, reinforced by misaligned incentives across industry and academia, lead to premature claims of therapeutic promise and tangible harm to patients. To address the resulting ALS clinical trial credibility gap, we call for rigorous adherence to established standards for reporting clinical trial results, clearer distinction between hypothesis generation and hypothesis testing, more measured description of trial results, and more disciplined triage of phase 2 programs. A cultural shift toward scientific skepticism and methodological rigor is essential to accelerate the development of genuinely effective ALS therapies.

Background

In the action-packed sci-fi movie The Matrix, directors Lana and Lilly Wachowski challenge the viewer to question their own reality and understand the deeper, often hidden, structures that control their lives. The hero, Neo, selects the red pill and sees the simulated world (the matrix) for what it truly is. In surveying the current landscape of early- and mid-phase amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) clinical trials, the narrative of this 1999 hit movie seems especially apt, even a quarter of a century later.

In the field of ALS, there is a long history of “promising candidates” being touted as demonstrating an effect on disease progression based on phase 2 clinical trial data, only for virtually all of these to fail in phase 3.1 While this might have been understandable a decade ago, the state of science has advanced sufficiently that the field can no longer claim to not know better. It is suggested that fallacious scientific reasoning is being encouraged by perverse incentives arising from a “clinical trial industrial complex” that greatly influences trial design and data interpretation. While we acknowledge the potential impact of fallacious reasoning on preclinical studies or inferences made between effects observed in animal models and the promise of benefit in people with ALS, our focus here is squarely on early- and mid-phase clinical trials. In shining a light on the issues, our goal is to elevate the quality of phase 1/2 ALS clinical trial study design, analysis, interpretation, and presentation, motivated above all by the urgent need to bring clinically meaningful benefit to those living with ALS or the future threat of it.

Scientific Fallacies

Fallacies are arguments that might seem convincing but which are actually flawed. Some fallacies result from invalid inference or faulty structure; others rely on irrelevant, ambiguous, or unsupported premises. A fallacious argument can be valid in form and yet be unsound if its premises are false, or it may fail validity altogether. While there is no uniformly agreed upon approach to classifying fallacies and our goal is not to provide a comprehensive classification, we have found it helpful to parse these along themes commonly encountered in the ALS literature.

False Premise Fallacy

The characteristic feature of this fallacy is that a foundational premise which forms the basis for some conclusion, is unreliable or mistaken. A common example in the ALS clinical trial arena is to abandon (or minimize) the use of a placebo group and to rely instead on a comparison between the prebaseline estimated rate of disease progression (typically quantified as the ΔFRS2) and the measured rate of decline of the revised ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS-R) after the initiation of the experimental therapeutic.3 In this example a slower rate of change on the ALSFRS-R compared with the ΔFRS is interpreted as a sign of therapeutic effect. This analytic approach, however, relies on assumptions (e.g., reliability of patient recollection of symptom onset date, that the ALSFRS-R score was 48 at the time of symptom onset) and ignores the repeatedly demonstrated fact that rate of ALSFRS-R decline is nonlinear, especially when considering the full course of disease.4,5 Another common example of the false premise fallacy in ALS studies arises from the use of biomarkers for drawing inferences about drug effects despite there being insufficient empirical evidence to support the particular context of use. For example, if the analytic performance characteristics (e.g., test-retest reliability) of a biomarker are not well established, or the natural history of the biomarker in the untreated state is unknown, then caution is warranted in interpreting changes in the biomarker in response to an experimental therapeutic, especially in the absence of an adequate control group. The claim that a drug normalizes EEG abnormalities in ALS,3 for example, is not supported by a robust body of evidence readying this particular measure for use as a response biomarker.

Biomarker Misuse Fallacy

While also related to how biomarker data are interpreted in clinical trials, this differs from the false premise fallacy insofar as it describes faulty inference rather than a flawed premise. Demonstrating the effect of an experimental therapeutic on a biomarker provides evidence of the pharmacodynamic effect of the drug but does not necessarily imply an effect relevant to the pathobiology of ALS. For example, while there is evidence for ferroptosis (iron-dependent, nonapoptotic, regulated cell death) in ALS6, the relationship between serum ferritin (which is impacted by many factors7) and ferroptosis (which depends heavily on local iron chemistry, for example) is indirect.8 Demonstrating that an experimental agent lowers serum ferritin, therefore, speaks to a mechanism of action of the drug, but not necessarily to any biology relevant to ALS.9 Similarly, the relationship between a reduction in neuron-derived exosomal TDP-43 concentration, for example, and the putative therapeutic effects of an experimental drug10 seems to neglect current understanding of the role of TDP-43 in ALS. It is the loss of normal nuclear TDP-43 function and the potential toxicity associated with cytoplasmic TDP-43 that are relevant to disease. Because the relationship between blood exosomal TDP-43 concentration and the functional effects of TDP-43 mislocalization in the CNS is far from clear, it is not credible to argue that a reduction in the former has relevance to the latter. There is enormous potential for obfuscation here given the vast number of biological processes (e.g., oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, hyperexcitability and excitotoxicity, synaptic loss) that have been purported to be perturbed in ALS, and the limited data tying biomarkers of these biological processes to something meaningful in ALS.

Design/Objective Mismatch Fallacy

This refers to a mismatch between aspects of study design and the sorts of scientific questions that can reasonably be answered by said study design. This differs from the well-known hasty generalization fallacy in which broad conclusions about a whole group are based on an unrepresentative sample of the population. The most common illustration of the design/objective mismatch fallacy is the reliance on small, often inadequately controlled, datasets to probe questions about clinical efficacy. The performance characteristics (e.g., rate of decline, variability) of the ALSFRS-R are such that several hundred patients would be needed for a trial to have ∼90% power to detect a clinically meaningful therapeutic effect,11 yet most phase 2 ALS trials are significantly underpowered.12 As such, ALSFRS-R results in phase 2 trials of typical size and duration should be interpreted with great caution. In fact, prudence is warranted even in larger, longer phase 3 trials.13,14 Moreover, in typically sized phase 2 trials, study arms are almost inevitably imbalanced with respect to known and unknown baseline confounding variables. Indeed, this has been one of the criticisms of the pivotal study that led to approval of edaravone—the study included only 137 patients (69 on drug and 68 on placebo),15 with a lower proportion of patients in the edaravone group classified as grade 2 based on the Japan ALS severity classification.16 We now have powerful prognostic tools such as the ENCALS prediction score and neurofilament light (NfL) that can adjust for some of the heterogeneity and potential imbalances,17,18 but these are not yet routinely used. Of these, NfL is the most powerful tool and adds value to the prognostic utility of the ENCALS score.17 While these prognostic tools were either not available (ENCALS score) or sufficiently well established (NfL) at the time of the pivotal edaravone trial, post hoc analyses do not seem to have explored whether such adjustments might affect results. Moreover, even after adjusting for known baseline characteristics, the only way to truly balance studies for unknown confounders is through large randomized studies. So while quantification of the change (or rate of decline) of the ALSFRS-R in a small early- to mid-phase ALS trial is reasonable, at best the results can be used to generate hypotheses. It is a mistake, however, to treat such analyses, whether prespecified or not, as hypothesis testing. Moreover, extreme caution is needed when generating such hypotheses in the context of other fallacies described here.

The Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy

Related to the problem of multiplicity (testing multiple hypotheses without appropriate statistical adjustment), the perils of which in ALS clinical trials are well described,1 this fallacy describes the process of ignoring “negative” results while overplaying purportedly “positive” results, which are often based on post hoc or subgroup analyses and typically confounded by relevant imbalances across treatment groups.19 A common instance of this fallacy in the realm of ALS clinical trials is illustrated by the strategy of performing multiple analyses on an already small sample set, often (especially egregiously) doing so with an outcome measure such as the ALSFRS-R, for which the trial is already underpowered.20 Examples of this include focusing on a subset of the trial population with faster or slower progression, as estimated by the ΔFRS for example, or a subset based on a particular biomarker value. The results of such analyses, especially when not prespecified, are especially troubling when they are presented as though reflecting hypothesis testing rather than hypothesis generating.

Open-Label Extension Fallacy

The source of this fallacy lies in the principal reliance on differences, for example, in changes from baseline in ALSFRS-R scores or in survival time, between drug and placebo-treated patients at some point during open-label extension (OLE) rather than at the end of the double-blind period. While there is value in an OLE, the longer the OLE relative to the double-blind phase of a trial, the more alike the 2 groups become.1 As a result, the likelihood is lower that observed differences in outcome are due to the effects of the drug rather than baseline characteristics of the cohort. Relevant to this fallacy, it is worth recalling the recent experience with AMX0035. Secondary analyses of the phase 2 data from the CENTAUR trial (6 months double blind, placebo controlled), for example, claimed survival benefits over maximum follow-up of 35 months.21 A subsequent, much larger, phase 3 trial, however, failed to demonstrate efficacy.22,23

Data Omission Fallacy

This fallacy describes the tendency to under (or not) report data that do not support the narrative of the preliminary efficacy of a drug. Typically, phase 2 ALS trials appropriately focus on safety and tolerability, followed by analyses of biomarker data and clinical efficacy measures. The data omission fallacy is apparent when biomarkers are prespecified as among the primary outcome measures (alongside safety) and the ALSFRS-R as secondary, for example, but study reports omit key biomarker data and focus instead of a host of different analyses of the ALSFRS-R. Most notable is the failure to report neurofilament light chain (NfL) data,24 presumably because of the modest or absent effect of the experimental therapeutic on NfL, a powerful (albeit incompletely validated) biomarker with multiple contexts of use including pharmacodynamic response.25 This is especially problematic when paired with an emphasis of the experimental drug on a poorly characterized biomarker, an instance of the biomarker misuse fallacy.

Data/Conclusion Discordance Fallacy

The term “discordance” in the name of this fallacy describes the inconsistency between data presented and the conclusions claimed. An obvious example would be to claim clinically meaningful effects of an experimental therapeutic when the data presented show no such effect.

Optimistic Interpretation Fallacy

This fallacy is typically summative, often supported by many of the aforementioned fallacies. The presentation of trial results, whether through press release, through abstracts and conference presentations, or peer-reviewed publication, as supporting meaningful clinical effects when predicated on having committed many of the foregoing fallacies, is misleading at best. While this fallacy builds on the foundation of optimism bias, it is described as a fallacy because it is typically reduced to practice rather than representing simply a psychological state.

Real-World Harms

The desperate need to bring meaningfully effective treatments to patients affected by ALS is clear, but the misrepresentation of trial results—purporting them to be more favorable than they are—will not yield benefits for patients. On the contrary, such practices are responsible for significant harm. Premature claims of efficacy create a scarcity dynamic where vulnerable patients and families believe an effective treatment exists but remains out of reach. A terminal diagnosis creates a uniquely susceptible state amenable to hopeful misinterpretation of trial outcomes, a rational response to overstated results, particularly when they come from respected key opinion leaders and public communications. These communications drive one-drug advocacy and demands for immediate access rather than attention to a scientifically sound drug development process. They result in the framing of regulatory agencies as barriers26 rather than accountability partners and undermine trust in the institutions and processes that exist to serve patients best. There is also an untold opportunity cost in the diversion of time and funding from research into the underlying biological mechanisms of disease that will ultimately inform better target selection for clinical trials with a greater likelihood of success.

Misinformation about putatively promising therapies also requires that ALS specialists devote an inordinate amount of time trying to correct misperceptions about purported therapeutic benefits of experimental compounds based on patients' exposure to misinformation spread through press release and amplified by social media—a real world example of Brandolini's law.27,28 Moreover, premature and false claims of efficacy also serve to drive a wedge between patients in desperate need of hope and those segments of the community who demand scientific rigor. To be clear, this is not a divide between patients and researchers, but rather between those who trade on false hope for patients by relying on fallacious scientific reasoning vs those who see the false claims for what they are and the segment of the patient community that refuses to be deceived. In raising these issues, our goal is to restore legitimacy to the field.

Understanding the Credibility Gap

At the heart of the problem lies an attempt to make efficacy claims based on phase 2 trial data. Ultimately, however, the small size of these studies (leading to group imbalances on important prognostic variables, few survival endpoints, and underpowered analyses based on the ALSFRS-R), the reliance on indirect comparative data (ΔFRS, historical controls), over-reliance on OLE data, misuse of biomarkers, and failure to adjust for multiplicity permit only hypothesis generation (rather than hypothesis testing). Overly optimistic interpretation of these data, as though hypotheses had been tested, then leads to mistaken claims about the therapeutic promise of the experimental therapeutic. Greater awareness of, and commitment to avoid, these fallacies will meaningfully accelerate progress toward the development of effective therapeutics.

Assuming that the perils associated with such practices are well-known, we believe that coherence of a complex array of interacting societal factors have resulted in an appearance that most are either unable or unwilling to perceive as simulated (the matrix) rather than genuine (reality).

Clinical stage biotechnology companies, often built around a single asset, understandably have strong financial interests in the successful outcomes of their early- to mid-phase clinical trials. As such, they are susceptible to confirmation bias. For example, they may be inclined to listen to and believe the voices of key opinion leaders in the field, typically from academic medical centers, who endorse claims of therapeutic promise and express the sentiments that they want and need to hear. In return, these commercial entities form alliances with academic investigators, selecting their medical centers to participate as enrollment sites for clinical trials, appointing them as the academic leads of studies, and including them as authors on abstracts, conference presentations and publications. (Parenthetically, many of these are ghost-authored by professional writers rather than academic investigators,29,30 and it is not always clear how much co-authors on these publications have critically reviewed the results, abstracts, presentations, and manuscripts given the speed with which approval is provided [personal communication]). The result is a vicious cycle, in which suspect trial results and the process whereby they are generated are perpetuated. Venture capital investment, predicated on following recommendations of key opinion leaders, especially when they are based on fallacious reasoning, adds fuel to this cycle. By contrast, the biotech industry is less willing to hear those who see purported encouraging results for what they are—a manifestation of post hoc analyses, which should only be considered hypothesis generating, data diving, and p-hacking. There is an urgent need to disrupt the symbiotic relationship that currently exists between those whose company's future/success depends on a favorable trial result and those who are willing to endorse, encourage, and perpetuate the practice of overstating purported benefits. Journal editors, peer reviewers, and conference abstract review committees as well as patient advocacy groups and investors all have a role to play in demanding greater rigor in the reporting of clinical trial results.

Fortunately, there is a rich pipeline of compounds that could be investigated for potential benefit to the ALS patient community. Elegant solutions, including platform trials,31-33 have been developed to help accelerate the pace of drug screening, including the use of NfL as an early response biomarker to provide evidence of biological effect. While we recognize the limitations of NfL—that levels may not change in response to drugs that target, for example, skeletal muscle contraction, and that the use of NfL as a response biomarker might be confounded by short-term toxicity or drugs that affect NfL clearance—this biomarker has emerged as a powerful tool for drug development that should be more routinely used.

An oft neglected strategy is to make better triage use of phase 2 trials, selecting only those with the most robust and genuinely promising biological effects for further development through large, often multinational, randomized controlled trials. Maximizing the triage value entails a dispassionate appraisal of the data, clear-sightedness in distinguishing hypothesis generation from hypothesis testing, as well as an honest and transparent communication of results. Failure to adequately triage has the net effect of funneling time and resources to experimental agents that are ultimately doomed to failure—which, in the long run, betrays the trust that patients place in science and delays investment in compounds that truly have the greatest potential to bring benefit to patients with ALS.

A Proposed Path Forward

The call to action is for all ALS therapeutic trials, irrespective of phase, to adhere to established and widely accepted standards for the conduct, analysis, and reporting of clinical trial results.34,35 In the words of Richard Feynman: “The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.”36 Scientists, clinicians, trialists, patients, family members, peer reviewers, and journal editors must all be skeptical, calling out hyperbole in press releases that feed the investment market, or in conference abstracts designed to raise the profile of the presenter.
