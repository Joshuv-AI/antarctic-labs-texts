Cholecystokinin Induces Ca2+ Transients in Brainstem Neurons, Astrocytes and Central Vagal Terminals in Jugular/Nodose Ganglia–Brainstem Slice Cultures
Authors: Adam Ujhelyi, Rana Soylu Kucharz, Jens Christian Rekling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCholecystokinin is a well‐established neuromodulator of brainstem homeostatic circuits, including circuits implicated in satiety and visceral sensation. To better understand the cellular basis of brainstem cholecystokinin signalling, we developed a novel jugular/nodose ganglion–brainstem slice culture, where microdissection isolated bilateral jugular/nodose ganglia attached to a slice of the brainstem via intact vagal rootlets. This preparation preserves the functional vagal afferent pathway to the nucleus of the tractus solitarius and allows for targeted viral transduction of genetically encoded Ca2+ indicators. We show that electrical jugular/nodose ganglion stimulation evokes glutamate‐driven Ca2+ transients in second‐order brainstem neurons, confirming functional connectivity. Using nuclear‐targeted Ca2+ imaging, we demonstrate that cholecystokinin directly activates a specific subpopulation of neurons in the dorsal and ventrolateral medulla, independent of action potential generation. Furthermore, we identify distinct populations of central vagal terminals: those recruited by electrical stimulation of the jugular/nodose ganglion and a separate subpopulation directly activated by cholecystokinin. Multiplex imaging revealed that cholecystokinin and Substance P triggered robust Ca2+ waves in brainstem astrocytes, and muscarine and thyrotropin‐releasing hormone selectively activate neuronal populations without recruiting astrocytic networks. Finally, we found that the jugular/nodose ganglion–brainstem slice cultures generate spontaneous respiratory‐like rhythmic bursting in the preBötzinger Complex, with a frequency significantly increased by cholecystokinin. Together, these findings establish jugular/nodose ganglion–brainstem slice cultures as a powerful model for dissecting the complex neuro‐astrocytic circuitry of cholecystokinin signalling in the brainstem.

Introduction

Visceral homeostasis depends on rapid, high‐fidelity transmission of mechanical, thermal and chemical information from the viscera to the central nervous system primarily through spinal and vagal afferent systems (Berthoud and Neuhuber 2000; Spencer et al. 2026). The gut–brain part of this communication relies heavily on vagal afferents that project centrally to the dorsomedial medulla, where they terminate predominantly in the nucleus of the tractus solitarius (NTS) (Berthoud and Neuhuber 2000; Cai et al. 2024; Wachsmuth et al. 2022). The NTS is the principal first‐order relay of the dorsal vagal complex (DVC); vagal inputs are integrated with hormonal and descending signals and then routed to premotor and hypothalamic targets controlling feeding, gastric motility and cardiorespiratory output (Rinaman 2010). Within this axis, cholecystokinin (CCK) is a peripherally released peptide playing a central role in food intake suppression (Gibbs et al. 1973). Enteroendocrine I cells in the proximal small intestine release CCK in response to luminal fat and protein via nutrient‐dependent releasing factors, producing circulating levels proportional to meal size (Buffa et al. 1976; Liddle 1995; Polak et al. 1975; Rehfeld 2017). CCK activates CCK1 receptors (CCK1R) on peripheral vagal afferent endings in the stomach and duodenum, increasing vagal firing (Cox and Randich 1997; Richards et al. 1996; Schwartz and Moran 1994), thereby contributing to inhibition of food intake and gastric emptying (Dourish et al. 1989; Gibbs et al. 1973; Owyang and Heldsinger 2011; Weatherford et al. 1992). However, CCK receptor mRNA is also present in central vagal afferents, and the NTS (Rogers and Hermann 2008), and recent cell type‐specific tracing and functional mapping have identified C‐type vagal fibres that monosynaptically excite CCK‐expressing NTS neurons, defining a pathway by which nutrient‐related signals access defined second‐order populations in the DVC (Winzenried et al. 2025).

In mice, this pathway is anatomically anchored by the fused jugular–nodose ganglion (JNG) complex (Han and de Araujo 2021), whose pseudounipolar vagal afferents convey thoracoabdominal information centrally to the NTS via the tractus solitarius (TS) (Berthoud et al. 2025; Berthoud and Neuhuber 2000; Kim et al. 2020). Acute brainstem slice work has begun to define CCK actions at central vagal synapses. Ca2+ imaging of vagal terminals in the NTS shows that CCK increases presynaptic Ca2+ via CCK1R‐dependent cascade involving protein kinase A, phospholipase C, L‐type Ca2+ channels and Ca2+ release from endoplasmic reticulum (Baptista et al. 2005; Rogers and Hermann 2008). Importantly, CCK‐evoked enhancement of spontaneous glutamate release at NTS synapses requires functional TRPV1 in primary vagal afferents, as TRPV1 pore block or genetic ablation abolishes the effect (Arnold et al. 2024). In NTS neurons, CCK‐8S increases both the frequency and amplitude of vagal‐driven glutamatergic EPSCs, indicating facilitation of transmitter release from central termini (Appleyard et al. 2007; Baptista et al. 2005). Microinjection of CCK‐8S into the DVC reduces gastric tone and modifies oesophageal–gastric reflexes—effects abolished by local CCK receptor antagonists, but not reproduced by systemic peptide delivery, establishing a direct DVC locus for CCK modulation of vagal reflexes (Holmes et al. 2009). Together, these studies establish that CCK acts on central vagal terminals and local circuits, but they do not resolve how its effects are distributed across JNG somata, postsynaptic neurons and non‐neuronal elements within an intact vagal brainstem microcircuit.

Astrocytes add an additional layer of complexity to NTS processing. TS stimulation evokes rapid, stimulus‐locked Ca2+ transients in NTS astrocytes that are abolished by AMPA receptor antagonists but unaffected by NMDA or metabotropic glutamate receptor blockade (McDougal et al. 2011). Structurally, astrocytes ensheathe vagal terminals in the NTS (Chounlamountry and Kessler 2011; SheikhBahaei et al. 2018), positioning them to sense glutamate released from TS inputs and, thus, to shape TS–NTS synaptic transmission (MacDonald and Ellacott 2020; Martinez and Kline 2021; McDougal et al. 2011). They influence synaptic transmission via lactate shuttling (Nagase et al. 2014), ATP release, and regulation of extracellular glutamate through EAAT2/GLT‐1 (Matott et al. 2017; Matott et al. 2016). Disrupting astrocyte metabolism or EAAT2 function alters NTS neuronal activity and blunts baroreflex and respiratory reflexes (Forsberg et al. 2017; Mastitskaya et al. 2020; Matott et al. 2016; Yamamoto and Mifflin 2018). In vivo, NTS astrocytes are activated by 5‐HT and vagal‐derived glutamate and modulate baroreflex sensitivity (Mastitskaya et al. 2020), while astrocytes in the lateral parabrachial nucleus are required for normal feeding control (Mishra et al. 2024). Together, these data support the concept of NTS astrocytes as ‘homeostatic integrators’ of multiple afferent signals (Lamy 2012; MacDonald and Ellacott 2020; Martinez and Kline 2021); however, to our knowledge, no reports have implicated CCK in brainstem astrocytic signalling.

Finally, respiratory control is another homeostatic system where CCK plays a role, generating ventilatory changes and senses of dyspnoea in humans (Katzman et al. 2002), and increasing respiratory rate and amplitude in in vivo/in vitro rodent experiments (Ellenberger and Smith 1999). However, the cellular mechanisms by which CCK‐sensitive vagal and NTS circuits influence brainstem respiratory circuits, including the inspiratory preBötzinger Complex (PBC), remain undefined.

Despite this large body of work on CCK actions at peripheral and central vagal terminals, and on DVC output, how these actions are distributed across an intact vagal–brainstem unit—JNG somata, central vagal terminals, second‐order NTS neurons, ventrolateral medullary neurons and the surrounding glia—has remained experimentally difficult to resolve. In vivo preparations preserve native connectivity, but offer limited spatial and temporal resolution of somatic, presynaptic and glial events, and constrain pharmacological access to deep brainstem nuclei (Nimmerjahn and Bergles 2015; Smith and Feldman 1987). Acute medullary slices improve optical and pharmacological control, but sever the JNG and long vagal rootlets, preventing study of intact vagal terminals (Appleyard et al. 2005; Baptista et al. 2005; Kondo et al. 2024; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Smith and Feldman 1987; Winzenried et al. 2025). Organotypic medullary cultures sustain NTS and PBC microcircuits for weeks in vitro (Phillips et al. 2016), but lack attached vagal sensory ganglia, while acute brainstem–cranial nerve explants retain the JNG only for short‐term recordings (Smith et al. 1998). We address this gap with a long‐term organotypic JNG–brainstem slice culture amenable to viral GECI transduction and multiplex Ca2+ imaging with pharmacological dissection across JNG somata, central vagal terminals, NTS neurons, astrocytes and the PBC. Using this preparation, we (i) verify the preserved connectivity and functionality of GECI‐labelled JNG neurons and central vagal terminals; (ii) identify CCK‐responsive neuronal populations in the JNG, dorsal medulla and ventrolateral medulla that remain active under tetrodotoxin, indicating direct postsynaptic CCK receptor activation; (iii) map central vagal terminals directly sensitive to CCK, including TTX‐resistant fibres, consistent with presynaptic CCK receptors; (iv) show, using simultaneous neuronal‐glial Ca2+ imaging, that CCK and Substance P (SP) selectively recruit medullary glial networks, whereas thyrotropin‐releasing hormone (TRH) and muscarine activate neurons without engaging glia; and (v) demonstrate functional integration of our preparation with the central respiratory network and where CCK acts as its excitatory modulator, increasing the frequency of spontaneous respiratory‐like bursting in the PBC.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of JNG‐BSTEM Slice Cultures

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the University of Copenhagen animal care committee's regulations. Slice cultures, containing the JNG still attached to the brainstem via vagal rootlets, were prepared using United States Naval Medical Research Institute (NMRI) mice of both sexes obtained from Taconic Biosciences (Denmark), postnatal ages P2.5–P10.5. The induction of general anaesthesia was performed by placing the animal in a closed glass container (100 mL) containing regular air and cotton wool at the bottom. About 100 μL of isoflurane (FORENE 100%, Abbott, Solna, Sweden) was dripped onto the cotton wool, and the animal was tested for pain response by tail‐pinch until general anaesthesia was achieved. Thereafter, euthanasia was performed by incising the thorax with a scalpel and rapidly removing the lungs and the heart. Mice were hereafter dissected in sterile‐filtered chilled dissection artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing the following: 135 mM glycerol, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 5 mM HEPES acid, 15 mM HEPES base, 25 mM D‐glucose, 5 mM sodium ascorbate, 2 mM thiourea, 3 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM MgSO4 and 0.5 mM CaCl2; final osmolarity adjusted to 300–310 mOsm using glycerol or water, pH 7.3, equilibrated by bubbling with 95% O2/5% CO2. The JNG was isolated using a ventral dissection approach; the vagal nerve peripheral to the JNG was cut close to the ganglion, the surrounding bone was removed, and the entire brainstem, with the JNG on both sides still attached, was removed from the skull. Using a vibrating microtome (ThermoFisher Scientific Microm 650 V, RRID:SCR_008452), a single, transverse medullary brainstem slice was cut (400 μm thickness), cutting from the rostral side of the mounted neuraxis, with one cut placed at a level where vagal rootlets connected to the JNG were visible at the surface‐cut, and a second cut 400 μm further along the neuroaxis. This procedure produced a JNG‐BSTEM preparation with one or two JNG attached (depending on the success of dissecting one or both JNGs) via intact vagal rootlets. After the acute dissection, three JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures were placed on semi‐porous culture well inserts using the Stoppini interface method (Millipore catalogue #PIC03050, RRID:SCR_008983). Cultures were kept in culture media containing: 95% Neurobasal‐A medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalogue#10888‐022, RRID:SCR_008452), 2% B‐27 supplement (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalogue #17504044, RRID:SCR_008452), 2 mM GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalogue #35050‐038, RRID:SCR_008452), 0.5 μM T3 (Sigma‐Aldrich, catalogue #T6397, RRID:SCR_008988), 0.5 μM T4 (Sigma‐Aldrich, catalogue #T1775, RRID:SCR_008988), 1 U/mL Catalase (Sigma‐Aldrich, catalogue #C40, RRID:SCR_008988), 200 U/mL penicillin, 5 μg/mL streptomycin and 10 mM HEPES and pH was set at 7.25. The cultures were kept in a humidified incubator at 35°C, with 5% CO2, until their use for experimentation and given fresh medium every 48 h.

Neuronal Labelling With AAVs

JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures were transduced with viral AAV constructs containing the genes for jGCaMP8s driven by a synapsin promotor (pGP‐AAV‐syn‐jGCaMP8s‐WPRE, AAV9. Addgene, catalogue#: 162374‐AAV9, abbr: AAV9‐syn‐jGCaMP8s), jRCaMP1b driven by a synapsin promotor (pAAV.Syn.NES‐jRCaMP1b.WPRE.SV40, AAV9, Addgene, catalogue#: 100851, abbr: AAV9‐syn‐jRCaMP1b) and jGCaMP8m fused to the histone H2B driven by a synapsin promotor (AAV‐Syn‐H2B‐jGCaMP8m‐WPRE, AAV9. Addgene, catalogue#: 176748‐AAV9, abbr: AAV9‐syn‐H2B‐jGCaMP8m), and jGCaMP8m driven by an astrocyte‐specific GFAB (GfaABC1D) promotor (pZac2.1‐GfaABC1D‐lck‐jGCaMP8m, AAV9. Addgene, catalogue#: 176760‐AAV9, abbr: AAV9‐GFAB‐jGCaMP8m). The AAV viruses were diluted in ultrapure water (final concentration 0.3–1.0 × 1013 vg/mL), and cultures were transduced the day after they were prepared, either by adding a single drop (1–2 μL, drop‐transduction) or 10–20 microdrops from the tip of a broken micropipette (microdrop‐transduction) of virus solution directly on top of the BSTEM or JNG part of the culture. Cultures were used for experiments 7–14 days after transduction.

Recirculating aCSF and Electrophysiology

Cultures were placed in an in vitro chamber with a recirculating system (29°C) filled with a normal‐aCSF containing the following: 120 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 1.0 mM D‐glucose, 1 mM ascorbic acid, 1.2 mM MgCl2 and 2.6 mM CaCl2 or high‐excitability aCSF (high‐aCSF) containing the following: 124 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 5 mM KH2PO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 25 mM D‐glucose, 1 mM ascorbic acid, 1 mM MgCl2 and 1.5 mM CaCl2 (all from Sigma‐Aldrich, RRID:SCR_008988), with pH 7.4, equilibrated by bubbling with 95% O2/5% CO2.

Electrical field‐stimulation was performed using a sharp bipolar tungsten electrode with the +/− poles ~500 μm apart. Trains (1–40 pulses) of unipolar pulses with constant voltage (interval: 20 ms, pulse duration: 2 ms, amplitude: 4.5–9 V) were used to stimulate fibres and somas between the two poles, using a stimulus isolation unit gated by a waveform generator (A.M.P.I. ISO‐Flex isolator, RRID:SCR_018945, AMPI Master 8 generator, RRID:SCR_018889). Stimulus electrodes were guided visually to the desired region using an MPC‐200 micromanipulator system (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA, United States) under 5× and 10× objective magnification.

Drug Application

CCK‐8 (50 nM, TOCRIS, CAS:25126‐32‐3, RRID:SCR_003689), SP acetate salt hydrate (100 nM, Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS:137348‐11‐9, RRID:SCR_008988), TRH (500 nM, Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS:24305‐27‐9, RRID:SCR_008988) and (±)‐Muscarine chloride hydrate (100 μM, Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS:2936‐25‐6, RRID:SCR_008988) were prepared in DI water as stock solutions and diluted into aCSF and bath applied or pressure injected using a patch pipette. Glutamatergic transmission was blocked using NBQX disodium salt (20 μM, AMPA antagonist, Tocris Bioscience, catalogue #1044, prepared in DI water as stock solution, RRID:SCR_003689) and CPP (20 μM, NMDA antagonist, Tocris Bioscience, catalogue #0173, prepared in DI water as stock solution, RRID:SCR_003689) bath applied. Sodium action potentials were blocked by TTX (1 μM, Tocris Bioscience, catalogue #1078, prepared in DI water as a stock solution, RRID:SCR_003689) bath applied.

Imaging of Ca2+

Imaging of GECIs was done using wide‐field fluorescence on a fixed‐stage upright microscope (modified Olympus BX51, RRID:SCR_018949), illuminated by a metal halide light source (PhotoFluor II, 89North, Burlington, United States) or a blue (470 nm) LED light source (M470L2, Thorlabs, Newton, NJ, United States). Red and green channel fluorescence was visualised using a dual‐bandpass filter set (Chroma 59022: excitation dual band‐pass 450–490 nm/555–590 nm, emission dual band‐pass 500–543 nm/603–665 nm). Time‐series acquisition of all three types of GECIs was performed with a sCMOS camera (Neo DC‐152Q, Andor Technology, Belfast, United Kingdom) controlled by SOLIS software (Andor Technology) at 10 frames/s. Imaging protocols employed ×5 (NA 0.15) dry Zeiss objective and 10× (NA 0.3), 20× (NA 0.5) Olympus water immersion objectives.

Immunofluorescence

To visualise jGCaMP8s expression following pGP‐AAV‐syn‐jGCaMP8s‐WPRE transduction, fixed (4% paraformaldehyde) JNG‐BSTEM cultures were processed for standard immunofluorescence. Briefly, somatic and fibre labelling was performed using a primary rabbit recombinant anti‐GFP monoclonal antibody (1:100; Thermo Fisher, Catalogue# G10362, RRID: AB_2536526) by washing the tissue in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) for 2 h, blocking with 3% goat serum in 1% Triton‐X in PBS for 60 min, and incubating the tissue with the primary antibody for 48 h, at 4°C, rotating in a roller drum in 1% goat serum in 1% Triton‐X in PBS. Following a wash in PBS, primary antibodies were detected using an iFluor 594‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:333; AAT Bioquest, Catalogue# 16698) by incubating the tissue with the secondary antibody for 48 h, at 4°C, rotating in a roller drum in 1% goat serum in 1% Triton‐X in PBS.

Experimental Design and Statistical Analysis

Fluorescent output from GECIs was measured in absolute values from a 14‐bit sCMOS camera and not bleach‐corrected. The relatively slow Ca2+ transients (s to min) in response to neuropeptide application or stimulus trains were plotted graphically and calculated as fluorescence relative to peak‐response values (F‐Rel) and not ΔF/F, as this measure carries no further quantitative value. Changes in fluorescence from different measured images were normalised to the same peak amplitude (F‐Norm). The intensity values used for the calculations were obtained by summing the field of view fluorescence using rectangular or oval regions (referred to as ‘Region Of Interest’ [ROI]) either encompassing equal‐sized (control vs. drug) ROIs over specific neuronal fields (e.g., fibres, JNG) or single 100 × 100 μm ROIs over astrocytic fields.

Optical data was analysed offline in a non‐blinded fashion using ImageJ 1.54j (ImageJ, RRID:SCR_003070), and Igor Pro 9 (IGOR Pro, RRID:SCR_000325). All statistical tests and graphics were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, RRID:SCR_002798). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; subsequently, groups were compared using either an unpaired Student's t‐test, an unpaired Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed rank test or Ordinary one‐way ANOVA as appropriate. Paired comparisons of respiratory burst frequency before and after CCK application were performed using the Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed‐rank test, with the W statistic and p value reported. Results are, in the text, reported as mean ± standard deviation, and graphically as mean ± SEM N = number of cultures. Statistical significance was set at p = 0.05 based on the culture number (ns—p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Establishment and Characterisation of a Novel JNG‐BSTEM Slice Culture Model

To investigate brainstem CCK signalling in a physiologically relevant context, we developed an organotypic in vitro preparation that preserves the anatomical and functional continuity between peripheral vagal sensory neurons in the JNG and their central brainstem targets. Earlier acute brainstem–cranial nerve explants retained vagal connectivity in a horizontal orientation for short‐term electrophysiological recording (Smith et al. 1998), and various NTS–DMV slice configurations have been used to dissect vagal circuitry (Appleyard et al. 2005; Baptista et al. 2005; Kondo et al. 2024; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Winzenried et al. 2025). Building on these approaches, our preparation keeps both jugular and nodose ganglia attached while extending the recording window to days and weeks. Careful ventral dissection along the occipital bone and tympanic bulla interface allowed for bilateral isolation of the JNG attached to the brainstem of newborn mice (Figure 1A). The combined jugular and nodose ganglia appeared as a mass of large neurons in a fibrous capsule, with a distally projecting vagal (X) nerve, multiple proximal rootlets connecting the JNG to the brainstem, and occasionally accessory (XI) and hypoglossal (XII) nerves embedded in adjacent meninges (Figure 1B). The tissue was subsequently vibratome‐sectioned (400 μm) at the level of the JNG rootlets (observed under a stereomicroscope during vibratome cutting), thus retaining the JNG attached to the medulla (Figure 1C, schematic, JNG‐BSTEM). The JNG‐BSTEM slice was placed under culture conditions and remained viable and maintained healthy morphology after 8–33 days in vitro (8–33 DIV), as evidenced by the brightfield image of the cultured slice (Figure 1C, right panel). To determine whether the afferent projections from the ganglia into the brainstem were preserved within the JNG‐BSTEM slice culture, we selectively transduced JNG neurons with the GECI AAV9‐syn‐jGCaMP8s using microdrop application onto the JNG (Figure 1D, top schematic). After 7–14 DIV, immunohistochemical analysis using a GFP antibody revealed robust labelling of JNG neuronal somata and their axonal projections (N = 7 cultures). Histochemical imaging revealed a dense fasciculation of afferent fibres (in four to five tracts) travelling through the TS and terminating in a dense arborisation within the NTS and intermediate part of the medulla (IP, Figure 1D, main panel). Higher magnification insets highlight the distinct morphology of the axon bundles in the TS and the complex innervation pattern with synapse‐like boutons within the NTS (Figure 1D, NTS, arrows) and the IP, confirming that the JNG‐BSTEM slice culture preserves primary vagal afferent pathways.

Subsequently, we assessed the functional properties of this circuit using Ca2+ imaging. To electrically activate JNG neurons, a bipolar stimulus electrode (Stim. +/−) was placed on each side of the JNG in JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures microdrop‐transduced with AAV9‐syn‐jGCaMP8s (Figure 1E). Stimulating the JNG with pulse trains with an increasing number of pulses (1…40#, 1 ms duration, 20 ms interval) elicited Ca2+ transients with progressively larger amplitudes in cumulated regions encompassing multiple JNG neurons in the JNG (Figure 1E, dotted oval, N = 4 cultures). The amplitude levelled off to a plateau at ~20‐pulse stimulus trains in group data (Figure 1F, right). A 10‐pulse stimulus gave rise to Ca2+ transients with a rise time of 216 ± 12 ms, half‐width of 1622 ± 216 ms and a single exponential decay time constant of 1738 ± 394 ms in a cumulated region encompassing all jGCaMP8s‐labelled JNG neurons. We therefore used 10‐pulse trains for subsequent experiments as a reproducible mid‐range stimulus below saturation. Additionally, 10‐pulse stimulus produced Ca2+ transients with a rise time of 199 ± 17 ms, a half‐width of 1738 ± 477 ms and a single exponential decay time constant of 2028 ± 758 ms in a cumulated region encompassing jGCaMP8s‐labelled TS regions in the BSTEM (all parameters not different from regions encompassing JNG neurons, unpaired Student's t‐test, t (6) = 0.4–1,7, p = 0.15–0.67 (ns), N = 4 cultures). The effect of varying the interval between pulse trains on Ca2+ transient amplitudes was tested using 10‐pulse trains (Figure 1G) in JNG neurons. Notably, Ca2+ transient amplitudes decreased markedly during the first few stimuli (1–3), with a greater decrease the shorter the stimulus intervals (Figure 1G, right), and a plateau was reached around 30 s after stimulus onset (N = 3 cultures). This interval dependence guided our choice of stimulation spacing to minimise activity‐dependent rundown during experiments.

Thus, the preparations captured GECI‐labelled JNG neurons and central vagal terminals in the NTS, facilitating investigation of the effects of CCK on Ca2+ dynamics in the somatic and presynaptic compartments of JNG and brainstem neurons.

Functional Glutamatergic Synaptic Connectivity Between Vagal Afferents and Brainstem Neurons

To determine if central vagal terminals form functional synaptic connections with brainstem neurons in JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures, we used dual‐colour Ca2+ imaging. We differentially labelled the presynaptic and postsynaptic components by microdrop application of the green Ca2+ indicator AAV9‐syn‐jGCaMP8s to the JNG and drop application of the red Ca2+ indicator AAV9‐syn‐jRCaMP1b to the brainstem slice (Figure 2A). This enabled optical separation of activity from vagal afferent fibres and local brainstem neurons during alternating tandem imaging sessions. Under control conditions, electrical stimulation of the JNG elicited robust, time‐locked Ca2+ transients in both the green‐labelled afferent fibres projecting into the NTS and the red‐labelled somata of surrounding brainstem neurons (Figure 2B, N = 4 cultures). The overlay of signals demonstrates the spatial proximity of the responsive fibres and neurons. To confirm that the activation of brainstem neurons was driven by glutamatergic synaptic transmission from the vagal afferents, we bath‐applied the ionotropic glutamate receptor antagonists NBQX (20 μM) and CPP (20 μM) to block AMPA/kainate and NMDA receptors, respectively. Following pharmacological blockade, the evoked Ca2+ transients in the postsynaptic brainstem neurons (red) were nearly abolished (Figure 2B, right panel; Figure 2C, bottom red trace, Figure 2D, N = 4 cultures). In contrast, the Ca2+ transients in the presynaptic vagal fibres (green) remained intact with a small bleaching‐related reduction in amplitude (~15% reduction, Figure 2C, green trace, Figure 2D), indicating that axonal propagation was preserved while glutamatergic synaptic transmission was blocked.

CCK Evokes Nuclear Ca2+ Transients in Subpopulations of Brainstem Neurons

To determine whether CCK directly modulates neuronal activity in JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures, we utilised a nuclear‐targeted Ca2+ indicator to track cellular responses with single‐cell resolution. We drop‐transduced the brainstem slice with AAV9‐syn‐H2B‐jGCaMP8m, which restricts the sensor to the neuronal nucleus, facilitating the identification and tracking of individual somas during large‐area imaging (Figure 3A). We applied CCK (50 nM) to the bath while recording Ca2+ dynamics throughout the slice at low magnification. CCK application elicited robust, long‐lasting Ca2+ increases in a distinct population of neurons located in the brainstem and JNG (Figure 3B). The responses were characterised by a slow rise (~60 s) and a transient response that tapered off during continued application (N = 4 cultures). To assess whether this activation was likely driven by direct postsynaptic action of CCK or via network‐driven synaptic activity, we repeated the experiment in the presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM) to block voltage‐gated sodium channels and silence action potential generation (N = 4 cultures). Strikingly, the robust nuclear Ca2+ transients persisted in the presence of TTX (Figure 3C), suggesting that CCK directly activates these neurons postsynaptically independent of network transmission. Spatial analysis of the responsive population revealed a specific anatomical distribution, where CCK‐sensitive neurons are clustered primarily in the dorsomedial, ventrolateral regions of the slice, corresponding anatomically to the vestibular nuclei, NTS and the ventrolateral medulla (VLM) (Figure 3D,E). An overlay of the responsive cells in control (red) and TTX (green) conditions demonstrates a high degree of overlap (Figure 3F), confirming that the direct postsynaptic responders constitute the primary population activated by CCK in this preparation. When superfused with a modified aCSF designed to elevate neuronal excitability (high‐aCSF), the cultures show irregular respiratory‐related burst activity. This is exemplified in Figure 3G, showing an endogenously bursting and later in the same experiment, a CCK + TTX‐responding neuron and the associated Ca2+ transients. In group data, Ca2+ transients in single neurons associated with endogenous bursting were smaller (34% ± 17%) than slow Ca2+ transients (normalised to 100% ± 37%) in the same neurons in response to ‘CCK in TTX’ (Figure 3G, right panel).

Distinct Populations of Vagal Afferent Fibres Respond to Electrical Stimulation and CCK

We next investigated whether subsets of brainstem vagal afferent fibres, coming from the JNG, directly respond to CCK. To test this, we first electrically stimulated the JNG to identify almost all excitable fibres projecting to the NTS, followed by bath application of CCK (50 nM, Figure 4A, N = 8 cultures). Electrical stimulation revealed a dense network of responsive fibres throughout the NTS (Figure 4B). Subsequent bath application of CCK elicited robust Ca2+ transients in a subset of fibres (Figure 4C). The fibres activated by CCK (Figure 4D) showed some overlap with the broad population of fibres activated by electrical stimulation (Figure 4B), as visualised in the merged image where a few fibres appear yellow (overlap) compared with the distinct red (stimulated) and green (CCK‐responsive) populations (Figure 4E). Since CCK was present in the bath the observed effect on fibres within the brainstem could in principle be due to CCK acting at the somatic level in the JNG with a propagating Ca2+ signal. To determine if CCK might in fact have a direct presynaptic effect on central vagal terminals, we employed a local puff application of CCK during ongoing electrical stimulation trains (Figure 4F, N = 5 cultures). This revealed that two distinct CCK‐responding fibre types can exist in the same field of view: Fibre‐1 responded both to the electrical stimulation pulses and the CCK puff (Figure 4H, yellow trace). Conversely, Fibre‐2 showed no response to the electrical stimulation but exhibited a large, slow Ca2+ transient immediately following the CCK puff (Figure 4H, green trace). Finally, to determine if the CCK‐induced activation of these fibres required action potential propagation or did indeed involve a direct effect on central vagal terminals, we applied CCK in the presence of TTX (1 μM) (Figure 4K, N = 4 cultures). While electrical stimulation failed to evoke responses in the presence of TTX (not shown), CCK application still elicited robust Ca2+ transients in some afferent fibres (Figure 4M). Image analysis confirmed that in the absence of sodium‐channel‐dependent spikes, a dense network of fibres responded to CCK (Figure 4N). An overlay with fibres stimulated prior to TTX application showed that the CCK‐responsive fibres (green) partially overlapped with the electrically excitable population (red) (Figure 4O). These results suggest that CCK activates a specific subpopulation of vagal afferents via a mechanism that does not require somatic action potentials, likely acting directly on presynaptic receptors at the central vagal terminals.

Differential Activation of Brainstem Neurons and Astrocytes by CCK, SP and TRH

To investigate whether CCK may produce Ca2+ signalling in not only dorsal and ventrolateral brainstem neurons, but also brainstem astrocytes, we selectively labelled brainstem neurons and astrocytes with two separate GECIs. We utilised a multiplexed illumination and image acquisition approach where brainstem neurons were labelled with the red‐shifted indicator jRCaMP1b (Drop‐BSTEM‐AAV9‐syn‐jRCaMP1b) and astrocytes were targeted with the green indicator jGCaMP8m (Drop‐BSTEM‐AAV9‐GFAB‐jGCaMP8m, Figure 5A). We then, in the same JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures, compared neuronal and astrocytic Ca2+ dynamics in response to sequential CCK, SP and TRH applications. All experiments were performed in the presence of TTX (1 μM) to block action potentials and isolate direct postsynaptic effects. Application of CCK induced robust Ca2+ transients in scattered neurons in the NTS and ventrolateral area and, surprisingly, also in the adjacent astrocytic networks (N = 6 cultures). A clear, slow‐onset Ca2+ wave was observed in the GFAB‐positive astrocytic network (Figure 5B, black trace; Figure 5K), which coincided with distinct Ca2+ increases in nearby neurons (Figure 5B, blue trace; Figure 5G). The overlay of signals demonstrates that CCK activates a mixed population of neurons and astrocytes in the NTS region. Similarly, in the same preparation, SP elicited a strong, synchronised response in both cell types, with a clear astrocytic Ca2+ signal (Figure 5C, grey trace; Figure 5L), accompanied by large‐amplitude transients in a subset of neurons (Figure 5C, red trace; Figure 5H). The spatial distribution of SP‐responsive cells showed some overlap between the neuronal and astrocytic networks (Figure 5Q). In contrast, TRH application produced a strictly neuronal response. While TRH evoked clear Ca2+ transients in brainstem neurons (Figure 5D, green trace; Figure 5I), the surrounding astrocytes remained unresponsive (Figure 5D, black trace; Figure 5M). This selective activation is further highlighted in the overlay images, where TRH‐responsive neurons are clearly visible against a dark background of silent astrocytes (Figure 5R) and in the group data (Figure 5E). The rise time of the neuropeptide‐induced Ca2+ transients in neurons and astrocytes with TTX present were: CCK‐neurons 28.5 ± 8.8 s, CCK‐astrocytes 43.6 ± 3.3 s, SP‐neurons 17.9 ± 3.4 s, CCK‐astrocytes 26.3 ± 1.9 s, TRH‐neurons 12.9 ± 4.2 s, in cumulated regions encompassing neuropeptide‐responding areas, with longer rise times in CCK and SP‐responding astrocytic regions compared with neuronal regions (CCK: unpaired Mann–Whitney test, U (10,26) = 0, p = 0.03, SP: unpaired Mann–Whitney test, U (10,26) = 0, p = 0.03, N = 4 cultures). Finally, to determine if individual neurons could integrate multiple peptidergic signals, we analysed the overlap of responses in the same field of view. We identified examples of single NTS neurons that responded to two combinations of all three peptides—SP and TRH (Figure 5N, N = 16 neurons in four cultures), CCK and SP (Figure 5O, N = 14 neurons in four cultures), CCK and TRH (not shown, N = 8 neurons in four cultures).

Muscarine Activates NTS Neurons With Oscillatory Dynamics but Fails to Recruit Brainstem Astrocytes

To further demonstrate that widespread neuronal activation is not the cause of the CCK/SP‐induced astrocytic Ca2+ transients, we investigated the response to muscarinic receptor activation and compared it with the CCK‐induced response. Using the dual‐colour imaging strategy established previously, brainstem neurons were labelled with jRCaMP1b (red) and astrocytes with GFAB‐jGCaMP8m (green, Figure 6A). Bath application of the muscarinic agonist muscarine (100 μM) in the presence of TTX (1 μM) elicited robust Ca2+ activity in a specific population of NTS neurons (Figure 6B, N = 5 cultures). Unlike the sustained Ca2+ plateau observed with CCK, the muscarinic response was characterised by distinct, varied low‐to high‐frequency oscillatory Ca2+ transients (0.8–26 bursts/min) in the responding neurons (Figure 6B, red traces; Figure 6F). Strikingly, while the neuronal network showed vigorous activation, the surrounding astrocytes remained completely silent during muscarine exposure (Figure 6B, black trace; Figure 6D,I), indicating that muscarinic signalling in this context does not directly trigger Ca2+ waves in NTS astrocytes. Following washout and subsequent application of CCK (50 nM) in the same slice, we observed a markedly different activation pattern. CCK stimulation recruited a broad population of both neurons and astrocytes (Figure 6C). The neuronal response to CCK was again characterised by a slower, non‐oscillatory Ca2+ rise (Figure 6C, blue trace), while the astrocytic network exhibited a widespread, coordinated Ca2+ elevation (Figure 6C, black trace; Figure 6J). Overlay images of the active populations reveal that while both agonists target neurons within the NTS, the muscarine‐responsive neurons (red) and CCK‐responsive neurons (blue) represent partially overlapping but distinct populations (Figure 6E). Furthermore, the complete absence of astrocytic activation during muscarine treatment (Figure 6H, red channel) contrasts sharply with the robust astrocytic response to CCK (Figure 6H, blue channel), demonstrating that NTS neurons can exhibit distinct firing modes (oscillatory vs. sustained) depending on the neuromodulator, and that astrocytic recruitment is not a generic consequence of neuronal hyperactivity but a specific feature of peptidergic signalling like that of CCK and SP.

CCK Modulates Respiratory Rhythm Frequency in the PBC

Finally, to assess whether the CCK‐responsive vagal circuitry in the JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures model is functionally integrated with the central respiratory network, we examined the effect of CCK on spontaneous respiratory‐related rhythmic activity. The brainstem slices were superfused with a modified aCSF designed to elevate neuronal excitability (high‐aCSF), which is known to facilitate robust respiratory rhythm generation in slice preparations. We utilised the green Ca2+ indicator AAV9‐syn‐jGCaMP8s to monitor population activity across the ventral respiratory column (Figure 7A). Under baseline conditions in high‐aCSF, we observed spontaneous, rhythmic Ca2+ bursting in a discrete region of the ventrolateral medulla corresponding to the PBC (Figure 7B, red trace; Figure 7F). This endogenous rhythm was somewhat irregular but persistent, likely due to the position of the PBC, which was not always captured in this JNG–brainstem bridge optimised preparation. However, upon bath application of CCK (50 nM), we observed a significant increase in the frequency of these respiratory‐like bursts (Figure 7B, red trace). This modulation coincided with the activation of the distinct CCK‐responsive neuronal population in the JNG, dorsal medulla (NTS) and ventrolateral regions, which exhibited a slow, sustained Ca2+ rise typical of the CCK response described earlier (Figure 7B, blue trace; Figure 7G). Quantification of the burst frequency revealed a consistent and statistically significant increase following CCK application across all slices tested (Figure 7C; Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed‐rank test, W = 74, p = 0.0015. The median difference between the paired observations was 24.20. N = 12 cultures). The overlay of the active populations demonstrates the spatial relationship between the rhythm‐generating PBC network (red), the broad population of transduced neurons (green), and the specific subset of CCK‐responsive neurons (blue) that could be involved in mediating this modulatory effect (Figure 7D). These results indicate that the JNG‐BSTEM slice cultures include essential regions for central respiratory rhythm generation, and that CCK acts as an excitatory modulator of this network, possibly through CCK‐responsive brainstem neurons.

Discussion

The present study introduces a JNG–BSTEM slice culture preparation that preserves intact vagal afferent pathways, enabling simultaneous imaging of neuronal and astrocytic network responses to CCK. Within this platform, we find that CCK directly activates defined neuronal populations in the dorsal medulla under action potential blockade, selectively recruits a subpopulation of central vagal terminals, and elicits robust Ca2+ waves in astrocytic networks. We show that CCK increases the frequency of spontaneous respiratory‐like bursting in the PBC, indicating functional coupling between CCK‐sensitive vagal–NTS microcircuits and the inspiratory rhythm generator. These observations are consistent with and complement prior acute slice and in vivo work on CCK actions at central vagal terminals and DVC output (Baptista et al. 2005; Ellenberger and Smith 1999; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Simasko and Ritter 2003; Simasko et al. 2002; Winzenried et al. 2025). We further discuss how this preparation refines current models of CCK signalling in central vagal terminals and NTS circuits, implicates astrocytes in peptidergic processing and suggests new mechanisms by which postprandial CCK may coordinate feeding, visceral sensation and respiratory control.

Functional Validation of a Novel Jugular/Nodose Ganglion–Brainstem Slice Culture

The JNG‐BSTEM slice culture occupies a specific niche among existing brainstem preparations. Acute transverse medullary slices sustain PBC inspiratory rhythmogenesis (Smith and Feldman 1987) but lack attached sensory ganglia; acute NTS slices, used to dissect CCK actions at central vagal terminals (Appleyard et al. 2005; Baptista et al. 2005; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Winzenried et al. 2025) similarly sever long‐range vagal projections; acute brainstem–cranial nerve explants preserve the vagus and other cranial nerves, but cannot sustain longer protocols (Smith et al. 1998); organotypic medullary cultures extend the exploratory window to weeks but lack attached vagal ganglia (Phillips et al. 2016); in vivo preparations retain native connectivity but offer limited optical and pharmacological access at depth (Nimmerjahn and Bergles 2015; Smith and Feldman 1987). By retaining the fused JNG via intact rootlets and remaining viable for weeks (Figure 1), the present preparation combines the longevity of organotypic cultures with the afferent connectivity of explants, allowing simultaneous experimental access to JNG somata, central vagal terminals and second‐order brainstem networks in a single culture.

Functionally, we demonstrate that electrically evoked activity in GECI‐labelled JNG neurons propagates along TS fibres and elicits glutamate‐dependent Ca2+ transients in second‐order brainstem neurons that are abolished by AMPA and NMDA receptor antagonists, while presynaptic responses persist (Figure 2). This confirms preservation of vagal afferent connectivity within the slice and recapitulates the basic organisation described in acute slice and anatomical studies (Baptista et al. 2005; Berthoud et al. 2025; Berthoud and Neuhuber 2000; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Winzenried et al. 2025). The capacity for long‐term culture also aligns our platform with established PBC‐containing organotypic slices that generate stable respiratory‐like rhythms for weeks (Phillips et al. 2016), but with the added advantage of an attached, experimentally accessible sensory ganglion.

The broader value of this methodological advance is that it removes the trade‐off between anatomical integrity and imaging resolution when interrogating vagal circuits: JNG somata, central vagal terminals, second‐order neurons, glia and respiratory networks can be examined simultaneously in a single preparation, enabling multi‐cellular analysis of the gut–brain signalling. Translationally, sustaining intact vagal–brainstem units for weeks opens the door to modelling chronic adaptations of the gut–brain axis in obesity, diabetes or inflammatory states—conditions in which persistent changes in vagal firing and NTS processing are thought to contribute to dysregulated appetite, dyspepsia and cardiovascular dysfunction (Doba and Reis 1973; MacDonald and Ellacott 2020; Owyang and Heldsinger 2011; Schreihofer and Sved 1992; Sved et al. 1985; Talman et al. 1981).

Limitations should be noted. First, the transverse slice samples only a segment of the elongated NTS, biasing the viscerotropic representation toward the level of the vagal rootlets. Second, post‐slicing fibre growth and synaptogenesis may produce de novo circuit reorganisation that does not fully recapitulate native in vivo connectivity. Third, the use of neonatal mice (P2.5–P10.5) means synaptic and receptor may not match adult states. Despite these constraints, the JNG‐BSTEM preparation now makes the diverse JNG neuron classes (Hermes et al. 2014; Kupari et al. 2019) experimentally accessible without severed central axons, complementing acute slices, in vivo imaging and PBC‐only organotypic cultures.

Direct Central Actions of CCK on Brainstem Neurons

A central observation is that CCK drives Ca2+ transients in discrete subpopulations of NTS, vestibular and ventrolateral medullary neurons in a TTX‐resistant manner (Figure 3). Nuclear‐targeted GECI imaging reveals slow, long‐lasting Ca2+ transients in these neurons that persist when voltage‐gated sodium channels are blocked. One interpretation of the CCK response under TTX is a CCK‐mediated presynaptic effect, since CCK enhances glutamatergic EPSCs in acute NTS slices via presynaptic mechanisms involving PKA, PLC, L‐type Ca2+ channels and ER Ca2+ release (Baptista et al. 2005; Holmes et al. 2009; Rogers and Hermann 2008; Winzenried et al. 2025). Interestingly, our data show that the CCK‐induced Ca2+ transients under TTX are larger than the Ca2+ transients associated with endogenous burst firing (Figure 3), which is hard to reconcile with a pure presynaptic effect on glutamate release. This difference in Ca2+ transient amplitudes may indicate that CCK receptors are also expressed postsynaptically on these cells and can drive Ca2+ influx, possibly via CCK2 receptors, independent of action potential‐mediated network transmission, as has been shown in acute slices containing the NTS (Branchereau et al. 1993). Receptor blocking experiments are needed to parse out the pre‐ and postsynaptic contributions to CCK‐signalling in this region. Prior work has emphasised CCK actions at peripheral vagal endings and central presynaptic sites: CCK1 receptors on vagal afferents promote satiety and gastric inhibition (Liddle 1995; Owyang and Heldsinger 2011; Schwartz and Moran 1994). Our data show that CCK evokes Ca2+ transients in defined neuronal populations of the NTS and ventrolateral medulla in an action‐potential‐independent manner, consistent with a DVC that integrates hormonal and afferent signals to regulate feeding, cardiorespiratory output and autonomic state (Browning and Carson 2021; Chrobok et al. 2022; Holmes et al. 2009; Strain et al. 2024).

Because intravenous CCK‐4 in humans elicits panic‐like anxiety and subjective dyspnoea through central mechanisms (Katzman et al. 2002; Shlik et al. 1997), and CCK‐8s applied directly to the DVC alters gastric tone via central vagal circuits (Holmes et al. 2009), central CCK receptors may mediate significant autonomic and interoceptive effects even when peripheral vagal sensory transmission is reduced—for example after partial vagotomy or in neuropathies (Kaczynska and Szereda‐Przestaszewska 2015; Shiraishi 1990). Plausible sources of the central CCK include the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN), which sends CCK‐producing projections to the brainstem (Kiss et al. 1984); local diffusion from the area postrema, given dense CCK‐immunoreactivity across the DVC (Fodor et al. 1994); and perivascular or circumventricular delivery during high postprandial levels, although circulating CCK‐8 appears to penetrate the blood‐CSF barrier poorly (van de Lisdonk and Li 2024; Zhu et al. 1986). Long‐range cortical or mesolimbic transmission, in which CCK conveys broader physiological and emotional state signals, cannot be excluded (Chrobok et al. 2022).

In summary, our data characterise central actions of CCK on brainstem neurons (slow, long‐lasting Ca2+ transients), but the precise central origin in vivo source of CCK remains speculative. Although peripheral satiety is classically attributed to CCK1R on vagal afferents (Corp et al. 1993), both CCK1R and CCK2R are present in the CNS (Chrobok et al. 2022; Lee and Soltesz 2011; Wank et al. 1992).

Neuromodulation of Central Vagal Terminals

Using GECI to label JNG‐derived axons, we show that electrical stimulation of the JNG recruits a broad population of electrically excitable fibres, while CCK application activates a partially overlapping subset. Puff experiments reveal a subgroup of fibres that respond to both electrical stimulation and CCK, and a subgroup that is electrically silent but robustly activated by CCK. The simplest explanation is that the latter group simply comprises fibres that were not activated by the electrical stimulation paradigm. Since the electrical stimulation likely biases recruitment toward larger, more excitable fibres, it may not fully mimic the natural afferent firing patterns. Critically, CCK‐evoked responses in central vagal terminals persist in the presence of TTX, indicating a presynaptic mechanism at central terminals that does not depend on somatic spike generation. Since approximately 40% of the JNG neurons express CCK receptors (Kinch et al. 2012; Simasko et al. 2002), and CCK1R‐positive neurons are more abundant in the right JNG than the left (Ritchey et al. 2026; Welch et al. 2025), one would expect to find a maximum of ~40% CCK‐responsive fibres in the NTS, but more detailed experiments are needed to determine the specific JNG neuronal identity.

Ligand‐Specific Recruitment of Medullary Astrocytic Networks via Peptidergic Transmission

A novel aspect of this work is the demonstration that brainstem astrocytes (Figure 5, Figure 6) show pronounced ligand specificity in their Ca2+ responses. Using dual‐colour GECI imaging, we find that both CCK and SP evoke slow, spatially extended Ca2+ waves in astrocytic networks, whereas TRH and muscarine elicit robust neuronal activation without detectable astrocytic responses under matched conditions. These experiments were conducted in the continuous presence of TTX, ruling out action potential‐mediated network propagation as a requirement for astrocyte activation, and muscarine drove pronounced oscillatory neuronal activity without glial recruitment, arguing against a simple threshold model in which astrocytes passively track overall neuronal activity.

These findings add a peptidergic dimension to the emerging view of NTS astrocytes as ‘homeostatic integrators’ of viscerosensory processing (Forsberg et al. 2017; Lamy 2012; MacDonald and Ellacott 2020; Mastitskaya et al. 2020; Matott et al. 2017; Matott et al. 2016; Mishra et al. 2024; Nagase et al. 2014; Yamamoto and Mifflin 2018). Beyond glutamatergic signals, we show that specific neuropeptides—CCK and SP—are potent astrocyte activators, whereas other modulators with strong neuronal actions (TRH, muscarine) are not. Within our four‐ligand panel, and under matched conditions (Figure 5, Figure 6), NTS astrocytes therefore appear to discriminate between peptidergic inputs (CCK, SP) and other modulators (TRH, muscarine), though we cannot yet generalise this beyond the ligands tested. The kinetics are also informative: CCK‐induced Ca2+ rise times in astrocytes were significantly slower compared with neighbouring neurons. Together, these results suggest that NTS astrocytes may participate in peptidergic processing alongside their established roles in shaping autonomic reflexes and facilitating glutamate uptake and metabolic support (MacDonald and Ellacott 2020; Mastitskaya et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2025; Yin et al. 2012). Whether this peptidergic glial activation is functionally required for downstream NTS output, or simply accompanies it, will need selective glial silencing or gliotransmitter blockade to test in future.

Several limitations remain. Because the peripheral vagal trunk is severed close to the JNG during the dissection, our preparation functionally constitutes a chronic peripheral vagotomy. Unilateral cervical vagotomy in adult rats alters NTS astrocyte and microglial morphology and changes both presynaptic Ca2+ and NMDA receptor signalling at NTS synapses within 1 week (Hofmann et al. 2024; Hofmann et al. 2021), so some astrocytic responses in Figure 5 and Figure 6 may reflect post‐axotomy reorganisation rather than the native CCK‐driven glial physiology—although the marked ligand specificity (CCK and SP vs. TRH and muscarine, in the same preparations) argues against a non‐specific change in astrocyte excitability. The neonatal/culture context may itself affect astrocyte morphology and gap‐junctional coupling, and our wide‐field imaging resolves population‐level waves propagating through astrocyte‐astrocyte gap junctions (Nagy and Rash 2000) rather than subcellular microdomains. Nonetheless, the clear divergence between CCK/SP and TRH/muscarine responses under identical conditions strongly indicates that astrocyte recruitment is not a by‐product of global neuronal activity—to our knowledge, a novel observation.

Integration of CCK Signalling With Respiratory Rhythmogenesis

Finally, we show that CCK modulates respiratory‐like rhythmic activity in PBC‐containing regions of the same JNG‐BSTEM slice. In high‐excitability aCSF, cultures generate spontaneous, irregular Ca2+ bursts in ventrolateral medullary regions consistent with the PBC; bath‐applied CCK reliably increases burst frequency while concurrently activating CCK‐responsive neurons in the NTS and ventrolateral medulla (Figure 7). CCK is known to alter respiratory patterning (Hurle et al. 1988) and to evoke subjective dyspnoea (Shlik et al. 1997), and PBC neurons drive inspiratory rhythm generation and the respiratory modulation of blood pressure (Lieske et al. 2000; Menuet et al. 2020). However, the cellular pathways linking viscerosensory peptides to PBC dynamics are unclear. Our findings suggest a plausible mechanism by which feeding state and visceral load could influence breathing: postprandial CCK may not only signal satiety and slow gastric emptying, but also adjust respiratory output to match changes in metabolic demand and mechanical constraint (Ellenberger and Smith 1999; Kaczynska and Szereda‐Przestaszewska 2015; Katzman et al. 2002; Owyang and Heldsinger 2011; Rinaman 2010). In pathological states with altered CCK signalling or vagal function—obesity, heart failure or chronic dyspnoea syndromes—such modulation could contribute to maladaptive respiratory‐autonomic coupling.

Our respiratory analyses are, however, limited by the bridge‐optimised slice geometry, which does not always capture the entire PBC, and by the lack of direct electrophysiological confirmation of PBC identity. We have also not yet identified whether CCK acts directly on PBC neurons or indirectly via upstream NTS or ventrolateral medullary populations. Nevertheless, the consistent rise in burst frequency, together with the spatial overlap between CCK‐responsive neurons and PBC‐containing zones, supports a modulatory role for CCK in central respiratory circuits. The platform offers direct access for investigating the bidirectional interactions between feeding‐related and respiratory circuits, and how these two homeostatic systems dynamically modulate one another.

Conclusion

We introduce a JNG–BSTEM slice culture that preserves intact vagal projections to the dorsomedial and ventrolateral medulla, enabling long‐term optical and pharmacological analysis of neurons, astrocytes and respiratory networks. Within this preparation, CCK engages multiple cellular compartments at the vagal entry point: TTX‐resistant activation of NTS and ventrolateral and medullary neurons; recruitment of heterogeneous central vagal terminals (including an electrically silent, CCK‐sensitive subset), selective engagement of astrocytic networks shared with SP, but not TRH or muscarine, and enhanced PBC burst frequency. Collectively, these results show that the DVC integrates CCK signals across JNG somata, central vagal terminals, second‐order neurons, astrocytic networks and respiratory‐rhythmic regions within a single in vitro preparation. Thus, this slice culture complements acute slices and in vivo approaches, offering a tractable platform for studying mechanisms relevant to disordered feeding, visceral pain and cardiorespiratory control. Future studies should combine cell‐type‐specific tools with chronic metabolic challenges to dissect disease‐related plasticity in JNG–NTS–astrocyte–PBC circuits and identify neuromodulation targets.
