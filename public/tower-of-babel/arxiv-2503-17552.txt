Partial permutations and character evaluations
Zachary Hamaker, Brendon Rhoades

Abstract
. Let I = ( i 1 , … , i k ) I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}) and J = ( j 1 , … , j k ) J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k}) be two length k k sequences drawn from { 1 , … , n } \{1,\dots,n\} . We have the group algebra element [ I , J ] := ∑ w ⁡ ( I ) = J w ∈ ℂ ⁡ [ 𝔖 n ] [I,J]:=\sum_{w(I)=J}w\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}] where the sum is over permutations w ∈ 𝔖 n w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n} which satisfy w ⁡ ( i p ) = j p w(i_{p})=j_{p} for p = 1 , … , k p=1,\dots,k . We give an algorithm for evaluating irreducible characters χ λ : ℂ ⁡ [ 𝔖 n ] → ℂ \chi^{\lambda}:{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]\to{\mathbb{C}} of the symmetric group on the elements [ I , J ] [I,J] . This algorithm is a hybrid of the classical Murnaghan–Nakayama rule and a new path Murnaghan–Nakayama rule which reflects the decomposition of a partial permutation into paths and cycles. These results first appeared in arXiv:2206.06567 , which is no longer intended for publication. We originally used the character theoretic results in this paper to prove asymptotic results on moments of certain permutation statistics restricted to conjugacy classes. A referee generously shared a combinatorial argument which is strong enough to prove these results without recourse to character theory. These results now appear in our companion paper [ 6 ] . However, the approach in this paper are more explicit, as we demonstrate with several examples.

Introduction

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}

The set

For

{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}:=\{\text{all partial permutations $(I,J)$ in ${\mathfrak{S}}_{n}$ of size $k$}\}.

For any

We let

for the associated group algebra element

The maps

Our main results are about character evaluations. Irreducible characters
\chi^{\lambda}:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are in one-to-one correspondence with partitions
\lambda\vdash n
. The
Murnaghan–Nakayama rule
is an algorithm which computes
\chi^{\lambda}(w)
for any permutation
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
as a signed sum over certain ribbon tableaux. We extend
\chi^{\lambda}
to a map
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]\to{\mathbb{C}}
by linearity:

(1.7)

\chi^{\lambda}\left(\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}a_{w}\cdot w\right):=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}a_{w}\cdot\chi^{\lambda}(w)

for
a_{w}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. For a given group algebra element
a=\sum_{w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}a_{w}\cdot w\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
, one can ask for a combinatorial rule for the character evaluation
\chi^{\lambda}(a)
. For general
a
, this problem is intractable: besides applying (
1.7
) and the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule
n!
times, nothing else can be said. Our main result may be paraphrased as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

(
\subset
Corollary
6.3
, Theorem
6.7
) Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. There is an explicit combinatorial rule given by a signed enumeration of ribbon tilings for calculating the family of irreducible character evaluation
\{\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])\,:\,\lambda\vdash n\}
.
This rule calculates
\{\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])\,:\,\lambda\vdash n\}
using a constant (and finite) set of tilings whenever
n\geq 2k
.

The naïve approach to computing
\{\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])\,:\,\lambda\vdash n\}
requires
(n-k)!
applications of the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule or each partition
\lambda\vdash n
. Theorem

is therefore a massive improvement.

Our algorithm for computing
\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])
uses the decomposition of the partial permutation
(I,J)
into disjoint cycles and paths. The classical Murnaghan–Nakayama rule computes the Schur expansion
p_{\nu}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}_{\nu}s_{\lambda}
of a power sum symmetric function

. We define a new

of the vector space of degree

which computes the Schur expansion

Let
{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
be the vector space of class functions

with its basis of irreducible characters

2.2.
Representation theory

All representations in this paper will be finite-dimensional and over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
G
be a finite group and let
V
be a
G
-module. Write
{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
for the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra of
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear maps
\varphi:V\to V
. The space
{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
carries the structure of a
(G\times G)
-module via

((g_{1},g_{2})\cdot\varphi)(v):=g_{1}\cdot\varphi(g_{2}^{-1}\cdot v)

for
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
,
\varphi\in{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
, and

.
The following result will be crucial in our analysis of local statistics.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Artin-Wedderburn) Let
G
be a finite group. We have an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebras

\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}[G]\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{V}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)

where the direct sum ranges over (isomorphism class representatives of) irreducible
G
-modules and
\Phi
is defined by

\Phi(a)\cdot v:=a\cdot v\quad\quad\text{for all $v\in V$}

for all
a\in{\mathbb{C}}[G]
, and each
G
-irreducible
V
.

Let
{\mathrm{Fun}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
be the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space of functions
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
. We identify
{\mathrm{Fun}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
with
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
by associating
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
to the group algebra element
\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\cdot g\in{\mathbb{C}}[G]
. A function
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a
class function
if
f(hgh^{-1})=f(g)
for all
g,h\in G
. Let
{\mathrm{Class}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
be the vector space of class functions
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
{\mathrm{Class}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
is a subspace of
{\mathrm{Fun}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
. The center
Z({\mathbb{C}}[G])
of the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
is given by

(2.1)

Z({\mathbb{C}}[G])=\left\{\sum_{g\,\in\,G}f(g)\cdot g\,:\,f\in{\mathrm{Class}}(G,{\mathbb{C}})\right\}.

The image of
Z({\mathbb{C}}[G])
under the Artin-Wedderburn isomorphism
\Phi
of Theorem
2.2
is as follows.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group with group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
. Let
Z({\mathbb{C}}[G])
be the center of
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
. The image of
Z({\mathbb{C}}[G])
under the isomorphism
\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}[G]\xrightarrow{\,\sim\,}\bigoplus_{V}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
of Theorem
2.2
is

\Phi(Z({\mathbb{C}}[G]))=\bigoplus_{V}{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\mathrm{id}_{V}.

The direct sum is again over (isomorphism class representatives of) irreducible
G
-modules

and
{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\mathrm{id}_{V}
is the 1-dimensional subspace of
{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
consisting of scalar maps
V\to V
.

Corollary
2.3
follows from Theorem
2.2
as follows. If

is a finite-dimensional vector space, then
{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
is isomorphic to the algebra of
(\dim V)\times(\dim V)
complex matrices. It is not difficult to show that the center of this matrix algebra consists of scalar matrices.

2.3.
Symmetric functions

Let
\Lambda=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\Lambda_{n}
be the graded ring of symmetric functions in an infinite variable set
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
over the field
{\mathbb{C}}
. If
f\in\Lambda
and
N\geq 0
, we write
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},0,0,\dots)
for the polynomial in
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
obtained by evaluating
x_{i}\to 0
for all
i>N
.

Bases of
\Lambda_{n}
are indexed by partitions
\lambda\vdash n
. We will use the
power sum basis

p_{\lambda}
and
Schur basis

s_{\lambda}
.
If
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})
, we have the power sum symmetric function

p_{\lambda}:=p_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda_{k}}\text{ where }p_{d}:=\sum_{i\,\geq\,1}x_{i}^{d}\text{ for $d>0$}.

The bialternant definition of Schur functions will be the most convenient for our purposes. Fix
N\geq 0
and let
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}]
be the group algebra element

(2.2)

\varepsilon:=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}}{\mathrm{sign}}(w)\cdot w

which antisymmetrizes over
{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
. The element
\varepsilon
acts on the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
. An
alternant
is a polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
such that
\varepsilon\cdot f=f
. For any sequence
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N})
of nonnegative integers we have an alternant

(2.3)

a_{\beta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\varepsilon\cdot x^{\beta+\delta}

where
\delta:=(N-1,\dots,1,0)
and
\beta+\delta
is componentwise addition of exponent vectors.
For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
, the
Schur function

s_{\lambda}\in\Lambda
is the limit

(2.4)

s_{\lambda}=\lim_{N\to\infty}s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon\cdot x^{\lambda+\delta}}{\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}}.

If
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is a permutation, the

is the partition of
n
obtained by writing the cycle lengths of

in weakly decreasing order. The
Frobenius characteristic map

{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}:{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow\Lambda_{n}

is defined by

{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}:f\mapsto\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(w)\cdot p_{\mathrm{cyc}(w)}.

The map
{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}
is an isomorphism of

-vector spaces

.

3.
Partial permutations and local statistics

This section develops basic properties of the space
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
of
k
-local statistics
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. The results are proven in various places and in various guises throughout the probability literature. We state and prove them here in using the language and terminology of algebraic combinatorics.

3.1.
The locality filtration

Recall that
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
is the vector space spanned by the indicator statistics
{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
for
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. The pointwise product of these indicator functions is as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
and
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Write
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
. We have

{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{1}}_{I\,i,\,J\,j}&\text{if $i\notin I$ and $j\notin J$,}\\
{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}&\text{if $i=i_{r}$ and $j=j_{r}$ for some $1\leq r\leq k$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Here
I\,i:=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k},i)
and
J\,j:=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k},j)
.

Proof.

For any
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we have

({\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{i,j})(w)={\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}(w)\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{i,j}(w)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w(I)=J$ and $w(i)=j$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma
3.1
implies that the spaces
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
nest as
k
varies.

Proposition 3.2
.

Any
k
-local statistic
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is
(k+1)
-local.

Proof.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. It suffices to show that
{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is
(k+1)
-local. Indeed, fix
1\leq j\leq n
with
j\notin J
. Since
{\mathbf{1}}_{1,j}+\cdots+{\mathbf{1}}_{n,j}=1
, Lemma
3.1
implies

{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}={\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}\cdot\left({\mathbf{1}}_{1,j}+\cdots+{\mathbf{1}}_{n,j}\right)=\sum_{i\,\notin\,I}{\mathbf{1}}_{I\,i,\,J\,j}

which is visibly
(k+1)
-local.
∎

Proposition
3.2
gives the filtration

(3.1)

{\mathbb{C}}={\mathrm{Loc}}_{0}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{Loc}}_{1}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq{\mathrm{Loc}}_{n-1}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})={\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})

where the equality
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{n-1}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})={\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
follows from

(3.2)

{\mathbf{1}}_{(1,2,\dots,n-1),\,(w(1),w(2),\dots,w(n-1))}:v\mapsto\begin{cases}1&v=w\\
0&v\neq w\end{cases}\quad\text{for all $w,v\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}$.}

The filtration (
3.1
) behaves well under pointwise product.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f,g:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be statistics. If
f
is
k
-local and
g
is
\ell
-local then
f\cdot g:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is
(k+\ell)
-local.

Proof.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,m}
with
I=(i_{1},\dots,j_{m})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
. Lemma
3.1
implies

{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}={\mathbf{1}}_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots{\mathbf{1}}_{i_{m},j_{m}}.

If
(I_{1},J_{1})\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
and
(I_{2},J_{2})\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,\ell}
, we deduce that there exist
1\leq a_{r},b_{r}\leq n
for
r=1,\dots,k+\ell
so that

{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{1},J_{1}}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{2},J_{2}}=\prod_{r\,=\,1}^{k+\ell}{\mathbf{1}}_{a_{r},b_{r}}.

Lemma
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
imply that
{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{1},J_{1}}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{2},J_{2}}
is
(k+\ell)
-local. The result follows by linearity.
∎

The product group
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\times{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
acts on the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
by left and right multiplication. The induced action on the function space
{\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
is given by

(3.3)

((u,v)\cdot f)(w)=f(u^{-1}wv)\quad\quad\text{ for all }u,v,w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\text{ and }f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}.

The filtration (
3.1
) is stable under this action.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u,v\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. We have

(u,v)\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}={\mathbf{1}}_{v(I),u(J)}.

Proof.

Let
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. We have

(3.4)

((u,v)\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J})(w)={\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}(u^{-1}wv)=\begin{cases}1&u^{-1}wv(I)=J\\
0&u^{-1}wv(I)\neq J\end{cases}=\begin{cases}1&wv(I)=u(J)\\
0&wv(I)\neq u(J)\end{cases}={\mathbf{1}}_{v(I),u(J)}(w).

∎

3.2.
Artin-Wedderburn and increasing subsequences

Recall that
V^{\lambda}
is the irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
associated to a partition
\lambda\vdash n
. The Artin-Wedderburn Theorem
2.2
in the case of
G={\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
therefore gives an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebras

(3.5)

\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]\xrightarrow{\,\,\sim\,\,}\bigoplus_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})

where
\Phi(a)
acts on the
\lambda
-component by
v\mapsto a\cdot v
for all
v\in V^{\lambda}
.
If we identify
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]={\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
, for any
k
the subspace
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})
maps to a subspace of
\bigoplus_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})
under
\Phi
. This image may be described explicitly as follows.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
n,k\geq 0
, the image of
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
under the Artin-Wedderburn isomorphism
\Phi
is

\Phi\left({\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\right)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\,\vdash\,n\\
\lambda_{1}\,\geq\,n-k\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda}).

Proof.

Consider the embedding of symmetric groups
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}\subseteq{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
where
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}
acts on the first
n-k
letters. Let
\eta_{n-k}
be the group algebra element

(3.6)

\eta_{n-k}:=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}w\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}].

In the correspondence
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
, we have
\eta_{n-k}\leftrightarrow{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{0},J_{0}}
where

I_{0}=J_{0}=(n-k+1,\dots,n-1,n).

Lemma
3.4
implies that the subspace
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
of
k
-local statistics corresponds to the two-sided ideal
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
generated by
\eta_{n-k}
. We therefore want to compute the image

\Phi({\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})\subseteq\bigoplus_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})

of this ideal under
\Phi
. Since
\Phi
is an algebra isomorphism, the subset
\Phi({\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})
is an ideal in
\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})
.

For any
\lambda\vdash n
, an easy computation shows that the matrix ring
{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})\cong\mathrm{Mat}_{f^{\lambda}}({\mathbb{C}})
is
simple
: it has no nontrivial two-sided ideals. Consequently, the ideals of the direct sum
\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda})
correspond to subsets of
\{\lambda\,:\,\lambda\vdash n\}
. In particular, there is a set
P(n,k)
of partitions of
n
such that

(3.7)

\Phi({\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})=\bigoplus_{\lambda\,\in\,P(n,k)}{\mathrm{End}}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\lambda}).

The definition of
\Phi
implies that

(3.8)

P(n,k)=\{\lambda\vdash n\,:\,\eta_{n-k}\cdot V^{\lambda}\neq 0\}.

If
V
is an
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
-module, it is not hard to see that

\eta_{n-k}\cdot V=V^{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}:=\{v\in V\,:\,w\cdot v=v\text{ for all $w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}$}\}.

In particular, we have
\eta_{n-k}\cdot V\neq 0
if and only if the trivial
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}
-module appears in the restriction
\mathrm{Res}^{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}(V)
of
V
from
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
to
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}
. We conclude that

(3.9)

P(n,k)=\left\{\lambda\vdash n\,:\,\mathrm{triv}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}\text{ appears in }\mathrm{Res}^{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}(V^{\lambda})\right\}=\{\lambda\vdash n\,:\,\lambda_{1}\geq n-k\}

where the second equality uses
\mathrm{triv}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}}=V^{(n-k)}
and the branching rule for symmetric group modules. This completes the proof.
∎

\Phi\left({\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}}\right))=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\,\vdash\,n\\

Apply Corollary

4.
Atomic symmetric functions and path power sums

4.1.
Atomic symmetric functions

In this section we turn our focus from general
k
-local statistics
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
to
k
-local class functions. Recall that if
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a function, its image
R\,f
under the Reynolds operator is given by

R\,f:w\mapsto\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(v^{-1}wv).

This formula defines a projection
R:{\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\to{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
. In particular, the space
{\mathrm{Loc}}_{k}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\cap{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
of
k
-local class functions is spanned by
\{R\,{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}\,:\,(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}\}
. We use the Frobenius characteristic
{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}:{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\xrightarrow{\,\sim\,}\Lambda_{n}
to encode
R\,{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}
as a symmetric function.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. The
atomic symmetric function

A_{n,I,J}
is

A_{n,I,J}:=n!\cdot{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(R\,{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}).

The factor of
n!
in Definition
4.1
makes our results more aesthetic. For example, it banishes a factor of
\frac{1}{n!}
from the power sum expansion of
A_{n,I,J}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. We have the power sum expansion

A_{n,I,J}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\\
w(I)\,=\,J\end{subarray}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(w)}

where
\mathrm{cyc}(w)\vdash n
is the cycle type of the permutation
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

Proof.

For any function
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we claim

(4.1)

{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(R\,f)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(w)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(w)}.

Equation (
4.1
) follows from the computation

(4.2)

{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(R\,f)=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\sum_{x\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}R\,f(x)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(x)}=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\sum_{x\,\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{v\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(v^{-1}xv)\right)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(x)}\\
=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{x,v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(v^{-1}xv)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(x)}=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{w,v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(w)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(vwv^{-1})}\\
=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{w,v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(w)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(w)}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(w)\cdot p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(w)}

where we made the change of variables
w=v^{-1}xv
and used
{\mathrm{cyc}}(vwv^{-1})={\mathrm{cyc}}(w)
. The proposition is proven by taking
f={\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}
.
∎

The factor of
n!
in Definition
4.1
also makes the Schur expansion of
A_{n,I,J}
look nicer. If
(I,J)
is a partial permutation of
[n]
, recall the group algebra element
[I,J]=\sum_{w(I)=J}w\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(I,J)
be a partial permutation of
[n]
. In the Schur expansion

A_{n,I,J}=\sum_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}c_{\lambda}\cdot s_{\lambda}

the coefficient
c_{\lambda}
is the trace of the linear operator
[I,J]:V^{\lambda}\to V^{\lambda}
. Equivalently, we have

c_{\lambda}=\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\\
w(I)\,=\,J\end{subarray}}\chi^{\lambda}(w).

Proof.

If
\mu\vdash n
, we have
p_{\mu}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}\cdot s_{\lambda}
. Applying Proposition
4.2
, we see that

(4.3)

A_{n,I,J}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\\
w(I)\,=\,J\end{subarray}}p_{{\mathrm{cyc}}(w)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\\
w(I)\,=\,J\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}\chi^{\lambda}(w)\cdot s_{\lambda}\right)\\
=\sum_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}\chi^{\lambda}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\\
w(I)\,=\,J\end{subarray}}w\right)\cdot s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])\cdot s_{\lambda}

and the result is proven.
∎

Proposition
4.3
gives a restriction on the support of the Schur expansion of
A_{n,I,J}
. The following result will also follow from our path Murnaghan–Nakayama formula.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
. In the Schur expansion

A_{n,I,J}=\sum_{\lambda\,\vdash\,n}c_{\lambda}\cdot s_{\lambda}

we have
c_{\lambda}=0
unless
\lambda_{1}\geq n-k
.

Proof.

Theorem
3.5
implies that
[I,J]\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
acts as the zero operator on
V^{\lambda}
whenever
\lambda_{1}<n-k
. Proposition
4.3
yields the corollary.
∎

4.2.
Path power sums and path-cycle factorization

In this subsection we prove an important factorization property of the atomic symmetric functions. To begin, we show that atomic functions are invariant under the action of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
on partial permutations.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
(I,J)
be a partial permutation of
[n]
. For any
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
we have

A_{n,I,J}=A_{n,w(I),w(J)}.

Proof.

Lemma
3.4
implies

R\,{\mathbf{1}}_{I,J}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}}_{v(I),v(J)}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}}_{vw(I),vw(J)}=R\,{\mathbf{1}}_{w(I),w(J)}

and the result follows from Definition
4.1
.
∎

Proposition
4.5
gives an alternative way to index atomic symmetric functions.
Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
be the family of directed graphs
G
on
n
unlabelled vertices which are disjoint unions of directed paths and directed cycles. For
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
, we set

(4.4)

A_{G}:=A_{n,I,J}

where
(I,J)
is any partial permutation of
[n]
whose graph
G(I,J)
coincides with
G
after erasing labels. Proposition
4.5
guarantees that
A_{G}
is independent of the choice of
(I,J)
. Graphs which consist only of paths will play a special role. In the next definition, we write
P_{n}
for the directed path on
n
unlabeled vertices.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})\models n
be a composition of
n
and let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
be the disjoint union

G:=P_{\mu_{1}}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup P_{\mu_{k}}

of directed paths. The
path power sum

\vec{p}_{\mu}\in\Lambda_{n}
is the symmetric function

\vec{p}_{\mu}:=A_{G}.

The notation
\vec{p}_{\mu}
is meant to evoke of the shape of the graph whose atomic function equals
\vec{p}_{\mu}
. Every atomic function factors as a product of a path and classical power sum.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
be a partial permutation, let
\mu
be the path type of
(I,J)
, and let
\nu
be the cycle type of
(I,J)
. We have

A_{n,I,J}=\vec{p}_{\mu}\cdot p_{\nu}.

Proof.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
, let
C
be a directed cycle in
G
, and let
G-C
be the graph obtained by removing the cycle
C
from
G
. Proposition
4.2
implies that

A_{G}=A_{G-C}\cdot p_{c}

where
c
is the number of vertices in
C
. The result follows by induction.
∎

Proposition
4.2
leads to an expansion of path power sums into classical power sums. To state this expansion, we introduce some notation which will be useful in this section and the next.

Notation 4.8
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
be a composition with
r
parts. If
B\subseteq[r]
, we set

\mu_{B}:=\sum_{b\,\in\,B}\mu_{b}.

Similarly, if
C
is a cycle of a permutation in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}
, we set

\mu_{C}:=\sum_{c\,\in\,C}\mu_{c}.

Finally, if
w=w_{1}\dots w_{k}
is a word over the alphabet
[r]
, we set

\mu_{w}:=\sum_{i\,=\,1}^{k}\mu_{w_{i}}.

For example, we have
\mu_{\{2,4,5\}}=\mu_{(2,5,4)}=\mu_{425}=\mu_{2}+\mu_{4}+\mu_{5}
. Recall that
\Pi_{r}
is the lattice of set partitions of
[r]
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
be a composition with
r
parts. We have

\vec{p}_{\mu}=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}}\prod_{C\,\in\,w}p_{\mu_{C}}=\sum_{\pi\,\in\,\Pi_{r}}\prod_{B\,\in\,\pi}(\#B-1)!\cdot p_{\mu_{B}}

where the product in the middle expression is over all cycles
C
belonging to
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}
.

Proof.

The first equality follows from Proposition
4.2
. The second equality applies the fact that there are
(\#S-1)!
ways to cyclically order a finite set
S
.
∎

For example, if
\mu=(a,b,c)
has three parts we have

\vec{p}_{(a,b,c)}=p_{(a,b,c)}+p_{(a+b,c)}+p_{(a+c,b)}+p_{b+c,a)}+2\cdot p_{(a+b+c)}.

For a partition
\mu\vdash n
, the terms
p_{\rho}
appearing in the
p
-expansion of
\vec{p}_{\mu}
are indexed by partitions
\rho
obtained by combining parts of
\mu
. Since the coefficient of
p_{\mu}
in
\vec{p}_{\mu}
is 1, the following corollary is a consequence of Proposition
4.9
.

Corollary 4.10
.

The set
\{\vec{p}_{\mu}\,:\,\mu\vdash n\}
is a basis of
\Lambda_{n}
.

There is a clean formula for the expansion of a classical power sum in the path power sum basis. If
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
is a composition with
r
parts we have

(4.5)

p_{\mu}=\sum_{\pi\,\in\,\Pi_{r}}(-1)^{r\,-\,\#\pi}\prod_{B\,\in\,\pi}\vec{p}_{\mu_{B}}.

The proof of Equation (
4.5
) uses the formula

(4.6)

\mu_{\Pi_{r}}(\hat{0},\pi)=(-1)^{r\,-\,\#\pi}\prod_{B\,\in\,\pi}(\#B-1)!

for the Möbius function of
\Pi_{r}
together with Proposition
4.9
and Möbius inversion.
We will have no use for Equation (
4.5
) and leave the details of its verification to the reader.

5.
Path Murnaghan–Nakayama Rule

5.1.
Classical Murnaghan–Nakayama via alternants

The classical Murnaghan–Nakayama rule describes the coefficients
\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}
in the expansion

p_{\mu}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}\cdot s_{\lambda}
in terms of ribbon tableaux.
There are several ways to prove this result. The proof that extends best to the path power sum setting uses alternants;
we recall this proof before treating the more elaborate case of

\vec{p}_{\mu}

Restricting to a finite variable set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, for
\lambda
with
\leq N
parts, one has

(5.1)

s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\frac{\varepsilon\cdot x^{\lambda+\delta}}{\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}}

where
\varepsilon=\sum_{w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}}{\mathrm{sign}}(w)\cdot w\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}]
and
\delta=(N-1,N-2,\dots,1,0)
.
The numerator is the alternant

a_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\varepsilon\cdot x^{\lambda+\delta}.

The set
\{a_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\,:\,\ell(\lambda)\leq N\}

is a basis of the vector space
\varepsilon\cdot{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
of alternating
polynomials.
Since
p_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
is symmetric, we have

(5.2)

p_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot(x^{\delta})=\varepsilon\cdot\left(p_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times x^{\delta}\right).

Multiplying through by
\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
, the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule is equivalent to finding the expansion of
(
5.2
) in the alternant basis
\{a_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,:\,\ell(\lambda)\leq N\}
.

Since the
p
-basis is multiplicative, the alternant expansion of (
5.2
) can be understood inductively. For
k\geq 1
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
we have

(5.3)

p_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\cdot a_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\varepsilon\cdot\left(p_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\cdot x^{\lambda+\delta}\right)\\
=\sum_{i\,=\,1}^{N}\varepsilon\cdot\left(x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+N-1}\cdots x_{i}^{k+\lambda_{i}+N-i}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{N}}\right).

Each term

\varepsilon\cdot\left(x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+N-1}\cdots x_{i}^{k+\lambda_{i}+N-i}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{N}}\right)
in this sum
is an alternant, the negative of an alternant, or zero according to the following combinatorial criterion.

Observation 5.1
.

For
1\leq i\leq N
, we have

\varepsilon\cdot\left(x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+N-1}\cdots x_{i}^{k+\lambda_{i}+N-i}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{N}}\right)\neq 0

if and only if it is possible to add a size
k
ribbon
\xi
to the Young diagram of
\lambda
such that the tail of
\xi
is in row
i

and
\lambda\cup\xi
is the Young diagram of a partition. In this case, we have

(5.4)

\varepsilon\cdot\left(x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+N-1}\cdots x_{i}^{k+\lambda_{i}+N-i}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{N}}\right)={\mathrm{sign}}(\xi)\cdot a_{\lambda\cup\xi}.

For example, let
k=3,\lambda=(3,3,1),
and
N=6
. Starting with the length

N=6
sequence
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{6})+(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{6})=(8,7,4,2,1,0)
, adding
k=3
in all possible positions yields

(11,7,4,2,1,0),\quad(8,10,4,2,1,0),\quad(8,7,7,2,1,0),\\
\quad(8,7,4,5,1,0),\quad(8,7,4,2,4,0),\quad\text{and}\quad(8,7,4,2,1,3).

The third and fifth sequences above have repeated terms, so their corresponding monomials in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{6}]

are annihilated by
\varepsilon
. Sorting the remaining sequences into decreasing order and applying
\varepsilon

introduces signs of
+1,-1,-1,
and
+1
into the first, second, fourth, and sixth sequences (respectively).
At the level of partitions, these are the four signs associated to the ways to add size
3
ribbons to
\lambda=(3,3,1)
.

The tails of these added ribbons are in rows 1, 2, 4, and 6. These agree with the relative values
of the terms in
\lambda+\delta=(8,7,4,2,1,0)
to which
k=3
was added. The illegal ribbon additions

obtained by adding a 3-ribbon with tail in rows 3 and 5 correspond precisely to the sequences

\lambda+\delta+(0,0,3,0,0,0)
and
\lambda+\delta+(0,0,0,0,3,0)
having repeated terms.

Observation
5.1
and induction on the number of parts of
\mu
show that
multiplying
p_{\mu}=p_{\mu_{1}}p_{\mu_{2}}\cdots
by
\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
corresponds to building up a standard ribbon
tableau of type
\mu
ribbon by ribbon, starting with the empty tableau
\varnothing
. That is, we have

(5.5)

p_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}=\sum_{T}{\mathrm{sign}}(T)\times a_{{\mathrm{shape}}(T)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})

where the sum is over all standard ribbon tableaux
T
of type
\mu
. Dividing by
\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
and taking the limit
as
N\rightarrow\infty
yields the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (Theorem
2.4
).

5.2.
Word arrays

We embark on finding the
s
-expansion of
\vec{p}_{\mu}
. The difficulty in adapting the proof
in the previous subsection to path power sums is that
\vec{p}_{\mu}
is not multiplicative in
\mu

so the induction argument breaks down. In spite of this, the
s
-expansion of the path power sum
\vec{p}_{\mu}
will admit
a reasonably compact expansion in terms of certain ribbon tilings.
In this section we set the stage for this result by giving a combinatorial description of the alternating
polynomial
\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
.

Fix an integer
N\gg 0
and a partition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
with
r
parts. A
\mu
-word array
of length
N

is a sequence
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
of finite (possibly empty) words
w_{1},\dots,w_{N}

over the alphabet
\{1,\dots,r\}
.
The word array
\omega
is
standard
if each letter
1,\dots,r
appears exactly once among the words

w_{1},\dots,w_{N}
.
We will mainly be interested in standard word arrays, but will pass through more general word arrays
in the course of our inductive arguments.

If
\omega=(w_{1}\mid w_{2}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N-1}\mid w_{N})
is a
\mu
-word array of length
N
, the
weight

{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)
is the sequence of integers

(5.6)

{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega):=(\mu_{w_{1}}+N-1,\mu_{w_{2}}+N-2,\dots,\mu_{w_{N-1}}+1,\mu_{w_{N}})\\
=(\mu_{w_{1}},\mu_{w_{2}},\dots,\mu_{w_{N-1}},\mu_{w_{N}})+(N-1,N-2,\dots,1,0)

where the addition in the second line is componentwise. Recall that
\mu_{w_{i}}
is shorthand for the sum of the parts
\mu_{j}

corresponding to the letters
j
of the word
w_{i}
.
The sequence
{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)
depends on
\mu
; we leave this dependence implicit to reduce clutter.
Word arrays have the following connection to path power sums.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
be a partition.
We have

(5.7)

\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}=\sum_{\omega}\varepsilon\cdot x^{{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)}

where the sum is over all standard
\mu
-word arrays
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
of length
N
.

Proof.

Proposition
4.9
implies

(5.8)

\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}}\prod_{C\,\in\,w}p_{\mu_{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})

where the notation
C\in w
indicates that
C
is a cycle of the permutation
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}
.
Multiplying both sides of Equation (
5.8
) by
\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
yields

\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}=\varepsilon\cdot\left(\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times x^{\delta}\right)=\varepsilon\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}}\prod_{C\in w}p_{\mu_{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times x^{\delta}\right).

Consider the monomial expansion of

(5.9)

\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}}\prod_{C\,\in\,w}p_{\mu_{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times x^{\delta}=\sum_{w\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}}\prod_{C\,\in\,w}(x_{1}^{\mu_{C}}+\cdots+x_{N}^{\mu_{C}})\times x^{\delta}.

A typical term in this expansion is obtained by first selecting a permutation
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}
, then assigning the cycles
C
of
w

to the
N
exponents of
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
(where a given exponent can get no, one, or multiple cycles), and finally
multiplying by
x^{\delta}
. Applying
\varepsilon
to both sides of
Equation (
5.9
) completes the proof.
∎

Each term
\varepsilon\cdot x^{{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)}
on the right-hand side in Lemma
5.2

is 0 when
{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)
has repeated entries,
or
\pm
some alternant

a_{\lambda(\omega)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})

where
\lambda(\omega)
is a partition.
Dividing by
\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}
and taking the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty

gives an
s
-expansion of
\vec{p}_{\mu}
, but this expansion is too large and
involves too much cancellation to be of much use.
Roughly speaking, the
s
-expansion
coming from Lemma
5.2
arises
by computing the
p
-expansion of
\vec{p}_{\mu}
,
and then applying classical Murnaghan–Nakayama
to each term.

5.3.
A sign-reversing involution

This subsection gives a sign-reversing involution
\iota
on word arrays
which removes a large part (but not all) of the cancellation
in Lemma
5.2
. The involution
\iota
will act by swapping ‘unstable pairs’ in word arrays.

Let
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})

be a
\mu
-word array. An
unstable pair
in
\omega
consists of two prefix-suffix factorizations

(5.10)

w_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{j}=u_{j}v_{j}

of the words
w_{i}
and
w_{j}

at distinct positions
1\leq i<j\leq N
such that we have the equality

(5.11)

\mu_{v_{i}}-i=\mu_{v_{j}}-j

involving the suffixes of these factorizations.
A word array
\omega
can admit unstable pairs at multiple pairs of positions
i<j
, and even at the same pair of positions
there could be more than one factorization

w_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
and
w_{j}=u_{j}v_{j}
of the words
w_{i}
and
w_{j}
witnessing an unstable pair.
It is possible for either or both of the prefixes
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
in an unstable pair to be empty, but at least one of the suffixes

v_{i}
or
v_{j}
must be nonempty.
We define the
score

of an unstable pair to be the common value
\mu_{v_{i}}-i=\mu_{v_{j}}-j
of (
5.11
).

Example 5.3
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{9})=(3,3,3,3,2,2,2,1,1)
and
N=6
. We have the standard
\mu
-word array

\omega=(w_{1}\mid w_{2}\mid w_{3}\mid w_{4}\mid w_{5}\mid w_{6})=(\varnothing\mid\varnothing\mid 3\,7\mid 8\,1\,4\mid\varnothing\mid 9\,5\,2\,6).

The ten unstable pairs in
\omega
correspond to the prefix-suffix factorizations

(w_{1},w_{3})=(\varnothing,3\,\cdot\,7),\quad(w_{1},w_{4})=(\varnothing,8\,1\,\cdot\,4),\quad(w_{1},w_{6})=(\varnothing,9\,\cdot\,5\,2\,6),

(w_{3},w_{4})=(3\,\cdot\,7,8\,1\,\cdot\,4),\quad(w_{3},w_{4})=(\cdot\,3\,7,8\,\cdot\,1\,4),\quad(w_{3},w_{6})=(3\cdot 7,9\,5\cdot 2\,6),

(w_{3},w_{6})=(\cdot\,3\,7,\cdot\,9\,5\,2\,6),\quad(w_{4},w_{6})=(8\,1\,4\,\cdot\,,9\,5\,2\,\cdot\,6),\quad(w_{4},w_{6})=(8\,1\,\cdot\,4,9\,5\,\cdot\,2\,6),

\text{ and }\quad(w_{4},w_{6})=(8\,\cdot\,1\,4,\,\cdot\,9\,5\,2\,6).

In reading order from the top left, the scores of these unstable pairs are

-1,-1,-1,-1,2,-1,2,-4,-1,\text{ and }2.

A
\mu
-word array
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
is
stable
if
\omega
has no unstable pairs.
Stability is sufficient to detect when the monomial
x^{{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)}
is annihilated by the antisymmetrizer
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mu
be a partition and let

\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
be a
\mu
-word array with weight sequence

{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)=({\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)_{1},\dots,{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)_{N})
. If there exist
i<j
such that
{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)_{i}={\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)_{j}

then
\omega
is unstable.

Proof.

This instability is witnessed by the prefix-suffix
factorizations
w_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
and
w_{j}=u_{j}v_{j}
where
u_{i}=u_{j}=\varnothing
are empty prefixes.
∎

Lemma
5.4
says that any individual term in the expansion

\vec{p}_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\times\varepsilon\cdot x^{\delta}=\sum_{\text{$\omega$ standard}}\varepsilon\cdot x^{{\mathrm{wt}}(\omega)}

of Lemma
5.2
which vanishes corresponds to an unstable
word array. We will use stability to cancel more terms of this expansion pairwise.
Before doing so, we record
the following hereditary property of stable word arrays which will be crucial for our inductive arguments.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mu
be a partition and let
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
be a stable
\mu
-word array.
For each position
i
, let
w_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
be a prefix-suffix factorization of the word
w_{i}
.
The
\mu
-word array

(v_{1}\mid\cdots\mid v_{N})
formed by the suffices
v_{i}
of the words
w_{i}
is also stable.

Proof.

The suffix array
(v_{1}\mid\cdots\mid v_{N})
is stable because
the condition (
5.11
) defining unstable pairs
depends only on the suffixes of the words
w_{1},\dots,w_{N}
.
∎

We define a swapping operation on unstable pairs as follows.
Consider an unstable
\mu
-word array
\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})
at positions
1\leq i<j\leq N

with prefix-suffix factorizations

w_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
and
w_{j}=u_{j}v_{j}
. The
swap

\sigma(\omega)
of
\omega
at this unstable pair is the

\mu
-word array

(5.12)

\omega=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w_{i}\mid\cdots\mid w_{j}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})\quad\leadsto\quad\sigma(\omega):=(w_{1}\mid\cdots\mid w^{\prime}_{i}\mid\cdots\mid w^{\prime}_{j}\mid\cdots\mid w_{N})

where

(5.13)

w^{\prime}_{i}:=u_{j}v_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad w^{\prime}_{j}:=u_{i}v_{j}.

That is, the swap
\sigma(\omega)
is obtained from
\omega
by interchanging the
prefixes
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
.
It is possible for
\sigma
to leave the
word array
\omega
unchanged:
if both prefixes
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
are empty, we have
\sigma(\omega)=\omega
.
The instability condition (
5.11
) implies

(5.14)

\mu_{w_{i}}+N-i=\mu_{u_{i}}+\mu_{v_{i}}+N-i=\mu_{u_{i}}+\mu_{v_{j}}+N-j=\mu_{w^{\prime}_{j}}+N-j

and

(5.15)

\mu_{w_{j}}+N-j=\mu_{u_{j}}+\mu_{v_{j}}+N-j=\mu_{u_{j}}+\mu_{v_{i}}+N-i=\mu_{w^{\prime}_{i}}+N-i

Definition 5.9

A

•

is the row occupied by the tail of the

We use the following method for computing

(1)

(2)

(3)

Use the classical Murnaghan–Nakayama rule and the result from the previous step to compute the Schur expansion of

7.
Statistic class functions

Our original motivation for introducing the symmetric functions
A_{n,I,J}
was to better understand class functions given by symmetrized permutation statistics.
In this section we discuss how to apply our results for this purpose.
A key tool for this approach is the theory of character polynomials, introduced in
[
16
]
.
We apply this theory to several examples including exceedances, inversion count.
The interested reader can find several other examples and additional details in our original paper
[
5
]
.

7.1.
Applications to permutation statistics

We begin by stating:

Theorem 7.1

(Character polynomials)
.

For
\lambda\vdash n
and
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
of cycle type
\mu
, there is a multivariate polynomial
f_{\lambda}
with rational coefficients so that

\chi^{\lambda}(w)=f_{\lambda}(m_{1}(\mu),m_{2}(\mu),\dots,m_{n}(\mu))

where
\deg(f_{\lambda})=n-\lambda_{1}
with the grading where
\deg(m_{k})=k
.

Viewing Theorem
6.7
as the Frobenius character of
R_{n}\,1_{IJ}
(with constant factor) and applying Theorem
7.1
, we obtain a result equivalent to Theorem 1.1 from our companion paper
[
6
]
:

Corollary 7.2
.

For
(I,J)
a partial permutation of size
k
, there is a polynomial
f_{IJ}(n,m_{1},\dots,m_{k})
of degree
k
where
\deg n=1
and
\deg m_{i}=i
so that

\frac{1}{n-\#(I\cup J)}R_{n}\,1_{IJ}=f_{IJ}(n,m_{1},\dots,m_{k}).

The proof of Corollary
7.2
in
[
6
]
is entirely combinatorial, avoiding representation theory and symmetric function theory entirely.
Using the fact that characters form a basis for class functions, results from
[
6
]
can be applied to recover Theorem
6.7
.
However, our proof in
[
6
]
is indirect, relying on Möbius inversion on the set partition lattice.
Therefore, the methods in this paper are easier to render effective.

A
permutation statistic
is a function
\Psi:\sqcup_{n\geq 1}{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Many classical permutation statistics can be expressed as linear combinations of
1_{IJ}
’s.
Recall that the Reynolds operator is the projection
R:{\mathrm{Fun}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})\to{\mathrm{Class}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathbb{C}})
defined by
(R\,f)(w)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{v\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}f(v^{-1}wv)
.
Applying the Reynolds operator
R_{n}
to
\Psi
, we obtain a class function whose associated character is then a linear combination of
A_{n,I,J}
’s.
As a consequence of Theorem
6.7
, we see the expansion of
R_{n}\,\Psi
into irreducible characters would have coefficients with growth governed by the polynomials
c_{\lambda}(n)
.
Further applying Corollary
7.2
, we obtain an explicit expansion of
R_{n}\,\Psi
as a function of
n
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}
.
Our companion paper
[
6
]
introduces a family of
regular statistics
whose expansion into
1_{IJ}
’s is particularly well-suited to such expansions.
Here, we limit our focus to several special cases, all of which are classical.

For any
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
we have a symmetric function
{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(R\,f)\in\Lambda_{n}
. Various authors
[
4
,
3
,
8
]
have studied the Schur expansion of

.
Our original motivation for introducing the path Murnaghan–Nakayama rule was to calculate these expansions systematically, which we explain how to do in this section before presenting examples of several special cases.

We present an in-depth analysis of the exceedance statistic

8.
Concluding Remarks

We present some further directions of inquiry.

8.1.
Parabolic subgroups

If
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})\vdash n
, the
parabolic subgroup

{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu}\subseteq{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu}:={\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu_{k}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. For any set
X\subseteq{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
of permutations, we write
[X]_{+}\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
for the group algebra element
[X]_{+}:=\sum_{w\in X}w
.

Let
(I,J)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}
be a partial permutation. As in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, there exist permutations
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
such that
[I,J]=u_{1}\cdot[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}]_{+}\cdot u_{2}
in the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}]
. For
\lambda\vdash n
, by cyclic invariance of trace we have

\chi^{\lambda}([I,J])=\chi^{\lambda}\left(u_{1}\cdot[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}]_{+}\cdot u_{2}\right)=\chi^{\lambda}\left(w\cdot[{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}]_{+}\right)

where
w:=u_{2}u_{1}
. Corollary
6.3
is a combinatorial rule for evaluating this trace. We can ask for analogous rules for parabolic subgroups more general than
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n-k}
.

Problem 8.1
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash n
and let
w\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Determine a combinatorial rule for evaluating

\chi^{\lambda}\left(w\cdot[{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu}]_{+}\right)=\sum_{v\,\in\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mu}}\chi^{\lambda}(wv).

8.2.
Unitary groups

We can also ask for a continuous analog of Corollary
6.3
in the context of unitary groups. For
n\geq 0
, let
U(n)
be the group of
n\times n
complex unitary matrices. The irreducible polynomial characters
\chi^{\lambda}:U(n)\to{\mathbb{C}}
are indexed by partitions
\lambda
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
. If
A\in U(n)
has eigenvalues
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, then
\chi^{\lambda}(A)=s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. This realizes
\chi^{\lambda}
as an explicit continuous function
U(n)\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

For
r\leq n
, we have a natural embedding
U(r)\subseteq U(n)
of unitary groups which sends a matrix
B\in U(r)
to the matrix direct sum
B\oplus I_{n-r}
where
I_{n-r}
is the size
n-r
identity matrix. Since
U(r)
is a compact Lie group, it admits a unique Haar measure, and any continuous function on
U(r)
is integrable against this measure.

Problem 8.2
.

Let
r\leq n
, let
\lambda
be a partition with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
, and consider the embedding of unitary groups
U(r)\subseteq U(n)
. For a fixed
A\in U(n)
, evaluate the integral

\int_{B\,\in\,U(r)}\chi^{\lambda}(AB)

with respect to Haar measure on
U(r)
.

8.3.

s_{\lambda[n]}

For any real-valued
f:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
has Schur expansion

{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(Rf)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}(n)\cdot s_{\lambda}.

Proposition 7.5 in
[
5
]
shows
c_{(n)}
is the expected value of
f
on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

Problem 8.3

Interpret the other coefficients
c_{\lambda}
appearing in the Schur expansion of
{\mathrm{ch}}_{n}(Rf)
.

For

, let

Gaetz and Pierson give an explicit formula for the coefficient of
