Feasibility of a short-term intensive home-based cognitive and physical training program for older adults: a single-arm pilot study
Authors: Seyoung Shin, Ikhyun Lim, Hongsuk Baik, EunYoung Park, Jeong Min Hong, Jong Moon Kim, MinYoung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies note the significant effects of Cognitive Training on older adults, but achieving consensus on optimal dosage is challenging due to intervention protocol heterogeneity. Short-term training seems preferable for clinical use due to potential dropout among older adults during long-term intervention. This feasibility study aimed to explore preliminary changes in cognitive and functional outcomes following a 4-week home-based cognitive and physical training program.MethodsThis prospective single-arm intervention study enrolled older adults aged ≥ 65 years who were capable of independently performing activities of daily living. Adults with a Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score of < 24 points, a history of any brain injury, or difficulty in continuing the intervention were excluded. The training program comprised 1–2-hour cognitive tasks and 1-hour physical exercise for 5 days per week for 4 consecutive weeks. The MMSE, Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and modified Barthel Index (MBI) scores were assessed before and after the intervention. Functional outcomes were analyzed according to compliance level.ResultsTwenty-five participants were enrolled and completed the trial. After the intervention, preliminary changes were observed in MMSE, RBANS-attention, BBS, and MBI scores (P < 0.05). The RBANS total score (primary outcome) did not significantly change. However, exploratory analyses showed a significant within-group change in the RBANS attention domain. The high-compliance group showed greater increments in RBANS short memory and delayed memory indices and BBS scores than the low-compliance group (P < 0.05).ConclusionThese findings suggest preliminary feasibility signals rather than evidence of efficacy, and controlled trials are needed to confirm these observations.Trial registrationThe trial registration number of this study is NCT06648343 (retrospectively registered: Oct 16, 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07655-9.

Introduction

The older adult’s population is increasing globally. With aging, the cognitive function progressively declines, eventually leading to dependence in daily living [1]. Previous large long-term follow-up study involving community-living older adults reported that 69.5% of older adults experienced difficulty in performing at least one instrumental activity of daily living (ADL) [2].

Some clinical trials on older adults have demonstrated significant effects of training-based interventions in addressing functional decline, regardless of presence of neurological disorders. For instance, in patients with Alzheimer’s disease, a recent systematic review found that cognitive training (CT) can lead to substantial global cognitive improvements [3]. Similarly, in normal old age population, the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability demonstrated that a long-term multi-domain intervention which included both CT and physical exercise (PE), not only improved cognitive function but also reduced the risk of new chronic diseases [4–6]. A meta-analysis of studies combining cognitive and physical training in both cognitively impaired and non-impaired older adults revealed that these combined interventions were significantly effective in enhancing overall cognitive and physical function [7].

The amount and method of delivering interventional contents are important issues for training aimed at enhancing the cognitive ability of older adults. Owing to high heterogeneity in intervention protocols, reaching a consensus on the optimal CT dose is difficult. With respect to the duration, short-term training is preferable for clinical application because of the possible drop-out in the older adults during a long-term intervention. In previous clinical studies targeting older adults without cognitive impairment, the duration varied from 2 weeks to 2 years (3–180 sessions), and the intervention dose per session ranged from 30 min to 2 h [7–9]. A short-term trial involving cognitively healthy participants showed that a protocol of 48-hour intervention with a 8-week duration was the most intensive and that multi-domain CT had a beneficial effect on executive function [10]. Kanaan reported that short-term intensive CT for 40–50 h over 2 weeks was feasible and effectively induced cognitive improvement in patients with early-stage Alzheimer’s disease [11].

Based on previous studies, short-term intensive CT appears to be acceptable and beneficial for older adults with or without known cognitive disorder. Moreover, regarding methods of delivering the training content, which is closely associated with the place of practicing the interventions, attempts to provide training at home are increasing. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the demand on home-based interventions for vulnerable older adults or patients susceptible to infection has increased [12, 13]. Considering this, investigating the effectiveness of short-term home-based cognitive interventions is necessary.

Dose–response findings from prior meta-analyses suggest that cognitive training interventions are more likely to demonstrate transfer and maintenance effects when at least 10 sessions are delivered and cumulative exposure exceeds approximately 30–60 h. The 40–60-hour protocol was chosen based on previous dose–response studies showing that 30–60 h of cognitive training is feasible for older adults and may yield measurable changes [7–10]. The lower bound in the present study was set at 40 h to ensure cumulative exposure exceeded minimal effective thresholds reported in previous literature and to account for potential variability in adherence in a home-based setting. Finally, the present study aimed to explore preliminary changes in cognitive function and daily living capability of older adults without current disabilities after a 4-week short-term home-based CT program combined with PE (20 sessions for 4 weeks, with a dose of 40–60 h in total). This range also balanced feasibility and participant burden for a home-based program. By adopting a shorter duration and similar or longer total intervention hours for the program than those in previous studies, we conducted a clinical investigation with a more intensive protocol than before.

Methods

Participants

This prospective single-arm intervention study was conducted from September 25 to November 6, 2020. The opportunity to participate in this study was open to the town residents of Yangcheon-gu in Seoul, Korea. The inclusion criteria were as follows: adults aged ≥ 65 years who were capable of independently performing usual ADL and who provided informed consent for this study. In order to exclude adults with noticeable risks of cognitive impairment, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (i) a Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score of < 24 points [14]; (ii) a history of any possible cause of brain dysfunction such as stroke, brain tumor, dementia, cerebral palsy, or Parkinsonism; and (iii) difficulty in continuing the intervention of this study because of poor systemic condition or medical diseases, as judged by the principal investigator of this study. As this was a feasibility study, no formal sample size calculation was performed.

This study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, particularly the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA Bundang Medical Center (IRB file no.: 2020-09-005). Written and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol has been made publicly available on the clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06648343, retrospectively registered at Oct 16, 2024).

Content of the training program

In all accessible previous studies [7–11], intensive treatment protocols generally involved interventions lasting less than 2 h per day. In contrast, this study implemented a more intensive approach, with daily interventions lasting between 2 and 3 h, to evaluate feasibility and observe potential changes in functional measures in older adults.

The intervention program comprised cognitive and physical training tasks, and performance was recorded in a workbook for personal use. The workbook used in this study and its contents were developed by our research team, which consisted of rehabilitation medicine physicians, psychologists, occupational therapists, and physical therapists.

As for creation of the CT content, protocols established by the clinical practice team of CHA Bundang Medical Center and existing papers on CT were referred to and combined [9, 15–19]. A team of experienced occupational therapists and rehabilitation physicians held several expert meetings to refine the program. The research team reviewed CT tools currently used in the hospital, along with cognitive activity workbooks available for older adults in Korea [20–23], selecting the most applicable elements for this population. A PE program was developed based on previous reports regarding exercise programs for patients with dementia [24–27], and a gymnastic video content produced by the Educational Broadcasting System, a Korean public broadcaster, was used [28].

Each day’s program comprised approximately 60–120 min of CT and 60 min of PE, performed on weekdays for 4 consecutive weeks (20 sessions in total). The CT tasks focused on memory, attention, and mental flexibility, and comprised: visual memory activity (CT-1), auditory memory tasks (CT-2), short-term memory tasks (CT-3), memory recall tasks (CT-4), and visual and auditory attention tasks (CT-5). PE involved stretching, muscle strength training, and coordination training. There was no fixed order between CT and PE within a day; participants were instructed to distribute the daily sessions at their own pace, taking rest periods between blocks as needed to minimize fatigue and accommodate individual circadian preferences. Training that could not be completed on a weekday was recommended to be supplemented on the following weekend, with the goal of fulfilling a 5-day-per-week schedule. Detailed information about the workbook and exercise program is presented in Additional file 1.

Implementation of training and content delivery

To begin the training, all participants attended a 2-hour orientation conducted by the study team, in which the purpose of the study and the detailed methods of performing the training were explained. Each participant was then provided with a workbook of approximately 200 pages, containing schedules, cognitive task pages, diary entries, and self-monitoring guidelines for the 4-week period, to be completed using paper-and-pencil. In addition, daily audio and video materials accompanying the cognitive and exercise tasks were distributed to participants via a smartphone messenger service.

To support adherence, the researchers contacted participants daily by phone or text message to encourage consistent participation and to address any difficulties. A social worker from the local public welfare center visited the homes of participants who specifically requested in-person assistance.

Following the 4-week intervention, adherence was quantified from each participant’s written workbook records. The implementation (completion) rate was calculated as the number of completed pages divided by the total number of pages. Because CT-1 and CT-3 did not have predefined grading standards, the success (correctness) rate — defined as the number of correct answers divided by the total number of questions — was calculated only for CT-2, CT-4, and CT-5. For physical activity, participants were surveyed by telephone regarding the percentage of prescribed exercise sessions they completed.

Outcome measures

Outcome assessments were conducted before and after the 4-week intervention using approved nationality-specific versions of standardized functional measures. In particular, the MMSE [29] and Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) [30] were used to evaluate cognitive function. The Berg Balance Scale (BBS) [31] was used to assess balance and gross motor ability, whereas the modified Barthel Index (MBI) [32] was used to evaluate capabilities in ADL independency.

The prespecified primary outcome was the RBANS total score. Secondary outcomes included MMSE, BBS, and MBI scores. The RBANS consists of five domain indices: (i) immediate memory (list learning and story memory), (ii) visuospatial/constructional ability (figure copy and line orientation), (iii) language (naming and semantic fluency), (iv) attention (digit span and coding), and (v) delayed memory. With the RBANS, one of the four types (namely, A, B, C, and D) was utilized to avoid a learning effect by repeated measurement with a relatively short-term interval. In this study, the participants were randomly divided into two groups to conduct an initial evaluation with type A or C test, respectively, and post-intervention evaluation was performed using other types of test.

As baseline assessment, outcomes were assessed within 2 weeks prior to the start of the training program. Post-intervention assessments were conducted within 1 week after the end of the program. Medical doctors evaluated cognitive and physical abilities after securing inter-rater reliability for measurements with intra-class coefficient > 0.9. An expert clinical psychologist also conducted part of the cognitive evaluation. The analyses included not only the total measurement scores but also the RBANS, MMSE, and MBI subscores. Given the nature of the intervention, participants, intervention providers, and baseline assessors were not blinded. Due to the single-arm design, blinding to intervention allocation or assessment time point was not feasible. However, to reduce assessment bias, post-intervention assessments were conducted by an independent assessor who was blinded to participants’ baseline scores.

After 4 weeks of training, feedback regarding the program was obtained from the participants. The participants were instructed to fill in a questionnaire focusing on satisfaction and comfort levels, which comprised ten questions about the following four subjective domains [33]: (i) satisfaction, (ii) pressure/tension, (iii) value/usefulness, and (iv) willingness to continue the program (see Additional file 2).

Adherence measures (implementation and success rates) are described in the Implementation of training and content delivery section. Beyond the prescribed intervention, participants were encouraged to engage in as much physical activity as possible during the study period. Apart from the exercise program provided as part of the intervention, we also recorded how many hours per week and what type of exercise each participant engaged in individually.

For the analysis of the effect of practice on the outcomes, the participants were divided into two groups (namely, the high-compliance [HC] group and low-compliance [LC] group) according to the median value of the implementation rate for each CT and PE task. Because the median implementation rate was 100%, participants with 100% completion were categorized as high-compliance, and all others as low-compliance.

To examine whether there were differences in outcomes according to the level of individual physical activity outside of the intervention, we conducted a comparative analysis by dividing participants into a high physical activity [HP] group (those who engaged in more than the average hours for total participants) and a low physical activity [LP] group (those who engaged in less than the average hours for total participants).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software version 27.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All participants were included in the analyses regardless of adherence to the intervention. Pre- versus post-intervention differences in functional scores were analyzed using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test according to the results of normality testing, and between-group comparisons by implementation and success rate were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test. To complement p-values, effect sizes were reported for all comparisons: Cohen’s dz (the mean of within-participant differences divided by their SD) for paired t-tests, and the matched-pairs / rank-biserial correlation r for Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann–Whitney U tests, respectively. Effect sizes are presented alongside p-values in Tables 2 and 3. Given the pilot feasibility design of this study, analyses of multiple secondary outcomes, subscores, and subgroup comparisons were considered exploratory; an uncorrected threshold of P < 0.05 was applied without adjustment for multiple comparisons, and statistically significant findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously rather than as confirmatory evidence of efficacy.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 27 adults (5 men and 22 women) were enrolled in the study from September 25 to October 5, 2020. Two female participants withdrew after screening visits. All 25 participants started the training program on October 5, 2020, which ended on October 30, 2020 (Fig. 1). All participants completed both pre- and post-intervention assessments, and no missing data were present; thus, no imputation procedures were required. Subsequently, all of them underwent a follow-up evaluation on November 4 or 6, 2020. The average age (mean ± standard deviation) of participants who completed the study was 72.88 ± 4.68 years (range, 65–87 years; men, 76.00 ± 6.36 years; women, 72.10 ± 4.00 years), with 8.60 ± 3.46 years of education (range, 5–18 years; men, 9.00 ± 3.08 years; women, 8.50 ± 3.62 years). The individual physical activities that participants engaged in other than the intervention during the study period mainly included walking, hiking, stretching, and strength training, with an average of 5.09 ± 4.28 h per week. Outcomes of the cognitive and physical training intervention

After training, statistically significant within-group changes were observed in the total MMSE, MBI, and BBS scores from 27.32 ± 1.66 to 27.96 ± 1.37 (P < 0.05), from 97.08 ± 2.53 to 98.56 ± 1.69 (P < 0.05), and from 54.68 ± 1.65 to 55.08 ± 1.50 (P < 0.05), respectively (Fig. 2). The total RBANS score did not significantly change (96.72 ± 15.40 to 98.20 ± 16.59, P = 0.655); however, the attention domain of RBANS subscore showed a significant positive change (99.60 ± 11.12 to 103.12 ± 11.58, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the attention/calculation domain of MMSE score showed statistically significant within-group changes from 4.16 ± 0.99 to 4.60 ± 0.65 (P < 0.05). Among the MBI subscores, the bathing (4.64 ± 0.49 to 4.92 ± 0.28, P < 0.05) and ambulation (14.04 ± 1.43 to 14.76 ± 0.83, P < 0.05) scores also showed statistically significant within-group changes (Table 2). Because analyses of multiple cognitive domains and subscores were exploratory, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Analysis according to cognitive task compliance and success rate

The CT implementation rate varied from 30% to 100% (mean ± standard deviation: 87.20 ± 20.31%, median: 100%). Therefore, the participants were divided into two groups: the HC group, which implemented all tasks (n = 15, 100%), and the LC group (n = 10, mean implementation rate 68.0 ± 20.4%). In the exploratory compliance subgroup analysis, the HC group exhibited significantly larger score changes for the short memory (HC 2.87 ± 12.9 vs. LC -10.8 ± 10.1, P < 0.05) and delayed memory (HC 3.80 ± 10.6 vs. LC -6.80 ± 11.0, P < 0.05) domains of RBANS and total BBS (HC 0.67 ± 0.72 vs. LC 0.00 ± 0.47, P < 0.05) than the LC group (Table 3). These subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

The implementation and success rates varied according to the tasks. The median success rate for the task of listening to a short article and solving given questions (CT-2, part I) was 58%. In the auditory task in which the participants had to listen and write down words (CT-2, part II), 13 out of 25 participants had median implementation and success rates of 0% for both. The median implementation and success rates were 90.8% and 75.7%, respectively, for the remaining 12 participants. The implementation and success rates for the visual attention task (CT-1) were as high as 100% and 99%, respectively.

Analysis according to weekly physical activity level

Out of 25 participants, only one did not respond to the survey regarding their individual physical activity outside of the intervention. Therefore, for the remaining 24 participants, we divided them into two groups based on the overall average of 5 h of individual activity per week. Participants who engaged in more than 5 h of activity were designated as the HP group (n = 14), and those with less than 5 h as the LP group (n = 10). In the group comparison, there were no significant differences between the HP and LP groups in terms of age, gender, or changes in MMSE, RBANS, and MBI total scores. Additionally, when comparing the compliance levels with the intervention between the two groups, no significant differences were found. (See Additional file 3)

Satisfaction survey

Overall satisfaction results indicated that 88% of participants reported positive responses (“very satisfied” and “satisfied”) (Q1). Furthermore, they expressed positive responses (“very satisfied” [76%] and “satisfied” [72%]) to each questionnaire for the CT and PE programs (Q2 and Q3) (see Additional file 4). With respect to Q4 and Q5, which pertained to the duration of the entire intervention, 56% and 32% of participants answered that the 4-week intervention was appropriate and long, respectively. In terms of daily program time consumption, 86% answered that it was appropriate. As for responses to the question about pressure and tension, 64% answered that the overall difficulty level of the program was appropriate, whereas 24% expressed that the tasks were difficult (Q6). With respect to the effectiveness of the program in relation to the cognitive, physical, and ADL functions, 64%, 84%, and 76% of participants answered that they were “very satisfied” or “satisfied” for each function (Q7–Q9). Finally, 76% expressed willingness to participate in the program again (Q10).

Discussion

In this study, the intensive short-term home-based program consisted of CT combined with PE. In this feasibility study, preliminary within-group changes were observed in attention, balance, and certain ADL items. These within-group patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy but rather as preliminary signals for potential areas of interest. Satisfaction with the workbook and exercise materials was generally high, suggesting that the home-based format was acceptable to participants.

Previous reports have described beneficial effects of combined cognitive and physical training in various older adult populations [34, 35]. Although direct comparison is not possible due to differences in design and intensity, our feasibility data suggest that such multimodal approaches may be acceptable for further testing. The lack of change in RBANS total scores, despite a small increase in MMSE, reinforces the need for cautious interpretation (see Limitations). Given the single-arm design, observed changes cannot be interpreted as causal effects of the intervention. Rather, they represent preliminary feasibility signals that warrant further investigation in controlled trials.

A previous systematic review of cognitive-physical interventions in healthy older adults [36] reported improvements in the attention domain following combined training. Because attention is essential for broader cognitive processing [37], the preliminary within-group attention changes observed in the present study may provide a rationale for future controlled investigation. When the participants were divided into two groups according to the compliance level, both the immediate and delayed memory indices of RBANS showed statistically greater within-group increases in immediate and delayed memory scores compared with the LC group, which showed decreases. Given the single-arm design and multiple comparisons, these subgroup differences should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Future controlled studies are needed to clarify whether compliance meaningfully influences cognitive trajectories.

In this study, the BBS was used to evaluate gross motor ability and balance, which showed statistically significant within-group changes after intervention. Moreover, the HC group showed larger score changes than the LC group in the exploratory subgroup analysis. The BBS is a powerful tool for discriminating fallers from non- fallers [38]. Changes in balance and attention were concurrently observed, consistent with prior literature suggesting potential associations between these domains. Attention control is known to be associated with the risk of falls in older adults [39], and attention control ability has been suggested to be linked to gait performance in older people with a high risk of falls [40]. The relationships among attention, balance, and fall risk observed in prior literature may inform hypotheses for future controlled trials.

As for ADL, participants demonstrated small within-group increases in walking and bathing scores. Given that BBS scores also showed modest changes, these ADL findings may reflect improved confidence or familiarity with the tasks rather than a direct effect of the intervention [41]. Some participants informally reported that they previously felt unsteady during bathing and needed to hold on for safety. A previous randomized controlled trial and systematic review also reported that cognitive and physical training led to increased total Barthel Index scores; however, there was no item-by-item analysis [42, 43]. The Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly (ACTIVE) project [44, 45] showed that reasoning training preserved the instrumental ADL function in older adults, which remained even at 10 years after the intervention.

It remains unclear how CT causes structural or functional changes in the brain. Structural brain volumetric studies reported that CT increased or preserved the gray matter volume [46]. A meta-analysis showed that CT activated the left middle frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and parietal cortex in older adults [47]. Increased physical activity was also linked to changes in prefrontal and hippocampal blood flow, potentially enhancing attention [48]. In the young and old, fitness was associated with attention [49]. While the underlying physiological mechanism remain uncertain, the concurrently observed within-group changes in attention and physical performance may be hypothesis-generating and warrant further investigation.

Despite the relatively long daily intervention time (approximately 2–3 h), participants achieved a median implementation rate of approximately 100% over the 4-week home-based program. Regular supervision through periodic contact has previously been reported to enhance compliance in older adults [50]; adopting this strategy may have contributed to the high implementation rate observed even within a short intervention period. While direct comparison with previous studies is limited by heterogeneity in intervention content and duration, our findings support the feasibility of conducting a short-term, intensive, home-based cognitive and physical training program in older adults.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-arm feasibility study without a control group, the observed within-group changes cannot be attributed causally to the intervention and may reflect natural variation, regression to the mean, or non-specific effects. Two such non-specific effects warrant explicit acknowledgement: (1) practice effects, as repeated administration of cognitive tests — particularly screening-level instruments such as the MMSE — is known to inflate post-intervention scores even in the absence of true cognitive change; and (2) the Hawthorne effect, whereby the increased researcher attention inherent in our protocol (daily phone/text contact and, where requested, social-worker home visits) may have improved performance and adherence independently of the intervention’s specific content. We attempted to partially mitigate practice effects on the RBANS by using alternate forms (Type A or C at baseline, with the other type at follow-up), but no analogous mitigation was available for the MMSE, MBI, or BBS. Future randomized controlled trials with attention-matched control conditions are needed to determine whether the observed changes reflect true intervention-specific effects.

Second, the small sample size (N = 25) and unequal sex ratio (5 men / 20 women) limit statistical power and generalizability. Moreover, our participants were community-dwelling volunteers who responded to a public recruitment notice and were independent in ADL, likely representing a motivated and higher-functioning subgroup of older adults. This volunteer-based selection bias, combined with relatively high baseline cognitive scores (mean K-MMSE 27.32, near the upper end of the normal range) — which may produce ceiling effects on screening-level instruments — constrains the generalizability of our adherence and feasibility findings to lower-functioning or less-motivated older populations. Future trials should consider stratified or quota-based recruitment to capture a broader functional spectrum.

Third, objective adherence could be quantified only for the three CT tasks with scoring criteria (CT-2, CT-4, CT-5); other CT components and the PE program relied on self-reported completion, and outside physical activity was captured by a brief, non-validated telephone survey. These measurement limitations may misclassify true engagement. As an exploratory check on external influences, we compared outcomes by self-reported outside physical activity and found no significant differences between the high- and low-activity groups, nor in compliance with the intervention between them (LP 91.00 ± 20.25% vs. HP 83.57 ± 20.98%, P = 0.212). Finally, no follow-up assessments were performed, precluding evaluation of whether the observed changes were maintained beyond the immediate post-intervention window. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up to assess durability of effects.

Conclusion

This feasibility study observed preliminary within-group changes in cognitive, physical, and ADL measures following a short-term home-based program combining CT and PE for older adults. Participants generally reported high satisfaction, suggesting that such an intensive home-based format may be acceptable and implementable in this population. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons, the observed significant findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Future randomized controlled trials with larger samples are needed to determine whether the observed changes are attributable to the intervention and to establish its potential clinical utility.
