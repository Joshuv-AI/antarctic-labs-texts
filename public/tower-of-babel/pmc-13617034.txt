Urban health performance configurations in Chinese cities: longitudinal fsQCA, 2019–2024
Authors: Donghai Ye, Juan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionComposite city indices can rank urban health but rarely explain which conjunctions of public provision, economic capacity, resident action, and environmental conditions are sufficient, whether those conjunctions persist, or how cities move between them over time. This study addresses this configurational and temporal gap using longitudinal evidence from Chinese cities.MethodsWe analyzed a balanced panel of 296 Chinese cities from 2019 to 2024, comprising 1,776 city-year observations. Urban health utility (HR), derived from the Tsinghua Urban Health Index (TUHI), was used as the outcome, while government health services (HS), health infrastructure (HI), health industry (HP), health behavior (HA), and health environment (HE) were treated as conditions. Longitudinal fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) was conducted using pooled calibration anchors, supplemented by annual models, pooled/between-year/within-city diagnostics, dominant-configuration transitions, robustness analyses, and a 1-year lag model.ResultsNo single condition met the necessity-consistency threshold of 0.90. Two sufficient configurations for high HR were identified: ~HS*HI*HA*~HE (consistency = 0.926; raw coverage = 0.329) and HS*HI*HP*HA (consistency = 0.877; raw coverage = 0.476). Their union had a consistency of 0.872 and coverage of 0.540. The four-domain HS*HI*HP*HA configuration recurred from 2021 to 2024 and was reproduced unchanged in the lagged model. Sample perturbations generally retained the main configurations, whereas compressed calibration anchors and subgroup analyses revealed sensitivity and heterogeneity.DiscussionHigh urban health performance is associated with multiple sufficient configurations rather than a single necessary condition. The longitudinal strategy distinguishes recurring configurational relations from changing city membership over time and supports differentiated policy portfolios while retaining environmental safeguards. These findings represent set-theoretic associations rather than causal effects.

Introduction

Cities concentrate development opportunities, environmental pressures, and social inequalities. Population health is therefore both an outcome of urban development and a condition that enables cities to remain productive, inclusive, and resilient. The 2030 Agenda connects good health and wellbeing with sustainable cities and communities, while the healthy-cities tradition emphasizes that health is shaped well-beyond the health-care sector. Urban services, physical infrastructure, economic organization, everyday behavior, and environmental quality operate jointly, and comparable health performance may arise from different combinations of these domains (1, 2). A sustainability-oriented analysis must therefore move beyond isolated determinants and examine how social, economic, governance, behavioral, and environmental conditions interact.

China's Healthy China agenda has intensified city-level monitoring and cross-sector governance (3–5). Substantive urban-health research shows that rapid urbanization simultaneously reshapes health-care access, infectious and chronic disease burdens, health behavior, and environmental exposure (6). Spatial access to medical facilities and primary care remains markedly unequal across Chinese regions and cities (7, 8), while multicity evidence links particulate air pollution with daily mortality and broader work documents the continuing health burden of degraded air and water (9, 10). These findings make services, infrastructure, behavior, and environment substantively relevant rather than merely convenient index domains. Composite indices and spatial assessments facilitate multidimensional comparison (11), but rankings and average-effect models do not show whether favorable performance is associated with one dominant domain, coordinated membership across domains, or multiple substitutable conjunctions. Existing configurational urban studies have focused mainly on general sustainability or green performance (12–14), leaving unresolved which urban-health configurations recur over time and whether the cities displaying them remain the same.

Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is suited to this question because it evaluates necessity, sufficiency, conjunctural relations, equifinality, and asymmetry rather than estimating the average net effect of each condition (15–17). QCA has been applied to complex public-health and implementation settings in which outcomes are associated with combinations of interventions and contexts (18–20). A conventional cross-sectional fsQCA cannot reveal whether a sufficient configuration recurs across years, and a regression coefficient cannot identify multiple logically substitutable set expressions. Conversely, a pooled solution may conceal temporal instability, whereas separate annual solutions may overlook whether the same cities sustain membership. Temporal QCA research therefore recommends repeated solutions, lag structures, and case-based sequence diagnostics (21–23).

The analytical framework is inductively derived from the TUHI hierarchy rather than presented as an independently validated causal theory. It is nevertheless aligned with whole-system healthy-cities thinking, which treats population health as jointly embedded in public provision, the built environment, economic organization, resident practices, and ecological conditions (1, 2, 6). Within this source-grounded framework, HS and HI represent public service and spatial provision, HP represents industrial and market capacity, HA represents resident practices, and HE represents environmental context. HR is the separate health-outcome domain. We ask whether any single condition is necessary; which configurations are sufficient for high and non-high HR; how stable the configurations and city memberships are across years; and whether the principal relations persist under lagging, calibration changes, sample perturbations, component removal, and subgroup diagnostics. The contribution is to replace a single ranking or average-effect account with a longitudinal, set-theoretic description of recurring and heterogeneous urban-health configurations, while explicitly bounding interpretation to associations within the TUHI measurement system.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This study draws on the Tsinghua Urban Health Index (TUHI), a city-level assessment system developed under the guidance of the National Health Commission by the Institute for China Sustainable Urbanization and the Vanke School of Public Health at Tsinghua University. The framework translates the broad “health + city” concept into a China-adapted measurement system using six first-level domains, 16 second-level components, and 39 third-level indicators. The public release provides annual first-level scores for 296 prefecture-level and above cities; the manuscript now distinguishes these source-provided scores from the unavailable raw records and aggregation files.

TUHI combines conventional official statistics with multi-source social big data. The public documentation identifies statistical yearbooks, departmental surveys, and census records as official inputs, and describes large-scale application, payment, enterprise, search-engine, and online-map data for behavior, consumption, industry, attention, and facility accessibility. The authors received only the released six-domain workbook and did not access personally identifiable records, provider-level microdata, or the raw normalization and weighting files. Consequently, the analysis audits the released panel but cannot independently verify every upstream data-collection or fusion procedure.

The workbook covered publication years 2020–2025. Following the portal's dating convention, each publication year was mapped to the preceding analytical year, yielding 2019–2024 while retaining publication year in the audit file. Administrative-code harmonization confirmed the same 296 cities in every year. The final balanced panel therefore comprised 1,776 observations (296 cities × 6 years); no city or year was excluded, and the six analysis variables contained no duplicate city-year records or missing values. Variable labels, units, and displayed precision were consistent across releases.

The design is observational and ecological, with the city-year as the unit of analysis. The 1,776 records are not treated as statistically independent replications. Large-N fsQCA remains appropriate because the estimand is a set-theoretic sufficiency relation rather than a sampling-based regression coefficient; however, repeated measurements can make a pooled relation appear more stable than it is. We therefore used common pooled calibration anchors, annual solutions, pooled/between-year/within-city cluster diagnostics, dominant-configuration transitions, a 1-year lag specification, and city-clustered train-test splits in which all observations from a city remained together. These diagnostics address temporal dependence descriptively but do not create an independent causal design. City size and the four macro-regions were used only for stratified diagnostics.

Measurement framework, outcome, and conditions

The TUHI structure aggregates 39 third-level indicators into 16 second-level components and then into six first-level domain scores. The outcome was urban health utility (HR), the English label used here for the source's health-outcome domain. HR combines infectious-disease control, chronic-disease management, and physical fitness; specifically, its three underlying indicators concern notifiable infectious-disease incidence, premature mortality from major chronic diseases, and the proportion meeting the National Physical Fitness Measurement Standards. HR is therefore a composite city-level health-performance score, not preference-based health utility, quality-adjusted life-years, an individual clinical outcome, or the overall TUHI. It is used as a bounded proxy for urban health-related sustainability because it captures whether cities simultaneously perform on communicable disease, chronic disease, and physical fitness.

The five conditions are HS, HI, HP, HA, and HE. The published hierarchy assigns each of the 39 third-level indicators to one and only one first-level domain: 36 distinct indicators feed the five condition domains, while three different indicators feed HR. Thus, no condition score is arithmetically included in HR and no third-level indicator is shared between a condition and the outcome in the published hierarchy (Table 1). Nevertheless, all six scores originate from the same TUHI provider and share an undisclosed normalization, weighting, and aggregation environment. The absence of item overlap reduces direct mathematical circularity but does not eliminate common-method dependence or correlations induced by shared scaling. Because the raw weights and aggregation files are unavailable, the study cannot independently reproduce the six domain scores; all findings are therefore interpreted as associations among released domains within the TUHI system rather than as external causal validation of independent constructs.

Analytical use of the published domain scores

Table 1 documents construct content and the non-overlapping assignment of the published third-level indicators; it does not imply access to or re-estimation of those indicators. All six first-level scores were analyzed exactly as released. No city-year value was reconstructed, rescaled, imputed, winsorized, deleted, or manually adjusted. The analysis consequently evaluates configurational associations within the original TUHI measurement system, and the conclusions do not claim that HR was independently measured outside that system.

Calibration

Descriptive statistics for the six source-provided indices are presented in Table 2, and the corresponding calibration anchors are shown in Table 3. Each index was transformed into fuzzy-set membership using direct logistic calibration. The main specification used pooled P5, P50, and P95 values as full non-membership, crossover, and full membership anchors, respectively, with an inclusion degree of 0.95. External policy thresholds were not available in comparable units for all six proprietary domain scores, so empirical anchors were necessary. P5/P95 were selected to distinguish the distributional tails while limiting leverage from extreme observed values, and the pooled median supplied a common point of maximum ambiguity. Pooling anchors across all years kept set membership comparable over time. Twelve variable-case combinations fell exactly at 0.5; these values were retained and were not shifted by an arbitrary constant. Sensitivity analyses repeated the models with pooled P10/P50/P90 and P25/P50/P75 anchors, thereby testing whether the main configurations depended on the chosen tail cut-offs.

Necessity and pooled sufficiency analysis

For high HR and non-high HR (~HR), every condition and its negation was assessed as potentially necessary. A consistency of 0.90 served as the prespecified necessity reference and was interpreted jointly with coverage and relevance of necessity (RoN) (17, 24). For pooled sufficiency, the truth table used consistency of 0.85, proportional reduction in inconsistency (PRI) of 0.70, and a case-frequency threshold of 10. Complex, parsimonious, and intermediate solutions were estimated, and the first intermediate solution is reported. Directional expectations favored membership in all five conditions for high HR and non-membership for ~HR. If no row met all prespecified thresholds, the result was recorded as no solution rather than obtained by post-hoc threshold relaxation.

Longitudinal diagnostics

We evaluated the pooled high-HR solution using cluster diagnostics that distinguish pooled, between-year, and within-city consistency and coverage (22, 25). Annual QCA models retained the same consistency and PRI thresholds and used a frequency threshold of 5. Year-specific configurations were linked into families when their signed-condition Jaccard similarity with a configuration in the preceding observed year was at least 0.50. For each city-year, the family with the greatest configuration membership was designated dominant when membership reached at least 0.50. These layers prevent the repeated city observations from being interpreted as independent evidence of temporal stability.

To assess temporal ordering, all five conditions were lagged by 1 year, linking conditions in 2019–2023 to HR in 2020–2024 (N = 1,480). The lagged model used the same pooled truth-table thresholds. Lagging does not eliminate time-varying confounding, identify a counterfactual, or establish a mechanism; it is interpreted only as a temporal robustness check on set-theoretic associations.

Robustness, component, and subgroup analyses

Following guidance on robustness in large-N QCA (26), we estimated a parameter grid combining consistency thresholds of 0.80, 0.85, and 0.90; PRI thresholds of 0.65, 0.70, and 0.75; frequencies of 5, 10, 15, and 20; and three pooled calibration schemes (95/50/5, 90/50/10, and 75/50/25). This produced 108 high-HR models. We also estimated 40 sample variations, five leave-one-condition-out models, and 100 city-clustered 70/30 train-test splits. All observations from a city remained in the same split, and held-out consistency, PRI, and coverage were calculated from each training solution.

Subgroup QCA was conducted separately by annual city-size category and by macro-region. City-size models used a frequency threshold of 5, whereas regional models used a threshold of 3. Nine cities changed size category during the study period and therefore contributed observations to both size strata. Because several subgroup solutions fell below the main consistency threshold and regional samples were smaller, these analyses are treated as exploratory heterogeneity diagnostics.

Software and reproducibility

Data management and QCA were performed in R 4.4.2 using QCA 3.23 and SetMethods 4.1 (27, 28).

Results

No single condition was necessary

No positive or negated condition reached the prespecified necessity-consistency reference of 0.90 for high HR (Table 4 and Figure 1). HP had the highest necessity consistency (0.756), followed by HI (0.734) and HA (0.731). Coverage was moderate, and RoN ranged from 0.694 to 0.820. Thus, across the pooled panel, high urban health performance was not constrained by membership or non-membership in any single condition.

Two equifinal configurations were sufficient in the pooled panel

The pooled intermediate solution comprised two sufficient configurations (Table 5). Configuration 1 (~HS*HI*HA*~HE) combined high membership in infrastructure and health behavior with lower membership in government health services and health environment. Its consistency was 0.926, PRI was 0.725, and raw coverage was 0.329. This expression is a set association under relative calibration; it does not mean that lower HS or HE improves HR. Configuration 2 (HS*HI*HP*HA) combined high membership in services, infrastructure, industry, and health behavior, while HE was unconstrained. Its consistency was 0.877, PRI was 0.733, and raw coverage was 0.476.

Between-year and within-city diagnostics showed unequal stability

Between-year consistency for Configuration 1 exceeded 0.85 in every year, rising from 0.878 in 2019 to 0.970 in 2024 (Figure 2). Configuration 2 fell below 0.85 in 2019 (0.810) and 2020 (0.822), but ranged from 0.882 to 0.902 during 2021–2024. The union similarly remained below 0.85 in 2019 and 2020 and exceeded the threshold from 2021 onwards. The pooled relation was therefore supported more clearly in the later releases.

Annual solutions changed, but one configuration recurred

At the annual thresholds, no high-HR solution was obtained for 2019, one configuration was identified for 2020, and two were identified in each year from 2021 to 2024 (Table 6; Figures 3, 4). The 2019 result is traceable to the truth table: no row simultaneously met consistency 0.85, PRI 0.70, and frequency 5. It was not produced by missingness, duplicate records, or city exclusion because all 296 cities were present and complete. We did not lower a threshold post-hoc. Because the available technical files do not permit independent verification of upstream aggregation changes, we cannot determine whether measurement or the pre-pandemic policy context contributed; the result should therefore be read as a threshold-bounded annual difference, not as evidence of a COVID-19 effect. The HS*HI*HP*HA configuration appeared in every annual model from 2021 through 2024; accompanying expressions combined HI, HP, and HA with ~HE in 2021 and 2023, HA with ~HE in 2022, and HS and HI with ~HE in 2024.

Appearance and disappearance of configuration families.

The ~HR analysis followed a different sequence: two configurations appeared in 2019, one in 2020, and one in 2021, whereas no ~HR solution was obtained from 2022 to 2024. This sequence records the occurrence of sufficient set relations within the observed panel and does not establish strict year-to-year causal change in population health.

Dominant city configurations changed over time

Among consecutive years for which high-HR families could be assigned, 67% of city-years dominated by HR_F1 remained in HR_F1 in the following year, 30% moved below the 0.50 dominant-configuration threshold, and 4% shifted to HR_F2 (Figure 5). Of city-years without a dominant configuration, 80% remained without one, 18% entered HR_F1, and 2% entered HR_F2. These are transitions in fuzzy-set configuration membership, not population migration, commuting, or inter-city movement.

The main four-domain configuration persisted in the lagged model

The 1-year lagged analysis produced two configurations: HA*~HE and HS*HI*HP*HA (Table 7). The first had consistency of 0.862, PRI of 0.709, and raw coverage of 0.507; the second had consistency of 0.900, PRI of 0.778, and raw coverage of 0.471. Their union achieved consistency of 0.853, PRI of 0.712, and coverage of 0.596. Relative to the contemporaneous pooled solution, the first lagged expression simplified to HA*~HE (signed-condition Jaccard similarity = 0.50), whereas the four-domain configuration was reproduced unchanged (similarity = 1.00). These are temporally ordered associations, not lagged causal effects.

Robustness was strongest under the main calibration

All 36 high-HR parameter models under the main 95/50/5 calibration produced a solution, with mean main-configuration retention of 0.870 (Table 8 and Figure 6). Under 90/50/10 calibration, 16 of 36 models produced a solution and mean retention among defined results was 1.000. Under 75/50/25 calibration, only 8 of 36 models produced a solution and mean retention was 0.500. Thus, the main configurations were reproducible across truth-table thresholds under the principal calibration, but solution recovery weakened when the calibration anchors were compressed.

Robustness was strongest under the main calibration

Each leave-one-condition-out model produced an alternative high-HR solution (Table 9 and Figure 7). Solution consistency ranged from 0.877 after excluding HE to 0.898 after excluding HS, whereas coverage ranged from 0.389 after excluding HA to 0.476 after excluding HE. No positive condition assessed alone reached sufficiency consistency of 0.90. Removing a condition therefore changed the minimized configuration without eliminating all sufficient expressions; this is evidence of configurational substitutability, not of a causal compensatory mechanism or an unimportant excluded domain.

Discussion

Principal findings and contribution to urban sustainability

This study identified two equifinal configurations associated with high health-related urban sustainability performance across 296 Chinese cities. Neither reduced to a single necessary or sufficient input. The more broadly covering configuration combined government health services, health infrastructure, health industry, and health behavior; the alternative combined infrastructure and behavior with lower service and environmental membership. These expressions do not estimate causal effects. Their contribution is twofold: they move beyond composite rankings and subsystem averages by identifying sufficient conjunctions, and they add pooled, annual, between-year, within-city, transition, and lagged diagnostics to urban configurational studies that have largely used cross-sectional samples or non-health sustainability outcomes (12–14).

The four-domain coordination configuration is the most recurrent empirical pattern. It appeared in every annual model from 2021 to 2024, had the largest unique coverage in the pooled solution, and was reproduced unchanged in the lagged model. This recurrence is compatible with a whole-system interpretation in which favorable HR membership co-occurs with aligned services, infrastructure, industrial capacity, and resident practices. It does not demonstrate an independent positive effect for any domain, establish a mechanism called synergy, or imply that mechanically increasing all four scores will necessarily raise HR.

Temporal stability and dominant-configuration transitions

The annual and cluster diagnostics qualify the pooled findings. Between-year consistency strengthened after 2020, yet only a small minority of cities sustained the same dominant configuration throughout the panel. The transition matrix showed frequent changes between HR_F1 and the no-dominant-configuration state. A set relation can therefore recur at the pooled level while individual cities enter or leave membership in the configurations that sustain it.

This distinction matters for sustainability monitoring. An aggregate indicator can suggest a stable pattern while masking changing conjunctions of services, infrastructure, industry, behavior, and environment. The diagnostics record recurring and shifting fuzzy-set membership within a 6-year panel; they do not measure population migration, commuting, or an individual health trajectory, and they do not establish that a change in any one condition produced a later change in HR.

Sustainability interpretation and policy implications

The lower-HE configuration requires separate empirical and normative interpretation. Empirically, ~HE means that high HI and HA membership can co-occur with high HR membership when HE is below its pooled crossover; it does not show that environmental weakness improves health or is causally compensated by other domains. Normatively, weak sustainability permits substitution between natural and human-made capital, whereas strong sustainability treats critical natural capital and ecological thresholds as non-substitutable (29, 30). Because air quality and urban green systems directly shape health and can involve irreversible or unequally distributed harm, the observed configuration cannot justify trading environmental protection for performance elsewhere. A strong-sustainability reading therefore treats environmental standards as safeguards external to the empirical sufficiency claim.

Policy priorities should differ by observed configuration. For cities resembling Configuration 1 (~HS*HI*HA*~HE), the immediate task is to preserve infrastructure access and health-supporting behavior while closing service and environmental gaps: strengthen primary and preventive services, improve air-quality and green-space performance, and monitor whether HR remains high without persistent deficits. For cities resembling Configuration 2 (HS*HI*HP*HA), policy should consolidate coordination among service delivery, accessible facilities, responsible health-industry capacity, and resident participation, while adding an explicit environmental floor because HE is logically unconstrained in that expression. Cities with no dominant configuration require diagnosis before portfolio transfer. In all cases, these are priorities informed by observed associations, not predictions of causal effects.

Transferability, strengths, and limitations

The empirical configurations are not directly generalizable beyond the 296 Chinese cities because TUHI content, national institutions, urban administrative boundaries, and pooled calibration anchors are context-specific. The transferable element is the analytical strategy: other countries can define locally valid, non-overlapping condition and outcome sets and test their own pooled, annual, and within-unit relations. Cross-national transfer would require measurement invariance or explicit redefinition of constructs, locally meaningful calibration anchors, and replication under different governance and environmental regimes. Methodologically, the balanced panel, prespecified thresholds, separate HR and ~HR analyses, clustered diagnostics, lagging, parameter and sample perturbations, component removal, held-out validation, and subgroup analyses improve transparency but do not remove the contextual boundary.

Several limitations define the validity boundary. First, the 39 published third-level indicators are assigned without overlap across the six first-level domains, so HR does not arithmetically include HS, HI, HP, HA, or HE; nevertheless, all scores share a provider and an undisclosed normalization, weighting, and aggregation environment. Common-method dependence and scaling-induced correlations therefore remain possible, and the unavailable raw weights prevent independent reconstruction. Second, the released values were retained without imputation or deletion, but year-to-year measurement comparability could not be independently verified. This uncertainty also limits interpretation of the 2019 no-solution result, which may reflect the observed truth-table structure, upstream measurement, policy context, or their combination. Third, percentile calibration is empirical rather than tied to external policy thresholds, and compressed anchors reduced solution recovery. Fourth, 6 years are a short sequence; repeated observations were addressed with cluster, annual, transition, lag, and city-split diagnostics, but temporal dependence remains and lagging does not establish causality. Fifth, QCA reports set relations rather than net effect sizes. Sixth, several subgroup unions fell below 0.85 and mean held-out PRI was 0.609. Finally, omitted institutional, demographic, economic, environmental, and spatial conditions may support other configurations.

Conclusion

Across 296 Chinese cities, high HR membership was associated with two equifinal sufficient configurations and no single necessary condition. The HS*HI*HP*HA configuration was the most recurrent across pooled, annual, lagged, and sample analyses; policy for cities displaying it should consolidate coordinated services, infrastructure, responsible industry, and resident participation while enforcing an environmental floor. Cities displaying ~HS*HI*HA*~HE should preserve infrastructure and behavioral strengths but prioritize service and environmental deficits rather than regard lower membership as desirable. Cities without a dominant configuration require local diagnosis before transferring either portfolio. These conclusions are contextual, set-theoretic associations within the TUHI system. Longitudinal fsQCA complements city rankings and regression-based averages by showing both recurrent sufficient configurations and changes in city membership over time, without claiming causal effects.
