Comparison of dominant foodborne and inhalant allergies developing in the first 5 years of life☆
Authors: Qingxiu Xu, Qing Jiang, Yaqi Yang, Wenjing Li, Dongxia Ma, Wei Zhang, Rongfei Zhu, Lin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring the first 5 years of life, atopic individuals may develop cutaneous or respiratory symptoms with foodborne or airborne allergens. The route and dose of exposure are associated with sensitization. However, limited studies compare food and airborne allergies in these children.ObjectiveTo characterize and compare the developmental patterns of symptoms, specific IgE (sIgE)/specific IgG4 (sIgG4), and B cell subsets in response to food or airborne allergens under 5 years old.MethodsChildren aged from birth to 5 years old with food allergy and/or respiratory allergy symptoms were recruited from Jan 2019 to Dec 2021. Clinical parameters were systematically documented, and the sIgE responses to 29 common allergens were quantified via ImmunoCAP. A subset of participants (n = 45, with 9 cases in each age group) underwent assessments of sIgE/sIgG4 to egg, milk, and house dust mite (HDM) components. Peripheral B cell subsets were phenotypically characterized using multiparametric flow cytometry (n = 20).ResultsAs the children ages, positivity rate to inhalant allergens (D. farina and D. pteronyssinus) rapidly increased to 57.04% and 58.23% respectively, while positivity for food allergens of eggs and milk dropped to 27.44% and 32.7%. Notably, allergen component-specific IgG4 levels against egg and milk allergens were significantly higher compared to those against HDM. However, all sIgG4 exhibited mild (0.3 < ρ < 0.52) or no correlation (ρ < 0.3) with corresponding components sIgE. Although most B cell subsets had no significant difference between the food and HDM sensitization, the tSNE analysis identified that the frequency of C6 (CD19+CD38+ IgG4+IgE++) effector B cells significantly increased in the 4–5 year HDM group, while memory B cell-like cells (C11, CD19+CD27+CD38+IgG4+) highly expressed in food allergies.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates the frequency, route, and dosage of exposure may contribute to distinct variations in the repertoire of allergen-specific IgE/IgG antibodies and B cell subsets, highlighting the importance of exposure modalities in shaping early immune responses.Keywords: Foodborne, Inhalant allergens, sIgE and sIgG4, Allergen component, B cell subsets

Introduction

The prevalence of hypersensitivity diseases has significantly increased in children worldwide over the past few decades.1,2 During early childhood, individuals with atopy typically manifest cutaneous or respiratory symptoms with dynamic changes. Analyzing sensitization to respiratory and food allergens in early life at onset can enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying allergy development and facilitate the design of preventive interventions.

Atopic individuals exhibit both IgG and IgE antibodies upon exposure to minute quantities of naturally occurring allergens.3 Specific IgE antibodies can play a crucial role in identifying the relevant allergen and offer a guide to therapy.4 The role of IgG antibodies in allergic reactions, contrary to that of IgE, remains controversial. Allergen-specific IgG has been considered as a marker of natural exposure5 and attributed to protective effect.6 However, several studies have demonstrated that IgG responses towards allergens are more frequent and/or stronger in individuals sensitized with IgE compared to non-sensitized patients.7,8 The corresponding production of IgG antibodies in children almost always precedes or occurs simultaneously with the development of an IgE response. The IgG response against allergens typically commences as an IgG1-dominated response and, upon continuous exposure, can gradually transform into an IgG4-dominated response, a phenomenon also characterizing allergen-specific immunotherapy.9 While previous studies have primarily focused on total IgG levels, our study will specifically investigate sIgG4 of dominant allergens before 5 years old.

B cells play an essential role in type I allergy. Unlike the stability of B-cells in adulthood, dynamic alterations have been witnessed for B-cells in peripheral blood (PB) during the initial several years. Total B-cell and memory B-cells (MBCs) were all reported to increase after birth, remain at a high level within 5 years of age, and gradually decrease thereafter to adult-like values 10, 11, 12, 13. Meanwhile, the disparate outcomes regarding the sIgE and sIgG4 induced by food or inhaled allergens suggest that B cells exhibit distinct immune responses to these 2 types of allergens. However, the comparation of the responses of B cell subsets to food or inhaled allergens in early years, remains limited. More profound understanding of B cells in the initial several years might contribute to better comprehend the pathogenesis of allergic diseases.

In this analysis, we investigated the age-related trends of foodborne and inhalant allergies from birth to 5 years of age. Our comprehensive cross-sectional study conducted on a large sample size, aimed to elucidate the frequency, route, and dose of exposure that contribute to distinct divergence in allergen-specific IgE/IgG4 antibody repertoires and B cell subsets.

Methods

Patients

Children aged 0–5 years were recruited from the Department of Allergy between January 2019 and December 2021. Participants exhibited symptoms of food allergy and/or respiratory allergy.

Inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of allergic rhinitis with or without asthma, based on the Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) guidelines and the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines.14,15 Patients with upper respiratory infections or chronic rhinosinusitis were excluded. (2) Presence of cutaneous symptoms, including atopic dermatitis diagnosed according to the Hanifin and Rajka criteria, and urticaria.

For allergen component-specific sIgE/sIgG4 analysis, a total of 45 participants were enrolled—9 children from each of the 5 predefined age strata (0–1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, and 4–5 years)—selected via stratified random sampling from the primary cohort. Stratification was performed by both age and sensitization profile (food-sensitized, house dust mite–sensitized, or co-sensitized) to ensure balanced representation across key clinical and demographic dimensions. For B cell subset phenotyping, 20 participants were included—2 children per age stratum (n = 2 × 5 age groups) within each of the 2 major sensitization subgroups (food-sensitized and house dust mite–sensitized; n = 2 × 5 × 2 = 20)—drawn exclusively from eligible donors with freshly isolated, viable PBMC samples meeting pre-specified quality criteria (eg, viability >90%, yield ≥1 × 106 cells).

Allergen sIgE, components sIgE and sIgG4 test

Serum samples were analyzed for allergen-specific IgE (sIgE) using the ImmunoCAP 250 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The allergens tested included: d1 (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Der p)); d2 (Dermatophagoides farina, Der f); f1 (egg); f2 (milk); f3 (codfish); f4 (wheat); f10 (sesame); f13 (peanut); f14 (soybean); f17 (hazelnut); f18 (brazil fruit); f23 (crab); f24 (shrimp); f26 (pork); f27 (beef); f35 (potato); f180 (river fish); e1 (cat hair); e5 (dog hair); m1 (Penicillium); m2 (Cladosporium); m3 (Aspergillus fumigatus); m6 (Alternaria alternata); i6 (Blatella germanica, Bg); w1 (Ambrosia artemisiifolia); w6 (Artemisia sieversiana, As); w22 (Humulus scandens); t6 (Juniperus formosana); t11 (Platanus acerifolia). Positive sIgE results were classified into 6 categories: class 1 (≥0.35 to <0.70 KU/L), class 2 (≥0.70 to <3.50 KU/L), class 3 (≥3.50 to <17.50 KU/L), class 4 (≥17.50 to <50 KU/L), class 5 (≥50 to <100 KU/L), and class 6 (≥100 KU/L).

Specific IgG4 against HDM (Der p 1, Der f 1, Der p 2 and Der f 2, Der p 7, Der p 10, Der p 21 and Der p 23), egg (purified allergen molecule: ovomucoid; crude extracts: egg, egg white, egg yolk) and milk (purified allergen molecules: α-lactalbumin, β-lactoglobulin, casein; crude extract: cow's milk) components were measured using a test kit (Hangzhou Zheda Dixun Biological Gene Engineering Co, LTD, Hangzhou, China). Recombinant components were pre-coated on a chip. After incubating serum on the chip for 1-h, anti-human IgG4 antibodies (biotin-conjugated) and alkaline phosphatase-streptavidin were sequentially added. IgG4 concentrations were calculated using a protein standardization curve. The positive cut-off value for sIgG4 was set at the 95th percentile of the upper limit from 210 healthy non-allergic donors, with sIgG4 levels above 156 UA/mL and sIgE levels above 0.35 IU/mL defined as positive.

Identification of maturation and isotypic subsets of PB B-cells

B-cells (CD19+ lymphocytes) were classified into maturation-associated subsets based on their expression of CD19, CD27, CD38, CD5, CD24, CD21, IgM, and IgD. Plasmablasts cells were defined by a CD38++CD27+CD24−CD21−CD5−CD19low phenotype. Pre-germinal center (GC) B-cells were identified by their unique CD27−IgM + IgDlowCD38+ features and further subdivided into immature/transitional CD38hiCD24hiIgM++IgD+ B-cells and CD27−CD38lowCD24+IgM+IgD++ naïve B-lymphocytes. Unswitched memory B-cells (MBCs) were characterized as CD38lowCD5−CD27+IgM++IgD+, while switched MBCs were defined as CD38lowCD5−IgM-IgD- CD27−B-cells. These marker combinations have been validated for reliably distinguishing between mature and immature B-cells.16

tSNE, FlowSOM algorithm and cluster explorer

The tSNE and FlowSOM algorithms were employed to analyze marker expression across PBMCs and identify novel subsets. Viable CD19+ cells were down-sampled to 3000 cells per triplicate and donor using the Downsample v3.3 plugin in FlowJo, then concatenated into one sample per group. FlowSOM was performed separately for each study group, with CD19+ live cells sorted into 12 metaclusters to capture expected and potentially novel cell populations. The Cluster Explorer plugin was used to generate interactive cluster profile graphs, heatmaps, and tSNE plots.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for normally distributed continuous data and frequencies/percentages for categorical data, were calculated. The t-test and Wilcoxon paired test were used for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparisons, and the Spearman rank test was used to assess correlations. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Age-related changes of allergen sensitization and multisensitization

Our study enrolled 1583 children (1002 males; 581 females) aged 0–5 years between January 2019 and December 2021 (Supplementary table S1). All 29 allergens were categorized into 5 groups, including Food (f1+f2+f3+f4+f10+f13+f14+f17+f18+f23+f24+f26+f27+f35+f180), HDM (d1+d2), Weed + Tree (w1+w6+w22+t6+t11), Mold (m1+m2+m3+m6), and Others (e1+e5+i6). Our cross-sectional study revealed significant shifts in allergic sensitization profiles across different age groups. As shown in Fig. 1, over 89% exhibited exclusive food sensitization in the 0–1 year group. In contrast, sensitization to HDM was limited to approximately 8.5%, with nearly half of these children also showing food sensitization. In the 1–2 year group, there was an increase in the variety of allergens observed, including pollen. The children who multisensitzed to Food + HDM (21.24%) and to Food + HDM + Mold (1.55%) were higher than monosensitization for HMD (5.18%) and Mold (0.52%), respectively. In the 2–3 year group, the prevalence of Food and HDM co-sensitization rose sharply to 35.44%, nearing the level of Food allergy alone (41.26%). Monosensitization rates for HDM also increased notably to 15.5%. In the 3–4 year group, there was a significant shift in sensitization profiles. HDM monosensitization rose to 32.39%, surpassing the 16.98% rate for Food alone. Prevalence rates for pollen, mold and animal hair increased compared to HDM; however, this increase was slower than that observed for HDM and most cases involved combined allergies with HDM. Finally, in the 4–5 year group, the percentage of children monosensitized to HDM increased to 40.99%. However, the co-sensitization rates for Food and HDM dropped to 22.36%, and monosensitization to Food alone decreased further to 12.11%.

Interestingly, there were mild or no correlations observed between most of the HDM components' sIgG4 and their corresponding sIgE levels. Discussion

In the study, we compared the age-associated shifts of food and airborne allergens in the first 5 years of life. As the children aged, the positivity rate for HDM were increased rapidly, while the positivity for eggs and milk decreased. Specific IgE levels for HDM, milk and egg showed mild or no correlation with most corresponding components sIgG4. Furthermore, B cell subsets had no significant difference between the food and HDM sensitization, except for transitional B cells. Notably, C6 (CD19+ CD38+ IgG4+IgE++) effector B cells significantly increased in the 4–5 year HDM group, whereas C11 (CD19+CD27+CD38+IgG4+) memory B cell-like B cells were highly expressed in children with food allergies.

We observed dynamic changes in both food and inhaled allergies among children under 5 years of age, noting that the progression of food allergy differs from that of inhaled allergy. The prevalence of food allergies, particularly sensitization to milk and eggs, exceeded that of inhaled allergies before the age of 3. However, the prevalence of food sensitization declined significantly with age, a trend consistent with findings from other studies 17, 18, 19. In contrast, specific IgE responses to airborne allergens persisted and were strongly associated with allergic rhinitis and asthma 20,21. We found an age-related increase in the prevalence and sIgE levels of positive inhaled allergens among children under 5 years old. Our data revealed that dust mites were the predominant airborne allergen in these children, with a prevalence order of mites > m6 > e5 > e1 > t11. These results suggest that the route of allergen exposure (respiratory or gastrointestinal) can significantly influence the onset time and duration of allergic diseases.

Inhalation allergens and food allergens exhibit distinct patterns in the concentrations of allergen component-specific IgG4, which is considered a marker of antigen exposure or immune modulation factor that competes with IgE. For children who were negative for HDM sIgE, their corresponding IgG4 levels were also negative. Some HDM-positive children exhibited IgG4 production, but this was not correlated with IgE levels. For food allergies, IgG4 levels against both crude extracts and purified allergen components of milk and egg were significantly higher than those against house dust mite (HDM) purified molecules. Some patients who are IgE-negative for milk or egg allergens also exhibit persistent IgG4 seropositivity. IgG4 responses to milk only mild correlated to sIgE, and egg allergens are not correlated with IgE levels. Other studies have similarly found that IgG4 and IgE responses to dust mites, eggs, and milk are only weakly correlated, uncorrelated, or even negatively correlated.22 These findings suggest that IgE and IgG4 may be generated through distinct pathways and are influenced by the route and dosage of allergen exposure. The frequency, route and dose of exposure may lead to a distinctly diverging repertoire of allergen-specific IgE/IgG antibodies.

We analyzed functional B-cell subsets using conventional surface markers, with the specific aim of exploring the regulatory effect of early-life allergy on B cell differentiation, due to B cells play a critical role in immunoglobulin production.23 No significant differences were observed in B cell subsets between food and HDM sensitization groups, except for transitional B cells significantly higher expression levels in the 0–3 year HDM group. Transitional B cells migrated from the bone marrow to peripheral lymphoid organs where they settle as naïve cells. The proportions of these subpopulations decline with age, with particularly pronounced changes occurring before the age of 5.24 Although transitional B cells have been implicated in autoimmune diseases and tumors, their role in allergies has not been extensively studied.25,26 Several studies have demonstrated that transitional B cells primarily function during the latency period of allergen presentation. At this stage, these cells have not yet fully developed into memory B cells, and thus their role in allergic reactions is predominantly focused on initiating the initial response and promoting IgE production.27,28 Our data demonstrate that inhalation allergens induce a more pronounced Th2-type immune polarization compared to food allergens during early life.

In the following study, we utilized tSNE, FlowSOM algorithm, and Flow Explorer plugins for data analysis, incorporating IgE and IgG4 expression. Cluster 6 (CD19+CD38+IgG4+IgE++) was significantly elevated in the HDM allergy group and increased sharply with age. Unlike conventional plasmablasts B cell subtypes (CD19+CD38+CD27+), this CD27-deficient cluster likely due to early plasmablast differentiation or pathological conditions. Previous studies show elevated CD19+CD24−CD38hi plasmablasts in active IgG4-related disease29 and accumulating CD19+CD27−CD38+ B cells during early HIV infection.30 Our findings highlight the unique phenotype of Cluster 6 which may represent a distinct pathogenic or transitional B cell subset in HDM allergy, diverging from conventional CD27+ plasmablasts. Further functional studies are warranted to elucidate whether this subset contributes to IgE/IgG4 dysregulation or serves as a biomarker for disease progression. Meanwhile, Cluster 11 (CD19+CD27+CD38lowIgG4+) showed high expression in the food allergy group aged 0–3 years and continued to increase from 30.24% to 46.64% in the 4–5 year age group. We posit that these cells represent switched memory B cells expressing IgG4. Previous studies have identified IgG + memory B cells, particularly IgG1 memory B cells, as precursors of IgE plasma cells.31,32 Our findings reveal a significant increase in IgG4+ switched memory B cells specifically in the food allergy group but not in the HDM allergy group. We hypothesize that these cells are associated with the production of antibodies of the IgG4 subclass, and that differential expression of IgG subsets may lead to distinct clinical outcomes.

There are several limitations in our study. Although the sample size of our cross-sectional study is large, only total 20 children provided PBMCs for analysis, mainly due to parental reluctance to consent to additional blood draws and research-related procedures, which may introduce bias due to the small sample size. Future studies with larger longitudinal cohorts are needed to validate the cluster findings from these analyses. Second, our study participants were children who presented with skin or respiratory symptoms and subsequently attended our allergy outpatient clinic, rather than being part of a birth cohort study. This selection bias results in a higher prevalence rate, which does not accurately reflect the general population prevalence. Finally, we were unable to recruit healthy controls aged 5 years and below due to ethical considerations and parental reluctance. The absence of a healthy control group in this age range limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the baseline characteristics of transitional B cells in early childhood.

In summary, our study demonstrates that during the first 5 years of life, IgE responses initially emerge in response to food allergens before transitioning to a dominance of airborne allergens. Furthermore, IgG4 expression was observed in both sensitized and non-sensitized children for egg and milk allergens, yet this expression was exclusively evident in HDM-sensitized children. These findings suggest distinct allergic patterns between food and airborne allergens. Future investigations incorporating the analysis of antigen-presenting cell (APC) activity within lymphoid tissues associated with the gastrointestinal tract (GALT) and the nasal and bronchial mucosae (NALT, BALT) may provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying B cell class switching and the development of allergic responses.
