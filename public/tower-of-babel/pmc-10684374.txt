Comparative Analysis of Female Leadership Styles in Public and Private Sector Universities: A Study in Pakistan
Authors: Uzma Sarwar, Rameez Tariq, Muhammad Aamir, Yurong Guan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare the styles of leadership practices of female leaders in public and private Universities in Pakistan. In this study, both quantitative and qualitative data were collected using a mixed-method approach. An adapted and developed questionnaire was used for quantitative data collection, whereas qualitative data were collected through a semi-structured interview schedule. A sample of 200 female leaders was selected for quantitative data collection, while 10 females from the said sample were selected for qualitative data collection through a simple random method. Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS, whereas qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Based on the statistical results, this study discovered statistically significant differences in female transformational and transactional leadership styles and significant differences in job performance based on the university sector. This study discovered statistically insignificant differences based on the different positions of female leaders regarding transformational and transactional leadership, and job performance. Moreover, qualitative data revealed that female leaders clearly understood both leadership styles and how to improve job performance by practicing them. The originality of this study concerns the identification of the differences between leadership styles (transformational and transactional) practiced by female leaders of public and private Universities in Pakistan and explains female leaders' perceptions of the role of leadership styles in their job performance.Keywords: Leadership style, Transformational, Transactional, Pakistan

Literature review

James MacGregor Burns introduced the concept of transactional leadership in 1978. Transactional leadership is a style in which leaders focus on the exchange of rewards and punishments to motivate and guide their followers in achieving specific goals. This leadership style emphasizes clear structures, expectations, and performance-based rewards. Transactional leaders do not work well in environments that promote innovation and creativity. Transformational leadership is often contrasted with transactional leadership. Self-motivated individuals who perform well in a structured and guided setting are necessary for transactional leadership.

The concept of transformational leadership was first introduced by James V. Downton in 1973. Transformational leadership is a leadership style characterized by inspiring and motivating followers to achieve their full potential and exceed their self-interests for the collective goals of the organization. Transformational leaders encourage creativity, innovation, and intellectual stimulation, thereby fostering a sense of empowerment and personal growth among their followers. In other words, transformational leadership chooses to influence rather than command people to inspire and encourage employees (Demirtas, 2020). Transactional leadership emphasizes the value of interactions between a supervisor and its employees [20]. This idea relies on the path-goal theory of incentives, which suggests that people are more likely to get things done if they are motivated to do so by progressing toward specific goals [21]. The transactional leader can inspire their team by determining what drives them and then providing it. This style of management is based on a barter system, in which employees' subservience is exchanged for financial rewards [22]. This style of leadership, which centers on the value of exchange, is appropriate only in certain cultural contexts. According to Ref. [23]; a transformative leader motivates followers through more than a simple moral example. Subordinates may develop their full potential and provide excellent results. As people perceive it, leadership is a process that affects its followers, raises the spirits of both the leader and the leader and is rooted in values and ideals [20]. According to Burns, transformational and transactional leadership are at different extremes on a continuum [22].

[23] argued that transformational and transactional styles of leadership are incompatible. Contrary to what you may have heard [24], stated otherwise: Instead, Bass argues that there is a mutual benefit between the two styles of leadership [25]. Bass classified leadership styles as transformative and transactional as complementary rather than mutually exclusive. In his "complete spectrum of leadership style " theory, a leader may effectively combine transactional and transformational approaches [26]. In addition [23], argued that any kind of change is beneficial. Alternatively, Bass (2018) argues that transformation may have both positive and negative effects. A transformational leader inspires followers to exceed their standards. Furthermore, they are moved to forego personal gains in favor of the collective [27]. By educating followers to adapt to new circumstances, boost their performance, and help the leader, team, and organization achieve their objectives, transformational leadership may also enhance transactional leadership. It improves morale and helps followers feel that their leader is making a difference [28]. The difference between transactional and transformational leadership is that the latter seeks to develop subordinates into future leaders, while the former seeks to maintain the status quo [26].

[29] found that female involvement in management positions has significant role in the finance and human talent departments, and that transactional and transformational leadership were the most appropriate for female leaders. Several studies have identified the following four dimensions of transformational leadership.

Transformational leadership dimensions

Idealized influence

A transformational leader may exert an idealized impact by setting a personal example of dedication to the organization's objectives via charismatic role modeling. In doing so, the leader earns the esteem of his or her subordinates and the confidence of those who follow [31]. Transformational leaders display such behavior, which makes them role models for subordinates [22]. These leaders are respected, acknowledged, and trusted because of their expertise, perseverance, and resolution [32]. They prioritize the needs of subordinates above their own, create and uphold higher moral and ethical standards, take calculated risks, and do not abuse their position of power [33]. These leaders are also regarded as good decision-makers and effective communicators of the organization's vision and purpose [20]. Because of these leadership behavioral traits, followers emulate these leaders and want to be like them. This dimension contains two conceptually separate components: the first is "idealized influence behavior,” which is tied to the leader's behavioral traits, and the second is "idealized influence ascribed,” which is linked to the traits that the leader's subordinates attribute to them [34]. The likelihood that followers will internalize and attain the conveyed objectives is higher when charismatic leaders have faith in their subordinates to do so [35].

Inspirational motivation

Inspirational motivation is a leader's capacity to motivate and inspire people. Transformational leaders must establish a clear vision of the future, explain group expectations, and commit to set objectives. Transformational leaders must express their messages accurately, with strength, and authority. Other crucial leadership traits include optimism, excitement, and positivity [31]. According to Naguni et al. (2006), inspirational motivation is the quality of charismatic leadership in which leaders arouse their followers' ambitions by making emotional appeals to them and offering enticing predictions of the future [20]. These people inspire the group's spirit, set clear expectations that their followers are prepared to meet and show a commitment to the group's goals and vision [34]. A leader who inspires followers is seen as knowledgeable, enlightened, and attentive to challenges; requires no trusting cooperation from followers; and increases the commands of followers by assisting them in achieving the set objectives [35].

[36] proposed that servant leadership is positively connected to employees' intrinsic motivation and service innovative behavior. Moreover, employees' intrinsic motivation partially mediates the link between servant leadership and their service innovative behavior. Additionally, employees’ identification with the leader not only moderates the association between servant leadership and their intrinsic motivation, but also controls the mediating role of intrinsic motivation, in such ways that these two effects are more clear among the workforces with higher levels of identification with the leader than lower ones.

Intellectual stimulation

Transformational leaders push their subordinates to be more innovative and creative by asking them questions and thinking critically about what they are doing. This kind of leadership also encourages followers to question their beliefs and morals, as well as those of the leader and organization. In addition, finding a solution is performed in a way that involves everyone. Followers' creative ideas are welcomed, even if they go against what the leader thinks. It is good when subordinates take different approaches to problems and solve them in their way [20,33]. Transformational leaders are encouraged to think independently and creatively: A good leader encourages his team to go outside the box when coming up with ideas, and he does this by including them in decision-making. Transformative leaders encourage followers to think critically and provide their ideas. A good leader influences people to reframe situations in a new way. Leaders inspire their teams to be great by sharing their vision of the future [37].

Individualized consideration

There are individual wants and requirements among the members of each organization. Some people, for instance, are driven by financial incentives, while others seek novel experiences. Therefore, transformative leaders pay attention to details and cater to each person's specific requirements. The leader needs eavesdropping and observing skills to determine what drives each team member. The transformative leader creates an opportunity for individualized training for each team member via one-on-one coaching and mentoring. By participating in these events, team members are allowed to learn, develop, and enjoy their work [37]. A transformational leader acts as a mentor and cares about subordinates' success and growth to the highest level (Avolio, 1999) [26]. say that a leader must develop himself or herself if he or she wants to help his or her followers grow. Here, the leader shows that he or she understands that each follower has different needs and goals by acting differently around different people [33]. The leader makes it easy for followers to learn and grow by maintaining two-way communication, tailoring their interactions with subordinates, listening to them carefully, and giving them tasks with ongoing evaluations and support if needed [33]. Consideration behavior is essential for both transformational and transactional leadership [24]. (Seltzer & Bass, 1987). There is much evidence from different organizations and cultures that transformational leaders are more effective than transactional leaders because of these traits [26].

Transactional leadership dimensions

Three dimensions of transactional leadership and "non-leadership behavior have been discussed in the literature [32,38]; Bass &Avolio, 1994; [39]. These four factors are exchanged on different levels between the leader and the people who follow them [24]. Here, unlike a transformational leader, neither the need nor the growth of subordinates is a focus. However, similar to transformational leadership, transactional leadership has an ethical aspect. [40]; p. 185) say that "the moral legitimacy of transactional leadership is demanding in many ways. It depends on giving others the same freedom and chances you want for yourself, telling the truth, keeping promises, giving everyone what they deserve, and using good incentives or punishments".

Contingent reward

Recognition and prizes for achieving objectives are used by transactional contingent reward leadership as motivational factors for members. Through transformational leadership, an organization's members become more personally and socially connected to leaders and the group's purpose and objectives (Demirtas, 2020). In this model, an exchange occurs between leaders and subordinates. In exchange for obedience and work, subordinates receive a certain amount of money [20]. The transactional leader deals with subordinates that outline the goals to be reached and the rewards for doing so. Once the goals are reached, the leader gives agreed-upon rewards (Naguni et al., 2006). Peters and Waterman (1982) stated that contingent rewards are an excellent way to give subordinates positive feedback and to make them happy. However, many studies show that contingent rewards are not as effective as other types of transformational leadership in terms of subordinates' satisfaction, performance, and growth [33]. However, the meaning of leadership practices depends on the cultural and organizational contexts in which they are used. Ingroup collectivism, on the other hand, looks at how attached, loyal, and devoted people are to their organizations and families, and how willing they are to make sacrifices for them [41]. Some countries, like Iran, Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, and India, are high in "humane orientation” and "ingroup collectivism,” which means that people have strong bonds, loyalty, and care for their families, institutions, and communities. In these countries, people would instead be thanked by their leaders and coworkers rather than get paid for the extra help or time they have given the institution [41]. Humane orientation is the degree to which a society or culture encourages people to be fair, selfless, generous, helpful, considerate, and sensitive to others [41].

Management-by-exception (active)

This is a more proactive and successful kind of transactional leadership that corrects behavior by looking for what was done incorrectly rather than what was done well. As in "micromanagement,” the leader carefully checks job performance for faults to address issues before they arise (Demirtas, 2020). This kind of leader pays close attention to their team's progress, keeps track of errors and deviations from the norm, and intervenes as necessary [20,40]. A passive kind of management known as "management by exception” A leader who uses management by exception waits for problems to escalate before taking action, may be unaware of problems until they are brought to their attention by subordinates, and is therefore unlikely to achieve desired results [33,40]. Both kinds of management by exception are less effective than the traditional types of leadership, yet they are sometimes necessary [42].

Data and methodology

This study used a mixed-method research design (Fig. 1). In this method, the quantitative data were collected in the first phase, while qualitative data were collected in the second phase. Two research instruments, an adopted questionnaire, and a self-developed interview schedule, were used for data collection. In this study we used mixed methods due to the following reasons: first, to collect both quantitative and qualitative data, which can provide a more comprehensive understanding. Secondly, in this study, we used qualitative data to explore and explain the results of quantitative data. The mixed method helped to provide a deeper understanding of the findings and their implications, which can make research more relevant and impactful to a wider audience. Overall, mixed methods research can be a valuable tool for researchers to gain a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of research. The Likert-based questionnaire had 33 statements and three sections. The first two sections were related to transformational and transactional leadership style, which was adopted from Ref. [48] MLQ Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire. The third section was related to job performance, which the researchers developed. Similarly, in a semi-structured interview schedule, there were seven questions. All deans, HODs, directors, Senior Faculty, and coordinators belonged to the female gender working in public and private sector universities in Punjab Province of Pakistan formed the population. Pakistan is a patriarchal society, and women face many challenges in achieving leadership positions. Therefore, it is important to understand the specific experiences of female leaders in Pakistan to develop effective strategies for increasing their representation in leadership positions. The following are the major reasons to choose females in our research. First, according to a 2021 report by the American Council on Education, women make up only 30 % of presidents of four-year colleges and universities in the United States. This means that there is a need to understand the experiences and challenges of female university leaders to increase their representation in these positions. Second, studies have shown that universities with more female leaders tend to have more inclusive and supportive cultures for all students. This is because female leaders are often more likely to be approachable and empathetic, and they are more likely to create policies and practices that benefit all students. Third, seeing female leaders in positions of power can inspire and motivate female students to pursue their own leadership goals. This is especially important in STEM fields, where women are still underrepresented.

Study design.

From the said population, a sample of 200 females from 10 universities (five public & five private) was selected through purposive and simple random sampling techniques. Before the application of the instruments, their validity and reliability were confirmed by seeking expert opinions and conducting pilot testing (with 50 responded data) on a small sample. The reliability of the quantitative instrument occurred as (α = 0.87). Later, the researchers collected the data personally and through the online method using Google Forms. SPSS was used to analyze quantitative data, while the thematic approach was used to analyze qualitative data.

Simple statistics are a valuable tool for researchers because they are relatively easy to understand and interpret. They can also be used to test a wide variety of hypotheses. In the context of the hypotheses listed above, simple statistics can provide valuable insights into the leadership styles, job performance, and perceptions of female leaders in public and private sector universities. In addition to these reasons, there are a few other reasons why it is important to rely on simple statistics to support the analysis of the hypotheses. First, simple statistics are relatively inexpensive to collect and analyze. This is important for researchers who are working with limited resources. Second, simple statistics are relatively easy to communicate to a wide audience. This is important for researchers who want to share their findings with policymakers, practitioners, and the general public. There are also some limitations to using simple statistics. For example, simple statistics can only be used to test hypotheses about the mean or median of a population. This means that they cannot be used to test hypotheses about the distribution of a population. Additionally, simple statistics can be sensitive to outliers, which can distort the results of a study. Despite these limitations, simple statistics are a valuable tool for researchers who are interested in testing hypotheses about female leadership in public and private sector universities. They are relatively easy to understand, interpret, and communicate, and they can be used to test a wide variety of hypotheses.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal notable differences between female leaders in public sector universities and private sector universities regarding their leadership styles and perceptions towards job performance. Specifically, female leaders in public sector universities exhibited a higher level of attitudes towards practicing both transformational and transactional leadership styles compared to their counterparts in private sector universities. The higher level of attitudes towards practicing transformational leadership style among female leaders in public sector universities suggests that they are more likely to inspire and motivate their followers, promote intellectual stimulation, and foster a sense of empowerment and personal growth. This finding aligns with the nature of public sector universities, where there may be a greater emphasis on social impact and community development.

Furthermore, the higher level of attitudes towards practicing transactional leadership style among female leaders in public sector universities suggests a focus on clear structures, expectations, and performance-based rewards. This may be attributed to the bureaucratic nature of public sector institutions, where processes, rules, and regulations play a significant role in decision-making and resource allocation. On the other hand, female leaders in private sector universities demonstrated a moderate level of attitude toward practicing both transformational and transactional leadership styles. This may be indicative of the unique characteristics and dynamics present in private-sector universities, which may prioritize efficiency, competitiveness, and business-oriented approaches.

These findings highlight the importance of considering the organizational context and environment when examining leadership styles. The contrasting leadership styles observed in public and private sector universities suggest that leaders adapt their styles based on the specific demands and expectations of their respective sectors. The differences in attitudes towards leadership styles between the two sectors also have implications for job performance perceptions. Further exploration is warranted to understand how these differences in leadership styles may impact the motivation, engagement, and performance outcomes of followers in public and private sector universities. Additionally, investigating the factors that contribute to the variations in leadership styles and attitudes, such as organizational culture, institutional policies, and individual characteristics, can provide valuable insights for leadership development programs and strategies in both sectors.

Overall, these findings shed light on the distinctive leadership landscape within public and private sector universities about female leaders. Understanding these differences can help universities and educational institutions create supportive environments that foster effective leadership and enhance overall organizational performance. Thus, female leaders use both leadership styles according to the nature of the situation because, in the Pakistani context, subordinates are less motivated and lack knowledge and skills regarding innovation. So, in said environment, females have to use both leadership styles considering the situation. Thus, female leaders using transformational and transactional styles can establish a positive link with their subordinates and improve their performance by achieving the university's goals. These findings are in line with the findings of [49] which exposed a positive link between transformational leadership style and team performance similarly, the findings of [50]; and [47] were linked with the results of this investigation because their studies also discovered a link between both leadership styles and job performance.

[51] stated that the positive association between identity leadership and group-based pride is stronger when the leader political skill is advanced. Politically skilled leaders can access innumerable evidence and effectively convey influence on followers so that identity leadership has a robust effect on the pride of groups in the team. Therefore, leader political skill strengthens the indirect effect of group-based pride by which identity leadership improves team performance. So, leader political skill promotes the effectiveness of identity leadership in affecting team outcomes. Our results are reliable with these findings and endorse that managers should involve in more identity leadership behaviors to obtain better work results. Furthermore, identity leadership needs boundary conditions to produce more positive team performance. Politically skilled leaders can access to various information and deliver to their ideas to followers effectively, which strengthens the positive effect of identity leadership. Consequently, managers can increase their political skills to improve their inspiration on group members [9]. analysis showed that transformational and transactional leadership are closely related to employees' job performances which also supports the findings of this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights significant differences between female leaders in public and private sector universities in terms of their attitudes toward transformational leadership, transactional leadership, and job performance. Female leaders in public sector universities demonstrated a higher attitude towards practicing both transformational and transactional leadership styles, as well as job performance, compared to female leaders in private sector universities who exhibited a moderate attitude towards these factors. Interestingly, when comparing female leaders across different positions, no significant differences were found in their adoption of transformational leadership, transactional leadership, and job performance. This suggests that leadership styles and performance expectations may be consistent across various positions held by female leaders in both sectors.

Qualitative data further supported the understanding that female leaders possessed clear knowledge and understanding of both leadership styles and their role in achieving job performance. This underscores the importance of providing comprehensive training, workshops, and courses to empower and equip female leaders with the necessary skills and knowledge related to transformational and transactional leadership styles. In light of these findings, it is recommended that universities and educational institutions implement targeted interventions to enhance the leadership capabilities of female leaders. Such interventions can include training programs focused on developing transformational and transactional leadership skills, fostering a supportive organizational culture, and creating opportunities for networking and mentorship. By investing in the development of female leaders and their understanding of effective leadership styles, universities can harness the potential of their leaders to drive positive change, foster innovation, and improve overall job performance. Ultimately, this can contribute to the advancement of the education sector and facilitate the achievement of organizational goals in both public and private sector universities in Pakistan.

Limitation and Future Direction

The study may have a limited sample size, which could affect the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of universities in Pakistan. Additionally, this study may not account for specific contextual factors that influence leadership styles, such as cultural, organizational, or regional differences. In future research we can extend the research to include a comparative analysis of female leadership styles in universities across different countries, considering cultural, social, and organizational factors. Moreover, we will investigate the impact of different leadership styles on organizational outcomes, such as employee satisfaction, performance, and organizational effectiveness. We can also explore the role of mentoring programs and support mechanisms in developing and nurturing female leaders in both public and private sector universities. Last but not least, we can consider the intersectionality of gender with other factors such as race, ethnicity, and socio-economic background to understand how these factors shape the experiences and leadership styles of female leaders in universities.
