Box-Constrained Softmax Function and Its Application for Post-Hoc Calibration
Kyohei Atarashi, Satoshi Oyama, Hiromi Arai, Kashima Hisashi

Abstract
Controlling the output probabilities of softmax-based models is a common problem in modern machine learning. Although the Softmax \mathrm{Softmax} function provides soft control via its temperature parameter, it lacks the ability to enforce hard constraints , such as box constraints, on output probabilities, which can be critical in certain applications requiring reliable and trustworthy models. In this work, we propose the box-constrained softmax ( BCSoftmax \mathrm{BCSoftmax} ) function, a novel generalization of the Softmax \mathrm{Softmax} function that explicitly enforces lower and upper bounds on output probabilities. While BCSoftmax \mathrm{BCSoftmax} is formulated as the solution to a box-constrained optimization problem, we develop an exact and efficient computation algorithm for BCSoftmax \mathrm{BCSoftmax} . As a key application, we introduce two post-hoc calibration methods based on BCSoftmax \mathrm{BCSoftmax} . The proposed methods mitigate underconfidence and overconfidence in predictive models by learning the lower and upper bounds of the output probabilities or logits after model training, thereby enhancing reliability in downstream decision-making tasks. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our methods experimentally using the TinyImageNet, CIFAR-100, and 20NewsGroups datasets, achieving improvements in calibration metrics.

Introduction

The
\mathrm{Softmax}
function, which maps a real-valued logit vector to a probabilistic one, is an essential and fundamental component in machine learning and statistics
[
33
,
39
,
43
]
.
The
\mathrm{Softmax}
function with a temperature parameter
\tau>0
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})[i]\coloneqq\frac{\exp(x_{i}/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(x_{k}/\tau)}\quad i\in[K]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,K\}.

(1)

The temperature
\tau
softly controls the output probability vector.
As
\tau\to 0
,
\mathrm{Softmax}
converges to the argmax, while as
\tau\to\infty
, it tends toward a uniform distribution.
This temperature-based control plays a crucial role in various applications, such as calibration of machine learning models
[
17
]
, controlling the exploration-exploitation trade-off in reinforcement learning
[
41
]
, and balancing the trade-off between novelty and coherence in texts generated by large language models (LLMs)
[
36
]
.

However, temperature-based control is essentially
soft
and lacks the ability to enforce strict guarantees on output probabilities.
In various critical applications, it is desirable to impose
hard constraints
on the output, such as upper bound constraints:
\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})[i]\leq b_{i}
, box constraints:
a_{i}\leq\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})[i]\leq b_{i}
, and sparsity constraints:
\lVert\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})\rVert_{0}\leq c
, which allow for fine-grained control over the output vector to the intended state.
Among such hard constraints, box constraints are critical and have been naturally employed in certain domains.

•

Fairness-aware machine learning.

In the area of fairness-aware machine learning, a classifier is sometimes required to predict similarly for similar individuals.
This notion of fairness is called individual fairness
[
14
]
, and this requirement can be reduced to box constraints on the output probabilities.
For example, a softmax-based classifier may be constrained as
\lvert p_{k}-p^{\prime}_{k}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\in[K]
, and these constraints, where
\bm{p},\bm{p}^{\prime}
are the outputs of the classifier for two similar individuals, and
\varepsilon>0
is a tolerance parameter of fairness constraints.
These fairness constraints are clearly equivalent to the box constraints
p^{\prime}_{k}-\varepsilon\leq p_{k}\leq p^{\prime}_{k}+\varepsilon
.

•

Calibration and security.

Modern neural networks tend to be overconfident in their predictions
[
17
]
.
This leads to issues in reliability and trustworthiness: it can result in inappropriate decisions and cause membership leakage, i.e., information leakage of training data
[
8
,
15
]
.
Enforcing box constraints on output probabilities can mitigate overconfidence in neural networks; thus help avoid these issues.

In this study, we propose the
box-constrained softmax
(
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
) function, an extension of the conventional
\mathrm{Softmax}
function, by incorporating box constraints on the output probability vector.
To achieve this in a principled way, we adopt an alternative formulation of the
\mathrm{Softmax}

[

,
5
]
.
Although this formulation could pose computational challenges, we develop an

\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
, which runs in
O(K)
time if only upper or lower bounds are applied; otherwise it runs in
O(K\log K)

While several studies have extended the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function to produce a sparse probability vector and control its sparsity

,
27
,
30
]
, we focus on directly controlling the range of each probability via explicit box constraints, thereby addressing a fundamentally different requirement.

Moreover, we propose two post-hoc calibration methods for classification models,

, based on
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
.
These methods calibrate classification models by learning the upper and lower bounds of either the output probability vector or logits.
Our experimental results demonstrate that the proposed methods can improve calibration metrics, such as the expected calibration error, for neural network classifiers.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

We formulate the
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function in a mathematically principled manner.

•

We develop an efficient and exact computation algorithm for
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}

•

We propose two post-hoc calibration methods for classification models based on the
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}

•

We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods on the CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, and 20NewsGroups datasets.

Notation.

We denote the probability simplex by
\Delta^{K}\coloneqq\{\bm{p}\in\real^{K}_{\geq 0}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}=1\}
.
We use
\bm{1}_{K}
and
\bm{0}_{K}
for the
K
-dimensional all-ones vector and all-zeros vector, respectively.
For two vectors
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\real^{m}
,
\bm{a}\circ\bm{b}\in\real^{m}
is their element-wise product.
For two vectors
\bm{a}\in\real^{m}
and
\bm{b}\in\real^{n}
,
\mathrm{concat}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\in\real^{m+n}
represents their concatenations.
Consider a vector
\bm{a}\in\real^{m}
and two integers
i,j\in[m]
,
\bm{a}_{i:j}
denotes the continuous subvector of
\bm{a}
from
i
-th element to
j
-th element:
\bm{a}_{i:j}\coloneqq(a_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{j})^{\top}
.
For
i>j
, we define
\bm{a}_{i:j}
as the empty vector:
\bm{a}_{i:j}\coloneqq()
and
\mathrm{concat}((),\bm{a})=\mathrm{concat}(\bm{a},())\coloneqq\bm{a}
.
We use
L^{K}
for the set of feasible lower bound vectors of
\Delta^{K}
, formally,
L^{K}\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in\real^{K}\mid\bm{0}_{K}\preceq\bm{a}\prec\bm{1}_{K},\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\leq 1\}
.
Similarly, we define
U^{K}
as the set of feasible upper bound vectors of
\Delta^{K}
, that is,
U^{K}\coloneqq\{\bm{b}\in\real^{K}\mid\bm{0}_{K}\prec\bm{b}\preceq\bm{1}_{K},\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}\geq 1\}
.
Subsequently, we denote the set of feasible box constraints, that is, feasible pairs of lower and upper bound vectors, of
\Delta^{K}
by
B^{K}\coloneqq\{(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in L^{K}\times U^{K}\mid\bm{u}\preceq\bm{v}\}
.

2
Related Work

2.1
Alternative Formulation and Sparse Extensions of
\mathrm{Softmax}

The
\mathrm{Softmax}
function can be expressed as the solution to the entropic regularized linear programming
[
4
,
5
,
34
]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})=\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_{k}\log y_{k}.

(2)

Based on this formulation, several researchers proposed sparse extensions of the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function.

Martins and Astudillo 2016
proposed
\mathrm{Sparsemax}
by employing a squared
L_{2}
norm instead of entropy:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sparsemax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}-\frac{\tau}{2}\lVert\bm{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K}}\lVert\bm{x}/\tau-\bm{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(3)

Although the outputs of
\mathrm{Softmax}
are always dense,
Laha et al. 2018
introduced
\mathrm{Sparsegen}
, which is a generalization of
\mathrm{Softmax}
and
\mathrm{Sparsemax}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sparsegen}_{\tau}(\bm{x};g,\lambda)\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K}}\lVert\bm{y}-g(\bm{x}/\tau)\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lambda\lVert\bm{y}\rVert_{2}^{2},

(4)

where
\lambda<1
and
g:\real^{K}\to\real^{K}
is a component-wise transformation function.

Bałazy et al. 2023
proposed a sparse extension of the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function,
r
-
\mathrm{Softmax}
, using an approach distinct from
\mathrm{Sparsemax}
and
\mathrm{Sparsegen}
.
First, they defined the weighted softmax function,
\mathrm{w\text{-}Softmax}
, as

\displaystyle\mathrm{w\text{-}Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},\bm{w})[i]\coloneqq\frac{w_{i}\cdot\exp(x_{i}/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\cdot\exp(x_{k}/\tau)},

(5)

where
\bm{w}\in\{\bm{u}\in\real^{K}\mid\bm{0}_{K}\preceq\bm{u},\sum_{k}u_{k}>0\}
is a weight vector.
The
i
-th probability of
\mathrm{w\text{-}Softmax}
is zero when
w_{i}=0
, thus
\mathrm{w\text{-}Softmax}
can produce sparse probabilities.
Subsequently, they proposed
\mathrm{t\text{-}Softmax}
as
\mathrm{t\text{-}Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},t>0)\coloneqq\mathrm{w\text{-}Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},w(t,\bm{x}))
, where
w(t,\bm{x})[i]\coloneqq\max(0,x_{i}+t-\max(\bm{x}))
.
Based on
\mathrm{t\text{-}Softmax}
, they proposed
\mathrm{r\text{-}Softmax}
as
\mathrm{r\text{-}Softmax}(\bm{x},r\in[0,1])\coloneqq\mathrm{t\text{-}Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},-\mathrm{quantile}(\bm{x},r)+\max(\bm{x}))
.
The parameter
r
represents the sparsity rate;
\mathrm{r\text{-}Softmax}
with
r=k/K
outputs a probability vector with
k
zero values.

Wang et al. 2024
proposed
\epsilon
-
\mathrm{Softmax}
to learn an accurate classifier from noisy labels.
Their proposed
\epsilon
-
\mathrm{Softmax}
outputs probability vectors that approximate one-hot vectors.

2.2
Controlling Output Probabilities of
\mathrm{Softmax}
via Temperature

The temperature
\tau
softly controls the output probability vector of the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function and plays a crucial role in several applications.

Guo et al. 2017
proposed temperature scaling (TS), which calibrates the output probabilities of a classifier by adjusting the temperature parameter
\tau
.
Although various calibration methods have been developed, TS is the most promising and widely used method.
We will describe TS in more detail in
Section

4.1
.

In reinforcement learning, a trade-off exists between exploration and exploitation.
When a policy function employs the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function, exploration and exploitation can be balanced by tuning
\tau
.
A higher
\tau
yields a more exploratory policy, while a lower
\tau
results in a more exploitative policy function.
Therefore, it is important to set
\tau
to an appropriate value; however, determining an appropriate value remains a challenge
[
41
]
.

He et al. 2018
proposed a metric for evaluating the effectiveness of the temperature
\tau
and proposed a method to determine the temperature using the proposed metric.

Recently, LLMs have achieved remarkable performance in various natural language processing tasks; consequently, they have been developed rapidly.
Fundamentally, LLMs primarily solve the next-token prediction problem: given an input sequence of tokens, predict the next token by computing a
\mathrm{Softmax}
probability vector over the set of tokens.

Peeperkorn et al. 2024
demonstrated that the trade-off between novelty and coherence in generated text can be controlled by adjusting
\tau
.

3
Box-Constrained Softmax Function

In this paper, we propose the box-constrained softmax function (
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
), an extension of the conventional
\mathrm{Softmax}
function with box constraints on the output probability vector.
As described in
Section

1
, introducing such constraints can be useful in certain applications that require reliable and trustworthy models
[
8
,
14
,
15
,
17
]
.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
be a logit vector, and let
(\bm{a},\bm{b})\in B^{K}
be a feasible pair of lower and upper bounds.
Our goal is to define the
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function such that it satisfies the following two properties:

\displaystyle\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))\simeq\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{a}\preceq\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))\preceq\bm{b}.

(6)

The first property ensures that
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
behaves similarly to
\mathrm{Softmax}
, while the second guarantees that its output lies within the specified box constraints.

A definition of
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
satisfying these properties is not obvious from the standard formulation of
\mathrm{Softmax}
in
Eq.

1
.
However, the alternative formulation in
Eq.

2
can be naturally extended for box constraints on outputs since it defines
\mathrm{Softmax}
as the solution to the convex optimization problem.
Thus, we define the
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function as

\displaystyle\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))\coloneqq\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{a}\preceq\bm{y}\preceq\bm{b}}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_{k}\log y_{k}.

(7)

By construction,
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
satisfies the second property in
Eq.

6
.
Moreover,
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
is obviously equivalent to
\mathrm{Softmax}
when
\bm{a}=\bm{0}_{K}
and
\bm{b}=\bm{1}_{K}
.
In this sense, the first property in
Eq.

6
holds.
We will later prove that the first property also holds even if
\bm{a}\neq\bm{0}_{K}
and
\bm{b}\neq\bm{1}_{K}
, in
Section

3.2
.

Challenge.

We defined
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
as the solution to the box-constrained convex optimization problem in
Eq.

7
.
Consequently, evaluating it can be computationally costly as it requires solving the optimization problem.
Moreover, when it is used in machine learning models trained using gradient-based optimization methods, computing its Jacobian becomes necessary.
Recently, differentiation methods for optimization problems have gained attention and have been actively developed
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
7
]
; in addition, a few of these methods are applicable to
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
.
However, using such methods remains computationally expensive.
Hence, our objective is to develop an efficient and exact algorithm for evaluating
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
.

3.1
Exact Computation Algorithm for Upper-Bounded Softmax

For simplicity, we first derive an efficient and exact computation algorithm for
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
with only upper-bound constraints.
The upper-bounded softmax (
\mathrm{UBSoftmax}

\displaystyle\mathrm{UBSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},\bm{b})\coloneqq\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{0}_{K},\bm{b}))=\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{K},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{y}\preceq\bm{b}}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_{k}\log y_{k}.

(8)

To derive an exact computation algorithm for
\mathrm{UBSoftmax}

\mathrm{UBSoftmax}

\mathrm{Softmax}

\tau>0
,
\bm{x}\in\real^{K}
, and
\bm{b}\in U^{K}

\displaystyle\mathrm{UBSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},\bm{b})[i]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x}-\bm{\beta})[i]

(9)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}b_{i},&\beta_{i}>0\\
{\exp(x_{i}/\tau)}/{z},&\beta_{i}=0\end{cases}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad z

\displaystyle\coloneqq r/s,s\coloneqq\sum_{i:\beta_{i}=0}\exp(x_{i}/\tau),\quad\text{and}\quad s\coloneqq 1-\sum_{i:\beta_{i}>0}b_{i}.

(11)

From

Furthermore,

3.3
Basic Properties and Jacobians

We show basic properties and compute the Jacobian matrices of

Basic Properties.

In
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
, changing the temperature is equivalent to changing the scale of the logits.
Moreover,
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
is invariant to constant offsets.

{prop}

For all
\tau>0
,
\bm{x}\in\real^{K}
,
(\bm{a},\bm{b})\in B^{K}
, and
z\in\real
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{bcsoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bcsoftmax}_{1}(\bm{x}/\tau,(\bm{a},\bm{b}))\quad\text{and}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{bcsoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{bcsoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x}-z\cdot\bm{1}_{K},(\bm{a},\bm{b})).

(22)

Jacobians.

When machine learning models employing
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
are optimized using gradient-based methods, the Jacobian matrices of
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
play a crucial role.
From
Eq.

21
, we can set
\tau=1
without loss of generality, which simplifies the derivation of the Jacobian.
The
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function is differentiable everywhere except at boundary points
(\bm{x}^{\prime},(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime}))
, where the indices of lower- or upper-bounded probabilities change.
For differentiable
(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))
, Jacobian matrices are derived directly from
Section

3.2
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{1}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))[i]}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{q})-\bm{q}\bm{q}^{\top}/s\right]_{i,j},

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{1}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))[i]}{\partial a_{j}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{g})-\bm{q}\bm{g}^{\top}/s\right]_{i,j},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{1}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))[i]}{\partial b_{j}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{h})-\bm{q}\bm{h}^{\top}/s\right]_{i,j},

(25)

where
\bm{q}=\bm{p}\circ(\bm{1}_{K}-\bm{g})\circ(\bm{1}_{K}-\bm{h})
,
\bm{p}=\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{1}(\bm{x},(\bm{a},\bm{b}))
,
\bm{g}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
is the flag vector such that
g_{i}=1
if
p_{i}=a_{i}
; otherwise
g_{i}=0
.
Similarly,
\bm{h}
is the
K
-dimensional boolean vector such that
h_{i}=1
if
p_{i}=b_{i}
; otherwise
h_{i}=0
.
Since all Jacobians are in the form of the difference between a diagonal matrix and a matrix of rank 1, both vector-Jacobian and Jacobian-vector products can be performed in
O(K)
time.
Therefore, our proposed
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function can be easily and efficiently used in various deep neural networks.

4
Application: Calibration of Classification Models

In this section, we propose new post-hoc calibration methods for classification models based on the proposed
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
function.

4.1
Problem Setup, Definitions, and Existing Methods

We begin by setting the problem, stating definitions, and referring to existing work.

Classification problem.

Consider classification problem with
K
classes.
Let the training data
D_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{(\bm{x}_{n},y_{n})_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}
, where
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
is the input feature vector and
y_{n}\in\mathcal{Y}=[K]
is the ground-truth label.
Our goal is to train an accurate and well-calibrated (defined later) classification model
f:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta^{K}
by
D_{\mathrm{tr}}
.
Furthermore, assume that we can observe the validation data
D_{\mathrm{val}}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N_{\mathrm{val}}}
in addition to the training data
D_{\mathrm{tr}}
.
The validation data are used to evaluate and calibrate (defined later) models.

Top-label calibration.

We assume that
\bm{x}
and
y
are sampled from an unknown distribution
\mathbb{P}(X,Y)=\mathbb{P}(Y\mid X)\mathbb{P}(X)
.
Then, the model
f
is said to be
top-label (argmax) calibrated
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=\hat{y}(X)\mid\hat{p}(X)=p)=p,

(26)

where
\hat{y}(X)=\argmax f(X)
and
\hat{p}(X)=\max f(X)
.
The degree of miscalibration is typically measured by the true expected calibration error (ECE), which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ECE}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X}[\lvert\hat{p}(X)-\mathbb{P}(Y=\hat{y}(X)\mid\hat{p}(X))\rvert].

(27)

When the model is calibrated, its true ECE is zero; thus, a lower true ECE indicates a better calibration.
However, the true ECE cannot be computed since the distribution
\mathbb{P}
is unknown.
Therefore, the following estimate of the true ECE, we call empirical ECE, using a finite test dataset
D_{\mathrm{te}}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N_{\mathrm{te}}}
is used:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{ECE}}(f,D_{\mathrm{te}})\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|\mathrm{Bin}_{m}|}{N}|\mathrm{acc}(\mathrm{Bin}_{m})-\mathrm{conf}(\mathrm{Bin}_{m})|,

(28)

where
\mathrm{Bin}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{Bin}_{M}\subset[N_{\mathrm{te}}]
are the sets of indices of samples in
D_{\mathrm{te}}
such that
\mathrm{Bin}_{m}\coloneqq\{n\in[N_{\mathrm{te}}]:\hat{p}(\bm{x}_{n})\in((m-1)/M,m/M]\}
,
\mathrm{acc}(\mathrm{Bin})\coloneqq\lvert\{n\in\mathrm{Bin}:y_{n}=\hat{y}(\bm{x}_{n})\}\rvert/\lvert\mathrm{Bin}\rvert
, and
\mathrm{conf}(\mathrm{Bin})\coloneqq\sum_{n\in\mathrm{Bin}}\hat{p}(\bm{x}_{n})/|\mathrm{Bin}|
.

Post-hoc calibration methods.

Several machine learning classification models, especially modern neural networks, achieve high classification accuracy but have calibration issues
[
17
,
18
,
44
]
.
Consequently, post-hoc calibration methods have been proposed
[
12
,
17
,
24
,
25
,
29
,
31
,
38
,
40
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
to reduce the calibration error of the model
f
by the validation data
D_{\mathrm{val}}
.
Typically, post-hoc calibration methods introduce a few additional parameters and fit only them.
Among such post-hoc methods, the temperature scaling (TS)
[
17
]
is widely used.
Let us assume that the uncalibrated baseline model
f
is defined as
f(\bm{x})=\mathrm{Softmax}_{1}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x}))
, where
\mathrm{logit}:\mathcal{X}\to\real^{K}
.
Then, TS introduces the temperature parameter
\tau
and fits it by minimizing the objective function on the validation dataset:

\displaystyle\min_{\tau>0}\sum_{(\bm{x},y)\in D_{\mathrm{val}}}\ell(\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x})),y),

(29)

where
\ell:\Delta^{K}\times[K]\to\real_{\geq 0}
is a loss function, such as the cross-entropy (CE) loss:
\ell_{\mathrm{ce}}(\bm{p},y)=-\log p_{y}
.
TS runs fast since it only fits the single scalar parameter
\tau
.
Moreover, TS preserves the model accuracy because
g_{\bm{z}}(\tau)\coloneqq\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\bm{z})
is a strictly order-preserving function.
Therefore, TS is widely used and performs well in various situations.
Another approach for model calibration is to train a well-calibrated model directly via regularizers or loss functions
[
16
,
26
,
32
,
42
,
44
]
.
We mainly consider the post-hoc calibration in this study because the post-hoc methods are more widely applicable, easy to use, and empirically work better than methods of this direct approach
[
44
]
.

4.2
Probability Bounding

In this section, we introduce a new post-hoc method using
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
, called
probability bounding (PB)
.
Before introducing the method, we state our additional assumptions to the model
f
: it is defined using the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function
f(\bm{x})=\mathrm{Softmax}_{1}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x}))
, such as TS, and the logit model can be written as
\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x})=\bm{W}z(\bm{x};\Theta)
, where
\bm{W}\in\real^{K\times d}
is the weight matrix,
z(\bm{x};\Theta)\in\real^{d}
is the embedding vector, and
\Theta
is the set of parameters of
z
.
If
f
is a neural network model, then
\bm{W}
corresponds to the weight matrix of the output layer and
z(\bm{x};\Theta)
is the output of the last hidden layer.

The idea behind PB is simple: calibrating the model by learning the lower and upper bounds of the output probabilities, which can mitigate the underconfidence and overconfidence of the model.
Formally, PB replaces the prediction of the baseline model
f(\bm{x})=\mathrm{Softmax}_{1}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x}))
with

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x};\tau,\Theta_{a},\Theta_{b})\coloneqq\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x}),(a(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})\cdot\bm{1}_{K},b(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})\cdot\bm{1}_{K})),

(30)

\displaystyle a(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})\coloneqq\frac{1}{K}\cdot\sigma(a^{\prime}(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})),\quad\text{and}\quad b(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})\coloneqq\frac{1}{K}+\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right)\cdot\sigma(b^{\prime}(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})),

(31)

and fits
\Theta_{a}
,
\Theta_{b}
, and
\tau
by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\tau>0,\Theta_{a},\Theta_{b}}\sum_{(\bm{x},y)\in D_{\mathrm{val}}}\ell(f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x};\tau,\Theta_{a},\Theta_{b}),y),

(32)

where
\Theta_{a},\Theta_{b}
are the parameters,
\sigma:x\in\real\mapsto 1/(1+\exp(-x))\in(0,1)
is the logistic-sigmoid function, and
a^{\prime}(\cdot;\Theta_{a}),b^{\prime}(\cdot;\Theta_{b}):\mathcal{X}\to\real
are user-defined functions parameterized by
\Theta_{a}
and
\Theta_{b}
, for example, constant functions, linear functions, and neural networks.

By definition,
a(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})\in(0,1/K)
and
b(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})\in(1/K,1)
for all
\bm{x}
.
Thus,
(a(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})\cdot\bm{1}_{K},b(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})\cdot\bm{1}_{K})
is always in
B^{K}
, indicating that the proposed definition is feasible.
In PB, all class probabilities are constrained by the same lower and upper bounds, denoted as
a(\bm{x};\Theta_{a})
and
b(\bm{x};\Theta_{b})
, respectively.
This design ensures certain desirable properties of the post-hoc calibrated prediction
f_{\mathrm{PB}}
.

{prop}

The top-1 prediction(s) of
f(\bm{x})
are always included in those of
f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x})
:

\displaystyle\argmax f(\bm{x})\subseteq\argmax f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x})\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(33)

Furthermore, if the upper bound function is fixed as
b(\bm{x})=1
, then PB exactly preserves the top-1 predictions and thereby accuracy:

\displaystyle\argmax f(\bm{x})=\argmax f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x})\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(34)

Remark.

When the upper bound function
b(\bm{x})
is introduced and learned, PB can change the top-1 prediction(s) because there can be ties among the top-class probabilities in
f_{\mathrm{PB}}(\bm{x})
, even if
f(\bm{x})
has no ties.
Consequently, accuracy is not strictly preserved in such cases.
However, such ties are rare in practice, and empirical results in
Section

5
show that applying PB with the upper bound function has little to no effect on accuracy.

4.3
Logit Bounding

We introduce another post-hoc calibration method called
logit bounding
(LB).
Our idea for LB, which is based on
Section

3.2
, is that we can bound the probabilities by learning
\bm{\gamma}
instead of
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
.
The following theorem states that, for scalar lower and upper bounds of probabilities, there exist scalar lower and upper bounds of logits.

{theorem}

For all
\tau>0
,
\bm{x}\in\real^{K}
, and
(a\cdot\bm{1}_{K},b\cdot\bm{1}_{K})\in B^{K}
, there exist two scalars; a lower bound of logits
c\in\real
and an upper bound of logits
C\in\real
such that
c\leq C
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{BCSoftmax}_{\tau}(\bm{x},(a\cdot\bm{1}_{K},b\cdot\bm{1}_{K}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\mathrm{clip}(\bm{x},c,C)),

(35)

where
\mathrm{clip}(\bm{x},c,C)\coloneqq\max(c\cdot\bm{1}_{K},\min(\bm{x},C\cdot\bm{1}_{K}))\in\real^{K}
,
\max(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\min(\cdot,\cdot)
is element-wise max and min operator, respectively.

Therefore, our LB modifies the prediction function as

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{LB}}(\bm{x};\tau,\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C})\coloneqq\mathrm{Softmax}_{\tau}(\mathrm{clip}(\mathrm{logit}(\bm{x}),c(\bm{x};\Theta_{c}),C(\bm{x};\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C}))),

(36)

\displaystyle c(\bm{x};\Theta_{c})\coloneqq h_{1}(c^{\prime}(\bm{x};\Theta_{c})),\quad\text{and}\quad C(\bm{x};\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C})\coloneqq h_{1}(c^{\prime}(\bm{x};\Theta_{c})+h_{2}(C^{\prime}(\bm{x};\Theta_{C}))),

(37)

where
h_{1}:\real\to\real
is some monotonically increasing function (e.g., the identity function),
h_{2}:\real\to\real_{>0}
is some positive function (e.g., the softplus function), and
c^{\prime},C^{\prime}:\mathcal{X}\to\real
are some user-defined functions parameterized by
\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C}
, like
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
in PB.
The parameters
\Theta_{c}
,
\Theta_{C}
, and
\tau
are fit in the same way as PB:

\displaystyle\min_{\tau>0,\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C}}\sum_{(\bm{x},y)\in D_{\mathrm{val}}}\ell(f_{\mathrm{LB}}(\bm{x};\tau,\Theta_{c},\Theta_{C}),y).

(38)

The advantage of LB compared to PB is its simplicity of implementation.

Equation

36
consists of canonical
\mathrm{Softmax}
and element-wise
\max
and
\min
; therefore,
Eq.

36
can be evaluated in
O(K)
.

4.4
Combination of PB/LB and Existing Post-Hoc Methods

Both PB and LB can be combined with various post-hoc calibration methods
[
12
,
25
,
31
,
40
,
48
]
because these existing methods modify the logits of the baseline model and compute the calibrated probability via the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function.
For example, the Dirichlet calibration
[
25
]
modifies the prediction function as

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{Dir}}(\bm{x};\bm{W}\in\real^{K\times K},\bm{b}\in\real^{K})=\mathrm{Softmax}_{1}(\bm{W}\log f(\bm{x})+\bm{w}).

(39)

Then, we can combine PB and LB with the Dirichlet calibration as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{PBDir}}(\bm{x})&=\mathrm{bcsoftmax}_{1}(\bm{W}\log f(\bm{x})+\bm{w},(a(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{1}_{K},b(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{1}_{K})),\\
f_{\mathrm{LBDir}}(\bm{x})&=\mathrm{softmax}_{1}(\mathrm{clip}(\bm{W}\log f(\bm{x})+\bm{w},c(\bm{x}),\bm{C}(\bm{x})).\end{aligned}

(40)

Because the Dirichlet calibration can be considered as an extension of TS, we do not introduce
\tau
in
Eq.

40
.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we introduced the box-constrained softmax (
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
) function, a generalization of the
\mathrm{Softmax}
function with box constraints on the output probability vector.
We proposed an efficient and exact computation algorithm for
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
.
Using
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
, we introduced two post-hoc calibration methods: probability bounding and logit bounding.
Our experimental results indicated that the proposed methods can calibrate the baseline models and outperformed the existing post-hoc calibration methods.
We believe that
\mathrm{BCSoftmax}
has broad applicability across various domains.
