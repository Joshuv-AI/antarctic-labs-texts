Sci Transl Med. 2026;18(849):eadz2557.Generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFS+) is an inherited epileptic disorder predominantly linked to autosomal-dominant, loss-of-function mutations in the sodium voltage-gated channel α subunit 1 (SCN1A) gene, which encodes the α subunit of the neuronal voltage-gated sodium ion channel type 1 (NaV1.1). Reduced NaV1.1 function in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic interneurons impairs inhibitory signaling and leads to neuronal hyperexcitability. Clinically, GEFS+ is characterized by a spectrum of seizure types, often beginning with febrile seizures in early childhood and progressing to generalized tonic-clonic seizures later in life. Here, we used prime editing to correct the pathogenic SCN1A-K1270 T mutation in the Scn1aKT/+ mouse model of GEFS+. Adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors were used to deliver an intein-split prime editor under the control of a neuron-specific promoter into the cerebral ventricles of neonatal mice. This enabled efficient in vivo editing, achieving 34.7 ± 14.5% correction of the mutant allele in cortical bulk DNA, 81.2 ± 5.9% correction of mRNA, and improved multiple disease-relevant phenotypes. Survival increased from 80% in control-treated animals to 100% in treated mice, cortical inhibitory neuron transmission was improved (frequencies of inhibitory postsynaptic currents were increased from 0.32 to 1.32 hertz), and the frequency of induced febrile seizures decreased from 78.6% to 13.3%, approaching the frequency seen in wild-type mice (8%). These findings suggest the therapeutic potential of prime editing for the treatment of patients with SCN1A-associated GEFS+. Sci Transl Med. 2026;18(849):eadx5999.Dravet syndrome (DS) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by drug-resistant epilepsy, temperature-sensitive seizures, cognitive impairment, and a high incidence of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). DS is caused by loss-of-function variants in SCN1A, which encodes the α subunit of the voltage-gated sodium channel (Nav1.1). Current approved treatments manage symptoms of DS but do not correct the root cause of the disease. Here, we describe the use of an adenine base editor (ABE) to directly correct SCN1AR613X, a recurrent variant found in patients with DS. We identified ABE strategies to efficiently correct R613X in engineered homozygous SCN1AR613X human embryonic kidney 293 T and mouse Neuro-2a cells (72 and 92% correction efficiencies, respectively). We then used a dual-adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) approach to deliver an optimized ABE system to Scn1aR613X/+ mice, which recapitulate several key DS pathologies. AAV9-ABE treatment of Scn1aR613X/+ neonates resulted in efficient DNA and mRNA editing (59 and 97%, respectively, in bulk neocortices), restoring parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory neuron excitability and sodium current to wild-type levels. This ameliorated both spontaneous and temperature-induced seizures and led to a 3.3-fold improvement in 45-day survival over vehicle-treated mice (ABE treated, 90%; and vehicle treated, 27%). Last, ABE treatment in 12-day-old mice resulted in a 3.0-fold improvement in 60-day survival over vehicle-treated mice (ABE treated, 82%; and vehicle treated, 27%). In conclusion, these data validate a strategy to correct SCN1A variants with ABE and highlight the potential of precision genome editing treatments for the treatment of DS and possibly other neurodevelopmental disorders.

Commentary

Pathogenic variants in SCN1A are among the most common monogenic causes of epilepsy, producing a spectrum of disorders ranging from generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFS+) to Dravet syndrome (DS), a severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. 1 SCN1A encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.1, which is highly expressed in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic inhibitory interneurons. The loss of NaV1.1 function reduces interneuron excitability, which can drive network hyperexcitability and seizures, as demonstrated in rodent models lacking Scn1a or carrying human loss-of-function SCN1A variants.2‐4 Current anti-seizure therapies remain largely symptomatic, without addressing the underlying genetic defect.

Precision genome editing has emerged as one of the most promising therapeutic strategies for monogenic epilepsies, offering the possibility of correcting disease-causing variants rather than mitigating their downstream consequences. Recent advances in base editing and prime editing have substantially expanded the genome editing toolkit. Base editing enables highly efficient correction of compatible single-nucleotide substitutions, whereas prime editing broadens the range of editable variants by enabling virtually any base substitution, as well as small insertions and deletions (indels) (Figure 1). Together, these complementary technologies raise the possibility of precision therapies tailored to specific classes of pathogenic variants in genetic epilepsies, although further improvements in delivery, efficiency, and long-term safety will be required before widespread clinical translation.

Two complementary studies by Kissling et al and Nelson et al explored this therapeutic potential in the context of SCN1A-related epilepsies using distinct editing approaches.5,6 Kissling et al used prime editing to repair the pathogenic K1270 T variant associated with GEFS+, while Nelson et al employed adenine base editing (ABE) to correct the R613X nonsense mutation recurrently found in DS. To overcome adeno-associated viral (AAV) packaging constraints, both groups delivered intein-split editors using dual AAV vectors injected into the cerebral ventricles of neonatal mice. Despite using different editing tools, both studies demonstrated that partial correction of pathogenic alleles is sufficient to improve inhibitory interneuron excitability, reduce seizure frequency, and prolong survival.

Editing efficiency. In the GEFS + mouse model, the best-performing prime editor (PE3b_c3) corrected 34.7 ± 14.5% of the mutant allele in bulk cortical DNA but achieved over 80% correction at the mRNA level specifically in cortical and hippocampal neurons. Editing rates were highest in forebrain regions, including the striatum (20%) and hippocampus (34%), but were minimal in the cerebellum (0.5%). In the DS model, Nelson et al achieved even higher editing efficiencies with their most efficient strategy (ABE8e-V106W-VRQR), correcting 59% of genomic DNA in bulk neocortex and 52% in hippocampus, with near-complete mRNA correction of 97% and 93%, respectively. The cerebellum again showed minimal transduction, consistent with the delivery strategy used in these studies. The higher editing efficiencies observed at the mRNA level likely reflect differences in the cell populations being sampled: genomic DNA was measured from all cells in the tissue, whereas mRNA analyses sampled cells actively expressing Scn1a, thereby effectively enriching for the edited neuronal population. These results highlight how editing efficiency varies across brain regions, which is a key consideration for future therapies that might aim to target specific regions, such as cardiorespiratory brain regions believed to be implicated in sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP).7‐9

Editing fidelity. Both studies addressed a critical consideration for clinical translation: off-target editing and on-target indel formation. Although base editors generally achieve higher editing efficiencies than prime editors, they are also more susceptible to bystander edits (ie, editing neighboring nucleotides). 10 Nelson et al addressed this limitation by using a variant (V106 W) of ABE8e, which alters the TadA8e deaminase active site to improve substrate discrimination, substantially reducing bystander editing while preserving editing efficiency. Notably, both studies detected minimal indel formation and no major evidence of off-target editing and demonstrated selective correction of the mutant Scn1a allele while preserving the intact copy. While further investigation is warranted, both studies demonstrate the potential for these tools to be optimized for a clinical context, enhancing the efficiency of variant-specific editing while minimizing off-target effects.

Interneuron excitability. Impaired GABAergic interneuron excitability is a central mechanism in SCN1A-related epilepsies. Loss of NaV1.1 function impairs sodium currents and action potential generation in GABAergic interneurons, particularly those that express parvalbumin.2‐4,11 Both groups independently confirmed that correcting mutant Scn1a alleles rescues these deficits. In the GEFS + model, prime editing restored action potential amplitude and maximum upstroke slope in cortical fast-spiking interneurons to near wild-type levels and reversed the reduction in spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic current frequency in cortical layer V pyramidal neurons. Similarly, ABE restored parvalbumin interneuron excitability and sodium channel function, demonstrating rescue of the core cellular defect underlying SCN1A-associated network hyperexcitability.

Seizure outcomes and survival. Both groups evaluated therapeutic efficacy using the hallmark features of SCN1A-related epilepsies: temperature-induced seizures, spontaneous seizures, and SUDEP. In the GEFS + model, prime editing reduced the frequency of heat-induced febrile seizures from 79% in control-treated mice to 13% with the highest efficiency editor. Importantly, the degree of seizure rescue correlated with correction efficiency: lower efficiency editors produced only modest rescue, highlighting a key association between expression of functional Scn1a and severity of seizure phenotypes. Survival also improved from 80% to 100% over the observation period. Interestingly, embryonic delivery of AAVs did not improve survival and produced lower transduction efficiency and altered biodistribution compared to neonatal delivery, highlighting the importance of empirically establishing a treatment window. In the DS model, all ABE-treated mice remained seizure-free at temperatures up to 42.5 °C, compared to vehicle-treated mice which experienced seizures at an average of 40 ± 1.4 °C, representing near-complete rescue of temperature-induced seizures. In a 24-h monitoring period, editing also fully prevented spontaneous seizures in ABE-treated mice, which affected more than half of vehicle-treated mice. ABE treatment led to a substantial reduction in SUDEP, decreasing mortality from 60-87% to 7-13% following editing in both male and female mice. Intriguingly, Nelson et al demonstrated that ABE treatment after seizure onset still substantially improved survival. This finding is particularly significant as it suggests therapeutically meaningful intervention may extend beyond the neonatal period.

Taken together, these studies provide compelling evidence to support genome editing as a potential treatment for monogenic epilepsies (Figure 2). Although ABE achieved higher editing efficiencies, its application is restricted to variants involving compatible base substitutions, such as the R613X nonsense mutation here. Prime editing offers substantially broader applicability but currently exhibits lower editing efficiency compared to other gene editing tools. Despite these differences, both studies demonstrate that partial restoration of Scn1a function was sufficient to rescue interneuron excitability deficits, reduce seizure burden, and improve survival. These findings are particularly notable because it suggests that complete allelic correction is not required to achieve meaningful therapeutic benefit. Despite this potential, several clinical considerations remain, as with most gene editing strategies. Both studies relied primarily on neonatal intracerebroventricular delivery, and while Nelson et al demonstrate improvement with treatment after seizure onset, the full therapeutic potential of intervention following the onset of seizures requires further investigation. Variability of editing efficiency throughout the brain observed in both studies is also a consideration, given evidence implicating cortical, subcortical, and brainstem circuits in SUDEP-related cardiorespiratory and autonomic dysfunction.7‐9 Additional questions include the maximum efficiency achievable in the larger human brain, the scalability of these approaches across the extensive allelic heterogeneity of SCN1A epilepsies, and whether long-term immune responses, durability, and safety will be permissible for clinical translation. Nevertheless, these findings represent a rigorous and exciting step toward precision therapies for monogenic epilepsies and demonstrate that different editing platforms provide complementary solutions for the genetic diversity of epilepsy.
