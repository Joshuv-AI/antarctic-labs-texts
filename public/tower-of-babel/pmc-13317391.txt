Structural interventions and policing in the United States: a scoping review of public health literature
Authors: Nolan Kline, Whitney D Margaritis, Russell Rice Manzano, Sualba Alejandro, Shabrina Dew, Linda Fulmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health researchers have called for increased attention to designing structural interventions that attempt to change underlying sociopolitical conditions that perpetuate poor health. However, what constitutes a structural intervention is not well known. In the United States, policing is a public health issue that could benefit from structural interventions, but little is known within the field of public health about existing structural interventions focused on police. We conducted a scoping review of public health literature to identify the current state of knowledge related to structural interventions and police.MethodsWe completed a PubMed search using key terms related to policing and structural interventions. Through this search, we identified 138 English language articles published between 2019 and 2024. After screening articles for relevance, 35 articles were included for this review.ResultsThematic analysis of the remaining articles identified key opportunities for designing structural interventions related to policing, including (1) focusing on Black, LGBTQ+, immigrant, Indigenous, and Asian populations; (2) using theoretical perspectives that consider structural factors driving poor health; and (3) using mixed methods and/or qualitative data collection techniques. Similarly, there is a need for multi-level rather than single level interventions to addressing policing as a social determinant of health.ConclusionsFindings from this review underscore the need for structural interventions focused on policing that are theory-driven, methodologically rigorous, and focused on communities that are disproportionately impacted by policing in the US. As policing continues to be a social determinant of health that requires change, public health researchers must focus on structural interventions rather than interventions focused solely on individual behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27473-3.Keywords: Policing, Structural interventions, Social determinants of health, Policy, Law

Background

Structural interventions

Public health researchers have recently called for addressing persistent health disparities through structural interventions [1]. Such interventions target underlying social, political, and economic processes that drive health inequity rather than focus on individual, interpersonal, or community-level factors [1]. By focusing on large-scale societal forces, structural interventions present an opportunity to change societal institutions that perpetuate health disparities and require more than behavioral and contextual setting changes. Recognizing the need for structural interventions, the National Institutes of Health (NIH)—the primary agency in the United States (U.S.) responsible for public health and biomedical research—created a funding mechanism specifically for structural intervention research led by community organizations in partnership with academic researchers [2]. However, given the term’s newness, researchers and community members may find it difficult to grasp what the term entails and how to design interventions that specifically focus on social structures. This may prove challenging when attempting to design structural interventions around social phenomena requiring structural change, such as policing.

The purpose of this review is to identify existing public health literature on structural interventions and policing. We focused specifically on public health and biomedical literature (rather than including legal and social science literature) because (1) public health and biomedicine are new to understanding policing as a health problem and therefore new to contemplating interventions around policing, and (2) “structural intervention” is a new concept for the field of public health. In this review, we examine results from a literature search in PubMed to summarize existing methodological, theoretical, and themes of existing scholarship and describe needs for future research on structural interventions.

Policing as a public health problem

Social and health scientists have increasingly recognized how policing is a persistent public health problem in the US that perpetuates violence, trauma, and mortality disparities—particularly in Black, Indigenous, Hispanic, immigrant, and LGBTQ+ communities [3–7]. Overall, policing related harms are not incidental but deeply embedded in broader social forces and structural inequalities, such as institutionalized racism, xenophobia, homophobia, and economic marginalization. Further, police historically have been used to reinforce societal notions of social deviance and perceived moral failings, contributing to deepening poor health for people who use drugs or experience homelessness.

Policing in the U.S. is historically rooted in systems of racial control such as slave patrols [8] and continues to reflect and reinforce socio-political hierarchies and racial inequity. In the US, communities of color experience elevated rates of police contact and incarceration compared to White communities [9–11]. These disparities are mirrored globally in countries with similar colonial legacies and punitive approaches to social control [12, 13].

Epidemiologically, minoritized race and ethnicity are also associated with police homicide risk [6]. Police were responsible for approximately 8% of all homicides in the U.S. between 2016 and 2018 [6, 14], and homicide by police is the leading cause of homicides among Black and Hispanic populations [14]. Further, Black men are 3.5 times more likely to be shot by police than White men [14]. Additionally, policing remains a prominent means by which racial inequality and health inequities persist and are reproduced, including risks for heart disease, cancer and stroke [14]. Specifically, fear of police violence has been shown to contribute to poor health outcomes for minoritized people by leading to acute or chronic health conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and asthma [15].

Racialized law enforcement practices have also merged with immigration enforcement actions in the US, and law enforcement officers have been used to target undocumented immigrants through tactics such as raids, routine traffic stops, and driver license checkpoints [16, 17]. The consequence of using police to aid in immigration enforcement matters is that some immigrants have reduced willingness to report crime, seek basic health services, and a persistent sense of fear and trauma [18, 19].

Police have also been used to target sexual and gender minorities in bar raids and covert arrest operations [20]. Sexual and gender minorities are often disproportionately affected by police violence, leading to a greater mistrust of police compared to cisgender heterosexual peers [21]. As a result, sexual and gender minorities are less likely to call the police to report a crime than cisgender heterosexual individuals [21] even though 50.5% of sexual and gender minority individuals fear hate crime violence compared to 29.6% of non-LGBTQ+ individuals [22]. Among transgender people, specifically, there is a high level of distrust of the police based on historical mistreatment and discrimination [23]. Many transgender individuals report negative encounters with the police, including verbal harassment, sexual assault, and intrusive questions about their transition [23].

Just as police have historically been used to uphold power inequalities based on race, sexual orientation, and gender identity, they have also been used to reinforce criminalizing attitudes about substance use and housing insecurity. Visibility of law enforcement and surveillance has been shown to discourage people who use drugs from accessing public health services [24, 25]. Policing to target drug use can also push drug activities further into urban spaces, which can contribute to displacement [26]. Furthermore, police avoidance is related to reduced access to health and social services [27]. Existing scholarships has also shown that housing insecurity leads to elevated health risks for drug users, including higher death rates [28].

Policing also has substantial traumatic potential for Black, Hispanic, Indigenous, and LGBTQ+ populations. Police violence results in trauma-related mental health concerns and can also cause depression, suicidal ideation and psychosis [7, 14]. These experiences often cause increases in mood and anxiety disorders, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and psychotic experiences [5, 13]. Indeed, violent encounters with the police result in individuals being 7 times more likely to have suicidal ideation or die by suicide [5].

Despite the deep rooted and historically driven factors that make policing a structural factor leading to poor health, public discourse on police violence often focuses on the individual behavior rather than structural factors [6].

Policing requires structural interventions

Given the multiple, sweeping impacts of policing, structural interventions are needed to address policing and its concomitant harms. Structural interventions can be difficult to identify, especially within policing since changes made within one particular agency may be confused for transformational changes in the role police play within society [29]. While the term “structural intervention” may seem self-explanatory, it carries specific connotations depending on discipline. From a sociological perspective, structural interventions alter the systems and structures that shape health over time [30]. For anthropologists, structural interventions may refer to addressing the deeper social causes that put people at risk in the first place [31]. Criminology, on the other hand, takes an approach centered around legal, penal, and state systems suggesting primarily policy reform for structural change to prevent social harm [32].

In the field of public health, structural interventions include addressing population-level health outcomes, pushing for multi-sectoral plans, and changing the underlying social structures that shape poor health [33]. These definitions share an emphasis on the overarching societal, cultural, and political factors that undergird behaviors and policies with a particular call for focusing on power relationships.

For this study, we follow the understanding of structural interventions as interventions that target underlying social, political, and economic processes responsible for health inequity [1]. These factors differ from individual, interpersonal, or community-level factors. Accordingly, structural interventions related to policing might be those that challenge the sociohistorical and political economic factors that underly the disproportionate harms minoritized people experience when in contact with law enforcement [34]. Relatedly, structural interventions might address structural factors, such as implicit biases like racism that operate in everyday police practices like using discretion since discretionary power may perpetuate inequality in vehicle searches, arrests, and other investigatory inactions with police [35].

Despite the need for structural interventions on policing, however, little is known about what, if any, structural interventions focused on law enforcement exist, particularly from the field of public health. Absent such knowledge, policing as a structural problem and its associated societal and health inequities will persist. While some scholarship has examined police interventions from an individual or organizational perspective, these interventions have limitations.

Structural interventions in other health-related contexts

While few structural interventions exist in policing, there are, however, some existing structural interventions to address other public health issues. For example, structural approaches to protect sex workers, investment in sanitation, and community mobilization for reproductive rights, all include efforts to change power relationships [36]. Such interventions often focus on long-term solutions and multi-sectoral engagement to address root causes of health inequalities and challenge existing power structures and systems of exclusion.

Similarly, PrOTECT-AL in Alabama is a structural intervention which focused on addressing drop-off rates in HIV prevention care by developing a statewide tracking system which partnered with community-based organizations and health systems to improve equitable access and implementation mapping to identify system-level gaps in care [37]. By using data infrastructure and holding institutions accountable, PrOTECT-AL reshaped the function of the system, effectively transforming health care for HIV patients in Alabama.

Existing individual and organizational interventions for policing

Despite a dearth of research on structural interventions in policing, research on police interventions broadly has examined interventions such as altering policing through officer training and education, diversifying the force [36] and teaching de-escalation techniques [38]. These interventions primarily operate at individual and behavioral levels rather than structural levels designed to change societal-level policy factors. Officer training typically includes a course on diversity and ongoing continuing education [39]. The specific content of diversity training is not well known, and additional trainings on specific populations, such as LGBTQ diversity training, may be met with both receptiveness and resistance [40]. Overall, recent research has shown that despite these trainings, training alone is unlikely to change individual officer behaviors related to bias [39]. Training and behavioral interventions may also ignore the inherent power inequalities in policing, including use of officer discretion to determine who may get stopped, searched, and arrested [41]. Despite specific useful instances for officer discretion, [42–44] discretion may problematically reinforce structural racism via officers’ implicit biases [41].

Some interventions may get close to structural levels with efforts which reconsider the role of law enforcement in responding to mental health emergencies. Police are often first responders in mental health crises, which may result in the perceived criminalization of people who are experiencing a mental health crisis and subsequent violence against them if the situation escalates [45]. In 2018, police fatally shot an unarmed Black man, Joseph Dewayne Robinson, who was experiencing a mental health crisis in Memphis, Tennessee [46]. In response, programs were developed like Crisis Intervention Teams (CIT) to reconsider the role and training of police.

CIT is a broad term for programs that focus on specific police training to avoid deadly interactions with civilians [46]. There are approximately 2,700 CIT programs throughout the U.S. that work to educate police officers about de-escalation tactics [47]. This specialized curriculum has been implemented both nationally and internationally [46]. However, there are mixed results about CIT programs and their effectiveness. In some instances, CIT officers have been able to de-escalate crisis situations without resorting to violence [47]. For example, in Colorado, the Support Team Assisted Response (STAR) is part of a CIT program that sends healthcare professionals to mental health crises rather than police officers. During this time, the STAR responders went on over 7,000 calls and 49% were referred to mental healthcare services without arresting individuals [47].

Another program designed to reduce the harms of policing, Crisis Assistance Helping Out On The Streets (CAHOOTS), includes a mobile crisis intervention team. CAHOOTS was established in the Eugene-Springfield, Oregon metro area which works side-by-side with first responders [48]. These teams are dispatched through the existing emergency response system, allowing the dispatcher to triage calls and route them appropriately. Each team has a medic and a crisis worker trained to address mental health concerns and guide individuals to the next stage of treatment, effectively keeping them out of the criminal justice system. This program has now been active for thirty years and diverts 3–8% of calls from police [49]. The police department of Eugene views CAHOOTS as a beneficial resource for the community because it has reduced police burdens in call responses. In addition to programs like CITs, some community activists and scholars have suggested diversity trainings for law enforcement may reduce potentially negative interactions with police [50].

The STAR and CAHOOTS programs are examples of success for CIT interventions. Despite their widespread adoption and effort to reduce police violence, many CIT programs fall short of creating effective change within police departments and reducing use of force and injury during encounters with the police, even though they provide connections to social services [45]. CIT models may include a foundation of questioning the role of police officers in interactions with people experiencing mental illness. This overall scope could arguably be the underpinnings of an intervention which may cause structural change; however, these teams and specialized curriculum are often implemented as behavioral interventions.

At a policy level, some organizations, such as the nonprofit organization, 8 Can’t Wait, advocate for legislative changes that operate as individual-level interventions in law enforcement [51]. These interventions include legislative bans on chokeholds, requirements for officer use of de-escalation techniques, and mandated warnings and exhaustion of possibilities before using deadly force, among others [51]. While the avenue of change the organization pursues is legislative, the changes themselves are solely individual-level interventions focused on officers. As such, these legislative changes are less about changing the structure of law enforcement and instead about changing officer behavior.

Given that policing is a structural public health issue that includes numerous social power elements, behavioral interventions like training, and organizational interventions like CIT programs may not comprehensively address the complexity of policing as a social structure itself requiring a structural intervention.

Methods

Search overview

To examine the state of knowledge on structural interventions and policing in public health and biomedical scholarship, we conducted a scoping review of articles related to policing, health, and structural interventions. On May 28, 2024, we conducted a search for papers using PubMed. Using the advanced search tools within PubMed, we developed a search likely to produce results related to interventions, police, law enforcement policy, and reform using a series of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). MeSH are terms from the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s controlled vocabulary thesaurus which are used to index articles within PubMed to ensure consistency and uniformity. To decide on terms, our team discussed background literature for this study, making notes of terminology we frequently saw. An iterative term test was conducted, and each result was assessed for the number of articles returned for practicality and the relevance and degree of alignment with the research focus [52, 53]. Terms in which a preliminary search returned at least 20 relevant articles were utilized in the final MeSH terms search. A single logic string was used to avoid duplication of article results. Our final search included the MeSH terms and logic in Appendix 1.

Our search was limited to PubMed to focus on health science literature because “structural interventions” is an emerging concept in public health, and health sciences disciplines have only recently conceptualized policing as a health issue compared to disciplines such as anthropology, criminology, sociology, and political science. Further, limiting a scoping review to one database is appropriate for examining a state of knowledge in a specific discipline [52, 54]. Therefore, we limited our search to PubMed as the primary database for public health and biomedical literature rather than including specific social science discipline databases such as AnthroSource (anthropology), SocINDEX (sociology, criminology, other social sciences), EconLit (economics), PsychInfo (psychology), and other similar databases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Only papers that were peer-reviewed, published in English, based in the U.S., and published within five years of the search date were included in our review. We chose to limit results the last five years because (1) the key article calling for structural interventions in public health [1] was published in the preceding five years (published in 2019 and the search was conducted in 2024), and (2) we aimed to find scholarship that was up to date given the rapid social and cultural changes around policing that occurred in the U.S. since 2020. Specifically, the 2020 death of George Floyd sparked international attention to law enforcement violence against Black people in the US and changed the social and political landscape around policing.

Articles were excluded if they were (1) published more than five years before the search date, (2) in a language other than English, (3) were unrelated to policing and health, or (4) were not available in full text. To determine appropriateness of the relationship to policing and health, we specifically focused on law enforcement actions and behaviors as a potential impact on a person’s health and wellbeing (e.g. responding to a call and using force). We excluded cardiovascular events from our search to reduce the number of articles regarding police responding to cardiac emergencies and referring to structural heart interventions, which are treatments for various forms of heart disease [55].

Article citation information was downloaded into Excel (see appendix 2). Four authors reviewed abstracts and articles as primary and alternating reviewers as shown in Table 1. After initial review, the included abstracts were split into two sections to facilitate coding by splitting the workload, and each section was assigned to two coders. Articles were excluded at abstract and full text level if they were deemed to be substantively unrelated to policing, health, and/or the U.S. For example, some articles mentioned policing as a contextual or tangential factor related to a different topic such as drug intervention programs. In these cases, articles focused primarily on officer use of naloxone or other responses to prevention of overdose deaths. As officer involvement in these articles was primarily as emergency response, these articles were excluded as they were outside the scope of our intended search. In each review process, authors independently reviewed their assigned section, then met in pairs to review any discrepancies and discuss until consensus was reached. Discrepancy referred to inclusion or exclusion. For full text review, authors independently identified (1) article type; (2) demographic foci of the articles (including race, sex, gender, sexual orientation, and immigration inclusion if any; 3) social ecological area of focus; 4) methodological focus; 5) theoretical focus; and 6) specific health issues discussed if any, for each article. Any disagreements and discussion on article content were not considered to be a discrepancy for the purpose of reporting but was still discussed between coders. Articles of all types were eligible including original research, opinions, and reviews. The entire authorship team then met to thematically summarize the articles using a consensus approach. While drug intervention was not our focus, as discussed above, some articles were still included which focused on decriminalization of drugs as an element of police reform.

Methodological approach in articles reviewed

Of the articles included in this review (n = 35), most were empirical research articles (n = 22). The research articles primarily focused on quantitative methods (n = 15), with a small number focused on mixed methods (n = 6) and a single qualitative method article. Twelve of the quantitative articles focused on statistical analysis of existing data sets with a majority (n = 8) focused specifically on public data related to incarceration, health, or policy. Among all the articles, few (n = 11), had clearly identifiable theoretical perspectives. A summary of articles and methodologies can be seen in Appendix 2.

Notably, only two (n = 2) articles were intervention studies themselves, whereas most articles only discussed needed, potential, or hypothetical interventions. One intervention study surveyed adults in two areas with low numbers of racial minority police officers [56]. Using surveys to assess beliefs about the need for diversity in the force, study staff provided information for participants to address their own biases [56]. After completing the first survey, another survey was administered that showed decreased trust in law enforcement and increased support for diverse hiring [56]. Another intervention study article used self-report surveys to measure the success of a procedural justice training initiative in three cities identified as high-crime residential areas [57]. This study supported the use of procedural justice training as a means of motivating fair and respectful policing and improving perceived effectiveness of police and crime prevention. Overall, no studies seemed to meet the definition of structural interventions as defined by Brown et al [1]. We reviewed these articles; however, to underscore the current literature gaps and opportunities in the field and to highlight what may be conceptual ambiguity.

Article demographics, methods, and social ecological area of focus

Of the 35 synthesized articles, 27 reported using race as a part of their demographic data collection. Twenty articles included White populations, 23 included Black populations, 17 included Hispanic/ Latinx populations, 8 included Asian populations, 8 included Indigenous/ Native American populations, and 8 included an “other” category that varied in responses including multiple, multiracial, mixed, non-Hispanic, and other. 20 articles reported using sex/ gender as a part of their demographic data collection. Seventeen articles included both Male and Female and 1 article included an “other” category. None of the articles included transgender individuals or sexual orientation (LGBTQ+ population) as a part of their demographic data collection. Immigration status was reported in 2 articles. Table 2 shows the demographic data identified in each article, and a summary of the number of articles capturing demographic data. Quantitative methodologies were used as a primary means of research in 15 articles, while qualitative methods were used in 12, and a mixed methods approach was used in 7. Quantitative methods included cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys, randomized controlled trials, event study analysis, and a variety of statistical models. Primary qualitative methods included ethnography, interviews and testimonials, observational fieldwork, and narrative thematic analysis. Mixed methods of articles included quasi-experimental and qualitative interviews, surveys and community event data, and randomized control trials with field observations and community perception measures.

We also examined the focus of articles from a social ecological perspective, finding interventions (hypothetical or existing) focused on individual (n = 9), community (n = 18), and population level (n = 9); (one article focused on two levels; individual and community). Individual level articles focused solely on interventions related to individuals, such as examining the impacts of parental incarceration on children [86]. Community level interventions considered the impacts of efforts to reform the criminal justice and policing systems on broader communities, such as using procedural justice trainings [57]. Population level interventions considered changes impacting all populations, such as Medicaid expansion and its possible impacts on arrests [81].

Upon thematically reviewing articles, we identified the following common themes: police reform, decriminalizing illicit drugs, and leveraging social justice movements. We also identified opportunities for future research given existing theoretical and methodological gaps.

Police reform

Most articles (n = 26) reviewed broadly discussed police reform specifically focusing on the following domains of reform: (1) officer trainings, (2) including mental health professionals to respond to crises; (3) neighborhood and community policing; and (4) preventing harms to children during arrest. Notably, these articles failed to address the ways in which police violence is perpetuated and reproduced through systemic structural issues.

Six (n = 6) articles mentioned the need or support for officers receiving training on civilian interaction to help reduce the negative effects of policing. Trainings including procedural justice trainings that emphasize fairness, transparency, voice, and impartiality to provide fair and respectful treatment to those they police [57]. Officers that underwent crisis intervention training showed improved knowledge and skills in identifying and treating people with serious mental illnesses [71, 77]. Crisis intervention trainings have also been shown to reduce the likelihood of officers using force against vulnerable communities such as people with serious mental illnesses. Reducing force used on minority populations also reduces the number of injuries community members may experience at the hands of law enforcement officers [71].

Nine (n = 9) articles described mental health professionals responding to crises with police. Traditionally, law enforcement agencies have been considered first responders to emergency calls involving individuals experiencing a behavioral health emergency (e.g. emergences related to behaviors and well-being of individuals living with substance use and mental health disorders) [59]. Approximately two million people living with serious mental illnesses are taken into police custody and processed into a US jail each year [59, 75]. Officers have reported feelings of distress or unhappiness when left without an alternative to taking people with serious mental illness (PwSMI) to jail due to an insufficient number of beds at care facilities [71]. Without immediate access to mental health professionals, individuals experiencing a behavioral health emergency are often arrested for nonviolent offenses resulting in an overrepresentation of PwSMIs in the legal system [75]. Reducing the number of PwSMIs in the criminal justice system has developed into a critical public health and safety issue [75].

Four (n = 4) articles described elements related to neighborhood and community policing. The community policing model encompasses officers assigned to a specific geographic area in which they patrol the area and identify any problems and work with the community to develop solutions [87]. A related aspect to support community policing is diversifying police forces. Officers are primarily White men and many might not understand the struggles and hardships people of color experience within their own communities [56]. Diversification of police forces allows for better trust and cooperation between police and civilians which increases crime reporting [56].

Seven (n = 7) articles discussed the need to prevent harm to children during arrest. These articles discuss the importance of minimizing trauma of children present during legal interventions including law enforcement and child protective services, where law enforcement is often present. The harm discussed focused primarily on the mental trauma children experience during legal interventions such as when separated from family or seeing a parent criminalized [18, 61, 86]. This harm is worse in Black communities where criminalization of the community often leads to more aggressive tactics that can harm children both directly and indirectly [74]. These traumatic experiences lead to an inability for the child to cope in school and home life. A strong focus of preventing harm to children was in minimizing trauma and advocating for approaches that reduce harm during interventions. Changes to current practices should prioritize the mental health of children during arrest situations, including the presence of child specialists or the use of alternative, prosocial training, trauma-informed approaches to arrest procedures with children to prevent further harm [64, 74, 86, 88, 89].

Five articles discussed drug policy reform, elaborating on the impact of drug control policy and enforcement on the health of people who use drugs (PWUD). The US incarcerates more people per capita than any other nation with approximately 2.2 million people in jail despite decreases in national crime rates [68]. Policy changes to decriminalize drugs would focus on removing criminal penalties and opting for a health-centered approach to PWUD. Studies focused on drug policy reform demonstrate that decriminalizing drugs reduces harm, improves health outcomes, alleviates added burdens on marginalized communities disproportionately affected by severe drug policies, and supports dignity and human rights [90, 91].

Among the articles calling for drug policy, one article specifically explains the role of drug policy in perpetuating racial inequalities in policing [61]. As such, to reduce racial inequalities related to policing, scholars call for decriminalization, decarceration, divestment, and redirection as key to radical transformation of a broken system writ large [61]. In addition to macrolevel changes [61] some researchers suggest specific mesolevel reforms to the criminal justice system. For example, research emphasizes the need for three specific reforms to the criminal justice system [68]. First, that police-related injury/death should be assessed in public health data which could increase oversight and accountability [68]. Second, research suggests policy changes to eliminate mandatory minimum sentences, reduction in reincarceration for technical parole violations, and alternatives to incarceration [68]. These factors could decrease high incarceration rates that are an added financial burden on the nation and disproportionately affect Black and Latinx populations. Lastly, collateral consequences of incarceration should be altered to ensure successful reentry to the community after release, with particular attention on voter rights restoration and access to federal assistance for previously incarcerated people [68].

Supporting decriminalization proposals, there is evidence of significant arrest rate decreases and reduced burdens on the criminal justice system following drug decriminalization in Oregon and Washington [62]. However, these changes can only be successful if they are well-communicated to the population to which they apply. For example, the PWUD were unaware of policy changes in Baltimore, Maryland, called Good Samaritan laws, which provided protection from arrest when reporting overdose to ensure those in need could receive prompt medical care [78]. This lack of awareness acts as a barrier to preventing overdose death as those capable of calling 911 did not believe they could without fear of being arrested themselves.

Among the articles advocating decriminalization, two (n = 2) articles suggested that in addition to changes to criminal justice policies, access to healthcare may play a significant role in arrest rates [68, 81]. For example, counties in places that expanded Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act (ACA) saw a significant decline in police arrests compared to non-expansion counties, with the largest decline being in drug-related arrests [71]. These studies suggest that while police reform is paramount, focusing on addressing social and health concerns may be just as important to changing the criminal justice system.

Leveraging the strengths of social justice movements

Five (n = 5) articles reviewed discussed the need to use the success of social justice movements to advance meaningful change in policing. Articles focused on this theme emphasized that social justice movements can be useful in advancing reform efforts responsive to structural problems, and that success in these movements lies in drawing attention to critical issues while advocating for necessary changes [61, 68, 89]. For instance, the frequent use of lethal weapons against Black citizens during Black Lives Matter protests emphasized how police weapons contribute to health inequalities and systemic racism [70]. After the murder of George Floyd, protests spurred significant and almost immediate change in some police departments, including dismantling the police department in favor of a public safety system in Minneapolis, and repealing a New York law that kept police disciplinary records private [68].

Literature on leveraging social justice movements for change emphasizes that social justice movements challenge dominant social narratives and push for humane and equitable changes in policing that can be achieved by aligning law enforcement practices and policies with public demands [70, 89]. Some scholars, however, caution that these movements must be strategic in using collective action tactics, such as protests, to effect change [80]. They further note that ingrained ideals such as prejudice conversely may be most likely to change through “well-designed social harmony interventions” rather than social conflict [80].

Social determinants of health and structural vulnerability

In this review, five (n = 5) articles specifically situated policing within a social determinants of health (SDOH) framework. Broadly defined as the conditions in which people live their lives, the SDOH framework encompasses five domains: economic stability, education access and quality, healthcare access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context [92]. Numerous social conditions themselves can be social determinants, including policy, such as immigration policy, and policing itself [93]. For example, existing research demonstrates that youth who have negative experiences with law enforcement are more likely to have adverse behavioral health issues such as drug use and psychiatric problems that are a result of trauma [64]. Further, as one article underscored, police raids on undocumented Latinx immigrants can often lead to anxiety that can cause Latina mothers to delay family planning, affecting reproductive autonomy [18].

Similarly, only two (n = 2) articles examined policing through the lens of structural vulnerability. Structural vulnerability is a positionality that is produced from an individual’s place in networks of power relationship where individuals are subjected to structural violence in broad terms [94]. The perspective allows for understanding poor health not as a condition resulting from individual behavioral conditions or failures, but rather as a subjective state produced by structural forces [95]. Certain factors of vulnerability are caused by structural violence such as negative encounters with the police, harassment, physical violence, and sexual violence [66]. Additionally, structural vulnerability is an important concept to consider for gun violence in Puerto Rican neighborhoods in Philadelphia due to the elevated risks for gun violence associated with specific Puerto Rican communities [66]. Since structural vulnerability is connected to negative encounters with the police, structural interventions must consider the monitoring, prevention, and response to police abuse to protect vulnerable populations [66]. The conditions that perpetuate structural vulnerability can lead to negative health and behavioral outcomes for people they affect. Therefore, addressing structural conditions that inform poor health can benefit from using theoretical perspectives similarly focused on structural conditions.

Discussion

Gaps in the literature and a need for conceptual clarity

The purpose of this review was to identify existing public health literature on structural interventions and policing. Our findings point to numerous knowledge gaps related to structural interventions and law enforcement harm in the US. Specifically, there are conceptual, theoretical, and methodological gaps given the few articles that use theoretical perspectives (n = 11) and qualitative perspectives (n = 1). Structural interventions focused on policing, then, must include conceptual clarity on what constitutes structural interventions, use theoretical approaches that consider structural factors shaping poor health, such as social determinants of health and structural vulnerability, and include multiple methodological approaches.

Conceptually, many of the articles identified in the review were paradoxically focused on trainings, which are behavioral and individual-level in focus, rather than structural interventions designed to bring about policy and societal change. Such results suggest perhaps a misalignment with how scholars are conceptualizing the notion of “structural interventions” and how the concept is intended to be used in public health research and practice. While trainings might be components of structural interventions, such structural changes should consider not only behavioral targets of change that have community impacts (such as trainings and CIT programs), but also include broader changes that alter the very nature of policing. Such changes might include, for example, revising policing in ways that reconceptualize power relationships between police and community members, thereby altering the societal role of policing. To start, some interventions might consider altering the types of calls police respond to and instead having community members or trained social workers respond, much in the way that community advocates for police change have suggested.

Police violence is a social determinant of health that affects minoritized communities and has sweeping health-related implications [58]. Indeed, populations that have had negative interactions with the police, including police brutality, are less likely to trust medical systems, therefore affecting their overall health in hidden ways [58]. Further, social factors such as policies to combat drug use negatively impact people of color through surveillance and arrests, thus negatively impacting their overall health and well-being [60]. In many instances involving police, race and ethnicity are social determinants of health that can lead to negative health consequences such as police brutality and the injuries that result from this type of violence [34]. Despite the well-established fact that policing is a social determinant of health, there appear to be no structural interventions on policing informed by social determinants perspectives.

In addition to employing theoretical perspectives that emphasize structural factors, there is opportunity for future research to advance qualitative approaches to designing structural interventions. Such approaches should include prioritizing desired intervention goals from key constituent groups, including law enforcement officers, community members, and elected officials. Methods to advance such work can include in-depth interviews and ethnographic approaches that can identify underlying conditions needing change, the most feasible actions for change, and barriers to possible change. Additionally, there is a need to focus on all populations harmed by police, including Black, LGBTQ+, Latinx, and immigrant populations.

Lastly, structural interventions must include multiple levels of focus. Our review of existing literature underscored that most existing scholarship on policing proposes interventions at single levels of focus. Such interventions may not be enough to meaningfully change the social structures shaping policing-related health harms.

Limitations

This article focused exclusively on public health and biomedical literature on structural interventions and policing. It is not a sweeping multidisciplinary review as a result. Further, it exclusively used PubMed. Although PubMed is a multidisciplinary database and we used an extensive search query, some articles may have been missed due to variation in language and jargon used in articles relating to the topic. Additionally, many of the articles included in the review do not contain specific structural interventions. In our search we cross-referenced two lists of terms that were determined to give the greatest breadth of search results that were still mostly related to the desired topic. As a result, many of the articles in the search results did not discuss the use of structural intervention but may have discussed legal intervention, policy reform, or police reform. While they did not discuss the use of structural intervention, many did give guidelines to types of interventions the authors would deem important or things to keep in mind in their development. Due to the small quantity of structural interventions on policing found, the research team decided that inclusion of these articles was still important to guide the development of future interventions.

Conclusion

As public health researchers and practitioners respond to calls for creating structural interventions that effectively address the underlying social, political, and economic circumstances that inform poor health, policing is a topic that demands scholarly attention. Structural interventions focused on policing have an opportunity to address the gaps identified through our review of existing literature, including (1) focusing on underrepresented populations and groups most harmed by police, such as Black, LGBTQ+, immigrant, Indigenous, and Asian populations; (2) including theoretical perspectives that consider structural factors driving poor health; and (3) making use of mixed methods or qualitative data techniques. Similarly, there is opportunity for interventions to focus on multilevel approaches (e.g. policy, community, interpersonal, and individual levels) rather than singular levels (e.g. individual level) of emphasis. Addressing such gaps is vital to developing fully informed interventions that address the underlying societal factors shaping policing-related health inequalities.

Further, there is a substantive need for community-based approaches to creating structural interventions focused on policing. Structural interventions, particularly those responsive to social and political issues such as policing, are well suited for community-engaged research perspectives. Many community-based organizations provide services to support minoritized people and are ideally positioned to understand how organizational and policy structures impede the lives of those they serve. Community organization leaders may be able to identify structural changes and policies that could improve the well-being of those they serve. Indeed, recognizing this reality, in 2023, the National Institutes of Health created community-led health equity structural intervention (CHESI) funding opportunities for community members to lead structural interventions as part of the Community Partnerships to Advance Science for Society (ComPASS) Program. These opportunities were unfortunately short-lived and terminated in 2025 because of changing political dynamics in the U.S., but they nevertheless showed promising potential to create structural interventions [2] including around topics like policing.
