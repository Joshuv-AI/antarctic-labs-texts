Introduction

Background and rationale

Anaemia is defined as a condition in which the concentration of haemoglobin, or the count and size of red blood cells, falls below a certain threshold. Pregnant women are considered anaemic if their haemoglobin is below 11.0 g/dL, and non-pregnant women if it's below 12.0 g/dL [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has estimated that globally, anaemia affects 269 million children aged 6 to 59 months and half a billion women aged 15 to 49. Anaemia afflicted 37% (32 million) of pregnant women aged 15–49 and 30% (539 million) of non-pregnant women in 2019. Africa and South-East Asia are the most impacted regions, with an estimated 244 million women and 83 million children in South-East Asia [2].

According to WHO, iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) affects 30% of the population worldwide, making it the most prevalent dietary deficiency. Reduced dietary iron and decreased iron absorption are additional contributing factors to IDA, even though gastrointestinal bleeding and women's menstruation are the most frequent causes. The goal of treatment for IDA patients should be to restore iron storage and bring haemoglobin levels back to normal [3].

Anaemia is a major public health concern in India. According to National Health Family Survey 5 (2019-21), 57.2% women of reproductive age group (15 to 49 years) suffer from anaemia in India [4]. To reduce the burden of anaemia in the country, the Government of India launched “Anaemia Mukt Bharat Programme” in 2018 with the objective of reducing the prevalence of anaemia by 3 percentage points per year among children, adolescents, and women in the reproductive age group (15–49 years), between the year 2018 and 2022 [5].

Explanation for the choice of interventions

A targeted prevalence reduction of anaemia in women of the reproductive age group was set from 57% (2016) to 35% (2022) under this programme Anaemia Mukt Bharat-Target [6]. However, the prevalence of anaemia has been found to be almost the same. The major issue with the standard care of anaemia, Iron and Folic acid (IFA) supplements is poor compliance due to forgetfulness and intolerable GI side effects [7] [8]. Iron deficiency and overload can cause oxidative stress, leading to intestinal mucosal damage and reduced iron absorption, which limits the effectiveness of oral iron supplements and may cause gastrointestinal issues [9]. The addition of Vitamin C to IFA is helpful in enhancing the absorption of iron by keeping it in a reduced ferrous form. However, it does not address the issue of gastrointestinal side effects. Conventional iron treatments, such as ferrous sulphate, are often poorly tolerated due to adverse effects like metallic taste, nausea, and constipation. Taking iron with food can reduce side effects but may also lower absorption. Therefore, there is a need for alternative treatments with better efficacy and fewer side effects [10].

Siddha is one of the oldest traditional medical systems, originating from South India, based on the principles of Panchabootham (five elements - earth, water, fire, air, and ether) and Mukkutram (three humors – vali, azhal, aiyam). It emphasizes holistic healing through herbal, metal, mineral, and lifestyle-based interventions to restore balance and promote well-being. In Siddha system of medicine, anaemia may be compared with the condition known as Paandu Noi, which literally means pallor. Clinically, this condition is characterized by pallor of the conjunctiva, tongue, and nail beds. It is also referred to by synonyms such as Veluppu Noi and Venmai Noi [11]. According to Siddha literature, noi varum kaaranam (causative factors) for Paandu Noi include athisaaram (chronic diarrhoea), excessive intake of spicy or sour foods, suppression or disturbance of sadaraakini (digestive fire), consumption of impure water, excessive intake of madhu (alcohol), and irregular lifestyle practices such as pagal urakkam (daytime sleep). A subtype of Paandu Noi, called Azhal Paandu, closely resembles Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA), where an imbalance in the azhal dosha results in reduced red blood cell production, which is a defining characteristic of IDA [12].

The Siddha system utilizes herbs like Karisalai (Eclipta alba (L.) Hassk.), Kadukkai (Terminalia chebula Retz.), Nellikkai (Emblica officinalis Gaertn.), and others in herbo-mineral formulations for the treatment of anaemia. These medicinal plants have been scientifically shown to enhance red blood cell formation, improve haemoglobin levels, and support liver function through various mechanisms, thereby contributing to effective anaemia management [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. In the proposed clinical trial, a Siddha regimen comprising Arakku Thylam (AT) [18], Naaku Poochi Kudineer (NPK) [18], Bavana Kadukkai (BVK) [18], and Aya Bhringaraja Paanitham (ABP) [19] will be employed, aligning with the core principles of Siddha medicine. NPK, a de-worming formulation, is recommended as the first step in anemia management. It has been shown to be equally effective as Ivermectin and Albendazole, thus preventing nutrient malabsorption and blood loss caused by parasitic infestations [20]. Following de-worming, BVK along with ABP, the primary iron supplement, will be administered. BVK, aids in iron absorption by stimulating red blood cell production, promoting gut health and reducing gastrointestinal side effects commonly seen with conventional iron supplement, IFA [21]. ABP combines purified elemental iron with herbal ingredients such as Thiripala and Thirikadugu, which enhance iron absorption and support gut health [22]. As reported by Vijaya Kumar et al., the formulation underwent a 28-day repeated oral dose toxicity study, which established a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) in animal models. Its clinical efficacy was further validated through a study on 82 patients with iron deficiency anaemia, where the drug was administered over a 45-day period and showed significant improvement in haematological parameters upon comparing pre- and post-treatment values within the same group [23]. Together, these formulations provide a comprehensive approach to managing iron deficiency anemia (IDA), ensuring effective treatment.

With the above rationale and on the basis of clinical experience, this study aims to compare the efficacy of Siddha regimen containing traditional oleation by medicated oil AT, deworming by NPK followed by BVK (as a supportive drug), ABP (as a main haematinic drug) constituted for anaemia with Standard of Care, IFA.

Objectives

The primary objective is to compare the efficacy of Siddha regimen versus iron and folic acid supplement in improving the haemoglobin level from baseline to the end of the 3rd month among mild to moderate iron-deficiency anaemic reproductive age group women (18-49 years).

The secondary objectives of this study include assessing changes in red cell indices, such as RBC count, Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin (MCH), and Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration (MCHC), along with the iron profile parameters, including serum iron, Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC), ferritin, and transferrin saturation. Additionally, the study aims to evaluate the frequency of adverse reactions in the study population and compare them across the different study groups. Furthermore, the mukkutram status of anaemic patients will be documented through naadi (pulse) assessment to provide insights into the Siddha perspective of anaemia.

Trial design

This trial is a randomized, controlled, open-label, multicentre equivalence clinical study with two arms, each comprising 85 participants, and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The primary endpoint is the improvement in haemoglobin levels from baseline to the end of the 3rd month. The study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) 2013 checklist [24], and the study flow is depicted as a flowchart and presented in Fig. 1. The trial will comply with the ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017) [25], and the results will be reported following the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines [26]. The protocol has been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC) at ICMR-National Institute of Traditional Medicine, Belagavi, (No. IHEC/NITM/IHEC approval/224-Sep/033) and the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) (No. NIS/25/IEC/2023/F.P/47) at the National Institute of Siddha, Chennai. Any amendments to the protocol or informed consent form (ICF) during the study will be submitted to the IEC and IHEC for approval. This study has been registered in Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI) (Identifier - CTRI/2025/01/079620) prospectively.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting

The study will take place at the outpatient departments (OPDs) of the National Institute of Siddha (NIS) in Chennai and the Siddha Clinic at ICMR-National Institute of Traditional Medicine (NITM) in Belagavi. The study population will consist of women of reproductive age (18 to 49 years) with mild anaemia (Hb 11-11.9 g/dL) or moderate anaemia (Hb 8-10.9 g/dL), who will undergo haemoglobin screening during the baseline visit for screening. Only those who meet the eligibility requirements and provide consent will be included in the study. A total of 170 participants will be enrolled and randomly allocated into two study arms, with 85 participants in each arm.

Eligibility criteria

Prior to enrolment, participants will be thoroughly assessed to ensure that they meet the study's eligibility requirements. In addition, provisions for voluntary withdrawal will be outlined to participants, allowing them to discontinue participation at any stage if they choose.

Inclusion criteria

The study will involve women aged 18 to 49 years, meeting the criteria of having mild (Hb 11-11.9 g/dL) to moderate (Hb 8-10.9 g/dL) iron deficiency anaemia, with serum ferritin levels below 30mcg/dL. Participants must be willing to consent to receive any type of intervention prior to randomization and should not be participating in any other research study during the course of this trial.

Exclusion criteria

The study will exclude participants who meet any of the following criteria: pregnant or lactating women, women planning pregnancy within the next 3 months at the time of screening, individuals with anaemia caused by factors other than iron deficiency (such as known case of thyroid dysfunction, chronic kidney failure, Thalassemia minor, sickle cell anaemia, and/or anaemia due to chronic blood loss), known diabetics on anti-diabetic medication, individuals with HbA1C levels equal or greater than 6.5, those who have been consuming iron and folic acid (IFA) supplements for more than 14 days, individuals with known allergies to iron or any ingredients in the Siddha regimen, patients with liver disease, and individuals with any other medical condition, as determined by their treating doctor, that may pose a risk due to participation in the study.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

Withdrawal criteria will apply if certain conditions arise, including suspected intolerance to the trial drug or the development of serious unexpected adverse reactions during the drug trial which compromises the participant's safety. In such cases, the trial interventions will be withdrawn, but the patient will be observed, and data will still be collected. Withdrawal of consent, drug compliance of less than 70%, or if a participant becomes pregnant during the study will also lead to discontinuation from the trial. The reasons for each withdrawal will be documented in the study findings. Regardless of any decision to modify or discontinue their assigned intervention, study participants will be in contact with the study team in the trial whenever possible to enable follow-up data collection and prevent missing data.

Informed consent

Trained investigators will introduce the trial to patients and provide them with information sheets outlining the key aspects of the study. These sheets, along with consent forms, have been translated into Tamil and Kannada for accessibility. Research faculty will review the trial details with patients based on the information provided. Patients will then have the opportunity to discuss the trial with the participating consultant to make an informed decision. Written consent will be obtained from those willing to participate. Participants will also be informed that they may withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their access to other services.

Interventions

Intervention description

This trial is designed to compare the composite Siddha regimen with IFA, rather than to evaluate the effects of its individual components. After screening, eligible subjects will be randomized equally into two arms, each following either the standard of care or the Siddha drug regimen. Participants in the standard of care arm will receive a single dose of Albendazole 400 mg at baseline, followed by a three-month course of IFA supplementation (Ferrous Sulphate 200 mg + Folic Acid 0.5 mg, taken orally twice daily). Participants in the Siddha drug regimen arm will begin with an oil bath using AT on Day 1. On Days 2 to 4, they will be administered 30 mL of NPK on an empty stomach each morning for deworming. A pre-measured 5 g of NPK coarse powder will be provided in labelled sachets. The contents of one sachet shall be added to 120 mL of potable water and boiled over a mild flame until reduced to approximately 30 mL. The decoction will then be filtered using a clean cloth or sieve and allowed to cool to a lukewarm temperature before consumption. The decoction should be prepared fresh for each dose and consumed within 2–3 h; storage beyond this period is not recommended. From Day 5, they will commence regular medication consisting of BVK tablets (2 tablets, taken orally twice daily) and ABP (15 mL, taken orally twice daily), continuing until the end of the study period.

After being assigned to their respective trial arms, participants will receive a 15day supply of medication. On the 15th day, drug compliance will be assessed, and any adverse effects will be evaluated. Subsequently, medications will be provided on the 30th and 60th days, with investigations conducted at designated intervals.

Adherence strategies

Face-to-face adherence reminder sessions will be conducted at the initial product dispensing and during subsequent follow-up visits during which the importance of taking the study medication regularly, including instructions on the correct dose, timing, storage, and the need to take pills with prescribed vehicles and in coordination with meals will be briefed. Participants will also be informed about what to do if a dose is missed and notified that a trial drug count will be conducted at each visit to account for any remaining medication. Unused capsules and bottles will be returned at each follow-up visit, and the remaining medications will be checked and recorded in patient's diary. The details like medicines given, follow-ups, expected visit date, deviation from protocol, consumption of concomitant medicine, diet log will be entered in this diary and will be evaluated by the investigator on every visit. Intake of predefined list of iron-rich foods will be monitored using the diet log, categorized into significant and non-significant sources, and used to estimate the proportion of the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) achieved for analysis.

Concomitant care

Concomitant care and interventions permitted during the trial include medications such as anti-hypertensives and other essential drugs, provided the Principal Investigator determines they do not interfere with the efficacy of the study drugs.

Post-trial care

Participants who attain normal Hb levels will be withdrawn from medication and advised to consume iron-rich diet, while those with below-normal levels will continue treatment with either Siddha or iron and folic acid supplements through institutional clinics.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome of the study is the average change in haemoglobin (Hb) levels at the end of 3rd month compared to baseline. Secondary efficacy outcomes include the average change in serum iron, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), ferritin, and transferrin saturation levels between baseline and 3 months, as well as the average change in red blood cell (RBC) count, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) at 3rd month compared to baseline. Additionally, the study will explore the association between baseline Mukkutram categories and haemoglobin improvement at the end of the study. Safety outcomes will focus on the proportion of treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs) reported during the trial.

Sample size

The sample size for this parallel two-arm equivalence trial was determined using n-Master software. Based on a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the calculation considered an anticipated clinically significant difference of 0.6 g/dL (Siddha Regimen vs. Standard of Care) and an equivalence margin of ±1 g/dL. This yielded a requirement of 77 evaluable participants per group. To account for a potential 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 85 participants per group, totalling 170 participants for the study.

Recruitment

The study has already been registered with the CTRI prospectively (Identifier - CTRI/2025/01/079620), and it is currently awaiting fund release. Following that, an official invitation to participate will be announced on the National Institute of Siddha (NIS) website and published in print media. Additionally, information regarding the study will be disseminated through the official social media platforms of the National Institute of Siddha and the Ministry of Ayush.

Methods: assignment of interventions

Randomization & sequence generation

The estimated sample size will be randomly assigned to two groups in a 1:1 allocation ratio to ensure equal distribution of participants across both arms of the study. The randomization schedule will be generated using a computer-based algorithm with permuted blocks of size 6 to maintain balance within the groups throughout the enrolment process. A statistician will be responsible for designing and generating the allocation sequence to ensure rigor and minimize bias in the randomization process.

Allocation concealment mechanism

The randomization codes will be securely prepared and sealed in opaque, tamper-proof envelopes to maintain allocation concealment and prevent selection bias. These sealed envelopes will be sequentially numbered and opened only after the participant has been enrolled in the study, thereby maintaining the integrity of the randomization process.

Implementation

Women of reproductive age (18–49 years) seeking treatment at the National Institute of Siddha for anaemia or related clinical features, or attending the Integrative Clinic for any condition, will undergo Hb screening. Screening will also be conducted through special medical camps organized at the Integrative Clinics for females in the reproductive age group. Women with Hb levels between 8 and 11.9 g/dL will be invited to participate and detailed information about the study will be provided. After obtaining written informed consent, a thorough clinical and drug history will be recorded, and blood samples will be collected for evaluation against inclusion and exclusion criteria. Once blood reports are reviewed, eligible participants will be called for the baseline visit. During this visit, the participants' vitals and Siddha diagnostic measures will be recorded. Participants will then be randomized by the allocation sequence generated by statistician. The study drugs will be dispensed accordingly.

Blinding

This study is conducted as an open-label trial, so blinding is not applied.

However, all the outcome assessors and laboratory personnel will remain blinded, with samples coded prior to submission to the laboratories.

Methods: data collection, management and analysis

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes

Data collection will be conducted by trained physicians following standardized procedures to ensure accuracy and consistency. Regular audits and quality control measures will be implemented to validate data integrity. The Principal Investigator will oversee the accuracy, completeness, legibility, and timeliness of Case Report Forms (CRF). A subsample will undergo double data entry for accuracy assessment, while the NIS and NITM data management teams will identify and resolve missing values or discrepancies. Participant files will be routinely monitored, with corrective feedback provided as necessary.

Plans to promote participant retention & complete follow-up

To encourage active participation in the study, the potential benefits of the Siddha regimen will be clearly explained to participants. Regular reminders via phone calls or text messages will be sent to ensure adherence to scheduled visits and active engagement. Participants will also receive reminders to take the prescribed medicines and to attend follow-up visits.

Data management

All study-specific data will be recorded in the CRF by the Investigator, assessor, or an authorized person, with explanations documented for any missing data. Source data will be maintained, and CRFs will be verified against source documents. Data discrepancies will be addressed through discussion and resolved by consensus.

Confidentiality

All study-related data will be securely stored in locked cabinets with restricted access. Participant information will be coded to maintain confidentiality, with consent forms stored separately. Local databases will be password-protected, and any documents linking IDs to personal details will be securely locked in a restricted area.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use

Screening parameters will be assessed during the baseline visit, while prognostic and safety parameters, including CBC, iron profile, LFT, and RFT, will be evaluated monthly. HbA1C and reticulocyte count will be measured at the study's conclusion in addition to baseline assessment. Following a 12-h fasting period, a 10 mL blood sample will be collected and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The serum samples will then be aliquoted into microtubes and immediately frozen at −80°C.

Statistical methods

All categorical variables will be summarized as frequency and percentage, while quantitative variables will be presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or median (IQR) if not normal. Baseline characteristics between groups will be compared using Chi-square/Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and t-test/U test for continuous variables.

The average change in outcome will be compared between groups using t-test/U test. The study is designed as an equivalence trial, with equivalence between the intervention and control arms assessed at the end of the 3rd month based on the change in haemoglobin levels from baseline. Equivalence will be concluded if the 95% confidence interval for the mean difference lies entirely within the pre-specified equivalence margin. This will be formally tested using the two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure. Between-group differences in the rate of haemoglobin improvement at the end of each month (month 1, month 2, and month 3) will be assessed using repeated measures analysis. For repeated measures, Two-way repeated measures ANOVA or GEE will be applied, with multiple regression/GEE models adjusting for confounders (for example, baseline Hb, age, site, baseline ferritin, baseline helminth infection status if available).

The primary analysis for equivalence will be conducted using the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) population, including all randomized participants who received at least one dose and had at least one post-baseline assessment. Equivalence conclusions will be based primarily on the mITT analysis. The per-protocol analysis will serve as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness and consistency of mITT findings in participants with adequate adherence. The per-protocol (PP) population will include participants with medication adherence ≥70% (assessed via pill count and diary), visit attendance ≥75%, and no major protocol deviations. Missing data will be handled using appropriate imputation methods. A p-value ≤0.05 will indicate statistical significance, and analyses will be performed using SPSS 25/EZR software.

Methods: monitoring

Data monitoring committee

The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) at the National Institute of Siddha, comprising independent experts in relevant fields, is responsible for monitoring participant safety, study integrity, and treatment efficacy. DMC will receive interim analyses and any additional requested data in strict confidence during the recruitment period.

Adverse event reporting and harms

Adverse events will be recorded from the time of informed consent until study completion. Events occurring before the study intervention will be documented as unrelated to the study drug. All adverse events during the trial will be reported, with serious adverse events (SAEs) notified to the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC). SAEs after study discontinuation will only be reported if deemed related to the study drug or protocol procedures. Investigators will assess relatedness based on temporal association, clinical context, medical history, and concomitant medications.

Plans for auditing trial conduct

The committee will audit data quality, verify source documents, interview project staff, and ensure protocol compliance. Monitors will confirm accurate documentation of adverse events per protocol guidelines.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

This protocol, along with the case report forms and informed consent forms, was reviewed and approved by the IEC and Scientific Advisory committee (SAC) of both institutes for scientific content and compliance with relevant research and human subject regulations.

Protocol amendments

Any changes affecting study conduct, patient benefits, or safety, such as modifications in objectives, design, population, sample sizes, or procedures, will require a formal protocol amendment approved by the Ethics Committee prior to implementation. Minor administrative changes will be documented and the Ethics Committee may be notified about these changes.

Availability of data and materials

The Data Management Coordinating Centre will manage intra-study data sharing, with input from the Data Management Subcommittee. Cleaned datasets will be accessible to all investigators via the Project Accept website or a dedicated file transfer protocol site, with password protection. Discussion

Anaemia continues to be a major public health concern, affecting 30% of the global population and 57.2% of women in India [1,4]. Although IFA supplementation is widely used and effective, its gastrointestinal side effects often affect adherence, highlighting the need for alternative or complementary approaches [28]. In this context, Siddha medicine, with its distinct underlying principles and formulations, may offer a promising option, particularly for women of reproductive age [29,30]. However, despite some emerging preclinical and clinical evidence, existing studies have certain methodological limitations. The present study aims to address these gaps and generate robust evidence to support the potential integration of Siddha interventions into national healthcare programs. For ease of reference, a glossary of Siddha-specific terms used in this manuscript, along with their definitions, is provided in Table 3.

A study by Sivaraj et al. compared Annabedi Chendhuram (ABC), a Siddha formulation, to elemental iron for treating iron deficiency anaemia (IDA). Both treatments effectively improved haemoglobin levels, but gastrointestinal adverse effects differed. In group I (elemental iron), 24% experienced nausea, while in group II (ABC), only 10% had constipation. While Siddha texts recommend combining Thiripala with iron supplements to prevent constipation, this practice was not followed in the mentioned study. However, our formulation includes Thiripala and other herbs, which may reduce gastrointestinal side effects and enhance iron absorption [31].

A systematic review by Arumugam Rajendra Kumar et al. analysed clinical efficacy studies of 37 Siddha drugs for anaemia, and the clinical evidence remains fragmented, with most studies limited by small sample sizes, lack of randomization, inadequate blinding, and incomplete data. Many also fail to consider confounding factors such as nutritional status, comorbidities, and standard preparatory practices like deworming recommended in classical Siddha texts. Despite these limitations, certain formulations particularly Madhulai Manappagu (MM) and ABC have already been integrated into public health initiatives in Tamil Nadu. However, to extend their inclusion into national health programs, robust study designs incorporating traditional Siddha protocols are essential. In the current study, medicated oleation and deworming were followed by the administration of a haematinic drug, along with a supporting formulation to minimize gastrointestinal side effects, aligning with classical guidelines for holistic anaemia management [32].

Similarly, a public health initiative in Virudhunagar demonstrated the potential of Siddha formulations such as ABC, BVK, MM, and Nellikai Legiyam (NKL), which were administered for 45 days following deworming with Chundaivattral Chooranam (CVC), in managing anaemia among 3783 adolescent girls. However, the study primarily relied on haemoglobin levels and clinical improvements, without extensively analysing critical biomarkers for IDA [33]. These findings underscore the need for a comprehensive study incorporating iron profiling markers such as serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and total iron-binding capacity to generate stronger evidence on the efficacy of Siddha formulations in managing IDA, which is incorporated in this proposed study.

Anaemia in adolescent girls affects growth, cognitive development, and future reproductive health, whereas its impact on pregnant women is more immediate and severe. Based on this understanding, our study focuses on reproductive-aged women, a high-risk group for anaemia, which can result in serious complications such as maternal mortality, preterm birth, and low birth weight, emphasizing the need for effective interventions [34,35]. Furthermore, the structured randomized controlled trial (RCT) design addresses methodological gaps, ensuring more robust and generalizable findings for integrating Siddha treatments into anaemia management strategies.

The Siddha regimen chosen for this study comprises of BVK tablets and ABP along with AT oil bath and NPK for de-worming were administered as per protocol before commencing proper treatment. In conventional treatment, de-worming will be done by albendazole and IFA supplement will be given from very next day for 90 days. Similarly, Ennai Kuliyal (oleation) with AT will be given on day 1 to balance mukuttram. Next three consecutive days, NPK will be administered for de-worming. From next day, regular medications, BVK and ABP begins and will be administered for 86 days.

Bino Sundar ST et al. demonstrated that NPK, equally effective as Ivermectin and Albendazole, disrupts the pinworm life cycle by preventing egg maturation, potentially leading to complete eradication in laboratory rodents [20]. This deworming action helps in anaemia management by eliminating parasitic infestations that contribute to blood loss and nutrient malabsorption, thereby improving iron absorption and overall health.

BVK contains Terminalia chebula Retz., as main ingredient, which improves iron absorption and reduces gastrointestinal side effects commonly seen with IFA [21]. Additionally, the presence of Sottruppu (Sodium chloride) enhances iron bioavailability, making BVK an effective supplement for managing iron deficiency anemia [36,37]. BVK is traditionally prepared by sequentially soaking ingredients in fermented rice water, ginger juice, and lemon juice, followed by sun-drying, a process supported by both traditional wisdom and modern science. Fermented rice water enhances iron bioavailability by breaking down phytates and promoting probiotic activity, improving iron absorption [[38], [39], [40]]. Ginger stimulates red blood cell production and reduces oxidative stress, further aiding iron absorption [41]. Lemon juice, rich in vitamin C, enhances non-heme iron absorption by converting ferric to ferrous iron [42,43]. These combined actions improve iron uptake, support digestion, and reduce the gastrointestinal issues associated with iron therapy. As demonstrated by Logamanian et al. [33], BVK is effective in managing IDA, with Siddha literature also supporting its role in digestive health alongside anemia treatment [18].

ABP combines purified elemental iron with herbal ingredients like Thiripala (Terminalia chebula Retz., Phyllanthus emblica Linn., Terminalia bellerica (Gaertn.) Roxb.) and Thirikadugu (Zingiber officinale Roscoe., Piper nigrum Linn., Piper longum Linn.). Thiripala, prescribed as an adjuvant for iron supplements in Siddha literature [44], enhances iron utilization through its antioxidant and gut health benefits. Terminalia chebula Retz. helps regulate iron overload, while Phyllanthus emblica Linn., rich in vitamin C, boosts iron absorption [45,46]. Thirikadugu improves iron absorption by stimulating gut motility and digestive enzymes [22]. Together, these ingredients promote better iron absorption and gastrointestinal health, making ABP an effective treatment for iron deficiency anemia. A clinical trial involving 82 patients showed ABP's efficacy in improving haemoglobin levels [23]. However, the study lacked a comparison with standard care. The proposed study aims to strengthen this evidence by incorporating a standard-of-care group, focusing on the reproductive-age female population, a group not yet studied in RCTs for Siddha treatments.

This protocol employs a parallel-group, randomized controlled trial design, ensuring robust and high-quality evidence on the efficacy and safety of the intervention. While the open-label nature of the study may introduce bias, the use of objective outcome measures such as haemoglobin levels, red cell indices, and iron profiles helps maintain result accuracy. Rigorous monitoring of adverse drug reactions further strengthens patient safety and provides a comprehensive safety profile. However, certain challenges must be addressed. Compliance with daily medication in a long-term trial remains a key concern, requiring effective strategies such as telephonic follow-ups, SMS reminders, and patient diaries for better adherence. Additionally, the heterogeneity of the study population in terms of dietary habits, socioeconomic status, and baseline health could impact both efficacy and the incidence of side effects, necessitating stratified analysis or statistical adjustments to refine the findings.

With anaemia being a major public health concern in India, evaluating the efficacy and safety of Siddha treatment in comparison to IFA could provide valuable insights into an alternative approach with potentially fewer gastro-intestinal side effects. If Siddha formulations can address these challenges while demonstrating comparable efficacy, they could offer a more holistic and patient-friendly alternative. Even if the efficacy of Siddha treatment does not exceed that of conventional therapy, its ability to mitigate the drawbacks of IFA could support its role in an integrative treatment model. By combining traditional and modern approaches, anaemia management could become more effective, particularly in regions where nutritional deficiencies are widespread.

This study, therefore, has the potential to pave the way for further research into Siddha formulations, exploring their long-term benefits, safety profile, and broader applicability across different demographic groups. Ultimately, these findings could contribute to shaping public health strategies aimed at reducing the burden of anaemia in India through a more comprehensive and sustainable treatment approach.

Conclusion

In summary, this proposed study aims to rigorously evaluate the efficacy and safety of a Siddha regimen for managing mild to moderate IDA among reproductive-age women compared to traditional IFA supplements. Given the promising preliminary findings regarding Siddha formulations' effectiveness and tolerability, this research could significantly contribute to public health strategies aimed at combating IDA in vulnerable populations. Future studies should focus on establishing robust clinical evidence that supports the integration of traditional medicine into contemporary healthcare practices for anaemia management.
