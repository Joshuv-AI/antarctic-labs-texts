Note on Martingale Theory and Applications
Xiandong Zou

Abstract
This note investigates core properties of martingales, emphasizing the measure-theoretic formulation of conditional expectation, the martingale transform, and the upcrossing lemma. These results lead to the Martingale Convergence Theorem, which we then apply to study the extinction behavior in Galton–Watson branching processes.

Conditional Expectation

Definition 2.1

(Conditional expectation)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra.
For
X\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, a
\mathcal{G}
-measurable random variable
Y\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})

is called a
conditional expectation
of
X
given
\mathcal{G}
if

\mathbb{E}[X\mathbb{I}_{A}]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbb{I}_{A}],\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{G}.

Such a
Y
exists and is unique
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.; we write
Y=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]
.

Martingale: Definitions and Transform

Definition 3.1

(Filtration)
.

A sequence
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a
filtration
if
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
for all
n\geq 0
, representing information accumulated over time
[
4
,
1
]
.

Definition 3.2

(Martingale)
.

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a filtration. A process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
adapted
if

X_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable for each
n
.
It is a
martingale
(resp. supermartingale, submartingale) if

X_{n}\in L^{1}
for all
n
and

\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=X_{n}\quad(\text{resp. }\leq X_{n},\ \geq X_{n})\qquad\text{a.s.}

Remark
.

If
\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]\leq X_{n}
, it is a
supermartingale
; if
\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]\geq X_{n}
, it is a
submartingale

[
2
,
1
]
.

3.1
Martingale Transform

A process
(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
predictable
if
C_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable for each
n\geq 1
.
Given an adapted process
(X_{n})
, define the transform

(C\cdot X)_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}(X_{i}-X_{i-1}),\qquad n\geq 1,

with
(C\cdot X)_{0}:=0.

If
(X_{n})
is a martingale and
(C_{n})
is bounded and predictable, then
(C\cdot X)_{n}
is a martingale.

4
Upcrossing Lemma and Convergence

To prove convergence, we quantify oscillations via upcrossings. Let
U_{N}[a,b]
be the number of times a process passes from below
a
to above
b
by time
N
.

Lemma 4.1

(Doob’s upcrossing inequality)
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a submartingale and fix
a<b
.
Then for every
N
,

(b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[U_{N}[a,b]]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}]-\mathbb{E}[(X_{0}-a)^{-}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}].

Intuition:
This inequality arises because each upcrossing of
[a,b]
picks up at least
(b-a)
in gains, and the final term accounts for the last open position. Because the transform of a supermartingale is a supermartingale, the expected total gain is non-positive, leading to the bound.

Proof.

Fix
N
and define the (random) sequence of crossing times by

\tau_{1}:=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\leq a\},\qquad\sigma_{1}:=\inf\{n>\tau_{1}:X_{n}\geq b\},

and inductively for
k\geq 2
,

\tau_{k}:=\inf\{n>\sigma_{k-1}:X_{n}\leq a\},\qquad\sigma_{k}:=\inf\{n>\tau_{k}:X_{n}\geq b\},

with the convention
\inf\varnothing=\infty
. Let
U_{N}[a,b]
be the number of completed upcrossings of
[a,b]
by time
N
, i.e.

U_{N}[a,b]:=\max\{k\geq 0:\ \sigma_{k}\leq N\}.

Define a predictable (i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable) process
(H_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by

H_{n}:=\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}<n\leq\sigma_{k}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}<\infty\}},\qquad n\geq 1.

Thus
H_{n}\in\{0,1\}
and
H_{n}=1
exactly on those indices
n
that lie strictly after a downcrossing time
\tau_{k}
and up to (and including) the corresponding upcrossing time
\sigma_{k}
.

Consider the discrete-time stochastic integral (finite sum)

S_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}H_{n}\,(X_{n}-X_{n-1}).

A direct telescoping argument over each excursion
[\tau_{k},\sigma_{k}]
(and the last, possibly incomplete excursion) gives the pathwise inequality

(b-a)\,U_{N}[a,b]\ \leq\ S_{N}\ +\ (X_{0}-a)^{-}\ -\ (X_{N}-a)^{-}.

(1)

Indeed, on each completed upcrossing
k
with
\sigma_{k}\leq N
we have

\sum_{n=\tau_{k}+1}^{\sigma_{k}}(X_{n}-X_{n-1})=X_{\sigma_{k}}-X_{\tau_{k}}\ \geq\ b-a,

while the contribution of the (possible) last incomplete interval is controlled by the correction terms

(X_{0}-a)^{-}
and
(X_{N}-a)^{-}
in (
1
); this is the standard bookkeeping that ensures the inequality holds for every sample path.

Now take expectations in (
1
). Since
H_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable and
(X_{n})
is a submartingale,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[H_{n}(X_{n}-X_{n-1})\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[H_{n}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{n}-X_{n-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]\right]\geq 0,

hence
\mathbb{E}[S_{N}]\geq 0
. Therefore

(b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[U_{N}[a,b]]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}]-\mathbb{E}[(X_{0}-a)^{-}].

Finally, since
(X_{0}-a)^{-}\geq 0
, we also have

\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}]-\mathbb{E}[(X_{0}-a)^{-}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}],

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Martingale convergence)
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a submartingale. If
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{+}]<\infty
, then
X_{n}
converges almost surely
to a finite random variable
X_{\infty}
.
If in addition
(X_{n})
is uniformly integrable (e.g.
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{n}|]<\infty
and UI holds),
then
X_{n}\to X_{\infty}
in
L^{1}
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{\infty}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]
.

Proof.

Assume
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{+}]<\infty
. Fix
a<b
. Apply Doob’s upcrossing inequality (Lemma
4.1
) to obtain, for every
N
,

(b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[U_{N}[a,b]]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{-}]+|a|=\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{N}]+|a|.

Since
(X_{n})
is a submartingale,
\mathbb{E}[X_{N}]
is nondecreasing in
N
, so
\inf_{N}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}]>-\infty
(because
\mathbb{E}[X_{N}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{+}]
and
\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{+}]<\infty
). Hence
\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}[(X_{N}-a)^{-}]<\infty
, and therefore

\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}[U_{N}[a,b]]<\infty.

Letting
U_{\infty}[a,b]:=\sup_{N}U_{N}[a,b]
and using monotone convergence,

\mathbb{E}[U_{\infty}[a,b]]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[U_{N}[a,b]]<\infty,

so
U_{\infty}[a,b]<\infty
almost surely. Thus, for each fixed pair
a<b
, the sample path can complete only finitely many upcrossings of
[a,b]
.

Now intersect over all rational
a<b
. With probability one, for every rational
a<b
there are only finitely many upcrossings of
[a,b]
. This implies that the limit superior and limit inferior of
(X_{n}(\omega))
must coincide: if
\liminf X_{n}(\omega)<\limsup X_{n}(\omega)
, choose rationals

\liminf X_{n}(\omega)<a<b<\limsup X_{n}(\omega),

which would force infinitely many upcrossings of
[a,b]
, a contradiction. Hence
X_{n}
converges almost surely to some extended real limit
X_{\infty}
.

It remains to show
X_{\infty}
is finite a.s. Suppose
\mathbb{P}(X_{\infty}=+\infty)>0
. Then for each
K>0
, on
\{X_{\infty}=+\infty\}
we eventually have
X_{n}\geq K
, so by Fatou,

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{+}]\geq\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{+}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{\infty}=+\infty\}}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty,

contradicting
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{+}]<\infty
. Thus
\mathbb{P}(X_{\infty}=+\infty)=0
. Likewise,
X_{\infty}
cannot be
-\infty
on a set of positive probability because then for some
a
there would be infinitely many downcrossings below
a
while also having finite upcrossing control for all rational intervals; equivalently, the existence of an (extended) limit already rules out
-\infty
unless the sequence diverges, which it does not. Therefore
X_{\infty}
is finite almost surely.

For the
L^{1}
claim, assume additionally that
(X_{n})
is uniformly integrable and
X_{n}\to X_{\infty}
a.s. Uniform integrability plus almost sure convergence implies
L^{1}
convergence (Vitali’s theorem), so
\|X_{n}-X_{\infty}\|_{1}\to 0
and in particular
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\to\mathbb{E}[X_{\infty}]
. Since
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]
is nondecreasing (submartingale), the limit exists and

\mathbb{E}[X_{\infty}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}].\qed

5
Application: Galton–Watson processes

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a Galton–Watson process with
Z_{0}=1
and offspring distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 0}

of mean
\mu=\sum_{k}kp_{k}\in(0,\infty)
. Given
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n})
, assume that conditional on
Z_{n}
,
the offspring counts in generation
n+1
are i.i.d. with law
(p_{k})
, independent of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Then

Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}}X^{(n+1)}_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[X^{(n+1)}_{i}]=\mu.

Proposition 5.1
.

The normalized process
M_{n}:=Z_{n}/\mu^{n}
is a nonnegative martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{n})

[
1
]
.

Proof.

(For a Galton–Watson process.) Let
Z_{n}
be the population size in generation
n
, and let

Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}}\xi_{n,i},

where, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the offspring variables
\{\xi_{n,i}\}_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. with mean
\mu
and are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}}\xi_{n,i}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n,i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}}\mu=\mu Z_{n}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Z_{n+1}}{\mu^{n+1}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{\mu^{n+1}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\frac{1}{\mu^{n+1}}(\mu Z_{n})=\frac{Z_{n}}{\mu^{n}}=M_{n},

so
(M_{n})
is a martingale. Since
Z_{n}\geq 0
, also
M_{n}\geq 0
.
∎

•

Subcritical (
\mu<1
).

Since
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\mu Z_{n}\leq Z_{n}
, the process
(Z_{n})
is a nonnegative supermartingale.
By the supermartingale convergence theorem, there exists a finite random variable
Z_{\infty}
such that

Z_{n}\to Z_{\infty}
almost surely.

Taking expectations yields
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]=\mu^{n}\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]=\mu^{n}\to 0
.
By Fatou’s lemma and nonnegativity,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty}]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]=0,

hence
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty}]=0
and therefore
Z_{\infty}=0
almost surely.

Finally, note that the extinction event is

\mathrm{Ext}:=\{\exists n:\ Z_{n}=0\}=\{Z_{\infty}=0\},

because once the population hits
0
it stays
0
, and if
Z_{\infty}>0
then in fact
Z_{n}\geq 1
for all large
n
.
Thus
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Ext})=1
in the subcritical case.

•

Critical (
\mu=1
).

Here
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=Z_{n}
, so
(Z_{n})
is a nonnegative martingale and therefore

Z_{n}\to Z_{\infty}
almost surely for some finite
Z_{\infty}\geq 0
.
Moreover
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]=1
for all
n
.

If the process is
non-degenerate
(equivalently
p_{1}\neq 1
), then extinction still occurs with probability
1
.
One way to see this is via generating functions: let

f(s):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}s^{k},\qquad s\in[0,1].

Then
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}=0)=f^{\circ n}(0)
(the
n
-fold iterate), and the extinction probability

q:=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Ext})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{\circ n}(0)
is the smallest solution of
f(s)=s
in
[0,1]
.
In the critical case,
f^{\prime}(1)=\mu=1
and
f
is strictly convex when
p_{1}\neq 1
, so the only fixed point in
[0,1]

is
q=1
. Hence
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Ext})=1
, i.e.
Z_{\infty}=0
almost surely.

Despite
Z_{n}\to 0
almost surely, we still have
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]=1
for all
n
, so
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty}]=0\neq 1
.
This shows that
(Z_{n})
is not uniformly integrable in the critical non-degenerate case.
