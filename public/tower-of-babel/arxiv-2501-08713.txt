Synthesis of Infinite State Systems
Ohad Drucker, Alexander Rabinovich

Abstract
. The classical Church synthesis problem, solved by Büchi and Landweber [ 10 ] , treats the synthesis of finite state systems. The synthesis of infinite state systems, on the other hand, has only been investigated few times since then, with no complete or systematic solution. We present a systematic study of the synthesis of infinite state systems. The main step involves the synthesis of 𝖬𝖲𝖮 \mathsf{MSO} -definable parity games, which is, finding 𝖬𝖲𝖮 \mathsf{MSO} -definable uniform memoryless winning strategies for these games.



[a]

1.
Introduction

Let Spec be a specification language and Pr be an implementation language. The following problem is known as the Synthesis problem:

Synthesis Problem

Input:
A specification
S(I,O)\in Spec
.

Question:
Is there a program
P\in Pr
which implements it, i.e.,
\forall I\ S(I,P(I))
holds.

Our aim is a systematic study of the generalized Church synthesis problem.

We first discuss the
Church synthesis problem over
\omega=(\mathbb{N},<)
, and then explain its generalizations.

1.1.
The Church synthesis problem over
\omega

A synthesis problem is parameterized by a class of specifications and a class of implementations.

The
specification language
for the Church synthesis problem over
\omega
is Monadic Second-Order Logic of Order (
\mathsf{MSO}
).
In an
\mathsf{MSO}
formula
\varphi(X,Y)
, free variables
X
and
Y
range over monadic predicates over
\mathbb{N}
.
Since each such monadic predicate can be identified with its characteristic
\omega
-string,
\varphi
defines a binary relation on

\omega
-strings. In particular a specification
S(I,O)
relates input
\omega
-strings
I
and output
\omega
-strings
O
.

The
implementations
should then be functions from
\omega
-strings to
\omega
-strings, which we will call operators. However, one would want to consider operators that may be implemented by machines. When a machine computes an operator,
at every moment
t\in\mathbb{N}
it reads an input symbol
X(t)\in\{0,1\}
, updates its internal state, and produces an output
symbol
Y(t)\in\{0,1\}
. Hence, the output
Y(t)
produced at
t
depends only on inputs
symbols
X(0),X(1),\dots,X(t)
. Such operators are called
causal
operators, and these are the implementations of the Church synthesis problem over
\omega
:

Church Synthesis problem over
\omega

Input:
An
\mathsf{MSO}
formula
\Psi(X,Y)
.

Question:
Is there a causal operator F such that

\forall X\ \Psi(X,F(X))
holds over
\mathbb{N}
?

1.2.
Büchi and Landweber’s solution

Büchi and Landweber
[
10
]
proved that the Church synthesis problem is
computable. Their main theorem can be stated as:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Psi(X,Y)
be an
\mathsf{MSO}
formula.

(1)

(Decidability) It is decidable whether there is a causal operator
F
such that
\mathbb{N}\models\forall X\ \Psi(X,F(X))
.

(2)

(Definability) If yes, then there is an
\mathsf{MSO}
formula that defines a causal operator which implements
\Psi
.

(3)

(Computability) There is an algorithm that constructs, for each implementable
\mathsf{MSO}
formula
\Psi(X,Y)
, an
\mathsf{MSO}
formula
\varphi(X,Y)
which defines a causal operator that implements
\Psi
.

Note that a causal operator is
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable if and only if it is computable by a finite state automaton.

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on the fundamental connections between Logic, Games and Automata. The proof has four main steps:

From logic to automata:

Construct a deterministic finite state automaton equivalent to a given logical formula.

From automata to games:

Convert the automaton into a (finite) game arena for a two person perfect information infinite
game.

Solve the game:

Find who wins the game and find a winning strategy.

From winning strategies to input-output automata:

Given a winning strategy construct an automaton that implements the logical specification.

It is worth mentioning that although the synthesis problem over
\omega
is traditionally attributed to Church, in
[
24
]
Church does not explicitly restrict the discussion to finite state
systems. He has a vague and general formulation of “logistic
systems” and “circuits” and in fact discusses infinite state systems:
“Given a requirement which a circuit is to satisfy, we may suppose the
requirement expressed in some suitable logistic system which is an
extension of restricted recursive arithmetic. The synthesis problem is
then to find recursion equivalences representing a circuit that satisfies
the given requirement (or alternatively, to determine that there is no
such circuit).”

However, following Büchi and Landweber
[
10
]
the community narrowed the
view of Church’s Problem to the finite-state case. Büchi and Landweber’s game theoretical
methods for solving the synthesis problem initiated a line of research in the
algorithmic theory of infinite games that has been extensively
developed in recent years. Enormous
technical and conceptual progress has been achieved in developing game
methods, and a game-theoretic
framework for models of interactive computation has been established.
Game-theoretic methods have also been extended to study synthesis
problems for real-time and hybrid
automata
[
18
,
5
,
3
,
1
,
17
]
. At the same time, comparatively little attention has been paid to the original
motivation of the Church problem, with a relatively small number of works directly addressing synthesis of infinite state systems. In that sense, our paper takes the discussion back to its original context.

1.3.
Review of synthesis results for infinite state systems

Before we introduce our results, we review the known results involving synthesis of infinite state systems.

In
[
44
]
Walukiewicz considered arenas generated by
pushdown automata
.
A configuration of a pushdown automaton contains the content of a stack and a control state.
The set of such configurations is infinite and a pushdown automaton defines in a natural way a transition relation (graph)
on its configurations.
Walukiewicz
[
44
]

proved: there is an algorithm that decides who is the winner of the parity game over the arena described by a given pushdown automaton; moreover, the winner
has a winning strategy which can be implemented by a pushdown transducer.

These results were generalized in
[
13
,
43
]
to
higher-order pushdown automata
.
Carayol et al. proved: there is an algorithm that decides who is the winner of the parity game over the arena described by a given higher-order pushdown automaton; moreover, the winner
has a winning strategy which can be implemented by a higher-order pushdown transducer.
Recently, it was shown that a similar theorem holds for collapsible pushdown automata
[
6
,
14
]
.

The arena for the above games is infinite, however the out degree of every node is finite
and these games can be considered as games over an infinite arena with a
finite alphabet
.

One case of games over an
infinite alphabet
that can be found in the literature is the synthesis of games over
prefix-recognizable graphs

[
11
]
. Cachat showed that the synthesis problem of such games may be efficiently reduced to the synthesis problem of pushdown automata.

The above examples are about synthesis of games over infinite arenas, namely, finding definable winning strategies for such games.
Brütsch and Thomas
[
7
,
8
]
explicitly considered a generalization of Church problem in which infinite state systems over infinite alphabets are to be synthesized.
They introduced
{\mathbb{N}}
-
automata
that define specifications over the alphabet of natural numbers. They also introduced transducers
that can implement such relations.
Their main results show that it is decidable whether the relation defined by an
{\mathbb{N}}
-automaton is implementable by a transducer, and if so, there is an algorithm that computes such a transducer.

There is also a line of work on the synthesis of reactive systems over infinite alphabets equipped with equality or a linear order
[
2
,
21
,
22
,
27
]
.
These works are based on the study of constraint sequences, which abstract the behaviour of register automata and allow the reduction of Church games to
\omega
-regular games.

Altogether, these examples are instances of synthesis of infinite state systems that were carefully studied.
However, there are no systematic studies of this topic.

1.4.
Overview of our results

Our first step in studying synthesis of infinite state systems is synthesis of
games over infinite arenas
. In order to study algorithmic questions about these games, we need a formalism that provides a finite description of infinite state arenas, and a formalism that provides a finite description of strategies over infinite arenas.

Examining a concrete example, in the synthesis of
{\mathbb{N}}
-automata considered in
[
7
,
8
]
, the resulting games are parity games

\mathsf{MSO}
-definable in
\omega=({\mathbb{N}},<)
. Although any parity game has simple winning strategies, in the sense that they are uniform and memoryless, their descriptive complexity might be high. Thus, we may ask a general question on
\omega
: given an
\mathsf{MSO}
-definition of a parity game in
\omega
, can we decide who wins the game and describe a memoryless winning strategy?

We answer this question positively by showing that this synthesis problem is decidable, and by finding a memoryless winning strategy that is definable
in
\omega
. The same positive answer applies for expansions of
\omega
by parameters. Moreover, an implementing
{\mathbb{N}}
-transducer may efficiently be constructed out of that definable strategy.

Another concrete example to examine is the parity game described by a pushdown graph. Such a game is
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable
in the
full binary tree
. The previous question now arises in this wider context: given an
\mathsf{MSO}
-definition of a parity game in the full binary tree, can we decide who wins the game and describe a memoryless winning strategy?

We answer this question positively as well, a result that generalizes the Walukiewicz theorem. Our proof can be modified
to obtain a simple proof of the Walukiewicz theorem (although this simple proof does not give any complexity analysis as the one given by Walukiewicz). Our result also applies to some expansions of the full binary tree by parameters.

More generally, our approach to investigate synthesis of games over infinite arenas is
parameterized by a structure
\mathcal{M}
and can be stated as:

Game Synthesis Problem for
\mathcal{M}

Input:
An
\mathsf{MSO}
definition of a parity game in
\mathcal{M}
.

Task:
Decide who wins the game;

Find an
\mathsf{MSO}
definition of a memoryless winning strategy.

For the precise definitions, see subsection
2.7
.

Our main results provide sufficient conditions for the decidability of the game synthesis problem for
\mathcal{M}
and the existence of a winning strategy that is
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable in
\mathcal{M}
.

The notion of
selection
plays a vital role in these results. Let
\varphi(X_{1},\dots X_{l})
be a formula. A formula
\psi(X_{1},\dots,X_{l})
is a
selector
for
\varphi
in a model
\mathcal{M}
if whenever
\varphi
is satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}
, there is a unique tuple that satisfies
\psi
and it satisfies
\varphi
. We say that
\mathcal{M}
has the
selection property
if all satisfiable formulas have a selector. Extensive investigation of the selection property may be found in the literature, such as
[
29
,
37
,
38
,
34
]
. Selection for iterated pushdown memory was studied in
[
23
]
.

In many cases there is a formula that describes the set of memoryless winning strategies for a given definable parity game. If selection fails for this formula, one cannot describe a single memoryless winning strategy. However, we show:

Theorem 2

(
Definable strategies in bounded degree games
)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property, and
G
is a parity game
MSO
-definable in
\mathcal{M}
such that
G
has bounded degree, then there are MSO-definable in
\mathcal{M}
uniform memoryless winning strategies for
G
.

This result is then extended to
unbounded degree games
, out of which we get:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an expansion by unary predicates of an ordinal
<\omega^{\omega}
, or an expansion by unary predicates of the full
k
-ary tree which has the selection property. Then MSO-definable in
\mathcal{M}
parity games have MSO-definable in
\mathcal{M}
uniform memoryless winning strategies.

The next step is applying these results on the
synthesis of infinite state systems
. In order to properly define the synthesis problem, we need a specification language that describes an input-output relation. A natural possibility is to consider a definable parity game between players input and output whose edges are labelled by some alphabets
\Sigma_{in},\Sigma_{out}
that are not necessarily finite. The transitions are deterministic, which is, a vertex has at most one outgoing edge for each label. However, a single outgoing edge may have many different labels. The input-output relation defined by the game is then the set of pairs
(x,y)\in{\Sigma_{in}}^{\omega}\times{\Sigma_{out}}^{\omega}
which label a play that is winning for player output.

The generalized Church problem can then be stated as:

Generalized Church Synthesis Problem for
\mathcal{M}

Input:
An
\mathsf{MSO}
definition of an edge labelled parity game in
\mathcal{M}
.

Question:
Is there a causal operator that implements the input-output relation defined by the game?

If yes, can the relation be implemented by a transducer that is
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable in
\mathcal{M}
?

For the precise definitions, see subsection
5.2
.

To obtain a solution of this generalized Church problem from the solution of the game synthesis problem, we need to be able to
choose
one label of the transition specified by a winning strategy
in a definable way
. When the set of labels is finite, that can easily be done. However, when the set of labels is infinite that is not necessarily possible, and we provide sufficient conditions for that definable choice of labels to exist.

In particular, we prove:

Theorem 4

(
Synthesis of definable infinite state systems
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an ordinal
<\omega^{\omega}
, or the full
k
-ary tree. Let
\mathcal{G}
be an MSO-definable in
\mathcal{M}
edge labelled parity game over alphabets
\Sigma_{in}
and
\Sigma_{out}
(not necessarily finite).

(1)

(Decidability) It is decidable whether there exists a causal operator that implements the relation induced by
\mathcal{G}
.

(2)

(Definability) If yes, then there is an MSO-definable in
\mathcal{M}
transducer over
\Sigma_{in}
and
\Sigma_{out}
that implements the relation induced by
\mathcal{G}
.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
contains preliminaries.
Section
3
considers definable games over graphs of bounded out degree.
It provides sufficient conditions for the existence of definable memoryless winning strategies for these games.
Section
4
generalises these results to games of unbounded out degree.
Section
5
considers the generalized Church problem over finite and infinite alphabets.

2.
Preliminaries

In addition, it covers elements of the composition method in subsection
2.3
and the selection property in subsection
2.5
.

2.1.
Notations and terminology

We use
n,m,i,j,k,l
for natural numbers,
\omega
for the set of natural numbers, and
\alpha,\beta
for ordinals.

If
X
is a set,
2^{X}
is the power set of
X
, which is, the set of subsets of
X
. A finite sequence of elements of
X
is called a word or a string over
X
.
The empty string is denoted by
\epsilon
. The set of words over
X
is denoted by
X^{<\omega}
or
X^{*}
. An
\omega
-word over
X
is a function
f:\omega\to X
.
If
u,w
are words over
X
,
u\cdot w
is their concatenation, and
u^{\omega}
is the
\omega
-word formed by infinitely many concatenations of
u
. If
L
is a set of words,
L^{+}=\{w_{0}\cdots w_{n}\ |\ n\in\omega,\forall i\ w_{i}\in L\}
, and
L^{*}=L^{+}\cup\{\epsilon\}
.

For a tuple of sets
\bar{A}=(A_{0},\dots,A_{n-1})
,
\bar{A}\subseteq X
means that for every
0\leq i\leq n-1
,
A_{i}\subseteq X
.

We use the symbol
\simeq
for isomorphism. If
f:A\to B
and
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
, then
f(A^{\prime}):=\{f(x)\ |\ x\in A^{\prime}\}
.

If
(A,<)
is a linearly ordered set, we use standard notation for its intervals:
(a,b)=\{x\in A\ |\ a<x<b\}
,
[a,b):=(a,b)\cup\{a\}
, etc. The interval
(a,\infty)
is
\{x\in A\ |\ x>a\}
, and
(-\infty,a):=\{x\in A\ |\ x<a\}
.

The term ‘effectively determines / represents / defines’ means that there is an algorithm that determines, or that finds the required representation or definition. Similarly, when an object is ‘computable’, it means that there is an algorithm that finds it.

2.2.
Monadic Second Order Logic

We use standard notations and terminology about Monadic Second Order logic (see, for example,
[
42
]
). In this version the first order variables are eliminated and only second order variables that are interpreted as unary predicates are used.

{defi}
[
\mathsf{MSO}
]

(1)

(Syntax) Let
\mathcal{L}=\langle R_{i}\ |\ i\in I\rangle
be a relational signature, where
R_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-ary relation symbol.
The
vocabulary
of
\mathsf{MSO}
over
\mathcal{L}
is
\mathcal{L}
together with the
monadic second order variables

\{X_{i}\ |\ i\in\omega\}
, binary relation symbols
=,\subseteq
, and unary relation symbols
{}^{\prime}empty^{\prime}
and
{}^{\prime}sing^{\prime}
(for ‘singleton’).

Atomic formulas
take the form
X_{i}=X_{j}
,
X_{i}\subseteq X_{j}
,
empty(X_{i})
,
sing(X_{i})
or
R_{i}(X_{i_{0}},\dots,X_{i_{k-1}})
.
The
Monadic Second Order

(=MSO)

formulas
are the closure of the atomic formulas by the usual logical connectives and second-order quantifiers
\exists X_{i},\forall X_{i}
. An
\mathsf{MSO}
-formula is a
sentence
if it has no free variables.

We use upper case letters
X,Y,Z,\dots
to denote variables; with an overline,
\bar{X},\bar{Y}
, etc. to denote finite tuples of variables (always assumed distinct). We denote by
|\bar{X}|
the length of a tuple
\bar{X}
, that is, the number of variables appearing in it. A formula
\phi(X_{0},\dots,X_{l-1})
is a formula with free variables among
X_{0},\dots,X_{l-1}
.

(2)

(Semantics) A
structure with

l

distinguished elements
is a pair
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})
where
\mathcal{M}=(M,\langle R_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}\ |\ i\in I\rangle)
is a structure and
\bar{P}=(P_{0},\dots,P_{l-1})
is an
l
-tuple of subsets of
M
.

Given a structure with
l
distinguished elements
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})
and a formula
\phi(X_{0},\dots,X_{l-1})
, we define the
satisfaction
relation
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models\phi

(
also denoted by
\mathcal{M}\models\phi(\bar{P})
)
as follows:
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models X_{i}=X_{j}
if
P_{i},P_{j}
are equal singletons,
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models X_{i}\subseteq X_{j}
if
P_{i}\subseteq P_{j}
,
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models empty(X_{i})
if
P_{i}=\emptyset
,
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models sing(X_{i})
if
P_{i}
is a singleton, and
(\mathcal{M},\bar{P})\models R_{i}(X_{i_{0}},\dots,X_{i_{k-1}})
if
P_{i_{0}},\dots,P_{i_{k-1}}
are singletons
\{m_{i_{0}}\},\dots,\{m_{i_{k-1}}\}
, respectively, and
(m_{i_{0}},\dots,m_{i_{k-1}})\in R_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}
. The boolean connectives are handled as usual and quantifiers range over subsets of
M
.

In weak-
\mathsf{MSO}
semantics, the quantification is over finite sets.

(3)

Given a structure
\mathcal{M}
, the
monadic theory
of
\mathcal{M}
,
MTh(\mathcal{M})
, is the set of all sentences
\phi
satisfied by
\mathcal{M}
.

Along the paper, when a calligraphic letter denotes a structure, its non-calligraphic uppercase form stands for the domain of that structure. All formulas in this paper are
\mathsf{MSO}
formulas, unless otherwise noted.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{M}=(M,\langle R_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}\ |\ i\in I\rangle)
be a structure, and let
\bar{Q}
be a tuple of subsets of
M
. A structure of the form
(M,\langle R_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}\ |\ i\in I\rangle,\bar{Q})
is called
\mathcal{M}

expanded by the parameters

\bar{Q}
(or simply
\mathcal{M}

with parameters

\bar{Q}
). It is denoted by
(\mathcal{M},\bar{Q})
.

{defi}
[first-order variables]
A free variable
x
in a formula
\phi(x,\bar{Y})
is a

if
\phi(x,\bar{Y})
is of the form
sing(x)\land\psi(x,\bar{Y})
.

We follow the convention that if a variable (or a tuple of variables) is denoted by a lowercase letter then it is a first order variable, unless otherwise noted. The structure
(\omega,<)
is usually simply denoted by
\omega
.

Given a structure
\mathcal{M}
, one may ask whether
MTh(\mathcal{M})
is a decidable set.

Theorem 5

(Büchi
[
9
]
)
.

The monadic theory of
\omega
is decidable.

{defi}

The
k
-ary tree
is
T_{k}=k^{<\omega}
. The
k
-ary tree with successors

\mathcal{T}_{k}=(T_{k},<,Succ_{0},\dots,Succ_{k-1})
is
T_{k}
with a binary relation

if

y
, and unary predicates

.

Theorem 6

(Rabin
[
33
]
)
.

The monadic theory of

{defi}

[Definability] Let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure.

Given a formula
\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
,

\psi^{\mathcal{M}}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in M^{k}\ |\ \mathcal{M}\models\psi(\{x_{1}\},\dots,\{x_{k}\})\}.

A relation on
M
,

, is (weak-)

\mathsf{MSO}
-definable
in

if there is an (weak-)

\mathsf{MSO}

.

We sometimes use ‘
\mathcal{M}
-definable’ for ‘
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable in

’.

{defi}
[Mutual definability] Let
(M,\bar{R}),(M,\bar{S})
be structures with the same domain

. We say that they are

if
\bar{R}
is definable in
(M,\bar{S})
and

.

2.3.1.
Types

We review the basic definitions and facts about types, as may be found in
[
41
]
.

In what follows,
\mathcal{L}
is a finite relational signature:
\mathcal{L}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}.
The quantifier depth of an
\mathsf{MSO}
formula is defined in a standard way. The set of formulas of quantifier depth
\leq d
and free variables among
X_{0},\dots,X_{l-1}
is denoted by
{\mathfrak{Form}^{d}_{l}}
.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
be
\mathcal{L}
-structures, and
\bar{A}_{1}\subseteq M_{1}
,
\bar{A}_{2}\subseteq M_{2}
be
l
-tuples of subsets. We say that

(\mathcal{M}_{1},\bar{A}_{1})\equiv_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{2},\bar{A}_{2})

if they agree on
{\mathfrak{Form}^{d}_{l}}
.

{obs}

For every
d
and
l
, there are finitely many formulas in
{\mathfrak{Form}^{d}_{l}}
, up to equivalence.

Proposition 7

(Hintikka formulas)
.

For every
d,l\in\omega
, there is a finite set
H^{d}_{l}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Form}^{d}_{l}}
such that:

(1)

For every
\mathcal{L}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
and an
l
-tuple of subsets
\bar{A}\subseteq M
, there is a unique
\phi_{d}^{\mathcal{M},\bar{A}}\in H^{d}_{l}
that is satisfied by
(\mathcal{M},\bar{A})
.

(2)

If
\phi\in H^{d}_{l}
and
\psi\in{\mathfrak{Form}^{d}_{l}}
, then
\phi\models\psi
or
\phi\models\neg\psi
.

Moreover, there is an algorithm that given such
\phi
and
\psi
, decides between
\phi\models\psi
and
\phi\models\neg\psi
(it might be that both hold, and in this
case only one of the options is chosen).

The elements of
H^{d}_{l}
are called
(d,l)
-Hintikka formulas
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
be
\mathcal{L}
-structures, and
\bar{A}_{1}\subseteq M_{1}
,
\bar{A}_{2}\subseteq M_{2}
be
l
-tuples of subsets. Then

\phi_{d}^{\mathcal{M}_{1},\bar{A_{1}}}\equiv\phi_{d}^{\mathcal{M}_{2},\bar{A_{2}}}

if and only if

(\mathcal{M}_{1},\bar{A}_{1})\equiv_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{2},\bar{A}_{2}).

{defi}

[
d
-type of a structure]
Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
\mathcal{L}
-structure, and
\bar{A}\subseteq M
an
l
-tuple of subsets. The
d

Definable strategies in bounded degree games

In the following subsection, we find a sufficient condition on
\mathcal{M}
such that parity games with bounded degree that are definable in
\mathcal{M}
will have definable uniform memoryless winning strategies. We will see why bounded degree, and even bounded out degree, simplifies the description of a memoryless strategy, up to the point that the selection property finds a definition of a uniform memoryless winning strategy.

{defi}

(1)

We will say a game has
bounded degree
if its arena has bounded degree, which is, there is
d
such that all vertices have at most
d
neighbours.

A game has
bounded out degree
if there is
d
such that all vertices of its arena have at most
d
out-neighbours.

(2)

A ternary relation
R(u,a,b)
is a
local linear order
of a graph
G=(V,E)
if for any vertex
u\in V
,
R_{u}(a,b)=\{(a,b)\ |\ R(u,a,b)\}
linearly orders all out-neighbours of
u
.

(3)

A binary relation
R(a,b)
is a
uniform local linear order
of a graph
G=(V,E)
if for any vertex
u
it linearly orders all out-neighbours of
u
.

(4)

Let
G
be an
\mathcal{M}
-definable graph. We will say that
G
has a
definable
(uniform) local linear order if there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable relation that is a (uniform) local linear order of
G
.

When a graph has out degree bounded by
d
and a definable local linear order
R(u,x,y)
, a
d
-tuple of monadic predicates
(P_{0},\dots,P_{d-1})
that forms a partition of the vertices of one of the players defines a positional strategy: given a vertex
u
, the player orders the out-neighbours of
u
by
R_{u}
, and if
P_{i}(u)
then the next move is the
i
’th vertex.

The following provides sufficient conditions for the existence of a definable local linear order:

Lemma 22
.

If
G
is a bounded degree graph definable in
\mathcal{M}
, and
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property, then
G
has an
\mathcal{M}
-definable uniform local linear order.

Moreover, if selection for
\mathcal{M}
is solvable then the local linear order is computable.

Proof 3.1
.

This is based on the following well-known coloring theorem:

Proposition 23

(De Bruijn-Erdos
[
19
]
)
.

If
G
is a graph whose vertices have degree at most
d
, then there is a
d+1
coloring of the graph, which is, a coloring with
d+1
colors such that an edge connects vertices of different colors.

Proof 3.2
.

For finite graphs, this is proven by induction on the number of vertices - if
v\in G
and
G\setminus v
is
d+1
colored, then since
v
has at most
d
neighbours, it may be assigned a color different from all of its neighbours.

For infinite graphs, it follows from compactness of propositional logic.

Given
G=(V,E)
a graph with bounded degree
d
, we define a new edge relation on the same vertices:

(v,u)\in E^{\prime}\iff(v\neq u)\land\exists w\ \big((w,v)\in E\land(w,u)\in E\big).

The resulting graph has degree at most
d^{2}
, so there exists a
d^{2}+1
coloring. This coloring induces a partition of
V
into sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{d^{2}+1}
. On the original graph
G
, this partition satisfies:

\phi(C_{1},\dots,C_{d^{2}+1})=\forall v,u_{1},u_{2}

(v,u_{1})\in E\land(v,u_{2})\in E\to c(u_{1})\neq c(u_{2})

(where
c(u_{1})\neq c(u_{2})
is formalizable in
\mathsf{MSO}(C_{1},\dots,C_{d^{2}+1})
).

We have so far shown that the formula
\phi(\bar{C})
is satisfiable. Since
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property, there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable
\bar{C}
that satisfies the formula. Using
\bar{C}
a uniform local linear order may be defined:

a<b\iff\bigvee_{1\leq i<j\leq(d^{2}+1)}\ C_{i}(a)\land C_{j}(b).

Theorem 24
.

If
\mathcal{M}
has the
selection property
, and
G
is a parity game definable in
\mathcal{M}
such that
G
has
bounded out degree
and a
definable local linear order
, then there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable uniform memoryless winning strategies
for
G
.

Moreover, if selection for
\mathcal{M}
is solvable then the winning strategies are computable.

Proof 3.3
.

The proof has
3
steps:

(1)

Code a uniform memoryless strategy as a tuple
\bar{P}
of unary predicates.

(2)

Write a formula
uWinSt(\bar{P})
saying that
\bar{P}
codes a uniform memoryless winning strategy.

(3)

Apply selection on the formula
uWinSt(\bar{P})
to get a definable uniform memoryless winning strategy.

Let
k
be the number of colors of the vertices, and
d
a bound of the out degree.
Let

V_{I}(x),V_{II}(x),E(x,y),\Pi_{0}(x),\dots,\Pi_{k-1}(x)

be formulas that define
G
in
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\bar{P}=(P_{0},\dots,P_{d-1}),\bar{Q}=(Q_{0},\dots,Q_{d-1})
be unary predicates. Let
R(u,a,b)
be an
\mathcal{M}
-definable local linear order of
G
.

•

Let
p_{I}(\bar{P}),p_{II}(\bar{Q})
be formulas saying that
\bar{P}
and
\bar{Q}
are partitions of
V_{I}
and
V_{II}
, respectively.

•

Let
st_{I}(x,y,\bar{P})
be a formula saying that
x\in V_{I}
, and for any
0\leq i\leq d-1
, if
P_{i}(x)
and the out-neighbours of
x
ordered by
R_{x}
are
y_{0}<\dots<y_{k}
(for
k\leq d-1
), then
y=y_{i}
. The formula
st_{II}(x,y,\bar{Q})
is defined similarly.

•

The following formula
reach(x,\bar{P},\bar{Q},Y)
says that playing from
x
by the strategies
\bar{P},\bar{Q}
leads to a vertex of the set
Y
:

\forall A\ \Big((x\in A)\land

\big(\forall z\in(A\cap V_{I})\ \forall y\ st_{I}(z,y,\bar{P})\to y\in A\big)\land

\big(\forall z\in(A\cap V_{II})\ \forall y\ st_{II}(z,y,\bar{Q})\to y\in A\big)\Big)

\to(A\cap Y\neq\emptyset).

•

The formula
win_{I}(x,\bar{P},\bar{Q})
says that playing from
x
by the strategies
\bar{P},\bar{Q}
results in a win of player I:

There is an even color
i
such that:

–

For every
j<i
, there is
y
satisfying
reach(x,\bar{P},\bar{Q},\{y\})\land\neg reach(y,\bar{P},\bar{Q},\Pi_{j})
.

–

For every
y
, if
reach(x,\bar{P},\bar{Q},\{y\})
then
reach(y,\bar{P},\bar{Q},\Pi_{i})
.

•

The formula
WinSt_{I}(x,\bar{P})
is

p_{I}(\bar{P})\land\big(\forall\bar{Q}\ p_{II}(\bar{Q})\to win_{I}(x,\bar{P},\bar{Q})\big),

which is,
\bar{P}
codes a winning strategy of player I from
x
.

•

This leads to a definition
winReg_{I}(x)
of the winning region of player I:
x
is in the winning region of player I if there is
\bar{P}
such that
WinSt_{I}(x,\bar{P})
.

•

Finally,
uWinSt_{I}(\bar{P})
, the set of codes of uniform winning strategies for
I
, is

\forall x\ winReg_{I}(x)\to WinSt_{I}(x,\bar{P}).

Since
\mathcal{M}\models\exists\bar{P}\ uWinSt_{I}(\bar{P})
, and
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property, there is a formula
uWinSt^{*}_{I}(\bar{P})
such that there is a unique
\bar{P}
satisfying
uWinSt_{I}^{*}(\bar{P})
, and this
\bar{P}
also satisfies
uWinSt_{I}(\bar{P})
. The formula

\exists\bar{P}\ uWinSt_{I}^{*}(\bar{P})\land st_{I}(x,y,\bar{P})

defines a uniform memoryless winning strategy for player I.

The existence of a definable uniform memoryless winning strategy for player
II
is proven similarly.

Together with Lemma
22
:

Corollary 25
.

If
\mathcal{M}
has the
selection property
, and
G
is a parity game definable in
\mathcal{M}
such that
G
has
bounded degree
, then there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable uniform memoryless winning strategies
for
G
.

Moreover, if selection for
\mathcal{M}
is solvable then the winning strategies are computable.

Applications

{exa}

Let

By Theorem

Corollary 26
.

Let

be a parity game locally definable in

. Then

has computable uniform memoryless winning strategies locally definable in

That may be applied to solve pushdown parity games:

Theorem 27

(
[
44
]
,
[
11
]

.

A pushdown parity game has computable uniform winning pushdown strategies.

Proof 3.4
.

A pushdown parity game with stack alphabet
\Gamma
and states

Other applications of Corollary

[Level predicates]

3.3.
Games over an Abstract Store

In this subsection we consider games over an abstract store. Abstract store generalizes pushdown store and higher-order pushdown store.
We obtain a generalization of Walukiewicz’s theorem as a consequence of the results of subsection
3.1
.

{defi}
[Abstract store]
An abstract store is a tuple
{\mathcal{AS}}:=({\mathcal{S}},\bar{P},OP)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a set,
\bar{P}
is a tuple of unary predicates on
\mathcal{S}
, and
OP
is a finite set of abstract store operations which are partial functions
from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}
.

For example, for every finite alphabet
\Gamma
, the pushdown store over
\Gamma
can be considered an instance of an abstract store,
where
{\mathcal{S}}:=\Gamma^{*}
is the set of finite strings over
\Gamma
, for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have a predicate
P_{\gamma}
which holds on the stores with top letter
\gamma
,
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
the operation
push_{\gamma}
pushes
\gamma
into the stack, and if the stack
s
is not empty the operation
pop
removes its top letter.

Higher-order pushdown automata and register automata can also be viewed as instances of this definition.

An abstract store
{\mathcal{AS}}=({\mathcal{S}},\bar{P},OP)
can be seen as a labelled graph.
The set of nodes is
\mathcal{S}
. The node labelling is
\bar{P}
.
There is an edge from
u
to
v
labelled by
f\in OP
if
f(u)=v
.

{defi}

[An automaton over an abstract store] Let
{\mathcal{AS}}=({\mathcal{S}},\bar{P},OP)
be an abstract store, such that
|\bar{P}|=l
. An automaton
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{AS}
is a tuple

{\mathcal{A}}:=(Q,\Delta)
where
Q
(
=Q_{\mathcal{A}}
) is a finite set of states and
\Delta\subseteq Q\times\{0,1\}^{l}\times OP\times Q
is a transition relation.
A
configuration
is a pair
(q,s)\in Q\times\mathcal{S}
. We define a labelled transition relation
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{AS}
over the configurations:

(q,s)\rightarrow_{f}(q^{\prime},s^{\prime})
if
f\in OP
,
s^{\prime}=f(s)
,
\bar{P}(s)=\bar{b}
and
(q,\bar{b},f,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
.

{defi}

[An abstract store game] Let
\mathcal{AS}
be an abstract store, let
\mathcal{A}
be an automaton over
\mathcal{AS}
, and let the set
Q
of states of
\mathcal{A}
be partitioned into
Q_{I},Q_{II}
- the sets of the first and the second player states, and into
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{d}
- the set of states with color
i:=1,\dots,d
. The abstract store parity game over
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{AS}
induced by these partitions
is the game over the transition system
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{AS}
where a configuration
(q,s)
is a node of the first (respectively, the second) player if
q\in Q_{I}
(respectively,
q\in Q_{II}
), and the color of
(q,s)
is the same as the color of
q
.

Note that the above game is definable by very simple formulas in
|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|
-copying of
\mathcal{AS}
.

A memoryless winning strategy for Player II maps every configuration of player II to a configuration of Player I.

By Proposition
20
and Corollary
25
:

Corollary 28
.

If an abstract store
\mathcal{AS}
has the selection property, and
G
is a parity game over
\mathcal{AS}
, then there are definable (in a copying of
\mathcal{AS}
) uniform memoryless winning strategies for
G
.
Moreover, if selection for
\mathcal{AS}
is solvable then the winning strategies are computable.

This may be rephrased in terms of abstract store strategies, which is a special type of automata over abstract stores that compute a strategy:

{defi}

[Abstract store strategies] Let
{\mathcal{AS}}:=({\mathcal{S}},\bar{P},OP)
be an abstract store, and
\mathcal{A}:=(Q,\Delta)
an automaton over
\mathcal{AS}
. Let
G
be the abstract store game over
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{AS}
.

(1)

An
abstract store strategy
of player
I
for the game
G
is
(Q^{\prime},{\mathcal{AS}}^{\prime},\Pi)
, where:

•

Q^{\prime}
is a finite set of states.

•

{\mathcal{AS}}^{\prime}=({\mathcal{S}}^{\prime},\bar{P^{\prime}},OP^{\prime})
is an abstract store such that
|\bar{P^{\prime}}|=l
.

•

\Pi
is a union of transition functions:

\Pi_{I}:Q^{\prime}\times\{0,1\}^{l}\to OP^{\prime}\times Q^{\prime}\times\Delta_{I}

(
update state and store and choose a transition
)

\Pi_{II}:Q^{\prime}\times\{0,1\}^{l}\times\Delta_{II}\to OP^{\prime}\times Q^{\prime}

(
read Player II transition and update state and store
)

where
\Delta_{I}
are transition rules in
\Delta
whose first coordinate is in
Q_{I}
, and
\Delta_{II}
is defined similarly.

An
abstract store strategy configuration
is
(q^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in Q^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

(2)

Given a player
I
node
(q,s)
and a transition rule
\delta=(q,\bar{P}(s),f,q^{\prime})\in\Delta_{I}
, we say that
player

I

plays the transition rule

\delta
if the next game position is
(q^{\prime},f(s))
. Similarly for player
II
.

(3)

A play of
\mathcal{G}
from a configuration
(q,s)\in Q\times\mathcal{S}
is
played by the abstract store strategy
of player
I

(Q^{\prime},{\mathcal{AS}}^{\prime},\Pi)
from configuration
(q^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{0})\in Q^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
if:

•

The initial abstract store strategy configuration is
(q^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{0})
.

•

Whenever the vertex is of player
I
, the abstract store strategy configuration is
(q^{\prime},s^{\prime})
, and
\Pi_{I}(q^{\prime},\bar{P^{\prime}}(s^{\prime}))=(f^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime},\delta)
, player
I
plays the transition rule
\delta
and updates the abstract store strategy configuration to
(q^{\prime\prime},f^{\prime}(s^{\prime}))
.

•

Whenever the vertex is of player
II
, the abstract store strategy configuration is
(q^{\prime},s^{\prime})
, player
II
plays
\delta\in\Delta_{II}
, and

.

(4)

An abstract store strategy of player

is a

if for every vertex
(q,s)
in the winning region of player

, there is an abstract store strategy configuration

such that any play of
\mathcal{G}
from

.

(5)

Strategies of player

Corollary 29

(Synthesis of Abstract Store Strategies)
.

If
{\mathcal{AS}}=({\mathcal{S}},\bar{P},OP)
is an abstract store with the selection property,

is an automaton over

, and

.

(2)

4.
Unbounded degree games

The following section continues the investigation of the following problem:

Problem 30
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure, and
G
a parity game definable in
\mathcal{M}
. Are there
\mathcal{M}
-definable uniform memoryless winning strategies?

In section
3
, we’ve seen one case in which the answer is positive - when
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property and
G
has bounded degree (Corollary
25
).

In the following section we consider unbounded degree games, and present a general scheme for finding definable strategies in
\mathcal{M}
-definable unbounded degree games:

(1)

Represent the game by a bounded degree game defined in a copying of
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

Find definable uniform memoryless winning strategies for the representing game.

(3)

Transfer these strategies into winning strategies for the original game.

As long as
\mathcal{M}
has the selection property, section
3
guarantees that step (2) may be performed, whereas step (3) does not require any new techniques. In the following section we will explain how to perform step (1).

In order to construct the required representation, we introduce the notion of definability by regular expressions. We use this notion to represent by bounded out degree games all unbounded degree games defined in
(\alpha,<,\bar{P})
for
\alpha
an ordinal below
\omega^{\omega}
, and also to represent games defined in
\mathcal{T}_{k}
expanded by parameters. Our main technical result follows (Theorem
47
).

The structure of the section is as follows:

In subsection
4.1
the notion of representation is defined, and in particular the notion of copy representation, in which an
\mathcal{M}
-definable game is represented in a copying of
\mathcal{M}
. Variants of this notion appear implicitly in the literature (see e.g.
[
8
,
11
]
). Although we’re only interested in representing games, the challenging part is representing their graphs.

We develop some simple technical lemmas that construct a game representation out of a graph representation.
This will allow us later to concentrate on the graph
representation only.

In subsection
4.2
, the correctness of the general scheme is proven.

In subsection
4.3
, the notion of definability by regular expressions is introduced. It is shown that definability by regular expressions is an instance of
\mathsf{MSO}
definability, and that if a graph is definable by regular expressions in a bounded degree graph then it is copy represented by a bounded degree graph. For the expansions of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
and
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
by parameters, we show in subsection
4.4
that
\mathsf{MSO}
definability is the same as definability by regular expressions in a bounded degree graph. That is used to represent graphs defined in these structures, and the main technical result – Theorem
47
– follows.

4.3.
Definability by regular expressions and representations

When a graph
G
represents a graph
H
, paths of
G
correspond to edges of
H
. Thus, a standard way of describing a set of paths of
G
helps in constructing a graph represented by
G
. Whenever

is a labelled graph, regular expressions over its labels are a standard way of describing sets of paths. If in addition

has bounded degree, the represented graphs are copy represented in

by a bounded degree graph, and that might be the first step in finding a definable strategy. That motivates the following definitions:

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}\}
.

{defi}

(1)

A
\Sigma
-labelled graph
is a graph whose vertices and edges have labels in
\Sigma
. As a structure, it has monadic predicates
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
for the vertices labelled by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
, respectively, and binary predicates
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
for the edges labelled by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
, respectively.

(2)

Given a path in a labelled graph, a

is a concatenation of labels of the vertices and edges that form the path. More precisely, if
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
is a path, and for every
1\leq i\leq n-1
,

is the edge
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
, then a label of the path is a concatenation of labels of the sequence
(v_{1},e_{1},\dots,v_{n-1},e_{n-1},v_{n})
.

In particular, the label of a single vertex path is a label of that vertex.

The label of the empty path is the empty string,

(3)

Let

be a language over
\Sigma
, and
G
a
\Sigma
-labelled graph.

defines an edge relation

on

by:

if there is a path from
u
to
v
and a label of that path in
L
.

to accept some label of a path in order for that path to induce an edge of

{defi}

Note that in the above definition there are no assumptions of finiteness, which is, neither the set of nodes nor the alphabet are assumed to be finite.

One can similarly define nondeterministic automata in this setting. However, in general such automata need not admit definable determinization. Determinism is essential for the corresponding game-theoretic applications, since strategies are induced by the unique runs of the automaton.

5.2.
Generalized Church Problem over infinite alphabets

In the case of finite alphabets, the input-output relation was defined in terms of an
\mathcal{M}
-definable transition system. The first step of the solution of the generalized Church problem over finite alphabets then involved the definition of a parity game. That parity game represented the transition system in the sense that player output wins the game if and only if the input-output relation defined by the transition system may be implemented by a causal operator.

For the proof to hold, this parity game had to be defined in a copying of the structure
\mathcal{M}
. Indeed, the game was defined in
\mathcal{M}\times(|\Sigma_{in}|+1)
, and each position of player output was composed of the previous position and the move player input has just played.

This cannot be done whenever
\Sigma_{in}
is infinite. For this reason, in the generalized Church
problem over infinite alphabets one cannot, in general, interpret the game arena in a deterministic automaton, or in a structure in which the automaton itself is interpreted. Instead, the input–output relation is specified directly by an edge-labeled game arena.

6.
Conclusion

The aim of our paper is to present a first systematic study of the generalized Church synthesis problem, a problem that hasn’t been stated before. The original Church synthesis problem is about finite automata, whereas its generalized version is an almost identical synthesis problem for definable infinite state systems. Apart from being a natural generalization, it can also have future applications, given that finite state systems usually serve as models for hardware, whereas software is frequently represented by infinite state systems.

Although the Church synthesis problem has always been considered a key result in the area of logic, automata and games, there were very few attempts to expand it to a more general context, as we do here. For that purpose, we provided:

•

A conceptual framework.

•

Some sufficient conditions.

•

Non-trivial technical results.

The outcome is a solution of the generalized Church problem in some important instances.
