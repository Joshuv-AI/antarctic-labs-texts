ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing prevalence of mental disorders, especially among young adults, highlights the growing need for adequate insurance coverage for mental health services, including e-mental health services. Given the importance of patient preferences, the design of such insurance coverage requires consideration of the views of consumers and service providers, who can largely represent consumers. This study aimed to identify the desirable features of e-mental health insurance for students from the perspectives of students and mental health service providers in Hamadan, Iran.MethodsThis qualitative study used a content analysis approach. Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews with 33 students and 15 mental health service providers in 2024. For students, stratified convenience sampling was used. Purposive sampling was applied for providers. Content analysis was used for data analysis, and the participants and researchers confirmed the trustworthiness of the data.ResultsThe mean ± standard deviation of age of providers and students was 41.8 ± 8.81 and 24.24 ± 4.13, respectively. The majority of participants in the study were female (32 persons). Data analysis led to the extraction of five main themes: financial support, credibility, prioritization, control and management of moral hazard, and appropriate process, and 11 sub-themes: the share of insurance coverage, the scope of insurance coverage, fulfilling financial obligations, how to buy insurance, provider competency and the trustworthiness of providers, observing economic equity and triage, preventing abuse by providers and preventing abuse by insured individuals and reducing bureaucratic barriers.ConclusionProviding e-mental health insurance for students requires policymakers and health insurance organizations to provide insurance coverage with premiums commensurate with students’ ability to pay, financial support for vulnerable socio-economic groups, and attention to the needs and preferences of students regarding mental health services covered by insurance. Also, the development of transparent guidelines for the approval of competence and supervision of the behavior of providers is another important factor in the success of the implementation of e-mental health insurance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13909-w.

Background

According to the World Health Organization, health is not merely a state of complete physical well-being; it also includes psychological and social dimensions. Mental health is defined as a state of well-being in which an individual recognizes their own abilities and can manage the normal stresses of life [1]. Mental disorders are among the most prevalent and debilitating diseases, with studies indicating that one in four individuals will experience some form of mental disorder during their lifetime [1, 2].

Today, mental disorders are recognized as the most significant health challenge globally, raising concerns among numerous politicians and health advocates [3]. Reports from the World Health Organization reveal that mental disorders account for 13.5% of the global disease burden, ranking them as the sixth leading cause of disease burden compared to other conditions [3].

The highest Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) for mental disorders occur in the 15 to 49 age group, which coincides with the economically active population. Therefore, these disorders can profoundly influence all aspects of their lives, such as education and employment, which in turn can negatively affect the economy of any nation [4, 5]. Take depression and anxiety as examples; according to the World Health Organization reports, depression and anxiety cost the global economy one trillion dollars each year [1].

In Iran, the burden of mental illnesses accounts for 10% of the total disease burden. Notably, more than 60% of individuals with mental illnesses do not receive any health services. Some of the barriers to the optimal provision and utilization of mental health services in low- and middle-income countries, such as Iran, include: the lack of prioritization of mental health issues, insufficient budget al.location, a shortage of specialized human resources, inadequate cost coverage, and the fear of discrimination and social stigma [1, 3, 6]. Several studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries have demonstrated the effectiveness of digital mental health interventions in overcoming these challenges. For instance, Chibanda et al. (2016) in Zimbabwe and Naslund et al. (2017) in India and Nepal found that mobile- and internet-based mental health programs helped reduce access barriers and improve user engagement. These findings underscore the growing relevance of electronic platforms in extending mental health care in resource-limited settings [7, 8].

In recent decades, the use of electronic mental health services has been on the rise, contributing to the more efficient allocation of limited resources such as financial resources and time, while also reducing barriers to accessing services and care [9]. In some definitions, e‑mental health insurance refers to a model that combines two key components: [1] coverage and reimbursement for digital mental health services, such as tele-mental health consultations, mobile therapeutic apps, and online counseling; and [2] digitalization of insurance infrastructure, including online enrolment, streamlined claims submission, and automated payment systems [10, 11]. In the present study, the first component of the above-mentioned model is set in the scope of the research.

Although specific implementations of the above-mentioned models remain nascent, especially in low‑ and middle‑income countries (LMICs), evidence suggests that health insurance schemes in these contexts are associated with increased utilization of mental health services, primarily through the reduction of financial barriers to care [12].

As far as the service provision method, systematic reviews of digital mental health interventions in LMICs demonstrate their effectiveness in increasing access and engagement, as well as their strong potential for integration within formal health financing strategies [8]. n addition, considering the scarcity of available resources and the growing cost of healthcare, the issues of prioritization and economic evaluation have gained increasing importance. Consequently, examining individuals’ preferences and tendencies within each society—and their role in informing sound policy decisions—has become more critical [13, 14].

Among various groups in society, particular attention should be paid to university students’ mental health, as they represent the human capital and are future leaders and professionals. According to recent reports, the global prevalence of mental disorders is increasing due to the unique challenges of student life. For instance, a global survey reported that approximately 35% of university students experienced mild depressive symptoms and nearly 41% reported anxiety, while evidence from Iran indicates that more than 40% of students suffer from psychological distress. Addressing students’ mental health is essential to prevent the loss of human capital productivity within societies [15–19]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that applying digital mental health interventions is associated with significant improvements in university students’ mental health, including reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms [20].

To the best of our knowledge, studies on identifying the characteristics of desirable mental health insurance coverage in Iran are limited, with the only existing study being “Development of Characteristics and Levels of Mental Health Insurance Services Using a Discrete Choice Design“ [21]. Moreover, to date, no study has been conducted worldwide to elicit individuals’ preferences for an insurance package for electronic mental health services. One of the closest studies to the present research is “Preferences for Electronic Mental Health Interventions in Germany”, conducted by Elena Philips and colleagues [9]. Since students are among the younger population in any society and given the importance of addressing the preferences of this group, identifying the characteristics of a desirable insurance coverage for electronic mental health services for students seems particularly important. Therefore, the present study aims to determine these characteristics using a qualitative approach.

Methods

To identify the characteristics of the desired insurance coverage for electronic mental health services for students in Hamedan, a qualitative content analysis approach was employed. Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with students and mental health providers in 2024. Considering the higher awareness and experience of some mental health service providers, purposive sampling was used for them. When it came to the students, due to the lack of a list of their names, a stratified convenience sampling method was applied to ensure diverse representation while benefiting from the ease and speed of access to participating students. To cover maximum variation, efforts were made to include students from various academic levels and fields of study.

These criteria ensured that all participants were directly engaged with or knowledgeable about the provision or receipt of mental health services within the university context. In Iran, there is no mental health insurance plan specified for students. Therefore, providers with experience in service delivery for other insured persons were familiar with the usual situation of the clients, including students, in terms of the insurance-related issues.

To develop the interview questions, semi-structured questions were initially selected through a literature review and library research, followed by the design of a preliminary interview guide. The questions were reviewed multiple times by the research team and subsequently finalized. The interview guide used in this study was specifically developed for this research. The English version of the interview is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1). Interviews were first conducted with students, each lasting an average of twenty minutes. The providers’ interviews lasted an average of thirty minutes. Out of 15 interviews with providers, 10 interviews were conducted in person and five by telephone. Regarding students, all interviews were face-to-face.

Data saturation—defined as the point at which no new information emerged—was carefully monitored during data collection. Saturation was achieved after the thirty-third interview with students and the fifteenth interview with providers, at which point data collection was discontinued.

It is important to note that throughout all stages of the research, ethical considerations in the aspects of informed consent, data confidentiality, and the right to withdraw from the interview were strictly adhered to. Additionally, all interviews were recorded with the participant’s informed consent.

After each interview, the researcher listened to the recordings as soon as possible, carefully transcribed them, and initiated data analysis concurrently with data gathering. For coding, three coders independently analyzed the data using MAXQDA2018 software. In the subsequent step, the main themes were extracted. Finally, through discussion, idea exchange, and consensus among the coders, a conclusion was developed.

To enhance the validity of the study, four criteria were applied: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [21]. To achieve credibility and confirmability, immersion, peer review, and a data source triangulation approach were used; participants were selected from both students and mental health service providers. Regarding immersion, it is important to note that six months were spent conducting interviews, analyzing them by three people, and coordinating the analyses. Seeking dependability, the interview guide was reviewed by the research team, including the student and three thesis professors. Coding was performed by three people, separately. To increase transferability, sampling was conducted among students from different universities, and students were selected among students studying various fields of fundamental and applied sciences (Natural, formal, and social sciences, medicine, and engineering).

The issue of insurance coverage and coverage of most costs was one of the most crucial factors in reducing costs and enhancing people’s access to mental health services, as noted by most participants. P5 (Provider 5) stated: “An insurance plan should offer a substantial level of coverage, not just cover one session, because counseling sessions are inherently very costly. As a rule, the process requires much time. It should cover at least 10–15 sessions. “.

S15 (Student 15) remarked, “I believe that if they cover more costs, people may be more inclined to seek treatment and follow up on their conditions.” The scope of coverage and the variety of services included in insurance were two key topics during the interviews, as individuals anticipated that all mental health services, such as counseling, psychology, and psychiatry, would be covered by insurance, rather than just a select few. P12 stated, “We need a variety of services because we face a range of issues! Now, health insurance has come and done a thousand favors for our people, claiming that we will also cover psychological services. That is a purely medical perspective! Furthermore, it has only come to cover health psychology and clinical psychology services, which reflects the same pathological viewpoint!” S5 added: “Cover all types of services, such as counseling, and not just psychiatric services.”

Reducing financial strain through considering an appropriate method of payment for insurance was a concern for the participants. S14 mentioned, " The insurance premium could be paid on a monthly basis.” Some participants expressed dissatisfaction with insurers’ lack of commitment to their obligations and expected that a good mental health insurance plan would fulfill its responsibilities. P 4 said: “Insurance companies in Iran mostly consider their own commercial interests; for example, … [our own supplementary insurance company] says we will pay for glasses for five people, but in practice they say if you get glasses and you want that [the insurer] pay for them, you must come [to the insurer office] and show them to the insurance expert. This is just ridiculous. The insurer claims that it would cover the cost, so it should do that. It is like they intentionally create obstacles so that the insured gives up [withdraws to pursue the process] and the money stays with the insurer”.

Among the issues raised by the participants was the skill and knowledge of the providers, as well as the trustworthiness of the therapists contracted with the insurance company, since adherence to this will foster greater trust and enhance treatment effectiveness. S1 stated: “They must have their credentials verified and be watched to prevent fraud.“, and S3 said: “They should sign contracts with reputable and well-known individuals and consultants.“.

Other anticipated features of insurance included equity in payment and increased coverage for financially vulnerable individuals. S6 stated: “Insurance should provide more support for those in weaker financial situations.“.

The issue of triage and prioritizing individuals with acute needs concerned both groups of providers and students. P2 said: “I think there should be really strong insurance coverage for marital counseling and severe disorders like schizophrenia or paranoia—something like the current supplementary insurance, which covers a large portion of the costs and is more effective than other types. It could really meet the needs.” S6 added: “They should also assess individuals based on their needs. For example, prioritize someone with an acute mental condition over someone who does not have urgent needs, similar to triage in a hospital.“.

Management of the moral hazards is another important issue in the insurance industry. Despite having insurance, some providers may be inclined to create induced demand or complicate the reporting of simpler problems. P7 stated: “It shouldn’t be like this—where, as a therapist, I’m forced to write a different diagnosis just so the insurance will cover the cost.“.

Concerns about the negative effects of the entirely free services on the insureds’ behavior were acknowledged by providers; they believed that the insureds’ participation in covering costs through a reasonable premium or copayment, or a proportionate penalty system, could mitigate this issue to some extent. P7 stated: “Of course, in my opinion, regarding psychotherapy issues, the client should also pay a fee. For instance, assume a client comes to me, and I feel sympathy for him/her, wanting to offer a discount. However, I do not provide it completely free. [As an instance,] if I charge the client even for [an amount as little as] five thousand tomans, while he/she knows that my visit costs 270 tomans, [but] I am charging for five thousand tomans, this has a more positive effect than offering it for free. This payment fosters treatment adherence. Paying money establishes treatment adherence. Of course, insurance premium payment creates a similar commitment because the client has already made the payment. However, in universities, we waste time in such a way that if a client makes an appointment and fails to show up, they are placed on a blacklist. For example, Bu Ali University implements this policy; if the client does not attend and does not inform in advance, they cannot return for three weeks, as the Ministry is compensating the consultant for that client”.

Other issues anticipated for suitable insurance included reducing bureaucratic matters and eliminating extra steps. It seems that people expected to receive any service covered by insurance without having to navigate additional procedures, if necessary. P13 stated, “Now, several companies offer psychological services to their employees, but one problem is that they often require prior approval from a psychiatrist.”

Discussion

The present study aimed to identify the desirable attributes of insurance coverage for electronic mental health services for university students from the perspectives of both students and mental health service providers in Hamedan city. The study involved 48 participants, comprising 15 providers and 33 students. Based on the findings, five main themes and eleven subthemes were identified.

The theme of ‘financial support provided by insurance’ was the most frequently mentioned issue. In this study, one of the key features for policyholders was adequate cost coverage. This aspect has always been crucial for insurance applicants across all health sectors. Research indicates that increasing service coverage from basic to intermediate levels enhances the likelihood of selecting an insurance package [22]. Furthermore, participants noted that suitable insurance for mental health services should not only cover the majority of costs but also encompass all mental health services, rather than just psychiatric ones. Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals prefer psychological and counseling services over psychiatric services. In the research conducted by McHugh et al., participants favored psychotherapy services three times more than drug treatments. It appears that the necessity to cover all services required by individuals is one of the most critical factors in designing mental health insurance packages [23, 24]. The Lancet Commission on Global Mental Health and Sustainable Development highlights that mental health care systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries, need to extend beyond psychiatric treatments to include community-based psychological, psychosocial, and digital interventions. This is particularly relevant for young adults who often seek non-pharmacological support and face financial barriers [12]. From this perspective, insurance models offering access to a broad spectrum of mental health services may contribute to more equitable service utilization and better meet the diverse needs and preferences of service users.

Another important aspect of financial support mentioned by participants was that suitable mental health insurance should impose the least financial burden on the insured. In this study, some individuals suggested monthly payments as a solution; previous research has also highlighted the significance of selecting the right payment method for purchasing insurance. However, it is crucial to note that while multiple payments offer significant advantages over single payments, they also incur loading costs. Therefore, it is advisable to have insurance that adjusts the number of installments in a way that minimizes the extra costs associated with multiple payments [25]. Flexible and income-sensitive premium structures have also been proposed in several LMIC contexts as a way to increase participation rates among youth, who often lack a constant income but exhibit high mental health needs [12].

Another issue of concern for participants was the adherence of insurers to their obligations. Failure to meet these obligations leads to a loss of trust among the insured and a decrease in demand for insurance. An excessive focus by insurance companies on profits and the implementation of systems to deny or delay valid claims [26] can indicate poor adherence by companies to their responsibilities. It is noteworthy that the failure of insurers to meet their obligations extends beyond clients and can also involve issues related to reimbursement for providers, which can hinder the attraction and cooperation of skilled professionals. A study by Rahman et al. (2022) similarly found that delayed reimbursements contributed to a reduction in provider engagement and quality of care in mental health settings, particularly in resource-constrained systems [27].

As demonstrated in the present study, the skill and reputation of providers are two critical factors in the success of the treatment process. These factors directly influence the quality of the relationship between the therapist and the client, the establishment of a safe and trusting environment, and ultimately the improvement of the client’s condition. Based on the findings from previous studies, even therapists themselves, when necessary, seek out someone with a reputation for skill and competence to refer to another therapist [28, 29]. Therefore, it appears that addressing insurance obligations towards policyholders and healthcare providers is one of the most crucial issues to consider when accepting insurance coverage.

As previously mentioned, insurance, as a financial and social tool, plays a crucial role in ensuring the economic security of individuals and society. However, this role is accomplished when insurance companies pay adequate attention to the insureds’ needs at the individual level, particularly those who are economically vulnerable or in poorer health. Previous studies have also highlighted the necessity of paying attention to the individualized health services and vertical equity within health systems. Therefore, they remind insurers of the importance of seeking personalized treatments and a greater emphasis on socio-economically vulnerable groups [30]. This perspective aligns with recommendations from the World Health Organization and World Bank in their 2017 Global Monitoring Report on Universal Health Coverage, which emphasizes targeted subsidies and equity-sensitive benefit packages as essential strategies for achieving equitable health access, particularly in mental health [31].

While the presence and support of insurance companies are essential for promoting mental health, they can inadvertently lead to increased moral hazard, necessitating that insurance policymakers implement strategies to manage moral hazard for both providers and insured. Previous studies have indicated that having insurance results in consumers lacking the incentive to reduce the services they utilize and being more inclined to consume services when insurance coverage is available compared to when they bear all costs themselves [32]. This finding is consistent with evidence from landmark studies, such as the RAND Health Insurance Experiment (Newhouse, 1996) and the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (Baicker et al., 2013), which demonstrate increased utilization among insured populations due to lower out-of-pocket costs [33, 34].

Conversely, providers may prescribe a higher volume of unnecessary care to clients to boost their income and profits. A study by Mahboubi et al. on chemical warfare victims in Gilan-e Gharb revealed that when medical services are free, some doctors may induce greater demand from clients for increased profit [35, 36]. The significance of moral hazard and the necessity for appropriate policies to control it have been confirmed. Price elasticity of demand is one of the most vital tools for addressing moral hazard. According to research aimed at controlling moral hazard, insurance coverage should be higher for health care services with lower price elasticity and lower for those with higher price elasticity [37]. Therefore, it is essential to manage moral hazard in e-mental health insurance by initially covering services with lower price elasticity and subsequently including those with higher price elasticity. Effective insurance design involves balancing the generosity of coverage with mechanisms to limit unnecessary utilization, such as adjusting cost-sharing according to the price elasticity of demand. This balance is crucial for optimizing resource allocation and reducing inefficiencies, which may be particularly important in e-mental health services where utilization patterns differ from traditional care.

Cumbersome bureaucracy, unclear coverage, and payment delays are among the issues contributing to insured individuals’ dissatisfaction and their unwillingness to utilize insurance [38]. In a study by Herd and Moynihan (2020), it was emphasized that administrative burdens, such as complex paperwork, eligibility verification, and time-consuming follow-ups, significantly hinder access to healthcare services, particularly for low-income individuals. These burdens not only create psychological costs, such as stress and frustration, but also act as structural barriers that reduce effective utilization of insurance benefits [39]. Another crucial aspect of e-mental health insurance is a transparent and efficient process for insured individuals to access insurance benefits and services. another crucial aspect of e-mental health insurance is the implementation of a transparent and efficient process for insured individuals to access insurance benefits and services. Butala et al. (2023) demonstrated that lack of transparency in insurance plans—manifested through inaccuracies and inconsistencies in provider directories—significantly restricts insured individuals’ ability to access and effectively utilize mental health services. Therefore, ensuring accuracy and clarity in such information is fundamental to enhancing both access to and utilization of mental health services, as well as fostering trust and satisfaction among insured individuals [40].

This study is among the first to explore the desirable attributes of insurance coverage for electronic mental health services from the perspectives of university students and mental health providers, offering valuable foundational insights in this emerging field. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted exclusively in the city of Hamedan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions with differing cultural, economic, or healthcare contexts. Participants were recruited from the major universities in Hamedan; two smaller universities were excluded due to accessibility constraints. Moreover, focusing solely on university students may restrict the applicability of the findings to other demographic groups. Additionally, the inclusion criteria for students and providers were limited; thus, perspectives from individuals with varying experiences in mental health insurance or electronic mental health service provision may not have been captured in this study. Future research should expand the geographic scope and diversify the participant pool to enhance the external validity and broader applicability of the results.

At last, the following actions are suggested to the health system policymakers:

Conclusion

This qualitative study examined university students’ preferences regarding desirable e-mental health insurance plans. The elicited key themes included: financial support, provider credibility, prioritization, moral hazard management, and appropriate processes. Both students and providers emphasized the economic burden of mental health care, underlining the need to improve premium affordability, cost coverage, and reimbursement timeliness. Beyond these core concerns, the participants highlighted the importance of launching digital platforms for mental health services as well as developing online mental health emergency systems. In addition, developing clear guidelines to qualify and monitor the providers’ behavior can enhance the policyholders’ satisfaction and interests. Furthermore, it is suggested that the medical residents, especially those in high-stress specialties, be obliged to regularly attend the online or hybrid mental health counseling sessions. The findings also underlined the importance of reducing bureaucratic barriers to service access and ensuring that mental health insurance mechanisms prevent moral hazards. Creating transparent regulatory frameworks will be critical to long-term success. Finally, similar studies exploring the preferences of the other segments of society can help achieve a comprehensive e-mental health insurance system.
