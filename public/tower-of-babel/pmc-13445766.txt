Artemisinin resistance and Nigeria’s malaria burden: urgent policy imperatives from the WHO World Malaria Report 2025!
Authors: Shuaibu Saidu Musa, Reuben Victor M Laguitan, Kehinde Precious Fadele, Reymark P Malinda, Abraham Fessehaye Sium, Don Eliseo Lucero-Prisno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe WHO World Malaria Report 2025 highlights growing artemisinin resistance, with Nigeria bearing the world’s highest malaria burden at risk. Although no validated resistance markers were found, non-canonical mutations correlate with delayed parasite clearance. The report urges Nigeria to implement multiple first-line therapies, expand surveillance, and diversify policy urgently.

Dear Editor,

The World Health Organization’s World Malaria Report 2025 places the growing threat of antimalarial drug resistance at the centre of global malaria control discourse, identifying partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives as a defining pharmacological challenge for the current decade [1]. Nigeria, which bears the world’s highest national malaria burden, faces an acute and underappreciated exposure to the risks described in the report, and its surveillance architecture and policy response require urgent attention.

Nigeria’s position in the global malaria landscape is without parallel. According to the 2025 report, the country accounted for an estimated 24.3% of global malaria cases and 30.3% of all malaria deaths in 2024 [1] (Table 1), making it the most malaria-overburdened country in the world. Artemisinin-based combination therapies remain the recommended first-line treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria globally. Genomic analyses spanning more than 100,000 parasite samples collected between 1980 and 2023 have demonstrated that once resistance genotypes become established in a region, their spread mirrors escalation patterns previously observed across Southeast Asia [2].

The WHO World Malaria Report 2025 confirmed partial artemisinin resistance in four African countries, specifically Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda, and the United Republic of Tanzania, and identified suspected resistance in four additional countries including Ethiopia, Namibia, Sudan, and Zambia [1]. Although Nigeria is not among these countries, its absence most plausibly reflects the scarcity of systematic molecular surveillance from West Africa rather than any confirmed safety from emerging resistance [3]. Partial resistance to artemisinin is mediated principally by single nucleotide polymorphisms in the P. falciparum Kelch13 protein, several of which are now validated markers of resistance based on clinical and laboratory criteria [3].

The available molecular evidence from within Nigeria presents a picture that is cautionary rather than immediately alarming. A 2025 study of Pfkelch13 polymorphisms in southwest Nigeria, sampling isolates before and after artemisinin-based combination therapy introduction, found no mutations within the WHO-validated beta-propeller domain that are established markers of artemisinin resistance [4]. However, the same study identified N-terminal domain mutations, specifically K189T and R255K, whose frequencies correlated significantly with prolonged parasite clearance times in treated patients, suggesting that non-canonical molecular pathways may contribute to reduced artemisinin susceptibility in Nigerian parasite populations [4]. These observations underscore the limitation of relying solely on validated beta-propeller markers to assess resistance risk and reinforce the call for coordinated molecular monitoring commensurate with Nigeria’s epidemiological footprint.

Some contend that the absence of validated beta-propeller mutations in Nigerian data indicates the threat is not imminent and that pre-emptive policy action is therefore unwarranted; this view is historically difficult to sustain. Genomic analyses have demonstrated that once resistance genotypes become established in a region, their trajectory may escalate comparably to patterns observed in Southeast Asia, and that surveillance gaps in West Africa preclude confident conclusions about the current distribution of emerging markers [2]. The call for coordinated, continent-wide molecular monitoring issued by Rosenthal and colleagues in direct response to the East African experience represents precisely the kind of infrastructure Nigeria currently lacks at the required scale [3].

The policy implications for Nigeria are specific and time-sensitive. The WHO’s 2024 implementation guide on multiple first-line therapies provides a framework to diversify treatment pressure, reducing the selective advantage of resistant parasite genotypes without withdrawing effective treatment from patients [5]. Although the use of multiple first-line ACTs is not entirely novel in the Nigerian context, Nigeria’s national malaria strategic framework would benefit from strengthening and formalising this practice; including the structured deployment of alternative artemisinin-based combinations alongside artemether-lumefantrine in high-transmission settings. Modelling studies in Rwanda have shown that introducing multiple first-line therapies is among the most effective policy interventions for slowing the spread of artemisinin-resistant Pfkelch alleles, with median treatment failure counts projected to be 49 per cent lower under a multiple first-line therapy strategy than under continued reliance on artemether-lumefantrine as the sole first-line therapy [6]. These approaches should be accompanied by expansion of sentinel therapeutic efficacy sites, integration of molecular genotyping into routine drug efficacy monitoring, and transparent data-sharing with the WHO’s Global Malaria Programme [1].

The WHO World Malaria Report 2025 provides compelling evidence that the pharmacological tools underpinning global malaria control are under accelerating pressure in Africa [1]. For Nigeria, which accounts for nearly a quarter of all malaria cases worldwide, the prospect of widespread treatment failure carries consequences that extend well beyond any single health system’s capacity to absorb. Nigeria’s policymakers, national malaria programme leadership, and international funding partners are encouraged to treat the resistance reports and disease burden findings as a direct and actionable signal, and to invest without delay in the surveillance capacity and policy infrastructure necessary to preserve the efficacy of artemisinin-based therapies for current and future generations.
