Living donor liver re-transplantation: a viable option?
Authors: Souhail A Thabet, Samar Semaan, Ashton A Connor, Linda W Moore, R Mark Ghobrial
Published: 2025

Liver transplantation is a life-saving intervention in patients with end-stage liver disease. However, an estimated 6–9% of patients experience liver allograft failure that requires liver re-transplantation (re-LT). Reasons for this include primary allograft nonfunction, hepatic artery thrombosis, cholangiopathies, and recurrent hepatitis (1). When compared to primary liver transplantation, re-LT outcomes have historically been considered inferior, with earlier studies reporting significantly lower overall survival (42% to 71%) and graft survival (36% to 60%), respectively (2). However, with advances in re-LT surgical techniques, perioperative management, and hepatic allograft machine perfusion, recent reports have shown remarkable improvement in survival outcomes.

Li et al. present their experience with re-LT at a single center in Taiwan from 2002 to 2020, highlighting the cultural and clinical barriers involved in countries with low deceased donor organ donation volumes (3). The authors compared two cohorts: a living donor liver re-transplantation (re-LDLT) cohort and a deceased donor liver re-transplantation (re-DDLT) cohort. ABO incompatible grafts were excluded from the study, and all re-LDLT donors were relatives above the age of 18 years within five degrees of consanguinity. Both re-LDLT and re-DDLT cohorts included a mix of primary LDLTs and DDLTs, with primary LDLT predominating, comprising 72.7% of the re-LDLT group and 85.7% of the re-DDLT group recipients. They reported significant differences in terms of graft weight and cold ischemia time, both of which were lower in the re-LDLT group. Patients in the re-DDLT also had significantly older donors and experienced more frequent early in-hospital mortality and high complications. Re-LDLT recipients had higher overall survival (100% vs. 70.8% at 5 years, P=0.02) in the preliminary analysis.

When stratified by year of transplantation [2010], overall survival became comparable between re-LDLT and re-DDLT groups (3). Nearly 75% of re-DDLT mortality was in pre-2010 recipients, and all but one re-LDLT procedure was post-2010. The authors attribute this survival difference to improved surgical techniques and perioperative management with fewer complications, claiming that cumulative experience improved both re-DDLT and re-LDLT outcomes. They go on to conclude that in resource-limited countries with long waitlist times, re-LDLT is a viable option, offering surgeons and patients an adequate preparation period and positive outcomes.

Does re-LT truly have poorer outcomes? Historically, re-LT was reported to be associated with worse outcomes than primary LT. However, modern evidence suggests that this is no longer necessarily the case. Currently, there are no definitive criteria to guide the utilization of living versus cadaveric donors. Our recent single-center study demonstrated that 5-year survival following re-LT approached 70%, comparable to primary LT (1). Improved outcomes were attributed to advancements in surgical techniques, antiviral medications, enhanced post-transplant care, and more refined donor selection (1). This showed that optimizing donor selection to focus on high-quality grafts was crucial to improving re-LT outcomes. In a meta-analysis of published series, we also showed that recipient selection is another important consideration, as older, sicker patients with multiple organ failures display increased post re-LT mortality (2). While we believe there are no absolute contraindications to re-LT using living donor grafts, donors must be appropriately educated about associated risks and potential benefits.

Cultural and clinical constraints may limit the availability of traditional deceased donor grafts for re-LT recipients. Li et al. have pointed out that organ allocation committees may be reluctant to allocate deceased donor grafts to re-LT recipients in fear of a “wasted” transplant, which leads to longer waitlist time and increased waitlist mortality (3). While living donor transplantation comes with its own technical challenges and potential complications, it has been shown to offer a survival benefit of 13 to 17 years to recipients after the primary transplant when compared to non-recipients who remain on the waitlist (4). In the setting of re-LT, a national study of re-LDLT recipients in the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database from 2010 to 2019 has shown positive outcomes comparable to re-DDLT (5). Increased re-LDLT volume and experience were associated with significantly improved 1- and 5-year survival, with minimal donor and recipient complications (5).

In conclusion, in cases where cultural constraints and expanding waitlist candidate volume limit deceased donor transplantation for re-LT candidates, increasing evidence suggests that re-LDLT presents a viable option. Optimizing donor quality, in conjunction with increasing LDLT volume and training, is essential to achieving positive recipient and donor outcomes. Based on our experience, critically ill recipients with high model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) scores may be better suited for cadaveric transplantation. In conclusion, re-LDLT might potentially become a more sought out option for re-LT candidates with low MELD and hemodynamic stability. Experience and transplant volume remain the primary drivers of successful LDLT outcomes.
