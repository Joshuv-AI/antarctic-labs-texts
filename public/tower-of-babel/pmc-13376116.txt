Identification of the critical immune-related drivers shared by non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and osteoporosis

AbstractBackgroundNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and osteoporosis (OP) are two closely linked diseases, with immune cell dysregulation as a pivotal shared cause. However, the key drivers behind their pathogenesis remain poorly understood.MethodsMendelian randomization studies were performed to identify immune-related risk and protective factors for NAFLD and OP, focusing on 731 immune cell traits and 91 circulating inflammatory proteins. Transcriptomic datasets associated with NAFLD and OP were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database, and their consistency and representativeness were assessed through differential gene expression and functional enrichment analyses. Background

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and osteoporosis (OP) are two closely linked systemic metabolic diseases that pose significant public health challenges, particularly in our aging population [1, 2]. As the most prevalent chronic liver disease (CLD), NAFLD spans a spectrum of liver abnormalities from non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFL) to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and can progress to cirrhosis and liver cancer [3]. OP severely hampers patient mobility and increases fragility fracture risk, accompanied by higher rates of mortality and disability post-fracture [4, 5]. Nearly three-quarters of CLD patients are at risk of developing severe OP, and bone mineral density (BMD) tends to decrease as the severity of NAFLD worsens, with NASH patients showing lower BMD levels compared to those with simple steatosis [6, 7]. Additionally, NAFLD and OP share common risk factors, including aging, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and physical inactivity [7, 8]. Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms linking NAFLD and OP remain to be elucidated.

Immune cell dysregulation is a critical pathological feature in NAFLD and OP [9, 10]. At the steady state, the liver and bone function as key immunological organs, harboring various innate and adaptive immune cells [11, 12]. However, under the influence of various pathogenic factors, the immune cell landscape undergoes significant alterations, fostering an uncontrolled inflammatory environment that exacerbates tissue damage [13, 14]. For instance, T-helper (Th) cells, abundant in the livers of NAFLD patients and the bones of individuals with OP, contribute to tissue inflammation and disease progression [15]. Our understanding of this inflammatory immune cell landscape in NAFLD and OP is still emerging and fragmented, largely based on studies that focus on immune cell dysregulation in a single disease context. Therefore, an integrated analysis of the immune alterations in NAFLD and OP could provide a more comprehensive understanding of their shared immune features and underlying mechanisms.

Cytokines have been shown to regulate immune cell functions by orchestrating their enrichment, polarization, and activation [16]. Members of the C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) family, for example, play crucial roles in inflammatory processes by mediating immune cell trafficking, particularly that of T cells [17, 18]. Despite these insights, the complex interactions between cytokines and immune cells in the context of NAFLD and OP require further exploration, and more importantly, the key factors driving the onset and progression of NAFLD and OP remain to be identified. Furthermore, mechanisms discovered based on human data are often not well recapitulated in NAFLD and OP animal models, such as the high-fat diet (HFD)-induced mouse model [19]. Therefore, uncovering critical inflammatory drivers from human data and animal model perspectives will deepen our understanding of comorbidity mechanisms and present novel selections for biomarker and therapeutic targets.

To investigate the causal effects of immune phenotypes on the risk of NAFLD and OP, we first employed Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses, which revealed numerous immune-related pathogenic and protective factors. These factors exhibited similar distribution patterns across NAFLD and OP, such as the involvement of T cells, B cells, and monocytes. We then collected public transcriptomic datasets and evaluated their consistency and representativeness. Based on these data, immune cell characteristics of NAFLD and OP were further investigated, identifying the dysregulation of Th cells in both diseases. Notably, three CXCL family hub genes—CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11—were closely associated with Th cells and acted as potential pathogenic drivers shared by NAFLD and OP through protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. Furthermore, the UK Biobank data and scRNA-seq data were used to explore the clinical relevance and the main cellular sources of these hub genes. Finally, we established an HFD-induced mouse model to recapitulate the pathological hallmarks of NAFLD and OP simultaneously and validate our bioinformatic discoveries. By elucidating the shared cellular and molecular characteristics of these two interrelated diseases, this study aims to offer novel insights into their underlying mechanisms, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of inter-organ coordination in both pathological and physiological conditions (Fig. 1).

Methods

Ethics statement

The research performed in this study complies with all ethical regulations [20]. The experiments involving animals were approved by Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital with the approved number: IPEAECA2024052. All mice experiments were terminated after 24 weeks of high-fat or control diets feeding.

MR study data collection and processing

All Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary data in this study was available and used in open databases. For the data of immune cell traits, the largest GWAS published to date for immune cell traits of peripheral blood was selected, of which 118 were absolute cell counts, 389 were median fluorescence intensities reflecting the levels of surface antigens, 32 were morphological parameters (forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC), which were proportional to the cell volume, and intracellular complexity and the surface texture of cells, respectively), and 192 were relative cell counts. The GWAS summary statistics for 731 immune cell traits could be publicly available in the GWAS Catalog (accession numbers from GCST0001391 to GCST0002121). The Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) data was retrieved from the study conducted by Valeria Orrù et al. [21] For the data of circulating inflammatory proteins (CIPs), we selected in preceding investigations, Genome-wide Proteome Quantitative Trait Loci analyses were executed on 91 CIPs across 11 cohorts encompassing a total of 14,824 participants [22]. The criteria for the genetic instrumental variables (IVs) were as follows: P < 1 × 10− 5, r2 < 0.001, MAF > 0.01 and kb < 10,000. The F statistic, F = R2 (n − 2)/(1 − R2), was used to measure the strength of each genetic tool, where N was the effective sample size for GWAS for SNP association. Typically, IVs with low F statistics (< 10) were removed from our analyses. For the outcome data, summary data on the associations of SNPs with NAFLD and OP were obtained from a recent published GWAS from FinnGen Study. For NAFLD, the GWAS included 412,181 participants, containing 2568 patients with NAFLD. In terms of OP, the GWAS included 399,054 participants, containing 8017 patients with OP (https://www.finngen.fi/en). After a comprehensive screening for multiple conditions, 416 SNPs were identified to serve as IVs for the exposure factors (including immune cell traits and CIPs). The F statistics, used to assess the validity of these IVs for both immune cells and CIPs, consistently exceeded the threshold of 10, suggesting that the possibility of bias due to weak instruments was low. These data is detailed in Table S1.

MR analysis

The random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analysis technique was employed as the primary part in our MR study, based on which we separately performed a false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini and Hochberg) to control for the proportion of false positives in multiple testing. The IVW method provides an unbiased estimate of the causal effects of exposure and outcome when the horizontal pleiotropy is absent or when the horizontal pleiotropy is balanced [23]. The weighted median, weighted mode, simple mode and MR-Egger regression methods were performed as sensitivity analyses, which hold different assumptions at the costs of reducing statistical power. If the five methods go in the same direction, the estimates are more reliable, although the simple mode, weighted median, weighted mode and MR-Egger regression are less accurate than IVW methods. Additionally, we performed multiple comprehensive sensitivity analyses to rule out the possible violations of the MR assumptions (i.e., heterogeneity and pleiotropy). Cochran’s Q test was performed to test heterogeneity in the IVW [24]. Then we conducted MR Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test to detect the presence of horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity [25, 26]. If horizontal the pleiotropy in the instrumental variables existed, MR-PRESSO outlier test would compare the observed and expected distributions of the tested variants to identify outlier variants. If significant outliers (p < 0.05) were found, they would be removed from the analyses to return an unbiased causal estimate. For each obtained exposure-outcome pair, we further tested the direction of the causal relationship through Steiger directionality test to avoid reverse causation. Then, we applied the IVW again. The MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) and the MRPRESSO package (version 1.0).

Transcriptomic data collection

Based on the following search and filtering methods, four datasets related to NAFLD and OP were selected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). NAFLD-related datasets were searched using “non-alcoholic fatty liver disease”, “liver tissue”, and “Homo sapiens [Organism]” and screened based on the following criteria: (1) each dataset included both healthy and diseased samples; (2) the number of samples was sufficient to ensure the accuracy of our analysis; (3) the experimental type was “Expression profiling by array”; (4) the raw data or matrix, probe ID, and gene symbol of sequencing platform were available in the GEO database. Based on such criteria, two NAFLD-related datasets (GSE89632 and GSE63067) were included in this analysis, comprising data from 50 NAFLD patients and 31 healthy individuals. OP-related datasets were searched using “osteoporosis”, “monocyte”, and “Homo sapiens [Organism]”, and the filtering criteria were the same as NAFLD. GSE56815 and GSE7158 datasets were included in this study, consisting of data from 52 OP patients and 54 healthy individuals. Two diseases contained similar numbers of disease and healthy cases.

Batch removal and differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analyses were performed using the limma package (version 3.56.0), and genes with P values < 0.05 and |log2-Fold Change| ≥ log2 1.1 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The consistency between the two datasets in each disease was evaluated based on differentials in rank abundances. The sva package (version 3.48.0) was utilized for batch correction. The distributions of samples and DEGs were visualized by the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.2).

Gene functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed with the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.3) [27]. GO enrichment analysis provides simple annotations of genes from three perspectives, namely the biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). At the same time, GSEA considers all genes and could reflect the change of the whole dataset. In addition, we also used the Metascape database, an online tool, to analyze and interpret the function of both up and down-regulated DEGs together [28]. Each term was depicted as a circle node and nodes of the same color belonged to the same cluster. Terms with similarity > 0.3 were linked by edges. The Cytoscape software (version 3.9.2), ggplot2 package, and enrichplot package (version 1.16.2) were used for visualization.

Immune cell infiltration analysis and correlation analysis

The Immune Cell Abundance Identifier (ImmuCellAI) was used to analyze the immune cell compositions, including 18 T-cell subtypes and 6 other immune cells (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/ImmuCellAI/#!/) [29]. Subsequently, the correlation among immune cells and hub genes was conducted with the stats package (version 4.3.2) by Spearman correlation and visualization was conducted using the ggcorrplot package (version 0.1.4).

PPI network construction

139 shared DEGs in NAFLD and OP were used to construct a PPI network with the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes, (STRING, version 11.5, http://string-db.org). Due to the inability of NPY6R, one up-regulated gene in both diseases, to match its protein in the STRING database, we ultimately obtained a PPI network consisting of 138 points and 155 edges. The settings and stats of the network are shown in Fig. S4A. The interaction network was extracted and visualized using Cytoscape software. The cytoHubba algorithm was employed in the analysis of the topology and node centrality of shared DEGs. Based on 7 algorithms, namely MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality), MNC (Maximum neighborhood component), EPC (Edge Percolated Component), degree, EcCentricity, Closeness, and Radiality, 12 hub genes were chosen through intersection by the UpSetR package (version 1.4.0). Then, the GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/) was used to predict their interacting genes and functions. The PPI network and GeneMANIA results were visualized using Cytoscape software.

Population based analysis design and population

The UK Biobank is an ongoing, deeply phenotyped, prospective, population-based cohort designed to investigate the relationships between genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors and human diseases. Between 2006 and 2010, over 500,000 adults aged 40–69 years were enrolled from 22 assessment sites across the United Kingdom. We utilized data from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), which measured 2,941 protein analytes (reflecting 2,923 unique proteins) in plasma samples collected at baseline using the Olink Explore platform across eight panels. For the NAFLD analysis, we initially included 53,030 participants with available proteomic data. We excluded participants with prevalent NAFLD at baseline, leaving 51,004 individuals (50,261 non-cases and 743 NAFLD cases) for the final NAFLD analysis. For the osteoporosis analysis, we excluded 1,301 individuals with osteoporosis before baseline and 4,701 participants with a history of fracture, resulting in 38,491 individuals (37,063 non-cases and 1,428 osteoporosis cases) for the final osteoporosis analysis [30]. This research was conducted using data from the UK Biobank under application number 105,398.

Plasma protein measurements

Baseline plasma samples were analyzed using the Olink Explore platform, which employs proximity extension assay technology for high-throughput proteomic profiling. We focused on three inflammatory chemokines from the CXCR3 ligand family: CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11. Protein concentrations were measured as normalized protein expression (NPX) values and subsequently standardized to z-scores (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) for analyses.

Outcome ascertainment

Incident NAFLD cases were identified through hospital admission records (ICD-10 codes: K76.0) and primary care records linked to the UK Biobank database. Incident osteoporosis cases were ascertained using ICD-10 codes M80–M82 (osteoporosis with pathological fracture) and M81 (osteoporosis without pathological fracture) from hospital inpatient records. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of baseline assessment to the date of first diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2023), whichever occurred first.

Covariates

Baseline covariates included age, sex, ethnicity (White vs. Non-white for NAFLD; White, Asian, Black/Mixed/Other, Unknown for osteoporosis), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (never, occasional, moderate, daily), Townsend deprivation index (a measure of socioeconomic status), and body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared). These variables were obtained from baseline questionnaires and physical measurements.

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between participants who did and did not develop NAFLD or osteoporosis using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to assess the magnitude of differences between groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between baseline plasma levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 (per standard deviation increase) and incident NAFLD or osteoporosis. We constructed four sequential models: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex); Model 3 (fully adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, Townsend deprivation index, and BMI); and Model 4 (joint model including all three chemokines simultaneously with full covariate adjustment). The discriminative ability of each model was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). To examine dose-response relationships, participants were categorized into quartiles based on baseline chemokine levels, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group. Linear trends across quartiles were tested by treating quartiles as continuous variables. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding participants who developed NAFLD or osteoporosis within the first one or two years of follow-up to minimize potential reverse causation. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

scRNA-seq data processing

2 datasets from human liver tissues (GSE159977 [31] and GSE136103 [13]; 30 samples) and 3 datasets from human bone tissues (GSE217792 [32], GSE169396 [33], and our previously published dataset [14]; 17 samples), based on the 10x Genomics platform, were collected from the GEO database. scRNA-seq data processing was performed using the Seurat R package (v5.3.1). Cells were filtered using the following thresholds: nFeature_RNA > 400, nFeature_RNA < 6,000, and percent.mt < 15. Read counts were normalized per cell, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified using the ‘vst’ method in Seurat. All datasets from the same tissue were integrated across studies and donors using Harmony with default parameters to correct for batch effects. Significant principal components were determined using the ‘JackStrawPlot’ and ‘ElbowPlot’ functions, and the first 50 principal components were retained for clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cell types were annotated based on their canonical marker genes. To identify the cellular origins of hub genes, we performed a permutation-based test [34]. For each hub gene and each cell type, we first calculated the mean expression across all cells of that type. Cell-type labels were then randomly permuted, and, using all cells as the background, we repeatedly (1,000 times) sampled the same number of cells as in the original population and computed the corresponding mean expression. This generated a null distribution of average expression values for each hub gene in each cell type. The P value was defined as the frequency with which the randomized mean expression exceeded the observed mean expression. Data visualization was performed using the Seurat (v5.3.1) and ggplot2 (v3.4.2) packages.

HFD-induced NAFLD and OP mouse model

6-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and kept in an environment maintaining a temperature of 22–26℃, 50 ± 5% humidity, and a 12:12 h light: dark cycle. To induce NAFLD and OP simultaneously, all mice were randomly divided into two groups, namely the HFD-induced group (HFD group) and the negative control (NC) group. HFD group mice were fed an HFD containing 60 cal% fat (D12492, Research Diets, USA), while NC group mice were fed with 10 cal% fat control diets (D12450J, Research Diets, USA) for 24 weeks. The body weight of the mice was measured every 2 weeks with the same scale. After 24 weeks of induction, livers and bones were harvested for further evaluation.

Oil red O staining

The fresh liver tissue was embedded in O.C.T. (optimal cutting temperature compound, SAKURA, USA) and then cut into slices with a thickness of approximately 8–10 μm. An improved Oil Red O Staining Kit (G1261, Solarbio, China) was used to stain following its manual. Briefly, liver sections were washed with ddH2O twice and soaked in 60% isopropanol for 20 s, and then stained with Oil Red O dye in the dark for 15 min. After washing with 60% isopropanol to remove the dye solution, the cell nuclei were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin staining solution for 1 min before washing with ddH2O. After wiping off the moisture, a water-based sealing solution was used to seal slices.

Micro-computed tomography analysis (Micro-CT)

The Inveon MM system (Siemens, Germany) was utilized to measure bone density and micro-structure with settings as follows: 60 kV voltage, 8.89 μm pixel resolution, 0.22 mA current, and a 1.5 s exposure during each of the 360 rotational stages. The resultant images included 1536 slices, with each voxel measuring 8.89 μm across every axis. We constructed three-dimensional visual representations using two-dimensional images. Subsequently, the Inveon Research Workplace (Siemens, Germany) was employed to derive bone-related parameters as follows: BMD, the ratio of bone volume to total volume (BV/TV), the count of trabeculae (Tb.N), the ratio of bone surface area to volume (BS/BV), the distance between trabeculae (Tb.Sp), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) within the femur’s area of interest.

Flow cytometry of mice liver and bone

Mice were anesthetized and fixed on an anatomical table. Open the abdominal cavity to expose the liver. Preheated Ethylene Glycol Tetraacetic Acid/ Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution was used for perfusion along the portal vein. Then, the liver was chopped thoroughly in a metal filter. Cells and liver tissue fragments were collected and shaken in digestion solution at 37℃ for 45 min. Lymphocytes were separated by centrifugation using a lymphocyte separation solution and washed with PBS. As for bone, after removal of surface tendons, blood vessels, and muscles, the femurs were removed and cut into pieces and then digested at 37℃ for 1 h. RBC lysis buffer (420302, Biolegend, USA) was used to remove red blood cells. Single-cell suspension from liver and bone was cultured in vitro overnight with the stimulation of Cell Activation Cocktail (with Brefeldin A, 423303, Biolegend, USA). The following day, cells were collected and washed using Cell Staining Buffer (420201, Biolegend, USA). Live/dead cells were stained using the Zombie Aqua™ Fixable Viability Kit (423102, Biolegend, USA), and T cells were stained with PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD3 (100217, Biolegend, USA), FITC anti-mouse CD4 (100406, Biolegend, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 700 anti-mouse CD8a (100730, Biolegend, USA). Fixation and permeabilization were carried out at room temperature using the Cyto-Fast™ Fix/Perm Buffer Set (426803, Biolegend, USA). PE anti-mouse IL-4 (504104, Biolegend, USA), APC anti-mouse IFN-γ (505810, Biolegend, USA) and Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-mouse IL-17 A (506926, Biolegend, USA) were added to stain the intracellular marker of Th1, Th2 and Th17 cells. Finally, flow cytometry analyses were conducted on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer from Beckman Coulter, and the FlowJo software was used for subsequent analyses of results.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, China) from frozen mice livers and bones. HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (R333-01, Vazyme, China) was then used to generate cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme, China) in a BIORAD CFX96 Touch machine. We measured the relative expression of genes in livers and bones by comparing them with Gapdh and Beta-actin, respectively, making use of the 2− ρρCT technique. Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1, California, USA) was used for data analyses. Values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC) was calculated to evaluate the potential diagnostic value of the expression of hub genes. For comparisons, we employed the independent Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann−Whitney U test for non-normal variables. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

The similar immunopathogenic background between NAFLD and OP

To investigate the role of immune phenotypes in NAFLD and OP, we conducted MR studies using 731 immune cell features and 91 CIPs as exposures, with NAFLD and OP as outcomes (Table S2,S3). A total of 27 traits of immune cells with causal implications for NAFLD were unveiled, comprising 14 risk and 13 protective factors, and 28 immune cell features were identified to increase the risk of OP while 20 act protectively (Fig. 2A, Table S4). Categorized into 11 categories based on cell types, these immune cell traits causally associated with NAFLD and OP showed similar distribution patterns and predominantly clustered in T cell, B cell, and monocyte categories. Differently, the traits of natural killer T (NKT) cells were more identified in the result of NAFLD, while myeloid cell traits were more associated with the risk of OP. (Fig. 2B). Of significance, two immune cell traits exhibited dual risk effects on both diseases, namely the NKT cells (odds ratio (OR): 1.06, p = 0.048 with NAFLD and OR: 1.04, p = 0.034 with OP) and CD45 on CD33+ HLA-DR+ myeloid cells (OR: 1.09, p = 0.050 with NAFLD and OR: 1.09, p = 0.017 with OP) (Fig. 2C). Moreover, there were various similar but not identical immune cell traits causally linked to the risk of NAFLD and OP (Fig. 2C, Table S4). For example, CD45RA+ CD8+ cells, typically associated with a naïve or less differentiated state of T cells, were found to increase the risk of NAFLD. In contrast, the absolute and relative counts of CD8+ cells were identified as pathogenic factors for OP. Furthermore, several similar phenotypes belonging to the terminally differentiated CD8+ T cells and HLA-DR+ NK cells were part of the shared protective traits underlying NAFLD and OP. The absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy (p > 0.05) within these MR studies, coupled with the results of the Steiger directionality test confirming the minimal likelihood of reverse causality, underscored the robustness of the inferred causal relationships. Furthermore, four CIPs were identified as exerting causal effects on NAFLD, with tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 9 (TNFRSF9), signaling lymphocytic activation molecule (SLAM), and fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) identified as risk factors, and interleukin-1-alpha (IL-1α) displaying a protective effect (Fig. 2D, Table S5). At the same time, five CIPs were found to be causally associated with OP, with C-X-C motif chemokine 11 (CXCL11), interleukin-18 (IL-18), and interleukin-18 receptor 1 (IL-18R1) positively linked to OP risk; while artemin and fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) displayed negative associations (Fig. 2D, Table S5). Among these CIPs, CXCL11, IL-18, TNFRSF9, and SLAM were all reported to regulate T cell function and inflammatory responses through similar signaling pathways [35–38]. Except for some heterogeneity observed in the results of TNFRSF9 and FGF19 levels, no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected within these MR analysis findings (Table S5). Overall, these identified immune-related factors causally linked to NAFLD and OP indicate a similar immunopathogenic background underlying the two diseases, and more importantly, such similar composition and distribution of these immune cell traits further suggest the presence of common upstream regulatory mechanisms.

The evaluation of the consistency and representativeness of transcriptomic data

To further explore the shared pathological drivers of NAFLD and OP, we collected transcriptomic data from the GEO database, including two NAFLD-related datasets (GSE89632 and GSE63067) and two OP-related datasets (GSE56815 and GSE7158). The principal components analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were used first to classify the variation among samples in each disease. The largest variance (Principal component 1, PC1) and the main cluster separated different datasets, indicating the apparent batch effects (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, to assess the consistency across different datasets, differential gene expression analyses were conducted separately and the rank differential of gene pair fold change was measured, demonstrating modest concordance in both diseases (Fig. S1A-B). Hence, we integrated two datasets in each disease and mitigated batch effects. The PCA and hierarchical clustering results revealed that the batch effect was significantly removed (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, we re-performed consistency analysis on each dataset to check their consistency, revealing these datasets still exhibited strong consistency after the removal of batch effects (Fig. 3C, Fig. S1C). Finally, we identified the DEGs in merged data. There were 3628 up-regulated and 2974 down-regulated genes by NAFLD, while 699 DEGs with 368 up-regulated and 331 down-regulated genes were obtained for OP (Fig. 3D).

The GO enrichment analyses were conducted to assess the function of DEGs in NAFLD and OP. Up-regulated DEGs by NAFLD were enriched in the functions of metabolic processes and positive regulation of autophagy pathway, while regulation of insulin-like growth factors receptor (IGF-R) signaling pathway, response to glucocorticoid, and response to nutrient levels pathways were strongly associated with DEGs decreased, consistent with the well-known pathological features of NAFLD, such as fat deposition and abnormal autophagy function [39, 40] (Fig. 4A). In turn, the imbalance between osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis has been regarded as the pathological phenotype of OP [4]. Similarly, down-regulated DEGs in OP were associated with the functions of bone development, osteoblast differentiation, and ossification, whereas up-regulated were significantly enriched in monocyte development pathways (Fig. 4A). In addition to these disease-specific pathway changes, down-regulated DEGs in each disease both enriched in the functions of positive regulation of angiogenesis and tissue remodeling significantly (Fig. 4A). Intriguingly, up-regulated DEGs in each disease were associated with immunocyte- and cytokine-related functions, such as T cell proliferation, cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, and positive regulation of inflammatory response pathways, indicating a close association between both diseases and immune response, particularly T cells (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the Metascape database was employed to take both increased and declined DEGs together into functional enrichment analysis. Consistent with the above results, inflammatory response, and cytokine-related functions were the top terms in pathway and process enrichment analysis in the DEGs of both NAFLD and OP (Fig. 4B, Fig. S2). Importantly, the cluster of cellular response to cytokine stimulus in NAFLD and the cluster of cytokine-mediated signaling pathway in OP were both connected with the inflammatory response cluster, reflecting the important role of the cytokine-mediated inflammatory response in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and OP (Fig. 4B). To further comprehensively evaluate changes across the entire datasets, the GSEA was conducted considering all genes, and these pathway alterations were confirmed (Fig. 4C). These functional enrichment analysis results, consistent with disease pathological features, indicate that these datasets could effectively represent the disease characteristics of NAFLD and OP and show the significant abnormality of immune cells and cytokines in both diseases.

The dysregulation of Th cells shared by NAFLD and OP

Based on the integrated transcriptomic data, we employed the ImmuCellAI algorithm to assess the abundance of immunocytes in NAFLD and OP and investigate the alterations in immune cell compositions further. The ImmuCellAI algorithm predicts the relative abundance of 24 types of immune cells in two layers, encompassing macrophages, monocytes, T cells, B cells, etc., by calculating the single sample GSEA enrichment score of the expression deviation profile per cell type based on the gene signature of each immune cell. Notably, the ImmuCellAI algorithm demonstrates superior resolution and accuracy in various tissues compared to other immune cell infiltration analytical methods, such as xCell, CIBERSORT, TIMER, etc [29]. The stacked bar plots depicted the relative abundance of each of the 24 immune cells in each sample (Fig. 5A-B). The heat graphs of the correlation among different immune cell subsets revealed robust positive correlations between CD4+ cells and Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg cells, as well as a significant positive relationship between CD8+ cells and MAIT cells, exhausted T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and CD8 naïve T cells, aligning with the lineage derivation during the polarization process of these T cell subsets (Fig. S3). Contrasting the abundance of cells in layer 1 between the disease and healthy groups, NAFLD patients exhibited markedly elevated levels of dendritic cells (DC), CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and gamma delta T cells (Tgd) and reduced monocytes, NK cells, and neutrophils, while no significant changes in the cell composition of OP were observed, potentially attributed to the OP datasets originating from monocytes (Fig. 5C-D). In the case of the 18 immune cell subsets in layer 2, there were significant increases in the abundances of Th1, Th2, Th17, follicular helper T cells (Tfh), CD8 naïve T cells, exhausted T cells, and effector memory T cells (Tem) in NAFLD samples. In turn, OP patients only displayed increasing trends in the abundances of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells (Fig. 5C-D). These results suggest that both NAFLD and OP feature abnormal immune cell infiltration, sharing similar immune characteristic changes, such as the elevation of Th cell subsets.

CXCL family members play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of both NAFLD and OP

The similar immunopathogenic background and immune cell infiltration alterations between NAFLD and OP prompted us to wonder whether there were shared pathogenesis drivers in these two disease conditions. Therefore, we extracted their shared DEGs, namely 82 up-regulated and 57 down-regulated DEGs, to investigate further (Fig. 6A). These shared DEGs were predominantly associated with the functions of immune response, including T cell chemotaxis, chemokine-mediated signaling pathway, CXCR chemokine receptor binding, and chemokine activity (Fig. 6B). Leveraging the STRING database, we construct a PPI network, comprising 138 nodes and 155 edges with a confidence interval of 0.400, using these shared DEGs (Fig. S4A). Subsequently, we utilized the cytoHubba algorithm in Cytoscape to analyze the network, and through seven distinct algorithms, we identified 12 hub genes by intersecting the top 20 members from each algorithm: CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, HIST2H2BE (encodes H2BC21), HIST1H4H (encodes H4C6), HIST1H2AJ (encodes H2AC14), HMOX1, TRIM28, EGR2, LCN2, JUN, S100A12 (Fig. 6C). To delve deeper into their functions, we turned to GeneMANIA analysis—an online tool that predicts correlations related to co-localization, shared protein structural domains, co-expression, prediction, and pathways—to unravel the relationships between these 12 hub genes and their interacting genes. The outer circle in the plot represents the predicted genes, while the inner circle showcases the 12 hub genes. Notably, three hub genes from the CXCL family were involved in the functions of cytokine activity, myeloid leukocyte migration, cell chemotaxis, and chemokine receptor binding (Fig. 6D). Moreover, GeneMANIA results highlighted the relationships of co-expression and physical interaction between these three CXCL family hub genes and inflammation-related genes, such as XCL1 and various C-C motif chemokine ligand (CCL) family genes (Fig. S4C).

According to the 1793 immune-related genes obtained from the ImmPort database, 7 of 12 hub genes, namely CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, HMOX1, LCN2, JUN, and S100A12, were proven to be immune-related (Fig. S4B, Table S6). Of these 7 immune-related hub genes, the expressions of three CXCL family hub genes were significantly elevated in NAFLD and OP patients compared to healthy individuals, while the remaining four hub genes displayed a marked decrease in expression (Fig. 6E). Spearman correlation analyses revealed the positive correlation of the three CXCL family hub genes with Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells in NAFLD, with only CXCL9 showing a positive relationship with these Th cells in OP (Fig. 6F). Additionally, correlation analyses revealed that the remaining hub immune genes exhibited distinct associations with immune cells, emphasizing the intricate interplay between these genes and immune responses in the context of both diseases (Fig. S4D). Previous studies have shown that CXCL family members coordinate the migration, differentiation, and activation of immune cells via binding to receptor CXCR3 [41, 42]. Therefore, we extracted the marker gene set of Th cells from the ImmuCellAI algorithm and constructed a PPI network involving the three CXCL family members and their receptor CXCR3. The PPI network revealed that CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 could interact with various Th1, Th2, and Th17 markers through CXCR3 receptor binding, and could also engage in direct interactions independent of CXCR3 (Fig. 6G). These findings underscore the pivotal role of the interaction between CXCL9/10/11 and Th cells as a potential pathological mechanism shared by NAFLD and OP.

Clinical relevance analysis of three CXCL family members

To further explore the relationship between these three CXCL family members and clinical phenotypes, we first divided these patients from the NAFLD-related datasets into three different disease stages, namely healthy control (HC), simple steatosis (SS), and non-alcoholic fatty liver fibrosis (NASH) group, based on the meta data. With the continuous progression of NAFLD, the expression of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 increased gradually (Fig. S5A). Among them, compared with patients in the SS group, the expressions of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in the livers of NASH patients further increased, while the CXCL11 expression only showed an increasing trend and did not reach statistical significance (Fig. S5A). Based on the clinical detailed parameters from the GSE89632 dataset, we performed the correlation analysis between the expression of these chemokines and NAFLD-related clinical features. These clinical features included the histological features (steatosis percentage, fibrosis stage, lobular inflammation severity, ballooning intensity, and NAFLD activity score (NAS)) and the serum biomarkers (the serum levels of aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine transaminase (ALT)) of NAFLD severity. We found that CXCL9 was closely associated with both categories of clinical parameters, whereas CXCL10 showed stronger correlations with histological features, and CXCL11 was more strongly related to serum biomarkers (Fig. S5B). Considering that the elevations of AST and ALT in serum typically occur at the earlier stage of NAFLD, whereas histological alterations progressively worsen as the disease advances, these results suggest that CXCL9 and CXCL10 may be more closely related to later-stage progression of NAFLD, while CXCL11 may be more strongly linked to the early development of the disease. Furthermore, we used the ROC curves to measure the performance of expressions of three CXCL family members in diagnosing NAFLD and OP. The ROC analysis demonstrated that the expressions of CXCL9 (NAFLD: AUC = 0.753 & P < 0.0001; OP: AUC = 0.638 & P < 0.01), CXCL10 (NAFLD: AUC = 0.655 & P < 0.01; OP: AUC = 0.641 & P < 0.01), and CXCL11 (NAFLD: AUC = 0.732 & P < 0.01; OP: AUC = 0.601 & P < 0.05) had diagnostic efficiencies, while combining three members in a clinical prediction model significantly increased the AUC to 0.783 (P < 0.0001) in NAFLD and to 0.695 (P < 0.001) in OP (Fig. S5C-D). To evaluate the predictive efficacy of these chemokines, we conducted a prospective cohort study using data from the UK Biobank. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the associations between baseline plasma levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 and the incidence of NAFLD and OP. Of 51,004 participants included in the NAFLD analysis, 743 developed NAFLD during a mean follow-up of 13.23 ± 2.19 years. Baseline plasma levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 were all significantly elevated in the NAFLD group (Table S7-1). Cox regression analyses revealed that in the fully adjusted model, each SD increase in CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 was associated with 22.1% (HR = 1.221, 95% CI: 1.138–1.309), 26.4% (HR = 1.264, 95% CI: 1.185–1.348), and 20.2% (HR = 1.202, 95% CI: 1.126–1.282) increased NAFLD risk, respectively (all P < 0.001). The models demonstrated good discriminative performance (concordance indices: 0.735–0.739). In the joint model incorporating all three proteins simultaneously, CXCL10 remained independently associated with NAFLD (HR = 1.184, 95% CI: 1.080–1.299, P < 0.001), followed by CXCL11 (HR = 1.096, 95% CI: 1.011–1.188, P = 0.025), while CXCL9 was no longer significant (HR = 1.035, 95% CI: 0.937–1.143, P = 0.495) (Fig. 7A & Table S7-2). To further examine dose-response relationships, quartile analyses revealed significant trends for all three chemokines (all P for trend < 0.001). Participants in the highest quartile showed approximately 2-fold increased NAFLD risk: CXCL9 (HR = 2.220, 95% CI: 1.740–2.832), CXCL10 (HR = 2.260, 95% CI: 1.769–2.888), and CXCL11 (HR = 1.633, 95% CI: 1.319–2.021) compared with the lowest quartile (Table S7-3). The proportional hazards assumption (PH test) was satisfied for CXCL9 and CXCL11; a marginal violation for CXCL10 (P = 0.020) did not affect result stability (Table S7-4). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed NAFLD within the first two years of follow-up yielded consistent results (Table S7-5).

Cox proportional hazards regression results of these three CXCL family members. A-B The forest plots illustrate the detailed results of the Cox proportional hazards regression models between baseline plasma levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 and the incidence of NAFLD (A) and OP (B)

In the OP analysis, 1,428 of 38,491 participants developed OP during a mean follow-up of 15.90 ± 1.02 years. Baseline CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 levels were significantly elevated in the OP group (Table S7-6). Cox regression analyses demonstrated that in the fully adjusted model, each SD increase in CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 was associated with 13.6% (HR = 1.136, 95% CI: 1.080–1.195), 12.1% (HR = 1.121, 95% CI: 1.065–1.179), and 10.0% (HR = 1.100, 95% CI: 1.047–1.156) increased OP risk with good discrimination (concordance indices: 0.756–0.757). In the joint model, only CXCL9 remained independently significant (HR = 1.088, 95% CI: 1.012–1.170, P = 0.023), while CXCL10 and CXCL11 were attenuated (P = 0.359 and P = 0.202, respectively) (Fig. 7B & Table S7-7). Quartile analyses showed that OP risk was primarily driven by the highest quartile: CXCL9 (HR = 1.298, 95% CI: 1.095–1.539, P = 0.003) and CXCL11 (HR = 1.228, 95% CI: 1.061–1.422, P = 0.006). For CXCL10, a non-linear pattern was observed, though the overall trend remained significant (P for trend = 0.005) (Table S7-8). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all three chemokines (P = 0.926, 0.478, and 0.704) (Table S7-9). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed osteoporosis within two years confirmed these findings (Table S7-10). These results indicate the good diagnostic and predictive efficacy of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 in NAFLD and OP.

Myeloid cells are the main cellular sources of the three CXCL family hub genes

To further investigate the primary cellular sources of the three chemokines, we integrated and reanalyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the liver and bone, as well as our published bone tissue data, related to these two diseases. After quality control, batch correction, and unsupervised clustering analysis, we identified ~ 150,000 cells in the liver and ~ 120,000 cells in the bone (Fig. 8A & C). According to their cell markers, we annotated the cell subpopulations shared by two types of tissues, including myeloid cells (LYZ), B cells (CD79B), T cells (CD3D), innate lymphoid cells (ILCs, KLRD1), endothelial cells (CDH5), and pericytes (ACTA2) (Fig. 8A and 8B). Hepatocytes (ALB) and cholangiocytes (TFF3) were liver-specific cell subpopulations, while in bone tissue, progenitor cells (MPO), mesenchymal stem cells (MSC, MFAP5), and erythroid cells (ALAS2) appeared (Fig. 8C & D).

The proportions of Th cells and the expression levels of CXCL family hub genes are elevated in an NAFLD and OP mice model

To further substantiate our findings based on human data above, we opted to employ an HFD intervention in male mice over 24 weeks. This model is well-established in previous studies on NAFLD and has been shown to induce bone mass loss, rendering it an ideal choice for simultaneously recapitulating the pathogenesis of both NAFLD and OP. Following 24-week HFD induction, HFD group mice exhibited a noticeable increase in both body and liver weight compared to the NC group (Fig. S6A-B). Oil red O staining further revealed conspicuous fat deposition and microstructural damage in the liver of HFD group mice (Fig. S6C). Assessment of bone structure via micro-CT unveiled a reduction in bone trabeculae and an expansion of bone marrow space, coupled with diminished BMD, BV/TV, Tb. Th, and Tb. N, and augmented BS/BV and Tb. Sp, corroborating the induction of bone loss by the HFD (Fig. S6D-E). Collectively, these observations support the efficacy of the HFD-induced mouse model in recapitulating the pathological hallmarks of both NAFLD and OP.

Subsequently, we employed flow cytometry to assess the proportions of Th cells in mice livers and bones. Commonly used intracellular cytokines, including IL-4, IFN-γ, and IL-17, were selected to label Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell subpopulations separately. Compared to the NC group mice, there were more Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells in the livers and bones of the HFD group mice following the 24-week induction, indicating a shift of T cell phenotype towards an inflammatory response (Fig. 9A-B). At the same time, we detected the expression of the three CXCL family hub genes with RT−qPCR and found that Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl11 were markedly up-regulated in the livers and bones of mice fed an HFD (Fig. 9C). These results indicate that the HFD-induced mouse model is an ideal choice for preclinical studies of NAFLD and OP, as it can simulate the pathological characteristics and key pathological mechanisms of these two diseases well.

Discussion

As prevalent age-related conditions, NAFLD and OP share similar pathological features, including chronic low-grade inflammation, microstructural damage, and metabolic disruptions. Furthermore, the critical roles of immune cells and cytokines in disease occurrence and progression have been paid more and more attention [43]. However, the complex relationship between immune cells and cytokines remains ambiguous, and further exploration of the key drivers in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and OP is also necessary. Our study sought to figure out these scientific problems through in silico analyses. Initially, we identified numerous immune-related factors causally linked to these diseases through MR analyses, and these factors showed similar composition and distribution, showing a similar immunogenic background between NAFLD and OP. Subsequently, leveraging bioinformatic tools, we scrutinized the alteration of immune cell compositions and found the abnormality of Th cell infiltration in NAFLD and OP. Seven immune-related hub genes (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, HMOX1, LCN2, JUN, and S100A12) were identified as crucial players in unveiling the core gene signature of NAFLD and OP, with an additional focus on three CXCL family hub genes, as shared pathogenesis drivers underlying NAFLD and OP. Finally, we validated our bioinformatic discoveries in the HFD-induced mouse model which recapitulated the pathological hallmarks of NAFLD and OP simultaneously. Building upon these findings and combined with previous research findings, our subsequent discussion will delve deeper into the broader implications of these results and their potential significance in advancing our understanding of the intricate relationship between NAFLD and OP.

Using MR as a method for causal inference, we first identified immune cell traits and CIPs that have causal relationships with NAFLD and OP. Through integrating the results in both diseases, interestingly, we found that the immune cells causally linked to the risk of NAFLD and OP exhibit a similar pattern, primarily involving T cells, B cells, and monocytes. This suggests that there may be similar upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of OP and NAFLD. Besides, we discovered two immune cell traits that are causally linked to the development of both NAFLD and OP: NKT cells and CD33+ HLA-DR+ myeloid cells, which have been reported in previous animal studies to play pathological roles in NAFLD and OP [44–49]. In addition, several immune cell traits with similar, though not identical, characteristic and linage origin were also detected to serve as common pathogenic factors for OP and NAFLD, including CD8+ T cell, HLA-DR+ T cell, CD3− lymphocyte, and HLA-DR+ NK cell traits. On the other hand, we found that these CIPs identified mostly played crucial roles in regulating T cell functions. CXCL11 and IL-18 directly affect T cell activation and function, while TNFRSF9 and SLAM enhance immune responses by promoting T cell proliferation and mediating inflammation. And more importantly, CXCL11 was proved to act as an immune-related hub gene in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and OP in the subsequent analysis.

Subsequently, we utilized the merged transcriptomic data to delineate the alterations in immune cell abundance in the contexts of NAFLD and OP. We leveraged the ImmuCellAI algorithm and observed escalating levels of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells. T-helper cells, a specialized subtype derived from CD4+ T cells, play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of various inflammatory conditions [50]. Numerous studies have highlighted the dysregulation of T-helper cells as a central component in the development of NAFLD and OP. For example, liver fibrosis, a hallmark of NAFLD, was closely associated with Th2 and Th17 cell activation [51]. Inflammatory cytokines secreted from Th2 cells, such as IL-4 and IL-13, possessed pro-fibrogenic properties by promoting fibroblast activation and stimulating extracellular matrix remodeling [52]. Th17 cells contributed to the progress of NAFLD by producing IL-17 and IL-22, leading to enhanced production of TGF-β in the liver, subsequently increasing collagen synthesis and triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by hepatic stellate cells [53]. Th17 cells, characterized by elevated expression of RANKL and pro-inflammatory cytokine, have been implicated in bone loss associated with OP [54]. Some studies have suggested that immune mechanisms represent a significant avenue through which NAFLD and OP interact. A study focusing on NASH reported that Ly6Clow monocytes in the bone marrow express the IL-15/IL-15Rα complex and osteopontin, promoting the maturation and survival of NK cell precursors which could migrate from the bone marrow to the liver and induce a shift in recruited monocytes toward an M1-like phenotype [55]. Additionally, the bone marrow has been identified as a secondary lymphoid organ for T cells [56]. These suggest that dysregulated immune cells and cytokines could drive a pro-inflammatory polarization within the liver, bone, and even immune system, potentially exacerbating liver and bone damage.

Focusing on their shared DEGs, we identified 12 hub genes through the PPI network and cytoHubba algorithm, with 7 of them being immune-related. These include CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, HMOX1, LCN2, JUN, and S100A12, sourced from the ImmPort database’s collection of 1793 immune-related genes. Among them, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 are notable members of the CXCL family, encoding secreted proteins crucial for immune regulation and inflammatory processes, particularly in T cell trafficking [57]. Their expression levels were significantly elevated in both NAFLD and OP, suggesting their critical role in the pathogenesis of these disease conditions. Similarly, studies have consistently linked CXCL family genes to liver disease pathology, where they serve as biomarkers of liver injury and contribute to the progression of conditions like NASH as inflammatory mediators [58–61]. For instance, CXCL9 has been found at higher concentrations in the serum of chronic liver disease patients, positively correlating with the severity of liver fibrosis [62]. CXCL10 was associated with steatosis and oxidative stress through up-regulating the adipogenic factors SREBP-1c and LXR, and CXCL10 levels in serum and liver, as a potential non-invasive biomarker, were significantly higher in NASH patients [60]. The expression of CXCL11 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was also significantly increased in NASH [61]. Moreover, NAFLD was a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with studies indicating that increased CXCL9 and CXCL10 levels correlate positively with NAFLD progression to HCC in animal models [63, 64]. CXCL10 promoted macrophage M1 polarization and infiltration in NAFLD, contributing to the inflammatory environment and fibrosis accumulation [17, 18]. Conversely, knockout or blocking of CXCL10 could attenuate the infiltration of monocytes and the development of hepatitis [65].

CXCL family members also play significant roles in bone diseases. CXCL9 inhibits bone formation and stimulates bone resorption in OP, and the increased serum level of CXCL9 is associated with a higher risk of osteoporotic hip fracture [66, 67]. In the ovariectomized mice model, neutralizing CXCL9 in bone marrow mitigated bone loss by promoting bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption, highlighting the impact of CXCL9 on bone homeostasis [67]. In addition, Huang et al. proved that CXCL9 secreted by osteoblasts (OBs) was a direct counter-regulatory molecule of VEGF signaling. It could interact with the VEGF molecule and prevent VEGF from binding to its receptors on endothelial cells and OBs to suppress angiogenesis and osteogenesis, indicating the multiple mechanisms CXCL9 affects bone homeostasis [68]. CXCL10 was a previously described osteoclast-inducing factor with its neutralization leading to reduced bone resorption [69, 70]. Notably, RANKL, a key regulator of OC differentiation, could trigger the expression of CXCL10 in OC precursors, and reciprocally, CXCL10 had been shown to enhance the expression of RANKL in CD4+ T cells by sustaining activation of the ERK and Akt, further implicating its involvement in bone remodeling processes [71, 72].

The Spearman correlation analysis and PPI network investigated the correlation between immune-related hub genes and immune cells and reflected a predominantly favorable correlation between three CXCL family hub genes and Th cells. However, due to the limitations in microarray sequencing data, it is challenging to conduct cell communication exploration to clarify precise sources and targets of these cytokines. Based on existing research findings, CXCL family members, as key inflammatory drivers, could coordinate the migration, differentiation, and activation of immune cells by interacting with receptor CXCR3 [41, 42]. CXCR3 predominantly expresses on Th1 cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and natural killer (NK) cells, and CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 all could stimulate directional migration responses of these immune cells to the focal sites [73]. Zohar et al. discovered that CXCL9 and CXCL10 possess the capability to enhance the transcription of T-bet and RORγ, thereby driving the polarization of naive T cells into specialized subsets, such as Foxp3− Tr1 cells or Th17 cells via phosphorylating STAT1, STAT4, and STAT5 [74]. In the context of bone melanoma, elevated CXCL9 expression in bone marrow cells would induce the infiltration of NK cells and Th1 cells from the intestinal tract while silencing of either CXCL9 or its receptor CXCR3 could attenuate such inflammatory infiltration [75]. It was observed that CXCL10 orchestrated the recruitment of Tgd into the bone marrow and induced PD-1 expression and IL-17 production in dysregulated Tgd in both multiple myeloma murine models and patients [76]. In the liver, CXCL9 plays a crucial role in shaping the positioning of resident immune cells and recruiting circulating immune cells. Through knocking out of CXCL9, periportal enrichments of Kupffer cells and NKT cells were decreased significantly [77]. Following liver injury, CXCL9 was released to facilitate the recruitment of monocytes via CCR2 (and CCR5) and T cells via CCR5 (and CXCR3) [51]. Similarly, CXCL10 could not only recruit T cells after HCV infection but also induce the re-localization of non-parenchymal cells within the liver to specific infection sites [78]. These studies suggest that, when pro-inflammation cytokines persist, these cytokines and recruited inflammatory cells would aggravate liver and bone damage further.

Furthermore, clinical relevance analysis across transcriptomic and prospective cohorts consistently implicates CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and OP, but with distinct patterns. In liver tissue, all three chemokines increased with advancing NAFLD stage, while only CXCL9 and CXCL10 were significantly higher in NASH than steatosis, suggesting a more prominent role in fibrotic progression. In the UK Biobank data, higher baseline plasma levels of all three CXCR3 ligands predicted incident NAFLD and osteoporosis in a dose–response manner, and such a phenomenon was also observed in other cohorts [66, 79]. scRNA-seq data analysis further localized these signals predominantly to myeloid cells in both liver and bone, with additional contributions from endothelial cells in liver and mesenchymal stem cells in bone, supporting a shared myeloid-driven CXCR3 chemokine axis linking liver and bone pathology, suggesting the key role of myeloid cells in the occurrence and development of NAFLD and OP.

In the end, we opted to establish an HFD-induced mouse model to mimic the pathological phenotypes of NAFLD and OP simultaneously and to validate our bioinformatic discoveries. Consistent with previous research findings, the HFD group mice exhibited significant obesity and liver fat infiltration, reflecting the hallmarks of NAFLD, while micro-CT results confirmed the presence of bone loss and microstructure destruction similar to OP patients [80, 81]. These results indicated that the HFD-induced mouse model was a good model for studying both NAFLD and OP simultaneously. Subsequently, we observed the significantly higher proportions of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells and the increased expression of Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl11 in both livers and bones after HFD induction, further validating our bioinformatic results and demonstrating the high efficiency of the HFD-induced mouse model in simulating the key pathological mechanisms of NAFLD and OP.

Of course, there are still some shortcomings that should be discussed here. Due to sample collection and experimental reasons, only human peripheral blood monocyte sequencing data is currently available, which may limit the reliability of the results. In terms of experiment content, the cell sources of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, as well as their specific mechanisms in regulating the immune dysfunction of NAFLD and OP need further exploration. Furthermore, because the HFD model was conducted in male mice, our results primarily reflect male-specific responses. The potential sex-dependent differences in the CXCL9/10/11–Th cell axis and in NAFLD–OP comorbidity could not be addressed here and in the above data analysis. Although we identified shared alterations in Th cell subsets and CXCL family gene expression in the mouse model with NAFLD and OP, these findings did not yet allow us to determine whether this immune signature was specific to the comorbid state or could also be observed in NAFLD or OP alone. In future work, isolated NAFLD and isolated OP clinical cohorts and animal models are needed to more clearly delineate the changes in Th cell proportions and CXCL family gene expression in the context of these two diseases.

Conclusions

In summary, our study investigated the immunopathogenic background and shared underlying mechanisms of NAFLD and OP. Through our analyses, the infiltration of immune cells plays a pivotal role in NAFLD and OP, while the interaction between CXCL9/10/11 and Th cells may be a critical mechanism shared by NAFLD and OP. These discoveries not only pave the way for promising prospects in immunotherapy but also establish a foundation for delving deeper into the immune mechanisms at play in these conditions.
