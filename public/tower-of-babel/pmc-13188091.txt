Asymptomatic endorectal coil migration following hemorrhoidal artery embolization: A case report
Authors: Brayden Bagla, Amiya Bagla, Sandeep Bagla, Alex Pavidapha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hemorrhoidal artery embolization (HAE) is an increasingly utilized minimally invasive treatment for symptomatic internal hemorrhoids with a favorable safety profile. As adoption expands, rare delayed complications may emerge. We report a case of delayed asymptomatic endorectal coil migration identified 11 months following HAE. The procedure was performed via common femoral arterial access using selective microcatheterization of distal branches of the superior rectal artery. Embolization was achieved using 600-µm microspheres and multiple pushable microcoils deployed distally to the hemorrhoidal plexus. The patient experienced partial symptom recurrence requiring subsequent rubber band ligation (RBL). Surveillance colonoscopy later demonstrated partial extrusion of a metallic coil through the rectal mucosa without associated pain, bleeding, infection, or ischemia. The patient was managed conservatively with no intervention. Potential mechanisms for delayed coil migration include mucosal thinning following devascularization, chronic inflammatory remodeling, and mechanical factors related to subsequent anorectal interventions such as RBL; however, causality cannot be definitively established. This case highlights the importance of long-term follow-up and awareness of delayed device-related findings as HAE continues to evolve in clinical practice.

Introduction

Hemorrhoidal artery embolization (HAE) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for the treatment of symptomatic internal hemorrhoids, demonstrating favorable clinical outcomes and safety profiles. As utilization increases, rare and delayed complications are being recognized, necessitating careful documentation and analysis to better understand procedural risks and device behavior over time.

Coil migration following embolization procedures has been described across multiple vascular territories, typically associated with either ischemic injury or mechanical erosion. In the context of HAE, reports of coil extrusion remain limited. Understanding potential mechanisms—including tissue remodeling, ischemic effects, and interactions with subsequent interventions—is important for optimizing technique and postprocedural management. We present a case of delayed, asymptomatic endorectal coil migration identified incidentally nearly one year following HAE.

Case presentation

A patient with symptomatic grade II–III internal hemorrhoids underwent HAE using a standardized technique. Vascular access was obtained via the common femoral artery. A guiding catheter was advanced into the inferior mesenteric artery, followed by selective microcatheterization of distal branches of the superior rectal artery using a coaxial microcatheter system.

Angiography demonstrated hypervascular hemorrhoidal cushions bilaterally. Embolization was performed in two distal corpus cavernosal recti branches using 600-µm microspheres (HydroPearl, Terumo MicroVention) followed by deployment of multiple pushable microcoils (Micronester, Cook Medical). Coils were placed distally within terminal branches supplying the hemorrhoidal plexus to achieve targeted devascularization while preserving proximal collateral circulation. Postembolization angiography confirmed technical success with elimination of arterial inflow to the treated hemorrhoidal cushions.

Discussion

Endovascular coil migration is an uncommon but recognized phenomenon that has been described in multiple vascular territories. In the setting of HAE, delayed coil extrusion is rare, with limited cases reported in the literature. As HAE becomes more widely adopted, recognition of such delayed findings is important for clinical management and procedural refinement [1].

Prior reports, including that by Simões et al. [2] have described coil-related complications associated with rectosigmoid ischemia requiring surgical intervention. In that study, coil deployment extended proximally along a longer arterial segment, potentially compromising collateral perfusion. In contrast, the present case utilized a distal, selective embolization approach with formed coils, which may reduce the risk of ischemic complications. However, conclusions regarding safety advantages of distal embolization should be interpreted cautiously given the single-patient nature of this report.

Several potential mechanisms may contribute to delayed coil migration. Devascularization of hemorrhoidal tissue may result in progressive mucosal thinning, reducing structural integrity over time. Additionally, chronic inflammatory remodeling at the site of embolization may facilitate gradual erosion of embolic material through the mucosa. Mechanical factors may also play a role. The patient underwent subsequent vacuum-assisted RBL, which introduces localized suction forces and tissue ligation that could theoretically contribute to mobilization or extrusion of embolic material. However, this relationship remains speculative, and causality cannot be established based on current data.

Importantly, the absence of symptoms in this case distinguishes it from previously reported complications involving ischemia or perforation. Recognition of asymptomatic coil extrusion is clinically relevant, as it may prevent unnecessary intervention and allow for conservative management in appropriately selected patients.

Overall, this case highlights the importance of long-term follow-up after HAE and contributes to the growing understanding of delayed device-related outcomes.

Conclusion

Asymptomatic coil migration following HAE is a rare but important delayed finding. While selective distal embolization may help preserve collateral perfusion, delayed extrusion can still occur. Awareness of this phenomenon and its potential mechanisms can guide follow-up strategies and support appropriate, often conservative, management.
