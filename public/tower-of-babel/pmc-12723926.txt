Unraveling dwarfing mechanisms in Hedychium: insights into HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 functionality
Authors: Xuanguo Liang, Fang Wang, XiaoShuang Tang, Xiuping Cai, Yunyi Yu, Rangcai Yu, Yanping Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relatively tall stature of Hedychium limits its application in landscape gardening and pot cultivation while also posing challenges for crop management. Therefore, an in-depth understanding of the regulatory mechanisms controlling plant height is urgently needed to facilitate the development of suitable dwarf cultivars. ResultsIn this study, HcEXPA4, a gene that is significantly differentially expressed, was identified in the internode transcriptomes of Hedychium plants with extremely tall and dwarf phenotypes. Sequence alignment revealed that HcEXPA4 is functionally conserved with EXPA4 homologs from other species and is involved in regulating cell elongation. Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of HcEXPA4 resulted in a dwarf phenotype in Hedychium seedlings. Conversely, the overexpression of HcEXPA4 significantly promoted plant height in tobacco. Through correlation analysis and RT‒qPCR, the upstream regulatory transcription factor HcNAC140 was further identified. Silencing HcNAC140 via VIGS notably reduced plant height in Hedychium seedlings and concurrently upregulated the expression of HcEXPA4. Moreover, the overexpression of HcNAC140 in tobacco led to a significant increase in plant height. The protein interaction assays further confirmed that HcNAC140 specifically binds to the CACG core motif within the HcEXPA4 promoter, directly activating its transcription.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the pivotal role of the HcNAC140-HcEXPA4 regulatory module in controlling plant height in Hedychium, providing a theoretical foundation and potential molecular targets for the architectural optimization and molecular breeding of this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07700-6.Keywords: Hedychium, Plant height, Dwarfing, HcNAC140, HcEXPA4

Introduction

Plant height is one of the key agronomic traits that affects plant yield and quality. Exploring the regulatory mechanisms of plant height and elucidating its molecular genetic networks are crucial for improving or enhancing plant quality [1, 2]. Dwarfing is a complex biological process, and the identification and functional characterization of genes related to plant height in taller plants has become an important research topic in the life sciences. Plant dwarfing typically manifests as reduced plant height through shortened internode length or decreased internode number. In maize, ZmTE1 positively regulates internode cell elongation, thereby increasing internode length to increase plant height [3]. Rice with OsUBR7 knockout presented significantly shortened internode length, with unchanged cell length but reduced cell number. In bamboo dwarfing mutants, the primary causes of dwarfism are reduced internode number and shortened internode length [4]. Studies have shown that the simultaneous downregulation of both internode cell number and internode cell length results in a dwarf phenotype, with the vascular bundle number also decreasing compared with that of wild-type plants [5]. The plasticity of the cell wall is considered one of the key limiting factors for cell expansion in the molecular mechanisms underlying internode elongation and cell growth. During growth, plant cells must overcome the physical constraints imposed by the cell wall, a process that relies on the remodeling and loosening of their structural components. Therefore, identifying and characterizing key proteins involved in cell wall relaxation and cell elongation is essential for elucidating the molecular basis of plant dwarfism. Expansins (EXPs) are a class of cell wall-loosening proteins present in plant cells and were classified into α-expansins (EXPAs), β-expansins (EXPBs), expansin-like A (EXLA), expansin-like B (EXLB), and expansin-like X (EXLX) [6, 7]. Expansins were first discovered in cucumber in 1992, and studies have shown that proteins encoded by α-expansin genes can affect cucumber cell wall extension [8]. OsEXPBs in rice promote pollen germination and pollen tube elongation by cleaving hydrogen bonds between cellulose and hemicellulose [9]. AtEXPA7 is specifically expressed in root hairs and plays an important role in root hair elongation [10]. Multiple transgenic plant studies have demonstrated that the overexpression of EXP genes leads to cell wall elongation and increases cellulose content [11].

Transcription factors play pivotal roles in regulating the expression of genes involved in cell wall remodeling. Numerous studies have demonstrated that transcription factors not only promote cell elongation by directly modulating the transcriptional activity of cell wall-related genes but also participate in phytohormone signaling pathways to coordinate longitudinal plant growth [12–14]. In particular, specific transcription factor families, such as the NAC, MYB, and bHLH families, have been identified as central regulators of cell wall biosynthesis, cell expansion, and plant height determination. The NAC transcription factor family serves as a regulator of plant meristem development, organ elongation and separation, and patterned secondary cell wall deposition [15]. Previous studies have shown that OsNAC103 in rice negatively regulates rice height through the regulation of plant hormones [16]. OsNAC3 in rice acts as a positive regulator of seed germination, directly binding to the OsABA8ox1 and OsEXP4 promoters to influence embryonic cell elongation [17]. Research in poplar revealed that PagNAC2a, which is specifically expressed in the phloem region, participates in regulating phloem fiber differentiation and that overexpression of PagNAC2a can increase plant height, stem diameter, and leaf area [18]. These studies highlight the pivotal role of NAC transcription factors as key mediators between phytohormone signaling and cell wall remodeling, effectively coordinating cell expansion and plant morphogenesis. Despite significant progress, the precise molecular mechanisms by which NAC transcription factors regulate dynamic cell wall modifications and contribute to plant height formation in Hedychium remain largely unresolved.

Hedychium is a genus of perennial herbaceous flowering plants in the Zingiberaceae family that are native to tropical and subtropical regions. Owing to the typically tall stature of Hedychium plants, their large size, to some extent, limits their use in courtyard landscaping, potted cultivation, and protected horticulture. Therefore, research on the regulatory mechanisms of plant height in Hedychium is highly important. Our previous studies demonstrated that internode cell shortening in Hedychium plants is the primary cause of internode shortening, ultimately resulting in phenotypic differences in Hedychium plant height [19]. Based on RNA-Seq analysis, we hypothesize that HcNAC140 acts as a key regulator of plant height in Hedychium by directly modulating the expression of the cell wall loosening gene HcEXPA4, thereby influencing cell elongation. This study aims to fill the knowledge gap by systematically testing this hypothesis and elucidating the specific regulatory roles of the HcNAC140-HcEXPA4 module. By comparing the cell structure, physiological characteristics, and gene expression profiles of the internode tissues of Hedychium varieties at different heights, we identified key biophysical and molecular events associated with dwarfism. Unlike known NAC-EXPA modules in other species, HcNAC140 acts as a positive regulator of internode elongation in Hedychium: it binds a specific motif in the HcEXPA4 promoter to activate its expression, which in turn drives internode cell elongation. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the regulatory network governing plant height in Hedychium but also provide a theoretical foundation and technical support for the molecular breeding and development of dwarf cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

DW1, DW2, and DW3 were the three shortest plants from the F1 hybrid population of Hedychium coranarium ‘BT’ × H. coranarium ‘Jin’ (JJH), which exhibited extreme phenotypic differences in height, whereas T1, T2, and T3 were the three tallest plants from the same hybrid population. GB represents the dwarf material that is not related to the population of plants, whereas JJH refers to the tall parental material of the population of plants. All experimental Hedychium materials were grown under natural light conditions at the Floriculture Research Center of South China Agricultural University, Guangzhou, China (23.16°N, 113.36°E). The H. coronarium seedlings and the tobacco plants (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Wisconsin 38) were obtained from the same facility. The plants were grown in a greenhouse under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod at 26 ± 2 °C and 60% relative humidity.

Plant height and cell length measurements

Plant height measurements were taken as the vertical distance (cm) from the base of the aboveground stem (ground contact point) to the top of the inflorescence (when reproductive structures were present) or to the tip of the vegetative leaves (in the absence of inflorescence). For tobacco plants, the third internode was selected, whereas for Hedychium seedlings, the stem growing point was chosen as the site for longitudinal stem sectioning observation. Internode cell observation was conducted via the nail polish peel method [20], with appropriate adjustments made according to actual conditions: the internodes of Hedychium and tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Wisconsin 38) were separated, and a 5–7 mm region from the upper end of the internode was excised and longitudinally sectioned into two equal parts. Colorless transparent nail polish was evenly applied to the inner surface of each section and allowed to air dry, and then the outer nail polish film was carefully peeled off and placed on a glass slide preloaded with a drop of distilled water. The coverslip was gently pressed, and excess water was absorbed, followed by microscopic observation. Images were acquired using a Axio Scope.A1 (Carl Zeiss) microscope with a 40× objective lens. Scale bars are included in all figures. Measurements of cell length were performed on ten randomly selected cells from the median region of the fifth internode of each plant. Cell measurements were performed via ImageJ software [21], with 10 biological replicates, and measurements were recorded in micrometers (µm). For clarity of presentation, cell observation images were processed with binarization and other treatments.

RNA-Seq

The RNA-seq experiments were performed with three biological replicates for each experimental group to ensure reliability and statistical significance. The sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, achieving a depth of 50 M reads per sample to ensure comprehensive transcriptome coverage. The data were normalized using Transcripts Per Million (TPM) to account for differences in sequencing depth across samples. The methodologies for RNA-seq assembly, alignment, and data quality control have been described in our prior research.

PCR and real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)

All the PCR primers used for DNA cloning and vector construction are listed in Table S1. The real-time quantitative PCR primers for the target genes were designed via Primer Premier 5.0 software. All the primers used are listed in Table S2. Initially, primers were designed according to quantitative PCR primer design principles via Primer Premier 5.0. Subsequently, quantitative PCR was performed to detect potential mismatches, primer dimers, and amplification efficiency, from which the optimal primers were selected. RT‒qPCR was conducted via SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China) with the following program: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s for amplification. Each RT‒qPCR sample was analyzed in triplicate, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene and ddH2O as the negative control. Data analysis was performed via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method following the completion of the reaction.

Tobacco overexpression assay

The full-length sequences of HcEXPA4 and HcNAC140 were ligated into the pCAMBIA1300 vector to construct recombinant plasmids. The recombinant plasmids were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, with the empty vector serving as a control. Gene overexpression experiments in tobacco were conducted via the tobacco leaf disc coculture method [22], with appropriate modifications to the experimental procedure on the basis of actual conditions. Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Wisconsin 38) seeds were sterilized in a laminar flow hood by sequential washing 3‒5 times with 75% anhydrous ethanol, 1% sodium hypochlorite, and sterile water and then sown on MS media. Seeds were germinated at 25–26 °C with a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod. When the seedlings developed 4–5 true leaves, mature leaves from sterile tobacco plantlets were excised, and the leaf margins were removed and cut into 0.5–1.0 cm fragments. These leaf segments were immersed in MS liquid medium containing suspended Agrobacterium GV3101 cells prepared for transformation and cocultured in a shaking incubator at 28 °C. The leaf segments were removed and blotted dry on sterile filter paper to remove the bacterial suspension, placed with the upper surface in contact with the medium and inoculated onto coculture media for cocultivation under dark conditions for 48–72 h. When white Agrobacterium colonies appeared at the edges where the leaf segments contacted the medium, the leaf segments were transferred to hormone-supplemented antibacterial differentiation medium and subcultured every two weeks. When the callus tissue produced green shoots, the shoots were excised and transferred to rooting medium containing antibiotics (supplemented with hygromycin, 25 mg/L) corresponding to the vector for selection culture. Shoots that successfully rooted in the rooting medium were excised and reinserted into rooting medium for continued selection under identical culture conditions. Tobacco plantlets capable of root formation were selected and transplanted to soil for growth. Following root development, DNA was extracted, and PCR detection was performed, with positive plants retained for continued growth. Once the transgenic plants exhibited normal growth, morphological changes were observed, the plant height was recorded, and photographic documentation was obtained. Three overexpression groups were established for each gene, with each overexpression group containing three plants grown from the same callus tissue, resulting in a total of 9 plants used for each gene.

Virus-Induced gene Silencing (VIGS) assay

The TRV1 and pTRV2 vectors were used as virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) vectors [23]. Adapter primer sequences were designed to target specific regions at the 5’ or 3’ termini of the full-length sequences of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4, with amplified fragments of approximately 200–400 bp. The target fragments were ligated into the pTRV2 vector to construct transient viral silencing vectors. The recombinant products were subsequently transformed into DH5α, and positive bacterial cultures were subjected to commercial sequencing. The pTRV2-HcNAC140 and pTRV2-HcEXPA4 plasmids were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105, along with the pTRV1 plasmid. Single positive colonies were cultured until the bacterial suspension reached an OD600 of 0.5–0.6, resuspended in MES buffer to achieve an OD600 of approximately 0.7 and cultured at 28 °C for at least 3 h. Agrobacterium suspensions containing pTRV1 and pTRV2-HcNAC140 or pTRV2-HcEXPA4 were mixed in equal volumes and incubated at 28 °C for at least 0.5 h. Hedychium seedlings were inverted and immersed in the bacterial suspension, followed by vacuum infiltration at 0.4–0.6 kPa for 5 min until the stems and leaves appeared dark green (indicating bacterial infiltration). After the surface bacterial suspension was removed, the seedlings were maintained in an incubator at approximately 25 °C for 22 days. The morphological characteristics of the Hedychium seedlings were observed, and plant height and cell length were measured. RNA was extracted from internode regions following silencing, and quantitative fluorescence PCR was used to detect the relative expression levels of target genes and related genes, with HcGAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. Hedychium seedlings with consistent growth statuses (plant heights of approximately 8–10 cm) were selected for the experiments. Three silencing groups were established for each gene, with each silencing group containing three plants, resulting in a total of 9 plants were used for each gene.

Subcellular localization assay

The full-length sequences of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 were amplified with stop codons removed and ligated into the subcellular localization vector p35S-GFP-pGreen. The constructs were subsequently transformed into DH5α competent cells, and plasmids from positive bacterial cultures with correct sequencing results were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Empty vector plasmids were simultaneously transformed as controls for infiltration of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The nuclear localization fluorescent signal vector RFP-NLS served as a nuclear localization reference, whereas the Arabidopsis cell wall marker protein AtPGIP2-mCherry (35 S-AtPGIP2-mCherry) served as a cell wall localization reference [24]. Successfully detected Agrobacterium cultures were expanded to an OD600 of approximately 1.0 and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 8 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in MES buffer to an OD600 of 0.4, and the suspension was incubated in a 28 °C incubator for 2–3 h. Tobacco plants to be infiltrated were preplaced under white light for 1–2 h to promote stomatal opening. Using a 1 mL syringe (without a needle), the resuspended solution was gently injected into the lower epidermis of tobacco leaves with appropriate marking. Following continued cultivation under low light conditions for 2–3 days, observations were conducted via fluorescence microscopy, and appropriate fields of view were photographed. Imaging was performed on an Axio Scope.A1 (Carl Zeiss) microscope with GFP/RFP filters at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/507 nm and 587/610 nm, respectively. Images were acquired with a 10× objective lens. Scale bars are included in all figures.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

A segmental truncation strategy was employed to divide the HcEXPA4 promoter region (−2184 bp to the transcription start site) into three independent fragments (P1, P2, P3), with lengths of P1 (−2184 to −1215 bp), P2 (−1035 to −539 bp), and P3 (−559 to −1 bp), respectively. On the basis of bioinformatics predictions, each fragment contained at least one conserved NAC family protein binding motif, CACG (P1: 1 site; P2: 1 site; P3: 2 sites). Using the yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) experimental system, the HcNAC140 coding sequence and each promoter fragment were cloned and inserted into pGADT7 and pAbAi vectors, respectively, and their interaction capacity was verified on SD-Leu-deficient media.

Dual-luciferase assay

The full-length HcNAC140 sequence with the stop codon removed was ligated into the pGreenⅡ62-SK vector, while the HcEXPA4 promoter sequence was ligated into the 0800-LUC/REN vector to construct recombinant plasmids. The recombinant plasmids were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (psoup-p19), with empty vectors serving as controls. Single colonies were selected and cultured in YEP + Kan + Rif liquid media at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 9–12 h. PCR detection was performed on the bacterial cultures, and positive Agrobacterium cultures were expanded overnight to an OD600 of approximately 1.0. Following centrifugation, the pellets were resuspended in MES buffer to an OD600 of approximately 0.4 and incubated at room temperature for 2–3 h to activate the Agrobacterium. The activated bacterial cultures were mixed at a volume ratio of HcNAC140:HcEXPA4 promoter = 7:1 for tobacco infiltration. A dual-luciferase reporter gene detection kit (Beyotime, China) was used to detect LUC and REN fluorescence values via a Thermo Scientific Luminoskan Ascent instrument. The data were entered into Excel to calculate the relative LUC/REN ratio.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The full-length HcNAC140 gene sequence (with the stop codon removed) was cloned and inserted into the pETMALc-H vector (with an MBP tag) and transformed into DH5α E. coli competent cells. Positive bacterial cultures were submitted for commercial sequencing and plasmid extraction. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli competent Rosetta (DE3) cells, with the pETMALc-H plasmid serving as a negative control. Single-strand upstream and downstream primers were synthesized for sequences flanking the target binding sites, along with mutant probes. Labeled probes, unlabeled cold probes, and unlabeled mutant probes were mixed with upstream and downstream primers at a 1:1 ratio for double-strand probe synthesis in a PCR instrument. The EMSA products were separated via electrophoresis on 6% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gels in TBE buffer. The EMSA binding reaction system is shown in Table S3. The results were visualized and processed via a MINICHEMI500 chemiluminescent/fluorescence imaging system (chemiluminescentMAGINGSYstem).

Statistical analysis

The Hedychium mature plant height, Hedychium seedling height, cell length, and gene expression data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). All the experimental datasets were compiled via Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and subsequently analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Correlation analysis was conducted via the Pearson correlation coefficient method. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed via Student’s t test. For multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was employed to determine the statistical significance of the observed differences.

Results

Differences in plant height and internodal cell length among various Hedychium accessions

Our preliminary phenotypic analysis of plant height in the population indicated that the extreme height variation observed in this F1 population resulted primarily from a significant reduction in the length of internodal cells in the dwarf plants. This reduction leads to shortened internodes, ultimately resulting in the overall dwarfing of the plants [19]. To identify candidate genes associated with the dwarfing trait in ginger, we selected six representative individuals from the F1 hybrid population, comprising three of the tallest plants (T1–T3) and three of the shortest dwarf plants (DW1–DW3), for analysis. These samples were used for transcriptomic sequencing of the internodal meristems. Additionally, to increase the credibility of subsequent differential gene expression analyses, we introduced nonrelated dwarf material (GB) and tall parental material (JJH) from the F1 population. In total, eight plant samples were analyzed, and the structure of the internodal cells in the fifth internode of each plant was observed under a microscope (Fig. 1A).

As shown in Fig. 1B, there was a significant difference in plant height between the dwarf and tall plants, with average heights for GB-DW3 being 80.3 cm, 74.8 cm, 72.2 cm, and 74.1 cm, whereas those for T1-JJH averaged 142.9 cm, 148.0 cm, 149.4 cm, and 139.1 cm. We statistically analyzed the lengths of the fifth internodal cells; GB-DW3 had lengths of 33.6 μm, 34.4 μm, 28.5 μm, and 31.8 μm, whereas T1-JJH had lengths of 60.3 μm, 58.8 μm, 63.0 μm, and 55.8 μm. The significantly shorter internodal cell lengths in the dwarf-type plants than in the tall-type plants provide evidence that dwarfing is associated with a reduction in cell elongation ability.

HcEXPA4 positively regulates cell elongation

With the criteria of a |log2(fold change)| ≥ 2 and a p value ≤ 0.05, a total of 491 DEGs were identified from the transcriptomic analysis, with 179 genes being upregulated and 312 genes being downregulated. Further filtering excluded genes lacking descriptive information and those with high intragroup variability, resulting in a final set of 269 significantly differentially expressed genes with high confidence. A clustering heatmap of these upregulated and downregulated genes is provided in Fig. S1. Notably, within this confidence range, we observed a particularly significant gene affecting cell wall extension, HcEXPA4, which is highlighted in the volcano plot (Fig. 2A).

We constructed a phylogenetic tree via the neighbor‒joining method to analyze HcEXPA4 (H. coronarium) alongside 21 representative EXPANSIN members from various plant species, including AtEXPA4 (Arabidopsis thaliana). The results revealed that the 21 EXPANSIN members could be classified into four distinct subfamilies (Fig. S2). HcEXPA4 and CmEXPA4 share a close phylogenetic relationship, forming a distinct clade. Previous studies have demonstrated that the silencing of CmEXPA4 inhibits normal root development in chrysanthemum by regulating root length [25], suggesting that HcEXPA4 may possess similar functional properties. We selected EXPA family members from eight representative plant species, AtEXPA4 (Arabidopsis thaliana) and OsEXPA4 (Oryza sativa L.), for global alignment (Fig. 2B). Comparative analysis revealed that HcEXPA4 shares a common DPBB domain with other EXPA members, along with a pollen allergen domain at the C-terminus. Notably, we identified an α-insertion region with a core sequence WCNP (Trp-Cys-Asn-Pro) that is completely conserved across the EXPA subfamily. These structural features support the unique functional differentiation of the EXPA subfamily in primary cell wall expansion.

To further analyze the functions of HcEXPA4 in regulating plant height in Hedychium, we silenced HcEXPA4 in Hedychium seedlings using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology. The heights of the three HcEXPA4-silenced groups were 68.8%, 61.9%, and 69.3% of those of the control control group (TRV-00), respectively (Fig. 2C). Further observation of cells through longitudinal sectioning of internode meristematic tissues revealed that the internode cell lengths of the three HcEXPA4-silenced groups were 56.5%, 52.8%, and 52.4% of those of the control group (TRV-00), respectively (Fig. 2D). Following transient silencing of HcEXPA4, the relative expression level of HcEXPA4 significantly decreased in the three silenced groups, with reductions of 54.5%, 63.6%, and 47.0% compared with those in the control group (Fig. 2E). The coding sequences of HcEXPA4 were subsequently cloned and inserted into the plant overexpression vector pCAMBIA1300 and subsequently introduced into tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Wisconsin 38) through Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation. Compared with those of the WT, the heights of the three HcEXPA4 overexpression groups (OE-E-1/2/3) were significantly greater, with increases of 65.5%, 102.9%, and 94.7%, respectively (Fig. 2F). Further observation of internode cells through longitudinal sectioning of tobacco stems revealed that the cell lengths of the three HcEXPA4-overexpressing groups (OE-E-1/2/3) were 126.8%, 135.1%, and 126.4% of those of the control plants, respectively (Fig. 2G), representing significant increases compared with those of the WT. These findings suggest that HcEXPA4 plays important roles in promoting cell elongation and regulating plant height.

HcNAC140 is highly correlated with HcEXPA4 expression and significantly influences cell elongation

To investigate the regulatory network involving HcEXPA4 with respect to plant height, we aimed to trace the upstream regulatory factors of HcEXPA4. Previous studies have indicated that NAC transcription factors can bind to the promoters of EXPANSIN genes and activate their expression. On the basis of this regulatory mechanism, we identified seven NAC family genes from the filtered differentially expressed genes, and their expression heatmap in relation to HcEXPA4 is shown in Fig. 3A. Further analysis of expression correlation revealed that three of these NAC genes (HcNAC126, HcNAC140, and HcNAC141) were strongly correlated (|r| ≥ 0.8) with HcEXPA4 expression in both tall and dwarf plants (Fig. 3B). RT‒qPCR analysis was conducted on the internodal meristems of these eight plants to validate the expression patterns of these candidate genes (Fig. 3C). The results indicated that the expression trend of HcNAC140 significantly differed between tall and dwarf plants, aligning most closely with our initial hypothesis. These findings suggest that HcNAC140 may act as an upstream regulatory factor of HcEXPA4, participating in the regulation of internodal elongation.

We utilized the BLAST tool from the NCBI database to screen for HcNAC140 homologous proteins from other species and constructed a phylogenetic tree via the neighbor-joining method (Fig. S3). The results demonstrated that HcNAC140 clustered with AtNAC45 (Arabidopsis thaliana) and other members within a single subfamily, indicating high homology. These genes belong to the NAP (NAC-like, activated by AP3/PI) family. Previous studies have shown that AtNAC45 is associated with auxin synthesis and root growth [26], suggesting that the homologous HcNAC140 may also perform growth regulatory functions. NAC family proteins contain a highly conserved NAM domain in their N-terminal region, whereas the variable domain at the C-terminus serves a transcriptional activation function [27]. We conducted motif analysis on protein sequences within the phylogenetic tree and performed conserved domain (CD) analysis based on the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (Figs. S4-5). The results revealed that all proteins possessed the conserved NAM domain, whereas all proteins except AcNAC86 (Ananas comosus), including HcNAC140, simultaneously contained motif 1, motif 2, motif 3, and motif 4, indicating high functional conservation during evolutionary processes.

To further analyze the functions of HcNAC140 in regulating plant height in Hedychium, we silenced HcNAC140 in Hedychium seedlings using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology. The heights of the three HcNAC140-silenced groups were 58.3%, 53.9%, and 57.0% of those of the control groups (TRV-00), respectively (Fig. D). Further observation of cells through longitudinal sectioning of internode meristematic tissues revealed that the internode cell lengths of the three HcNAC140-silenced groups were 56.2%, 51.1%, and 58.5% of those of the control groups (TRV-00), respectively (Fig. 3E). After transient silencing of HcNAC140, the relative expression levels of HcNAC140 decreased by 55.9%, 54.6%, and 48.4%, respectively, whereas the relative expression level of HcEXPA4 decreased by 41.9%, 36.3%, and 44.6%, respectively (Fig. 3F). The coding sequences were subsequently cloned and inserted into the plant overexpression vector pCAMBIA1300 and subsequently introduced into tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Wisconsin 38) via Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation. After 120 days of cultivation following successful transformation of HcNAC140 into tobacco, the plant heights of three HcNAC140 overexpression groups (OE-N-1/2/3) were significantly greater than those of the wild type (WT), with increases of 91.3%, 88.7%, and 69.6%, respectively, compared with those of the WT (Fig. 3G). Further observation of internode cells through longitudinal sectioning of tobacco stems revealed that the cell lengths of the three HcNAC140 transgenic groups were 131.4%, 132.9%, and 131.6% of those of the control plants, respectively (Fig. 3H). These results demonstrate that HcNAC140 plays important roles in promoting cell elongation and regulating plant height.

Subcellular Localization of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 and gene expression analysis of the internodes of tall and dwarf Hedychium

To determine the subcellular localization of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4, we constructed 35 S-HcNAC140-GFP and 35 S-HcEXPA4-GFP subcellular localization vectors. The subcellular localization results revealed (Fig. 4A) that cells transformed with 35 S-HcNAC140-GFP displayed green fluorescence exclusively in the nucleus, indicating that HcNAC140 is localized to the nucleus. The green fluorescence signal of 35 S-HcEXPA4-GFP completely overlapped with the red fluorescence signal of the cell wall localization marker, demonstrating that HcEXPA4 is localized to the cell wall.

Furthermore, we systematically examined the spatiotemporal expression patterns of these genes across developmental stages by analyzing their transcript abundance in distinct internodes at multiple leaf stages (Fig. 4B). Statistical evaluation revealed significant differential expression between the tall and dwarf lines for both genes at nearly all the examined developmental stages (P < 0.05), with the sole exception of HcEXPA4 expression in the eighth internode at the sixth leaf stage, which did not reach statistical significance. Notably, maximal transcriptional upregulation of both genes in tall plants was observed in fifth internode tissues at the eighth leaf stage, which presented the most substantial fold-change difference compared with dwarf plants.

HcNAC140 directly actives the expression of HcEXPA4

To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of the HcNAC140 transcription factor on the HcEXPA4 promoter, we employed a segmental truncation strategy to divide the HcEXPA4 promoter region into three independent fragments (P1, P2, P3). The interaction capability was verified through a yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay system. The results showed that only the cotransformed yeast strain carrying the P1 fragment and HcNAC140 exhibited significant growth activity on 150 ng/mL AbA selective medium, indicating specific binding between the P1 fragment and HcNAC140 (Fig. 5A). Concurrently, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter gene assays to test the transcriptional activation activity of HcNAC140 on the HcEXPA4 promoter. A schematic diagram of the dual-luciferase reporter vector constructed for this assay is shown in Fig. 5B. As demonstrated in Fig. 5C, the normalized LUC/REN activity ratio of HcNAC140-pGreenⅡ62-SK was 0.089 ± 0.012, which was significantly greater than that of the control group (0.004 ± 0.001), with an activation fold of 22.847, demonstrating that HcNAC140 can specifically activate the transcriptional activity of the HcEXPA4 promoter (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, our electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) results revealed that when the HcNAC140 protein was incubated with biotin-labeled HcEXPA4 promoter probes, DNA‒protein complex signal bands appeared, with retarded band signals gradually weakening in a stepwise manner, confirming the sequence-specific nature of the binding (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

The height of Hedychium plants is determined primarily by internode length or the number of internodes, with internode length being dependent mainly on the length of internode cells or the number of internode cells. Currently, the dwarfing mechanisms of cereal crops such as rice and maize, as well as horticultural crops such as cucumber and tomato, have been extensively studied, establishing relatively comprehensive dwarfing research systems [28–33]. However, little is known about the plant height regulatory mechanisms of ornamental flowers, particularly those belonging to the genus Hedychium.

HcEXPA4, a member of the EXPANSIN family, functions closely related to cell wall loosening and cell elongation, suggesting its crucial role in cell elongation. Lower levels of HcEXPA4 may restrict cell wall loosening, thereby inhibiting cell elongation [34] and ultimately resulting in the dwarfing of Hedychium. In this study, systematic analysis of transcriptome sequencing data led to the identification of a significantly highly expressed EXPANSIN family gene, HcEXPA4. On the basis of its expression pattern, we further employed a correlation analysis strategy to infer its potential upstream transcriptional regulator, HcNAC140. This investigative approach represents an exploratory and innovative attempt. Transient silencing of either HcEXPA4 or HcNAC140 through VIGS resulted in a dwarfed phenotype in Hedychium seedlings with shortened cell length, whereas tobacco genetic transformation experiments revealed that overexpression of HcEXPA4 or HcNAC140 promoted cell elongation and significantly increased tobacco plant height. This phenotype is highly consistent with the function of EXPANSINs, which drive longitudinal cell elongation by disrupting hydrogen bonds between cellulose microfibrils and hemicelluloses in the cell wall, thereby promoting cell wall extension [35].

Moreover, through transcriptome analysis and RT‒qPCR validation, we found that the expression of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 was significantly downregulated in dwarf Hedychium plants, with their expression levels positively correlated with plant height and longitudinal elongation. Furthermore, molecular interaction experiments, including dual-luciferase assays, yeast one-hybrid assays, and EMSAs, confirmed that HcNAC140 activates HcEXPA4 expression by specifically binding to the CACG core motif in the HcEXPA4 promoter region. This finding is consistent with the functional characteristics of NAC family transcription factors, as previous studies have demonstrated that NAC proteins can participate in the regulation of cell wall remodeling by binding to CATGTG or CACG-type cis-acting elements in target gene promoters [36]. While the regulatory logic of NAC transcription factors directly controlling EXPANSINs is emerging across species, the functional outcomes are highly context dependent. In rice, OsNAC3 regulates the expression of its target gene, OsEXPA4, by binding to its promoter, thereby affecting cell elongation [17], whereas in rose, the RhNAC2–RhEXPA4 axis confers dehydration tolerance during petal expansion, a response critical for cut flower quality [37]. Similarly, in Arabidopsis, the NAC25/NAC1L–EXPA2 cascade, integrated within the GA/DELLA signaling pathway, specifically controls endosperm cell expansion to facilitate the seed-to-seedling transition [38]. This contrasts with the role of the MdNAC1–MdEXP-A1 module in apple, which primarily expedites fruit softening [39]. In addition to interacting with the EXPA subfamily, studies in maize revealed that ZmNAC11 and ZmNAC29, can activate ZmEXPB15 expression, promoting cell expansion and positively regulating maize grain size and weight [40]. Our study positions the HcNAC140–HcEXPA4 module within this spectrum as a key regulator of internode elongation in Hedychium, a process central to plant architecture in ornamental species. Mechanistically, the identification of the CACG motif as the binding site for HcNAC140 provides a specific molecular detail that distinguishes it from the binding sites reported for other NAC-EXPA pairs, such as those in rose and apple. This suggests that while the overarching theme of NAC-EXPA interaction is conserved, the precise cis-element recognition and the upstream signals fine-tune the module’s function to suit specific developmental or environmental contexts. Therefore, this work not only elucidates a key mechanism controlling plant height in Hedychium but also contributes to the broader understanding of how a core growth-regulatory module is adapted to govern diverse physiological processes across the plant kingdom.

In conclusion, we validated the expression patterns of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 in various tall and short varieties of Hedychium. Upon transient silencing of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 in Hedychium seedlings, we observed a dwarf phenotype, whereas overexpression of these genes in tobacco plants resulted in increased plant height, confirming their functional roles. Interaction assays demonstrated that HcNAC140 directly activates the expression of HcEXPA4. Our findings reveal the crucial roles of HcNAC140 and HcEXPA4 in regulating plant height in Hedychium, providing the first elucidation of the “NAC-EXPA” regulatory mechanism in this species and offering new targets for improving plant architecture in Hedychium.
