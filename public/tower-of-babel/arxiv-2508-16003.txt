Multiscale Analysis of a Kinetic Model of Confined Suspensions of Self-Propelled Rods
Leonid Berlyand, Spencer Dang, Pierre-Emmanuel Jabin, Mykhailo Potomkin

Abstract
. The behavior of active matter under confinement poses significant challenges due to the intricate coupling between dynamics near boundaries and those in the bulk. A defining feature of active matter systems is that a substantial portion of their dynamics takes place near confining boundaries. In our previous work, we developed a kinetic framework that enables direct computation of the probability distribution functions for both the position and orientation of active rods. A distinguishing aspect of this approach is its explicit treatment of wall accumulation through the use of two coupled probability distribution functions: one describing the bulk population and the other representing rods accumulated at the boundary. Another novel feature is the structure of the governing equation, which is degenerate: it is second-order in one non-temporal variable and first-order in another. The main focus of this paper is to rigorously justify this model via multi-scale analysis. We first establish well-posedness of the system and then employ two distinct multi-scale derivations to obtain the model as a singular limit of a more classical kinetic system in the regime of vanishing translational diffusion. For analytical clarity, we consider the case in which active rods, once accumulated at the wall, remain permanently confined there. This work provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for reduced kinetic models of confined active matter, bridging microscopic dynamics and macroscopic accumulation phenomena.



Keywords
: Multi-scale analysis; Confined active particles; Kinetic Theory.

Introduction

The concept of a self-propelled rod, also called an active rod, was successfully applied to study dynamics of a wide variety of microswimmers, ranging from motile rod-shaped bacteria

The overall transport properties of dilute active microswimmers through microfluidic channels or microscale biological conduits are typically governed by the dynamics of the accumulated population
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Therefore, an accurate model that captures both microswimmer accumulation and the behavior of the accumulated population is essential for various biological and biomimetic applications.

An active rod is a thin segment with a front and rear, capable of autonomous motion toward its front. A basic model for an individual active rod swimming in a planar domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is governed by equations for the rod’s center location
\boldsymbol{r}(t)
and orientation angle
\varphi(t)
, defined as the counterclockwise angle between a line parallel to the rod’s orientation and
x
-axis.
If expressions for its translational velocity
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{p})
and angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{p})
are given, then the stochastic differential equations governing the active rod’s dynamics are:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\text{d}t+\sqrt{2D_{\text{tr}}}\,\text{d}W_{\boldsymbol{r}},\\
\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\text{d}t+\sqrt{2D_{\text{rot}}}\,\text{d}W_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}.\end{cases}

Here,
D_{\text{tr}}
and
D_{\text{rot}}
are translational and orientational diffusion coefficients, respectively, and
W_{\boldsymbol{r}},W_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
are Wiener processes. An
example for
\boldsymbol{V}
and
\Phi
is
[
16
,
15
]

(1.2)

\boldsymbol{V}=V_{\text{prop}}\boldsymbol{p}+\boldsymbol{u}_{BG}+\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{ext}}\text{ and }\Phi=[\text{I}-\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{T}}]\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{BG}}\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{p}=(\cos(\varphi),\sin(\varphi))^{\text{T}}
is the rod’s orientation vector,
V_{\text{prop}}
is the self-propulsion speed,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{BG}}
is the background flow, and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{ext}}
stands for an external force. If no external force is applied,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{ext}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, then the velocity of the active rod relative to the background flow is the propulsion velocity:
\boldsymbol{V}-\boldsymbol{u}_{BG}=V_{\text{prop}}\boldsymbol{p}
.

Due to randomness coming from translational and rotational diffusion as well as uncertainty in initial data, it is convenient to translate the system (
1.1
) into the Fokker-Planck equation for the probability distribution function
f(t,\boldsymbol{r},\varphi)
:

(1.3)

\partial_{t}f+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi f)-D_{\text{rot}}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}f+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{V}f)-D_{\text{tr}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{r}}f=0.

This equation for the distribution of the locations and orientations of rod-like particles resembles the Smoluchowski equation
[
17
,
18
]
for passive rods and has been used to analyze both dilute and semi-dilute active suspensions
[
1
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

Translational diffusion in microswimmer’s dynamics is typically negligible. To illustrate this using the Fokker-Planck equation (
1.3
), introduce non-dimensional variables
t=\hat{t}T
and
\boldsymbol{r}=\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}L
where
T=D_{\text{rot}}^{-1}
and
L
is the length associated with the domain of active rod’s swimming
\Omega
. For example, if
\Omega
is bounded, then
L=\text{diam}(\Omega)
, and if
\Omega
is a slab of thickness
H

\Omega=\{(x,y)|0<y<H\}
, then

. After rescaling, we get the non-dimensional Fokker-Planck equation:

(1.4)

\partial_{\hat{t}}f_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\hat{\Phi}f_{\varepsilon})-\partial_{\varphi}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}\cdot(\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}f_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\Delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}f_{\varepsilon}=0.

Here,
\hat{\Phi}=\Phi D^{-1}_{\text{rot}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}=\boldsymbol{V}/(LD_{\text{rot}})
, and

(1.5)

\varepsilon=\dfrac{D_{\text{tr}}}{D_{\text{rot}}L^{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\underbrace{\dfrac{D_{\text{tr}}}{D_{\text{rot}}r^{2}}}_{\sim 1}\cdot\underbrace{\dfrac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}}_{\ll 1}}.

Here,

is the linear size of the microswimmer. For various types of microswimmers, the coefficient
\varepsilon
is small, as indicated in (
1.5
), and thus the translational diffusion term is typically neglected in the modeling of active rod trajectories by the system (
1.1
),
e.g.
in Refs.
[
16
,
6
]
. For example, for a flagellated bacterium, values of translation diffusion may attain
D_{\text{tr}}=2\times 10^{3}\mu\text{m}^{2}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}

[

]
whereas a representative value for the rotational diffusion coefficient is
D_{\text{rot}}=3.0\,\text{rad}^{2}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}

[
26
]
. For these values and
L=1\,\text{mm}
, the geometric scale is
\varepsilon=6.6\times 10^{-4}\ll 1
.

This work focuses on the limit
\varepsilon\to 0

Equation (

(1.6)

(\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}f_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}}f_{\varepsilon})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in\partial\Omega,\,-\pi<\varphi\leq\pi.

The long-time behavior of individual active Brownian particle transport in confined flows based on the system of the kinetic equation (

The boundary condition (

of the distribution function
f_{\varepsilon}
singular in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0

\partial\Omega

) (see, e.g. Refs.
[

) and experiments with biological microswimmers
[

,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

It was conjectured in Ref.
[
31
]
, based on the formal multiscale asymptotic expansions, that the probability distribution function admits the following representation in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
:

(1.7)

f\to\rho_{\text{bulk}}+\delta_{\partial\Omega}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}})\rho_{\text{wall}},

where
\rho_{\text{bulk}}
and
\rho_{\text{wall}}
are distributions of rods that swim freely in the bulk and rods that are accumulated on the wall
\partial\Omega
, respectively. The term
\delta_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the
\delta
-function along
\partial\Omega
. The distributions
\rho_{\text{bulk}}
and
\rho_{\text{wall}}
satisfy a coupled system of Fokker-Planck equations. The main advantage of the system for
\rho_{\text{bulk}}
and
\rho_{\text{wall}}
is that it explicitly distinguishes accumulated rods and it does not have large variations of density values near the wall. A similar model of coupled in-bulk and wall distributions for active Brownian particles without background flow was formulated in Ref.
[
27
]
and their large-time behavior was analyzed.

The primary contribution of this work is the rigorous justification of the coupled bulk-boundary dynamics in a kinetic model of active rods in the singular limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. The key challenge in proving convergence is the singular limit; naive asymptotic expansions of bulk-boundary dynamics leads to an ill-posed system. Instead, a two-scale matched asymptotic expansion was used along with a matching condition which captures the accumulation of rods on the wall (e.g., bordertaxis) to rigorously derive the limiting system (
2.3
).

To this end, we start with the classical matching asymptotic expansion method
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
resulting in the formal series accounting for the at-wall and away-from-wall (so called inner and outer solutions, respectively, with an appropriate matching condition). From the composite expansion
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
, rigorous
L^{1}
weak convergence
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}\to f=\rho_{\text{bulk}}+\delta_{\partial\Omega}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}})\rho_{\text{wall}}
is proven.
Our result requires establishing the well-posedness of the limiting system. The main challenge lies in the fact that the limiting system is degenerate
[
35
]
: it takes the form

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{A}\mathcal{U}=0,

where the differential operator
\mathcal{A}
is of second order with respect to the orientational variable and first order with respect to the spatial variable. This mixed structure complicates the application of standard powerful tools, such as semigroup theory.
To address this issue, we approximate the operator
\mathcal{A}
with a non-degenerate version and derive the necessary
a priori
bounds to establish the well-posedness of the limiting problem.

Finally, we consider an alternative method for deriving the limiting system based on an orthogonal decomposition ansatz for the pre-limiting solution. The key idea is to represent the solution as the sum of two orthogonal components: a boundary-layer part and a bulk distribution part, with the latter being orthogonal to boundary-layer-like functions.
As a result, we are able to prove weak
L^{2}
-convergence (noting that the spatial domain is unbounded) of this decomposition toward a solution of the form (
1.7
)
in the case
D_{\mathrm{rot}}=0
.

In this work, we restrict our analysis to the case in which active rods are unable to escape from the wall. Mathematically, this corresponds to the condition
\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\geq g>0
for some constant
g>0
. For instance, this condition is satisfied when an external force field
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{ext}}
, directed toward the wall and exceeding the propulsion force, is applied, while the background flow
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{BG}}
in (
1.2
) vanishes at the wall due to the no-slip boundary condition.
Relaxing this constraint in the proof of (
1.7
) is challenging, as allowing active rods to escape introduces new boundary layers associated with escape orientations.

Rigorous studies of singular limits for kinetic equations in bounded domains were discussed for the hydrodynamic limit from the Boltzmann equation to fluid models in Refs.
[
36
,
37
]
. As pointed out in Ref.
[
31
]
, in the system (
1.4
)-(
1.6
), where the active rod has the ability to escape, boundary layers arise on a nontrivial scale distinct from
\varepsilon^{1}
, analogous to the corner and parabolic layers characteristic of small-diffusion problems involving both advection and boundaries
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
The other important distinguishing feature of the system (
1.4
)-(
1.6
) is the anisotropic Laplacian leading to degenerate problem in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The development of efficient numerical schemes for anisotropic Laplacians have been studied, e.g., in Refs.
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
and the rigorous asymptotic analysis (without advection) in Ref.
[
45
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, such singular limits in the presence of advection and non-penetrable boundaries, resulting in a coupled bulk-boundary limit system of the type obtained here, have not been addressed before.

The limiting problem and main results are formulated in Section
2
. Section
3
contains the proof of well-posedness of the limiting system. Section
4
derives the limiting system and rigorously proves the
L^{1}
-convergence of the composite expansion to the solution of the limiting problem. Section
5
proves weak
L^{2}
-convergence in the particular case with no rotational diffusion.

2.
Main results

We consider the Fokker-Planck equation in half-space
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\left\{(x,y):y>0\right\}
. We impose the following simplifying assumptions on the advection
\boldsymbol{V}
and reorientation
\Phi
terms as follows:

(
A1
)

\boldsymbol{V}
depends on orientation angle
\varphi
only:
\boldsymbol{V}=V(\varphi)\boldsymbol{e}_{y}
, where
V\in C^{1}[0,2\pi]
is a periodic function and it satisfies

(2.1)

V(\varphi)\geq g>0,\quad\varphi\in[0,2\pi)

for some positive constant
g>0
.

(
A2
)

\Phi=\Phi(\varphi)
and
\Phi\in C^{1}_{\text{per}}[0,2\pi]
is periodic.

For clarity of presentation, we assume that
\boldsymbol{V}
and
\Phi
are independent of the spatial variables
x
and
y
. We note that our results can be extended to the case where
\boldsymbol{V}
and
\Phi
vary smoothly in space.
Note also that assumptions (A1) and (A2) are satisfied, for example, in the case of (
1.2
) with the shear flow
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{BG}}=\gamma y\,\boldsymbol{e}_{x}
and the wall-attracting external force
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}=-g\,\boldsymbol{e}_{y}
, where
\gamma>0
and
g>1
are given constants. In this case, we have

V(\varphi)=V_{\text{prop}}\sin(\varphi)-g
and
\Phi(\varphi)=-\gamma\sin^{2}(\varphi),

which satisfy conditions (A1) and (A2). This case is also relevant when the problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is viewed as an approximation of the vicinity of a point on the wall, where the linear size of the region is much larger than the length of an active rod. In such a setting, it is reasonable to treat the wall as flat and to approximate the background Poiseuille flow by its linearization, which corresponds to a shear flow.

Under this assumption, we can exclude dependence on variable
x
from the unknown probability distribution function
f_{\varepsilon}
. Then the problem (
1.4
,
1.6
) reduces to

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi f_{\varepsilon})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}-V\partial_{y}f_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}=0,\\
-Vf_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}f|_{y=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

It was shown in Ref.
[
31
]
by means of formal multi-scale asymptotic expansion that the solution
f_{\varepsilon}
of system (
2.2
) converges to
\rho_{\text{bulk}}+\delta_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\rho_{\text{wall}}
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and functions
\rho_{\text{bulk}}(t,y,\varphi)
and
\rho_{\text{wall}}(t,\varphi)
solve the following equations:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{bulk}}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi\rho_{\text{bulk}}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\rho_{\text{bulk}}-V\partial_{y}\rho_{\text{bulk}}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{wall}}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi\rho_{\text{wall}}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\rho_{\text{wall}}=V\rho_{\text{bulk}}|_{y=0}.\end{array}\right.

The system is subject to initial conditions:

\rho_{\text{bulk}}|_{t=0}=\rho_{\text{bulk}}^{(0)}\text{ and }\rho_{\text{wall}}|_{t=0}=\rho_{\text{wall}}^{(0)}.

Unlike the original initial-boundary value problem (
2.2
), which is a parabolic equation with no-flux boundary conditions, the system (
2.3
) is non-classical in several important respects.
First, it is
degenerate
in the sense that it lacks a second derivative in the variable
y
. In other words, if we treat
y
and
\varphi
as spatial variables, the equation resembles a parabolic one, but with an incomplete Laplacian operator.
Second, rather than specifying boundary conditions for the bulk distribution
\rho_{\text{bulk}}
, the model incorporates a separate Fokker-Planck equation defined on the boundary of the domain.
In Section
3
, we establish the following well-posedness result for the limiting system (
2.3
).

Theorem 2.1

(Well-posedness & regularity of system (
2.3
))
.

Let assumptions (A1) & (A2) hold. Assume also that
\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}^{(0)}\in H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}^{(0)}\in H^{1}_{\varphi}
. Then there exists a unique solution of the system (
2.3
) such that
\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}\in C([0,T];H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y})
and
\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}\in C([0,T];H^{2}_{\varphi})
. Here,
H^{2}_{\varphi}=H^{2}_{\text{per}}(0,2\pi)
and
H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}
is the space of functions

,
2\pi
-periodic in

, such that

\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|D^{2}_{\varphi}v|^{2}+|D_{\varphi}v|^{2}+|v|^{2}+|D_{y}v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi<\infty.

Moreover, for every integer

, if
\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}^{(0)}\in H^{k+2}_{\varphi}H^{k+1}_{y}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}^{(0)}\in H^{k+1}_{\varphi}
, then
\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}\in C([0,T];H^{k+2}_{\varphi}H^{k+1}_{y})
and
\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}\in C([0,T];H^{k+2}_{\varphi})
.

Our main focus is justifying the limit of initial-boundary value problem (
2.2
) to system (
2.3
).

Theorem 2.2

(
L^{1}
-approximation)
.

Let assumptions (A1) & (A2) hold. Let
f_{\varepsilon}
and
(\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}},\rho_{\mathrm{wall}})
be solutions of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), respectively. Assume also that
f_{\varepsilon}|_{t=0}=\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}|_{t=0}
is independent of

\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}|_{t=0}=0
. Then define an approximation

\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}(t,y,\varphi):=\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}(t,\varphi)\cdot\dfrac{V(\varphi)}{\varepsilon}e^{-V(\varphi)y/\varepsilon}+\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}(t,y,\varphi).

Then for all

(2.5)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|f_{\varepsilon}(t,y,\varphi)-\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}(t,y,\varphi)|\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi=0.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the term
\dfrac{V(\varphi)}{\varepsilon}e^{-V(\varphi)y/\varepsilon}
on the right-hand side of (

) serves as an approximation of the Dirac delta function. Taking this into account, one can observe the correspondence between the definition (

) and the limiting representation given in (

).

Remark 2.4
.

The assumption that

is independent of

, and that

Next, we turn to the case of the zero rotational diffusion

:

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi f_{\varepsilon})-V\partial_{y}f_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}=0,\\
-Vf_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}f|_{y=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

In this case, using a different approach from the one used in the proof of Theorem

and a key ansatz for the solution

, we proved weak

-convergence of

to a singular representation (

).

Theorem 2.5

(weak
L^{2}
-convergence)
.

Let assumptions (A1) & (A2) hold. Let
f_{\varepsilon}
be a solution of (
2.6
). Introduce
m_{\varepsilon}
and
u_{\varepsilon}
such that

(2.7)

f_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi)e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,y)\quad\text{with}\quad\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}u_{\varepsilon}e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

Then

\displaystyle m_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup m\text{ in $L^{2}(0,2\pi)$}

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u\text{ in $H^{1}(0,\infty)\times L^{2}(0,2\pi)$.}

and
\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}:=m/V
and
\rho_{\mathrm{bulk}}:=u
satisfy the limiting equations:

(2.8)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\rho_{\mathrm{wall}})-V\partial_{y}\rho_{\mathrm{wall}}=0,\\

Remark 2.6
.

Given
f_{\varepsilon}
, functions
m_{\varepsilon}
and

are uniquely defined by (

):

(2.9)

m_{\varepsilon}=2V\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}f_{\varepsilon}e^{-V(\varphi)y/\varepsilon}\,\text{d}y\text{ and }u_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi)e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}.

Remark 2.7
.

As is evident from the proof of Theorem

, the term
(1/\varepsilon)m_{\varepsilon}e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}
characterizes the boundary layer structure. It satisfies the pre-limiting equation at order

, namely

and

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1
: Well-posedness of the limiting problem

3.1.
Formulation of the limiting problem

In this section, we study the limiting problem for the bulk and wall distributions,
u(t,y,\varphi)=\rho_{\text{bulk}}
and
w(t,\varphi)=\rho_{\text{wall}}
, respectively. The limiting problem is

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u-V\partial_{y}u=0,\quad y>0,\,t>0,\,0\leq\varphi<2\pi,\\
\partial_{t}w+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi w)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u=Vu|_{y=0},\quad y=0,\,t>0,\,0\leq\varphi<2\pi.\\
\end{array}\right.

We aim to show the well-posedness result for system (
3.1
) using the semi-group theory.
We begin by fixing notations

•

I:=[0,2\pi)
and
L^{2}_{\varphi}:=L^{2}(I)
,

•

L^{2}_{y}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

•

We use the shorthand
L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}
to denote the space of functions
g(\varphi,y)
with finite

(3.2)

\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}}=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|f|^{2}\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}\varphi\right)^{1/2}

We will also denote
X:=L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}
.

Higher order Sobolev spaces are introduced similarly with the account of periodic boundary conditions with respect to variable
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
and zero boundary conditions for
|y|\to\infty
. For example,

\displaystyle H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}:=\bigg\{v(y,\varphi)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|D^{2}_{\varphi}v|^{2}+|D_{\varphi}v|^{2}+|v|^{2}+|D_{y}v|^{2}\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi<\infty,

(3.3)

\displaystyle v(0,y)=v(2\pi,y)\text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lim_{|y|\to\infty}v(\varphi,y)=0\text{ for }\varphi\in I\bigg\}

We rewrite (
3.1
) in the form

(3.4)

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{L}\mathcal{U}=0,

where
\mathcal{U}=\left\{u,v\right\}
and the operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined as follows:

(3.5)

\mathcal{L}\mathcal{U}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}B&0\\
VT&A\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
w\end{array}\right)

Here, operators
A
,
B
, and
T
are

(3.6)

\displaystyle Aw

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi w)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}w,

\displaystyle\qquad A:

\displaystyle D(A)=H_{\varphi}^{2}\subset L^{2}_{\varphi}\to L^{2}_{\varphi},

\displaystyle Bu

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u+V\partial_{y}u,

\displaystyle\qquad B:

\displaystyle D(B)=H_{\varphi}^{2}H^{1}_{y}\subset L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}\to L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y},

\displaystyle Tu

\displaystyle=u|_{y=0}

\displaystyle\qquad T:

\displaystyle D(T)=H_{\varphi}^{2}H^{1}_{y}\subset L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}\to L^{2}_{\varphi}.

Now, we can define the domain of operator
\mathcal{L}:\,D(\mathcal{L})\subset\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
and the space
\mathcal{X}
this operator acts on:

(3.7)

D(\mathcal{L})=D(B)\otimes D(A)\text{ and }\mathcal{X}=X\otimes L^{2}_{\varphi}=L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}\otimes L^{2}_{\varphi}.

3.2.
B
is an infinitesimal generator of
C_{0}
-semigroup

Since the first equation in (
3.1
) is independent of
v
and is of the form
\partial_{t}u+Bu=0
, we consider its well-posedness separately. To this end, we aim to show in this subsection that
B
is an infinitesimal generator of
c_{0}
-semigroup. According to Lumer-Phillips Theorem
[
46
]
it is sufficient to show that there exists
\mu>0
such that

(
i
)

(
Accretivity
)
\langle[B+\mu]u,u\rangle_{X}\geq 0
for all
u\in D(B)
;

(
ii
)

(
Maximality
)
\mathrm{Range}(B+\mu)=L^{2}_{y}L^{2}_{\varphi}
, that is, for any
f\in L^{2}_{y}L^{2}_{\varphi}
there exists
u\in D(B)
such that
(B+\mu)u=f
.

Lemma 3.1

(Accretivity of
B
)
.

There exists
\mu_{0}>0
such that for every
\lambda>\mu_{0}
and every
u\in D(B)
,

(3.8)

\langle(B+\lambda)u,\,u\bigr\rangle_{X}\geq(\lambda-\mu_{0})\|u\|_{X}^{2}+D\|\partial_{\varphi}u\|_{X}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
u\in D(B)
.
Integration by parts in
\varphi
and in
y
, together with periodic boundary conditions and decay at
|y|\to\infty
, gives

\displaystyle\langle B\,u,\,u\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u)\,u\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi-D\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u\,u\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi+\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}V\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{y}u\,u\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi^{\prime}\,u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi+D\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{\varphi}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\|\Phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{X}^{2}+D\|\partial_{\varphi}u\|_{X}^{2}.

Then, denoting
\mu_{0}:=\dfrac{1}{2}\|\Phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}+1
we obtain

\langle(B+\lambda)u,\,u\rangle_{X}=\lambda\|u\|_{X}^{2}+\langle B\,u,\,u\rangle_{X}\geq(\lambda-\mu_{0})\|u\|_{X}^{2}+D\|\partial_{\varphi}u\|_{X}^{2}.

∎

Lemma
3.1
implies accretivity of
B
. The proof of maximality is split into three steps. First, we prove existence of a solution in
H^{1}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}
for the regularized operator
B_{\varepsilon}:=B-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}
. Second, we obtain bounds uniform in
\varepsilon
. Third, we conclude via a standard compactness argument as
\varepsilon\to 0
the limiting solution satisfies
(B+\mu)u=f
, for all
f\in X
.

Lemma 3.2

(Maximality of
B
)
.

Let
\lambda>\mu_{0}
. For each
\varepsilon>0
define

B_{\varepsilon}:=B-\varepsilon\,\partial_{y}^{2},

and set
V=H^{1}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}
.

Then for every
f\in X
the regularized equation

(3.9)

(B_{\varepsilon}+\lambda)\,u_{\varepsilon}=f

has a unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}\in V
, and there is
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
, so that

(3.10)

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}\leq C\,\|f\|_{X}^{2}.

Moreover, there exists a subsequence of
u_{\varepsilon}
converging weakly in
H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{1}_{y}
and strongly in
X
to
u\in D(B)
solving

(3.11)

(B+\lambda)\,u=f.

Proof.

STEP 1.

Existence and uniqueness for the regularized problem.

Define the bilinear form for
u,v\in V
:

a_{\varepsilon}(u,v):=\lambda\langle u,v\rangle_{X}+b(u,v)+\varepsilon\,\langle\partial_{y}u,\partial_{y}v\rangle_{X},

where
b(u,v)
is the bilinear form corresponding to the operator
B
, namely,

b(u,v):=\langle\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi u\right),v\rangle_{X}+D\langle\partial_{\varphi}u,\partial_{\varphi}v\rangle_{X}+\langle V\partial_{y}u,v\rangle_{X}.

By the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we get

a_{\varepsilon}(u,u)\geq(\lambda-\mu_{0})\|u\|_{X}^{2}+D\,\|\partial_{\varphi}u\|_{X}^{2}+\varepsilon\,\|\partial_{y}u\|_{X}^{2}\geq\min\{\lambda-\mu_{0},D,\varepsilon\}\|u\|_{V}^{2}

implying coercivity of the form
a_{\varepsilon}
. Continuity of the form
a_{\varepsilon}
,
a_{\varepsilon}(u,v)\leq C\|u\|_{V}\|v\|_{V}
is straightforward. Then the Lax–Milgram theorem yields a unique
u_{\varepsilon}\in V
solving

(3.12)

a_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{X}\quad\forall v\in V.

STEP 2.

A priori estimates for
u_{\varepsilon}
, independent of
\varepsilon
.

First
, substitute
v=u_{\varepsilon}
into (
3.12
) and use coercivity of
a_{\varepsilon}
established in the previous step:

(\lambda-\mu_{0})\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+D\,\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\varepsilon\,\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}\leq\langle f,u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}.

Using Young’s inequality to estimate the right-hand side:

\langle f,u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}\leq\dfrac{\lambda-\mu_{0}}{2}\,\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2(\lambda-\mu_{0})}\,\|f\|_{X}^{2},

we obtain

\dfrac{\lambda-\mu_{0}}{2}\,\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+D\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{2(\lambda-\mu_{0})}\,\|f\|_{X}^{2}.

This inequality implies an
a priori
bound

(3.13)

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}\leq C\,\|f\|_{X}^{2}

with a positive constant
C
depending on
\lambda-\mu_{0}
and
D
, but independent of
\varepsilon
.

Next
, substitute
v=\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}
into (
3.12
). Note that this substitution is admissible since
f\in X=L^{2}_{\varphi}L^{2}_{y}
, then, similar to Section §6.3.1 in Ref.
[
47
, §6.3.1]
,
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}_{\varphi}H^{2}_{y}
and substitution into the weak formulation (
3.12
) can be treated as multiplication of the strong formulation
(\lambda+B_{\varepsilon})u_{\varepsilon}=f
by
v=\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}
. We obtain

(3.14)

\lambda\,\langle u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}+\langle B\,u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}+\varepsilon\,\langle\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}=\langle f,\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}.

Next, due to assumption (
2.1
) we have

\displaystyle\langle B\,u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi u_{\varepsilon}\right),\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}+D\langle\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}+\langle V\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon},\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle\geq-\dfrac{\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{g}\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\dfrac{3g}{4}\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}.

Using this bound in (
3.14
) and noting that due to the derivative of square formula (
\partial_{y}(v^{2})=2v\partial_{y}v
) and the fundamental theorem of calculus, the first and third terms in the equality above vanish, we obtain

(3.15)

\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}\leq\dfrac{16}{9g^{2}}\|f\|^{2}_{X}+\dfrac{8\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}}{3g^{2}}\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}.

Using (
3.13
) we get the existence of a positive constant
C
(may be different from the one in (
3.13
)), depending on
\lambda-\mu_{0}
,
D
,
g
, and
\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}
but independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

(3.16)

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}\leq C\,\|f\|_{X}^{2}.

Finally, we obtain the bound for
\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}
from (
3.10
). To this end, rewrite the equation (
3.9
) for
u_{\varepsilon}
in the form

(3.17)

-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}=g,

where

g:=f-\lambda\,u_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{\varphi}\bigl(\Phi\,u_{\varepsilon}\bigr)+V\,\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}.

Squaring the left-hand side of (
3.17
) and integrating by parts, we get

\displaystyle\|-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{2}\|\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}+\varepsilon^{2}\|\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}+2D\varepsilon\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{2}\|\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}+2D\varepsilon\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{\varphi}\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon})^{2}\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle D^{2}\|\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}.

On the other hand, the right-hand side of (
3.17
) can be estimated by an expression of the form
C\|f\|^{2}_{X}
in view of the bound (
3.16
). Hence, there exists a constant
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that
\|\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{X}\leq C\|f\|^{2}_{X}
. Thus, we proved the estimate (
3.10
) from formulation of this Lemma.

STEP 3.

Passing to the limit in (
3.9
) with respect to
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The estimate (
3.10
) implies a weakly converging subsequence of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
to
u\in D(B)
solving
(B+\lambda)\,u=f
.
∎

For any

Next, we turn to the second equation in (

Here, the dependence on variable

-semigroup

.

Lemma 3.4

(Solvability of
\partial_{t}w+Aw=T(Vu)
)
.

Let

be a solution of (

This concludes the proof of Theorem

.

4.
Proof of Theorem
2.5
:
L^{1}
-convergence for
D>0

We first construct asymptotic expansions for the solution in two distinct regions: an outer region (or bulk) where the solution varies slowly, and an inner region (the boundary layer) where the solution varies rapidly. The overall dynamics are then found by enforcing a matching condition between these two expansions. Finally, we prove that the
L^{1}
norm of the difference between the composite expansion and the original solution vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Outer solution

In the outer region, away from the boundary (
y>0
), we assume a regular asymptotic expansion for the probability distribution function:

(4.1)

f_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,y)\sim u_{0}(t,\varphi,y)+\varepsilon u_{1}(t,\varphi,y)+...

Substituting this expansion into the governing equation from (
2.2
) we get

\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi f_{\varepsilon})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}-V\partial_{y}f_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}f_{\varepsilon}=0,

and collecting terms of like powers in
\varepsilon
yields a hierarchy of equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u_{0})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u_{0}-V\partial_{y}u_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\varepsilon\left[\partial_{t}u_{1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u_{1})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u_{1}-V\partial_{y}u_{1}-\partial_{y}^{2}u_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+...

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt=0.

The leading-order,
O(1)
&
O(\varepsilon)
, problems govern the bulk distributions
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u_{0})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u_{0}-V\partial_{y}u_{0}=0,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u_{1})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u_{1}-V\partial_{y}u_{1}=\partial_{y}^{2}u_{0}.

Inner solution
-
Boundary layer at
y=0

We introduce a rescaled spatial variable
z=y/\varepsilon
and then the unknown function
f_{\varepsilon}
at the boundary
\{y=0\}
can be represented as
f_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,y)=p_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,z)
. In terms of the inner variable
z
, the governing equation with boundary conditions in (
2.2
) become:

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}p_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi p_{\varepsilon})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}p_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-V\partial_{z}p_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{z}^{2}p_{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\left[-Vp_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{z}p_{\varepsilon}\right]|_{z=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

We expand
p_{\varepsilon}
as follows starting from
\varepsilon^{-1}
term:

p_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,z)\sim\varepsilon^{-1}p_{-1}(t,\varphi,z)+p_{0}(t,\varphi,z)+...

The leading
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
term is necessary to capture the steep gradient of
f_{\varepsilon}
near the boundary. Substituting this expansions into the governing equation and the boundary condition in (
4.4
) we get, respectively,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\left[-V\partial_{z}p_{-1}-\partial_{z}^{2}p_{-1}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\varepsilon^{-1}\left[\partial_{t}p_{-1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi p_{-1})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{-1}-\left\{-V\partial_{z}p_{0}-\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+...

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt=0,

and

\displaystyle\left[-Vp_{-1}-\partial_{z}p_{-1}\right]|_{z=0}+\varepsilon\left[-Vp_{0}-\partial_{z}p_{0}\right]|_{z=0}+...

These expansions for
p_{\varepsilon}
yield the following equations at leading orders of
\varepsilon
. Specifically, we have the following boundary value problem for
p_{-1}
:

(4.5)

\begin{cases}-V\partial_{z}p_{-1}-\partial_{z}^{2}p_{-1}=0,\quad&z>0,\\
(-Vp_{-1}-\partial_{z}p_{-1})|_{z=0}=0.\end{cases}

and for
p_{0}
:

(4.6)

\begin{cases}V\partial_{z}p_{0}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0}=\partial_{t}p_{-1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi p_{-1})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{-1},\quad z>0\\
(-Vp_{0}-\partial_{z}p_{0})|_{z=0}=0\end{cases}

Matching asymptotic expansions

The asymptotics are matched by their corresponding order of
\varepsilon
, noting that the inner and outer expansions must be consistent. This is enforced via a matching condition, which requires that the behavior of the inner solution far from the wall (in terms of variable
z
) corresponds to the short-range behavior of the outer solution:

(4.7)

\lim_{z\to\infty}p_{k}(t,\varphi,z)=u_{k}(t,\varphi,0),\quad k=-1,0,1,\dots

We take
u_{-1}\equiv 0
.

Next, we note that the boundary value problem (
4.5
) for
p_{-1}
has the general solution:

(4.8)

p_{-1}(t,\varphi,z)=\hat{p}_{-1}(t,\varphi)\cdot V(\varphi)e^{-zV(\varphi)}.

Here, we factor out
V(\varphi)
to simplify calculations below. Note that
\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}Ve^{-zV}\,\text{d}z=1
so
Ve^{-zV}
can be viewed as an approximation of
\delta
-function and thus
\hat{p}_{-1}
is the limiting wall distribution.

To determine the governing equation for
\hat{p}_{-1}
, we integrate equation (
4.6
) with respect to
z
from
0
to
\infty
:

(4.9)

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(V\partial_{z}p_{0}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0})\,\text{d}z=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\partial_{t}p_{-1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi p_{-1})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{-1}\right)\,\text{d}z.

The left-hand side of (
4.9
) evaluates to:

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(V\partial_{z}p_{0}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0})\,\text{d}z=\left.\left[Vp_{0}+\partial_{z}p_{0}\right]\right|_{0}^{\infty}=Vu_{0}|_{y=0}.

Here we used the matching condition for
p_{0}
and the no-flux boundary condition from (
4.6
). The right-hand side of (
4.9
) becomes:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\right)\left(\hat{p}_{-1}Ve^{-Vz}\right)\,\text{d}z

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\right)\left[\hat{p}_{-1}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}Ve^{-Vz}\,\text{d}z\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\right)\hat{p}_{-1},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}(\cdot):=\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\,\cdot)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}(\,\cdot\,)
. Equating representations we found for the each side of (
4.9
) we get the evolution equation for the wall distribution
\hat{p}_{-1}
:

(4.10)

\partial_{t}\hat{p}_{-1}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\hat{p}_{-1})-D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}\hat{p}_{-1}=Vu_{0}|_{y=0}.

This equation, coupled with the equation for the bulk distribution
u_{0}
, forms the limiting system; after identifying
u=u_{0}
,
w=p_{-1}
the limiting system is exactly (
3.1
). Though we managed to find a closed form system for
u_{0}
and
p_{-1}
, to prove convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}
to the combination of solutions of the limiting system, we need to find more information about
p_{0}
. To this end, substitute (
4.8
) into the equation in the boundary-value problem (
4.6
):

(4.11)

V\partial_{z}p_{0}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0}=(A_{1}+A_{2}z+A_{3}z^{2})e^{-Vz},

where
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, and
A_{3}
are independent of
z
and defined by

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\varphi})(\hat{p}_{-1}V)=\partial_{t}\left(\hat{p}_{-1}V\right)+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi\hat{p}_{-1}V\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\left(\hat{p}_{-1}V\right),

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-V^{\prime}V\Phi\hat{p}_{-1}+2DV^{\prime}\partial_{\varphi}\left(\hat{p}_{-1}V\right)+DV^{\prime\prime}V\Phi\hat{p}_{-1},

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D\left(V^{\prime}\right)^{2}V\Phi\hat{p}_{-1}.

Integration of (
4.11
) with respect to
z
together with matching condition (
4.7
) gives

(4.12)

p_{0}(t,z,\varphi)=\hat{u}_{0}(t,\varphi)+m_{3}(z,t,\varphi)e^{-zV},

where
m_{3}
is the cubic polynomial with respect to
z
with coefficients independent of
\varepsilon
and depending on
t
and
\varphi
and
\hat{u}_{0}(t,\varphi)=u_{0}(t,\varphi,0)
.
The representation (
4.12
) implies

(4.13)

\partial_{t}p_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi p_{0}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{0}=\partial_{t}\hat{u}_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi\hat{u}_{0}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}\hat{u}_{0}+m_{5}e^{-Vz}.

Here,
m_{5}=m_{5}(z,t,\varphi)
is the polynomial with respect to
z
of the degree 5 with coefficients independent of
\varepsilon
and depending on
t
and
\varphi
.

Composite expansion

The composite expansion
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
for the solution of the boundary-value problem (

) is given by

(4.14)

\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}(t,\varphi,y):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}p_{-1}\left(t,\varphi,\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)+p_{0}\left(t,\varphi,\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)+u_{0}(t,\varphi,y)-u_{0}(t,\varphi,0),

where
p_{-1}
,

, and

are given by (

), (
4.10
), (
4.13
), and (
4.2
). Next, we write the boundary-value problem for
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
.
Specifically, the PDE for
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\hat{f}_{\varepsilon})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}+\left[-V\partial_{y}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\partial_{t}p_{-1}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi p_{-1}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{-1}\right]-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left[V\partial_{z}p_{-1}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{-1}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt+\left[\partial_{t}p_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}\left(\Phi p_{0}\right)-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}p_{0}\right]-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[V\partial_{z}p_{0}+\partial_{z}^{2}p_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt+\left[\partial_{t}u_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi u_{0})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}u_{0}-V\partial_{y}u_{0}\right]-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}u_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt-\left[\partial_{t}\hat{u}_{0}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\hat{u}_{0})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\hat{u}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=m_{5}\left(\dfrac{y}{\varepsilon},t,\varphi\right)e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}u_{0}.

Similarly, the boundary conditions for
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
at
\{y=0\}
:

\displaystyle-V\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{y}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[-Vp_{-1}-\partial_{z}p_{-1}\right]+\left[-Vp_{0}-\partial_{z}p_{0}\right]

\displaystyle+\left[-Vu_{0}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}u_{0}\right]+V\hat{u}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\partial_{y}u_{0}-V(u_{0}-\hat{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\partial_{y}u_{0}.

Therefore,
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following boundary-value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi\hat{f}_{\varepsilon})-D\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}+\left[-V\partial_{y}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}\right]=m_{5}\left(\dfrac{y}{\varepsilon},t,\varphi\right)e^{-Vy/\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}u_{0},\\
-V\hat{f_{\varepsilon}}-\varepsilon\partial_{y}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}|_{y=0}=-\varepsilon\partial_{y}u_{0}|_{y=0}.\end{array}\right.

Note that if we denote right-hand sides of the boundary-value problem above by

and

(4.15)

then

(4.16)

\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|R_{\varepsilon}|\,\text{d}y\text{d}\varphi+\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}|r_{\varepsilon}|\,\text{d}\varphi=O(\varepsilon).

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}J^{\prime}_{\eta}(E_{\varepsilon})\left(-\partial_{\varphi}(\Phi E_{\varepsilon})+D\partial^{2}_{\varphi}E_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi

\displaystyle\hskip 90.0pt+2\pi\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\eta+\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}|r_{\varepsilon}|\,\text{d}\varphi+\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|R_{\varepsilon}|\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\varphi
