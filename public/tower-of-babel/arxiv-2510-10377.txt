Decomposition of Polysymmetric Functions and Stack Partitions
David Martinez

Abstract
. Polysymmetric functions, introduced by Asvin G. and Andrew O’Desky, provide a new framework linking combinatorics and algebraic geometry through motivic measures in the Grothendieck ring of varieties. Building on this foundation and the combinatorial results of Khanna and Loehr, we study the non-tensor bases of the polysymmetric algebra, introducing a new basis { H τ + } \{H^{+}_{\tau}\} and deriving explicit expansions among the families { H τ } \{H_{\tau}\} , { E τ } \{E_{\tau}\} , { E τ + } \{E^{+}_{\tau}\} , { P τ } \{P_{\tau}\} , and { H τ + } \{H^{+}_{\tau}\} , where each basis is indexed by stack partitions (types) that generalize integer partitions by recording both degrees and multiplicities. Using the Pieri-type rule established by Khanna and Loehr for related families, we extend it to the H + H^{+} basis, give a combinatorial description of the product of monomial polysymmetric functions, and identify an involution for the P P basis.



1.
Introduction

Asvin G and Andrew O’Desky in
[
7
]
have recently
several new bases in the graded algebra
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
of polysymmetric functions, which is a generalization of the algebra
\Lambda
of symmetric functions. They define the notion of a
type

\tau
, which plays an important role for polysymmetric functions analogous to that of partitions in the theory of symmetric functions. These types defined in
[
7
]
have been studied by Hultquist, Mullen, and Niederreiter in
[
9
]
to enumerate Association Schemes; the authors refer to types as
factorization patterns of

n
. Later, Agarwal and Mullen’s 1988 work
[
1
]
provided combinatorial interpretations of factorization patterns, establishing connections to plane partitions and highlighting their natural occurrences in polynomial algebra. Since then, the study of factorization patterns has expanded significantly, appearing in a wide range of contexts, including associated lattice paths
[
2
]
, mock theta functions
[
5
]
, split perfect partitions
[
8
]
, beaded partitions
[
11
]
, scalar polynomial functions
[
4
]
, and non-squashing partitions
[
6
]
.

The goal of Asvin G and Andrew O’Desky paper was to use polysymmetric functions to extend results in algebraic geometry through combinatorial means, with a focus on exploring connections to the Grothendieck ring of varieties. Khanna and Loehr in
[
10
]
extend the work in
[
7
]
by studying Pieri Rule on these bases, developing combinatorial formulas for some multiplication rules and transition matrices between the bases defined in
[
7
]
.

This work continues the investigation of
[
7
,
10
]
, giving the bases of the polysymmetric function and their combinatorial interpretations. In this work, we refer to types as
stack partitions
. We begin
Section 2
by giving an introduction to polysymmetric functions and stack partitions. We frame the theory of polysymmetric functions in terms of stacked partitions, a visual interpretation of types, which allows for a more natural and intuitive description of the polysymmetric bases. In
Section 3
we present a new interpretation of the four bases in terms of monomial polysymmetric functions. We accomplish this by counting specific matrices. In
Section 4
, we introduce a new basis, the signed homogeneous polysymmetric function, and cement its place in the existing literature. Our main results appear in
Section 5
, where we develop analogues of classical symmetric function theory in the polysymmetric setting. In particular, we analyze the involution defined in
[
7
]
and its action on the basis
\{P_{\tau}\}
, using this to derive explicit expressions relating the various bases of
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
to one another.

2.
Introduction to polysymmetric functions and stack partitions

In this section, we introduce polysymmetric functions and stack partitions, assuming the reader has some familiarity with the theory of symmetric functions and their standard bases
e_{\lambda},h_{\lambda},p_{\lambda},m_{\lambda},s_{\lambda}
(see
[
12
,
3
]
).

2.1.
Polysymmetric Functions

Let
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra of symmetric functions. For each positive integer
m
, let
\Lambda_{(m)}
be a copy of
\Lambda
in which each homogeneous symmetric function of degree
n
in
\Lambda
is regarded as having degree
mn
. The
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra of polysymmetric functions is defined as the tensor product,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P\Lambda}=\Lambda_{(1)}\otimes\Lambda_{(2)}\otimes\Lambda_{(3)}\otimes\cdots.

Then
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
can be viewed as having the set of indeterminates
\mathbf{x}_{**}=\{x_{m,j}\}_{m,j=1}^{\infty}
, where we say
x_{m,j}
has degree
m
and view
\Lambda_{(m)}
as the ring of symmetric functions in a variable set
\mathbf{x}_{m*}=\{x_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
Let
\mathbb{Q}[[\mathbf{x}_{**}]]
denote the ring of all formal infinite sums of monomials in
\mathbf{x}_{**}
with rational coefficients and bounded degree.
A formal series
f=f(\mathbf{x}_{**})
is in
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
if for each fixed
m
,
f
remains unchanged by any permutation of the variables in
\mathbf{x}_{m*}
.
An isomorphism between the tensor and series versions of
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
is defined by sending the pure tensor
f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes f_{3}\otimes\cdots
to the formal series
f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1*})f_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2*})f_{3}(\mathbf{x}_{3*})\cdots
. Therefore,
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
has the structure of a graded algebra

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P\Lambda}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathsf{P\Lambda}^{n},

where
\mathsf{P\Lambda}^{n}
is the vector space of homogeneous polysymmetric functions of degree
n
.

Example 2.1
.

Let

(3)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i,k}x_{2,i}^{2}x_{3,k}^{2}+\sum_{i<j,\,k}x_{2,i}x_{2,j}x_{3,k}^{2}+\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{1,i}x_{2,j}x_{3,k}x_{4,\ell}.

Each monomial is symmetric in the variable sets
x_{1,*},x_{2,*},x_{3,*},x_{4,*}
, thus
f\in\mathsf{P\Lambda}
.
Since every term of
f
has degree
10
, we also have
f\in\mathsf{P\Lambda}^{10}
.

2.2.
Stack Partitions

A
stack
is an ordered pair of positive integers
(d,m)
, which we write as
d^{m}
. We say that
d^{m}
has degree
d
and multiplicity
m
. We order stacks by having
d_{i}^{m_{i}}\geq d_{j}^{m_{j}}
if
d_{i}>d_{j}
, or if
d_{i}=d_{j}
and
m_{i}\geq m_{j}
. A
stack partition
of
n
is a weakly decreasing sequence of stacks
\tau=(d_{1}^{m_{1}},d_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,d_{s}^{m_{s}})

such that
d_{1}m_{1}+d_{2}m_{2}+\ldots+d_{s}m_{s}=n
.
We write
\tau\Vdash n
or
|\tau|=n
to indicate that
\tau
is a stack partition of
n
.
When every stack in
\tau
has multiplicity
1
(i.e.,
\tau
is an ordinary partition), we write
\tau\vdash n
.

For any positive integer
r
, we define the
stack partition of
n^{r}
as the stack partition of
n
with the multiplicities of each stack scaled by
r
.

Example 2.2
.

The stack partitions of
4
and
4^{3}
are given by

\displaystyle 4:\quad 4,\quad 31,\quad 22,\quad 211,\quad 1111,\quad 2^{2},\quad 21^{2},\quad 1^{2}11,\quad 1^{2}1^{2},\quad 1^{3}1,\quad 1^{4};

\displaystyle 4^{3}:\quad 4^{3},\quad 3^{3}1^{3},\quad 2^{3}2^{3},\quad 2^{3}1^{3}1^{3},\quad 1^{3}1^{3}1^{3}1^{3},\quad 2^{6},\quad 2^{3}1^{6},\quad 1^{6}1^{3}1^{3},\quad 1^{6}1^{6},\quad 1^{9}1^{3},\quad 1^{12}.

The
length
of a stack partition
\tau
is the number of stacks it contains, denoted by
\ell(\tau)
. For a fixed
d
, let
\tau\mid_{d}
denote the partition consisting of the multiplicities of the stacks with degree
d
in
\tau
. Let
\operatorname{area}(\tau\mid_{d})
be the sum of the multiplicities of the stacks with degree
d
in
\tau
, and define
\operatorname{area}(\tau)
as the total sum of all multiplicities within
\tau
, so that
\operatorname{area}(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\tau)}m_{i}
.
We define the
sign
of the stack partition
\tau
as

(4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{\operatorname{area}(\tau\mid_{i})}=(-1)^{\operatorname{area}(\tau)}.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\tau=(3^{3},3^{2},3^{2},2^{7},2^{2},2^{1},1^{5},1^{1})
, so that
\ell(\tau)=8
,

\tau|_{3}=(3,2,2)
,
\tau|_{2}=(7,2,1)
, and
\tau|_{1}=(5,1)
.
Furthermore,
\operatorname{area}(\tau)=23
and
\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)=(-1)^{23}=-1
.

Our interpretation of stack partitions aligns with
[
7
,
10
]
, where they are referred to as
types
. We use the term
stack partitions
to emphasize their construction from ordinary partitions by visualizing each number as a box and allowing the action of “stacking” boxes with the same number. This construction connects naturally to the concepts of stack, degree, and multiplicity introduced earlier, where each box represents a stack, its label gives the degree, and the number of boxes in each column gives its multiplicity. After making a stacking, we order the resulting stacks as described earlier in the section.

2.3.
Polysymmetric Bases

In
[
7
]
, four bases of
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
are introduced, denoted by
\{H_{\tau}\}
,
\{E^{+}_{\tau}\}
,
\{E_{\tau}\}
, and
\{P_{\tau}\}
, which are non-tensor bases. We provide interpretations in terms of stack partitions using the monomial polysymmetric function
M_{\tau}
, also introduced in
[
7
]
, which is a pure tensor product.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a stacked partition
\tau=(d_{1}^{m_{1}},d_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,d_{s}^{m_{s}})\Vdash n
, define the
monomial polysymmetric function
M_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
of type
\tau
by

(5)

\displaystyle M_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha},

where each monomial
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
is of the form

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}=x_{d_{1},\alpha_{1}}^{m_{1}}x_{d_{2},\alpha_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots x_{d_{s},\alpha_{s}}^{m_{s}}.

The sum is taken over all sequences
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
of positive integers such that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
whenever
d_{i}=d_{j}
and
i\neq j
.

Remark 2.6
.

We have that
M_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
equals
m_{\tau\mid_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1*})m_{\tau\mid_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2*})\cdots m_{\tau\mid_{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{s*})
, where
m_{\lambda}
is the monomial symmetric function indexed by the partition
\lambda
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\tau=(2^{2},1^{3},1^{2},1^{2})
, then
M_{\tau}
is the sum

\displaystyle M_{2^{2}1^{3}1^{2}1^{2}}(\mathbf{x})=m_{(3,2,2)}(\mathbf{x}_{1*})m_{(2)}(\mathbf{x}_{2*})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j,\;k,l\\
k\notin\{i,j\}\end{subarray}}x_{1,i}^{2}x_{1,j}^{2}x_{1,k}^{3}x_{2,l}^{2}.

We define four bases of
\mathsf{P\Lambda}
, denoted by
\{H_{\tau}\}
,
\{E^{+}_{\tau}\}
,
\{E_{\tau}\}
, and
\{P_{\tau}\}
. These serve as polysymmetric analogues of the symmetric bases
\{h_{\lambda}\}
,
\{e_{\lambda}\}
, and
\{p_{\lambda}\}
.

The
d
th complete homogeneous polysymmetric function
is

(7)

H_{d}=\sum_{\alpha\Vdash d}M_{\alpha}.

The
d
th unsigned elementary polysymmetric function
is

(8)

E^{+}_{d}=\sum_{\alpha\vdash d}M_{\alpha}.

The
d
th elementary polysymmetric function
is

(9)

E_{d}=\sum_{\alpha\vdash d}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)M_{\alpha}.

The
d
th power polysymmetric function
is

(10)

P_{d}=\sum_{k\mid d}kM_{k^{d/k}},

where
k\mid d
means
k
divides
d
.

Example 2.8
.

Let
d=4
. Then the expansions in the monomial polysymmetric basis are given by

(11)

\displaystyle H_{4}

\displaystyle=M_{4}+M_{31}+M_{22}+M_{2^{2}}+M_{211}+M_{21^{2}}+M_{1111}+M_{1^{2}11}+M_{1^{2}1^{2}}+M_{1^{3}1}+M_{1^{4}},

(12)

\displaystyle E^{+}_{4}

\displaystyle=M_{4}+M_{31}+M_{22}+M_{211}+M_{1111},

(13)

\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle=-M_{4}+M_{31}+M_{22}-M_{211}+M_{1111},

(14)

\displaystyle P_{4}

\displaystyle=4M_{4}+2M_{2^{2}}+M_{1^{4}}.

We define
H_{d^{m}}(\mathbf{x}_{**})=H_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{**}^{m})
, meaning every variable

appearing in every monomial of

gets replaced by

. We define similarly
E^{+}_{d^{m}}(\mathbf{x}_{**})=E^{+}_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{**}^{m}),E_{d^{m}}(\mathbf{x}_{**})=E_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{**}^{m})
, and

, where all belong in
\mathsf{P\Lambda}^{dm}
. Lastly, for any stack partition
\tau=(d_{1}^{m_{1}},d_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,d_{s}^{m_{s}})
, define

(15)

\displaystyle H_{\tau}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}H_{d_{i}^{m_{i}}},\quad E_{\tau}^{+}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}E^{+}_{d_{i}^{m_{i}}},\quad E_{\tau}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}E_{d_{i}^{m_{i}}},\quad P_{\tau}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}P_{d_{i}^{m_{i}}}.

It was shown in
[
7
]
that each of the sets
\{H_{\tau}:\tau\Vdash n\},\{E^{+}_{\tau}:\tau\Vdash n\},\{E_{\tau}:\tau\Vdash n\}
, and

is a linear basis of

.

The generating functions for the polysymmetric bases

,

,

.

Proposition 2.9

(
[

]
)
.

The generating functions for

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}H_{d^{m}}t^{md}=\prod_{i,j}(1-x_{i,j}^{m}t^{mi})^{-1},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E^{+}_{d^{m}}t^{md}=\prod_{i,j}(1+x_{i,j}^{m}t^{mi}),

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E_{d^{m}}t^{md}=\prod_{i,j}(1-x_{i,j}^{m}t^{mi}),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}P_{d^{m}}t^{md-1}=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i\mid d}i\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{i,j}^{md/i}\right)t^{md-1}.

Proof.

Since
F_{d^{m}}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=F_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{*}^{m})

for

\displaystyle(1-x_{i,j}t^{i})^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{i,j}^{k}t^{ik},

Multiplying over all pairs

enumerates every possible choice of

as the generating function. For (

)
after the substitution

and (

the definition of the polysymmetric power sums.
∎

3.
A New Combinatorial Interpretation of the Expansion in the
M
Basis

We now present a new combinatorial interpretation of the expansions of the four bases in terms of the monomial polysymmetric functions. This result was previously established by the authors of
[
10
]
using brick tabloids. Here, we provide an alternative interpretation by counting certain matrices.

3.1.
The Product of Monomials
M

In this subsection, we give a matrix counting rule for products of monomial polysymmetric functions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
be a sequence of stack partitions and fix

\tau=(d_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,d_{s}^{m_{s}})
.
Define
s^{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u)
to be the number of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
-matrices with
r
rows and
\ell(\tau\mid_{u})
columns such that the
i
th row is a permutation of
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\mid_{u}
and whose sequence of column sums equals
\tau\mid_{u}
.
Here
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\mid_{u}
denotes the sequence obtained from
\alpha_{i}\mid_{u}
with trailing zeros (if necessary) so that its length equals
\ell(\tau\mid_{u})
.
If any
\ell(\alpha_{i}\mid_{u})>\ell(\tau\mid_{u})
, then
s_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\tau}(u)=0
.

We combine these counts over all values of
u
by defining

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\prod_{u=1}^{\infty}s^{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u)

Example 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
, where

\alpha_{1}=(2,2,1^{2},1)
,
\alpha_{2}=(2,1)
, and
\alpha_{3}=(3^{3},1)

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
be a sequence of stack partitions and

n=\sum_{i=1}^{r}|\alpha_{i}|
. Then

(25)

\displaystyle\prod^{r}_{i=1}{M_{\alpha_{i}}}=\sum_{\tau\Vdash n}\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\tau}M_{\tau}.

Proof.

Fix a positive integer
u
and a stack partition
\tau\Vdash n
. Write
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
.
For each
i
, choose a term from
M_{u^{\alpha_{i}\mid_{u}}}
of the form

(26)

\displaystyle x_{u,1}^{a_{i1}}x_{u,2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots x_{u,\ell}^{a_{i\ell}},

where
\ell=\ell(\tau\mid_{u})
and
(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{i\ell})
is a permutation of
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\mid_{u}
. Then a term in
\prod_{i=1}^{s}M_{u^{\alpha_{i}\mid_{u}}}
is obtained by choosing terms
x_{u,1}^{a_{i1}}x_{u,2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots x_{u,\ell}^{a_{i\ell}}
from each
M_{u^{\alpha_{i}\mid_{u}}}
such that

(27)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\big(x_{u,1}^{a_{i1}}x_{u,2}^{a_{i2}}\cdots x_{u,\ell}^{a_{i\ell}}\big)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{u,j}^{\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{ij}}.

Then a contribution to the coefficient of
M_{u^{\tau\mid_{u}}}
occurs precisely when

(28)

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{i}a_{i1},\ldots,\sum_{i}a_{i\ell}\big)=\tau\mid_{u}.

But this is the same as choosing an
\mathbb{N}
-matrix
A=(a_{ij})
whose
i
th row is a permutation of
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\mid_{u}
and whose column-sum sequence equals
\tau\mid_{u}
. The number of such choices is
s^{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u)
by definition.
Variables with distinct degrees

are disjoint, so choices at different degrees are independent and so we can multiply. Therefore

(29)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}M_{\alpha_{i}}=\sum_{\tau}\Big(\prod_{u}s^{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u)\Big)\,M_{\tau}=\sum_{\tau}S_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\tau}M_{\tau},

and the coefficient of

is

, as claimed.
∎

5.
Identities on Polysymmetric Functions

In this section we present our main results. We give several identities between the bases of polysymmetric functions. Many of which fall analogues to the classical identities for symmetric functions, but with twists that come from working in the polysymmetric setting.

Throughout this section, we work with the case
m=1
, as all the proofs follow without loss of generality. This assumption will be used without further mention and the result will be given inherently. In many of the proofs, we also apply the involution
\Omega
to give the dual identities.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any positive integer
d
and
m
,

(71)

\displaystyle E_{d^{m}}=(-1)^{d}\det(H_{(1-i+j)^{m}})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d},

(72)

\displaystyle H_{d^{m}}=(-1)^{d}\det(E_{(1-i+j)^{m}})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}.

Proof.

We aim to prove (
71
). Recall that
\mathcal{H}(t)\mathcal{E}(t)=1
, so the identity

(73)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}H_{i}E_{d-i}=0\quad\text{for }d\geq 1

follows by equating coefficients of
t^{d}
. We proceed by induction on
d
. For the base case
d=1
, equation (
73
) gives
H_{1}E_{0}+H_{0}E_{1}=0
, so

(74)

\displaystyle E_{1}=-H_{1}=-\det(H_{1}),

as desired. Assume the determinantal formula holds for all
d<k
. Consider the matrix

(75)

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&H_{2}&H_{3}&\cdots&H_{d}\\
H_{0}&H_{1}&H_{2}&\cdots&H_{d-1}\\
0&H_{0}&H_{1}&\cdots&H_{d-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&H_{1}\end{bmatrix},

whose determinant is
\det(H_{1-i+j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
. Let
A_{i,j}
denote the submatrix obtained by deleting the
i
th row and
j
th column of
A
. Expanding along the first column, we obtain

(76)

\displaystyle\det A=H_{1}\det A_{1,1}-H_{0}\det A_{2,1}.

By the inductive hypothesis,
\det A_{1,1}=(-1)^{d-1}E_{d-1}
, and since
H_{0}=1
, we have

(77)

\displaystyle\det A=(-1)^{d-1}H_{1}E_{d-1}-\det A_{2,1}.

We expand
\det A_{2,1}
in the same manner, applying the inductive hypothesis to each resulting minor. Continuing recursively through all lower order subdeterminants, we obtain

(78)

\displaystyle\det A=(-1)^{d-1}(H_{1}E_{d-1}+H_{2}E_{d-2}+\cdots+H_{d}E_{0}).

Thus,

(79)

\displaystyle(-1)^{d-1}\det(H_{1-i+j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}H_{i}E_{d-i}.

Substituting (
73
) into this expression gives

(80)

\displaystyle E_{d}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}H_{i}E_{d-i}=(-1)^{d}\det(H_{1-i+j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d},

as claimed. Applying the involution
\Omega
yields (
72
).
∎

The determinantal relationship also holds between the signed analogs of the polysymmetric bases.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any positive integers
d
and
m
, we have

(81)

\displaystyle E^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d}\det\left(H^{+}_{(1-i+j)^{m}}\right)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d},

(82)

\displaystyle H^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d}\det\left(E^{+}_{(1-i+j)^{m}}\right)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}.

Proof.

As in Theorem
5.1
, the result follows by induction using the identity
\mathcal{H}^{+}(t)\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)=1
, replacing
H
and
E
with
H^{+}
and
E^{+}
. The involution
\Omega
yields (
82
).
∎

The next identities are introduced to help complete the relationships among the four bases
{H,E,H^{+},E^{+}}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any positive integers
d
and
m
, we have

(83)

\displaystyle H_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}H_{k^{2m}}E^{+}_{(d-2k)^{m}},

(84)

\displaystyle H^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}H_{k^{2m}}E_{(d-2k)^{m}},

(85)

\displaystyle E_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}E_{k^{2m}}H^{+}_{(d-2k)^{m}},

(86)

\displaystyle E^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}E_{k^{2m}}H_{(d-2k)^{m}}.

Proof.

We begin with the generating function

(87)

\displaystyle\sum_{d}H_{d^{2}}t^{2d}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}(1-x_{i,j}^{2}t^{2i})^{-1}=\prod_{i,j}(1-x_{i,j}t^{i})^{-1}(1+x_{i,j}t^{i})^{-1}.

Multiplying both sides by
\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)
, we obtain

(88)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{d}H_{d^{2}}t^{2d}\right)\left(\sum_{d}E^{+}_{d}t^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}(1-x_{i,j}t^{i})^{-1}.

Thus, we have the identity

(89)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{d}H_{d^{2}}t^{2d}\right)\left(\sum_{d}E^{+}_{d}t^{d}\right)=\sum_{d}H_{d}t^{d}.

Equating coefficients of
t^{d}
gives equation (
83
). Replacing
\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)
with
\mathcal{E}(t)
yields equation (
84
). Finally, applying the involution
\Omega
to each of these identities gives equations (
85
) and (
86
), respectively.
∎

Given a partition
\lambda=(1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\ldots,r^{m_{r}})

denotes the number of times the part

appears in
\lambda

\displaystyle N_{j}(\tau)=\sum_{i}m_{i,j}(\tau),

\displaystyle\kappa_{\tau}=\prod_{j}\binom{N_{j}(\tau)}{m_{1,j}(\tau),\,m_{2,j}(\tau),\,\ldots},

where the product runs over all

for which
m_{i,j}(\tau)>0
for some

.

We now use this coefficient to express the bases

and

in terms of their counterparts.

Theorem 5.4
.

Given a positive integer

(92)

\displaystyle E^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\,\kappa_{\tau}\,E_{m\tau},

(93)

\displaystyle H^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\,\kappa_{\tau}\,H_{m\tau},

where the sum is over all stack partitions
\tau
of

such that each term has multiplicity in
\{1,2\}
and at most one term has multiplicity 2. The notation
m\tau
denotes the stack partition obtained by multiplying each multiplicity in
\tau
by

Proof.

It is well known that the determinant in (

(94)

\displaystyle\det(E_{1-i+j})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}=\sum_{|\alpha|=d}(-1)^{d-\ell(\alpha)}\binom{\ell(\alpha)}{m_{1,1}(\alpha),\,m_{2,1}(\alpha),\,\ldots}\,E_{\alpha}.

Substituting this into the identity (

\displaystyle E^{+}_{d}=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}E_{k^{2}}\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=d-2k}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)}\binom{\ell(\alpha)}{m_{1,1}(\alpha),\,m_{2,1}(\alpha),\,\ldots}\,E_{\alpha}\right).

Expanding the product yields terms of the form

is a stack partition in which each term has multiplicity either

or

, exactly the form appearing in (

\Omega
to both sides then gives (

A similar result follows when expressing

and

in terms of

and

Theorem 5.5
.

Given a positive integer

\displaystyle E^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}(-1)^{N_{2}(\tau)}\kappa_{\tau}H_{m\tau},

\displaystyle H^{+}_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}(-1)^{N_{2}(\tau)}\kappa_{\tau}E_{m\tau},

The proof proceeds analogously to Theorem

We now present the inverse relation, expressing

and

and

Theorem 5.6
.

For any positive integer

, we have

(97)

\displaystyle E_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}H^{+}_{m\tau},

(98)

\displaystyle H_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}E^{+}_{m\tau},

where the sum is over all stack partitions
\tau
of
d
such that each term appears with a distinct power of
2
as its multiplicity. Here,
m\tau
denotes the stack partition obtained by multiplying each multiplicity in
\tau
by

Proof.

It is enough to prove (
98

) then follows by applying the involution
\Omega
.
Let
M_{\lambda}
be a monomial in
H_{r}
, where
\lambda=(d_{1}^{m_{1}},d_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,d_{s}^{m_{s}})
. For each stack

m_{i}
in binary as a sum of distinct powers of

with the collection

(99)

\displaystyle d_{i}^{2^{0}}d_{i}^{2^{1}}\ldots d_{i}^{2^{k_{i}}},

are the powers appearing in the binary decomposition of

for some

(100)

\displaystyle E^{+}_{\mu}=\prod_{k}E^{+}_{n_{k}^{2^{k}}},

∎

To understand the action of the involution
\Omega
on

Theorem 5.8
.

For any positive integers
d
and
m

(106)

\displaystyle H_{d^{m}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}P_{i^{m}}H_{(d-i)^{m}}.

Proof.

Consider the generating function for
P_{d}

(107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i\mid d}i\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{i,j}^{d/i}\right)t^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}ix_{i,j}^{r}t^{ir-1}

(108)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}ix_{i,j}t^{i-1}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(x_{i,j}t^{i})^{r}

(109)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-x_{i,j}t^{i}}\right).

Therefore,

(110)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-x_{i,j}t^{i}}=\frac{d}{dt}\log\mathcal{H}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)}{\mathcal{H}(t)}.

Multiplying both sides by
\mathcal{H}(t)
yields

(111)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)\cdot\mathcal{H}(t)=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t).

Comparing coefficients of
t^{d-1}
on both sides gives our result.
∎

Remark 5.9

Solving for

(112)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}H_{d}t^{d}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{P_{k}t^{k}}{k}\right)=\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E_{d}t^{d}\right)^{-1}.

Replacing

with
\mathcal{E}(t)^{-1}
in equation (

\displaystyle P_{d^{m}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(d-i)E_{i^{m}}H_{(d-i)^{m}}.

Theorem 5.11
.

For any positive integers
d
and
m
, we have

(114)

\displaystyle E_{d^{m}}=-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}P_{i^{m}}\,E_{(d-i)^{m}}.

Proof.

We use the identity
\mathcal{H}(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)^{-1}
, and so

(115)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log\mathcal{H}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}(t)}\right)=-\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(t)}{\mathcal{E}(t)}.

We obtain

(116)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(t)=-\mathcal{P}(t)\mathcal{E}(t).

Equating coefficients of

.

For any stack partition

Definition 5.14

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}H_{d}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}P_{d}\frac{t^{d}}{d}\right)

(131)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{P_{d}t^{d}}{d}\right)

(132)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k_{d}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(P_{d}t^{d})^{k_{d}}}{d^{k_{d}}k_{d}!}

(133)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{1}{z_{\lambda}}P_{\lambda}t^{|\lambda|}.

Equating the coefficients of
t^{d}
on both sides, corresponding to partitions
\lambda
of size
d
, yields the desired identity.
∎

to

and

gives a structure similar to that of their counterparts.

Theorem 5.16
.

Given any positive integers
d
and

, we have

(134)

\displaystyle E_{d^{m}}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}z_{\lambda}^{-1}z_{\mu}^{-1}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}P_{m\lambda}P_{(2m)\mu},

(135)

\displaystyle H_{d^{m}}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}z_{\lambda}^{-1}z_{\mu}^{-1}(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}P_{m\lambda}P_{(2m)\mu},

where
\lambda
and
\mu
range over partitions such that
|\lambda|+2|\mu|=d
. Here,
m\lambda
denotes the partition obtained by multiplying each part of
\lambda
by
m
, and similarly for
(2m)\mu
.

Proof.

We prove (
134
), the identity (

) follows by applying
\Omega
.

(136)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}H_{d^{2}}t^{2d}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P_{k^{2}}\frac{t^{2k}}{k}\right).

By equations (

) and (

), we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}P_{h^{2}}\frac{t^{2h}}{h}\right)\cdot\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E_{d}^{+}t^{d}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k}\right).

Solving for the generating function of

, we get

(138)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E_{d}^{+}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}P_{h^{2}}\frac{t^{2h}}{h}\right).

Expanding both exponentials yields

(139)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}E_{d}^{+}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\lambda}z_{\lambda}^{-1}P_{d\lambda}t^{|\lambda|}\right)\left(\sum_{\mu}z_{\mu}^{-1}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}P_{2\mu}t^{2|\mu|}\right).

Equating the coefficient of

, we obtain

(140)

\displaystyle E_{d}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}z_{\lambda}^{-1}z_{\mu}^{-1}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}P_{\lambda}P_{2\mu}.

where
\lambda
and

range over partitions such that

, which is our result.
∎

Theorem 5.17
.

Given positive integers
d
and
m
, we have

(141)

\displaystyle P_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}(-1)^{\ell(\tau)-1}\,\frac{d}{\ell(\tau)}\cdot\binom{\ell(\tau)}{m_{1,r}(\tau),m_{2,r}(\tau),\ldots}E^{+}_{m\tau},

(142)

\displaystyle P_{d^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}(-1)^{\ell(\tau)}\frac{d}{\ell(\tau)}\cdot\binom{\ell(\tau)}{m_{1,r}(\tau),m_{2,r}(\tau),\ldots}H^{+}_{m\tau},

where the sums range over all stack partitions
\tau=(d_{1}^{r},d_{2}^{r},\ldots,d_{s}^{r})
of
d
in which every part appears with the same multiplicity, and that multiplicity is a power of
2
.

Proof.

It suffices to only prove for (
141
) since (
142
) follows by applying the involution
\Omega
. Take (
138
) and apply the logarithm on both sides, we obtain

(143)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}P_{d}\frac{t^{d}}{d}-\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}P_{d^{2}}\frac{t^{2d}}{d}=\log(\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)).

We now take the derivative of both sides, we obtain

(144)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}P_{d}t^{d-1}-\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}2P_{d^{2}}t^{2d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)\right)^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)}

(145)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{E}^{+}(t)\right)^{\prime}\mathcal{H}^{+}(t)

(146)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}iE^{+}_{i}t^{i-1}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}H^{+}_{j}t^{j}\right).

Next, we apply the determinant expansion for (
82
) to
H^{+}
, then the LHS becomes

(147)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}iE^{+}_{i}t^{i-1}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{|\lambda|=j}(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1,1}(\lambda),\,m_{2,1}(\lambda),\,\ldots}\,E^{+}_{\lambda}\right)t^{j}\right).

Equating the same powers of
t^{d-1}
for
d>0
, we obtain the following

(148)

\displaystyle P_{d}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}i\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1,1}(\lambda),\,m_{2,1}(\lambda),\,\ldots}E_{\nu}^{+}+2P_{(d/2)^{2}},\,\,&\text{if $d$ is even,}\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\lambda}(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}i\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1,1}(\lambda),\,m_{2,1}(\lambda),\,\ldots}E_{\nu}^{+},&\text{if $d$ is odd},\end{cases}

where the first summation ranges over all integers
i,j
such that
i+j=d
with
i>0
, and the second summation ranges over all partitions
\lambda
with
|\lambda|=j
, with
\nu=\lambda\cup i
. Notice that
i>0
implies
\ell(\nu)=\ell(\lambda)+1
, so
(-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}=(-1)^{\ell(\nu)-1}
.

One can see that

(149)

\displaystyle i\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1,r}(\lambda),\,m_{2,r}(\lambda),\,\ldots}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\ell(\nu)}\binom{\ell(\nu)}{m_{1,r}(\nu),\,\ldots,m_{i,r}(\nu)-1,\ldots}

(150)

\displaystyle=\frac{im_{i,r}(\nu)}{\ell(\nu)}\binom{\ell(\nu)}{m_{1,r}(\nu),\,\ldots,m_{i,r}(\nu),\ldots},

since
d=\sum im_{i,1}(\nu)
, we have

(151)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{im_{i,r}(\nu)}{\ell(\nu)}\binom{\ell(\nu)}{m_{1,r}(\nu),\,\ldots,m_{i,r}(\nu),\ldots}=\frac{d}{\ell(\nu)}\cdot\binom{\ell(\nu)}{m_{1,r}(\nu),\,\ldots,m_{i,r}(\nu),\ldots}.

The sign remains consistent for the sum since we are using the same partition throughout. This completes the argument for the odd case as we sum over all partitions of
d
. The even case follows similarly, except for the appearance of the additional term
2P_{(d/2)^{2}}
, which accounts for all stacked partitions where each part appears with multiplicity 2. Due to the recursive nature of this term, it further captures stacked partitions where the multiplicities are
4
,
8
, and so on, continuing through all powers of
2
that divide
d
. The coefficient
2
ensures that the factor in front remains
d
. This recursive expansion thus captures exactly the stack partitions in which each part appears with multiplicity a power of
2
, as desired.
∎
