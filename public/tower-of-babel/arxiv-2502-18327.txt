Sparsity covariance: a source of uncertainty when estimating correlation functions with a discrete sample of observations in the sky
Pierre Fleury

Abstract
Cosmological observables rely heavily on summary statistics such as two-point correlation functions. In many practical cases (e.g. the weak-lensing cosmic shear), those correlation functions are estimated from a finite, discrete sample of measurements that are randomly distributed in the sky. The result then inevitably depends on the sample at hand, regardless of any experimental noise. This sample dependence is a source of uncertainty for cosmological observables which I call sparsity covariance. This article proposes a mathematical definition and a generic method to compute sparsity covariance. It is then applied to the concrete case of cosmic shear, showing that sparsity covariance mostly enhances shape noise, whose amplitude is determined by the apparent ellipticity of galaxies rather than their intrinsic ellipticity. In general, sparsity covariance is non-negligible when the signal-to-noise ratio of individual measurements in the sample is comparable to, or larger than, unity. Keywords: cosmology; statistics; covariance matrix; large-scale structure; cosmic shear

Introduction

According to the current understanding of cosmology, inhomogeneities and their structure originated from quantum fluctuations in the early Universe
(
Peter & Uzan, 2013
)
. In this picture, physical fields may be seen as realisations of some Gaussian random fields that have subsequently evolved according to the laws of physics.

In order to extract the properties of those primordial random fields, it is customary to rely on summary statistics, such as the two-point correlation of astronomical measurements. In many practical cases, the correlation function is estimated from a discrete sample of measurements corresponding to observations made in more or less random directions in the sky. Examples include the weak-lensing cosmic shear
(
Kaiser & Squires, 1993
;
Asgari et al., 2021
;
Secco et al., 2022
;
Dalal et al., 2023
)
, based on the two-point correlation of the apparent ellipticity of galaxies; baryon-acoustic-oscillation measurements exploiting the two-point correlation of the Lyman-
\alpha
absorption in quasar spectra
(
Delubac et al., 2015
;
Adame et al., 2025
)
; or measurements of the stochastic gravitational-wave background with pulsar timing arrays (PTA)
(
Hellings & Downs, 1983
;
Agazie et al., 2023
;
Reardon et al., 2023
;
Antoniadis et al., 2023
;
Xu et al., 2023
)
.

Such estimates of summary statistics inevitably depend on the samples at hand, and more specifically on their distribution in the sky. This dependence is a source of uncertainty that is independent of experimental noise. In the context of cosmic shear, for example, even if galaxies were intrinsically spherical, so that we could perfectly measure the effect of weak lensing on their apparent shape, the measured two-point correlation function would still depend on their exact distribution in the sky, resulting in an uncertainty that is distinct from shape noise, cosmic variance or even source-clustering bias
(
Bernardeau, 1998
)
.

This statistical source of uncertainty is what I shall refer to as
sparsity covariance
from now on. The present article aims to precisely define sparsity covariance, establish a general mathematical framework to compute it, and apply it to the concrete case of cosmic shear for illustration.

To the best of my knowledge, sparsity covariance has never been studied in the literature. However, the general question of how to estimate summary statistics with discrete data sets has received some attention. In the context of galaxy surveys,
Baleato Lizancos & White (2024)
;
Tessore et al. (2025)
;
Wolz et al. (2025)
developed methods to estimate angular power spectra directly from discrete observations, and
Hall & Tessore (2025)
considered the effect of pixelisating such discrete data; the bias of the underlying estimators was computed, but not their covariance. In the context of PTA,
Pitrou & Cusin (2024)
analysed the bias on the angular spectrum of the Hellings--Downs correlation due to the finite number of observed pulsars; in this case, however, the issue lies specifically in the transformation from real space to harmonic space -- if I measure the correlation function of a random field at some specific angular separations, how well can I reconstruct its multipoles?
This is quite different from sparsity covariance.

In fact, and perhaps counter-intuitively, there is no sparsity covariance for PTAs in the limit of long pulsar monitoring times. In other words, in the absence of noise, the Hellings–Downs correlation is measured exactly for each pair of pulsars, because temporal averaging plays the role of cosmic averaging. This may seem at odds with the notion of pulsar variance introduced by
Allen (2023)
, but the latter appears for a finite number of gravitational-wave sources, which makes the signal statistically anisotropic.

FAQ

•

What is sparsity covariance?
When estimating the statistical properties of a field that is measured on a finite, discrete sample, the result necessarily depends on this very sample. Sparsity covariance quantifies the uncertainty specifically associated with the
discreteness
of the sample. This article focuses on sparsity covariance in the estimation of two-point correlation functions.

•

How is it different to cosmic covariance and sample covariance?
Cosmic or sample covariance refer to the uncertainty in any estimate of the statistical property of a physical field due to the finite
extension
of the observable universe, or of the survey. Sparsity covariance thus adds to it, due to the fact that such a finite region of the Universe is randomly sampled. This distinction is made explicit in
eq.

8
, and illustrated in
fig.

1
.

•

How is it different to shot noise?
Shot noise emerges when measuring a physical quantity by counting things (electrons, photons, galaxies, etc.); the result is then subject to Poisson fluctuations. Sparsity covariance applies to quantities that may be intrinsically continuous, but that are
evaluated
on a discrete sample. This distinction is, arguably, a mere matter of terminology.

•

When should I care?
Sparsity variance is typically
\sim\xi^{2}(0)/P
, where
\xi
is the field’s correlation function and
P
the number of pairs of measurements used to estimate it. It is non-negligible compared to cosmic variance if
\xi^{2}(0)/P
is comparable or larger than the celestial average of
\xi^{2}(\theta)
. In the presence of noise, sparsity covariance matters if the signal-to-noise ratio of the individual measurements constituting the sample is comparable to unity, irrespective of the number of measurements.

•

Does it matter for cosmic shear?
Since the weak-lensing cosmic shear is estimated on a discrete sample of galaxy observations, it is, in principle, subject to sparsity covariance. In sec.
3
, I show that sparsity covariance acts as an extra contribution to shape noise, implying that its net amplitude should be determined by the
apparent
, rather than intrinsic, ellipticity of galaxies. This is already the case in practice, because the intrinsic ellipticity of galaxies is not observable; therefore, sparsity covariance is already, though accidentally, taken into account in cosmic shear analyses.

2
What is sparsity covariance?

This section defines sparsity covariance, and introduces the relevant conceptual and mathematical tools to compute it in practice. I choose to focus on a specific problem, namely the estimation of the two-point correlation function of a random scalar field on the two-sphere, as this strikes a balance between mathematical simplicity and practical relevance.

2.1
Set-up: a finite sample of observations of a field

Consider a realisation of a random field
f
on the celestial sphere, for which we have a finite, discrete set of noiseless observations
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N})
made in different directions in the sky.
We aim to estimate the two-point correlation function
\xi
of
f
from this discrete set of observations, as well as the uncertainty on this estimate.

Throughout this section, I shall make the following
assumptions
:

1.

f
is a real-value scalar field on the two-sphere,
f:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
; as such, it only depends on the direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, or the corresponding polar coordinates
(\theta,\varphi)
in which it is evaluated.

2.

f
has vanishing expectation value,
\left\langle f\right\rangle=0
.

3.

f
is statistically isotropic: its correlation between two directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
only depends on the angle
\theta_{12}
formed by those directions,
\left\langle f(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})f(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})\right\rangle=\xi(\theta_{12})
.

The above assumptions are made for the sake of simplicity, so as to efficiently convey the main point. In sec.
3
, with the complete example of cosmic shear, I shall show how to relax assumption
1
and work with a complex, spin-two field with an additional redshift dependence.

2.2
Estimator of the correlation function

In order to estimate the two-point correlation function of
f
, we may consider pairs
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j})
of directions and bin them depending on their angular separation
\theta_{ij}
. Let us take
A
such bins
[\theta_{a},\theta_{a+1})_{a=1,\ldots,A}
, and denote with

\mathcal{P}_{a}\equiv\left\{(ij)\;|\;\theta_{ij}\in[\theta_{a},\theta_{a+1})\right\}

(1)

the
symmetrised
set of pairs of observations that fall into the
a
th
bin of angular separations. ‘Symmetrised’ means here that we are double-counting each pair, in the sense that if
(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
, then
(ji)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
too. This choice eases the subsequent calculations. Hence, the cardinality of
\mathcal{P}_{a}
,

P_{a}\equiv|\mathcal{P}_{a}|\ ,

(2)

is
twice
the number of pairs of directions in the
a
th
bin.

In such conditions, a simple estimator of the two-point correlation
\xi
of
f
in the
a
th
bin is

\hat{\xi}_{a}\equiv\frac{1}{P_{a}}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}}f_{i}f_{j}\ ,

(3)

with
f_{i}\equiv f(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})
. A more general definition would involve non-trivial weights assigned to each direction, or each pair of directions. A suitable choice of weights may lower the overall variance of the estimator
(
Pitrou & Cusin, 2024
)
, but this goes beyond the scope of the simple presentation intended here.

2.3
General idea of sparsity covariance

Sparsity covariance stems from the intuitive fact that the estimated correlation
\hat{\xi}_{a}
depends on the finite, sparse sample of directions
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N})
in which the field is actually observed. As we only make use of a single, finite sample, this sample-dependence is thus a source of uncertainty in our estimation of the field’s correlation. In the theoretical limit of an infinite sample,
N\to\infty
, sparsity covariance vanishes if the sky is evenly covered.

This notion of covariance must be distinguished from the so-called cosmic covariance, which comes from the fact that we observe a single sky, and hence a single
realisation
of the random field
f
. In particular, cosmic covariance would persist even if we had an infinite sample.

Both cosmic and sparsity covariance are sources of uncertainty when estimating the correlation function from a finite sample of a single realisation of the field.

2.4
Definition and expression of sparsity covariance

I now turn to a more technical discussion, explicitly defining sparsity covariance and deriving an analytical expression to predict its value for any given application.

2.4.1
Cosmic and sample averaging

Since this discussion involves two distinct notions of randomness, I must start with defining the corresponding notions of average. The first one, denoted with
\left\langle\ldots\right\rangle
, is the averaging over realisations of the field. Since we eventually aim at applications in cosmology, where
f
is some cosmic field,
\left\langle\ldots\right\rangle
may by extension correspond to averaging over realisations of the Universe, and hence be referred to as
cosmic averaging
.
In practice, cosmic averaging will only be used to define correlation functions, such as in
\xi(\theta_{12})=\left\langle f(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})f(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})\right\rangle
.

If we are interested in a quantity
X
that explicitly depends on observations made in a discrete set of directions
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n})
in the sky – such as the estimator of the correlation function defined in
eq.

3
– then
sample averaging
corresponds to an average over all such sets of directions,

\left\llbracket X\right\rrbracket\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}\;p(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}|\mathcal{U})\,X(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n})\ ,

(4)

where
p(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}|\mathcal{U})
denotes the probability density of the directions in the sample, given a realisation
\mathcal{U}
of the Universe. We indeed expect sampling to generally depend on some cosmic field. For example, if our observable
X
is related to the Lyman-
\alpha
absorption in quasars, then
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n})
stands for the set of quasar lines of sight, which are more likely to be observed in regions of the sky where those sources are more abundant, which in turn correlates with the overall density field in
\mathcal{U}
. Another realisation of the Universe would therefore lead to a different probability distribution for those lines of sights.

Having said that, however, I shall make another simplifying
assumption
in the remainder of this article. Namely, I assume that the observation directions are independent and randomly distributed with a homogeneous distribution across the footprint
\mathcal{F}
of some survey,

p(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}|\mathcal{U})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\ ,\quad p(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})=\begin{cases}\Omega^{-1}&\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{F},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(5)

where
\Omega
is the solid angle covered by
\mathcal{F}
, i.e. the survey area. Doing so, I choose to neglect what is usually referred to as clustering bias in the cosmology literature – see e.g.
Bernardeau (1998)
in the context of cosmic shear. As we shall see in the following, this simplified framework is sufficient to capture the essence of sparsity covariance while making practical calculations much easier to handle.

A notable advantage of the assumption of statistically homogeneous sampling is that, since the underlying distribution does not depend on the cosmic realisation
\mathcal{U}
any longer, the two averaging procedures commute,

\left\llbracket\left\langle X\right\rangle\right\rrbracket=\left\langle\left\llbracket X\right\rrbracket\right\rangle.

(6)

2.4.2
Defining cosmic and sparsity covariances

Equipped with the distinct notions of cosmic and sample averaging, we are ready to define the cosmic and sparsity covariances of the estimator (
3
) of the correlation function. The full covariance matrix is defined as the average of the product minus the product of averages; for any two angular bins
a,b
,

\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})\equiv\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle-\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle.

(7)

Adding and subtracting the term
\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rrbracket\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle
, we may then split the above into two contributions,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket-\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rrbracket\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle}_{\text{sparsity covariance}~\mathrm{SCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rrbracket\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle-\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle\big\langle\big\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rrbracket\big\rangle}_{\text{cosmic covariance}~\mathrm{CCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})}\ ,

(8)

which defines the two types of covariance.

Let me take a moment to connect those expressions to the illustration of
fig.

1
. For a given realisation of the field,
\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket
is nothing but the binned correlation function of that realisation; it therefore corresponds to the blue solid line in each bottom panel of
fig.

1
. It follows that
\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}^{2}\rrbracket-\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket^{2}
is the variance of the fluctuations of the red semi-transparent lines around each blue line, which is sparsity variance. To be really precise,
\langle\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}^{2}\rrbracket-\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket^{2}\rangle
is the cosmic average of sparsity variance, that is the average jitter across realisations of the field. Cosmic covariance is easier to identify: since
\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket
corresponds to the blue curves,
\langle\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket^{2}\rangle-\langle\llbracket\hat{\xi}_{a}\rrbracket\rangle^{2}
is simply the variance of their fluctuations around the black dashed line.

Albeit theoretically eloquent, the separation of terms in
eq.

8
is not necessarily the most convenient one in practice. An alternative is to recall that cosmic covariance is the contribution to
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})
that persists when the sample size goes to infinity,

\mathrm{CCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})\ ,

(9)

while sparsity covariance is the remainder,

\mathrm{SCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})\equiv\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})-\mathrm{CCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})\ .

(10)

In the following, I derive analytical expressions for cosmic and sampling covariances. As it turns out, the easiest path starts with using the (generally approximate) commutation of cosmic and sample averaging, in order to rewrite the full covariance of
eq.

7
as

\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{b})=\big\llbracket\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rangle\big\rrbracket-\big\llbracket\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rangle\big\rrbracket\big\llbracket\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rangle\big\rrbracket.

(11)

The advantage of this expression is that the cosmic average is applied before the sample average, which henceforth allows me to work with deterministic functions instead of random fields.

2.4.3
Expectation value of the estimator

As an illustration of the above point, let me first consider the expectation value of
\hat{\xi}
. Its cosmic average reads

\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{P_{a}}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}}\xi_{ij}\ ,

(12)

with

\xi_{ij}=\xi(\theta_{ij})\equiv\left\langle f_{i}f_{j}\right\rangle.

(13)

When averaging
eq.

12
over samples, the number of pairs
P_{a}
is in principle subject to Poisson-like fluctuations. In the limit of large numbers, however, such fluctuations may be neglected and
P_{a}
may be identified with its mean value. Besides, all the pairs
(ij)
are statistically equivalent. In other words,
i,j
are dummy indices that may be arbitrarily replaced with
1,2
, so that the right-hand side reduces to a single term,

\big\llbracket\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\big\rangle\big\rrbracket=\xi_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}\ ,

(14)

where the shorthand notation
\xi_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}
refers to the directional average of
\xi_{12}=\xi(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})
for all possible configurations of
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})
such that
\theta_{12}
falls in the
a
th
bin.

An explicit integration scheme then consists in taking
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
to cover the whole
\mathcal{F}
, while
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
spans an annulus
\mathcal{A}_{a}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})
drawn around
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
such that
\theta_{a}\leq\theta_{12}<\theta_{a+1}
, provided this region remains within
\mathcal{F}
(see
fig.

2
). If I denote with
\Omega_{a}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})
the solid angle covered by
\mathcal{A}_{a}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})\cap\mathcal{F}
, then

\xi_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}=\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{a}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})\cap\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}}{\Omega_{a}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1})}\;\xi(\theta_{12})\ .

(15)

For a full-sky survey (
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{S}^{2}
),
\mathcal{A}_{a}
is always contained in
\mathcal{F}
, so that the
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
dependence drops; I may then place
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
at the North pole
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
to get the simpler expression

\xi_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}=\int_{\cos\theta_{a+1}}^{\cos\theta_{a}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{\cos\theta_{a}-\cos\theta_{a+1}}\,\xi(\theta)\ ,

(16)

since
\Omega_{a}=2\pi(\cos\theta_{a}-\cos\theta_{a+1})
. More generally, if one focuses on small angular separations compared to the survey footprint, the annulus only gets truncated in the immediate vicinity of
\partial\mathcal{F}
, and one may simply neglect such boundary effects. This may be referred to as the
pseudo-full-sky approximation
. Furthermore, for small angular separations
\theta_{a}\ll 1
, one may also neglect the curvature of the sky so that

\xi_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}\approx\frac{1}{\theta_{a+1}-\theta_{a}}\int_{\theta_{a}}^{\theta_{a+1}}\mathrm{d}\theta\;\theta\,\xi(\theta)\ ,

(17)

which is the
flat-sky approximation
.

2.4.4
The mathematical origin of sparsity covariance

Let me now tackle the quadratic term of
eq.

11
; starting with cosmic averaging, I have

\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{P_{a}P_{b}}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}}\sum_{(kl)\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}\Xi_{ijkl}

(18)

where

\Xi_{ijkl}=\Xi(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{l})\equiv\left\langle f_{i}f_{j}f_{k}f_{l}\right\rangle

(19)

denotes the four-point correlation function of
f
, which depends on the shape and size of the quadrilateral formed by the four directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{l}
.

Applying the sample-averaging operator, where I neglect again the Poisson fluctuations of the number of pairs
P_{a},P_{b}
, I am left with

\big\llbracket\big\langle\hat{\xi}_{a}\hat{\xi}_{b}\big\rangle\big\rrbracket=\frac{1}{P_{a}P_{b}}\left\llbracket\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}}\sum_{(kl)\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}\Xi_{ijkl}\right\rrbracket.

(20)

At this point, it is crucial to notice that, contrary to
eq.

12
,
not all the terms of the double sum are equivalent
.
This is because the pairs
(ij)
and
(kl)
are drawn from the same sample of directions
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N})
, and hence they sometimes have one direction in common – sometimes even two if
a=b
.

For instance, in the sample depicted in
fig.

3
, the pair of directions
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2})
falls in the
a
th
bin, while
(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{4})
falls in the
b
th
bin, so
(12)\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
and
(14)\in\mathcal{P}_{b}
. Thus, in this specific configuration, the double sum of
eq.

20
features the term
\Xi_{1214}
. Averaging over all
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{4}

2.6
Discussion

It is interesting to examine the result of
41
in the case where the correlation function quickly decreases with angular separation. In that case, the sparsity contribution to the variance of
\hat{\xi}_{a}
is indeed dominated by

\mathrm{SCov}(\hat{\xi}_{a},\hat{\xi}_{a})\approx\frac{2}{N^{2}}\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{a}}\,\xi^{2}(0)=\frac{2}{P_{a}}\,\sigma_{f}^{4}\ ,

(44)

where
\sigma_{f}
denotes the standard deviation of
f
.

The interpretation of this result is quite straightforward, for
\hat{\xi}_{a}
is the average of
f_{i}f_{j}
over
P_{a}
pairs. From one pair to the next, both
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
typically varies by
\sigma_{f}
, so that their product typically varies by
\sigma_{f}^{2}
; and since there are
P_{a}/2
actual pairs in a bin, the standard deviation of their arithmetic mean is
\sigma_{f}^{2}/\sqrt{P_{a}/2}
.

The case discussed here, where the correlation function quickly decreases with the angular separation, is particularly relevant to cosmology. The presence of
\xi(0)
in the sparsity contribution thus tends to boost it with respect to the cosmic contribution. As a rule of thumb, sparsity variance must be taken into account if the number of pairs
P_{a}
in a bin is not much larger than
\xi^{2}(0)/(\text{mean $\xi^{2}$ over $\mathcal{F}$})
.

2.7
Comparison with shot noise

The concept of sparsity covariance presented here may be reminiscent of shot noise. Shot noise emerges as one attempts to measure the macroscopic features of a physical quantity that is discrete at a more fundamental level. Pressure, in a fluid, is perturbed by discreteness of the fluid’s constituents which is responsible for Brownian motion; electric current, in a conductor, is subject to fluctuations due to the discreteness of electrons; similarly, the intensity of a laser fluctuates due to the corpuscular nature of photons. In physical cosmology, shot noise typically appears as one aims to estimate the matter density contrast from galaxy number counts.

Although sparsity covariance encodes fluctuations that are related to the discreteness of a sample, I believe that it is conceptually distinct from shot noise. The key difference is that, contrary to, e.g., galaxy number counts, the general field
f
considered in this section is not fundamentally discrete: it is a continuous field that is
evaluated
in a discrete set of directions.

Having said that, the techniques employed in this article would also allow one to compute the contribution of shot noise in, e.g, galaxy-clustering or galaxy–galaxy lensing estimators. I refer the interested reader to
Duboscq et al. (2025)
for a detailed illustration.

3.1.1
Weak gravitational lensing

Gravity bends light rays. In the immediate vicinity of matter concentrations, this phenomenon can lead to significant distortions in the observed images of distant sources. Far from such concentrations, distortions are still present but typically unnoticeable by eye. This is the weak-lensing regime that applies to the vast majority of observed sources.

In particular, when observing a galaxy at cosmological distances, the deflection of light first implies that the galaxy is not observed where it actually is; second, and most importantly, the image of the galaxy is slightly distorted. This is because the image is composed of many rays that experience an inhomogeneous gravitational field as they propagate from the source to the observer, and hence are not deflected identically.

At leading order in the gradients of the gravitational field, the distortions of an image reduces to shear: a circular source would appear slightly elliptical. As a consequence, if we denote with
\varepsilon_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
the intrinsic ellipticity of a galaxy,
4
4

4

We use a complex number to encode both the amplitude and the orientation of the ellipticity. A popular choice is
\varepsilon=(a-b)/(a+b)\exp(2\mathrm{i}\psi)
, where
a,b
are the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the ellipse, and
\psi
the angle formed by the major axis with a fiducial direction
(
Bartelmann & Schneider, 2001
)
.
then its observed ellipticity reads

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}+\gamma\ ,

(45)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
represents the shear distortion due to weak gravitational lensing.

Technically speaking, shear is a spin-two field on the celestial sphere, which also depends on the redshift
z_{*}
of the source to which it is applied,
\gamma(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},z_{*})
. It is convenient to express it in terms of a scalar potential
\Psi(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},z_{*})
as

\gamma(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},z_{*})=\frac{1}{2}\,\eth^{2}\Psi(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},z_{*})\ ,

(46)

where
\eth
is called the edth (or spin-raising) derivative, which may be seen as an angular derivative acting on the
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
variable. See e.g.
Castro et al. (2005)
for details.

In a spatially flat cosmological background with perturbations devoid of anisotropic stress, the lensing potential associated with those perturbations reads

\Psi(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}},z_{*})=2\int_{0}^{\chi_{*}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\chi}\;q(\chi;\chi_{*})\,\Phi(\eta_{0}-\chi,\chi,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})\ ,

(47)

where
\chi
denotes the comoving distance from the observer, so that
\chi_{*}=\chi(z_{*})
is the comoving distance to the source;
\Phi(\eta,\chi,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})
is the Bardeen potential, that is the gravitational potential produced by inhomogeneities in the distribution of matter in the Universe, evaluated at conformal time
\eta
and comoving distance
\chi
along a direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
in the sky;
\eta_{0}
is conformal time today; and

q(\chi;\chi_{*})\equiv\frac{\chi_{*}-\chi}{\chi_{*}}

(48)

is a dimensionless integration kernel.

Together,
eqs.

46
and
47
show that
\gamma
is sensitive to cosmological perturbations all along the line of sight from the source to the observer down its past light cone. Therefore, measurements of the weak-lensing shear tell us about the matter distribution in the Universe.

3.1.2
Cosmic shear estimators

For individual galaxies, it is impossible to distinguish the contribution of intrinsic ellipticity from the contribution of the weak-lensing shear. However, while galaxies are randomly oriented – modulo intrinsic alignments, see e.g.
Kirk et al. (2015)
– shear exhibits correlations over large scales, which offers us the possibility to measure its effect in a statistical manner. In practice, this can be done using the two-point correlation function of
\varepsilon
, so as to extract the two-point correlation function of
\gamma
. Since
\varepsilon
is a spin-two quantity, and since galaxies are distributed in redshift, the definition of such a correlation function is more subtle than in the scalar case of sec.
2

The construction goes as follows (see

\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\pm}\equiv\frac{1}{P_{aIJ}}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}}\left(\varepsilon^{+}_{ij}\varepsilon^{+}_{ji}\pm\varepsilon^{\times}_{ij}\varepsilon^{\times}_{ji}\right),

with

. The fact that two different correlation functions,
\xi^{+}
and

\xi^{+}
captures small-scale correlations, when pairs of galaxies are subject to the same shear field,
\gamma_{i}\approx\gamma_{j}
and hence get aligned along the same direction. On the other hand,
\xi^{-}
captures the tangential alignment of galaxies all around matter overdensities, which translates as
\gamma_{ij}\approx\gamma_{ij}^{*}
; it is therefore more sensitive to correlations on larger scales.

3.1.3
Expectation value of the estimators

Just like in sec.
2
, one computes the expectation value of the estimator by first applying cosmic averaging, and then sample averaging. When taking the cosmic average of the summand in
eq.

51
,
\varepsilon^{+}_{ij}\varepsilon^{+}_{ji}\pm\varepsilon^{\times}_{ij}\varepsilon^{\times}_{ji}
, the contribution of intrinsic ellipticities vanishes if one assumes their orientations to be uncorrelated for
i\neq j
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\langle\varepsilon^{+}_{ij}\varepsilon^{+}_{ji}\pm\varepsilon^{\times}_{ij}\varepsilon^{\times}_{ji}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\gamma^{+}_{ij}\gamma^{+}_{ji}\pm\gamma^{\times}_{ij}\gamma^{\times}_{ji}\right\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle=\xi^{\pm}_{ij}\equiv\xi^{\pm}(\theta_{ij};z_{i},z_{j})\ ,

(53)

where
\xi^{\pm}(\theta,z_{1},z_{2})
are the correlation functions of shear only, for two lines of sights ending at redshifts
z_{1},z_{2}
and separated by an angle
\theta
.

The theoretical expression of
\xi^{\pm}(\theta,z_{1},z_{2})
is more conveniently established in harmonic space – see e.g.
Kilbinger et al. (2017)
for details. As one decomposes the shear field into spin-weight spherical harmonics, the correlation functions read

\xi^{\pm}(\theta;z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)d^{\ell}_{\pm 22}(\theta)\,C_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

(54)

in terms of the shear angular power spectrum
C_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})
, where
d^{\ell}_{s_{1}s_{2}}(\theta)
are elements of the Wigner
d
-matrices. In the flat-sky approximation, i.e. for
\theta\ll 1
, these matrices can be expressed in terms of Bessel functions as
d^{\ell}_{22}(\theta)\approx J_{0}(\ell\theta)
and
d^{\ell}_{-22}(\theta)\approx J_{4}(\ell\theta)
, and
\ell
may be treated as a continuous variable to get

\xi^{\pm}(\theta;z_{1},z_{2})\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\;\ell J_{0/4}(\ell\theta)\,C_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})\ .

(55)

The advantage of the angular power spectrum
C_{\ell}
is that it is more easily connected to that of the lensing potential,
C^{\Psi}_{\ell}
.
Equation

46
indeed implies

C_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{4}\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}\,C^{\Psi}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

(56)

which, together with
eq.

47
, Poisson’s equation and Limber’s approximation, eventually yields

C_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})\approx\left(\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{H_{0}^{2}}{c^{2}}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\chi\;[1+z(\chi)]^{2}\\
\times q(\chi;\chi_{1})q(\chi;\chi_{2})P_{\mathrm{m}}\left[z(\chi),\frac{\ell+1/2}{\chi}\right],

(57)

where
P_{\mathrm{m}}(z,k)
is the matter power spectrum at redshift
z
and comoving scale
k
;
H_{0}
the Hubble constant;
c
the speed of light in vacuum, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
the matter density today normalised by the cosmic critical density.

The second step consists in averaging over samples. The procedure is essentially the same as in subsubsec.
2.4.3
, except that galaxies are distributed in three dimensions. In practice, this means that one must average over all possible redshifts
z_{i},z_{j}
on top of angular positions for pairs
(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}
. Let
p_{I}(z)
denote the probability density function of redshift in the
I
th
bin. Assuming the latter to be independent of the angular positions of galaxies, it is straightforward to get

\left\llbracket\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\pm}\right\rangle\right\rrbracket=\xi^{\pm}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\;p_{I}(z_{1})\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\;p_{J}(z_{2})\\
\times\int_{\cos\theta_{a+1}}^{\cos\theta_{a}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}{\cos\theta_{a}-\cos\theta_{a+1}}\;\xi^{\pm}(\theta;z_{1},z_{2})\ .

(58)

In the above, I generalised the notation
X_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{a}}
of sec.
2
into
X_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}}
by adding redshift integration.

Remark

In the cosmic-shear literature, it customary to integrate over redshift much earlier in the calculation. In
Kilbinger et al. (2017)
for instance, this is done in the very first equation, by defining an effective lensing potential, and hence an effective shear, that is already marginalised over the galaxy sample’s redshifts, and hence only depends on the direction of observation
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
. This leads to the binned angular power spectra

\displaystyle C^{IJ}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\;p_{I}(z_{1})\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\;p_{J}(z_{2})\;C_{\ell}^{\gamma}

(59)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{2}\,H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\chi\;[1+z(\chi)]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times q_{I}(\chi)q_{J}(\chi)P_{\mathrm{m}}\left[z(\chi),\frac{\ell+1/2}{\chi}\right],

(60)

with

q_{I}(\chi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\;p_{I}(z)\,q[\chi;\chi_{*}(z)]

(61)

Albeit technically correct, this approach has, in my opinion, the pedagogical disadvantage of putting the variables
z
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
on different footings. This may be misleading, in particular, when dealing with sample averaging in the calculation of the covariance matrix.

3.2
Full covariance matrix of cosmic shear

The covariance matrix is defined as

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right)\\
=\left\llbracket\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma}\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right\rangle\right\rrbracket-\left\llbracket\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma}\right\rangle\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right\rangle\right\rrbracket,

(62)

with
\sigma,\eta=\pm
. It was first computed by
Schneider et al. (2002)
;
a more modern and comprehensive treatment is presented by
Reischke et al. (2024)
, whose results are implemented in the
OneCovariance
code,
6
6

6

https://onecovariance.readthedocs.io
but still apparently miss the sparsity term.

I propose an updated derivation here. Following the same rationale as in sec.
2
, I shall not attempt to calculate sparsity covariance on its own, but rather extract it from the full final expression. However, since the equations will quickly become heavy, and since most of the relevant expressions were already presented in
Schneider et al. (2002)
, I shall mostly describe the general method and give the final results in the main text; further details on the intermediate steps can be found in appendix

.

The expectation values of
\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}

62

3.2.1
Cosmic averaging

Similarly to the simple scalar case of sec.
2
, the cosmic expectation value of the quadratic term reads

\left\langle\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma}\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{P_{aIJ}P_{bKL}}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}}\sum_{(kl)\in\mathcal{P}_{bKL}}\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}\ ,

(63)

with the four-point correlation function

\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}\equiv\left\langle\left(\varepsilon^{+}_{ij}\varepsilon^{+}_{ji}+\sigma\varepsilon^{\times}_{ij}\varepsilon^{\times}_{ji}\right)\left(\varepsilon^{+}_{kl}\varepsilon^{+}_{lk}+\eta\varepsilon^{\times}_{kl}\varepsilon^{\times}_{lk}\right)\right\rangle.

(64)

The explicit expression of
\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}
[see
eqs.

85
,
86
and
87

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}+\gamma
. Since
\varepsilon_{0},\gamma
are treated as independent, Gaussian random fields, Isserlis’s theorem implies that
\Xi
is a sum of terms of the form
\zeta_{1}\times\zeta_{2}
, where
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\sim\left\langle\gamma\gamma\right\rangle
or
\left\langle\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\right\rangle
. On the one hand,
\left\langle\gamma\gamma\right\rangle
can be expressed in terms of
\xi^{\pm}
; on the other hand,
\varepsilon_{0}

\left\langle\varepsilon_{0i}\varepsilon_{0j}\right\rangle=0\ ,\quad\left\langle\varepsilon_{0i}\varepsilon_{0j}^{*}\right\rangle=\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ij}\ .

(65)

The mean square of intrinsic ellipticity
\sigma^{2}
considered here matches the convention of
Schneider et al. (2002)
. It is, however, two times larger than the
\sigma^{2}_{1}
used in
Reischke et al. (2024)
and

\varepsilon_{0}
.

3.2.2
Sample averaging

The second step consists in taking the sample average of

Just like in subsubsec.

4
Conclusion

When estimating the statistical properties of a random field from a finite, discrete sample of measurements, the result depends on the sample at hand. Sparsity covariance quantifies the uncertainty specifically due to the discreteness of the sample. It thus differs from sample (or cosmic) covariance, which is due to its finite extension.

In this article, motivated by questions relevant to cosmology, I have mathematically defined sparsity covariance and shown how to evaluate it, in the context of measurements of the two-point correlation function of a random field observed in random directions of the sky. I first treated the case of a generic scalar field
f:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, observed without noise, in order to convey the general idea. The result was validated with a numerical experiment. As a rule of thumb, sparsity covariance is
\sim\xi^{2}(0)/P
, where
P
is the number of pairs of directions used to estimate
\xi(\theta)
. For comparison, cosmic covariance is typically given by the celestial average of
\xi^{2}(\theta)
.

I then turned to the more concrete case of cosmic shear, where I showed that sparsity covariance essentially acts as an extra shape noise. Specifically, accounting for the dominant contribution of sparsity covariance is equivalent to replacing, in the expression of shape noise, the mean square of the intrinsic ellipticity of galaxies,
\langle|\varepsilon_{0}|^{2}\rangle
, by the mean square of their apparent ellipticity,
\langle|\varepsilon|^{2}\rangle=\langle|\varepsilon_{0}|^{2}\rangle+\langle|\gamma|^{2}\rangle
, which accounts for weak lensing. As it turns out, since shape noise is, in practice, estimated from apparent ellipticities, sparsity covariance is already taken into account in standard cosmic shear analyses. If it were not, the associated error on the covariance matrix would remain negligible anyway, since
\langle|\gamma|^{2}\rangle/\langle|\varepsilon_{0}|^{2}\rangle<1\%
.

Things would be different if the signal (here
\gamma
) were comparable or stronger than the noise (here
\varepsilon_{0}
). More generally, the analysis presented here reveals that sparsity covariance must be taken into account when dealing with measurements whose individual signal-to-noise ratio is comparable, or larger, than unity. It will be the case, e.g., for cosmological applications of the line-of-sight shear of strong lenses
(
Hogg et al., 2023
;
Fleury, 2024
)
.

Appendix A
Details on the derivation of the covariance matrix of cosmic shear

This appendix provides further details on the derivation of the covariance matrix of cosmic shear.

A.1
Expression of
\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}

The first technical step consists in expressing the four-point correlation

\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}\equiv\left\langle\left(\varepsilon^{+}_{ij}\varepsilon^{+}_{ji}+\sigma\varepsilon^{\times}_{ij}\varepsilon^{\times}_{ji}\right)\left(\varepsilon^{+}_{kl}\varepsilon^{+}_{lk}+\eta\varepsilon^{\times}_{kl}\varepsilon^{\times}_{lk}\right)\right\rangle.

(79)

in terms of the building blocks
\xi^{\pm}_{ij}\equiv\left\langle\gamma^{+}_{ij}\gamma^{+}_{ji}\right\rangle\pm\left\langle\gamma^{\times}_{ij}\gamma^{\times}_{ji}\right\rangle
, and the mean square of
\varepsilon_{0}
,
\left\langle\varepsilon_{0i}\varepsilon_{0j}^{*}\right\rangle=\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Note that intrinsic ellipticity can actually be treated just like shear with the substitution
\xi^{+}_{ij}\to\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ij}
and
\xi^{-}_{ij}\to 0
.

Using the definition of the plus and cross components of ellipticity,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon_{ij}^{+}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{ij}^{\times})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ij}}=(\varepsilon_{ik}^{+}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{ik}^{\times})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ik}}\ ,

(80)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon_{kl}^{+}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{kl}^{\times})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\psi_{kl}}=(\varepsilon_{ki}^{+}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{ki}^{\times})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ki}}\ ,

(81)

it is straightforward to get

\displaystyle\left\langle\varepsilon_{+ij}\varepsilon_{+kl}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ik}\right)\cos 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}-\psi_{ki}+\psi_{kl})+\xi^{-}_{ik}\cos 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}+\psi_{ki}-\psi_{kl})\right],

(82)

\displaystyle\left\langle\varepsilon_{\times ij}\varepsilon_{\times kl}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ik}\right)\cos 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}-\psi_{ki}+\psi_{kl})-\xi^{-}_{ik}\cos 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}+\psi_{ki}-\psi_{kl})\right],

(83)

\displaystyle\left\langle\varepsilon_{+ij}\varepsilon_{\times kl}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ik}\right)\sin 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}-\psi_{ki}+\psi_{kl})+\xi^{-}_{ik}\sin 2(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}+\psi_{ki}-\psi_{kl})\right].

(84)

Then, in the flat-sky limit (
\psi_{ij}=\psi_{ji}
), after a tedious expansion of
eq.

79
in terms of the above and the application Isserlis’s theorem, I find

\displaystyle\Xi^{++}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\xi^{+}_{ij}\xi^{+}_{kl}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ik}\right)\big(\xi^{+}_{jl}+\sigma_{j}^{2}\delta_{jl}\big)+\xi^{-}_{ik}\xi^{-}_{jl}\cos 4(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{jl})+(k\leftrightarrow l)\right],

(85)

\displaystyle\Xi^{--}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\xi^{-}_{ij}\xi^{-}_{kl}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma^{2}_{i}\delta_{ik}\right)\big(\xi^{+}_{jl}+\sigma^{2}_{j}\delta_{jl}\big)\cos 4(\psi_{ij}-\psi_{kl})+\xi^{-}_{ik}\xi^{-}_{jl}\cos 4(\psi_{ik}-\psi_{ij}+\psi_{jl}-\psi_{kl})+(k\leftrightarrow l)\right],

(86)

\displaystyle\Xi^{+-}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\xi^{+}_{ij}\xi^{-}_{kl}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\xi^{+}_{ik}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\delta_{ik}\right)\xi^{-}_{jl}\cos 4(\psi_{jl}-\psi_{kl})+\text{3 permutations ($i\leftrightarrow j$ or $k\leftrightarrow l$)}\right].

(87)

A.2
Sample averaging step

The full covariance matrix reads

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right)=\left\llbracket\frac{1}{P_{aIJ}P_{bKL}}\sum_{(ij\in\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}}\sum_{(kl)\in\mathcal{P}_{bKL}}\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}\right\rrbracket-\xi^{\sigma}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}}\xi^{\eta}_{\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\ .

(88)

The application of the sample-averaging operator is then very similar to subsubsec.
2.4.4
. I first divide the sum into terms that have zero, one, or two indices in common. Here the presence of potentially different redshift bins
I,J,K,L
makes things somewhat more complicated, because for example
i=k
is only possible if
I=K
, etc. Accounting for all possibilities,

\left\llbracket\frac{1}{P_{aIJ}P_{bKL}}\sum_{(ij\in\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}}\sum_{(kl)\in\mathcal{P}_{bKL}}\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{ijkl}\right\rrbracket=F_{0}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\\
+F_{i=k}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 14\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}+F_{i=l}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 14\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}+F_{j=k}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 14\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}+F_{j=l}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 14\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\\
+F_{(ij)=(kl)}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aKL}}+F_{(ij)=(lk)}\,\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 21\right\rrbracket_{aKL}}\ ,

(89)

where
F_{i=k}
denotes the fraction of terms such that
i=k
, etc. The fraction of terms with no common indices is
F_{0}=1-F_{i=k}-F_{i=l}-\ldots
The various fractions are estimated just like in subsubsec.
2.4.5
. For instance, the number of terms with
i=k
is

C_{i=k}=\delta_{IK}N_{I}\times\frac{N_{J}}{\Omega}\,\Omega_{a}\times\frac{N_{L}}{\Omega}\,\Omega_{b}

(90)

while the total number of pairs of pairs in
\mathcal{P}_{aIJ}\times\mathcal{P}_{bKL}
reads

P_{aIJ}P_{bKL}=\left(N_{I}\times\frac{N_{J}}{\Omega}\,\Omega_{a}\right)\left(N_{K}\times\frac{N_{L}}{\Omega}\,\Omega_{b}\right),

(91)

so that

F_{i=k}=\frac{C_{i=k}}{P_{aIJ}P_{bKL}}=\frac{\delta_{IK}}{N_{I}}\ .

(92)

Putting everything together, I get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}-\xi^{\sigma}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}}\xi^{\eta}_{\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}}_{\mathrm{CCov}\left(\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right)}

(93)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\delta_{IK}}{N_{I}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 14\right\rrbracket_{bIL}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)+\frac{\delta_{IL}}{N_{I}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 31\right\rrbracket_{bKI}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\delta_{JK}}{N_{J}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 24\right\rrbracket_{bJL}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)+\frac{\delta_{JL}}{N_{J}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 32\right\rrbracket_{bIL}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{+\frac{\delta_{ab}\delta_{IK}\delta_{JL}}{P_{aIJ}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)+\frac{\delta_{ab}\delta_{IL}\delta_{JK}}{P_{aIJ}}\left(\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 21\right\rrbracket_{aJI}}-\Xi^{\sigma\eta}_{\left\llbracket 12\right\rrbracket_{aIJ}\left\llbracket 34\right\rrbracket_{bKL}}\right)}_{(\mathrm{SCov}+\mathrm{NCov})\left(\hat{\xi}_{aIJ}^{\sigma},\hat{\xi}_{bKL}^{\eta}\right)},

where the first line, which is independent of sample size, is cosmic covariance, while the other terms encode both sparsity and noise covariances. The latter two may be conventionally distinguished by defining
\mathrm{SCov}
as the terms of
\mathrm{SCov}+\mathrm{NCov}
that do not depend on
\sigma^{2}
.
