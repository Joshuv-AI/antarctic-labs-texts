LGMN regulates endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition via the p38 MAPK pathway to promote pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Yuntao Zou, Jiangsheng Zhang, Dongni Chen, Yingxin Zhang, Jiayang Fan, Kaican Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive, fatal lung disease driven by fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition. Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) critically contributes to vascular injury and fibrotic remodeling in IPF. Legumain (LGMN), a lysosomal cysteine protease involved in excessive extracellular matrix processing and signaling modulation, has been implicated in vascular remodeling and fibrosis. This study aimed to investigate whether LGMN influences pulmonary fibrosis by regulating the EndMT process. Independent transcriptomic analyses revealed LGMN is significantly upregulated in IPF and associated with poor prognosis. Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing datasets identify endothelial cells as a major source of elevated LGMN in human IPF and murine fibrosis, with LGMN expression positively correlating with disease severity. Our studies demonstrated that LGMN knockdown in endothelial cells inhibited EndMT and enhanced migratory and tube formation capabilities; conversely, LGMN overexpression promoted EndMT and suppressed these functions. Transcriptomic profiling indicated that LGMN knockdown significantly influenced the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. To validate the mechanism, pharmacological inhibition of p38 MAPK reduced EndMT and partially reversed LGMN overexpression-induced EndMT. In conclusion, these results indicate LGMN promotes pulmonary fibrosis by activating the p38 MAPK pathway to regulate EndMT, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for pulmonary fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10142-026-01996-9.

Background

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive interstitial lung disease of unknown etiology. It is characterized by repetitive alveolar epithelial injury, fibroblast proliferation, differentiation into myofibroblasts, and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, ultimately leading to architectural distortion and respiratory failure (Henderson et al. 2020, Richeldi et al. 2017, Benegas Urteaga et al. 2022). The global incidence of IPF continues to rise, and the median survival following diagnosis remains approximately 2–3 years (Farkas et al. 2011). Although antifibrotic agents such as pirfenidone and nintedanib can slow disease progression, they do not halt fibrosis and are limited by high cost and adverse effects (Hou et al. 2025, Xie et al. 2024).

Recent studies suggest that endothelial cells can contribute to the fibroblast population through endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) (Ma et al. 2020). EndMT is a dynamic process in which endothelial cells lose characteristic markers, including platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1/CD31) and vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin), while acquiring mesenchymal features such as spindle-shaped morphology and expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and vimentin (Pérez et al. 2017, Brief ). Given its critical involvement in vascular remodeling and fibrogenesis, targeting EndMT represents a promising approach to attenuate pulmonary fibrosis.

Legumain (LGMN), also known as asparagine endopeptidase, is a cysteine protease of the C13 family that specifically hydrolyzes asparaginyl bonds (Structure ). It is broadly expressed and predominantly localized to the endo-lysosomal compartment. LGMN has been implicated in a variety of pathological conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular disease, organ fibrosis, and inflammation (Luo et al. 2006, Zhang and Lin 2021, Briggs et al. 2010, Bai et al. 2019, Ozawa et al. 2019, Hypoxia-driven , Wang et al. 2018). Functionally, LGMN interacts with integrin receptors; for example, in epithelial ovarian cancer cells, an exosome-associated LGMN–integrin α5β1 complex promotes peritoneal metastasis (Li et al. 2020). Moreover, its interaction with integrin αvβ3 plays a pivotal role in pulmonary granuloma formation and fibrotic remodeling (Liu et al. 2026). As a protease, LGMN also contributes to precursor protein processing and ECM regulation. In chronic pancreatitis and pulmonary hypertension, LGMN activates matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9) and regulates transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) activation, leading to excessive ECM deposition (Ren et al. 2020).

Although the specific role of LGMN in IPF has not been investigated, its involvement in ECM turnover and tissue remodeling suggests that LGMN may modulate EndMT-driven fibrogenesis. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that LGMN regulates EndMT and contributes to fibrotic progression in pulmonary fibrosis, highlighting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

The public datasets for bioinformatics analysis were selected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database based on the following criteria: (1) Disease focus: Datasets specifically related to pulmonary fibrosis ; (2) Sample types: Inclusion of human lung tissue samples, human bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and mouse lung tissue samples to provide a comprehensive analysis; (3) Data quality and completeness: Availability of high-quality raw data and associated clinical survival information where applicable; and (4) Statistical power: Preference for datasets with sufficient sample sizes to ensure the robustness of the results. The selected datasets are shown in Table 1. For the microarray datasets, we compared LGMN expression between healthy donors and IPF patients and validated that there were differences in survival among IPF patients. TimeROC was utilized to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and evaluate the ability of LGMN expression to distinguish between different survival states in IPF patients. The Spearman correlation analysis was performed to determine the correlation between the expression of LGMN and fibrosis-related gene. Based on the median LGMN expression (cutoff 10.34) in IPF patients from GSE70866, patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to compare survival differences between the groups. ScRNA-seq data was analyzed using the SeuratR package (V.4.3.0) (Hypoxia-driven ). Initially, quality control measures were implemented by filtering cells with mitochondrial gene expression > 10% and a gene expression level between 200 and 6000 genes. After quality control, SCTransform was applied to normalize and scale scRNA-seq data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on the processed data for dimensionality reduction. The Harmony method mitigated batch effects in the dissociated scRNA-seq data. The FindNeighbors function in Seurat generated a shared nearest-neighbor graph (NNG), and the FindClusters function used the Louvain algorithm for cluster analysis, visualized by tSNE scatter plots. To ensure the accuracy of cell annotation, the “pseudobulk” function was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEG) between fibrotic and normal cells. Annotation of major cell clusters was performed using known cell type marker genes (Morse et al. 2019). Furthermore, the Wilcoxon test was employed for pairwise comparisons of LGMN expression levels among the three groups: Sham, Bleo-14-days, and Bleo-35-days.

Bleomycin -mediated induction of pulmonary fibrosis

Healthy C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhiyuan Biotech Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China (stock no. Z20240827). Mice were kept on a regular diet in a specific pathogen-free conditions with an environmental temperature control at 21–24 °C and a 12 h light-dark cycle. After anesthesia, mice were randomly assigned to receive a single intratracheal instillation of bleomycin (1.5 U/kg) or an equal volume of sterile saline as control. Briefly, the mice were secured in a supine position following a midline cervical incision to expose the trachea. Bleomycin or saline was administered via a 30-gauge needle inserted into the tracheal lumen. To ensure uniform distribution of the agent throughout the lungs, the mice were maintained in a vertical position and rotated gently for 30 s immediately following the instillation. After the procedure, the incision was sutured, and the mice were kept in a head-up position during recovery to prevent aspiration. Mice were sacrificed three weeks after instillation and lung tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of The Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription PCR

For tissue samples, 50 mg of lung tissue was weighed, RNA was extracted using the Tissue RNA Kit (GBCBIO, Cat#R3105), the extracted RNA was quantified by nanodrop, and 500ng of RNA was taken for reverse transcription using the Reverse Transcription Kit Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, Cat#11141ES60), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. for qPCR, 500 ng of RNA was taken for reverse transcription, and the resulting cDNA was subjected to fluorescence quantitative PCR using the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green premix (Yeasen, Cat#11201ES08); sFor cellular samples, the number of cells greater than 2 × 106 was collected by centrifugation, using the rapid extraction kit ALFA-SEQ Tissue/Cell Ultra-Rapid Total RNA Kit (FINDROP, Cat#RZ320) was used to extract cellular RNA; reverse transcription (Takara, Cat#RR036A) and fluorescence quantitative PCR (Vazyme, Cat#P520-02) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions; Roche 480 was used to detect the CT values, and 2−ΔΔCT was used for relative quantification. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2. All qPCR experiments were performed in accordance with the Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments (MIQE) guidelines (Bustin et al. 2009). GAPDH was selected as the internal reference gene due to its high and constitutive expression across various cell types and tissues, which ensures reliable amplification signals. Furthermore, GAPDH expression is known to remain stable under diverse experimental conditions, including those utilized in this study, making it a robust normalization control.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from lung tissues or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 2% protease inhibitor cocktail (PI). Protein concentration was determined using a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio, Cat# PC0021) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer (NCM, Cat#NO.WB500D), targeting LGMN (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# 15095-1-AP), GAPDH(1:2000; Proteintech, Cat# 10494-1-AP), CD31 (1:2000; Proteintech, Cat# 80530-1-RR), α-SMA (1:10000; Proteintech, Cat# 14395-1-AP), P38 (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# 14064-1-AP), Phospho-P38 (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# 28796-1-AP). Membranes were washed three times (10 min each) with TBST and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000; Boster, Cat# BA1054) diluted in blocking buffer(GBCBIO, Cat# G7205). After three additional TBST washes (10 min each), immunoreactive bands were visualized using an Ultra-Sensitive Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Kit (NCM Biotech, Cat# P10100) and detected using a Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Tecan, Infinite F Pro series, software v9.0), and protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining were conducted following the protocol described in reference (Wan et al. 2023). The mouse lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Four-micrometer sections were routinely deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. HE and Masson’s trichrome staining was performed for morphologic detection using a kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of collagen deposition in Masson’s trichrome stained sections was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed as previously described (Yu et al. 2018). Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Four-micrometer sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol into distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (Solarbio) at 95 °C for 10 min. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated with 3% H₂O₂ at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with the primary antibody against LGMN (Proteintech, Cat#15095-1-AP; 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with a biotin-labeled secondary antibody (Beyotime) at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were then incubated with a streptavidin-HRP conjugate (Beyotime) at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate signal amplification. Finally, the signal was developed using a DAB working solution, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin and mounting with a mounting medium. Images were captured using light microscopy. The intensity of LGMN IHC staining was quantified using ImageJ software. Briefly, images were converted to 8-bit format, and the positive staining area and integrated optical density (IOD) were measured. The results were normalized to the total tissue area and expressed as the mean IOD per field.

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections were processed for deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval using the same procedures as described for Immunohistochemistry. Sections were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. To block non-specific binding, sections were incubated with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. For double immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Mouse anti-LGMN (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# 15095-1-AP) and Rabbit anti-CD31 (1:2000; Proteintech, Cat# 80530-1-RR). After washing three times with PBS (5 min each), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies: Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# RGAM004) for LGMN (red fluorescence), and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-2) for CD31 (green fluorescence). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and slides were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Images for quantification were acquired using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope at 63x magnification, with 6 randomly selected fields captured from each experimental group (Control and Bleo). Double immunofluorescence images for LGMN and CD31 were analyzed using ImageJ software. The percentage of LGMN area relative to the total endothelial cell area was calculated for each analyzed field.

Micro-CT imaging

Twenty-one days after administration of bleomycin or saline, in vivo micro-CT analysis of the whole lungs was performed on a Bruker SkyScan 1176 micro-CT (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). Mice were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane and fixed in the supine position. Scanning parameters were 50 kV X-ray voltage, 25 W maximum power, and a 0.5 mm Cu filter. Exposure time was adjusted to achieve approximately 60% average intensity without flat-field correction (approximately 60 ms for the 1176). Projections were collected over 180° with a rotation step of 0.7°. Reconstructed datasets were produced using NRecon software. Quantitative analysis was performed in CTAn, and CTVol was used to render 3D lung models.

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in ECM (ScienCell; #1001) medium containing 5% fetal bovine serum(FBS). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS. The WI-38 cells were grown in MEM (Gibco, #C12571500BT), fortified with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. HUVECs were treated with SB203580 (MedChemExpress; 150µM), TGF-β1 (MedChemExpress; 15 ng/mL), or the control vehicle for 24 h.

Generation of stable LGMN-knockdown HUVEC cell lines and LGMN-overexpress HUVEC cell lines via lentiviral transduction

Target plasmids for lentivirus production were extracted using an endotoxin-free plasmid medium-scale extraction kit (TIANGEN, Cat#DP230630) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the respective target construct (either the LGMN-targeting shRNA construct, PLV-U6-shRNA-EF1α-CopGFP-Puro, or the LGMN-HA overexpression construct, pCDH-CMV-LGMN-HA-EF1-CoGFP-T2A-puro), along with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Transfection was performed using PEI, with a total of 8 µg plasmid DNA per 10 cm dish, comprising 4 µg target plasmid, 3 µg psPAX2, and 1 µg pMD2.G. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and stored at -80 °C. Viral titers were determined by functional titration: HUVECs were infected with serially diluted viral supernatants, and GFP-positive cells were counted using a fluorescence microscope 48–72 h post-transduction. Titer was calculated as infectious units per milliliter (IFU/mL) and was typically 5 × 10^8 IFU/mL. For transduction, HUVEC cells were incubated with the viral supernatants at an optimized Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) of 20, supplemented with 8 µg/mL polybrene. Stable LGMN-knockdown and LGMN-overexpressing cell pools were selected by culturing in medium containing 2 µg/mL puromycin, starting 48 h post-transduction.

Wound-healing assay

Cell migration was measured by wound-healing assays. A marker pen was used to make a mark at the bottom of a sterile 6-well plate. HUVEC cells were then added to a sterile 6-well plate and cultured overnight. Cells were subjected to overexpression or knockdown of LGMN. When the cells grew to 90–100% confluence, a sterile pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell surface, followed by PBS washing to remove cellular debris. Cells were then cultured in serum-free medium for the duration of the assay (12 h) to minimize proliferation interference and ensure that the observed wound closure primarily reflects migratory capacity. The scratch was photographed using an inverted system microscope at 0 h and 12 h. The scratch width was quantified using ImageJ software (by measuring the distance between the wound edges at multiple predetermined points along the scratch), and the relative migration rate, expressed as the percentage of wound closure, was calculated as: (initial scratch width - remaining scratch width at 12 h) / initial scratch width × 100%.

Transwell invasion assays

WI-38 cells were digested and resuspended in serum deprived DMEM. A total of 100 µL of cell suspension (2 × 104 ) was introduced into the upper Matrigel-coated transwell chamber, and 600 µL with treated complete culture medium was added to the lower chamber. The 24-well plate with a transwell chamber was incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. After 24 h, invading cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, then stained with crystal violet solution for 10 min, visualized using a microscope and counted in three randomly selected fields. The number of invaded WI-38 fibroblasts were quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration assays

HUVEC cells were digested and resuspended in serum deprived DMEM. A total of 100 µL of cell suspension (2 × 104 ) was introduced into the upper transwell chamber, and 600 µL with complete culture medium was added to the lower chamber. The 24-well plate with a transwell chamber was incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. After 24 h, invading cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, then stained with crystal violet solution for 10 min, visualized using a microscope and counted in three randomly selected fields. The number of migrated HUVEC cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs (5 × 105) suspended in ECM containing CM were seeded on to a Matrigel-coated dish. After 6 h culture, tube-like structures on Matrigel were photographed by an inverted microscope (Olympus). The state of tube forming in the picture was analyzed using ImageJ software. The number of vascular junctions and total vascular length were quantified using ImageJ software.

Next generation sequence. (NGS)

Cells were collected from control HUVEC cells and LGMN-knockdown HUVEC cells (n = 3 per group). These cells were immediately lysed in TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting TRIzol-lysed cell homogenates were then quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Total RNA was subsequently extracted from these frozen TRIzol-lysed samples by JingRui. (Guangzhou, China). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech, Cat# 1000006983) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were quantified and quality-controlled before sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech) in paired-end 150 bp (PE150) mode. Raw reads were processed with Trim Galore v0.6.10, trimming adapters and low-quality bases (Phred < 25), retaining reads ≥ 36 bp. The resulting high-quality clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with default parameters. Alignment files were sorted and indexed using Samtools (v1.5). Gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts (part of Subread package v2.1.1). The raw gene expression count matrix was imported into R (v4.3.2) and normalized. Counts were transformed using log2 for downstream differential expression analysis.

Differential expression genes (DEGs) and enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0). Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathways was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) based on the hypergeometric test. Enriched terms with false discovery rate (FDR) q-value < 0.05. All results were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.2), including volcano plots for differential expression and dot plots for enrichment results.

Statistics analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. For differential expression analysis, P values were calculated as described above. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 7 and R software.

1. LGMN expression is increased in pulmonary fibrosis patients and associated with poor prognostic

This study utilized three publicly available GEO datasets of pulmonary fibrosis–related transcriptomes derived from human lung tissues and BALF samples. Compared with healthy controls, LGMN expression was significantly elevated in pulmonary fibrosis patients across all three datasets(Fig. 1A-C). In the GSE70866 dataset (which utilized BALF samples), survival analysis using timeROC demonstrated AUC values of 0.738, 0.731, and 0.697 for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival, respectively (Fig. 1D). Further, correlations between LGMN and fibrosis-related genes COL3A1, COL1A1, and CXCL12 were analyzed, showing positive correlations (r = 0.442 for COL3A1, r = 0.409 for COL1A1, r = 0.438 for CXCL12) (Fig. 1E–G). Based on the median LGMN expression (cutoff 10.34) in 112 IPF patients from GSE70866, patients were stratified into high- expression and low- expression groups according to survival data; baseline characteristics are shown in Table 3. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that the low- expression group had significantly better survival than the high- expression group (Fig. 1H). We also performed cell type–specific expression analyses using three public single-cell RNA-seq datasets derived from human and mouse lung tissues. After dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell annotation, we found high LGMN expression in endothelial cells in both human and mouse samples (Supplementary Fig. S1). Notably, LGMN expression was higher in IPF patients and mouse fibrosis models than in the respective controls (Fig. 1I–J). Previous studies have indicated that pulmonary fibrosis in mouse models is partially reversible, with fibrosis peaking at day 14 and regressing by day 35 post-bleomycin challenge (Büttner et al. 2004). Consistently, we observed that LGMN expression in endothelial cells mirrored the dynamic changes in fibrosis severity in mice(Fig. 1K).

2. Elevated LGMN expression in fibrotic lung tissues of bleomycin-treated mice

To verify LGMN expression in vivo and closely replicate the pathological process of pulmonary fibrosis, we established a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model (Fig. 2A). Mice received intratracheal injections of either bleomycin (Bleo group) or saline (Control group), and lung tissues were harvested at day 21. Compared to the Control group, mice in the Bleo group exhibited a significant decrease in body weight by day 21 (Fig. 2B). Micro-CT imaging and 3D reconstructions revealed increased lung density and parenchymal opacity in the Bleo group compared to controls (Fig. 2C). Gross inspection suggested increased lung volume, edema, and congestion in the bleomycin-treated mice (Fig. 2D). Histological analyses with HE and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated destruction of lung architecture and increased collagen deposition in the Bleo group (Fig. 2E). Immunohistochemistry for LGMN further showed markedly elevated protein expression in the fibrotic lungs of the Bleo group. Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses corroborated a significant upregulation of LGMN protein and mRNA levels in lung tissues from the Bleo group compared to controls (Fig. 2F–G). Furthermore, double immunofluorescence (IF) staining for LGMN and the endothelial cell marker CD31 revealed LGMN co-localization with CD31-positive endothelial cells in the bleomycin-treated lung tissues. Quantitative analysis showed a significant increase in the percentage of LGMN area relative to the total endothelial cell area (% of LGMN area to endothelial cells area) in the Bleo group compared to the Control group (Fig. 2H).

3. Impact of LGMN knockdown on EndMT and endothelial cell functions

Previous studies have demonstrated that endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) occurs under specific pathological conditions, contributing to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. To elucidate the role of LGMN in EndMT, we established an in vitro EndMT model by treating HUVECs with 5, 10, and 15 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h. Western blot analysis revealed that as the TGF-β1 concentration increased, both the degree of EndMT (indicated by the α-SMA/CD31 protein ratio) and LGMN expression significantly increased (Fig. 3A). Next, we established stable LGMN-knockdown HUVECs (shLGMN). Western blot confirmed a significant reduction of LGMN protein in the shLGMN group compared to the Ctrl and shControl groups (Fig. 3B). Under TGF-β1 stimulation, EndMT was notably suppressed in the shLGMN group compared to the shControl group. This was evidenced by a significantly decreased α-SMA/CD31 protein ratio and significantly reduced mRNA levels of fibrosis-related genes Vimentin (VIM), COL1A1, and SNAI1 (Fig. 3C–D). Functional assays showed that the wound healing rate at 12 h was significantly higher in the shLGMN group than in both the Ctrl and shControl groups (Fig. 3E). Correspondingly, the number of migrated cells at 24 h was also significantly increased in the shLGMN group (Fig. 3F). Tube formation assays demonstrated that, at 6 h, both the number of vascular junctions and total vascular length were significantly increased in the shLGMN group compared to the shControl group (Fig. 3G). In transwell invasion assays, conditioned media from shLGMN HUVECs significantly reduced the invasion of WI-38 fibroblasts at 24 h compared to media from shControl HUVECs (Fig. 3H).

5. LGMN regulates EndMT through activation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway

5. LGMN regulates EndMT through activation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway

5. LGMN regulates EndMT through activation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway

As shown above, altered LGMN expression significantly impacts EndMT and HUVECs function. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, transcriptome sequencing was conducted in LGMN-knockdown (shLGMN) and control HUVECs. Differential gene expression analysis identified 1,710 upregulated and 1,895 downregulated genes between shLGMN and control groups, visualized in volcano plot (Fig. 5A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were mainly associated with extracellular encapsulating structures and the extracellular matrix (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). These findings suggested that LGMN may promote EndMT through the MAPK pathway. The MAPK family consists of ERK1/2, p38 MAPK, and JNK, among which p38 MAPK is critically involved in cell survival and inflammatory responses, and is known to be associated with pulmonary fibrosis (Ren et al. 2020, et , Morse et al. 2019). To investigate whether LGMN regulates EndMT through the p38 MAPK pathway, we treated HUVECs with the p38 inhibitor SB203580 (50, 100, 150 µM) under TGF-β1 stimulation (Fig. 5D). SB203580 treatment effectively reduced the phosphorylated p38 (p-p38)/total p38 ratio and decreased the α-SMA/CD31 ratio, thereby indicating an attenuated EndMT phenotype. Under TGF-β1 stimulation, compared to the shLGMN HUVECs, the p-p38/p38 ratio was significantly reduced in the shLGMN + SB203580 co-treatment group. This was accompanied by further suppression of EndMT, as evidenced by a significantly lower α-SMA/CD31 ratio and reduced expression of VIM, COL1A1, and SNAI1 (Fig. 5E-F). Conversely, under TGF-β1 stimulation, the p-p38/p38 ratio was significantly higher in oeLGMN HUVECs compared to the oeLGMN + SB203580 co-treatment group (Fig. 5G). This reduction in the p-p38/p38 ratio following SB203580 treatment led to a significant suppression of EndMT, as indicated by a decreased α-SMA/CD31 ratio and reduced expression of EndMT-related genes (VIM, COL1A1, and SNAI1) compared to the oeLGMN group (Fig. 5H). Together, these results suggest that LGMN promotes p38 MAPK phosphorylation, thereby driving EndMT initiation and maintenance.

Discussion

Three independent transcriptomic datasets consistently show that legumain (LGMN) expression is elevated in BALF and lung tissue from pulmonary fibrosis patients compared with healthy controls. The positive correlations between LGMN and established fibrosis markers (COL1A1, COL3A1, CXCL12) suggest that LGMN participates in the broader fibrogenic network characteristic of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) (Costello et al. 2012, Phillips et al. 2004, Büttner et al. 2004, Samokhin et al. 2018). Importantly, higher LGMN expression associates with poorer prognosis, with time-dependent ROC analysis indicating moderate discriminatory capacity (AUCs 0.697–0.738 at 1–3 years). These bioinformatic findings suggest LGMN’s potential as a prognostic biomarker in IPF. The diagnosis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis predominantly relies on clinical history and high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT). Invasive procedures such as bronchoscopy or surgical lung biopsy are reserved only for cases where diagnosis remains unclear. Consequently, high-quality lung tissue samples from IPF patients are exceedingly rare and difficult to procure for research purposes. Without validation of LGMN expression in human IPF clinical samples, its utility as a prognostic biomarker, comparable to existing markers such as circulating fibrocytes (Moeller et al. 2009) and matrix metalloproteinases (Pulito-Cueto et al. 2025), requires further investigation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analyses indicate that LGMN is highly expressed in endothelial cells, with elevated expression in endothelial cells from both human IPF and murine fibrosis models. Given the central role of endothelial cells in EndMT-driven fibrogenesis, this cell-type specificity is noteworthy (Gaikwad et al. 2020). The temporal dynamics observed in mouse endothelial cells—LGMN expression paralleling fibrosis severity, peaking around day 14 and subsiding by day 35 during resolution—further implicate LGMN in fibrotic progression.

Studies indicate that in the bleomycin-induced mouse pulmonary fibrosis model, male mice typically exhibit more pronounced inflammatory responses and robust fibrotic progression (D’Avino et al. 2026). This characteristic facilitates the study of core pathological mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis. Furthermore, their relatively stable hormonal levels help minimize experimental data variability. Consequently, our in vivo experiments utilized male mice and demonstrated that LGMN is markedly elevated in fibrotic mouse lungs. Besides, consistent with these transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing data, immunofluorescence analysis directly demonstrated that LGMN protein expression is markedly elevated within CD31-positive endothelial cells in fibrotic mouse lungs.

Since endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) has been recognized as an important source of profibrotic cells in pulmonary fibrosis (Dietrich et al. 2025, Zhang et al. 2024), we focused on HUVECs treated with TGF-β1 to induce EndMT. In our in vitro EndMT model, TGF-β1 induced a dose-dependent increase in α-SMA/ CD31 ratio and LGMN, indicating that LGMN is upregulated during EndMT. Functional knockdown of LGMN in HUVECs significantly attenuated TGF-β1-induced EndMT: α-SMA/CD31 ratio and mRNA levels of mesenchymal markers (VIM, COL1A1, SNAI1) (Song et al. 2024, Wogonoside n.d., Li et al. 2021) were decreased. Conversely, LGMN overexpression exacerbated EndMT under the same conditions, indicating that LGMN plays an important role in driving EndMT. Notably, in the absence of TGF-β1 stimulation, neither LGMN overexpression nor knockdown significantly altered the α-SMA/CD31 ratio. This observation suggests that LGMN alone is insufficient to initiate a robust EndMT program under basal conditions. Instead, LGMN is more likely to act as a context-dependent modulator that amplifies or facilitates endothelial responses to profibrotic cues, rather than serving as an autonomous trigger of phenotypic transition.

In our study, knockdown of LGMN enhanced endothelial migration and angiogenic capacity (increased junction formation and vessel length), suggesting that inhibiting LGMN may help maintain or restore endothelial vascularization, reduce aberrant vascular remodeling and capillary rarefaction observed in IPF (Gaikwad et al. 2020, Fang et al. 2025, Ackermann et al. 2024). Microvascular remodeling is a hallmark pathology in IPF, characterized by a paradoxical combination: capillary rarefaction within fibrotic foci alongside excessive, aberrant neovascularization in perilesional areas. This pathology drives local ischemia and hypoxia, exacerbating lung tissue injury and propelling fibrosis progression (Barratt and Millar 2014). The observed enhancement of physiological endothelial functions (migration and angiogenesis) upon LGMN inhibition suggests a protective role, potentially by stabilizing endothelial integrity and function, thereby limiting further vascular damage and slowing the rate of disease progression. Importantly, IPF-associated vascular remodeling can precipitate pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Prior inhibition of LGMN has been shown to significantly alleviate vascular remodeling and fibrosis in animal model of PAH (et ). Therefore, although patients are typically diagnosed with IPF at advanced stages where vascular restoration is challenging, we contend that LGMN inhibition retains significant therapeutic value.

Moreover, conditioned medium from LGMN-overexpressing endothelial cells promoted fibroblast invasion, implying that LGMN may facilitate paracrine activation of fibroblasts (Martín-Vicente et al. 2025, Burgy and Königshoff 2024). Previous studies have identified mechanisms linking LGMN to activation of profibrotic signaling. For instance, Bai et al. reported that macrophage-derived LGMN promotes vascular remodeling in experimental pulmonary hypertension by activating MMP-2, which subsequently enhances TGF-β1 signaling and stimulates smooth muscle extracellular matrix synthesis and deposition (et ). Similarly, Ren et al. demonstrated that in chronic pancreatitis, macrophage-derived LGMN facilitates the conversion of latent TGF-β1 into its active form via MMP-2 activation, resulting in fibroblast activation and tissue fibrosis (Ren et al. 2020). These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for the paracrine activation observed in our study. We speculate that endothelial LGMN may regulate fibroblast function by modulating MMP-2–mediated TGF-β1 activation, thus contributing to fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition in pulmonary fibrosis (Wang et al. 2025, Ozawa et al. 2019, Bai et al. 2019, Khalil et al. 2005).

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms, we performed transcriptome sequencing in LGMN-knockdown and control HUVECs. Differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses highlighted the MAPK signaling pathway. Considering the established role of p38 MAPK in stress responses and fibrogenic signaling (Goda et al. 2020, Nie et al. 2017), we hypothesized that p38 MAPK might serve as a key downstream mediator of LGMN’s pro-EndMT effects under profibrotic conditions. Our pharmacological inhibition experiments, conducted under TGF-β1 stimulation, confirmed this hypothesis. Treatment with the p38 inhibitor SB203580 effectively reduced the p-p38/p38 ratio and, concomitantly, attenuated the EndMT phenotype as evidenced by a decreased α-SMA/CD31 ratio. This directly implicated p38 MAPK activation in the EndMT process. Further experiments elucidated the direct link between LGMN and p38 MAPK activation under TGF-β1 stimulation. Specifically, compared to the shLGMN HUVECs, co-treatment with SB203580 further reduced the p-p38/p38 ratio in the shLGMN + SB203580 co-treatment group. This was accompanied by a further suppression of EndMT, indicated by a significantly lower α-SMA/CD31 ratio and reduced expression of mesenchymal markers (VIM, COL1A1, and SNAI1). This synergistic effect strongly suggests that LGMN acts upstream of p38 MAPK in driving EndMT. Conversely, under TGF-β1 stimulation, in oeLGMN HUVECs, the p-p38/p38 ratio was significantly higher compared to the oeLGMN + SB203580 co-treatment group. This reduction in the p-p38/p38 ratio following SB203580 treatment led to a significant suppression of EndMT, as indicated by a decreased α-SMA/CD31 ratio and reduced expression of EndMT-related genes (VIM, COL1A1, and SNAI1) compared to the oeLGMN group. Collectively, these findings firmly establish a mechanistic model where LGMN promotes the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, and this activated p38 MAPK subsequently serves as a critical downstream effector, driving the initiation and maintenance of EndMT under profibrotic conditions.

This study has several limitations. First, the acute bleomycin-induced fibrosis model we used does not fully recapitulate the chronic, progressive nature of human IPF, and future studies using a chronic bleomycin model (more than 12 weeks) would be more representative. Second, while TGF-β1–treated HUVECs are a widely utilized in vitro model for studying EndMT, endothelial cell responses can differ significantly between fetal and adult tissues. Consequently, this model may not fully recapitulate the complex EndMT process observed in adult IPF lungs. Therefore, future investigations should aim to validate these findings in adult endothelial cell lines, such as human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells. Third, our study primarily focused on the role of LGMN in endothelial cells. However, single-cell RNA sequencing data indicate significant LGMN expression in other cell types within the fibrotic lung, particularly macrophages. LGMN expression in macrophages has been shown to be elevated in various fibrotic diseases, influencing diverse fibrosis-related processes. For example, Liu et al. demonstrated that LGMN in granulomatous macrophages interacts with integrin αvβ3 to inhibit the mTORC1/STAT1 signaling axis, thereby limiting M1 polarization and modulating inflammatory responses and fibrotic progression (Liu et al. 2026). Wang et al. reported that M2-polarized macrophages can overexpress and secrete LGMN, which promotes the degradation of extracellular matrix components, thereby exerting an anti-fibrotic effect in renal interstitial fibrosis (Wang et al. 2018). These findings suggest that future studies could explore the role of macrophage-derived LGMN in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis. Finally, this study lacks the in vivo experimental evidence to demonstrate that LGMN regulates EndMT via the p38 MAPK pathway. Therefore, the endothelial-specific LGMN knockout mice are required in the future study.

In summary, we demonstrate LGMN promotes EndMT through p38 MAPK activation and modulates endothelial function. These findings suggest LGMN’s potential as a novel biomarker and therapeutic target in pulmonary fibrosis.
