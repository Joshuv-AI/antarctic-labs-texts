lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 suppresses hepatitis B virus-related hepatocellular carcinoma by regulating miR-210-3p to upregulate CPEB2
Authors: Huimin Cong, Guobing Zhu, Yingzhou Xu, Linlin Wu, Hongfang Gu, Chunyan Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the biological functions, clinical significance, and regulatory mechanism of long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) ZNF667-AS1 in hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsOne hundred and seven patients with HBV-related HCC, one hundred and five patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and one hundred and five healthy controls were enrolled in this study. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was used to detect the expression levels of ZNF667-AS1, miR-210-3p, CPEB2. The association between ZNF667-AS1 expression and clinical characteristics of patients were analyzed, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate its diagnostic value. Bioinformatics prediction and dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted to verify the targeted associations between ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p, as well as between miR-210-3p and CPEB2. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and Transwell assays were applied to assess the biological functions of HCC cells after cell transfection.ResultsThe expression levels of ZNF667-AS1 and CPEB2 were significantly downregulated in tissue samples from patients with HBV-related HCC and in HBV-associated HCC cell lines. ROC curve analysis showed the area under the curve (AUC) of ZNF667-AS1 for diagnosing HBV-related HCC from CHB reached 0.841. miR-210-3p was validated as a direct target of ZNF667-AS1, and a significant negative correlation was observed between their expression levels. Overexpression of ZNF667-AS1 inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells, whereas this inhibitory effect was reversed by transfection with miR-210-3p mimic.ConclusionsLncRNA ZNF667-AS1 is downregulated in HBV-related HCC and exerts in vitro tumor‑suppressive effects by targeting miR-210-3p to upregulate CPEB2 expression, thus providing a potential auxiliary biomarker for HBV-related HCC diagnosis and a novel regulatory axis for understanding disease pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03260-9.Keywords: lncRNA ZNF667-AS1, hepatocellular carcinoma, miR-210-3p, hepatitis B virus, CPEB2

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for nearly half of all newly diagnosed liver cancer cases worldwide each year that occur in China [1, 2]. Current studies have confirmed that the development of HCC is closely associated with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, which is the main cause of viral liver diseases worldwide [3, 4]. Globally, approximately 350 million individuals are chronic HBV carriers, making them the largest high-risk population for primary liver cancer [5]. Traditional biomarkers for HCC exhibit insufficient sensitivity and specificity for early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of the disease. Therefore, research into the diagnosis, treatment, prognostic assessment, and specific pathogenesis of HBV-related HCC are of great clinical importance. Recent studies have demonstrated that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) act as key regulators and liquid biopsy-based biomarkers during HBV-driven hepatocarcinogenesis, providing novel directions for precision oncology [6, 7].

Multiple studies have confirmed that ncRNAs participate in the progression of HCC [8], and the dysregulation of lncRNAs is tightly associated with tumor proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and other malignant biological processes [9]. In the context of HBV-related HCC, numerous lncRNAs have been proven to modulate disease progression. For instance, HDAC2-AS2 facilitates tumor progression via extracellular vesicle-mediated transportation [10]; High expression of MALAT1 [11] and KCNQ1OT1 [12] correlates with poor clinical prognosis and exerts oncogenic activities in HCC patients. In contrast, OIP5-AS1 [13] and TPT1-AS1 [14] play tumor-suppressive functions by inhibiting tumor metabolism and cell cycle progression. In addition, MyD88 [15] has been identified as a promising biomarker for HBV-related HCC and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)-negative HCC, highlighting the multiple functional values of lncRNAs in HBV-related HCC. Notably, lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 has been reported as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer [16], gastric cancer [17], and ovarian cancer [18]. Its overexpression can significantly inhibit the proliferation and migration of tumor cells. Moreover, bioinformatics analysis based on the GSE55092 database indicated that ZNF667-AS1 is downregulated in HBV-related HCC samples [19], suggesting its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of HBV-related HCC. Nevertheless, its detailed regulatory mechanisms and clinical value remain largely unclarified.

Given the above research background, this study aimed to systematically explore the biological functions and clinical significance of ZNF667-AS1 in HBV-related HCC through clinical sample analysis, bioinformatics prediction, and cellular functional experiments. The findings of this study will help elucidate the molecular regulatory network underlying HBV-related HCC.

Materials and methods

Participants

One hundred and seven patients diagnosed with HBV-related HCC who received treatment at Rudong People’s Hospital from December 2020 to March 2022 were enrolled as the experimental group. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with HCC met the diagnostic criteria for liver cancer [20]; (2) No antiviral therapy and anti-tumor treatment were administered before admission; (3) All patients had a confirmed history of HBV infection. Patients who had received radiofrequency ablation, were co-infected with hepatotropic or non-hepatotropic viruses, or suffered from other malignant tumors were excluded from this study. Meanwhile, 105 patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and 105 healthy individuals were selected as two independents control groups.

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all enrolled subjects. After centrifugation, plasma biochemical indicators, including AFP, alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST), were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer. Clinical data, such as gender, age, TNM stage, were also recorded for each patient.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their family members prior to sample collection. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rudong People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2020056).

Cell culture and experimental grouping

Normal liver cells (THLE-2), non-HBV hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2), and HBV-related HCC cell lines (Hep3B2.1-7, HCCLM3, FOCUS, and MHCC97H) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Cell subculture was performed when cell confluency reached approximately 85% according to the cell growth status.

Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent was used to deliver ZNF667-AS1 overexpression plasmid (pc-ZNF667-AS1), miR-210-3p mimic, and corresponding negative controls (NC) into target cells. Cells were divided into four experimental groups: pc-NC group, pc-ZNF667-AS1 group, pc-ZNF667-AS1 + NC mimic group, and pc-ZNF667-AS1 + miR-210-3p mimic group.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent, and phase separation was performed via centrifugation with chloroform. Isopropanol was then added to the aqueous supernatant to precipitate RNA, and 75% ethanol was used to remove impurities and purify total RNA. RNA purity was determined using a spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit. RT-PCR amplification was carried out on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System, with U6 and GAPDH serving as reference genes. The relative gene expression levels were calculated accordingly.

CCK-8 assay for detecting cell proliferation

Transfected cells were seeded and cultured in a cell incubator for 48 h. 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and cells were incubated for another 4 h. The absorbance value at 450 nm was measured to evaluate cell proliferative capacity.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells were separately seeded into 24-well culture plates for routine incubation. Wild-type and mutant reporter plasmids of ZNF667-AS1 (pGL3-WT-ZNF667-AS1, pGL3-MUT-ZNF667-AS1), and CPEB2 (pGL3-WT-CPEB2, and pGL3-MUT-CPEB2) were co-transfected into the cells with miR-210-3p mimic or NC, respectively. After 48 h of culture, luciferase activity was detected in accordance with the instructions of the dual-luciferase reporter assay kit.

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

Cells from each group were collected, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and lysed with RIP lysis buffer. Cell lysates were centrifuged to collect supernatant. Ago2 antibody or IgG antibody was incubated with Protein A/G magnetic bead suspensions under rotation overnight to conjugate antibodies to beads. The obtained supernatants were mixed with antibody-bead complexes, and the beads were thoroughly washed with RIP Wash Buffer. Proteinase K digestion buffer was added to degrade bound proteins. RNA was extracted from precipitated complexes, and the enrichment levels of target RNAs were detected by RT-qPCR.

Western Blotting (WB) assay

Collected cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer, and supernatants were harvested after centrifugation. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA method. A total of 25 µg of protein samples were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h. Primary antibodies against CPEB2 and GAPDH were applied, and membranes were incubated at 4 ℃ overnight. On the following day, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 h of incubation at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit.

Detection of cell migration and invasion

For the migration assay, transfected cells were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts with culture medium containing 0.1% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The lower chambers were filled with 600 µL of medium supplemented with 20% FBS. For the invasion assay, the upper surface of Transwell inserts was evenly coated with Matrigel matrix, and inserts were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow gel solidification. After incubation in a cell incubator, Transwell inserts were removed. Cells that failed to migrate or invade across the membrane were gently wiped off with cotton swabs. Cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet, respectively. Five random visual fields were selected under an inverted microscope for cell counting, and the relative proportions of migrated and invaded cells were calculated.

Cell apoptosis assay

Washed cells were resuspended in 500 µL Binding Buffer. Then 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI staining solution were added and mixed gently. After 15 min of dark incubation at room temperature, cell apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry.

Cell cycle assay

Transfected cells from each group were collected and fixed with pre-cooled 70% ethanol overnight. After centrifugation and PBS washing, 100 µL of RNase A solution was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then 400 µL of PI staining solution was added and samples were incubated at room temperature for another 30 min. Flow cytometry was used to analyze cell cycle distribution.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 statistical software and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate data normality. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed by independent samples t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple groups), while non-normal data were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons to reduce type I error. Before Pearson correlation analysis, scatter plots were used to examine the linear association between variables, which verified the rationality of the linearity assumption. For survival analysis, patients were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the mean value of ZNF667-AS1. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression characteristics of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 in patients with HBV-related HCC and HCC cell lines

RT-qPCR results revealed that the expression level of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 was significantly lower in patients with HBV-related HCC than that in CHB patients and healthy controls (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1A). Compared with normal liver THLE-2 cells, the expression of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 was downregulated in all HBV-related HCC cell lines (Hep3B2.1-7, HCCLM3, FOCUS, and MHCC97H). Meanwhile, the expression of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 in the non-HBV HCC HepG2 cells was higher than that in HBV-related HCC cell lines but still lower than that in THLE-2 cells (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1B).

Clinical significance of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1

Statistical analysis revealed that the expression of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 was significantly correlated with TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, serum AFP level, and serum AST level in HCC patients (P < 0.05, Table 1). ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 for distinguishing CHB patients from healthy controls was 0.917, with a sensitivity of 89.52% and a specificity of 78.10% (Fig. 2A). The AUC of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 for distinguishing differentiating HCC patients from CHB patients was 0.841 (sensitivity: 77.57%, specificity: 79.05%) (Fig. 2B). Survival analysis based on the expression level of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 showed that patients with high lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 expression had a significantly better overall survival than those with low expression, and the difference was statistically significant (P = 0.0022, Fig. 2C). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis further confirmed that lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 expression was an independent prognostic factor for patients with HBV-related HCC (P = 0.009, Fig. 2D).

miR-210-3p is a target miRNA of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1

Two online databases, DIANA and LncBook, were used to predict potential target miRNAs of lncRNA ZNF667-AS1. Venn diagram analysis identified miR-210-3p as a common candidate target miRNA predicted by both databases (Fig. 3A), and a specific binding site was found between ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p (Fig. 3D). The expression of miR-210-3p in HCC patients was significantly upregulated in patients with HBV-related HCC compared with CHB patients and healthy individuals (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3B). In cell lines, the level of miR-210-3p in HBV-related HCC cells was higher than that in THLE-2 and HepG2 cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 3C). Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that co-transfection of pGL3-WT-ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p mimic significantly reduced relative luciferase activity in Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells (P < 0.0001, Figs. 3E-F). RIP assays further showed that both ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p were significantly enriched in Ago2 antibody-precipitated complexes (P < 0.0001, Figs. 3G-H). Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between the lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p expression in patients with HBV-related HCC (r = -0.654, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3I).

lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 regulates the biological functions of HBV-Related HCC cells by targeting miR-210-3p

Transfection efficiency detection confirmed that the expression of miR-210-3p was elevated in cells co-transfected with pc-ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p mimic compared with the pc-ZNF667-AS1 single transfection group (P < 0.0001, Figs. 4A-B). CCK-8 assays showed that overexpression of ZNF667-AS1 inhibited the proliferation of Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells, while miR-210-3p mimic reversed this inhibitory effect (P < 0.0001, Figs. 4C-D). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ZNF667-AS1 overexpression promoted cell apoptosis in both Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells, and this pro-apoptotic effect was markedly attenuated by miR-210-3p mimic (P < 0.0001, Figs. 4E-F). Cell cycle analysis showed that ZNF667-AS1 overexpression induced G1 phase arrest and decreased the proportion of cells in S phase in both cell lines, while miR-210-3p mimic rescued these cell cycle changes (P < 0.0001, Figs. 4G-H). Transwell assays revealed that overexpression of ZNF667-AS1 suppressed the migration and invasion abilities of Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells, as evidenced by a significant decrease in the relative number of migrated and invaded cells. However, co-transfection with miR-210-3p mimic effectively restored the migratory and invasive capacities of HCC cells (P < 0.0001, Figs. 4I-J).

CPEB2 is a target gene of miR-210-3p and regulated by the ZNF667-AS1/miR-210-3p axis

EVmiRNA, TargetScan, and miRWalk databases were used for cross-screening, and CPEB2 was identified as a potential target gene of miR-210-3p (Fig. 5A). The mRNA expression of CPEB2 was downregulated in patients with HBV-related HCC relative to CHB patients and healthy controls (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5B), and a significant negative correlation was observed between the expression of CPEB2 and miR-210-3p in clinical samples (r = -0.683, P < 0.0001, Fig. 5C). Compared with THLE-2 cells, both mRNA and protein levels of CPEB2 were decreased in HBV-related HCC cell lines (Hep3B2.1-7, HCCLM3, FOCUS and MHCC97H) (P < 0.0001, Figs. 5D-E). The uncropped WB images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The ENCORI database predicted a specific binding site between miR-210-3p and the 3′ untranslated region (3’UTR) region of CPEB2 (Fig. 5F). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that miR-210-3p mimic significantly reduced the relative luciferase activity of wild-type CPEB2 (WT-CPEB2) reporter in Hep3B2.1-7 and HCCLM3 cells (P < 0.0001, Figs. 5G-H). RIP assays further verified the direct targeting interaction between miR-210-3p and CPEB2 (P < 0.0001, Figs. 5I-J). RT-qPCR and WB results demonstrated that miR-210-3p mimic abrogated the up-regulatory effect of ZNF667-AS1 overexpression on CPEB2 expression (P < 0.0001, Figs. 5K–N). The uncropped WB images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

ZNF667‑AS1 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of HBV‑Related HCC cells by upregulating CPEB2

RT‑qPCR results showed that overexpression of ZNF667‑AS1 upregulated CPEB2 mRNA levels in Hep3B2.1‑7 and HCCLM3 cells, and co‑transfection with si‑CPEB2 markedly reversed this upregulation (P < 0.0001, Figs. 6A‑B). WB analysis obtained consistent results at the protein level (Figs. 6C‑D,Supplementary Fig. 2 shows uncropped full WB images). CCK‑8 assays verified that the proliferation of HCC cells was inhibited by ZNF667-AS1 overexpression, while knockdown of CPEB2 restored cell proliferative ability (P < 0.0001, Figs. 6E‑F). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that overexpression of ZNF667‑AS1 promoted cell apoptosis and induced G1 phase arrest, and these biological effects were partially reversed by si‑CPEB2 (P < 0.0001, Figs. 6G‑J). Furthermore, Transwell assays further confirmed that knockdown of CPEB2 counteracted the inhibitory effects of ZNF667‑AS1 overexpression on cell migration and invasion in both Hep3B2.1‑7 and HCCLM3 cells (P < 0.0001, Figs. 6K‑L).

Discussion

HCC is the fifth most prevalent malignant tumor worldwide and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths globally [21]. HBV infection is a key driver for the occurrence and advancement of HCC, and it contributes greatly to the high mortality and poor prognosis of liver cancer patients [22, 23]. HBV promotes HCC development through multiple mechanisms, including activation of proto-oncogenes, inhibition of tumor suppressor genes, and induction of chronic hepatocellular inflammation and damage to hepatocytes [24]. HCC is often diagnosed at advanced stages due to insidious early symptoms. Traditional diagnostic biomarkers (e.g., AFP) have limited sensitivity and specificity. Hence, the majority of patients are delayed in receiving a diagnosis until the later stages, leading to unsatisfactory treatment outcomes and poor long-term prognosis [25]. Therefore, identifying key molecules involved in HBV-related HCC pathogenesis and developing novel diagnostic/prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets are essential for improving clinical outcomes of patients.

Long non-coding RNAs have become research hotspots in oncology, as they regulate tumor initiation and progression through epigenetic modifications [26]. A large body of evidence has proven that lncRNAs are closely involved in the development of HCC, particularly HBV-related HCC. For instance, lncRNAs POLR2J4 [27] and XIST [28] promote malignant phenotypes of HCC cells by targeting and inhibiting tumor-suppressive miRNAs. In contrast, lncRNAs TSPEAR-AS1 [29] and OIP5-AS1 [13] exert tumor-suppressive functions by inhibiting tumor metabolism or cell cycle progression. Meanwhile, certain lncRNAs (e.g., LINC01793 and HOTAIR) also possess diagnostic and prognostic potential, providing new directions for non-invasive diagnosis of HCC [30, 31]. In the study, lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 was downregulated in clinical samples and cell lines of HBV-related HCC, and its expression level was lower than that in the non-HBV HCC HepG2 cells. This finding suggests that the downregulation of ZNF667-AS1 is closely associated with HBV infection-mediated hepatocarcinogenesis, exhibiting a specific expression pattern in HBV-related HCC. This result is consistent with the results from the GSE55092 database [19]. Combined with previous reports that ZNF667-AS1 acts as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and ovarian cancer [16–18], the results further support its in vitro tumor-suppressive activity in HBV-related HCC. The specific downregulation of ZNF667-AS1 in HBV-related HCC cells suggests a potential interaction between HBV infection and lncRNA dysregulation. Hepatitis B virus X protein (HBx), a key viral protein encoded by HBV, can alter the expression of host ncRNAs through transcriptional and epigenetic regulation [32]. Future studies are required to explore whether HBx directly suppresses ZNF667-AS1 expression, which will help clarify the mechanistic link between HBV infection and this regulatory axis.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are major downstream targets of lncRNAs and modulate tumor progression by post-transcriptionally inhibiting the expression of target genes [33]. In HBV-related HCC, miR-210-3p has been identified as a key oncogenic molecule. Its upregulation is closely associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor, metastasis, and it can serve as a biomarker for disease diagnosis and prognostic assessment [34–36]. In the present study, bioinformatics prediction and dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed the direct targeting relationship between ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p. A significant negative correlation was observed between ZNF667-AS1 and miR-210-3p in HCC patients, with miR-210-3p highly expressed in both HBV-related HCC patients and cell lines. This regulatory relationship is consistent with a potential competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA)-like mechanism, in which lncRNAs may act as molecular sponges to sequester miRNAs [27, 28]. However, we acknowledge that without subcellular localization analysis, we cannot definitively conclude that ZNF667-AS1 exerts its regulatory effect exclusively through the classical cytoplasmic ceRNA mechanism. Alternative mechanisms, such as transcriptional regulation or protein interactions, cannot be excluded at this stage. Cellular assays proved that ZNF667-AS1 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of HBV-related HCC cells, and miR-210-3p mimic could reverse these inhibitory effects. These results indicate that ZNF667-AS1 mediates in vitro tumor-suppressive effects by targeting and inhibiting miR-210-3p. This regulatory mechanism is consistent with the mode of action of ZNF667-AS1 in other tumors-for example, inhibiting tumor progression by regulating EMT-related proteins in gastric cancer [17] and impeding tumor dissemination by suppressing proliferation and invasion in ovarian cancer [18] -and also aligns with the known function of miR-210-3p in promoting HCC metastasis and EMT [36]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the ZNF667-AS1/miR-210-3p regulatory axis in HBV-related HCC, which enriches the molecular regulatory network of HBV-related liver cancer and providing a novel perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of the disease.

The study confirmed that CPEB2 is a direct target gene of miR-210-3p and its expression is precisely regulated by the ZNF667-AS1/miR-210-3p axis. Extensive studies have demonstrated that CPEB2 acts as a tumor suppressor gene and a favorable prognostic biomarker in HCC: Low CPEB2 expression is closely associated with increased risk of recurrence and poor clinical outcomes in HCC patients, whereas its high expression indicates a better prognosis for patients [36, 37]. CPEB2 inhibits HCC progression by suppressing EMT and tumor metastasis, and its expression can be negatively regulated by multiple oncogenic miRNAs such as miR-624 in HCC cells [36, 38]. These findings are consistent with the findings of the study that CPEB2 is downregulated in HBV-related HCC patients and cell lines, and negatively correlated with the expression of the oncogenic molecule miR-210-3p. Further cellular experiments confirmed that ZNF667-AS1 can upregulate CPEB2 expression by sponging miR-210-3p, and the overexpression of miR-210-3p abolishes this effect. Collectively, ZNF667-AS1 may exert tumor-suppressive effects and partially through modulating miR-210-3p and subsequently affecting CPEB2 expression. While this pattern is compatible with a ceRNA-like regulatory axis, further studies including subcellular localization and RIP-seq are warranted to confirm the precise molecular mechanism. Accumulating evidence also shows that CPEB2 plays a protective role in liver tissues by regulating lipid metabolism and inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, suggesting that CPEB2 may suppress HBV-related HCC through multiple biological pathways [39].

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‑center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may reduce the generalizability of the diagnostic and prognostic values of ZNF667‑AS1. Second, although the expression of ZNF667‑AS1 was consistently downregulated in multiple HBV‑related HCC cell lines, functional experiments were only performed in Hep3B2.1‑7 and HCCLM3 cells using overexpression approaches. Loss‑of‑function assays based on siRNA or shRNA knockdown in additional cell lines are warranted to further validate the conclusions. Third, this study did not detect the expression levels of apoptosis, cell cycle, or EMT-related markers, which limits the in‑depth exploration of molecular mechanisms. Fourth, in vivo experiments using xenograft or metastatic mouse models were not performed to verify the tumor‑suppressive role of ZNF667‑AS1, which limits the translational value of this study. Fifth, the upstream regulatory mechanism by which HBV infection downregulates ZNF667‑AS1 remains unclear. Large-sample, multi‑center clinical data and in vivo animal experiments will be carried out in future research to validate the findings and elucidate the complete regulatory network of the ZNF667‑AS1/miR‑210‑3p/CPEB2 axis in HBV‑related HCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study confirms that lncRNA ZNF667-AS1 is significantly downregulated in HBV-related HCC, indicating its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for the disease. Mechanistically, ZNF667-AS1 exerts in tumor-suppressive effects in vitro by targeting miR-210-3p and subsequently upregulating its downstream target gene CPEB2, thereby suppressing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HBV-related HCC cells. The findings of this study provide a potential auxiliary biomarker for early detection and preliminary molecular insights that may guide future therapeutic investigations of HBV-related HCC. However, given the lack of in vivo validation in the present study, any translational or therapeutic implications remain speculative and require further preclinical and clinical investigation.
