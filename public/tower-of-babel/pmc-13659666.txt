Durability of a physician-modified endograft constructed using the Terumo Aortic TREO device: A postmortem evaluation
Authors: Nicolas A. Stafforini, Sara L. Zettervall, Allison Larimore, Benjamin W. Starnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fenestrated endovascular repair using physician-modified endografts has become an important treatment option for complex abdominal aortic aneurysms, although long-term graft durability data remain limited. We present a unique postmortem evaluation of a physician-modified endograft implanted for a juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm as part of an Investigational Device Exemption trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01538056). Postmortem examination demonstrated no evidence of stent fracture, graft migration, or structural failure. The graft remained well incorporated within the aortic neck with preserved fixation and seal. These findings provide the first reported human postmortem evidence supporting the durability of a physician-modified Terumo Aortic TREO endograft.

Endovascular repair has become the standard treatment for patients with infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) who are anatomically suitable for repair. Over the past two decades, endovascular management of complex AAAs has similarly demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy, leading to the widespread adoption of fenestrated and branched techniques. Fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair gained further acceptance in the United States following U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval of a commercially manufactured fenestrated device in 2012.1

Before commercial availability, several investigators obtained Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) approval to modify commercially available endografts with physician-created fenestrations, allowing treatment of patients who were unsuitable for standard endovascular aneurysm repair or open repair or unable to wait for a custom-manufactured device. Physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) have demonstrated that, despite reinterventions being common, aneurysm-related morbidity and mortality remain low, further supporting the safety and effectiveness of this approach for the treatment of juxtarenal AAAs.2,3

In addition, we previously reported a postmortem evaluation of a PMEG constructed using a Cook endograft (Cook Medical), providing unique evidence supporting long-term device integrity.4 However, the durability of PMEGs constructed using the Terumo Aortic TREO platform (Terumo) has not been reported. We present a rare postmortem evaluation of a Terumo-based PMEG implanted for the treatment of a juxtarenal AAA as part of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved IDE trial, providing a unique opportunity to directly assess device durability, fixation, and structural integrity. Informed consent was obtained for publication of this report.

Case report

A 93-year-old woman with an 8 cm juxtarenal AAA underwent PMEG repair in September 2021. During the procedure, a 28 × 120-mm bifurcated TREO main body was modified to create three fenestrations using ophthalmic cautery. The device was subsequently completed with a 15 × 120 mm contralateral limb and a 15 × 120 mm ipsilateral limb. The fenestrations were created using gold snares sutured to the graft fabric to outline each opening. During the operation, the renal arteries were stented using balloon-expandable covered stents (7 × 22 mm; ICast stents, Atrium Medical Corporation), and the superior mesenteric artery was left unstented. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on postoperative day 2.

Over 27 months of follow-up, the aneurysm sac regressed to 6.4 cm, with no evidence of endoleak. The patient underwent routine follow-up surveillance at 6 months, 1 and 2 years, as per our standard protocol. Follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated continued target vessel alignment and stability throughout the monitoring period. In addition, follow-up X rays revealed no evidence of stent fractures or migration.

In May 2024, the patient died while receiving hospice care after an ischemic stroke. The patient's aorta was donated for ongoing study. The specimen was received intact and included the thoracoabdominal aorta from the external iliac arteries to the proximal thoracic aorta, with preserved visceral arterial branches. Detailed necropsy and dissection of the donated specimen were subsequently performed. After dissection, a high-resolution digital photograph was obtained to document the structural integrity and findings in detail (Fig 1). Once the endograft was removed, a multistep evaluation was performed that included treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide and 50% sodium hypochlorite (Fig 2). After completion of our institutional analysis, the explanted graft was sent to Terumo Aortic for an independent detailed evaluation. Each device component was examined by direct visual inspection, a ×1.5 magnifying lens, and microscopy at ×10 magnification, with fabric defects measured using calibrated calipers. The graft was completely disassembled, enabling detailed inspection of all graft components and junctional zones. The findings from the manufacturer’s analysis were consistent with those identified during our independent institutional assessment.

Detailed analysis of the explanted graft demonstrated minor areas of fabric wear and several isolated suture breaks involving the proximal, middle, and iliac stent segments. Small fabric defects were identified adjacent to stent struts and between stent segments, consistent with chronic stent-fabric interaction. Several larger fabric defects were also observed, including defects measuring approximately 40.6 and 4.0 mm2. Evaluation of these defects demonstrated geometric correlation with adjacent stent components, including linear and crescent-shaped patterns corresponding to the configuration of neighboring stent struts and apices. In several regions, the defects were located adjacent to isolated suture breaks and areas of presumed contact between the fabric and stent framework, suggesting chronic stent-fabric interaction as the underlying mechanism. Importantly, there was no associated stent fracture, loss of structural integrity, or evidence of device fatigue. However, the morphology and location of the largest defects suggested that specimen handling and graft removal during explantation may have contributed to their final appearance and measured size, rather than representing clinically significant in vivo device failure.

No nitinol-related abnormalities, stent fractures, or evidence of stent fatigue were identified throughout the device. The proximal stent and first middle stent remained well opposed to the aortic wall, whereas the distal stent segments were located within the aneurysm sac. Evaluation of the iliac limb components revealed no fabric defects or nitinol abnormalities, although a small number of isolated suture breaks were present.

Assessment of the fenestrations demonstrated preserved structural integrity without evidence of suture disruption, enlargement, distortion, or fenestration failure. A single small fabric defect measuring <0.5 mm2 was identified between the renal stents and was considered most likely secondary to stent manipulation during graft removal (Fig 3). Discussion

PMEGs have become an important treatment option for patients with complex aortic aneurysms who are unsuitable for standard endovascular repair or unable to wait for a custom-manufactured fenestrated device. Multiple studies have demonstrated favorable early-, mid-, and long-term outcomes with PMEGs.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 However, direct evaluation of device durability remains limited.

The present case provides a unique opportunity to assess the structural integrity of a PMEG constructed using the Terumo Aortic TREO platform. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported human postmortem evaluation of a Terumo-based PMEG. Previous postmortem analysis of a PMEG constructed using a Cook endograft demonstrated preserved device integrity after long-term implantation.4 Similar findings were observed in the present case, further supporting the durability of physician-modified devices.

Several important observations emerged from this analysis. First, no evidence of stent fracture, nitinol fatigue, graft migration, loss of fixation, target vessel instability, or fenestration failure was identified. The proximal fixation segment remained well apposed to the aortic wall, and the fenestrations demonstrated preserved structural integrity without evidence of enlargement, distortion, or suture disruption. These findings are particularly relevant because concerns regarding fenestration durability with the mechanical fatigue caused by repeated aortic and renovisceral vessel movement over time.

Second, although several areas of isolated suture breaks were identified, these findings appeared limited and did not result in any evidence of device failure. In addition, several larger fabric tears were identified. However, their morphology and location strongly suggested that they were related to specimen dissection and graft removal during explant analysis rather than in vivo structural deterioration.

Although these findings were not associated with graft instability or structural compromise in the present case, the observation of isolated suture disruption highlights the importance of continued surveillance of implanted devices. Additional postmortem analyses and larger explant series will be valuable to determine whether these changes represent isolated findings or have implications for future endograft design and material optimization. Given the single-case nature of this report, our findings do not support specific recommendations regarding alternative suture materials or device construction at this time.

The durability demonstrated in this case is further supported by the patient’s clinical course. Our IDE protocol places particular emphasis on identifying stent graft structural compromise and migration. In addition to CTA, radiographs with anteroposterior, lateral, and bilateral oblique views are obtained at each surveillance visit. Review of all postoperative CTA and plain radiographs demonstrated no evidence of stent graft compromise, fracture, or migration. Serial postoperative imaging showed progressive aneurysm sac regression without evidence of endoleak, and no device-related complications were identified during follow-up.

Finally, this case also highlights, consistent with previous reports, the safety and effectiveness of PMEG repair in carefully selected elderly patients.8 The patient underwent successful repair of an 8 cm juxtarenal AAA at 93 years of age and experienced an uncomplicated postoperative recovery, with discharge on postoperative day 2.

Several limitations warrant consideration. As a single-case study, the findings, although informative, may not be generalizable. In addition, no other necropsy specimens were available for comparison and the specimen handling during extraction may have contributed to some of the observed fabric defects.

Furthermore, as the patient died 27 months after implantation, this analysis does not provide information regarding the long-term structural durability of the device. Nevertheless, the combination of favorable clinical outcomes and preserved structural integrity on postmortem examination provides complementary evidence supporting the performance of this device platform.

Conclusions

This case provided a unique opportunity to assess the durability of a PMEG constructed using the Terumo Aortic TREO platform. Postmortem evaluation demonstrated no evidence of stent migration, stent fracture, graft failure, or fenestration disruption, and the graft remained well incorporated within the healthy aortic neck. The fabric defects identified were most likely related to specimen dissection during graft explantation. These findings provide rare human evidence supporting the durability and structural integrity of PMEGs.
