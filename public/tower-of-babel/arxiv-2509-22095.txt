Trivializing Torsors for Difference Algebraic Groups Thanks: This work was supported by DFG (Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft): SFB-TRR 195
Michael Wibmer, Annette Bachmayr

Abstract
. We study torsors for groups defined by algebraic difference equations. Our main result provides necessary and sufficient conditions on the base difference field for all such torsors to be trivial. We also present an application to the uniqueness of difference Picard-Vessiot extensions for linear differential equations.

1.
Introduction

It is a classical question in algebraic geometry and field arithmetic to understand conditions on the base field
k
that guarantee that
H^{1}(k,G)
is trivial or small for large classes of linear algebraic groups
G
over
k
. (See e.g.,
[
Ser02
]
). For example, perfect fields
k
of cohomological dimension at most one (which include
C_{1}
-fields) are characterized by the property that
H^{1}(k,G)
is trivial for all connected linear algebraic groups over
k
and this is equivalent to
H^{1}(k,G)
being trivial for all semisimple algebraic groups
G
over
k
. It is also known that
H^{1}(k,G)
is finite for every linear algebraic group
G
over
k
if
k
is perfect and bounded, i.e.,
k
has only finitely many algebraic extensions of any fixed degree. There has been a sustained interest in obtaining analogs of these results for differential algebraic groups (
[
Pil17
]
,
[
MO19
]
,
[
CP19
]
,

[
SP21
]
,
[
LSMP24
]
,
[
MP25
]
). One of the most profound results in the area is the following theorem due to Anand Pillay.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Pil17
]
)
.

For an ordinary differential field
k
of characteristic zero the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

For every linear differential algebraic group
G
over
k
and every
G
-torsor
X
we have
X(k)\neq\emptyset
.

(ii)

We have
H^{1}_{\delta}(k,G)=0
for every linear differential algebraic group
G
over
k
.

(iii)

The field
k
is algebraically closed and for every
m\geq 1
and every
A\in k^{m\times m}
the linear differential equation
\delta(Y)=AY
has a solution in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
.

Here (ii) refers to Kolchin’s constrained cohomology and the equivalence of (i) and (ii) is well known (
[
Kol85
, Chapter VII]
). So the striking part of Theorem
1.1
is that (iii) implies (i) or (ii). As a finite Galois extension defines a torsor for a finite constant group scheme, we see that a field
k
of characteristic zero is algebraically closed if and only if all torsors for all linear algebraic groups over
k
are trivial. Moreover, for a differential field
k
and
A\in k^{m\times m}
, the differential algebraic variety
X=\{Y\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \delta(Y)=AY\}
over
k
is a torsor for the linear differential algebraic group
\operatorname{GL}_{m}^{\delta}=\{g\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \delta(g)=0\}
. Thus, Theorem
1.1
can be reformulated as follows: If for all
m\geq 1
all torsors for
\operatorname{GL}_{m}^{\delta}
are trivial and all torsors for linear algebraic groups are trivial, then all torsors for all linear differential algebraic groups are trivial.

Generalizations of Theorem
1.1
to partial differential fields are discussed in
[
CP19
]
and
[
MO19
]
. Another generalization of Theorem
1.1
, that can be seen as a relative version, not requiring any assumptions on the ordinary base differential field, can be found in
[
MP25
]
.

The question of an appropriate difference analog of Theorem
1.1
was raised in
[
Pil17
, Section 3]
and in
[
CP19
]
it is shown that the
immediate
analog of Theorem
1.1
fails for difference algebraic groups. The counterexample provided is related to the difference algebraic subgroup
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ x^{-2}\sigma(x)=1\}
of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{G}_{m}
and the
G
-torsor
X=\{x\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ x^{-2}\sigma(x)=a\}
, where
a\in k^{\times}
. More generally, for
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha_{m}\neq 0
and
a\in k^{\times}
, we have a difference algebraic subgroup
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(g)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(g)^{\alpha_{m}}=1\}
of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
and a
G
-torsor
X=\{x\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ x^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(x)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(x)^{\alpha_{m}}=a\}
.

The main result of this paper is an analog of Theorem
1.1
for difference algebraic groups.
In essence, it states that the solvability of these multiplicative equations
y^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(y)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(y)^{\alpha_{m}}=a
is the only obstruction to the validity of the immediate difference analog of Theorem
1.1
. However, as difference algebraic geometry is somewhat more intricate than differential algebraic geometry, this has to be taken with a grain of salt. Specifically, while differential algebraic geometry is more or less synonymous with the model theory of differential fields, the connections are more subtle in the difference case.

Before precisely stating our main result, we need to explain other obstructions to the validity of the immediate difference analog of Theorem
1.1
, that are not related to difference algebraic subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
and can be resolved by passing to higher powers of the endomorphism
\sigma
. We note that the philosophy that things can be smoothed out by passing to higher powers of
\sigma
is also present in other work (e.g.,
[
CHP02
]
or
[
Wib12
]
).

Some torsors for linear difference algebraic groups do not have a point in any difference field. For example, the difference variety
X=\{x\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ x^{2}=1,\ \sigma(x)=-x\}
is a torsor for the linear difference algebraic group
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{2}=1,\ \sigma(g)=g\}
(under left multiplication) but
X(k)=\emptyset
for every difference field
k
. In other words, this torsor will never be trivial, no matter over which base difference field it is considered.
From a model theoretic perspective, where one is confined to working inside a monster model of the theory of difference fields, the torsor
X
does not exist, i.e., it collapses to the empty set. However, this perspective is unsatisfactory from the point of view of difference algebraic geometry, because the system of equations
x^{2}=1,\ \sigma(x)=-x
is consistent, in the sense that it has a solution in a difference ring. For example,
(1,-1,1,-1,1,\ldots)
is a solution in the ring of sequences (equipped with the shift).

The problem that not every torsor has a point in a difference field can be resolved by looking for solutions slightly beyond the base difference field
k
. In the above example, we have
(1,-1)\in X(k^{[2]})
, where, for an integer
n\geq 1
, we let
k^{[n]}=k\times\ldots\times k
denote the product of
n
copies of
k
, considered as a difference algebra over
k
via

k\to k^{[n]},\ a\mapsto(\sigma^{n-1}(a),\ldots,\sigma(a),a)
and

\sigma\colon k^{[n]}\to k^{[n]},\ (a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\mapsto(\sigma^{n}(a_{n}),a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}).

A similar obstruction to the immediate difference analog of Theorem
1.1
occurs if we look at linear difference algebraic groups of the form
G=\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma(g)=\psi(g)\}
, where
\mathcal{G}\leq\operatorname{GL}_{m}
is an algebraic subgroup defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and
\psi\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
is a morphism of algebraic groups. For example, we can take
G=\{g\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \sigma(g)=(g^{T})^{-1}\}
. It is known that all
G
-torsors are of the form
X=\{x\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \sigma(x)=(x^{T})^{-1}A\}
for some
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
. The equation
x^{T}\sigma(x)=A
is non-linear and there appears to be no reason why we should be able to find a solution in the base difference field
k
, just because
k
is algebraically closed and we can solve linear difference equations in
k
. However, if we pass from
\sigma
to
\sigma^{2}
, then every
x\in X
satisfies
\sigma^{2}(x)=x(A^{T})^{-1}\sigma(A)
(a linear difference equation over
(k,\sigma^{2})
) and we have a difference variety

X_{2}=\{x\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \sigma^{2}(x)=x(A^{T})^{-1}\sigma(A)\}

over the difference field
(k,\sigma^{2})
. Thus, if every linear difference equation over
(k,\sigma^{2})
is solvable, then
X_{2}(k)\neq\emptyset
. Furthermore,
X_{2}(k)\neq\emptyset
turns out to be equivalent to
X(k^{[2]})\neq\emptyset
(Remark
3.6
).
In Section
3
below we will explain systematically how a difference variety
X
over
(k,\sigma)
gives rise to a difference variety
X_{n}
over
(k,\sigma^{n})
for every
n\geq 1
. We will also see that for a linear difference algebraic group
G
, the cohomology sets
H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,G_{n})
naturally form a direct system. The main result of this paper is the following difference analog of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For an ordinary difference field
k
of characteristic zero the following statements are equivalent:

(P1)

For every linear difference algebraic group
G
over
k
and for every
G
-torsor
X
, there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
X(k^{[n]})\neq\emptyset
.

(P2)

We have
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,G_{n})=0
for every linear difference algebraic group
G
over
k
.

(P3)

(a)

The field
k
is algebraically closed.

(b)

For every
m\geq 1
and every
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that the equation
\sigma(Y)=AY
has a solution in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k^{[n]})
.

(c)

For all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha_{m}\neq 0
and
a\in k^{\times}
, there exists an
n\geq 1
such that the equation
y^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(y)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(y)^{\alpha_{m}}=a

has a solution in
k^{[n]}
.

We note that we do not make any assumptions on the constants
k^{\sigma}=\{a\in k|\ \sigma(a)=a\}
of
k
. The constants of an algebraically closed differential field are automatically algebraically closed. However, this need not be the case in the difference world. Condition (P3) (b) is similar to what is called “strongly PV-closed” or “linearly closed” in
[
CP19
]
,
[
CK23
]
and
[
KP07
]
. A difference field
k
is strongly PV-closed if for every
m\geq 1
and every
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
the equation
\sigma(Y)=AY
has a solution in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
. Our condition (P3) (b) is weaker than being strongly PV-closed and is more in line with the Picard-Vessiot theory of linear difference equations (
[
vdPS97
]
), where one allows zero-divisors in the solution rings and usually assumes the constants to be algebraically closed. The constants of a strongly PV-closed difference field have absolute Galois group
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
(because for every
m\geq 1
the equation
\sigma^{m}(y)=y
has to have
m
solutions in
k
that are linearly independent over the constants). On the other hand, our condition (P3) (b) does not obviously imply any restrictions on the constants (although it follows from (P3) (a) that the absolute Galois group of the constants is procyclic).

The multiplicative difference equations in (P3) (b) are intimately related to toric difference varieties studied in
[
GHWY17
]
,
[
GHY17
]

[
Wan20
]
,
[
Wan18
]
.

Condition (P3) (b) is equivalent to the special case of (P1) where
G=\operatorname{GL}_{m}^{\sigma}=\{g\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \sigma(g)=g\}
, while condition (P3) (c) is equivalent to the special case of (P1) for one-relator subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, i.e., for difference algebraic subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
of the form
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(g)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(g)^{\alpha_{m}}=1\}
.

For a linear difference algebraic group
G
over a difference field
k
, we say that
k
is
G
-
trivial
if for all
G
-torsors
X
there exists an
n\geq 1
such that
X(k^{[n]})\neq\emptyset
. With this nomenclature our main result can be reformulated as follows: If
k
is
G
-trivial for all
G
where
G
is a linear algebraic group, a difference algebraic group of the form
G=\operatorname{GL}_{m}^{\sigma}=\{g\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}|\ \sigma(g)=g\}
(for some
m\geq 1
), or a one-relator subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, then
k
is
G
-trivial for all linear difference algebraic groups
G
over
k
. In this sense, the one-relator subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
are the only obstruction to the immediate difference analog of Theorem
1.1
.

Note that the integer
n
in the first condition of Theorem
1.2
depends on both the difference algebraic group
G
and the
G
-torsors
X
. Similarly, in the third condition, the integer
n
depends on
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
or
a\in k^{\times}
. We can replace both conditions by a stronger condition where an
n
has to exist uniformly for all
G
-torsors, and where
n
is not allowed to depend on
A
and
a
. As an additional result we show that these stronger conditions are still equivalent:

Theorem 1.3
.

For an ordinary difference field
k
of characteristic zero the following statements are equivalent:

(P1
u
)

For every linear difference algebraic group
G
over
k
there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
X(k^{[n]})\neq\emptyset
for every
G
-torsor
X
.

(P2
u
)

For every linear difference algebraic group
G
over
k
, there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that the natural map
H^{1}_{\sigma}(k,G)\to H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,G_{n})
is trivial.

(P3
u
)

(a)

The field
k
is algebraically closed.

(b)

For every
m\geq 1
, there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that for every
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
the equation
\sigma(Y)=AY
has a solution in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k^{[n]})
.

(c)

For all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha_{m}\neq 0
, there exists an
n\geq 1
such that for all
a\in k^{\times}
the equation
y^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(y)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(y)^{\alpha_{m}}=a

has a solution in
k^{[n]}
.

There appears to be no previous difference analogs of Theorem
1.1
in the literature. However, the connection between
H^{1}(k,\operatorname{GL}_{m}^{\sigma})
and solutions of linear difference equations was already noted in
[
CK23
, Section 7.3]
, where also the counterexample from
[
CP19
]
is interpreted in cohomological terms.

In Section
6
we show that every
\sigma
-closed difference field satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.3
. While we establish our main result ((P3)
\Rightarrow
(P1)) only in characteristic zero, we think that it is interesting to note that an algebraically closed field
k
of positive characteristic

, equipped with a Frobenius endomorphism
\sigma_{q}\colon k\to k,\ a\mapsto a^{q}
(with

), satisfies property (P3

). Indeed, that
(k,\sigma_{q})
satisfies (P3

) (b) with

is a special case of the Lang-Steinberg Theorem and that
(k,\sigma_{q})
satisfies (P3

follows from

being algebraically closed.

Our main result has applications in the difference Galois theory of linear differential equations (
[
DVHW14
]
), a special case of the parameterized Picard-Vessiot theory, where one considers a linear differential equation and an auxiliary operator such as a derivation or an endomorphism that is acting on the coefficients of the linear differential equation. A typically example is a linear differential equation with coefficients that depend on a parameter and the auxiliary operator is the derivation with respect to the parameter or a shift operator on the parameter.
Our application concerns the uniqueness of the solution rings in the context of this Galois theory, known as
\sigma
-
Picard-Vessiot rings.
This is similar to the application in
[
Pil17
, Cor. 1.4]
of Theorem
1.1
to the uniqueness of the solution rings in the case when the auxiliary operator is a derivation. However, our situation is slightly different because
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings are generally not unique, even when the constants are
\sigma
-closed. In general, two
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings for the same equation only become isomorphic when passing to higher powers of
\sigma
. More precisely, in
[
DVHW14
, Cor. 1.17]
it is shown that if
K
is a
\delta\sigma
-field such that the
\delta
-constants
K^{\delta}
are a
\sigma
-closed
\sigma
-field and
R_{1},R_{2}
are two
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings for the equation
\delta(y)=Ay
(for some
A\in K^{m\times m}
), then there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic as
K
-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras. We note that the integer
n
is allowed to depend on
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. In particular, the need to pass to higher powers of
\sigma
also naturally arises in the parameterized Picard-Vessiot theory and this phenomenon is well-aligned with our philosophy underlying the statements of Theorems
1.3
and
1.2
.

We improve the above uniqueness result in two ways. Firstly, we show that the same uniqueness result also holds if we only assume that
K^{\delta}
satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.2
. Secondly, we show that if
K^{\delta}
satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.3
(e.g.,
K^{\delta}
is
\sigma
-
closed), then the integer
n
can be chosen uniformly for all pairs
R_{1},R_{2}
of
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings.

We conclude the introduction with an outline of the content. In Section
2
we set the stage by recalling definitions and introducing our notation. In Section
3
we formally introduce, for every difference variety
X
over
(k,\sigma)
, the difference varieties
X_{n}
over
(k,\sigma^{n})
and we establish the important induction principle. It states that if
N
is a normal difference algebraic subgroup of a difference algebraic group
G
, such that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,N)=0
and
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,(G/N)_{n})=0
, then
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}H^{1}_{\sigma^{n}}(k,G_{n})=0
. This principle allows us to use decomposition theorems for difference algebraic groups to reduce the proof to certain types of mostly quite explicitly described classes of difference algebraic groups. In Section
3
we also show that (P1) and (P2) are equivalent, which is fairly straight forward once the appropriate definitions are in place. The fact that (P1) implies (P3) is also fairly immediate and established in Section
4
along with some reformulations of property (P3).
The most difficult part of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is the implication (P3)
\Rightarrow
(P1). This relies on several structure and decomposition theorems for difference algebraic groups. We first treat special classes of difference algebraic groups (e.g., order zero, diagonalizable, difference dimension theory zero) and build up to the general case. In Section
6
we show that a
\sigma
-closed difference field satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.3
. Finally, in Section
7
we present the application to the parameterized Picard-Vessiot theory.

3.

Let

be an exact sequence of
\sigma
-algebraic groups over

\Rightarrow
(P1) because, after decomposing a general
\sigma
-algebraic group into more manageable pieces, it allows us to restrict our considerations to those more manageable pieces. In this section we also show that (P1) and (P2) are equivalent and similarly, their uniform variants (P1
u
) and (P2
u
) are equivalent.

-field (of arbitrary characteristic).

For (c) let

As indicated in the introduction, to show that (P1) implies (P3) it suffices to consider (P1) for a few particular

Proposition 4.5

\sigma

Proof.

To prove (a) in both cases, we need to show that (P1) implies that

Moreover, if

To prove (b), consider the

For

To prove (c) consider the

5.
The proof of (P3)
\Rightarrow
(P1)

By now we have seen that (P1) and (P2) are equivalent (Proposition

) and that (P1) implies (P3) (Propsition
4.5
) and similarly for their uniform variants.
Thus to complete the proof of our main result, it suffices to prove (P3)
\Rightarrow
(P1) (and (P3
u
)
\Rightarrow
(P1
u
)). This however is the most difficult part.

From now on we assume that

is a
\sigma
-field of characteristic zero.

It is an interesting question to ask for a generalization of Theorem
1.3
to positive characteristic. It may seem plausible to expect that the theorem also holds for a difference field
k
of positive characteristic

if (P3
u
) (b) is replaced by

(P3
u
) (
b_{p}
)
For every
m\geq 1
and every power

of

there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that for every

the equation
\sigma_{q}(Y)=AY
has a solution in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k^{[n]})
.

Here
\sigma_{q}
is defined by
\sigma_{q}(y)=\sigma(y)^{q}
. Note that the equation

defines a torsor for the
\sigma
-algebraic group

. Moreover, any Frobenius difference field, i.e., an algebraically closed field of positive characteristic equipped with a Frobenius endomorphism, satisfies (P3

) (

) with

(due to the Lang-Steinberg Theorem).

Many of our intermediate results (and proofs) in this section also work in arbitrary characteristic. However, there are also several steps where we make use of the characteristic zero assumption. At those steps, in positive characteristic, one usually would have to contend with non-reduced algebraic or
\sigma
-
algebraic groups and the Frobenius endomorphism. We hope to establish a variant of Theorem
1.3
in positive characteristic in a future project dedicated to the study of torsors for
\sigma
-algebraic groups over Frobenius difference fields.

One of the advantages of working in characteristic zero is that every Hopf algebra is reduced. Therefore, a
\sigma
-algebraic group
G
is connected if and only if it is integral (
[
Wib21
, Lemma 6.7]
).

In this section we show that if
k
satisfies (P3) (or (P3
u
)), then
k
is (uniformly) strongly
G
-trivial for any
\sigma
-
algebraic group
G
over
k
. The proof heavily relies on the induction principle from Section
3
and the structure theory of
\sigma
-algebraic groups. Any
\sigma
-algebraic group can be decomposed into
\sigma
-algebraic groups belonging to certain more manageable classes of
\sigma
-algebraic groups and we first show that if
k
satisfies (P3), then

is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial for any

belonging to one of those more manageable classes. We start from classes of small (e.g., finite, order zero,
\sigma
-dimension zero)
\sigma
-algebraic groups and then build up to the general case.

5.1.
The proof for
\sigma
-algebraic groups of order zero

In this subsection we show that an inversive, algebraically closed
\sigma
-field is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial for every
\sigma
-algebraic group
G
of order zero. The key ingredient for the proof is a decomposition theorem for
\sigma
-étale
\sigma
-algebraic groups (
[
Wib24
, Theorem 6.38]
).
We first show that an algebraically closed
\sigma
-field is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial for every
\sigma
-algebraic group
G
coming from an algebraic group and for finite
\sigma
-algebraic groups.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed
\sigma
-field.
If
G=[\sigma]_{k}\mathcal{G}
for some algebraic group
\mathcal{G}
over
k
, then
k
is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial.

Proof.

Because
G_{n}
comes from an algebraic group (Example
3.4
), it suffices to show that
k
is uniformly
G
-trivial.
By
[
BW22
, Cor. 3.3]
every
G
-torsor

is of the form
X=[\sigma]_{k}\mathcal{X}
for some
\mathcal{G}
-torsor
\mathcal{X}
. Thus
X(k)=\mathcal{X}(k)\neq\emptyset
because
k
is algebraically closed.
∎

A
\sigma
-algebraic group
G
is called

if
k\{G\}
is a finite dimensional
k
-vector space.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
G
be a finite
\sigma
-algebraic group over an algebraically closed
\sigma
-field

. Then

is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial.

Proof.

Because
G_{n}
is a finite
\sigma^{n}
-algebraic group for every

, it suffices to show that

is uniformly
G
-trivial.

Let
m=\dim_{k}(k\{G\})
denote the dimension of
k\{G\}
as a
k
-vector space. We claim that
X_{n}(k)\neq\emptyset
for every
G
-torsor

, where

. As
k\{X\}\otimes_{k}k\{X\}\simeq k\{G\}\otimes_{k}k\{X\}
, we see that
\dim_{k}(k\{X\})=m
. So there are at most

prime ideals in
k\{X\}
. Considering the map
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto\sigma^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
on
\operatorname{Spec}(k\{X\})
shows that there exists a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
in
k\{X\}
such that
\sigma^{-l}(\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{p}
for some

with

. As

divides

, we also have
\sigma^{-n}(\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{p}
. Since

is algebraically closed, the residue field

of

equals

. The canonical map

is a morphism of
k

\sigma^{n}
-algebras. So

.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
G
be a
\sigma
-algebraic group of order zero over an algebraically closed
\sigma
-field
k
.
Then

is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial.

Proof.

Since we work in characteristic zero, a
\sigma
-algebraic group of order zero is
\sigma
-étale (
[
Wib24
, Cor. 4.9]
). According to
[
Wib24
, Theorem 6.38]
any
\sigma
-étale
\sigma
-algebraic group has a Babbitt decomposition, i.e., there exists a subnormal series

G\supseteq G_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq G_{n}\supseteq 1

such that

(i)

G_{1}=G^{\sigma o}
is the
\sigma
-identity component
of

as defined in
[
Wib24
, Def. 3.16]
. The exact definition of

is not relevant for our purpose. All we need to know is that the quotient

is finite (cf.
[
Wib24
, Prop. 3.13]
).

(ii)

is isomorphic to a

-algebraic group of the form

is an étale algebraic group for

and

(iii)

is
\sigma

. Again, for our purpose, the exact meaning of

is finite and this is the case because a

-closed subgroup of

-étale

) and a

).

It follows from Lemma

that

is uniformly strongly

-trivial and uniformly strongly

-

Furthermore,

is uniformly strongly

-trivial for

. Thus, according to the induction principle (Corollary

),

is uniformly strongly
G
-trivial. ∎

The following corollary will allow us to reduce to the case of connected
\sigma
-algebraic groups in the sequel.

Corollary 5.4
.

Assume that

is algebraically closed and let

be a
\sigma
-algebraic group. If

is (uniformly) strongly

-trivial, then

is (uniformly) strongly

-trivial.

Proof.

As

is
\sigma
-étale it has order zero (

, Prop. 4.8]
). So the assertion follows from Proposition

and Proposition

(ii) applied to the exact sequence

.
∎

5.2.
A Jordan-Hölder type theorem

With the order zero case out of the way, the next step is to look at
\sigma
-algebraic groups of finite positive order, i.e.,
\sigma
-algebraic groups of
\sigma
-dimension zero. This is the most elaborate part of the proof.

We first treat some special cases (diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups, abelian
\sigma
-algebraic groups and Zariski dense
\sigma
-closed subgroups of simple algebraic groups) and then combine these via a Jordan-Hölder type decomposition. Since the Jordan-Hölder type decomposition is also helpful for the diagonalizable case we establish it first.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
G
be a
\sigma
-algebraic group of finite positive order. Then
G
is
almost-simple
if
\operatorname{ord}(N)=0
for every proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup
N
of
G
.

As
G^{o}
is a normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
with
\operatorname{ord}(G^{o})=\operatorname{ord}(G)
(
[
Wib21
, Cor. 6.13]
), we see that an almost-simple
\sigma
-algebraic group is connected.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
G
be a connected
\sigma
-algebraic of finite positive order. If
H
is a proper
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
, then
\operatorname{ord}(H)<\operatorname{ord}(G)
.

Proof.

Fix an embedding of
G
into some algebraic group and let
H[i]\leq G[i]
denote the corresponding Zariski closures. Because
G
is connected, all the
G[i]
’s are connected (
[
Wib21
, Lemma 6.12]
). We have
\operatorname{ord}(G)=\dim(G[i])
and
\operatorname{ord}(H)=\dim(H[i])
for all sufficiently large
i
. Suppose
\operatorname{ord}(H)=\operatorname{ord}(G)
so that
\dim(H[i])=\dim(G[i])
. Because we are in characteristic zero and
G[i]
is connected, this implies
H[i]=G[i]
for all
i
. Thus
H=G
; a contradiction.
∎

The classical Jordan-Hölder Theorem decomposes a finite group into simple groups. Our variant decomposes a connected
\sigma
-algebraic group of finite positive order into almost-simple
\sigma
-algebraic groups.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
G
be a connected
\sigma
-algebraic group of finite positive order. Then there exists a subnormal series

G=G_{0}\supseteq G_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq G_{n}=1

such that
G_{i}
is connected and
G_{i}/G_{i+1}
is almost-simple for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\operatorname{ord}(G)
. If
\operatorname{ord}(G)=1
, then any proper
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
has order
0
by Lemma
5.6
. Thus the theorem holds with
n=1
.

Assume
\operatorname{ord}(G)>1
.
If every proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
has order
0
the theorem also holds with
n=1
. So we may assume that there exists a proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
of positive order. Among all such subgroups let
N
be of maximal order. Because
N^{o}
is a characteristic subgroup of
N
(
[
Wib21
, Prop. 6.17]
) and
N
is normal in
G
, it follows that
N^{o}
is normal in
G
.

We claim that
G/N^{o}
is almost-simple. A proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G/N^{o}
is of the form
N^{\prime}/N^{o}
for some proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup
N^{\prime}
of
G
containing
N^{o}
(
[
Wib21
, Theorem 5.9]
). By the choice of
N
we have
\operatorname{ord}(N^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{ord}(N)=\operatorname{ord}(N^{o})
, where the last equality uses
[
Wib21
, Cor. 6.13]
. Thus
\operatorname{ord}(N^{\prime})=\operatorname{ord}(N^{o})
and
\operatorname{ord}(N^{\prime}/N^{o})=\operatorname{ord}(N^{\prime})-\operatorname{ord}(N^{o})=0
. This shows that
G/N^{o}
is almost-simple.

Set
G_{1}=N^{o}
. Then
G_{1}
is connected, normal in
G
and
G_{0}/G_{1}
is almost-simple. As
\operatorname{ord}(G_{1})<\operatorname{ord}(G)
(Lemma
5.6
) it follows from the induction hypothesis there exists a subnormal series
G_{1}\supseteq G_{2}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq G_{n}=1
with
G_{i}
connected and
G_{i}/G_{i+1}
almost-simple for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.
So the subnormal series
G=G_{0}\supseteq G_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq G_{n}=1
has the desired properties. ∎

5.3.
The proof for diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups of
\sigma
-dimension zero

A
\sigma
-algebraic group is
diagonalizable
if it is isomorphic to a
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}
for some
n\geq 1
. These groups and their torsors are well understood. Abbreviating
g^{\alpha}=g^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(g)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(g)^{\alpha_{m}}
for
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\sigma+\ldots+\alpha^{m}\sigma^{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
and
g^{\beta}=g_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\ldots g_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
for
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]^{n}
, every
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}
is of the form

G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}|\ g^{\beta_{1}}=1,\ldots,g^{\beta_{m}}=1\},

for some
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]^{n}
. Every
G
-torsor is of the form

X=\{x\in\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}|\ x^{\beta_{1}}=a_{1},\ldots,x^{\beta_{m}}=a_{m}\}

for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in k^{\times}
. So
X
is described by a system of
m
multiplicative difference equations in
n
difference variables. We need to show that this system has a solution in some
k^{[d]}
using (P3
u
) (b), which only states that
one
multiplicative difference equation in
one
difference variable has a solution in some
k^{[d]}
. In other words, we need to reduce the general case to the case of one-relator subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. Before going into the details of the reduction proof, we need to provide the foundational properties of diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups.

5.3.1.
Diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups

In this section we discuss the basic properties of diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups, as relevant for the proof of our main results.
Diagonalizable algebraic groups are equivalent to finitely generated abelian groups. Diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups are equivalent to abelian groups
M
equipped with an endomorphism
\sigma\colon M\to M
such that
M
is finitely
\sigma
-generated, i.e., there exists a finite subset
B
of
M
such that
B,\sigma(B),\sigma^{2}(B),\ldots
generates
M
as an abelian group. Let
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
denote the univariate polynomial ring in the variable
\sigma
aver the integers. To specify an endomorphism of an abelian group
M
is equivalent to defining a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module structure on
M
. Moreover,
M
is finitely
\sigma
-generated if and only
M
is a finitely generated as
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module. As a matter of convenience, we mostly use the language of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-modules, rather than classical difference algebra jargon.

For
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\sigma+\ldots+\alpha^{m}\sigma^{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
and
g\in R^{\times}
, where
R
is a
k
-
\sigma
-algebra, we set

g^{\alpha}=g^{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(g)^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{m}(g)^{\alpha_{m}}.

Then
(gh)^{\alpha}=g^{\alpha}h^{\alpha}
for
g,h\in R^{\times}
and
(g^{\alpha})^{\beta}=g^{\alpha\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
. Thus
R^{\times}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module under
(\alpha,g)\mapsto g^{\alpha}
. For a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module
M
we denote with
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})
the abelian group of morphisms of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-modules from
M
to
R^{\times}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module. The functor
D_{\sigma}(M)
from the category of
k
-
\sigma
-
algebras to the category of groups, given by
D_{\sigma}(M)(R)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})
, is a
\sigma
-algebraic group over
k
. It is represented by
k\{D(M)\}=k[M]
, the group algebra of
M
over
k
, equipped with the natural extension of
\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
k[M]
denote the group algebra of the abelian group
M
over
k
. As
M
is abelian,
k[M]
is a commutative
k
-algebra. We define
\sigma\colon k[M]\to k[M]
by
\sigma(\sum_{i}{\lambda_{i}m_{i}})=\sum_{i}\sigma(\lambda_{i})\sigma(m_{i})
for
\lambda_{i}\in k
and
m_{i}\in M
. This makes
k[M]
a
k
-
\sigma
-algebra. If
B\subseteq M
generates
M
as a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module, then
B\subseteq M\subseteq k[M]
generated
k[M]
as a
k
-
\sigma
-algebra. Thus
k[M]
is finitely
\sigma
-generated over
k
. Moreover,

\operatorname{Hom}_{k,\sigma}(k[M],R)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})

functorially in
R
. ∎

Definition 5.9
.

A
\sigma
-algebraic group is
diagonalizable
if it is isomorphic to
D_{\sigma}(M)
for some finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module
M
.

Example 5.10
.

Specifying a morphism
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]^{m}\to R^{\times}
of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-modules, is equivalent to choosing
n
elements from
R^{\times}
(the images of the
n
basis vectors). It follows that
D_{\sigma}(\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]^{n})=[\sigma]_{k}\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}
.

Example 5.11
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
and let

be the
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
given by
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{\alpha}=1\}
. To specify an element of
\mathbb{G}_{m}(R)=R^{\times}
is equivalent to specifying a morphism
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]\to R^{\times}
of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]

Let

Lemma 5.12

Let

be a finitely generated

-module. If

Proof.

Because

The following proposition collects all the properties of diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups that we will need.

Proposition 5.13
.

(i)

The (contravariant) functor
M\rightsquigarrow D_{\sigma}(M)
from the category of finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-
modules to the category of diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups is an equivalence of categories.

(ii)

A sequence
0\to M^{\prime}\to M\to M^{\prime\prime}\to 0
of finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-modules is exact if and only if the sequence
1\to D_{\sigma}(M^{\prime\prime})\to D_{\sigma}(M)\to D_{\sigma}(M^{\prime})\to 1
of
\sigma
-algebraic groups is exact.

(iii)

A
\sigma
-algebraic group is diagonalizable if and only if it is isomorphic to a
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}
for some
n\geq 1
.

(iv)

Quotients and
\sigma
-closed subgroups of diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic groups are diagonalizable.

(v)

For a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module

we have
\sigma\text{-}\dim(D_{\sigma}(M))=\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M)
and
\operatorname{ord}(D_{\sigma}(M))=\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M)
.

(vi)

The
\sigma
-algebraic group

is connected if and only if

has no
\mathbb{Z}
-torsion.

Proof.

For a
\sigma
-algebraic group

let

denote the abelian group of all morphisms

of
\sigma
-
algebraic groups. Then

is a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module with action of
\sigma
obtained by composition with
\sigma\colon\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m},\ g\mapsto\sigma(g)
. We will show that

defines a quasi-inverse to
D_{\sigma}
.

Recall that an element

in a
k
-Hopf algebra is group-like if
\Delta(a)=a\otimes a
. For example, the variable
y\in k\{y,y^{-1}\}=k\{\mathbb{G}_{m}\}
is group-like. A morphism
G\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
of
\sigma
-algebraic groups is determined by the image of

under the dual map
k\{y,y^{-1}\}=k\{\mathbb{G}_{m}\}\to k\{G\}
and this is a group-like element of

. Conversely, a group-like element

defines a morphism
\chi\colon G\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
of
\sigma
-algebraic groups via
\chi(g)=g(a)
for

for any

-
\sigma
-algebra

. The group-like elements of

are stable under
\sigma
and this action of
\sigma
corresponds to the action of
\sigma
on

(as a
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module) with the set of all group-like elements of

.

If

is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-module, the elements of

are group-like elements of

. Moreover, since group-like elements are

-linearly independent (
[

), it follows that

agrees with the set of group-like elements of

.
Thus

and it follows that

is a quasi-inverse to

. This implies (i).

Let

be a sequence of finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
-modules. First assume it is exact.
The dual map
k[M^{\prime\prime}]\to k[M]
to

is just the

-linear extension of
M^{\prime\prime}\to M
. So, since

is surjective, also

is a
\sigma
-closed embedding. Similarly, since

is injective, also

is a quotient map. For a
k
-
\sigma
-algebra

we have

\displaystyle\ker(D_{\sigma}(M)\to D_{\sigma}(M^{\prime}))(R)

\displaystyle=\ker\big(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M^{\prime},R^{\times})\big)

\displaystyle=\{g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})|\ g|_{M^{\prime}}=1\}\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M^{\prime\prime},R^{\times})=D_{\sigma}(M^{\prime\prime})

Thus the sequence of
\sigma
-algebraic groups is exact.

Conversely, if

is injective and

is injective and

is a
\sigma
-closed embedding and

means that for every

-
\sigma
-algebra

, the map

(4)

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M^{\prime\prime},R^{\times})\to\{g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]}(M,R^{\times})|\ g|_{M^{\prime}}=1\}

is bijective. Let

be in the kernel of

.
Then, using (

gets mapped to

under every

\sigma

, it follows that

lies in

. This shows that

If

-closed subgroup of a diagonalizable

-algebraic group

of

is the

If

is a diagonalizable
\sigma
-algebraic group, we can find an

and a surjective morphism

of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]

A quotient of a diagonalizable

Let

We know that

∎

5.3.2.
Reduction to one-relator subgroups of
\mathbb{G}_{m}

We will need the following result about ideals in

.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
I
be an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
such that
I\cap\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}
. Then there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
of positive degree such that
I\subseteq(\alpha)
.

Proof.

We may assume that
I
is not the zero ideal.
The prime ideals of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
are

•

the zero ideal,

•

ideals of the form
(p)
with

a prime number,

•

ideals of the form
(\alpha)
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
is irreducible and of positive degree,

•

ideals of the form
(p,\alpha)
, where
p
is a prime number and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
is a monic polynomial, irreducible mod
p
(
[
Mum99
, Chapter II, §1, Example H]
).

Suppose that all prime ideals minimal above

are of the form
(p)
or
(p,\alpha)
. Then their intersection, which agrees with the radical of
I
, contains a non-zero integer (the product of all the relevant
p
’s). Thus also
I
contains a non-zero integer; a contradiction.

Therefore, at least one of the prime ideals minimal above
I
is of the form
(\alpha)
. In particular,
I\subseteq(\alpha)
.
∎

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
have positive degree. Then
G=\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{\alpha}=1\}
is almost-simple if and only if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
is irreducible.

Proof.

Assume that
G
is almost-simple and that
\alpha
factors as
\alpha=\beta\gamma
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\beta
has positive degree. As
\{g\in\mathbb{G}_{m}|\ g^{\beta}=1\}
is a
\sigma
-closed subgroup of

of order
\deg(\beta)>0
, it follows that the morphism
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]/(\alpha)\to\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]/(\beta)
is an isomorphism, i.e.,
(\alpha)=(\beta)
. This is only possible if
\alpha=\pm\beta
. Thus
\alpha
is irreducible.

Assume that
\alpha
is irreducible and let
N
be a proper
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
. By Proposition
5.13
the inclusion
N\leq G
corresponds to a surjection
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]/(\alpha)\to\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]/I
for some ideal
I
of
\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
with
(\alpha)\subsetneqq I
. Suppose
I\cap\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}
. Then, by Lemma
5.16
, there exists a polynomial
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]
with
I\subseteq(\beta)
. So
(\alpha)\subsetneqq(\beta)
. This contradicts the irreducibility of
\alpha
. Thus
I\cap\mathbb{Z}\neq\{0\}
and therefore
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}[\sigma]/I)=0
. Hence
\operatorname{ord}(N)=0
as desired. ∎

\sigma
-closed subgroup of

. We call a

also exists but it may depend on

. We fix an

and abbreviate
B=\sigma^{(l-1)d}(A)\ldots\sigma^{d}(A)A
. Note that

because
\mathcal{G}\leq\operatorname{GL}_{m}
is defined over
k^{\sigma}
.
Let

be as in the statement of Lemma
5.34
but applied to the difference field
(k,\sigma^{dl})
.
We claim that there exists a
\hat{Y}\in\mathcal{G}(k)
with
\sigma^{sdl}(\hat{Y})=\sigma^{(s-1)dl}(B)\ldots\sigma^{dl}(B)B\hat{Y}
. Note that this implies the claim of the proposition by Lemma
4.1
because
\sigma^{(s-1)dl}(B)\ldots\sigma^{dl}(B)B=\sigma^{(ls-1)d}(A)\ldots\sigma^{d}(A)A
and

is uniform in

if (P3
u
) holds.

Define
{\tilde{\sigma}}=\sigma^{dl}
and let
T=(T_{ij})
be the matrix of coordinate functions on
\operatorname{GL}_{m}
.
Consider the coordinate ring
k[\operatorname{GL}_{m}]=k[T,1/\det(T)]
of
\operatorname{GL}_{m}
over
k
as a
k
-
{\tilde{\sigma}}
-algebra via
{\tilde{\sigma}}(T)=BT
.
Let
\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{G})\subseteq k[\operatorname{GL}_{m}]
be the defining ideal of
\mathcal{G}
and let
f=f(T)\in\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{G})
have constant coefficients. Then
\tilde{\sigma}(f(T))(g)=f(\tilde{\sigma}(T))(g)=f(BT)(g)=f(Bg)=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}(k)
because
Bg\in\mathcal{G}(k)
. So
\tilde{\sigma}(f)\in\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{G})
. Because
\mathcal{G}
is defined over
k^{\sigma}
this shows that
\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{G})\subseteq k[\operatorname{GL}_{m}]
is a
{\tilde{\sigma}}
-ideal.

Thus we can find a maximal
{\tilde{\sigma}}
-ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq k[\operatorname{GL}_{m}]
that contains
\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{G})
. The quotient
R=k[\operatorname{GL}_{m}]/\mathfrak{m}
is then a
\tilde{\sigma}
-simple
k
-
{\tilde{\sigma}}
-algebra. By construction, the image
\tilde{Y}
in
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(R)
of
T
is contained in
\mathcal{G}(R)
and satisfies
\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{Y})=B\tilde{Y}
. Recall, from the first paragraph, that we have a
Y\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
with
\tilde{\sigma}(Y)=BY
. Since two solutions matrices only differ by a constant matrix, there exists a
C\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(R^{\tilde{\sigma}})
with
\tilde{Y}=YC
and we conclude that
R=k[\tilde{Y},1/\det(\tilde{Y})]=k[C,1/\det(C)]
is generated as a
k
-algebra by the elements in
R^{\tilde{\sigma}}
. As
R
is
\tilde{\sigma}
-simple,
R^{\tilde{\sigma}}
is a field. Moreover, it is an algebraic extension of
k^{\tilde{\sigma}}
. (This is well-known in case
k^{\tilde{\sigma}}
is algebraically closed
[
vdPS97
, Lemma 1.8]
and follows similarly in the general case. It also follows from
[
Wib12
, Prop. 2.11]
). Note, however, that
R^{\tilde{\sigma}}
need not be contained in
k
because
R
need not be an integral domain. In any case,
R
is generated as a
k
-algebra by elements that are integral over
k
and therefore
R
itself is integral over
k
. By Lemma
5.33
we have

R=k^{r}
with
\tilde{\sigma}\colon k^{r}\to k^{r},(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\mapsto(\tilde{\sigma}(a_{r}),\tilde{\sigma}(a_{1}),\dots,\tilde{\sigma}(a_{r-1}))
for some
r\geq 1
. Then
R^{\tilde{\sigma}}=\{(a,\tilde{\sigma}(a),\dots,\tilde{\sigma}^{r-1}(a))\mid a\in k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}\}
. So there exists a matrix
D\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}})
with
C=(D,\tilde{\sigma}(D),\dots,\tilde{\sigma}^{r-1}(D))
. Thus
\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{G}(R)
can be rewritten as
\tilde{Y}=YC=(YD,Y\tilde{\sigma}(D),\dots,Y\tilde{\sigma}^{r-1}(D))
. Because
\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{G}(R)
, we conclude that
Y\tilde{\sigma}^{i}(D)\in\mathcal{G}(k)
for

. This implies
D^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{i}(D)=(YD)^{-1}Y\tilde{\sigma}^{i}(D)\in\mathcal{G}(k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}})
.

The Galois group
\Gamma_{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}
of
k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}
over
k^{\tilde{\sigma}}
is generated by
\tilde{\sigma}\colon k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}\to k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}
. (However, the order of
\tilde{\sigma}\in\Gamma_{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}
could be less than

). In any case, we can define a map
\chi\colon\Gamma_{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}\to\mathcal{G}(k^{\tilde{\sigma}^{r}})
by
\chi(\tilde{\sigma}^{i})=D^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{i}(D)
for

. It is straight forward to check that
\chi
is a cocycle. Note that
\chi
may not be principal because we don’t know if

lies in
\mathcal{G}(k^{\tilde{\sigma}^{r}})
. By the choice of
s
, the induced cocycle
\overline{\chi}\colon\Gamma_{\overline{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}\to\Gamma_{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{r}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}\xrightarrow{\chi}\mathcal{G}(k^{\tilde{\sigma}^{r}})\to\mathcal{G}(\overline{k^{\tilde{\sigma}}})
becomes principal, when restricted to
\Gamma_{\overline{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}^{s}}}\leq\Gamma_{\overline{k^{{\tilde{\sigma}}}}/k^{\tilde{\sigma}}}
. In particular, there exists a matrix
E\in\mathcal{G}(\overline{k^{\tilde{\sigma}}})
such that
E^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{s}(E)=D^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}^{s}(D)
. But then
\tilde{\sigma}^{s}(DE^{-1})=DE^{-1}
and so

.
Set
\hat{Y}=YDE^{-1}\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(k)
. Because

and

we have
\hat{Y}\in\mathcal{G}(k)
. Since
\tilde{\sigma}(Y)=BY
, we have
\tilde{\sigma}^{s}(Y)=\tilde{\sigma}^{(s-1)}(B)\ldots\tilde{\sigma}(B)BY
and because

,

must satisfy the same linear

-equation. So

and
\tilde{\sigma}^{s}(\hat{Y})=\tilde{\sigma}^{(s-1)}(B)\ldots\tilde{\sigma}(B)B\hat{Y}
as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.36
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\leq\operatorname{GL}_{m}
be a closed subgroup defined over
k^{\sigma}
and consider the
\sigma
-algebraic group
G=\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma(g)=g\}
. If
k
satisfies
(P3)
(or
(P3
u
)
) then
k
is (uniformly) strongly
G
-trivial.

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
the
\sigma^{n}
-algebraic group
G_{n}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma^{n}(g)=g\}
is of the same form as
G
. So, using Lemma
4.4
, it suffices to show that
k
is
G
-trivial.

According to
[
BW22
, Cor. 6.3]
(with
d=1
and
\psi
the identity map) every
G
-torsor is isomorphic to a
G
-torsor of the form

X^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}A^{\prime}\}

for some
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(k)
, where

is acting by
(g,x)\mapsto gx
. Let
X=\{x\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma(x)=Ax\}
where

and

is acting on

by
(g,x)\mapsto xg^{-1}
. Then

is an isomorphism of

-torsors. The stronger versions of assumption (P3) (b) and (P3

) (b) in Proposition

(with

. If

), then

-torsors.
∎

Recall that a non-commutative algebraic group of positive dimension (over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero) is called
(almost-)simple
if every proper normal closed subgroup is trivial (respectively finite). The simple algebraic groups are sometimes also referred to as the almost-simple algebraic groups of adjoint type.

Proposition 5.37
.

Let
G
be a proper Zariski-dense
\sigma
-reduced
\sigma
-closed subgroup of a simple algebraic group
\mathcal{G}
. If
k
satisfies
(P3)
(or
(P3
u
)
) then
k
is (uniformly) strongly
G
-trivial.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to show that for some
d\geq 1
the difference algebraic group
G_{d}
is isomorphic to one of the difference algebraic groups treated in Lemma
5.36
.

By
[
DVHW17
, Prop. A.19]
(Cf. the proof of
[
CHP02
, Prop. 7.10)]
.) we know that

G=\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma^{n}(g)=\varphi(g)\}

for some
n\geq 1
and some isomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathcal{G}\to{{}^{\sigma^{n}}\!\mathcal{G}}
of algebraic groups over
k
.
Let us fix an embedding of
\mathcal{G}
into
\operatorname{GL}_{m}
such that
\mathcal{G}
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
inside
\operatorname{GL}_{m}
. (This is possible because the simple algebraic groups are defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.)
As in the proof of
[
DVHW17
, Theorem A.20]
it follows that there exists a matrix
h\in\mathcal{G}(k)
and an integer
e\geq 1
such that

\sigma^{ne}(g)=hgh^{-1}
for all
g\in G
.

Consider the equation
\sigma^{ne}(Y)=hY
. The stronger versions of (P3)(b) and (P3
u
)(b) obtained in Proposition
5.35
(applied with
d=ne
) together with Lemma
4.1
both imply that there exists an integer
l\geq 1
such that the equation
\sigma^{nel}(Y)=\sigma^{(l-1)ne}(h)\ldots\sigma^{ne}(h)hY
has a solution
u\in\mathcal{G}(k)
. From
\sigma^{ne}(g)=hgh^{-1}
it follows that
\sigma^{nel}(g)=\sigma^{(l-1)ne}(h)\ldots hg(\sigma^{(l-1)ne}(h)\ldots h)^{-1}
. Therefore

\sigma^{nel}(u^{-1}gu)=u^{-1}gu
for all
g\in G
. Now

g\mapsto u^{-1}gu
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{G}
that maps
G
isomorphically to a
\sigma
-closed subgroup
G^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}
. Indeed,

G^{\prime}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\ \sigma^{n}(g)=v\varphi(g)v^{-1}\},

where
v=\sigma^{n}(u^{-1})\varphi(u)\in\mathcal{G}(k)
.

Let
T=(T_{ij})
denote the
m\times m
-matrix of coordinate functions on
\mathcal{G}
. Then the
\sigma
-coordinate ring
k\{G^{\prime}\}
of

equals

k\{G^{\prime}\}=k[\mathcal{G}^{n}]=k[T,1/\det(T),\sigma(T),1/\det(\sigma(T)),\ldots,\sigma^{n-1}(T),1/\det(\sigma^{n-1}(T))]

with
\sigma
determined by
\sigma(\sigma^{n-1}(T))=v\varphi(T)v^{-1}
.
As
\sigma^{nel}(g)=g
for all

we have
\sigma^{nel}(T)=T
. Thus
\sigma^{nel}(\sigma^{i}(T))=\sigma^{i}(T)
for

. This shows that for

the
\sigma^{d}
-algebraic group

is isomorphic to
the
\sigma^{d}
-algebraic group
H=\{h\in\mathcal{G}^{n}|\ \sigma^{d}(h)=h\}
.
Therefore, also
G_{d}
is isomorphic to

. As
(k,\sigma^{d})
satisfies (P3) or (P3
u
), respectively, by Lemma
4.4
, we know from Lemma
5.36
that

is (uniformly) strongly
(k,\sigma^{d})
-trivial. So
G_{d}
is (uniformly) strongly
(k,\sigma^{d})
-trivial, and therefore
G
is (uniformly) strongly
k
-trivial (Lemma
3.17
). ∎

5.6.
The proof for
\sigma
-algebraic groups of
\sigma
-dimension zero

In this subsection we show that if
k
satisfies (P3) (or (P3

)) then

is (uniformly) strongly
G
-
trivial for every
\sigma
-algebraic group

with
\sigma\text{-}\dim(G)=0
. The idea for the proof is to reduce to the three special cases treated in the three previous subsections (diagonalizable

-algebraic groups,
\sigma
-algebraic vector groups and Zariski-dense
\sigma
-reduced
\sigma
-closed subgroups of simple algebraic groups).

Lemma 5.38
.

Assume that
k
is algebraically closed. Let
G
be a connected
\sigma
-algebraic group of positive order. Then there exists a proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup
N
of
G
such that
G/N
is isomorphic to a Zariski-dense
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
,
\mathbb{G}_{m}
or a simple algebraic group.

Proof.

First of all, note that if
\mathcal{G}
is a connected algebraic group of positive dimension, then there exists a proper normal closed subgroup
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}
,
\mathbb{G}_{m}
or a simple algebraic group. To see this, simply choose a proper normal closed subgroup
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of maximal dimension. If
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is abelian, then it follows from the structure theory of abelian algebraic groups
[
Mil17
, Cor. 16.15]

that
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}
or
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. If
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is non-commutative, then
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is almost-simple and therefore
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}/Z(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
is simple.

Now let
\mathcal{G}
be an algebraic group such that
G
embeds into
\mathcal{G}
as a Zariski-dense
\sigma
-closed subgroup. Then
\mathcal{G}
is connected and has positive dimension. As argued above, there exists a proper normal closed subgroup
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}
is isomorphic to a simple algebraic group,
\mathbb{G}_{a}
or
\mathbb{G}_{m}
.

Now
N=\mathcal{N}\cap G
is a normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
G
. Suppose
\mathcal{N}\cap G=G
, then
G\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. So
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{G}
, because
\mathcal{G}
is Zariski-dense in
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore
N
is properly contained in
G
and we have an embedding
G/N\to\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}
. As
G
is Zariski-dense in
\mathcal{G}
, we see that
G/N
is Zariski-dense in
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Proposition 5.39
.

Let
G
be a
\sigma
-algebraic group of
\sigma
-dimension zero. If

satisfies
(P3)
(or
(P3

)
) then

is (uniformly) strongly

-trivial.

Proof.

We will prove this by induction on

.
The case when

. So we can assume

.
By Corollary

we may assume that

is connected.
According to Lemma

there exists a proper normal
\sigma
-closed subgroup

of

such that

is isomorphic to a Zariski-dense

-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
,
\mathbb{G}_{m}
or a simple algebraic group.
As

, we can use the induction hypothesis together with Proposition
3.20
(ii) to reduce to the case that

is a Zariski-dense
\sigma
-closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
,
\mathbb{G}_{m}
or a simple algebraic group. The case when

is a
\sigma
-closed subgroup of

and the case when

is a
\sigma
-closed subgroup of

is a Zariski-dense
\sigma

is a proper

to conclude. Thus we can assume that

\sigma
-reduced
\sigma

In this subsection we complete the proof of (P3)

.

Lemma 5.41
.

If

is a connected

As

Theorem 5.42
.

Let

be a
\sigma
-algebraic group. If

(or

)
) then

By Lemma

6.
Torsors over
\sigma

-fields

In this short section, we show that
\sigma
-closed
\sigma
-fields satisfy condition (P1

).

Recall that a
\sigma
-field

is
\sigma
-closed
if every system of algebraic difference equations over
k
that has a solution in a
\sigma
-field extension of
k
, already has a solution in
k
. Note that, due to the basis theorem (
[
Lev08
, Theorem 2.5.11]
), the system of algebraic difference equations in this definition can be assumed to be finite. The model theory of
\sigma
-closed
\sigma
-field has been studied extensively (see e.g.,
[

]
). In the model theoretic context,
\sigma
-closed
\sigma
-fields are usually referred to as models of ACFA. The following simple lemma translates the definition of
\sigma
-closed
\sigma
-fields into the language of
k
-
\sigma
-algebras.

Lemma 6.1
.

A
\sigma
-field
k
is
\sigma
-closed if and only if for every finitely
\sigma
-generated
k
-
\sigma
-algebra
R
such that there exists a
\sigma
-ideal of
R
that is prime, there exists a morphism
R\to k
of
k
-
\sigma
-algebras.

Proof.

First assume that
k
is
\sigma
-closed. Let
R
be a finitely
\sigma
-generated
k
-
\sigma
-algebra
R
such that there exists a prime
\sigma
-ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
R
. Then
\mathfrak{p}^{*}=\{r\in R|\ \exists\ n\geq 1:\ \sigma^{n}(r)\in\mathfrak{p}\}
is a prime ideal of
R
with
\sigma^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{p}
. Therefore

\sigma

Writing

-closed, this shows that there exists a solution of

of

-
\sigma
-algebras.

Conversely, let

define a system of algebraic difference equations over

-field extension

of

\sigma

\sigma

-
\sigma

∎

As not every finitely

If

Let

-closed
\sigma

7.
An application to Picard-Vessiot theory

Difference algebraic groups arise naturally as Galois groups
of linear differential equations depending on a discrete parameter. The solution rings in this context are called
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings. They play a role similar to the splitting field in classical Galois theory. Torsors for difference algebraic groups arise when studying isomorphisms between two
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings for the same linear differential equation. In this section we apply our main results (Theorems
1.3
and
1.2
to improve the known uniqueness results for
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings.

We briefly recall the setup from
[
DVHW14
]
. A
\delta\sigma
-ring
is a commutative ring
R
together with a derivation
\delta\colon R\to R
and a ring endomorphism
\sigma\colon R\to R
such that
\delta(\sigma(r))=\hslash\sigma(\delta(r))
for all
r\in R
for some fixed unit
\hslash\in R^{\delta}
. If moreover
R
is a field, it is called a
\delta\sigma
-field. If
K
is a
\delta\sigma
-field, then a
K
-algebra
R
with the structure of a
\delta\sigma
-ring such that
K\to R
is compatible with
\delta
and
\sigma
is called a
K
-
\delta\sigma
-algebra
. A
\delta
-ideal
is an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
such that
\delta(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
. If
\{0\}
and
R
are the only
\delta
-ideals of
R
, the
R
is called
\delta
-simple
.

The
\delta
-constants
of a
\delta\sigma
-ring
R
are defined as
R^{\delta}=\{r\in R\mid\delta(r)=0\}
. Note that
R^{\delta}
is a
\sigma
-ring, and in particular, if
K
is a
\delta\sigma
-field, then
K^{\delta}
is a
\sigma
-field.

In what follows we work over a
\delta\sigma
-field
K
of characteristic zero and let
k=K^{\delta}
be the
\sigma
-field of
\delta
-constants. We consider a linear differential equation
\delta(y)=Ay
for some matrix
A\in K^{m\times m}
.

Definition 7.1
.

A
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot ring

for
\delta(y)=Ay
is a
K
-
\delta\sigma
-algebra
R
such that

•

there exists a matrix
Y\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(R)
with
\delta(Y)=AY
and
R=K\{Y,1/\det(Y)\}
,

•

R
is
\delta
-simple,

•

R^{\delta}=k
.

The
\sigma
-Galois group
G
of
R/K
is defined as the functor from the category of
k
-
\sigma
-algebras to the category of groups given by

G(S)=\operatorname{Aut}^{\delta\sigma}(R\otimes_{k}S/K\otimes_{k}S)

for any
k
-
\sigma
-algebra
S
, i.e., we consider automorphisms of
R\otimes_{k}S
that are the identity on
K\otimes_{k}S
and commute with both
\sigma
and
\delta
, where
R\otimes_{k}S
is considered as a
\delta\sigma
-ring with
\delta
being the trivial derivation on
S
, i.e.,
\delta(s)=0
for
s\in S
.
It is a
\sigma
-algebraic group over
k
(
[
DVHW14
, Prop. 2.5]
).

In general, a
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot ring for a given differential equation
\delta(y)=Ay
is not unique (up to isomorphism). Under the assumption that the
\sigma
-field
k=K^{\delta}
is
\sigma
-closed, it is known that a
\sigma
-Picard Vessiot ring is unique up to powers of
\sigma
, i.e., if
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are
\sigma
-Picard Vessiot rings for the same differential equation
\delta(y)=Ay
, then there exists an integer
n\geq 1
(possibly depending on
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
) such that
(R_{1},\delta,\sigma^{n})
and
(R_{2},\delta,\sigma^{n})
are isomorphic as
k
-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras (
[
DVHW14
, Cor. 1.17]
). We improve this as follows:

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
K
be a
\delta\sigma
-field of characteristic zero and let
A\in K^{m\times m}
.

(i)

If
k=K^{\delta}
satifies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.2
, then for any two
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings
R
and
R^{\prime}
for
\delta(y)=Ay
, there exists an
n\geq 1
such that
R
and
R^{\prime}
are isomorphic as
K
-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras.

(ii)

If
k=K^{\delta}
satifies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
1.3
(e.g.,
k
is
\sigma
-closed), then there exists an
n\geq 1
such that any two
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings for
\delta(y)=Ay
are isomorphic as
K
-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras.

Proof.

Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be
\sigma
-Picard-Vessiot rings for
\delta(y)=Ay
and let
G
be the
\sigma
-Galois group of
R/K
. Consider the functor

from the category of
k
-
\sigma
-algebras to the category of sets given by

for every
k
-
\sigma
-algebra

. So

is the set of

-
\delta\sigma
-
isomorphisms from

to

, where

is considered as a constant

In the first step of the proof of
[

, Theorem 6.4]
is is shown that

is a

-torsor.
Thus, if (P1) holds, then there exists an

such that

(8)

R\otimes_{k}k^{[n]}\to R^{\prime}\otimes_{k}k^{[n]}

of

-
\delta\sigma
-algebras. The projection

onto the last factor is a morphism of
k
-
\sigma^{n}
-

of

-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras. This proves (i).

If

satisfies (P1

), then we can find an

that is uniform in

. The dependence of

and

be two

-Picard-Vessiot rings for

. Then

is isomorphic to

-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebra and

is isomorphic to

as a

-algebra. Thus

and

are isomorphic as
K
-
\delta\sigma^{n}
-algebras.
∎
