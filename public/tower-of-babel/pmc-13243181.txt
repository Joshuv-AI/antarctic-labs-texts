From Identification to Intervention: Bridging the Gap Highlighted by National Frailty and Surgery Outcomes Research
Authors: Xiao Wen, Kong Qingfei
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

My colleagues and I read with great interest the retrospective cohort study by Gonzalez et al., which powerfully demonstrates the profound impact of frailty on surgical outcomes across all specialties, particularly in emergent settings [1]. We commend the authors for this large‐scale analysis, which provides compelling real‐world evidence for integrating frailty assessment into perioperative care.

The study's application of the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) effectively highlights its utility as a pragmatic screening tool. The striking finding that over 90% of the highest‐risk patients underwent emergent surgery [1] starkly illustrates the clinical context—often one of acute decompensation with limited time for optimization. While the HFRS is validated for risk prediction, its reliance on administrative codes may not fully capture actionable phenotypic domains like sarcopenia or functional decline. This underscores a key implication: the need to bridge risk identification with detailed clinical assessment to guide care.

However, as a retrospective analysis, it inherently cannot answer how to use this identification to improve outcomes. This gap points to several interconnected challenges:

The Emergency Conundrum: The high rate of emergent surgery in frail patients suggests a cycle where frailty contributes to crises requiring high‐risk interventions with little preoperative recourse.

From Screening to Intervention: The HFRS signals “high risk” but does not delineate specific deficits (e.g., malnutrition, polypharmacy) for targeted intervention.

Fragmented Care: Frailty is patient‐centric, yet care remains siloed. Surgical teams may identify frailty but lack resources for comprehensive geriatric management, while primary care is often not engaged in preoperative planning.

We propose a paradigm shift toward proactive, integrated models:

Solution 1: Implement Proactive Frailty Screening and Prehabilitation. For elective surgery, the preoperative period is a critical window. Systematic frailty screening in primary care should trigger referral to multimodal prehabilitation programs (targeting exercise, nutrition, medication optimization) for frail patients on waiting lists, aiming to improve physiological reserve [2].

Solution 2: Embed Advance Care Planning (ACP) into Routine Care. For frail older adults, emergency surgical decisions are not hypothetical. Normalizing ACP conversations in primary care—focusing on goals and values for potential surgical emergencies—is crucial to ensure crisis care aligns with patient preferences [3].

Solution 3: Develop Perioperative Geriatric Co‐Management Pathways. Hospitals should establish protocols where a positive frailty screen triggers automatic geriatric consultation. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment can identify modifiable risks (e.g., delirium prevention, polypharmacy), a model with proven benefits [4].

Solution 4: Foster Seamless Data Sharing. Interoperable health records that share frailty status across care settings are essential for continuity, allowing primary care teams to reinforce recovery plans and manage frailty long‐term [5].

In conclusion, Gonzalez et al.'s research provides the indispensable epidemiological imperative for action [1]. The logical next step is to build upon this foundation by designing, implementing, and rigorously evaluating the integrated care models outlined above. The goal must shift from simply predicting poor outcomes to actively engineering better ones for our growing population of frail older adults facing surgery.
