Multi-omics and machine learning reveal the mechanisms underlying cultivar-driven flavor differentiation in fermented ciba chili
Authors: Tianyang Wang, Yiling Xiong, Changbo Gao, Zhaohui Huang, Zihao Liu, Defu Xu, Huachang Wu, Ju Guan, Liang Zhang, Bo Zu, Xiaoming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCiba chili quality is strongly influenced by raw material characteristics, but the cultivar-driven mechanisms underlying quality differentiation remain unclear. Ciba chili made from Inner Yellow New Generation (ICP), Lantern (LCP), and Devil's (DCP) peppers was comprehensively investigated using physicochemical analysis, GC-IMS, UHPLC-MS/MS untargeted metabolomics, 16S rRNA sequencing, and machine learning. Cultivar specificity profoundly shaped fermentation results. Volatile profiling revealed late-stage acid and ester enrichment in LCP, higher aldehyde retention alongside earlier ester formation in ICP, and an enrichment of alcohols, ketones, furans, and pyrazines in DCP. Furthermore, untargeted metabolomics showed that LCP, ICP, and DCP were enriched in organic oxygen-containing compounds, organic acids and organoheterocyclic compounds, and lipids and benzenoids, respectively. A random forest model utilizing these metabolites showed superior classification performance, and SHAP analysis further identified 20 core discriminatory metabolites contributing to cultivar classification. KEGG analysis traced flavor differentiation to phenylpropanoid metabolism, lipid oxidation, purine cofactor metabolism, and l-glutamine-mediated nitrogen metabolism. Cultivar-dependent bacterial succession was observed: DCP was dominated by Lactiplantibacillus, LCP by Weissella, and ICP showed delayed, less coordinated Weissella dominance. Crucially, correlation analysis revealed bacteria metabolite networks: LCP exhibited strong negative associations, ICP showed a balanced network linking acidification, nitrogen metabolism, and flavor precursors, while DCP displayed pronounced coupling associated with complex aromas and pungency. This study establishes an integrated multi-omics and machine learning framework to elucidate how chili cultivar drives quality differentiation, providing theoretical support for cultivar selection and flavor oriented industrial fermentation.Keywords: Ciba chili, Chili varieties, Flavoromics, Metabolomics, Machine learning, Metabolic pathway

Introduction

Ciba chili is a traditional fermented seasoning from southwestern China, particularly Yunnan and Guizhou (Xiong et al., 2024). Its production involves rehydration of dried chilies, mechanical crushing, and sealed anaerobic spontaneous fermentation for about one month. This process facilitates the liberation of free and bound capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin from plant cell walls. Together with organic acids and ester aroma compounds in the fermentation system, these compounds contributed to a more harmonious and complex pungency with reduced harshness in Ciba chili (Liu et al., 2024). Furthermore, spontaneous fermentation also drives secondary metabolite bioconversion, while enhancing the release of bioactive compounds including flavonoids, polyphenols, and ascorbic acid (Guo et al., 2022a; Jiang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2021). These bioactive components can positively contribute to human health by scavenging free radicals, alleviating oxidative stress, and modulating inflammation (Rana et al., 2022). Although earlier research has clarified the metabolic mechanisms of flavor formation during spontaneous fermentation, matrix cultivar specificity remains a critical determinant shaping the final flavor profile and product quality of Ciba chili (Wang et al., 2025).

Raw material varieties establish the initial chemical profile of fermentation systems and are therefore critical for quality formation in fermented chili products. Chili cultivars differ significantly in available carbon sources, amino acid precursors, capsaicinoids, and volatile aldehydes or alcohols (Liu et al., 2024). These endogenous compounds not only define the baseline aroma, pungency, and taste of raw chilies but also provide carbon and nitrogen sources and flavor precursors for fermenting microorganisms (Ye, Shang, Li, et al., 2022; Ye, Shang, Zhang, et al., 2022). As a result, cultivar specific compositional differences ultimately leading to distinct acidity, pungency, color, and overall flavor quality in Ciba chili (Xu et al., 2021). Previous studies on Ciba chili have mainly focused on the effects of fermentation parameters, such as salt concentration, fermentation duration, and microbial succession, on physicochemical properties and flavor development (Xiong et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). However, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding how chili cultivars influence quality attributes and flavor development in fermented Ciba chili. Therefore, three representative chili cultivars were selectively screened based on three sensory dimensions: color, aroma, and pungency. These sensory attributes are considered key quality determinants of traditional fermented chili products in Southwest China, where chili cultivars with diverse pigment, aroma, and pungency characteristics are widely used as fermentation materials. These included Inner Yellow New Generation chili pepper (Shan et al., 2024), known for its high red pigment content, Lantern chili pepper (Li et al., 2024), characterized by an intense aroma rich in esters and terpenes; and Devil's chili pepper, distinguished by its extremely high pungency (Bosland & Baral, 2007). Together, these cultivars represent typical raw material types used in Southwest China fermented chili production, providing contrasting chemical backgrounds for investigating cultivar-dependent fermentation characteristics. Fermented Ciba chili was separately prepared using these three chili cultivars.

Ciba chili fermentation is a complex biotransformation involving many components, making comprehensive characterization with a single analytical method difficult. Multi-omics integration with machine learning is a powerful approach to deciphering quality formation in traditional fermented foods (Ji et al., 2025). LC-MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics captures dynamic changes in lipids, free amino acids, and organic acids and derivatives during fermentation in fermented chili, as demonstrated by Chen et al. (2025). GC-IMS's two-dimensional separation makes it ideal for semi-quantitative profiling of fermentation -derived characteristic VOCs proven in prior studies to accurately distinguish both fermentation stages and raw material specific flavor profiles (Cai et al., 2024). Beyond flavor and metabolite profiling, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing reveals core bacterial community structure and succession and is widely applied to characterize dominant microbiota composition and temporal dynamics during fermentation (Wang et al., 2024). It is worth noting that the intrinsic high dimensionality and nonlinearity of multi omics data limit the effectiveness of conventional statistical methods for advanced feature screening and pattern recognition (Ye, Shang, Zhang, et al., 2022). In recent years, machine learning algorithms, including random forest, support vector machine, and XGBoost, have been extensively used in metabolomics research (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). By applying a random forest algorithm for in depth data mining, Gu et al. (2023) successfully identified 40 key biomarkers from 873 metabolites (AUC = 0.964), further demonstrating the ability of machine learning to compress redundant variables effectively while retaining essential features.

In this study, a multi-time-point and multi-omics approach was employed to investigate the role of chili cultivar differences in shaping quality and flavor development during Ciba chili fermentation. The study focused on cultivar-dependent changes in fermentation trajectories, microbial succession and metabolite transformation, as well as the potential of metabolomics-based machine learning for cultivar discrimination and biomarker discovery. To achieve these objectives, physicochemical analyses, LC-MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics, GC-IMS, and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing were integrated with machine learning and SHAP analysis. This comprehensive strategy provides a systematic approach for understanding cultivar-dependent quality differentiation during fermentation. Ultimately, this study highlights the importance of raw material selection in fermented chili production and provides a theoretical basis for cultivar selection and quality standardization of Ciba chili.

Materials and methods

Preparation of ciba chili sample

Ciba chili was prepared from three chili cultivars, namely Inner Yellow New Generation chili pepper (ICP), Lantern chili pepper (LCP), and Devil's chili pepper (DCP). All dried chilies were purchased from Chengdu Food Technology Co., Ltd. and screened for intact shape, similar size, uniform color, and no insect infestation or mechanical damage. According to the product specification provided by the supplier, the dried chili raw materials had a moisture content of 12.36 ± 0.25%. Samples were prepared according to Wang et al. (2025). Briefly, dried chilies were rehydrated, drained, and mixed with edible salt, fresh garlic, and fresh ginger at a ratio of 100:8:5:3 (w/w, chili:salt:garlic:ginger). The mixed materials were crushed into coarse particles using a sterile food grinder, and 500 g of each sample was transferred into food-grade fermentation jars. Except for the chili cultivar, all processing variables, including raw and auxiliary material ratios, pretreatment, filling conditions, and fermentation environment, were kept consistent, with each cultivar fermented separately in triplicate. All formulations were identical among cultivars except for the chili variety. Fermentation was terminated at 28 days, with samples collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 d and immediately preserved at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Determination of pH, titratable acidity, and amino acid nitrogen in ciba chili

The pH of Ciba chili was measured using a calibrated PHS-25 pH meter (Shanghai Electronic Science Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Total acidity was determined by NaOH titration and expressed as lactic acid equivalents (Gao et al., 2024). Amino acid nitrogen was measured by formaldehyde titration (Yao et al., 2026). Briefly, samples were titrated to pH 8.30 with standardized NaOH, mixed with 10.00 mL the formaldehyde reagent, and further titrated to pH 9.20. Amino acid nitrogen was calculated from NaOH consumption and expressed as g/kg.

Determination of volatile components

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in Ciba chili were analyzed using an HS-GC-IMS system (FlavourSpec®, G.A.S., Germany) equipped with an MXT-WAX column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 1 μm, Restek, USA), with slight modifications to the reported method. The analysis was performed with slight modifications based on the method described by Yuan et al. (Yuan et al., 2025). Briefly, 0.50 g of pretreated samples was sealed in a 20.00 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 60 °C for 15 min. Then, 0.20 mL of headspace gas was injected in splitless mode using an 85 °C heated syringe. High-purity nitrogen (99.99%) was used as carrier and drift gas, with the drift gas flow set at 150 mL/min. The GC flow program was 2 mL/min for 5 min, 10 mL/min for 10 min, 15 mL/min for 5 min, 50 mL/min for 10 min, and 100 mL/min for 10 min. The retention indices (RIs) of volatile compounds were calculated using C4-C9 n-ketones as external reference compounds. Volatile compounds were identified by matching the calculated RI and drift time with those in the GC-IMS database. Relative Odor Activity Value (ROAV) was calculated as ROAVi = 100 × (Ci/Ti) / (Cmax/Tmax), where Ci represents the normalized peak volume of compound i obtained from GC-IMS analysis, Ti is the corresponding odor threshold, and Cmax/ Tmax is the maximum odor activity ratio among all detected compounds. Compounds with ROAV ≥ 1 were considered key aroma-active compounds.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from Ciba chili samples using an Omega Bio-tek DNA extraction kit (Norcross, GA, USA), and its quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338F/806R. PCR was performed in a 30 μL mixture containing 50 ng DNA, 0.90 μL of each primer, 6 μL dNTPs, 0.60 μL KOD FX Neo polymerase, and nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions were 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were verified by 1.80% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using a Monarch DNA Gel Recovery Kit, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 PE250 platform.

The metabolites of ciba chili were extracted by non-targeted metabolomics analysis based on LC-MS/MS

Metabolite extraction was performed using 0.50 g of Ciba chili mixed with 400 μL of methanol/water solution (4:1, v/v) containing 2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL) as the internal standard (Fang et al., 2023). The mixture was homogenized in a grinder at −10 °C for 6 min, followed by ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C for 30 min. The extracts were then kept at −20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C using an H1850R refrigerated centrifuge (Xiangyi Centrifuge Instrument Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.20 μm PTFE membrane, and 100 μL of the filtrate was used for LC-MS/MS analysis. To monitor analytical performance, a pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combining 20.00 μL aliquots from each extract.

UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X orbitrap MS analysis of non-volatile compounds

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap mass spectrometer using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.10 mm, 1.80 μm). The analysis was performed with slight modifications based on the method described by Li et al. (2023). The mobile phases were 0.10% formic acid in water/acetonitrile (A) and 0.10% formic acid in acetonitrile/isopropanol aqueous solution (B). The column temperature was 40 °C, injection volume was 2.00 μL, and gradient elution was performed at 0.40–0.60 mL/min. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes using DDA over an m/z range of 70–1050, with spray voltages of 3.50 kV and 2.50 kV, respectively. Full MS and MS/MS resolutions were 60,000 and 7500, and the capillary temperature was 325 °C. Metabolites were annotated against the KEGG and METLIN databases, and differential metabolites were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0.

Training and testing of machine learning models

All machine learning analyses were performed using Python 3.14. Data normalization, dataset splitting, cross validation, model training, and performance evaluation were mainly conducted using scikit-learn, while extreme gradient boosting tree (XGBoost) was used for extreme gradient boosting modeling. Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was applied to evaluate feature contributions and identify core biomarkers, and matplotlib and seaborn were used for visualization. Six classifiers, including logistic regression (LR), support vector classifier (SVC), random forest (RF), XGBoost, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and neural network (NNET), were used to discriminate different Ciba chili samples. The main model configurations were specified to ensure reproducibility. RF and XGBoost were constructed using tree-based ensemble architectures, SVC was implemented with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, and NNET was configured with a single hidden layer. Other classifier settings followed the corresponding implementation in scikit-learn. Prior to modeling, metabolomics data were preprocessed and normalized by Z-score transformation. The standardized dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio, with the training set used for model fitting and hyperparameter optimization, while the testing set used for model generalization assessment. In addition to the random train–test split, an independent time-based external validation strategy was further performed to evaluate model generalization and potential overfitting. Samples from the early fermentation stages (0, 7, and 14 d) were used as the training set, whereas samples from later fermentation stages (21 and 28 d) were completely withheld as an external test set. Three-fold cross-validation was further performed within the training set to improve model robustness and reduce partition-related bias. Model discrimination performance was primarily assessed based on the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC). Besides ROC AUC, accuracy, F1 score, Cohen's kappa coefficient, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and the confusion matrix were also computed to provide a comprehensive assessment of the overall classification performance and category identification effectiveness of the different models. In multiclass tasks, these metrics were computed by weighted or macro-averaging according to the class-specific confusion matrices, so that all sample categories were incorporated into the assessment of model performance.

To further interpret the metabolic basis of the model's discriminatory results, SHAP analysis was applied to quantify the contribution of individual metabolic features to model predictions. Based on the Shapley value from game theory, SHAP decomposes a model prediction into the additive contributions of all input features. Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed using three biological replicates (n = 3), corresponding to three independent fermentation jars. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.5). PCA was performed using the FactoMineR and factoextra packages. Differential metabolites were identified using the limma package. The voom function was applied to estimate the mean–variance relationship and generate precision weights, followed by the lmFit function to fit linear models for metabolite expression profiles. The eBayes function was subsequently used for empirical Bayes moderation of variance estimates to improve statistical robustness. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and metabolites with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significant differential metabolites were further filtered according to fold change. Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the psych package with the corr.test function to evaluate the associations between differential metabolites and other variables. Correlation coefficients (r) and corresponding P values were calculated using the Spearman rank correlation method. Correlation networks were visualized using the igraph and ggraph packages.

Results and discussion

Analysis of pH, total acidity and amino acid nitrogen of ciba chili

Amino acid nitrogen, total acid, and pH are key indicators of fermentation progress and quality, reflecting microbial metabolism and microecological changes in fermented foods (Yao et al., 2026). During fermentation, all Ciba chili samples showed continuous increases in total acid (Fig. 1a) and amino acid nitrogen (Fig. 1b; Table S1), together with a marked decrease in pH (Fig. 1c). The most pronounced changes occurred within the first 14 days, indicating that early fermentation was the critical stage for acidification and nitrogenous flavor precursor accumulation (Wang et al., 2025). Although the initial pH differed among cultivars, with ICP (Inner Yellow New Generation chili pepper) showing the highest value, all samples stabilized at pH 3.7–3.8 after 14 days. This plateau may be related to acid induced inhibition of acidogenic bacteria and the buffering effect of amino acids and peptides generated by protein hydrolysis. Clear cultivar dependent differences were observed in total acid and amino acid nitrogen accumulation. ICP consistently showed higher total acid levels than LCP (Lantern chili pepper) and DCP (Devil's chili pepper), reaching 25.77 ± 0.41 g/kg at day 28, possibly due to differences in fermentable carbon source availability among raw materials. This stronger acidification may enhance acidic taste, microbial stability, and ester related aroma formation (Wang et al., 2019). Amino acid nitrogen also increased continuously in all samples, mainly owing to protein hydrolysis by endogenous enzymes and microbial extracellular proteases (Moormann et al., 2022). ICP showed the highest amino acid nitrogen content, suggesting a greater capacity to generate umami related compounds and provide free amino acid precursors for the Ehrlich pathway, thereby promoting higher alcohol and ester formation and increasing aroma complexity (Zhao et al., 2016).

Different uppercase letters indicate significant differences within the same sample at different fermentation stages (p < 0.05), whereas different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among different samples at the same fermentation stage (p < 0.05).

Analysis of volatile compounds in ciba chili

Intuitively visualized in the Headspace Gas Chromatography-Ion Mobility Spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) 2D maps are the dynamic changes of Ciba chili volatiles, characterized by retention time, migration time, and signal intensity (Fig. 2). These results indicated that LCP, ICP, and DCP generated similar numbers of volatile organic chemicals (VOCs), but differed in volatile accumulation patterns and flavor fingerprints, suggesting a marked influence of chili cultivar on aroma development during fermentation. Numerous new volatile signal peaks appeared in all fermented LCP, ICP, and DCP samples, suggesting that fermentation facilitated the conversion of flavor precursor compounds (Fig. 2ab). The appearance of new peaks and increased peak intensities after fermentation suggested that microbial metabolism, together with endogenous enzymes, contributed to the degradation of macromolecular precursors under sealed anaerobic conditions. The conversion of sugars, amino acids, lipids, and secondary metabolites further promoted the accumulation of volatile flavor substances. Notably, the high resolving power of IMS allowed certain compounds to be identified in both monomeric and dimeric forms, thereby enhancing the recognition of volatile constituents in the complex fermented matrix. In total, 57, 57, and 58 VOCs were identified in the three groups, respectively, showing comparable compound numbers among the samples (Fig. 2c). The differences among chili matrices were primarily manifested in the intensities and occurrence timing of characteristic peaks, as well as the overall fingerprint profiles, rather than in the total number of volatile compounds. This suggests that different chili matrices influenced not only the overall VOC levels but also their accumulation dynamics and abundance patterns. Moreover, the unfermented samples were clearly distinguished from the fermented samples across the three groups. Relative to the 0 d samples, fermented samples showed a greater number of stronger signal peaks on both sides of the reaction ion peak (RIP), suggesting marked enrichment of volatile compounds, including alcohols, esters, aldehydes, and ketones. Changes were most evident between 14 and 21 d, indicating that this period was crucial for the rapid accumulation of aroma compounds and reshaping of the flavor profile. During 21–28 d, the map differences narrowed and the fingerprint profiles converged, indicating that the volatile flavor structure had been essentially established and had become relatively stable. Together with the preceding physicochemical results, these findings suggest that acidification, substrate degradation, and intensified microbial metabolism in the early and middle stages of fermentation were the primary drivers of rapid volatile flavor formation.

Compound category analysis showed that fermentation markedly reshaped the volatile profile of Ciba chili, with a sharp decline in pre-existing alkanes and significant accumulation of alcohols, acids, esters, and ketones as major flavor contributors (Fig. 3). Aldehydes and alcohols showed clear cultivar specific dynamics. The study by Smit indicated that aldehydes and alcohols are closely linked through metabolic conversion (Smit et al., 2009). During fermentation, aldehydes serve as active metabolic intermediates, originating primarily from the Ehrlich pathway mediated decarboxylation of free amino acids and the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids (Zhou et al., 2026). In DCP, aldehydes decreased sharply after fermentation began, whereas alcohols remained abundant and increased markedly during 21–28 d, suggesting strong reductive metabolism that promoted aldehyde-to-alcohol conversion. In contrast, ICP retained higher aldehyde levels throughout fermentation and showed slower alcohol accumulation, indicating a higher retention of aldehyde-related volatile compounds, which may contribute to potential fresh aroma characteristics (Chen et al., 2020). The temporal patterns of acids and esters revealed that esterification was strongly dependent on substrate availability. Throughout fermentation, volatile organic acids accumulated progressively in the three sample groups. Notably, DLJ exhibited the most pronounced volatile acid producing capacity, remaining significantly higher than the other groups from day 7 through the end of fermentation. The sustained accumulation of volatile acids supplied abundant precursors for later esterification and condensation reactions in the fermentation system (Jiang et al., 2025). This phenomenon was especially prominent in LCP, whose ester content peaked at day 21 after extensive organic acid accumulation, indicating a sequential pattern of acid accumulation followed by ester generation. As previous studies have suggested, this delayed ester formation essentially reflects the early stage preferential accumulation of acid precursors through carbon metabolism. When fermentation progressed into the middle to late stages, the gradual production of alcohols together with enhanced microbial esterase activity facilitated the extensive synthesis and enrichment of esters (Liao et al., 2023). In contrast, esters in ICP rose rapidly to high levels as early as 7–14 d, suggesting a markedly advanced onset of esterification. This pronounced divergence could be ascribed to cultivar specific differences in the release of endogenous acid and alcohol precursors and in the intrinsic activity of related enzymatic reactions within different chili matrices (Cai et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2020). In contrast, DCP showed relatively low overall ester abundance, presumably because of limited volatile acid precursor availability or restricted esterification activity within its fermentation microenvironment (Wang et al., 2019).

The dynamics of ketones and heterocyclic compounds further accentuated the flavor divergence among the three Ciba chili samples. Ketones are linked to free fatty acid oxidation and microbial lipase activity, which are often associated with fatty, floral, fruity, and milky aroma characteristics in fermented foods (Zhou et al., 2025). From 14 to 28 d, DCP maintained relatively high ketone abundance, implying more active liberation and conversion of lipid derived flavor precursors, which may facilitate the accumulation of fatty and floral fruity aroma components (Chen et al., 2023, Xiong et al., 2024). By comparison, LCP exhibited more evident enrichment of alkenes in the later fermentation stage, suggesting that chili matrices can drive divergent accumulation patterns of volatile flavor compounds by modulating endogenous secondary metabolite release and microbial conversion. It is noteworthy that, despite their low proportion among total volatile compounds, heterocyclic compounds including furans and pyrazines accumulated continuously in each fermentation system, suggesting that fermentation promoted the accumulation of heterocyclic volatile compounds potentially associated with roasted, nutty, and caramel-like aroma characteristics (An et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2025). Since the present study was conducted under room temperature spontaneous fermentation, thermally accelerated Maillard reactions were not expected to dominate. Thus, the formation of these heterocyclic compounds may rely more heavily on the sustained provision of flavor precursors by microbial metabolism (Liu et al., 2022). Existing evidence indicates that microorganisms such as Bacillus and yeasts facilitate the accumulation of precursors, including free amino acids, reducing sugars, and carbonyl compounds, through proteolysis, carbohydrate metabolism, and amino acid conversion (Nofiani et al., 2021). These precursors can then undergo deamination, decarboxylation, Strecker degradation, condensation, and cyclization, thereby promoting the generation of roasted aroma associated compounds such as pyrazines and furans (Guo et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024). During 7–28 d, DCP maintained the highest abundance of heterocyclic compounds, suggesting active precursor transformation that may contribute to roasted and nutty aroma-related characteristics.

Multivariate statistical analysis of key aroma compounds in ciba chili

By calculating Relative Odor Activity Value (ROAV) values, 17, 15, and 18 key aroma-active compounds were identified in LCP, ICP, and DCP, respectively (Table S2-S4). The resulting VOC matrix was subsequently subjected to multivariate analysis to further characterize cultivar-dependent aroma differentiation (Fig. 4). Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) showed clear stage-dependent separation among samples. The first two latent variables (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 81.60%, 86.20%, and 84.70% of the cumulative variation for LCP, ICP, and DCP, respectively, while the remaining variation was represented by subsequent latent variables (Fig. 4a). In all groups, unfermented samples 0 d were located on the positive side of PC1 and clearly separated from fermented samples, indicating substantial remodeling of volatile flavor after fermentation began. LCP showed the most pronounced separation between 0 d and 7 d, followed by gradual convergence from 14 to 28 d, suggesting that its flavor changes were mainly concentrated in early fermentation. ICP exhibited continuous phase shifts from 7 to 14 d to 21–28 d, indicating progressive flavor development, whereas DCP showed more distinct stage wise shifts, suggesting sustained evolution of its volatile profile throughout fermentation. The PLS-DA permutation test confirmed model robustness, with a negative Q2 intercept indicating no obvious overfitting and reliable VOCs based sample discrimination (Fig. 4b).

Multivariate analysis of VOCs in Ciba chili. (a) PLS-DA score plots, (b) Permutation test plots of PLS-DA models, (c) PLS-DA loading plots of volatile compounds. Note: The percentages indicated on the axes represent the variance explained by each principal component.

The PLS-DA biplot loading plot (Fig. 4c) and ROAV heatmap (Fig. 4d) collectively revealed the cultivar and stage-dependent contributions of key volatile compounds to aroma differentiation. In LCP, the 0 d samples were mainly associated with aldehydes such as butanal, whereas the 14–28 d samples were more closely related to isovaleric acid methyl ester, 2,3-pentanedione, and several alcohols. Consistently, the ROAV analysis showed relatively high contributions from acetic acid, 3-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanol, and selected ester related compounds, indicating that the aroma profile of LCP during fermentation was shaped by the combined contribution of acid, aldehyde, alcohol, and ester related volatiles. In ICP, the 0 d samples were associated with propionaldehyde and 3-octanone, while the later fermentation stages were more strongly linked to 3-methylbutanol, 1-hexanol, and 3-hydroxy-2-butanone. The relatively high ROAV contribution of 3-methylbutanal and several aldehyde and alcohol related compounds was consistent with the greater retention of aldehyde related volatiles in ICP. Moreover, the contribution of 3-methylbutanol, a product associated with branched chain amino acid degradation, corresponded with the higher amino acid nitrogen content of ICP, further suggesting a close relationship between amino acid metabolism and its volatile profile (Wu et al., 2024). In DCP, early stage samples were mainly associated with lipid oxidation products such as hexanal and butanal, whereas later samples shifted toward 1-penten-3-one, 3-methylbutanol, α-pinene, and acetic acid. Notably, α-pinene exhibited a particularly high ROAV contribution in DCP, together with appreciable contributions from acetic acid and 3-methylbutanol, indicating that terpene-, alcohol, and acid related compounds made important contributions to its characteristic volatile profile.

Analysis of the UHPLC-MS/MS based untargeted metabolome of ciba chili

Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) based untargeted metabolomics was used to characterize non-volatile metabolites in Ciba chili during fermentation. In the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) score plot, QC samples clustered tightly, indicating good instrumental stability, low analytical variation, and reliable metabolomics data (Fig. 5a) (Wang et al., 2024). Overall, 2143 metabolites were identified across LCP, ICP, and DCP and classified into 15 categories (Fig. 5b). Lipids and lipid-like molecules were the most abundant class (26.70%), followed by organic acids and derivatives (19.30%) and organoheterocyclic compounds (14.60%), consistent with previous findings (Wang et al., 2025).

The Fig. 5 cd showed that cultivar dependent non-volatile metabolic differences were shaped by multiple metabolite classes rather than a single category. LCP was characterized by a higher proportion of organic oxygen compounds, suggesting early stage metabolic changes. ICP contained higher levels of organic acids and derivatives as well as organoheterocyclic compounds, indicating more continuous metabolic succession related to acid taste and complex fermented flavor formation (Guan et al., 2025). In contrast, DCP showed higher proportions of lipids and lipid-like molecules and benzenoids, suggesting richer lipid derived flavor precursors and aromatic secondary metabolites that may support sustained flavor remodeling during middle to late fermentation.

PCA further revealed distinct temporal migration patterns among the three cultivars. In LCP, the 0 d samples were clearly separated from fermented samples, while the 14–28 d samples gradually clustered, suggesting that its metabolic changes were mainly concentrated in the early to middle stages and then stabilized. ICP showed a more continuous chronological migration from 0 to 28 d, indicating sustained metabolic succession throughout fermentation. In contrast, DCP showed clear separation between 0 d and fermented samples, and the 7–28 d samples still covered a broad spatial range, suggesting a longer duration and greater extent of metabolite remodeling. Overall, LCP showed rapid early stage changes, ICP displayed continuous metabolic succession, and DCP underwent broader metabolic alterations. Although the three groups shared broadly similar metabolite class compositions, their relative abundances differed considerably. Lipids and lipid-like molecules were the dominant class, accounting for 27.63%, 28.62%, and 30.24% of metabolites in LCP, ICP, and DCP, respectively.

Lipids and lipid-like molecules constituted the dominant metabolite category in the Ciba chili groups, suggesting a critical role of lipid metabolism in the fermentation process. Glycerides, phospholipids, and unsaturated fatty acids can be hydrolyzed by endogenous enzymes and microbial lipases to release free fatty acids such as oleic, linoleic, and linolenic acids. These compounds further undergo lipoxygenase-mediated oxidation or autoxidation to form lipid hydroperoxides, which are cleaved into volatiles such as hexanal, pentanal, butanal, and 1-penten-3-one, contributing green, fatty, and mildly oxidized notes. In addition, aldehydes can be reduced to alcohols, while fatty acids may participate in acid and ester formation, such as ethyl hexanoate and ethyl butyrate. Therefore, lipids and lipid-like molecules act as important precursors linking non-volatile metabolic remodeling with VOCs development.

Among the three groups, DCP showed the highest proportion of lipids and lipid-like molecules (30.24%), followed by ICP (28.62%) and LCP (27.63%), suggesting that DCP contained a richer pool of lipid derived flavor precursors. This support the sustained formation of lipid degradation related volatiles, including aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, during middle to late fermentation, consistent with its higher flavor diversity and stronger flavor remodeling. In contrast, the lower lipid proportion in LCP suggests that its flavor development may rely less on lipid degradation and more on organic acid accumulation, carbohydrate metabolism, and esterification. ICP showed an intermediate lipid level but the highest proportion of organic acids and derivatives, indicating that its flavor formation may be jointly shaped by lipid metabolism and organic acid driven ester formation, thereby contributing both basic aroma compounds and fruity fermented notes.

Organic acids and derivatives reflect the metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, and lipids, and can serve as important precursors for volatile esters (Liu et al., 2023). Their proportions were 14.36%, 15.86%, and 13.53% in LCP, ICP, and DCP, respectively, with the highest level in ICP indicating a richer pool of acid related substrates. These compounds directly affect sourness, pH, and total acidity, and can react with alcohols to form esters such as ethyl acetate, ethyl lactate, isoamyl acetate, and ethyl hexanoate, contributing fruity and fermented notes (Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025). Therefore, the higher abundance of organic acids and derivatives in ICP provide more precursors for ester formation during middle to late fermentation and help explain its earlier ester accumulation. In contrast, the lower proportion in DCP suggests that its ester formation be relatively limited by acid precursor availability, while its flavor development depends more on lipid degradation.

Organoheterocyclic compounds were highly represented in all three Ciba chili samples, with the highest proportion observed in ICP (15.90%), followed by LCP (14.36%) and DCP (13.53%), suggesting that ICP was richer in non-volatile metabolites related to nitrogen or oxygen containing heterocyclic structures. These compounds are largely related to the conversion of amino acids, nucleotides, and other nitrogenous metabolites, providing a metabolic foundation for the later generation of complex aroma compounds, pyrazines and furans (Diez-Simon et al., 2020). Although non-volatile organoheterocyclic compounds are not directly equivalent to the volatile heterocyclic compounds detected by GC-IMS, their higher abundance in ICP indicates a more substantial heterocyclic metabolic background, potentially supplying precursors for characteristic aroma compounds pyrazines and furans and thus contributing to the higher complexity of its fermented aroma.

LCP showed the highest proportion of organic oxygen compounds (18.84%), exceeding ICP (13.18%) and DCP (14.03%). As sugars, sugar alcohols, carbonyl compounds, and other oxygen containing small molecules can serve as utilizable carbon sources and reactive substrates, their higher abundance in LCP suggests sufficient early stage substrates for rapid microbial conversion (Gänzle, 2015; Peng et al., 2025). This is consistent with the PCA pattern of LCP, which showed clear early separation followed by late stage clustering, indicating that its metabolic shifts mainly occurred during early utilization of sugars and oxygen containing compounds. Benzenoids accounted for 4.35%, 6.88%, and 10.72% in LCP, ICP, and DCP, respectively, with the highest level in DCP. As benzenoids are associated with aromatic metabolism, phenolic compounds, and flavor precursors, their enrichment indicates a stronger aromatic secondary metabolic background in DCP (Filannino et al., 2018). Together with its high proportion of lipids and lipid like molecules, this suggests that DCP metabolism was biased toward lipid derived and aromatic secondary pathways, which may partly explain its broader flavor remodeling during later fermentation.

Limma based differential metabolite analysis of ciba chili

Differential non-volatile metabolites in Ciba chili across fermentation stages were identified using the limma (Fig. 5e). An overall comparison between the initial and final fermentation states showed that all three samples experienced marked metabolic reconstruction over the 0–28 d fermentation period, with cultivar dependent differences in both the direction and extent of these changes. When day 0 was compared with day 28, ICP showed a predominance of downregulated metabolites, including 97 significantly downregulated and 48 significantly upregulated metabolites, suggesting more intensive consumption of initial substrates and metabolic conversion during fermentation. By comparison, DCP exhibited more significantly upregulated metabolites, with 69 upregulated and 55 downregulated, indicating greater accumulation of derivative metabolic products by the end of fermentation. This finding agreed with the results regarding changes in VOCs in DCP. From the comparison of fermentation stages, the changes in non-volatile metabolites among the three samples displayed clear stage specific patterns. In the early and middle phases of fermentation, especially during 7–14 d and 14–21 d, evident downregulation of metabolites was observed in all three samples. Specifically, 94 metabolites were significantly downregulated in DCP between 7 and 14 d, whereas both LCP and ICP showed 86 significantly downregulated metabolites between 14 and 21 d. At this stage, the reduction in the abundance of many metabolites was likely related to the sustained consumption of raw material substrates, including sugars, lipids, amino acids, and phenolic compounds, through microbial growth and enzymatic reactions. With the progression of fermentation, macromolecules or bound forms were progressively degraded and transformed into small molecule precursors, thereby supplying substrates for the later formation of flavor associated metabolites. In the later stage of fermentation, the three samples all exhibited increased numbers of upregulated metabolites. Notably, in the 21 vs. 28 d stage, the number of significantly upregulated metabolites in DCP reached 115, whereas only 6 metabolites were downregulated, suggesting sustained metabolic activity and strong product accumulation capacity in the final fermentation stage. At this stage, 67 metabolites were significantly upregulated in LCP, suggesting pronounced late stage metabolite accumulation.

Pairwise comparisons among cultivars further revealed substantial inter-cultivar differences in non-volatile metabolite profiles (Fig. 5f). A total of 146 differential metabolites were identified between LCP and ICP, including 49 upregulated and 97 downregulated metabolites. The LCP vs DCP comparison contained 115 differential metabolites, with 70 upregulated and 45 downregulated metabolites, whereas 143 differential metabolites were detected between ICP and DCP, including 104 upregulated and 39 downregulated metabolites. The contrasting directions of these changes indicate that the three cultivars differed not only in the magnitude of metabolic variation but also in the accumulation patterns of specific metabolite groups. In particular, the larger number of upregulated metabolites in ICP vs DCP suggests that ICP accumulated a broader set of non-volatile metabolites relative to DCP, whereas the predominance of downregulated metabolites in LCP vs ICP reflects marked metabolic divergence between these two cultivars. Venn analysis further showed that the three pairwise comparisons contained both shared and comparison-specific differential metabolites (Fig. 5g). Specifically, 57, 66, and 47 metabolites were unique to LCP vs ICP, LCP vs DCP, and ICP vs DCP, respectively. In addition, 21 metabolites were shared between the LCP vs ICP and LCP vs DCP comparisons, 68 between LCP vs ICP and ICP vs DCP, and 28 between LCP vs DCP and ICP vs DCP. Notably, no differential metabolite was common to all three pairwise comparisons. The relatively large overlap involving the two comparisons with ICP indicates that a considerable proportion of cultivar differences was associated with the distinct metabolic background of ICP, whereas LCP and DCP retained more comparison-specific metabolic features. This pattern suggests that cultivar differentiation was reflected in distinct metabolite response profiles, with each pairwise comparison showing its own combination of shared and unique metabolic changes.

Machine learning model evaluation and SHAP feature contribution analysis

To address the high dimensionality and nonlinearity of untargeted metabolomics data, this study constructed classification models using six machine learning algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Classification (SVC), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Neural Network (NNET), to assess the discriminatory power of non-volatile metabolite profiles for LCP, ICP, and DCP samples. As shown by the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, all six models achieved Area Under the Curve (AUC) values greater than 0.960 (Fig. 6a). Specifically, the RF model yielded an AUC of 1.000 and the XGBoost model an AUC of 0.991, suggesting superior discrimination by tree based ensemble models in this dataset (Dong et al., 2026). This finding demonstrated that metabolite based machine learning models were capable of accurately discriminating fermented samples from different chili cultivars. Furthermore, the model metrics presented in Fig. 6b showed that, relative to LR, SVC, KNN, and NNET, which had limited classification performance with accuracies of approximately 0.800, RF achieved values of 1.000 for Accuracy, F1 score, Kappa, and MCC, indicating better overall discrimination than XGBoost. The observed differences in model performance may be attributed to the characteristics of untargeted metabolomics datasets, which typically contain high-dimensional, correlated features with limited sample sizes. LR assumes linear relationships between variables and class labels, whereas KNN may be affected by the curse of dimensionality (Zhou et al., 2026). Although SVC and NNET can capture nonlinear patterns, their performance may be influenced by parameter optimization and sample size constraints (Bolinger et al., 2021). In contrast, RF integrates multiple decision trees through bootstrap aggregation and random feature selection, RF is suited to managing high dimensional features and underlying nonlinear relationships (Park et al., 2026). The confusion matrices provided further insight into the classification outcomes and error patterns of the six machine learning models for DCP, ICP, and LCP samples (Fig. 6c). In general, different models showed varying capacities for class recognition, suggesting that the non-volatile metabolite profiles of the three fermented samples included cultivar discriminating features as well as partial metabolic overlap. LR, SVC, and NNET showed frequent misclassification between DCP and ICP, as 3 out of 5 actual DCP samples were classified as ICP, implying similarities between these two groups in some metabolites and metabolic pathways. In contrast, XGBoost primarily showed confusion between ICP and LCP, misclassifying 2 ICP samples as LCP. KNN exhibited a more dispersed misclassification pattern, suggesting that distance-based similarity measures may be insufficient to capture complex relationships among features in high-dimensional metabolic data (Wang et al., 2026). The RF model achieved the highest classification accuracy, with all five samples from each of DCP, ICP, and LCP correctly classified and no misclassifications observed. These findings further support the suitability of RF for high-dimensional untargeted metabolomics datasets. To further evaluate model robustness and minimize potential overfitting, an independent external validation strategy based on fermentation stages was additionally performed. Samples collected at 0, 7, and 14 d were used as the training set, whereas samples from 21 and 28 d were reserved as an external test set. Under this time-dependent validation scheme, all six models maintained effective discrimination ability, with RF showing the highest overall performance among the evaluated classifiers (Fig. S1). The external validation results confirmed that the classification capability of RF was not solely attributable to random data partitioning, but reflected stable cultivar-related metabolic differences across fermentation stages. Accordingly, RF was used as the foundational model for the following SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis. SHAP analysis was applied to assess the contribution of individual metabolic features to model classification, and the top 20 discriminatory metabolites were screened according to the mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 6d).

Enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways for metabolites

To clarify how cultivars affect flavor in fermented Ciba chili, performed pathway enrichment analysis on high-contribution metabolites of DCP, ICP, and LCP and built a metabolic pathway network (Fig. 7). As shown in the enrichment bubble plots (Fig. 7a), the enrichment patterns differed among the three cultivars, although several pathways were commonly observed, including arginine biosynthesis, nitrogen metabolism, and purine metabolism. These results suggest that cultivar-driven metabolic differentiation in Ciba chili was closely associated with amino acid and nitrogen metabolism, lipid oxidation and oxylipin metabolism, purine and cofactor metabolism, and phenylpropanoid and polyphenol metabolism. In addition, because this analysis focused on cultivar-specific metabolites, it reflected metabolic differences among Ciba chili prepared from different chili cultivars rather than the overall metabolic trajectory of the fermentation process.

KEGG enrichment and integrated metabolic pathway analysis of cultivar-associated metabolites in fermented Ciba chili. (a) KEGG enrichment bubble plots of differential metabolites in DCP, ICP, and LCP. (b) Integrated metabolic pathway network showing the enrichment status of high-contribution metabolites across the three cultivars.

Fig. 7b shows that glycolysis-derived carbon skeletons enter the shikimate pathway to synthesize aromatic amino acids L-tryptophan and phenylalanine. This process represents a key link between primary carbon metabolism in plants and downstream secondary metabolic networks (Maeda & Dudareva, 2012; Vogt, 2010). After entering phenylpropanoid and polyphenol metabolism, trans-cinnamate showed a close network association with C04164. This finding indicates that cinnamate derived glycosylation is a core metabolic transformation route in the fermented Ciba chili system (Tiwari et al., 2016). Glycosylation can increase the hydrophilicity and stability of phenolic compounds. In fermented systems, this modification may also reduce the potential inhibitory effects of free phenolic acids on microorganisms, thereby contributing to the retention of flavor precursors (Sricharoen et al., 2017). In addition, polyphenol remodeling was observed during the middle and late stages of fermentation, and the metabolites showed pronounced cultivar specific differentiation. Several phenolic-related metabolites, including chlorogenic acid (C00852), prenyl caffeate (C10487), 4′-hydroxyflavanone (C14241), and Gardenin B (C15109), showed higher accumulation in DCP and LCP. In addition, trans-cinnamoyl-β-D-glucoside (C12205) and 5-hydroxy-L-tryptophan (C00643) also contributed to the cultivar-specific metabolic profiles. Chlorogenic acid and prenyl caffeate, as potent antioxidants among hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives, accumulated at higher levels in DCP and LCP, potentially conferring stronger free radical scavenging capacity to these cultivars (Tajik et al., 2017). 4′-Hydroxyflavanone is a key precursor in flavonoid biosynthesis, and its cultivar specific co-enrichment with Gardenin B in DCP and LCP highlights the advantage of these two cultivars in directed flavonoid biosynthesis. Meanwhile, the involvement of 5-hydroxyconiferyl alcohol suggests that the monolignol biosynthetic pathway was regulated in a cultivar specific manner. In contrast, fine downstream metabolic branching showed an interlaced pattern among cultivars. L-Tryptophanamide (C00977) was enriched only in DCP and ICP, whereas zingerone (C17497), which possesses distinctive pungent and characteristic aroma attributes, accumulated specifically in LCP and ICP. This staggered enrichment pattern indicates that the metabolic partitioning of aromatic amino acids among different chili cultivars is strongly genotype dependent (Akhter et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023).

In addition, at the terminal stage of primary metabolism, metabolic flux shifted toward nitrogen metabolism through the central node l-glutamine (C00064). Glutamine serves as an important amino group donor and nitrogen assimilation hub in biological systems. Its metabolism is closely associated with amino acid biosynthesis, nitrogen utilization, and purine-related metabolism during fermentation as well as purine and cofactor metabolism, within the fermentation system (Bernard & Habash, 2009). In the present study, xanthine (C00385) and uric acid (C00366) were commonly enriched in all fermented chili. As key intermediates involved in purine turnover and salvage related metabolism, the widespread accumulation of these two compounds may reflect intensive microbial biomass renewal and active nucleic acid transformation within the fermentation system (Kilstrup et al., 2005). Meanwhile, the common enrichment of biotin (C00120), an important cofactor for carboxylases, N6-acetyl-l-lysine (C02727), a marker of protein acetylation, and l-aspartate-semialdehyde (C00441), a key precursor for lysine and threonine biosynthesis, indicated that all chili cultivars maintained active nitrogen assimilation and microbial protein remodeling during fermentation (Sierocka et al., 2025). However, N-acetylornithine (C00437) showed strong cultivar specificity and was significantly enriched only in DCP and LCP. N-acetylornithine is a limiting intermediate in arginine biosynthesis (Ledwidge & Blanchard, 1999). In fermentative microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria, arginine metabolism is often associated with the arginine deiminase pathway, which can generate small amounts of ammonia to help cells resist severe acid stress during the middle and late stages of fermentation (Xu et al., 2026). Therefore, the specific enrichment of N-acetylornithine in DCP and LCP suggests that the fermentation microenvironments of these two cultivars. Considering the role of the arginine deiminase pathway in acid stress adaptation, this metabolic feature may be associated with microbial communities possessing improved acid tolerance. In the lipid oxidation and oxylipin metabolism network, different cultivars showed both convergent polyphenol lipid metabolic evolution and characteristic oxidative profiles through fatty acid biosynthesis. In all chili cultivars, OPC-8:0 (C04780), a key precursor involved in jasmonate biosynthesis, and nordihydrocapsaicin (C20216), a core pungency related compound, were commonly enriched. As a typical capsaicinoid, the stable accumulation and enrichment of nordihydrocapsaicin during fermentation not only indicated that the characteristic pungent compounds of Ciba chili were well retained, but also suggested that the fermentation microecology be associated with the preservation or transformation of fatty acyl moieties and vanillylamine derived pungency related metabolites (Aza-González et al., 2011; Murakami et al., 2011). However, the downstream branches of lipid oxidation showed strong cultivar dependent differentiation. The arachidonic acid derived oxidized lipid 15(S)-HETE (C04742) and geranyl acetate (C09861), a volatile ester with typical floral and fruity aroma characteristics, were significantly enriched only in DCP and LCP, contributing to richer aroma complexity and lipid related oxidative features in these two cultivars (Wasternack & Feussner, 2018). C14767 showed DCP and ICP specific enrichment, whereas Kukoamine D (C17618), a spermine alkaloid derivative, was specifically enriched in ICP and LCP. The interlaced accumulation of oxylipins and volatile compounds driven by lipoxygenase mediated or non-enzymatic oxidation outlined distinct metabolic footprints among chili cultivars in terms of cell wall softening, host–microbe defense interactions, and final flavor development (Peltier & Cournac, 2002). Overall, metabolic differentiation during Ciba chili fermentation resulted from the interaction between cultivar specific endogenous substrates and the fermentation microecology. Within a shared metabolic framework, different cultivars exhibited distinct branch allocation patterns in key pathways. DCP and LCP showed greater enrichment of metabolites associated with shikimate-derived phenylpropanoid metabolism and lipid oxidation pathways, resulting in higher accumulation of antioxidant polyphenols, flavonoids, and arginine-related precursors. In contrast, ICP was more closely associated with l-glutamine mediated nitrogen metabolism, resulting in the enrichment of basic amino acids and nucleotide intermediates, which may contribute to stronger early acidification and the retention of characteristic flavor compounds.

Changes of microbial community of ciba chili during fermentation

Analysis of bacterial community diversity

To elucidate microbial community dynamics during the fermentation of different chili cultivars, this study assessed sample α and β-diversity using 16S rRNA sequencing (Fig. S2). α-Diversity analysis revealed that Abundance-based Coverage Estimator (ACE), Chao1 richness estimator (Chao1), and Shannon diversity index (Shannon) declined sharply in all three groups during the first 0–7 d of fermentation (Fig. S2a-d), suggesting that fermentation initiation rapidly shifted the complex surface microbiota of the raw materials toward a few dominant microorganisms adapted to the fermentation conditions. The ACE and Chao1 indices showed the greatest decline rates in ICP (−36.10 and − 36.19 per day, respectively), followed by LCP (−21.81 and − 21.86 per day) and DCP (−16.95 and − 16.81 per day), indicating that microbial richness was rapidly reduced in ICP during fermentation initiation. In contrast, the Shannon index exhibited the fastest decline in DCP (−0.34 per day), whereas ICP showed the smallest decrease (−0.03 per day), suggesting that ICP maintained relatively higher community evenness during the early fermentation stage (Table S5). This pattern agreed with the rapid decrease in pH and accumulation of total acid during 0–7 d, suggesting that anaerobic sealing and acidification jointly suppressed aerobic, spoilage associated, and acid sensitive microorganisms, whereas acid tolerant fermentative communities expanded rapidly and became dominant. Despite the similar early filtering patterns observed across the three groups, the chili matrix continued to shape the middle and late stage community structures in different ways. In LCP, the Shannon index stayed at a low level after day 7, suggesting that its late stage microbial community was relatively simplified and more strongly dominated by core taxa. The ICP group showed a rebound in the Shannon index at 21 and 28 d, together with a relatively high late stage ACE index, indicating that higher microbial richness was still maintained toward the end of fermentation. Together with the physicochemical data, the elevated accumulation of total acid and amino acid nitrogen in may ICP have supplied continuous substrates for acid tolerant microorganisms, which may be associated with the persistence of a relatively complex microbial network and more diverse flavor characteristics during late fermentation (Huang et al., 2025). Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) analysis of β-diversity further demonstrated that fermentation markedly reshaped the microbial community structures of all three groups (Fig. S2e-g). The low stress values (0.059–0.116) supported the robustness of the NMDS ordination and indicated that the observed spatial relationships accurately reflected microbial community dissimilarities. For LCP, ICP, and DCP, the unfermented samples were distinctly separated from the fermented samples and clustered independently, suggesting that the natural microbiota of the raw materials was rapidly restructured following fermentation initiation. The different chili systems displayed distinct late stage succession. LCP and DCP were both characterized by marked early changes and later stabilization, as reflected by the overlap of the day 21 and day 28 confidence ellipses in LCP and the near clustering of day 21 and day 28 samples in DCP, suggesting that their late stage communities were close to stability. Together with the gradual stabilization of pH and total acidity, this suggests that a stable physicochemical environment provide the microecological foundation for subsequent flavor compound accumulation. By contrast, ICP exhibited a more sustained succession trajectory, with samples migrating continuously through the ordination space from day 0 to day 21, while a clear shift remained at day 28, indicating continued community remodeling in the final fermentation stage. Given its higher species richness and late stage Shannon index fluctuations, ICP have maintained a certain degree of community adaptability during late fermentation, related to substrate composition, metabolite accumulation, and interactions among functional taxa (Zhang et al., 2020).

Analysis of bacterial community structure during ciba chili

As shown in Fig. 8, the dynamic changes in bacterial community composition of DCP, ICP, and LCP during the fermentation period at both the phylum and genus levels. At the phylum level, all three chili underwent a deterministic shift in community structure, gradually transitioning from highly complex and heterogeneous initial microbiota to communities overwhelmingly dominated by Firmicutes (Fig. 8a). This convergence represents a typical anaerobic ecological filtering pattern driven by organic acid accumulation and rapid pH decline, a phenomenon commonly observed in spontaneous vegetable fermentation (Xiong et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2018). At the initial stage, day 0, the raw chili matrix contained diverse non-specialized phyla, mainly including unclassified bacteria, Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, and a small proportion of Firmicutes. As anaerobic acidification intensified, acid sensitive taxa, particularly Proteobacteria, were strongly inhibited by intracellular acid stress caused by the penetration of undissociated organic acids across the cell membrane (Yang et al., 2021). Meanwhile, Firmicutes proliferated exponentially and established a stable microbial community structure. However, the assembly of this community and the stability of Firmicutes dominance were highly dependent on the chili matrix. LCP exhibited the fastest microbial response and superior community buffering capacity. By day 7, the abundance of Firmicutes had sharply increased to 97.87%, effectively outcompeting and replacing the baseline Proteobacteria and Cyanobacteria. This accelerated succession indicated that the physicochemical properties of LCP provided a highly favorable microenvironment and reduced the colonization barrier for early lactic acid bacteria (LAB) (Zang et al., 2018). The DCP system showed a three stage succession pattern, characterized by rapid early turnover, mid stage disturbance, and late stage recovery. Initially, Firmicutes accounted for only 0.80%, whereas Proteobacteria reached 37.98%, indicating the presence of abundant environmental or epiphytic non LAB competitors on the raw material. Firmicutes rapidly became dominant, reaching 93.68% on day 7, but underwent a marked setback on day 14, decreasing to 79.43%. This mid stage fluctuation indicated instability in the microbial network, which may have been triggered by rapid substrate consumption, transient changes in acidification kinetics, or intense cross feeding competition (Chen et al., 2023). Eventually, the community stabilized, with Firmicutes recovering to above 93% during days 21–28. The microbial community succession of the DCP system exhibited three stage characteristics, with rapid turnover in the early stage, fluctuation in the middle stage, and stabilization in the later stage. On day 7, Firmicutes increased to 93.68% and became the overwhelmingly dominant bacterial group. On day 14, its abundance dropped sharply to 79.43%, while other bacterial groups temporarily rebounded, reflecting microbial network instability that may have resulted from rapid substrate depletion, transient changes in acidification rate, or interspecies cross feeding competition (Chen et al., 2023). During days 21–28, Firmicutes recovered to above 93%, and the community became stable. In contrast, the ICP matrix exerted a marked inhibitory effect on the establishment of the core fermentative microbiota. On day 0, unclassified bacteria were highly abundant in the ICP matrix, reaching a peak of 62.16%. During days 7–21, the abundance of Firmicutes increased slowly from 47.34% to 79.14%, whereas Proteobacteria remained steadily at approximately 14%. This prolonged coexistence indicated a weak competitive exclusion effect, resulting in a substantial delay in the assembly of the core functional community, possibly due to constraints imposed by the matrix conditions or the low compatibility of indigenous LAB in the initial inoculum (Wang et al., 2021). Firmicutes abundance did not reach 97.65% until day 28, at which point the community structure became comparable to those of LCP and DCP.

Bacterial community composition in fermented Ciba chili peppers. (a) Phylum level, (b) Genus level.

Phylum level analysis showed a convergent trend, whereas genus level analysis (Fig. 8b) indicated that the three chili substrates selectively enriched specific core lactic acid bacterial genera, thereby driving distinct metabolic pathways. DCP developed a Lactiplantibacillus dominated microbial succession pattern. At day 0, the initial community was dominated by environmental Acinetobacter, accounting for 17.08%. By day 7, Lactiplantibacillus (66.36%) and Weissella (21.79%) had proliferated rapidly. Interestingly, at day 14, the community composition changed markedly, with Weissella increasing to 75.90%, whereas Lactiplantibacillus sharply decreased to 0.37%. This transition reflected a typical succession mechanism in traditional vegetable fermentation, involving the alternation from heterofermentative to homofermentative bacteria (Yang et al., 2021). Early heterofermentative bacteria, Weissella, initiated the acidification process, but their growth was gradually inhibited as the pH dropped below their growth threshold. LCP formed a distinct microbial pattern dominated by Weissella. The baseline microbiota in the initial stage of the LCP system was relatively complex and was dominated by unclassified Cyanobacteriales, with Lactococcus accounting for 10.09%. At day 14, the LCP system underwent substantial restructuring involving multiple bacterial genera. At this stage, Pediococcus (35.24%) and Leuconostoc (22.87%) increased rapidly, temporarily reducing Weissella to a minor position of 2.78%. However, by days 21–28, Weissella rapidly regained dominance and eventually stabilized at approximately 78% in the later stage. This suggests that the specific carbon sources and protective microenvironment of LCP favored the long term survival of Weissella, a genus known for producing dextran and mild, desirable aroma characteristics in fermented vegetables (Prihanto et al., 2024). ICP displayed a prolonged community restructuring process with weaker succession coordination. In the initial blank environment dominated by unclassified bacteria (62.16%) and other taxa (31.75%), weak and scattered lactic acid bacteria appeared by day 7, while unclassified bacteria (33.90%) and unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (10.08%) remained at stable proportions. During days 14–21, ICP did not reach a stable structure but entered a transitional stage dominated by Pediococcus (35.37%–41.42%) and Leuconostoc (16%–17%). It was not until day 28 that Weissella reached 83.43% and became the dominant genus, indicating that the ICP substrate resisted rapid colonization and required prolonged succession to establish core dominance. These results indicate that the original chili matrix played a key role in regulating the assembly rate, metabolic stability, and overall maturation time of the core fermentative microbiome.

Correlation analysis of bacterial genera with volatile compounds and metabolites

To further clarify the potential associations among physicochemical properties, bacterial communities, key volatile compounds, and high-contribution metabolites, Spearman correlation networks were constructed for Ciba chili prepared from the three chili cultivars (Fig. 9). Only strong and statistically significant correlations with |r| ≥ 0.8 and p < 0.05 were retained. Overall, bacteria served as major connecting nodes, indicating that microbial variation was associated with changes in volatile compounds, metabolites, and physicochemical properties. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that microbial succession is closely linked to the dynamics of volatile compounds during chili fermentation, and that lactic acid bacteria are associated with aroma-related carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolic pathways (Guo et al., 2022b; Watts et al., 2018). However, the correlation patterns were clearly cultivar-dependent, suggesting that raw material characteristics shaped distinct microbial metabolic interaction networks during Ciba chili fermentation. In LCP, 37 negative and 21 positive correlations were identified, and the correlation network was dominated by negative associations, particularly those involving Unclassified_Cyanobacteriales, Unclassified_Bacteria, and Acinetobacter. These bacterium were negatively correlated with 1-hexanol, 2,3-pentanedione, and total acid, whereas Lactiplantibacillus was positively correlated with total acid. Notably, Unclassified_Bacteria was positively correlated with pH but negatively correlated with total acid, suggesting that acidification-related environmental changes were associated with shifts in bacterial community structure in LCP. Acid accumulation during vegetable fermentation generally favors acid-tolerant lactic acid bacteria while inhibiting microorganisms with weaker acid adaptability (Cai et al., 2021). The positive correlations between Weissella or Pediococcus and several metabolites suggested that these taxa were potentially associated with metabolite variation in LCP. These associations may partly correspond to the late-stage accumulation of acids and esters. Compared with LCP, ICP exhibited a more balanced network structure, with 40 negative and 42 positive correlations. Strong positive associations were observed among Unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae, Unclassified_Cyanobacteriales, Acinetobacter, pH, metabolites C04164 and C00064, and the volatile compound 3-octanone. These patterns indicate that ICP maintained a more complex microbial-chemical interaction network during fermentation Notably, ICP showed relatively higher amino nitrogen levels together with delayed dominance of Weissella compared with other cultivars. The sustained availability of nitrogenous compounds may indicate a stronger buffering capacity of the fermentation matrix, which could be associated with a slower decline in pH and delayed ecological transition toward acid-tolerant lactic acid bacteria. Thus, the slower enrichment of Weissella in ICP may be associated with the physicochemical characteristics of the cultivar matrix rather than solely microbial succession. In particular, Lactococcus was correlated with the accumulation of metabolite C17618 (r = 1.00). Given that ICP also showed higher total acid and amino acid nitrogen levels, the close microbial metabolic associations reflect stronger coupling among acidification, nitrogen metabolism, and flavor precursor transformation. These correlation patterns were consistent with the higher aldehyde retention and earlier ester formation observed in ICP. In DCP, positive correlations were mainly concentrated between bacterium and metabolites, whereas negative correlations were mainly observed between bacterium and volatile compounds, including 1-hexanol, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, and 2-pentylfuran. Weissella and Pediococcus showed strong positive correlations with metabolites such as C00366 and C10487, whereas Acinetobacter and Enterobacter related taxa were negatively correlated with 1-hexanol. This pattern suggests that DCP flavor development was associated with the simultaneous accumulation of specific metabolites and the transformation of alcohols, ketones, and furan compounds. Considering that DCP was dominated by Lactiplantibacillus and showed relatively high levels of alcohols, ketones, furans, and pyrazines, these correlations suggest that cultivar-specific microbial succession patterns were associated with differences in fermented aroma profiles and pungency-related flavor characteristics.

Taken together, the three correlation networks revealed that bacterial taxa were central nodes associated with physicochemical properties, volatile compounds, and metabolite profiles during Ciba chili fermentation. LCP was characterized by stronger negative associations between bacteria and volatile compounds, ICP exhibited the most complex and balanced interaction network, and DCP showed pronounced positive coupling between bacterium and metabolites. These findings suggest that cultivar differences regulate Ciba chili flavor quality through distinct microbial metabolic interaction patterns. Nevertheless, the microbial-metabolite and microbial-volatile relationships identified in this study represent correlation-based associations rather than direct causal interactions. The functional roles of core lactic acid bacteria, such as Weissella and Lactiplantibacillus, in flavor development require further validation through strain isolation, inoculation experiments, and controlled fermentation studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that chili cultivar characteristics were closely associated with distinct fermentation trajectories and flavor-related chemical differentiation in Ciba chili. Inner Yellow New Generation chili pepper (ICP), Lantern chili pepper (LCP), and Devil's chili pepper (DCP) exhibited cultivar-specific patterns in physicochemical evolution, microbial succession, and metabolite transformation, indicating that the initial chemical background of raw materials influenced fermentation outcomes. Multi-omics integration further revealed that differences in bacterial communities and metabolic pathways were associated with the formation of distinct flavor-related chemical profiles among cultivars. In addition, the random forest model combined with SHAP analysis identified key discriminatory metabolites, providing potential metabolic markers for cultivar discrimination. These findings highlight the importance of raw material selection in shaping fermentation characteristics and provide a theoretical basis for cultivar-oriented production and quality control of Ciba chili. However, this study was conducted using three representative chili cultivars under specific spontaneous fermentation conditions, and the observed cultivar-associated differences may also be influenced by factors such as cultivation environment, raw material batch variation, and initial microbial communities. Moreover, flavor interpretation was mainly based on chemical composition and microbial-metabolite associations rather than direct sensory evaluation or functional validation. Future studies incorporating QDA, GC-olfactometry, targeted LC-MS quantification, strain-level, metatranscriptomic validation, and storage-period monitoring are needed to further strengthen the sensory, mechanistic, and practical interpretation of these findings. Furthermore, cultivar selection strategies and fermentation regulation schemes based on production objectives and cultivar-specific microbial succession patterns should be further explored to enhance the industrial application of Ciba chili.
