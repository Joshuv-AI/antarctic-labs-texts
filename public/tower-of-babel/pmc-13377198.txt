Decoding peri-urban transformation through multi-scale mapping of bidirectional urbanisation
Authors: B. Linda Theres, R. Selvakumar, Vasu Sathyakumar, S. Anandha Kumar, Shankar Karuppannan, Keerat Kumar Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanisation in emerging economies is reshaping the geography of human settlement at an unprecedented pace, placing acute pressure on the principles of sustainable development. In India, these transformations are particularly pronounced in non-metropolitan regions, where expansion routinely surpasses statutory (de jure) administrative boundaries. Yet conventional analyses of urban sprawl fail to capture these processes’ complex, bidirectional nature, providing an incomplete account of the functional and spatial extent of contemporary urban growth. Here, we present a novel, data-driven geospatial framework for delineating dynamic urban expansion boundaries and quantifying bidirectional growth patterns. We apply this methodology to Salem and its surrounding towns in Tamil Nadu, India, spanning the period 2001–2020. Our three-tiered approach combines: (i) buffer analysis to identify the spatial threshold of built-up expansion; (ii) centroid shift analysis to track macro-scale directional change; and (iii) landscape metrics to measure micro-scale fragmentation and aggregation. Over two decades, Salem’s built-up area increased by ~ 170%, generating a compact urban core encircled by a fragmented, outwardly expanding periphery. Neighbouring towns, including Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi, exhibited marked bidirectional expansion towards Salem, underscoring the emergence of strongly interconnected regional growth corridors. By redefining urban boundaries through quantitative spatial metrics, our framework enables early detection of emergent urban linkages and sprawl intensities. These findings not only advance the methodological frontier of urban spatial analysis, but also provide actionable intelligence for planners and policymakers seeking to manage expansion sustainably and equitably in rapidly transforming city-regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52525-4.

Introduction

Urbanisation is a global phenomenon, exerting transformative effects in developing nations where rapid population growth, infrastructure development, and economic transitions drive significant spatial change1,2. In India, urban growth has often proceeded in an unplanned and fragmented manner, especially in emerging non-metropolitan cities, resulting in an imbalance between the demand and supply for urban infrastructure and services, especially in emerging non-metropolitan cities3–5. Class-1 cities, typically with populations ranging from 0.1 to 1 million and categorised as tier-2 or tier-3, have limited resources and institutional constraints that make efficient urban administration challenging. According to the National Institute of Urban Affairs (NIUA), only 51.4% of households in these cities have access to clean drinking water, highlighting the inequitable distribution of urbanisation benefits and underscoring the urgent need for spatially inclusive development strategies. In this context, Remote Sensing (RS) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have emerged as indispensable tools for capturing and analysing urban dynamics, with landscape metrics derived from satellite imagery offering critical insights into patterns of fragmentation, aggregation, and land-use composition6–8. Despite these technological advances, urban sprawl research in India has remained disproportionately concentrated on large metropolitan centres, with relatively limited studies exploring non-metropolitan class-1 cities such as Mysore9, Lucknow10, Chandigarh11, Bhopal12, Thiruvananthapuram13, Mangaluru14, and Dehradun15.

Beyond the Indian context, international research has extensively examined peri-urbanisation and sprawl in rapidly growing regions of Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, often using similar remote sensing and spatial metrics-based approaches16–18. Studies in the Greater Cairo region, for instance, have combined land-cover mapping with urban growth modelling to show how informal expansion and infrastructure-led development jointly reshape metropolitan footprints17. Comparable work in Oman and Morocco demonstrates how CA–Markov models and multispectral imagery can capture mountainous and arid-city growth trajectories, reinforcing the value of geospatial techniques for decoding complex urban–rural transitions in diverse climatic and governance settings18. These international cases highlight the transferability of spatial analytic methods, while underscoring the need to adapt them to the specific institutional, demographic, and policy environments of countries such as India. These studies are constrained by three key limitations: restricted scope, simplistic metrics, and inadequate directional analysis. They are confined within municipal boundaries and fail to account for peri-urban or suburban expansion. Additionally, they rely solely on Shannon’s entropy9,14, which only captures spatial concentration of built-up areas, not directional or structural growth patterns. Some research addresses ribbon development13,15, but most ignores the multidirectional or bidirectional dynamics of urban expansion, especially in polycentric or functionally interconnected regions. Furthermore, the widespread use of wind rose overlays and buffer zones based on arbitrary thresholds19–22, lacks empirical justification, rendering them ineffective for identifying zones of growth pressure or planning infrastructure allocation with precision.

Urbanisation in such contexts can be conceptually framed using the core–periphery model, where a dominant core city concentrates economic activities, infrastructure, and institutional capacity, while surrounding settlements function as subordinated peripheries that both depend on, and are reshaped by, core-driven development processes23. Over time, rising land and labour costs, congestion, and environmental stresses in the core trigger the diffusion of growth functions towards nearby towns, giving rise to new sub-centres and reinforcing spatially uneven but functionally interconnected patterns of expansion. In parallel, polycentric development theory highlights how regions comprising multiple, proximate urban centres can evolve into networks of interdependent nodes, where spillovers from one centre “borrow size” from others, potentially reducing intra-regional disparities while reshaping daily mobility, land markets, and infrastructure demands24. Situating emerging Indian class-1 cities within these frameworks underscores that their trajectories are rarely isolated; rather, they are embedded in multi-nodal urban regions in which core–periphery dynamics increasingly coexist with tendencies towards polycentric urbanisation25.

Recognising that urban expansion is inherently interdependent, with the growth of one settlement influencing land use, infrastructure, and socio-economic conditions in adjacent towns, a more comprehensive and regionalised perspective on urbanisation is essential. Analyses considering solely the de jure administrative boundaries fail to capture the dynamic evolution of urban peripheries. Although recent studies have attempted to define functional urban boundaries through parameters such as street-edge length, built-up density26,27, and socio-economic indicators28,29, these methods remain data-intensive and often struggle with practical applicability in Indian urban contexts. While the exploration of conurbations and urban–rural transition zones30,31 marks progress, such approaches remain insufficient to delineate emerging urban morphologies in fast-growing non-metropolitan regions. There is a demanding need for a scalable, empirically grounded methodology that uses high-resolution spatial data to detect growth directionality and define functional urban extents.

Within India, these methodological gaps intersect with broader structural trends in urbanisation, including the rapid growth of tier-II and tier-III cities, fiscal and capacity constraints in urban local bodies, and persistent inequalities in access to basic services. National assessments emphasise that infrastructure deficits in transport, water supply, sanitation, and housing are particularly acute in non-metropolitan regions, where urban expansion routinely outpaces planning and investment cycles. As a result, peri-urban belts around cities like Salem frequently emerge as de facto urban spaces governed by rural institutions, producing fragmented regulatory regimes and reinforcing the disconnect between functional urban regions and their formal administrative delineations. Situating Salem within these patterns allows the present study to speak not only to local dynamics but also to the wider challenge of governing India’s dispersed, multi-nodal urban transition. Within a core–periphery and polycentric lens, such a methodology must not only map where built-up expansion occurs but also differentiate whether growth reinforces a dominant core, strengthens emerging sub-centres, or consolidates multi-nodal interaction corridors between towns. Capturing these nuances of regional structure is critical for understanding whether non-metropolitan regions are moving towards more balanced, polycentric configurations or deepening existing core–periphery inequalities.32,33.

This study focuses on Salem, a class-1 non-metropolitan city in Tamil Nadu, as a model case for examining regional interactions and directional patterns of urban expansion. Policy initiatives such as the Smart Cities Mission and state-led infrastructure investments have intensified Salem’s role as a regional growth pole by concentrating improvements in roads, junction redesign, transit hubs, and public spaces within the statutory city. While these programmes aim to enhance liveability and economic competitiveness, their benefits often radiate along highway corridors and into peri-urban panchayats in the form of speculative real estate, logistics facilities, and incrementally serviced layouts, without corresponding upgrades in local governance and service delivery. In this context, the bidirectional expansion documented in this study can be interpreted as an emergent spatial footprint of such policy-driven interventions, making it essential to align Smart City investments with regional-scale planning instruments that explicitly recognise and manage Salem’s extended functional urban region. Existing literature, including Malligai and Jegankumar34, has provided limited insights into peri-urban growth, yet lacks methodological rigour in delineating buffer extents or capturing inter-town dynamics34–38. Addressing these gaps, this study proposes a robust geospatial framework that redefines urban growth boundaries and systematically analyses Salem’s bidirectional expansion with its surrounding towns—Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi. The framework integrates buffer-based built-up density thresholds to delineate functional urban extents, centroid shift analysis to trace macro-level directional growth trends, and landscape metrics (PLAND, PD, AREA_MN, AI) to evaluate micro-scale spatial complexity across multiple directional and distance-based gradients.

Landscape metrics are particularly well suited to operationalise urban form dynamics because they translate categorical land-use patterns into quantitative indicators of composition and configuration that are sensitive to fragmentation, clustering, and patch size distributions6,7. Metrics such as PLAND and AREA_MN describe the proportion and average size of built-up patches, thereby indicating the intensity and consolidation of urban land, while PD and AI capture the degree of patch subdivision and aggregation, revealing whether growth is dispersed, leapfrogging, or coalescing into more contiguous urban fabric. When computed along directional and distance-based gradients, these metrics provide a fine-grained profile of how core, peri-urban, and inter-urban corridors differ in their spatial structure, enabling empirical discrimination between monocentric sprawl, corridor-led expansion, and emergent polycentric configurations8.

This research offers a holistic approach to understanding urban expansion dynamics by employing a macro- micro analytical synthesis. At the macro scale, centroid shift analysis elucidates the direction and magnitude of urban sprawl, while at the micro scale, landscape metrics capture the heterogeneity and spatial structure of built-up areas. This macro–micro coupling allows the study to link measurable shifts in urban form to broader core–periphery and polycentric reconfigurations of the Salem region, offering a theory-informed yet empirically tractable basis for interpreting observed growth trajectories. This integrated approach facilitates deeper insights into the interplay between core and peripheral urban zones, enabling more effective planning for infrastructure, land use, and governance. Ultimately, the study aims to deliver a replicable, data-driven urban growth monitoring and boundary delineation model, with practical implications for spatial planning and sustainable development in India’s non-metropolitan cities.

In this context, the aim of the study is to develop a scalable, empirically grounded geospatial framework to decode bidirectional urbanisation and delineate functional urban boundaries in a non-metropolitan, polycentric regional setting such as Salem and its surrounding towns. Specifically, the study seeks to (i) delineate the functional urban extents of Salem, Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi using buffer-based built-up density thresholds that move beyond de jure administrative limits; (ii) characterise macro-scale directional growth patterns and core–periphery interactions through centroid shift analysis; and (iii) quantify micro-scale spatial structure using directional landscape metrics (PLAND, PD, AREAMN, AI) across distance gradients to distinguish between compact, infill-driven growth and fragmented, peri-urban sprawl, thereby providing a replicable evidence base for sustainable and regionally integrated planning in India’s non-metropolitan cities.

Area of interest

Salem, a Class-1 non-metropolitan city in Tamil Nadu, India, exemplifies the dynamics of regional urbanisation in emerging urban centres. As per the 2011 Census, Salem had a population of approximately 0.92 million spread across 124 km², with recent estimates suggesting the population exceeded 1 million by 2021. Geographically, the city lies along the Thirumanimuthar River, with natural topographic constraints such as the Sheveroy and Jarugumalai Hills limiting expansion to the northeast and southeast, respectively. Economically, Salem serves as a regional hub with a diversified base comprising steel production, textiles, agriculture, and retail, further reinforced by its strategic location along three major national highways—NH 44, NH 79, and NH 544—that facilitate robust intra- and inter-city connectivity.

Governed by the Salem City Municipal Corporation (SCMC), the city’s statutory boundary extends up to 9 km from the centre. However, urban development has increasingly spilt into peri-urban areas under rural governance, highlighting a disconnect between functional urban expansion and statutory jurisdiction. Designated under India’s Smart Cities Mission, Salem is undergoing infrastructure modernisation, yet the rapid peri-urban growth underscores the need for integrated spatial planning. The city substantially influences a constellation of nearby towns—Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi—forming a polycentric urban region. Each town exhibits unique functional linkages with Salem: Omalur as a commuter suburb, Sankari as an industrial node, Rasipuram with textile-driven growth, and Vazhapadi showing linear expansion along NH 79. This emerging urban network reflects a bidirectional growth dynamic, in which Salem’s evolution influences and is influenced by regional interactions, requiring a holistic planning approach that goes beyond conventional administrative limits.

Unlike studies confined to the statutory limits of the Salem City Municipal Corporation, this research adopts a regional perspective that explicitly includes the four major neighbouring towns of Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi. To delineate this broader functional urban region in a spatially meaningful way, a uniform radial buffer around the Salem city centre was not used, as such an artificial boundary would cut across settlements and fail to reflect actual patterns of urban influence. Instead, revenue village boundaries falling largely within an empirically determined influence zone were selected and merged to form the ‘Region of Interest’ shown in Fig. 1. Consequently, the resulting outer boundary is irregular and the buffer width varies by direction, which is intentional: it follows administratively recognised village units while still capturing the full urbanised and peri‑urban continuum between Salem and the four neighbouring towns (Fig. 1).

Data and software

This study is based on the analysis of multi-temporal Land Use/Land Cover (LU/LC) datasets to characterise the spatial and directional growth patterns of Salem and its surrounding towns. The primary spatial dataset used in this study consists of classified Land Use/Land Cover (LU/LC) maps developed, derived from Landsat imagery at 30-meter resolution for the years 2001, 2011, and 2020. All satellite images underwent geometric correction, radiometric calibration, and atmospheric correction to ensure data consistency and reliability. Table 1 details the satellite sources, temporal coverage, classification methods, and preprocessing steps. The study used two analytical methods to study spatial changes over time in urban expansion. The first was a centroid analysis using ArcGIS 10.3, which identified spatial shifts in built-up area centroids. The second was a landscape metrics analysis using FRAGSTATS v4.2, which quantified landscape classes’ spatial configuration and pattern. The data was projected to the Universal Transverse Mercator for consistency. This approach ensures transparency, reproducibility, and robust macro-scale urban dynamics and micro-scale landscape structure analysis.

Methodology

Data preparation and built-up feature extraction

The study uses a multi-tiered geospatial analytical framework to quantify urban growth in Salem and its surrounding towns. It uses built-up density analysis, centroid shift analysis, and landscape metrics across three temporal timescales (2001, 2011, 2020) to assess urbanisation trends in Salem and its four surrounding towns—Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi. Direction landscape metrics analysis

To assess the micro-level spatial structure and directional patterns of urban growth, a wind rose-based analytical framework was implemented. Each city or town was divided into eight cardinal directions—North (N), Northeast (NE), East (E), Southeast (SE), South (S), Southwest (SW), West (W), and Northwest (NW)—radiating from the geographic centre. For each sector and across all three time periods (2001, 2011, and 2020), four class-level landscape metrics were calculated using FRAGSTATS v4.2. These metrics were selected based on their ability to describe urban sprawl patterns in the context of composition, fragmentation, and aggregation of built-up parcels. The study uses class-level metrics to analyse urbanisation processes in built-up land, providing insights into each directional zone (Refer to Table 2). Combining these metrics with centroid shift and buffer-based delineation offers a multi-scalar understanding of urban sprawl, including regional shifts in gravity and structural transformation patterns.

Analysis of results

Built-up area dynamics (2001–2020)

The built-up land use was extracted from the LULC data prepared for the year 2001, 2011 and 2020 and plotted as map as shown in Fig. 3a–c using ArcGIS 10.3. The spatial and temporal analysis of built-up land between 2001 and 2020 reveals a substantial transformation across the study region. The total built-up area increased from 59.61 km² in 2001 to 82.82 km² in 2011 and nearly doubled to 160.81 km² by 2020. This marks a 38.9% growth during the first decade and a much steeper 94.2% increase in the subsequent decade, highlighting a significant acceleration of urbanisation. This expansion followed a two-stage land transformation process: initially, agricultural land was converted into barren land (2001–2011), followed by a transition of these barren lands into built-up areas (2011–2020), driven by intensified infrastructure development and housing demand. Spatially, Salem exhibited both core densification and peripheral sprawl, reinforcing its status as the regional urban nucleus. Omalur’s growth remained relatively contained, suggesting compact development, while Vazhapadi displayed linear expansion along the NH 79 corridor. Rasipuram and Sankari experienced more extensive peripheral growth, indicating increasing development pressure from adjacent rural landscapes (Fig. 3a–c).

Urban boundary delineation

To better quantify the actual extent of urban growth, built-up density was assessed using concentric buffers. The saturation thresholds, or the distances beyond which built-up density plateaued, were calculated for each settlement. Salem showed a threshold at 12 km, which exceeds its statutory boundary of 9 km, suggesting that existing administrative limits underestimate the city’s functional urban area. The thresholds for Vazhapadi, Rasipuram, Sankari, and Omalur were found to be 2–3 km, indicating more compact yet expanding growth footprints. These findings underscore the value of a data-driven, scalable method for delineating urban extents, especially in non-metropolitan contexts where administrative boundaries often fail to reflect true urban influence (Fig. 4).

Centroid shift and directional urban influence

The centroid shift analysis further revealed directional patterns of urban growth (Fig. 5). Salem’s built-up centroid shifted southwestward, constrained by the Sheveroy and Jarugumalai Hills to the northeast and southeast, respectively. Rasipuram and Vazhapadi demonstrated marginal but consistent centroid movement toward Salem along major corridors (NH 44 and NH 79), indicating increasing functional interdependence and peri-urban integration. Sankari recorded the most pronounced centroid shift toward Salem, reflecting a strong urban pull, while Omalur showed an inconsistent trajectory but ultimately trended closer to the city. These movements collectively affirm the presence of bidirectional urbanisation, where growth occurs both outward from Salem and inward from adjacent towns, suggesting a gradual emergence of a polycentric urban structure.

Landscape metrics and directional urban form

Four landscape metrics—PLAND, PD, AREA_MN, and AI—were computed directionally and across distance buffers to characterize the structural nature of urban expansion.

Percentage of landscape (PLAND)

PLAND values increased consistently across all directions, with a notable surge between 2011 and 2020. High PLAND values in the southwest, west, and northwest sectors are attributed to the redirection of growth away from the eastern hills. Exceptions to the distance-based decline in PLAND highlight the influence of institutional land, open spaces, and infrastructure (Fig. 6). Similar directional growth patterns were observed in the adjacent towns, with Vazhapadi expanding linearly along NH 79, Omalur growing toward Salem and along its bypass corridor, and Rasipuram and Sankari spreading along key regional axes.

Plot showing the directional aspect of PLAND for Salem.

Patch density (PD)

Patch Density (PD) remained stable from 2001 to 2011 but increased substantially during 2011–2020 in peripheral sectors. PD decreased within 3 km of Salem’s centre, suggesting infill development, while higher values at outer buffers indicated scattered or leapfrog development (Fig. 7). In adjacent towns, PD patterns reflected dispersed settlements (e.g., in Vazhapadi and Omalur) and fragmentation toward Salem (e.g., in Rasipuram and Sankari). These trends suggest that newly built-up development near the core filled the gaps in the existing patches (infill development). In contrast, built-up development in the peripheries was mostly isolated (expansion or ribbon development).

Mean patch area (AREA_MN)

AREA_MN decreased across most sectors of Salem, signifying patch fragmentation. Larger isolated patches in the south and southwest suggest targeted real estate development. A spike at 12 km northeast indicates a possible future peri-urban growth hotspot between Salem and Vazhapadi. Peripheral towns show varying AREA_MN trends (Fig. 8). Vazhapadi exhibits increased values at the fringe, indicating emerging densification zones. Omalur and Sankari showed patterns that suggest a shift from compact to fragmented development as distance increases. Rasipuram showed fluctuations reflecting hybrid settlement typologies with emerging village-town transitions.

Aggregation Index (AI)

Aggregation Index (AI) values declined over time across most towns and directions, signalling a broader shift from compact to fragmented urban forms. In Salem, AI remained relatively stable within the urban core but decreased toward the edges, indicating disaggregated development at the periphery. However, in the northwest, both PLAND and AI increased, suggesting planned and aggregated corridor development. In the southeast, AI remained stable despite PLAND increases, likely constrained by the Jarugumalai Hills (Figure. 9). Similar disaggregation patterns were observed in the surrounding towns, with lower AI values near the periphery of Vazhapadi and Rasipuram, and notable dips in Sankari due to interruptions by agricultural or vegetated land. These metric-based observations confirm that while Salem’s core continues to densify, its periphery and neighbouring towns are experiencing increasingly fragmented and unregulated sprawl.

To evaluate the robustness of the observed spatial patterns, one-way ANOVA tests were performed across the three time periods (2001, 2011, and 2020) for each town and landscape metric. The results confirmed statistically significant transformations in urban structure. Notably, changes in the Percentage of Landscape (PLAND) were highly significant across all towns (p < 0.01), affirming consistent expansion in built-up area. The Patch Density (PD) metric exhibited significant increases (p < 0.05) in Salem, Rasipuram, and Sankari, reflecting growing fragmentation in urban form. The Mean Patch Area (AREA_MN) showed a significant decline (p < 0.05) across all towns, indicating the proliferation of smaller and more dispersed urban parcels. Moreover, the Aggregation Index (AI) experienced a statistically significant decrease (p < 0.01), particularly in Salem and Omalur, suggesting a clear transition toward spatially disaggregated development.

These statistically validated trends illustrate a systematic intensification of urban sprawl, characterised by spatial expansion (PLAND), increasing fragmentation (PD), the disintegration of larger coherent urban blocks (AREA_MN), and declining spatial cohesion (AI). Together, they reflect a dual pattern of densification at the urban core and unplanned sprawl at the periphery. From a planning standpoint, these statistically significant trends move beyond mere numerical change to indicate qualitatively different urbanisation regimes within the region. Increasing PLAND combined with declining AREA_MN and AI confirms that Salem and its satellite towns are not simply growing larger but are reorganising into finer-grained, less cohesive built-up mosaics that are harder and more expensive to service with conventional infrastructure networks. The significant rise in PD, especially at outer buffers, signals the proliferation of small, isolated patches typical of speculative layouts and ribbon development, which fragment agricultural land, complicate public transport provision, and constrain future options for compact, transit-supportive growth. By statistically validating these shifts, the analysis provides planners with robust evidence that current trajectories are pushing the region toward a more fragmented urban form, thereby underscoring the urgency of instituting growth management and corridor-based planning strategies.

Discussions

Urban sprawl studies in India have predominantly relied on remote sensing-based assessments of built-up change within administrative boundaries, often using single metrics such as Shannon’s entropy to quantify sprawl9,14,34. While such approaches have been useful for capturing overall spatial dispersion, they provide limited insight into directional growth, inter-settlement interactions, and the structural evolution of urban regions. By contrast, the present study adopts a multi-scalar and bidirectional framework that integrates buffer-based delineation, centroid shift analysis, and landscape metrics, thereby addressing key methodological gaps identified in earlier work11,13,15. Thus the present study advances the understanding of peri-urban transformation by situating the observed spatial dynamics of Salem within a broader body of urban sprawl and peri-urbanisation literature.

The finding that Salem’s functional urban extent extends up to 12 km beyond its statutory boundary aligns with a growing body of literature emphasising the inadequacy of administrative boundaries in representing actual urban systems. Studies on city delineation using spatial metrics and machine learning approaches have similarly demonstrated that functional urban areas often exceed legal limits due to socio-economic and infrastructural linkages26,27. Comparable observations have been made in peri-urban regions globally, where urban expansion transcends governance boundaries, resulting in fragmented planning regimes and service disparities30,31. In the Indian context, such discrepancies are particularly pronounced in tier-II and tier-III cities, where institutional capacity often lags behind spatial growth5.

The centroid shift results further reinforce the interpretation of Salem as a dominant regional node exerting spatial influence on surrounding towns. The observed inward shifts of Sankari, Omalur, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi toward Salem reflect a pattern consistent with core–periphery dynamics, where economic and infrastructural concentration in the core drives peripheral restructuring23. At the same time, the reciprocal nature of this movement, where peripheral towns also expand toward the core—indicates a transition toward polycentric urbanisation, as described in recent regional development studies24,25. Similar bidirectional or convergent growth patterns have been documented in peri-urban regions of the Global South, including Cairo and parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where infrastructure corridors and economic linkages foster interconnected urban systems rather than isolated city growth16,17.

The landscape metrics analysis provides further evidence of structural transformation in urban form. The increase in PLAND alongside declining AREA_MN and AI, coupled with rising PD, indicates a shift toward fragmented and dispersed development patterns. These findings are consistent with prior studies that have used landscape metrics to characterise urban sprawl in cities such as Mangaluru, Lucknow, and Dehradun, where peripheral growth is often marked by leapfrog development and patch fragmentation8,10,15. However, unlike these studies, which largely interpret sprawl as an outward diffusion process, the present analysis reveals a dual dynamic: simultaneous core densification and peripheral fragmentation. This duality has also been observed in international studies using CA - Markov and landscape-based approaches, where urban cores intensify while peri-urban areas exhibit discontinuous expansion due to speculative land markets and infrastructure development40–42.

Directional analysis further highlights the role of infrastructure in shaping urban form. The concentration of growth along NH 44, NH 79, and NH 544 supports the widely documented phenomenon of corridor-led urbanisation, where transport networks act as primary drivers of spatial expansion19,20. Similar linear growth patterns have been identified in peri-urban studies of Thiruvananthapuram and Kandy, where road networks significantly influence land-use transitions and settlement morphology13,20. In Salem, this effect is amplified by topographic constraints, which redirect growth toward accessible corridors, resulting in asymmetrical urban expansion.

From a planning perspective, the observed increase in fragmentation (high PD, low AI) in peripheral zones is particularly significant. Previous research has linked such patterns to inefficient land use, increased infrastructure costs, and environmental degradation, especially in rapidly urbanising regions lacking coordinated planning frameworks12,16. The decline in aggregation and mean patch size suggests that urban growth is increasingly driven by small-scale, often unregulated developments, which can lead to long-term challenges in service provision and sustainability. These findings resonate with studies on peri-urban India, where fragmented governance and speculative land markets contribute to dispersed and inequitable urbanisation patterns[,41,43.

Importantly, this study contributes to the literature by empirically demonstrating bidirectional urbanisation as a measurable and spatially explicit phenomenon. While earlier studies have acknowledged peri-urban growth and urban–rural transitions, few have quantitatively captured the mutual influence between core cities and surrounding towns. By integrating centroid shifts with directional landscape metrics, this research provides a more nuanced understanding of how urban regions evolve as interconnected systems rather than isolated entities.

Furthermore, the methodological framework developed here offers a scalable alternative to data-intensive approaches that rely on socio-economic datasets or complex modelling techniques. By leveraging widely available satellite imagery and spatial metrics, the approach aligns with recent efforts to develop practical and transferable tools for urban monitoring in data-constrained environments44,45. This makes it particularly relevant for non-metropolitan cities in developing countries, where rapid urbanisation often outpaces data availability and institutional capacity.

Overall, the findings situate Salem within a broader trajectory of emerging polycentric urban regions in India and the Global South, characterised by infrastructural corridors, peri-urban expansion, and evolving core–periphery relationships. By bridging methodological gaps and grounding the analysis in established urban theories and empirical studies, this research strengthens the evidence base for regional-scale planning and highlights the need to move beyond administrative boundaries toward functionally integrated urban governance frameworks.

Conclusion

The study presents a comprehensive spatial analysis of urban expansion in Salem and its surrounding towns—Omalur, Sankari, Rasipuram, and Vazhapadi—between 2001 and 2020, offering critical insights into the region’s transition toward a polycentric urban structure. Using a robust three-tiered methodology comprising built-up density-based urban boundary delineation, centroid shift analysis, and directional landscape metrics, the research establishes that Salem’s growth is strongly influenced by regional dependencies and directional pull from adjacent towns. The findings demonstrate that towns such as Vazhapadi, Sankari, and Rasipuram are expanding toward Salem, while Omalur exhibits the most rapid and pronounced integration due to its strategic connectivity. This spatial configuration reflects a functional consolidation of peripheral towns into the larger urban system, thereby confirming the bidirectional nature of regional urbanisation.

Across all towns, a common trend is observed: compact urban cores are giving way to fragmented and dispersed peripheral development, characterised by leapfrog and ribbon patterns—hallmarks of unregulated sprawl. While Salem’s centre exhibits infill-driven densification, its outskirts and neighbouring towns are undergoing scattered expansion, necessitating an urgent shift in planning paradigms.

In response, the study recommends revising urban growth boundaries based on empirical built-up density thresholds, fostering decentralised infrastructure in rapidly integrating towns like Omalur, and establishing a dedicated regional planning authority to harmonise land use, infrastructure investment, and corridor development across the region. The methodological framework developed herein—combining spatial metrics with directional analysis—is both scalable and transferable, offering a valuable planning diagnostic tool for Class-I and other emerging non-metropolitan cities in India.

While the study provides a comprehensive spatial analysis of urban expansion, certain limitations are acknowledged. First, the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) may influence the interpretation of landscape metrics near administrative boundaries. Second, the reliance on 30-meter resolution Landsat data may underrepresent fine-scale changes, particularly in high-density or informal urban pockets. Third, socioeconomic drivers, such as income distribution, migration patterns, and land market dynamics, were not integrated due to data constraints. Lastly, the assumption of built-up land as a sole proxy for urbanisation may overlook emerging non-residential forms such as industrial corridors, institutional zones, and Special Economic Zones (SEZs).

As a future scope of study, it is suggested to incorporate high-resolution satellite imagery and nighttime light data to improve spatial detection of built-up changes. Machine learning-based predictive models could also be employed to simulate future urban growth under different policy and infrastructure scenarios. Moreover, the inclusion of demographic, economic, and environmental datasets would provide a more holistic perspective on the spatial, functional, and socio-economic dimensions of urban expansion.

Ultimately, this study contributes significantly to the urban sprawl literature by providing an empirically grounded, regionally contextualised, and policy-relevant framework to guide equitable and sustainable urbanisation in rapidly growing Indian city regions.
