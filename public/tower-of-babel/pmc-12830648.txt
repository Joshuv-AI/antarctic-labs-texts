Impact of N-terminal domain on the sHSP Lo18 function
Authors: Tiffany Bellanger, Camille Eicher, Fabien Garces, Amélie Cresson, Cosette Grandvalet, Hervé Alexandre, Stéphanie Weidmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExternal stress conditions can induce disturbances in the internal environment of cells, sufficient for protein misfolding or membrane disorganization. To cope with this phenomenon, cells have developed several strategies, including the production of heat stress proteins (HSPs). These include small heat shock proteins (sHSPs). sHSPs share a characteristic three-dimensional architecture, with a central α-crystalline domain (ACD) flanked by two terminal domains. This structural organization enables them to form a variety of oligomeric complexes, giving these proteins their function. As large oligomers, all sHSPs act as chaperones. Conversely, some of them adopt a lipochaperone role when present in dimeric form. While the role of the ACD domain in oligomerization and the mechanism of action of sHSPs has been studied extensively, the functions of the terminal domains, in particular the N-terminal domain, remain poorly explored. In this context, the role of the N-terminal domain of sHSP Lo18, produced by the bacteria Oenococcus oeni, was investigated. To assess its importance, a truncated protein, depleted of the first 27 amino acids, was compared with the wild-type protein. Analyses focused on the oligomeric structure, the ability to prevent protein aggregation, and the ability to maintain membrane fluidity. In parallel, the involvement of the N-terminal domain in the interaction between Lo18 and membranes was examined by RP-HPLC. The results reveal that Lo18’s N-terminal domain plays a decisive role, both in the formation and stabilization of oligomeric structures, and in interaction with protein and lipid substrates, essential parameters in chaperone and lipochaperone activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33004-8.Keywords: Small heat shock protein, Protein–membrane interaction, Molecular chaperon, Lipochaperone, Structure–functionSubject terms: Chaperones, Biochemistry, Proteins, Chaperones, Protein folding

Introduction

To maintain protein homeostasis, cells produce stress proteins, notably heat shock proteins (HSPs). Universally present in living organisms1–6, these proteins act as molecular chaperones by sequestering and repelling denatured proteins in an ATP-dependent or independent manner7–10. Within this superfamily, small heat shock proteins (sHSP) represent the smallest class, with a mass of less than 35 kDa.

All sHSPs share a common three-dimensional structure11,12 consisting of an α-crystalline domain (ACD) and two terminal domains. The ACD is composed of around 90 amino acid residues forming seven or eight antiparallel β-strands, which arrange to form the characteristic immunoglobulin (IgG)-like sandwich structure3,4,13–15. The C-terminal domain contains a highly conserved I/L-X-I/L motif, essential for the oligomeric assembly of sHSPs. This motif specifically interacts with the β4 and β8 ACD leaflets of a neighboring dimer, promoting oligomer formation11,16,17. Finally, the N-terminal domain, although little conserved in terms of sequence, is thought to play a role in stabilizing oligomeric structures. It could interact with the hydrophobic groove formed by the junction of the β2 and β7 sheets of two adjacent monomers15,18,19.

Due to their three-dimensional structure, sHSPs are able to form complex oligomeric structures, ranging from dimers to large oligomers (over 40 subunits). This oligomeric organization, combined with the dynamic association between dimers forming more complex structures, confers a diversity of biological functions on sHSPs. As large oligomers, all sHSPs act as ATP-independent chaperones20. By contrast, in dimeric form, some of them adopt a lipochaperone role, contributing in particular to the stabilization of membranes by limiting their fluidification21–26.

While the role of the ACD and its three-dimensional structure has been studied extensively, allowing understanding of the mechanisms of action of sHSPs, few research has focused on the terminal domains and in particular the N-terminal domain, especially on prokaryotic sHSPs11,27–34. Indeed, if the N-terminal domain of so-called “metazoan” sHSPs, derived from eukaryotic cells, is described in the literature for its impact on substrate interaction and oligomeric stabilization, it remains largely unknown on so-called “bacterial” sHSPs, derived from prokaryotes or viruses. It should be noted that the major difference between these two groups lies precisely in this N-terminal domain, which is generally shorter in “bacterial” sHSPs4,17,28,35,36.

In this context, the role of the N-terminal domain in the functions of the sHSP Lo18, produced by the lactic acid bacterium Oenococcus oeni, was investigated To assess its involvement of Lo18’s oligomeric structure, chaperon and lipochaperon activity, a deletion of the first 27 amino acids of Lo18 was performed (Fig. 1)15,37,38. This deletion corresponds to a truncated form frequently observed in vitro and in vivo in sHSPs, notably in the Lo18 protein22,31,39–41.

Results

Deletion of the N-terminal domain results in loss of structural oligomer stability of Lo18

The importance of the N-terminal domain in Lo18 oligomerization was highlighted by immunostaining after formaldehyde cross-linking treatment in a heterologous E. coli system to observe the small sub-units and DLS measurement for the higher oligomers. The oligomeric profiles of partial N-terminal domain-deleted protein (Lo18Δ27) were compared with those of the wild-type protein (Fig. 2).

Oligomeric profils of Lo18 and Lo18Δ27. (A) In vitro cross-linking interaction reveals oligomeric structure of Lo18 WT or Lo18Δ27. Immunolabelling of Lo18 after in vitro cross-linking. The pentagon-shaped numbers from 1 to 12 indicate the number of sub-units on the oligomeric structure. The bands visible in the bottom inset correspond to those in the top inset, but with an accentuated contrast. (B) Multi-Angle Dynamic Light Scattering of 20µM of Lo18 (black) and Lo18Δ27 (blue).

The analysis revealed a marked alteration in the oligomeric structure of Lo18Δ27 compared with the native form. From the immunostaining assay (Fig. 2A), this quaternary structure modification can be observed through the higher number of bands detected for Lo18Δ27. Most of these additional bands corresponded to incomplete oligomeric forms, with apparent masses of around 22 kDa, 43 kDa and 45 kDa, suggesting partial dimeric and trimeric organization, respectively. A significant increase of background was observed solely during immunolabeling, as distinct from the electrophoresis gel (Figure S1), in the wells corresponding to Lo18Δ27. This background, specific to the truncated protein, seemed to reflect its high oligomeric instability. The distribution of oligomeric structures also differed between the two forms of the protein. Moreover, Lo18Δ27 predominantly formed small structures (monomer and dimer), which seems less present in the wild-type profile. Moreover, Lo18Δ27 predominantly formed small structures (monomer and dimer), which seems less present in the wild-type profile.

From the DLS assay (Fig. 2B), particle sizes corresponding to the different oligomeric structures of Lo18 were compared to those of the protein partially deleted at the N-terminal domain. For the native protein, most of the peaks observed correspond to small oligomeric structures, similar to dimers or their higher assemblies (tetramers, octamers, 16-mers, and 48-mers). In contrast, very large oligomers (> 90 subunits) are detected for the protein partially deleted from its N-terminal domain. It is important to note that their large size induces significant light scattering, which can 1) overestimate the quantity of these larges subunits and 2) mask the detection of smaller subunits observed by immunolabeling but absent in DLS. Similarly, the peaks corresponding to decamers, 30-mers, and 48-mers, similar to those of the native protein, show a strong signal despite the masking phenomenon caused by the large oligomers that overwhelm these peaks. This suggests that these structures are particularly abundant in the protein deleted from its N-terminal domain, which does not seem to be the case for the native protein where the intensity of the peaks and the sizes of the subunits appear to be more balanced.

Essential role of the N-terminal domain in Lo18 functions

Contribution to the chaperone activity

The impact of deletion of the first 27 amino acids of the Lo18 protein (Lo18Δ27) on its chaperone activity was assessed by measuring the thermostability of an O. oeni protein lysate. To do so, 3.5 mg/mL of lysate was incubated with 10 µM of either native Lo18 or the truncated Lo18Δ27 protein, and then subjected to a 30-min heat stress at 55 °C. The rate of protein aggregation in the lysate after the heat stress is an indicator of chaperone activity: the more proteins aggregate, the less they have been protected, and therefore the weaker the chaperone activity. In the presence of Lo18, the proportion of aggregated protein remains unchanged, increasing from 100% non-aggregated protein to 104.7% after heat stress. Conversely, a significant increase in aggregated proteins of around 21.3% and 25.5% respectively is observed for the negative control or in the presence of truncated proteins. These results indicate that the deletion of the first 27 amino acids inhibits the chaperone activity of Lo18 compared with the entire protein (Fig. 3).

Thermostability of crude extract proteins from O. oeni. Three point five mg/mL of protein from crude extract was incubated without sHSP protein (Control −) or with 10µM of Lo18 or Lo18Δ27. The protein concentration of the cell lysate was initialy measured and fixed at 100% (red dotted line, red dots). The amount of unaggregated protein was measured after heat treatment (30 min at 55°C) and compared in percentage with the initial value (black bar, grey dots). The data represents the means and SD of three independent experiments.

Implication in membrane interaction and stability

Putative site of interaction with the membrane

The protein domains involved in membrane interaction were identified using an enzymatic digestion approach followed by chromatographic analysis. The Lo18 protein was digested with trypsin, either in the presence of liposomes from O. oeni or in their absence. Trypsin is a proteolytic enzyme that cuts polypeptide chains at the arginine and lysine levels. The polypeptide fragments generated by this digestion were then separated by reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), according to their hydrophobicity. By comparing the profile peaks of the chromatograms obtained for the different controls (trypsin alone, Lo18 alone, and liposomes alone) with that of trypsin-digested Lo18, it was possible to identify 19 peaks corresponding to 21 expected peptide fragments, two fragments being co-eluted (Fig. 4A).

Interaction between Lo18 protein and O. oeni membrane. (A) Chromatogram of the digestion of Lo18 alone (blue) and Lo18 in the presence of liposomes from an O. oeni culture (purple), after treatment with trypsin. Identified peptide fragments have been annotated. Red arrows indicate peaks absent in the liposome-containing condition. (B) 3D modeling of the Lo18 protein made using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). Segments in red correspond to peptide fragments indicated by arrows on the chromatogram. Representative chromatogram of the three equivalent replicates.

The presence of these 19 peaks was then verified in the condition where Lo18 was digested in the presence of liposomes. It is primarily noteworthy that the intensity of all the peaks are reduced in the presence of liposomes, suggesting partial protection of the lipid membrane on Lo18. In addition, three peaks appear to be lacking in this condition. The absence of certain peaks indicates that the polypeptides were coprecipitated with the liposomes during the centrifugation steps, suggesting then an interaction between these peptides and the O. oeni membrane. Of the 19 fragments, three were no longer detectable in the presence of liposomes: peptides IELPGLDK, IDYSNDNLTVSGLSSK, and NDGLMDVSDMMGNLMNNFFGPR, eluted at 8.59 min, 10.3 min and 10.81 min respectively. These fragments corresponded to the regions between β3 and β4 sheets, the β4 and β5 sheets, as well as a large portion of the N-terminal domain from residue N9 to residue R30 (Fig. 4B). Consequently, the N-terminal domain, like certain domains of ACD, appears to be involved in the Lo18/membrane interaction/insertion.

N-terminal domain contributing to lipochaperone activity

RP-HPLC results highlighted an interaction between the N-terminal domain and the O. oeni membrane. Therefore, it seems reasonable to assume that this domain may play an essential role in maintaining membrane fluidity. The impact of the deletion of the first 27 amino acids of Lo18 on its lipochaperone activity was assessed by monitoring the membrane fluidity of liposomes, from purified lipids of an O. oeni culture, using fluorescence anisotropy. Fluorescence anisotropy is inversely proportional to membrane fluidity: the lower the value, the more fluid the membrane, and vice versa. These liposomes, prepared from lipids extracted from a culture of O. oeni, were placed in the presence or absence of Lo18 or Lo18Δ27, then subjected to heat shock at 45°C (Fig. 5). Fluorescence anisotropy measurements were carried out using the DPH fluorescent probe. The absence of quenching of the DPH probe by Lo18 was experimentally verified (Figure S2). Three experimental phases were defined to monitor the interaction between the protein and liposomes. An equilibration phase, during which the reaction mixture contained only liposomes and the DPH probe in buffer. A second phase corresponding to the addition of the protein, to monitor its possible insertion into the membranes. Then a third phase, during which thermal stress at 45°C was applied to assess the protein’s effect on membrane fluidity.

Measurement of the fluorescence anisotropy of DPH inserted into O. oeni liposomes during a thermal shock at 45 °C, alone (pink diamond) or in the presence of Lo18 (black circle) or Lo18Δ27 (blue square). For each curve, three phases were defined: the equilibration phase (before 0 min), in which the reaction mixture consists of liposomes and the DPH probe in buffer, the addition of sHSPs (represented by a solid arrow) and the application of heat shock at 45 °C (dotted arrow). The data represent the means and SDs of three independent experiments.

During the first phase, initial fluorescence anisotropy was similar under all conditions, with a value of around 0.165. The addition of Lo18 or Lo18Δ27 proteins resulted in an increase in fluorescence anisotropy of 42% (from 0.164 to 0.234) and 11% (from 0.167 to 0.186) respectively. These results suggest that the presence of the proteins rigidifies the liposomes, although this ability is strongly reduced in the case of the truncated protein, indicating altered membrane insertion. Upon heat shock at 45°C, rapid fluidization of liposomes was observed under all conditions, with a decrease in fluorescence anisotropy of 4%, 22% and 43% (from 0.167 to 0.157 in the presence of Lo18, from 0.167 to 0.130 with Lo18Δ27, and from 0.165 to 0.094 without protein, respectively). These data show that the deletion of the first 27 amino acids of Lo18 resulted in a significant loss of its lipochaperone activity, without, however, abolishing it completely. In summary, the partial deletion of Lo18’s N-terminal domain reduced both its ability to insert into lipid membranes and its lipochaperone activity.

Discussion

To cope with environmental stresses, organisms have developed various strategies, including the production of heat stress proteins. Among these, small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) play an important role by acting as chaperones20. Some of them also act as lipochaperones, helping to stabilize cell membranes under stressful conditions28,42,43. A growing number of studies agree that the activities of sHSPs are closely linked to their three-dimensional structure and oligomeric organization. While the role of the central α-crystallin domain is well documented13,15,24, the terminal domains, in particular the N-terminal domain, remain poorly explored, particularly in sHSPs of microbial origin11,27–29. In this context, the involvement of the N-terminal domain in the chaperone and lipochaperone functions of the Lo18 protein, produced by O. oeni, was investigated.

To do this, the first 27 amino acids of the protein were deleted. This truncation strongly altered the oligomeric organization of the Lo18 protein, as suggest on immunostaining profiles and DLS measurement. Compared with the native form, the truncated form (Lo18Δ27) displays a higher proportion of monomers and dimers and the capability to form very high subunits oligomers (> 90). This huge dispersity of oligomeric structures could reflect a difficulty to form and stabilize oligomer. Moreover, for the similar statuses of oligomer between the truncated protein and the native protein a shift of some subunits is often observed (8 to 10 and 16 to 36 respectively for the native and the truncated protein).. Similar results have been observed by fluorescence spectroscopy and chromatography for proteins truncated at their N-terminal domain, including HSP16.3 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis44 and HSPB5 from humans45, respectively. For HSP16.3, the mutation induced an accumulation of dimers and trimers, as compared to the 9-mer classically found for the native protein. For the HSPB5 protein, as for HSP16.3, a significant increase in monomeric forms has also been reported44–47. This increased prevalence of small oligomeric structures could indicate an interaction defect between subunits, promoting the formation of monomers or small oligomers (dimer, trimer). This defect could also be explain by the loss of stability of multimeric forms, as shown by the presence of smears and additional bands in Fig. 2 or the presence of large oligomer which can be related to aggregates. However, it is important to note, that although reduced, inter-subunit interactions are not completely abolished and similar to both protein. Taken together, results suggest that the N-terminal domain of sHSPs plays a major role in the formation of oligomers and potentially their structure favoring structural reorganization. This is strengthened by crystallographic work on HSP16.9 in wheat, HSP20 and HSPB1 in humans, which showed that, during the association and dissociation cycles of oligomeric structures, the highly flexible N-terminal domain can interact and bind with the grooves formed by the β-sheets present at the ACD35 the β7 sheet (present on its monomer or on a neighboring subunit) and stabilize the structure of the β2 sheet35,48,49. Both these sheets are directly involved in dimer formation, in so-called metazoan (β7) and bacterial (β2) sHSPs, respectively4,17,35.

If the role of the N-terminal domain in the formation of oligomeric structures seems undeniable, how about its contribution to Lo18’s chaperone and lipochaperone functions?

Chaperone analysis of Lo18 revealed a significant loss of function in the absence of its N-terminal domain. Indeed, the ability of Lo18Δ27 to limit cellular protein aggregation after heat stress is significantly decreased compared to the native protein. This result suggests that the N-terminal domain contributes to the efficiency of the chaperone mechanism, possibly through two complementary mechanisms: on the one hand, by promoting the formation of oligomers required for chaperone activity as previously discussed by22, and on the other hand, by participating directly in the substrate binding via transient interactions50. Indeed, during oligomer association, the N-terminal domain of Lo18, rich in hydrophobic residues, could interact directly with peptide substrates (supplementary data S3). This interaction mechanism has been demonstrated in several sHSPs, including HSP16.5 from Methanococcus jannaschii34, HSP16.3 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis44, HSP21 from humans49, HSP16.2 from Caenorhabditis elegans44, HSP18.1 in pea, HSP16.9 in wheat30, as well as in the sHSPs of Bradyrhizobium japonicum44.

Deletion of the N-terminal domain significantly affects the interaction of the Lo18 protein with the membrane and its ability to maintain membrane stability. To assess the role of this domain, chromatographic (RP-HPLC) and fluorescence anisotropy analyses were carried out. Both approaches showed that the N-terminal domain plays a role in recognizing and interacting with the lipid substrate. Indeed, among the three fragments found by RP-HPLC to interact with the membrane, the peptide NDGLMDVSDMMGNLMNNFFGPR corresponded to a large part of the N-terminal domain from residue N9 to R30.

In addition, fluorescence anisotropy results showed that the truncated Lo18Δ27 protein, lacking almost the entire N9-R30 segment, exhibited a three-fold reduction in membrane insertion capacity compared with the native protein. These results reinforce the hypothesis that the N-terminal domain facilitates Lo18’s interaction with the membrane, presumably by promoting recognition of the lipid substrate. This loss of affinity could be explained by both structural and physico-chemical alterations induced by the deletion. Indeed, the N-terminal domain displays a high degree of hydrophobicity, particularly in the M13-N23 region (Figure S3), leading to the establishment of Van der Waals-type interactions with membrane fatty acids51. Previous work on the lipochaperone activity of Lo18 has also shown that the protein’s capacity for insertion is highly dependent on membrane composition, further underlining the importance of hydrophobic interactions in this process26. Thus, partial loss of the N-terminal domain could alter these hydrophobic forces and significantly compromise membrane anchoring.

As regards lipochaperone activity itself, i.e. the ability to limit membrane fluidization, the truncated protein was also less effective. After 15 min at 45°C, membrane fluidity reached 78% of its initial value in the presence of Lo18Δ27, compared with just 96% with the native protein. While hydrophobic interactions play a central role in this function, electrostatic interactions also contribute to Lo18’s lipochaperone activity26. Nevertheless, as the electrostatic profile was only moderately modified between Lo18Δ27 and Lo18, it seems more realistic to conclude that the decrease in lipochaperone activity is linked to the loss of interaction between the protein and the membrane caused by the absence of the N-terminal domain. This hypothesis is supported by work on Synechocystis HSP17, in which the substitution of residues in the N-terminal domain, replacing a glutamine and a leucine by an arginine and a proline, enhanced the protein’s membrane insertion and lipochaperone activity52,53. These mutations did not alter the electrostatic profile, but locally increased hydrophobicity, suggesting once again that this property is crucial to the interaction of sHSPs with membranes.

In light of these results, it appears that the N-terminal domain of the Lo18 protein plays an essential role, both in the formation and stabilization of high oligomeric structures, and in interaction with substrates, whether protein or lipid. Both parameters are crucial to the establishment of the protein’s chaperone and lipochaperone functions.

Materials and methods

Media growth conditions

E. coli cells were grown at 37°C, 150 rpm, in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium broth, pH 7, supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin (Sigma, Saint-Louis, US). O. oeni ATCC BAA-1163 was grown at 28 ◦C and at pH 5.3 in FT80 medium modified by the addition of meat extract instead of casamino acids54.

Strain and plasmid construction

The Lo18 protein truncated from its first 27 amino acids (Lo18 Δ27) was obtained by amplification of the hsp18 gene as follows. Genomic DNA was extracted from a culture of the O. oeni strain ATCC BAA-1163 using the NucleoSpin Microbial DNA kit (Macherey–Nagel, Germany). The hsp18 gene was then amplified by PCR using the Expand High Fidelity PCR System (Roche, Meylan, France) and two specific primers: Lo18dNter27_histag (NdeI): 5’- GCACAGCATATGGGACCACGCGATGGCTTG -3’ (forward) and Lo18C-HindIII 5′-TTGGCTAAGCTTTTATTGGATTTCAATATGATGAGTTTGACTTTCG-3′ (reverse). These primers were designed to amplify from the 27th codon to the stop codon of the hsp18 open reading frame. The PCR product was purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean‐up kit (Macherey–Nagel, Germany), and the amplified DNA fragment was cloned between the NdeI and HindIII sites of the pET28a expression vector (Invitrogen, USA). In this way, the hsp18 gene is under the control of the inducible T7 promoter and an N-terminal His•Tag® sequence is added to the protein. Cloning is performed using T4 DNA ligase (ThermoScientific, USA). E. coli DH5α was used as a host strain for cloning and transformed by electroporation55. Positive clones were selected on LB plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. Plasmids were then extracted using the NucleoSpin Plasmid kit (Macherey–Nagel, Germany) and the hsp18 sequence was verified by Sanger sequencing using T7 primers (5’-TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG-3’ and 5’-GCTAGTTATTGCTCAGCGG-3’). E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells were transformed with the resulting vectors according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, US). Briefly, the resulting vectors were introduced into competent E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells following a 2-min heat shock at 42°C. Positive clones were selected on LB plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. All the kits and enzymes were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Protein expression and purification

Lo18 and Lo18Δ27 were produced as described by Bellanger and collaborators25. Briefly, protein synthesis was induced by adding 50 μM IPTG (isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside) and Lo18 his-tagged was purified by affinity chromatography using a HisTrap HP column, 1mL (Cytiva, US).

Protein modeling

The tertiary structure of the native and modified protein was generated using AlphFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) and then analyzed on PyMol.

Structure analysis by cross-linking

The oligomeric statuses of small subunits of Lo18Δ27 were analyzed by cross-linking experiments in vivo as described by Bellanger and collaborators25. To do this, cellular extracts were treated with formaldehyde, a bridging agent, to a final concentration of 1% (v/v). Five µg of cross-linked samples was separated on to 12% SDS-PAGE gel before Western blot analysis using anti-Lo18 antibodies.

Structure analysis by DLS

The oligomeric statuses of large subunits of Lo18Δ27 were analyzed by DLS. To do this, purified protein of Lo18 and Lo18 Δ27 were diluted at 0.5 mg/mL on phosphate buffer pH 7. Six hundred microliter of purified protein were fill in DTS1070 cell (Malvern Panalytical, Belgium) and analyzed with Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical, Belgium) at 25°C with an equilibration time of 120s. Each measurement was made in triplicate.

Assay for thermostability

Cellular lysate from O. oeni cell cultures in exponential phase, was prepared as described above. In order to test the thermostability of O. oeni cellular proteins, the total protein concentration of the lysate was measured by the qubit assay (Qubit, France). Then 3.5 mg/mL of cellular lysate was incubated without or with 10µM of Lo18 or Lo18Δ27. To measure protein denaturation, lysates were heated at 55 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 12 000 g for 10 min at room temperature. The protein concentration of the cell lysate was initialy measured and fixed at 100%. The amount of unaggregated protein was measured after heat treatment and compared in percentage with the initial value. Each measurement was made in triplicate.

Lipochaperone activity

Lipochaperone activity was assayed by measuring the membrane fluidity of O. oeni liposomes in the presence or absence of Lo18 or Lo18Δ27. Liposomes were produced as described by Bellanger and collaborators25. Then membrane fluidity was quantified by fluorescence anisotropy measurement using a Fluorolog 3 spectrofluorimeter (FLUOROLOG-3, Jobin Yvon Inc, USA). The anisotropy values (inversely proportional to the membrane fluidity) were calculated according to56. Excitation and emission wavelengths were 360 nm and 431 nm, respectively. More specifically, the measurement was performed for 25 min (with a determination every 10 s) in a 3 mL quartz cuvette filled with 250 μL of liposomes prepared as described above after inserting the 1,6-diphenyl-1,3,5-hexatriene (DPH) probe at 1µM (Sigma Aldrich, Saint-Louis, US). During these thirty minutes, three distinct stages were monitored. The first was a 5-min equilibration phase at 20°C. This was followed by a second 5-min step at 20°C, during which 10 µM of protein and 3 mL of phosphate buffer were added to the reaction mixture. Finally, a final 15-min phase at 45°C corresponding to a heat shock applied to the liposome suspension was monitored. Each experiment was done in triplicate.

Interaction protein/membrane

To observe interactions between peptide fragments of Lo18 and the O. oeni membrane, 5 mg/mL of purified Lo18 was incubated with/without 250 µL/mL of liposomes prepared as above and 5mg/mL trypsin (pancreatic) for 2 h at 37°C. The resulting protein solutions were then centrifuged for 1 h at 10,000 g, and the supernatant was recovered. Membrane-bound and unbound Lo18 fragments were analyzed by reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), consisting of a quaternary pump (Reference Agilent G7111B), a Diode Array Detector (G7115A), and an autosampler (G7129A). Data was collected and analyzed using OpenLab CDS software (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Separation was performed on an XBridge BEH C18 column (130Å, 5 µm, 4.6 mm × 100 mm; Waters Company, Milford, MA, USA), using a gradient from 0 to 100% solvent B in solvent A from 0 to 35 min; then from 100 to 0% solvent B in solvent A from 35 to 38 min; and 100% solvent A from 38 to 40 min (solvent A: 0.1% (v/v )trifluoracetic acid (TFA) in H₂O; solvent B: 0.1% (v/v) TFA in acetonitrile). The flow rate and column temperature were set at 1 mL/min and 35°C, respectively. The injection volume was set at 10 µL. UV detection was applied at 2 different wavelengths (214 nm and 280 nm). This analysis was also performed individually on trypsin, purified Lo18, and liposomes to serve as controls. Each analysis was performed on 3 independent repetitions.

Statistical analysis

At least three independent measurements were performed for each of the conditions tested on fluorescence anisotropy and thermostability. Statistical analyses were performed by the statistical RStudio software (version 1.2.5033). The normality of the distribution and homogeneity of the variances of each condition were tested by the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Bartlett test, respectively. Then, a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test and Wilcoxon test were used to compare the samples with a significance level of α = 0.05. All the statistical tests were considered significant at a p-value < 0.05.
