Enhancing Vehicle Platooning Safety via Control Node Placement and Sizing under State and Input Bounds
Yifei She, Shen Wang, Ahmad Taha, Xiaofeng Tao

Abstract
Vehicle platooning with Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control improves traffic efficiency, reduces energy consumption, and enhances safety but remains vulnerable to cyber-attacks that disrupt communication and cause unsafe actions. To address these risks, this paper investigates control node placement and input bound optimization to balance safety and defense efficiency under various conditions. We propose a two-stage actuator placement and actuator saturation approach, which focuses on identifying key actuators that maximize the system’s controllability while operating under state and input constraints. By strategically placing and limiting the input bounds of critical actuators, we ensure that vehicles maintain safe distances even under attack. Simulation results show that our method effectively mitigates the impact of attacks while preserving defense efficiency, offering a robust solution to vehicle platooning safety challenges.



I
Introduction and Paper Contributions

In modern transportation systems, vehicle platooning has emerged as a promising technology to improve traffic efficiency, reduce energy consumption, and ensure road safety
[
1
]
. Utilizing advanced control systems such as Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC)
[
2
]
, vehicles can maintain safe distances and speeds; see Fig.
1
for a typical platooning system with
n
vehicles. However, disruptions from attackers can lead to severe accidents, causing significant damage to life and property. As a result, vehicle platooning is a safety-critical issue that requires continuous effort to maintain its reliability and safety
[
3
]
.

A key challenge in vehicle platooning is ensuring that system states impacted by throttle and brake actuators remain within safe limits under different conditions. These actuators control the engine or motor to regulate speed, enabling the vehicle to maintain consistent acceleration or deceleration. However, attackers can exploit vulnerabilities in the CACC system. For example, a man-in-the-middle attack
[
4
]
or a jamming attack
[
5
]
could intercept and manipulate vehicle communication and alter critical data, leading to improper actions such as sudden braking or acceleration. This can disrupt the platooning system’s reachable set and push it into unsafe states; see Fig.
1
for illustrations. This issue is studied through control theory as a reachability problem, assessing whether the system’s reachable set intersects predefined danger zones
[
6
,
7
]
.

While attacks can severely impact a platoon, defense strategies can mitigate these risks. For instance, the authors in
[
7
,
8
]
propose redesigning actuator bounds to limit attackers’ capacity through actuator saturation, as physical constraints prevent actuators from injecting excessive energy into the system. Although this method enhances safety, it reduces the system’s defense efficiency by restricting all actuators, limiting its ability to maintain safety with minimal resource use.

Instead of restricting all actuators’ bounds, focusing on selecting a subset of critical actuators with the most significant impact on the system’s controllability as defensive positions is more effective. Limiting only these actuators’ bounds allows the system’s safety and defense efficiency to be maintained and balanced, even in the worst-case scenario where all actuators are compromised. If only some actuators are attacked, this defense strategy offers even greater efficiency, as only the key actuators need to be managed. Therefore, the primary focus of this paper is determining the optimal number, placement, and input bounds for these critical actuators to ensure vehicle platooning safety.

II-B

System Model Representation and Dangerous Set

We introduce the differences between actual and desired distances and velocities as new variables, that are
\Delta d_{i}=d_{i}-d_{i}^{*}
and
\Delta v_{i}=v_{i}-v_{i}^{*}
, and let the state variable
\boldsymbol{x}(k)=[\Delta d_{1}(k),\cdots,\Delta d_{n-1}(k),\Delta v_{1}(k),\cdots,\Delta v_{n}(k)]^{\top}
, and the secondary control input variable
\boldsymbol{u}(k)=[u_{1}(k),\cdots,u_{m}(k)]^{\top}
. Thus, we have a discrete-time Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) system featuring individually bounded control inputs, described by

\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}(k)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}(k),

(2)

where
k\in\mathbb{N}
;
{\boldsymbol{x}}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}
denotes the state;
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n-1)\times(2n-1)}
is the state matrix;
{\boldsymbol{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n-1)\times m}
represents the
m
inputs, and note that the none-zeros in
{\boldsymbol{B}}
have two meanings:
(i)
the location of actuators and
(ii)
the amplification factor of the system input
\boldsymbol{u}
;
\boldsymbol{u}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
stands for symmetrically bounded control input such that

-\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}=\underline{u}_{i}\leq u_{i}(k)\leq\bar{u}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma_{i}},\quad i=1,\dots,m,

(3)

where
\gamma_{i}>0
is a constant determining the bound for each control input, and
u_{i}(k)>0
(
u_{i}(k)<0
) indicates the vehicle acceleration (deceleration). The actuator bounds are collected in the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}:=\diag(\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}}).

In summary, the reachable set
\mathcal{R}
of LTI system
(
2
)
from the initial state
\boldsymbol{x}(0)
with actuator locations and corresponding bounds are

{\mathcal{R}}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\left\lvert\,\begin{array}[]{l}\hskip-6.00006pt\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}(k)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}(k)\\
{\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{0},u_{i}^{2}(k)\leq{\gamma}_{i}}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

(4)

We define the dangerous set
\mathcal{D}
as

\mathcal{D}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \cup_{i=1}^{\kappa}\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b}_{i}\},

(5)

where each
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\geq{\boldsymbol{b}}_{i}
represents a half-space. Any vector in
\mathcal{D}
signifies the failure to maintain safe distances and speeds, necessitating the redesign of actuator bounds to prevent such a situation.

The actuator locations and bounds are pivotal factors affecting the system’s reachable set
\mathcal{R}
, ultimately determining the system’s ability to avoid entering a dangerous state
\mathcal{D}
. We can select a proper number of actuators and redesign the corresponding bounds to ensure
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{D}=\emptyset
. Through the optimization of the actuator locations and input bounds, it becomes possible to enhance the safety of the vehicle platooning system while selecting minimum numbers of actuators and maintaining defense efficiency.

II-C

System Model Transformation

From system model (
2
), we can see that both actuator locations and bounds significantly impact the final reachable set. That is, both input matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
and control input
\boldsymbol{u}
in (
2
) induce reachable set
\mathcal{R}
, but in a coupled way, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}
.

We assume the sets
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{S}
contain all possible locations and selected actuator locations. It is clear that
|\mathcal{S}|\leq|\mathcal{V}|=m
. We decouple their impact on system states by assuming the input matrix is only composed of zeros or ones, i.e., only encodes the actuator locations (denoted as
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
), and thus the actuator bounds are taken care of by control input
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{S}}
. That is,
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n-1)\times m}
is a matrix such that if the actuator location is selected in
\mathcal{S}
, the corresponding entry of
\boldsymbol{B}
is one; otherwise it is zero. Similarly, each entry in
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{S}}
equals the corresponding entry in
{\boldsymbol{u}}
multiplied by a coefficient from the original
\boldsymbol{B}
. That is,
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{E})\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\boldsymbol{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
stores the coefficients. In the end,

\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}(k)+\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{S}}(k),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
only encodes actuator locations and

only encodes actuator bounds as claimed. If all actuator locations are selected (i,e.,

II-D

Actuator Saturation and Actuator Placement Problem

The Actuator Saturation and Actuator Placement (
ASAP
) defense strategy aims to enhance the safety and defense efficiency of vehicle platooning by
(i)
selecting a small fraction of the actuators maximizing the controllability and
(ii)
redesigning their bounds. Mathematically, the defense strategy can be formulated as an optimization problem

\displaystyle\text{{ASAP:}}\;\;\;\underset{\mathcal{S},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{\text{min}}

\displaystyle\quad g(\mathcal{S},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})

(8a)

s. t.

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V},|\mathcal{S}|\leq m,

(8b)

\displaystyle\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\geq\boldsymbol{\Gamma},

(8c)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}\cap\mathcal{D}=\emptyset

(8d)

where the constant
m
is the number of actuator locations (i.e., system inputs) to defense in a vehicle platooning system set up by system operators due to defense cost, the newly designed actuator bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
should be smaller than the original
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
(i.e., constraint (
8c
) holds) to ensure the safety (i.e., constraint (
8d
)). The objective function
g(\mathcal{S},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})
is a function of actuator set
\mathcal{S}
and the corresponding bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
to be redesigned, and we choose it as the maximization of controllability of the LTI system representation of (
6
).

This problem is a set-function optimization problem that involves both discrete and continuous variables. The selection of the actuator set
\mathcal{S}
is a discrete variable, representing a binary decision for each potential actuator location, i.e., whether an actuator is selected to defend against attack. Meanwhile, the actuator bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
are continuous variables, representing the continuous redesign of the bounds for the chosen actuators. Combining these discrete and continuous variables creates a vast search space due to the exponential growth with respect to
\mathcal{S}
. This combination significantly increases the computational complexity, as the solver must explore both binary set selections and continuous permutations.

It is challenging to solve the
ASAP
problem directly because of the undefined mathematical representation of reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
. To directly overcome the difficulty of describing a reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
, we can choose an approximate method to formulate a reachable set, greatly reducing the computation cost and obtaining a feasible solution.

III
Enhance Safety of Vehicle Platooning by a two-stage ASAP algorithm

In this section, we propose a two-stage algorithm to solve the
ASAP
problem (
8
). Initially, we involve the optimal actuator locations in determining the approximate shape of the reachable set for the defense strategy. Then, by solving an optimization problem, we determine the input bounds and scale the sizes of the reachable set to avoid intersecting with dangerous states. Thereby, we can ensure the safety of the vehicle platooning system. Finally, we present the final algorithm to solve the
ASAP
problem.

(ii)

III-B

Actuator Placement to Determine the Defense Locations

The actuator placement problem
[
15
]
aims to actuate a small fraction of states of (
6
) to optimize system performances. In the vehicle platooning system, it selects the optimal defense locations to maximize the vehicle platooning system’s controllability to mitigate attacks. That is, if we do not consider the actuation bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
or equivalently consider the actuation bounds as unit-energy inputs, and the objective function
g(\mathcal{S},\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})
turns into
g(\mathcal{S};\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})
. Therefore, the actuator placement problem can be written as

\text{{AP:}}\;\;\underset{\mathcal{S}}{\text{min}}\;\;g(\mathcal{S};\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})\;\;\text{s. t.}\;\;\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V},|\mathcal{S}|\leq m,

(9)

where the system performance
g(\mathcal{S};\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})
is a set function defined as
\log\det(\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1})
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{c}
is the controllability Gramian
[
19
]
of (
6
), that is
\boldsymbol{W}_{c}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}^{i}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})^{i}
.

Inspired by the fact for a controllable LTI system with unit-energy inputs, the reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
can be encapsulated by an ellipsoid
\{\boldsymbol{x}|\ \boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\alpha\}

[
19
]
. Note that a standard ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha)
is defined as
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha):=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\alpha\right\}
, where
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\succ{0}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are the shape matrix and a positive constant of an ellipsoid. When
\alpha=1
, we omit writing it out explicitly, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},1)=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P})
. Besides that, the objective function
g(\mathcal{S};\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}})=\log\det(\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1})
has some special properties that we can take advantage of, and we give a conjecture directly as follows without proof.

Conjecture 1
(Supermodularity of
\log\det(\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1})

[
15
]
)

Consider the discrete-time LTI system (
6
) with system matrices
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}})
and individually-bounded control inputs
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mathcal{S}}
, the function
\log\det(\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1})
of the minimum-volume ellipsoid
V(\mathcal{E}_{min})
which encapsulates the entire reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
is supermodular with respect to the choice of
\mathcal{S}
.

We note that a set function
g(\cdot)
is supermodular if and only if
g(\mathcal{S}\cup\{e\})-g(\mathcal{S})\leq g(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cup\{e\})-g(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
for any subsets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
\{e\}\in\mathcal{V}\backslash\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

[
20
,
15
]
. A set function
g(\cdot)
is submodular if
-g(\cdot)
is supermodular. Intuitively, submodularity is a diminishing returns property where adding an element
e
to a smaller set gives a larger gain than adding one to a larger set
[
21
,
20
]
.

The supermodularity framework in set function optimization allows greedy algorithms, which are computationally efficient and perform well despite being suboptimal. Notably, the set function
g(\mathcal{S})
is supermodular
[
15
]
, making it suitable for a classic greedy algorithm that can solve the NP-hard problem
AP
and yield a solution
\mathcal{S}
with at least 63% of the optimal value
g(\mathcal{S}^{*})

[
22
]
. Empirical studies
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
20
]
suggest that greedy algorithms can often achieve near-optimal solutions, not just 63% of the optimal.

III-C

Actuator Saturation to Redesign Bounds

With the optimal defense location
\mathcal{S}^{*}
for the
AP
problem fixed in
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\mathcal{S}}
, the reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
defines the distances and velocities the vehicle platooning system can reach. This section addresses the (
AS
) problem
[
8
,
7
,
9
]
, aiming to prevent collisions or excessive speeds—represented by the dangerous set
\mathcal{D}
—by redesigning the input bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
. Specifically, we frame the
AS
problem as an optimization task to minimize the trace of the new bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
while ensuring the redesigned ellipsoid for the reachable set does not intersect with
\mathcal{D}
.

Note that although the true reachable set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
of (
6
) is cumbersome to formulate, we can find a minimum-volume ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha)
with
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\alpha
as the shape matrix and center encapsulating reachable set. The ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha)
is a kind of over-approximations of the reachable set (i.e.,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{S}}
). As long as
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha)\cap\mathcal{D}=\emptyset
, the constraint (
8d
) can be satisfied.

Hence, to compute the distance between the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{P},\alpha)
and a single hyperplane
\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=b
(which represents part of the dangerous set
\mathcal{D}
), the distance
d
is given by
d=\frac{|b|-\sqrt{\alpha\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{c}}}

[
7
]
. The critical condition occurs when the ellipsoid is tangent to the hyperplane, which implies
d\geq 0
. This leads to the following inequality
\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{c}\leq\frac{b^{2}}{\alpha}
, where
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}
is positive definite.

We adopt the results in
[
7
,
9
]
to find the ellipsoid. Consider LTI system (
6
) with known matrices
(\boldsymbol{A},\ \boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{S}})
, the original actuator bounds
\gamma_{i}>0
,
i=1,\dots,m
collected in matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, and a set
\mathcal{D}
of dangerous states bounded by the hyperplanes as (
5
). For given
a\in(0,1)
, if there exists a positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n-1)\times(2n-1)}

A Two-stage ASAP Algorithm

In AP Stage, i.e., Lines 4 to 9 in Algorithm
1
, the actuator set
\mathcal{S}
is set to empty. Then, a loop is initiated, and an actuator location element

\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{S}

is chosen as a supermodular function
\log\det(\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1})
as mentioned in Section
III-B
. This ensures that the selected actuator provides the maximum controllability increment to the system. Note that when system is not controllable (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{W}_{c}^{-1}

V
Conclusion

This paper addresses maintaining safety and defense efficiency in vehicle platooning systems under potential cyber-attacks. By focusing on control node placement and optimizing input bounds for key actuators, we develop a defense strategy that limits the impact of attacks while preserving the system’s ability to operate effectively. Rather than restricting all actuators with low defense efficiency, we demonstrate that selectively constraining only critical actuators enhances safety. Our analysis of the system’s reachable set under state and input bounds shows that this approach provides a practical balance between safety and defense efficiency in vehicle platooning. Future work could explore the real-world implementation of these strategies and extend the approach to more complex multi-lane systems.
