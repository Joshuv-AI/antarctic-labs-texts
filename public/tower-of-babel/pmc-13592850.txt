Integration of freshness monitoring and active packaging technologies for aquatic products
Authors: Maria Ali, Abdul Waheed Ghanghro, Bheem Raj Serani, Mahrukh Zakir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAquatic products are nutritionally valuable but highly perishable, with quality loss driven by microbial spoilage, biochemical changes, including total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), trimethylamine (TMA) and histamine, and physicochemical deterioration, including lipid oxidation, gas production and texture loss. Conventional packaging slows these processes but does not report real-time freshness. This review analyzes how active packaging (antimicrobial, antioxidant, enzyme-inhibiting, and oxygen-scavenging systems, including bio- and nanocomposites) can be combined with freshness monitoring technologies (colorimetric/pH indicators, gas sensors, biosensors, and digital tools such as radio frequency identification (RFID) and Internet of Things (IoT) systems) specifically for fish, shrimp, and mollusks. We first map key spoilage mechanisms and markers, then evaluate active systems for aquatic products and compare three main monitoring families in terms of sensitivity, response time, cost—complexity and qualitative technology readiness. Integrated case studies (peptide-grafted films, modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) with time—temperature integrators (TTIs), smart labels with active papers and battery-free near-field communication (NFC) systems) are critically assessed for scalability, cost-effectiveness, safety, and regulatory feasibility. The review concludes that low-cost visual indicators coupled to simple active functions are closest to near-term deployment, while sensor- and biosensor-based systems remain at prototype level. Future work must prioritize marker- and species-specific validation, safe and scalable material systems, techno-economic evaluation, and early integration of regulatory and consumer aspects to justify industrial adoption in aquatic supply chains.Keywords: Active packaging, Aquatic products, Intelligent packaging, Smart packaging

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

A quatic products, including fish and other seafood, are significant for global nutrition. They provide high quality proteins, essential fatty acids, and minerals, including phosphorus, magnesium, iron, zinc, and iodine. However, due to their perishable nature, freshness strongly influences taste and nutrition and poses significant challenges for consumers, especially in the context of increasing food-borne diseases [1, 2]. Therefore, maintaining freshness is essential to ensure both product quality and safety [1].

Freshness is a central quality indicator during seafood processing, distribution, and consumption. It reflects the degree of physical, chemical, biochemical and microbial change in aquatic products [3]. Packaging plays a key role in controlling these changes and preserving quality, and safety of seafood from processing to consumption [4]. Its purpose is to incorporate products in a cost-effective way that meets the needs of industry and consumers, preserves food quality and safety, and limit food waste and environmental impact [5]. While, inventions in food packaging aim to combine and enhance the four basic functions. Protection, Communication, Convenience and product containment as illustrated in Fig. 1 [6].

Main functions of food packaging: protection, communication, convenience and containment.

Traditional (passive) packaging primarily delays spoilage, extends shelf life, and limits external impacts, and it has been essential for the development of current food distribution systems. Yet stricter monitoring requirements, globalized supply chains and more demanding consumers show that passive packaging alone no longer meets expectations [2]. In response to these limitations, functional packaging, and in particular active packaging (AP), has been developed to improve food safety and quality [7]. As originally described by Labuza and Breene (1989) and later defined in European Regulation (EC) No. 450/2009, AP systems deliberately incorporate components that release or absorb substances in the package headspace in order to maintain or improve the condition of packaged food [8]. Active compounds can be added to the packaging material or placed inside the package to provide antioxidant, antimicrobial, or other functions, with the aim of maintaining quality and safety while extending shelf life. Active systems act either as scavengers (removing oxygen, carbon dioxide, moisture, odors or ethylene) or as emitters (releasing CO2, ethanol, flavors, antimicrobials or antioxidants) during seafood storage and distribution [9]. However, even when they effectively delay spoilage, these systems do not provide direct, real-time information on the freshness status of seafood.

This limitation has motivated the development of freshness-monitoring tools within intelligent packaging (IP) systems. These use colorimetric indicators, gas sensors, resistive or conductive sensors, biosensors and radio frequency identification (RFID)-based digital tools to monitor quality during storage [2]. IP devices detect, sense or record internal or external changes in the package and can improve supply-chain management by providing real-time information on product condition and location, reducing the risk of loss and quality defects [10]. Despite this progress, an important gap remains: many studies focus on active packaging alone, and others on intelligent indicators, but only a few analyses show how freshness-monitoring tools are integrated into active packaging systems specifically designed for aquatic products. There is also relatively little discussion of how such combined systems respond to real spoilage patterns, safety risks and quality attributes of seafood in practice.

This review addresses that gap. First, it examines studies where active and intelligent functions are combined in packaging for specific aquatic species. It then summarizes the main spoilage mechanisms and quality indicators that are most relevant for seafood and for the design of packaging systems. Recent examples of active packaging for aquatic products are presented and their performance analyzed. The principal types of freshness-monitoring technologies used in IP is then introduced and their measurement targets explained. Finally, the review discusses current industrial and regulatory limitations and proposes future research directions for integrated active/intelligent packaging (A/IP) in the aquatic products sector.

1.2. Literature search strategy

This review is based on a structured search of the scientific literature on AP and IP for aquatic products. The main databases consulted were Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Searches combined keywords related to the product (fish, seafood, aquatic products, shrimp, mollusks) with terms referring to preservation and monitoring systems (AP, antimicrobial film, antioxidant coating, oxygen scavenger, IP, smart packaging freshness indicator, colorimetric label, biosensor, RFID, NFC, nanocomposite). The critical time period covered January 2010 to January 2026, with earlier seminal works included when they were frequently cited or relevant for definitions and regulatory aspects. Only peer-reviewed journal articles and book chapters written in English and reporting experimental data, case studies or critical reviews on packaging systems for aquatic products or closely related model foods were retained. Papers dealing exclusively with non-food applications, theoretical sensor design without any food testing, or generic food reviews without clear links to seafood were excluded. Reference lists of selected articles were also screened to identify additional relevant studies.

2 Integration of active and monitoring functions

Fish, shrimp, and mollusks deteriorate due to their perishable nature and are more susceptible to microbial spoilage and oxidative rancidity. For this reason, it’s essential for packaging to have features that keep track of the activity and freshness of the product. Furthermore, active systems, such as antimicrobial or antioxidant films, including chitosan-based films coated by infusing essential oils to protect against rancidity and microbial damage. Up-to-date information on freshness for retailers, for example total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) and microbial load come from monitoring devices such as pH-sensitive indicators and gas sensors.

2.1. Synergistic designs (antimicrobial films with embedded freshness indicators)

Synergistic packaging designs, such as antimicrobial films embedded with freshness indicators, have shown significant potential for aquatic products. A smart film was designed by integrating polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) with Amomum tsao-ko essential oil (AEO) and purple potato anthocyanin (PPA) into polymer matrix, developed for mantis shrimp preservation and for tracking product freshness. The PVA/CNC-AEO-PPA film achieved bactericidal efficacy against Shewanella putrefaciens and Vibrio parahaemolyticus, respectively. The film shows significant applicability for shrimp preservation and freshness [11]. Chitosan-gelatin films incorporating anthocyanins from Zingiber striolatum Diels (ZSD) have been developed to indicate fish freshness. These films exhibit pH-responsive color changes, which demonstrate that the film is highly pH-sensitive for quantifying fish freshness in real-time [12]. Another study developed an intelligent and antibacterial chitosan-based film by infusing oregano essential oil (OEO) and black rice bran anthocyanin (BRBA) into the matrix. The film exhibited strong antibacterial, antioxidant, ultraviolet—visible (UV—vis) barrier and pH/ammonia (NH3)-responsive properties, effectively improving pork freshness and preservation during 12 days of storage at 4 °C by reducing spoilage bacteria associated with stress, tolerance and pathogenicity [13].

2.2. Case studies in seafood packaging

Several attempts have been made to incorporate active and intelligent monitoring functions in aquatic food packaging, with the dual aim of preserving quality and providing real-time freshness assessment. A study investigated the effects of polypropylene-based antimicrobial AP functionalized with the peptide 1018K6 on microbial growth and sensory qualities of raw salmon. As a result, after 7 days of storage, this showed significant inhibition of pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms. Similar results have also been reported in hamburgers [14]. Beyond antimicrobial effects, freshness tracking has also been explored. Under modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) with carbon dioxide (CO2) emitter pads (MAP-PADS) and enzymatic time—temperature integrators (TTIs), gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata) fillets benefited from extended microbial and sensory shelf life and from the possibility of following quality loss through TTI color changes under dynamic cold-chain conditions [15].

A battery-free, stretchable, autonomous smart packaging system represents a more advanced integration of monitoring and control functions. In this fish case study, the package includes a gas sensor for real-time spoilage detection, a Near-Field Communication (NFC) antenna, and a controlled release mechanism for active compounds as shown in Fig. 2. When the food starts to spoil, the package triggers the release of antimicrobial compounds, which can extend shelf life by up to 14 days through controlled release of antioxidant and antibacterial agents [16]. However, another study similarly combines active and smart packaging in tuna fillets, evaluating the color change profile of a smart indicator and the effectiveness of active paper contain different proportions of garlic extract during storage at room temperature. The results highlighted the color transition from dark red to yellow in the smart label indicator as spoilage of fillets progressed. Active paper containing 15% garlic extracts showed great potential of minimizing deterioration compared with the control group as illustrated in Fig. 3. A positive correlation was found between the smart label color response, effectiveness of active paper, and spoilage parameters (TVB-N, total plate count (TPC), pH), demonstrating that combined AP and IP can reliably track and limit the decomposition rate in fish [17].

Components of the battery-free NFC smart packaging. The device consists of a multilayer polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) patch in which a poly (N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) top layer, an electrospun polypropylene carbonate (PPC) mat loaded with 5% cinnamon essential oil (CEO; PPC/5% CEO) and a poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) heater form the controlled-release module (left). This module is integrated with a wireless platform (right) including an ammonia (NH3)-sensitive single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) gas sensor, an NFC antenna and chip, and a silver electrode. When spoilage gases are detected by the sensor, the NFC platform drives the heater, promoting on-demand release of the active compounds from the PPC layer [16].

Hue angle (°Hue) of the smart color indicator attached to tuna fillets packaged with active paper containing 15% garlic extract during storage at room temperature. The label color gradually shifts from deep red (fresh) to yellow, with a yellowish-red stage around 6–8 h that coincides with the consumption limit, and a yellow color at longer times corresponding to spoiled fillets [17].

Altogether, these case studies clearly demonstrate that integrating preservation and monitoring functions in one package can improve microbial and sensory quality and provide useful freshness information. However, their real-world applicability is still uncertain. The peptide-functionalized polypropylene system is presented as a proof of concept; scaling peptide synthesis, grafting and quality control to industrial volumes, and obtaining regulatory approval for a new antimicrobial in direct contact with seafood would require additional cost and validation steps that are not addressed in the study. The MAP-PADS plus enzymatic TTI approach depends on careful calibration of the TTI to the product and cold-chain profile and on correct interpretation of color changes by operators; this raises questions about standardization and robustness in heterogeneous distribution networks. The battery-free, stretchable smart package is technologically impressive, but the integration of a gas sensor, NFC antenna and triggered release system into a flexible substrate implies more complex materials, manufacturing and converting operations than conventional films or printed labels. The study does not discuss unit cost, high-speed industrial production, or recyclability, so its feasibility for large-scale seafood packaging remains unclear. Even the tuna system combining active paper and smart labels, although simpler, is evaluated under controlled laboratory conditions and short storage times, without a detailed cost analysis or assessment of consumer handling.

Overall, these examples show that combining active and intelligent functions in seafood packaging is technically promising, but most of the reported systems remain at an experimental or pilot stage. The main unresolved issues concern material and processing costs, scalability of the more complex designs, and the regulatory and operational requirements that would need to be met before such integrated solutions can be deployed widely in commercial seafood chains.

2.3. Benefits of active and intelligent packaging in seafood

Within active packaging, MAP has significant potential to stop the microbial growth and slow down the oxidation process, thereby prolonging the shelf life of seafood products. For example, MAP has been shown to preserve fish quality and freshness by changing the gas composition in headspace, thereby reducing the formation of spoilage compounds such as trimethylamine (TMA) and TVB-N [15]. In addition, IP systems, including TTIs, gas sensors and biosensors, provide real-time feedback on the freshness of seafood. These systems monitor parameters such as pH changes, ammonia production, hydrogen sulphides and biogenic amines as well as histamine, xanthine and E. coli, providing real-time information (on the condition and location of product) to retailers and end-users during distribution and storage [10]. Moreover, these systems provide additional information on spoilage and freshness, toxicity, food-borne illness, environmental contaminants etc. For instance, biogenic amines often build up in seafood during spoilage, making them significant indicator for seafood freshness detection [18]. Furthermore, smart labels with quick response (QR) codes and freshness indicators (mostly dependent on color) help consumers make purchasing decisions, and limit food waste only through visually highlighting the freshness status of seafood. This transparency builds trust and improves market confidence regarding the safety and quality of seafood products [19].

2.4. Challenges: stability, cost, consumer acceptance and regulatory aspects

Stability and reliability are significant issues, as seafood is not always packaged under appropriate condition. Humidity, temperature fluctuation and poor hygiene, or improper handling during storage and transportation of product lead to reduce effectiveness of antimicrobial and antioxidant agents, biosensors/gas sensors and smart labels indicators making them less reliable in real world situations [20]. Another main cause is cost. In today’s world, end-users are more dynamic, complex, and have unique demands than in the past. Surveys have been conducted to understand consumer attitudes and the result shows the economic viability of food. Integration of sensing devices into the package material is costly, which creates a major barrier to IP implementation. The ideal cost of an IP device should be below 10% to basic product packaging cost but the real cost contributed to IP systems has been estimated at between 50% and 100% of the final packaging cost. It leads to an imbalance between the new technology and market potential greatly restricts the widespread adoption of IP [10]. Another challenge relates to consumer purchasing behavior: some consumers value the added information and functionality of active or intelligent packaging, whereas others are skeptic of new technologies and prefer conventional packages. It is critical to provide information to consumers about the benefits of these new technologies and to offer training on their use to support acceptance and market expansion [21]. Both packaging systems (AP, IP) should align with food contact and safety regulations and provide assurance that they do not pose any harmful effects to food quality or consumer health. In this regard, the European Union’s Regulation No. 1935/2004 and No. 450/2009 enforce specific requirements on migration limits, consumer health and safety and on the use of active and intelligent materials. Gaining regulatory clearance, including the filing of safety dossiers and assessment by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), is a time-consuming and costly process [22].

Safety of the packaging material itself is also important when AP and IP systems are designed for aquatic products. These systems are not only passive barriers. Active films, coatings and scavengers may release or absorb compounds inside the package, while intelligent indicators, sensors and biosensors may contain dyes, nanoparticles, metal oxides, enzymes, antibodies, conductive materials, inks or adhesives. Because of this, the package can interact with the food or with the package headspace. This interaction is useful for delaying spoilage and monitoring freshness, but it also means that the material must be evaluated for food-contact safety [23, 24].

In the studies reviewed in this paper, the proposed packaging systems showed useful effects such as reducing microbial growth, limiting oxidation, extending shelf life, detecting volatile amines, responding to pH change, or giving visual freshness information. However, these results do not automatically prove that all the materials are suitable for direct food-contact use. Some studies tested the packaging system directly on fish, shrimp, meat or other food samples and compared the results with TVB-N, TMA, pH, microbial count, sensory quality, color response or shelf-life changes. Such studies give stronger support for possible food-packaging application. Other studies mainly reported functional properties such as antimicrobial activity, antioxidant capacity, mechanical strength, barrier performance, gas sensitivity or sensor response. These findings are useful, but they do not fully confirm food-contact suitability.

This distinction is important because active and intelligent packaging may contain compounds that can migrate from the packaging material into food during storage. In seafood, this issue is more relevant because fish, shrimp and mollusks usually have high moisture content, and some products also contain salt and fat, which can affect the movement of packaging-derived substances. Essential oils, plant extracts and anthocyanins are often described as natural and safer alternatives, but their release level, migration behavior and possible effect on odor and taste should still be controlled. Similarly, nanocomposite films and metal-based sensing materials can improve barrier, antimicrobial or detection performance, but nanoparticles and inorganic fillers need more safety attention because their migration and toxicological effects are still a concern in food packaging [25, 26].

For this reason, the safety of AP and IP systems should be judged together with their preservation and monitoring performance. Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 requires food-contact materials not to transfer their constituents into food in amounts that may endanger human health, change food composition or affect sensory quality [27]. Regulation (EC) No. 450/2009 is directly relevant to active and intelligent materials because it sets specific requirements for these systems when they are intended to come into contact with food [28]. For plastic films, coatings and multilayer materials, Regulation (EU) No. 10/2011 is also relevant because it gives overall and specific migration requirements for plastic food-contact materials [29]. Thus, before commercial use, AP and IP systems for aquatic products should be tested under realistic storage conditions, including migration testing, release behavior, food-simulant selection, cytotoxicity or toxicological screening where needed, and clear regulatory compliance. This can show whether the packaging is not only effective in preserving or monitoring seafood, but also safe for food-contact application.

3 Spoilage mechanisms and freshness markers

3.1. Microbial spoilage

Microbial spoilage is a key cause of seafood deterioration. This is because fish tissues provide a nutrient-rich environment with a high content of water. The microbial load of fish consists of Pseudomonas, hydrogen sulphide (H2S)-producing bacteria, Moraxella, Alcaligenes, Vibrio, Serratia, and Micrococcus, which vary according to their aquatic environment (warm and cold water) [9]. Microorganisms that produce amines, and other volatile compounds such as histamine, sulphides, cadaverine, putrescine, organic acids, aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones, are the major cause of fish spoilage. These metabolites impart off-odors and off-flavors that are unacceptable to consumers. Therefore, it’s important to distinguish between spoilage bacteria and non-spoilage microorganisms because many of the bacteria present don’t contribute to spoilage [30]. Raw, unpreserved fish typically spoil due to Gram-negative fermentative bacteria, such as Vibrionaceae. In contrast, chilled fish spoilage is caused by psychrotrophic Gram-negative bacteria, specifically Pseudomonads and Shewanella spp. Therefore, it is very important to distinguish between microbiota that are part of the total viable count (TVC) and specific spoilage organisms (SSO), which are the main cause of fish spoilage. In addition, the Photobacterium phosphoreum is an important spoilage microorganism in seafood. For example, haddock fillets are kept at either cold or high temperatures. Pseudomonas spp. and S. putrefaciens are also part of the spoilage microbiota that gives off odors during storage [9].

3.2. Biochemical changes

3.2.1. Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N)

TVB-N is a common way of seafood freshness marker. It shows how much volatile nitrogenous compounds, mainly ammonia, TMA, and dimethylamine (DMA), are produced when proteins and trimethylamine oxide (TMAO) break down by microbes and enzymes. Current studies conducted in 2025 confirm that TVB-N is directly proportional to storage time and temperature, correlating well with sensory deterioration in fish species such as mackerel and catfish under refrigeration [31, 32]. For instance, a color indication was made by sticking bromocresol green (BCG) to the polyacrylamide (PAAm) hydrogel. It changed color in a way that showed how fresh the fish was while it was being stored. The indicator showed a very linear connection to TVB-N levels between 4.3 and 30.80 mg/100 g at 4 °C (R2 =0.98). [33]. While TVB-N is a standard and widely regulated error index that is commonly associated with microbial growth and sensory rejection, though the range values depend on species and product type [34, 35]. But it also has limitations such as TVB-N tends to be low during early storage period and only increases significantly after spoilage occurs, which makes it less sensitive to initial freshness loss [36]. In addition, most reference methods for TVB-N rely on distillation followed by titration in Conway micro-diffusion cells or Kjeldahl-type systems, or on automated steam-distillation units, as summarized in the critical review on TVB-N and TMA determination [37]. These procedures are destructive, require significant amounts of chemicals, and are relatively time-consuming, which restricts their use for real-time or in-package freshness monitoring.

For packaging design, TVB-N is mainly used as a reference marker rather than a direct in-pack measurement. Many colorimetric labels and gas sensors for seafood are calibrated against TVB-N or related volatile amines, and their color change or electrical response is interpreted in terms of TVB-N thresholds. However, because TVB-N often increases only after significant deterioration, freshness indicators based solely on this marker are better suited for detecting the end of shelf life than for signaling early quality loss, and they should ideally be combined with more sensitive markers or time—temperature information.

3.2.2. Trimethylamine (TMA)

TMA is a volatile amine produced when spoilage bacteria reduce TMAO, a compound naturally abundant in many marine species. In fresh or properly chilled fish, TMA is almost absent, and its content remains very low during the first days of storage. Studies on cold-water cod show that good-quality fish generally have TMA levels below about 1.5 mg TMA-N/100 g, with a progressive increase as storage in ice continues and spoilage develops [38]. Similar work on different marine products stored on ice confirmed that TMA rises with time and tracks both TVB-N and sensory rejection, provided that the species is TMAO-rich.

From a critical point of view, TMA is a useful but not universal freshness marker. It performs well in marine species with high TMAO content, where the characteristic fishy and ammoniacal odor appears in parallel with the increase in TMA. In freshwater fish or species with low TMAO, however, TMA may remain low even when other quality indicators already show clear deterioration, which limits its value as a general index. TMA is also sensitive to storage temperature, ice quality and microbiota composition, so values obtained under one set of conditions cannot be transferred directly to other products or distribution chains. Unlike TVB-N or histamine, there is no widely adopted legal limit for TMA, and it is mainly used as a complementary marker in quality control. Gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) is still the main reference method, while spectrophotometric assays after derivatization offer simpler but sometimes less specific alternative [39]. Packaging systems that rely on TMA therefore need to be selected and calibrated by species, and should not be applied as a universal freshness indicator across all aquatic products.

3.2.3. Histamine

Histamine is the most relevant biogenic amine in fish from a safety point of view, because its accumulation can lead to scombroid poisoning in susceptible species. In general, histamine contents below about 10 mg/kg are associated with good quality, whereas levels around 30–50 mg/kg already indicate significant deterioration. Regulatory or guideline limits are usually set between 50 and 200 mg/kg depending on the species and product category, reflecting both quality and toxicological concerns. Histamine is therefore widely used as a safety marker to detect temperature abuse and poor handling, even though it does not increase equally in all species and often requires relatively advanced analytical methods such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or enzymatic—colorimetric assays for accurate quantification [40].

In terms of packaging and monitoring, histamine serves as a risk-oriented marker rather than a universal freshness index. Biosensors or colorimetric kits targeting histamine are especially relevant for high-risk, scombroid species and for detecting temperature abuse, where regulatory limits must not be exceeded. For many other seafood products, histamine does not consistently rise, so packaging concepts that include histamine sensors need to be targeted to specific species and supply chains and analyzed in combination with more general freshness markers such as TVB-N, TMA, or microbial counts. Table 1 shows that recent analytical methods differ greatly in their practical suitability for seafood freshness monitoring. Chromatographic methods such as HPLC-FLD and HS-SPME/GC-FID provide stronger quantitative reliability, but they remain laboratory-based and are not suitable for direct package-level monitoring. Colorimetric indicators, gas sensors, fluorescence methods and electrochemical sensors are more promising for rapid or on-site screening, but many still require stronger validation in real seafood matrices. In particular, LOD and LOQ values are not consistently reported for all indicator systems, and several methods remain sensitive to matrix effects, humidity, temperature, sensor stability and species-specific spoilage pathways. Therefore, future packaging-based freshness systems should be compared not only by sensitivity, but also by sample type, analytical range, response time, calibration requirement and agreement with reference methods.

3.3. Physicochemical changes

3.3.1. Lipid oxidation

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) can quickly oxidize into aldehydes, which change the taste, odor and texture of food. This is known as rancidity, and it mainly occurs because unsaturated lipids in fish are easily oxidized during storage. The oxidation reaction may additionally render food less healthy, and some of the things that happen during oxidation can be harmful for the body as well. The peroxide value is the most widely used indicator for determining primary lipid peroxidation products. One of these products is hydroperoxide. It is unstable and breaks down into other chemicals, such aldehydes, which cause fish to decay. The thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS) test is the most popular way to find-aldehydes. The main benefit of this sign is that when unsaturated fats oxidize, they modify the odor, taste, texture, hue, and nutritional value of food. So, it helps us check the freshness of fish from all around the world [52].

3.3.2. pH

Anaerobic muscle cannot maintain its normal level of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) after death. After death, glycolysis results in the buildup of lactic acid, which lowers the pH of the muscle and inorganic phosphates are released when ATP decomposes. This decrease in pH alters the texture of the muscle by denaturing proteins and also favors the growth of spoilage microorganisms, which accelerates the spoilage process [53].

3.3.3. Gas production

Common spoilage-related bacteria, including Shewanella putrefaciens and Photobacterium phosphoreum, can produce different gases such as CO2, H2S and NH3 during seafood storage. The accumulation of these gases in the package headspace can change the pressure and odour inside the package. For instance, H2S can contribute to a rotten-egg-like smell, while ammonia can produce a pungent odour. Therefore, multi-gas sensors can be used to track these gases as a non-destructive method for detecting spoilage in real time. These methods are also useful for modified atmosphere packaging, where changes in gas composition can indicate the spoilage process [54].

3.3.4. Color changes

Seafood spoils quickly because of its high moisture and protein content, which makes it more prone to microbial and enzymatic breakdown. Colorimetric markers have become useful for keeping track of freshness in real-time by finding spoiling metabolites such volatile amines (TVB-N), H2S, biogenic amines (histamine, putrescine), and changes in pH. For example, films made with anthraquinone, azo chromophore, and alizarin utilizing screen printing and dip-coating can be used to monitor cooked crab and fish fillet by showing a clear transition in color from green to purple to red and yellow to purple [55].

3.3.5. Texture

Texture is one of the most vital traits of fish and meat. It impacts how well consumers accept the products and how successfully they market themselves. Firmness is an important part of fish quality, but tenderness is something people like when they consume meat. During the postmortem ageing of muscle in cold conditions, the breakdown of muscle proteins causes the flesh to soften quickly. These changes happen because of the action of natural enzymes that break down muscle proteins, connective tissue, and fat. The enzymes that do this are called cathepsins and calpains. Textural softening is frequently employed alongside sensory and biochemical indications to facilitate a thorough assessment of freshness [56]. 4. Active packaging for aquatic products

4.1. Antimicrobial and antioxidant release systems

Recent advances in antimicrobial and antioxidant release systems have been focused on integrating bioactive compounds with biodegradable polymer matrices to extend shelf life and preserve quality attributes of seafood. Peptide-based films and edible coatings (derived from proteins or food by-products) for instance, have shown both antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. It can effectively slow the spoilage process of fish fillets by reducing the growth of pathogen and scavenging of free radicals [57]. Likewise, chitosan-based films have been treated with essential oils (EOs) or metal nanoparticles are being used to keep lipid oxidation and microbial load in a controlled manner in aquatic products. Application of chitosan films and coatings with EOs instead of pure chitosan films and coatings usually prepared fish and meat products to an extended shelf life and less lipid peroxidation [58]. Metal-based nanomaterials can improve barrier and antibacterial properties. Films of alginate and natural extracts of plants have shown effective reduction in lipid peroxidation while preserving their sensory characteristics [59].

Beyond metal-based nanomaterials, CNCs have been modified to act as rechargeable antibacterial nanofillers. CNCs obtained from cotton textile waste were functionalized with methacrylamide (MAM) and incorporated into cellulose acetate (CA) and poly vinyl chloride (PVC) films; the resulting MAM-CNC composites improved mechanical and UV-barrier properties and, after chlorination, achieved more than 6 log reductions of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli within 1 h, with antibacterial activity that could be renewed by repeated chlorination as shown in Fig. 5 [60]. In a related study on meat systems, which prepared an AP film combining carboxymethyl cellulose, PVA, and tamarind seed coat waste, crosslinked with citric acid by solvent casting method, showing its application prospect for pork preservation and was proposed as a natural alternative for active meat packaging as shown in Fig. 6 [61]. Deshmukh et al. [62] developed an active antioxidant packaging utilizing a guar gum/carboxymethyl cellulose-based antioxidant film integrated with halloysite nanotubes and litchi shell waste extract. The film showed up to 91.52% antioxidant activity, prolonging the shelf life of oxygen-sensitive products as shown in Fig. 7. This study, yet conducted on meat and other model systems instead of seafood, demonstrates the potential adaptation of biodegradable matrices infused with plant-derived antioxidants for aquatic products.

4.2. Enzyme inhibitors and oxygen scavengers

Currently some enzyme-inhibiting compounds or substances, including polyphenols are playing a great role to suppress enzymatic spoilage in seafood by integrating into edible coatings and packaging films. For instance, a strong chlorogenic acid can greatly reduce the activity of enzyme such as lipoxygenase, similarly oxidized resveratrol derivatives lowered lipase activity thereby decreasing lipid peroxidation (initially) and oxidation activity in seafood, simultaneously involved in stabilization polyunsaturated fatty acid. In addition, the chitosan composite coatings significantly reduced the activities of cathepsin B, B +L, and calpain, which reduced proteolytic degradation and structure relaxation in refrigerated grass carp fillets [64]. In crustaceans, the combination of chitooligosaccharide (CHOS) with polyphenol and chitosan coatings with plant polyphenols (e.g., catechin and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)) on Pacific white shrimps demonstrated that chitooligosaccharide (CHOS) with catechin acts as an anti-melanosis in shrimps by reducing polyphenol oxidase activity, resulting in improved color with maintained sensory qualities [65]. Furthermore, the combination of chitosan coating and green tea extract (GTE) is regarded as a natural and efficacious anti-melanosis agent that improves the quality of shrimp during ice storage [66]. In summary, these results prove the enzyme inhibitory effect of polyphenols suggest a promising tool to restrict autolytic enzymatic processes and preserve physicochemical properties of seafood.

4.2.1. Oxygen scavengers

An oxygen absorber is defined as a package component that absorbs any oxygen present inside the packaging environment or product headspace and can be applied in the form of sachets, powders, labels, or edible coatings, thus producing a low-oxygen atmosphere to reduce spoilage. Common mechanisms include chemical scavenging, which reduces oxygen to trace levels (<0.01%), as seen in commercial sachets such as Ageless and FreshPax, in which iron particles turn it into iron oxide in the presence of moisture (Fe2+→Fe3+) [67, 68], and glucose oxidase oxidized to form gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide, which is further catalyzed by the catalase enzyme to prevent nutrient loss is the mechanism [67]. Polyphenols and ascorbic acid are examples of natural antioxidant-scavenging-based compound sets in packaging film that react with reactive oxygen species (ROS) to delay the spoilage process and have been largely seen in aquatic products: Fortification of cranberry extract (CE) or grape seed extract (GE) in alginate or pectin films applied to herring fillets notably reduced the TVB-N and the formation of TBARS, thereby hindering spoilage due to the antimicrobial and antioxidant activity of the extracts for up to 18 days of refrigerated storage [69]. Both chemical and natural antioxidant scavenging activities successfully implemented in seafood products significantly enhanced physicochemical and oxidative stability and extended shelf life. 5 Freshness monitoring technologies

5.1. Colorimetric and pH indicators

Colorimetric and pH indicators have gained global consideration for assessing freshness owing to their capacity to provide a simple, real-time visual method for monitoring changes in food quality. These indicators react to spoilage metabolites such as volatile basic nitrogen and amines, leading to color shifts that can be easily noticed. Synthetic dyes (e.g., bromocresol purple, bromothymol blue) and natural pigments (e.g., anthocyanins, curcumin) both have been embedded into films, papers, or biopolymer matrices for tracking applications. Current studies reveal effective use in fish, meat, and seafood (including shellfish, mollusk). In these tests, it was simple to observe the color change because of labels or films that highlighted how it was connected to indicators of chemical or microbiological deterioration. 5.3. Biosensors

There are different types of biosensors, such as electrochemical, optical, piezoelectric, and thermal biosensors. These types are based on the type of transduction system used and the type of bio recognition element (bio component or bio receptor). The bio component or bio receptor, which might be single enzymes, whole cells, tissues, or aptamers, is what makes biosensors work. It makes it possible to find and interact with certain analytes. The energy from this exchange is turned into an electrical signal that may be measured. Antibodies and enzymes are two common biological parts. There are many kinds of biosensors in science and engineering, but not all of them are good for food packaging since they have different levels of sensitivity, selectivity, and application domain. Some biosensors can be used in food packaging to improve advanced food systems since they can find target analytes including food pathogens, spoiling biomarkers, freshness indicators, temperature, moisture, and other factors [81]. 5.4. Smart/digital tools: RFID, IoT and data-driven systems

Smart technologies like RFID and Internet of Things (IoT) help the seafood industry track fish better and further improve business operations. This technology itself makes the entire process more efficient. Essentially, RFID technology tracks seafood products in real-time, from their catch to their sale. As per current developments, the device makes the supply chain more transparent regarding operations. The system becomes more open for all parties involved. Moreover, as per the system design, the tracking mechanism reduces fraud chances regarding all process stages. This monitoring approach directly prevents fraudulent activities at any point [86]. IoT devices track water quality and fish behavior all the time, which helps to keep fish healthy and makes feeding more efficient [87]. Data platforms collect information from different sources and provide clear insights to help people make better decisions and follow proper rules [88]. These technologies further help make the seafood industry stronger and sustainable. They meet the growing demand for responsibly sourced seafood products.

5.5. Comparative analysis of freshness monitoring technologies

Freshness-monitoring technologies for aquatic products can be compared through their sensing principle, target markers, response behavior, cost, system complexity and practical readiness for packaging use. The three main groups discussed in this review are colorimetric/pH indicators, gas sensors and biosensors. Each group can support seafood quality monitoring, but their practical value is different because aquatic products contain high moisture, active enzymes, different spoilage microbiota and complex headspace compounds during storage.

Colorimetric and pH indicators are the simplest and most practical option for individual seafood packages. These systems usually use synthetic dyes or natural pigments immobilized in paper, polymer films, biopolymer matrices or labels. They respond to pH changes, volatile basic nitrogen, amines, H2S or CO2 through a visible color change. Luo et al. [72] showed that colorimetric indicators are widely used in intelligent food packaging because they are low cost, simple to prepare and easy to read without advanced instruments. This makes them suitable for high-volume aquatic products, where the monitoring system must be cheap, simple and directly attached to the package.

Several studies also show that colorimetric systems can be linked with freshness changes in real food products. Nadi et al. [89] developed a basil seed gum/chitosan/red cabbage anthocyanin film for real-time fish freshness monitoring, showing the relevance of natural pigment-based labels for seafood. Zhai et al. [77] reported a gellan gum-silver nanoparticle H2S sensor with a low detection limit and visible color change during meat spoilage monitoring. Although this example was not designed only for seafood, it shows the sensitivity that can be achieved by simple colorimetric detection of spoilage gases. Shao et al. [90] also described intelligent freshness indicators as useful tools for food quality and safety monitoring because they can supplement date labels with direct visual information about product condition.

However, colorimetric and pH indicators still have important limitations. Their response can be affected by storage humidity, temperature fluctuation, dye stability, indicator migration and differences between food matrices. Some labels also need time before the final color becomes stable. Fauzan et al. [91] showed that anthocyanin-based edible film indicators required different response times depending on the pH level. For aquatic products, this means that the same indicator cannot be assumed to work equally well for all fish, shrimp and mollusks. These indicators are therefore close to practical application, but they still need better standardization against seafood species, packaging atmosphere, storage temperature and accepted freshness limits.

Gas sensors provide stronger analytical sensitivity than simple visual labels because they detect volatile compounds directly in the package headspace. The most relevant targets for aquatic products include NH3, TMA, H2S and CO2, which are associated with microbial spoilage, protein degradation and modified-atmosphere changes. Amine gas sensor studies have shown that improved sensing materials and device designs can support detection of spoilage-related volatile amines [76]. Wu et al. [78] focused specifically on metal—oxide—semiconductor resistive gas sensors for fish freshness detection and showed that these systems have strong potential for detecting volatile markers linked to fish spoilage. Zhao et al. [79] also reported an Au nanoparticle-sensitized WO3 nanosheet sensor for rapid TMA detection and seafood freshness assessment.

The main strength of gas sensors is that they can provide non-destructive and real-time information from the headspace without opening the package. CO2-sensitive optical films can also be useful for monitoring modified-atmosphere packaging. Mills and Yusufu [80] developed a highly CO2-sensitive fluorescent plastic indicator film, showing the usefulness of optical gas-sensitive materials for packaging-related gas monitoring. However, gas sensors are more complex than colorimetric labels. Their response can be affected by humidity, temperature, gas diffusion, air flow and interfering gases. Robbiani et al. [92] explained that physical confounding factors can affect gas sensor response and that compensation strategies are often needed in electronic-nose applications. For this reason, gas sensors may be more realistic first in cold rooms, transport containers, retail storage areas, centralized inspection points or high-value seafood products, rather than as low-cost labels for every package.

Biosensors provide high specificity because they use biological recognition elements such as enzymes, antibodies, cells or aptamers to detect selected targets. These systems can be designed for pathogens, toxins, allergens, contaminants, spoilage biomarkers, pH, moisture and gases. Sobhan et al. [81] discussed IoT-enabled biosensors in food packaging and showed how biosensors can support real-time risk monitoring when linked with digital systems. Guruprasath et al. [85] described nanobiosensors as promising tools for food safety because nanomaterials can improve sensitivity, portability and rapid detection. Tsegay et al. [93] also discussed biosensing strategies for monitoring contaminants and additives in fish, meat, poultry and related products, which is relevant to targeted detection in aquatic product chains.

Despite this potential, biosensors remain the most complex monitoring group. They require stable biorecognition elements, suitable immobilization methods, careful calibration, signal processing, packaging integration and validation in real food matrices. Their cost and technical requirements are also higher than those of simple colorimetric indicators. In seafood packaging, these issues are important because moisture, salts, proteins, fats and mixed volatile compounds can affect the performance of biological recognition systems. Therefore, biosensors may first be more suitable for batch-level testing, supply-chain monitoring, high-value seafood products or critical control points, where the cost and technical complexity can be justified.

When these three technologies are compared, colorimetric and pH indicators offer the best balance between cost, simplicity and near-term use on individual seafood packages. Gas sensors provide stronger analytical performance and better headspace monitoring, but they require calibration, power or readout systems and control of environmental interference. Biosensors provide high specificity for selected targets, but their stability, cost and packaging integration still limit routine use. For aquatic products, the most practical direction is therefore not to treat one technology as universally superior. Low-cost visual indicators can be used for simple package-level freshness communication, while gas sensors and biosensors can be used where higher analytical detail is needed, especially in high-value products, cold-chain monitoring, centralized inspection and batch-level quality control.

5.6. Deployment balance between low-cost visual indicators and high-cost electronic sensors

From a deployment point of view, there is a clear tension between low-cost visual indicators and more complex electronic or biosensing systems. Colorimetric and pH indicators rely on cheap substrates (paper, simple polymers) and food-grade dyes or pigments, and they already show convincing performance for fish and meat in realistic storage studies [72]. This cost structure and simplicity explain why these indicators are close to commercial roll-out in IP, especially for high-volume seafood products where margins are limited.

In contrast, MOS-based gas sensors and electronic-nose systems offer higher analytical power. Wu et al. [78] summarize that MOS-based sensors can achieve high sensitivity and rapid response to volatile amines and other spoilage markers relevant to fish freshness, and that multi-sensor arrays coupled with pattern recognition can distinguish different stages of spoilage with good accuracy. However, Wu et al. [78] also point out that MOS-based sensors suffer from cross-sensitivity to humidity and interfering gases, require calibration, and need power and read-out electronics, which increases system complexity and cost compared with simple visual labels.

Recent reviews underline that, despite high analytical performance, many food biosensors still face practical barriers to wider deployment. In^es and Cosme emphasize limitations related to the stability and shelf life of enzymes and antibodies, the need for rigorous calibration and standardization, and integration challenges within existing food-safety management systems and regulatory frameworks [82]. Tsegay et al. [93] further stress that biosensor platforms are not yet widely incorporated into hazard analysis and critical control point (HACCP)-type schemes and that scaling up from laboratory prototypes to commercial-grade devices requires substantial investment and more systematic validation against reference methods.

Current evidence suggests that low-cost colorimetric indicators are still the most practical near-term option for seafood freshness labels. They fit better with high-volume aquatic products because they can be prepared on cheap substrates, attached to individual packages and interpreted without complex equipment [72]. However, a visual label will only be useful in real seafood packaging if the color response is stable under refrigeration, humidity and temperature fluctuation. High sensitivity alone is not enough if the method is slow, expensive, difficult to read, or needs trained users, calibration and special equipment. This is also why the cost of sensing devices remains an important barrier to wider IP application [10].

A feasible way forward is to improve simple visual labels rather than only developing more complex sensors. Colorimetric and pH indicators should be designed with stronger dye immobilization, better color stability, simple reference scales and clearer links with freshness limits such as TVB-N, TMA, pH, microbial count and sensory rejection. Smartphone-assisted reading is also a practical breakthrough because the color response can be captured as image or red—green—blue (RGB) data instead of depending only on the human eye. Chen et al. [94] developed a smartphone-assisted low-cost paper-based colorimetric and fluorescent dual-response sensor for visual evaluation of food freshness. An earlier related study by Chen et al. [95] also developed a low-cost paper-based colorimetric sensor array that used a smartphone camera to read color information from a barcode-like sensor for food quality estimation.

For gas sensors, the practical route should be different. These systems should not be expected to enter every low-cost seafood package immediately because they still involve calibration, power supply, read-out devices, signal drift and data handling. MOS-based gas sensors and electronic-nose systems can respond to volatile amines and other spoilage gases, but their response can be influenced by humidity, interfering gases and temperature changes [78]. Therefore, gas sensors may be more useful first in cold rooms, transport containers, retail storage areas, central inspection points and high-value seafood chains, where one device can monitor many products and the cost can be justified.

Biosensors also have strong potential because they can detect specific targets such as pathogens, toxins, spoilage biomarkers, pH, moisture and gases [81]. However, their routine use in seafood packaging is still limited by the stability of biological components, immobilization, storage conditions, standardization and integration into existing packaging systems. For this reason, biosensors may first be more suitable for batch-level or supply-chain-level monitoring rather than direct use in every package.

Matrix effects are another practical limitation that should be considered before packaging-based sensors are used in real aquatic product systems. Fish, shrimp and mollusks are not simple test solutions. They contain proteins, lipids, salts, pigments, enzymes, moisture and many volatile compounds that change during storage. These components can influence sensor response by blocking active sites, changing dye behavior, affecting gas diffusion, producing background signals or causing non-specific binding. This means that a sensor that works well in buffer, standard gas or a simple model system may not show the same accuracy when it is used directly on real seafood without sample pretreatment.

This issue is important for all three monitoring groups. In colorimetric and pH indicators, the seafood matrix and package humidity can change the color response and make the signal difficult to interpret, especially when the same label is applied to different species. In gas sensors, the package headspace contains a mixture of volatile amines, sulfur compounds, carbon dioxide, water vapor and other spoilage-related compounds. Therefore, cross-sensitivity can occur, and the sensor may respond to more than one compound rather than only the target marker. In biosensors, proteins, salts and fats from seafood can interfere with enzyme, antibody or aptamer binding and reduce sensitivity or accuracy. Although Li et al. [96] focused on tetrodotoxin detection rather than freshness monitoring, their study is useful here because it showed that typical seafood matrices can affect aptamer stability, aptamer—protein interaction and detection performance. This supports the need for matrix validation before packaging-based sensors are used in commercial seafood chains.

For practical application, packaging-based monitoring systems should therefore be validated directly in real seafood matrices, not only in buffer solutions, standard gases or laboratory simulants. Future studies should compare sensor response with reference methods such as TVB-N, TMA, histamine, microbial count and sensory rejection under refrigerated and fluctuating cold-chain conditions. The same sensor should also be tested across different species, fat levels, salt levels, packaging atmospheres and storage temperatures. Where possible, anti-fouling sensing surfaces, selective membranes, matrix-resistant recognition elements, simple dilution or extraction steps for batch-level testing, and chemometric or machine-learning correction should be used to reduce matrix interference. This will make freshness monitoring more reliable and will help to show whether a sensor can work in real aquatic product packaging rather than only under controlled laboratory conditions.

A tiered monitoring strategy is therefore more realistic for aquatic products. Simple colorimetric or pH labels can be used on individual packs for direct consumer and retailer interpretation. More advanced gas sensors, biosensors, RFID and IoT tools can be used at batch, container or cold-chain level to provide more detailed information for processors, distributors and retailers. Future studies should report not only sensitivity, detection limit and shelf-life extension, but also response time under refrigerated conditions, read-out time, signal stability, calibration needs, power or reader requirements, approximate cost per label or device, compatibility with existing packaging lines, and accuracy against standard freshness markers. This would make the results more useful for deciding which technologies can be applied in high-volume seafood packaging and which ones are better suited for high-value or centralized monitoring.

6. Conclusion and future perspectives

This review shows that active and intelligent packaging for aquatic products has moved well beyond simple concepts but has not yet reached routine industrial use. Antimicrobial and antioxidant films, enzyme inhibitors and oxygen scavengers can clearly delay microbial spoilage, lipid oxidation and quality loss. Freshness indicators, gas sensors, biosensors and digital tools can track key markers such as TVB-N, TMA, gases, pH, and selected hazards. Case studies that combine these two functions demonstrate that preservation and real-time monitoring can be integrated in the same system. At the same time, most reported solutions remain prototypes: stability under real cold chains, nanomaterial safety, unit cost, process scalability and regulatory approval are not yet resolved in a convincing way.

The main conclusion is that integration alone is not enough. AP and IP must be designed around the actual spoilage pathways of specific species and products, must rely on materials whose migration and toxicological profiles are understood, and must fit within realistic economic and regulatory constraints. The reviewed literature shows that this safety evidence is still incomplete for many prototype systems, especially where active compounds, dyes or nanomaterials are used. Complex NFC-based smart packs or multi-sensor platforms bring impressive analytical performance, but they will not be adopted if they cannot be produced on existing lines at acceptable cost or if their signals are hard to interpret for operators and consumers. Conversely, simpler combinations such as biodegradable films with oxygen scavengers and robust color indicators already align better with current manufacturing capabilities and price structures, but still need standardized validation across species and supply chains.

Based on this, future work should move from general recommendations to quantitative, marker-based and application-specific targets. The first step is to define the freshness class that the package is expected to identify. Although exact limits differ by species, product form, storage temperature and regulation, useful working ranges can still be applied during validation. For many fishery products, total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) values below about 15–20 mg N/100 g can be treated as early freshness, values around 20–30 mg N/100 gas declining but still acceptable quality, and values above about 30–35 mg N/100 g as the rejection or spoiled range for many marine products [34, 35]. Trimethylamine (TMA) should be used mainly for marine, trimethylamine oxide (TMAO)-rich species. Good-quality cold-water fish often show TMA-N values below about 1.5–2 mg/100 g, while values around 5–10 mg TMA-N/100 g may be used as a warning range and values above about 10–15 mg TMA-N/100 g may indicate spoilage, depending on species and storage conditions [38, 39]. Histamine is different because it is mainly a safety marker for scombroid and other high-histidine fish. Values below about 10 mg/kg usually indicate good quality, values around 30–50 mg/kg may indicate deterioration or temperature abuse, and values around 100–200 mg/kg should be treated as an action or regulatory range depending on the product category [40]. These values should not be treated as universal limits, but they provide practical targets for calibrating active—intelligent packaging systems.

6.1. Marker- and species-specific validation

Future studies should validate sensors against defined freshness classes instead of reporting only color change, sensitivity or shelf-life extension. For example, a colorimetric label for chilled fish should be tested to show whether it can distinguish fresh samples below 15–20 mg TVB-N/100 g, acceptable samples around 20–30 mg TVB-N/100 g and spoiled samples above 30–35 mg TVB-N/100 g. A TMA-responsive gas sensor for marine fish should provide a warning before TMA-N reaches about 5–10 mg/100 g, while histamine-targeted systems for tuna, mackerel and other high-histidine species should warn before the product enters the 30–50 mg/kg risk range or approaches the regulatory action range. Validation should include representative product groups such as lean marine fish, fatty fish, shrimp and mollusks, and should be conducted under realistic cold-chain conditions such as 0–2 °C, 4 °C and 8–10 °C. Sensor output should be compared with TVB-N, TMA, histamine, microbial count and sensory rejection. Useful performance targets should include correlation with reference methods of at least R2 ≥0.90, freshness-classification accuracy of at least 85–90%, and a false-safe rate below 5–10%, because a label that classifies spoiled seafood as acceptable would not be useful in practice.

6.2. Safe, scalable material systems

Future material development should connect preservation performance with measurable safety and processing targets. Active films, coatings, scavengers and indicators should aim to extend refrigerated shelf life by at least 20–30%, or by a clearly stated number of days compared with the control package. For example, a system that extends shrimp shelf life from 5 to 7 days or fish fillet shelf life from 8 to 10–11 days gives a clearer practical outcome than reporting only antimicrobial activity in vitro. At the same time, migration, release behavior, odor transfer, color transfer and toxicological safety should be tested under the same storage conditions used for the shelf-life trial. Materials should also be designed for existing coating, printing, casting or laminating processes, with active compound loading reported as mg/g film, mg/cm2 label or another reproducible unit. This will make it easier to compare studies and to judge whether a system can move from laboratory casting to pilot-scale packaging production.

6.3. Techno-economic and technology readiness level (TRL)-oriented development

Future studies should report cost and operational performance in a way that industry can use. For low-cost visual labels, the target should be individual package application with a cost increase that remains close to or below 10% of the basic packaging cost. For electronic gas sensors, biosensors, radio frequency identification (RFID), near-field communication (NFC) or Internet of Things (IoT)-linked systems, the intended use should be stated clearly, because these systems may be more realistic for cold rooms, transport containers, retail storage areas or high-value seafood products rather than every low-cost pack. Each study should report response time under refrigeration, read-out time, calibration frequency, signal stability, power or reader requirements, estimated cost per label or device, and compatibility with existing packaging lines. Practical targets can include visual response within 30 min, gas-sensor readout within minutes or continuous monitoring, and pilot testing on at least several production batches or hundreds to thousands of packages before claiming industrial relevance.

6.4. Human and regulatory integration

Future active—intelligent packaging systems should also be tested with the people who will use them. Consumer and operator studies should measure whether freshness signals are understood correctly, not only whether the label changes color. For example, a useful study could test at least 100 consumers and 20–30 retail or processing operators and report the percentage who correctly classify the label as fresh, acceptable or spoiled. A practical target would be at least 80–90% correct interpretation without specialist training, with handling or reading time kept below a few seconds per package for simple visual labels. Regulatory planning should also begin early. Studies should state whether the material is intended for direct food contact or only headspace monitoring, whether migration testing is required, whether the active or intelligent component is separated from the food, and how the system could be fitted into hazard analysis and critical control point (HACCP)-type quality management. If these quantitative targets are addressed, integrated active—intelligent packaging can move from isolated proof-of-concept studies towards practical seafood applications where the added cost and complexity are justified by measurable gains in safety, shelf life and waste reduction.
