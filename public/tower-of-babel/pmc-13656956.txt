Sustainable enhancement of acidic soil fertility through integrated soil amendments: A metagenomic evaluation of fungal functional improvement
Authors: Ireng Darwati, Muchamad Yusron, Tiwit Widowati, Edi Yatno, April Hari Wardhana, Gina Aliyah Sopha, Octivia Trisilawati, Devi Rusmin, Raden Vitri Garvita, Rudi Suryadi, Indarti Puji Lestari, Yati Haryati, Agustina E Br Marpaung, Muhammad Hikmat, Suryani, Tika Anggraeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcid soil is characterized by low pH, which restricts nutrient availability and disrupts overall soil biological functioning. Soil ameliorants, such as Hermetia illucens (black soldier fly; BSF) frass, lime, and biochar, are widely applied to restore the fertility of acidic soils by improving both physicochemical and biological properties. This study evaluated the dynamics of the fungal community in acidic Typic Hapludult soil under three conditions: untreated soil, BSF frass, and ameliorant-treated soil. For each condition, 1000 g of material was placed in plastic containers and incubated at room temperature for three months. The ameliorant-treated condition consisted of 5 g BSF frass, 5 g lime, 2.5 g homogenized arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi inoculum, and 987.5 g soil. Fungal communities were characterized using Illumina-based next-generation sequencing, while soil chemical parameters were analyzed by spectrophotometry and atomic absorption spectrometry. Metabarcoding analysis provides novel evidence that ameliorant application restructured fungal communities in acidic soils and changed soil chemical properties. Order Onygenales and families Aspergillaceae, Chaetomiaceae, and Terramycetaceae correlated positively with soil pH, base cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+), and soil organic carbon, and negatively with Al3+. In contrast, families Microascaceae and Saccharomycetaceae correlated positively with Al3+ and available phosphorus, and negatively with base cations and cation exchange capacity. The findings highlight the potential of combining biological and organic ameliorants as a sustainable soil amelioration strategy to enhance fungal community activity.Keywords: Ameliorant; Amplicon sequencing, black soldier fly; Ecological function; Enzyme; Microorganism

Introduction

Acidic soil is characterized by a low pH (<5.5), low phosphorus (P) availability, low cation exchange capacity (CEC) and base cation levels, and high exchangeable aluminum (Al), which can cause acidic toxicity and adversely affect plant growth.1 Globally, acidic soil covers 3950 mha, dominated by Spodosols, Alfisols, Oxisols, Ultisols, and Histosols.2 Ultisol and Oxisol soils develop naturally from acidic, low-fertility parent materials.3 Notably, a specific methodological approach is required to enhance soil fertility in acidic soil. Efforts have been made to improve the fertility of acidic soil, including the application of ameliorants.

The use of ameliorants aims to improve soil biophysical conditions and fertility. Various studies have demonstrated that the application of ameliorants can enhance soil biophysical and chemical properties.4, 5 The most common ameliorant used to improve acidic soil is agricultural lime, including agricultural limestone, hydrated lime, dolomite, and calcite, which increases soil pH and reduces exchangeable Al.6 However, combining it with organic matter can provide more sustainable results.7 For instance, black soldier fly (BSF) frass, the feces of BSF larvae (H. illucens), is an organic matter widely used to improve soil fertility. It often contains molted larval exoskeletons,8 undigested feed residues, and beneficial microorganisms.

Black Soldier Flies frass is generated through the breakdown of food waste by BSF larvae. It holds significant promise as a soil ameliorant, an agent that improves soil's physical, chemical, and biological characteristics, including the promotion of nitrogen (N)-fixing and phosphate-solubilizing microbes.9 The BSF frass is rich in beneficial microorganisms that play a vital role in maintaining soil fertility. These microbes enhance nutrient uptake efficiency, help plants withstand environmental stress, and support overall plant development.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 As a bio-ameliorant, BSF frass is effective due to its high content of essential macroelements, such as N, P, potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and micronutrients (e.g., zinc [Zn] and iron [Fe]), as well as microbes that aid in decomposing organic matter. According to Mulatu and Bayata,15 bio-ameliorants enhance soil productivity both directly by increasing nutrient availability and indirectly by improving soil structure. Moreover, its nearly neutral pH helps reduce soil acidity, making it a practical solution for enhancing soil health and fertility. However, the nutrient profile of BSF frass, particularly P, K, and micronutrients, can vary depending on the larval feed.13, 16, 17, 18

Several studies have demonstrated that applying BSF frass as fertilizer can enhance plant growth and increase crop yields (literature). While the effects of BSF frass on the physical and chemical properties of soil have been extensively investigated, its influence on the biological characteristics of acidic soils, particularly soil fungal populations, remains insufficiently understood. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of ameliorant treatment on the dynamics of soil fungal communities in acidic Red Yellow Podzolic soil using next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based metagenomics, a widely adopted method for comprehensive genetic analysis of diverse biological samples.19

Materials and methods

Materials

This study was conducted using Red Yellow Podzolic soil, an acidic soil type originating from Leuwiliang (6°37′56″S, 106°36′42″E), Bogor, West Java. It examined three conditions: untreated soil (US), BSF frass (BF), and soil treated with three ameliorants (TS; BSF frass, lime, and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi [AMF]). The BSF frass was sourced from farms in Depok (6°21′49″S 106°52′9″E), West Java, and had a pH of 8.80, organic carbon (C) content of 40.34%, total N content of 2.10%, diphosphorus pentoxide (P₂O₅) content of 2.94%, and exchangeable K+ content of 105.99 cmolc/kg. The lime was a commercial dolomitic lime containing approximately 29%–32% calcium oxide and 18%–22% magnesium oxide. The AMF inoculum comprised a consortium of Glomus sp., Acaulospora sp., and Gigaspora sp.

Soil incubation

The incubation experiment was conducted in Bogor, West Java, and examined three conditions: US, BF, and TS. The air-dried soil was sieved using a 2 mm sieve and homogenized before incubation. For each condition, 1000 g of material was placed into plastic containers, replicated 3 times (n = 3) and incubated at room temperature for three months. The TS condition consisted of 987.5 g soil, 5 g BSF frass, 5 g lime, and 2.5 g AMF (50 spores), which were thoroughly homogenized. The US and BF treatments were respectively soil and BSF Frass solely incubated. After 3 months of incubation, soil chemical analysis and DNA extraction were performed separately for each parameter. The incubation were arranged at approximately 25–30 °C, and soil humidity was maintained approximately 75–80%.

Soil pH was measured in H₂O at a soil-to-water ratio of 1:5. Organic C was determined using the Walkley and Black method, and total N was measured using the Kjeldahl method. Potential P₂O₅ and K₂O were determined by 25% HCl extraction, while available P was measured using the Bray-1 method. Exchangeable cations were extracted with 1 M NH₄OAc (pH 7.0) and quantified by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). Exchangeable Al3+ and H+ were determined by 1 M KCl extraction, and cation exchange capacity (CEC) was measured by the NH₄OAc (pH 7.0) distillation method. All procedures refer to the Technical Guidelines for Soil Chemical Analysis, Soil and Fertilizer Assembly and Testing Center.20

Library preparation and sequencing

ITS1 metabarcoding analysis was conducted on samples from the three conditions: US, BF, and TS. gDNA samples were amplified with target specific primer (ITS1). Library preparation was performed using the final PCR products.21, 22, 23 The final library was sequenced on (Illumina) platform to generate paired-end raw reads.

Data analysis

Adapter and PCR primer sequences were removed from the paired-end reads using Cutadapt.21 Sequencing errors were corrected, low-quality sequences were removed, and chimeric sequences were removed using DADA2.22, 24 The resulting of fungal amplicon sequence variant (ASV) data were used for taxonomic classification against the UNITE (ITS) database. Downstream analyses and visualizations were performed using appropriate packages in RStudio (R version 4.2.3) and Krona tools. Fungal ASV data were processed using QIIME2 to infer putative functional potential from taxonomically assigned ASVs”. Functional inference from metabarcoding data is indirect and only generates hypotheses.25 Protein sequences were assigned to clusters of orthologous groups (COGs) using eggNOG-mapper. Putative enzyme functions were identified using ECPred to obtain corresponding Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. In parallel, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology identifiers (KOs) were generated using BlastKOALA and then mapped onto specific KEGG pathway identifiers using the KEGG Mapper platform, enabling the reconstruction of the functional profiles and metabolic pathways of the fungal communities. The relationships between soil nutrient content and the 10 most abundant fungal genera were visualized as a heatmap using MetaboAnalyst. Principal component analysis (PCA) of soil nutrient content and the 10 most abundant fungal genera was performed using SIMCA. Input data were normalized and log-transformed according to the platform's recommended protocol to ensure stability and reliability of the multivariate analysis.

Results

Fungal community dynamics

The fungal community dynamics affected by the ameliorant treatments are depicted in Fig. 1, highlighting the dominant fungal communities in three conditions (US, BF, and TS). In the US condition, the fungal community was primarily composed of saprophytic fungi, including Humicola (8%), Talaromyces (5%), and Staphylotrichum (6%). At the species level, the fungal community was dominated by Humicola degenerans, which accounted for approximately 7% of the total fungal population. Other species present in relatively high proportions included Talaromyces sp. (≈5%), Staphylotrichum acaciaicola (≈3%), and Staphylotrichum boninense (≈3%).

Krona diagrams of the fungal communities in the three conditions: untreated soil (S), BSF frass-treated soil (BSF), and ameliorant-treated soil (TS).

The composition of the fungal community differed for the BF condition, with approximately 68% of the total fungal community dominated by the genera Sodiomyces and Scopulariopsis (21%). Among them, the dominant species was Scopulariopsis candida, a saprophytic Ascomycota fungus that plays a vital role in the decomposition of solid organic matter. In addition to S. candida, several other genera and species were detected in smaller proportions, including Aspergillus spp. (≈3%), Issatchenkia orientalis (3%) and Sterigmatomyces halophilus (≈1%).

The TS condition exhibited notable shifts in fungal community composition. The dominant genus was Arcopilus, accounting for approximately 32% of the total fungal population, whereas it comprised only a minority of the community in the US condition. In addition to Arcopilus, several other genera were present in moderate proportions, including Talaromyces, Penicillium, Staphylotrichum, and Boothiomyces. The dominant species identified was Arcopilus amazonicus, a filamentous fungus with strong saprophytic capabilities. Other species detected in lower proportions included Talaromyces spp., Penicillium menonorum, Penicillium madriti, Penicillium raperi, S. boninense, and H. degenerans.

Fungal and soil chemical correlation

Spearman's correlation analysis, combined with scatterplot matrices, revealed a clear pattern in the relationship between soil chemical properties and fungal family composition (Fig. 2). The distribution and correlation matrices indicated that fungal community structure was strongly related to soil chemistry, particularly acidity and base saturation (BS). Significant correlations (p < 0.05) were largely concentrated on these two main axes, indicating that environmental filtering shapes fungal community structure.

Spearman's correlations (upper triangle) with scatterplot matrices (lower triangle) for soil chemical properties and the 10 dominant fungal families.

Exchangeable base cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+), BS, and CEC formed a group with a strong positive correlation (r > 0.85, p < 0.01). Total N content also exhibited a strong positive correlation with base cations and CEC (r > 0.90, p < 0.01). This pattern indicated a consistent soil fertility gradient. Soil pH correlated negatively with Al3+ content (r = −0.61, p = 0.081) and H+ content (r = −0.75, p = 0.020). The significant negative correlation between soil pH and H+ content confirmed that increasing soil acidity was directly associated with increasing proton concentration. Although the relationship between soil pH and Al3+ content was not statistically significant, the negative correlation still supported the role of Al3+ in the soil acidity system.

Several fungi showed a clear relationship to the soil chemical gradient. Order Onygenales, family incertae sedis, and families Aspergillaceae, Chaetomiaceae, Terramycetaceae correlated positively with pH, organic C content, and base cation content (r > 0.70, p < 0.05), and negatively with Al3+ content (r < −0.75; p < 0.05). This pattern suggests that these fungi tend to associate with soil conditions with higher BS and lower acidity. Conversely, the families Microascaceae and Saccharomycetaceae correlated positively with Al3+ content and available P (P₂O₅ Bray), and negatively with base cation content and CEC (|r| > 0.80, p < 0.05). This pattern indicates a preference for more acidic soil conditions. Some other fungi, such as the family Nectriaceae, showed a stronger relationship with total N and base cation content than with pH directly, indicating greater sensitivity to nutritional factors than to acidity.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between conditions based on fungal community structure and soil chemical properties (Fig. 3a). The first two principal components (PCs) explained 99.3% of the total variation in the data, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 73.5% and 25.8%, respectively. The BF samples were distributed on the positive side of PC1, while the US and TS samples separated primarily along PC2, indicating differences in soil biological and chemical responses between conditions.

(a) PCA and PCA 3D plot, showing the distribution of samples based on fungal community composition and soil chemical properties. (b) PCA biplot showing the relationship between fungal communities (taxa) and soil property parameters.

The consistent separation pattern of replicates and the absence of extreme outliers indicated that the observed variation reflects a real treatment effect, rather than random variation. However, PCA is only descriptive and does not directly identify which variables contribute most to the separation between conditions. Therefore, factor scores and loadings were overlayed using SIMCA to identify the fungal taxa and soil parameters predominantly shaping this separation pattern (Fig. 3b). The PCA biplot showed that separation on PC1 was primarily influenced by soil fertility variables, including pH, organic C content, total N content, CEC, BS, and exchangeable base cations, which correlated positively with several major decomposer fungal taxa. This finding provides a mechanistic basis for explaining the sample separation patterns observed in the PCA.

Overall, the PCA biplot (Fig. 3b) revealed that the soil chemical gradient significantly influenced fungal community structure. The first two PCs explained more than 99% of the total variation in the data, indicating that the relationship between fungal taxa and soil parameters can be interpreted with a high degree of reliability.

Fungal phylogenetic tree

A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree of the evolutionary relationships among the ASVs in the microbial community data is presented in Fig. 4. Its structure shows how different ASVs (e.g., ASV1, ASV2, and ASV3) are related based on their genetic similarity. Closely related ASVs are connected by shorter branches and cluster together. Certain ASVs are shared between conditions, while others are unique to a specific condition. Some clusters (e.g., ASV62–ASV46) contain multiple colors, suggesting that they are shared across conditions, whereas others (e.g., ASV58 or ASV31) have only one color, indicating that they are unique to one condition.

Neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree of fungal ASVs across conditions: untreated soil (S), BSF frass (BSF), and ameliorant-treated soil (TS).

Fungal community function

The fungal functional profiles were derived using two complementary methods: fungi functional guild (FUNGuild) and evolutionary gene genealogy non-supervised orthologous groups (eggNOG). The FUNGuild method classifies fungal communities based on their ecological guilds or trophic modes (Fig. 5), whereas the eggNOG method provides functional annotations for genes or metagenomic sequences with previously uncharacterized functions (Table 1). The relative composition of fungal functional guilds (Fig. 5) indicated that the US condition supported a diverse range of guilds, including saprotrophs, plant pathogens, and animal parasites. This diversity suggested a balanced fungal community between decomposers and pathogens, characteristic of a natural soil ecosystem. In contrast, the BF condition supported a fungal community almost entirely dominated by animal pathogens, endophytes, fungal parasites, plant pathogens, and wood saprotrophs.

In the TS condition, the addition of ameliorants altered the functional balance of soil fungi, leading to a shift toward a more balanced distribution of saprotrophic and parasitic groups, along with increased animal parasites and undefined saprotrophs. Bio-ameliorant and soil fertility

After three months, ameliorant treatment (BSF frass, lime, and AMF) of Podsolic soil increased potential potassium oxide (K2O) content, exchangeable Mg and K content, and BS, but not soil pH, organic C content, total N content, C/N ratio, potential P2O5 content, exchangeable Ca and Na content, and CEC (Table 2). The potential K2O content increased from 10.80 mg/100 g (low) to 93.62 mg/100 g (very high). The exchangeable Mg and K content increased from 2.93 cmolc/kg (high) and 0.43 cmolc/kg (medium) to 4.74 cmolc/kg (high) and 3.32 cmolc/kg (very high), respectively. BS increased from 33.86% (low) to 44.89% (moderate). However, the addition of the ameliorants did not change the CEC. Although the organic matter content in BSF frass was relatively high, the applied dosage was insufficient to increase the CEC.

Discussion

Fungal community dynamics

The application of BSF frass, lime, and AMF altered the dominant genera in fungal communities. The fungal community was dominated by three main genera in the US condition—Humicola, Talaromyces, and Staphylotrichum—whereas it was dominated by the genus Arcopilus in the TS condition. In the US condition, the dominant fungal species was H. degenerans, a saprophytic fungus well known for its strong lignocellulose-degrading ability, playing a vital role in the breakdown of complex organic matter.26 This shift in dominance indicates a change in the fungal growth environment following the addition of BSF frass.27 Although the addition of BSF frass, lime, and AMF did not significantly affect soil acidity, Other studies have reported that BSF frass contains considerable amounts of heavy metals.28 Arcopilus is a genus capable of surviving and growing in lead-contaminated soil.29 Therefore, this environmental change likely inhibited the growth of Humicola and Staphylotrichum, while favoring the proliferation of Arcopilus, whose relative abundance increased from less than 3% in the US condition to 32% in the TS condition.

In contrast, the BF condition yielded markedly different results, with the high organic matter content leading to notably low fungal diversity. The fungal community was dominated by the genera Sodiomyces and Scopulariopsis. Sodiomyces, one fungal genus found in the BF condition,30 can produce enzymes and antibiotics and survive and persist under extreme conditions.31 Scopulariopsis, a predominant genus in the BF condition, is a saprophytic genus commonly found in soil, decomposing organic matter, and humid environments.4 Members of this genus are known for their ability to degrade keratin, cellulose, and other complex organic compounds. They are often associated with organic residues such as BSF frass and compost. Among them, the dominant species was S. candida, which typically grows on nutrient-rich substrates such as insect frass, plant debris, and moist organic material. The dominance of this species suggests that the examined condition supports active decomposition processes, particularly of residues rich in protein and crude fiber.

In the TS condition, the dominant species identified was A. amazonicus, a filamentous fungus with strong saprophytic capabilities. The predominance of this species suggests that the fungal community in this condition primarily decomposes solid organic matter, particularly in fiber-rich substrates such as BSF frass. The endophytic fungus A. amazonicus is known to produce oosporein, a red-pigmented dibenzoquinone secondary metabolite.32 Oosporein is synthesized by several fungal genera, including Beauveria, Chaetomium, and Arcopilus, and its functions vary depending on ecological and biological contexts. According to Mascarin et al.33 and Fan et al.,34 oosporein exhibits strong antibacterial and antifungal activities, particularly against soil-borne plant pathogens. In entomopathogenic fungi such as Beauveria bassiana, oosporein contributes to virulence by inducing oxidative stress and damaging insect hemocytes (immune cells). Moreover, previous studies have have Mascarin et al., Nakamura et al., and Geng et al. reported that, as a phenolic quinone capable of redox cycling, oosporein can serve as an antioxidant at low concentrations and as a pro-oxidant at higher concentrations.33, 35, 36 Indeed, oosporein produced by the fungus Chaetomium cupreum enhanced the growth of the plant Miscanthus sinensis under Al stress.37

Fungal and soil chemical correlation

In the PCA, the first PC (PC1) represented the soil fertility gradient, characterized by high pH, organic C, total N, CEC, BS, and exchangeable base cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and Na+). These variables formed a highly correlated group, indicating that soil fertility increased simultaneously across them. This state reflects more chemically stable soils and supports higher biological activity.38

Several fungal taxa, such as Aspergillus, Chaetomiaceae, and Onygenales, were strongly associated with these groups of soil fertility variables,39 indicating that they tend to thrive in soils with relatively higher pH and organic matter content.4 Aspergillus and Chaetomiaceae are known as active decomposers that play a role in organic matter mineralization and the N cycle, so their presence is consistent with increasing soil organic C and total N.40, 41

Conversely, the exchangeable H+ and Al3+ variables were on opposite sides of PC1, exhibiting negative correlations with pH, BS, and CEC. This state represents acidic soils with higher Al saturation.42 Fungal taxa such as Microascaceae and Plectosphaerella were associated with this state, indicating a higher tolerance to acidic soil environments and potentially reflecting early stages of decomposition or chemical soil stress.

The second PC (PC2) separated several fungal groups, including Sordariales and Nectriaceae, which likely respond to environmental factors other than soil chemical fertility, such as specific substrate availability, aeration, or soil disturbance levels. This finding suggests that, in addition to chemical factors, fungal community structure is influenced by more complex microecological conditions.

Our findings confirm that changes in soil chemistry, particularly increases in pH, organic matter, and base cations, play a significant role in shaping the composition of soil fungal communities.43 Their ecological implications are that soil management that improves chemical fertility can orient fungal communities toward taxa that are more active in decomposition and nutrient cycling, thus supporting the sustainability of soil systems.

The Spearman's correlation analysis (Fig. 3) revealed that soil pH and BS are the primary factors influencing fungal community diversity and composition. These findings are consistent with other studies.38, 44 Acidic soils with high Al3+ levels tend to reduce fungal diversity45, 46 and favor acid-tolerant taxa such as Saccharomycetaceae. In contrast, neutral and organic-rich soils support fungal communities dominated by families such as Sordariaceae, Chaetomiaceae, and Tremamycetaceae.

Fungal phylogenetic tree

In the neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4), a single condition with many unique ASVs clustered together suggests a distinct microbial community shaped by environmental conditions. ASVs shared across all conditions could indicate core community members. Microorganisms can correlate positively, neutrally, or negatively for numerous reasons, such as co-occurrence in the same place or competition for niches and nutrients.47

Fungal community function

In the BF condition, the fungal community was less diverse and primarily composed of multifunctional pathogenic–saprotrophic fungi, likely due to the high organic matter content and nutrient-rich conditions48 that promote the growth of opportunistic fungi.49 Fungal functional profiles derived from two complementary methods, FUNGuild and eggNOG, indicate that the application of BSF frass, lime, and AMF influences not only the taxonomic composition but also the ecological functions of soil fungal communities. Francioni et al.50 reported similar findings, finding that adding BSF frass reduced microbial diversity while promoting decomposition and enhancing soil fertility. Arcopilus is a saprophytic fungal genus commonly found in decomposing organic matter,51, 52 such as BSF frass, compost, and humus-rich soils. Members of this genus possess strong lignocellulose-degrading capabilities, contributing significantly to C and nutrient cycling in organic-rich environments.53 In addition to Arcopilus, several other genera were present in moderate proportions, including Talaromyces, Penicillium, Staphylotrichum, and Coniothyrium.

Bio-ameliorant and soil fertility

The K2O and exchangeable K content likely increased due to the release of potential K2O and exchangeable K from BSF frass into the sorption complex and soil solution. The increase in Mg content was likely due to the release of Mg from the BSF frass and dolomite into the soil solution. The high Mg and exchangeable K contents contributed to the increase in BS. Boudabbous et al.54 reported that BSF frass significantly (p < 0.001) influenced K+ content in soil. The pattern across treatments was greater for K+ content in BSF frass–treated soils (30 t/ha), an increase of 41.86% compared to untreated soils.

The significant increase in potential K₂O and exchangeable K content under the TS condition is likely due to the supply of these elements through BSF frass application. The very high total K and exchangeable K contents in BSF frass contribute to the increase in K content, both as K₂O and exchangeable K.

The exchangeable Ca content in the soil did not differ significantly between before and after ameliorant treatment(BSF frass, lime, and AMF). In contrast, the ameliorant treatment increased exchangeable Mg content, although not to the same extent as exchangeable K content. This phenomenon reflects the much lower exchangeable Ca and Mg contents in BSF frass than the exchangeable K content. The three-month incubation following ameliorant application contributed to the relatively modest increases in exchangeable Ca and Mg contents. Similarly, Minato et al.6 reported that six months of lime application influenced exchangeable Ca and Mg contents.

Although divalent cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+ have a stronger affinity for the soil cation exchange complex, the amount of exchangeable Ca and Mg added through BSF frass is far smaller than the amount of exchangeable K and is insufficient to increase their soil contents. In addition, the ameliorant treatment did not significantly alter exchangeable Na content, despite the relatively high exchangeable Na content in BSF frass. Within the lyotropic series, Na+ exhibits the weakest adsorption affinity to soil colloids relative to H+ and K+55. Consequently, Na+ is highly labile and has a limited capacity to displace other exchangeable base cations, unless present in exceedingly high concentrations. Overall, the ameliorant treatment increased soil BS by up to 10.3%, indicating an improvement in the soil's ability to provide plants with nutrients.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that environmental factors and ameliorant treatment (BSF frass, lime, and AMF) significantly influenced the fungal community structure in acidic soils. These microbial changes coincided with improvements in soil chemical properties, suggesting a close relationship between fungal community dynamics and soil physicochemical conditions. Among the tested treatments, BSF frass showed considerable potential as a sustainable bio-ameliorant by promoting favorable changes in both the soil fungal community and soil fertility indicators. These findings highlight the promise of BSF frass as an environmentally friendly strategy for improving soil quality and enhancing the resilience of acidic soils. Future research should investigate the long-term effects of BSF frass in various soils and cropping systems to further confirm its role in sustainable agriculture and ecological restoration in acidic environments.
