To Investigate the Involvement of Mast Cells in the Pathogenesis of Oral Lichen Planus and Oral Lichenoid Reactions
Authors: Neeharika Soorneedi, Mekala M. Sujatha, Tabassum Fatima, P.G.R Pankaj Mallik, Mohammed Imran, Bhavani Nagendra Sangala, Sana Vakeel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Role of mast cells in the development of oral lichen planus (OLP) and oral lichenoid reactions (OLR).Material and Methods:The tissue slices used in this investigation were obtained from the archives of the Department of Oral Pathology and were formalin-fixed and embedded in paraffin. The sections were obtained from 40 cases of OLP that were identified by histological examination, as well as 40 cases of OLR. Twenty buccal mucosa samples were obtained from individuals who showed no mouth lesions and did not participate in any oral practices. The study samples were subjected to haematoxylin and eosin stain, as well as a specialized toluidine blue stain.Results:In oral lichen planus, the mast cell counts were 15.25 ± 2.58, which was substantially greater than in OLR (8.63 ± 1.74) and normal oral mucosal tissues (1.99 ± 0.58). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the average number of mast cells across three groups, and the result was statistically significant (P < 0.001). We found statistically significant results when we compared several groups using the Mann–Whitney U test.Conclusion:This experiment’s findings point to a greater concentration of mast cells in OLP than in OLR. Because of this, we argue that counting mast cells could be an important histopathological component in pathogenesis of these lichenoid reactions and also help distinguishing OLP from OLR.

INTRODUCTION

OLP is a common immune-mediated lesion of the oral mucosa mediated by the formation of the mast cells with clinical symptoms ranging from reticular [Figure 1] to papular to plaque-like to atrophic to bullous to erosive lesions.[123] Mast cells may be distinguished with the application of toluidine blue dye, which facilitates metachromatic staining of their granules.[4] It is also possible to detect mast cells in the material using immunohistochemical labelling methods, which use antibodies that are specific to tryptase and chymase.[567] One important step in initiating the death of basal epithelial cells (basal cell degeneration) is the creation of tumour necrosis factor-α, CD8+ cytotoxic cells, which are numerous in the subepithelial zone of oral lichen planus. Basal epithelial cells that have been antigenically changed secrete RANTES, which stimulates mucosal mast cells and the activated mast cells further triggers mast cell activation and degranulation.[89] Membrane basement membrane breakdown is aided by mast cell-derived enzymes such as chymase and tryptase as well as T-cell–derived matrix metalloproteinase.[1011] Because of this procedure, basal keratinocytes are killed by cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes.[12] Another important immune-mediated lesion is lichenoid reactions whose histopathological examination reveals mixed lymphohistiocytic cell inflammatory infiltrate spreads beyond the epithelium and into the connective tissue.[13]

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The current analysis made use of tissue sections that were encased in paraffin and formalin, and these sections were acquired from the archives of the Department of Oral Pathology. These sections were derived from histopathologically diagnosed instances of OLP (40) (Group B) and OLR (40) (Group C). Department of Medical Records was contacted to get the patients’ demographics and medical history. All the assessments were conducted by individual rate who was a full time researcher. The study underwent complete evaluation and obtained approval from the ethical board SP dental crafts (SPDC/2022/SC27). Twenty buccal mucosa tissues were obtained from healthy volunteers who did not have any oral lesions and did not use their mouths for anything (Group A). Prior to collection, informed permission was acquired from each participant. The tissues were then processed using the same methods used for regular pathology specimens. The research samples underwent staining using both a conventional histopathological haematoxylin and eosin stain, as well as a specialised toluidine blue stain. The H and E staining method from sections of lichen planus revealed a hyperplastic hyper keratinized oral epithelium with an intense subepithelial band of chronic inflammatory lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate changes [Figure 2] along with basal cell degeneration [Figure 3]. Simultaneously, histopathology from sections pertaining to lichenoid reaction cases revealed an inflammatory infiltrate going deep into the lamina propria [Figure 4].

Special stain: Toluidine blue

Toluidine blue O (sigma) dissolved in 100 mL of 70% alcohol yielded a 1 gm stock solution. A 1% sodium chloride solution was prepared, and the pH was varied between 2.0 and 2.5 using glacial acetic acid. A 2.3 pH solution was produced by combining 5 mL of toluidine blue stock solution with 45 mL of a 1% sodium chloride solution. Every time, a freshly made, functional solution was tested and then discarded.

The rotary microtome was used to cut tissue slices measuring 4 μm, which were then placed onto microscopic slides coated with egg albumin. The slides were subjected to a temperature of 58°C on the slide warmer for a duration of 15 minutes. Subsequently, the slides underwent deparaffinization by immersion in three separate instances of fresh xylene, with each immersion lasting for a period of 10 minutes. Subsequently, the specimens underwent dehydration in three successive immersions in 100% alcohol, each lasting for a duration of 5 minutes. The next step was a 10-minute rinsing under running water from the faucet. Following 2 or 3 minutes in a working solution of toluidine blue, the portions were rinsed three times with distilled water. Subsequently, the slides underwent rapid dehydration using 95% alcohol, followed by two rounds of 100% alcohol. They were then subjected to two rounds of xylene for clearing purposes and then mounted with DPX. The mast cells exhibited a rich purple staining pattern in contrast to the blue backdrop. The sections revealed a intact mast cells in oral lichenoid reaction lesions and lichen planus along with degranulation of mast cells seen in lichen planus [Figures 5 and 6]. A total of ten randomly chosen fields were seen using a compound light microscope at a greater magnification of 40x to count mast cells.

RESULT AND DISCUSSION

Participants’ ages range from 18 to 68 years with Group A patients with an average age of 30.25 ± 3.85 years, Group B patients with an average age of 44.15 ± 4.25 years, and Group C patients with an average age of 44.78 ± 3.69 years. Males to females ratio in Group A was 1:22, in Group B was 1.67:1, and in Group C 1:1.86. Four distinct anatomical areas—the buccal mucosa, tongue, lip, and gingiva—constituted the research sample. The buccal mucosa was the only source of normal tissue specimens used in this study. 96% of cases with oral lichen planus and 58% of cases with oral lichenoid reactions showed Wickham’s striae. DISCUSSION

Oral lichen planus is an autoimmune disorder mediated by T cells, namely, CD8+ T lymphocytes. Mast cells and intraepithelial CD8+ T lymphocytes contribute to the pathophysiology of OLP since they coexist at the locations of basement membrane rupture in this disorder.[13] Mast cells and T cells work together to promote the development of persistent lesions in OLP. This theory recognizes the possibility that T-cell migration from blood vessels to epithelium and lamina propria is aided by the extracellular matrix’s breakdown.[14] In OLP, many proinflammatory mediators, such as chymase, TNF-α, and tryptase, are produced during mast cell degranulation. When mast cells degranulate, TNF-α is released, which causes T-cell RANTES expression to increase. Matrix metalloproteinases are secreted also by activated T cells inside lesions, and degradation of the basement membrane is caused by these matrix metalloproteinases.[15]

In cases of OLR, mast cells are recruited to the affected region in response to the interaction with foreign antigens, which triggers a hyperimmune response in the setting of OLR. This subsequent process leads to the induction of apoptosis in keratinocytes.[16] Hence, our work has effectively shown the challenges associated with distinguishing between the two circumstances due to the overlapping nature of their value ranges. From a clinical perspective, it is worth noting that OLR has the potential to resemble OLP. However, it is important to highlight that these reactions often manifest unilaterally, in contrast to the bilateral presentation commonly seen in cases of OLP.[17] The lamina propria of OLR has also been shown to contain wide spread mast cells in the connective tissue in contrast to OLP where the inflammation is predominantly subepithelially. Nevertheless, the exact contribution of these cells to the development of OLR, akin to OLP, is still poorly understood. Detecting intact mast cells in highly populated inflammatory processes using traditional pathology approaches is very difficult, but in or study, we were able to effectively identify these intact mast cells by using ckit/CD117 immunohistochemistry labelling. One specific method for labelling mast cells involves the use of an antibody that binds to this receptor, which is also known as the mast/stem cell growth factor receptor. The use of this staining approach reveals a prominent staining pattern in the cytoplasm or cytoplasmic membrane of mast cells, which proves to be especially useful in identifying cells that are structurally intact. However, the process of mast cell degranulation is an initial occurrence that may exhibit a strong correlation with the age of the lesion.[1718]

OLP mostly affects individuals over the age of 40 years, with a higher incidence seen in females.[18] However, our sample exhibited a modest inclination for males. This phenomenon may be ascribed to a rather limited sample size. Our research[19] likewise observed a strong preference for OLR among females, regardless of its underlying causes. Numerous investigations have consistently shown that the buccal mucosa is the primary location for the incidence of OLP and OLR inside the oral cavity.[20] In the cohort of 80 cases examined, it was shown that around 75% of the lesions were located in the buccal mucosa, a finding that aligns with previous investigations. Starting with normal oral tissues, the samples showed a steady increase in mast cell population, moving on to OLR, and eventually to OLP. Mast cells are involved in the formation and progression of both OLP and OLR, as the mast cell count was shown to be considerably greater in the former case compared to the latter. Several additional investigations done by other authors have shown similar results to those seen in our research.[21]

CONCLUSION

The findings of this experiment indicate that there is a greater abundance of mast cells in OLP in comparison to OLR. Hence, we suggest that assessing the mast cell count might be a crucial histopathological determinant for distinguishing between OLP and OLR.
