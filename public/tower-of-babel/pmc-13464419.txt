Nitrogen-doped rGO-supported 2D MnO2 catalysts for improved CO2 electroreduction
Authors: Abhishek Dhasmana, Sravendra Rana, Jagriti, Ajay, Pravin P. Ingole, Soni Mishra, Sumant Upadhyay, Abhishek K. Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (NrGO), manganese dioxide (MnO2), and a composite (NrGO–MnO) were synthesized via hydrothermal treatment, where the confined hydrothermal conditions facilitate controlled nucleation and anisotropic two-dimensional growth. Structural and morphological analyses confirmed the formation of poorly crystalline MnO fragments uniformly dispersed on the NrGO framework, effectively preventing graphene restacking and enhancing surface accessibility. BET surface area analysis revealed that NrGO–MnO exhibited a significantly higher specific surface area (62.56 m2 g−1) and pore volume (0.39 cm3 g−1) compared to pristine materials. Electrochemical characterization under CO2 saturated electrolytes demonstrated that the NrGO–MnO composite outperformed both NrGO and MnO2. Linear sweep voltammetry revealed a higher current density of approximately 12.0 mA cm−2 and 16.0 mA cm−2, respectively at −1.7 V vs. Ag/AgCl for both NrGO and NrGO–MnO electrodes under CO2 saturated conditions, accompanied by a more positive onset potential around −0.90 to −1.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl In contrast, MnO2 exhibited a lower current density of about 5.0 mA cm−2 with a more negative onset potential. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed lower charge-transfer resistance for NrGO and NrGO–MnO composites compared to MnO2, indicating enhanced electron and ion transport. The developed NrGO–MnO catalyst demonstrates improved in CH4 synthesis with a maximum faradaic efficiency of about 27%, emphasising its unique selectivity for deeper CO2 reduction products. These findings highlight interfacial synergy in catalyst design and offer guidance for developing scalable, high-performance CO2 electroreduction systems.

Introduction

The electrochemical conversion of CO2 into valuable fuels is a promising strategy for mitigating climate change. Excessive CO2 emissions have driven severe global warming, and turning this greenhouse gas into feedstocks can help close the carbon cycle.1 In particular, CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) has emerged as a transformative electrochemical technology that enables the conversion of carbon dioxide into value-added chemicals and fuels,1 where the process starts with CO2 adsorption on the catalyst surface that further goes reaction intermediates to convert the value added products via proton-coupled electron transfer CO2RR pathways. Recent reviews highlight that two-dimensional (2D) graphene-based materials are desirable as CO2RR catalysts, due to their unique electronic and structural characteristics (high surface area, tunable chemistry, and conductivity), which can deliver high activity, selectivity, and stability.2 These carbon materials are also earth-abundant and non-toxic, aligning with the goals of green catalysis.2 Moreover, key design strategies such as heteroatom doping and hybridization with metals can further optimize graphene's catalytic performance.3 Graphene oxide (GO) is a layered carbon material derived from graphene but decorated with oxygen-containing groups. It retains the single-atom-thick hexagonal carbon lattice of graphene while being rich in functional groups like hydroxyls, epoxides, and carboxyls. These oxygen groups make GO chemically versatile, but also render it less electrically conductive than pristine graphene.4 Specifically, lattice of sp2 carbon of GO is still intact (it is a true 2D material), but its ordered structure is disrupted, making it relatively inert toward direct CO2 binding.5 Pristine GO (like graphene) shows limited CO2RR activity on its own because the unmodified carbon surface binds CO2 weakly.2 GO serves as a pivotal support material in catalytic systems. The high surface area and hydrophilicity of GO facilitate the adsorption of catalyst particles. In CO2RR systems, GO frequently acts as a scaffold for metal or metal–oxide nanoparticles, helping to create large electrode–electrolyte interfaces.6 Recently, CNTs coordination-embedded into copper–pyrazole MOFs for selective electrocatalytic CO2 to C2H4 demonstrated that incorporating conductive carbon nanostructures into electrocatalysts significantly enhances electron transport and improves CO2 electroreduction performance.7 This finding underscores the importance of conductive carbon supports, which motivates the use of nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (N-rGO) in the present work to facilitate charge transfer and enhance the electrocatalytic activity of MnO2. Incorporating heteroatoms into graphene is an effective way to create active sites for electrocatalysis.8 Nitrogen (N) is particularly effective since it has a similar atomic size to carbon and can integrate into the graphene lattice in various bonding configurations (pyridinic, pyrrolic, graphitic N, etc.).9 N doping breaks the symmetry of the carbon network and introduces localized electronic states, which can enhance adsorption and activation of reactant molecules.10 Wang et al. reported the remarkable metal free N doped reduced graphene oxide (NrGO) which itself can catalyse CO2 reduction, converting CO2 into formate.11 Yuan et al. reported that pyridoxine-functionalized graphene oxide (GO), with a pyridinic nitrogen content of 2.32%, exhibited the highest catalytic activity for electrochemical CO2 reduction, achieving a faradaic efficiency of up to 45.8% for liquid fuel production.12 They further demonstrated that different pyridine derivatives functionalized on GO selectively converted CO2 into ethanol and acetone, highlighting the role of pyridinic N content and molecular structure in tuning catalytic performance.12 Later, Chang et al. reported through quantum mechanical simulations at −1.0 V vs. SHE that only 2 out of 26 screened N-doped graphene sites could effectively reduce CO2 to CO, featuring a rate-determining barrier of approximately 0.55 eV and thermodynamically favourable pathways.13 However, the activity of the CO2RR changes according to the content of pyridinic N, making it challenging to achieve consistent performance across different synthesis batches with varying nitrogen type distributions.14

Recently, 2D transition metal oxides (2D TMOs) have been actively investigated for CO2 capture and conversion, and they are already established as a better photocatalytic/electrocatalytic material compared to other materials in the same class.15,16 Unlike NrGO's inconsistent conductivity, 2D TMOs have enhanced electronic properties, such as high electron mobility and carrier concentration, which dramatically improve electron transfer from the electrode to CO2 molecules. The metal centers in TMOs are considered active catalytic sites, providing intrinsic activity that GO lacks due to its neutral carbon atoms.17,18 2D manganese oxide (MnO2) is a layered transition-metal oxide that can form ultrathin nanosheets or nanoplatelets. MnO2 exists in several polymorphs (α, β, γ, δ (birnessite), etc.),19 is one of the efficient materials to electrochemically reduce CO2. Peng et al. demonstrated that MnO2 nanosheet arrays supported on nickel foam convert CO2 into CO with a large current density (14.1 mA cm−2) at a potential of −0.95 V, excellent faradaic efficiency (71%), and high electrochemical stability (10 h).20 MnO2 has rich oxidation states (Mn2+, Mn3+, Mn4+) that participate in redox reactions. This multivalency gives MnO2 a wide electrochemical potential window and the ability to mediate multi-electron transfers.21 Hybrid catalysts combining carbon and metal-oxide components can exploit synergistic effects.22,23 An NrGO–MnO nanocomposite is one such approach; the carbon layer supplies conductivity and additional active sites, while MnO2 provides its redox-active metal centres.

Despite reports of CO2 electroreduction using Mn-based catalysts supported on nitrogen-doped carbon materials, these catalysts mostly favour CO generation. For example, MnO/N-doped graphene aerogel with an 86% CO faradaic efficiency was reported by Wang et al.24 On the other hand, the MnO2–NrGO catalyst created in this work, a rarely studied system for CO2RR, shows improved CH4 synthesis with a maximum faradaic efficiency of about 27%, emphasising its unique selectivity for deeper CO2 reduction products. In this work, we report four significant 2D materials: graphene oxide (GO), nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (NrGO), manganese dioxide (MnO2), and the NrGO–MnO nanocomposite, and discuss their roles in CO2 electroreduction toward useful fuels.

Experimental section

Chemicals and materials

Graphite powder (1 g, 99% purity), sodium nitrate (NaNO3, 0.5 g, 99% purity), concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 23 mL, 98% purity), potassium permanganate (KMnO4, 3–6.32 g, 99% purity), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 10 mL, 30% aqueous solution), ethanol (C2H5OH, analytical grade, ≥99% purity), melamine (C3H6N6, 200 mg, 99% purity), and ammonium oxalate ((NH4)2C2O4, 2.52 g, 99% purity) were all purchased from Loba Chemie (India). Deionized (DI) water was consistently used in all steps for solution preparation, washing, and purification.

All chemicals were of analytical grade (≥99% purity) and employed as received without any further purification or treatment. Graphite powder served as the precursor for graphene oxide (GO) synthesis. Sodium nitrate and concentrated sulfuric acid provided the acidic and oxidative medium in the modified Hummers' method,25 while potassium permanganate acted as the principal oxidizing agent. Hydrogen peroxide was used to quench excess permanganate and terminate the oxidation reaction. Ethanol functioned as both a dispersion medium and a washing solvent during various synthesis stages. Melamine acted as a nitrogen source for the preparation of nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (NrGO). Ammonium oxalate served as the reductant, while potassium permanganate provided the oxidant in the hydrothermal synthesis of MnO2 nanosheets.

Preparation of GO and NrGO

GO was synthesized using a modified Hummers' method4, where to increase the spacing between graphene sheets, graphite powder (1 g), sodium nitrate (NaNO3; 0.5 g), and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4; 23 mL) were mixed in a round-bottom flask and stirred in an ice bath for about 15 minutes. Potassium permanganate (3 g) was then added gradually, with the mixture showing a progressive color change from green to nearly black over the next 2 hours (stirring continued for an additional 2 hours). Following this, deionized (DI) water (46 mL) was added dropwise, which resulted in the evolution of purple fumes due to oxidizing species. After a short interval, more DI water was added, turning the reaction mixture purplish black. Subsequently, hydrogen peroxide (10 mL) was added slowly to stop the reaction, followed by the addition of more DI water (140 mL) for dilution. The final product was repeatedly centrifuged and washed with DI water/ethanol to remove acidic residues and isolate graphene oxide. Finally, the GO powder was obtained after drying at 70 °C in a vacuum oven for 8 h. Further to obtain NrGO, 80 mg GO powder was dispersed in ethanol. Then, 200 mg of melamine was added to the mixture. Grey powder was obtained after stir-drying the mixture. The obtained powder was annealed at 600 °C for 10 minutes under an argon atmosphere. The powder was then washed with DI water and ethanol to obtain NrGO, which was further dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven for 12 h.

Preparation of MnO2 and NrGO–MnO nanocomposite

MnO2 nanosheets were synthesized through a redox-assisted hydrothermal approach. Initially, 2.52 g of ammonium oxalate was dissolved in 140 mL of deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring to ensure homogeneous mixing, followed by the gradual addition of 6.32 g KMnO4. In this system, permanganate (Mn7+) acts as a strong oxidizing agent, while oxalate ions serve as the reducing counterpart, leading to the reduction of Mn7+ to Mn4+ and subsequent precipitation of MnO2. The reaction mixture was then transferred into a 200 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 90 °C. The confined hydrothermal conditions facilitated controlled nucleation and anisotropic two-dimensional growth, thereby favoring the formation of sheet-like morphologies. After natural cooling to room temperature, the product was washed repeatedly with deionized water to remove residual ions and soluble by-products, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C. Further to synthesize NrGO–MnO nanocomposite, we followed the same route used in NrGO synthesis. However, 80 mg of as-synthesized MnO2 nanosheets were also added while adding melamine. Finally, NrGO–MnO nanocomposite was obtained (see Fig. 1).

Characterization techniques

Materials characterization

The structural properties of the synthesized samples were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Bruker D8 Advance EcoPro diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.54056 Å). Data were collected over a 2θ range of 5°–80°, enabling phase identification and crystallinity assessment. Surface morphology and microstructural features were analysed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, ZEISS Ultra Plus) operated at 15 kV. High-resolution images were acquired at magnifications ranging from 5K to 150K, corresponding to spatial resolutions between 5 µm and 200 nm. Specific surface area and porosity were evaluated using N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms obtained on an Autosorb 6100 FKM MP-AG analyser. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was employed to calculate surface area, while pore volume was derived from the adsorption branch of the isotherm. Thermal stability of the samples was assessed under a nitrogen atmosphere by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using a NEXTA STA 200 instrument (Hitachi High-Tech Science Corporation, Japan). Measurements were performed up to 800 °C to evaluate weight loss behaviour and decomposition characteristics under heating. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out to analyse the elemental composition and chemical states of the NrGO–MnO nanocomposite.

Electrochemical measurements

2 mg of catalyst was dissolved in 30 µL of isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and 20 µL of 1 weight percent PVDF binder solution to create catalyst inks. Before being used, the resultant suspension was drop-cast onto a glassy carbon electrode, ultrasonicated, and allowed to air dry. A two-compartment, three-electrode electrochemical cell was used for the electrochemical measurements. The working electrode was a glassy carbon electrode coated with catalyst, the reference electrode was an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode, and the counter electrode was a Pt mesh. 0.1 M KHCO3 saturated with either Ar or CO2 served as the electrolyte.

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were made in the potential range of −0.2 to −1.6 V versus Ag/AgCl/Cl− at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. An AC perturbation was used to evaluate electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at −1.5 V versus Ag/AgCl/Cl− throughout a frequency range of 1 Hz to 20 kHz.

Product quantification

Experiments using controlled-potential chronoamperometry were conducted in CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte at −1.2, −1.4, and −1.6 V vs. Ag/AgCl. At each applied potential, electrolysis was carried out for one hour. A Thermo Scientific TRACE 1110 gas chromatograph fitted with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a flame ionisation detector (FID) was used to collect and analyse the gaseous products produced during electrolysis from the cathodic compartment. For product analysis, 1 mL gas samples were injected into the GC using argon as the carrier gas.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction analysis

The crystalline structure of the as-prepared GO, NrGO, MnO2, and NrGO–MnO samples was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), as shown in Fig. 2a and b.

XRD pattern of (a) GO and NrGO, (b) MnO2 and NrGO–MnO. SEM images with 150K magnification of (c) GO, (d) NrGO, (e) MnO2, (f) NrGO–MnO nanocomposite at 15 kV.

In Fig. 2a the diffraction pattern of GO exhibits a sharp peak at 2θ = 11.95°, corresponding to the (001) plane with an interlayer spacing of 7.4 Å. This large d-spacing arises due to the intercalation of oxygenated functional groups and adsorbed moisture between the graphene oxide layers, which increases the interlayer distance as compared to pristine graphite.26 In contrast, the XRD pattern of NrGO shows a prominent peak at 2θ = 26.30°, indexed to the (002) plane, with a calculated interlayer spacing of 3.38 Å. The shift of the diffraction peak from low angle (GO) to higher angle (NrGO) clearly indicates the removal of oxygen functionalities and the partial restoration of the sp2 graphitic framework upon reduction and nitrogen doping. Additionally, the broader nature of the (002) reflection in NrGO suggests the formation of a more disordered and turbostratic structure compared to crystalline graphite.27 These observations confirm the successful conversion of GO into NrGO with decreased interlayer spacing and enhanced graphitic character.

XRD pattern of MnO2 and NrGO–MnO nanocomposite is shown in Fig. 2b, the XRD patterns of pristine MnO2 (black curve) and the NrGO–MnO nanocomposite (red curve) reveal distinct structural characteristics. For pure MnO2, the diffraction peaks observed at 2θ ≈ 12.2° (001), 25.7° (002), 37.4° (100), and 66.1° (110) can be indexed to δ-MnO2, in agreement with JCPDS No. 80-1098.28 However, the peaks are relatively broad and of low intensity, which indicates that the MnO2 crystallites are poorly crystalline and exist predominantly in nanoplate and nanosheet morphologies, where limited long-range ordering is retained. In the NrGO–MnO nanocomposite, a broad feature around 2θ ≈ 24–26° is attributed to the (002) plane of nitrogen doped reduced graphene oxide, signifying partial reduction of NrGO and reformation of sp2 carbon domains. Alongside this, sharp peaks at 2θ ≈ 36.9° (111), 42.7° (200), 56.6° (220), 66.3° (311), and 75.5° (222) correspond to the cubic MnO phase (JCPDS 07-0230), suggesting that part of the MnO2 undergoes reduction to MnO during the composite synthesis due to the reductive environment provided by NrGO. The broadening of the carbon (002) peak and the suppressed crystallinity in the composite imply intimate interaction between MnO nanoparticles and NrGO sheets, with MnO domains well-dispersed across the carbon matrix. This interaction not only hinders agglomeration of MnO2 nanosheets but also enhances structural stability and conductivity of the hybrid. Thus, the XRD results confirm that the NrGO–MnO composite consists of poorly crystalline MnO2 nanoplates/nanosheets embedded within a conductive NrGO framework, along with the formation of crystalline MnO domains, which together are expected to improve electrochemical performance by combining high surface area, abundant active sites, and enhanced charge transport pathways.

SEM analysis

The SEM images in Fig. 2c–f reveal that the graphite-derived materials (GO and NrGO) appear as thin, crumpled nanosheets, while the MnO2 phase shows a distinct platy/flower-like morphology. In GO (Fig. 2c), the sheets form multi-layered stacks with extensive wrinkling and folding, reflecting the layered nature imparted by oxygen functionalities. After reduction and N-doping, the NrGO (Fig. 2d) still retains the sheet-like topology but exhibits more loosely stacked, ultrathin layers. Indeed, literature reports confirm that GO typically appears as large, wrinkled lamellar sheets,29 whereas reduced graphene materials show much thinner with more corrugation.29 The SEM thus indicates that thermal reduction/N-doping exfoliates the carbon network, producing a highly porous, networked sheet morphology with many open edges and voids. These voids and wrinkles between sheets create a high surface area (“mesoporous”) carbon scaffold, consistent with prior observations that post-treated GO yields ultra-thin sheets.27 In contrast, the bare MnO2 sample (Fig. 2e) exhibits a markedly different morphology. The oxide appears as aggregates of nanoflakes or nanoplates, forming a porous, “flower-like” assembly. Rather than smooth sheets, the MnO2 is composed of interconnected platelets or petals that cluster into micron-scale agglomerates. Such hierarchical nanoflower architectures are well documented for MnO2 prepared by hydrothermal routes.30 These assemblies consist of ultrathin MnO2 nanosheets that radiate from a core, yielding a rough surface and abundant interstitial porosity. Although the SEM does not show the internal crystallinity, the XRD (Fig. 2b) confirms poor crystalline MnO2 phases, consistent with literature reports of flower-structured MnO2 nanomaterials.31,32

The NrGO–MnO composite (Fig. 2f) combines these two motifs. In the SEM image, the darker graphene-like sheets form a continuous background, with lighter MnO fragmented nanosheets intimately decorating their surfaces. This intimate integration is evident for MnO nanosheets are embedded on the NrGO surface, interrupting and separating the graphene sheets. Such uniform dispersion prevents MnO aggregation and stabilizes the layered structure, as previously noted for NrGO–MnO hybrids.33 The composite morphology thus shows a hierarchical porous network MnO2 fragments fill the inter-sheet gaps and create additional nano-pores. In sum, the SEM suggests that the NrGO and MnO phases are well mixed at the nanoscale, forming a highly convoluted sheet–platelet architecture. (XRD results are consistent with this interpretation, showing only broad GO/rGO peaks and distinct MnO reflections, indicating amorphous carbon stacking and crystalline MnO in the composite.)

BET analysis

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms (Fig. 3a) and BET data reveal significant differences among the three samples (Table 1). Bare nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (NrGO) displays a very low surface area (6.06 m2 g−1) and limited pore volume (0.12 cm3 g−1), characteristic of restacked graphene sheets with minimal accessible porosity. By contrast, MnO2 exhibits a markedly higher BET surface area of 42.33 m2 g−1 and a pore volume of 0.42 cm3 g−1, consistent with its nanocrystalline morphology and porous grain boundaries. Strikingly, the NrGO–MnO composite achieves the highest BET surface area (62.56 m2 g−1), surpassing both constituents, while its pore volume (0.39 cm3 g−1) remains close to that of pure MnO2. This enhancement suggests that MnO fragments anchor onto NrGO sheets, effectively preventing graphene restacking while simultaneously introducing interfacial mesopores. Similar synergistic increases in surface area have been reported for other graphene metal oxide hybrids,34 where oxide nanosheets serve as spacers to preserve exfoliation and maximize accessible surface.35,36 Clearly, the BET results confirm that coupling MnO with NrGO yields a highly porous composite structure favourable for electrochemical reactions.

TGA analysis

The thermogravimetric (TGA) curves (Fig. 3b–d) further illuminate the material differences (Table 1). For NrGO (Fig. 3b), only a minor weight loss occurs at low temperature (typically <10% below ∼200 °C) from desorbing water and labile oxygen groups. Above ∼200–300 °C, the mass remains essentially constant, reflecting that the graphene lattice (now largely reduced) is thermally stable. This behavior parallels literature reports: fully oxidized GO can lose ∼50% of its mass by 800 °C, whereas reduced graphene (rGO) shows much smaller losses because most oxygen groups are absent.37 Likewise, nitrogen-doped graphene (NrGO) behaves like rGO, exhibiting only modest weight change on heating beyond moisture removal.

Pure MnO2 (Fig. 3c) also shows limited weight loss. An initial ∼5–10% loss below ∼200 °C is typical, due to removal of adsorbed or bound water. Above this range, as synthesised MnO2 is quite stable, only slight changes occur at higher temperatures where MnO2 can decompose (e.g. to Mn2O3 or Mn3O4) in air.38 Such small mass changes for metal oxides have been reported, Gil et al. reported only a few percent loss for MnO2 up to 500–600 °C.39 In the NrGO–MnO composite (Fig. 3d), the TGA profile combines these features. The weight loss below ∼200 °C (∼3–8%) arises mainly from water on the MnO and any remaining oxygen functionalities on NrGO. Above that, the composite shows the gradual, low-rate loss characteristic of the carbon component, while the MnO phase remains mostly intact. Overall, the composite retains most of its mass up to ∼500 °C, indicating good thermal stability of both the reduced carbon and the MnO framework. These TGA trends minimal carbon loss for reduced graphene and only modest water loss in MnO, are consistent with prior studies40 and confirm that the NrGO–MnO hybrid is stable under heat.

XPS analysis of NrGO–MnO nanocomposite

The XPS survey confirms the successful formation of the NrGO–MnO composite by the clear presence of C, N, O and Mn core-level features (Fig. 4a). A π–π* shake-up satellite near ∼290–292 eV further corroborates the preserved aromatic character of the carbon framework. A weak high-binding-energy shoulder in the C 1s region is assigned to C–F species and is most likely an adventitious impurity rather than an intrinsic feature of the NrGO–MnO framework. Such fluorinated contamination can originate from PTFE tubing/filters, or trace surface residues introduced during sample handling and does not reflect the intended chemistry of the composite. The N 1s spectrum is composed principally of pyridinic (∼398–399 eV) and graphitic (∼400.8–401.6 eV) nitrogen species (Fig. 4c), with a notable pyridinic fraction that is expected to act as anchoring sites for Mn and to enhance charge transfer kinetics. O 1s deconvolution (Fig. 4d) resolves lattice Mn–O at ≈530–530.5 eV, an intermediate Oads/CO component at ≈531.5–532.5 eV, and an H2O/OH feature at ≈533–534 eV, implying coexistence of lattice oxygen, defect oxygen and surface hydroxyls. The relatively large intermediate Oads contribution suggests abundant surface oxygen species and probable oxygen vacancies, which increase surface reactivity. In the Mn 2p region (Fig. 4e), the spin–orbit doublet (2p3/2 ≈ 641.6–641.8 eV, 2p1/2 ≈ 653.2–653.5 eV) deconvolutes into dominant Mn2+ component with a smaller Mn4+ shoulder, indicating a mixed-valence MnOx environment rather than a pure Mn4+ oxide. Such mixed valence favors oxygen-vacancy formation to maintain charge balance and supports redox activity beneficial for catalytic oxidation. Modest binding-energy shifts in both C 1s and Mn 2p, combined with the N-speciation, point to electronic coupling at the NrGO–MnO interface where pyridinic and graphitic N stabilize Mn centers and facilitate interfacial charge transfer. The XPS fingerprints indicate a material architecture that balances high electronic conductivity (restored sp2 carbon and graphitic N) with abundant active sites (pyridinic N, oxygen vacancies, mixed-valence Mn), rationalizing enhanced catalytic/electrochemical performance. Overall, XPS supports a model in which NrGO stabilizes mixed-valence MnOx species, promotes oxygen vacancy formation and interfacial charge transfer, and thereby creates an active, electronically connected platform for catalytic oxidation for CO2 conversion.

Electrochemical analysis and product quantification

Electrochemical measurements were performed in a two-compartment, three-electrode cell configuration. The catalyst inks (2 mg catalyst dispersed in IPA and 1% PVDF binder) were drop-casted onto glassy carbon electrodes, while a platinum mesh and an Ag/AgCl/Cl− (sat. KCl) electrode served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. A 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte was employed under both Ar and CO2 saturated conditions. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was used to evaluate CO2 reduction activity, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted at −1.5 V vs. Ag/AgCl/Cl− (sat. KCl) to probe charge-transfer kinetics.

The LSV profiles (Fig. 5a–c) reveal distinct electrochemical signatures for each material system across the potential window of −0.2 to −1.6 V vs. Ag/AgCl/Cl−. For the NrGO electrode (Fig. 5a), the current density exhibits a gradual increase with increasingly negative potentials, reaching approximately −20 mA cm−2 at −1.7 V in an Ar-saturated environment and −12 mA cm−2 at 1.7 V in a CO2-saturated environment. The onset potential for significant current generation occurs at approximately −1.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl/Cl− under CO2 saturation. The bare MnO2 electrode (Fig. 5b) exhibits markedly different electrochemical behavior, with current densities reaching approximately −6.0 mA cm−2 at −1.7 V. The material shows minimal activity difference between Ar and CO2 environments, suggesting limited direct CO2 reduction capability. The onset potential is shifted to more negative values (∼−1.2 V vs. Ag/AgCl/Cl−), indicating higher overpotential requirements for electrochemical processes. The NrGO–MnO composite (Fig. 5c) demonstrates the most promising electrochemical performance, achieving current densities exceeding −20 mA cm−2 at −1.7 V in Ar saturated and 16 mA cm−2 at −1.7 V in a CO2-saturated environment. The synergistic effect between the two components is evident, as the composite exhibits enhanced current response under CO2 saturation compared to individual components. The onset potential is favourably shifted to approximately −0.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl/Cl−, representing an optimal balance between the constituent materials properties. The EIS measurements (Fig. 5d) provide crucial insights into the charge transfer characteristics and electrode kinetics. The Nyquist plots reveal semicircular features at high frequencies, characteristic of charge transfer resistance (Rct), followed by linear regions at low frequencies indicative of diffusion-controlled processes.

Our NrGO–MnO composite exhibits a favourable onset potential (−0.8 V) and delivers a current density of 16 mA cm−2 under CO2 saturation, exceeding values reported for a binder-free MnO2 nanosheet array (14.1 mA cm−2, 71% faradaic efficiency for CO)41 and a halogen/nitrogen dual-coordinated Mn single-atom catalyst (∼10 mA cm−2 at a 0.49 V overpotential, 97% FE(CO)).42 This enhancement highlights the benefit of coupling MnO with a conductive, nitrogen-doped carbon matrix, which likely improves charge transfer and active-site accessibility relative to bulk or atomically dispersed Mn-oxide systems alone. Wang et al.,24 , described three-dimensional nitrogen-doped graphene aerogel-supported MnO nanoparticles (MnO/NGA) as an effective catalyst for CO2 reduction to CO, reaching a maximum CO faradaic efficiency of almost 86%. In contrast, the present NrGO–MnO2 composite promotes deeper reduction pathways leading to methane formation, achieving a maximum CH4 faradaic efficiency of approximately 27% at −1.4 V vs. Ag/AgCl. Similarly, due to poor stabilisation of hydrogenated *CO intermediates, pure MnO2 catalysts described in literature typically show strong selectivity toward CO and little hydrocarbon synthesis. Thus, the improved CH4 production for the NrGO–MnO2 composite indicates the advantageous synergistic interaction between the conductive nitrogen-doped graphene framework and MnO2 catalytic sites.

Equivalent circuit models comprising solution resistance (Rs), charge-transfer resistance (Rct), constant phase elements (CPE), and additional resistance-capacitance elements as needed were used to fit the EIS spectra. The fitted parameters and circuits are shown in Fig. 6. For NrGO, MnO2, and NrGO–MnO2, the solution resistance values were determined to be 5.98, 6.33, and 4.62 Ω, respectively. Significantly better electron-transfer kinetics at the electrode/electrolyte interface were indicated by the charge-transfer resistance, which dropped from 62.8 Ω for NrGO and 21.4 Ω for MnO2 to just 11.2 Ω for the NrGO–MnO2 composite. Additionally, the composite showed higher CPE admittance values than the individual components, indicating a greater electrochemically accessible surface area and improved interfacial charge buildup.

Fitted equivalent EIS circuits.

The MnO2 electrode exhibits the highest impedance values, consistent with its poor electrochemical activity observed in LSV measurements. This high impedance reflects sluggish charge transfer kinetics and limited electrical conductivity of the pristine oxide material. In contrast, the NrGO electrode demonstrates significantly lower impedance characteristics, confirming the superior electrical conductivity imparted by the nitrogen-doped graphene framework. The NrGO–MnO composite exhibits intermediate impedance behavior, representing an optimal compromise between the high surface area and catalytic activity of MnO and the excellent electrical conductivity of NrGO. The impedance analysis corroborates the LSV findings, demonstrating that the composite architecture successfully addresses the conductivity limitations of MnO2 while maintaining its beneficial surface properties for electrochemical applications. The reduced charge transfer resistance in the composite system directly correlates with the enhanced current densities observed in the voltametric measurements, confirming the synergistic effect of the hybrid electrode design.

Product quantification

The product quantification was done using Gas Chromatography system. Faradaic efficiencies (FE) for CO2 electroreduction on MnO2, NrGO, and NrGO–MnO composite electrodes were evaluated at −1.2, −1.4, and −1.6 V vs. Ag/AgCl (Fig. 7). For all catalysts, H2 evolution remained the dominant reaction. On MnO2, CH4 showed a slight increase with increasing cathodic potential, reaching a maximum at −1.4 V and then decreasing again. The CH4 faradaic efficiency for MnO2 rose from roughly 10% at −1.2 V to 17% at −1.4 V before falling to 14% at −1.6 V. In contrast, the CO faradaic efficiency decreased from roughly 9% to 6% before rising to 11%.

FE vs. Potential curves for (a) MnO2, (b) NrGO and (c) NrGO–MnO composite.

The NrGO electrode exhibited improved CO2RR selectivity compared to MnO2, following the same trend with increasing potentials. The faradaic efficiencies of CH4 roughly changed from 13%, 19%, and 16% at −1.2, −1.4, and −1.6 V, respectively, while the corresponding CO faradaic efficiencies were roughly 10%, 6%, and 10%.

Out of all the catalysts, the NrGO–MnO2 composite showed the highest methane selectivity. Before declining to roughly 20% at −1.6 V, the CH4 faradaic efficiency rose from roughly 15% at −1.2 V to a maximum value of roughly 27% at −1.4 V. Concurrently, the CO faradaic efficiency dropped from roughly 11% to 8% before rising to roughly 12%. In comparison to the separate components, the composite catalyst encourages deeper hydrogenation of reaction intermediates, as seen by the increased methane generation. Overall, the composite electrode promotes deeper CO2 reduction pathways compared to the individual components.

The voltage-dependent CO2 reduction routes and competition with the HER are responsible for the fluctuation in faradaic efficiency with applied voltage. Relatively little CH4 is formed at −1.2 V vs. Ag/AgCl due to restricted CO2 activation. The maximum CH4 faradaic efficiency for all catalysts is achieved by raising the potential to −1.4 V, which improves CO2 adsorption and stabilises important intermediates like *COOH and *CO, which promote the subsequent proton-coupled electron transfer stages.

Even though the total current density rises at −1.6 V, CH4 selectivity falls because of the growing HER contribution and potential variations in *CO adsorption strength, which lower the likelihood of additional hydrogenation to CH4. An ideal balance between CO2 activation, reaction intermediate stabilisation, effective electron transfer, and suppression of competing HER is indicated by the NrGO–MnO2 composite's better CH4 selectivity at −1.4 V.

Conclusion

In this work, NrGO, MnO2, and a hybrid composite (NrGO–MnO) were successfully synthesized and systematically characterized through XRD, SEM, BET, TGA, and XPS analyses, followed by detailed electrochemical evaluation. Structural studies revealed that MnO2 exists in a poorly crystalline nanosheet/nanoplate form, and its incorporation into the NrGO framework effectively enhanced dispersion while preventing graphene restacking. BET analysis confirmed that the NrGO–MnO composite exhibited a significantly higher surface area and pore volume relative to pristine materials, providing a porous architecture conducive to CO2 adsorption and reactant accessibility. XPS analysis further confirmed nitrogen functionalities in NrGO and strong Mn–O interactions in the composite, both of which are known to facilitate CO2 activation by stabilizing key intermediates.

Electrochemical measurements demonstrated the superior performance of NrGO–MnO in CO2 reduction electrocatalysis compared to the individual components. LSV revealed enhanced current density under CO2 saturated conditions, reflecting an increased density of electrochemically active sites, while EIS confirmed markedly lower charge-transfer resistance, highlighting efficient electron and ion transport. The synergistic interplay between the conductive NrGO matrix and the redox-active MnO phase not only improves electron mobility at the electrode–electrolyte interface but also promotes stabilization of CO2− intermediates, thereby facilitating multi-electron transfer pathways. This dual functionality underpins the enhanced CO2 reduction efficiency of the composite. Product analysis confirmed that the NrGO–MnO2 composite performed better, with a maximum CH4 faradaic efficiency of ∼27% at −1.4 V against Ag/AgCl, as opposed to ∼19% for NrGO and ∼17% for MnO2. Higher current density, reduced charge-transfer resistance, and improved methane selectivity all demonstrate how MnO2 and NrGO work in concert to promote CO2 activation and deeper multi-electron reduction pathways. Future work should address optimization of composite composition, mechanistic investigations via in situ spectroscopy, and long-term stability testing under practical electrolysis conditions to advance the material toward scalable CO2 conversion technologies.
