Parent-reported activity and health-related quality of life after botulinum toxin in spastic cerebral palsy
Authors: Geylanur Kaya Yavuz, Güney Yılmaz, Mintaze Kerem Günel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate changes in activity levels and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in children with spastic cerebral palsy (CP) before and after botulinum toxin (BTX) injection, based on parental reports within the International Classification of Functioning Disability and Health (ICF) framework.Methods:This retrospective study included 60 children with spastic CP (20 girls, 40 boys) who underwent BTX injection for spasticity management. The age range was 2-11 years (mean age, 5.44 ± 2.09 years). Gross motor function level was classified using the Gross Motor Function Classification System–Family Report. Activity was evaluated with the Pediatric Outcome Data Collection Instrument (PODCI), and HRQoL was assessed using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) before and after BTX injection. Parents completed all questionnaires 6-12 months after BTX injection; pre- and post-injection scores were compared.Results:The PODCI scores improved significantly after BTX in upper extremity function (P = .001), transfers/basic mobility (P = .005), pain/comfort (P = .009), happiness/satisfaction (P < .001), and global score (P < .001), while the physical function/sports subscale did not change significantly (P = .136). The PedsQL total score improved (P < .001), with significant improvements in daily activities (P < .001), school (P = .034), activity/balance (P < .001), pain/ache (P = .015), fatigue/prostration (P = .002), and nutrition (P = .027); the speech/communication domain showed no significant change (P = .150).Conclusion:From a parental perspective, BTX injection was associated with improved activity and HRQoL in children with spastic CP, with gains seen across multiple functional and well-being domains. Clinically, these findings support counseling families that BTX within an ICF-oriented rehabilitation approach may translate into meaningful improvements in daily function and overall well-being, although speech-related outcomes may be less responsive.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is defined as a group of permanent disorders in movement and posture development, caused by non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain. These disorders often coexist with sensory, cognitive, and communication impairments, leading to limitations in activity and participation from early childhood onward.1-3 Spasticity is the most common motor impairment associated with upper motor neuron lesions.4 Spastic-type CP is the most prevalent form, and spasticity in the extremity muscles is often accompanied by hypotonia in the trunk.5

Spasticity results from a hyperactive stretch reflex mechanism, and its consequences such as contractures or muscle-tendon imbalances around joints can be managed through interventions including botulinum toxin (BTX), baclofen, selective dorsal rhizotomy, and orthopedic surgery.6 Although the brain injury causing the motor deficits cannot be cured, symptomatic treatments are applied throughout life to manage emerging problems due to growth and aging.7-9

The BTX is considered a clinically effective and safe method for managing focal spasticity.10 According to a systematic review by Novak et al, 11 BTX, diazepam, and selective dorsal rhizotomy have high-level evidence for their effectiveness in reducing spasticity, whereas tizanidine, oral baclofen, intrathecal baclofen, dantrolene, alcohol, and phenol have moderate-level evidence.12

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is closely related to the child’s physical and psychological status, level of independence, social environment, and living conditions.13,14 Spasticity not only impacts the child’s level of independence but also their overall quality of life. The limitations in mobility can affect the child’s well-being and psychosocial status. The HRQoL encompasses physical, emotional, and social well-being, as well as access to education and community inclusion.15-17 A multidisciplinary approach with appropriate assessments and interventions is essential to support the HRQoL of children with CP.18

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of BTX applications on children’s activity levels and quality of life from a parental perspective. In addition, the study investigated how the treatment influenced the quality of life of the caregivers themselves.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

This study is a retrospective analysis of children with spastic-type CP who had received BTX injections. Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Hacettepe University (GO22/95-18.01.2022).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: a diagnosis of spastic-type CP, being between the ages of 2 and 12, having received at least 1 BTX injection in the lower or upper extremities at least 6 months prior to the study, the ability of the parent to read and write, and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were a history of lower extremity orthopedic surgery within the past 6 months and receiving a BTX injection within the last 6 months.

Of the 66 children initially recruited, 2 were excluded due to age criteria, and 4 were excluded because their parents did not complete the assessment questionnaires. The questionnaires were conducted via telephone and were sent electronically. The survey questionnaires were applied between 6 and 12 months after BTX injection.All questionnaires were completed by parents.

Doses were based on widely accepted recommendations in the literature, typically ranging between 2 and 6 U/kg for lower-limb muscles (e.g., gastrocnemius, soleus, hamstrings) and 1-3 U/kg for upper-limb muscles, consistent with guidelines by Molenaers et al19 and Love et al.20

Questionnaires

The Turkish version of the Gross Motor Function Classification System-Family Report (GMFCS-FR) was used to determine gross motor function levels. The GMFCS-FR includes age groups of 2-4, 4-6, 6-12, and 12-18 years. Unlike the standard GMFCS, in this version, higher levels indicate better motor capacity. For cognitively capable individuals aged 12-18, a self-care report form is also available.21 It explicitly states that the GMFCS-FR does not differ from the standard GMFCS and that, in this version, higher levels indicate better motor capacity. The GMFCS was developed to provide a more useful classification system because the levels are identified as dull, average, and heavy at GMFM. Level I refers to the least activation, and Level V refers to the most activation for children with CP at GMFCS. The aim determines which level is the best way to indicate the situation. The Turkish translation and reliability were performed by Incesu Oral and Kerem Gunel.22

To evaluate activity levels, the Pediatric Outcome Data Collection Instrument (PODCI) was used. This instrument assesses functional health status and HRQoL in children with physical disabilities. It includes 5 subscales: Upper Extremity Function (UEF), Physical Function and Sports (PFS), Transfer and Basic Mobility (TBM), Pain/Comfort (PC), and Happiness/Satisfaction (HS). An additional section on treatment expectations is included for the child. Each subscale score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. The Turkish validity and reliability of both tools have been established by Dilbay et al.23

To assess HRQoL, the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) was administered. The PedsQL is a 35-item instrument suitable for both healthy children and those with chronic conditions, scored between 0 and 100. Higher scores indicate better HRQoL.24,25 For all children, subscale scores, total scale scores, and the psychosocial health summary score (which includes emotional, social, and school functioning) were calculated. The PedsQL 3.0 CP Module Turkish version was validated by Kutlay et al.26

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means, standard deviations, minimums, and maximums for numerical variables. The normality of the data was assessed using visual (histograms and probability plots) and analytical methods (Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness and kurtosis values, coefficient of variation). As the data were normally distributed, paired-sample t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention (BTX) scores. Correlations between variables were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation tests, depending on distribution. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: 0.00-0.20 very weak, 0.21-0.40 weak, 0.41-0.60 moderate, 0.61-0.80 strong, and 0.81-1.00 very strong. A Type I error level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

The sample size of the study has been determined through a power analysis conducted with G*Power (version 3.1.9, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) using pilot data (n = 15). The PODCI global score value has been used in the power analysis. When the effect size obtained from the pilot data (d = 0.475) was determined with a Type 1 error of 5% and a power target of 95%, the analysis determined that a minimum of 60 participants is needed. Following completion of the study, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using the Dependent Groups t-test results used to compare PODCI global scores. For analyses with a Type 1 error of 5% and a sample size of 60, post-hoc power (1−β) was found to exceed 99.9%.

Results

A total of 60 children with spastic-type CP (20 girls, 40 boys), aged between 2 and 11 years, participated in the study. The mean age of the children was 5.44 ± 2.09 years. The sociodemographic characteristics of the children and their families are presented in Table 1. According to the GMFCS-FR levels, 8 children were classified as Level I, 22 as Level II, 10 as Level III, 11 as Level IV, and 9 as Level V.

The mean age at which children received their first BTX injection was 3.23 ± 1.07 years. The mean number of repeated BTX injections was 2.38 ± 1.99. The children received an average of 3.17 ± 1.08 physical therapy and rehabilitation (PTR) sessions per week. After BTX injection, 61.67% of the children received casting. BTX was applied to the lower extremities in 76.67% of cases, to the upper extremities in 1.67%, and to both upper and lower extremities in 21.66%. All children used orthoses, and 56.67% used assistive devices. Additionally, 25% of the children received supplementary therapy (Table 2).

A comparison of pre- and post-BTX PODCI subscale and total scores is presented in Table 3. Statistically significant improvements were observed in upper extremity function, transfer and basic mobility, comfort, happiness/satisfaction, and global scores after BTX injection (P < .05).

A comparison of pre- and post-BTX PedsQL subscale and total scores is shown in Table 4. All subscale and total scores, except for the speech domain, showed statistically significant improvements following the BTX intervention (P < .05).

Discussion

This study was designed to investigate the effects of BTX injections on activity levels and HRQoL in children with spastic CP, based on parental perspectives and within the framework of the International Classification of Functioning Disability and Health (ICF). The study provides novel insights by jointly examining parental perceptions of changes in children’s activity and quality of life, as well as the impact of BTX treatment on caregivers’ own well-being, an aspect that is rarely addressed in the BTX literature. Outcomes were compared between the pre- and post-BTX periods using parent-reported assessments.

Tedroff et al.27 evaluated the long-term effects of BTX injections into the gastrocnemius muscle and reported that BTX may temporarily prevent contracture formation. The improvements in HRQoL observed in the present study may, at least in part, be related to this potential effect of BTX in delaying or reducing the development of contractures. Prevention of contracture formation is clinically important, as it may reduce the need for future surgical interventions and pain, both of which are key determinants of activity levels in children with CP.

Early application of BTX is considered clinically important, and the mean age of the children included in the present study supports this emphasis. Molenaers et al19 reported that the most effective age range for BTX treatment is between 2 and 6 years, highlighting the importance of the timing of the first injection. However, repeated BTX injections may impose both emotional and physical burdens on children and their families. In line with previous literature, frequent treatment repetitions may negatively affect family quality of life. When BTX is applied at an early age, the risk of contracture development may be reduced, which in turn may contribute to improvements in children’s quality of life

According to a synthesis of evidence by Lukban et al,28 BTX reduces muscle tone, which may have beneficial effects on both children and their caregivers. The primary therapeutic aim of BTX is to prevent secondary musculoskeletal deformities and thereby facilitate improvements in activity levels. Consistent with this perspective, families in the present study reported improvements in both their own quality of life and that of their children following BTX treatment.

Previous studies have reported that the most substantial motor gains following BTX injections typically occur within the first 2 treatment cycles.29 Moreover, improvements in motor function may be reflected in increased activity and participation, consistent with the ICF framework.

According to an international consensus statement published by Love et al.20 The BTX is effective in reducing lower extremity spasticity in children with CP. Consistent with this evidence, the majority of BTX applications in the present study were administered to lower extremity muscles. This treatment distribution may have contributed to the improvements in activity levels and HRQoL observed in younger children, particularly those who received lower extremity injections.

Physical therapy and BTX interventions are reported to be most effective when applied in a coordinated manner.28 This was also reflected in the present study, in which all participating children were receiving ongoing physical therapy, a factor that may have contributed to the positive outcomes observed following BTX treatment.

Parental expectations and goals play a critical role in the physiotherapy and rehabilitation process following BTX application. As children’s functional status improves, caregivers are those who most directly experience changes in quality of life. Improvements in activity levels following BTX interventions are reflected positively in both the child’s and the family’s daily life.30 As GMFCS level increases, caregiving demands also increase, making care more challenging for families. However, in children with higher GMFCS levels, BTX is administered less frequently due to life-threatening and developmental risks, whereas BTX is applied more frequently at lower GMFCS levels.31 In the present study, the majority of children were classified at higher GMFCS levels. According to PODCI results, families reported a reduced caregiving burden and improved functional outcomes following BTX.

Optimal outcomes following BTX treatment depend largely on the integration of rehabilitation interventions. In the present study, improvements were also observed in children’s self-confidence. Consistent with these findings, Blumetti et al,32 in a systematic review, reported that families observed a significant reduction in spasticity following BTX treatment and associated improvements in children’s activity levels with enhancements in their own quality of life.

Casting following BTX application has been shown to positively influence muscle tone.20,33 In the present study, the majority of children received casting and used orthoses, with 61.6% undergoing casting and 60% using an orthosis. The widespread use of casts and orthoses among participants may have enhanced the overall effectiveness of BTX treatment. Families frequently associated reported increases in happiness with greater ease during orthotic use and caregiving tasks, which may be attributed to reduced spasticity.34 Improvements in daily functioning are known to have a substantial impact on both children’s and families’ lives.10

There are some limitations to this study. Because PedsQL and PODCI were completed by families, the findings may be subject to reporting bias. Although parental reports may lack full objectivity, caregivers are often the individuals who best observe and describe their children’s daily functioning. A placebo effect following injection may also have influenced participant responses; however, the evaluation of multiple outcome domains allowed us to capture effects that extend beyond a placebo response. As the questionnaires were completed by Turkish families, the findings may be specific to the Turkish population with respect to GMFCS distribution. The majority of children included in the study were classified at GMFCS Level II, and children with higher functional levels tend to demonstrate greater gains in activity and quality of life. Nevertheless, children across other GMFCS levels also showed clear improvements. Subgroup analyses based on GMFCS levels were not performed, as the sample sizes within individual GMFCS subgroups were insufficient to yield reliable statistical results.

In conclusion, within the ICF framework, the present study demonstrated that BTX applications had positive effects on activity levels and HRQoL in children with spastic CP. Reductions in spasticity facilitated children’s movement and personal care activities and eased caregiving demands for families. Overall, BTX contributed to improvements in quality of life for both children with CP and their caregivers. Raising parental awareness and supporting the most active lifestyle possible are essential for optimizing outcomes in children with CP, with social inclusion and quality of life remaining key priorities. Given the limited literature examining BTX effectiveness from a family-centered perspective, further studies are warranted to expand and strengthen this area of research.
