Children’s experiences of willpower and self-regulation: a phenomenological study of emotions, strategies, and metaphorical meanings in school-aged children
Authors: Esma Gokkoyun, Ebru Kilicarslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWillpower and self-regulation are central developmental capacities associated with children’s academic success, psychosocial adjustment, and long-term well-being. Although these constructs have been widely studied in preschoolers and adults, limited research has explored how school-aged children themselves perceive and experience willpower in their everyday lives. This study aimed to explore school-aged children’s lived experiences of willpower and self-regulation, focusing on their emotional processes, strategies, role models, and metaphorical meanings.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological design was employed. The study was conducted with 21 children aged 6–12 years attending a public primary and secondary school in the Anatolia region of Türkiye. Participants were selected using purposeful criterion sampling. Data were collected between September and October 2025 through three focus group interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method. Sociodemographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Trustworthiness was ensured following Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.ResultsFour main themes emerged: (1) Being caught between desire and responsibility, (2) Persisting despite difficulty and associated emotions, (3) Perceptions of strong willpower and role models, and (4) Self-regulation strategies. Children described willpower as navigating internal conflicts between immediate desires (e.g., play, screen time) and responsibilities (e.g., studying, helping at home). Emotional experiences were central; persistence was initially accompanied by hesitation or discomfort but ultimately reinforced by pride and satisfaction. Children identified parents and teachers as key role models, emphasizing patience, planning, and consistency as indicators of strong willpower. They reported diverse self-regulation strategies, including planning, time management, goal setting, cognitive redirection, pairing tasks with enjoyable activities, and breaking tasks into smaller parts. Metaphorically, willpower was described as a “shield” or a “robot,” symbolizing protection, strength, and control.ConclusionsSchool-aged children experience willpower and self-regulation as dynamic, emotionally charged, and socially shaped processes enacted through context-sensitive strategies. Understanding children’s subjective meanings may inform educational and pediatric nursing practices aimed at fostering flexible and developmentally appropriate self-regulatory capacities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04510-9.Keywords: Willpower, Self-regulation, School-aged children, Phenomenology, Metaphor, Self-control strategies, Pediatric nursing

Introductıon

The school-age period represents a substantial segment of the global child population, with nearly 2.3 billion children worldwide currently in this developmental stage [1]. From a developmental perspective, this period generally encompasses children between 6 and 12 years of age [2]. It constitutes a critical stage during which children’s cognitive, emotional, and social skills advance markedly, academic competencies are shaped, and behavioral patterns that may persist across the lifespan are established [3]. At the same time, the school-age period includes preadolescent characteristics and serves as a transitional phase that lays the groundwork for the physical and psychosocial changes observed during adolescence [4].

According to Erik Erikson’s psychosocial development theory, this stage corresponds to the phase of “industry versus inferiority,” during which children develop a sense of competence and self-worth through experiences of achievement [5, 6]. In this context, the development of willpower and self-regulatory capacities emerges as a key determinant of children’s academic performance, psychosocial adjustment, and lifelong functioning. The skills and motivations that enable willpower—particularly the ability to inhibit strong and immediate impulses in favor of future goals—are regarded as important early indicators of long-term mental and physical development [7].

Willpower refers to an individual’s capacity to resist short-term temptations in order to achieve long-term goals [8], and research has shown that individuals can utilize willpower to enhance their regulatory capacities [9]. Children who are able to regulate their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in accordance with valued long-term goals are better prepared for school life [10]. In this regard, willpower is closely related to and often conceptualized alongside constructs such as self-control, self-regulation, and delay of gratification [11]. Strong self-control has been associated with numerous positive outcomes, including career success, healthier social relationships, and longevity [12, 13]. Moreover, willpower and self-regulation developed during childhood have been shown to predict later academic achievement, mental health, interpersonal functioning, and healthy lifestyle behaviors [10, 14].

Longitudinal evidence further highlights the developmental significance of these capacities. Self-regulation in the preschool period (approximately age 4) has been positively associated with social competence, school engagement, and academic achievement in the early school years (around age 8), and negatively associated with internalizing problems, peer victimization, and externalizing problems. In turn, self-regulation in the early school years has been positively associated with academic achievement (mathematics and literacy) and negatively associated with externalizing behaviors (e.g., aggression and delinquency), depressive symptoms, obesity, smoking, and illicit substance use in early adolescence (approximately age 13) [14].

From a cognitive developmental perspective, self-regulation and willpower are closely linked to executive functions (EF), commonly conceptualized as a set of cognitive control processes including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility that support goal-directed behavior [15]. Although these processes begin to emerge in the preschool years, their differentiation and integration continue across early schooling, supporting children’s increasing capacity to regulate impulses, manage attention, and persist in goal-directed activities [16]. While the foundations of executive functions emerge early, they rapidly mature during the elementary school years; working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility strengthen, while delay of gratification and decision-making skills follow a related developmental trajectory [17]. In a study of children aged 6–11, the development of self-regulation and executive functions showed minimal differences between children aged 7–9 and 10–12. Executive functions largely develop together between ages 7 and 12, begin to differentiate in adolescents aged 13–15, and reach adult-like levels of performance by around 17–18 years of age [18].

The development of willpower and self-regulation is influenced by a wide range of factors. These include biological, neurophysiological, and psychological processes associated with general cognitive development, as well as contextual influences such as the child’s environment and cultural background. For instance, cultural values related to autonomy and the extent to which children are encouraged to set and pursue goals in a self-regulated manner may contribute to individual differences [19]. To better understand such differences, there is a need for qualitative research that examines children’s perspectives in depth. Exploring constructs such as willpower and self-regulation through children’s own expressions particularly necessitates qualitative approaches [20].

Despite the growing body of research, most studies on willpower have focused either on preschool-aged children or adults [10, 21], and relatively little is known about motivation regulation and its development among school-aged children [19]. A deeper understanding of school-aged children’s experiences may provide valuable insights for developing more effective interventions and ultimately contribute to improvements in school functioning and overall well-being.

Within the pediatric nursing literature, self-regulation is conceptualized as a fundamental developmental capacity related to children’s ability to manage emotional, behavioral, and cognitive processes [22]. In this context, pediatric nurses play important educational and counseling roles in supporting children’s self-regulatory capacities [5]. Understanding willpower from the child’s perspective may therefore contribute meaningfully to supporting children’s psychosocial development.

Accordingly, this qualitative study aimed to explore school-aged children’s perceptions of willpower and self-regulation. Specifically, the study sought to examine how children conceptualize these constructs, how they describe using them in their daily lives, which strategies they report employing to regulate their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors, and which factors they perceive as facilitating or hindering their willpower and self-regulation.

Methods

Study design

Phenomenological research emphasizes that individuals’ awareness and perceptions shape their personal experiences, aiming to explore the meaning of these lived experiences and reveal the essential structure of a phenomenon as perceived by those who experience it [20]. Accordingly, this study adopted a descriptive phenomenological approach to understand how children experience and interpret the phenomenon of willpower in their everyday lives. Because willpower represents an internal, subjective, and experience-based process, a phenomenological approach was considered appropriate for capturing children’s perspectives and meaning-making processes in everyday contexts. In line with descriptive phenomenology, the study focused on identifying the essential meanings embedded in children’s narratives. To systematically analyze participants’ lived experiences, Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method was employed, which allows researchers to interpret and structure experiential meanings derived from participants’ accounts. This approach is widely used in qualitative health and social science research to explore subjective experiences that are difficult to capture through quantitative methods [23, 24].

Data were collected through focus group interviews, which enabled children to express their experiences in an interactive environment and facilitated the articulation of abstract concepts through peer interaction. Although phenomenological studies commonly rely on individual interviews to explore lived experiences, focus group interviews may also be used in qualitative research [25, 26]. Furthermore, the use of focus group discussions has been discussed in the literature as a method that can align with phenomenological inquiry by facilitating dialogue and the co-construction of meaning among participants [27, 28]. Interaction among peers can sometimes help children articulate their experiences more comfortably, recall everyday situations, and discuss abstract concepts through shared dialogue.

Participants

The study population consisted of children aged 6–12 years attending a primary and secondary public school located in a district of the Anatolia region of Türkiye. Participants represented early and later school-age periods, allowing the study to capture experiences across different developmental stages. The participants were school-age children living in a rural area, mostly from middle-income families. Concepts such as willpower and self-regulation were explained, and the questions were phrased in a child-friendly manner to ensure understanding.

The sample comprised children who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate with both their own assent and parental consent.

In phenomenological research, participants are selected based on their experience with the phenomenon of interest. Accordingly, purposeful criterion sampling was used, and children who were considered to have daily experiences requiring willpower and self-regulation were included. An exclusion criterion was the presence of communication and/or cognitive difficulties that could hinder participation in the interviews.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a Descriptive Characteristic Form and a Semi-Structured Interview Guide developed by the researchers. The Descriptive Characteristic Form included questions regarding age, gender, and grade level.

The Semi-Structured Interview Guide was developed based on relevant literature [8, 9] and reviewed by three experts in child health nursing, psychology, and qualitative research. Based on their feedback, revisions were made to simplify wording, clarify ambiguous items, and ensure the questions were appropriate and understandable for school-age children. The final guide included one warm-up question and six main open-ended questions exploring children’s experiences and perceptions of willpower.

Examples of interview questions included:

“What does willpower mean to you?”

“What does willpower mean to you?”

“Can you describe a situation where you felt you used your willpower?”

“Can you describe a situation where you felt you used your willpower?”

“What do you do when something is difficult but you still need to continue?”

“What do you do when something is difficult but you still need to continue?”

Data collection procedure

Before data collection, children were informed about the purpose and content of the study in age-appropriate language. The study was conducted between September and October 2025 in a public school where primary and secondary school buildings were located adjacent to each other.

Prior to data collection, meetings were held with the school principal and teachers to provide information about the study and to arrange the interview settings. Data were collected through three focus group interviews, each consisting of seven children. Focus group interviews were preferred as they facilitated peer interaction and supported children in recalling and articulating their experiences related to willpower.

To facilitate age-appropriate interaction and minimize developmental differences during discussions, participants were grouped with peers of similar age. Accordingly, three focus groups were formed: children aged 6–8 years (seven students), 9–10 years (seven students), and 10–12 years (seven students). Due to the distribution of participants and school scheduling constraints, a small age overlap occurred between the second and third focus groups. However, grouping participants with peers of similar developmental stages helped facilitate age-appropriate discussion.

The interviews were conducted face-to-face by the researchers during school hours in a quiet classroom separate from regular teaching areas to minimize distractions. A semi-structured interview guide was used, and probing questions such as “Can you tell me more about that?” and “Can you give an example?” were asked to deepen the narratives. At the beginning of the focus group discussions, a warm-up question was used to present children’s experiences as situations that can happen to anyone, emphasizing that situations in which they strongly want something but need to wait or refrain from doing it can happen to everyone. The researcher also emphasized that there were no right or wrong answers and encouraged children to share both situations in which they were able to control themselves and situations in which they experienced difficulty with self-control. In the group setting, one child’s sharing often encouraged others to share similar experiences. Each interview lasted approximately 50–60 min. To assess the applicability of the interview guide, pilot interviews were conducted with two children. No revisions were required. The pilot data were excluded from the study.

Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes or meaningful insights related to children’s experiences of willpower emerged across the focus group discussions. Although participants represented different age groups, the aim of the study was not to compare developmental stages but to explore shared meanings associated with the experience of willpower. Similar patterns and themes were repeatedly observed across the interviews, indicating sufficient depth and redundancy in the data. To confirm saturation, an additional interview was conducted with two children and no new themes emerged. These data were not included in the analysis. All interviews were audio-recorded with permission and transcribed verbatim.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of a state university (Approval Date: 04.06.2025; Approval No: E-72381019-600-321166). Institutional permission was subsequently obtained from the relevant school authorities. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [29].

Parents were informed in detail about the study purpose, data collection tools, interview techniques, duration, confidentiality, and the use of data solely for research purposes, and written informed consent was obtained. Verbal and written assent was obtained from the children prior to participation. Participation was entirely voluntary, and both children and parents were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. Confidentiality was ensured by restricting access to study materials to authorized research team members only. All data were anonymized and securely stored in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation [30].

Data analysis

Sociodemographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation).

Qualitative data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method with an inductive approach [31]. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and anonymized using participant codes (C1, C2, etc.). Transcripts were read repeatedly by the researchers to gain a comprehensive understanding of the data. Significant statements related to children’s experiences of willpower were identified, and meanings were formulated from these statements. Similar meanings were clustered to form themes and subthemes.

A comprehensive description of the phenomenon was developed based on the identified themes, and only themes directly related to the phenomenon of willpower were retained. Direct quotations were included to reflect participants’ experiences authentically and to enhance the credibility of the findings.

Trustworthiness of the study

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, were followed [32].

Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants, focus group interviews, data saturation, and the use of direct quotations to reflect children’s experiences authentically. Data saturation was confirmed through an additional interview.

Dependability was ensured by maintaining a transparent and systematic audit trail throughout the research process, including detailed documentation of data collection, transcription, and analysis procedures.

Confirmability was supported through reflexivity, with researchers acknowledging their professional backgrounds in pediatric nursing and actively minimizing the influence of personal assumptions during data interpretation.

Transferability was addressed by providing rich and detailed descriptions of the research context, participants, and procedures, allowing readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar contexts.

Theme 1. Being caught between desire and responsibility

Children frequently described situations in their daily lives in which they felt torn between activities they found enjoyable and the responsibilities they were expected to fulfill. These situations commonly involved choices between play, leisure activities, or screen use and obligations related to schoolwork or household duties. Children’s narratives reflected an internal struggle in which personal desires conflicted with external expectations, highlighting moments where the need for willpower became particularly salient.

One child expressed this conflict in relation to academic demands:

K3 (11 years, girl): “I want to play games with my friends when I know I should be studying for the exam.”

Similarly, younger children described comparable tensions between play and school responsibilities:

K4 (8 years, boy): “I want to play football, but I have to study.”

In some cases, children emphasized that this struggle was shaped by parental expectations and family responsibilities:

K16 (10 years, girl): “I want to play games on my phone, but my mom calls me to help with the housework.”

Overall, these accounts suggest that children most clearly experience willpower in situations where immediate desires are postponed in favor of responsibilities, requiring them to navigate competing motivations within their everyday lives.

Theme 2. Persisting despite difficulty (use of willpower) and associated emotions

Children described willpower primarily as the ability to continue with a task despite experiencing difficulty, temptation, or internal resistance. These moments were often accompanied by distinct emotional responses. When children managed to persist and complete what they perceived as their responsibility, they reported positive emotions such as feeling good, satisfaction, and pride. In contrast, when their attention remained on what they desired but could not do, feelings of hesitation or embarrassment were expressed.

One child openly reflected the internal pull of temptation:

K1 (12 years, girl): “My mind stays a little on the phone…” (said with a shy expression).

Several children associated persistence with positive emotional outcomes:

K5 (9 years, girl): “I felt good. For example, when I studied.”

K13 (9 years, boy): “I feel good because I studied and finished my task.”

Some children described pride as a particularly strong emotion linked to resisting temptation, especially in situations involving health-related choices:

K19 (11 years, girl): “My friends wanted to have our monthly noodle day before the month was over and invited me. I didn’t go because it’s unhealthy. At times like that, I feel proud of myself.”

Overall, children’s narratives indicate that emotional experiences play a central role in how willpower is perceived and reinforced. Positive emotions appeared to strengthen children’s willingness to persist, whereas moments of lingering desire were accompanied by emotional discomfort, highlighting the affective dimension of willpower in everyday life.

Theme 3. Perceptions of strong willpower and role models

Children described strong willpower as a set of personal qualities reflected in everyday behaviors, such as patience, planning, consistency, and the ability to prioritize long-term needs over immediate desires. When talking about individuals with strong willpower, children most frequently referred to parents and teachers, indicating that their understanding of willpower was largely shaped through observation of significant adults in their daily lives.

Patience emerged as a prominent characteristic, particularly in the context of teacher–student relationships. One child highlighted patience as a sign of strong willpower and emotional control:

K17 (8 years, girl): “I think our classroom teacher has strong willpower because she is patient with our misbehavior. When she is patient, we later understand her love and we love her very much.”

Parents were commonly described as role models who demonstrated willpower through deliberate decision-making and self-restraint. One child explained how observing a parent’s ability to delay gratification influenced their own understanding:

K8 (10 years, boy): “When my mom wants to buy something, she doesn’t buy it right away. She waits and buys according to need. Sometimes I get upset when I don’t get what I want, but later when I think about it, I understand that she did the right thing.”

Consistency and commitment were also emphasized, particularly in relation to health-related behaviors. One child described how a parent’s persistence served as a meaningful example:

K20 (10 years, girl): “My mom goes to sports regularly. Sometimes I tell her not to go because she’s tired, but she still goes. She shows me that small excuses shouldn’t come before the things we want to do.”

Overall, children’s accounts suggest that perceptions of strong willpower are constructed through observing repeated, consistent behaviors in trusted adults, rather than through isolated acts. These role models appeared to provide concrete and relatable examples that helped children make sense of willpower in their own lives.

Theme 4. Self-regulation strategies

Children described a variety of strategies they used to regulate their behavior and manage competing desires. These strategies reflected children’s active efforts to cope with temptation, persist in tasks they found challenging, and maintain motivation. The strategies described included time management, planning, goal setting, combining less preferred tasks with enjoyable activities, cognitive redirection, and breaking tasks into manageable parts.

Some children described behavioral strategies that involved completing tasks efficiently in order to access desired activities:

K1 (12 years, girl): “For example, I need to solve tests but I want to play on my phone, so I quickly finish the questions I need to do and then play.”

Others described motivational strategies in which they paired an undesired task with something they enjoyed:

K2 (12 years, girl): “When I have to do something I don’t want to do—like studying—I do it with something I like, such as drinking coffee.”

Anticipating a rewarding activity later in the day was another strategy used to sustain effort, particularly in situations involving reluctance or fatigue:

K9 (9 years, girl): “I don’t like coming to school on Thursdays, but I have tennis that day, so I feel happy. When it’s hard to come to school in the morning, I think about playing tennis at the end of the day, and then it becomes easier to come.”

Older children emphasized planning and goal setting as central elements of self-control:

K10 (11 years, girl): “You need to make a program… You need to plan and have goals.”

K19 (11 years, girl): “You set goals, continue regularly, and don’t give up.”

Younger children more often described cognitive distraction strategies, shifting their attention away from restricted desires toward alternative positive thoughts:

K14 (6 years, girl): “When they don’t let me do what I want, I think about something else. If I can’t play on the phone, I think about going to the park with my dad.”

Some children also demonstrated an ability to reframe and structure challenging tasks in order to reduce feelings of overwhelm and increase persistence:

K15 (9 years, boy): “When a task feels too big and I realize I might avoid it because it seems difficult, I make it smaller. I break it into little parts and start with the easiest one.”

Some differences were observed across age groups in the strategies children described. Younger children more frequently referred to distraction-based strategies, such as thinking about alternative activities when they could not do what they wanted. In contrast, older children more often emphasized planning, goal-setting, and organizing their tasks as ways to maintain self-control.

Overall, these findings indicate that children employ both behavioral and cognitive strategies to regulate their actions. The diversity of strategies described suggests an emerging capacity for self-regulation, enabling children to manage difficulty and sustain effort in everyday situations.

Children’s perspectives on willpower: metaphorical representations

Children expressed their understanding of the concept of willpower through metaphorical representations, either by personifying it or by describing it using concrete objects. These metaphors provided insight into how children conceptualize willpower as a state associated with strength, control, discipline, and order.

Some children personified willpower by describing individuals they perceived as having strong willpower. These descriptions emphasized characteristics such as success, organization, responsibility, and reliability:

K20 (10 years, girl): “A successful, organized, hardworking person.”

K19 (11 years, girl): “Someone who takes care of their appearance.”

K6 (9 years, boy): “A middle-aged person.”

K13 (9 years, boy): “Someone who does things on time.”

Other children described willpower through symbolic and concrete objects, reflecting protective and controlled qualities attributed to the concept:

K1 (12 years, girl): “I think it’s a shield. It protects you from difficulties coming from outside.”

K7 (9 years, girl): “I think it’s a robot. In difficult situations, you can resist feelings like pain or sadness, just like a robot.”

Taken together, these metaphorical expressions suggest that children perceive willpower not merely as an abstract internal trait, but as a protective and regulating force that helps them manage challenges, maintain order, and remain consistent in the face of difficulty.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to deepen understanding in this field by exploring how school-aged children perceive the concepts of willpower and self-regulation within their everyday life experiences and through which strategies they enact these capacities. Willpower and self-regulation continue to be important constructs that have been studied for decades [11, 12]. The present study makes visible how children currently conceptualize willpower and self-regulation and how they experience these processes in their daily lives, drawing directly on their own expressions. The findings indicate that, for children, willpower and self-regulation are dynamic processes shaped by competing desires, emotional challenges, and social expectations.

Children described experiencing willpower and self-regulation as an ongoing negotiation between competing motivations in their daily lives, often feeling caught between responsibilities and personal desires. In this study, children reported having to choose between engaging in enjoyable activities, such as playing, and fulfilling school- or home-related responsibilities, frequently experiencing this situation as an internal struggle. The literature similarly emphasizes that such conflicts are inherent in self-regulation processes [33]. In this context, willpower is conceptualized as the process by which individuals make deliberate choices between immediate pleasure-oriented impulses and goal-directed behaviors. Research indicates that children who are able to delay immediate desires in favor of long-term goals demonstrate more positive academic, social, and life satisfaction outcomes [12, 14]. Taken together, these findings suggest that self-regulation is not merely compliance with external instructions or suppression of pleasure; rather, it represents a cognitively and emotionally integrated process in which children make choices while being aware of their own desires, expectations, and responsibilities. Indeed, delay of gratification is understood not simply as the ability to postpone pleasure, but as a central component of broader self-regulatory processes [34].

The findings indicate that children experience willpower as a process intertwined with intense emotional experiences. When attention remains focused on desired but postponed activities, children reported experiencing internal conflict accompanied by challenging emotions such as hesitation, shyness, or emotional discomfort. Conversely, when they completed their responsibilities, children described feelings of well-being, satisfaction, and particularly pride. These findings underscore that willpower and self-regulation are inseparable from emotional experience. Similarly, Reinecke et al. reported that successfully regulated social media use enhances psychological well-being through mood management, emotion-focused coping, and need satisfaction, whereas insufficient self-regulation may lead to goal conflict, distraction, and the displacement of other activities, thereby negatively affecting well-being [35]. Furthermore, feelings of pride and satisfaction following successful acts of willpower have been shown to strengthen children’s self-efficacy beliefs and increase their likelihood of persisting in similar situations in the future [12, 36]. Thus, the present findings suggest that exerting willpower may initially be emotionally demanding for children but ultimately becomes reinforced by positive emotions. Although the process requires short-term sacrifice of pleasure, fulfilling responsibilities and demonstrating willpower may transform into a secondary source of pride and happiness.

The study also indicates that children perceive strong willpower not solely as an individual trait but as a socially learned process shaped through observation. Their descriptions of patience, planning, consistency, and prioritizing long-term needs as indicators of strong willpower suggest internalization through adult role models. This perspective aligns with research proposing that the development of self-regulation and willpower is grounded in observing and learning from the behavioral strategies of parents and teachers. For example, a study focusing on early adolescence found that the presence of an adult identified as a role model was associated with higher levels of self-regulation among youth, highlighting the importance of social observation and adult modeling in the development of self-regulatory skills [37]. Within social cognitive theory, Bandura similarly emphasized that individuals learn not only behaviors but also cognitive strategies through observing and imitating role models [36].

The findings further demonstrate that children’s self-regulation strategies consist of diverse and dynamic cognitive, behavioral, and motivational processes that develop according to age and context, consistent with contemporary research conceptualizing self-regulation as the interaction of cognition, emotion, and behavior [19, 34]. Goal attainment is not solely associated with the frequency of suppressing desires in response to temptations [38]. Studies of everyday self-control suggest that individuals rely not only on effortful inhibitory strategies but also on a wide range of self-regulatory strategies [33]. Consistent with the literature, children in this study described various strategies, including planning, time management, pairing less preferred tasks with enjoyable activities, cognitive reframing, and breaking tasks into manageable parts. These findings suggest that children are capable of developing contextually appropriate strategies to meet self-regulatory demands. However, the results also indicate that not all strategies are functional in every situation. For example, a child who aimed to complete homework quickly in order to use a mobile phone reported that their mind remained focused on the phone throughout the task, suggesting that the chosen strategy was insufficient in regulating attention and cognitive resources. This highlights that effective self-regulation involves not only possessing strategies but also selecting those appropriate to contextual and task-related demands. Thus, children’s self-regulation appears to be characterized by flexible and context-sensitive strategy use rather than reliance on singular solutions.

Descriptions conveyed through metaphors revealed that children associate willpower with strength, order, control, and protection, perceiving it as a mechanism that provides structure and resilience in the face of everyday challenges. Metaphors such as “shield” and “robot” suggest that children view willpower as serving a protective function against emotional and environmental threats. This indicates that children conceptualize willpower not merely as an internal trait but as an active and functional force that enables them to cope with challenging situations. These findings further suggest that children’s beliefs about the meaning of willpower influence the behavioral strategies they employ. Indeed, Haimovitz et al. demonstrated that when children perceive willpower as a non-depleting and energizing resource, they exhibit greater resistance to temptation and generate more effective strategies [10]. Thus, the beliefs children adopt when choosing between competing desires (e.g., play versus responsibility) may shape their self-regulatory performance.

From a pediatric nursing perspective, it is important not only to expect compliance with health-related behaviors but also to understand children’s internal conflicts, emotional challenges, and the strategies they employ. An approach in which nurses recognize children’s efforts, reinforce positive emotions, and guide the development of developmentally appropriate strategies may contribute to strengthening self-regulatory capacities. Furthermore, nurses’ consistent and patient behaviors may serve as important role models for children.

In this context, the development of willpower and self-regulation cannot be reduced to age alone; longitudinal research that considers individual and contextual differences is needed [19]. Addressing pediatric nursing practices from a holistic perspective that acknowledges this multidimensional structure is critically important for supporting children’s long-term health and well-being.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to the literature by examining children’s experiences of willpower and self-regulation directly from their own perspectives, thereby offering valuable insight into their everyday lived experiences and subjective meaning-making processes. One strength of the study lies in its qualitative phenomenological approach, which enabled the exploration of children’s experiences, the strategies they employ, and the metaphorical meanings they attribute to willpower. The findings may also provide insights for future research and interventions aimed at supporting children’s self-regulatory capacities by highlighting how children themselves conceptualize and experience self-regulation.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted within a specific social and educational context, which may limit the transferability of the findings to different cultural or socioeconomic settings. In addition, the relatively small sample size and the use of purposive sampling may restrict the diversity of experiences represented in the data. Because willpower and responsibility are socially valued attributes, some children may have tended to present themselves in a socially desirable manner despite efforts to create a supportive and non-judgmental interview environment. Furthermore, although focus group interviews facilitated interaction among participants and supported the articulation of experiences, group dynamics may have influenced how some children expressed their views. Finally, the relatively wide age range of participants may have introduced developmental differences in how children understood and described their experiences. Future studies may benefit from combining focus group discussions with individual interviews and exploring developmental differences across age groups in greater depth.

Conclusion

This study provides an understanding of how school-aged children experience and construct willpower in their everyday lives. The findings of this study demonstrate that school-aged children experience willpower and self-regulation as dynamic processes in which emotional, cognitive, and social dimensions are closely intertwined within everyday life. Children described willpower and self-regulation in the context of internal conflicts between competing desires and responsibilities, emphasizing that these processes involve not only emotional challenges but also the reinforcement of positive emotions.

The findings further indicate that children conceptualize willpower not merely as an internal control mechanism, but as a functional skill shaped through observation of adult role models and enacted through context-sensitive strategies. The strategies children reported, such as planning, time management, associating tasks with rewards, and cognitive redirection, reflect their active engagement in self-regulatory processes. However, not all strategies appear to be equally effective across situations; successful self-regulation seems closely linked to selecting strategies that are appropriate to the specific context and task demands.

Metaphorical expressions revealed that children conceptualize willpower as a source of strength, order, and protection, suggesting that these beliefs may support coping and persistence in challenging situations. Thus, children’s meaning-making processes regarding willpower appear to shape their self-regulatory performance, highlighting the importance of addressing not only behavioral choices but also underlying beliefs.

Overall, by making children’s lived experiences of willpower and self-regulation visible through their own expressions, this study provides an important foundation for educational and clinical practices aimed at supporting the development of these capacities. The findings suggest that intervention and support programs should create space for children’s emotional experiences, acknowledge the influence of role models, and promote flexible and context-sensitive strategy use. Future research involving more diverse contexts, larger samples, and longitudinal designs is needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the development of willpower and self-regulation in childhood.
