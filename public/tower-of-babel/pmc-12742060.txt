Ambulatory management of primary spontaneous pneumothorax in the emergency department: EFFI-PNO protocol – a multicentre, cluster-controlled, stepped-wedge, randomised interventional study
Authors: Sabrina Kepka, Valérie Wilmé, Martin Duracinsky, Cezar Matau, Arnaud Nze Ossima, Cedric Gil Jardine, Pierrick Le Borgne, Nicolas Marjanovic, Tania Marx, Mickaël Ohana, Olivier Peyrony, Anne-Laure Philippon, Damien Viglino, Aline Chenou, Raphael Clere-Jehl, Pascal Bilbault, Isabelle Durand-Zaleski, Erik André Sauleau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFor large primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP), drainage or simple aspiration are the two first-line treatment options. Outpatient ambulatory strategies have a success rate of almost 80% with few complications. New French recommendations suggest that an outpatient strategy should be preferred if an appropriate care network is in place. However, establishing this care network remains the main obstacle to the use of this strategy. Thus, outpatient management of PSP remains rare, which is neither optimal for the patient, with a likely impact on quality of life (QOL) and satisfaction with care, nor for the healthcare system, with increased costs. We hypothesise that outpatient treatment of PSP compared with usual inpatient management could improve quality of care and represent a more efficient, generalisable and sustainable strategy.Methods and analysisIn this multicentre, cluster-controlled, randomised interventional study with stepped wedge implementation, patients aged 18–50 presenting to the emergency department (ED) with a first episode of large PSP will be enrolled in seven university hospitals in France. The main objective of this study is to evaluate the impact on changes in QOL of an ambulatory strategy for the management of large PSP in the ED compared with usual inpatient management. The primary outcome is the difference in QOL as measured by SF-36 score, between drain placement and 6 months. Clinical criteria (pulmonary expansion at 6 days, pain, dyspnoea, complications, recurrence), perceived quality of care (satisfaction, patient preference, anxiety) and ease of implementation of the care pathway will also be assessed. A cost-utility analysis will be carried out to evaluate the incremental cost-utility ratio at 1 year, defined as the difference in costs divided by the difference in utility estimated by the EQ-5D scores.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord Ouest III N° 2024-36. Study findings will be disseminated by publication in a high-impact international journal. Results will be presented at national and international emergency healthcare meetings, and participating patients notified of the main conclusions.Trial registration numberThis trial is registered with Clinical Trials Registry NCT06471608. The trial protocol and statistical design are fully described in this study protocol. Additional data can be provided on reasonable request to the sponsor. Introduction

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) corresponds to a pleural detachment occurring in a healthy subject. PSP is generally considered a benign condition, with the main risk being recurrence in around 30% of cases.1 2 The practitioners involved in the initial management of patients are most often emergency physicians, but this care is carried out in collaboration with thoracic surgeons and pulmonologists.

There are several therapeutic options available for the management of patients with a first episode of PSP: therapeutic abstention and monitoring; for large PSP, exsufflation and thoracic drainage (surgery is not indicated as a first-line treatment for a first episode). Elements usually taken into account in this decision of intervention are the tolerance of the pneumothorax, its size, the preference of the treating physician and the patient’s follow-up possibilities.3 For a first episode of large PSP, physicians generally choose chest tube drainage or simple aspiration. Furthermore, some authors have been proposing less invasive, ambulatory strategies for over 10 years, with success rates of almost 80%.4,7 Despite the recommendations of the British Thoracic Society, which promoted less invasive treatment by aspiration, these recommendations are poorly followed, and the most frequent strategy of first-line treatment remains chest tube drainage.8 9

Recently published French recommendations10 encourage outpatient ambulatory management based on simple aspiration or placement of a mini thoracic chest tube and unidirectional valve in the case that the patient is stable after evacuation of intrapleural air; a dedicated outpatient care system is already in place and a consultation with ultrasound or chest X-ray at 24–72 hours is scheduled to monitor progress. The recommendations state that ambulatory management of PSP should only be undertaken if the patient is fully able to comprehend instructions on discharge, has been provided with an information leaflet outlining how to act in case of problems and is accompanied for the first 24–48 hour after discharge.

The latest French recommendations are based solely on expert opinions due to the lack of high-quality comparative studies comparing outpatient strategies and usual strategies. Recent evidence from the UK radomized ambulatory management of primary pneumothorax (RAMPP) trial conducted in 24 UK hospitals between 2015 and 2019 demonstrated that ambulatory management reduced the duration of hospitalisation including re-admissions in the first 30 days, but with the expense of increased adverse events. The consequences of outpatient management involve different outcomes for assessment: efficacy and safety, as highlighted by the RAMPP study, patients’ quality of life (QOL) and the consequences for the healthcare system.

First, QOL is a key factor to be assessed. QOL may be impacted by persistent pain, the risk of recurrence and limitations on certain activities. To our knowledge, only one study has considered QOL as a primary outcome measure for evaluating treatment of PSP, comparing two types of surgery. But a questionnaire specific to cancer was used, which does not seem to be appropriate for this condition.11 The RAMPP study detailed QOL (quality of life) for the cost-utility analysis using EQ-5D-5L but SF-36 may provide information different from EQ-5D-5L. In addition to the impact on QOL associated with the disease, the care pathway itself can have an impact. It must be taken into consideration that patients could improve their QOL through outpatient care compared with hospital care.

Second, the real-world implementation of such strategies across diverse emergency departments (EDs) remains uncertain. The major obstacle remains an absence of organised follow-up network, which is an essential prerequisite for implementation of an ambulatory strategy.10 Thus, recourse to outpatient management of PSP remains rare, which is neither optimal for the patient, with a likely impact on perceived quality of care, nor for the healthcare system, which experiences increased costs. Although the technical aspect is not complicated to implement, the structuring of a multidisciplinary out-patient network involving all players in the care pathway is challenging. Indeed, the management of pneumothorax is multidisciplinary. A previous study conducted in 14 French EDs revealed that while the procedure to evacuate intra-pleural air is carried out in 65% of cases by an emergency physician,12 downstream services are most often provided by the surgical and pulmonology departments. Among 1868 spontaneous pneumothoraxes included in the study, an intervention was performed for 883 patients (47%), by emergency physicians in 71% of cases and thoracic drainage was the most frequent choice of intervention (670 patients, 76%). The main difficulty for practitioners in offering outpatient treatment is the 24-hour organisation of this care chain to ensure follow-up of these patients. In a Global Emergency Medicine Journal Club forum, practitioners who were surveyed following publication of the study by Voisin et al7 13 seemed to agree that ambulatory management was feasible for emergency physicians. However, the problem of follow-up for these patients was highlighted, as most of them had no organised downstream care pathway to enable this strategy to be applied. In order to use this strategy, it is essential to have a thorough understanding of the different pathways and to be able to implement them according to a specific methodology. The RAMPP14 study provided important insights into the effectiveness of outpatient care. The EFFI-PNO trial aims to evaluate QOL outcomes, the implementation, the safety and cost-effectiveness of an ambulatory care pathway in France.

Finally, the lack of ambulatory strategies has repercussions on the care system in terms of cost. In the USA, the cost to the healthcare system of spontaneous pneumothorax is estimated at 130 million dollars per year, involving over 20 000 patients.15 The economic consequences of treating this pathology are essentially attributable to hospitalisation costs, which depend on length of stay and type of treatment. A study by Bobbio et al revealed 59 637 hospitalisations for pneumothorax in France from January 2008 to December 2011, representing around 0.09% of hospitalisations during this period.1 The average length of stay was 7 days, corresponding to the high average length of hospital stay usually found in cases of PSP, which ranges from 4 to 6 days, depending on the study.1 2 In the French study by Voisin et al, evaluating ambulatory management with a drain left in place connected to a Heimlich valve, a success rate of 78% was reported,7 with few complications, and a cost reduction of US$3350 per patient compared with conventional strategies. However, there is a lack of data on the medico-economic impact of the ambulatory strategy, particularly in France. In a general sense, there is a lack of published evidence concerning the cost-efficacy of different PSP treatments, whether in an ambulatory setting or not: the Cochrane meta-analysis concluded that no study had provided data on the cost-effectiveness ratio of therapeutic strategies.16 Recent studies provide data on the economic impact of the ambulatory strategy as a whole, but focus on the Anglo-Saxon care system. An initial study conducted in the UK revealed that the ambulatory strategy was cost-effective, reducing costs (−£788, 95% CI −1527 to −50; p=0.037) compared with standard treatment. No difference was found in the number of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained (mean difference: −0.001, 95% CI −0.032 to 0.030; p=0.95) in the RAMPP study.17 Finally, a second study in Ireland demonstrated the efficiency of an integrated care pathway for pneumothorax patients with a focus on outpatient care, reducing costs by €2431 per patient without providing an efficiency ratio.18 The ambulatory strategy may therefore present the opportunity to reduce PSP-associated costs for the healthcare system, while maintaining or improving QOL for the patient.

In this study, we hypothesise that an ambulatory strategy will positively impact on patient QOL compared with the standard of care, namely in-patient treatment. To evaluate the implementation of an outpatient strategy, we will undertake a pragmatic design by an interventional, multicentre, cluster-controlled, randomised trial, with a stepped-wedge implementation.

The primary study objective is to evaluate the impact of an ambulatory strategy on QOL as compared with the standard hospitalisation in-patient strategy in the context of PSP. A secondary hypothesis is that the ambulatory strategy represents a more cost-effective strategy in this setting.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a multicentre, cluster-controlled, randomised interventional study with stepped wedge implementation. This study will take place at seven University Hospitals in France (Grenoble, Bordeaux, Poitiers, Paris (two centres), Besançon, Strasbourg) over a study period of 43 months, with an estimated start date of July 2025. The timeline reflects sequential stepped-wedge randomisation and projected recruitment feasibility. The duration of participation for each subject will be 12 months.

Eligibility criteria

Patients aged 18–50 presenting to the ED with a first episode of large PSP diagnosed by chest X-ray or CT scan. Large PSPs are defined by a visible rim along the entire axillary line, ≥2 cm between the lung margin and the chest wall at the hilum level on chest X-ray and CT scan.10 19

Recruitment and consent

An initial information visit will take place in the ED by the emergency physicians to verify that the subject meets the eligibility criteria. Participants will be informed of the study at the time of their emergency room visit for suspected PSP(Online supplemental file 1). If the patient meets all the eligibility criteria and agrees to participate, the emergency physician in charge of the patient will obtain their informed consent, at least 15 min after the information visit and prior to any research-related examination.

The patients receive either outpatient or inpatient management based on the randomisation of the hospital at which they present. The randomisation will be conducted according to a stepped wedge design (figure 1). Due to organisational constraints that prevent offering both pathways simultaneously at a centre, patient-level randomisation was not suitable, and the stepped wedge design was the most appropriate for this study. Moreover, this design will allow for the implementation analysis that is proposed to establish a sustainable care pathway. The centres recruiting patients are distributed across France to ensure representativeness.

Then, in the context of this project, the centres will progressively move into the experimental group (ambulatory strategy), with all centres initially implementing the conventional strategy, followed by a gradual transition of centres based on randomisation. Randomisation was conducted to ensure an equal number of participants in each group. The randomisation would be performed automatically by ePRO software and that centres would be randomly assigned to the outpatient group in three stages, based on the number of patients included in each group, in order to ensure recruitment. At the start of enrolment, all patients will be included in the control group. Once 140 patients have been enrolled in the control group, three randomly selected centres will implement the outpatient strategy. Then, when 170 patients have been enrolled in the control group, three other centres will switch to the outpatient group. A final stage will take place when all patients in the control group have been enrolled, allowing for ambulatory strategy in all centres.

All data will be collected on an electronic case report form eCRF (cleanweb) after a training of assessors. Following written informed consent, patients will be assigned a subject number in the eCRF.

Initial management and drain installation

Initial management in the ED will be in line with standard practice for both control and experimental groups. A clinical examination will be performed by the emergency physician with the collection of vital parameters (oxygen saturation, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature), a cardiopulmonary auscultation, verification for signs of respiratory distress and the collection of medical and surgical history (thoracic surgery, asthma, chronic bronchitis, allergies), concomitant treatments and PSP risk factors (especially tobacco and cannabis use).

Furthermore, a biological work-up will be carried out.1 The biological assessment will be performed according to routine care and may include, depending on the assessment made by the physician as part of the treatment: blood count, coagulation, electrolyte panel, creatinine, blood gases if necessary. A thoracic imaging (by chest X-ray or ultra-low-dose thoracic CT scanner depending on the equipment available in the participating centre) will be performed to confirm the diagnosis of pneumothorax and assess its size. In both groups, the proposed treatment will be thoracic drainage according to current French recommendations.10 Patients may withdraw their consent and ask to leave the study at any time. In this case, they will be cared for in accordance with standard practice.

In the control group (inpatient management), the type of material used for chest tube drainage will be a straight drain or pigtail drain, diameter less than ≤14 Fr inserted using the Seldinger versus or standard drainage technique with an internal mandrel drain. The drain will be connected to the suction system at −5 to −10 cm H2O. Thoracic imaging (chest X-ray or low-dose CT scan, as detailed above) will be performed in the ED prior to transfer to a hospital department: pulmonology, thoracic surgery, emergency short-stay hospitalisation unit, critical care, depending on the usual pathway of the centre in which the patient is included. They will then be monitored in hospital until the pneumothorax has completely resolved and the drain has been removed (on average 4–6 days).

In the experimental group (outpatient management), the drain will also be placed in the ED. The drain used will be a Furhmann drain connected to a Heimlich valve. It will then be connected to the suction system at −5 to −10 cm H2O until bubbling stops. The patient will be monitored for 4 hours after placement of the chest tube, in the ED. After the 4-hour monitoring period, and before the patient is discharged home, the patient will undergo follow-up imaging (chest X-ray or low-dose CT scan, as detailed above), in accordance with French recommendations.10

If the pneumothorax is still very large, or if clinical tolerance is unsatisfactory (dyspnoea, unrelieved pain, abnormal vital parameters), the patient should remain hospitalised. In this case, these patients will nevertheless be analysed within the ambulatory group, as this is inherent to this care pathway, which requires good clinical progress in the ED for the patient to be able to return home with a chest drain. If the lung is in the process of reattachment, with minimal detachment remaining, and if clinical tolerance is good (assessed by vital parameters, with oxygen saturation above 98%, good haemodynamic stability and pain effectively relieved by analgesics), the patient can be discharged and return home.

A checklist to be carried out by the ED nurse and doctor will be validated before discharge and signed by the ED physician and nurse taking charge of the patient. This checklist will include the following elements: the verification of the drain in place (assembly, dressing, valve position), ensuring that the ambulatory care criteria are met, that the patient file has been properly transmitted to the follow-up department and that the patient has received the information sheet (including instructions for drain use and contact number). An information sheet will also be given to the patient, explaining the device and monitoring instructions which will also have been explained to the patient by the nurse and doctor. The patient will be contacted by the department in charge of follow-up to define the times of follow-up consultations at days 2, 4 and 6. A dedicated telephone number will be issued to the patient and noted on their provided information sheet, so that they can contact a practitioner 7 days a week, 24 hours a day (pulmonologist or surgeon, and ED).

After drain insertion, still during the initial management in the ED, both groups will receive the following surveys to complete: QOL questionnaire (SF-36), satisfaction questionnaire (Short Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS)), patient preference assessment, anxiety questionnaire (STAI) and symptoms (pain and dyspnoea). The surveys will be completed by the patient via ePro software (Cleanweb). This will allow patients to fill in the surveys directly on their smartphones or tablets, improving completion rates. In addition, several reminders will be sent via this application to minimise the risk of missing data

Follow-up

In the control group, close follow-up will be carried out by the physician in charge of the patient during hospitalisation (pulmonologist, surgeon, emergency physician), lasting on average between 4 and 6 days, in line with current practice. Every 2 days (D2, D4 and D6) until the end of hospitalisation (and at most until D6) data will be collected by clinical examination, collection of symptoms and complications. Chest X-rays will also be taken, in line with standard practice, to determine the evolution of the pneumothorax.

In the experimental group, close monitoring will consist of three follow-up consultations at day 2 after patient discharge, day 4 after patient discharge and day 6 after patient discharge. Consultations will be conducted in the department responsible for follow-up (pulmonology, surgery, or possibly in the ED, depending on the predefined organisation adopted by the centre). If the consultation days fall on weekends, the patient may be assessed in the ED, in liaison with the follow-up department.

The removal of the drain will be decided by the physician in charge of the patient based on lung re-expansion assessed through the follow-up chest X-ray. In treatment is considered successful and the drain removed before V3 at D6, the patient will not be systematically reviewed at subsequent follow-up visits. Success will therefore be considered at D6 at the latest. If treatment has not been successful, the patient will be hospitalised and follow a surgical pathway, as is routine.

In both groups, a visit at 1 month will take place with clinical examination (vital parameters, cardiopulmonary auscultation, search for signs of respiratory distress), surveys (SF-36, SAPS, STAI and patient preference), symptoms (pain and dyspnoea), care consumption and complications.

Symptoms and care consumption will be collected remotely every month up to 12 months, via ePRO software. Surveys (satisfaction, QOL, anxiety, patient care preference) will be collected remotely at 6 months after inclusion via ePRO software.

The 12-month visit is the final study visit for both groups performed in hospital by the physicians responsible for follow-up (pulmonology, surgery or possibly in the emergency department, depending on the organisation adopted by the centre), after which the subject’s participation in the research ends. It will include a clinical examination (vital parameters, cardiopulmonary auscultation, search for signs of respiratory distress), verification of any recurrences during the study year, collection of major and minor complications, collection of surveys (SF-36, SAPS, STAI and patient preference) and care consumptions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study is the difference in QOL as measured by SF-36 score, between drain placement and 6 months for an ambulatory strategy for the management of large PSP compared with usual management. The 6-month period allows us to capture long-term effects (recurrences, return to work, psychological adjustment).

Data collection, storage and verification

Socio-demographic and clinical data

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of patients will be recorded by an investigating physician: age, sex, medical and surgical history (pneumothorax, thoracic surgery, asthma, chronic bronchitis, allergy), smoking, cannabis use, STAI anxiety score, QOL score (SF-36), satisfaction assessed by SAPS questionnaire, patient preference (outpatient or inpatient, coded from 0 to 5), symptoms: pain (numerical pain scale coded from 0 to 10, 10 being the maximum) and dyspnoea using mMRC classification (0 to 4), minor and major complications, time spent in ED, hospitalisation service in case of hospitalisation, success of treatment at 6 days and recurrence at 1 year.

Monitoring

The study monitoring is performed by the promotor Hôpitaux Universitaires de Strasbourg to ensure that data are complete and correctly registered consistent with the source data, in addition to reviewing compliance of the study protocol, laws and regulation.

Adverse events and safety

Adverse events occurring during the study period will be documented and evaluated for severity and causality.

Data management

The study data will be collected electronically using an eCRF designed with Telemedicine software by the data managers at the Clinical Research Unit of Hôpitaux Universitaires de Strasbourg. Data entry will be performed by investigators from each centre. The system used to create the eCRF (cleanweb telemedicine) and collect the required data will comply with the current regulations mandated by Commission nationale de l'informatique et des libertés (CNIL) (MR001). Access to the eCRF will be limited to authorised personnel including the sponsor, principal investigators and clinical trial assistants. Rights such as reading, writing and access to specific CRF pages will be allocated based on the individual’s role in the study and their centre. User authentication will be achieved through a unique username and password customised for each user.

Coherence tests can be scheduled according to the study’s risk level in coordination with the study’s sponsor team. After these tests, queries (requests for clarification) may be sent to investigators to correct or confirm the data entered into the eCRF.

Documents and data relating to the research will be kept by the sponsor and the investigator for the duration of the research and for a period of 15 years following the end of the research (research involving drugs, medical devices or in vitro diagnostic medical devices or research not involving a product mentioned in article L.5311-1 of the French Public Health Code.

All data, documents and reports may be audited or inspected. These documents must be kept in such a way as to be readily available to the competent authorities and accessible to them on request.

Qualitative comparative analysis

The qualitative comparative analysis will be implemented in three stages. Interviews will be conducted by a team of PhD university researchers, experts in the field of qualitative assessments.

In-depth interviews with patients

The first stage involves in-depth interviews with patients. Inclusion for this stage will take place at the start of the project in all centres, when patients are enrolled in the inpatient or outpatient programmes. A randomly selected subset of patients (n=10 for each group) will be invited to participate in qualitative interviews. Inclusion will occur through purposive sampling, with attention to diversity of age, gender, profession and level of education.

Although the sampling of individual interviews (10 inpatients and 10 outpatients, for a total of 20 patients) may not be representative of the entire study population, it allows for an in-depth exploration of lived experiences, in line with a qualitative research approach.20 Prior to the interviews, an interview guide will be developed based on existing literature on implementation and care pathways assessment. The guide will address a list of characteristics found in the literature on implementation21:

Data will be collected through audio recordings by a researcher (using a dictaphone). Semi-structured interviews will last 45 min. Audio recordings will then be transcribed and anonymised, following an ethical protocol. Once transcribed, the qualitative interviews will be imported into NVivo 12 software. NVivo 12 will facilitate systematic data coding and analysis. The interview data will undergo thematic content analysis, with NVivo 12 enabling structured analysis through different levels of coding, ultimately leading to a final thematic tree. The interviews will be coded by the team of researchers. Following coding, and in accordance with consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) recommendations, a researcher triangulation process will be conducted to ensure the relevance and reliability of the results.

Individual interviews with health professionals

At the end of enrolment, interviews with caregivers (n=10) will be carried out. Health professionals in several study centres will be randomly selected from among surgeons, pulmonologists, emergency physicians and nurses involved in pneumothorax management and who have dealt with the device on an outpatient basis. The similar diversification criteria will be applied, as for participating patients (age, gender, profession and level of education). An interview guide will be created to address this population, following the same characteristics as for in-depth interviews with patients. The role of the professional in the management of PSP, and his opinion of the outpatient care pathway, the implementation of the outpatient care pathway and its consequences, the patient relationship, the care pathway evaluation and sustainability will be the items guiding the interviews with professionals. The data will be collected and treated as described above.

Focus group with health professionals

2 months after the implementation of the outpatient pathway (ie, first patient included in the ambulatory management group), a focus group will be organised with health professionals to gain a deeper understanding of the themes emerging from the in-depth interviews. The focus group will be conducted via a teleconference application and led by the researcher who conducted the interviews. It will last 2 hours. Participants (6–8 members) will be recruited from all centres, ensuring diversity among healthcare professionals involved in the management of the PSP (including emergency physicians, pulmonologists, surgeons and nurses). The purpose of the focus group is to discuss the findings from the in-depth interviews and provide input for the implementation analysis, particularly in assessing the impact of the system on medical practices and the organisation of care.

Participants will respond to a series of questions developed from the thematic analysis of the interviews, focusing on key dimensions identified in the literature on implementation20:

Penetration: assessment of the integration of outpatient care into the healthcare system.

Fidelity: need for practice adaptations to ensure the implementation of the pathway and strategies for adoption.

Feasibility: feasibility from the perspective of care management, including the organisation of the institution, the service and the caregiver-patient relationship.

The focus group will be recorded and will follow the same data analysis procedure as described above to produce a report on each characteristic of implementation.

Statistical analysis

Sample size and power calculations

To estimate the number of participants to be included in the study, we use the calculation technique described in Hemming et al.22 It is assumed that, for an identical QOL measure at study entry, the difference at the end of the study will be five evolution points in the mean SF-36 score between the two arms. A SD of 15 is assumed. The number of participants to be included (for a power of 80% and an alpha risk of 5% in a one-tailed test) is then 226 (113 per group). It is assumed that the inter-cluster variance is equal to or even greater than the intra-cluster variance: the hypothesis is that participants within a centre will be fairly homogeneous, and that a significant difference in QoL measures will be due to the difference between centres. The coefficient used is 0.6. The calculations lead to a design effect of 1.40. The number of participants to be included is then 323.85. To take account of study withdrawals, this number is increased by 20%, which gives 55 participants to be included in each centre (ie, a total of 386 participants).

Description of statistical methods used

Statistical analyses will include a descriptive phase, followed by an inferential phase using fully Bayesian techniques. Markov chains and Monte Carlo integrations will be used for the bulk of estimates, with a preference for a priori laws that approximate conjugate situations. Unless convergence diagnostics indicate otherwise, three chains will be used with separate starting points, a burn-in of 100 000 for each chain, then 100 000 useful iterations with a thinning of 25, to create a total sample of 12 000 iterations, on which Monte Carlo integration will be used to extract distributional characteristics. All analyses will be carried out using R software (with ad hoc packages, notably R2jags). Sensitivity analyses will be systematically carried out and will include modification of the hyperparameters of the prior distributions, considering two scenarios, in addition to the default choice of low-information priors (Jeffreys prior, for example): an optimistic scenario and a pessimistic one.

In the descriptive phase, all the variables collected will be summarised: numbers and frequencies for qualitative variables on the one hand, and minimum, percentiles (2.5, 25, 50, 75 and 97.5), maximum, mean and SD for quantitative variables on the other. For variables collected regularly over time, these descriptions will be global and at each time. Graphs (boxplot, histogram, etc.) will complete the descriptions.

This description will be enriched by an inference allowing the observed and summarised values to be extrapolated to the population. For quantitative variables, a normal likelihood will be assumed, matched on the mean, conditional on the variance, by a vague normal a priori law (of a mean deemed reasonable for the variable studied and of large variance, of the order of a coefficient of variation of 100) and on the inverse of the variance (precision) by a vague gamma a priori law (of parameters 0.0001 and 0.001, therefore of mean 0.1 and variance 100). For categorical variables (for which we have to estimate several frequencies summing to 1), a multinomial likelihood will be assumed, with a vague Dirichlet a priori law (Jeffreys prior, with parameters 1/2).

The main objective of the study is to compare the change in QOL, measured by the SF-36 score (from 0 to 100), between inclusion in the study (after drain placement) and at 6 months, between the outpatient group and the inpatient group. The analysis should take into account the stepped-wedge study design. As the study comprises a reduced number of stages and time points, the models will be simple (in particular, the Generalized Estimating Equations approach will not be attempted).

The model used will be a generalized linear model (GLM) mixed regression. SF-36 is a doubly-bounded variable and will therefore be analysed by Beta regression. If the number of modalities and their positions allow, an additional linear regression will be attempted. The SF-36 ranges from 0 to 100. All the values of this score will have to be reduced to the interval (0,1), first by dividing by 100 and then by using the Smithson-Verkuilen transformation23: if y is the value of the score divided by 100 and n the number of values, y is replaced by (y (n−1)+0.5)/n. A beta distribution of parameters a and b is then assumed for each of these new values. The distribution will be reparameterised to facilitate modelling and understanding of the estimates, in the form of mean, μ, and precision, φ, using the formulas: a = μ φ and b = φ−μ φ. We will then model only the mean. On the precision, a vague gamma a priori distribution will be placed (of parameters 0.005 and 0.01). The link used in GLM is logit.

The linear predictor will include an overall mean (with an a priori normal distribution of mean 0. 7, for a mean QoL score of 70, and variance 1, that used for calculating the number of participants, scaled to 0–1), a random cluster effect (in the form of two indicator variables for the three clusters) whose a priori distribution will be a normal distribution centred on 0 and variance 43, that used to calculate the number of participants), a time effect (in the form of three indicator variables for the four clusters, with the baseline serving as a reference) and a fixed effect of the cluster and time: variables Xij will be created to represent the group (0 for inpatient and 1 for outpatient) at the time of the QoL measurement (i is the cluster and j is the time).

Secondary analyses will be of the same type as the main Bayesian mixed GLM analysis, but depending on the type of variable studied, the GLM’s likelihood and link will change. The parameters will be identical to those of the main analysis.

To test the significance of a variable, we construct a 95% symmetrical a posteriori credibility interval for its parameter, bounded by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the sample distribution. The variable will be considered ‘significant’ if 0 is not continuous in this interval (or 1 if it is an OR in a logistic regression). In addition, the probability of a parameter being above or below a given value (clinical significance) is estimated by the frequency with which, in the a posteriori sample, the parameter is above or below this value.

Missing, unused or invalid data

No statistical procedure will be used to replace missing values.

Descriptive analyses will use all available data for each variable to be described. In univariate inference, the participants included will be those for whom the outcome variable and the sub-study variable are met. In multivariate inference, the participants included will be those for whom all the data for the variables involved at the start of the multivariate phase are known.

Analyses will be performed on all intention-to-treat participants.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The medico-economic analysis will be carried out in accordance with the recommendations of the French National Authority for Health and the CHEERS methodology, measuring costs and outcomes at 12 months for the same population.24 25

The economic evaluation will be carried out from a collective perspective, with a 12-month time horizon. A cost-utility analysis will be carried out, as QOL is the study’s primary endpoint. The resources consumed will be collected prospectively in electronic case report form supplemented by PMSI data (a French national hospital discharge database), and via a secure application for patient follow-up (outpatient consultations, medication, alternative therapy). For in-hospital resource utilisation, hospitalisations are valued using the corresponding French disease-related group cost, adjusted by length of stay and number of days in intensive care. For out-of-hospital data, costs will be based on health insurance tariffs. QOL will be assessed using the SF-36 from inclusion to the end of follow-up. A mapping of the SF-36 to the EQ-5D-3L will be applied to obtain an overall utility score from the SF-36 responses for the medico-economic analysis. The results will be presented in the form of an incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR) relating the mean of the differences in costs between the outpatient strategy and the inpatient strategy to the mean of the differences in gains in QALYs of these two strategies.

Costs and QOL measures in the two arms will be compared by establishing CIs of differences using the bootstrap technique. 95% CIs for ICUR will be estimated, also using the bootstrap method. The results will be plotted on a cost-effectiveness plane, showing the differences in QALYs and the differences in costs between the two arms of the study.

Second, an acceptability curve will plot the probability of the outpatient strategy being cost-effective as a function of the amount the healthcare payer would be willing to pay for its implementation.

The robustness of the results will be assessed using a deterministic sensitivity analysis to take into account the uncertainty of the parameters studied by varying their values, in particular the inclusion of indirect costs.

Discussion

This study will enable all the consequences of the outpatient strategy to be taken into account at patient level, at the level of the professionals involved and at societal level, with a view to proposing a sustainable and transferable care pathway. First, the collaborative healthcare pathway enabling outpatient management will take into account the impact on QOL. The RAMPP study revealed that at day 30, the median hospitalisation was significantly shorter in the 114 patients with available data who received ambulatory treatment (0 days (IQR 0–3)) than in the 113 with available data who received standard care (4 days (IQR 0–8); p<0.0001; median difference 2 days (95% CI 1 to 3)).14 Furthermore, 110 (47%) of 236 patients had adverse events, including 64 (55%) of 117 patients in the ambulatory care arm and 46 (39%) of 119 in the standard care arm. In this study, QOL was described as part of the cost-utility study with the EQ-5D-5L. However, the use of the SF36 questionnaire might have allowed for a better assessment of the consequences on all dimensions of QOL than EQ-5D-5L. To date, no previous study has focused on the impact on patients’ QOL and at the same time on the analysis of the implementation of an ambulatory care pathway for the management of PSP. However, care pathways and management habits vary from centre to centre, and from practitioner to practitioner. A study conducted in the UK assessed the management of pneumothorax in practice by questioning practitioners, highlighting wide variations in practice: 50% would manage conservatively a significant primary pneumothorax with minimal symptoms, compared with only 3% in the case of significant symptoms; 64% use suction if the pneumothorax has not resolved after>2 days, 15% always clamp the chest tube before removing it, while 30% never do so.26 Taking these factors into account to propose a generalisable and sustainable ambulatory care pathway requires consideration of implementation factors. Only one study has shown the efficiency of an integrated care pathway for patients with pneumothorax.18 However, implementation analysis is essential and enables assessment of the interactions between the intervention process and the implementation context in producing effects.27 The results of this research will make it possible to propose a healthcare pathway that can be generalised. The aim is to provide the keys to establish the ambulatory care pathway, taking into account the feedback from patients and professionals in the qualitative analysis. In addition, qualitative approaches are an integral part of the implementation process, since they enable us to build on individual findings to help generalise the approach and thereby improve the health of beneficiaries.28

Finally, the present study will evaluate the efficiency with a medico-economic evaluation about PSP treatment strategies. Then, the aim of a cost-utility study is to rank different options according to their ability to produce the best possible results from the resources to be mobilised. In a context of limited resources, it is important to be able to identify the least costly strategies with superior or comparable efficacy to the reference strategy, as the choice of resource allocation is essential to ensure the sustainability of the healthcare system. In this study, medico-economic analysis is essential because of the strategy’s potential impact on hospital resources, since the costs associated with pneumothorax management are essentially related to hospital admissions. In addition, the implementation analysis proposed in this study must evaluate costs, which represent one of the variables to be taken into account.
