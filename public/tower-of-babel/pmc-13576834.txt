Population Pharmacokinetics and Exposure–Response of Sasanlimab, a Checkpoint Inhibitor, in Patients with Non‐Muscle Invasive Bladder Cancer and Other Solid Tumors
Authors: Joanna C. Masters, Brian Jermain, Alan Liu, Rossano Cesari, Anna Maria Calella, Elisabete Michelon, Caimiao Wei, Susana Liu, Joseph Piscitelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSasanlimab is a monoclonal antibody (mAb) which blocks human programed cell death protein 1. It is administered subcutaneously (SC), currently under development for non‐muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) with a recommended flat dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W). The objectives of the analyses were to characterize the population pharmacokinetics (popPK) of sasanlimab and to determine the exposure–response (ER) relationship between sasanlimab and safety endpoints of interest pooling data across clinical studies including various tumor types. Analyses for efficacy aimed to determine additional ER relationships within the NMIBC population. Sasanlimab PK was typical of IgG4 mAbs and was well characterized by a two‐compartment model, with a small volume of distribution (central volume of 3.67 L, peripheral volume of 2.19 L), low systemic clearance (0.142 L/day), and elimination half‐life of ∼29 days. Bioavailability from SC dosing was ∼70%. There was no evidence of time‐dependent change in sasanlimab clearance, and no meaningful impact of body weight on exposure. There was no association found between sasanlimab exposure and any safety endpoint of interest: treatment‐emergent adverse events of Grade ≥2 and Grade ≥3, and immune‐related adverse events of Any Grade and Grade ≥3. For efficacy, no association was found between exposure and the key endpoints: investigator‐assessed event free survival and complete response (in participants with carcinoma in situ). In total, the popPK results and absence of ER associations for safety and efficacy endpoints support the recommended flat dose of sasanlimab 300 mg SC Q4W.

Introduction

Sasanlimab is a humanized, monoclonal antibody (mAb) with antagonistic activities specific for human programed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1). 1 Sasanlimab can selectively and reversibly bind to human PD‐1 and acts as a checkpoint inhibitor of the PD‐1 pathway, serving as an immuno‐oncology therapy for treatment of cancer. To date, it has been evaluated across several solid tumor types with multiple dosing regimens. 2 , 3 , 4 Sasanlimab pharmacokinetics (PK) are typical of IgG4 mAbs with a small volume of distribution and a low systemic clearance. Based on safety, efficacy, and clinical pharmacology data, the recommended dose for sasanlimab was determined to be 300 mg subcutaneously (SC) every 4 weeks (Q4W). Administration of this checkpoint inhibitor via the subcutaneous route with a flat dose is less invasive (no need for venous access), enables a short administration time, and allows for a simplified administration with no need to calculate dose based on body weight or size. Lastly, the dosing of sasanlimab also reduces complexity for healthcare providers and minimizes risk of calculation errors.

Bladder cancer is among the top 10 cancers globally, and most cases (∼75%) are non‐muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). 5 Current standard of care for high‐risk NMIBC, transurethral resection of bladder tumor followed by intravesical Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) induction and maintenance, offers limited durability and often necessitates radical cystectomy or systemic therapy. 6 , 7 This underscores the urgent need for bladder‐preserving treatments that provide sustained disease control while maintaining quality of life. Support for evaluation of sasanlimab in high‐risk NMIBC came from demonstrating durable anti‐tumor activity and a manageable safety profile in two clinical studies. The first was a Phase 1 study (NCT02573259, B8011001) evaluating sasanlimab in participants with advanced solid tumors, followed by a Phase 1b/2 study (NCT04181788, B8011007) evaluating sasanlimab in participants with advanced malignancies. 2 , 3 , 4

The CREST trial (NCT04165317, B8011006), a global, Phase 3, randomized trial, evaluated sasanlimab in combination with BCG induction and maintenance (BCG‐I+M) (Arm A), sasanlimab in combination with BCG induction (BCG‐I) (Arm B), or BCG‐I + M (Arm C, control) in BCG‐naïve, high‐risk NMIBC. The study met its primary objective by demonstrating a clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvement in investigator‐assessed EFS and, for participants with carcinoma in situ (CIS) at randomization, a clinically meaningful increase of the complete response (CR) rate. 8 For participants treated with sasanlimab + BCG, toxicities were generally manageable, and the observed safety profile was consistent with the known safety profile for each individual agent. 8

The objectives of these population modeling and simulation analyses were to characterize the population PK (popPK) of sasanlimab using data from the three clinical trials, identify potential covariates which impact exposure, provide post hoc exposure predictions, and then to determine the relationship between sasanlimab exposure and clinical safety endpoints of interest treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs [Grade ≥2 and Grade ≥3]) and immune‐related adverse events (irAEs [Any Grade and Grade ≥3]) and key efficacy endpoints from the CREST trial (investigator‐assessed EFS and CR in participants with CIS).

Methods

All three trials were conducted in accordance with International Council on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the ethical principles originating in or derived from the Declaration of Helsinki. An institutional review board approved the protocols, and all participants provided written informed consent before undergoing any study procedures.

Bioanalytical Methods

Serum PF‐06801591 concentrations were determined using a validated electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunoassay on the Meso Scale Discovery platform. The assay calibration range was 48 to 1250 ng/mL. Data were analyzed using a five‐parameter logistic model with 1/Y2 weighting. Assay accuracy and precision met pre‐specified acceptance criteria consistent with regulatory guidance for bioanalytical method validation. Anti‐drug antibodies (ADA) to PF‐06801591 were assessed using a validated high‐sensitivity ECL bridging immunoassay. Samples underwent acid dissociation to minimize target interference. ADA responses were reported as endpoint titers (log10), defined as the reciprocal serum dilution corresponding to the assay cut point. Assay performance met regulatory acceptance criteria for sensitivity, accuracy, and precision.

Analysis Datasets

For the popPK and ER safety analyses, data were pooled from three clinical studies (B8011001, B8011006, and B8011007) (Table 1). These studies included participants with multiple tumor types, with the main cancers being NMIBC, urothelial cancer (UC), and non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). For the ER efficacy analyses, only data from Cohort A of the CREST trial (B8011006) were included. Primary efficacy and safety data from each study have been published. 2 , 3 , 4 , 8

Population PK Analysis

All participants from the three studies who had at least one sasanlimab dose and a corresponding postdose PK concentration were included in the analysis. A nonlinear mixed effect modeling approach was used for the popPK analysis with first‐order conditional estimation with interaction (FOCEI) estimation in NONMEM version 7.5 (ICON, Maryland). The PK of sasanlimab in serum was described by a two‐compartment model with linear clearance (CL). Absorption from the SC compartment was described using first‐order absorption from a depot compartment that received the SC dose via a zero‐order process. The zero‐order process is incorporated by including a parameter, D1, which estimates the duration of the zero‐order process while the first‐order process is modeled by the rate constant, ka. The incorporation of body weight effects on CL and volume of distribution in the central compartment (V2) was also explored, for consistency with other antibody‐based immuno‐oncology treatments. 9 , 10 Time‐varying CL was also assessed multiple ways, as the data allowed, since this phenomenon has been reported with several other immuno‐oncology agents. 11 , 12 Model adequacy from base to final popPK model stages was evaluated (e.g., diagnostic plots, residuals over time, and model predictive performance over time) along with consideration of multiple alternative popPK model structures which aimed to characterize time dependency. The interindividual variability (IIV) of the PK parameters was modeled using a multiplicative exponential model, and residual variability was modeled additively based on log‐transformed observation data. Goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) of each key model to the data was evaluated using the following criteria: change of objective function value (OFV), condition number, visual inspection of various diagnostic plots (observed, individual predicted, and population‐predicted concentrations [DV, IPRED, and PRED, respectively] and conditional and individual weighted residuals [CWRES and IWRES, respectively]), precision of the parameter estimates, and decreases in IIV and residual variability. Outliers were identified as points with weighted residuals with absolute value greater than 6. Outliers were considered influential if estimates of the key PK parameters differed by more than 20% with outlier exclusion.

Potential covariates including baseline demographic factors, laboratory values, and tumor/ disease characteristics were evaluated (Table 2), initially by graphical plotting against individual ETAs (ηs) to identify any relationships. Next, potential covariates identified were tested for significance using stepwise covariate modeling (SCM) with statistical criteria of P = 0.05 for forward inclusion and P = 0.001 for backward elimination. SCM was implemented using Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN) 4.9 followed by a separate NONMEM run, including covariance estimation for final model results.

The performance of the final model was evaluated using a prediction‐corrected visual predictive check (VPC) to evaluate concordance between observed and simulated values and distributions. Simulations (N = 1000 replicates) were performed and concentration–time data was summarized using median, 5th, and 95th percentiles, with stratification as appropriate for dosing route/regimen or final key covariates. To confirm the appropriateness of a flat dosing regimen recommendation, simulations were performed comparing a weight‐based (4 mg/kg assuming a median of 75 kg) and flat dosing regimen (300 mg) using the popPK analysis population's post hoc parameter estimates to derive single and steady‐state Cavg.

To assess the potential impact of presence of ADA on CL of sasanlimab, inter‐occasion variance on CL was added to the base popPK model to allow for a different CL estimate before and after detection of ADA (for participants with ADA positive status). The impact of organ impairment on sasanlimab PK was further assessed in supplemental analyses by categorizing participants by renal function using baseline creatinine clearance (CCL) (calculated using the Cockroft and Gault formula), 13 and by hepatic impairment using the National Cancer Institute Organ Dysfunction Working Group (NCI ODWG) classification. 14 These categorical covariates replaced any related lab‐based covariates during this covariate testing to avoid confounding. An additional sub‐analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential effect of obesity (body mass index [BMI] and body surface area [BSA]) on sasanlimab PK, including only select participants with available information.

Exposure–Response Analyses

Both the ER safety and ER efficacy for investigator‐assessed CR in participants with CIS at randomization analyses were conducted using R 4.2.1 or higher, and ER efficacy for EFS was conducted using NONMEM version 7.5.

ER Safety

The ER analyses for safety endpoints of TEAEs Grade ≥2, TEAEs Grade ≥3, irAEs Any Grade, and irAEs Grade ≥3 (graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [NCI CTCAE version. V.5] severity grade) were conducted based on pooled data from across the three studies. Sasanlimab exposure metrics of Ctrough, Cmax, and Cavg following single dose and at steady state were used to evaluate the ER relationship with the safety endpoints of interest in a univariate model. In addition to sasanlimab exposure, continuous and categorical covariates of interest were evaluated for their impact on safety endpoints in a subsequent multivariate model (Table 2).

ER safety models were developed using generalized binomial logistic regression, starting with a base model with a logit link function to describe the relationship between the probability (in the form of log‐odds) of the event occurring and the most significant sasanlimab exposure metric (Equation 1), selected based on change in deviance, or −2 * log‐likelihood, between models. This was followed by full model development including all potentially clinically relevant covariates on the regression parameters in addition to exposure, followed next by backward elimination of covariates to yield a final model subjected to model evaluation. An α of 0.01 was used for the chi‐squared test for log‐likelihood difference in deviance between models. The Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test was used to test GOF for the models, and receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curve and concordance (C) statistic were used to assess the model predictive performance. 15 , 16 (1) logitP=logP1−P=β0+β1∗Exposure where P denotes the probability of the event of interest, β0 represents the intercept (baseline log‐odds when exposure = 0), and β1 represents the change in log‐odds associated with a one‐unit increase in exposure.

Additional supplemental analyses were conducted to evaluate renal and hepatic impairment by testing the impairment categories and also to evaluate the potential effect of obesity (each as described above for popPK) on safety endpoints. Further, a sub‐analysis was conducted to evaluate potential effects of covariates only relevant to the NMIBC population treated in the B8011006 study (see Table 2).

ER Efficacy

The ER analyses for efficacy were conducted to characterize the relationship between sasanlimab exposure and investigator‐assessed EFS as well as investigator‐assessed CR (for participants with CIS at randomization) using data from Study B8011006 Cohort A (Arm A and Arm B). PopPK‐model‐derived sasanlimab exposure metrics of Ctrough, sd and Cavg, sd (from first cycle) were evaluated for their relationship with both efficacy endpoints; first cycle metrics were specifically used based on recommendations from previous immuno‐oncology ER efficacy experience. 11 , 17 In addition to sasanlimab exposure, potential prognostic factors (covariates) were evaluated for impact on efficacy (Table 2).

First, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to explore the distribution of investigator‐assessed EFS versus time including stratification by exposure quartile or other covariates to explore trends. Then, ER analysis of EFS by investigator was performed using a parametric time‐to‐event (TTE) model where the available data were fit to a survival function (e.g., exponential, Weibull, Gompertz, log‐normal, and log‐logistic), starting with a base model to assess the distribution of EFS versus time using a cumulative hazard distribution function, with model selection based on the likelihood ratio test. Next a univariate evaluation of the influence of sasanlimab exposure was conducted (selecting the most significant exposure metric based on P‐value), and finally a multivariate analysis including influential covariates to yield a final model. Inclusion into the full model by forward selection was based on α = 0.05 and backward elimination for the final model was based on α = 0.001. During this SCM, continuous covariates were evaluated using a linear, exponential or power model, and categorical covariates evaluated using a linear relationship. Graphical assessment of TTE VPCs, a simulation‐based diagnostic, was used to compare KM curves derived from observed data with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) generated from final model‐simulated curves to evaluate the performance of the final model.

The population ER analysis for investigator‐assessed CR, conducted only for participants with CIS at randomization, was performed using a binomial logistic regression modeling approach to estimate the probability of complete response based on serum sasanlimab exposure, including evaluation of covariates (Table 2). The method for this analysis was the same as described above for safety ER.

Results

Population PK Analysis

A total of 5360 PK serum concentrations from 1007 participants were used for the popPK analysis. The most common tumor type included in this analysis was NMIBC (70.1%). Other baseline covariate data from the popPK analysis set are summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Sasanlimab PK were adequately characterized by a two‐compartment model with sequential zero‐/first‐order absorption and allometric scaling of baseline body weight on CL and V2. The absorption model describes an absorption process for SC administration where sasanlimab enters the absorption compartment through a zero‐order process and then is absorbed into V2 through a first‐order process. The addition of allometric scaling with estimated exponents on V2 and CL best described the data out of all permutations of allometry assessed. Based on the popPK model, mean elimination half‐life (t1/2) was estimated to be ∼29 days, and geometric mean steady‐state volume of distribution (VSS) was estimated to be 6.34 L. There was no evidence of time‐varying CL observed throughout the model development process. As anticipated based on the metabolic pathway of sasanlimab and the localized administration of BCG, there was no evidence suggesting a drug–drug interaction in the combination setting. Twelve data points were identified as potential outliers based on the criteria described above and were removed from the model, resulting in minimal impact on key parameter estimates. Of the evaluated covariates, baseline albumin (BALB), baseline alkaline phosphatase (BALK), baseline serum creatinine (BSCR), and tumor type were found to be significant and were retained as covariates on CL in the final model. The final popPK model parameters for sasanlimab are shown in Table 3, and equations for the parameterization of CL and V2 from the final model are provided in Equations (S1) and (S2).

Justification for Flat Dose

For evaluation of flat dosing, Figure 3 shows predicted Cavg after a single dose as well as at steady state for both weight‐based (4 mg/kg) and flat dosing; the substantial overlap across body weight supports flat dosing for sasanlimab. The largest discrepancy of Cavg predictions between the weight‐based and flat dosing approaches occurs in the smallest body weight grouping (0–25th percentile) where the flat dose Cavg is higher than the weight‐based dose.

Exposure Response Analyses

ER Safety

The population included in the pooled ER safety analysis was the same population as the popPK analysis. Across all three studies, 85% of the participants had TEAE Grade ≥2, 43% had TEAE Grade ≥3, 55% had irAE Any Grade, and 15% had irAE Grade ≥3. Given the sufficient number of reported events for each, all four safety endpoints were considered appropriate for ER modeling. Summaries of the demographics and other baseline covariate characteristics of the ER safety analysis population are presented in Tables S1 and S2.

For all four safety endpoints evaluated, there was no significant association found between sasanlimab exposure and the event (based on significance level of 0.01). The flat exposure–safety relationship indicates a similar probability of experiencing these events with the 300 mg SC Q4W dose versus lower doses, including those evaluated in Study B8011001. Table 4 lists the final ER safety model parameter estimates. Figure S2 illustrates the flat ER relationships, demonstrating no increase in event probability with higher exposure. There was no impact of ADA, body size (BMI and BSA), or organ function (based on renal and hepatic impairment categories) on the incidence of any of the safety endpoints evaluated.

ER Efficacy

For the ER efficacy analyses, summaries of the demographics and other baseline covariate characteristics of analysis population (participants from CREST trial Cohort A) are presented in Tables S1 and S2. In both analyses, the sasanlimab exposure metrics Cavg, sd and Ctrough, sd were evaluated; the exposure summaries can be found in Table S3.

A thorough evaluation of an ER relationship for investigator‐assessed EFS is limited as there were 158 events in 698 total participants (median EFS not reached) (Figure 4 and Figure S3). Of the evaluated hazard distributions, the log‐normal distribution best fit the observed data. Sasanlimab exposure was not found to be significant and therefore was not included in the final model, while three other covariates were included: treatment arm (receipt of BCG induction + maintenance being beneficial to EFS), age (older age being detrimental), and body weight (higher weight being detrimental). Final model parameters for the EFS analysis are presented in Table S4. Upon evaluation of all covariates according to quartiles of sasanlimab exposure (Cavg, sd), there were no notable trends suggesting an imbalance in covariates across the exposure range that would be expected to confound the ER results or mask an underlying meaningful ER relationship. Additionally, sasanlimab exposures did not differ between the treatment arms. The VPC (Figure S4) shows that the final log‐normal parametric TTE model appropriately characterizes the observed EFS from participants treated with sasanlimab in Cohort A.

Similarly, a thorough evaluation of an exposure–response relationship for investigator‐assessed CR in participants with CIS at randomization is also limited considering the relatively low number of non‐responders. Out of 179 total participants the rate of complete response was nearly 90%. Therefore, there is no demonstration of a clear ER relationship for this efficacy endpoint.

Discussion

This collection of population PK, exposure–safety and exposure–efficacy analyses were conducted to support the use of sasanlimab at the recommended dose of 300 mg SC Q4W in the intended population of BCG‐naïve, high‐risk NMIBC.

The characterization of sasanlimab via popPK analyses enabled investigation into the potential impact of a variety of factors on the drug's disposition. Unlike other mAbs in this class, there was no evidence of time‐varying CL, with stable kinetics over time in the clinical data pooled across cohorts. 9 , 10 This approach also was used to support the selection of the flat dose of 300 mg SC compared to weight‐based dosing, based on PK simulations of flat and weight‐based dosing across various body weight quartiles. Despite some discrepancy in sasanlimab exposure predictions between the weight‐based and flat dosing approaches in the lowest weight quartile, other mAbs with wide therapeutic windows have observed this trend and have similarly demonstrated acceptability for either weight‐based or flat dosing. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 The popPK simulations coupled with a lack of apparent relationship between sasanlimab exposure and the safety and efficacy outcomes modeled here together support the flat dosing approach. Similarly, all other covariates that were evaluated in the popPK analysis do not warrant any dose adjustment due to lack of meaningful impact on the PK, which is further informed by the apparent flat exposure–response relationships.

For each of the ER safety analyses conducted for sasanlimab, none demonstrated a significant relationship with increasing sasanlimab exposure, including TEAEs Grade ≥2, TEAEs Grade ≥3, irAEs Any Grade, and irAEs Grade ≥3. The flat exposure–safety relationship evident when evaluating pooled data including dose escalation cohorts (0.5–10 mg/kg IV Q3W) indicates a similar probability of experiencing events with the 300 mg SC Q4W dose versus any higher or lower doses or alternative regimens evaluated across studies; decreasing the dose would not meaningfully mitigate AEs, supporting use of the full treatment dose. 8 In addition, the absence of apparent ER relationships suggests sasanlimab does not have a narrow therapeutic index, further supporting the use of a flat dosing regimen. Given the lack of a relationship with exposure, investigation into other intrinsic or extrinsic covariates was considered secondary, and any apparent trends for those retained in final models have limited interpretability in the context of ER.

Lastly, the trends observed for sasanlimab displaying a lack of association between exposure and safety or efficacy endpoints are aligned with reports for other checkpoint inhibitors, including those with indicated flat dosing regimens. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 In particular, for ER efficacy analyses which include just a single dosing regimen, flat or shallow ER relationships have been reported by other anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐PDL1 agents. 23 , 24

A robust evaluation in the BCG‐naïve, high‐risk NMIBC population of the exposure–response relationship for either of the selected efficacy endpoints of investigator‐assessed EFS and CR (in participants with CIS at randomization) was limited due to the maturity and/or nature of the observed events at data cutoff. Multiple factors limit the utility of an ER model including the nearly 90% rate of CR, the suggested negative ER relationship, and model evaluation indices (not shown) corresponding to an inability to distinguish outcome based on exposure level. For investigator‐assessed EFS, a robust conclusion regarding the ER relationship for this endpoint is also limited considering the number of events, in which the median EFS was not yet reached at the time of analysis. Still, based on the data available, these ER analyses showed no associations between sasanlimab exposure and the efficacy endpoints of interest. Given high rates of CR in 179 participants with CIS at baseline and current estimates of investigator‐assessed EFS in 698 participants, both ER analyses of efficacy support the 300 mg SC Q4W dosing regimen as yielding exposures in the therapeutic range for this indication of BCG‐naïve, high‐risk NMIBC. Further, it is important to note that despite the limitations described, there was no evidence in either analysis that a higher sasanlimab dose would increase efficacy, which provides additional support for the use of a flat dose of sasanlimab in this indication.

While the low (<10%) ADA incidence for sasanlimab across studies limits robust formal evaluation of the impact of immunogenicity on the PK, safety, and efficacy, exploratory modeling was used to investigate signals on the key outcomes within each pharmacometric analysis. Comparisons across immunogenicity groups (ADA positive vs ADA negative) and across visits for participants with ADA positivity were leveraged for signal finding in the popPK analysis, yielding no clear trends suggesting impact of immunogenicity on the PK behavior. Additional exploration in the ER analyses concluded that no clear association was observed between the emergence of ADA to sasanlimab and the PK, safety, or efficacy of the drug.

Lastly, sasanlimab's SC administration and flat recommended dose offer potential benefits for both patients and healthcare providers. SC dosing is faster and less invasive compared to IV infusions, requiring shorter clinic visits once per month and enabling greater flexibility in scheduling (i.e., potential administration outside of infusion centers). This may benefit the healthcare setting by reducing the drug administration time for patients, infusion‐related resource use, and clinic staff workload. From a flat dosing perspective, the need for weight‐based dose calculations is eliminated, reducing preparation complexity while minimizing the risk of dosing errors. Together, these features enhance sasanlimab's overall treatment experience without compromising any of the therapeutic effects. Based on the features of the drug administration described above, along with the overall results of sasanlimab's PK behavior and the conclusions presented here regarding the modeled safety and efficacy relationships (limited by the event data available), administration of this novel checkpoint inhibitor via the SC route approximately once monthly with a flat dose of 300 mg is supported as the recommended dose for sasanlimab.

Conclusion

Here we present the characterization of the PK, ER safety, and ER efficacy of sasanlimab to support the clinical dosing regimen of 300 mg SC Q4W for participants with BCG‐naïve, high‐risk NMIBC. Based on the totality of data for this mAb, the flat dosing approach is supported, no dose adjustments are needed based on patient factors, and there is no evidence of impact of immunogenicity on clinical outcomes.
