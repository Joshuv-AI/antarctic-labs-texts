A legacy of genetic entanglement with wolves shapes modern dogs
Authors: Audrey T. Lin, Regina A. Fairbanks, Jose Barba-Montoya, Hsiao-Lei Liu, Logan Kistler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAlthough wolves and dogs can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, hybridization between the two is far more rare than domestic and wild populations of other species. We combined highly sensitive local ancestry inference and phylogenomic analyses of genomes representing 2,693 ancient and modern dogs and wolves. Almost two-thirds of breed dogs have wolf ancestry within their nuclear genome from admixture that occurred nearly a thousand generations ago, and all analyzed free-ranging dog genomes carry some ancient wolf ancestry. Wolf ancestry correlates with traits like size, function, and personality characteristics. Altogether, the majority of dogs today have low, but detectable levels of postdomestication wolf ancestry that has shaped their evolution and conferred unique advantages to their survival in diverse human environments.

Hybridization between domestic and wild taxa has been observed across a wide variety of plants and animals (1–4). For instance, Near Eastern ancestry in domestic pigs was almost entirely erased through gene flow with wild European boar (5), and all maize worldwide carries adaptive, postdomestication gene flow from wild highland teosinte (4). Dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) and wolves (Canis lupus) have long been considered the notable exception, despite inhabiting the same geographic ranges, and their ability to produce fertile offspring. In recent years, analysis of dozens of ancient dog and wolf genomes has suggested that gene flow between the two is rare (6, 7).

Gene flow has been observed from village dogs into geographically proximate modern wolf populations in Europe, the Near East, and East Asia (8). Previous work has also revealed gene flow from free-roaming dogs into wolves, especially where the density of wolves is low such as the recently extinct populations in Sicily (9) and Japan (10). Introgression of dog alleles into North American gray wolf populations has also been observed, including a dog-derived canine beta-defensin KB allele (CBD103) linked to black coat color and epithelial immunity (11). This 3 bp deletion in the coding region of CBD103 was introduced to North American gray wolves from domestic dogs between 1,000 to 10,000 y ago, where it then underwent a selective sweep (12–14).

Wolf ancestry in dogs, however, has been mostly undetectable at the genome-wide level. In a previous study, wolf ancestry was not detected in the nuclear genomes of 27 ancient dogs spanning ~11,000 y across the Northern Hemisphere using f4 statistics (8). Czechoslovakian and Saarloos wolfdogs, 20th century breeds created through deliberate hybridization, are notable exceptions, and they possess ~11 to 12% and 18 to 33% wolf ancestry, respectively (15). There are also some examples of wolf alleles that have been shown to confer a selective advantage in dogs. For instance, there is an excess of highland gray wolf ancestry at the EPAS1 locus in highland domestic dogs in the Tibetan plateau functioning in high-altitude hypoxia response (16, 17). In general, however, most genomic analyses point to a long history of reproductive isolation among dogs with little genetic input from wolves.

In contrast, mitochondrial (18, 19) and Y-chromosome (20, 21) phylogenies have consistently shown paraphyletic and intermingled dogs and wolves. This autosomal–uniparental incongruence suggests a broader history of gene flow that is lost to genetic drift and selection in the nuclear genome, but becomes maintained in nonrecombining uniparental markers. Phylogenomic strategies can provide insights into admixture in local genetic contexts, and local ancestry inference (22) is a powerful strategy for fine-scale admixture detection in the presence of appropriate reference data (22). The availability of thousands of modern and ancient dog and wolf genomes combined with these methods offers the ability to seek out ever smaller proportions of introgression with higher confidence and greater resolution.

Here, we used these methods to explore historical dog–wolf gene flow using a large published set of wolves, breed dogs, village dogs, and other canids spanning the Late Pleistocene to the present (Fig. 1A). In a set of 1,066 modern and ancient canids, we estimated nuclear maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenies using a concatenated nuclear alignment, 1,582 individual ML gene trees after screening the complete gene set for phylogenetic informativeness, and constructed a species tree to establish the global nuclear phylogenetic pattern. We used gene concordance factors (gCF) (23) and site concordance factors (sCF) (24) to test for gene-level and alignment site-level support of dog monophyly, respectively. We computed complete mitochondrial genome and y-chromosome ML trees to contrast uniparental and global genomic phylogenetic structure. Using 1,929 phased and imputed dogs and wolves from the Dog10k consortium (25), we then used local ancestry inference to probe admixture throughout dog and wolf genomes at high resolution. We compared the timing of dog-to-wolf and wolf-to-dog admixture on the basis of introgressed haploblock lengths. Additionally, we tested for functional correlates to wolf ancestry using gene ontology (GO), phylogenetics, and comparisons with breed standards.

Results

Uniparental Structure and Nuclear Gene Trees Contradict Dog Monophyly.

Consistent with previous analyses (8, 25, 26), dogs and wolves segregate strongly in genome-wide principal components analysis (Fig. 1B). The first principal component (PC1) explains 32.2% of the variance, and separates all modern and ancient dogs from all modern and ancient wolves. Saarloos and Czechoslovakian Wolfdogs fall along the PC1 axis between the dog and wolf clusters, consistent with their recent hybrid ancestry (27).

To test whether this monophyly extended to individual genes, we constructed individual ML trees from 1,582 of the most phylogenetically informative genes (SI Appendix) and used these gene trees to estimate a nuclear phylogeny with ASTRAL-III [Fig. 1C; (28)]. The nuclear tree shows dogs as a well-supported monophyletic group (ASTRAL posterior probability = 0.99). We then computed sCF (29), a metric that quantifies the fraction of decisive quartets at alignment sites supporting a given bipartition. Under this metric, a random topology with no support is expected to yield sCF = 33.33%. In the dog clade of our nuclear tree, on the other hand, sCF = 49.98% marks substantial genomic support for the clustering of all dogs.

We then considered gCF, which provide a metric of support for bipartitions among individual gene trees (23, 29). gCF thus reflects biological variability in phylogenetic congruence driven by incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) or hybridization. On this basis, 0 of the 1,582 gene trees contain a well-supported bipartition supporting dogs as a monophyletic cluster (gCF = 0), while 391 contain a well-supported competing bipartition (≥70% bootstrap support) with at least one wolf that interrupts the dog cluster. In total, these results reveal a global genomic signal supporting dogs as monophyletic, but with persistent evidence of wolf–dog gene flow in the form of incongruent individual gene trees.

Uniparental markers strongly differ from the nuclear tree and also suggest a history of wolf–dog gene flow. Unlike the nuclear tree, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and y-chromosome trees contain numerous well-supported mixed dog and wolf clades (Fig. 2). For dogs, we recapitulated previously described haplogroups A, B, C, D, and X [Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1; (18, 30)]. Four out of the five mitochondrial clades (A, B, D, and X) are more closely related to wolf populations than other dogs (Fig. 2B). This paraphyly could have resulted from either multiple haplogroups segregating in the wolf population that eventually gave rise to dogs, or postdomestication hybridization after which wolf mitochondrial haplogroups became fixed in dog populations following pulses of admixture.

There is also geographic and temporal structuring within dog and wolf mitochondrial genomes. A is the most common haplogroup represented in contemporary dog populations (19, 31, 32), and modern dogs are represented in many of the mitochondrial haplogroups, including B, C, and D (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Basal to the A-haplogroup dogs are ancient wolves from Siberia, spanning 3.6 kBP to 18.4 kBP, and one modern wolf from the Heilongjiang Province of northeast China. Haplogroup B, currently consisting of modern dogs only, forms a polytomy with two clades of Eurasian/Central Asian wolves. Today, C haplogroup dogs are distributed among a wide range of modern dogs but may have had its origins in pre-Neolithic Europe (33). D forms a sister clade to two ~10 kBP wolves from France and is posited to have entered Europe through the expansion of Near Eastern farmers during the Neolithic (33). At the root of the mtDNA tree is the Himalayan wolf, which has been previously shown to be a divergent group basal to Holoarctic gray wolves (34). However, other Himalayan wolf mitogenomes are dispersed throughout the tree, possibly due to modern gene flow from dogs as a result of human-mediated encroachment on their dwindling populations (35–37).

Dog–wolf paraphyly has also previously been observed in Y-chromosome studies (20, 21). In our y-chromosome phylogeny, we recapitulated eight known dog/wolf haplogroups, identifying HG1-3 and HG27 as dog-only, HG6 and HG0 as wolf-only, and the others (HG8, HG9, HG23, and the newly observed haplogroup, termed thereafter HGY) that contain both dogs and wolves [Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2; (20, 21)]. Y-haplogroups exhibit some geographic structuring, where most of the HG1-3 dogs come from Asia and Central Asia, with the exception of the Basenji, a basal southern African breed (38). At the root of the tree is a modern wolf from the Great Lakes Region of North America, whose Y-chromosome diverged from the rest of the wolves and dogs between 186,000 to 257,000 y ago [Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2; (20)].

The mitochondrial and Y-chromosome trees are also incongruent with each other. Among pairs of dogs sharing a mitogenomic haplogroup, only 48.17% also share a Y-chromosome haplogroup. In comparison, random allocation of haplogroups in 10,000 permutations leads to 46.17 to 49.55% co-occurrence (mean = 47.86%, SD = 1.15%). Similarly, mitochondrial nucleotide diversity between all pairs of dogs correlates only trivially with shared nuclear genetic drift measured by outgroup-f3 statistics (r2 = 0.00045; P < 2.2 × 10−16 due to large sample size). In the nuclear genome, breeds tend to form strong reciprocal clusters based on shared drift but breeds routinely carry several different mtDNA and Y-chromosome haplogroups in our analysis. In total, nuclear autosomal, mitogenome, and Y-chromosome genomic clustering are no more congruent with each other than predicted by chance. The observed phylogenetic patterns are consistent with a history of dog–wolf gene flow leading to the inference of highly incongruent unlinked loci, accurately reflecting the independent legacies of uniparental and nuclear inheritance in the presence of hybridization. This explanation could be consistent with previous reports of sporadic gene flow (39), which may have been more common than presently understood. We also cannot rule out, however, a role for ILS that contributed to the observed patterns, nor can we segregate the effects of hybridization and ILS using the present analyses.

Widespread Evidence for Ancient Wolf-to-Dog Gene Flow.

In light of the phylogenetic discordance, we used local ancestry inference (22, 40) to determine whether evidence for dog–wolf gene flow extended to the nuclear genome among 1,929 phased and imputed dog (n = 1,872) and wolf (n = 57) genomes (SI Appendix). This method, developed for human genomic analyses (40), uses phased SNP datasets to estimate local chromosomal ancestry throughout the genome of a test individual based on two reference populations—dogs and wolves in our case (SI Appendix). On this basis, we found that 264 breeds (79.8% of the total breeds analyzed) comprising 1,016 individuals (64.1% of breed dogs) contain at least 250 kbp of introgressed wolf ancestry, equating to ~0.005% of the genome including both chromosome copies (mean = 6.44 Mbp, SD = 16.3 Mbp). In addition, 100% of analyzed village dogs (n = 280) from global populations carry detectable wolf ancestry ranging from 862 kbp to 67.3 Mbp.

Altogether, the majority of dogs carry low but detectable fractions of genomic DNA from wolf-to-dog gene flow. In comparison, 56% of modern wolves (32 out of 57) show dog-to-wolf gene flow ranging from 14.9 Mbp to 338 Mbp—up to ~7.6% of the genome in the most admixed wolves from Iran (Dataset S5). Saarloos and Czechoslovakian Wolfdogs (n = 4 each) range from 23 to 40% wolf ancestry, consistent with their recent mixed ancestry (27). In total, 96.64% of the autosomal reference genome is covered by wolf ancestry in at least one breed dog or village dog, not including the hybrid wolfdogs. This genome-wide ubiquity of wolf ancestry blocks in dog genomes is likely sufficient to cause the observed lack of gene tree support for dog monophyly without invoking ILS (gCF = 0; Fig. 1C).

To ensure that local ancestry results robustly reflect admixture rather than ILS or false positive error, we carried out a set of simulations to test FLARE’s performance (SI Appendix). We found no evidence of false positive wolf-to-dog admixture under a range of relevant models but found evidence for some false positive dog-to-wolf admixture based on the asymmetry in dog and wolf sample sizes. We paired these simulations with a likelihood ratio method to assess whether inferred ancestry blocks were more likely derived from dogs or wolves based on present-day allele frequencies (SI Appendix). Under this framework, we found that admixture erroneously inferred from simulations without gene flow was easily falsified at the genome-wide level. In contrast, the vast majority cross-group admixture inferred in the main analysis is supported (SI Appendix), suggesting that our application of FLARE reveals a robust admixture signal rather than false positive results.

The length distribution of introgressed haploblocks corresponds to the timing of admixture since recombination shortens introgressed regions over time (e.g., ref. 41). In this context, wolf ancestry blocks distributed throughout the genome of dogs have an average total length of 145.9 kbp (Fig. 3A). Dog ancestry tracts in wolves are ~13× longer at 1.96 Mbp, suggesting more ancient wolf-to-dog gene flow and more recent dog-to-wolf gene flow (Fig. 3 B and C). The wolf ancestry blocks in most dogs are too short for an estimation of admixture timing using population genetic methods such as TRACTS (41) or ALDER (42). However, a rough estimation of timing can be made by fitting negative exponential functions to block length distributions for each dog and deriving a value for the rate parameter (λ) that represents the degree of fragment decay (SI Appendix). Assuming 1 Mbp ≈ 1 cM, an absence of selection on introgressed regions, and a single time point of admixture per genome, this approach yields ~873 generations since average wolf-to-dog gene flow, but only 72 generations since average dog-to-wolf gene flow. Wolfdogs show the longest wolf tracts indicating an average 26 generations since wolf admixture (range 14 to 39 generations; Fig. 3 B and C), directly in line with documented breed histories of the Saarloos wolfdog (formed in the 1930s) and the Czechoslovakian wolfdog (formed in the 1950s) (43, 44). Village dogs have 38% more wolf ancestry than breeds on average (t test P = 2.4 × 10−4; wilcoxon P < 2.2 × 10−16), but with 23% shorter fragments suggesting on average more ancient origins, compared to breed dogs (t test P = 7.5 × 10−9; wilcoxon P = 1.8 × 10−13; Fig. 3C).

Three individual dogs have much longer, more recent wolf tracts more consistent with wolfdogs than other dogs (Fig. 3 B and C)—two Great Anglo-French Tricolor Hounds (5.7% and 4.7% wolf ancestry; ~24 and ~17 generations since admixture) and one Shiloh Shepherd (2.7% total wolf genomic ancestry; ~31 generations since admixture). The Shiloh Shepherd originated from breeding efforts to create healthier, more family-friendly shepherd dogs in the United States during the latter half of the 20th century (45). Based on this one representative of the breed, these efforts likely included wolfdogs or other recent dog–wolf hybrids. The Dog10k Shiloh Shepherd previously analyzed shares 78% of F2 sites (alleles occurring in only two individuals) with wolves (25).

The recent and extensive wolf ancestry in Great Anglo-French Tricolor Hounds is unexpected. This breed shows the highest variance in wolf ancestry among all breeds analyzed (0.03 to 5.47%), with lower but also variable values in the related Great Anglo-French White and Orange Hound (0.004 to 1.8%). Great Anglo-French Tricolor Hounds (also known as Grand Anglo-Français Tricolore) are one of three forms of the large Anglo-French hounds that were created by crossing French hounds such as the Poitevin with English tricolor foxhounds and bred as pack dogs to hunt large prey such as red deer, roe deer, and wild boar. The Great Anglo-French Tricolour Hound is considered the most common hound in modern France (46), and it is unclear where the high wolf ancestry in these individuals may have originated.

Besides these individuals, the Tamaskan (n = 1 analyzed) is the only breed with >2% wolf ancestry (164 Mbp, 3.68%). Tamaskans are a “wolfalike” breed originating in the UK in the 1980s from selecting Arctic dogs (Huskies and Malamutes) and other breeds with the specific goal of producing a wolf-like appearance (47). The breeding program apparently had the effect of increasing wolf ancestry along with wolfish phenotypic traits. However, unlike the wolfdogs, Shiloh Shepherd, and Great Anglo-French hounds, wolf tract lengths in the Tamaskan are consistent with other breeds, suggesting ~1,089 generations since admixture. This pattern suggests that Tamaskan breeding did not significantly integrate wolves or wolfdogs directly, but instead may have positively selected the genome-wide wolf-borne tracts from the constituent breeds. For example, the three Alaskan Malamutes included in the analysis carry 0.63 to 0.92% wolf ancestry. However, the majority of these wolf tracts (72.7%) are only observed on one chromosome among the three dogs, so they combine to cover 3.35% of the haploid reference genome. A breeding program targeting wolf-like phenotypic traits may have enriched the individual wolf components of many dogs and resulted in a higher overall wolf fraction than any individual contributor.

Wolf Ancestry Correlates with Breed Traits.

Though wolf ancestry is found in a wide range of dog breeds across phenotypes, personality traits, and roles in human life (SI Appendix), we observed several specific correlations between wolf ancestry and the characteristics of breeds. First, breed size and average wolf ancestry are weakly but significantly correlated (average male weight vs. fraction of wolf ancestry r2 = 0.031, P = 0.0017). A piecewise approach reveals that higher wolf ancestry concentrated within the larger dogs is driving this signal. When the breeds are separated into empirical thirds based on size, the lower two-thirds of dogs have similar wolf ancestry (P = 0.27), but the top one-third has significantly more (Fig. 4D, P = 0.001 and P = 0.006 for the lower and middle thirds compared with the top third). Thus, the presence of wolf ancestry crosscuts all sizes of dogs including ~0.2% of the chihuahua genome, but the larger breeds tend to have a larger overall proportion of wolf ancestry.

Second, breeds are classified into functional categories according to kennel clubs and other sources (SI Appendix) (e.g., gundogs, sighthounds, guardians, etc.). Breeds within all these categories carry detectable wolf ancestry (Fig. 4C). Some groups, however, including the Arctic sled dogs, pariah breeds, and hunting dogs have the greatest wolf ancestry, while on average, terriers, gundogs, and scent hounds have the least. The guardian breeds have low median wolf ancestry (0.023%), but a strong positive skew driven by several individual breeds with high wolf ancestry (Fig. 4C). These mostly include large livestock-protecting landrace breeds from Türkiye and elsewhere in west and central Asia—specifically the Sarabi, Alabai (Central Asian Herder Dog), Turkish Mastiff, Anatolian Shepherd, Sarplaninac, Akbash, Kars Shepherd, Caucasian Ovcharka, and the Mongolian Bankhar Dog. These landrace breeds contain 0.553 to 1.19% wolf ancestry. The close association with wolves as flock guardians has led to documented gene flow in shepherd dogs in the Caucasus (39). Other large guardians such as the Neapolitan Mastiff, Bullmastiff, and the St. Bernard, in contrast, carry no detectable wolf ancestry, underscoring the historical specificity of dog evolution and trait selection.

Third, kennel club breed standards are used by breeders and enthusiast groups to describe the characteristic personality traits of many breeds and can be useful as a high-level proxy for breed disposition and behavior. We compiled and standardized these descriptors for the breeds analyzed (Dataset S2) and compared their occurrence between dogs with high wolf ancestry (>5 Mbp) and low wolf ancestry (<500 kbp). A caveat to this analysis is that breed standard behavioral terms provided by dog breeders and kennel clubs are qualitative descriptors of behavioral trends. Despite the potential biases in the application of these terms, some patterns emerge.

The descriptor most differentially associated with low wolf-content dogs was friendly, while the term most skewed toward high wolf-content dogs was “suspicious of strangers” (Fig. 4E). Also highly differentially associated with low wolf-content were “eager to please,” “easy to train,” “courageous,” “lively,” and “affectionate.” In contrast, high wolf-content dogs are more often described as “independent,” “dignified,” “alert,” “loyal,” “reserved,” and territorial. Many descriptors, including “intelligent,” “obedient,” “good with children,” “dedicated,” “calm,” and “cheerful” occurred with similar frequency in low wolf and high wolf-content dogs, indicating that these terms either do not segregate according to wolf ancestry, or that they fail to capture heritable breed behaviors.

The present data cannot establish whether i) wolf genes are responsible for the associated phenotypes, ii) genomic wolf ancestry is better tolerated when these characteristics are sought by humans, or iii) correlations derive from ancestry overlapping breeding priorities rather than functional links. Previous behavioral research revealed that breed standards are poor descriptors of individual dogs’ behaviors and have limited value in discriminating between breeds (48). However, some key traits such as human sociability (cf. friendly and suspicious of strangers) were previously shown to be heritable and breed-linked (48). The present analysis does not consider individual dog behaviors or the concordance of breed standards and behavior. However, it reflects strong associations at an aggregated level between breeds’ level of wolf ancestry and some of the terms commonly used to describe those breeds and suggests a path for future dog behavioral research.

Finally, Tibetan Mastiffs have been shown to carry Tibetan Wolf-like Endothelial PAS domain-containing protein 1 (EPAS1) alleles conferring tolerance to the hypoxic high altitude of the Tibetan Plateau and Himalayas (16, 17, 49). We interrogated this previously documented instance of gene flow in a large dataset to test for broader patterning of EPAS1 variants. We used a phylogenetic approach by extracting both individual haplotypes from the same 1,929 phased genomes used for local ancestry analysis, plus 10 Tibetan Mastiffs, nine Tibetan Wolves, 10 Diqing Indigenous dogs, and village dogs from Gansu (n = 9), Hubei (n = 1), Xi’an (n = 3), Anqing Anhui (n = 2), Ya’an Szechwan (n = 4), and Kathmandu (n = 7) (Fig. 4B and Dataset S3).

We confirmed that all 10 Tibetan Mastiffs are homozygous for an EPAS1 variant also found among Tibetan wolves. In addition, Diqing Indigenous dogs native to the southeastern edge of the Tibetan plateau are all either homozygous or heterozygous. Most village dogs in the Tibetan Plateau and surrounding highlands carry at least one copy. A small fraction of lowland Chinese village dogs (n = 10 out of 120; Fig. 4B) are heterozygous, but homozygotes are only found narrowly in high-elevation individuals from this region, hinting at possible deleterious effects and purifying selection in lowland populations.

Among highland wolves, four from Tajikistan are homozygous for the key variant, but four of nine Tibetan Wolves analyzed carry no copies—a surprisingly high proportion of individuals lacking a key adaptive trait to the high-altitude environment. However, adaptation to altitude likely has other as-yet-described, polygenic solutions, and highland wolves have had more time than more recently-introduced highland dogs to adapt. As such, wolf introgression of an adaptive EPAS1 allele may be an efficient solution to an environmental challenge for dogs, but only one of many standing adaptive mechanisms for long-habituated wolves.

Village Dogs’ Olfactory Reception Is Augmented by Wolf Alleles.

Low levels of wolf ancestry are ubiquitous among village dogs (Fig. 4C, SI Appendix, Fig. S3, and Dataset S8). To investigate the presence of functional traits that may be enriched by wolf alleles, we summarized the coverage of wolf ancestry in village dogs across the dog reference genome in 10 kbp windows. We carried out a GO analysis over regions in the top 1% of wolf ancestry among village dogs—sites where ≥10 individual village dogs carry wolf ancestry.

This analysis yielded a single significant Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) term—“Olfactory transduction” with a strongly significant P-value (P = 1.7 × 10−10). This term comprises 57 genes in two major regions on chromosome 18 and 35 intersecting regions of enriched wolf ancestry. Village dogs throughout Eurasia and Africa possess wolf ancestry over various combinations of these genes (Fig. 4A). In summary, GO analysis yielded a single, strong KEGG result showing that wolf ancestry overlaps olfactory receptors in village dogs. This result suggests that wolf-to-dog introgression has enhanced olfactory acuity in free-living village dogs.

Discussion

Humans have claimed to intentionally cross-breed wolves and dogs for centuries, as reported by Aristotle (ca. 2400 BP) and Pliny (ca. 1900 BP) (50). Inuit peoples in the Arctic and pre-Hispanic Indigenous peoples in modern-day Mexico also reportedly utilized hybrids to strengthen the lineages of working dogs (51). Ancient genomes from the Roman era, however, show no evidence of wolf ancestry (8, 52), nor has wolf ancestry been detected in ancient dogs in the Arctic (30) or the pre-colonial Americas (19). In modern times, wolf–dog hybrids and wolfalike breeds have been purposefully bred and are even recognized by some Kennel Clubs as distinct breeds (such as the Czechoslovakian Wolfdog, the Saarloos Wolfdog, the Kunming dog, the Lupo Italiano, and the Tamaskan) (43, 44). Tamaskans, a fairly new wolfalike breed, are allowed up to 30% wolf ancestry in breeding parents (47), but the single Tamaskan analyzed here includes only 3.68% wolf ancestry.

In the wild, dog–wolf hybridization has been documented on rare occasions. In Iberia, the dog–wolf hybridization in wolf populations is mostly mediated by male dog–female wolf crosses (53). In modern contexts, dog-to-wolf admixture from free-roaming feral dog packs is considered a threat to conservation efforts of endangered wolves, including Himalayan wolves (54) and wolves in the Iberian peninsula, Italy, and Bulgaria (53, 55–57). In the Iberian Peninsula, hybridization frequencies were found to be 4 to 5% (53). In Southern Europe, a continuum of hybrids form mixed packs, suggesting an underestimation of the extent of dog–wolf hybridization in highly humanized wolf habitats (53).

It has been suggested that the male progenitor dogs of the dog–wolf hybrids are hunting dogs or livestock guardian dogs that live as human companions but roam freely in wolf habitats (53, 58). The wolf females would be a product of unstable pack dynamics and likely are solitary individuals from a wolf pack disrupted by human-mediated disturbance such as hunting, habitat encroachment, or forest fires. They may also be dispersers—for example, a wolf permanently leaving their natal pack territory to reproduce—at the edge of a marginal or low-density wolf population (53). In contrast to wolves who breed seasonally just once per year (59), most female dogs can go into heat more than once per year (60). The gestation period is approximately two months—similar to wolves (59).

The strikingly different social organization between dogs and wolves is well known: Wolves form nuclear family units with monogamous paired adults, juveniles, and puppies, and the success rates of hunting and territory defense is often dependent on the cohesion of the pack (61, 62). Free-ranging dogs, on the other hand, occupy human environments and subsist on a diet consisting primarily of human food waste, with much more flexible pack organization (62). Village dogs are typically free-ranging and do not have a dedicated owner but may rely on humans for feeding (63, 64). The global population of free-ranging dogs is estimated to range upward of 687 million (65) to 987 million (66), with particularly high concentrations in urban areas (67). Village dogs living in urban environments have high rates of mortality (68). Where alloparental care within the wolf pack is common, only the mother cares for her offspring among free-ranging dogs (69). Male free-ranging dogs are the only canids who do not provide for their mate or offspring (70). Through domestication, humans have taken over alloparental care to dogs, and the removal of humans is reflected in reports of feral dog pup mortality rates: no greater than 37% survival after the first five months, and a 16% survival rate for feral dogs in urban environments (70–72).

There is additional evidence suggesting that free-breeding village dogs undergo natural and sexual selection more similar to wild canids than to breed dogs and that selective pressures act upon traits associated with development, behavior, metabolism, and nervous system function (73). Wolves depend greatly on their sense of smell to navigate their surroundings, find prey, mark their territories, and to recognize one another (74–76). In dogs, physiological changes such as reduced cribriform plate morphology and changes to the olfactory bulb have been associated with reduced olfactory capacity relative to wolves (77–79). The increased dependency of humans on food and shelter may have relaxed selective pressure during domestication on the olfactory capacity necessary for wolves’ long-distance tracking and hunting (77). The enrichment of wolf introgression overlapping olfactory receptors may have then returned adaptive advantages to village dogs in these environments where mortality is high, in the absence of direct human care (64, 65).

In contrast to ancient average wolf-to-dog gene flow, we estimate that there are only 72 generations on average since the observed dog-to-wolf gene flow found in modern wolf genomes (range 36 to 163 generations, interquartile range or IQR 48 to 82). With an average generation time of 3 y (80), the average admixture would therefore have taken place roughly during the early 19th century. Some possible reasons for these relatively recent admixture episodes could be the human encroachment on wolf territories in concert with growing dog population sizes as urbanization became more widespread globally.

The evolution of dog domestication [~23 kya (81)] substantially predates the timeframe of introgression observed here on the basis of introgressed block sizes, and more ancient gene flow would have been overprinted by the later introgression analyzed here. It is therefore impossible to discuss the role of introgression during the selective landscape of domestication based on the present analysis. Additionally, a third of breed dogs contain no detectable tracts of wolf ancestry, indicating that the present analysis is revealing gene flow into domesticated dogs rather than genomic remnants of the wolf gene pool of domestication. More ancient dog–wolf gene flow likely shaped the evolution of domestication in dogs, but high-quality ancient genomes sufficient for phasing and imputation will be necessary to enable local ancestry inference deeper into the archaeofaunal record.

Our results here suggest that wolf-derived genomic ancestry in dogs may have served as valuable source material for adaptability in diverse postdomestication environments. Introgression of local wolves’ EPAS1 variant likely confers hypoxia tolerance in local landrace breeds, enabling these dogs to survive in the Tibetan highlands. As for free-ranging village dogs living in diverse human-adjacent environments, enhanced olfactory acuity acquired from wolf gene flow also offers advantages for increased survival. However, the current analysis cannot distinguish between human-driven selection for wolf ancestry and simply tolerance of wolf ancestry during historical selection for breed traits. The association between wolf ancestry and traits desirable in certain contexts such as alertness, size, and suspicion toward strangers in “guardian” breeds, for example, hints at the possibility that wolf genetic material may have reinforced some human-sought traits in dogs. The full scope, scale, and functional implications of the evolutionary interplay between dogs and wolves remain to be explored.

Materials and Methods

Sample Selection.

We downloaded raw genomic sequencing data from all ancient and modern wolves and dogs analyzed in ref. 26, as well as all whole genomes of extant canids from ENA/NCBI (1,066 total individuals, 286 wolves, 756 dogs, and 24 outgroups; Dataset S1). We aimed to construct mitochondrial, Y-chromosome, and nuclear gene trees from the same samples, hence using only whole genomes. There are far more mitochondrial genomes of dogs and wolves publicly available than whole genomes, but we did not utilize those because we aimed to be consistent in using the same samples for phylogenetic comparison. Although there is some geographic and temporal heterogeneity in sampled genomes, our dataset is constructed to capture the breadth of documented lineage diversity in dogs and wolves (8, 26, 82).

Detailed descriptions of the samples downloaded for this study, genomic read mapping, gene ontology, breed classification, and other computational analyses in this study are provided in SI Appendix.
