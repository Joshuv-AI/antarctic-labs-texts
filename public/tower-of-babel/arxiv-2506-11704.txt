Isometric-Universal Graphs for Trees
Edgar Baucher, François Dross, Cyril Gavoille

Abstract
We consider the problem of finding the smallest graph that contains two input trees each with at most n n vertices preserving their distances. In other words, we look for an isometric-universal graph with the minimum number of vertices for two given trees. We prove that this problem can be solved in time O ⁡ ( n 5 / 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{5/2}\log{n}) . We extend this result to forests instead of trees, and propose an algorithm with running time O ⁡ ( n 7 / 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{7/2}\log{n}) . As a key ingredient, we show that a smallest isometric-universal graph of two trees essentially is a tree. Furthermore, we prove that these results cannot be extended. Firstly, we show that deciding whether there exists an isometric-universal graph with t t vertices for three forests is NP-complete. Secondly, we show that any smallest isometric-universal graph cannot be a tree for some families of three trees. This latter result has implications for greedy strategies solving the smallest isometric-universal graph problem.

Introduction

Trees are one of the most natural and basic graph classes that can be studied. Studying problems on trees is thus natural, and is at the crux of the intersection between Graph Theory and Computer Science. Even for a general problem such as “finding a graph that contains two input trees”, there are a multitude of variants. To cite few of them, there is the case where the final graph is constrained to be a tree, where “containing” can be either as a subgraph or as a topological embedding
[
GN98
]
. There is also the more general case where the input is two graphs instead of two trees
[
AbuKhzam14
,
AKBS17
,
BJK00
]
, or where the input is composed of more than two trees contained as minors
[
Bodini02
,
GKLPS18
]

In this paper, we consider the problem of constructing a graph containing as isometric subgraphs two given trees (or forests) with the aim of minimizing its number of vertices. An

[

]

[

]
with important applications to spanners and distance labeling schemes.

This basic problem can be extended in many ways. First of all, one can consider a family of graphs as input rather than two trees. This leads to the notion of

graphs, a notion that has been widely studied. More precisely, a graph
\mathscr{U}
is

for a graph family

appears as an isometric subgraph in

. In this line of research,
\mathscr{U}
and

are often constrained. For instance, if

and

are limited to caterpillars

1.1
Our contribution

On the positive side, we prove that constructing a minimum isometric-universal graph for two forests, each with at most
n
vertices, can be done in time
O(n^{7/2}\log{n})
. On the negative side, we show that this result cannot be extended to three forests by proving that the associated decision problem is NP-complete.

More precisely, in
Section

2
, we demonstrate that a minimum isometric-universal graph for two trees can be constructed in time
O(n^{5/2}\log{n})
thanks to an algorithm due to Gupta and Nishimura
[
GN98
]
. Additionally, we show how to reduce the problem for two forests from the
Assignment
Problem, a variant of the maximum matching of maximum weight problem in bipartite graphs, with an
O(n)
slow-down factor in time complexity. As a key ingredient, we show that two trees always admit a minimum isometric-universal graph that is a tree. We believe that this nice property for two trees is an important technical contribution of the paper.

On the other hand, in
Section

3
, we show that these positive results cannot be extended in at least two ways. First, we show that deciding whether there exists an isometric-universal graph with
t
vertices for three forests is NP-complete. This is achieved by a technical reduction from the
3D-Matching
Problem. Second, we exhibit an infinite family of triples of trees having no minimum isometric-universal graph that is a tree. Implications to approximation algorithms are discussed in
Section

3.2
. In particular, we demonstrate that no greedy strategy can build a minimum isometric-universal graph, even with access to an optimal oracle for a pair of graphs. Finally, we show that the property used for two trees cannot be extended to minimum
k
-isometric-universal graphs if
k<(n-8)/3
.

Preliminaries and definitions

We consider finite and undirected graphs with no loops and no multi-edges. The

of two graphs

Given a graph

A subgraph

A

of

is isomorphic to a

-isometric subgraph of

-isometric embedding

Whenever

as introduced by Esperet et al.
[

]
.

•

Case
C_{1}
:
U_{k}=\widehat{G}_{i}\cup\widehat{H}_{j}
,

•

Case
C_{2}
:
U_{k}=\widehat{G}_{i}
and Case
C_{1}
does not occur, or

•

Case
C_{3}
:
U_{k}=\widehat{H}_{j}
and Case
C_{1}
does not occur.

Since one cannot have
U_{k}=\widehat{G}_{i}
and
U_{k}=\widehat{H}_{j}
without Case
C_{1}
, the three cases are indeed disjoint.

Let
D=\{{(G_{i},H_{j})\in X\times Y:\exists k,U_{k}=\widehat{G}_{i}\cup\widehat{H}_{j}}\}
be the set of all pairs of components for which Case
C_{1}
holds. We have
D\subseteq E(B)
.

Claim 5
.

If
\mathscr{U}
is minimum and minimal, then
D
is a maximum matching of
B
, and
|V({\mathscr{U}})|=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-w(D)
.

Proof.

If
D
is not a matching, then we have for some
i^{\prime}\neq i
or
j^{\prime}\neq j
:

(G_{i},H_{j}),(G_{i},H_{j^{\prime}})\in D\mbox{ or }(G_{i},H_{j}),(G_{i^{\prime}},H_{j})\in D.

It means that
\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{H}_{j},\widehat{H}_{j^{\prime}}
or
\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{G}_{i^{\prime}},\widehat{H}_{j}
are contained in a same component
U_{k}
of
\mathscr{U}
: a contradiction with
Claim

4
.

If the matching
D
is not maximum, then
|D|<s
, since
B
is (isomorphic to) a complete bipartite graph
K_{s,r}
with smallest part of size
s
. So we can select from
E(B)
a pair
(G_{i},H_{j})\notin D
, i.e., such that Case
C_{1}
does not hold. In particular, there must exist two indices
k_{i}\neq k_{j}
for which
U_{k_{i}}=\widehat{G}_{i}
(Case
C_{2}
) and
U_{k_{j}}=\widehat{H}_{j}
(Case
C_{3}
). We can construct a new minimal isometric-universal graph for
\left\{{G,H}\right\}
obtained from
\mathscr{U}
by merging
U_{k_{i}}
and
U_{k_{j}}
by one vertex, so saving one vertex from
\mathscr{U}
: a contradiction with the fact that
\mathscr{U}
was already minimum.

Let us calculate the number of vertices of
\mathscr{U}
. From the classification of
\mathscr{U}
’s components, three cases occur. For each
i\in\left\{{1,2,3}\right\}
, we denote by
K_{i}
the set of
U_{k}
’s component indices
k
such that Case
C_{i}
occurs.

By case disjunction, we have:

\left|{V\!\left({\mathscr{U}}\right)}\right|=\sum_{k}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|=\sum_{k\in K_{1}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|+\sum_{k\in K_{2}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|+\sum_{k\in K_{3}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|~.

If
k\in K_{2}
, then
|V({U_{k}})|=|V({\widehat{G}_{i}})|=|V({G_{i})})|
. Similarly, if
k\in K_{3}
, then
|V({U_{k}})|=|V({\widehat{H}_{j}})|=|V({H_{j}})|
.
By definition of
D
, if
(G_{i},H_{j})\in D
, then Case
C_{1}
occurs for
\widehat{G}_{i}
and
\widehat{H}_{j}
, and by case disjunction, there is no index
k
for which Case
C_{2}
occurs for
\widehat{G}_{i}
or Case
C_{3}
occurs for
\widehat{H}_{j}
. Thus, the sum for
k\in K_{2}\cup K_{3}
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in K_{2}\cup K_{3}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|{V\!\left({G_{i}}\right)}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left|{V\!\left({H_{j}}\right)}\right|-\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}(\left|{V\!\left({G_{i}}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H_{j}}\right)}\right|)

\displaystyle=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-\left({\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({G_{i}}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H_{j}}\right)}\right|}\right)~.

Consider some
k\in K_{1}
, i.e.,
U_{k}=\widehat{G}_{i}\cup\widehat{H}_{j}
for some
(i,j)
, which is equivalent to say that
(G_{i},H_{j})\in D
. The minimality of
\mathscr{U}
(in terms of number of vertices) implies that
U_{k}
has to be minimum for
\left\{{G_{i},H_{j}}\right\}
, i.e.,
|V({U_{k}})|=|V({\widehat{G}_{i}\cup\widehat{H}_{j}})|=|V({S(G_{i},H_{j})})|
. Otherwise, one could decrease
|V({\mathscr{U}})|
by simply replacing
U_{k}
by
S(G_{i},H_{j})
. Thus, the sum for
k\in K_{1}
can be rewritten as:

\sum_{k\in K_{1}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|=\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({S(G_{i},H_{j})}\right)}\right|~.

It follows that:

\displaystyle\left|{V\!\left({\mathscr{U}}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K_{2}\cup K_{3}}\left|{V\!\left({U_{k}}\right)}\right|+\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({S(G_{i},H_{j})}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-\left({\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({G_{i}}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H_{j}}\right)}\right|}\right)+\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({S(G_{i},H_{j})}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-\left({\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}\left|{V\!\left({G_{i}}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H_{j}}\right)}\right|-\left|{V\!\left({S(G_{i},H_{j})}\right)}\right|}\right)

\displaystyle=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-\sum_{(G_{i},H_{j})\in D}w(G_{i},H_{j})

\displaystyle=\left|{V\!\left({G}\right)}\right|+\left|{V\!\left({H}\right)}\right|-w(D)

by definition of the weight function
w
.
∎

It is easy to check that the maximum matching
D
, if
\mathscr{U}
is minimum and minimal as in
Claim

5
, is also of maximum weight. Indeed, by way of contradiction, assume
B
has a maximum matching
M
with
w(M)>w(D)
. By
Claim

5
,
|V({\mathscr{U}})|=|V({G})|+|V({H})|-w(D)
. Consider the universal graph
\mathscr{U}_{M}
as in
Claim

3
. It has
|V({\mathscr{U}_{M}})|=|V({G})|+|V({H})|-w(M)
vertices. So, if
w(M)>w(D)
, then
|V({\mathscr{U}_{M}})|<|V({\mathscr{U}})|
: a contradiction with the fact that
\mathscr{U}
is minimum.

To summarize,
Claim

3
and
Claim

5
tell us that the minimum number of vertices for an isometric-universal graph for
\left\{{G,H}\right\}
is
|V({G})|+|V({H})|-w(M)
, where
M
is a maximum matching for
B
with maximum weight. Moreover, given
\mathscr{K}
and
M
, such a graph can be constructed in time
O(n)
by
Claim

3
. It remains to compute
M
, given the weighted bipartite graph
B
.

The
Assignment
Problem asks for a maximum matching of maximum weight in a bipartite graph
\mathscr{B}
with integral edge weights. According to
[
RT12a
]
, this problem can be solved in time
O(m\sqrt{t}\log{(tC)})
, where
m
is the number of edges of
\mathscr{B}
,
t
the size of the maximum matching, and
C
an upper bound on the edge weights. Using the Thorup’s technique
[
Thorup04b
]
, in conjunction with
[
RT12a
]
, the computation can also be done in time
O(mt+t^{2}\log\log{\rho})
, where
\rho
is the size of the smallest part of
\mathscr{B}
.

For our purpose,
\mathscr{B}=B
, and thus
m=rs
,
t=\rho=s
, and
C=n
, since
B
is isomorphic to
K_{s,r}
, and since edge-weights are taken in
\left\{{{1},\dots,{n}}\right\}
. Therefore, the computation of a maximum matching
M
of maximum weight for
B
, given
B
, can be done in time:

\displaystyle O\left({\min\left\{{m\sqrt{t}\log{(tC)},\,mt+t^{2}\log\log{\rho}}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left({\min\left\{{rs^{3/2}\log{n},\,rs^{2}+s^{2}\log\log{s}}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left({s\min\left\{{r\sqrt{s}\log{n},\,rs+s\log\log{s}}\right\}}\right)

From
Claim

2
,
\mathscr{K}
, and thus
B
, can be computed in time
O(r\min\left\{{f(2n),sf(n)}\right\})
. Overall, the time to construct
B
,
M
, and
\mathscr{U}_{M}
, which is a minimum isometric-universal graph for
\left\{{G,H}\right\}
, is:

O\left({n+r\min\left\{{f(2n),\,sf(n)}\right\}+s\min\left\{{r\sqrt{s}\log{n},\,rs+s\log\log{s}}\right\}}\right)~.

The leftmost term ”
n+
”, coming from
Claim

3
and the construction of
\mathscr{U}_{M}
, can be omitted from the overall complexity by remarking that, by superlinearity,
f(n)/n\geqslant f(1)/1
, which implies
f(n)=\Omega(n)
. Indeed, as
f(n)
is an upper bound on a time complexity,
f(1)>0
holds necessarily. Hence, the term
r\min\left\{{f(2n),sf(n)}\right\}=\Omega(n)
.

This completes the proof of
Theorem

1
.
∎

2.2
The case of two trees

To compute the minimum isometric-universal graph of two forests in polynomial time, we will combine
Theorem

1
and the next result about two trees.

Theorem 6
.

Every minimum and minimal isometric-universal graph for two trees is a tree.

Before presenting its technical proof, we remark that both properties,
minimum
and

, are required. Indeed, the disjoint union of any pair of trees is isometric-universal for this pair, is minimal but not minimum (as implied by
Theorem

6
, the minimum one must be connected). Moreover, the graph composed of two pendant vertices attached to some vertex of a
C_{4}
(see

\{{K_{1,4},K^{+}_{1,3}}\}
composed of two
5

denotes here the graph

where one edge has been subdivided into two edges. However, it is not minimal: it contains
K^{+}_{1,4}
as proper subgraph which is isometric-universal for
\{{K_{1,4},K^{+}_{1,3}}\}
. This example can be generalized to two trees with
n
vertices, for every
n\geqslant 5
.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Let
\mathscr{U}
be any minimum and minimal isometric-universal graph for

and

in
\mathscr{U}
, and denote by

and
A_{2}
their corresponding embedded subgraphs in
\mathscr{U}
. Since
\mathscr{U}
is minimal,
\mathscr{U}=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
. So, each vertex or edge of
\mathscr{U}
, belongs either to
A_{1}\setminus A_{2}
,
A_{2}\setminus A_{1}
, or to
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
.

For convenience, let us denote by

, and by
|P|
the

of path

P
of

, for some

, satisfies

, where
u,v
are the extremities of
P
. This is because every path of a tree is a shortest path in this tree, and because
A_{i}
is an isometric subgraph of
\mathscr{U}
.

\mathscr{U}
must be connected, otherwise we could merge two connected components of
\mathscr{U}
by a vertex, and this without modifying any distance in
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
which are finite. This would contradicting the fact that
\mathscr{U}
is minimum. If
\mathscr{U}
is a tree, we are done. It remains to show that if
\mathscr{U}
has a cycle, then
\mathscr{U}
is not minimum. Consider the shortest cycle

in
\mathscr{U}
. Let us show that:

Claim 7
.

The edges of
C
can be partitioned into two paths
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
such that (see

•

•

P_{1}\setminus P_{2}\subset A_{1}\setminus A_{2}

•

P_{2}\setminus P_{1}\subset A_{2}\setminus A_{1}

Proof.

Consider the connected components of
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
. Obviously, such components are trees.
In particular,
C\subset A_{1}\cap A_{2}
is impossible. It is also clear that
C
intersects at least two such components. Indeed, first, if it intersects no components, then
C
is wholly contained in
A_{1}
or in
A_{2}
, contradicting they are trees. Secondly, if it intersects only one, then
C
is wholly contained in
A_{1}
or in
A_{2}
as well, since the part of
C
outside the component exists and belongs to one tree, and the part inside the component belongs to both trees.

Define
u
and
v
as any two vertices of
C
that belong to two different components of
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
, and such that
\dist_{C}(u,v)
is minimum. Let
P_{1}
be a path of
C
realizing this distance. By construction, the only vertices of
P_{1}
that are in

are precisely its extremities
u,v
. Indeed, if

contains a vertex
w
in

, then both
\dist_{C}(u,w)
and
\dist_{C}(v,w)
would be less than
\dist_{C}(u,v)
, which is not possible by the choice of

. Thus,

contains only vertices and edges of a same tree
A_{i}
, because if two consecutive edges belong to different trees, their common vertex must be in the intersection. W.l.o.g., assume that

. Note that we have

because

is a path of
A_{1}
. We set

to be the path of

.

Clearly, the first two points hold:

and

, and we have seen that

, and thus

.

For the third point, by way of contradiction, assume that

. We have

. By the choice of

. On the other hand, let us consider the path

. By minimality of

. But, if

Proof.

3.2
Consequences for greedy strategies

As we will see,

\mathscr{F}

More formally, let
