Multicentre prospective cohort study to develop and validate a machine learning-based model for predicting 6-month all-cause mortality in elderly patients with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in China: study protocol
Authors: Hongshan Pu, Liheng Liu, Yiting Chang, Lin Su, Xiaofeng Zeng, Wenwu Cheng, Yan Jiang, Jinhan He, Li Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has an unpredictable clinical course, causing difficulties in short-term mortality prediction, overtreatment and delayed palliative care. Existing prediction models are limited and lack applicability to Chinese elderly patients with advanced COPD. Given the heavy disease burden and limited palliative care in China, we designed this multicentre cohort study to develop a 6-month mortality prediction model for elderly patients with advanced COPD to aid risk stratification, timely palliative care and efficient healthcare resource allocation.Methods and analysisPatient recruitment has been ongoing since May 2024 and will be completed by December 2026, with a 12-month follow-up to be completed by December 2027. Eligible patients are being enrolled, and multidimensional baseline data including demographic characteristics, clinical indicators, laboratory results, comprehensive geriatric assessment and COPD-specific prognostic factors are being systematically collected. All participants will receive 12 months of standardised follow-up (monthly for the first 6 months and quarterly thereafter) to monitor 6-month all-cause mortality (primary outcome), as well as survival duration, end-of-life healthcare utilisation and do-not-resuscitate status (secondary outcomes). After completion of data collection, we will employ multiple machine learning algorithms to develop and internally validate a 6-month mortality prediction model with pre-specified centres reserved for external validation. Model performance will be evaluated by discrimination and calibration and head-to-head comparisons with the Body Mass Index, Airflow Obstruction, Dyspnoea and Exercise Capacity (BODE) and Age, Dyspnoea and Airflow Obstruction (ADO) indices will be conducted to verify its clinical value. The findings will provide a China-specific prediction tool for elderly patients with advanced COPD to guide clinical intervention, palliative care referral and healthcare resource allocation.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2024-2662) and registered at ChiCTR2500100351. Informed consent is being obtained from all participants. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at academic conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500100351.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Aged; Machine Learning; Mortality; Protocols & guidelines

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the fourth leading cause of death worldwide, according to WHO data, and is projected to become the third by 2030.1 Its incidence increases with age.2 Approximately 10% of individuals aged 40 and older have COPD,3,5 and this figure increases to 20%–25% for those over the age of 70.6 7 In China, COPD is one of the three most common chronic diseases.8 9

COPD imposes a significant symptom burden10,12 and economic burden.13 14 In 2021, China reported approximately 4.43 million new cases of COPD and 50.59 million existing cases, representing nearly one-quarter of the global COPD prevalence.15 Over 10% of deaths in China were attributed to COPD.16

Prognostic challenges in advanced COPD

Advanced COPD is often a progressive and terminal illness,17 posing significant challenges for prognosis and the delivery of optimal care, primarily due to inherent prognostic uncertainty. Accurate information on the disease trajectory, treatment goals and prognosis is important for both patients and clinicians.18 Unlike certain cancers, there is no universally accepted definition for ‘advanced’ COPD,19 and its clinical course is highly variable and difficult to predict,20 particularly over the short term.

This prognostic challenge has profound implications for clinical practice. A well-documented consequence is the systematic overestimation of survival by both clinicians and patients.21,23 This optimism bias frequently leads to a misalignment in treatment goals, resulting in two critical and opposing problems: overtreatment24,26 and undertreatment.27,29 Overly aggressive care drives overuse of invasive interventions such as mechanical ventilation and repeated hospitalisations11 30; while these measures may prolong survival, they often come at the cost of increased patient suffering, reduced quality of life and substantial healthcare system burden.31 Concurrently, the predominant focus on life-prolonging treatment leads to delayed or absent integration of palliative care,27,29 leaving patients without timely symptom management and quality-of-life support.

Beyond impacting individual patient care, prognostic inaccuracy exacerbates the patient-clinician tensions. Mismatches between predicted and actual survival outcomes frequently cause significant dissatisfaction among patients and families. Notably, in China, a substantial proportion of medical disputes in respiratory medicine stem from such prognostic discrepancies, particularly in end-of-life care for advanced chronic respiratory diseases.32 33

Limitations of existing prognostic tools

COPD prognosis is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, comorbidities and—to a lesser extent—acute exacerbations. Frequent exacerbations and repeated invasive mechanical ventilation for acute respiratory failure are well-recognised markers of poor outcomes. Nevertheless, there is no universally accepted tool for predicting short-term (≤6 months) survival in patients with COPD.20 34 35 Widely used prognostic indices, such as the Body Mass Index, Airflow Obstruction, Dyspnoea and Exercise Capacity (BODE) index36 and the Age, Dyspnoea and Airflow Obstruction index37 were primarily designed for long-term (1–5 years) risk stratification38 and show limited performance in short-term prognosis.35 These models also fail to incorporate dynamic clinical variables, emerging biomarkers (eg, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, inflammatory markers) and psychosocial factors (eg, social support, living alone), which are particularly relevant for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities. Although some newer models have attempted to include these elements, their predictive validity for short-term outcomes remains to be further validated.

Notably, these existing prognostic tools were predominantly developed in developed countries.38 Their construction was shaped by local demographic characteristics, healthcare resources and social contexts, which limits their generalisability to developing nations. Significant differences in population structure, disease spectrum, healthcare accessibility and sociocultural environment exist between China and Western countries. The direct application of Western-derived models may lead to biased predictions and suboptimal clinical decision-making. Therefore, it is essential to develop and validate a tailored short-term prognostic assessment tool for COPD that incorporates population-specific features and healthcare realities in China.

The potential of machine learning

Machine learning (ML) algorithms—such as random forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and deep learning—offer a promising approach to integrate high-dimensional data from electronic health records, including clinical, laboratory, imaging and social-behavioural variables.39 These methods can capture complex, nonlinear relationships and improve predictive accuracy.

Study rationale and objectives

Considering the unique characteristics of COPD in China, such as late diagnosis,40 a high symptom burden,16 41 socioeconomic diversity42,45 and an underdeveloped palliative care infrastructure,46 there is an urgent need for a tailored and accurate short-term survival prediction model. Such a tool could enable timely palliative care referrals, reduce unnecessary interventions and enhance resource allocation.

This protocol outlines the development and validation of an ML-based model designed to predict 6-month survival in elderly patients with advanced COPD in China. The model uses multidimensional data to overcome the limitations of current prognostic tools. The final prediction model will be translated into a simplified, easy-to-use clinical nomogram and a free online risk calculator, to facilitate the application of the model in real-world clinical settings for early identification of high-risk patients and timely palliative care referral.

Methods/design

Study design

This protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval No. 2024-2662) and prospectively registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500100351). The full protocol, including the pre-specified study design, endpoints and statistical analysis plan, was submitted to the registry immediately after ethics approval, locked prior to patient recruitment and remained unmodified during the registration review period. Written informed consent is being obtained from all participants before any study procedures; for those with impaired decision-making capacity due to cognitive impairment, delirium or other conditions, consent is being provided by their legally authorised representative (legal guardian). The study workflow is shown in figure 1, and the final formal registration number was issued in January 2025 after the registry platform’s routine administrative review. This study is part of a dual-cohort research project approved under the same ethics approval (No. 2024-2662). A parallel protocol focusing on elderly patients with heart failure will be conducted separately under the same ethical approval. The two cohorts are independent and non-overlapping in patient enrolment. Patient recruitment has been ongoing since May 2024 and will be completed by December 2026. Follow-up will be completed by December 2027. In addition, the final two centres to initiate patient enrolment will be reserved as a pre-specified independent external validation cohort, and their data will be fully withheld from all model development and internal validation procedures. The identity of these external validation centres will be kept concealed from all study personnel at participating centres until the data analysis phase to eliminate potential systematic bias in clinical assessments including the Surprise Question (SQ) evaluation.

Study setting

The study is conducted across multiple medical institutions nationwide, including West China Hospital of Sichuan University, West Tianfu Hospital of Sichuan University, West Xiamen Hospital of Sichuan University, West Sanya Hospital of Sichuan University, Fudan University Cancer Hospital, Peking University Cancer Hospital, the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, the Affiliated Hospital of Huazhong University of Science and Technology and the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

Patients and public involvement

Patients or the public will not be involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Study participants

Patients with COPD aged 60 and older are potential research subjects for this study.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible patients must meet Condition 1 and simultaneously meet either Condition 2 or Condition 3.

Condition 1. Core COPD diagnosis:

Confirmed COPD medical history in accordance with the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) COPD guidelines, or documented post-bronchodilator spirometry with both forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity <70% and FEV1% predicted <50% in medical records.

Condition 2. ‘Surprise Question (SQ)’ eligibility:

Assessment via the standardised SQ: “Would you be surprised if this patient died within the next 6 months?”, with responses dichotomised as Yes or No. Assessment is exclusively conducted by respiratory attending physicians with ≥5 years of experience. All investigators complete mandatory standardised SQ training, with required documentation of clinical rationale for each response and quarterly coordinating centre audits to ensure inter-centre consistency.

Condition 3. Resting dyspnoea with functional decline eligibility:

Patients first receive guideline-recommended standard doses of short-acting beta-agonist combined with short-acting muscarinic antagonist, and only those meeting both Criterion A and Criterion B 30 min after administration are eligible.

Criterion A (either of the following): (1) severe activity limitation (nearly sitting/lying down): inability to stand unsupported for >5 min, reliant on seated/semi-recumbent position for dyspnoea relief, confirmed by two observations within 24 hours (≥6 hours interval), non-COPD dyspnoea causes excluded; (2) documented FEV1% predicted <30%.

Criterion B (any one of the following): (1) measured in room air without supplemental oxygen: partial pressure of oxygen ≤55 mm Hg or peripheral oxygen saturation ≤88%; (2) partial pressure of carbon dioxide ≥50 mm Hg; (3) partial pressure of arterial oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2)≤250.

Quality control

A three-tiered quality control system is established across nine centres to ensure standardised implementation of inclusion/exclusion criteria and consistent study execution. This system includes mandatory pre-study centralised training (≥90/100 qualification score required for patient enrolment), a standardised Case Report Form with pre-set quantifiable enrolment options, quarterly random audits of 15% of enrolled cases with corrective feedback for non-compliance and central adjudication of borderline cases by an independent expert committee to mitigate inter-centre bias.

Sample size

In patients with advanced or end-stage COPD (including those with GOLD stage 4 disease, severe acute exacerbations requiring non-invasive ventilation support, or refractory chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure), clinical studies and meta-analyses have consistently documented a 6-month all-cause mortality rate of 20%–30%.35 47 48 Based on this evidence, a conservative baseline event rate of 25% (0.25) was pre-specified for sample size calculation. All calculations were performed to cover all pre-specified statistical analyses, with the ML model development calculation designated as the primary and definitive basis for final target enrolment, aligned with the study’s core objective.

For univariate association analysis,47 48 a minimum of 245 cases was required with core parameters set as two-sided α=0.05, 80% statistical power, expected OR=1.8 for key predictors, an exposed group event rate of 0.3 and a baseline event rate of 0.25. For multivariate regression modelling, the widely accepted 10 events per variable criterion required a minimum of 100 outcome events (corresponding to 400 cases) to avoid overfitting.49 For the primary ML model development, power calculation using a validated ML-specific prognostic model framework50 51 confirmed that at least 103 outcome events were needed in the 70% training set (two-sided α=0.05, 80% power, 10 pre-specified candidate predictors, minimum clinically meaningful C-statistic of 0.7), corresponding to an effective training sample size of 412 cases.

After adjusting for a 20% anticipated follow-up attrition rate and the pre-specified 70/30 train-test split for internal validation, the final target enrolment was set at 736 patients. This sample size is expected to yield approximately 184 6-month mortality outcome events, which fully meets the requirements of all pre-specified analyses, including ML model development, cross-validation, univariate and multivariate analyses and external validation.

Baseline assessment

Based on a literature review and Delphi expert consultation, we are currently collecting factors associated with survival duration in late-stage patients with COPD, encompassing the following aspects:

Demographic characteristics: including name, gender, age, date of birth, ID number, hospital registration number, ethnicity, body mass index, residency status, marital status, education level, income status, medical insurance type, sampling unit, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, contact information, etc.

Chronic disease-related risk factors: including current disease types, total medications, major diagnoses leading to death, age-adjusted comorbidity index, etc.

Blood markers and related test indicators: including complete blood count, liver function, kidney function, electrolytes, cardiac markers, lipids, blood glucose, coagulation function, inflammatory markers, infection and pathogen-related indicators, arterial blood gas analysis, ECG, echocardiography, pulmonary function tests, etc.

Vital signs and symptoms: including blood pressure, heart rate, pulse rate, respiratory rate, orthopnea, dyspnoea, consciousness status, oedema, episodes of hypoglycaemia and so forth.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment: encompassing the Barthel Index for Activities of Daily Living, mood evaluation, pain assessment, nutritional assessment, frailty assessment, pressure ulcer assessment, bedridden time assessment, delirium assessment, choking assessment, and social support assessment, among others.

The specific evaluation of prognostic indicators in advanced COPD subjects: including the COPD history and symptom control, COPD classification and symptom score and COPD treatment.

All direct personal identifiers undergo strict pseudonymisation and de-identification immediately on data collection, in compliance with ethical requirements and personal health information protection regulations, with no identifiable information retained in the final study dataset or published results. A unified multicentre data management system is established for this study: all data will be encrypted during transmission, stored in a dedicated password-protected database at the coordinating centre with access restricted exclusively to authorised study personnel and the de-identified dataset will be retained for 5 years after publication of the primary study results, followed by permanent destruction in accordance with institutional data management regulations.

Intervention

This is an observational, prospective cohort study without any study-specific intervention for enrolled patients. All clinical treatments for participants are determined by their attending physicians in accordance with the GOLD COPD guidelines and local clinical practice standards, with no restrictions imposed by the study protocol.

Follow-up

After patient enrolment and completion of the baseline assessment, all participants receive standardised follow-up for a total of 12 months: monthly follow-up for the first 6 months, and quarterly follow-up from month 7 to month 12. Each follow-up assessment documents: (1) survival status (alive, deceased or lost to follow-up (LTFU)); (2) for deceased patients: date of death, cause of death, location of death, do-not-resuscitate (DNR) status, healthcare utilisation (emergency room visits, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, tumour-related treatments and supportive care) in the last month of life, and palliative care received before death; and (3) for patients LTFU: the reasons for LTFU and the date of last confirmed contact.

Management of dropped-out and LTFU cases

Dropouts are defined as participants who voluntarily withdraw from the study at any time after enrolment. LTFU is defined as participants who remain uncontactable for 3 consecutive months despite multiple scheduled contact attempts via telephone and other communication channels at different time points.

The reason for withdrawal or LTFU, along with the date of last confirmed follow-up, will be systematically recorded and transparently reported for all dropouts and LTFU cases. For the primary 6-month all-cause mortality binary analysis, LTFU patients with no confirmed vital status at the 6-month endpoint will be excluded from primary model development and validation. Baseline characteristics will be compared between included and excluded patients to assess potential selection bias, with pre-specified best-case/worst-case scenario sensitivity analyses to be performed to verify the robustness of the primary findings. For the supplementary exploratory time-to-event survival analysis, all dropouts and LTFU cases will be right-censored at the date of their last confirmed follow-up, consistent with standard survival analysis guidelines.

Outcome measurement

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is all-cause mortality within 6 months from study enrolment, defined as a binary endpoint (dead vs alive at 6 months). The primary performance metric for the prediction model is the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) for this binary endpoint.

Secondary outcomes

Overall survival duration from enrolment to 12 months; healthcare utilisation before death, including emergency room visits, ICU admission, invasive mechanical ventilation and length of hospital stay in the last month of life; DNR status at the time of death; receipt of specialist palliative care services before death.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and ML procedures will be conducted in Python (Python Software Foundation), with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p value<0.05. The full pre-specified statistical analysis plan was locked prior to data extraction to prevent p-hacking and overfitting. Core Python libraries used cover the full analysis workflow, including pandas, numpy, scipy, fancyimpute, scikit-learn, xgboost, lifelines, and shap. At 6-month follow-up, all patients with confirmed survival status will be used for model building and internal validation, except for pre-specified external validation data.

Data preprocessing

Continuous variables will be expressed as mean±SD if normally distributed (assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test), or as median (IQR) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables will be expressed as frequencies (percentages).

Prior to any preprocessing or imputation, the full model development dataset will be randomly split into a 70% training set and a 30% held-out internal validation set via stratified sampling by the 6-month all-cause mortality primary endpoint to ensure balanced event distribution. Study centre will be added as a random effect in the mixed-effects model during training to adjust for inter-centre heterogeneity, and leave-one-centre-out cross-validation will be conducted as the core internal validation method to quantify the model’s cross-centre generalisability. The internal validation set will be kept completely separate throughout the entire model development process.

Within the training set only, variables with ≥30% missing values will be excluded from further analysis. Multiple imputation by chained equations will be performed for the remaining missing data, and all study outcomes will be excluded from the imputation model to eliminate data leakage. Five imputed datasets will be generated, with pooled estimates calculated in accordance with Rubin’s rules.

All preprocessing steps will be saved as a serialised pipeline and applied uniformly to validation datasets without refitting. Sensitivity analyses comparing multiple imputation and complete-case analysis will be conducted to verify the robustness of the study findings.

Feature selection

All feature selection steps will be restricted solely to the training set, with no access to the validation sets, to avoid overfitting. To ensure model stability, reproducibility and clinical plausibility, we will adopt a triangulated feature selection approach that integrates three complementary methods: (1) Univariate logistic regression: A liberal initial filter with a p value threshold of <0.10 will be used to avoid premature exclusion of clinically relevant predictors. (2) LASSO regression: 10-fold cross-validation with the one SE rule (lambda.1se). (3) Boruta algorithm: A RF-based, non-parametric method for statistically robust feature selection.

Features selected by at least two of the three methods form the final consensus feature pool. To reduce multicollinearity, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients will be calculated for all features in the consensus pool. For feature pairs with |r|≥0.7, only the clinically more relevant or more reliably measured feature will be retained, based on pre-specified clinical expert consensus.

Model development and training

Six ML algorithms—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), artificial neural network (ANN) and Naïve Bayes (NB)—will be trained and compared on the training set using the final consensus feature set.

Hyperparameter tuning for all models will be conducted exclusively on the training set via fivefold cross-validated grid search, with the optimisation objective pre-specified as maximising the AUC-ROC for the primary endpoint. The pre-specified hyperparameter search space for each algorithm will be locked prior to data extraction, with no post-hoc adjustment allowed to avoid data-driven overfitting.

To avoid subjective selection bias, a locked, tiered model selection framework will be pre-specified prior to data unblinding. First, only models with internal validation AUC-ROC ≥0.70 will be eligible. Eligible models will be ranked according to the following non-adjustable priorities: (1) primary priority: calibration performance; (2) secondary priority: discriminative performance; (3) tertiary priority: clinical interpretability; (4) final priority: clinical applicability and simplicity.

A pre-specified interpretable fallback rule is integrated into the model selection framework, with detailed implementation rules specified in the interpretability analysis section.

Interpretability analysis

To address the inherent ‘black-box’ limitation of ML models and ensure the clinical interpretability and plausibility of the final prediction model, we have established a multi-level, full-process interpretability assurance framework:

A priori clinical anchoring: All candidate predictors are pre-specified through a systematic literature review and formal clinical expert consensus. This ensures that only variables with biologically and clinically plausible associations with 6-month all-cause mortality in advanced COPD are included at the source of model development.

Core post-hoc interpretability tool: For the final best-performing model, we will calculate Shapley Additive exPlanations values to quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to individual risk predictions. This method enables dual interpretability: global interpretability (cohort-wide feature importance ranking) and local interpretability (patient-specific key drivers of risk prediction).

Sensitivity analysis

The following pre-specified sensitivity analyses will be conducted to verify the robustness of the primary findings: (1) Best-case/worst-case scenario analyses: To assess the impact of loss to follow-up on the primary outcome, assuming all LTFU patients survived to 6 months (best-case) versus all LTFU patients died within 6 months (worst-case). Tipping point analysis will also be conducted to evaluate how extreme assumptions about LTFU outcomes would affect model conclusions. (2) Complete-case analysis versus multiple imputation: To assess the impact of missing data on model performance and feature selection. (3) Competing risk analysis: All deaths will undergo standardised, blinded cause-of-death adjudication, classified as COPD-related or non-COPD-related. The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model will be applied to identify predictors of COPD-specific mortality, treating non-COPD death as a competing event. HRs from the cause-specific competing risk model will be compared with those from the primary all-cause mortality model, to assess the impact of competing risks on model findings. (4) Alternative feature selection strategies: To assess the stability of model performance when using individual feature selection methods (rather than the consensus approach). (5) Centre effect sensitivity analysis: We will fit a mixed-effects logistic regression model with the study centre as a random effect to adjust for inter-centre heterogeneity, and compare the model performance and feature importance ranking with the primary model to verify the robustness of the findings.

Adverse events

This is a prospective observational study with no intervention and no associated adverse events.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Strengths

Key strengths of this study include: a prospective multicentre design across nine Chinese institutions with good population representativeness; advanced ML algorithms to capture non-linear associations for improved short-term mortality prediction; standardised multidimensional data collection and a three-tiered quality control system ensuring model reliability and reproducibility; pre-specified external validation, head-to-head comparison with gold-standard prognostic indices and a full interpretability framework guaranteeing clinical applicability; and planned translation of the model into a clinical nomogram and online calculator to facilitate real-world implementation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all study sites are in China, limiting generalisability to non-Chinese populations and requiring further international validation. Second, only baseline predictors are included, without time-varying covariates for dynamic risk updating. Third, the inherent trade-off between ML model complexity and interpretability is addressed via a pre-specified interpretable model fallback strategy. Fourth, potential selection bias from loss to follow-up is mitigated through pre-specified detailed handling and sensitivity analysis strategies.

Modification of the protocol

Formal protocol amendment is mandatory for any modifications affecting study conduct, patient benefits or safety, including changes to core study design, objectives, population, sample size and procedures. All amendments must be approved by the ethics committee, be submitted to relevant health authorities per local regulations, and be simultaneously updated in the China Clinical Trial Registry in a timely manner.

Ethics and dissemination

The results of this study will be submitted for publication in international peer-reviewed journals, and the study findings will also be presented at relevant academic conferences.
