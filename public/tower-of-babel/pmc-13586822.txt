A sonographic pitfall in penile fracture: Spongiosal displacement mimicking urethral injury, with surgical correlation
Authors: Sunil K Ravuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenile fracture is a urological emergency in which high-resolution ultrasound can help localize tunical disruption and define associated soft-tissue injury. We report a 40-year-old man who sustained penile fracture during intercourse in the female-superior position. High-resolution ultrasound with color Doppler (Voluson E8, 12–15 MHz linear transducer) identified a tunical defect at the mid third of the dorsal surface of the left corpus cavernosum, measuring 7.8 × 2.6 mm, with an intracavernosal hematoma measuring 33.9 × 21.7 × 32.0 mm and causing marked lateral displacement of the corpus spongiosum. Because this displacement raised concern for associated urethral injury, the urethra and corpus spongiosum were assessed in multiple transverse and longitudinal planes. Preserved spongiosal echotexture, visualized continuity of the urethral course, absence of perispongiosal fluid, and preserved Doppler perfusion supported preservation of urethral integrity. Same-day surgical exploration confirmed the tunical tear, intact corpus spongiosum and urethra, and hematoma containment within Buck’s fascia. No MRI, retrograde urethrography, or cystoscopy was required in this clinically concordant case. This case illustrates how hematoma-related spongiosal displacement may mimic urethral injury and shows the complementary value of systematic multiplanar ultrasound in pre-operative assessment. Additional urethral imaging or endoscopic evaluation remains appropriate when clinical or sonographic suspicion persists.Keywords: Penile fracture, Tunica albuginea, Corpus spongiosum, Urethral injury, Doppler ultrasonography, Genitourinary trauma

Introduction

Penile fracture is a urological emergency defined as traumatic rupture of the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa, typically caused by abrupt bending forces applied to the erect penis [1]. During erection, the tunica thins from approximately 2 mm to 0.25–0.5 mm, making it highly susceptible to rupture at relatively modest applied pressure [2]. The condition classically presents with an audible snap, immediate detumescence, acute pain, rapidly progressive swelling, and the aubergine (eggplant) deformity [3].

The female-superior position is a recognized mechanism of penile fracture [4,5]. The precise direction of force and resulting tear location vary with the angle of buckling and point of impact; therefore, a causal relationship between coital position and a specific tunical surface cannot be established from a single case. In this patient, the defect was located on the dorsal aspect of the left corpus cavernosum on ultrasound and at surgery. Published ultrasound reports demonstrate variable tear locations, including ventral and dorsolateral defects [[6], [7], [8], [9]], underscoring the importance of deliberate circumferential interrogation rather than assuming a specific surface of injury.

Urethral injury complicates approximately 10–22% of penile fractures and is clinically suspected when hematuria, blood at the urethral meatus, or voiding difficulty are present [10,11]. However, a dorsal tunical tear producing a large intracavernosal hematoma may displace the corpus spongiosum sufficiently to raise sonographic concern for urethral involvement even in the absence of these clinical signs — a diagnostic pitfall that, to our knowledge, is infrequently emphasized in the ultrasound literature.

We present this case to highlight a diagnostic pitfall in which hematoma-related displacement of an intact corpus spongiosum and urethra may mimic associated urethral injury. We also describe a structured multiparametric ultrasound assessment used in this patient as a proposed imaging approach that requires validation in larger series.

Case presentation

Clinical history and examination

A 40-year-old man presented to the emergency department approximately 10–11 hours after sustaining penile trauma during sexual intercourse in the female-superior position. He reported hearing an audible snap at the moment of injury, followed by immediate detumescence, acute penile pain, and rapidly progressive swelling. There was no hematuria, no blood at the urethral meatus, and no voiding difficulty or urinary obstruction, making clinical urethral injury less likely. However, given the severity of the swelling and the mechanism, formal imaging exclusion was considered necessary before surgical planning.

Physical examination demonstrated the classical aubergine deformity: a markedly swollen, diffusely ecchymosed penile shaft with relative sparing of the glans and no external skin breach. The degree of swelling and interval since injury rendered clinical palpation of the tunical defect unreliable, and urgent pre-operative ultrasound mapping was requested (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Sonographic technique

High-resolution grey-scale and color Doppler ultrasound was performed using a Voluson E8 platform with a 12–15 MHz linear transducer. The flaccid penis was examined systematically in transverse and longitudinal planes from the glans to the penoscrotal junction. Dorsal and ventral aspects of both corpora were interrogated circumferentially. For clarity, the term ``dorsal'' is used for the surface opposite the corpus spongiosum and ``ventral'' for the spongiosal surface; the lesion described as posterior on the original scan orientation corresponded to the dorsal aspect. A structured five-parameter assessment was used in this case: (1) tunica albuginea continuity; (2) cavernosal echotexture and hematoma characterization; (3) Buck’s fascia integrity; (4) corpus spongiosum morphology and displacement; and (5) color Doppler assessment of cavernosal and spongiosal perfusion. This represents a proposed case-based approach rather than a validated protocol.

Sonographic findings

Grey-scale imaging demonstrated heterogeneous echotexture of the left corpus cavernosum with focal discontinuity of the tunica albuginea at the mid third of its dorsal aspect, representing the tunical tear. Caliper measurement confirmed a defect measuring 7.8 mm in length and 2.6 mm in width (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Deliberate circumferential assessment was important because the defect was not conspicuous on a limited ventral-only examination.

A large heterogeneous intracavernosal hematoma of mixed echogenicity was identified within the left corpus cavernosum, measuring 33.9 × 21.7 × 32.0 mm on three-axis measurement (estimated volume approximately 13 mL) (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The hematoma extended posteriorly and exerted significant mass effect, causing lateral displacement of the corpus spongiosum from its expected midline position. This finding prompted immediate systematic assessment for urethral injury.

Targeted evaluation of the displaced corpus spongiosum demonstrated preserved intrinsic echotexture without disruption of spongiosal architecture (Fig. 7). The urethral course was assessed in multiple transverse and longitudinal planes, including adjacent to the prepuce (Fig. 8) and at the level of displacement by the hematoma (Fig. 10). Across these planes, the urethra appeared continuous, and no perispongiosal fluid collection was identified. Taken together with the absence of hematuria, blood at the meatus, or voiding difficulty, these findings supported preservation of urethral integrity; this was subsequently confirmed at surgery. Color Doppler demonstrated preserved cavernosal and spongiosal perfusion without evidence of a hemodynamically significant vascular complication (Fig. 9). This excluded a major flow abnormality at the time of examination; however, long-term erectile outcome cannot be inferred from Doppler findings in a single case, particularly because long-term functional follow-up was unavailable. Buck’s fascia was intact, with the hematoma contained within the deep fascial compartment and no sonographic extension into the perineum.

Surgical findings and outcome

The patient underwent same-day surgical exploration on the basis of the clinical diagnosis together with the sonographic localization and assessment. MRI, retrograde urethrography, and cystoscopy were not performed because there were no clinical signs of urethral injury and multiplanar ultrasound did not show urethral or spongiosal disruption. A circumferential degloving incision confirmed a transverse tunical tear at the mid third of the dorsal aspect of the left corpus cavernosum with associated intracavernosal hematoma. The corpus spongiosum and urethra were intact. The operative findings were concordant with the sonographic localization and demonstrated that the apparent spongiosal/urethral displacement represented mass effect rather than disruption. Hematoma evacuation and primary tunical repair were completed without intraoperative complication. Long-term postoperative functional follow-up was unavailable because subsequent care occurred at the tertiary referral center.

Discussion

Penile fracture is usually diagnosed clinically, but ultrasound can provide valuable pre-operative anatomical information when the site of tunical disruption is uncertain or associated injury is suspected. Prior series describe the value of high-frequency grey-scale and color Doppler ultrasound for identifying tunical discontinuity, hematoma, and alternative diagnoses [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Comparative imaging data also support a high diagnostic yield for ultrasound when performed by experienced operators, while MRI remains useful when ultrasound is equivocal [12]. In the present case, systematic circumferential interrogation identified a dorsally located tunical defect that could have been overlooked with a limited examination.

The relationship between sexual position and tear orientation should be interpreted cautiously. Although female-superior intercourse is frequently reported in penile-fracture cohorts [4,5], the exact surface involved depends on the moment of buckling and cannot be predicted reliably from mechanism alone. Accordingly, the practical implication is not that a specific mechanism mandates a dorsal tear, but that the entire tunica should be examined in orthogonal planes regardless of the presumed force vector.

Pre-operative localization may assist the surgeon in planning exposure, but the operative approach is determined by the overall clinical and intraoperative context. In this patient, circumferential degloving provided access to the dorsally located defect and confirmed the ultrasound findings.

A large intracavernosal hematoma can distort normal penile anatomy and displace the corpus spongiosum. In this case, marked lateral displacement raised concern for associated urethral injury despite the absence of hematuria, blood at the meatus, or voiding difficulty. Urethral injury should be considered particularly when such clinical features are present [10,11].

The key educational point is that displacement should not be equated with disruption. Multiplanar ultrasound demonstrated preserved spongiosal architecture, continuity of the visualized urethral course, and absence of perispongiosal fluid. These findings supported an intact urethra and were confirmed at surgery. However, ultrasound cannot be considered a universal stand-alone test for excluding urethral injury. When clinical suspicion persists, or when the urethra cannot be confidently assessed sonographically, retrograde urethrography, cystoscopy, MRI, or other appropriate evaluation should be considered according to the clinical setting [10,13].

The five elements applied here—tunical continuity, cavernosal/hematoma assessment, Buck’s fascia integrity, spongiosal morphology, and Doppler perfusion—are best regarded as a proposed structured imaging checklist derived from this case rather than a validated protocol. Similar ultrasound literature supports systematic evaluation of tunical defects, hematoma extent, vascularity, and associated injuries [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Larger prospective studies would be required to establish diagnostic thresholds, reproducibility, and the incremental value of each component.

In this clinically concordant case, high-resolution ultrasound supplied sufficient anatomical information to proceed directly to surgery without MRI, retrograde urethrography, or cystoscopy, and the operative findings were concordant. This single case should not be interpreted as evidence that ultrasound routinely replaces these modalities. Rather, ultrasound can serve as a rapid, accessible first-line or complementary examination, particularly when performed by an experienced operator, while MRI or urethral evaluation remains appropriate when sonographic findings are equivocal or clinical suspicion persists [[10], [12], [13]].

For practical reporting, radiologists may consider documenting the site and size of tunical discontinuity; hematoma dimensions and direction of mass effect; Buck’s fascia; spongiosal morphology; the urethral course in multiple planes when visible; and Doppler perfusion. The completeness and diagnostic confidence of the examination should be stated, and limitations should be acknowledged when swelling, pain, probe compression, or technical factors restrict assessment.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. It describes a single patient and therefore cannot establish diagnostic performance or validate the proposed structured ultrasound approach. Ultrasound is operator-dependent, and severe swelling, pain, probe compression, or limited acoustic access may reduce confidence. The assessment of urethral integrity was based on combined clinical findings and multiplanar ultrasound and was confirmed surgically; there was no direct comparison with MRI, retrograde urethrography, or cystoscopy. Long-term functional follow-up was unavailable. The relationship between the female-superior mechanism and the dorsal tear location is biologically plausible but cannot be considered causal from a single observation. When ultrasound is nondiagnostic or clinical suspicion for urethral injury persists, additional imaging or endoscopic evaluation remains appropriate [10,13,12].

Conclusion

This case highlights a sonographic pitfall in penile fracture: a large intracavernosal hematoma may markedly displace an intact corpus spongiosum and urethra, creating concern for associated urethral injury. Systematic high-resolution ultrasound in multiple transverse and longitudinal planes accurately localized the tunical tear and demonstrated findings that supported preserved urethral integrity, subsequently confirmed at surgery. The case supports ultrasound as a useful component of pre-operative assessment in selected clinically concordant patients, while emphasizing that persistent clinical or sonographic concern for urethral injury warrants additional dedicated evaluation. The structured assessment described here should be regarded as a proposed approach requiring validation rather than an established protocol.
