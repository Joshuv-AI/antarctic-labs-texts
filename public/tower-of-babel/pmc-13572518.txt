Evaluation of Portulaca oleracea (Purslane) extract as a natural anticoccidial agent in japanese quails: Effects on growth performance, immune response, and gut health
Authors: Muslimah N. Alsulami, Mohamed T. El-Saadony
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of synthetic anticoccidial agents in poultry production has raised concerns regarding drug resistance and tissue residues, prompting the search for natural alternatives. This study investigated the therapeutic potential of dietary Portulaca oleracea (purslane) extract against coccidiosis in Japanese quails compared to Amprolium (100 µg/mL) treatment as an anticoccidial drug. A total of 300 one-day-old quails were allocated into six groups: T1 (control), T2 (Eimeria-infected, untreated), T3 (uninfected+Amprolium), T4 (uninfected+purslane 400 µg/mL), T5 (Eimeria-infected+purslane 400 µg/mL), and T6 (Eimeria-infected+Amprolium). At 14 days old, infected groups were challenged with mixed Eimeria spp. oocysts. Results showed that infection (T2) significantly (P < 0.05) depressed growth performance (final weight: 145.2 g; FCR: 2.31; survival: 82%) compared with controls (182.5 g; FCR: 1.89; survival: 100%). Both treatments improved these parameters, with purslane (T5) achieving superior growth (179.8 g; FCR: 1.88; survival: 96%) compared to Amprolium (T6: 172.8 g; FCR: 1.96; survival: 92%). Infection compromised hematology (PCV: 24.5%; Hb: 8.1 g/dL), with better restoration in T5 (PCV: 33.9%; Hb: 11.6 g/dL) than T6 (30.8%; 10.5 g/dL). The stress indicator H/L ratio was elevated in T2 (0.90) and reduced by both treatments (T5: 0.53; T6: 0.54). Purslane extract showed superior hepatoprotective and lipid-modulating effects, with lower ALT, AST, and cholesterol levels than those observed with Amprolium treatment. Crucially, T5 upregulated MUC2 (1.65-fold) and β-defensin (2.20-fold) expression. In vitro assays confirmed potent antioxidant activity (86.7% DPPH scavenging) and moderate direct anticoccidial effects (73.6% inhibition of sporulation). Parasitologically, the Amprolium achieved higher oocyst reduction (84.2% vs. 60.6%), yet both treatments yielded excellent anticoccidial indices (T5: 185; T6: 170). Histopathological studies confirmed marked preservation of villi in both treated groups. In conclusion, a 400 µg/mL dose of purslane extract effectively alleviated coccidiosis through direct anticoccidial activity, antioxidant effects, immunomodulation, and gut barrier fortification, offering a promising natural alternative for sustainable poultry production.

Introduction

The global poultry industry, including the specialized Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) production sector, faces a persistent and costly challenge such as avian coccidiosis (El Bakrey et al., 2024). This parasitic disease, caused by several species of the genus Eimeria, is characterized by the invasion and replication of protozoa within the intestinal epithelial cells (El-Saadony et al., 2026). The resulting intracellular damage leads to severe clinical symptoms, including hemorrhagic enteritis, malabsorption syndrome, and increased susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections, such as necrotic enteritis (Hussain et al., 2021). As quails are increasingly utilized for both meat and egg production due to their rapid growth cycles and high nutritional value, the economic impact of coccidiosis in this species has become a focal point for veterinary research, particularly concerning the disruption of gut homeostasis and the subsequent decline in feed efficiency (Galamatis et al., 2025).

For decades, the primary strategy for managing coccidiosis has relied on the prophylactic and therapeutic use of synthetic anticoccidial agents and ionophores (Attia et al., 2025), while these chemicals have been effective in maintaining high production volumes, their prolonged use has led to two significant global concerns and the widespread emergence of drug-resistant Eimeria strains and the presence of chemical residues in poultry products (Dedousi et al., 2024). Resistance has been documented against almost all major classes of synthetic drugs, rendering many conventional treatments less effective and forcing producers to use higher dosages or complex "shuttle" programs furthermore, the global trend toward "antibiotic-free" (ABF) production, driven by consumer demand for organic and residue-free meat, has pressured industry to seek sustainable, bioactive alternatives that can provide similar protection without the risks associated with synthetic compounds (Adetunji et al., 2025).

Phytogenic feed additives (PFAs) have emerged as a leading candidate in this search for alternatives (Aminullah et al., 2025; Nurhayati et al., 2025). These plant-derived compounds, including essential oils, herbs, and botanical extracts, possess a diverse array of secondary metabolites, such as phenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids, that exhibit biological activities beneficial to avian health (Khanam et al., 2025; Okoye et al., 2025). Unlike synthetic drugs, which often have a single mode of action, PFAs typically act through multiple pathways, including direct antiparasitic effects, stimulation of digestive enzymes, and modulation of the host immune system (Xu et al., 2024). This multi-faceted approach not only helps control the parasite load but also supports the bird's overall physiological resilience during the stressful period of infection (Xu et al., 2024).

Portulaca oleracea, commonly known as purslane, is a prime example of a pharmacologically active botanical with significant potential in poultry nutrition (Sadeghi et al., 2016). Traditionally, purslane used in human medicine for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties also, purslane is uniquely rich in high-quality nutrients and bioactive molecules (Sadeghi et al., 2016; Aoudeh et al., 2024). It is one of the few plant sources that contain high levels of alpha-linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) and is packed with antioxidant vitamins (A, C, and E), glutathione, and various flavonoids, such as quercetin and kaempferol (Xu et al., 2024). In addition, these components are critical in mitigating the oxidative stress that occurs when Eimeria species invade the intestinal mucosa, the inflammatory response triggered by the parasite leads to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further damages the gut wall and impairs nutrient transport (Salem et al., 2022). By neutralizing these free radicals, purslane extract may preserve the integrity of the intestinal villi and maintain the surface area available for absorption (Galamatis et al., 2025).

The protective mechanism of Portulaca oleracea extends beyond simple antioxidant activity; it appears to play a vital role in fortifying the gut barrier (Sadeghi et al., 2016). The intestinal barrier is the first line of defense against pathogens, consisting of a physical mucus layer and specialized junctional proteins (Sadeghi et al., 2016). Research into intestinal health has highlighted the importance of mucins and antimicrobial peptides in preventing the attachment and penetration of Eimeria sporozoites (Xu et al., 2024). When these barriers are compromised, the bird enters a state of systemic inflammation, often reflected in altered hematological parameters (Hussain et al., 2021).

Moreover, the impact of coccidiosis often extends to the liver and metabolic health, as the bird diverts energy from growth to immune defense (Jespersen, 2023). Elevated levels of liver enzymes, such as aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), often indicate hepatic stress or damage during severe infections (Galamatis et al., 2025). Concurrently, lipid metabolism is frequently disrupted, leading to fluctuations in serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels, natural extracts that exhibit hepatoprotective and lipid-modulating effects could therefore offer a significant advantage over conventional drugs that may themselves place a metabolic burden on the liver (Jespersen, 2023; Galamatis et al., 2025).

Despite the promising attributes of Portulaca oleracea, there is a lack of comparative data on its efficacy against Eimeria challenges in Japanese quails, particularly compared with standard anticoccidial drug treatments. While previous studies have examined its use in chickens or swine (Xu et al., 2024), the specific physiological responses of quails, which have distinct metabolic rates and immune sensitivities, warrant dedicated investigation. There is a need to move beyond general observations of growth performance and delve into the molecular and histological changes in the gut microenvironment under the influence of purslane (Xu et al., 2024).

Botanical anticoccidials represent a promising, sustainable alternative to conventional anticoccidial drugs for controlling coccidiosis in poultry (Liu et al., 2023; Yu-Hao et al., 2025). These plant-derived products offer multiple advantages, including reduced resistance risk, lower toxicity, environmental safety, and additional health benefits beyond parasite control (Gul et al., 2024; Mba et al., 2025). The efficacy of botanical anticoccidials is derived from their diverse array of bioactive compounds including alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, terpenes, and phenolic compounds which exert their effects through multiple, integrated mechanisms (Chen et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2026). Key mechanisms include direct antiparasitic effects (inhibition of oocyst sporulation, sporozoite invasion, and parasite replication), modulation of host immunity, antioxidant activity, maintenance of gut barrier function, and regulation of gut microbiota. The multifunctional nature of these botanicals provides comprehensive protection against coccidiosis that is less susceptible to resistance development.

Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of a 400 µg/mL dose of Portulaca oleracea in Japanese quails challenged with a mixed Eimeria infection via examining a wide spectrum of parameters, including growth performance, hematological status, serum biochemistry, gene expression of gut barrier markers (MUC2 and βdefensin), and histopathological changes, the research aims to determine if this natural extract can serve as a viable, sustainable replacement for conventional anticoccidial programs. This investigation seeks to contribute to the development of sustainable poultry production strategies that prioritize animal welfare, environmental safety, and public health.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation and aqueous extraction

Fresh purslane (Portulaca oleracea) samples were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove dirt and surface contaminants. The samples were shade-dried at ambient temperature (25°C) for 7 days, then oven-dried at 45°C until a constant weight was achieved. The dried plant material was ground into a fine powder using a mechanical grinder, passed through a 40-mesh sieve, and stored in airtight amber glass containers at 4°C until further analysis.

For proximate composition analysis, the dried powder was used directly without any further extraction. For phytochemical analysis, an aqueous extract was prepared as follows: Exactly 10 g of the dried purslane powder was macerated with 100 mL of distilled water in a conical flask. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 60°C for 30 minutes with intermittent stirring, then allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 hours with occasional shaking. The mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the residue was re-extracted twice with 50 mL of distilled water under the same conditions. The combined filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 50°C and finally lyophilized to obtain a dry aqueous extract, which was reconstituted in distilled water for the respective assays. All extractions and determinations were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

The ash content was 12.09% (Table 1). Crude Protein was estimated by the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 990.03). Total nitrogen was multiplied by a conversion factor of 6.25. Phytochemical compounds analysis

All phytochemical assays were performed using the aqueous extract prepared in Section 2.1. The findings were expressed per dried sample weight. Total Alkaloids were determined spectrophotometrically using Dragendorff's reagent. An aliquot of the aqueous extract was acidified with 2 N HCl and precipitated with Dragendorff's solution. The precipitate was dissolved in distilled water, and absorbance of the bismuth complex was measured at 435 nm (Sreevidya and Mehrotra, 2003). Caffeine was used as the standard. The alkaloid content in purslane was 194.17 ± 3.97 mg/100 g DW.

Total flavonoids (TFs) were determined by the aluminum chloride (AlCl₃) colorimetric assay. An aliquot of the extract was mixed with 10% AlCl₃ and 1 M potassium acetate, incubated for 30 minutes, and absorbance was read at 420 nm (Shraim et al., 2021). Quercetin was used as the standard. the TFs content was 111.12 ± 1.06 mg/100 g DW (Table 1).

Total Phenolic Compounds were determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) method. An aliquot of the extract was mixed with FC reagent and 7.5% sodium carbonate, incubated in the dark for 90 minutes, and absorbance was measured at 725 nm (Perez et al., 2023). Gallic acid was used as the standard. The TPC was 35.20 ± 0.05 mg/g (Table 1).

Total Glycosides were evaluated colorimetrically using the anthrone-sugar method after acid hydrolysis. The aqueous extract was hydrolyzed with 2 N HCl, neutralized, and the released sugars were quantified using anthrone reagent at 620 nm (Leng et al., 2016). Glucose was used as the standard. The total glycoside content in the purslane extract was 37.40 ± 4.98 µg/g (Table 1).

The 400 µg/mL dose was selected based on previous studies in which concentrations of 100, 200, 400, and 800 µg/mL were evaluated. The 400 µg/mL concentration showed the optimal balance between anticoccidial efficacy and safety, consistent with previous reports by Sadeghi et al. (2016) and Habibian et al. (2025), who demonstrated that moderate doses of purslane extract (300-500 µg/mL) provide maximum therapeutic benefits without adverse effects.

In vitro antioxidant activity assays

The antioxidant activity of purslane extract was evaluated using standard in vitro assays (Habibian et al., 2020). The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging activity was determined by mixing the extract at different concentrations (50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) with a methanolic DPPH solution. After incubation in the dark for 30-60 minutes, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm, and the percentage of inhibition was calculated. The ABTS radical scavenging activity was assessed by generating ABTS radical cations through reaction with potassium persulfate, followed by dilution to an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Extract samples were added, and after incubation, the decrease in absorbance was measured. Ascorbic acid was used as a positive control in both assays. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated from the dose-response curves.

In vitro anticoccidial activity assay

The direct anticoccidial activity of purslane extract was evaluated using the oocyst sporulation inhibition assay (Bǎieş et al., 2023; Fatemi et al., 2015). Unsporulated Eimeria oocysts were collected from the feces of infected birds and purified using standard sedimentation and flotation techniques. The oocysts were incubated with different concentrations of purslane extract (50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) in petri dishes containing 2.5% potassium dichromate solution at 28°C for 48-72 hours to allow sporulation. Amprolium (100 µg/mL) was used as a reference anticoccidial drug. The percentage of sporulation inhibition was calculated by counting sporulated and unsporulated oocysts under a light microscope.

Birds, experimental design, and management

A total of 300 one-day-old Japanese quails (Coturnix japonica) were obtained from a commercial hatchery and randomly allocated into six experimental groups (n = 50 per group, with 5 replicates). The treatment groups were designated as follows: T1 (Control, non-infected, untreated), T2 (Eimeria-infected, untreated), T3 (Uninfected + Amprolium for 5 days), T4 (Uninfected + Purslane Extract at 400 µg/mL in drinking water throughout the experimental period), T5 (Infected + Purslane Extract at 400 µg/mL water for 5 days), and T6 (Infected + Amprolium for 5 days). Birds were housed in battery brooders under standard management conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and ventilation. A basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of growing quails, in accordance with established guidelines (Table 2). The diet did not include any anticoccidial drugs. Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period from day 1 to day 28. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the animal welfare guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee. Anticoccidial drug

Amprolium is a synthetic coccidiostat widely utilized in poultry production to combat Eimeria species by acting as a structural analogue of thiamine. Chemically identified as 1-[(4-amino-2-propylpyrimidin-5-yl) methyl]-2-methylpyridinium chloride, it inhibits the active transport of thiamine across the parasite's cell membrane. Ten mg of Amprolium hydrochloride powder and dissolved it in 10 mL of distilled water or Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) to create a 1 mg/mL (1000 µg/mL) stock solution; since Amprolium is highly water-soluble, simple vortexing should suffice for complete dissolution. From this stock, perform a 1:10 dilution by transferring 1 mL of the stock solution into a new container and adding 9 mL of the solvent, resulting in a final concentration of 100 µg/mL. For the best experimental results, the solution has been prepared fresh and used in drinking water for 5 days after the onset of clinical signs.

Parasite challenge and infection protocol

At 14 days of age, all birds in groups T2, T5, and T6 were orally challenged with Eimeria bateri, E. uzura, and E. dispersa mixed inoculum containing sporulated oocysts of pathogenic species affecting quails (Elmorsy et al., 2021). The inoculum was prepared from stock cultures maintained in the laboratory, and each bird received a predetermined number of sporulated oocysts suspended in 0.5 mL of distilled water via oral gavage. Groups T1, T3, and T4 received 0.5 mL of sterile distilled water as a sham control. The infection was allowed to establish, and observations were recorded during the post-infection period.

Oocyst count and anticoccidial index

Fecal samples were collected from each replicate during the peak shedding period (day 8 post-challenge) for oocyst enumeration. The number of oocysts per gram (OPG) of feces was determined using the McMaster counting chamber technique with saturated sodium chloride solution. The oocyst index was calculated based on the OPG values using a 0-4 scale: 0 = 0 OPG, 1 = 1-10,000 OPG, 2 = 10,000-50,000 OPG, 3 = 50,000-100,000 OPG, and 4 = >100,000 OPG, as described by Pajić et al. (2018). The reduction in oocyst shedding (%) was calculated relative to the infected untreated control group (T2). The Anticoccidial Index (ACI) was calculated using the standard formula: ACI = (relative body weight gain + survival rate), (lesion score + oocyst index), where values >180 indicate excellent efficacy, 160-180 indicate good efficacy, and <120 indicate poor efficacy (Pajić et al., 2018; Habibi et al., 2022).

Growth performance and survival rate

Growth performance parameters were evaluated at weekly intervals throughout the study period. Individual body weights were recorded at day 14 (challenge day) and day 28 (end of experiment). Body weight gain (BWG) was calculated as the difference between final and initial body weights. Feed intake (FI) was recorded per replicate by weighing the offered and leftover feed. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ratio of total feed intake to total body weight gain. Mortality was recorded daily, and the survival rate (%) was calculated for each group at the end of the experiment.

Hematological parameters

At the end of the experiment (day 28), blood samples were ethically collected from the wing vein of birds in each group into two types of tubes: Ethylene Diamine Tetraacetic Acid (EDTA)- treated tubes for hematological analysis and plain tubes without anticoagulant for serum biochemistry. Hematological parameters, including red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, packed cell volume (PCV), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (Ogbu and Onuh, 2015). White blood cell (WBC) counts and differential leukocyte counts (heterophils and lymphocytes) were determined manually from blood smears stained with Giemsa, and the heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio was calculated as an indicator of stress.

Serum biochemical parameters

Blood samples collected in plain tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes to separate serum. Serum biochemical parameters including total protein (TP), albumin, globulin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), creatinine, uric acid, cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) were analyzed using commercial diagnostic kits following the manufacturer's protocols on a semi-automatic biochemistry analyzer.

Gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Jejunal tissue samples were collected at day 28, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer's instructions (Abdel-Kafy et al., 2026). The quantity and purity of RNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green master mix on a real-time PCR detection system with specific primers for Mucin-2 (MUC2) and β-defensin genes. The housekeeping gene β-actin was used as an internal control for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method and expressed as fold change relative to the control group (T1).

Histopathological examination and lesion scoring

Jejunal tissue samples were collected from 3 birds/ replicate after the end of the treatment (5 days post-onset of clinical signs) and then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin or Davidson's solution for at least 24 hours (Assis et al., 2010). The fixed tissues were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of 5 µm thickness were cut using a microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic examination (Assis et al., 2010). Additionally, Alcian Blue staining was performed to visualize goblet cells where required. Histopathological changes, including villus height, crypt depth, epithelial integrity, and inflammatory cell infiltration, were evaluated under a light microscope equipped with a digital camera. Villus height was measured using image analysis software. The presence of Eimeria developmental stages (schizonts and merozoites) was recorded, and lesion scoring was performed using a standardized scoring system (0-4 scale) based on the severity of pathological changes (Belote et al., 2019).

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to parametric analysis, data were tested for normality of distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. Since all assumptions were satisfied, statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons among the six treatment groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. The individual bird served as the experimental unit for all parameters measured, except for feed intake and feed conversion ratio, where the replicate pen served as the experimental unit. Exact P‑values are reported for significant differences where applicable. Correlation analysis between selected parameters was performed using Pearson's correlation coefficient.

Results

In vitro antioxidant and anticoccidial activity

Fig. 1, Fig. 2 establish the mechanistic basis for why purslane extract exhibits behavior different from that of the anticoccidial drug in vitro. Fig. 1 illustrates the robust, dose-dependent antioxidant capacity of purslane extract in both DPPH and ABTS assays. At 400 µg/mL, the extract exhibited 86.7% DPPH and 89.3% ABTS scavenging, approaching but not surpassing those of the synthetic antioxidant ascorbic acid (95.3% and 96.1%, respectively). This potent antioxidant activity elucidates the systemic advantages observed in vivo, including reduced liver stress (lower ALT/AST levels in T5 relative to T2) and improved lipid profiles, as oxidative damage is a significant component of coccidiosis pathology. Fig. 2 demonstrates the direct anticoccidial efficacy of purslane extract, characterized by inhibition of oocyst sporulation. At 400 µg/mL, the extract inhibited sporulation by 73.6%, a noteworthy figure, though lower than the 94.5% inhibition achieved by the reference anticoccidial drug, Amprolium, at 100 µg/mL. The 21% disparity in the direct anticoccidial activity between purslane and the reference drug in vitro correlates notably with the 23.6% difference in oocyst reduction observed in vivo. This correlation substantiates that the anticoccidial effect of purslane extract observed in live birds is at least partially attributable to its direct activity against the parasite. Eimeria infection parameters and oocyst output

Table 3 directly measures the anticoccidial effectiveness of both treatments against the parasite itself. The untreated infected group (T2) shed large numbers of oocysts (285.4 × 10³ OPG), had a high lesion score (3.5), and a very low Anticoccidial Index (ACI) of 85, indicating uncontrolled infection. The purslane extract treatment (T5) resulted in a significant 60.6% reduction in oocyst shedding (down to 112.3 × 10³ OPG), a lesion score of 0.8, and a "Good" ACI of 185. The Amprolium treatment (T6) showed superior direct anticoccidial activity, reducing oocyst shedding by 84.2% (to 45.2 × 10³ OPG) and achieving an excellent ACI of 170. The 23.6% difference in oocyst reduction between purslane extract and the Amprolium directly explains the anticoccidial drug modest advantage in parasite clearance. However, the ACI of 185 for purslane extract is clinically meaningful and falls within the range considered excellent for anticoccidial products. Notably, the reduction in oocyst shedding by the purslane extract (T5) is sufficient to break the infection cycle and reduce environmental contamination, which is vital for flock health management. Hematological parameters

The comprehensive hematological profile in Table 5 shows the systemic effects of coccidiosis and how both treatments help recovery. The infected untreated group (T2) exhibited typical signs of anemia caused by coccidiosis, with significantly lower Red Blood Cell (RBC) count (2.45 × 10⁶/µL), Hemoglobin (Hb) level (8.1 g/dL), and packed cell volume (PCV) (24.5%), due to intestinal bleeding from the parasite. The red cell indices (MCV, MCH, & MCHC) remained consistent across all groups, indicating normocytic and normochromic anemia, typical of blood loss from intestinal bleeding. The white blood cell profile offers important insights into inflammation and stress responses. Both treatments markedly reduced this stress response, with T5 reaching an H/L ratio of 0.53 and T6 reaching 0.54. Jejunal gene expression

Fig. 3 displays the gene expression data for key mucosal defense genes, revealing the molecular mechanisms behind the protective effects of the treatments. Infection alone (T2) downregulated the protective Mucin-2 (MUC2) gene (0.55-fold relative to control), which forms the protective mucus barrier, while moderately increasing the antimicrobial peptide β-defensin (1.80-fold) as a natural defense response. Both treatments significantly boosted the expression of these protective genes. The purslane extract treatment (T5) achieved the highest MUC2 upregulation (1.65-fold) among all infected groups, indicating a strong ability to reinforce the physical gut barrier. The Amprolium treatment (T6) also increased MUC2 expression (1.25-fold), but to a lesser degree than purslane. For β-defensin, Amprolium treatment (T6) resulted in the greatest upregulation (2.45-fold), reflecting its potent antimicrobial activity. The purslane extract (T5) also strongly stimulated β-defensin (2.20-fold), nearing the level achieved by the anticoccidial drug. Most notably, in uninfected birds, purslane extract alone (T4) enhanced both MUC2 (1.45-fold) and β-defensin (1.35-fold) expression above baseline, suggesting it proactively strengthens the intestinal barrier and innate immunity, a preventive effect not provided by anticoccidial drugs. Discussion

The poultry industry, particularly the production of Japanese quails, is currently navigating a paradigm shift necessitated by the global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (Sana et al., 2025). For decades, the management of avian coccidiosis has been synonymous with the prophylactic use of ionophores and synthetic chemicals (Ahmad et al., 2023; El-Sherry et al., 2025). However, the biological plasticity of Eimeria species has led to the widespread emergence of resistant strains, rendering many traditional treatments ineffective (Hussain et al., 2021; Abbas et al., 2026).

Furthermore, the bioaccumulation of drug residues in quail meat and eggs poses significant public health risks, fueling stringent regulations and consumer demand for residue-free products (Dedousi et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025). In this context, the search for safe, natural alternatives is no longer a luxury but a veterinary necessity. Portulaca oleracea represents a biological alternative; unlike synthetic drugs that target single metabolic pathways, purslane offers a multi-component defense strategy. The findings of the present study demonstrate that dietary supplementation with purslane extract at 400 µg/mL (T5) significantly alleviated the detrimental effects of coccidiosis in Japanese quails, with efficacy comparable to, and in several parameters exceeding, that of conventional Amprolium treatment (T6). These results underscore the potential of purslane as a multifunctional natural alternative that addresses both the parasite and the pathophysiological consequences of infection. Studies by Ntsongota et al. (2025) emphasize that phytogenic additives can effectively bridge the gap between production efficiency and food safety, providing a holistic approach to managing intestinal protozoa without the selective pressure that drives drug resistance.

The therapeutic potential of Portulaca oleracea extract lies in its unique ability to simultaneously attack the parasite and protect the host (Kumar et al., 2022; Kureshi et al., 2025). Eimeria sporozoites and merozoites rupture intestinal epithelial cells, triggering an inflammatory cascade that results in the massive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (López-Osorio et al., 2020). This oxidative burst leads to lipid peroxidation, damaging the very tissues required for nutrient absorption. Purslane is uniquely well-equipped to handle this challenge due to its high concentrations of α-linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid), glutathione, and flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol. These compounds act as high-efficiency radical scavengers, neutralizing ROS before they can cause irreversible damage to the mucosal architecture (Xu et al., 2024). The in vitro results corroborate this, demonstrating that purslane extract exhibits potent dose-dependent antioxidant activity, achieving 86.7% DPPH radical scavenging at 400 µg/mL (Fig. 1). This antioxidant capacity explains the superior preservation of intestinal architecture and the enhanced recovery of hematological parameters observed in the purslane-treated group (T5) compared to the Amprolium-treated group (T6).

Simultaneously, the extract exerts a direct anticoccidial effect, the alkaloids and saponins found in purslane are known to interact with the sterols in the cell membranes of Eimeria oocysts (Bǎieş et al., 2023). This interaction can disrupt membrane permeability, leading to the leakage of internal components and the parasite's death before it can complete sporulation. Evidence from Galamatis et al. (2025) suggests that such phytobiotic mixtures can significantly reduce the "oocyst per gram" (OPG) count in feces, effectively breaking the cycle of reinfection within the flock. Our in vitro sporulation inhibition assay (Fig. 2) confirmed that purslane extract at 400 µg/mL achieved 73.6% inhibition, demonstrating a moderate but substantial direct effect against the parasite. This direct activity translated to a 60.6% reduction in oocyst shedding in vivo (T5, Table 3), which, while lower than the Amprolium's 84.2% reduction, was sufficient to achieve an Anticoccidial Index (ACI) of 185. By combining these direct parasiticidal actions with systemic antioxidant support, purslane provides a defense that is absent in conventional anticoccidial drug treatments (Hussain et al., 2021).

The efficacy of purslane as an antioxidant and anticoccidial agent is well-documented in recent literature. For instance, Xu et al. (2024) investigated the impact of purslane on immune function and oxidative status, finding that it significantly upregulated total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) while lowering MDA levels, a key marker of lipid peroxidation. This is particularly relevant in quails, which have high metabolic rates and are highly susceptible to oxidative stress during infection (Abd El-Ghany, 2019). Our findings align with these observations, as birds treated with purslane (T5) showed significantly lower liver enzyme levels (ALT, AST, ALP) than those treated with Amproliums (T6), indicating reduced oxidative damage to hepatocytes.

Furthermore, Galamatis et al. (2025) demonstrated that birds receiving plant-derived additives showed significantly improved lesion scores in the duodenum, jejunum, and caeca compared to untreated infected groups. Their research highlighted that the bioactive compounds in these extracts could achieve an ACI comparable to, or even exceeding, that of synthetic drugs such as monensin or amprolium. In our study, purslane extract achieved an ACI of 185, which falls within the category and actually exceeds the anticoccidial drug's ACI of 170 (classified as "Good"). This paradoxical finding, where purslane achieves a higher ACI despite lower direct parasite killing, reflects the multifactorial nature of the ACI calculation, which incorporates growth performance and survival. The superior growth performance of T5 (179.8 g final weight vs. T6′s 172.8 g) contributed significantly to this outcome. Also, purslane achieved a higher ACI (185) compared to Amprolium (170) despite lower oocyst reduction (60.6% vs. 84.2%) and that could be contributed to the antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and gut barrier-strengthening effects of purslane contributed to better overall health and growth, compensating for its slightly lower direct parasiticidal activity in addition to purslane enhanced the bird's ability to cope with infection through multiple pathways, resulting in superior nutrient utilization and weight gain, which elevated the ACI score.

Additionally, Štrbac et al. (2023) confirmed that botanical extracts not only inhibit parasite development but also stimulate the host's innate immunity, providing a secondary layer of protection that synthetic chemicals cannot. Our gene expression data (Fig. 3) strongly support this conclusion. Purslane extract (T5) achieved the highest MUC2 upregulation (1.65-fold) among all infected groups, indicating enhanced mucus production and barrier function, while also strongly stimulating β-defensin expression (2.20-fold). The Amprolium (T6) achieved even higher β-defensin expression (2.45-fold), reflecting its potent direct antimicrobial action, but failed to match purslane's effect on the physical barrier. This distinction is crucial: anticoccidial drugs primarily kill the pathogen, while purslane both kills the pathogen and fortifies the host's defenses, creating a more resilient intestinal environment.

Coccidiosis is a systemic disease that manifests most visibly as a decline in growth performance and an alteration in blood chemistry (AL-Saegh, 2018; Bedford and Apajalahti, 2022; Abbas et al., 2025). The infection forces the bird to divert energy from muscle development to mount an immune response (Jespersen, 2023; Aljohani and Zaman, 2024). Purslane extract effectively reverses this trend by maintaining gut health, the extract ensures that the feed conversion ratio (FCR) remains optimized, allowing quails to achieve their genetic potential for growth despite the presence of the parasite (Adetunji et al., 2025). In our study, T5 achieved an FCR of 1.88, significantly better than T6′s 1.96 and approaching the uninfected control's 1.89. The superior growth performance of T5 (179.8 g) compared to T6 (172.8 g) suggests that the additional benefits of purslane, including antioxidant protection, enhanced barrier function, and metabolic support, translated into more efficient nutrient utilization (Srivastava et al., 2023). This finding has significant economic implications for producers, as improved FCR directly reduces feed costs, which constitute the largest expense in poultry production.

From a hematological perspective, Eimeria causes significant blood loss via intestinal hemorrhage, leading to anemia characterized by a drop in packed cell volume (PCV) and hemoglobin (Hb) (Abdalsalam et al., 2026). Purslane is rich in essential minerals, including iron and magnesium, and vitamins like vitamin C, which play a direct role in erythropoiesis and blood stabilization (Desta et al., 2020). Studies have shown that birds treated with purslane maintain significantly higher PCV and Hb levels, indicating reduced internal bleeding and improved oxygen transport (Faryadi et al., 2020; Habibian et al., 2025). Our results confirmed this, with T5 achieving PCV of 33.9% and Hb of 11.6 g/dL, compared to T6′s 30.8% and 10.5 g/dL. The H/L ratio, a well-established stress indicator, was markedly elevated in T2 (0.90) and significantly reduced by both treatments, with T5 achieving 0.53 and T6 achieving 0.54. The uninfected purslane group (T4) achieved the lowest H/L ratio (0.38), suggesting that purslane has intrinsic anti-stress properties that may benefit birds even in the absence of infection.

Biochemically, the extract provides vital hepatoprotection. The liver often suffers from collateral damage during coccidiosis due to systemic inflammation and toxin release (Xu et al., 2025). Purslane's ability to stabilize hepatocyte membranes is reflected in lower serum ALT and AST levels (Xu et al., 2024). The results showed that T5 achieved lower ALT (22.5 vs. 24.1 U/L), AST (108 vs. 115 U/L), and ALP (275 vs. 295 U/L) than T6, indicating superior hepatoprotection. Furthermore, its lipid-modulating effects help maintain cholesterol and triglyceride balance, ensuring that the bird's metabolic health is preserved during the peak of the infection (Dedousi et al., 2024). The uninfected purslane group (T4) demonstrated remarkable hypolipidemic properties, achieving the lowest cholesterol (142 mg/dL), the lowest LDL (72 mg/dL), the highest HDL (52 mg/dL), and the lowest triglycerides (98 mg/dL) among all groups. This confirms that purslane extract possesses intrinsic cholesterol-lowering activity that the anticoccidial drug completely lacks, representing a unique therapeutic advantage (Christyaningsih et al., 2024).

The most profound effect of purslane may occur at the genetic and microscopic levels. The intestinal barrier is the primary battlefield in coccidiosis (Lun et al., 2025). A healthy barrier consists of a robust mucus layer and tight junction proteins that prevent the translocation of pathogens (Capaldo et al., 2017). This study highlights the upregulation of MUC2 and β-defensins, which are critical components of this defense. MUC2 encodes the major gel-forming mucin of the intestinal mucus, which acts as a physical lubricant and a decoy for pathogens, also, β-defensins are innate antimicrobial peptides that can directly kill sporozoites (El-Dirany et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2025).

Xu et al. (2024) confirmed that purslane supplementation enhances the expression of these defensive genes, creating a more hostile environment for Eimeria colonization. Our data showed that T5 achieved the highest MUC2 expression (1.65-fold) among all infected groups, indicating that purslane actively stimulates mucus production. This physical barrier makes it more difficult for sporozoites to reach and invade epithelial cells, reducing the establishment of infection (Ménard, 2001). The previous observation also, could be contributed to the enhanced mucus barrier (MUC2) and antimicrobial peptide production (β-defensin) create a hostile environment for parasite invasion, which explains the reduced lesion scores and preserved villus height despite moderate direct anticoccidial activity.

These genetic changes are corroborated by histopathological findings (Fig. 4). In infected, untreated quails (T2), the intestinal villi are typically blunted, fused, or completely destroyed (villus height: 298.7 µm), drastically reducing the surface area for nutrient absorption. However, the inclusion of purslane extract (T5) preserved villus architecture (485.3 µm), achieving a villus height slightly greater than that of the Amprolium-treated group (T6: 475.6 µm). Galamatis et al. (2025) consistently show that birds supplemented with high-quality phytogenics maintain a more intact mucosal architecture. This histological preservation is the mechanical reason behind the improved growth performance; by protecting the "machinery" of the gut, purslane ensures that every gram of feed is effectively utilized, even under the stress of a mixed Eimeria challenge. The near-complete absence of Eimeria stages in T5 sections, despite only a 60.6% reduction in oocysts, suggests that the enhanced mucus barrier and immune response may trap and eliminate parasites before they can complete their life cycle.

The findings of this study have significant implications for the poultry industry. The superior performance of purslane extract (T5) compared to Amprolium treatment (T6) in multiple parameters, including growth, FCR, hematological restoration, hepatoprotection, and lipid modulation, positions it as a viable alternative for producers seeking to reduce anticoccidial drug use. The "Excellent" ACI of 185 achieved by T5 demonstrates that natural products can match or exceed the efficacy of conventional drugs when evaluated using comprehensive metrics that consider both parasite control and host performance. Furthermore, the absence of mortality or adverse effects in the uninfected purslane group (T4) confirms the extract's safety, while the enhanced performance in this group suggests potential applications as a growth promoter in non-infected flocks.

The multifunctional mechanism of purslane, combining direct anticoccidial activity, potent antioxidant effects, immunomodulation, gut barrier fortification, and metabolic support, addresses the limitations of single-target synthetic drugs (Qing et al., 2025). By reducing the selective pressure for resistance through multiple concurrent mechanisms, purslane may help preserve the efficacy of existing anticoccidials while providing a sustainable tool for integrated parasite management. The reduction in oocyst shedding (60.6%) achieved by T5 is particularly valuable for breaking the cycle of reinfection within flocks, reducing environmental contamination, and minimizing the need for therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

This study indicates that dietary supplementation with Portulaca oleracea extract is a promising natural strategy for controlling coccidiosis in Japanese quails. Its efficacy derives from a multifunctional mode of action, integrating anticoccidial, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and gut barrier protective effects. Unlike conventional drugs targeting single pathways, purslane exerts a holistic response by mitigating parasite burden and associated oxidative stress, inflammation, metabolic imbalance, and intestinal dysfunction. Treated birds exhibited improved growth performance, hematological recovery, hepatoprotection, and lipid regulation. Enhanced expression of mucosal defense genes and preservation of intestinal architecture further indicate strengthened host immunity and barrier integrity. This dual mechanism reduces pressure of resistance and supports its role in sustainable parasite management. Additionally, its safety and growth-promoting effects in uninfected birds highlight its utility as both a preventive and therapeutic feed additive. By decreasing oocyst shedding and environmental contamination, purslane contributes to breaking reinfection cycles. In the context of antimicrobial resistance and demand for residue-free production, it represents a viable alternative to conventional anticoccidials. Standardization of bioactive compounds, optimization of dosing strategies, and validation under commercial conditions remain necessary. Overall, Portulaca oleracea shows strong potential as a key component of integrated, drug-free parasite control systems in poultry production.
