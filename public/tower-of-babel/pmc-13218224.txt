SDG progress and the challenge of inclusive wealth sustainability
Authors: Yogi Sugiawan, Shunsuke Managi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study investigates whether progress toward the sustainable development goals (SDGs) translates into long-term sustainability, measured through changes in inclusive wealth. Using a balanced panel of 90 countries (2000–2019), we model the relationship between all 17 SDGs and per-capita produced, human, and natural capital within the inclusive wealth index framework. We apply Cubist regression to capture nonlinear and heterogeneous effects, and project SDG and wealth trajectories to 2040 under business-as-usual, pessimistic, and accelerated scenarios. Results show that SDG progress is positively associated with inclusive wealth, largely driven by gains in produced and human capital, but often accompanied by declines in natural capital. Accelerated SDG progress yields the highest projected wealth gains, though outcomes vary substantially across countries and are not uniformly sustainable. These findings validate the SDGs’ universality and indivisibility while highlighting the need to complement indicator-based monitoring with wealth-based metrics to better align development pathways with long-term sustainability.

Introduction

The sustainable development goals (SDGs) have reshaped the global development trajectory by guiding nations to align economic growth with social advancement while preserving the environment. Although the SDG framework aspires to achieve inclusive growth, progress of social and ecological goals consistently falls behind the pace of economic development.1,2 Moreover, as highlighted by Bigerna and Micheli,3 several goals are experiencing persistent divergence and are unlikely to align across nations. This enduring gap seems inevitable, as each nation has unique strengths and weaknesses. These differences foster specialized development paths that undermine the integration and indivisibility of the SDGs.4,5 Major challenges often stem from institutional, financial, social, and environmental factors (see, for instance, Barua,6 Biermann et al; ,7 Filho et al.8; Hole et al.9; Liu et al.10 and Singh et al.11). The most notable barrier, however, may come from the inherent design of the SDGs. The silos of the SDGs create unwanted trade-offs among goals, where advancing one may inadvertently hinder another.12,13,14,15,16,17 Nevertheless, these challenges do not dismiss the pivotal role of the SDG framework in providing a baseline for quantitative assessment of sustainable well-being.18

The SDGs represent a complex framework, comprising 17 universal goals which are further divided into 169 targets, and then measured by 234 unique indicators. The complexity of the SDG framework is not beyond expectation, given that the framework aims to measure something as inherently multifaceted as well-being, a notion that cannot be captured by simple metrics.19,20,21,22,23 Such complexity poses significant challenges for tracking, evaluating, and conducting comparative assessments of national progress toward sustainable well-being. This necessitates the development of composite tools,24 such as the SDG Index, which aim to consolidate a vast array of indicators into more straightforward and interpretable scores.25 The simplicity offered by the SDG Index has contributed to its widespread adoption as both a benchmarking tool and an advocacy instrument to promote national accountability and policy alignment with the 2030 agenda (see, for instance, Zhang et al.,26 Ma et al.,5 Xing et al; ,27 and Moinuddin and Olsen28). However, it is worth noting that the simplicity of the SDG index also creates its own limitations. This restricts the interpretive power of the index and prevents it from delving into the root causes of issues or properly evaluating the impact of particular policies.29,30

While the SDG Index has proven valuable in simplifying the complexity of the SDG framework and fostering accountability, its predominantly retrospective orientation places strict constraints on policymaking. The index is mainly anchored in the past: it draws on historical data to provide diagnostic indications of where countries are at, but offers little guidance as to whether current trend lines are sufficient for addressing emerging challenges or managing dynamic interactions among goals. This backward-looking nature limits its value for anticipatory policy formulation and evaluation of what current development strategies might presage. Hence, forecasting future SDG progress serves a critical diagnostic function. First, it allows the early identification of divergences between observed trends and planned development trajectories. Second, it raises the prospect of stagnation or backsliding and is also a reference point for judging whether short-run policy momentum coheres with deeper objectives. The forward-looking role of SDG projections has received increasing attention in the literature on sustainability foresight and system transitions.31,32,33 However, projections of SDG indicators alone remain insufficient for evaluating sustainability, as improvements in indicators can coexist with structural erosion of the economy’s productive base.34,35

In response, a growing array of innovative tools has emerged to complement the SDGs—see, for instance, Biggeri et al.,30 Du et al.,36 and Lozano and Saavedra-Nieve37—particularly to address the inherent trade-offs and synergies among goals. Among these, Dasgupta et al.38 propose the inclusive wealth index (IWI), a novel framework for tracking sustainability grounded in the concept of wealth accumulation. Reflecting the paradigm shift advocated by the SDGs, the IWI also encourages movement beyond conventional metrics like gross domestic product, a widely used development indicator that often overlooks critical aspects of environmental degradation and the depletion of natural resources.19,39 The IWI comprises three principal components of wealth—produced, human, and natural capital—representing the economic, social, and environmental dimensions, respectively. In alignment with the SDGs, the IWI adopts a weak sustainability framework, permitting the substitutability among wealth components provided that the aggregate level of inclusive wealth is maintained. By focusing on the evolution of these capital stocks, wealth-based frameworks offer a forward-looking perspective on sustainable development by capturing a society’s ability to sustain and enhance well-being within and across generations.21,40

Within this broader context, multiple empirical studies have investigated the link between SDG performance and long-term sustainability outcomes. Findings from cross-country and regional analyses consistently suggest that progress on the SDGs does not translate into sustainability in a linear or uniform manner, but instead, involves threshold effects and interaction structures that are difficult to capture using conventional regression techniques.31,34,35,36 Recent advances in machine learning (ML) methods have further enabled the modeling of these complex relationships. For example, Sugiawan et al.41 show that SDG scores are significant predictors of changes in multiple wealth components, revealing both reinforcing and offsetting effects across produced, human, and natural capital. Similar patterns of heterogeneity and trade-offs are also found in previous empirical work linking SDG performance to long-term development capacity.30,34,35 Collectively, this literature underscores the need for integrated analytical frameworks that connect SDG monitoring with forward-looking, wealth-based assessments of sustainability.

This study addresses a critical gap in the sustainability literature by integrating forward-looking SDG projections with a wealth-based assessment of sustainability. We offer two core contributions. First, we empirically establish a direct, quantitative link between progress on the SDGs and changes in disaggregated wealth components within the IWI framework, which reflect the social, economic, and environmental dimensions of society. Second, we empirically investigate whether progress toward the SDGs constitutes a sufficient condition for achieving true sustainability. This is accomplished by seeking empirical evidence to validate the SDGs’ defining characteristics, particularly their universality, indivisibility, and inclusivity. For these purposes, we propose and implement an SDG-wealth model that forecasts the accumulation of wealth components—natural, human, and produced capital—until 2040. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a nonparametric ML approach to directly link future SDG progress to projected wealth accumulation at both aggregate and disaggregate levels. Unlike the previous study of Sugiawan et al.,41 our analysis moves beyond a simple correlation, identifying which specific forms of capital respond most significantly to progress on individual SDGs. This provides a novel, evidence-based foundation for policymakers to strategically prioritize interventions that maximize comprehensive wealth accumulation. Furthermore, by employing three distinct future scenarios for SDG progress—a business-as-usual (BAU) trajectory, an accelerated (ACC) progress pathway, and a pessimistic (PES) outlook—our model rigorously evaluates the long-term sustainability of diverse development pathways. Although existing studies have forecasted SDG progress through either individual indicators (e.g., Friedman et al.31) or by using composite indices (e.g., Chenary et al.42 and Xu et al.43), they fail to integrate these projections with a holistic measure of sustainability. Our study, therefore, offers a more comprehensive analysis that not only assesses the potential for achieving alignment among economic, social, and environmental goals but also provides an empirical basis for assessing whether current global efforts are on a trajectory to meet genuine sustainability targets.

Results

SDG performance trajectories under alternative pathways

Figure 1 reports projections of the SDG Index to 2040, computed as the average of model-based forecasts across all 17 goals. We take 2019 as the baseline year, consistent with the most recent availability of the IWI data, thereby enabling a coherent comparison between SDG progress and long-run sustainability trajectories. We use three different scenarios to illustrate potential future trajectories, i.e., BAU, PES, and ACC scenarios. The BAU scenario extends current trends and yields only modest progress with heterogeneous outcomes across goals. The PES scenario forecasts the SDG scores along a more adverse pathway, where progress slows further, or reversals intensify relative to BAU. Post-estimation diagnostics confirm that our forecasts are well specified and robust. As reported in Table S1 (supplemental information), the models generally yield low values for the mean error (ME), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean percentage error (MPE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), with the mean absolute scaled error (MASE) consistently below 1.5. Residual diagnostics further suggest that systematic patterns such as trends and autocorrelation have been effectively removed. Consistent with earlier studies by Moallemi et al.,32 Chenary et al.,42 and Liu et al.,10 our results point to a challenging outlook for the full realization of the 2030 agenda. While incremental progress is projected for many countries, no country is expected to fully achieve all SDGs by 2030, nor even by 2040 under any of the scenarios considered.

Our forecast highlights broader disparities in resources and institutional capacities, reflected in substantial score gaps across income levels, between G20 and non-G20 countries, OECD and non-OECD members, as well as both across and within continents. Even as global sustainability scores rise toward 2040, a persistent divide between high- and low-income nations remains a stubborn reality across all scenarios. While the ACC scenario could narrow the 24-point wealth gap to 17, a PES outlook threatens to leave the world’s most vulnerable regions in near-total stagnation. G20 countries and OECD members consistently achieve higher scores than their peers, reflecting stronger institutional frameworks and resource bases. Under the BAU scenario, the gap between G20 and non-G20 countries contracts to roughly 16 points, while the ACC pathway reduces it further to about 13 points. The gap between OECD and non-OECD countries was smaller, at around 11 points in 2019, and is projected to decline to approximately eight points under BAU and four points under ACC. Intercontinental comparisons show that Europe stands out as the most promising region in terms of sustainability, followed closely by North America, both characterized by high scores and steady convergence among members. Asia’s trajectory is more uneven: while countries such as Japan continue to lead, others, including Pakistan, lag significantly behind. Nonetheless, our models forecast that the gap between Europe and Asia will narrow from around 18 points in 2019 to about ten points under ACC. In Africa, several countries, such as Madagascar, demonstrate promising progress in SDG index scores; yet, even under the ACC scenario, the Europe-Africa gap remains substantial at around 20 points. Oceania displays the widest contrasts, with Australia and Papua New Guinea following very different pathways, though both make gradual improvements over time.

The influence of individual SDGs on wealth accumulation

This study presents a novel SDG-wealth model that elucidates the significant relationship between SDG scores and disaggregated wealth, which includes produced, human, and natural capital. The model’s robust predictive performance, as evidenced by consistently low MAE and MAPE values during both in-sample and out-of-sample tests (see Table S3 in the supplemental information), validates the strong link between these variables. We further benchmark the Cubist model against alternative ML approaches (Table S4 in the supplemental information) to assess its relative predictive accuracy. Among the models evaluated, Cubist achieves the strongest predictive performance for this application, while also offering greater interpretability than alternative ML approaches.

To further examine the influence of each SDG on wealth accumulation, we utilize the variable importance plot (VIP) derived from the Cubist model. This approach quantifies the relative importance of each predictor based on its frequency of use in rule conditions within the decision tree structure and its contribution to the linear models embedded at the terminal nodes. Additionally, we use partial dependence plot (PDP) to ascertain the nature of the relationship (positive, negative, or more complex) between each SDG and wealth. The visualizations supporting these analyses are provided in Figure 2. The order of variables in each capital in Figure 2 is arranged according to the VIP scores. Within this figure, the variables for each capital are arranged according to their VIP scores. These scores are normalized such that the most influential variable receives a score of 100, with all other variables scaled proportionally. Our analysis, supported by Figure 2, demonstrates that the random effect intercepts derived from the RE-EM tree—denoted by RANPC, RANHC, and RANNC, representing country-specific random intercepts for produced, human, and natural capital, respectively—act as the most influential predictors for all capital types. These intercepts serve as crucial baselines, accounting for unobserved heterogeneity across countries by capturing systematic differences not explained by the SDGs. Additionally, the PDPs reveal that these intercepts have a positive influence on capital accumulation, suggesting that a country’s wealth level is highly determined by its initial endowment. Figure 2 also reveals that SDG 9 (industry, innovation, and infrastructure) consistently ranks among the top five predictors across all capital types. This finding is not unexpected, as SDG 9 measures a country’s economic advancement through industrialization, while also aiming to balance social and environmental goals.44 This should be reflected in the accumulation of produced, human, and natural capital. However, a deeper look into the nature of this relationship using PDPs clarifies a significant trade-off: SDG 9 exerts a positive influence on the accumulation of produced and human capital but a negative influence on natural capital. This finding corroborates the work of Kynčlová et al.,45 who highlighted the challenges faced by emerging industrial economies in balancing economic, social, and environmental goals as they expand their industrial infrastructure by drawing more natural resources and energy.

Similar to SDG 9, SDG 3 (good health and well-being) emerged as one of the top-five predictors of wealth, exerting a positive influence on both human and produced capital, but a negative influence on natural capital accumulation. Therefore, while progress on SDG 3 yields dual benefits for wealth accumulation by reinforcing both social and economic dimensions, it creates a trade-off by depleting the stock of natural capital. This finding is consistent with the previous study of Küfeoğlu,46 which argues that progress in SDG 3 will lead to greater citizen involvement in education and economic activities, thereby promoting the accumulation of both produced and human capital. However, the negative impact of SDG 3 on natural capital underscores the necessity of a more holistic and inclusive implementation of this goal, as emphasized by Eckermann.47

Additionally, we identified a distinct set of SDGs among the top-five predictors of wealth. Produced and human capitals are significantly influenced by SDG 12 (responsible consumption and production) and SDG 13 (climate action), both of which exhibit a negative impact on capital accumulation. Our findings highlight the persistent link between socio-economic development and environmental degradation, suggesting that efforts to combat climate change—such as transitioning to low-carbon economies, as emphasized by SDG 13—and initiatives to improve the efficient use of natural resources, as promoted by SDG 12, may inadvertently hinder the accumulation of other forms of capital. This finding aligns with the study by Kartal et al.,48 which found that current energy and environmental policies continue to fall short in detaching economic development from the rising ecological footprint, particularly in emerging economies.

In the context of natural capital, SDG 6 (clean water and sanitation) and SDG 7 (affordable and clean energy) emerge as influential predictors of wealth, exhibiting distinct non-linear relationships. Specifically, SDG 6 demonstrates a negative correlation at higher performance levels; whereas, SDG 7 maintains a positive association under comparable conditions. The observed decline in wealth correlation with elevated SDG 6 scores reflect the Jevons paradox in water management, as articulated by Sears et al.,49 wherein efficiency gains may inadvertently trigger a rebound effect, leading to increased overall water consumption. In response to this dynamic, Sadoff et al.50 advocate for a paradigm shift—emphasizing the need to treat water as a fundamentally scarce resource rather than an abundant one. Our findings related to SDG 7 align with the study by Sugiawan and Managi19 which identified a strong correlation between energy consumption and the depletion of natural capital. However, they contend that such depletion may be mitigated as economic growth progresses. Furthermore, our results underscore the phenomenon of energy addition, as described by York and Bell,51 wherein renewable energy sources tend to supplement—rather than replace—fossil fuel-based energy systems.

Given that the IWI is proposed to complement the SDGs and address their inherent limitations, it is essential to establish an integrated framework that links the two, as illustrated in Figure 3. The Sankey diagram in Figure 3 is constructed based on model estimations using historical data up to 2019. The width of the first-level nodes reflects the normalized VIP scores, while the second-level nodes represent the proportional contributions of produced, human, and natural capital to total wealth. This hierarchical framework offers two key analytical features. First, it illustrates the relative influence of individual SDGs on specific forms of capital. Second, it enables a clear distinction between short- and medium-term objectives—measured by progress in the SDGs—and long-term objectives—measured by wealth accumulation. As noted by Sugiawan et al.,41 without appropriate policy interventions, progress in the SDGs may not necessarily translate into increased total wealth. Accumulation of wealth under different SDG scenarios

Figure 4 illustrates both the historical evolution and the 2040 projections of average per-capita wealth (in USD thousand) under three SDG progress scenarios. Between 2000 and 2019, the average inclusive wealth per capita for the countries in this study increased by 3.66%, from USD 252.72 thousand to USD 261.98 thousand, despite periods of fluctuation. Furthermore, our model projects a favorable outlook for global wealth accumulation by 2040, irrespective of the scenarios. Even under the PES scenario, which assumes a slow pace of SDG progress, a 5.85% increase in average inclusive wealth is forecasted. More promising futures are projected under the BAU and ACC scenarios, with total wealth projected to increase by 16.20% and 28.57%, respectively. These findings, which highlight the positive correlation between progress in SDG scores and wealth accumulation, align to some extent with Du et al.,36 who found a positive correlation between SDG evenness and subjective well-being. While our findings do not dismiss the imperfect indivisibility of the SDGs, as suggested by McGowan et al.,34 they challenge the prioritization of certain SDGs over others, as proposed by Asadikia et al.52 Our analysis indicates that such a fragmented approach could lead to substantial potential wealth losses. Instead, our results align with the conclusions of Fuso Nerini et al.,33 who underscore the critical need for a comprehensive and integrated strategy for SDG implementation.Figure 4The historical evolution and 2040 projections of average per-capita wealth at both the aggregated and disaggregated levels (in constant 2015 USD)

Despite the overall increase in inclusive wealth, examination of its disaggregated components reveals an alarming trend. The increase in inclusive wealth is primarily driven by significant growth in produced and human capital, accompanied by a notable trade-off involving the depletion of natural capital. Historical data show a clear shift in the composition of wealth between 2000 and 2019. The domination of natural capital, which comprised around 44.16% of total wealth, was gradually replaced due to the significant growth in produced and human capital. This resulted in a restructuring of wealth components, with human capital becoming the dominant share at 44.62%, followed by natural capital at 31.23% and produced capital at 24.15%. Our forecast projects a similar wealth composition in 2040 for the BAU and PES scenarios. A notable divergence, however, is projected under the ACC scenario. Under this pathway, the share of produced capital is expected to exceed that of natural capital, a significant outcome achieved without further depletion of natural resources. In fact, the global average of natural capital is forecasted to increase by 2.40% by 2040 compared to 2019 levels. While the PES scenario also projects a modest increase of approximately 0.83% in natural capital, the accompanying growth of human and produced capital is notably restrained, limited to less than 10%. These results reinforce the enduring association between socio-economic development and environmental degradation evident in the partial dependence plot. Our results align with those of Bennich et al.,53 who emphasize that achieving more than half of the SDGs is contingent upon the utilization of natural systems.

A disaggregated analysis at the regional and group levels reveals heterogeneous effects of SDG progress scenarios on development trajectories, as shown in Figure 5. Between 2000 and 2019, the inclusive wealth growth exhibited pronounced variation across geographical regions and income groups. East Asia and the Pacific led all regions, driven by a surge in produced capital. At the same time, North America and Europe and central Asia recorded more moderate, but still positive, inclusive wealth gains. In contrast, sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America and the Caribbean experienced net declines in inclusive wealth, underpinned by steep losses in natural capital. Across income levels, high-income countries achieved robust inclusive wealth growth (22.2%), middle-income countries posted intermediate gains, and low-income countries saw a slight contraction (−1.7%). Disaggregated by development club, G20 wealth increases were principally propelled by produced capital, while OECD members relied more heavily on human capital expansion. Projected to 2040, inclusive wealth trajectories diverge sharply under three SDG progress scenarios. Under the BAU scenario, East Asia and the Pacific continue to lead regional growth by 65.5%, and high-income countries grow by 13.9% versus 0.4% in low-income countries. G20 and OECD are projected to follow a similar growth pattern, with G20’s growth (14.8%) and OECD’s growth (13.9%) being driven by produced and human capital, respectively. This is expected to occur even as natural capital declines persist. Under the PES scenario, outcomes remain restrained or turn negative. Under this scenario, sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America and the Caribbean contract further, low-income inclusive wealth falls by 5.3%, middle-income countries gain only 4.7%, and high-income countries still register a 6.4% increase. Natural capital depletion is most severe in sub-Saharan Africa. By contrast, the ACC scenario delivers the strongest rebounds. Under this scenario, sub-Saharan Africa’s inclusive wealth rises by 178.6% and east Asia and the Pacific by 132.8%. High-income countries grow by 26.5% and low-income countries by 169.5%. The G20 group’s wealth increases by 27.9% driven by human capital, and the OECD group’s wealth by 26.1% also led by human capital.

Building upon our regional and group-level analysis, we extended our investigation to country-specific trends. Our findings reveal that each nation exhibits a distinct development trajectory with varying impacts on its inclusive wealth. Historical data from 2000 to 2019 show that 24 of the 90 countries in our study experienced a decline in their inclusive wealth, predominantly driven by a significant loss in natural capital that outweighed gains in other wealth components. For instance, countries such as Mauritania and Peru saw sharp drops in their natural capital—by more than half—which led to negative inclusive wealth growth despite positive increases in human and produced capital. However, 58 countries managed to compensate for the loss in natural capital by increasing their human and produced capital. We also identified two countries that experienced negative growth across all three capital components: Gabon and Nigeria, resulting in inclusive wealth growth of −41.68% and −31.33%, respectively.

Looking ahead to 2040, a country’s wealth trajectory will be significantly influenced by the SDG growth scenarios. Under the PES Scenario, 21 countries are projected to experience negative growth in inclusive wealth. This is double the number of countries projected to have negative growth under the BAU and ACC scenarios, which each project a decline in ten countries. Compared to historical data, the number of countries with declining inclusive wealth is expected to decrease significantly under the BAU and ACC scenarios. These findings therefore suggest a strong alignment between progress in achieving the SDGs and the notion of sustainability within the IWI framework, as evidenced by an expanding productive base of the economy. However, it is important to note that while accelerating SDG progress, as in the ACC scenario, is not a guarantee of a wealthier future compared to the BAU scenario, neglecting the SDGs altogether, as modeled in the PES scenario, will almost certainly lead to a more challenging future.

To illustrate the policy relevance of our findings, we examine several representative national trajectories that exemplify distinct sustainability challenges across development contexts. We found that eight countries are expected to experience declining inclusive wealth across all three scenarios, highlighting vulnerabilities that persist regardless of policy orientation. Six of these are high-income economies having relatively high SDG Index scores, including Australia, Denmark, the United Kingdom, Iceland, Norway, and the United States. The projected decline in inclusive wealth for these nations is attributable to a declining rate of natural capital depletion, or even natural resource recovery in some cases. However, this has come at the expense of declining growth in produced and human capital, ultimately leading to a decrease in the total wealth of the nation. With the exception of Norway, its decline in inclusive wealth was already evident during the 2000–2019 period and is expected to accelerate by 2040, unlike Iceland, which is projected to mitigate its rate of decline. This trend in Norway may be linked to its comparatively low projected performance on SDG 12 and SDG 13, which are the weakest among the high-income economies in this category.

Two other countries that fall into this category are Gabon and Venezuela, but with notably different development trajectories. Both countries have SDG index scores in the low-60s, well below the developing countries in this category, and are expected to increase to the low-70s under the BAU scenario. Despite still having negative growth in inclusive wealth, Gabon shows an impressive improvement by significantly reducing the rate of natural capital depletion and is expected to have a positive growth in human and produced capital under the ACC scenario. Venezuela, on the contrary, is expected to have a higher decline in inclusive wealth since it is projected to have a significant decrease in human and produced capital, despite a steady decline in its natural capital depletion rate. The contrasting trends between the two countries may be linked to their projected performance on SDG 3. While Gabon is projected to see improvement, Venezuela is expected to have a slight decline in its SDG 3 score. Additionally, Gabon is projected to have a significant increase in its SDG 9 score compared to Venezuela, which is highly influential for the accumulation of produced and human capital. Moreover, Gabon’s better performance in SDG 7 may also be beneficial for reducing its rate of natural capital depletion.

Discussion

This study enriches the understanding of sustainable development dynamics, particularly in light of the varied progress seen during the first decade of SDGs implementation. By using the IWI, which measures the combined value of produced, human, and natural capital, our analysis provides a more complete view of long-term development and the sustainability of national growth. Our findings reveal that SDGs progress generally has a positive effect on wealth accumulation, particularly for produced and human capital. The impact on natural capital is less pronounced but remains beneficial, helping to prevent further depletion and, in some cases, supporting natural resource recovery. These results underscore a dual challenge: the widely documented trade-offs among the SDGs themselves, and the less-explored trade-offs between the components of wealth. Our study provides specific evidence of this latter challenge, demonstrating how pursuing progress in one SDG can affect the balance among produced, human, and natural capital. From a sustainability theory perspective, these findings contribute directly to debates on weak versus strong sustainability.54 The strong view of sustainability suggests that such trade-offs indicate a deviation from a truly sustainable development path.55 However, within a weak sustainability framework, such as the IWI, these trade-offs are permissible and still align with a sustainable trajectory, provided that the total value of wealth is maintained. This is the central rationale for advocating the integration of the IWI framework into the SDG monitoring process. The IWI serves as a crucial sustainability constraint, mitigating the risk that inherent inter-goal trade-offs among the SDGs lead to a net erosion of total wealth.

Our proposed model also provides empirical evidence to validate the key characteristics of the SDGs, with regard to their universality, indivisibility, and inclusivity. Our models support the universality of the SDGs, as evidenced by the projected increase in average global wealth across all analyzed scenarios. This finding suggests that regardless of income levels or regions, progress toward the SDGs is beneficial for wealth accumulation, indicating a promising pathway toward sustainability. However, our analysis at the country level also identifies specific cases that portray short-run sustainability, a concept highlighted by Sugiawan et al.,41 in which progress toward the SDGs corresponds with a decline in inclusive wealth. Drawing on historical data, we identify two distinct patterns underlying this phenomenon. The first emerges in resource-dependent economies, where SDG progress is accompanied by substantial depletion of natural capital, insufficiently offset by gains in produced or human capital. The second pattern is observed in advanced economies, where efforts to slow natural resource depletion—a core component of SDG progress—unintentionally result in negative growth in both produced and human capital. Crucially, these findings partially challenge the presumed inclusivity of the SDGs with respect to future generations, as evidenced by the declining productive base of the economy. The limited capacity of the SDG framework to ensure long-term sustainability is not entirely unexpected, given that it was not comprehensively designed to incorporate the principle of intergenerational equity.35 Consequently, our findings underscore the strategic importance of the IWI in ensuring that sustainability is not confined to short-term achievements reflected in SDG progress, but is realized as a long-term outcome through sustained growth in inclusive wealth.

To further illustrate how these dynamics unfold in practice, it is instructive to examine a large emerging economy where rapid development, strong institutional capacity, and explicit sustainability ambitions intersect. China provides a concise illustration of how SDG progress interacts with development policy and wealth dynamics under strong state coordination. Our projections indicate that SDG performance improves under BAU and ACC pathways, supported by continued growth in IWI driven mainly by produced and human capital accumulation. However, natural capital recovery remains comparatively weak, reflecting a persistent structural imbalance between economic modernization and ecological sustainability. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence showing that China’s development strategy has relied heavily on infrastructure investment, industrial upgrading, and human capital formation, while environmental pressures and resource depletion continue to constrain long-term resilience.56,57 Although recent policy shifts—such as the ecological civilization framework and strengthened environmental regulation—signal greater attention to sustainability, their effects remain uneven across regions, reinforcing the need to integrate wealth-based metrics into SDG monitoring to better assess long-term development outcomes.58,59

Although China’s experience underscores the importance of institutional capacity and long-term planning, our broader analysis reinforces that effective SDG implementation ultimately hinges on contextual adaptation across countries. The high VIP scores for the random intercepts in our models, which represent unique country-specific characteristics, provide compelling evidence in support of this. Our findings demonstrate that country-specific characteristics play a pivotal role in shaping national development trajectories. In the context of our Cubist models, the VIP score does not reflect the direct contribution of a variable to predictive power, but rather its frequency of use within the model’s rules and leaves. The consistently high VIP scores for random intercepts suggest that the patterns of wealth accumulation are profoundly influenced by country-specific factors. Despite the heterogeneity in national growth patterns, our analysis, framed within the IWI, reveals that a majority of these diverse trajectories ultimately converge on a sustainable development path. Our historical data indicate that approximately one-third of the countries in our study experienced a decline in inclusive wealth per capita, thereby deviating from a sustainable trajectory. However, our projections suggest that progress toward the SDGs will reduce this number by more than 50%. We therefore conclude that a uniform growth pattern across all nations is neither a necessary nor a realistic expectation in the pursuit of sustainable development.

With respect to the indivisibility of the SDGs, our findings challenge the effectiveness of isolated interventions and the prioritization of individual goals in pursuing the SDGs. Our models indicate that such an approach results in dual losses. First, the prioritization of select SDGs—as illustrated by the PES scenario—while not necessarily incompatible with long-term sustainability, yields significantly lower gains in total wealth compared to more integrated strategies. Second, in cases of short-run sustainability, where rising SDG Index scores coincide with declining inclusive wealth, we find that low SDG index evenness exacerbates the decline. As Meilland and Lecocq60 argue, the prioritization of SDGs is common in both developing and developed countries, though the underlying motives differ. In developing nations, this prioritization is primarily driven by resource constraints and specific national strategic issues. In contrast, developed countries often prioritize SDGs based on political agendas and sectoral silos. Our findings indicate that this prioritization, irrespective of its origin, leads to suboptimal outcomes. The IWI is thus pivotal in promoting integrated, balanced progress across all SDGs, thereby securing both short- and long-term sustainability.

Shifting to an analysis of the temporal dynamics between SDG progress and the IWI, we find that anticipating substantial global inclusive wealth gains by the 2030 target remains premature. Our projections show that even with the most ambitious ACC pathway, the gain in inclusive wealth is predicted to be less than 15%. For certain countries, the choice of a particular SDG pathway offers no discernible advantage in wealth accumulation by 2030. However, a more distinct pattern of wealth accumulation becomes evident when the forecast horizon is extended to 2040. The ACC scenario is projected to increase global wealth by up to 30%, which is considerably higher than the 16.2% and 5.8% gains predicted for the BAU and PES scenarios, respectively. These findings suggest that while SDG progress may lead to short-term fluctuations in wealth, its positive effects on wealth accumulation are primarily observable over the long term. This underscores the unsuitability of short- to medium-term expectations for realizing the full benefits of the SDGs. Accordingly, the SDGs should be adopted as a long-term commitment consistent with the concept of sustainability, which necessitates both intragenerational and intergenerational equity. Therefore, we concur with Fuso Nerini et al.33 in advocating for the extension of the SDG implementation horizon beyond 2030.

A caveat worth mentioning from our forecast is that major structural shocks—such as the COVID-19 pandemic—can generate systemic disruptions that affect both SDG progress and wealth dynamics. For example, Fan et al.56 demonstrate that during such crises, short-term indicator performance often becomes decoupled from underlying capital accumulation, with the full consequences emerging only over longer horizons. Accordingly, we interpret our pre-pandemic SDG projections as counterfactual benchmarks that reflect historical momentum rather than forecasts of post-crisis development trajectories. By integrating wealth-based metrics with SDG indicators, our framework offers a more robust basis for evaluating long-term development prospects and the durability of progress under heightened uncertainty.

As the 2030 agenda for sustainable development approaches its final stage, substantial challenges to achieving genuine sustainability remain. This study reconceptualizes sustainability under the SDG framework by explicitly linking SDG progress to the accumulation of produced, human, and natural capital within the IWI framework. Through this linkage, we assess whether the SDGs fulfill their defining attributes of universality, indivisibility, and inclusivity. Using panel data from 90 countries over the period 2000–2019, we estimate an SDG-wealth model employing a Cubist rule-based machine-learning approach and project future wealth trajectories through 2040 under three alternative SDG-progress scenarios.

Our findings provide strong evidence that the SDGs are both universal and indivisible: broad-based, integrated progress across goals is associated with sustained wealth accumulation, whereas the selective prioritization of individual goals entails substantial potential losses in total wealth. However, we also uncover a cautionary tale regarding inclusivity. Several countries with high SDG scores failed the sustainability criteria of the IWI framework, proving that short-term indicator gains can mask the erosion of a nation’s productive base. Moreover, projected wealth gains are driven predominantly by increases in produced and human capital, while persistent trade-offs continue to place natural capital at risk—highlighting a fundamental tension between current development trajectories and long-term sustainability.

From a policy perspective, our findings support three concrete and actionable recommendations for strengthening SDG monitoring and national development planning. First, we propose the systematic integration of the IWI into existing SDG monitoring frameworks. This can be operationalized by complementing SDG indicator dashboards with regular reporting on changes in produced, human, and natural capital. Such integration would allow policymakers to distinguish between short-term improvements in SDG performance and long-term sustainability outcomes, thereby reducing the risk of pursuing development strategies that erode national wealth. At the national level, this implies embedding wealth accounting within official statistical systems and aligning it with SDG reporting cycles.

Second, we advocate the adoption of wealth-consistent SDG planning, whereby development strategies are evaluated not only by their contribution to SDG target achievement but also by their expected impact on inclusive wealth accumulation. In practice, this can be implemented through ex ante policy screening tools that assess whether SDG interventions generate balanced gains across produced, human, and natural capital. Such an approach is particularly relevant for medium- and long-term planning frameworks, where trade-offs between economic growth and environmental sustainability must be explicitly managed.

Third, we emphasize the strategic role of research and innovation policy in enabling this transition toward wealth-based sustainability. This includes investing in data systems, analytical tools, and interdisciplinary research that support the measurement and modeling of inclusive wealth dynamics. In particular, governments should promote the development of decision-support systems—such as integrated assessment models and ML applications—that enable forward-looking evaluation of SDG pathways and their implications for different forms of capital. Furthermore, aligning public research funding and innovation incentives with sectors that generate complementary gains across produced, human, and natural capital—such as clean energy, sustainable infrastructure, and human capital development—can help reduce trade-offs and enhance long-term wealth accumulation.

Together, these measures provide a practical pathway for aligning SDG implementation with intergenerational sustainability objectives and ensuring that progress toward the 2030 Agenda translates into durable improvements in national wealth.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our ML framework is designed for predictive and scenario analysis rather than causal inference, the relationship between SDG progress and inclusive wealth is potentially bidirectional, as wealth accumulation may also facilitate SDG improvements. In this regard, our results should be treated as empirical associations rather than evidence of direct cause and effect. Second, our analysis relies on data covering 2000–2019, reflecting the latest availability of consistent IWI estimates. Therefore, the projections should be understood as counterfactual extensions of pre-pandemic trajectories rather than forecasts of post-COVID outcomes. Third, while the three main wealth components are examined separately, they may interact in ways that are not fully captured here—particularly within aggregated natural capital. Hence, future research could benefit from causal and disaggregated approaches to further refine sustainability assessments. Finally, the IWI components could be further disaggregated into their respective subcomponents. For instance, the natural capital component of the IWI aggregates subcomponents with distinct characteristics, i.e., renewable and non-renewable natural capital. To achieve a deeper, more nuanced understanding of these dynamics, future studies should expand the analysis to the wealth subcomponent level.

Method details

Forecasting the SDG index score

Our analysis begins by forecasting the growth of individual SDGs scores to define two base scenarios: business-as-usual (BAU) and pessimistic (PES). The BAU scenario represents a continuation of observed historical trends, whereas the PES scenario depicts a dampened growth pathway that reflects structural or systemic constraints, such as economic shocks, institutional limitations, or declining policy momentum. For the BAU forecast, we adopt Holt’s linear trend method,64 consistent with Chenary et al.,42 but tailor it to our annual dataset by omitting the seasonal component. Holt’s method extends simple exponential smoothing via three main equations to generate a forecast as follows:(Equation 1)yˆt+h|t=lt+hbt(Equation 2)lt=αyt+(1−α)(lt−1+bt−1)(Equation 3)bt=β(lt−lt−1)+(1−β)bt−1

Equations 2 and 3 represent the level and trend equations, respectively, which are combined in Equation 1 to form the forecast equation. This combination yields forecasts that follow a linear path. Both Equations 2 and 3 contain smoothing parameters, α and β, with values between 0 and 1. A higher value for α indicates a stronger reaction to each new observation, while a higher value for β suggests more frequent changes of slope over time. The estimation and forecasting procedures for Holt’s method are implemented in R using the forecast package, ensuring reproducibility and consistency with established time-series practices.62 Furthermore, to ensure the validity of our forecasts, we impose boundary constraints on the projected SDG Index scores, which are inherently bounded between 0 and 100. If a projected value falls below zero, we apply an exponential trend adjustment to maintain non-negativity. Conversely, any value exceeding 100 is capped at 100, signifying full goal attainment. To construct the PES scenario, we introduce the damping parameter inherent to Holt’s method. This parameter dampens the long-term trend, preventing the aggressive, indefinite extrapolation typical of undamped models.65 By introducing this parameter, we simulate a trajectory where SDG growth is restrained, representing a slower pace of progress or a potential decline in progress.

Our third pathway, the accelerated (ACC) scenario, provides a normative benchmark to illustrate the scale of progress required for rapid, aligned development. For SDGs exhibiting a positive CAGR under BAU, the ACC scenario assumes a growth rate twice that of BAU. For SDGs with a negative BAU CAGR, the ACC scenario applies an equivalent growth magnitude in the positive direction. This ensures that all goals show progress, consistent with the SDG literature, which emphasizes that achieving the goals by 2030 requires progress rates exceeding historical trajectories. By utilizing a CAGR-based adjustment, we establish a transparent reference trajectory that avoids embedding speculative assumptions regarding fiscal capacity or political coordination. Crucially, this pathway is not intended as a realistic policy forecast; rather, it serves as an aspirational upper bound that provides a vital counterpoint to the BAU scenario, which projects existing historical trends, and the PES scenario, which models dampened trajectories.

The forecasting performance of our model will be assessed by using accuracy metrics, which include mean error (ME), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean percentage error (MPE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and mean absolute scaled error (MASE). Furthermore, we will also employ the residual diagnostics to ensure that systematic patterns, including trends, autocorrelation, and non-normality, have been adequately captured in our models.

Linking the SDGs and disaggregated wealth

To evaluate the impact of progress on SDG scores on wealth accumulation, we develop an SDG–wealth model in which the components of inclusive wealth serve as the dependent variable, and the 17 SDG scores are treated as independent variables, following the empirical relationship outlined below:(Equation 4)HCPCit=f(SDG1it,SDG2it,SDG3it,…,SDG17it)(Equation 5)NCPCit=f(SDG1it,SDG2it,SDG3it,…,SDG17it)(Equation 6)PCPCit=f(SDG1it,SDG2it,SDG3it,…,SDG17it)

where HCPC, NCPC, and PCPC denote per capita human capital, natural capital, and produced capital, respectively; and SDG1-SDG17 represent the 17 SDG scores; and the subscripts i and t represent country and time, respectively.

The interactions between SDG scores and the IWI index may exhibit complex functional forms; therefore, we employ an ML approach using the Cubist package in R, which is developed by Kuhn et al.63 Cubist regression is particularly well suited to our application for several reasons. First, unlike traditional parametric regression models, Cubist allows for unconstrained interactions among predictors without requiring prior specification of functional forms.66 This is critical given the multidimensional nature of SDG scores and the possibility that the effect of progress in one goal depends on the level of progress in others. Second, compared with purely tree-based methods such as random forests or gradient boosting, Cubist retains continuous linear relationships within terminal nodes, which improves predictive accuracy when outcomes vary smoothly with predictors.67,68 Third, relative to artificial neural networks, Cubist offers substantially greater transparency and interpretability, as model behavior can be examined through rule sets and variable usage frequencies.66 This interpretability is particularly important in a policy-oriented context, where understanding the conditions under which SDG progress aligns with wealth accumulation is as important as prediction accuracy itself.

We acknowledge that the relationship between SDG scores and inclusive wealth is potentially bidirectional, as wealth accumulation can facilitate SDG progress through increased investment capacity, while SDG-oriented policies may reshape the composition of wealth. However, as emphasized by Mullainathan and Spiess69 and Athey and Imbens,70 ML methods are fundamentally predictive tools that aim to approximate conditional expectations rather than identify causal parameters. Consequently, concerns such as endogeneity, which threaten causal interpretation in regression models, do not invalidate the use of ML models for forecasting purposes. Importantly, the Cubist model performance is evaluated using rigorous out-of-sample validation, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy. This indicates that the model captures stable empirical regularities between SDG profiles and wealth outcomes, irrespective of the direction of causality. The Cubist model is therefore used to characterize systematic patterns linking SDG performance to subsequent wealth outcomes, consistent with a co-evolutionary interpretation of development and sustainability. Results are interpreted as identifying conditions under which SDG progress is associated with sustainable wealth trajectories, rather than as estimates of causal effects.

Given that our analysis utilizes a panel data structure, capturing unobserved, subject-specific baseline heterogeneity across countries is necessary to enhance the predictive accuracy of our model. The initial step in our analysis is to estimate the country-specific fixed-effects components using the RE-EM Tree method.61 Following this, we proceed to train and tune the parameters of the Cubist regression model. To ensure robust model evaluation and enhance the reliability of performance estimates, we employ a repeated cross-validation strategy, setting the resampling parameter to 10 folds. Furthermore, we optimize Cubist’s ensemble structure and local adjustment mechanism via a grid search over key hyperparameters. Specifically, we set a range of committee sizes from 10 to 100 in increments of 10, and vary the number of nearest neighbors from 0 to 9. While offering high predictive performance, the ML approach presents a critical caveat for long-term forecasting. ML models derive patterns exclusively from historical data. Consequently, predictions that extend significantly beyond the observed data range risk unrealistic extrapolation, particularly when historical trends exhibit continuous growth (e.g., indefinite improvement or exponential wealth projections). To ensure realistic and bounded forecasts, our study incorporates explicit growth limits for natural capital projections. Specifically, depletion trends are constrained to prevent the capital from reaching zero, and growth trends are capped at the maximum value observed within the historical training data.

Prior to model estimation, we partition the dataset into training and test sets. The analysis is based on a balanced panel comprising 90 countries observed annually from 2000 to 2019. We designate the years 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2015 as the test set, while the remaining years serve as the training set. The forecasting performance of our model will be assessed by using accuracy metrics, which include mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for both in-sample and out-of-sample tests. Detailed forecasting results for each specification are reported in Table S3 of the supplemental information.

Data and parameters

As a proxy of wealth, we utilize the components of the Inclusive Wealth Index (IWI), sourced from the Inclusive Wealth Report 2023,21 as our dependent variables. These wealth components are expressed in constant 2015 USD. However, it is worth mentioning that although the IWI methodology has evolved to encompass a broader spectrum of wealth components, certain intangible assets remain difficult to quantify in monetary terms. Hence, the value indicated by the IWI may not reflect the actual wealth. For the independent variables, we rely on SDG scores reported in the Sustainable Development Report 2024.25 Each SDG is assigned a score of up to 100, representing an aggregate of multiple indicators that reflect a country’s progress toward optimal achievement. However, this aggregation presents a notable limitation, as it can obscure the nuanced progress or regression of individual targets within each SDG. Furthermore, to ensure the reliability of the time series data for direct year-to-year comparisons, we use the backdated SDG scores. This specific dataset recalculates prior years using the current year’s methods and indicators, thereby ensuring methodological consistency across the time dimension. These scores can be accessed at https://dashboards.sdgindex.org/explorer.

Our analysis involves a balanced panel comprising 90 countries over the years 2000 to 2019 (see Table S2 in the supplemental information for summary statistics of the variables). The scope of the dataset is primarily determined by data availability constraints. The temporal coverage reflects the most recent year for which harmonized IWI data are available, namely 2019, while the country sample is restricted to those with complete SDG score coverage over the study period. As a result, the analysis does not capture developments after 2019, including the substantial economic, social, and environmental disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. Given evidence that the pandemic altered SDG trajectories, public investment patterns, and wealth dynamics in many countries, projections based on pre-2020 trends should be interpreted as counterfactual extensions of historical dynamics rather than as predictions of post-pandemic outcomes.
