Drynachromoside A from Drynaria fortunei attenuates oxidative stress and fibrosis of diabetic nephropathy via activating Nrf2 in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Huankai Yao, Jindong Li, Ruiqing Wu, Dan Du, Fengwei Ai, Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is the most common complication of diabetes mellitus. It has shown that the activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) could attenuate DN. To identifiy novel Nrf2 activators targeting DN, we explored drynachromoside A (DCSA) from Drynaria fortunei in vitro and in vivo.Methods:To determine the effects of DCSA, the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose was evaluated. And the renal function of db/db mice was evaluated. Meanwhile, oxidative stress and renal fibrosis in vitro and in vivo were further investigated. To elucidate the mechanism of DCSA, Nrf2 activation and its role in these effects were explored, and the interaction between Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) and DCSA was examined.Results:DCSA inhibited the proliferation of mesangial cells resulting from high glucose and improved renal function in db/db mice. DCSA attenuated oxidative stress and fibrosis in vitro and in vivo, which was closely associated with Nrf2 activation in a Keap1-dependent manner.Conclusion:DCSA identified in Drynaria fortunei is a Keap1-dependent Nrf2 activator with the potential to attenuate DN. This investigation could provide novel insights into the discovery of Nrf2 activators and new therapeutic approaches for DN.KEYWORDS: Drynachromoside A, Drynaria fortunei, diabetic nephropathy, oxidative stress, fibrosis, Nrf2, mesangial cell, db/db mice

Introduction

Oxidative stress is a pathophysiological condition resulting from the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) whelming the consumption [1]. In patients with diabetes, oxidative stress arises because of the aberrant glucose metabolism [2]. Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus that is closely associated with oxidative stress [3]. The pathogenesis of DN has revealed that hyperglycemia results in oxidative stress in diabetic kidneys, which mediates renal fibrosis via enhancing the secretion of transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) [4]. As the key pro-fibrotic cytokine, transforming growth factor-β1 promotes the synthesis and accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins including collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin [5]. Without timely prevention, excessive ECM will lead to basement membrane thickening and mesangial expansion, followed by glomerular hypertrophy, glomerulosclerosis, and renal fibrosis, which finally causes end-stage renal disease [6]. Dialysis and transplantation must be performed to improve renal failure [7]. In the clinic, DN is characterized by proteinuria and progressive damage of renal function, but there are no effective approaches for its treatment in addition to strict control of blood pressure and glucose [8]. Therefore, the development of novel therapies for DN is imperative.

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a transcription factor, that regulates the transcription of target genes encoding antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) [9]. Under unstressed conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered by Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) in the cytosol to form the Keap1-Nrf2 complex, which facilitates the degradation of Nrf2 after ubiquitination [10]. However, in the presence of oxidants or electrophiles the Keap1-Nrf2 complex is disrupted and Nrf2 is not degraded. Free Nrf2 translocates from the cytosol into nucleus and binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) regions in the promoters of target genes, which enhances the transcription of these genes [11]. Therefore, Nrf2 activation may be a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of oxidative stress-associated diseases, including DN [12]. However, there are no Nrf2 activators used in DN treatment. Though bardoxolone, a synthetic derivative of oleanolic acid, has been recognized as a potent Nrf2 activator in clinical trial of stage 3, the unexpected cardiovascular mortality has resulted in the termination [13]. So, it is necessary to discovery of novel Nrf2 activators targeting DN.

Polyphenols are a class of compounds with phenolic groups, that are abundant in the diet and provide health benefits [14]. Many natural compounds such as flavonoids, lignans, coumarins, phenolic acids, and stilbenes, are polyphenols. As a flavonoid, chromone is a privileged scaffold in drug discovery [15]. Many natural chromones have been reported to have bioactivities [16,17]. In our previous investigation, we have found tiliroside, a chromone derivative, attenuated DN via activating Nrf2 in vitro [18]. Drynaria fortunei (Kunze ex Mett.) J. Sm. (Polypodiaceae) is a medicinal plant found in China, Japan, and Korea [19]. In traditional Chinese medicine, this plant was considered to have the effects of strong bone via tonifying the kidney and has been used to treat osteoporosis and related diseases [20]. However, the effects of Drynaria fortunei on renal diseases were not reported at present. Phytochemical investigations of Drynaria fortunei have shown chromone derivatives are major phytochemicals [21,22]. To discover natural Nrf2 activators targeting DN, we identified drynachromoside A (DCSA) from Drynaria fortunei and explored its protective effects against oxidative stress and fibrosis in vitro and in vivo via activating Nrf2.

Phytochemical investigation

Rhizomes of Drynaria fortune were collected from Lianyungang, Jiangsu, China in May 2020 and identified by Prof. Yan Li at Xuzhou Medical University. Voucher specimen (no. Y2020003) was deposited in the Department of Microbial and Biochemical Pharmacy, School of Pharmacy, Xuzhou Medical University.

The air-dried rhizomes of Drynaria fortune (30.0 kg) were ground and extracted with ethanol (20.0 L × 3) under reflux for 4 h. After removing the solvent in vacuum, the residue (183.0 g) was suspended in water (2.0 L) and partitioned successively with petroleum ether (2.0 L × 3) to remove the lipophilic organic molecules such as fatty acids and then with acetyl acetate (2.0 L × 3). The acetyl acetate fraction was condensed and subjected to column chromatography on silica gel eluted with gradient dichloroform/mechanol (from 0:100 to 100:0, v/v) to yield 12 fractions. Faction 8 was subjected to Toyopearl HW-40C column chromatography and eluted using isocratic dichloroform/methanol (1:1, v/v) to obtain six subfractions. Subfraction 3 was purified by recrystallization from methanol to obtain a white amorphous powder (576.0 mg). NMR spectra of this compound were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE III NMR spectrometer.

Cell culture and treatment

Human glomerular mesangial cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in a humid atmosphere with 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 °C. The cells were cultured at 60%−80% confluence and divided into control (Ctrl), high glucose (HG), and experimental groups (DCSA or SFN). The cells in high glucose group and experimental groups were starved in serum-free medium for 24 h and then exposed to high glucose (30 mM) for 24 h. Simultaneously, the cells in the experimental groups were subjected to DCSA or SFN dissolved in the medium including high glucose. The cells in the control group were starved and incubated in normal DMEM medium with 5.6 mM glucose.

CCK-8 assay

Mesangial cells were seeded in 96-well microplates and treated as described above. Then 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured on a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) at 450 nm. The cell viability was expressed as the relative percentage of absorbance in the experimental groups against that in control group.

BrdU incorporation assay

To assess the effects of DCSA on the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose, we performed a BrdU incorporation assay using an assay kit. According to the suppliler’s protocol, the mesangial cells were seeded in 96-well microplates and synchronized for 4 h. Subsequently the cells were treated as above and incubated with 10 μL BrdU solution at 37 °C for 3 h. After fixing with 100 μL FixDenat solution at room temperature for 30 min, the cells were exposed to 100 μL anti-BrdU-POD working solution at room temperature for 90 min. The cells were then washed with PBS and incubated with substrate solution at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was recorded on a microplate reader at 370 nm.

LDH release assay

To further reveal the inhibitory effects of DCSA on the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose, LDH release assay was performed using the LDH release assay kit herein. In brief, the cells were treated as above and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Thereafter the supernatant was collected and transferred to 96-well microplates. After treated according to the supplier’s protocol, the absorbance was recorded on a microplate reader at 490 nm.

ROS production

The treated mesangial cells were exposed to 100 μL 2’,7'-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) in the dark for 20 min at 37 °C. After washing with serum-free medium, the fluorescene intensity was recorded on a fluorescence microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) at 488/525 nm as the excitation and emission wavelength. The ROS level was expressed as the relative percentage of fluorescence intensity versus the control group.

MDA content

The thiobarbituric acid method was employed using an assay kit to detect MDA content. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the treated cells were lysed using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and mixed with the working solution (200 μL) from the assay kit. After boiling for 15 min and cooling to room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min and transferred to a 96-well microplate. Absorbance was determined on a microplate reader at 532 nm. The MDA content was expressed as the relative percentage of absorbance against that of control group.

SOD, CAT and GPx activities

The activities of antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, and GPx were determined using commercially available kits. Mesangial cells were treated as described above and lysed using ice-cold lysis solution. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected for further analysis following the determination of protein concentration using a BCA assay kit. The samples were then handled according to the supplier’s protocols, and the absorbance was read on a microplate reader at 560, 520, and 412 nm for SOD, CAT, and GPx, respectively.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

HO-1, NQO1 and p-Smad3 expression in mesangial cells was detected using ELISA. In brief, the mesangial cells were treated as described above and subjected to repeated freeze–thaw cycles. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 20 min, supernatants were collected as samples. To determine the secretion of ECM proteins, including collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin, the treated mesangial cells were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min to obtain supernatants. The samples were handled according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the absorbance was determined on a microplate reader at 450 nm. The levels of HO-1, NQO1, p-Smad3, collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin were calculated from absorbance versus that of the control group.

Proteins extraction and western blot analysis

To obtain total proteins in mesangial cells, the treated cells were harvested and lysed on ice with RIPA lysis solution for 30 min. The lysates were centrifugated at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 5 min. After quantification using the BCA assay kit, the supernatant was collected as the total proteins. Nuclear proteins were extracted using a nuclear and cytosolic protein extraction kit according to the supplier’s protocol. Briefly, treated cells were exposed to 200 μL buffer solution A with 1 mM PMFS. After vortexing for 5 s, incubation was performed on ice for 15 min, and 10 μL buffer solution B was added. Following centrifugation at 16,000 × g and 4 °C for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was incubated with 50 μL nuclear protein extraction solution containing 1 mM PMSF on ice for 30 min. The supernatant was collected as nuclear protein for further analysis after its concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. The samples were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TGF-β1 (1:1000) (Cat. no. ab215715), Nrf2 (1:1000) (Cat. no. ab137550), GAPDH (1:1000) (Cat. no. ab9485), β-actin (1:1000) (Cat. no. ab8227), and lamin B1 (1:10,000) (Cat. no. ab16048) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed with TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000) (Cat. no. ab295718) at room temperature for 2 h. The bands were visualized using ECL substrate, and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was used to visualize the localization of intracellular Nrf2. Mesangial cells were seeded in 12-well microplates with one coverslip in each well. The cells were treated as above and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 15 min. After exposure to PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min to enhance cell permeabilization, cells were blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min. The cells were treated with a primary antibody against Nrf2 (1:200) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with DyLight 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat. no. ab96885) at 37 °C in the dark. DAPI staining was performed in the dark for 2 min. The coverslips were mounted on glass slides and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, mesangial cells were transfected with the ARE luciferase reporter plasmid together with the Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid using lipofectamine 2000. The cells were treated as described above, and luciferase activity was recorded on a Promega GloMax luminometer (Madison, WI) using a dual luciferase reporter assay system. The relative capacity of Nrf2 binding to ARE was derived from the normalized firefly luciferase activity versus Renilla luciferase activity.

RNA interference assay

To clarify the role of Nrf2 in the effects of DCSA, Nrf2 was knocked down using siRNA. Briefly, the mesangial cells were transfected with NC-siRNA or Nrf2-siRNA according to the supplier’s protocol, and then western blot analysis was performed to validate successful transfection. The cells were treated as described above, and ROS, MDA, and ECM proteins including collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin were detected.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying Nrf2 activation, a co-immunoprecipitation assay was performed using a commercial assay kit. According to the supplier’s protocol, mesangial cells were treated as described above and then lysed using ice-cold lysis buffer. After centrifugation at at 10,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was carefully aspirated and protein A-agarose was added to remove the nonspecific combinations. After incubation at 4 °C for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and incubated with anti-Nrf2 or rabbit IgG at 4 °C for 1 h, after which protein A-agarose was added. After shaking at 4 °C overnight, centrifugation at 1000 × g and 4 °C was performed for 5 min. Thereafter the supernatant was carefully removed, and the pellets were rinsed with lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation as described above. The pellets were then suspended in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer and boiled for 3 min. The precipitated proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE and detected with respective antibodies.

Molecular docking

To visualize the interaction between DCSA and Keap1, molecular docking was performed using the SYBYL X2.1. The 3D structure of DCSA was established using the SYBYL sketch and optimized using the Tripos force field and Gasteiger-Huckel charges. The crystal structure of Keap1 Kelch domain was obtained from the RSCB Protein Data Bank (PDB code: 4IQK). After extracting the ligand and removing water molecules, hydrogen atoms and charges were added. Then, the protomol file was generated to produce the docking envelope, and the Surflex-Dock program with default parameters was used for the docking calculations.

Animal and treatment

Male BKS db/db mice and nondiabetic db/m mice at 6–7 weeks were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University. The mice were housed in cages (3 mice per cage) under the standard conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, approximately 60% relative humidity, and 25 ± 2 °C temperature. Mice were allowed free access to water and food. After a week of adaptive feeding, according to the following in vivo experiments and sufficient statistical power, db/db mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): model group, DCSA group (DCSA 20.0 mg/kg), and ML385 group (DCSA 20.0 mg/kg; ML385 30.0 mg/kg). The doses of DCSA and ML385 were employed based on previous reports for chromone analogues and ML385 as well as the amount of DCSA obtained in present studies [23,24]. Db/m mice were designated as the control group (n = 6 per group). Blood and urine samples were collected prior to the experiments. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) and urine albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR) were determined using the assay kits. It was considered to be an established diabetic nephropathy when the FBG was more than 16.7 mM and the urine albumin level was elevated. DCSA was dissolved in 0.5% CMC-Na solution and administered via oral gavage. But ML385 dissolved in DMSO diluted with corn oil was intraperitoneally injected. Db/m mice in the control group and db/db mice in the model group received an equal volume of 0.5% CMC-Na solution. The treatments were performed once daily for eight weeks. At the end of eight weeks, blood and urine samples were obtained for biochemical assays. The mice were sacrificed with cervical dislocation, and the kidneys were isolated. After recording the weight of the kidneys, the right kidney was cut into two parts. One part was rinsed with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde was used to fix the tissues for histological analysis. The other, together with left kidney was stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Renal function assessment

To assess the renal function of these mice, the levels of FBG, BUN, serum creatinine (SCr), and UACR were measured using assay kits following the manufactor’s protocols. The average kidney weight was calculated from the average weight of the two kidneys of each mouse.

H&E staining

The fixed renal tissue was embedded in paraffin and cut into 4 μm sections. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated with gradient alcohol and water, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The sections were observed under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to assess histological changes.

Masson staining

The fixed renal tissue was cut into 4 μm sections after embedding in paraffin. Then xylene was used to remove paraffin. After rehydration with a gradient of alcohol and water, the sections were stained with Masson’s staining solution following the supplier’s instructions. Images were captured under a microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The fixed renal tissue was prepared as 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were boiled in citrate antigen retrieval buffer (pH = 6) and slowly cooled to room temperature. The sections were then incubated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min to eliminate endogenous peroxidases. After washing with PBS for three times, the sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibody against TGF-β1 (1:500) at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were stained with DAB for 5 min followed by hematoxylin for 1 min. After conventional dehydration and neutral balsam mounting, sections were observed under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons and Student’s t-test for single comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Effects of DCSA on the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose

To assess the effects of DCSA on mesangial cells with or without high glucose, a CCK-8 assay was performed. There was no significant inhibitory effect of DCSA on normal human mesangial cells even at 200 μM (Figure 1B). For the mesangial cells cultured in high glucose, from 10.0 μM DCSA showed significant inhibition to the increased viability of mesangial cells while the cells cultured in normal medium was set as control (Figure 1C). Further investigation by BrdU incorporation assay showed that the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose was inhibited by DCSA at 10.0 μM (Figure 1D). Meanwhile, LDH release assay disclosed DCSA at 10.0 μM didn’t induce significant apoptosis and necrosis of mesangial cells under high glucose (Figure 1E). These facts indicate the need for further exploration of DCSA at a dosage of 10.0 μM.

DCSA inhibits oxidative stress induced by high glucose in mesangial cells

To reveal the effects of DCSA, the redox status of the mesangial cells was determined. High glucose resulted in the overproduction of ROS (360.3 ± 20.3%), which was reversed by DCSA at 10.0 μM (176.4 ± 24.0%) (Figure 2A). Meanwhile, as a product of lipid peroxidation, MDA content was elevated in mesangial cells cultured in high glucose (385.0 ± 24.6%), and DCSA remarkably reduced the level of MDA (173.5 ± 17.8%) (Figure 2B). In addition, high glucose has led to the reduced activities of antioxidant enzymes including SOD (60.7 ± 10.5%), CAT (74.8 ± 11.6%) and GPx (42.9 ± 7.6%), while in the presence of DCSA, the activities for these enzymes were increased to 174.7 ± 10.5%, 161.8 ± 9.0% and 196.1 ± 10.7%, respectively (Figure 2C–E). These findings demonstrate that DCSA attenuates oxidative stress resulting from high glucose in mesangial cells.

DCSA reduces accumulation of ECM in mesangial cells under high glucose

To reveal the effects of DCSA on the ECM of mesangial cells, TGF-β1 was detected herein. As a result, high glucose induced excessive synthesis of TGF-β1, but after exposure to DCSA, the level of TGF-β1 in mesangial cells sharply decreased (Figure 3A and B). The secretion of ECM proteins detected by ELISA showed high glucose elevated collagen IV (182.6 ± 14.7%), fibronectin (263.1 ± 10.5%) and laminin (182.6 ± 10.2%). When DCSA was added, these ECM proteins were reduced to 130.0 ± 10.6%, 160.3 ± 11.2% and 128.3 ± 9.4% (Figure 3C–E), respectively. In addition, it was found the p-Smad3 level was up-regulated in mesangial cells under high glucose, but DCSA could reduce the level though the Nrf2 inhibitor, ML385 could block the effects of DCSA (Figure 3F). These results imply that DCSA reduced the accumulation of ECM in mesangial cells.

DCSA activates Nrf2 in mesangial cells under high glucose

To elucidate the mechanism of DCSA action in mesangial cells, Nrf2 was explored. As shown in Figure 4A, Nrf2 was stained as red while nucleus was stained as blue. When Nrf2 located in nucleus, the nucleus was purple due to the merge of red and blue. Herein, compared with the high glucose group, DCSA increased free Nrf2 and resulted in its translocation into the nucleus from the cytosol, which indicated that DCSA activated Nrf2 and was similar to SFN. Further western blot analysis together with densitometric analysis also showed DCSA led to the increased expression of nuclear and total Nrf2, which validated DCSA promoted translocation and activation of Nrf2 (Figure 4B–D). Meanwhile, the dual luciferase reporter assay revealed that DCSA could elevate the capacity of Nrf2 in mesangial cells to regulate the translation of target genes via binding to the ARE region (Figure 4E). As the downstream enzymes regulated by Nrf2, the levels of HO-1 and NQO1 were decreased by high glucose in mesangial cells, whereas DCSA enhanced the levels of these enzymes (Figure 4F and G). These findings indicate that DCSA activates Nrf2 in mesangial cells under high glucose.

Activated Nrf2 is involved in the effects of DCSA

To unravel the role of Nrf2 activation in the effects of DCSA, Nrf2 knockdown was performed using siRNA interference. After successful transfection with NC-siRNA or Nrf2-siRNA (Figure 5A), oxidative stress was detected in mesangial cells. As shown in Figure 5B and C, in cells transfected with NC-siRNA, DCSA protected the cells against oxidative stress via reducing overproduction of ROS and excessive formation of MDA. However, in the cells transfected with Nrf2-siRNA the effects of DCSA on oxidative stress in mesangial cells were blocked obviously (Figure 5B and C). At the same time, although DCSA significantly inhibited excessive secretion of ECM proteins including collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin in mesangial cells transfected with NC-siRNA, the effects of DCSA were reduced when Nrf2 was knocked down (Figure 5D–F). In addition, it was observed high glucose resulted in the oxidative stress and following fibrosis compared with respective control groups in both NC-siRNA and Nrf2-siRNA treated cells, which means the antioxidant defense system was devastated by high glucose. These observations revealed that the effects of DCSA on mesangial cells under high glucose were closely associated with the activation of Nrf2.

DCSA activates Nrf2 in a Keap1-dependent manner

Molecular docking was performed to elucidate the mode of Nrf2 activation. As shown in Figure 6A and B, DCSA could enter the Nrf2 binding cavity of Keap1 Kelch domain and interact with the amino acid residues including Leu365 (2.0 Å), Asn382 (2.1 Å), Asn414 (2.0, 2.1, and 2.5 Å) and Arg415 (2.1 and 2.5 Å) via hydrogen bonds. Meanwhile, from the 2D interaction diagram π-π stacking, hydrophobic interaction, electrostatic effect and Van der Waals’ force were also found to be the driving forces for the formation of the DCSA-Keap1 complex (Figure 6C), which resulted in the activation of Nrf2. In addition, when Nrf2 was precipitated using its primary antibody, Keap1 in the control and high glucose groups was pulled down. However, in the presence of DCSA, Keap1 did not precipitate after Nrf2 activation. Meanwhile, rabbit IgG was used to confirm the specifity of primary antibodies herein (Figure 6D). These results revealed that Nrf2 activation by DCSA was Keap1-dependent.

DCSA protects renal function of diabetic mice

Renal function of db/db mice was evaluated to explore the effects of DCSA in vivo. Compared with db/m mice, the body weight, kidney weight, FGB, BUN, Scr and UACR in db/db mice were significantly elevated (Figure 7A–F). H&E staining showed abnormal morphological changes in glomerular volume and lumen expansion (Figure 7G). However, in the presence of DCSA, renal dysfunction in db/db mice was attenuated, even though ML385 suppressed the effects of DCSA. These results indicate that DCSA protects the kidneys of diabetic mice.

As shown in Figure 8A, more MDA was produced in db/db mice than in db/m mice (Figure 8A). DCSA reduced the formation of MDA in the kidneys of diabetic mice, while ML385 blocked the effects of DCSA. Meanwhile, DCSA enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, and GPx, although these activities were inhibited in db/db mice (Figure 8B–D). However, treatment with ML385 decreased the antioxidant capacity of DCSA in db/db mice. These observations imply that DCSA inhibits oxidative stress in the kidneys of diabetic mice via activating Nrf2.

To check the effects of DCSA the renal fibrosis, we first determined ECM proteins in the kidneys of db/db mice. Compared with db/m mice, collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin were synthesized in the kidneys of db/db mice, but DCSA reduced the overproduction of these proteins, although ML385 markedly disrupted the effects of DCSA (Figure 9A–C). Masson staining together with the statistical analysis further revealed that fibrosis in the kidneys of diabetic mice was attenuated by DCSA via Nrf2 activation (Figure 9D and E). As a key fibrogenic cytokine, TGF-β1 was monitored herein. Immunohistochemical staining showed TGF-β1 expression was enhanced in the kidneys of db/db mice, whereas DCSA remarkably reduced its secretion. In addition, ML385 inhibited the effects of DCSA (Figure 9D). At the same time, western blot analysis, together with densitometric analysis, also showed that DCSA repressed the excessive expression of TGF-β1 in the kidneys of db/db mice, and the effects were reduced when exposed to ML385 (Figure 9F and G).

Discussion

As one of the most common microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus, DN is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease [25]. Clinically, DN is characterized by proteinuria and progressive renal function damage [6]. Histopathological investigations have revealed glomerular basement membrane thickening and mesangial expansion, with mesangial cell proliferation and ECM accumulation [26]. In the early stage of DN, high glucose induces self-limited proliferation of mesangial cells, which contributes to glomerular hypertrophy [27]. Herein, we observed that DCSA inhibited the proliferation of mesangial cells under high glucose. Meanwhile, DCSA can attenuate the renal morphological changes and improved renal function in diabetic mice. Following the presence of ML385, these effects of DCSA were reduced, which implied renal protection for DCSA was closely associated with Nrf2 activation.

Oxidative stress resulting from aberrant glucose metabolism plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of DN [28]. High blood glucose and abnormal metabolism lead to overproduction of ROS [29]. In the mitochondria, ROS are generated as superoxide anions from the one-electron reduction of molecular oxygen through the electron transport chain or the action of NADPH oxidases [30]. Superoxide can then be converted into hydrogen peroxide under the catalysis of SOD, and the latter is decomposed to water by CAT or GPx [31]. In addition to irreversible damage to cellular macromolecules, excessive ROS mediates renal fibrosis in DN via upregulating the synthesis of TGF-β1 [32]. Herein, we found that DCSA inhibited the overproduction of ROS and decreased the excessive formation of MDA in mesangial cells under high glucose. At the same time, DCSA increased the activities of direct antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, and GPx, which were even more than those in control group since 10 μM is the maximum dose for DCSA in our cell viability assay. And after Nrf2 knockdown, the antioxidant effects from DCSA was significantly suppressed. Then we observed that DCSA protected the kidneys of diabetic mice against oxidative stress since DCSA reduced renal MDA and elevated the activities of renal SOD, CAT, and GPx. But ML385 repressed the antioxidant effects of DCSA. Therefore, it can be derived that DCSA can inhibited oxidative stress in vitro and in vivo via activating Nrf2.

Renal fibrosis remains the key pathological change in DN, which is closely associated with excessive secretion and accumulation of ECM [33,34]. TGF-β1 is the predominant isoform of the TGF-β family that is involved in renal fibrosis [35]. The synthesis of ECM proteins including collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin is mainly regulated by TGF-β1 and contributes to basement membrane thickening and mesangial expansion, which results in changes in glomerular structure and function [36]. In addition, Smad3 is a critical mediator of TGF-β signaling and plays a pathogenic role in renal fibrosis [37]. Smad3 can be activated by TGF-β1 through phosphorylation and interact with other signaling pathways such as MAPK and NF-κB to mediate fibrosis [38]. In this study, we found that DCSA inactivated Smad3 in mesangial cells under high glucose via activating Nrf2 due to the addition of ML385. And DCSA also ameliorated renal fibrosis by downregulating TGF-β1 in vitro, whereas these effects of DCSA were prevented while Nrf2 was knocked down. Furthermore, renal fibrosis due to the accumulation of ECM in diabetic mice was attenuated following the addition of DCSA, that involved down-regulation of TGF-β1. In addition, ML385 impeded the inhibitory effects of DCSA on renal fibrosis. Collectively, it can be implied DCSA attenuated fibrosis of DN in vitro and in vivo through activating Nrf2.

As an important transcription factor, Nrf2 promotes the transcription of target genes encoding antioxidant enzymes via binding to their ARE regions [39]. Antioxidant systems are essential in balancing redox homeostasis. A main part of the antioxidant defense is performed by enzymes, such as CAT, SOD and GPx. In the pathogenesis of DN, high glucose has resulted in the surplus ROS and generation of oxidative stress. High level of ROS reshapes normal cellular function, causes alterations in the protein and lipid metabolism, and impairs mitochondrial activity. Therefore, normal Nrf2 together with antioxidant enzymes will be damaged and lose their function [40]. In this study, it was observed that there was no significant difference in the cells transfected with NC-siRNA and transfected with Nrf2-siRNA under high glucose, which means a failure of antioxidant mechanisms. Meanwhile, Nrf2 activation and translocation, as well as enhancement of downstream antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and NQO1, were observed in vitro and in vivo. When Nrf2 was blocked, the effects of DCSA were remarkably reduced, indicating Nrf2 activation is involved in the effects of DCSA in vitro and in vivo.

Structural analysis of the Keap1-Nrf2 complex showed that two Keap1 monomers dimerize using their BTB domains, and the homodimer interacts with DLG and ETGE motifs of one Nrf2 molecule using the Kelch domains [41]. Since the affinity of DLG for the Kelch domain is much lower than that of ETGE, the ‘hinge and latch’ model was established to describe Nrf2 binding to Keap1 [42]. If the Nrf2 binding cavity of a Kelch domain is occupied, Nrf2 will be free to activate [43]. Based on molecular dynamics simulations and free energy calculations, the Nrf2 binding cavity of a Kelch domain can be divided into six subpockets (P1-P6) [44]. Small molecules interacting with the residues in these subpockets hinder the formation of the Keap1-Nrf2 complex to prevent the degradation of Nrf2, which results in the activation of Nrf2 [45]. Among the residues interacting with DCSA, Asn414 and Asn382 belong to subpocket P2 and Arg415 belongs to P1. In addition, electrophiles can react with cysteine residues in both the BTB and IVR domains of Keap1, resulting in changes in Keap1 conformation. As a result, the hinge and latch models are destroyed and the degradation of Nrf2 is interrupted [46]. Michael receptors are electrophiles with olefin structures conjugated to carbonyl groups. As soft Lewis acids, Michael receptors can react with the critical cysteine thiolate (soft base) groups in Keap1 to induce Nrf2 activation [47]. From the structure of DCSA, it is found that there is an α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety that may be engaged in the induction of Nrf2 activation. Herein, it can be concluded that the effects of DCSA result from the activation of Nrf2 in a Keap1-dependent manner. But the detailed mechanisms for DCSA interacting with Keap1 is not fully clarified, since it is not validated that DCSA binds to Keap1 via covalent or non-covalent mode. Our future work will focus on the exocellular biophysical experiments and establishment of specific mutated cell lines to reveal that issue, and the results will be reported in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, we evaluated DCSA identified from Drynaria fortunei for its effects on DN using mesangial cells cultured in high glucose and db/db mice and explored the underlying mechanisms. The results showed DCSA inhibited the proliferation of mesangial cells induced by high glucose and improved renal function in diabetic mice. DCSA attenuated oxidative stress and fibrosis in vitro and in vivo. The effects of DCSA were closely associated with Nrf2 activation in a Keap1-dependent manner. These findings provide novel insights into the discovery of Nrf2 activators and new therapeutic approaches for DN treatment.
