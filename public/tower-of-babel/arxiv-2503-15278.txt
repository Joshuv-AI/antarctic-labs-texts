Symplectic integration of guiding-center equations in canonical coordinates for general toroidal fields
Christopher G. Albert 1, Georg S. Graßler 1, Sergei V. Kasilov 1,2, Markus Markl 1, Jonatan Schatzlmayr 1

Abstract
Symplectic integrators with long-term preservation of integrals of motion are introduced for the guiding-center model of plasma particles in toroidal magnetic fields of general topology. An efficient transformation to canonical coordinates from cylindrical and flux-like coordinates is discussed and applied using one component of the magnetic vector potential as a spatial coordinate. This choice is efficient in both, theoretical and numerical developments and marks a generalization of magnetic flux coordinates. The transformation enables the application of conventional symplectic integration schemes formulated in canonical coordinates, as well as variational integrators on the guiding-center system, without requiring magnetic flux coordinates. Symplectic properties and superior efficiency of the implicit midpoint scheme compared to conventional non-symplectic methods are demonstrated on perturbed tokamak fields with magnetic islands and stochastic regions. The presented results mark a crucial step towards gyrokinetic models that conserve physical invariants.



I
Introduction

Magnetized plasmas appear in nature as well as science and technology,
notably magnetic confinement fusion. One of the foundations of plasma
theory and computation is the tracing of charged particle orbits in
electromagnetic fields. In the case of strong magnetization, their
gyrofrequency
\omega_{c}
is large and their gyroradius
\rho_{L}

is small compared to other scales of frequency and length in the system.
This separation of scales led to the introduction of the guiding-center
system, where the rapid gyromotion
\phi
appears as an ignorable
variable together with the perpendicular adiabatic invariant
J_{\perp}

as a constant of motion. Within its range of validity, the guiding-center
ansatz has two main features: it reduces the dimension of the relevant
phase-space and allows numerical treatment at larger time-steps than
the gyration period
2\pi/\omega_{c}
. However, these features come at a price
– the loss of canonical coordinates.

Why are canonical coordinates important? First, they form the basis for analytical treatment in terms of action-angle variables
(
Hazeltine et al. 1981
;
Mahajan and Chen 1985
;
Cary et al. 1988
;
Chavanis 2007
)
.
Analytical and semi-analytical methods can describe resonant
wave-particle interaction and the emergence of Hamiltonian
chaos
(
Chirikov 1979
;
Abdullaev et al. 2006
)
. Second, canonical coordinates
underlie an important class of numerical methods:
symplectic integrators
(
Hairer et al. 2006
)
. Such integrators
and their generalizations retain a discrete symplectic structure of
phase-space and thereby essential features of continuous Hamiltonian
systems. This includes conservation of energy and momentum within
fixed bounds. In application to alpha particle losses in stellarator
optimization, symplectic integrators outperform conventional
methods by factor 3-5 in computing time and do not require high integration accuracy for orbit classification methods based on the properties of Hamiltonian systems
(
Albert et al. 2020a
;
Albert et al. 2020b
;
Albert et al. 2023
)
.

Symplectic integrators are a subset of more general structure-preserving
methods. Such approaches based on non-canonical coordinates include
variational integrators
(
Qin and Guan 2008
;
Qin et al. 2009
;
Morrison 2017
;
Kraus 2017
;
Burby and Ellison 2017
;
Ellison et al. 2018
)
,
methods based on exact integration in lowest-order fields
(
Eder et al. 2020
;
Schatzlmayr et al. 2024
)

and methods based on slow manifolds
(
Burby and Hirvijoki 2021
;
Xiao and Qin 2021
)
,
line integrals
(
Brugnano et al. 2020
)
and average vector fields

(
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Due to the special nature
of the guiding-center system being a degenerate Hamiltonian system
formulated in non-canonical coordinates, various methods
come with specific limitations.
The schemes are either not fully symplectic, e.g. preserve only energy,
are of low order, or require stabilization. Straightforward application
of standard symplectic integrators in canonical coordinates is
of interest to obtain simple, reliable and fully structure-preserving
integration schemes.

For an
explicit
transformation with canonical coordinates given
as functions of non-canonical ones, it is well known
(
Hairer et al. 2006
)

that symplectic integrators can be applied in a straight-forward manner.
This has been leveraged for analytical approximations of fields in
tokamak plasmas
(
Khan et al. 2012
;
Khan et al. 2015
;
Khan et al. 2017
)
. Also
for an
implicit
transformation to canonical coordinates it
is possible to formulate symplectic integrators. The original idea
by
Cary 1989
of solving the implicit transformation together
with the implicit time-step of a symplectic integrator has
been picked up recently
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Zhu et al. 2016
;
Albert et al. 2020a
)
.
An implicit transformation to canonical coordinates is also required to preserve
symplectic structure in variational integrators
(
Kraus 2017
;
Burby and Ellison 2017
;
Ellison et al. 2018
)
. There, the transformation to a form where canonical momenta can be identified is referred to as a
gauge
choice with an existence proof. Below, we describe a procedure to actually construct this transformation in practice.

Implicit spatial transformations to obtain canonical guiding-center
coordinates have been realized for magnetic flux coordinates to first
order in gyroradius
(
White and Chance 1984
;
Boozer 2005
;
Boozer 2015
)

and exactly
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2016
;
Albert et al. 2020a
)
. Such coordinates
exist only in configurations with nested magnetic flux surfaces and
exclude, for example, the tracing of particles up to the wall of a
magnetic confinement configuration with a divertor. Also in the case of magnetic perturbations,
flux surface geometry is destroyed, and the topology of the magnetic
field includes islands and ergodic regions.
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
also provide a canonicalization in general field topology as a series expansion that requires a transformation in both, guiding-center position and velocity space coordinates.

A general and straightforward construction of canonical coordinates by a purely spatial transformation has been proposed
by
Meiss and Hazeltine 1990
. This procedure consists of two steps:
a coordinate transformation to eliminate one covariant component of
the magnetic field, and a gauge transformation for the according covariant
vector potential component. In the present work, we build on that
approach of “Meiss-Hazeltine coordinates” with three adjustments for practical applicability:
1) The toroidal angle is modified instead of a poloidal variable.
This directly yields a unique-valued transformation in usual toroidal
magnetic confinement devices where the toroidal magnetic field component
is never zero. 2) Based on this variant, canonical coordinates of
cylindrical topology are constructed in addition to toroidal flux-like coordinates.
3) One vector potential component is used as the radial variable, thereby
simplifying analytical derivations and numerical implementations.
In particular, we show that the obtained coordinates are a generalization
of magnetic flux coordinates.

The described spatial coordinate transformation and resulting symplectic integrators are implemented
in the code SIMPLE
(
Albert et al. 2020a
;
Albert et al. 2020b
)
.
Results from numerical experiments confirm the validity and efficiency
of symplectic orbit integration in both, tokamaks with broken symmetry and stellarators with magnetic islands and stochastic
regions.

II
Transformation to canonical coordinates

We write the guiding-center Lagrangian
L_{\mathrm{gc}}
with Hamiltonian

H
skipping the term
J_{\perp}\dot{\phi}
of the ignorable pair
as

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{gc}}(\bm{x},z^{4})=

\displaystyle\frac{e_{\alpha}}{c}\left(A_{1}^{\star}(\bm{x},z^{4})\dot{x}^{1}+A_{2}^{\star}(\bm{x},z^{4})\dot{x}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+A_{3}^{\star}(\bm{x},z^{4})\dot{x}^{3}\right)-H(\bm{x},z^{4})

(1)

in terms of non-canonical coordinates
\bm{z}=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3},z^{4})
,
where

A_{i}^{\star}(\bm{x},z^{4})=\frac{v_{\parallel}(\bm{x},z^{4})}{\omega_{c\alpha}(\bm{x})}B_{i}(\bm{x})+A_{i}(\bm{x})

(2)

contain both, covariant components
A_{i}
of the vector potential
and
B_{i}
of the magnetic field. In addition, parallel velocity

v_{\parallel}
, speed of light
c
, species charge
e_{\alpha}

and gyrofrequency
\omega_{c\alpha}
appear. Choices of the fourth
phase-space coordinate
z^{4}
besides three spatial variables
\bm{x}

include
v_{\parallel}
itself, Hamiltonian
H
, or the toroidal
canonical momentum
p_{\varphi}
. As a first step
(
Meiss and Hazeltine 1990
;
Albert et al. 2020a
)
,
we transform to new spatial coordinates
x_{\text{c}}^{1},x_{\text{c}}^{2},x_{\text{c}}^{3}

(subscript “c” for “canonical”) with the requirement to eliminate the first covariant component of

\bm{B}
, so

\displaystyle B_{1\mathrm{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x^{i}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}B_{i}(\bm{x}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}}))\overset{!}{=}0.

(3)

Assuming prescribed transformations for two components
x^{1}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})

and
x^{3}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})
this requires

\frac{\partial x^{2}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}=-\frac{\frac{\partial x^{1}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}B_{1}(\bm{x}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}}))+\frac{\partial x^{3}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}B_{3}(\bm{x}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}}))}{B_{2}(\bm{x}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}}))},

(4)

under the restriction that
B_{2}
never vanishes. For any fixed pair
x_{\text{c}}^{2},x_{\text{c}}^{3}
, Eq. (
4
) is a nonlinear ordinary differential equation with independent variable

x_{\text{c}}^{1}
.

To eliminate the component
A_{1}^{\text{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})
in Eq. (
2
), a gauge transform
\bm{A}^{\text{c}}=\bm{A}+\nabla\chi
is applied
(
Meiss and Hazeltine 1990
)
in addition to the covariant coordinate transform. Thus, condition
A_{1}^{\text{c}}=0
yields an ordinary differential equation for the gauge function
\chi(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial x^{i}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}}A_{i}(\bm{x}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})).

(5)

with
\partial x^{2}/\partial x_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}
given by Eq. (
4
) .
The solution of Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) is found via
a generic integrator for ordinary differential equations. Thus, we
have eliminated
A_{1}^{\star\text{c}}
in Eq. (
2
), which allows us to identify canonical
momenta

p_{2}=\frac{e_{\alpha}}{c}A_{2}^{\star\text{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},z^{4})\text{ and }p_{3}=\frac{e_{\alpha}}{c}A_{3}^{\star\text{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},z^{4})

(6)

paired to
x_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
and
x_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}
, respectively,
inside
L_{\mathrm{gc}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},z^{4})
in Eq. (
1
). Note that this formulation
still contains two generally non-canonical coordinates,
z^{1}=x_{\text{c}}^{1}

and
z^{4}
. Now, values of these coordinates can be computed implicitly
from Eq. (
6
) for points given in canonical coordinates

(x_{\text{c}}^{2},p_{2},x_{\text{c}}^{3},p_{3})
. This enables the application of symplectic
(
Albert et al. 2020a
)
and variational
(
Burby and Ellison 2017
;
Ellison et al. 2018
)
integrators to the guiding-center system.

Let us now consider special cases useful in toroidal magnetic configurations,
such as tokamaks or stellarators with non-zero toroidal component
B_{\varphi}
everywhere in the domain of interest. We fix
x^{2}=\varphi
to a toroidal angle and choose a transformation with

\displaystyle x^{1}

\displaystyle=x_{\text{c}}^{1},

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\text{c}}+\lambda(x_{\text{c}}^{1},\varphi_{\text{c}},x_{\text{c}}^{3}),

\displaystyle x^{3}

\displaystyle=x_{\text{c}}^{3}.

(7)

In cylindrical-type coordinates, we have
x^{1}=R,\,x^{3}=Z
, and
in flux-like coordinates,
x^{1}=r
is a radial coordinate and
x^{3}=\vartheta

a poloidal angle. Eqs. (
4
-
5
) become

\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial x_{\text{c}}^{1}}=-\frac{B_{1}}{B_{\varphi}},\quad\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial x_{\text{c}}^{1}}=-A_{1}+\frac{B_{1}}{B_{\varphi}}A_{\varphi}.

(8)

In an additional step, we replace
x_{\text{c}}^{1}
by using the vector
potential component

A_{3}^{\text{c}}=A_{3}+\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial x_{\text{c}}^{3}}A_{\varphi}+\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial x_{\text{c}}^{3}}\equiv\psi_{\text{c}}

(9)

to replace the first coordinate
x_{\text{c}}^{1}
(
A_{3}^{\text{c}}
is a monotonous function of the original
x^{1}_{\text{c}}
due to non-vanishing contra-variant toroidal field component
B^{\varphi}_{c}
as follows from
\partial A_{3}^{\text{c}}/\partial x^{1}_{\text{c}}=-\sqrt{g_{c}}B^{\varphi}_{c}
). This step does
not affect values of covariant vector field components. Similar to
a magnetic flux label, using
\psi_{\text{c}}
as a coordinate has
the advantage that
A_{3}^{\text{c}}
will depend (identically) on
\psi_{\text{c}}

only. Following the convention of magnetic flux coordinates, we finally
rename
x_{\text{c}}^{3}\equiv\vartheta_{\text{c}}
. In these final spatial coordinates

\bm{x}_{\text{c}}=(\psi_{\text{c}},\varphi_{\text{c}},\vartheta_{\text{c}})
we express the metric
determinant by magnetic quantities via

\sqrt{g_{\text{c}}}=\frac{1}{B^{2}}\left(B_{\vartheta}^{\text{c}}\frac{\partial A_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}-B_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}\right).

(10)

Combined with the choice
z^{4}\equiv A_{\varphi}^{\star\text{c}}=p_{\varphi}
,
the guiding-center Lagrangian takes the form

L_{\mathrm{gc}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})=p_{\varphi}\dot{\varphi}_{\text{c}}+p_{\vartheta}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})\dot{\vartheta}-H(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi}).

(11)

Here, poloidal canonical momentum and parallel velocity respectively are

\displaystyle p_{\vartheta}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=m_{\alpha}v_{\parallel}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})\frac{B_{\vartheta}^{\text{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{B(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}+\frac{e_{\alpha}}{c}\psi_{\text{c}},

(12)

\displaystyle v_{\parallel}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\frac{B(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}{m_{\alpha}B^{\text{c}}_{\varphi}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})}\left(p_{\varphi}-\frac{e_{\alpha}}{c}A_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})\right),

(13)

and the Hamiltonian is

H(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})=\frac{m_{\alpha}v_{\parallel}^{\,2}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})}{2}+J_{\perp}\omega_{c\alpha}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}})+e_{\alpha}\Phi_{e}(\bm{x}_{\text{c}}),

(14)

with perpendicular invariant
J_{\perp}
and electric potential

\Phi_{e}
. Magnetic field line equations are recovered from the Euler-Lagrange equations obtained from Eqs. (
11
-
14
) with
J_{\perp}=\Phi_{e}=0
using
z^{4}=v_{\parallel}
instead of
p_{\varphi}
.
Only two out of three equations corresponding to spatial variables are independent in the limit
m_{\alpha}/e_{\alpha}\rightarrow 0
,

\dot{\psi}_{\text{c}}=\frac{\partial A_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}}{\partial\vartheta_{\text{c}}}\dot{\varphi}_{\text{c}},\qquad\dot{\vartheta}_{\text{c}}=-\frac{\partial A_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\dot{\varphi}_{\text{c}},

(15)

and the fourth equation is
B_{\vartheta}^{\text{c}}\dot{\vartheta}_{\text{c}}+B_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}\dot{\varphi}_{\text{c}}=Bv_{\parallel}
, so that
\dot{x}^{k}_{\text{c}}=v_{\parallel}B^{k}_{\text{c}}/B
. Replacing time with a new independent variable
\varphi_{\text{c}}
, Eqs. (
15
) turn into field line equations in the canonical Hamiltonian form for a gauge
with
A_{1}^{\text{c}}=0
(see, e.g.,
(
Abdullaev et al. 2006
;
Boozer 2015
)
).
In addition, our toroidal angle
\varphi_{\text{c}}
is different
from the cylinder angle
\varphi
, which is not necessary for the
canonical representation of field lines, but essential for guiding-centers
where we require
B_{1}^{\text{c}}=0
via Eq. (
4
).

III
Symplectic orbit integration

Symplectic integration schemes with an implicit transformation to canonical coordinates
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Albert et al. 2020a
)
have been implemented
on the new canonical coordinates in the code SIMPLE. Here, the implicit transformation
from canonical coordinates (
6
) is solved together with the canonical symplectic time-stepping scheme for the Hamiltonian flow. For the guiding-center system in the present coordinates
\bm{z}=(\psi_{\text{c}},\varphi_{\text{c}},\vartheta_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})
, the Hamiltonian flow for the according canonical coordinates
\bm{z}_{\text{c}}=(p_{\vartheta},\varphi_{\text{c}},\vartheta_{\text{c}},p_{\varphi})
is given by canonical phase-space velocity components
V_{\text{c}}^{k}
as functions of non-canonical
\bm{z}
(Eqs. (63-66) of
Albert et al. 2020a
),

\displaystyle V_{\text{c}}^{p_{\vartheta}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vartheta}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\vartheta},

\displaystyle V_{\text{c}}^{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{B}{B_{\varphi}^{\text{c}}}\left(v_{\parallel}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\right)^{-1}\frac{B_{\vartheta}^{\text{c}}}{B}\right),

\displaystyle V_{\text{c}}^{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle V_{\text{c}}^{p_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varphi}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\psi_{\text{c}}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial\varphi}.

(16)

For the symplectic midpoint rule
Hairer et al. 2006
;
Zhang et al. 2014
with timestep
h
, implicit equations in non-canonical
\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)}
that represent the midpoint
\frac{1}{2}(\bm{z}_{\text{c},(n)}+\bm{z}_{\text{c},(n+1)})
in canonical coordinates are

\displaystyle p_{\vartheta}(\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)})

\displaystyle=p_{\vartheta,(n)}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}V_{\text{c}}^{p_{\vartheta}}\left(\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle z^{k}_{(n+1/2)}

\displaystyle=z^{k}_{\text{c},(n)}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}V_{\text{c}}^{k}\left(\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)}\right),\quad k>1,

(18)

where the left-hand side of Eq. (
17
) is computed from Eq. (
12
). The remaining equations for the next step
(n+1)
become explicit with

z^{k}_{\text{c},(n+1)}=z^{k}_{\text{c},(n)}+\Delta t\,V_{\text{c}}^{k}\left(\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)}\right),

(19)

re-using values of the phase-space velocity (
16
) evaluated at the midpoint
\bm{z}_{(n+1/2)}
.

Orbits are traced in the magnetic field of a typical medium-sized tokamak configuration with a lower single-null divertor and resonant magnetic perturbations as in Refs
(
Martitsch et al. 2016
;
Suttrop et al. 2018
)
. We compare the symplectic scheme to two non-symplectic adaptive methods: Runge-Kutta-Fehlberg of order 4/5 and Dormand&Prince of order 8/5/3
(
Hairer et al. 1993
;
Dormand 2018
)
. Each setup has been chosen such that the total number of magnetic field evaluations is similar for all compared integration methods.

. While the symplectic midpoint integrator retains the shape and integrals of motion, the non-symplectic methods deviate up to a destruction of the orbit topology.

This is especially relevant for transport induced by such resonances, that can be shown to be a main mechanism for the loss of alpha particles

(

IV
Conclusion and Outlook

Symplectic integration of the guiding-center system of charged particle dynamics
in strong magnetic fields has been realized via an effective transformation
to canonical coordinates. Starting with a short review of previously
available methods, we derived a variant of Meiss-Hazeltine coordinates for this purpose.
In contrast to related approaches, this transformation requires neither
nested magnetic flux surfaces nor a full phase-space transformation.
Starting with the general description, we pursue a realization for cylindrical
coordinates. Based on these canonical coordinates, we implemented
the symplectic midpoint rule based on the SIMPLE algorithm. For realistic
conditions in a tokamak field with resonant magnetic perturbations,
the method outperforms conventional non-symplectic integrators to
a similar degree as observed in previous works. This is significant
for efficient and physics-consistent numerical implementations of
drift- and gyrokinetic models from the plasma core to the wall. Due
to their similarity to canonical magnetic flux coordinates,
the new canonical coordinates are also of interest to generalize
theoretical results that previously relied on nested magnetic flux
surfaces.
