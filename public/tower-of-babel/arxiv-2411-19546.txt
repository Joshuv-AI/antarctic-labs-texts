Fundamental bounds on precision and response for quantum trajectory observables
Tan Van Vu

Abstract
The precision and response of trajectory observables offer valuable insights into the behavior of nonequilibrium systems. For classical systems, trade-offs between these characteristics and thermodynamic costs, such as entropy production and dynamical activity, have been established through uncertainty relations. Quantum systems, however, present unique challenges, where quantum coherence can enhance precision and violate classical uncertainty relations. In this study, we derive trade-off relations for stochastic observables in Markovian open quantum systems. Specifically, we present three key results: (i) a quantum generalization of the thermo-kinetic uncertainty relation, which bounds the relative fluctuations of currents in terms of entropy production and dynamical activity; (ii) a quantum inverse uncertainty relation, which constrains the relative fluctuations of arbitrary counting observables based on their instantaneous fluctuations and the spectral gap of the symmetrized Liouvillian; and (iii) a quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation, which bounds the response of general observables to kinetic perturbations in terms of dynamical activity. These fundamental bounds, validated numerically using a three-level maser and a boundary-driven XXZ spin chain, provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the interplay between precision, response, and thermodynamic costs in quantum systems.

In this paper, we advance the understanding of quantum trajectory observables by deriving fundamental bounds for their precision and response, building upon the aforementioned two backgrounds. Focusing on Markovian open quantum dynamics with quantum jump and diffusion unravelings, we present three main results (see Table

Notably, this relation is quantitatively tighter than the KUR and recovers the KUR in the classical limit. Our findings are validated numerically using a three-level maser engine and a quantum many-body spin system.

II
Setup

We consider a
d
-dimensional open quantum system, whose dynamics is described by the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
[
75
,
76
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{\varrho}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\varrho_{t}),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\circ)

\displaystyle\coloneq-i\commutator{H}{\circ}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k\circ L_k^\dagger-\acomm{L_k^\dagger L_k}{\circ}/2).

Here,
\varrho_{t}
denotes the system’s density matrix at time
t
, and both Hamiltonian
H
and jump operators
\{L_{k}\}
are time-independent.
We assume that after a sufficiently long time, the system relaxes toward a unique stationary state
\pi
, which can be nonequilibrium.
Throughout this study, both the Planck constant and Boltzmann constant are set to unity,
\hbar=k_{B}=1
.

We mainly focus on two scenarios that can be described by the equation (
2
).
The first is thermodynamically dissipative dynamics, wherein the system is attached to single or multiple heat baths.
Assuming that the coupling between the system and the baths is weak and the environment is memoryless, the time evolution of the system’s state is governed by the GKSL equation.
Each jump operator
L_{k}
can, for example, characterize a jump between the energy eigenstates.
To guarantee the thermodynamic consistency for thermodynamically dissipative dynamics, we assume the local detailed balance condition
[
77
]
, which is fulfilled in most cases of physical interest
[
78
]
.
That is, each jump operator
L_{k}
can be associated with a reversed jump
L_{k^{*}}
such that
L_{k}=e^{\Delta s_{k}/2}L_{k^{*}}^{\dagger}
.
Here,
\Delta s_{k}
denotes the entropy change of the environment due to the jump.
Note that
k^{*}=k
is possible (i.e.,
L_{k}
is a self-adjoint operator and
\Delta s_{k}=0
).
The second scenario is generic Markovian dynamics, where we do not impose any conditions on jump operators, and they can have arbitrary forms.
Examples include quantum measurement processes, wherein
L_{k}
represents a measurement operator performed on the system
[
79
]
.

We remark on the validity and applicability of the GKSL dynamics. Its derivation from microscopic principles relies on the Born-Markov approximation, which assumes weak coupling between the system and the environment, as well as a fast relaxation of the environment
[
80
]
. Consequently, it fails to describe non-Markovian dynamics, where memory effects and finite environmental relaxation times play a crucial role. This limitation restricts its applicability to strongly interacting systems, where system-environment correlations and entanglement become significant. Despite these constraints, the GKSL dynamics remains a fundamental framework in nonequilibrium physics due to its mathematical tractability and its ability to capture key aspects of dissipative processes, quantum decoherence, and steady-state behavior
[
80
,
81
]
. It continues to serve as an indispensable tool for studying open quantum systems in controlled experimental and theoretical settings.

II.1
Quantum jump unraveling and observables

The dynamics of Markovian open quantum systems can be unraveled into quantum jump trajectories
[
82
,
77
,
83
,
84
]
.
That is, the GKSL dynamics (
2
) can be interpreted as a stochastic process of the pure state
\ket{\psi_t}
such that
\varrho_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\outerproduct{\psi_t}{\psi_t}]
, where the average
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
is taken over all stochastic trajectories.
The method of quantum jumps is a convenient way to describe the evolution of a quantum system that is constantly monitored, particularly when it is coupled to dissipative environments or is subject to continuous measurements.
For a small time step
dt\ll 1
, the master equation
\varrho_{t+dt}=(\mathbb{1}+\mathcal{L}dt)\varrho_{t}
can be expressed in the Kraus representation
\varrho_{t+dt}=\sum_{k\geq 0}M_{k}\varrho_{t}M_{k}^{\dagger}
with the operators given by

M_{0}\coloneq\mathbb{1}-iH_{\rm eff}dt,~M_{k}\coloneq L_{k}\sqrt{dt}~(k\geq 1).

(3)

Here,
H_{\rm eff}\coloneqq H-(i/2)\sum_{k\geq 1}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
is the effective Hamiltonian and
\mathbb{1}
denotes the identity operator.
The operator
M_{0}
represents a smooth nonunitary evolution, whereas operators
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
induce probabilistic jumps in the system state.
With this interpretation, the GKSL equation (
2
) can be unraveled into stochastic trajectories, wherein the pure state
\ket{\psi_t}
either smoothly evolves or discontinuously jumps to another pure state at random times.
Specifically, the time evolution of the pure state
\ket{\psi_t}
can be described by the stochastic Schrödinger equation
[
80
]
,

\displaystyle d\ket{\psi_t}

\displaystyle=\quantity(-iH_{\rm eff}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\ge 1}\ev{L_k^\dagger L_k}_t)\ket{\psi_t}dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(\frac{L_k\ket{\psi_t}}{\sqrt{\ev{L_k^\dagger L_k}_t}}-\ket{\psi_t})dN_{k,t},

(4)

where
\expectationvalue{\circ}_{t}\coloneqq\matrixelement{\psi_t}{\circ}{\psi_t}
and
dN_{k,t}
is a stochastic increment that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[dN_{k,t}]=\expectationvalue{L_k^\dagger L_k}_{t}dt
and takes value of
1
if
k
th jump is detected and
0
otherwise.

Each stochastic trajectory
\{\ket{\psi_t}\}
of time duration
\tau
is uniquely determined by the noise trajectory
\{dN_{k,t}\}
.
Therefore, we can define a time-integrated observable
\phi
for individual trajectories as

\phi\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{k\geq 1}dN_{k,t}c_{k},

(5)

where
\{c_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are real counting coefficients.
By this definition, the observable
\phi
increases by
c_{k}
for each
k
th jump occurred.
For thermodynamically dissipative dynamics,
\phi
is called a
current
whenever the counting variables are antisymmetric (i.e.,
c_{k}=-c_{k^{*}}
for all
k
).
Examples of relevant observables include particle current (
c_{k}=1
for absorption and
c_{k}=-1
for emission), jump activity (
c_{k}=1
for all jumps), and heat flux (
c_{k}=q_{k}
where
q_{k}
is heat dissipated from the system to the environment due to
k
th jump).
The average of the counting observable can be analytically calculated as

\expectationvalue{\phi}=\tau\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr(L_k\pi L_k^\dagger).

(6)

The higher-order moments of observable
\phi
can be computed using the method of full counting statistics.
Defining the generating function
G_{\tau}(u)\coloneqq\tr e^{\mathcal{L}_{u}\tau}(\pi)
, the
n
th moment can be calculated as

\expectationvalue{\phi^n}=(-i\partial_{u})^{n}G_{\tau}(u)\big|_{u=0},

(7)

where the tilted super-operator
\mathcal{L}_{u}
is given by
[
1
]

\mathcal{L}_{u}(\varrho)\coloneqq-i[H,\varrho]+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(e^{iuc_k}L_k\varrho L_k^\dagger-\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\varrho\}/2).

(8)

It is worth noting that the stochastic unraveling of the GKSL dynamics is not unique, as it depends on the choice of measurement on the environment
[
79
]
. While our primary focus in this study is on quantum jump unraveling, we also demonstrate that similar results can be obtained for quantum diffusion unraveling using the same approach.

II.2
Entropy production and dynamical activity

We introduce two relevant quantities for thermodynamically dissipative dynamics and generic dynamics.
The first is irreversible entropy production, which quantifies the degree of time-reversal symmetry breaking
[
85
]
.
According to the framework of quantum thermodynamics, entropy production is generally defined as the sum of the entropic changes in the system and the environment
[
86
]
, and its rate is given by

\sigma\coloneqq\sigma_{\rm sys}+\sigma_{\rm env}.

(9)

Here,
\sigma_{\rm sys}
and
\sigma_{\rm env}
denote the entropic rate contributed by the system and the environment, respectively. In the generic setup where the system and the environment undergo a unitary evolution, the resulting entropy production can be expressed in terms of a correlation between the system and the environment
[
86
,
87
]
. For the weak coupling regime considered here, they are explicitly quantified as
[
77
]

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\tr(\dot\varrho_t\ln\varrho_t),

(10)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm env}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}\tr(L_k\varrho_tL_k^\dagger)\Delta s_{k}.

(11)

In the stationary state
\pi
[i.e.,
\mathcal{L}(\pi)=\mathbb{0}
], the entropic contribution from the system vanishes (i.e.,
\sigma_{\rm sys}=0
).
Thus, the irreversible entropy production rate is equal to the entropic rate produced by quantum jumps due to the interactions with the environment,

\sigma=\sum_{k\geq 1}\tr(L_k\pi L_k^\dagger)\Delta s_{k}.

(12)

We can prove that
\sigma\geq 0
, which is nothing but the second law of thermodynamics.
Another crucial quantity is dynamical activity
[
88
]
, which quantifies the frequency of quantum jumps that occurred in the system and can be explicitly calculated as

a=\sum_{k\geq 1}\tr(L_k\pi L_k^\dagger).

(13)

Physically, the dynamical activity characterizes the strength of the system’s thermalization.
These two quantities (
\sigma
and
a
) play essential roles in constraining the precision of observables in fluctuating dynamics and other nonequilibrium aspects such as the thermodynamic speed limit
[
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
]
.

II.3
Symmetrized Liouvillian gap

Next, we describe another relevant quantity called the symmetrized Liouvillian gap, which plays a crucial role in constraining the fluctuation of observables.
To this end, we introduce the following inner products:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{A,B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tr(A^\dagger B),

(14)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{A,B}_{s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tr(A^\dagger\pi^{s}B\pi^{1-s})

(15)

for
s\in[0,1]
.
We also define the norms
\|A\|^{2}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{A,A}
and
\|A\|_{s}^{2}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{A,A}_{s}
for the notational convenience.
We consider the adjoint time evolution of an operator
A_{t}
in the Heisenberg picture,
\dot{A}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(A_{t})
, where the super-operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\circ)\coloneqq i[H,\circ]+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k^\dagger\circ L_k-\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\circ\}/2).

(16)

This adjoint super-operator has the following property for any operators
A
and
B
:

\expectationvalue{A,\mca{L}(B)}=\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}(A),B}.

(17)

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
be the adjoint super-operator of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{s}
.
Specifically,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
is defined such that the following equality is fulfilled for arbitrary operators
A
and
B
:

\expectationvalue{A,\widetilde{\mca{L}}(B)}_{s}=\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}^*(A),B}_{s}.

(18)

The super-operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
can be explicitly expressed as
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}=\pi^{-s}\mathcal{L}(\pi^{s}\circ\pi^{1-s})\pi^{s-1}
.
The symmetrized Liouvillian is then defined as
[
93
]

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}\coloneqq\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}}{2},

(19)

which is self-adjoint with respect to the inner production
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{s}
,

\expectationvalue{A,\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(B)}_{s}=\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A),B}_{s}.

(20)

In the literature,
s=0
and
s=1/2
are the most studied cases.
Throughout this study, we only consider
s=0
and
s=1/2
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
possesses some relevant properties.
First, since
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\mathbb{1})=\mathbb{0}
, it is evident that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}(\mathbb{1})=\mathbb{0}
.
This means that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
has a zero eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenvector
\mathbb{1}
.
Second,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
is negative semi-definite with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{s}
.
For the
s=0
case, this was proved in Ref.
[
93
]
.
In Lemma
1
(Appendix
A
), we prove the
s=1/2
case.
Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
, which are sorted in the descending order as

0=\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d^{2}-1}.

(21)

The symmetrized Liouvillian gap is defined using the second largest eigenvalue,

g_{s}\coloneqq-\lambda_{1}>0.

(22)

The gap
g_{s}
quantifies the slowest decay mode of the generator.
As shown later, this gap is essential in constraining the relative fluctuation of observables.

III
Main results

III.1
Quantum thermo-kinetic uncertainty relation

We consider thermodynamically dissipative dynamics, wherein the local detailed balance condition is assumed.
Our first main result is the quantum TKUR, which establishes a lower bound on the relative fluctuation of an arbitrary
current

\phi
.
Applying the quantum Cramér-Rao inequality, we prove that the relative fluctuation of current
\phi
is always lower bounded by entropy production and dynamical activity as

\frac{F_{\phi}}{(1+\delta_{\phi})^{2}}\geq\frac{4a}{\sigma^{2}}\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2a})^{2}\geq\max\quantity(\frac{2}{\sigma},\frac{1}{a}),

(23)

where
\delta_{\phi}
is a contribution to the current average from quantum coherent dynamics [cf. Eq. (
79
)] and
\Phi(x)
denotes the inverse function of
x\tanh(x)
.
The relation universally holds for arbitrary times, and its second inequality is a consequence of
\Phi(x)\geq\max(\sqrt{x},x)
.
Using the vectorization of operators, the asymptotic long-time value of
\delta_{\phi}
can be explicitly given by

\delta_{\phi}=-\frac{(\mathbb{1}|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{+}\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}|\pi)}{(\mathbb{1}|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}|\pi)},

(24)

where
|A)\coloneqq\sum_{m,n}a_{mn}\ket{m}\otimes\ket{n}
for
A=\sum_{m,n}a_{mn}\outerproduct{m}{n}
,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the vectorized super-operator
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
[cf. Eq. (
72
)] and

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}\ell_{k}\quantity[ L_k\otimes L_k^* - \frac{1}{2} ( L_k^\dagger L_k )\otimes\mbb{1} - \frac{1}{2} \mbb{1}\otimes( L_k^\dagger L_k )^\top],

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}L_{k}\otimes L_{k}^{*}.

(25)

The real coefficients
\{\ell_{k}\}
are given in Eq. (
67
).
The detailed derivation of the result is presented in Appendix
B
.
We also demonstrate that a similar relation can be obtained for quantum diffusion unraveling, differing only in the quantum contribution
\delta_{\phi}
(see Appendix
B.1
).

Remarks on this result are given in order.
(i) First, in the classical limit, we can show that
\delta_{\phi}=0
[cf. Eq. (
85
)] and the result (
23
) reduces to

F_{\phi}\geq\frac{4a}{\sigma^{2}}\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2a})^{2}\geq\max\quantity(\frac{2}{\sigma},\frac{1}{a}),

(26)

which recovers the TKUR obtained in Ref.
[
29
]
and the conventional TUR
[
8
,
9
]
and KUR
[
25
,
26
]
for classical Markov jump processes. Therefore, it can be regarded as the quantum TKUR. (ii) Second, inequality (
23
) clearly illustrates the role of quantum coherent dynamics in enhancing the precision of currents. That is, the conventional TKUR is possibly violated if
-2<\delta_{\phi}<0
. Otherwise, the TKUR is valid whenever
|1+\delta_{\phi}|\geq 1
. (iii) Third, the bound can be further tightened by considering multiple currents
[
94
]
and leveraging their correlations, as has been done in the classical case
[
95
,
34
]
. (iv) Last, we compare our result with an extant uncertainty relation derived in Ref.
[
58
]
, which reads

\frac{F_{\phi}}{(1+\widetilde{\delta}_{\phi})^{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\sigma+\upsilon},

(27)

where
\upsilon
denotes a quantum contribution from the quantum coherent dynamics and
\widetilde{\delta}_{\phi}
is another correction to the current average.
The quantity
\upsilon
can be either negative or positive, and there is no definite hierarchical relationship between Eqs. (
23
) and (
27
).
While relation (
27
) clarifies the role of quantum coherent dynamics in constraining current fluctuations, the separate contributions
\upsilon
and
\widetilde{\delta}_{\phi}
make it intractable to characterize the violation of the TUR in general cases.
In contrast, the new relation (
23
) allows for examining the violation of the TUR through the value of
\delta_{\phi}
.
Furthermore, by consolidating all quantum contributions into the term
\delta_{\phi}
, it can derive novel trade-off relations in other contexts as corollaries, as demonstrated below.

Power-efficiency trade-off relation for quantum heat engines

We demonstrate that the quantum TKUR (
23
) can derive a trade-off relation between power and efficiency for quantum steady-state heat engines.
Consider a heat engine simultaneously coupled to two heat baths, one hot at temperature
T_{h}
and one cold at temperature
T_{c}~(<T_{h})
.
Let
\phi_{h}
be the heat current supplied from the hot heat bath,
\phi_{c}
be the heat current absorbed by the cold heat bath.
From the first law of thermodynamics, the power current can be expressed in terms of these heat currents as
P=(\phi_{h}-\phi_{c})/\tau
.
Applying relation (
23
), we can derive the following trade-off relation between power and efficiency:

P\frac{\eta}{\eta_{C}-\eta}\frac{T_{c}(1+\delta_{P})^{2}}{\Delta_{P}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(28)

Here,
\eta_{C}\coloneqq 1-T_{c}/T_{h}
is the Carnot efficiency and
\Delta_{P}\coloneqq\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\tau\mvar[P]
.
Notably, unlike the classical trade-off relation between power and efficiency
[
18
]
, there exists a quantum contribution
\delta_{P}
in inequality (
28
), which vanishes in the classical limit.
Relation (
28
) implies that achieving the Carnot efficiency at finite power without divergent fluctuation of power necessitates
|1+\delta_{P}|\ll 1
.
More specifically,
|1+\delta_{P}|
should vanish at the same order of
\sqrt{\eta_{C}-\eta}
as
\eta\to\eta_{C}
.
This provides insights into the design of efficient heat engines operating at the boundary of the fundamental limitations.

III.2
Quantum inverse uncertainty relation

Next, we consider generic dynamics (
2
) without the assumption of local detailed balance.
We deal with an
arbitrary
counting observable
\phi
, including currents studied in the previous subsection
III.1
.
Our second main result is the quantum inverse uncertainty relation, which sets an upper bound on the relative fluctuation of observable
\phi
, expressed as (see Appendix
C
for the proof)

F_{\phi}\leq\frac{\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}}{\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}}\quantity(1+\frac{2\kappa}{g_s}).

(29)

Here,
J_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}^{n}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
(
n=1,2
) are self-adjoint operators defined in terms of jump operators and counting coefficients, and
\kappa
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\|J_{1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}}{\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}},

(30)

\displaystyle J_{\pi}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}L_{k}\pi L_{k}^{\dagger}.

(31)

Physically,
J_{1}
can be identified as the observable operator as its expected value with respect to the stationary state
\pi
yields the observable average; that is,
\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}=\tau^{-1}\expectationvalue{\phi}
. On the other hand,
J_{2}
can be regarded as the instantaneous-fluctuation operator as
\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\tau^{-1}\mvar[\phi]
. These immediately derive

\frac{\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}}{\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}}=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\tau\mvar[\phi]}{\expectationvalue{\phi}^{2}}.

(32)

Thus,
{\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}}/{\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}}
can be interpreted as the instantaneous relative fluctuation of the observable.
Additionally, it can be observed that
\kappa
vanishes when either
J_{1}\propto\mathds{1}
or
J_{\pi}\propto\pi
, indicating that
\kappa
quantifies the deviation of the observable operator from the identity operator.
Inequality (
29
), valid for
s\in\{0,1/2\}
and for arbitrary times, reveals that the precision of observables is fundamentally constrained by the symmetrized spectral gap and the instantaneous relative fluctuation.
In general, there is no strict hierarchical relationship between the bounds for
s=0
and
s=1/2
; however, they converge and become identical in the classical limit. A similar bound for quantum diffusion unraveling is presented in Appendix
C.1
.
Combined with the quantum TKUR (
23
), this bound offers a comprehensive picture for understanding the precision of currents in finite-time processes.
Furthermore, this result extends the classical findings of Ref.
[
96
]
for Markov jump processes, which relied on concentration techniques, into the quantum domain.
While some quantum bounds on the moment generating function utilizing the
s=1/2
spectral gap have been established for simple counting processes
[
97
]
and diffusive processes
[
98
]
, our approach takes a different route by directly bounding the second moment and accounting for the spectral gap in both
s=0
and
s=1/2
cases.
This provides a more comprehensive and versatile framework for exploring the precision constraints in quantum systems.

III.3
Quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation

Last, we investigate the static response of observables to kinetic perturbations.
In contrast to the previous subsections, we focus on observables expressed in a general form
f({\bf\it\phi})
, where
f
is an arbitrary function and
{\bf\it\phi}=[\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}]^{\top}
is a vector of arbitrary counting observables.
Notably, when
f(x)=x
and
N=1
, this observable reduces to the conventional counting observable.
We consider the case where the Hamiltonian and the jump operators are parameterized by a control variable
\epsilon
.
That is, the dependence of each jump operator on
\epsilon
is explicitly given by

L_{k}=e^{\omega_{k}(\epsilon)/2}V_{k},

(33)

where
\omega_{k}(\epsilon)
is a function of
\epsilon
and
V_{k}
is independent of
\epsilon
.
In contrast, the Hamiltonian can depend on
\epsilon
in an arbitrary manner; that is,
H
can take any form
H(\epsilon)
.
Examples of
\epsilon
include measurement amplitude, energy-level spacing, reservoir temperature, and the coupling strength between the system and the reservoir.

As the third main result, we obtain the following response kinetic uncertainty relation for arbitrary observables
f({\bf\it\phi})
(see Appendix
D
for the proof):

\frac{\|\nabla\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}\|_{1}^{2}}{\mvar[f({\bf\it\phi})]}\leq\tau a.

(34)

Here,
\nabla\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}\coloneqq[d_{\omega_{k}}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}]_{k}^{\top}
is the vector of the static responses of the observable to each jump perturbation
\omega_{k}\to\omega_{k}+\delta\omega_{k}
and
\|{\bf\it x}\|_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{n}|x_{n}|
denotes the
1
-norm.
More precisely, the total derivative symbol
d
is defined as
d_{w}\expectationvalue{\circ}\coloneqq\lim_{\delta w\to 0}[\expectationvalue{\circ}_{w+\delta w}-\expectationvalue{\circ}_{w}]/\delta w
, where the initial state under perturbations remains fixed at
\pi
.
Relation (
34
), which holds for arbitrary times, indicates that the precision of observable response to small perturbations is always bounded by dynamical activity.
While our focus here is on the stationary state, this result can also be extended to the transient regime, retaining the same structure (see Appendix
D.1
for the detailed derivation).
Specifically, we can prove that the following relation holds for any operational time and arbitrary initial quantum states:

\frac{\|\nabla\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}\|_{1}^{2}}{\mvar[f({\bf\it\phi})]}\leq\mathcal{A}_{\tau},

(35)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{k\geq 1}\tr(L_k\varrho_tL_k^\dagger)
is the dynamical activity, quantifying the average number of quantum jumps over the duration
\tau
.
This generalization demonstrates that the trade-off persists across both stationary and transient processes, thereby significantly broadening the applicability of the derived bounds to encompass a wider range of nonequilibrium quantum dynamics.
Furthermore, the bound also holds for quantum diffusion unraveling, as its derivation is independent of the unraveling method.
In the following, we demonstrate its twofold applications.

is varied in the range

,

,

First, it can derive an upper bound on the observable response to a small change in the parameter
\epsilon
.
To this end, note that
|d_{\omega_{k}}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}|\geq|d_{\epsilon}\omega_{k}(\epsilon)d_{\omega_{k}}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}|/\omega_{\rm max}
, where
\omega_{\rm max}\coloneqq\max_{k}|d_{\epsilon}\omega_{k}(\epsilon)|
.
Using this fact and considering the case where the Hamiltonian is independent of
\epsilon

\displaystyle\|\nabla\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\omega_{\rm max}}|\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{\epsilon}\omega_{k}(\epsilon)d_{\omega_{k}}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}|

\displaystyle=\frac{|d_{\epsilon}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}|}{\omega_{\rm max}}.

(36)

Consequently, we can show that the response of observables to the parameter perturbation is bounded by dynamical activity and its fluctuation,

[d_{\epsilon}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})}]^{2}\leq\tau\omega_{\rm max}^{2}\mvar[f({\bf\it\phi})]a.

(37)

Inequality (
37
) implies that achieving a large response of an observable requires either significant fluctuations or high dynamical activity in the system.
Additionally, this result provides a quantum generalization of the classical response uncertainty relation reported in Refs.
[
73
,
74
]
for Markov jump processes, offering a broader framework that goes beyond counting observables.

Second, relation (
34
) can recover the KUR in the classical limit.
This can be verified from the fact that
\|\nabla\expectationvalue{\phi}\|_{1}\geq|\expectationvalue{\phi}|
(see Appendix
D.2
for the proof), which immediately yields the KUR
[
25
,
26
]
,

\frac{\expectationvalue{\phi}^{2}}{\mvar[\phi]}\leq\tau a.

(38)

In other words, relation (
34
) is quantitatively tighter than the KUR.

IV.1
Three-level maser engine

We exemplify the main results in a three-level maser engine
[

]
, which is resonantly modulated by an external electric field and simultaneously coupled to a hot and a cold heat bath.
The maser can operate as a heat engine or refrigerator depending on the parameter regime.
The Hamiltonian and jump operators are explicitly given by

\displaystyle H_{t}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+V_{t},

(39)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{h}n_{h}}\sigma_{31},~L_{1^{*}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{h}(n_{h}+1)}\sigma_{13},

(40)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{c}n_{c}}\sigma_{32},~L_{2^{*}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{c}(n_{c}+1)}\sigma_{23}.

(41)

Here,
H_{0}=\omega_{1}\sigma_{11}+\omega_{2}\sigma_{22}+\omega_{3}\sigma_{33}
is the bare Hamiltonian,
V_{t}=\Omega\quantity( e^{i\omega_0t}\sigma_{12}+ e^{-i\omega_0t}\sigma_{21})
is the external classical field, and
\sigma_{ij}=\outerproduct{i}{j}
for some basis
\{\ket{i}\}
.
To remove the time dependence of the full Hamiltonian, we consider operators in an appropriate rotating frame
X\to\tilde{X}=U_{t}^{\dagger}XU_{t}

[

, where
U_{t}=e^{-i\bar{H}t}
and
\bar{H}=\omega_{1}\sigma_{11}+(\omega_{1}+\omega_{0})\sigma_{22}+\omega_{3}\sigma_{33}
.
In this rotating frame, the GKSL master equation reads
[

]

\dot{\tilde{\varrho}}_{t}=-i[H,\tilde{\varrho}_{t}]+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k\tilde{\varrho}_tL_k^\dagger-\acomm{L_k^\dagger L_k}{\tilde{\varrho}_t}/2),

(42)

where
H=-\Delta\sigma_{22}+\Omega(\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{21})

\Delta=\omega_{0}+\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
.

We consider a current
\phi
with
{\bf\it c}=[1,-1,-1,1]^{\top}
, which is proportional to the net number of cycles.
To examine the validity of the main results, we vary only

while fixing other parameters.
The relative fluctuation of current
\phi
is numerically calculated using the method of full counting statistics.
All the numerical results are plotted in Fig.

As shown in Fig.

increases with increasing

Notably, the quantum TKUR (

always lies strictly above the lower bound

.
In contrast, when the quantum correction
\delta_{\phi}

falls below the lower bound for

The quantum inverse uncertainty relation (

.
The upper bound is calculated for both the
s=0
and

cases.
As shown, the relative fluctuation is always constrained by the upper bound.
Notably, the bound for

is tighter than that for

Finally, we validate the quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation (

). To calculate the response precision, we sample the observable
\phi

randomly chosen from the range

IV.2
Boundary-driven XXZ spin chain

Next, we illustrate our results in a quantum many-body spin system, specifically the spin-
1/2
XXZ chain on one-dimensional lattices.
The boundary-driven XXZ spin chain is a paradigmatic model for studying nonequilibrium quantum dynamics, offering profound insights into transport phenomena, steady-state properties, and the role of integrability in dissipative systems
[
102
]
.
By coupling the spin chain to external reservoirs at its boundaries, this model provides a controlled platform to investigate how energy, spin, or particle currents emerge and evolve under nonequilibrium conditions.

We consider a spin chain consisting of
L
sites with the Hamiltonian given by

H_{\rm XXZ}=\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}(\sigma_{n}^{x}\sigma_{n+1}^{x}+\sigma_{n}^{y}\sigma_{n+1}^{y}+\Delta\sigma_{n}^{z}\sigma_{n+1}^{z}),

(43)

where
\sigma_{n}^{a}

(a\in\{x,y,z\})
are the standard Pauli matrices at site
n
and
\Delta
is the anisotropy parameter.
The spin chain is coupled to reservoirs at only the first and last sites, which drive the system out of equilibrium.
The dissipative operators are specified as

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma(1+\mu)}\sigma_{1}^{+},~L_{1^{*}}=\sqrt{\gamma(1-\mu)}\sigma_{1}^{-},

(44)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma(1-\mu)}\sigma_{L}^{+},~L_{2^{*}}=\sqrt{\gamma(1+\mu)}\sigma_{L}^{-},

(45)

where
\sigma_{n}^{\pm}=(\sigma_{n}^{x}\pm i\sigma_{n}^{y})/2
,
\gamma
describes the coupling strength to reservoirs, and
\mu\in[0,1]
reflects the strength of incoherent driving at the boundaries.
It has been shown that the system possesses a unique nonequilibrium stationary state
[
103
]
.
Under symmetric driving conditions (i.e.,
\mu=0
), the steady state corresponds to a thermal state at infinite temperature,
\pi\propto\mathbb{1}
.
In contrast, for asymmetric driving (i.e.,
\mu>0
), a persistent nonzero current emerges in the steady state.
This setup allows us to analyze transport properties and serves as an ideal platform to verify the derived trade-off relations for precision, response, and thermodynamic costs in quantum systems.

We consider two observables: the magnetization current
\phi_{m}
and the activity observable
\phi_{a}
.
Since the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm XXZ}
conserves the total magnetization
M=\sum_{n=1}^{L}\sigma_{n}^{z}
(i.e.,
[H_{\rm XXZ},M]=\mathbb{0}
), the time evolution of the total magnetization
\expectationvalue{M}_{t}\coloneqq\tr(M\varrho_t)
can be described as

\frac{d\expectationvalue{M}_{t}}{dt}=\iota_{1}(t)+\iota_{L}(t),

(46)

where
\iota_{1/L}(t)
denote the magnetization fluxes injected from the reservoirs at the boundaries.
At the stationary state, the fluxes satisfy
\iota_{1}^{\rm ss}+\iota_{L}^{\rm ss}=0
, and
\iota_{1}^{\rm ss}
can be explicitly expressed as

\iota_{1}^{\rm ss}=2\quantity[\tr(L_1\pi L_1^\dagger)-\tr(L_{1^*}\pi L_{1^*}^\dagger)].

(47)

Therefore, the stochastic magnetization current
\phi_{m}
can be specified using the vector of counting coefficients
{\bf\it c}_{m}=[2,-2,0,0]^{\top}
.
On the other hand, the activity observable is defined by choosing the counting coefficients
{\bf\it c}_{a}=[1,1,0,0]^{\top}
.
This observable simply counts the number of spin flips occurring at the first site of the spin chain.

We numerically verify the quantum TKUR (
23
) and the response kinetic uncertainty relation (
34
) in the spin chain with
L=5
sites. One of the parameters
\gamma
(coupling strength to reservoirs),
\mu
(boundary driving asymmetry), and
\Delta
(anisotropy) is varied, while the others are fixed.
As shown in the top panel of Fig.
2
, the quantum TKUR remains valid for the magnetization current across all parameter ranges.
Moreover, the bound accurately captures the behavior of the magnetization precision as the dissipative strength is varied in Fig.
2
(b).
In contrast, the conventional TKUR can be significantly violated. Specifically, the current precision is enhanced by quantum coherent dynamics when either the coupling strength to the reservoirs or the boundary driving asymmetry increases. Notably, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(c), the conventional TKUR is most strongly violated in the regime
0\leq\Delta\leq 1
, where transport is known to be ballistic
[
102
]
.
These results demonstrate the critical role of quantum effects in enhancing precision and reveal their intricate interplay with thermodynamic quantities in boundary-driven systems.
In the bottom panel of Fig.
2
, the response precision of both the magnetization current and the activity observable is consistently constrained by the dynamical activity, confirming the validity of the quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation (
34
).

V
Summary and discussion

In this study, we derived fundamental bounds on the precision and response of trajectory observables for Markovian dynamics. Our first result is the quantum TKUR (
23
), which provides a lower bound on the relative fluctuations of currents in terms of entropy production and dynamical activity. Unlike the classical version, the new relation includes a quantum correction term
\delta_{\phi}
, highlighting the role of quantum coherent dynamics in enhancing the precision of observables. Applying this result to quantum heat engines, we derived a novel trade-off relation between power and efficiency, offering insights into designing quantum engines capable of achieving finite power at the Carnot efficiency without divergent fluctuations. A promising direction for future research is exploring this possibility through numerical optimization to identify scenarios where maximal efficiency and minimal quantum correction
|1+\delta_{P}|
are achieved at finite power. It is also relevant to derive an analogous relation to (
23
) for the first passage time of currents
[
58
,
104
,
105
,
106
]
.

The second result is the quantum inverse uncertainty relation (
29
), which sets an upper bound on the relative fluctuations of observables in terms of their instantaneous fluctuation and spectral gap. This result complements the first, as combining them yields a sandwich bound for current fluctuations. An open question is whether the upper bound can be refined to include contributions from entropy production.

Our third result is the quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation (
34
), which provides an upper bound on the precision of the static response of general observables to small parameter changes, expressed in terms of dynamical activity. The result can be experimentally verified, particularly in the context of quantum continuous measurement
[
79
]
, where all the quantities in the bound are measurable. Exploring its connection with the quantum KUR
[
56
,
58
,
60
]
and extending the framework to derive a trade-off relation in terms of entropy production for current-type observables remains an intriguing avenue for future research.

[

]
had obtained a similar result to relation (

).

Appendix A
Properties of the symmetrized Liouvillian
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}

Lemma 1
.

The symmetrized Liouvillian
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
is negative semi-definite with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{s}
for
s=1/2
. That is, the following inequality holds true for any operator
A
:

\expectationvalue{A,\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A)}_{s}\leq 0.

(48)

Furthermore, the spectral gap
g_{s}
can be expressed in the following variational form:

g_{s}=-\max_{\expectationvalue{A,\mbb{1}}_{s}=0}\frac{\expectationvalue{A,\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A)}_{s}}{\expectationvalue{A,A}_{s}}.

(49)

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{L}_{s}\coloneqq(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{*})/2
, where
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\circ)\coloneqq\pi^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\pi^{-1/2}\circ\pi^{-1/2})\pi^{1/2}
.
We can show that
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is an adjoint super-operator of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}
as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}^*(A),B}

\displaystyle=\tr{\pi^{-1/2}\mca{L}(\pi^{1/2}A\pi^{1/2})^\dagger\pi^{-1/2}B}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\mca{L}(\pi^{1/2}A\pi^{1/2}),\pi^{-1/2}B\pi^{-1/2}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pi^{-1/2}B\pi^{-1/2},\mca{L}(\pi^{1/2}A\pi^{1/2})}^{*}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\pi^{-1/2}B\pi^{-1/2}),\pi^{1/2}A\pi^{1/2}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{A,\pi^{1/2}\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\pi^{-1/2}B\pi^{-1/2})\pi^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{A,\mca{L}^*(B)}.

(50)

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is an adjoint super-operator of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}
because

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A),B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\ev{\widetilde{\mca{L}}(A),B}+\ev{\widetilde{\mca{L}}^*(A),B}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\ev{A,\mca{L}(B)}+\ev{A,\mca{L}^*(B)}]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{A,\mca{L}_s(B)}.

(51)

From the definition
\mathcal{L}_{s}=(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{*})/2
, we can explicitly express it as

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{L}_{s}(\circ)

\displaystyle=G\circ+\circ G^{\dagger}+\sum_{k\geq 1}(L_{k}\circ L_{k}^{\dagger}+\widetilde{L}_{k}\circ\widetilde{L}_{k}^{\dagger}),

(52)

where we define
\widetilde{H}\coloneqq\pi^{1/2}H\pi^{-1/2}
,
\widetilde{L}_{k}\coloneqq\pi^{1/2}L_{k}^{\dagger}\pi^{-1/2}
, and

G\coloneqq-iH+i\widetilde{H}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k^\dagger L_k+\pi^{1/2}L_k^\dagger L_k\pi^{-1/2}).

(53)

Since
\mathcal{L}(\pi)=\mathbb{0}
, we have

\displaystyle G+G^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=i(\pi^{1/2}H\pi^{-1/2}-\pi^{-1/2}H\pi^{1/2})-\sum_{k\geq 1}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}(\pi^{1/2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\pi^{-1/2}+\pi^{-1/2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\pi^{1/2})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\geq 1}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+\pi^{-1/2}\quantity[-i(H\pi-\pi H)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\ge 1}\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\pi\}]\pi^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\geq 1}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+\pi^{-1/2}\quantity(\mca{L}(\pi)-\sum_{k\ge 1}L_k\pi L_k^\dagger)\pi^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\geq 1}(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+\widetilde{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\widetilde{L}_{k}).

(54)

The operator
G
can be decomposed as the sum of self-adjoint operators as
G=A-iH_{s}
, where
A=(G+G^{\dagger})/2
and
H_{s}=(G^{\dagger}-G)/(2i)
are self-adjoint operators.
Consequently, it can be easily verified that
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is the generator of a completely positive trace-preserving map,

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{L}_{s}(\circ)

\displaystyle=-i[H_{s},\circ]+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k\circ L_k^\dagger-\frac{1}{2}\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\circ\}+\widetilde{L}_k\circ\widetilde{L}_k^\dagger-\frac{1}{2}\{\widetilde{L}_k^\dagger\widetilde{L}_k,\circ\}).

(55)

Thus, all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{s}
have nonpositive real parts.
Additionally, we can show that
\mathcal{L}_{s}^{\prime}\coloneqq\pi^{-1/4}\mathcal{L}_{s}(\pi^{1/4}\circ\pi^{1/4})\pi^{-1/4}
is a self-adjoint super-operator, that is,

\expectationvalue{\mca{L}_s'(A),B}=\expectationvalue{A,\mca{L}_s'(B)}.

(56)

Indeed, it can be proved as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\mca{L}_s'(A),B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{\pi^{-1/4}\mca{L}(\pi^{1/4}A\pi^{1/4})\pi^{-1/4}+\pi^{-1/4}\mca{L}^*(\pi^{1/4}A\pi^{1/4})\pi^{-1/4},B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{\pi^{-1/4}\mca{L}(\pi^{1/4}A\pi^{1/4})\pi^{-1/4}+\pi^{1/4}\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\pi^{-1/4}A\pi^{-1/4})\pi^{1/4},B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{\mca{L}(\pi^{1/4}A\pi^{1/4}),\pi^{-1/4}B\pi^{-1/4}}+\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\pi^{-1/4}A\pi^{-1/4}),\pi^{1/4}B\pi^{1/4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{A,\pi^{1/4}\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\pi^{-1/4}B\pi^{-1/4})\pi^{1/4}}+\expectationvalue{A,\pi^{-1/4}\mca{L}(\pi^{1/4}B\pi^{1/4})\pi^{-1/4}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{A,\mca{L}_s'(B)}.

(57)

For any super-operator
\mathcal{S}
using calligraphic fonts, we denote by

its corresponding vectorization representation, which vectorizes operators as

(58)

By algebraic calculations, one can show that

|BA)=(\mathbb{1}\otimes A^{\top})|B)

It is evident from Eq. (

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{\prime}
is a Hermitian matrix and its spectral decomposition is given by

are real eigenvalues and the eigenvectors

are real nonpositive numbers sorted in the descending order

|r_{0}^{\prime})=|\pi^{1/2})

(60)

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
is the adjoint super-operator of

(61)

as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{A,\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A)}_{s}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{A,\pi^{1/2}\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(A)\pi^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=(A|[\pi^{1/2}\otimes(\pi^{1/2})^{\top}]\widehat{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{s}|A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d^{2}-1}\lambda_{i}(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|r_{i}^{\prime})(r_{i}^{\prime}|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d^{2}-1}\lambda_{i}|(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|r_{i}^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}\lambda_{i}|(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|r_{i}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq 0.

(62)

\displaystyle=(A|[\pi^{1/2}\otimes(\pi^{1/2})^{\top}]|A)

\displaystyle=(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]\sum_{i=0}^{d^{2}-1}|r_{i}^{\prime})(r_{i}^{\prime}|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d^{2}-1}|(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|r_{i}^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\expectationvalue{A,\mbb{1}}_{s}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}|(A|[\pi^{1/4}\otimes(\pi^{1/4})^{\top}]|r_{i}^{\prime})|^{2}.

(64)

Using these expressions, we immediately obtain

(65)

where we apply the inequality
\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}/\sum_{i}x_{i}\leq\max_{i}a_{i}
for any nonnegative numbers
\{x_{i}\}
and the maximum is achieved by
|A)=[\pi^{-1/4}\otimes(\pi^{-1/4})^{\top}]|r_{1}^{\prime})
or
A=\pi^{-1/4}r_{1}^{\prime}\pi^{-1/4}

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{+}\coloneqq\sum_{i>0}\chi_{i}^{-1}|r_{i})(l_{i}|.

(72)

B.1
Analogous relation for quantum diffusion unraveling

Here we demonstrate that an analogous relation can be derived for quantum diffusion unraveling using the same approach.
To this end, we first briefly outline the method of quantum diffusion unraveling
[
80
]
(see also Ref.
[
1
]
for a review). Unlike quantum jump unraveling, where the quantum state evolves through discontinuous jumps, quantum diffusion unraveling describes a continuous stochastic evolution due to a diffusion-like noise process.
For simplicity, we consider real coherent fields, while generalization to complex cases is straightforward. In this case, the pure state
\ket{\psi_t}
follows the stochastic differential equation:

d\ket{\psi_t}=\quantity(-iH_{\rm eff}+\sum_{k\ge 1}\qty[\ev{L_k^\dagger}_tL_k-|\ev{L_k}_t|^2/2])\ket{\psi_t}dt+\sum_{k\geq 1}\quantity(L_k-\ev{L_k}_t)\ket{\psi_t}dW_{k,t}.

(86)

Here,
\{dW_{k,t}\}
are the independent Wiener increments satisfying
\mathbb{E}[dW_{k,t}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[dW_{k,t}dW_{k^{\prime},t}]=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}dt
.
The current observable
\phi
along a stochastic trajectory is given by

\phi=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\quantity(\ev{L_k+L_k^\dagger}_t\dd{t}+dW_{k,t}).

(87)

Its ensemble average can be calculated as

\expectationvalue{\phi}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr[(L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger})\varrho_{t}]=\tau\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr[(L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger})\pi].

(88)

The quantum Cramér-Rao inequality (
66
) remains valid even for quantum diffusion unraveling. The key distinction from the case of quantum jump unraveling lies in the partial derivative of the current observable. In this case, it is evaluated as follows:

\displaystyle\evaluated{\partial_{\theta}\expval{\phi}}_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\evaluated{\partial_{\theta}\qty[\int_0^\tau\dd{t}\sum_{k\ge 1}c_k(1+\ell_k\theta/2)\tr[(L_k+L_k^\dagger)(\pi+\theta\varphi_t)]+O(\theta^2)]}_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\ell_{k}\tr[(L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger})\pi]+\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr[(L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger})\varphi_{t}]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\phi}+\expectationvalue{\phi}_{*}+\expectationvalue{\phi}_{\varphi},

(89)

where
\expectationvalue{\phi}_{*}\coloneqq\tau\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}(\ell_{k}/2-1)\tr[(L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger})\pi]
.
Defining the quantum correction term
\delta_{\phi}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\expectationvalue{\phi}_{*}+\expectationvalue{\phi}_{\varphi})/\expectationvalue{\phi}
, we obtain the following quantum TKUR for quantum diffusion unraveling:

\frac{F_{\phi}}{(1+\delta_{\phi}^{\prime})^{2}}\geq\frac{4a}{\sigma^{2}}\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2a})^{2}.

(90)

This relation retains the same structure as Eq. (
23
) derived for quantum jump unraveling, differing only in the quantum contribution
\delta_{\phi}^{\prime}
.

Appendix C
Derivation of the quantum inverse uncertainty relation (
29
)

Here we provide a detailed derivation of the second main result (
29
).
For notational convenience, we denote
\partial_{x}f_{x}
as
f^{\prime}_{x}
and
\partial_{x}^{2}f_{x}
as
f^{\prime\prime}_{x}
, respectively.
We first calculate the variance of observable
\phi
.
Applying the equality

\partial_{u}e^{\mathcal{L}_{u}t}=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}e^{\mathcal{L}_{u}(t-s)}\mathcal{L}_{u}^{\prime}e^{\mathcal{L}_{u}s},

(91)

the second moment of observable
\phi
can be calculated from Eq. (
7
) as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\phi^2}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{u}^{2}G_{\tau}(u)\big|_{u=0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\tr\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\pi)-2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\tr{\mca{L}_0'e^{\mca{L}s}\mca{L}_0'(\pi)}.

(92)

Here, we use the facts that
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}
,
e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\pi)=\pi
, and
\tr e^{\mathcal{L}t}(A)=\tr A
for any operator
A
.
We now individually calculate the terms in Eq. (
92
).
For convenience, we define the following self-adjoint operators:
J_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
,
J_{2}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}^{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}
, and
J_{\pi}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}L_{k}\pi L_{k}^{\dagger}
.
Evidently,
\tau^{-1}\expectationvalue{\phi}=\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}=\tr(J_\pi)
.
Since
\tr\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\pi)=-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}^{2}\tr(L_k\pi L_k^\dagger)=-\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}
, the first term in Eq. (
92
) becomes

-\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\tr\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\pi)=\tau\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}.

(93)

In addition, note that
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}(\circ)=i\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}L_{k}\circ L_{k}^{\dagger}
and

\displaystyle\tr\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\prime}(\circ)

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr(L_k \circ L_k^\dagger)

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\tr(L_k^\dagger L_k\circ)

\displaystyle=i\expectationvalue{J_1,\circ}.

(94)

The second term in Eq. (
92
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\tr{\mca{L}_0'e^{\mca{L}s}\mca{L}_0'(\pi)}

\displaystyle=-2i\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\expectationvalue{J_1,e^{\mca{L}s}\mca{L}_0'(\pi)}

\displaystyle=-2i\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}s}(J_1),\mca{L}_0'(\pi)}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}s}(J_1),J_\pi}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}(\tau-t)\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(J_1),J_\pi}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}(\tau-t)\quantity[\ev{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(J_1-\ev{J_1,\pi}\mbb{1}),J_\pi}+\ev{J_1,\pi}^2]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\phi}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}(\tau-t)\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(\bar{J}_1),J_\pi},

(95)

where we define
\bar{J}_{1}\coloneqq J_{1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}
, which is a self-adjoint operator satisfying
\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_1,\pi}=0
.
For each operator
A
, we define the corresponding operator
A_{t}
that evolves in the Heisenberg picture as
A_{t}\coloneqq e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}t}(A)
.
Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{s}
, the last term in Eq. (
95
) can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(\bar{J}_1),J_\pi}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},J_\pi-\alpha\pi}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\pi^{-s}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)\pi^{s-1}}_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}(J_{\pi}-\alpha\pi)\pi^{s-1}\|_{s}

\displaystyle=\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}\sqrt{\tr{\pi^{s-1}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)\pi^{-s}\pi^{s}\pi^{-s}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)\pi^{s-1}\pi^{1-s}}}

\displaystyle=\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}\sqrt{\tr{\pi^{s-1}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)\pi^{-s}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)}}.

(96)

Here,
\alpha
is an arbitrary real number.
The last term in Eq. (
96
) can be expressed as a quadratic function of
\alpha
as

\displaystyle\tr{\pi^{s-1}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)\pi^{-s}(J_\pi-\alpha\pi)}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}+\tr(\pi^{s-1}J_\pi\pi^{-s}J_\pi).

(97)

This quantity can be minimized by
\alpha=\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}
and the minimum is given by

\displaystyle\tr(\pi^{s-1}J_\pi\pi^{-s}J_\pi)-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}\|_{s}^{2}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}\|_{s}^{2}-\expectationvalue{\pi^{-s}J_\pi\pi^{s-1},\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}^{2}.

(98)

Here, we use the fact that the following equality holds true for operator
A=\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}

\displaystyle\|A-\expectationvalue{A,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tr{[A^\dagger-\ev{A,\pi}\mbb{1}]\pi^s[A-\ev{A,\pi}\mbb{1}]\pi^{1-s}}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{s}^{2}-\expectationvalue{A,\pi}^{2}.

(99)

Consequently, the following upper bound can be attained from Eq. (
96
):

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(\bar{J}_1),J_\pi}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}.

(100)

Next, we evaluate
\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}
.
Note that
\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_1,\pi}=0
, which implies
\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\mbb{1}}_{s}=\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\pi}=\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_1,e^{\mca{L}t}(\pi)}=0
.
Following the approach in Ref.
[
93
]
and applying Lemma
1
, we can show that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\bar{J}_{1,t}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\bar{J}_{1,t}),\bar{J}_{1,t}}_{s}+\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\bar{J}_{1,t})}_{s}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\widetilde{\mca{L}}^*(\bar{J}_{1,t})}_{s}+\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\widetilde{\mca{L}}(\bar{J}_{1,t})}_{s}

\displaystyle=2\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(\bar{J}_{1,t})}_{s}

\displaystyle=2\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\bar{J}_{1,t}}_{s}\frac{\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\widetilde{\mca{L}}_s(\bar{J}_{1,t})}_{s}}{\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\bar{J}_{1,t}}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq-2g_{s}\expectationvalue{\bar{J}_{1,t},\bar{J}_{1,t}}_{s},

(101)

which consequently derives
\|\bar{J}_{1,t}\|_{s}\leq e^{-g_{s}t}\|\bar{J}_{1}\|_{s}
.
Therefore, we arrive at the following inequality:

\expectationvalue{e^{\widetilde{\mca{L}}t}(\bar{J}_1),J_\pi}\leq e^{-g_{s}t}\|\bar{J}_{1}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}=e^{-g_{s}t}\|J_{1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}.

(102)

Using this inequality and taking the time integration in Eq. (
95
), the second term in Eq. (
92
) can be upper bounded as

-2\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\tr{\mca{L}_0'e^{\mca{L}s}\mca{L}_0'(\pi)}\leq\expectationvalue{\phi}^{2}+\frac{2(e^{-g_{s}\tau}+g_{s}\tau-1)}{g_{s}^{2}}\|J_{1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}.

(103)

Finally, by combining Eqs. (
92
), (
93
), and (
103
), we obtain the following upper bound on the variance:

\mvar[\phi]\leq\tau\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}+\frac{2(e^{-g_{s}\tau}+g_{s}\tau-1)}{g_{s}^{2}}\|J_{1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}\|\pi^{-s}J_{\pi}\pi^{s-1}-\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}\mathbb{1}\|_{s}.

(104)

By rearranging Eq. (
104
) and noting that
e^{-g_{s}\tau}-1\leq 0
, we achieve the desired relation (
29
),

F_{\phi}\leq\frac{\expectationvalue{J_2,\pi}}{\expectationvalue{J_1,\pi}^{2}}\quantity(1+\frac{2}{g_s}\frac{\|J_1-\ev{J_1,\pi}\mbb{1}\|_s\|\pi^{-s}J_\pi\pi^{s-1}-\ev{J_1,\pi}\mbb{1}\|_s}{\ev{J_2,\pi}}).

(105)

Appendix D
Derivation of the quantum response kinetic uncertainty relation (

Here we provide a detailed derivation of the third main result (

We employ an approach similar to that in Appendix

H_{\theta}=H
) and the jump operators are perturbed by a parameter

(110)

where
\sign(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
and
\sign(x)=-1
otherwise.
Note that
x\sign(x)=|x|

According to the quantum Cramér-Rao inequality, the following inequality holds true:

\frac{\mvar[f({\bf\it\phi})]}{(\partial_{\theta}\expectationvalue{f(\vb*{\phi})})^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{{I}_{q}(\theta)},

(111)

where

{I}_{q}(0)=4\evaluated{\partial^2_{\theta_1\theta_2}\ln|\tr\varrho_{\vb*{\theta}}(\tau)|}_{\vb*{\theta}=\vb*{0}},

(112)

where
\varrho_{{\bf\it\theta}}(\tau)=e^{\mathcal{L}_{{\bf\it\theta}}\tau}(\pi)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\bf\it\theta}}(\varrho)

\displaystyle=-i[H,\varrho]+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{e^{\omega_{k}(\epsilon[1+z_{k}\theta_{1}])+\omega_{k}(\epsilon[1+z_{k}\theta_{2}])}}V_{k}\varrho V_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{\omega_{k}(\epsilon[1+z_{k}\theta_{1}])}V_{k}^{\dagger}V_{k}\varrho-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{\omega_{k}(\epsilon[1+z_{k}\theta_{2}])}\varrho V_{k}^{\dagger}V_{k}.

(113)

Following the same procedure outlined in Appendix
B
, we obtain the explicit form of the quantum Fisher information as

{I}_{q}(0)=\tau\epsilon^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\tr(L_k\pi L_k^\dagger)=\tau\epsilon^{2}a,

(114)

which is proportional to the dynamical activity.
On the other hand, the partial derivative of the observable average with respect to
\theta
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\evaluated{\partial_{\theta}\expval{f(\vb*{\phi})}}_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\evaluated{\sum_{k\ge 1}\partial_\theta\omega_k(\epsilon[1+z_k\theta])d_{\omega_k(\epsilon[1+z_k\theta])}\ev{f(\vb*{\phi})}}_{\theta=0}

(115)

Substituting Eqs. (
114

D.2
Recovery of the kinetic uncertainty relation in the classical limit

We show that relation (
34
) derives the classical KUR for Markov jump processes.
In the classical limit, the GKSL dynamics is equivalent to Markov jump dynamics, characterized by the transition matrix
{W}=[w_{mn}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Let
w_{mn}=e^{\omega_{mn}}
for each
m\neq n
.
Then, by applying the triangle inequality, we obtain

\|\nabla\expectationvalue{\phi}\|_{1}=\sum_{m\neq n}|d_{\omega_{mn}}\expectationvalue{\phi}|\geq\quantity|\sum_{m\neq n}d_{\omega_{mn}}\ev{\phi}|.

(121)

In what follows, we prove that
\sum_{m\neq n}d_{\omega_{mn}}\expectationvalue{\phi}=\expectationvalue{\phi}
, which immediately yields the KUR.
Let
\{c_{mn}\}
be the counting coefficients, then
\expectationvalue{\phi}=\tau\sum_{m\neq n}c_{mn}w_{mn}\pi_{n}
.
Using this, we can calculate as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{m\neq n}d_{\omega_{mn}}\expectationvalue{\phi}

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}\sum_{m\neq n}d_{\omega_{mn}}(w_{ij}\pi_{j})

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}\sum_{m\neq n}[(d_{\omega_{mn}}w_{ij})\pi_{j}+w_{ij}d_{\omega_{mn}}\pi_{j}]

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}\sum_{m\neq n}(\delta_{mi}\delta_{nj}w_{ij}\pi_{j}+w_{ij}d_{\omega_{mn}}\pi_{j})

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}w_{ij}\pi_{j}+\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}\sum_{m\neq n}w_{ij}d_{\omega_{mn}}\pi_{j}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\phi}+\tau\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}w_{ij}\sum_{m\neq n}d_{\omega_{mn}}\pi_{j}.

(122)

From the equalities
{W}{\bf\it\pi}={\bf\it 0}
and
{\bf\it 1}^{\top}{\bf\it\pi}={\bf\it 1}
, we get
{Z}{\bf\it\pi}={\bf\it e}_{1}
, where
e_{nk}=\delta_{nk}
and
{Z}
is obtained from
{W}
by replacing the first row of
{W}
with
{\bf\it 1}
.
Here,
{\bf\it 1}=[1,\dots,1]^{\top}
is the all-one vector.
Note that matrix
{Z}
is invertible
[
70
]
.
Taking the derivative of equality
{Z}{\bf\it\pi}={\bf\it e}_{1}
with respect to
\omega_{mn}
, we get
