A Study on Distributed Strategies for Deep Learning Applications in GPU Clusters
Md Sultanul Islam Ovi

Abstract
As deep learning models grow in size and complexity, training them efficiently on single GPUs becomes increasingly infeasible. This study investigates the effectiveness of several distributed training strategies—Distributed Data Parallel (DDP), Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP), and Parameter Server (PS) models—for scalable deep learning on GPU clusters. We conduct empirical evaluations across multiple models and datasets to assess trade-offs in memory usage, training time, GPU utilization, and model accuracy. Our results show that while FSDP reduces GPU memory usage by over 60%, it increases training time by up to 6× compared to DDP. In contrast, asynchronous PS training improves throughput but can lead to degraded accuracy due to stale updates. Through comprehensive analysis, we provide practical insights into the strengths and limitations of each strategy, offering guidance for selecting suitable methods based on system constraints and training objectives.

II
Literature Review on Distributed Training Strategies

The exponential rise in data availability and the increasing complexity of deep learning models have necessitated the development of distributed training techniques that can fully exploit the computational power of modern GPU clusters. Traditional single-GPU approaches quickly become inadequate when training models with hundreds of millions of parameters, due to limitations in memory bandwidth, compute power, and I/O throughput. Consequently, a rich body of literature has emerged exploring various strategies for distributing workloads across multiple GPUs to improve scalability, training efficiency, and resource utilization.

Data parallelism
has been one of the earliest and most widely adopted distributed strategies. It partitions the input data across multiple GPUs, each with a complete replica of the model, and performs local computations before synchronizing gradients. Krizhevsky et al.
[
1
]
demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach by significantly reducing the training time of convolutional neural networks through dual-GPU processing. However, synchronization during gradient aggregation introduces communication overhead, especially in large-scale settings. To address this, Shi et al.
[
12
]
proposed optimizations in MPI communication to reduce transfer latency, enhancing the scalability of data-parallel approaches. Later works have focused on optimizing collective operations such as all-reduce and overlapping communication with computation to further reduce synchronization delays.

Model parallelism
tackles the problem from the opposite direction by dividing the model itself across multiple GPUs. This is particularly useful for extremely large models that cannot fit into the memory of a single GPU. Dean et al.
[
13
]
explored this approach in Google’s large-scale distributed systems, distributing different layers of deep networks across compute nodes. However, this technique introduces significant inter-GPU communication, which can become a bottleneck in performance. Shazeer et al.
[
14
]
addressed this by proposing the sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts architecture, which activates only a small subset of model parameters per forward pass, thereby reducing communication and memory overhead.

An evolution of model parallelism is
pipeline parallelism
, which organizes the computation of a neural network into a pipeline across GPUs. By processing different mini-batches simultaneously at different pipeline stages, this method improves utilization and minimizes idle time. Huang et al.
[
15
]
introduced GPipe, which incorporates automatic pipeline splitting and micro-batching to enable near-linear scaling across multiple devices. Their work demonstrated that pipelined execution could significantly shorten training time without sacrificing convergence or model accuracy.

Hybrid parallelism
combines the benefits of data and model parallelism by distributing both the data and different parts of the model across GPUs. This is particularly advantageous in scenarios involving heterogeneous hardware or varied batch sizes. Akintoye et al.
[
16
]
investigated hybrid approaches and demonstrated their effectiveness in adapting to different system architectures while maintaining scalability and training throughput. These methods are often implemented in large-scale deep learning frameworks such as Megatron-LM and DeepSpeed, where careful orchestration of compute and communication is key.

Another well-established distributed training framework is the
parameter server
(PS) architecture, which separates computation and parameter management. Worker nodes calculate gradients based on local data and send updates to centralized parameter servers, which maintain the global state of model parameters. Li et al.
[
6
]
provided one of the foundational implementations of this architecture, showing its ability to scale across large clusters. Enhancements such as Stale Synchronous Parallel (SSP)
[
8
]
were introduced to balance the trade-off between update freshness and communication efficiency. More recently, Jiang et al.
[
17
]
proposed adaptive scheduling techniques to improve load balancing and resource allocation in PS systems.

Federated learning
offers a distributed training framework that prioritizes data privacy and locality. Rather than transferring raw data to a central server, federated learning aggregates model updates from distributed clients. McMahan et al.
[
11
]
introduced the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, which enables decentralized training while addressing privacy concerns. While not originally GPU-centric, federated learning is increasingly being adapted for edge devices with GPU acceleration, broadening its applicability in privacy-sensitive domains.

Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP)
represents a recent advancement in memory-efficient distributed training. By sharding model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across all devices, FSDP significantly reduces the per-GPU memory footprint. Rasley et al.
[
18
]
described how this approach enables the training of models previously limited by memory constraints, such as trillion-parameter transformers. FSDP incorporates strategies like overlapping communication with computation and dynamically managing memory allocation to improve throughput and scalability. Initially introduced in FairScale, FSDP has since been integrated into PyTorch and adopted in industrial-scale training pipelines.

In summary, the literature on distributed deep learning highlights a spectrum of strategies, each with distinct advantages and trade-offs. While data parallelism offers simplicity and ease of implementation, model and pipeline parallelism address the challenges of scaling large architectures. Hybrid methods combine their strengths for better resource efficiency, and parameter server models offer flexible synchronization modes. Meanwhile, FSDP and federated learning point to the future of scalable and privacy-conscious model training. Continued research is needed to unify these strategies under adaptive, intelligent scheduling frameworks that can automatically optimize communication, memory, and compute efficiency in diverse cluster environments.

III
Research Methodology

This study addresses a core limitation in distributed deep learning: the dependence on gang scheduling. Traditional training paradigms require synchronized scheduling across all GPUs to execute training steps collectively. While this coordination ensures consistency, it also introduces inefficiencies, such as idle time on faster nodes and difficulty scaling across heterogeneous environments. Our objective is to explore distributed strategies that reduce or eliminate this rigid synchronization requirement, offering more flexible and efficient alternatives.

We evaluate three major distributed training strategies—Distributed Data Parallelism (DDP), Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP), and the Parameter Server (PS) model—across a range of deep learning models. The evaluation considers multiple metrics including GPU utilization, training time, memory consumption, and accuracy. Experiments are conducted under varying hardware configurations and workload intensities to understand the trade-offs and practical implications of each strategy.

III-C

Distributed Strategies

Distributed Data Parallel (DDP)

DDP replicates the entire model across multiple GPUs, each processing a distinct data shard. After local forward and backward passes, gradients are synchronized using an all-reduce operation to ensure a consistent model state
[
26
]
. While DDP provides high throughput and strong scalability, it requires each GPU to store a full copy of the model, leading to increased memory consumption. Figure
1
visually illustrates the DDP setup, highlighting how each GPU independently processes data and participates in global gradient synchronization.

Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP)

FSDP introduces a memory-optimized alternative to DDP by sharding model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across participating GPUs
[
27
]
. In this strategy, each GPU holds only a fragment of the model. During the forward pass, parameters are gathered on-demand using an all-gather operation. The backward pass employs reduce-scatter to compute averaged gradients. This design allows training larger models with significantly lower memory usage per GPU. Figure
2
illustrates the sharding and communication steps involved in FSDP, emphasizing its efficient memory footprint
[
28
]
.

Parameter Server Model

The Parameter Server (PS) model
[
6
]
decouples computation from model state management. Worker nodes compute gradients based on local data and send updates to centralized parameter servers, which maintain the global model parameters. This design allows asynchronous or synchronous updates, offering flexibility in performance and consistency.

Figure
3
shows the overall architecture of a PS-based system, where worker nodes fetch and update parameters stored on separate server nodes. Two variants of this model are described below.

•

Synchronous Parameter Server:
In this mode, all workers perform computations in parallel and synchronize with the server to update model parameters in a lockstep fashion. Figure

demonstrates this approach, showing that while it ensures consistency, it can lead to idle time if workers have variable speeds.

•

Asynchronous Parameter Server:
This variant allows each worker to compute and apply parameter updates independently, without waiting for others. Although it improves system throughput, it may introduce staleness in model parameters. Figure

illustrates how asynchronous updates can proceed in parallel, potentially improving speed at the cost of convergence stability.

Experiment 1

In the synchronous mode, all workers coordinated their updates with the parameter server after each iteration. In contrast, the asynchronous mode allowed workers to update parameters independently, potentially improving throughput at the cost of model consistency. V-C

Summary of Observations

Our findings reveal that:

•

DDP offers superior training speed
across all models but requires each GPU to hold a full model replica, leading to higher memory consumption.

•

FSDP significantly reduces memory usage
and enables the training of larger models, albeit at the cost of longer training times due to communication overhead.

•

Asynchronous parameter server training improves throughput
, making it suitable for faster experimentation or low-priority tasks, but leads to degraded model performance compared to synchronous training.

•

Synchronous parameter server training maintains higher accuracy
, making it more appropriate for production-grade or mission-critical models.

These observations reinforce that the choice of distributed strategy should be guided by application requirements, such as speed versus memory efficiency or accuracy versus training throughput. Each strategy has its distinct strengths and should be selected accordingly based on the target deployment environment.

VI
Conclusion and Future Scope

This study explored distributed training strategies for deep learning across GPU clusters, focusing on Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP), Distributed Data Parallel (DDP), and the Parameter Server (PS) model. Our findings highlight that while distributed strategies are essential for training complex models at scale, each method introduces specific trade-offs involving memory usage, training time, and model accuracy.

FSDP was found to be highly memory-efficient, enabling the training of larger models by sharding parameters and optimizer states across GPUs. However, this advantage comes with increased communication overhead and a more complex tuning process. DDP, by contrast, offered faster training due to simpler synchronization, but required each GPU to hold a full model replica, increasing memory usage. The PS model provided architectural flexibility, where the asynchronous variant improved throughput by eliminating synchronization barriers but led to reduced accuracy, while the synchronous mode preserved consistency with longer training times.

Throughout the experiments, key challenges emerged—such as balancing performance and stability in asynchronous updates, and optimizing inter-GPU communication in memory-efficient strategies like FSDP. These underscore the need to match the distributed approach to the system architecture and application-specific constraints.

In future work, we aim to build on these findings through the following directions:

•

Evaluating these strategies on a broader set of models, particularly in domains like natural language processing and speech, to assess their generalizability.

•

Re-implementing the Parameter Server framework on high-memory, high-throughput hardware to better understand how hardware impacts scalability and communication efficiency.

•

Exploring improvements to FSDP that reduce communication costs, such as more efficient collective operations or adaptive sharding techniques.

•

Investigating techniques to stabilize asynchronous PS training, including staleness-aware updates or hybrid synchronization approaches.

These directions will help advance distributed deep learning by improving the scalability, robustness, and deployment readiness of training strategies across a range of real-world scenarios.
