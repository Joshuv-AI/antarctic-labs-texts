Effect of use of ambient listening technology on patient-reported communication and satisfaction ratings
Authors: Sandra L Taylor, Debbie Aizenberg, Melissa Jost, Yunyi Ren, Courtney R Lyles, Jason Y Adams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEvaluate the impact of ambient listening AI scribe technology for drafting notes on patient-reported communication and satisfaction ratings of their physician during ambulatory visits.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a pilot (April—August 2024) during which 31 physicians across multiple specialties could use an AI scribe during ambulatory visits. Patient surveys from AI-scribe supported visits were compared with pre-pilot surveys in the previous 12 months, and to 125 control physicians, following a difference-in-differences approach. Logistic regression evaluated change in the odds of receiving the highest score for 5 questions, focused on patient ratings of communication (4 items) and overall satisfaction with their physician (1 item).ResultsAmong 31 917 surveys (7164 pilot; 24 753 control), patient ratings were high. The pre-post change in communication and satisfaction scores did not differ significantly between pilot and control physicians. However, for 3 questions (concern provider showed, explanation provider gave, and likelihood of recommending their provider), ratings increased for pilot physicians while declining for controls without reaching statistical significance.DiscussionWe did not find evidence of AI scribes worsening or enhancing patient experiences. Continued evaluation is warranted to ensure positive patient experiences as AI tools expand in clinical care.

Introduction

The role of artificial intelligence (AI) is growing exponentially in the domain of supporting clinicians in their daily workload. AI ambient-listening scribe technologies are being rapidly adopted in ambulatory care settings across US health systems.1,2 This technology captures patient-provider conversations and generates draft visit notes that are reviewed and approved by the provider.3,4 Use of AI scribes has been shown to reduce documentation time5,6 and received positive endorsement from physicians.7–9

While others have focused on the physician workload and perceptions with implementation of AI scribes,3,4,9–15 the potential impact of this technology on patient experiences with health care is largely unexamined.16 In several studies, physicians reported improved patient engagement,7,17 but studies directly evaluating the patient experience ratings are generally lacking. One previous pediatrics study found the percentage of patients who would recommend their provider increased significantly when an AI scribe was used versus the pre-AI scribe period,11 but another study17 did not find a significant difference in a patient’s likelihood to recommend their provider between AI scribe and traditional visits. In an ambulatory care setting, patients reported a better experience when an AI scribe was used and commented that their physicians spent more time looking at them than previously.18 A recent assessment of patient attitudes found 71.5% of patients neutral or comfortable with the use of an AI scribe, 64.8% trusted their accuracy, and about 50% thought use improved patient-provider interaction, but only 38.2% endorsed AI scribe use in the future visits.19

Understanding the impact of AI scribes on patient experience is critical due to potential effects on patient care and outcomes, especially since there is previous literature that providers typing or using the computer during visits disrupts patient communication during the encounter.20 Furthermore, positive correlations between patient experience with objective health outcomes, self-rated health and well-being outcomes, and adherence to treatment and preventative care recommendations have been consistently reported.21 Specifically, much of the previous literature has focused on patient ratings of provider communication and overall satisfaction with providers, which are commonly measured with subscales from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS).22–25 Thus, to complement existing literature on AI scribes, we evaluated these specific patient experience ratings during a real-world pilot implementation at University of California, Davis Health (UCDH) system in 2024. Our study was a quantitative examination comparing benchmarked patient ratings of care for encounters with vs without use of an AI scribe, using a pre-post plus control group analysis structure.

Methods

The UCDH system is an academic medical center with a 653-bed teaching hospital and the only Level I adult and pediatric trauma center in inland Northern California, serving a 33-county region of about 6 million residents with more than 1.9 million patient encounters annually. UCDH conducted a pilot program evaluating use of an AI scribe to assist in preparation of clinic visit notes in an ambulatory care setting. The program started April 24, 2024 with 5 pilot physicians initially in a proof-of-concept phase, followed by an additional 26 pilot physicians in July 2024. During the pilot, physicians had the option of using the AI scribe on a visit-by-visit basis. Patients seeing pilot physicians were asked if they consented to its use during their visit. Specialties of pilot physicians included family practice (12 [39%]), internal medicine (11 [36%]), otolaryngology (3 [10%]), pediatrics (2 [6%]), and 1 (3%) each of dermatology, obstetrics/gynecology, and endocrinology. This study was determined by our Institutional Review Board to not be human subjects research (IRB ID 2367684-1).

Patient survey data collection

Patient surveys are routinely distributed to patients following every clinic visit, using an external vendor as is typical in healthcare systems across the US Patient surveys were collected during April 24, 2024 to August 30, 2024 covering the pilot period, and July 1, 2023 to April 23, 2024 to provide a pre-pilot period. For pilot physicians, the pilot period started when the AI scribe was activated for the individual physician. For example, if the AI scribe was activated on July 1 for a physician, surveys when the AI scribe was used were only available after this date. Finally, because we tracked the use of AI scribe at every visit during the pilot, we included patient surveys for pilot physicians only when the encounter used the AI scribe. Patient surveys were not available for 3 pilot physicians (2 pediatrics, 1 family practice).

Control group

In addition to these pre-post data for pilot physicians, we selected physicians similar to each pilot physician as contemporaneous controls. For each pilot physician, we sought to identify 5 unique physicians as controls. We required controls to have the same specialty, and further looked for physicians with at least 30 surveys completed from their panel and with similar pre-pilot patient ratings. To achieve similar patient ratings, we first selected exact matches and progressively extended selecting the next closest. However, it was not possible to identify 5 unique controls per pilot, resulting in only 125 controls. We analyzed all patient ratings obtained covering the period July 1, 2023 to August 30, 2024 for the control group, using April 24, 2024 to define pre- and pilot periods.

Patients were asked to rate their communication and satisfaction with their physician on the following validated survey items:

Responses were on a Likert scale of 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100, reflecting worst to best. As is convention for the ceiling effects seen in these data nationwide, we used “top-box” scoring, collapsing responses as 100 vs all other scores (<100) in our analysis.26

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and survey responses were summarized as counts and percentages or median and quartiles. A difference-in-differences analytical approach was used to evaluate the effect of AI scribe use on patient responses. For the pre-pilot period, graphical review showed monthly percentages of surveys with a score of 100 for pilot and control physicians to be stable with no discernable trends, supporting the parallel trends assumption. Logistic regression was used to evaluate response differences between physician group and time period. A robust covariance matrix was used to account for within-physician correlation from multiple survey responses per physician. Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical computing software Version 4.5.1. All statistical tests were 2-sided and evaluated at a significant level of 0.05.

Results

A total of 31 917 patient surveys were collected, with 7164 from 28 pilot physicians and 24 753 from 125 peer physicians. Patients completing the survey were generally older adults (median = 68.5 years old [IQR: 58.7-75.4]), more commonly female (61.3%), and predominantly White (62.9%). Survey responders were from less disadvantaged areas based on national rank of Area Deprivation Index (median = 17 [IQR: 11-26]) and from relatively healthier community conditions (CA HPI median = 68 [IQR = 51-81]) (Table S1). Patient characteristics were similar between pilot and control physicians (Table S1).

Patient ratings were uniformly high across pilot and control physicians for both pre and pilot periods (Table 1). For all questions, patients gave their physician the highest score possible >88% of the time. However, the full range of scores were represented for all questions, physician type, and study period, with scores of 0 for approximately 0.5% of the responses (Table S2).

Discussion

AI tools are being rapidly deployed in all aspects of health care. While these tools hold promise to improve the quality and efficiency of health care delivery, physician well-being and patient outcomes, it remains imperative to keep the patient experience and ultimately patient outcomes in the forefront as new technologies are incorporated. Our study is one of the first to evaluate patient ratings of care providers when AI scribes are integrated into ambulatory care settings.

Using a pre-post study design with controls, we evaluated the effect of employing an AI scribe in ambulatory care visits on patient communication ratings and overall satisfaction with their physician. We hypothesized that use of the AI scribe would improve patient experience ratings by increasing engagement and communication. We found no evidence that use of the technology worsened patient experience, and the direction of our findings were toward small improvements in patient ratings of care. Our results are consistent with those of Pelletier et al11 from a pediatric care center except that like Haberle et al17 we did not find a significant effect on patient’s likelihood to recommend their physician.

Peer-reviewed studies suggest that AI scribes and ambient clinical documentation tools are associated with improvements in physician-centered outcomes, including reduced documentation time, lower after-hours EHR work or “pajama time,” improved documentation-related well-being and job satisfaction, reduced burnout or burnout risk, lower cognitive load or mental documentation burden, and greater physician-reported engagement with patients.4,5,7,10,12,13,15 Whether these changes translate to improved patient experience and outcomes remains largely unexamined and patient comfort with and response to AI scribes remains equivocal.19,27 Patient-centric evaluations are emerging with some finding improved patient experience and more direct patient-physician engagement with AI scribes.18 While we did not demonstrate a positive effect of AI scribe use on patient experience, our findings provide some reassurance that the technology does not detract from or interfere with the patient-physician interaction. Nevertheless, patients’ needs and perceptions must remain at the forefront of implementation with deliberate attention to comfort, trust, and privacy concerns and recognition that acceptability varies across patients.19 Thus, health systems using these tools should ensure transparent consent, emphasize responsibility of clinicians for reviewing AI-generated notes, monitor note quality and safety, and continue assessment of patient experience.

The finding that patient ratings remained high with use of the AI scribe is encouraging, but our study did have limitations. Most notably, our results relied on routine clinical data from voluntary patient surveys, with respondents who were generally older, white, females from socially advantaged areas, and those who generally reported high ratings of their providers. Thus, patients with lower ratings of their providers may be underrepresented in our sample. However, patient ratings of provider communication are known to vary by patient demographic characteristics and health status,28 and the response rates in our study reflect overall patient experience reporting standardly used in healthcare settings nationwide. Further, trust in AI varies and is lower among patients with less familiarity with technology,29 which was not measured directly in our study and can vary with educational attainment, age, sex, and socioeconomic status. Given the positive correlation between patient experience and health outcomes,21 further investigation of demographic heterogeneity regarding the acceptability of the technology, its influence on the patient-provider interaction, and trust in the healthcare system is warranted.

Because physicians volunteered to participate in the pilot, they and their patients could have differed from controls. We attempted to select controls that were comparable to pilot physicians and we found patient demographic characteristics to be similar between pilots and controls. We also employed a difference-in-differences study design to ameliorate potential selection bias effects. Nevertheless, these factors may limit generalizability of the findings. Continued work to understand how AI scribes affect patient-provider engagement as well as patient-centric outcomes is paramount.
