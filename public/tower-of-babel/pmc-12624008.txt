Evaluation of fluoride and calcium ion release and fluoride recharge capacity of glass-ionomer materials modified with experimental bioactive glass
Authors: Antonela Šošić, Ivan Šalinović, Salvatore Sauro, Ivan Nemet, Nikolina Ilić, Sanda Rončević, Ana Ivanišević
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the study was to evaluate fluoride(F) release and re-release after recharging, and calcium(Ca) release from Fuji IX(Fuji) and Equia Forte HT(E) modified with 5wt% of experimental bioactive glasses BAG and BAG-F. F release was recorded over 91 days, and F re-release over 21 days in six groups (n = 12): (1) Fuji; (2) Fuji BAG; (3) Fuji BAG-F; (4) E; (5) E BAG; (6) E BAG-F. Ca release was recorded over a period of 21 days in samples stored in distilled water or lactic acid(LA) in eight groups (n = 10): (1) Fuji; (2) Fuji LA; (3) Fuji BAG-F; (4) Fuji BAG-F LA; (5) E; (6) E LA; (7) E BAG-F; (8) E BAG-F LA. F and Ca were recorded using ion selective electrodes. ANOVA and LSD test were performed at α = 0.05. F release was significantly higher in modified variants of Fuji and Equia (BAG-F > BAG > original) at all time points, before and after recharging (p < 0.001). Increased F release in modifications was more pronounced in E than in Fuji (p < 0.05). F release after recharging was lower but dropped more slowly in both materials and modifications. The differences in Ca release were significant regarding material and time intervals (p < 0.001). The highest Ca release was recorded in Fuji BAG-F LA, followed by Fuji BAG-F, Fuji LA, Fuji, E BAG-F LA, E BAG-F, E LA, and E.Keywords: Bioglass, Calcium, Fluoride, Leaching, Glass ionomer cementSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

The Minamata Convention on Mercury was adopted in 2013 and provides measures to phase down dental amalgam. In 2023, a proposal to completely phase out the production of dental amalgam by 2030 was not adopted. However, a decision on this matter could be taken at the 6th Conference of the Parties in November 20251. The main alternatives to dental amalgam are composite resins and glass ionomer (GI) cements. Composites have good aesthetic and mechanical properties; however, the prevalence of secondary caries has been shown to be higher with composite restorations than with amalgam, regardless of the estimated caries risk2. In the context of anticariogenic activity, fluoride-releasing materials such as GI materials could represent a viable alternative to composite resins3. In addition to the release of fluoride, the advantages of GIs include the leaching of ionic species that delay demineralization or promote remineralization (such as calcium and phosphate ions), bioactivity through the development of an ion exchange layer at the interface with the dental tissue and biocompatibility3. The mechanical properties of conventional GIs are inferior to the mechanical properties of composite resins. However, the clinical performance of newer GI-based materials has been shown to be comparable to composite resins in Class I cavities and smaller Class II cavities3,4. The mechanical properties are improved by a higher powder-to-liquid ratio, small reactive glass particles and nano-filled coatings3,5. Considering the advances in the mechanical properties of high-viscosity GICs and glass hybrid materials and their ion-leaching properties, these materials may be a preferred choice when cariostatic and demineralizing effects are crucial, such as in patients with high caries risk2.

The kinetics of fluoride ion release from commercially available GIC materials have been studied in detail under neutral and acidic conditions6–8. It was found that the release of fluoride ions occurs in two steps: After the initial “burst” effect due to the dissolution of the non-matured material, the release rate decreases as the ions diffuse out of the mature material. The sustained slow release of fluoride ions is responsible for the caries-protective effect and remineralization9. In addition to fluoride ions, GIs have also been shown to release other ions and ion groups under neutral conditions: sodium, aluminum, silicate and phosphate groups 8. Under acidic conditions, the release of calcium ions was observed, and the release of sodium and aluminum ions, silicates and phosphates was more pronounced than under neutral conditions7. The release of calcium or strontium ions indicates the remineralization potential of the material, especially in the presence of fluoride10.

First commercialized bioactive glass was Bioglass® 45S5 which consists of 45 wt% SiO2, 24.5 wt% Na2O, 24.5 wt% CaO and 6 wt% P2O5. Other formulations may contain CaF2, B2O3, fluorapatite, volastonite, diopside and tricalcium phosphate. The bioactive and antimicrobial properties of BAG depend on its composition, structure and ion release kinetics, which is why BAG is increasingly used in dentistry11. The addition of BAG to GIs can influence the mechanical properties and the release of ions that promote remineralization. It has been shown that the addition of 5 wt% BAG with 74% crystallinity improves the mechanical properties of GICs12. It was also found that fluoride release and uptake were improved when experimental GIC material was modified with strontium-containing BAG13. The effects of modifying strontium-containing GICs such as Fuji IX (GC, Tokyo, Japan) and Equia Forte HT Fil (GC, Tokyo, Japan) with experimental zinc- and fluoride-containing BAG have not been investigated in the available literature.

The aim of this study was to determine the influence of the modification of Fuji IX and Equia Forte HT Fil with 5 wt% experimental bioactive glasses on the release and re-release of fluoride ions after recharging. In addition, the effect of the modification with 5 wt% bioactive glass on the release of calcium ions under neutral and acidic conditions was to be determined. The null hypothesis is that the modification of the commercial GI restorative materials with experimental BAGs would have no effect on the release and re-release of ions after recharging.

Results

The release and re-release of fluoride ions from the original and modified samples

The fluoride release measurements on days 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 14 and 91 represented eight contiguous measurements in six independent groups (2 materials and 3 types). At all time points, both Fuji modifications released significantly more fluoride ions than the original formulation (pBAG = 0.013–0.032 and pBAG-F = 0.000). The two Equia modifications also released significantly more fluoride than the original material in all measurements (p = 0.000 for BAG and BAG-F). The differences between the modified variants were also significant for both Fuji and Equia: the BAG-F type showed a higher fluoride release than BAG at all time points (p < 0.05). The differences between the original materials Fuji IX and Equia Forte HT were not significant at any point (p > 0.05). This effect of modification with BAG and BAG-F on increased F release was more pronounced in Equia than in Fuji: although Equia released the least F, Equia BAG-F released the most F (Table 1, Figs. 1 and 2).

To evaluate the effects of recharging, fluoride release per day was compared across all groups and time points. Fluoride release after recharging was lower than initial fluoride release, but also decreased more slowly (Fig. 1, Table 1). Comparison of adjacent groups before and after recharge (day 1 before recharging to day 1 after recharging, day 2 before recharging to day 2 after recharging, etc.) showed that the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001) for all Fuji types at all time points, with higher initial values at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 days and higher values after recharging at 14 and 21 days (Table 1). Similar results were observed in the Equia Forte HT group, where initial fluoride levels were significantly higher at 1, 2, 3 and 4 days (p < 0.001), after 7 days the values were comparable (p > 0.05), and levels were significantly higher at 14 and 21 days after recharging (p < 0.001). For Equia BAG type the pattern seemed similar, but the differences after 14 and 21 days were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). In Equia BAG-F, the initial values were significantly higher after 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 days (p < 0.001), the difference was not significant after 14 days, and it was significantly higher after recharging at day 21 (p = 0.017) (Table 1).

The increase in fluoride release observed in BAG-F and BAG modifications compared to the original formulations (BAG-F > BAG > original) was statistically significant in both materials at all time points, before and after recharging (p < 0.001).

Release of calcium ions in neutral and acidic environments

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that there were statistically significant differences between the groups (p < 0.001) and between the different time points (p < 0.001) with regard to the release of calcium ions. The LSD test showed that all Fuji groups released significantly more calcium (p < 0.05) than all Equia groups, regardless of modification and storage (distilled water or lactic acid solution). The highest Ca release was observed with Fuji BAG-F LA, followed by Fuji BAG-F, Fuji LA, Fuji, E BAG-F LA, E BAG-F, E LA and E (Fig. 3). The differences between all Fuji IX groups were significant (p < 0.05): Fuji released the least amount of calcium, followed by Fuji LA, Fuji BAG-F and Fuji BAG-F LA. The trend was similar in the Equia groups, but the differences were significant between Equia vs Equia BAG-F, Equia vs Equia BAG-F LA, Equia LA vs Equia BAG-F LA (Fig. 3)(Table 2).

Discussion

The release of ions and the cariostatic effect are a comparative advantage of GIs as amalgam substitutes. The results of the present study show that the release and re-release of fluoride ions from glass ionomer materials modified with experimental BAGs containing Zn and fluoride is significantly increased compared to Fuji IX and Equia Forte HT. The release of calcium was higher in the BAG-F modified samples compared to the original materials in both neutral and acidic environments. The null hypothesis that the modification of commercial GI restorative materials has no effect on ion release was therefore rejected.

The results of the present study strongly suggest that the modification of Fuji and Equia with 5 wt% BAG has a positive caries-protective effect due to the increased leaching of F and Ca ions, which may have clinical implications. This is supported by previous findings linking the increased ion leaching (Ca, F and P, but also Sr) to the inhibition of secondary/recurrent caries formation, the arresting and reversal of initial caries on adjacent tooth surfaces and the remineralisation of partially demineralised carious dentin under the restoration2,8,14,15. It is expected that the increased F release of BAG and BAG-F variants reduces bacterial acid production retard or inhibits demineralisation15,16, while the increased Ca release could shift the balance of ionic species (Ca2+, PO43−, HPO42−, H2PO4−) towards hydroxyapatite further promote remineralization15,17.

The weight percentage of the experimental bioactive glasses in the present study (5 wt%) was chosen based on the results of Zandi Karimi et al.12, according to which the optimal mechanical and demineralizing properties of hybrid GIs containing 45S5 BAG were achieved at 5 wt%. The setting reaction of GIs is an acid–base reaction, so reduced alkalinity (reduced ratio of alumina to silica) of the GI powder modified with bioactive glass slows down the reaction, and the weight percentage should be carefully balanced18. Although GIs modified with ZnO exhibited improved antibacterial activity and mechanical properties19, some variants of Zn-containing bioglass were shown to impair the kinetics of polycondensation silica gel layer formation and the release of calcium and phosphate20. Nevertheless, the addition of biological glass containing zinc and fluoride in the present study increased the release of F and Ca ions and improved the remineralization and regeneration capacity of the material. These results are comparable to previous studies using different chemical compositions of bioactive glass, which showed that restorative materials modified with bioglass were biologically active and could induce remineralization21,22.

The pattern of fluoride release in the present study was similar in the original and modified formulations and followed a typical curve reported in many previous studies, reflecting the “initial burst” due to the high diffusion gradient of “loosely bound” F ions that are not involved in the cross-linking of the cement matrix23. During the setting of conventional glass ionomer cements, the glass particles are attacked by the polyalkenoic acid that forms the liquid component of the material, cations are released to the surface, and gelation occurs through the cross-linking of Ca2⁺ with the carboxyl groups. A stronger network is formed by the slow replacement of some Ca2⁺ cross-links by Al3⁺ during the maturation process over a period of a few months23. In newer formulations such as Fuji IX and Equia, the replacement of Ca by Sr accelerates matrix formation, as Sr has a lower ionisation energy and is more reactive. Fluoride ions are not involved in cross-linking and remain unbound in the fully set cement. They are mainly released by diffusion. Maturation of the matrix inhibits F diffusion, resulting in low F leakage. The Equia glass hybrid material contains highly reactive small particles, and the larger surface area of the glass fillers can improve hydration and cross-linking of the cations with the carboxyl groups, which increases the rate of maturation. As the maturation of the cement increases, the F diffusion in the cement matrix is lower, which may explain why Equia releases less F than Fuji. This is consistent with the results that showed that high viscosity GI materials with a higher proportion of smaller fillers release less F8. In addition, the increased release of F from the modified materials, particularly Equia BAG-F, could be attributed to the higher total amount of fluoride in the reacting powder mixture. In addition to the fluoride content, altered cross-linking kinetics could also have influenced the F release. In the previous section, it was already mentioned that particle size influences curing, but the composition of the material and external factors such as pH and excess water also play a role24. The increased F leaching in the groups modified with BAGs was more pronounced in Equia than in Fuji. This can be explained by the fact that Equia is a glass hybrid, so that the average particle size in Equia has been increased by the modification and the effects of highly reactive small particles on rapid curing have been reduced.

Fluoride ions can be taken up by the set ion-leaching material, such as GI, during recharging. In general, materials with higher F-release exhibit higher F re-release after recharging8. This positive correlation between F release/re-release was also observed in the present study and is consistent with previous studies6,8. Most F was released from the BAG-F-modified Equia and Fuji, followed by the BAG modifications. In contrast, the original formulations of Fuji and Equia released the least F, and the same pattern was observed for F re-release. It has already been shown that the recharge capacity is influenced by the hydrogel thickness and porosity of the material6. For this reason, water-based GI materials are generally effectively recharged, which was also confirmed in the present study. The dissolution of the fluoride-containing glass fillers could explain the initially higher F release from the BAG F and BAG-modified samples. This could also explain the higher F uptake and re-release due to the higher porosity.

The cumulative F release in the present study was sufficient for all materials to show anticariogenic activity. El-Adl et al.25 related the leaching of fluoride to the inhibitory effect on secondary caries. They found that the cumulative amount of 60.3 ppm F over 2 weeks in the form of a self-adhesive hybrid composite resulted in a clinically relevant ability to inhibit secondary caries. Nevertheless, the inhibition area was recognised at the margin of the cavity with a much lower F release in the alkasite material of 17.56 ppm. This means that all materials tested in this study released enough F to inhibit secondary caries, even over a short period of 14 days. Furthermore, the data suggest that a sufficient cumulative amount of F can be achieved to inhibit caries after 3 weeks when recharged. This was particularly emphasised in the Equia samples modified with BAG-F.

Fuji and Equia are based on SiO2, SrO and Al2O3. The zinc oxide contained in the experimental BAGs acts as a network-modifying oxide and disrupts the Si–O–Si bond of the GI glass, making the original glass particles more susceptible to attack by polyalkenoic acid26. However, zinc can affect the hydrolytic stability of polyalkenoate cements if they contain CaO26, but alumino-zinc-silicate glasses yield hydrolytically stable cements with high compressive strengths26. Therefore, the SrO contained in the powder of the original Fuji and E formulations and the increased ZnO content in the modified formulations may contribute to the stable material.

The release of calcium ions could be due to the dissolution of the material, especially in the initial phase when it is not yet fully set and matured23. Although maturation takes several months, today’s highly viscous glass ionomers are probably less susceptible to water absorption or loss before maturation, even without protective coatings or varnishes, so the release of calcium cannot be fully explained by the dissolution of the material. Furthermore, it is stated by the manufacturer that Fuji IX and Equia do not contain calcium. On the contrary, our results indicate that the original formulations do contain some calcium, but these amounts are low. The cumulative calcium release in three weeks under neutral conditions was 1.54 and 0.71 mg/l (ppm) for Fuji IX and Equia, respectively (ppm = mg/L ÷ ρ (H2O) g/mL). It is important to note that not all calcium in the set GI formulations (original and modified) is uniformly bound and that leaching of calcium cannot simply be associated with the breaking of ionic bonds with carboxyl groups, i.e. disintegration. Most calcium leaching is due to the surface dissolution of glass particles and the diffusion of loosely bound calcium or unbound excess Ca2+ that is not integrated into cross-links. This is not just a surface phenomenon; diffusion of unbound ions can occur through microchannels when the main material is hydrated27. The increased calcium release from the modified samples could be attributed to the increased content of loosely bound calcium in the modified material, as BAG-F and BAG contained 23.45 and 26.4 wt% CaO, respectively, as revealed by ICP-MS analysis. In this study, the coating was not applied to Equia material, so the initial water uptake should be higher23, which could theoretically influence the ion release. However, the release of Ca from Equia was the lowest.

It has already been reported that acidic exposure decomposes the material by protonating the carboxyl group and releasing Ca2+27. It is difficult to speculate on the source of the calcium released in the present study, but it could be that in addition to the surface dissolution of the glass particles in the acidic environment, some of the eluted Ca2+ comes from the broken ionic bonds. This is probably because lactic acid (pKa = 3.86) is stronger than polyacrylic acid (pKa = 6), so that calcium lactate is formed, which then dissociates28. Regardless of the source of the released calcium, the calcium release in Fuji and its modification was significantly higher at a pH around 5.5. On the other hand, this effect was not statistically significant in Equia and its modification. This could indicate that Fuji is more susceptible to dissolution at a pH of 5.5, which is considered critical for caries initiation. Considering the significantly higher fluoride release from the Equia BAG-F modification and the fact that the acidic environment does not increase the solubility of fluoride compared to neutral pH, it could therefore prove to be a suitable material for restoring proximal surfaces in an open sandwich technique or as a liner in a long-term restoration. It ensures an environment with increased F concentration, which contributes to cariostatic effects and increased calcium content, favoring a shift towards remineralization.

As GI materials are water-based, mass loss measurement is not a suitable method for measuring the dissolution of the materials and estimating disintegration. It does not provide any information about the dissolution rates of the individual components of GI cement. In the case of hydrated GI cement, the dissolved species differ from the species in the undissolved material. Most of the elements that leach out are present in multiple phases in the set cement27. Therefore, analyzing the amount of each dissolving species is the most appropriate method to analyze the dissolution of the cement, although it does not directly reflect the disintegration of the matrix, as not all species are actively involved in ionic binding (such as F and loosely bound Ca). However, a limitation of the design used in the present study and other leakage studies is the refreshment of the solution in which the sample is stored. The frequency of refreshening is proportional to the cement dissolution. If the solution were not replaced, the solute concentration would increase and, depending on the ionic strength, the solutes (including Ca2+) could be absorbed by the solid cement beyond a certain point. Another limitation concerns the fluoride-selective electrode, which cannot be used in acidic environments, while the calcium-selective electrode allows recordings in acidic environments and can register very small amounts of calcium ions. On the other hand, ICP-MS can be used to simultaneously analyze several released elements in the eluate, but fluoride cannot be detected, which is why we opted for ion-selective electrodes to record the ions using the same method. In the present study, the samples were not recharged with calcium. A future study could attempt to recharge the high viscosity (HV) GI materials and their modifications with varnishes containing amorphous calcium phosphate and estimate Ca re-release. Although the materials used in the present study are Sr-based, a previous report has shown that all HVGI materials except Equia can accumulate and re-release Ca, even if the initial levels of Ca re-released were low or below the detection limit8. Calcium can be incorporated into partially demineralized hydroxyapatite during recrystallisation29. A recent study has shown that strontium can also penetrate demineralized dentin30, suggesting that the remineralization capacity of Sr-based GIs is not reduced. Although there are doubts about the potentially harmful effects of Sr, a recent study has shown that Sr can replace calcium in bioactive materials without adverse local or systemic effects31.

Although the BAG modifications used in the present study introduced zinc ions into the material, the release was not quantified, that is one of the limitations of this study. The explanation for this is that zinc in ion-releasing materials has a proven antibacterial effect only under certain conditions19,32. However, this does not necessarily correlate with the cariostatic effect, as this depends on several factors such as the presence of fluoride and calcium ions, pH and biofilm composition. Further studies should also measure zinc release to better understand its role in improving the properties of the ion-releasing material. Although the increased fluoride and calcium levels have been associated with cariostatic benefits, the direct biological effects, particularly with regard to the impact on the biofilm, need to be further verified33. Furthermore, it would be useful to determine the composition of the modified Fuji IX and Equia powders, as the analysis could show differences in the percentage of F and Ca in the modified versions, which could influence the remineralization capacity of the experimental materials. Future studies should also include the evaluation of the remineralization capacity of materials modified with these bioactive glass variants by analyzing the elemental composition of demineralized dentin or enamel in contact with the experimental modified materials using SEM EDS.

In conclusion, the addition of experimental BAGs promotes the release of fluoride ions from Fuji and E. Since all powders (experimental BAGs and commercially available materials) contain fluoride salts, these could originate from BAG or GI particles, but given that the proportion of bioactive glass in the modified materials was 5 wt%, it can be assumed that the majority of the fluorides released originated from the GI particles. The F release after recharging was lower, but decreased more slowly for both materials and modifications. Modification of Fuji and E with BAG significantly increased the release of Ca ions, which originated mainly from the BAGs incorporated into the GI materials, as the GI powder contained strontium aluminofluorosilicate glass. When Fuji was exposed to a more acidic medium, the release of Ca ions increased significantly. The acidic environment had no significant effect on the release of Ca ions in Equia.

Materials and methods

Composition of the glass ionomer materials and the experimental bioactive glass

The commercially available encapsulated materials used in the study were Fuji IX GP (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and Equia forte HT Fil (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) which are listed in Table 3. Fluoride-containing zinc-doped BAG powder (< 30 µm) was prepared as previously described20,34. Nominal composition of fluoride containing BAG34: 40.7 wt% SiO2, 23.3 wt% CaO, 25.7 wt% Na2O, 9.0 wt% P2O5, 1.2 wt% CaF2. According to the manufacturer, the composition of the powder component of Fuji IX is 90–100% fluoro-aluminosilicate glass, 5–10% polyacrylic acid, and of Equia Forte HT Fil 95% strontium fluoro-aluminosilicate glass, 5% polyacrylic acid.

Fuji IX, Equia samples and experimental BAG powders were subjected to elemental analysis by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) after prior microwave-assisted digestion. Approximately 20 mg of pulverized sample was weighed and mixed with 3 mL hydrochloric acid (Suprapur, 30%, Merck, Germany), 3 mL nitric acid (Ultrapur, 60%, Merck, Germany) and 100 µL hydrofluoric acid (pro analysi, 40%, Merck, Germany) in a Teflon digestion vessel. The vessels were then placed in a Microwave System (Multiwave GO, Anton Paar), where the following temperature program was applied: 10 min at 130 °C, 20 min at 180 °C and 15 min at 150 °C. After digestion, the clear solutions were cooled to room temperature, transferred to 50 mL tubes and filled with deionized water. Before measurement, all digested solutions were filtered and diluted 1:1000 with nitric acid (3%, ν/ν).

Elemental analysis of the samples by ICP-MS (Agilent 7900 ICP-MS, Agilent, Tokyo, Japan) was performed after a preceding microwave-assisted digestion step under the following experimental conditions: Output power of 1500 W, 0.9 L/min Ar as auxiliary gas and 0.2 L/min Ar as nebuliser gas. A MicroMist nebuliser with a sample flow rate of 0.3 mL/min was used. After nebulisation, the sample was passed through a Scott double-pass spray chamber. The analysis focussed on the following elements, which are listed in order of increasing m/z (mass-to-charge ratio): 23Na, 27Al, 28Si, 31P, 43Ca, 66Zn, 88Sr. Quantification of selected elements was performed using external five- or six-point calibration curves, with calibration ranges adjusted to the expected concentrations of each analyte. All measurements were performed in triplicate. Operating parameters of the device: sample depth 10 mm; ORS mode NO GAS/ He; nebuliser carrier gas flow 1.09 L min − 1; Ext 1 lens(V) 0; Ext 2 lens(V) − 190; RF power 1550 W; RF matching 1.80 W; detection mode—spectral; integration time—0.1 s; Repetitions—3. Preparation of samples for measuring the release of F and Ca ions

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software, version 3.1.9.6, assuming a large effect size (f = 0.4) for fluoride release and re-release for 6 groups and an effect size (f) of 0.58 for calcium release for 8 groups. For the measurements of fluoride release/ re-release, 6 groups with 12 samples each were prepared (n = 12): (1) Fuji; (2) Fuji BAG-F; (3) Fuji BAG; (4) E; (5) E BAG-F; (6) E BAG. Four groups of samples (n = 20) were prepared for the measurement of calcium ion release: (1) Fuji; (2) Fuji BAG-F; (3) E and (4) E BAG-F. Each group was divided into two subgroups according to the storage media: distilled water and lactic acid with a pH of 5.5 (LA). In this way, a 98% power for F and a 90% power for Ca was achieved to detect mean differences at the significance level 0.05.

To prepare the samples of glass ionomers modified with BAG-F and BAG, the capsules of the commercially available materials were carefully opened using lower molar extraction forceps so that the membrane enclosing the liquid was not damaged. Each capsule contained 0.4 g of powder. The contents of 13 capsules for fluoride and 21 capsules for calcium measurement were placed in a mortar and weighed. Five per cent by weight of the glass powder was replaced by the same mass of experimental BAG powder. The mixture was mixed manually with a mortar and pestle for 10 min to achieve as homogeneous a distribution as possible. Twelve capsules for the fluoride measurements and 20 capsules for the Ca measurements were each filled with 0.4 g of the modified powder and carefully sealed. The remnants of the modified powder weighing less than 0.4 g were discarded.

The capsules of the commercial and experimental materials modified with 5 wt% BAG were activated and mixed for 10 s in 3M™ ESPE™ CapMixTM (3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the samples were prepared using 8 mm × 2 mm high cylindrical Teflon molds35. The polyester strips were placed under the molds on a glass tile to avoid air entrapment and the material was lightly compressed by glass on both sides of the mould. The samples were left to set for one hour.

Measurements of the release of fluoride and calcium ions

Ion concentrations in aqueous solutions were analysed in accordance with the ISO 19,448:2018 standard36.

For the fluoride release measurements, the samples were stored in vials (Laboroprema, Zagreb, Croatia) with 5 mL deionized water at 37 °C in an ES 120 incubator (Nüve, Ankara, Turkey) after curing for one hour. Prior to recording, the material samples were removed from the vials and dried with absorbent paper. Fluoride concentrations in aqueous solutions were quantified using an ion-selective electrode (F800 DIN, Xylem Analytics Germany, Weilheim, Germany) coupled to an ion analyzer (inoLab Multi 9630 DS; Xylem Analytics Germany, Weilheim, Germany). The electrode was calibrated daily with a series of standard solutions (20 µM/L NaF, 50 µM/L NaF and 100 µM/L NaF). In a neutral environment, fluoride remains free as F- so that the electrode provides accurate results. In an acidic environment, F- begins to react with hydrogen ions and forms hydrofluoric acid, so that the measurements are not accurate. After each measurement, the electrode was rinsed with fresh deionized water and dried with absorbent paper. A 5 mL aliquot of the aqueous solution in which the material samples had previously been immersed was mixed with another 5 mL of a buffer to adjust the total ionic strength (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and the fluoride concentrations were determined in triplicate after a 5-min incubation period. The results were expressed in mg/L (ppm was calculated according to the following formula: ppm = mg/L ÷ ρ(H2O) g/mL). After the recordings, each material sample was decanted into a vial of fresh distilled water. Fluoride ion release measurements were performed after 24 h and on days 2, 3, 4, 7, 14, 21 and 91. After the recordings on day 91, the samples were recharged with Fluor Protector Gel (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). All samples were immersed in approx. 3 g Fluor Protector Gel for 5 min. The excess gel was wiped off, the samples were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water for 2 min and carefully dried with absorbent paper. After the recharging, fluoride re-release was recorded after 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 14 and 21 days. The amount of fluoride since the last measurement corresponded to the daily fluoride release during the first 4 days, while the recording periods for later measurements were longer.

Calcium release was determined using a Ca 800 DIN calcium-selective electrode (Xylem Analytics Germany GmbH, Weilheim, Germany). The original materials (Fuji and Equia) and their modifications with 5 wt% BAG-F (n = 20) were randomly divided and placed in either distilled water or lactic acid (LA, pH 5.5), resulting in a total of 8 groups (n = 10): (1) Fuji; (2) Fuji LA; (3) Fuji BAG-F; (4) Fuji BAG-F LA; (5) E; (6) E LA; (7) E BAG-F; (8) E BAG-F LA. The concentrated lactic acid was diluted to a pH value of 5.5. Prior to the measurements, the samples were removed from the storage medium and 2% of an ISA/Ca solution (WTW, Weilheim, Germany) was added. Measurements were performed in triplicate for each sample. The results were expressed in milligrams per liter (mg/L).

Statistical analysis

The reliability of the ion release measurements was checked using the Pearson correlation coefficient (test–retest correlations) for all groups at all time points. For the fluoride release measurements, the correlations between the different measurements were higher than 0.999, with a common variance of more than 99.8%. For the calcium release measurements, almost all correlations were very high (> 0.9), while the lowest value was observed in group E and at the 14-day measurement (0.74). Therefore, the median values were used for each sample.

The normality of the data distribution was checked with the Shapiro–Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variances for the release of fluoride and calcium ions was tested with the Levene test, so that an ANOVA and a post-hoc LSD (Least Significant Difference) test were performed. The analysis was performed with a SAS statistical package on a Windows platform. The significance level was set at p = 0.05.
