Demonstrating Petrous Dural Incision Technique for Vascular Releasing and Transposition of Dura-Embedded Subarcuate Loop of Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery During Retrosigmoid Approach
Authors: Mustafa Sakar, Eldaniz Mammadli, Onur Erdoğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:In practice, the retrosigmoid approach is one of the most preferred approaches for cerebellopontine tumors, vascular compression syndromes, brainstem lesions, and many other pathologies. The anatomy of this region can vary, and the anterior inferior cerebellar artery is rarely involved. A dura-embedded subarcuate loop is one of these; it needs to be released and transposed to expand the surgical corridor. We present this report on expanding surgical exposure during the retrosigmoid approach in a case involving a dura-embedded subarcuate loop.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:A 44-year-old man underwent microvascular decompression by the retrosigmoid approach because of trigeminal neuralgia. During surgery, a dura-embedded subarcuate loop of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery was encountered. The subarcuate loop was hanging from the petrous bone spur. We safely released the subarcuate loop from the petrous bone spur by the dural incision technique, and further deep dissection and microvascular decompression were performed. The critical steps of the surgery were reviewed in a surgical video.CONCLUSION:These types of vascular variations can be encountered in any surgery using the retrosigmoid approach, and if the vessel is not released, it may be required to work through a narrow surgical corridor in prolonged surgeries. With this petrous dural incision technique, the vessel can be safely dissected from the bone, and the surgical corridor can be widened.

The retrosigmoid approach is a widely used and reliable method for the surgical treatment of cerebellopontine angle (CPA) tumors, posterior circulation aneurysms, brainstem lesions, and microvascular decompression (MVD). However, this region contains numerous neurovascular structures with significant anatomical variations.1,2 Among these, the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) is the most critical vessels in the CPA, and they may demonstrate highly variable courses. Rarely, these arteries may form a subarcuate loop, adhere to the dura, or even become embedded within the petrous bone.3 Such variations can limit surgical maneuverability. The petrous dural incision technique allows for the safe dissection and mobilization of durally embedded vessels, thereby expanding the surgical corridor.

Herein, we demonstrate a petrous dural incision technique used for releasing and transposition of the AICA during MVD for trigeminal neuralgia.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 44-year-old male patient presented with a 9-year history of left-sided trigeminal neuralgia. He had been taking carbamazepine 400 mg twice daily for several years. Although he underwent a nerve block 1 year earlier, his symptoms had not improved. Neurological examination was unremarkable. Preoperative cranial imaging revealed no clear vascular compression or lesion responsible for the neuralgia. However, Constructive Interference in Steady State (Siemens Healthineers) magnetic resonance imaging showed the dura-embedded AICA, and 3-dimensional reconstructed computed tomography revealed a bone spur to posterosuperior to the internal auditory canal (Figure 1).

A, Constructive interference in steady state magnetic resonance images shows a left AICA (blue arrow) embedded into petrous dura. B, 3-dimensional reconstruction of brain computed tomography demonstrates petrous bone spur (red arrow) located posterosuperior to IAC (yellow arrow). AICA, anterior inferior cerebellar artery; IAC, internal acoustic canal.

According to institutional regulations, formal approval from the IRB/Ethics committee was not required for this study. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Technique

After positioning the patient in the lateral decubitus position with the head turned to the right, the head was secured using a three-point head fixation clamp. Under neurophysiological monitoring, a curvilinear skin incision was made, followed by a retrosigmoid craniotomy. The dura was incised parallel to the sigmoid sinus. Arachnoid bands in the lateral cerebellomedullary cistern were opened to release cerebrospinal fluid. Discharging of the cerebrospinal fluid helps spontaneous cerebellar retraction. Sharp dissection continued from the lower cranial nerves toward the middle CPA. A durally embedded AICA was encountered in the subarcuate fossa, posterosuperior to the internal auditory canal, hanging from a petrous bone spur (Figure 2A). This vascular anomaly limited our ability to dissect further for the MVD. Initial attempts at dissection of the AICA from the petrous dura using microscissors and blunt dissectors were unsuccessful. The petrous dural incision technique was performed after evaluating the relationship between possible branches and surrounding nerves. The dura around the AICA was incised using a No. 11 blade and dissected from the bone, releasing the AICA loop and the attached dura from the spur (Figure 2B). No subarcuate artery was observed originating from the loop. The AICA was then gently mobilized medially, creating a sufficient corridor to complete the MVD safely (Figure 2C and 2D) (Video).

C, The AICA detached from petrous bone leaving some of the dura with it. D, Further dissection for microvascular decompression performed safely. AICA, anterior inferior cerebellar artery.

The patient tolerated the procedure well, with immediate resolution of symptoms and no postoperative neurological deficits. He was discharged on the postoperative third day.

DISCUSSION

Vascular variations in CPA surgery are critical anatomical challenges. Variants involving the AICA—such as dural embedding, subarcuate fossa adhesion, or integration within the petrous bone—can significantly narrow the surgical corridor, increasing the risk of vascular injury during cerebellar retraction. Rupture of the AICA can lead to permanent neurological deficits such as lateral pontine syndrome.4 Moreover, in cases where the posterior inferior cerebellar artery is hypoplastic or aplastic, AICA may be the sole supplier of large cerebellar territories, thus requiring meticulous preservation.5 Temporal bone-embedded AICA variants have been particularly associated with increased stroke risk and postoperative morbidity.6

The petrous dural incision technique offers a safe and effective method for such anomalies. Dural- or bone-embedded vascular segments—such as the subarcuate AICA loop—can be safely dissected and mobilized using this method, thus expanding the surgical corridor. This is crucial to adequately relax the cerebellum to visualize the root entry zone during MVD or other in long-duration surgeries. In such vascular anomalies, releasing and mobilizing the vessel is necessary before proceeding with deeper dissection. Otherwise, the vessel tearing can happen during cerebellar retraction.

In our case, this technique allowed medialization of the AICA without requiring subarcuate drilling, reducing the risk of vascular or cranial nerve injury. However, drilling should be required to release the vessel intraosseous variants.

Although durally embedded AICA loops have been well documented during vestibular schwannoma surgery,7-10 intraoperative description of such vascular variations in non-neoplastic procedures, including MVD, remains scarce. In our case, the AICA was embedded into the dura of the subarcuate fossa without an accompanying subarcuate artery. According to Xu's classification, this corresponds to a type 2 AICA loop.9 During dissection, attention should be given to any potential branches arising from the adherent vessel to avoid iatrogenic injury. Intraosseous subarcuate loops have been reported in the literature with a prevalence ranging between 0.2% and 1.7%,3,11 and are considered high-risk during translabyrinthine approaches.

Notably, although Hamrick et al12 described a petrous apex bony spur during a middle fossa approach, there are no previous reports of an AICA loop hanging from a petrous bone spur. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of such an anatomical configuration, highlighting a rare but significant surgical consideration.

CONCLUSION

Recognition of vascular variants in the CPA is crucial for safe and effective surgery. The petrous dural incision technique provides a reliable means of safely mobilizing such vessels, thereby facilitating surgical access and minimizing complications during the retrosigmoid approach.
