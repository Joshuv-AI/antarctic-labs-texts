Audiological Phenotype, Natural History, and Intervention in Chinese Patients With Auditory Neuropathy Caused by OTOF Variants
Authors: Hongyang Wang, Kaili Wu, Jin Li, Mengqian Zhang, Xin Gao, Jing Guan, Lan Lan, Linyi Xie, Fen Xiong, Qiujing Zhang, Xiaochuan Xu, Danyang Li, Dayong Wang, Qiuju Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To explore the correlations between genotype and phenotype, disease progression, and outcomes of interventions in patients with auditory neuropathy (AN) caused by OTOF gene variants.Design:Patients with AN associated with OTOF variants were identified using whole-exome or panel sequencing. Variants were interpreted according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. Audiometric tests were conducted, including auditory brainstem response, distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE), behavioral audiometry/pure-tone audiometry, etc. The genetic and audiological characteristics of the patients were analyzed. The disease progression and intervention of the patients were followed up, and phenotype differences were analyzed in combination with genotype.Results:A total of 43 AN patients were identified with OTOF gene variants, including seven novel variants. The pure-tone average was 89.20 ± 17.81 dB HL, with 91.11% having severe hearing loss or greater. The auditory steady-state response and DPOAE results deteriorated with increasing disease duration. Twenty-five patients underwent cochlear implantation, with the Category of Auditory Performance score of 7.00 (5.00, 7.50). Patients with biallelic loss-of-function variants showed a trend toward worse hearing but a higher DPOAE response rate. Similarly, patients with a single allele variant causing protein truncation also had a higher DPOAE extraction rate.Conclusions:Seven novel mutations of the OTOF gene were identified, which enriched the spectrum of OTOF gene variants. Most of the AN patients with OTOF variants showed severe to profound hearing loss, with the progression of the disease course. The cochlear implantation effects were good, which were related to the intervention age and duration. There is a correlation trend between OTOF variants' genotype and phenotype.

INTRODUCTION

Auditory neuropathy (AN) is a disorder of auditory information processing due to dysfunction of inner hair cell (IHC), synapse, spiral ganglion cell, and/or the auditory nerve itself (Starr et al. 1996; Rance & Starr 2015; Chinese Multi-center Research Collaborative Group on Clinical et al. 2022), occurring at all stages of the life cycle, especially in infants and adolescents. AN affects 1.2 to 8.4% of cases with hearing loss from different populations (Foerst et al. 2006; Penido & Isaac 2013; Vignesh et al. 2016), and 0.006 to 0.039% in the low-risk newborn population (Korver et al. 2012). Both genetic factors and environmental risk factors are attributed to AN. A dozen genes have been reported to be related to AN (Shearer & Hansen 2019; H. Wang et al. 2024b), among which OTOF is the first gene to be identified as associated with autosomal recessive non-syndromic AN. Otoferlin, the protein encoded by the OTOF gene, is concentrated in the basolateral part of the cochlear IHC and is an important component of the presynaptic structure of IHC. Otoferlin, as a calcium sensor, triggers membrane fusion at IHC ribbon synapses, thereby playing a critical role in the exocytosis of synaptic vesicles. OTOF gene variants have been reported in different AN populations, with varying carrier frequencies across studies (Varga et al. 2006; Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2008; Romanos et al. 2009; Matsunaga et al. 2012; Iwasa et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2016a). Basic and clinical gene therapies have been successfully performed on AN patients with OTOF variants (Lv et al. 2024; Qi et al. 2024a, b, 2025; H. Wang et al. 2024a). The first crucial step in these studies is to confirm the pathogenicity of the OTOF variants.

Our previous study showed that OTOF gene variants are the most common genetic cause of infant AN (Zhang et al. 2016a). In this study, a total of 43 AN patients with OTOF homozygous or compound heterozygous variants were recruited, and genotype–phenotype correlation was analyzed to provide evidence for clinical management and genetic counseling.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Participants (Cases Recruitment and Clinical Evaluations)

A total of 43 AN patients with OTOF homozygous or compound heterozygous variants were recruited. Audiometric evaluations included pure-tone audiometry/behavioral audiometry, auditory brainstem responses (ABR), distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE), auditory steady-state response (ASSR), and electrocochleography (ECochG) (Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B851). Pure-tone averages (PTA) were defined as the average threshold of 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz frequencies (Thorpe et al. 2021). The severity of hearing impairment was defined as mild (20 to 35 dB HL), moderate (35 to 50 dB HL), moderately severe (50 to 65 dB HL), severe (65 to 80 dB HL), profound (80 to 95 dB HL), or deafness (≥95 dB HL) according to PTA. Cochlear microphonics (CMs) were recorded using insert earphones with reverse polarity and by blocking the stimulus tube to minimize electrical artifacts. Aided hearing thresholds were tested in the sound field based on pure-tone audiometry/behavioral audiometry technology, with the subject wearing a hearing aid device. High-resolution computed tomography and/or magnetic resonance imaging were performed in some cases to assess for structural abnormalities of the middle and inner ears. Telephone follow-up was performed based on an independently designed questionnaire (Song et al. 2021), to collect the patients’ condition change, intervention, language development, growth, and education, as well as vestibular function, including: Vertigo visual analog scale (VAS), Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), and Vertigo Symptom Scale-Chinese (VSS-C).

Inclusion criteria: Cases diagnosed with AN by (1) presence of otoacoustic emission (OAE) and/or CM; (2) abnormal or absent ABR (Gravel et al. 2004; Hayes et al. 2008; Dworsack-Dodge et al. 2012; Feirn et al. 2013; Constantina Georga et al. 2019; Chinese Multi-center Research Collaborative Group on Clinical et al. 2022); (3) positive genetic testing results according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) (Richards et al. 2015; Oza et al. 2018). Exclusion criteria: (1) acoustic neuroma; (2) cases or the guardians who refused genetic testing; (3) cases with only one OTOF variant being identified. For the cases with family history, only the proband was recruited in this study, and the family members were only recruited for segregation analysis, instead of phenotype analysis.

Study Oversight

The study was approved by the Committee of Medical Ethics of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital (No. S2020-228-01). Written informed consent from all the participants or their guardians were obtained.

Genetic Testing

Peripheral blood samples were collected, and EDTA was added for anticoagulation, and the samples were then stored at −80°C. DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the MagPure Buffy Coat DNA Midi KF Kit whole-blood DNA extraction kit.

Exon Capture Sequencing and Variants Detection

Targeted region capture and sequencing were performed on the extracted DNA samples. The average effective sequencing depth of the target region was ≥100X, and the proportion of sites with the average depth of the target region >20X was >95%. The sequencing quality of the raw reads was evaluated to remove low-quality reads and adaptor reads. Sequence alignment was performed with the HG19 reference sequence, and the effect of sequence capture was evaluated. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms and small insertions and deletions were queried to generate base polymorphism results in the target region. The pathogenicity classification of variants was based on the ACMG and the Association for Molecular Pathology sequence variants interpretation guidelines (Richards et al. 2015; Oza et al. 2018), and the detailed interpretation of the guidelines was made by referring to the ClinGen sequence variant interpretation working group and the Association for Clinical Genomic Science, for the candidate variants, polymerase chain reaction amplification and Sanger sequencing was performed to verify the high-throughput sequencing results.

Variants Classification

To investigate the correlation between genotypes and phenotypes, the variants were classified into loss-of-function (Lof) and non-Lof variants. Lof variants include nonsense variants, frameshift variants, in-frame insertion or deletion, and splice variants; non-Lof variants mainly refer to missense variants and synonymous variants. According to whether the variant was a combination of loss-of-function mutations, the patients were classified into the LL group with Lof variants on the bi-alleles, ML group with one missense and one Lof variant, and MM group with two missense variants on the bi-alleles.

The variants were classified according to whether they caused protein truncation. The variants that caused protein truncation include nonsense mutations and frameshift mutations. The variants that did not cause protein truncation include in-frame insertion or deletion, splicing variants, missense mutation, and silent mutation. According to whether the variants caused the protein truncation, the patients were divided into the TT group with two variants causing protein truncation on the bi-alleles, the TN group with one variant causing protein truncation, and the NNt group with no variant causing protein truncation on the bi-alleles.

The variants were classified according to whether they affected the C2 domain. The variants mainly affected the C2 domain, which mainly refers to the fact that the variant position was located on the C2 domain, or the C2 domain was included in the scope of truncation. The variants did not affect the C2 domain, mainly referring to the fact that they were neither located on the C2 domain nor caused truncation of the C2 domain. Based on whether the variants affected the C2 domain, the patients were classified into the CC group with two variants affecting the C2 domain on the bi-alleles, the CN group with one variant affecting the C2 domain, and the NNc group with no variant affecting the C2 domain on the bi-alleles.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 7. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages. The matched samples t test was used to analyze the divergences of the associated normal distribution continuous variables, the nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used to analyze the differences of the associated abnormal distribution continuous variables and ranked categorical variables, and the McNemar test was used to analyze the differences of the paired sample non-ranked categorical variables. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to test the correlation of abnormally distributed samples. An independent samples t test was used to analyze the differences of independent samples of normally distributed continuous variables, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyze the differences of independent samples of abnormally distributed continuous variables and ranked categorical variables, and a chi-square test was used to analyze the differences of independent samples of non-ranked categorical variables. Variance analysis was used to analyze the divergences of continuous variables with normal distribution in groups. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 43 AN patients with OTOF variants were recruited in this study, including 23 males (23/43, 53.49%) and 20 females (20/43, 46.51%). Among the 43 patients, 3 had missing ethnic data, 1 was of Manchu ethnicity, and the remaining 39 were Han Chinese. The test age ranged from 0.33 to 28 (3.35 ± 5.45) years, and the age of onset ranged from 0 to 3 (1.02 ± 0.75) years. Four cases (4/43, 9.30%) were diagnosed as temperature-sensitive auditory neuropathy. The universal newborn hearing screening results of 16 patients (16/43, 37.21%) were obtained, the pass rate of OAE was 100% (16/16), and the pass rate of automatic auditory brainstem response was 0% (0/3). No symptoms indicative of vestibular dysfunction (e.g., vertigo, imbalance) were reported.

Genotype of AN Patients With OTOF Variants

A total of 60 OTOF gene variants were identified among the 43 patients (Fig. 1A, Table S1 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852, Figure S1 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853), 96.67% (58/60) of which were pathogenic or likely pathogenic, 3.33% (2/60) were variants with uncertain significance. The identified variants included 12 (12/60, 20.00%) frameshift variants, 10 (10/60, 16.67%) nonsense variants, 27 (27/60, 45.00%) missense variants, 7 (7/60, 11.67%) splice variants, 3 (3/60, 5.00%) in-frame insertion or deletion variants, and 1 (1/60, 1.67%) synonymous variant. Thirty-seven (37/60, 61.67%) variant loci affected the C2 domain, including 19 loci located on the C2 domain and 18 loci that caused the C2 domain to be truncated, while 23 (23/60, 38.33%) were not located on the C2 domain. The total allele occurrence frequency was 88 times, 41 variants (41/60, 68.33%) were detected only once, 19 variants (19/60, 31.67%) were detected twice or more (Fig. 1B), and the locus with the highest detection frequency was c.5000C>A (p.Ala1667Asp). Seven novel variants (7/60, 11.67%) were reported for the first time in this study, of which 3 were pathogenic, and 4 were likely pathogenic (Table S2 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852).

According to the pathogenicity analysis of the ACMG variant guideline, OTOF gene variants were the cause of 43 patients (Table S3 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852). Among the 43 patients, 3 (3/43, 6.98%) patients had a family history with more than two patients in the family, and 40 (40/43, 93.02%) patients were sporadic cases. Five cases (5/43, 11.63%) were homozygous variants, and 38 cases (38/43, 88.37%) were compound heterozygous variants.

Audiological Characteristics

ABR testing was absent or severely abnormal bilaterally in all 43 patients (100%) with OTOF variants. Among them, 42 patients (97.7%) showed no response even at maximum acoustic output, whereas one patient (2.3%) had a severely abnormal response, with only wave V recorded at 100 dB nHL; latency was prolonged (left ear: 7.95 msec, right ear: 8.05 msec) and the threshold was elevated (left ear: 95 dB nHL, right ear: 90 dB nHL). CM waves were recorded bilaterally in 31 patients (100%) (Figure S2A in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853).

The average response rate of DPOAE in 43 patients (86 ears) was 68.15% (0 to 100.00%), the extraction rate in 59 ears (59/86, 68.60%) was greater than 62.5% (5/8), and that in 27 ears (27/86, 31.40%) was less than 62.5%. The distribution of extraction rates in each ear was shown in Figure 2A. The bilateral extraction rate of 24 cases (24/43, 55.81%) was greater than or equal to 62.5% (5/8), the monaural extraction rate of 11 patients (11/43, 25.58%) was greater than or equal to 62.5%, and the binaural extraction rate of 8 patients (8/43, 18.60%) was less than 62.5%. The extraction rates of each frequency from 0.75 to 8 kHz were 41.25%, 50.60%, 63.75%, 75.00%, 75.00%, 77.11%, 80.25% and 74.39%, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Twenty-three patients (45 ears) underwent pure-tone audiometry or behavioral audiometry, with an average hearing threshold of 89.20 ± 17.81 dB HL (Fig. 3B), with 1 ear (1/45, 2.22%) moderate, 3 ears (3/45, 6.67%) moderately severe, 8 ears (8/45, 17.78%) severe, 12 ears (12/45, 26.67%) profound, and 21 ears (21/45, 46.67%) deafness. Most patients (21/22, 95.55%) had bilaterally symmetrical hearing loss or hearing threshold difference within 15 dB. For the audiogram types, 11 ears (11/44, 25.00%) were flat, 11 ears (11/44, 25.00%) were up-sloping, 9 ears (9/44, 20.45%) were down-sloping, 8 ears (8/44, 18.18%) were U-shape, 3 ears (3/44, 6.82%) were peak-shape, and 2 ears (2/44, 4.55%) were other types. Forty patients (80 ears) underwent tympanometry, among which 52 ears (52/80, 65.00%) showed “A” type curves and 28 ears (28/80, 35.00%) showed “As” type curves. Acoustic reflex testing was performed in 39 patients (78 ears), with both ipsilateral and contralateral reflexes absent in all cases.

Correlation analysis was conducted between the pure-tone or behavioral audiometry and DPOAE in 28 patients (55 ears). Spearman correlation analysis showed that there was no significant relationship between the average hearing threshold and DPOAE extraction rate (R = 0.103, p = 0.456). For the results of pure-tone or behavioral audiometry and ASSR in 25 patients (49 ears), no significant relationship between audiometry and ASSR threshold at each frequency of 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz (0.5 kHz: R = 0.232, p = 0.109; 1 kHz: R = 0.070, p = 0.632; 2 kHz: R = 0.214, p = 0.141; 4 kHz: R = 0.114, p = 0.435).

Natural History

Fifteen patients (27 ears) underwent follow-up DPOAE. The follow-up period ranged from 0.17 to 3.58 years, the mean age of first examination was 1.70 ± 0.82 years, and the mean age of reexamination was 2.95 ± 1.43 years (t = −2.742, p = 0.012). The median extraction rate was 62.50% (37.50%, 87.50%) at the first examination and 50.00% (25.00%, 75.00%) at the reexamination, and the difference was statistically significant (Z = 2.077, p = 0.048) (Fig. 2C). At the first evaluation, 17 ears (17/27, 62.96%) showed extraction rates ≥62.5%, while 10 ears (10/27, 37.04%) were <62.5%. At reevaluation, 13 ears (13/27, 48.15%) had extraction rates ≥62.5%, and 14 ears (14/27, 51.85%) were <62.5%. The difference between the two evaluations was not statistically significant (p = 0.388; Table S4 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852). The average annual decrease was 17.35%. Among the 27 ears, 6 ears (22.22%) showed an increase in the extraction rate, 15 ears (55.56%) showed a decrease, and 6 ears (22.22%) showed no change (Fig. 2D). There was a decrease in the extraction rate at different frequencies, but the difference was statistically significant only at 0.75 kHz (p = 0.007) and 1 kHz (p = 0.017), while other frequencies showed no statistically significant difference (Fig. 2E). A linear equation of extraction rate changes with age was fitted (Fig. 2F), and the correlation was statistically significant between the extraction rate and age by Spearman correlation analysis (p = 0.001, R = −0.329) (Figure S3A in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853).

Nineteen patients (38 ears) underwent follow-up ASSR testing. The follow-up period ranged from 0.25 to 4.75 (1.36 ± 1.39) years. The mean age of the first examination was 1.35 ± 0.69 years, and the age of reexamination was 2.71 ± 1.44 years (t = −3.712, p = 0.001). Significant differences were observed in threshold changes across frequencies from 0.5 to 4 kHz between the first and reevaluations (Fig. 3C). The average annual increase of the hearing threshold from 0.5 to 4 kHz was 9.56, 1.99, 4.30, and 14.10 dB (Table S5 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852), respectively, and the changes in the hearing threshold in each ear were shown in Figure S3B in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853. The linear equation of the hearing threshold of each frequency from 0.5 to 4 kHz with age was fitted (Figure S3B in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853). Spearman correlation analysis showed that the correlation between hearing thresholds at each frequency and age was statistically significant (0.5 kHz: p = 0.004, R = 0.258; 1 kHz: p = 0.008, R = 0.235; 2 kHz: p = 0.008, R = 0.235; 4 kHz: p < 0.001, R = 0.343) (Figure S3B in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853).

Fifteen patients (28 ears) underwent pure-tone audiometry follow-up, over a period of 0.17 to 9.83 (2.31 ± 2.73) years, with the first examination at the age of 1.75 (1.33, 2.50) years, and reexamination at 3.08 (2.04, 5.08) years (Z = −2.516, p = 0.012). The average hearing threshold at the first examination was 91.56 ± 13.91 dB HL, and 93.94 ± 11.76 dB HL at reexamination (t = −0.801, p = 0.430). Except for a statistically significant difference in thresholds at 8 kHz between the first examination and reexamination (Z = −2.098, p = 0.036), no significant differences at other frequencies (Fig. 3D). The average hearing threshold increased 1.34 dB per year, among which 18 ears (18/28, 64.29%) showed hearing threshold increase and 10 ears (10/28, 35.71%) showed hearing threshold decrease (Fig. 3E). There was no statistically significant correlation between the average hearing thresholds and age by Spearman correlation analysis (p = 0.896, R = −0.014) (Figure S3C in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853).

Vestibular function was assessed using questionnaires in 28 patients. Among the 28 successfully followed-up patients, no significant vestibular abnormalities were observed, including Patient 1607625, who reported transient dizziness associated with cochlear implant programming discomfort, which resolved after adaptation, whose scores of VAS, DHI and VSS-C were 0; Patients 1607480 and 2008553 reported mild dizziness (VAS = 1) with minimal perceived lifestyle impact, with other scales (DHI, VSS-C) scored 0; Patient 400250 demonstrated mild symptom burden (DHI = 22; VSS = 42), the DHI score of whom primarily related to positional factors. Responses on the VSS-C scale indicated a low frequency of vertigo episodes; vestibular dysfunction could not be confirmed for this patient. Patient 1006754 had low-grade scores (DHI = 4; VSS-C = 5), whose DHI and VSS-C scores mainly stem from sensations of dizziness triggered by rapid head movements, which occurred occasionally.

Intervention

Thirty-four patients (34/43, 79.07%) were informed of the intervention mode at follow-up, and 2 patients (2/34, 5.88%) with bilateral cochlear implantation (CI), 8 patients (8/34, 25.53%) with CI+ hearing aid (HA), 15 patients (15/34, 44.12%) with unilateral CI, 2 patients (2/34, 5.88%) with bilateral HA, and 7 patients (7/34, 20.59%) did not receive any intervention (Fig. 4A). Ten patients (17 ears) were recorded the hearing threshold before and after the intervention (Figure S4 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B853), including 13 ears with HA and 4 ears with CI, with a threshold improvement of 29.58 (18.75, 36.25) dB under HA intervention and 73.75 (45.00, 81.25) dB improvement under CI intervention.

Among the 25 patients who underwent CI, the first implantation age was 3.00 (2.00, 4.00) years, the intervention duration was 3.50 (2.25, 6.00) years, the average Categories of auditory performance (CAP) score was 7.00 (5.00, 7.50), the speech intelligibility rating (SIR) score was 4.00 (3.00, 4.50) (Fig. 4B). The CAP score in CI patients was negatively correlated with the age at implantation (p = 0.747, R = −0.071), and was proportional to implantation duration (p = 0.001, R = 0.652). The SIR score was negatively correlated with the age of implantation (p = 0.278, R = −0.236) and proportional to the implantation duration (p = 0.016, R = 0.497) (Figs. 4C, D).

DPOAE changes after a period of intervention were recorded in 11 patients (20 ears), including 17 ears with HA and 5 ears with CI. The follow-up period of 17 years of HA intervention was 0.83 (0.33, 1.67) years, with a range of 0.17 to 5.58 years, with an average annual decrease of 6.05%, including 9 ears with reduced extraction rate, 5 ears with increased extraction rate, and 3 ears with unchanged extraction rate. The DPOAE extraction rate in HA intervention patients was 63% (50%, 88%), and 63% (38%, 75%) in nonintervention patients, showing no statistically significant difference (Z = −0.224, p = 0.823) (Table S6 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852). In 4 patients (5 ears) with CI, the extraction rate of DPOAE decreased in 3 ears before and after CI, and remained unchanged in 2 ears (Table S7 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852).

Genotype and Phenotype Correlation Analysis

According to the combination of loss-of-function mutations in bi-alleles, 11 cases (11/43, 25.58%) had Lof variants in bi-alleles (LL), 21 cases (21/43, 48.84%) had mutations in one missense and one Lof variant (ML), and 11 cases (11/43, 25.58%) had mutations in bi-alleles as Lof variants (MM). There were no significant differences in the age and gender among the three groups with LL, ML or MM variants, nor in the DPOAE, pure-tone hearing threshold, and ASSR test (except for the pure-tone hearing threshold at 0.5 kHz) (Table S8 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852 and Fig. 5). There was a trend that the LL group was slightly higher than the ML group and the MM group in the extraction rate of DPOAE. The threshold at the frequency of 0.25 to 1 kHz in the LL group was slightly better than that in the ML group. The ASSR frequency thresholds of 1, 2, and 4 kHz in the LL group were slightly worse than those in the ML group and MM group, and the frequency thresholds of the ML group and the MM group were similar.

According to the combination of protein truncation on biallelic genes, 4 cases (4/43, 9.30%) of the mutations on the biallelic led to protein truncation (TT), 20 cases (20/43, 46.51%) of a monoallelic mutation led to protein truncation (TN), and 19 cases (19/43, 44.19%) of the biallelic did not lead to protein truncation (NNt). There were no significant differences in the age and gender among the three groups with TT, TN, or NNt variants, nor in the pure-tone hearing threshold, and ASSR test (except for the pure-tone hearing threshold at 4 kHz) (Table S8 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852 and Fig. 5). The extraction rate of DPOAE in the TN group was higher than that of the NNt group (p = 0.032). The pure-tone threshold of the TN group at the frequency of 0.25–8 kHz was slightly better than that of the NNt group. The ASSR frequency thresholds of the TN group and the NNt group were similar.

According to the combination of variants affecting C2 domain on bi-alleles, 16 cases (16/43, 37.21%) of the variants on the biallelic affected the C2 domain (CC), 20 cases (20/43, 46.51%) of the mutations on the monoallelic affected the C2 domain (CN), and 7 cases (7/43, 16.28%) of the mutations on the bi-allele did not affect the C2 domain (NNc). There were no significant differences in the age and gender among the three groups with CC, CN or NNc variants, nor in the DPOAE, pure-tone hearing threshold, and ASSR test (except for the ASSR threshold at 1 and 2 kHz) (Table S8 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B852 and Fig. 5). There was a trend that the extraction rate of DPOAE in CC group was slightly higher than the CN group and the NNc group. The PTA of CC group was slightly better than that of the CN group. The ASSR thresholds of the CC group were slightly better than the NNc group and the CN group, and the differences at 1 and 2 kHz were statistically significant (1 kHz: CC versus CN: p = 0.010, CC versus NNc: p = 0.002; 2 kHz: CC versus CN: p = 0.003, CC versus NNc: p < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

The OTOF gene is the first gene identified as being associated with autosomal recessive non-syndromic AN (Chaib et al. 1996), accounting for 41.2% of infant AN cases (Zhang et al. 2016a). The affected lesions of OTOF mutations related to AN are predominantly synaptic and presynaptic (Roux et al. 2006; Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2008; D. Y. Wang et al. 2010). To date, a total of 98 papers have been published on OTOF gene variants, and 199 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the OTOF gene have been reported, involving 31 countries or regions, with 72 variants and 38 patients reported in the Chinese population, followed by 45 variants in Japan and 21 variants in Pakistan. In this study, we identified 7 novel variants, which were pathogenic or likely pathogenic, extending the OTOF genotype spectrum and enriching the data of the OTOF patient population. To further provide evidence for clinical management and genetic counseling, genotype–phenotype correlations were analyzed in the 43 AN patients with OTOF variants.

Analysis of the Genetic and Epidemiological Situation of OTOF Genes

The most common variant in this study was c.5000C>A (p.Ala1667Asp) (7/108), which was previously classified as a variant with uncertain significance. We upgraded its pathogenicity and categorized it as likely pathogenic (likely pathogenic = PM2 + PM3_Strong + PP3), and the identification of this locus in patients can be followed in the future. According to previous studies, the most frequent variant in OTOF is c.5816G>A (p.R1939Q), which has been detected in Japanese (Matsunaga et al. 2012; Iwasa et al. 2013, 2019, 2022), Korean (Chang et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2018), Chinese (Xie et al. 2021), and American (Varga et al. 2003) populations. This variant is considered to be a common pathogenic variant of AN in the Japanese and Korean populations, and it was also detected in this study (3/108). The second common variant is c.2485C>T (p.Q829X), which is common in the Spanish population (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003, 2008; Gallo-Teran et al. 2006), as well as French (Rouillon et al. 2006), Argentinean (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2008), Mexican (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2008), and British (Varga et al. 2006; Carvalho et al. 2016) populations. However, this variant has never been detected in the Chinese population, nor was it detected in this study. OTOF c.5098G>C (p.E1700Q) also has a high rate in the Chinese (Taiwan) population (Chiu et al. 2010), and the locus was also detected in this study (2/108).

Except for the hotspot mutations difference, the OTOF variants showed consistency between different populations, as well as consistent phenotype. Among the hundreds of variants of OTOF, some variants appeared in different populations, such as OTOF c.2122 C>T, p.R708X, which was found in Spanish, Pakistani, Indian, Algerian, Colombian, and Chinese (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003; Varga et al. 2006; Choi et al. 2009; Ammar-Khodja et al. 2015; Pandey et al. 2017; Richard et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2020; Fareed et al. 2021).

Phenotype Characterization of Patients With OTOF Gene Variants Associated AN

In this study, the onset age of all the AN patients with OTOF gene variants was infants (less than 3 years old), which is consistent with the current research results. Universal newborn hearing screening showed a clear response to OAE but no clear response to the automatic auditory brainstem response. Most of the cases showed absent (97.67%) or grossly abnormal (2.33%) ABR, indicating desynchronization of auditory nerve fibers, lack of neural activity, or nerve conduction block. The recorded rate of CM was 100%, while the extraction rate of DPOAE was 68.60% in this study. DPOAE and CM tests together suggested the function preservation of outer hair cells (OHC). The extraction rate of DPOAE was lower than that of CM, because DPOAE is susceptible to a variety of factors, such as the state of the patient’s outer middle ear, the test environment, and the adaptation of the equipment; DPOAE is more sensitive to OHC damage; DPOAE and CM reflect different functions of OHC. In the previously reported AN patients associated with OTOF gene variants, the absence rate of ABR was 89.83% (106/118), and the abnormality rate was 10.17% (12/118) (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003; Varga et al. 2003, 2006; Tekin et al. 2005; Gallo-Teran et al. 2006; Rouillon et al. 2006; Santarelli et al. 2009, 2015; Zadro et al. 2010; L. P. Zhang et al. 2013; Jin et al. 2014; Chang et al. 2015; Fedick et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2016a; Tang et al. 2017; Almontashiri et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2018; Y. Wang et al. 2018; C. C. Wu et al. 2018; Qiu et al. 2019; D. Wu et al. 2020; Zhai et al. 2020; Iwasa et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2022); OAE responses could be documented in 93.13% (149/160) of the ears (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003, 2008; Varga et al. 2003, 2006; Tekin et al. 2005; Gallo-Teran et al. 2006; Rouillon et al. 2006; Santarelli et al. 2009, 2015; Chiu et al. 2010; Zadro et al. 2010; Iwasa et al. 2013, 2019, 2022; L. P. Zhang et al. 2013; Jin et al. 2014; Yildirim-Baylan et al. 2014; Chang et al. 2015; Fedick et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2016a; Tang et al. 2017; Almontashiri et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2018; Y. Wang et al. 2018; C. C. Wu et al. 2018; Qiu et al. 2019; D. Wu et al. 2020; Zhai et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2022), and the recorded rate of CM was 100% (27/27) (Varga et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2016a; Tang et al. 2017; Y. Wang et al. 2018; C. C. Wu et al. 2018; Zhai et al. 2020). The results of this study are consistent with them.

There were three manifestations of ECochG in patients in this study: (i) −SP/AP amplitude ratio greater than 0.4 and less than 1 (25.00%); (ii) −SP/AP amplitude ratio greater than 1 (66.67%); (iii) SP and AP waveforms were not observed (8.33%). There were individual differences in SP and AP amplitudes. −SP amplitudes can be as low as 0.12 μV or as high as 3.68 μV. It is hypothesized that the low −SP amplitude may be due to the accumulation of neurotransmitters at the presynaptic membrane to producing toxicity, leading to the impairment of IHC. The high −SP amplitude may be due to reduced activation of auditory nerve efferent fibers and abnormal inhibition of IHC. Normal −SP amplitudes were also present, suggesting normal IHC function. AP amplitude is influenced by individual differences on the one hand, and reflects the degree of preservation of synaptic release of neurotransmitter function on the other (McMahon et al. 2008; Santarelli et al. 2008, 2009; Rance & Starr 2015). Santarelli et al. (2015, 2021a, 2021b) have explored the ECochG classification of OTOF-positive AN patients: (i) Negative potentials that start at SP and last for a prolonged period of time are considered to have a neurogenic component, that is, synaptic release is abnormal, and there are still nerve fibers that are activated but do not produce synchronized discharges; (ii) In the sustained potentials after SP, there were also identifiable residual compound action potential, which indicates that synaptic release was able to synchronize neural discharges, but the number of activated nerve fibers was limited, so the amplitude of compound action potential is not high. (iii) The presence of only SP, followed by no sustained potential, suggests that vesicle release is completely impaired and that little or no nerve fibers are activated. According to this criterion, this study mostly belongs to the second mode, suggesting that synapses retain part of the transmitter release capacity to enable the limited nerve fibers to discharge synchronously.

The threshold curve of the ASSR test was “√”-shaped, indicating the retention of high-frequency residual hearing. The results of pure-tone audiometry/behavioral audiometry showed that both ears were basically symmetrical (95.55%), and the degree of hearing loss, which was distributed from moderate hearing loss to complete deafness, mainly in severe hearing loss and above deafness (91.11%). There were various types of audiograms, including relatively common flat patterns (25.00%) and rising patterns (25.00%), as well as falling patterns (20.45%), trough patterns (18.18%), and peaked or saucer patterns (6.82%), and there was a trend that the auditory threshold of high frequency was better than that of low and middle frequencies. The results of the acoustic immittance test showed type A or type As tympanograms, indicating that the middle ear was normal. Among the previous reports of AN patients related to OTOF gene variants, the degree of hearing loss was mainly severe or above, and the type of audiogram was mainly flat patterns (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003, 2008; Romanos et al. 2009; Matsunaga et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2016a; Kim et al. 2018; C. C. Wu et al. 2018; Iwasa et al. 2022), which were consistent with this study.

The follow-up rate was 74.42% in this study. During the follow-up period, the ASSR threshold increased, and the annual increase threshold was different among different frequencies, with the largest increase at 4 kHz (14.10 dB HL cg) and the smallest increase at 1 kHz (1.99 dB HL cg). The extraction rate of DPOAE decreased with the disease course, with an average annual decrease of 17.35%. The thresholds of pure-tone audiometry showed no significant progression. In general, AN patients with OTOF gene variants did not have significant progression of natural disease over a follow-up period of 0.25 to 12.17 (3.31 ± 3.78) years and only showed a certain degree of deterioration in ASSR and DPOAE tests. A total of 36 patients (Chiu et al. 2010; Matsunaga et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2016a) discussed the progression of the disease, of which 30 cases (30/36, 83.33%) were stable without progression, and 6 cases (6/36, 16.67%) showed progressive hearing loss, suggesting that the hearing of OTOF variants related AN patients was mostly stable, only a few would show progressive hearing loss. Temperature-sensitive auditory neuropathy cases with OTOF mutations are unique and may have improved hearing (Zhang et al. 2016b). At the same time, this study analyzed the impact of interventions on DPOAE, showing no relationship between the intervention and the change in DPOAE. This issue is controversial, but studies have suggested that HA should not be avoided to preserve the OAE response (Iwasa et al. 2022).

The majority (73.53%) of the patients in this study opted for the CI intervention with good results. The CAP score was 7.00 (5.00, 7.50), the SIR Score was 4.00 (3.00, 4.50), and the aided hearing threshold was increased by 73.75 (45.00, 81.25) dB compared to an unaided hearing threshold. A total of 18 papers (Rodriguez-Ballesteros et al. 2003; Loundon et al. 2005; Rouillon et al. 2006; Chiu et al. 2010; Zadro et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2011; Runge et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2013; Santarelli et al. 2015; Miyagawa et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2016b; Park et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2018; Hosoya et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2023) have reported CI interventions in AN patients with OTOF gene variants, involving 67 patients in seven countries. For the effect of the intervention, the aided hearing threshold under CI was 25 to 45 dB, which was better than that under HA intervention of 75 dB (Loundon et al. 2005; Rouillon et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2013, 2016b; Chen et al. 2018). The categories of auditory performance (CAP) were 6 to 7 points when the implant is less than 2 years old and 3 to 4 points when the implant is more than 2 years old (Park et al. 2017), and patients with implant age of less than 18 months improve faster than those with implant age of more than 18 months (Kim et al. 2018). The duration of implantation was 4 to 5 points within 1 year, 4 to 7 points after 2 years, and 7 points after 3 years (C. C. Wu et al. 2011; Kim et al. 2018). Compared with patients with hearing loss caused by GJB2 and SLC26A4 gene variants and cytomegalovirus infection, the CAP score after CI showed no difference (Hosoya et al. 2018; C. C. Wu et al. 2018). Some scholars discussed the effect of CI intervention with the age of implantation 2 years and the duration of implantation 1 year as the boundary (Park et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2021). In this study, according to this standard analysis, it was found that AN patients with OTOF gene variants had better results after implantation within 2 years of age and intervention over 1 year. Moreover, the influence of intervention duration seemed to be greater in this study, which may be due to the narrow distribution of implantation age data and the relatively large span of intervention duration data.

Previous studies have shown that truncation variants were associated with severe to profound hearing loss (Iwasa et al. 2022). Patients with missense/missense (MM) or missense/Lof variants (ML) had better hearing threshold than patients with Lof/Lof variants (LL) Thorpe et al. (2021). This study classified the combination of patient variant types in three ways, and a certain trend was observed. The Lof and truncation variants were associated with worse hearing thresholds but better DPOAE elicitation. Variants affecting the C2 domain seemed to be associated with better hearing threshold and DPOAE elicitation. However, no significant statistical difference was found, which may be due to insufficient sample size. The specific mechanism and the reasons leading to such a trend have not been clarified, which can be further studied.

Previous study found that Lof variants have a tendency to be associated with worse hearing threshold (Thorpe et al. 2021), and patients from the TT group have a greater proportion of severe hearing loss (Iwasa et al. 2022), consistent with the reports (p = 0.027). However, the results for variants affecting the C2 domain were inconsistent. The patients from CC group seemed to be associated with a worse hearing phenotype, but the distribution of hearing loss was only different from patients with monoallelic variants affecting the C2 domain (p = 0.010). Therefore, the correlation between C2 domain-related variants and phenotype still needs further exploration.

There are some limitations in this study: Since no patients described symptoms indicative of vestibular dysfunction (e.g., vertigo, imbalance), routine vestibular function testing was not performed on these individuals in the original study protocol. To further follow up on vestibular symptoms experienced by all 43 patients, telephone calls were conducted to supplement information on vestibular function. Among the patients followed up, no significant vestibular symptoms were reported. We will further emphasize inquiries about vestibular symptoms during subsequent follow-up visits and conduct corresponding examinations with the video head impulse test and vestibular evoked myogenic potentials.

In conclusion, this study deciphered the phenotype and genotype profiles of AN patients with OTOF variants, as well as their natural history and intervention. Seven novel mutations of OTOF gene were identified, which enriched the spectrum of OTOF gene variants. A recurrent locus c.5000C>A (p.Ala1667Asp) was found in 6 unrelated patient families, which might be a hotspot of AN patients. Most of the AN patients with OTOF variants exhibited severe to profound hearing loss, with the progression of the disease course. The CI effects were good, which were related to the intervention age and duration. Genotype and phenotype correlation analysis suggested that different genotype classifications might be related to different phenotype characteristics, providing evidence for clinical management, genetic screening, and genetic counseling.
