Deep SPI: Safe Policy Improvement via World Models
Florent Delgrange, Raphael Avalos, Willem Röpke

Abstract
Safe policy improvement (SPI) offers theoretical control over policy updates, yet existing guarantees largely concern offline, tabular reinforcement learning (RL). We study SPI in general online settings, when combined with world model and representation learning. We develop a theoretical framework showing that restricting policy updates to a well-defined neighborhood of the current policy ensures monotonic improvement and convergence. This analysis links transition and reward prediction losses to representation quality, yielding online, “deep” analogues of classical SPI theorems from the offline RL literature. Building on these results, we introduce DeepSPI , a principled on-policy algorithm that couples local transition and reward losses with regularised policy updates. On the ALE-57 benchmark, DeepSPI matches or exceeds strong baselines, including PPO and DeepMDPs , while retaining theoretical guarantees.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning
(RL) trains agents to act in complex environments through trial and error
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
. To scale to high-dimensional domains, modern approaches rely on function approximation, making
representation learning

(
Echchahed and Castro 2025
)
essential for constructing latent spaces where behaviorally similar states are mapped close together and policies and value functions become easier to estimate. A complementary approach is
model learning
, where a predictive model of the environment is trained
(
Ha and Schmidhuber 2018
)
. Such models can be leveraged for planning, generating simulated experience, or improving value estimates
(
Hafner et al. 2021
;
Schrittwieser et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2019
)
.

In the online setting, where the agent updates its policy during interaction, avoiding catastrophic errors is critical. Two key challenges arise:
out-of-trajectory (OOT) world models
and
confounding policy updates
. OOT issues arise when the world model fails to capture rarely visited regions of the state space, leading to unreliable predictions and unsafe updates when the latent policy explores these regions
(
Suau et al. 2024
)
. Confounding updates occur when both the policy and its underlying representation are updated simultaneously: poor representations can lock the agent into suboptimal behavior, while the policy itself prevents corrective updates to the representation.
Safe Policy Improvement
(SPI) mitigates such risks by ensuring that new policies are not substantially worse than their predecessors
(
Thomas et al. 2015
)
. Classical SPI methods provide rigorous results in tabular MDPs but depend on exhaustive state–action coverage, making them unsuitable for continuous or high-dimensional spaces.

We address this gap by directly connecting representation and model learning with safe policy improvement in complex environments with general state spaces. Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a novel neighborhood operator that constrains policy updates, enabling policy improvement with convergence guarantees. Second, we combine this operator with principled model losses to bound the gap between a policy’s performance in the world model and in the true environment, thereby enabling safe policy improvement in complex MDPs. This analysis also shows that our scheme enforces representation quality by ensuring that states with similar values remain close in the learned latent space. Third, we connect our theory to PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
and propose
DeepSPI
, a practical algorithm that achieves strong empirical performance on the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE;
Bellemare et al. 2013
) while retaining theoretical guarantees.

Related Work

Regularizing policy improvements.

Regularized updates, as in TRPO, PPO, and related analyses, are now standard for stabilizing policy optimization
(
Schulman et al. 2015
;
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Geist et al. 2019
;
Kuba et al. 2022
)
. Our work extends this perspective to the joint training of a world model and a representation, where we constrain policy updates in a principled neighborhood while controlling model quality through transition and reward losses.

SPI
methods provide principled guarantees on policy updates from fixed datasets (offline RL)
(
Thomas et al. 2015
;
Ghavamzadeh et al. 2016a
;
Laroche et al. 2019
;
Simão et al. 2020
;
Castellini et al. 2023
)
.
These methods assume tabular state spaces and offline data, where error bounds must hold globally across all state–action pairs, often via robust MDP formulations
(
Iyengar 2005
;
Nilim and Ghaoui 2005
)
. Our setting is fundamentally different: we study
online
RL with high-dimensional inputs, where such global constraints are intractable. We take inspiration from the SPI literature but introduce local, on-policy losses that make safe improvement feasible in practice.

Representation learning and model-based RL.

Auxiliary transition and reward prediction losses are central to many model-based methods, from
DeepMDP
to
Dreamer
and related world-model approaches
(
Gelada et al. 2019
;
Hafner et al. 2021
)
.
In particular, the losses we consider for learning transitions and rewards generalize a wide range of objectives used across the model-based RL literature
(
François-Lavet et al. 2019
;
van der Pol et al. 2020
;
Kidambi et al. 2020
;
Delgrange et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Alegre et al. 2023
)
.
Other works design representations that cluster states into groups within which the agent is guaranteed to behave similarly
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Castro et al. 2021
;
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Avalos et al. 2024
)
, typically under restrictive conditions (e.g., deterministic dynamics or Gaussian-kernel assumptions).
By contrast, we directly link representation quality and model accuracy to our safe policy improvement analysis, yielding tractable guarantees in the online setting.

Background

In the following, given a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
, we write
\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X}}}\right)
for the set of distributions over
\mathcal{X}
. For any distribution
\mu\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X}}}\right)
, we denote by
\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right)
its support.

Markov Decision Processes
(MDPs) offer a formalism for sequential decision-making under uncertainty.
Formally, an MDP is a tuple of the form
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,{R},s_{\mathit{I}},\gamma\rangle
consisting of a set of states
\mathcal{S}
, actions
\mathcal{A}
, a transition function
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{S}}}\right)
, a bounded reward function
{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\left\|{R}\right\|_{\infty}={R}_{\textsc{max}}
, an initial state
s_{\mathit{I}}\in\mathcal{S}
, and a discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
.
Unless otherwise stated, we generally assume that
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are compact.
An agent interacting in
\mathcal{M}
produces
trajectories
, i.e., infinite sequences of states and actions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
visited along the interaction so that
s_{0}=s_{\mathit{I}}
and
s_{t+1}\sim P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)
for all
t\geq 0
.

At each time step
t
, the agent selects an action according to a (stationary)
policy

\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}}}\right)
mapping states to distributions over actions. Running an MDP under
\pi
induces a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
over trajectories
(
Revuz 1984
)
, with associated expectation operator
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
; we write
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot\mid s_{0}=s]
when the initial state is fixed to
s\in\mathcal{S}
. A policy has
full support
if
\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi(\cdot\mid s)}}\right)=\mathcal{A}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and we denote the set of all policies by
\Pi
. The
stationary measure
of
\pi
is the distribution over states visited under
\pi
, defined by
\xi_{\pi}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\xi_{\pi}}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[P(\cdot\mid s,a)]
.
This measure is often assumed to exist in continual RL
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
, is unique in episodic RL
(
Huang 2020
)
, and corresponds to the occupancy measure in discounted RL
(
Metelli et al. 2023
)
.

Value functions.

The performance of the agent executing a policy
\pi\in\Pi
in each single state
s\in\mathcal{S}
can be evaluated through the
value function

V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}{R}(s_{t},a_{t})\mid s_{0}=s\right]
.
The goal of an agent is to maximize the
return
from the initial state, given by
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathcal{M}}}\right)=V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\mathit{I}}}}\right)
.
To evaluate the quality of any action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we consider the
action value function

Q^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)={R}(s,a)+\gamma\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}{{V}^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)}
, being the unique solution of
Bellman 1957
’s equation with
{V}^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}{Q^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)}
.
Alternatively, any given action can be evaluated through the
advantage function

A^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)=Q^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)-V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)
, giving the advantage of selecting an action over the current policy.

Representation learning in RL.

In realistic environments, the state–action space is too large for tabular policies or value functions. Instead, deep RL employs an encoder
\phi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
that maps states to a tractable
latent space

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
, from which value functions can be approximated. Learning such encoders is referred to as
representation learning
. To improve representations, agents are often trained with additional objectives, commonly
auxiliary tasks
requiring predictive signals. Policy-based methods then optimize a
latent policy

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\colon\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}\to\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}}}\right)
jointly with
\phi
, executed in the environment as
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s))
. By convention, we write
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid s)
for
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi(s)
when
\phi
is clear, and denote the set of all latent policies by
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. For any
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, the composed policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi
belongs to
\Pi
.

Model-based RL
augments policy learning with a
world model

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}=\langle{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}},\mathcal{A},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{{R}}},\bar{s}_{I},\gamma\rangle}
, which can improve (i) sample efficiency by generating trajectories, (ii) value estimation through planning, and (iii) representation learning by grouping states with similar behavior. When
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}=\mathcal{S}
, the model must replicate environment dynamics, which is often intractable. Instead, we focus on
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
defined by the learned representation
\phi
, so that
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
becomes an abstraction of
\mathcal{M}
. Learning transition and reward functions then additionally serves as an auxiliary signal for the representation, encouraging states with similar behavior to map close in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
. Since
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
is the latent space,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
corresponds to the policies of
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
. We further assume
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
is equipped with a metric
\,\overline{\!{d}}\colon\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}\times\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}\to[0,\infty)
to measure distances.

No way Home: when world models and policies go out of trajectories

World models are usually learned toward minimizing a
reward loss

L_{{R}}
and/or

L_{P}
from experiences
\eta

Those experiences are either gathered in the form of a

or a

In general, the loss functions take the following form:
L_{{R}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{B}}f_{{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi,\,\overline{\!{{R}}};\,\eta}}\right)
and
L_{P}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{B}}f_{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu;\,\eta}}\right)

(resp.

) assign a “cost” relative to the error between
{R}
and
\,\overline{\!{{R}}}
(resp.
P
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
) according to the experiences
\eta
and their representation.
Henceforth, we refer to the policy
\pi_{\text{b}}
used to insert experiences in
\mathcal{B}
as the

.

3.1
Out-of-trajectory world model

One may consider leveraging the model
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
to improve the policy
\pi_{\text{b}}
.
This can be achieved by
directly planning a new policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
or
drawing imagined trajectories in the world model to evaluate new actions and improve on sample complexity during RL.
However, since the world model is learned from experiences stored in
\mathcal{B}
, we can only be certain of its average accuracy according to this data.
This is problematic because some regions of the state space of
\mathcal{M}
may have been rarely, or not at all, visited under
\pi_{\text{b}}

In that case, the predictions made in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
might cause the agent to “hallucinate” inaccurate trajectories in the latent space and spoil the policy improvement.
This problem, known as the

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
deviates substantially from

5
With great world models comes great representation

This section explains how the neighborhood operator of Eq.
2
enables safe policy improvement during world-model planning and representation updates in complex environments. Standard SPI methods ignore representation learning and require exhaustive state–action coverage in
\mathcal{B}
to obtain guarantees, making them unsuitable for general state-action spaces. Even in finite domains, bounding the count of each state–action pair does not scale.
Laroche et al. 2019
proposed
baseline bootstrapping
for under-sampled pairs, but their approach remains impractical in large-scale settings despite conceptual similarities to our operator. Further discussion of SPI limitations is provided in Appendix
D
.

Learning an accurate world model.

SPI typically relies on optimizing a policy with respect to a latent model learned from the data stored in
\mathcal{B}
.
In contrast to previous methods, our approach scales to high-dimensional feature spaces by

1.

learning a representation
\phi
and

2.

considering
local error measures
as opposed to global measures across the whole state-action space.

We formalize them as tractable
loss functions
. Their local nature makes them compliant with stochastic gradient descent methods.
Formally, given a distribution
\mathcal{B}\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}}\right)
, we define the
reward loss

L_{{R}}^{\mathcal{B}}
and the
transition loss

L_{P}^{\mathcal{B}}
as

\displaystyle L_{{R}}^{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,a\sim\mathcal{B}}\left|{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s},a}}\right)\right|,

\displaystyle L_{P}^{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,a\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{W}\left(\phi_{\sharp}P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),a}}\right)\right)

where
\phi_{\sharp}P
is the
pushforward measure
of
P
by
\phi
, and
\mathcal{W}
the
Wasserstein distance

(
Vaserstein 1969
)
.

\mathcal{W}
between
\mu,\nu\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\right)
is defined as
\mathcal{W}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\nu}}\right)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s},\bar{s}^{\prime}}}\right)\sim\lambda}\;\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s},\bar{s}^{\prime}}}\right)
, where
\Lambda(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
.
While the Wasserstein operator may seem scary at first glance, it generalizes over transition losses that can be found in the literature.
In particular, when the latent space is discrete, this distance boils down to the
total variation distance
. Another notable case is when the transition dynamics are deterministic, in which case the transition loss reduces to
L_{P}^{\mathcal{B}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,a,s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{B}}\,\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),a}}\right)}}\right)
.
Finally, in general, a tractable upper bound can be obtained as
L^{\mathcal{B}}_{P}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,a,s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),a}}\right)}{\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right),\bar{s}^{\prime}}}\right)}
(proof in Appendix
C
).

Lipschitz constants.
To provide the guarantees, for any particular policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, we assume the world model is equipped with
Lipschitz constants

K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\,\overline{\!{{R}}}}
,
K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
defined as follows:
for all
\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{1}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{1}}}\right)}\,\overline{\!{{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s}_{1},a_{1}}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{2}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{2}}}\right)}\,\overline{\!{{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s}_{2},a_{2}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\,\overline{\!{{R}}}}\cdot\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{1}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{1}}}\right)}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{1},a_{1}}}\right),\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{2}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{2}}}\right)}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\bar{s}_{2},a_{2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\cdot\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}}}\right).

Intuitively, the Lipschitzness of the latent reward and transition functions guarantees that the latent space is well-structured, so that nearby latent states exhibit similar latent dynamics.

Gelada et al. 2019
control those bounds by adding a
gradient penalty term
to the loss and enforce Lipschitzness
(
Gulrajani et al. 2017
)
.
One can also obtain constrained Lipchitz constants as a side effect by enforcing the metric
\,\overline{\!{d}}
to match the
bisimulation distance
in the latent space
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
.
Interestingly, when the latent space is discrete, Lipschitz constants can be trivially inferred since
K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\,\overline{\!{{R}}}}={2{R}_{\textsc{max}}}
and
K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}=1

(
Delgrange et al. 2022
)
.
Note also that as the spaces are assumed compact, restricting to continuous functions ensures Lipschitz continuity.

For the sake of presentation, we restrict our attention to the episodic RL setting; we consider the standard RL framework where the environment is almost surely always eventually reset.
Our results extend to general settings where a stationary distribution is accessible (cf. Remark
, Appendix
E
).

World model quality.

Before introducing our safe policy improvement theorem, we first show that the local losses effectively measure the world model’s quality with respect to the original environment.
Namely, their difference in return obtained
under any latent policy in a well-defined neighborhood
is bounded by the local losses
derived from the base policy’s state-action distribution
.
This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\gamma>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
.
Let
C\in\mathopen{(}1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}\mathclose{)}
,
\pi_{\text{b}}\in\Pi
be the base policy,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi}}\right)\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}}}}\right)
where
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is a latent policy and
\phi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
a state representation.
Then,

\left|\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi,\mathcal{M}}}\right)-\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)\right|\leq\textsc{AEL}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}}}}\right)\cdot\frac{\nicefrac{{L_{{R}}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}}}{{\gamma}}+K_{V}\cdot L_{P}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}}{{\nicefrac{{1}}{{D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right)}}{-}\gamma}},

where
\textsc{AEL}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}}}}\right)
denotes the
average episode length
when
\mathcal{M}
runs under
\pi_{\text{b}}
and
K_{V}=\nicefrac{{K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\,\overline{\!{{R}}}}}}{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma K^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}\right)}}
.

In simpler terms, if the deviation (
supremum
IR, or SIR for short) between the base policy and
any
new
policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
stays stricly lower than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
, the gap in return between the environment and the world model for this new policy can be bounded using data collected via
\pi_{\text{b}}
.
Minimizing local losses from
\pi_{\text{b}}
’s data ensures that refining the representation
\phi
for
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
improves model quality: when these losses vanish,
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
are almost surely equivalent under
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. The bound depends on the Average Episode Length (AEL), but even a loose upper bound is sufficient to preserve guarantees. It is also strongly influenced by the discount factor
\gamma
, which defines an implicit horizon. Smaller values permit larger deviations from
\pi_{\text{b}}
and relax the accuracy required of the world model.

Safe policy improvement.
We consider the setting where the world model is used to improve the baseline policy
\pi_{\text{b}}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b}\circ\phi
, with
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b}\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and the representation
\phi
is fixed during each update. Restricting updates to a well-defined neighborhood guarantees that
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi,\mathcal{M}}}\right)-\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mathcal{M}}}\right)\geq\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)-\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b},\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)-\zeta
, where
\zeta
is defined as the cumulative
modeling error
from the local losses.

Theorem 3
.

(Deep, Safe Policy Improvement)

Under the same preamble as in Thm.
2
,
assume that
\phi
if fixed during the policy update and the baseline is a latent policy with
\pi_{\text{b}}\coloneqq\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b}\circ\phi
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b}\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
.
Then, the improvement of the return of
\mathcal{M}
under
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
can be guaranteed on
\pi_{\text{b}}
as

\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi,\mathcal{M}}}\right)-\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mathcal{M}}}\right)\geq\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)-\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{b},\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)-\zeta,\\
\text{ where }\zeta\coloneqq\textsc{AEL}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nicefrac{{L_{{R}}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}}}{{\gamma}}+K_{V}L_{P}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right)}}-\gamma}+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}\right).

Theorem
3
addresses the OOT issue (Section
3.1
): if the SIR of the baseline remains strictly below
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
, then minimizing the local losses reduces the error
\zeta
, ensuring safe policy improvement when the world model is used to enhance the policy. While our focus is not on offline SPI, Appendix
E
(Thm.
) additionally provides a PAC variant of the result, following the standard use of confidence bounds in the SPI literature.

Representation learning.

Finally, we analyze how learning a world model using our loss functions as an auxiliary task facilitates the learning of a useful representation.
A good representation should ensure that environment states that are close in the representation also have close values, directly supporting policy learning.
Specifically, we seek “
almost
” Lipschitz continuity
(
Vanderbei 1991
)
of the form
\exists K\colon\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S},\left|V^{\pi_{\text{b}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)-V^{\pi_{\text{b}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|\leq K\cdot\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{\text{old}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right),\phi_{\text{old}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{\text{b}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{\text{old}}}}\right)
where
\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{\text{b}}}
is an auxiliary loss
depending on the data collected by
\bm{\pi_{\text{b}}}
.
Notably, a critical question is whether updating the policy and its representation, respectively to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\phi
, maintains Lipschitz continuity.
Crucially, as the baseline
\pi_{\text{b}}
is updated to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\circ\phi
with respect to the experience collected under
\pi_{\text{b}}
, the bound must hold for
\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{\text{b}}}
.
The following theorem is a probabilistic version of this statement, formalized as a concentration inequality:

Theorem 4
.

(Deep SPI for representation learning)

Under the same preamble as in Thm.
2
,
let
\varepsilon>0
and

\delta\coloneqq 4\cdot\frac{L_{{R}}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}+\gamma K_{V}\cdot L_{P}^{\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}}}{\varepsilon\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right)}}-\gamma}}\right)}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
under
\xi_{\pi_{\text{b}}}
, we have for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
that

\left|V^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)-V^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|\leq K_{V}\cdot\,\overline{\!{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right),\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right)+\varepsilon.

Theorem
4
addresses confounding policy updates (Section
3.2
): minimizing the losses increases the probability that learned representations remain almost Lipschitz under controlled policy changes (with an SIR below
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
). This prevents distinct states from collapsing into identical latent representations that degrade performance.
We note that
Gelada et al. 2019
proved a similar bound when
\pi_{\text{b}}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
(the policy update was disregarded), which in contrast to ours,
surely
holds with

\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{L_{{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}+\gamma K_{V}\cdot L_{P}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}}\right).

However, in general spaces, for any specific
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)
might simply equal zero, making the bound undefined.
In particular, in the continuous setting,
\mathcal{S}
is widely assumed to be endowed with a Borel sigma-algebra, where the probability of every single point is indeed zero.

Across the SPI-verse: PPO comes into play

These theorems inspire a practical RL algorithm that combines policy improvement and guarantees with solid empirical performance.
The critical part of our approach is to make sure updates are restricted to the policy neighborhood while minimizing the auxiliary losses
L_{{R}}
,
L_{P}
.
In fact, our neighborhood operator has close connections to PPO

Algorithm 1

DeepSPI

From this loss, we propose
DeepSPI
,
a principled algorithm leveraging the policy improvement and representation learning capabilities developed in our theory
.
As our losses rely on distributions defined over the current policy, we focus on the on-policy setting.
While model-based approaches are not standard in this setting, we stress that
highly parallelized collection of data
(e.g., via vectorized environments)
enable a wide coverage of the state space
(cf.
Mayor et al. 2025
;
Gallici et al. 2025
), which is suitable to optimize the latent model.

DeepSPI
updates the world model, the encoder, and the policy simultaneously while guaranteeing the representation is suited to perform safe policy updates.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We developed a theoretical framework for safe policy improvement (SPI) that combines world-model and representation learning in nontrivial settings. Our results show that constraining policy updates within a well-defined neighborhood yields monotonic improvement and convergence, while auxiliary transition and reward losses ensure that the latent space remains suitable for policy optimisation. We further provided model-quality guarantees in the form of a “deep” SPI theorem, which jointly accounts for the learned representation and the reward/transition losses. These results directly address two critical issues in model-based RL: out-of-trajectory errors and confounding policy updates.
Building on this analysis, we proposed
DeepSPI
, a principled algorithm that integrates the theoretical ingredients with PPO.
On ALE,
DeepSPI
is competitive with and often improves upon PPO and
DeepMDPs
, while providing SPI guarantees.

This work opens several directions. A first avenue is to make pure deep SPI model-based planning practical. Our experiments with
DreamSPI
suggest that this is feasible but requires improved sample efficiency. Another direction goes beyond return optimization: a principled world model, grounded in our theory, can support safe reinforcement learning via formal methods, through synthesis
(
Delgrange et al. 2025
;
Lechner et al. 2022
)
, or shielding
(
Jansen et al. 2020
)
.

Appendix A
Remark on value functions and episodic processes

An
episodic process
is formally defined as an MDP
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,{R},s_{\mathit{I}},\gamma\rangle
where:

1.

there is a special state
s_{\textit{reset}}\in\mathcal{S}
, intuitively indicating the termination of any
episode
;

2.

the reset state does not incur any reward:
{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\textit{reset}},a}}\right)=0
for all actions
a\in\mathcal{A}
;

3.

s_{\textit{reset}}
is almost surely visited under any policy: for all policies
\pi\in\Pi
,
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}\mid\exists i\colon s_{i}=s_{\textit{reset}}\right\}}}\right)=1
; and

4.

\mathcal{M}
restarts from the initial state once reset:
P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\{s_{\mathit{I}}\right\}\mid s_{\textit{reset}},a}}\right)=1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Note that by items
3

and
4
,
s_{\textit{reset}}
is almost surely
infinitely often
visited: we have for all
\pi\in\Pi
that

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}\mid\forall i\geq 0,\,\exists j>i\colon s_{j}=s_{\textit{reset}}\right\}}}\right)=1.

Alternatively and equivalently, an episodic process may also be defined without a unique reset state by the means of several
terminal states
, which go back to the initial state with probability one.

An
episode
of
\mathcal{M}
is thus the prefix
s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},s_{t}
of a trajectory where
s_{t}=s_{\textit{reset}}
and for all
i<t
,
s_{i}\neq s_{\textit{reset}}
.
Notice that our formulation embeds (but is not limited to) finite-horizon tasks, where an upper bound on the length of the episodes is fixed.
The
average episode length
(AEL) of
\pi
is then formally defined as
\textsc{AEL}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)=\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathbf{T}\right]
with

\mathbf{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i+1}}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}\left\{s_{i}=s_{\textit{reset}}\,\text{ and }\,\forall j<i,\,s_{j}\neq s_{\textit{reset}}\right\}

for any trajectory
\tau=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

Often, when considering episodic tasks, RL algorithms stops accumulating rewards upon the termination of every episode.
In practical implementations, this corresponds to discarding rewards when a flag
done
, indicating episode termination, is set to
true
.
In such case, we may slightly adapt our value functions as:

\displaystyle V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\left\{s_{i}\neq s_{\textit{reset}}\right\}\cdot\gamma}}\right){R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t}}}\right)\,\Big|\,s_{0}=s\right]&\text{if }s\neq s_{\textit{reset}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

or, when formalized as
Bellman 1957
’s equation:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)+\gamma\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)}V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)&\text{if }s\neq s_{\textit{reset}}\\
0&\text{otherwise; and}\end{cases}

\displaystyle V^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}Q^{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right).

All our results extend to this formulation (cf. Remark
).

Remark 1

(Occupancy measure)
.

In RL theory, the discounted occupancy measure

\mu^{\gamma}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{i},a_{i}}}\right)_{i\geq 0}\mid s_{t}=s\right\}}}\right)

is often considered as the default marginal distribution over states the agent visit along the interaction, mostly because of its suitable theoretical properties.
In fact, for any arbitrary MDP,
\mu^{\gamma}_{\pi}

is the stationary distribution
of the episodic process obtained by considering a reset probability of
1-\gamma
from every state of the original MDP
(
Puterman 1994
;
Metelli et al. 2023
)
.
Again, we contend that all our results can be extended to the occupancy measure with little effort.

Appendix B
Policy Improvements through Mirror Learning and Convergence Guarantees

In this section, we prove that
\mathcal{N}^{C}
(Eq.
2
) is a proper
mirror learning neighborhood operator
.
As a consequence, appropriately updating the policy according to
\mathcal{N}^{C}
is guaranteed to be an instance of mirror learning, yielding the convergence guarantees of Theorem
1
.

For completeness, we recall the definition of neighborhood operator from
Kuba et al. 2022
.

Definition 1

(Neighborhood operator)
.

The mapping
\mathcal{N}\colon\Pi\to 2^{\Pi}
is a (mirror learning)
neighborhood operator
, if

1.

(continuity)
It is a continuous map;

2.

(compactness)
Every
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
is a compact set; and

3.

(closed ball)
There exists a metric
d\colon\Pi\times\Pi\to\mathopen{[}0,\infty\mathclose{)}
, such that for all policies
\pi\in\Pi
, there exists
\epsilon>0
, such that
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq\epsilon
implies
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
.

The trivial neighborhood operator is
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)=\Pi
.

Lemma 1
.

\mathcal{N}^{C}
is a neighborhood operator.

Proof.

Henceforth, fix a policy
\pi\in\Pi
.

When taking the supremum, infimum, maximum, or minimum value over states and actions, we always consider actions to be taken from the support of the baseline policy (in the denominator of the quotient).

Item 2 (compactness) is trivial due to
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq C
for any
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
.
This means
\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
contains its extrema, i.e., all the policies
\pi^{\prime}
satisfying

D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)=2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq C
, or
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)=C
.

In the following, for any
\pi\in\Pi
and sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
, we write
\pi_{n}\to\pi
for the convergence of the sequence to
\pi
with respect to the metric

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}}\right)=\begin{cases}\left\|\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}\right\|_{\infty}&\text{if }\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)=\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\text{ and}\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

In other words,
\pi_{n}\to\pi
means that
\pi_{n}
converges to
\pi
in supremum norm as
n\to\infty
when the support of the converging policy stabilizes and becomes the same as the limit policy.

Let us prove item 1 (continuity).
We show that
\mathcal{N}^{C}
is a continuous
correspondence
by showing it is upper and lower
hemicontinuous

(
Ok 2007
)
.

\mathcal{N}^{C}
is
upper hemicontinuous
(uhc) if it is
compact-valued
(item 1) and, for all policies
\pi\in\Pi
and every sequences
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
with
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)
for all
n\geq 0
,
\pi_{n}\to\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\to\pi^{\prime}
implies
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
.
Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
be sequences of policies with
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)
for all
n\geq 0
.

Fix
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Consider the mapping

f_{s,a}\colon\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\in\Pi\times\Pi\mid a\in\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)\right\}\to\mathopen{[}0,\infty),\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\mapsto\frac{\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}.

It is clear
f_{s,a}
is continuous since the application of
\pi
to
\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)
is continuous and the division of two continuous functions is also continuous (when considering actions from the support of
\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)
).
Importantly, for
\ext\in\left\{\sup,\inf\right\}
,
D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)=\ext\left\{f_{s,a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\colon s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)\right\}

is also continuous:
since
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are finite, the supremum (resp. infimum) boils down to taking the maximum (resp. minimum) of finitely many many continuous functions, which is a continuous operation.

Now, assume that
\pi_{n}\to\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\to\pi^{\prime}
.
The continuity of
D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}
means that

D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n},\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)\to D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)

Since
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)
, we have
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n},\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)\geq 2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n},\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)\leq C
for all
n\geq 0
.
By the fact that
D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n},\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)
converges to
D^{\ext}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)
for

, we also have that
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq C
.

Then,
\mathcal{N}^{C}
is uhc.

\mathcal{N}^{C}
is
lower hemicontinuous
(lhc) if, for every policy
\pi
, sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
with
\pi_{n}\to\pi
, and policy
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
with
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\to\pi^{\prime}
and such that there is a

from which, for all

,
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)
.
Therefore, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of policies so that
\pi_{n}\to\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
.
Since
\pi_{n}\to\pi

\forall\delta>0,\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\colon\forall n\geq n_{0},\left\|\pi_{n}-\pi\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta\;\text{ and }\;\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)=\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

\pi_{\min}=\min\left\{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\colon s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)\right\}.

Let
n_{0}\geq 0
be the step associated with
\delta<\nicefrac{{\pi_{\min}}}{{2}}
and

Write

Construct a sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 0}

and

Consequently,

stably converges to

Having that said, we start by showing the upper bound:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{\prime}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{C\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\cdot\frac{C\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)-\delta_{n}}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{C}{1-\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}}}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{C}{1-\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}+\epsilon_{n}.

Note that for all

\frac{1}{1-x}\leq 1+2x\;\text{ because }\;1+2x-\frac{1}{1-x}\geq 0\iff\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+2x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x}}\right)-1}{1-x}\geq 0\iff\frac{x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2x}}\right)}{1-x}\geq 0.

Then, since
0<\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we have

\frac{\pi^{\prime}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mid s}}\right)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\cdot C\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\nicefrac{{2\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}.

Let

\epsilon_{n}=\frac{2C\delta_{n}}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}=\frac{2C\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}{C+2C\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}-1}=\frac{-2C\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}{1-C-2C\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}=\frac{C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}}}\right)}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{\prime}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)x_{n}\cdot C+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n}\cdot C-\epsilon_{n}\cdot x_{n}\cdot C+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n}\cdot C-\frac{C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}}}\right)}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}\cdot x_{n}\cdot C+\frac{C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}}}\right)}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{n}\cdot C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}\cdot C}}\right)-x_{n}\cdot C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}}}\right)+C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-x_{n}}}\right)}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{n}\cdot C-x_{n}^{2}C^{2}-x_{n}\cdot C^{2}+x_{n}^{2}C^{2}+C-x_{n}\cdot C}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}

\displaystyle=\frac{-x_{n}\cdot C^{2}+C}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}

\displaystyle=C\cdot\frac{1-x_{n}\cdot C}{1-x_{n}\cdot C}

\displaystyle=C,

which means that
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n},\pi^{\prime}_{n}}}\right)\leq C
.

We now show the lower bound:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{\prime}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}+\epsilon_{n}

(because
\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\geq(2-C)\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)
)

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)+\delta_{n}}+\epsilon_{n}

(because
\pi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\leq\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)+\delta_{n}
)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\,\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{1+\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}}}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\,\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{1+\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{\pi_{\min}}}}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}

(because for all
x\in\mathbb{R},\,\frac{1}{1+x}\geq 1-x
)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C-2\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}+C\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}-\epsilon_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}+\epsilon_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}+\epsilon_{n}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}

\displaystyle=2-C+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-2}}\right)\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}+\frac{2C\delta_{n}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}+\frac{2C\delta_{n}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}\cdot\nicefrac{{\delta_{n}}}{{{\pi_{\min}}}}

\displaystyle=2-C+\delta_{n}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{C-2}{\pi_{\min}}+\frac{2C\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}+\frac{2C\delta_{n}\cdot\pi_{\min}^{-1}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2-C.

To see how we obtain the last line, note that it suffices to show the content of the parenthesis multiplied by
\delta_{n}
is greater than zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{C-2}{\pi_{\min}}+\frac{2C\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}+\frac{2C\delta_{n}\cdot\pi_{\min}^{-1}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{2C\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}\cdot\pi_{\min}^{-1}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)}{\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2-C}{\pi_{\min}}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 2C\pi_{\min}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 2C\pi_{\min}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)+2C\delta_{n}-2C\delta_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 2C\pi_{\min}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 2C

\displaystyle\geq{2-C},

which is always satisfied because
C\geq 1
.
Therefore, since this holds for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and both
\pi^{\prime}_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
have the same support, we have that
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 2-C
.

Thus, we have
D^{\inf}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 2-C
and
D^{\sup}_{\text{IR}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq C
,
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{N}^{C}
is lhc.

Since
\mathcal{N}^{C}
is uhc and lhc, it is continuous. This concludes the proof of item 1.

It remains to show item 3.
Let
\epsilon=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)\cdot\min_{s,a}\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)
, with
a
taken from
\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)
.
Assume
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}}}\right)\leq\epsilon
(cf. Eq.
6
).
For all
s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)\cdot\min_{s,a}\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+C-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot C,

or equivalently:

\frac{\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}\leq C.

It remains to show the lower bound:

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)-\epsilon

\displaystyle=\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)\cdot\min_{s,a}\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C-1}}\right)\cdot\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-C+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot C

\displaystyle\geq\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-C}}\right),

or equivalently:

\frac{\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}{\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)}\geq 2-C.

This concludes the proof of item 3.
∎

Then, Theorem
1
is obtained as a corollary of Lemma
1
, and the fact that the update process

\pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\argsup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n}}}\right)}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim\xi_{\pi_{n}}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid{s}}}\right)}\left[A^{\pi_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)\right],

is an instance of mirror learning
(
Kuba et al. 2022
)
.

Appendix C
Crude Wasserstein Upper Bound

Lemma 2
.

Let
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the following upper bound holds:

\mathcal{W}\big(\phi_{\sharp}P(\cdot\mid s,a),\;\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)\big)\leq\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\;\mathbb{E}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)}\,\,\overline{\!{d}}\big(\phi(s^{\prime}),\bar{s}^{\prime}\big).

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\big(\phi_{\sharp}P(\cdot\mid s,a),\;\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)\big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert f\rVert_{\text{Lip}}\leq 1}\;\Bigg[\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}f\big(\phi(s^{\prime})\big)-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)}f(\bar{s}^{\prime})\Bigg]

(1)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Bigg[\sup_{\lVert f\rVert_{\text{Lip}}\leq 1}\;f\big(\phi(s^{\prime})\big)-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)}f(\bar{s}^{\prime})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\mathcal{W}\big(\delta_{\phi(s^{\prime})},\;\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Bigg[\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\delta_{\phi(s^{\prime})},\,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a))}\mathbb{E}_{(\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2})\sim\lambda}\,d(\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2})\Bigg]

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\;\mathbb{E}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\cdot\mid\phi(s),a)}\,\,\overline{\!{d}}\big(\phi(s^{\prime}),\bar{s}^{\prime}\big).

Here, (1) corresponds to the dual Kantorovich–Rubinstein formulation
(
Kantorovich and Rubinstein 1958
)
where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\text{Lip}}
corresponds to the Lipschitz norm, while (2) follows from the primal Monge formulation
(
Monge 1781
)
, with a trivial coupling induced by
\delta_{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)}
, the Dirac measure with impulse
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)
.
∎

Appendix D
Remark on Safe Policy Improvement Methods

Standard principled
safe policy improvement
methods (SPI;
Thomas et al. 2015
;
Ghavamzadeh et al. 2016a
;
Laroche et al. 2019
;
Simão et al. 2020
;
Castellini et al. 2023
;
Wienhöft et al. 2023
) do not consider representation learning.
Instead, SPI methods assume
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{S}
and learn
\,\overline{\!{{R}}}
,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
by maximum likelihood estimation with respect to the experience stored in
\mathcal{B}
collected by the baseline
\pi_{\text{b}}
.
Then, the policy improvement relies on finding the best policy in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
that is (probably approximately correctly) guaranteed to improves on the baseline policy (up to an error term
\zeta>0
) against a set of all admissible MDPs, called
robust MDPs

(
Iyengar 2005
;
Nilim and Ghaoui 2005
;
Wiesemann et al. 2013
;
Ghavamzadeh et al. 2016b
;
Suilen et al. 2024
)
:

\displaystyle\argsup_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}}\right)

such that

\displaystyle\arginf_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\in\Xi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}},e}}\right)}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\right)\geq\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\text{b}},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\right)-\zeta,\text{ where}

\Xi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}},e}}\right)\coloneqq\left\{\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,{R},s_{\mathit{I}},\gamma\rangle\,\bigg|\!\begin{array}[]{rl}\left|{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)\right|&\leq{R}_{\textsc{max}}\cdot e\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)\quad\quad\text{ and}\\[2.84526pt]
d_{{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right),\,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)}}\right)&\leq e\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)\quad\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}\end{array}\right\},

e\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)
being an
error
term depending on the number of times each state
s
and action
a
are present in the dataset
\mathcal{B}
, and
d_{{TV}}
being the
total variation distance

(
Müller 1997
)
which boils down to the
L_{1}
distance when the state-action space is finite.
To provide
probably approximately correct
(PAC) guarantees, the state-action pairs need to be visited a
sufficient amount of time
, depending on the size of the state-action space, to ensure
e
is sufficiently small.

Note that the reward and total variation constraints are very related to our local losses
L_{{R}}
and
L_{P}
: the representation corresponds here to the identity and
d_{{TV}}
coincides with Wasserstein as the state space is discrete
(
Villani 2009
)
.
The major difference here is that the bounds need to hold
globally
, i.e., for all state-action pairs, which make their computation typically intractable in complex settings (e.g., high-dimensional feature spaces).

We argue
this objective is ill-suited to complex settings
.
First, classic SPI does not apply to general spaces.
Second, assuming we deal with
finite
, high-dimensional feature spaces (e.g., visual inputs or the RAM of a video game), it is simply unlikely that
\mathcal{B}
contains all state-action pairs.

SPI with baseline bootstrapping

(
Laroche et al. 2019
)
allows bypassing this requirement by updating
\pi_{\text{b}}
only in state-action pairs where a
sufficient
number of samples are present in
\mathcal{B}
. Nevertheless, this number is gigantic and is linear in the state-action space while being exponential in the size of the encoding of
\gamma
and the desired error
\zeta
. This deems the policy update intractable.
Finally, as mentioned, standard SPI does not consider representation learning.
This is a further obstacle to its application in complex settings.
