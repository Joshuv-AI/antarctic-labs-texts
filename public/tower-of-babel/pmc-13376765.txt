Targeting CCR1-mediated macrophage phenotypic switching to alleviate bladder pain and urinary dysfunction in interstitial cystitis
Authors: Ruixiang Luo, Wenshuang Li, Bolong Liu, Junlong Huang, Honglu Ding, Chi Zhang, Xiaoyang Li, Ziqiao Wang, Zheng Liu, Xiangfu Zhou, Jialiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic inflammatory condition with limited treatments. Although macrophages are implicated in its pathogenesis, the mechanisms driving their phenotypic switching remain unclear. This study identifies CCR1’s role in IC/BPS and evaluates CCR1 inhibition as a therapeutic strategy. Integrated bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing reveal enrichment of pro-inflammatory CCR1⁺ macrophages in bladder tissue from patients with IC/BPS. In a lipopolysaccharide-induced rat model, pharmacological inhibition of CCR1 suppresses M1 polarization, promotes M2 polarization, and improves pain thresholds, urinary symptoms as well as bladder inflammation. Mechanistically, CCR1 knockdown enhances FOXO1 phosphorylation and degradation, reduces its nuclear translocation, and activates PPARγ signaling to promote M2 polarization. Analysis of clinical samples shows increased CCL7 levels in bladder tissue and urine, with urinary levels correlating with symptom severity. These findings identify CCR1 as a candidate target for further therapeutic evaluation in IC/BPS. Subject terms: Chronic inflammation, Bladder

Introduction

Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic urological disorder characterized by pelvic pain and urinary dysfunction, affecting 2.7–6.5% of the population annually1. It significantly reduces quality of life and is frequently associated with depression and suicidal ideation2. Despite ongoing research, its etiology remains unclear, with proposed mechanisms including urothelial injury, immune dysregulation, and neurogenic inflammation3. Current treatments offer limited effectiveness, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the disease.

Recent studies point to immune imbalance as a key factor in IC/BPS, with notable chemokine overexpression and macrophage infiltration in bladder tissue4–6. Macrophages are critical regulators of inflammation, capable of shifting between M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (pro-repair) phenotypes7–10. M1 macrophages release cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, promoting chronic inflammation, tissue damage, and impaired healing11,12. In contrast, M2 macrophages facilitate tissue repair and resolution of inflammation13. Increasing evidence suggests that sustained M1 activation in the bladder contributes to persistent inflammation, structural damage, and exacerbation of lower urinary tract symptoms in IC/BPS patients14,15. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating macrophage polarization in the bladder microenvironment remain poorly defined. Understanding how chemokines influence macrophage plasticity is crucial for identifying novel therapeutic targets.

C-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CCR1) is a known regulator of macrophage activity in various inflammatory diseases16. Inhibition of CCR1 has been shown to promote M2 polarization and reduce inflammation in models of atherosclerosis and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease17,18. CCR1 inhibitors have also demonstrated therapeutic potential in clinical trials for rheumatoid arthritis and allergic dermatitis19,20. Yet, its role in IC/BPS remains largely unexplored, positioning CCR1 as a candidate target for intervention.

Our study investigates the involvement of CCR1 in macrophage-associated inflammatory responses and its potential contribution to IC/BPS pathogenesis. We identify CCL7 as a major ligand associated with CCR1 activation in our experimental systems and provide evidence that the CCL7–CCR1 axis may modulate inflammatory communication between bladder epithelial cells and macrophages. These findings suggest that CCL7 may represent a candidate biomarker associated with IC/BPS and that CCR1 inhibition warrants further evaluation as a potential therapeutic target. By linking CCR1 signaling with macrophage activation and bladder inflammation, our work provides a mechanistic framework for future studies and support proof-of-concept involvement of the CCL7–CCR1 axis in bladder inflammatory pathology.

Results

Multi-algorithmic identification and clinical validation of CCR1 as a novel therapeutic target in IC/BPS

Differential gene expression analysis between IC/BPS patients and controls identified 866 significantly altered genes, including 440 upregulated and 426 downregulated (Fig. 1a). GO enrichment revealed involvement in leukocyte migration, immune activation, and cytokine signaling (Fig. 1b), while KEGG analysis highlighted chemokine signaling as a top dysregulated pathway (Supplementary Fig. 1a), underscoring immune dysregulation in IC/BPS.

To further explore gene co-expression patterns, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). The data were clustered into seven modules (Fig. 1c), with the MEturquoise module showing the strongest correlation with IC/BPS clinical traits (r = 0.76, P = 7.2 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1b). This module contained 1027 hub genes. By intersecting these with differentially expressed genes and immune-related genes, we identified 90 overlapping candidates (Fig. 1e). To pinpoint critical genes within this overlapping set, we applied three machine learning algorithms. LASSO regression and SVM each identified six candidate genes (Fig. 1f, g), while the random forest (RF) model highlighted the top ten ranked by importance (Fig. 1h). Notably, CCR1 and PLCG2 were consistently identified across all three methods (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Comparative expression analysis of bladder tissue revealed that CCR1 was significantly more highly expressed than PLCG2 (P = 0.02), leading us to focus on CCR1 (Fig. 1i).

Validation experiments confirmed CCR1 upregulation in IC/BPS (Fig. 1j). Western blotting showed more than a twofold increase in CCR1 protein in bladder tissues from both IC/BPS patients and LPS-induced cystitis rats compared to controls (Fig. 1k, l). qPCR results further confirmed elevated CCR1 mRNA levels in both groups (Fig. 1m). Importantly, CCR1 expression positively correlated with symptom severity, as reflected by ICPI (r = 0.65, P < 0.05) and ICSI (r = 0.77, P < 0.05) scores (Fig. 1n, o), reinforcing its clinical relevance in IC/BPS.

CCR1 inhibition ameliorates bladder inflammation, pain-related symptoms and urological dysfunction in LPS-induced cystitis

In our study, evaluation of bladder specimens from IC/BPS patients revealed elevated CCR1 expression, which positively correlated with clinical symptom severity scores. This suggests that CCR1 may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of IC/BPS. To further investigate this relationship, we utilized BX471, a specific CCR1 inhibitor, to assess the therapeutic efficacy of CCR1 inhibition in the LPS-induced cystitis models (Fig. 2a).

We first assessed nociceptive responses and urinary tract function in the rat model. BX471 treatment significantly elevated the mechanical withdrawal threshold, suggesting reduced bladder hypersensitivity (Fig. 2b). Linear regression analysis demonstrated a significant negative correlation between bladder CCR1 mRNA levels and mechanical withdrawal threshold in IC/BPS models (r = −0.64, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2c). Additionally, BX471 treatment extended micturition intervals, indicating improved urinary tract function (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Next, we assessed inflammation in bladder tissue. H&E staining showed severe edema, congestion, and epithelial damage in LPS-treated rats. These pathological features were markedly reduced following BX471 treatment (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Gross bladder images also confirmed that BX471 ameliorated LPS-induced bladder redness and edema (Fig. 2g). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, which increased more than 1.5-fold following LPS instillation, were nearly restored to baseline after BX471 treatment (Fig. 2h, j). Moreover, the wetted bladder-to-body mass ratio, an indicator of inflammatory response, increased by 20% following LPS instillation and was normalized after BX471 administration (Fig. 2k).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CCR1 with BX471 is a promising therapeutic strategy for managing IC/BPS by modulating inflammatory responses and alleviating symptoms.

CCR1 predominantly expressed in M1 macrophages

To elucidate the role of CCR1 in IC/BPS, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from the GEO database (GSE175526), which included 54584 quality-filtered cells from five IC/BPS patients and two healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Graph-based clustering identified nine distinct cell types, visualized in the tSNE plot (Fig. 3a). Utilizing the FindAllMarkers function and referencing established markers from the literature and the Cell Marker database (Fig. 3b), we identified specific cell populations including T cells (CD3E), neutrophils (CSF3R), endothelial cells (PECAM1), macrophages (CD68), fibroblasts (COL1A1), epithelial cells (KRT19), mast cells (CPA3), B cells (CD19), and plasma cells (MZB1). CCR1 expression across these cell types is illustrated in Fig. 3c, with 51.6% of CCR1 expression observed in macrophages (Fig. 3d).

To further investigate CCR1’s role in macrophages, we analyzed 4263 macrophages via unsupervised clustering (Fig. 3e). These macrophages were categorized into two subpopulations based on CCR1 expression: high CCR1-expressing (clusters 2, 6, 7, 14) and low CCR1-expressing (clusters 0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 8-13) (Fig. 3f). Notably, high CCR1-expressing macrophages were more abundant in the IC/BPS group compared to controls (Fig. 3g). GSEA revealed that immune activation pathways were significantly upregulated in the high CCR1-expressing macrophage subpopulation (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 3c). Dot plot analysis demonstrated that these CCR1 high-expression macrophages exhibited M1-like characteristics, with elevated expression of markers such as CD86, NLRP3, TNF, and IL12A (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 3d).

To verify the relationship between CCR1 and M1 macrophages, we examined bladder tissue samples from IC/BPS patients and healthy controls. Compared to controls, IC/BPS tissues consistently exhibited elevated M1 macrophage infiltration (CD86+) and increased CCR1 expression (Fig. 3j–l). Furthermore, in a rat model of LPS-induced cystitis, immunofluorescence staining revealed clear co-localization of CCR1 with M1 macrophages (iNOS+) in bladder tissue (Fig. 3m). These findings provide strong histological evidence supporting the association between CCR1 expression and M1 macrophage polarization.

CCR1-mediated M1 macrophage polarization promotes inflammation and bladder epithelial cell injury in IC/BPS

To clarify CCR1’s role in M1 macrophage polarization, we established a plasmid-mediated CCR1- overexpression (OE) system in macrophages, with overexpression efficiency presented in Supplementary Fig. 4a–c. Quantitative PCR revealed that CCR1-OE significantly upregulated M1-associated markers IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, and CD86 by over twofold (Fig. 4a). Flow cytometry analysis showed a roughly 10% increase in the proportion of M1 macrophages in the CCR1-OE group (Fig. 4b, c), indicating that CCR1 promotes M1 polarization.

To assess the impact of CCR1-activated M1 macrophages on bladder epithelial cells, we treated bladder epithelial cells with conditioned media from either NC or CCR1-OE macrophages (Fig. 4d). qPCR analysis confirmed that conditioned media from CCR1-OE macrophages significantly increased the expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, NF-κB, NLRP3) in bladder epithelial cells (Fig. 4e). Scratch assays showed a marked impairment in cell migration (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 4d). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed a reduction in tight junction protein levels: ZO1 decreased by 55%, Occludin by 46%, and UPKIII by 25% (Fig. 4g–j). These findings suggest that CCR1-driven M1 macrophage polarization promotes inflammation and contributes to urothelial barrier damage in IC/BPS.

CCR1 inhibition regulates macrophage phenotype switching in a bladder epithelium–macrophage co-culture model and LPS-induced cystitis rat model by suppressing M1 polarization and enhancing M2 polarization

Given the role of CCR1-mediated M1 macrophages in bladder epithelial injury and the observed therapeutic benefit of CCR1 inhibition in LPS-induced IC/BPS rat models, we hypothesized that CCR1 inhibition alleviates IC/BPS by modulating macrophage polarization.

To test this, we established a Transwell co-culture system to model the inflammatory microenvironment in vitro. Control or TNF-α–treated bladder epithelial cells were seeded in the upper chamber, while macrophages (NC or CCR1-knockdown) were seeded in the lower chamber (Fig. 5a). Knockdown efficiency is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. Morphological analysis revealed distinct changes indicative of macrophage polarization states. LPS treatment induced M1 polarization in macrophages, characterized by a flattened, pancake-like morphology and elevated CD86 expression; these changes were also observed in NC macrophages co-cultured with inflammatory bladder epithelium (Fig. 5b). In contrast, IL-4/IL-13 stimulation promoted M2 polarization, marked by elongated cells and increased CD206 expression. CCR1-knockdown macrophages co-cultured with inflammatory bladder epithelium exhibited similar M2-like changes (Fig. 5c). qPCR showed that, compared to control macrophages, M1 markers (IL-1β, iNOS, and CD86) were elevated by over 1.5-fold in NC macrophages co-cultured with inflammatory bladder epithelium. CCR1 knockdown reduced these M1 markers by over 60% (Fig. 5d). Conversely, CCR1 knockdown led to a more than 100% increase in M2 markers (IL-10, TGFβ, and CD163) in siCCR1 groups macrophages compared to the NC group (Fig. 5e).

To examine whether CCR1 inhibition similarly regulates macrophage polarization under bladder inflammatory conditions in vivo, we used immunofluorescence co-staining and flow cytometry to evaluate M1 and M2 macrophages in bladder tissues from experimental rats. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked accumulation of iNOS+CD68+ M1 macrophages in the subepithelial region following LPS administration (Fig. 5f). However, BX471 treatment significantly reduced M1 polarization while promoting an increase in CD206+CD68+ M2 macrophages (Fig. 5g). Quantitative analysis showed a 15% reduction in M1 macrophages (Fig. 5h) and a 20% increase in M2 macrophages compared to the LPS group (Fig. 5i). Flow cytometry further validated these findings, identifying macrophages based on CD45+, CD11b+, and F4/80+ markers, followed by CD86 and CD206 staining (Supplementary Fig. 6). In LPS-induced cystitis rats, CD86+ M1 macrophages were predominant, reflecting a pro-inflammatory environment. However, BX471 treatment reversed this trend, decreasing M1 macrophages while increasing M2 macrophage levels (Fig. 5j–m).

Together, these results demonstrate that CCR1 inhibition effectively regulates macrophage polarization, reducing inflammatory M1 phenotype and augmenting tissue-reparative M2 subpopulations in both experimental animal models and cell co-culture systems in IC/BPS.

CCR1 regulates macrophage polarization via the FOXO1/PPARγ signaling axis in inflammatory conditions

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CCR1 influences macrophage polarization, we performed transcriptome sequencing on macrophages under two conditions: the NC group and the siCCR1 (CCR1-knockdown) group, both co-cultured with inflammatory bladder epithelial cells. Differential expression analysis identified 227 upregulated and 205 downregulated genes (FC ≥ 2) in the siCCR1 group (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). CCR1 knockdown mediated a reduction of pro-inflammatory markers concurrent with enhanced expression of pro-healing markers (Fig. 6a). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted significant involvement of the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 6b). To determine the specific PPAR isoform regulated by CCR1, we performed Western blot analysis to assess the expression levels of PPAR family members (PPARα, PPARγ, and PPARδ). The results showed a 1.5-fold increase in PPARγ protein expression in siCCR1 macrophages, while PPARα and PPARδ levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6c–f). Given the established role of PPARγ signaling in macrophage polarization21, we propose that CCR1 inhibition modulates macrophage phenotype by activating the PPARγ pathway, thereby suppressing M1 polarization and promoting a shift toward the M2 phenotype.

To validate the functional role of PPARγ in CCR1-mediated regulation of macrophage inflammation, we utilized GW9662, a potent PPARγ antagonist. CCR1 knockdown reduced TNF-α and IL-1β expression, but this anti-inflammatory effect was reversed by GW9662 (Fig. 6g–i). Flow cytometry confirmed that CCR1 knockdown reduced M1 macrophages and increased M2 populations—effects that were partially reversed by GW9662 (Fig. 6j–m). Immunofluorescence staining supported these findings, showing decreased CD86 and increased CD206 expression in CCR1-knockdown cells, both of which were reversed by GW9662 (Fig. 6n–p). These results indicate that CCR1 inhibition activates PPARγ, thereby reducing M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization in vitro.

We next investigated the molecular mechanism by which CCR1 inhibition activates PPARγ, focusing on FOXO1, a known negative regulator of PPARγ expression and activity22. Western blot analysis showed that NC macrophages exposed to inflammatory bladder epithelium exhibited reduced FOXO1 phosphorylation and increased FOXO1 expression, which suppressed PPARγ expression. CCR1 knockdown reversed these effects by enhancing FOXO1 phosphorylation, decreasing its expression, and relieving its inhibitory effect on PPARγ, thereby upregulating PPARγ levels (Fig. 6q–u). These molecular alterations were further confirmed through the FOXO1-specific inhibitor AS1842856, which elevated PPARγ expression similarly to PPARγ activation and was no longer influenced by CCR1 activation (Fig. 6v–x). Molecular docking analysis confirmed a direct interaction between FOXO1 and PPARγ, with a binding free energy of −7.5 kcal/mol, indicating a strong molecular association (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CCR1 regulates macrophage plasticity through the FOXO1/PPARγ signaling axis.

Bladder epithelium–derived CCL7 orchestrates CCR1-dependent macrophage polarization in IC/BPS

To identify the principal ligand activating CCR1 in the IC/BPS immune microenvironment, we reviewed known CCR1 ligands—CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL7, et al.23,24, and compared their expression in urine and bladder tissues from IC/BPS patients and controls (Fig. 7a). Quantitative PCR revealed CCL7 as the most significantly upregulated CCR1 ligand in bladder tissues (Fig. 7b). After normalizing urine samples to urinary creatinine, we reached a consistent conclusion that urinary CCL7/creatinine was most prominently elevated in the urine of IC/BPS patients (Fig. 7c). ELISA confirmed elevated urinary CCL7 protein levels (normalized by urinary creatinine) in IC/BPS patients (Fig. 7d) compared to SUI controls, which positively correlated with ICPI (r = 0.71, P < 0.01) and ICSI scores (r = 0.69, P < 0.05), linking CCL7 expression to symptom severity (Fig. 7e, f). Additionally, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of urinary CCL7/creatinine in distinguishing IC/BPS patients from SUI controls, yielding an AUC of 0.89 (P < 0.05). This suggests that urinary CCL7 has potential diagnostic value for IC/BPS (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Immunofluorescence staining of bladder tissue from LPS-induced cystitis rats revealed strong co-localization of CCL7 with UPK3+ bladder epithelial cells, indicating that the epithelial is the primary source of CCL7 in the disease context (Fig. 7g). Similarly, TNF-α–stimulated inflammatory bladder epithelial cells in vitro secreted significantly higher levels of CCL7 protein, consistent with in vivo findings (Fig. 7h).

Functionally, recombinant CCL7 treatment upregulated CCR1 and induced M1 polarization in macrophages, as shown by increased IL-1β, iNOS, and CD86 expression (Fig. 7i–j). In contrast, CCR1 knockdown suppressed M1 markers and elevated M2 markers (Fig. 7k), which was further validated by flow cytometry and Western blotting (Fig. 7l–s).

Collectively, these results identify CCL7 as a key ligand of CCR1 within the IC/BPS inflammatory microenvironment. CCL7 levels correlate positively with disease severity and promote CCR1-mediated M1 macrophage polarization, thereby contributing to IC/BPS progression.

Discussion

In this study, we identified CCR1 as a key mediator in IC/BPS through bioinformatics and experimental validation. CCR1 was significantly upregulated in bladder tissues from IC/BPS patients and rat models, correlating with symptom severity. CCR1 inhibition with BX471 in LPS-induced cystitis rats reduced pain, urinary frequency, and bladder inflammation. Single-cell RNA-seq revealed CCR1 expression mainly on M1 macrophages, which caused bladder epithelial damage. CCR1 inhibition reversed M1 polarization in both in vitro and in vivo models, promoting a shift to the tissue-reparative M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, CCR1 knockdown enhanced FOXO1 phosphorylation and degradation, activating PPARγ signaling and driving M2 repolarization. Additionally, CCL7, the CCR1 ligand, was elevated in the urine and bladder tissue of IC/BPS patients, with urinary CCL7 levels correlating with disease severity, highlighting its potential as a candidate biomarker. Together, these findings underscore the critical role of the CCL7–CCR1–FOXO1–PPARγ axis in IC/BPS pathogenesis and support CCR1 antagonism as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Immune dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a central driver of IC/BPS pathogenesis, with chemokines and their receptors playing key roles in recruiting immune cells and sustaining chronic inflammation5,25. Our previous work showed that these inflammatory mediators not only perpetuate a vicious cycle of bladder epithelial injury but also contribute to C-fiber hypersensitivity, leading to chronic pelvic pain, urinary urgency, and frequency in IC/BPS26. In this study, we identified CCR1 as a significantly upregulated gene in bladder tissues from IC/BPS patients, with its expression closely correlating with symptom severity. This finding aligns with observations in other chronic pain conditions27. for instance, in neuropathic pain models, spinal cord CCR1 mRNA levels increase progressively with pain intensity28.

CCR1 plays a critical role in regulating immune cell migration, activation, and function, particularly in macrophages29,30. Macrophages exhibit phenotypic plasticity, shifting from pro-inflammatory M1-like to pro-healing M2-like states31,32. Although the M1/M2 classification represents a simplified dichotomy, it provides a useful framework for understanding immune responses in inflammatory diseases, including IC/BPS. In our study, we observed pronounced M1 macrophage infiltration in the bladder tissues of IC/BPS patients, accompanied by elevated CCR1 protein expression. Furthermore, CCR1 was found to drive M1 macrophage polarization, contributing to inflammation and epithelial injury. This is consistent with findings from other inflamed tissues, where M1 macrophages are major producers of pro-inflammatory mediators—including TNF-α, IL-1β, and ROS—that intensify inflammation and tissue damage19.

Targeting CCR1 to modulate macrophage polarization has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for chronic inflammatory diseases33–35. Both preclinical and clinical studies have shown that CCR1 antagonists can effectively reduce M1 macrophage recruitment and inflammatory cytokine production. For example, CCR1 inhibitors such as BX471 have demonstrated efficacy in models of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and lupus nephritis, leading to improved inflammatory profiles and clinical outcomes19,36. Our study extends these findings to IC/BPS, showing that CCR1 inhibition with BX471 reverses M1 polarization within the IC/BPS inflammatory microenvironment and significantly reduces bladder inflammation and symptoms in LPS-induced cystitis models. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of CCR1 antagonism in IC/BPS management.

FOXO1 is a key regulator of cell proliferation, metabolism, and immune cell survival37,38, while PPARγ plays a central role in immune modulation and macrophage polarization21. The FOXO1/PPARγ axis operates through two main mechanisms: FOXO1 suppresses PPARγ either by binding its promoter to inhibit transcription or by directly interacting with the protein to block its activity39. Phosphorylation of FOXO1 disrupts these effects, leading to its cytoplasmic localization, degradation, and subsequent activation of PPARγ signaling40. Our gene expression profiling revealed a novel link between CCR1 signaling and macrophage polarization. Specifically, CCR1 knockdown promoted FOXO1 phosphorylation and degradation, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on PPARγ and enhancing M2 polarization. These findings suggest a mechanistic pathway by which CCR1 influences macrophage function.

Our findings indicate that CCL7 is the primary ligand for CCR1 in IC/BPS, with elevated levels strongly correlating with symptom severity. This suggests its potential as a candidate biomarker for both diagnosis and disease monitoring. Similar correlations have been reported in other chronic inflammatory conditions, such as cardiometabolic and renal diseases, where CCL7 reflects disease activity and predicts outcomes41,42. Immunofluorescence localized CCL7 mainly to the bladder epithelium, identifying it as a key source during IC/BPS. The CCL7–CCR1 interaction appears central to epithelial–macrophage crosstalk in IC/BPS. Epithelial cells release CCL7 early in the inflammatory response, recruiting macrophages and promoting M1 polarization. These M1 macrophages, in turn, exacerbate epithelial damage and inflammation, creating a self-perpetuating cycle. This aligns with established IC/BPS pathogenesis, where epithelial injury triggers downstream immune cascades3,43. Beyond serving as a barrier, the bladder epithelium actively modulates immune responses4,44. The CCL7/CCR1 axis has also been implicated in other inflammatory diseases, such as abdominal aortic aneurysm, where it contributes to tissue remodeling and disease progression45. Therefore, targeting this axis may disrupt the self-sustaining inflammation in IC/BPS and represents a promising therapeutic approach.

Nonetheless, there are some limitations to our study. First, we primarily used an LPS-induced IC/BPS model with preliminary validation in a cyclophosphamide-induced model (Supplementary Fig. 9); neither fully replicates the multifactorial nature of human IC/BPS. Second, patient stratification by IC/BPS subtype was not performed, and concomitant medications may have confounded immune readouts and CCR1-related signatures. Additionally, the non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer controls were older than the IC/BPS cohort, which may introduce age-related bias. Finally, CCR1-mediated M1 macrophage activation likely represents a downstream event following epithelial barrier disruption; identifying the upstream triggers of urothelial CCL7 secretion warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, our study identifies a distinct subset of CCR1⁺ macrophages within the IC/BPS immune microenvironment and suggests that this population may contribute to inflammatory responses and symptom-related phenotypes. In experimental models, pharmacological inhibition of CCR1 attenuated inflammatory readouts and improved pain- and urinary-related measures, supporting CCR1 as a pathway of interest for further investigation. In addition, CCL7 was elevated in IC/BPS samples and showed an exploratory association with disease severity, indicating that it may represent a candidate biomarker. Collectively, these findings provide proof-of-concept evidence implicating the CCL7–CCR1 axis in IC/BPS pathophysiology.

Methods

Bioinformatics

Bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from IC/BPS patients were obtained from GEO datasets GSE11783, GSE57560, and GSE175526. For bulk RNA sequencing analysis, eligible datasets containing bladder tissue samples from IC/BPS patients were selected and integrated after batch effect correction, resulting in a combined dataset of IC/BPS cases and healthy controls. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma package (P < 0.05, |log₂FC|≥ 1). Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), were performed using the clusterProfiler package to characterize biological processes and pathways associated with IC/BPS. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) identified gene modules associated with IC/BPS. Immune-related genes from the ImmPort database were intersected with DEGs and hub genes. Machine learning approaches (LASSO, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machine) were applied for feature selection.

For scRNA-seq analysis, processed count matrices from GSE175526 were analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.4.0). Low-quality cells were excluded based on gene counts, mitochondrial content, and hemoglobin gene expression. After quality control and doublet removal, high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Data were normalized and scaled, and highly variable genes were identified for dimensionality reduction. Principal component analysis and batch correction were performed prior to clustering and visualization. Cellular subpopulations were identified using graph-based clustering and annotated based on canonical marker genes, including CD3E, CD4, and CD8A for T cells; CD68 and LYZ for macrophages; CD19 and MS4A1 for B cells; CDH5 and PECAM1 for endothelial cells; COL1A1 and COL1A2 for fibroblasts; KRT18 and KRT19 for epithelial cells; CSF3R for neutrophils; and CPA3 and KIT for mast cells. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using differential expression analysis to validate cell type annotation. For macrophage-specific analysis, the same analytical framework was applied to macrophage subsets. CCR1-positive and CCR1-negative populations were defined at the cluster level, and differential gene expression between these groups was analyzed. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was subsequently performed using clusterProfiler with Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets to identify pathways associated with CCR1-related transcriptional programs.

Cell culture

Human ureteral epithelial cells (SV-HUC-1) and human myeloid leukemia mononuclear cells (THP-1) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). SV-HUC-1 cells were cultured in F12 medium (C11330500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 164210, Procell) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (C22400500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (21985023, Thermo Fisher) under the same conditions.

THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, P1585, Sigma-Aldrich) for 12 h. M1 polarization was induced by stimulation with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h, whereas M2 polarization was achieved using 20 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4, AF-200-04, Thermo Fisher) and interleukin-13 (IL-13, AF-200-13, Thermo Fisher) for 48 h.

To mimic the IC/BPS inflammatory microenvironment in vitro, a Transwell co-culture system was established. SV-HUC-1 cells were treated with 10 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, PHC3015, Thermo Fisher) for 24 h to induce inflammation, while control cells were treated with PBS. Cells were then seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well into the upper chamber of the Transwell system. In the lower chamber, macrophages (2 × 10⁵ cells per well) from the control, negative control (NC), or CCR1 knockdown (siCCR1) groups were seeded. The co-culture system was maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h, after which macrophages were collected for downstream analyses.

siRNA-mediated knockdown

CCR1-targeting siRNAs (sequences in Supplementary Table 1) were obtained from Shanghai Jima Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. Transfection was performed using reagents from D-Nano Bio. The siRNA complex was prepared by mixing 5 μL siRNA with 35 μL Reagent A and adding 10 μL Reagent B. After vortexing, the mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. Then, 150 μL of complete medium was added to the lipid-siRNA complexes. THP-1 cells were induced with 100 ng/mL PMA for 12 h to promote differentiation into M0 macrophages. The transfection complex was added dropwise and gently mixed. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium to minimize cytotoxicity.

Plasmid-mediated overexpression and macrophage conditioned medium preparation

CCR1 overexpression in macrophages was achieved using a CCR1 overexpression plasmid from Jima Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (sequence details in Supplementary Table 2) and the PolyJet™ DNA transfection reagent. THP-1 cells were induced with PMA to differentiate into M0 macrophages. The transfection complex was prepared by mixing 1 μg plasmid DNA with 3 μL transfection reagent in RPMI 1640 medium. The original culture medium was discarded, and fresh medium containing the transfection complex was added. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 12 h, then switched to complete culture medium for an additional 48 h.

Supernatants from the CCR1 overexpression (CCR1-OE) and negative control groups were collected and centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min to remove debris, ensuring sterile conditioned media. These media were subsequently applied to bladder epithelial cells for 48 h to investigate their effects on epithelial inflammation and function.

RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR

RNA purification was performed using the Cell/Tissue Total RNA Fast Extraction Kit (M5105; New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China). Reverse transcription was conducted with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (RR036A; Takara Bio, Japan) to generate cDNA templates. Gene expression was quantified via SYBR Green-based real-time PCR on a StepOne Plus platform (Applied Biosystems, USA) using Hieff® qPCR Master Mix (11184ES08; Yeasen Biotech, China). Gapdh served as the endogenous reference, with primer sequences listed in Supplementary Table 3.

RNA sequencing and data processing

Monocytic THP-1 cells were differentiated into M0 macrophages through a 12-h PMA stimulation (100 ng/mL). These M0 macrophages, including siRNA-mediated CCR1 knockdown variants, were exposed to conditioned medium from inflammatory SV-HUC-1 cells for 48 h. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen), with purity and concentration confirmed via a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer.

Stranded mRNA-seq libraries were constructed by LC-Bio Technologies and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-controlled and adapter-trimmed using fastp with default parameters, and only high-quality filtered reads were used for downstream analysis. Filtered reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome using HISAT2 with default settings, and gene models were defined according to the Ensembl v107 annotation. Multi-mapped reads were handled according to the default HISAT2 behavior. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie, and counts were obtained at the gene level. Gene-level count matrices were analyzed using DESeq2 to identify differentially expressed genes (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05, |log2FC|> 1). Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler against the KEGG 2023 database.

Animal models and experimental protocols

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2024-000833) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 reporting standards. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Female Sprague–Dawley rats (180–210 g) were housed under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 55% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with a 7-day acclimatization period prior to experimentation.

To establish the IC/BPS model, rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), and anesthesia depth was monitored by respiratory rate and toe-pinch reflex. A PE-50 catheter was inserted transurethrally for bladder instillation. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 mg/mL, 0.5 mL; Sigma-Aldrich) was instilled into the bladder and retained for 30 min, followed by irrigation with sterile saline. This procedure was repeated every 48 h for a total of four administrations. Control animals received equivalent volumes of sterile saline.

For CCR1 inhibition, BX471 (50 mg/kg, subcutaneous injection; MedChemExpress) was administered daily for 7 consecutive days, while control animals received vehicle treatment. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Sample sizes for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Different subsets of animals were used for specific experimental endpoints as required.

Flow cytometry

Bladder tissues from saline-treated, LPS-induced, and BX471-treated rats were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions. Erythrocytes were removed using ACK lysing buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were stained with fluorescent-conjugated antibodies against CD45 (PE), CD11b (PerCP/Cy5.5), F4/80 (APC), CD86 (Brilliant Violet 605), and CD206 (Brilliant Violet 421) (all from BioLegend, USA) to assess macrophage populations and polarization status. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended for analysis. The proportions of CD86⁺ (M1-like) and CD206⁺ (M2-like) macrophages were quantified. Gating strategies are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

For THP-1-derived macrophages, cells were differentiated into M0 macrophages using PMA and subsequently co-cultured with control or inflammatory SV-HUC-1 cells46. Cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD86 and PE-conjugated anti-CD206 antibodies (BioLegend, USA) at 4 °C under light-protected conditions. After washing, cells were prepared for flow cytometric analysis.

Flow cytometry data were acquired using a Beckman CytoFLEX S system and analyzed using CytExpert 2.4 and FlowJo™ (v10) software.

Mechanical allodynia assessment

Von Frey testing was conducted using an ascending–descending protocol47. Stimulation began with a 2 g filament applied to the lower abdomen, and subsequent filament selection was based on the animal’s response (licking or freezing lasting >2 s).

Urodynamics evaluation

Under urethane anesthesia (20%, i.p.), rats underwent transurethral catheterization with PE-50 tubing connected to an infusion pump (6 mL/h saline) and pressure transducer for continuous intravesical pressure monitoring using BL New Century 2.1 software (Taimeng Technology, Chengdu, China) at 100 Hz sampling frequency, following a 30-min stabilization period to establish consistent voiding patterns.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Bladder tissues from all experimental groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were prepared using a microtome, followed by deparaffinization with xylene and graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0; ZLI-9072, ZSGB-BIO, China) at 95 °C for 30 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Non-specific binding sites were blocked using blocking reagent (P0260, Beyotime, China) for 60 min.

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD68 (ab303565, Abcam; 1:300), iNOS (ab283655, Abcam; 1:200), and CD206 (24595S, CST; 1:500). After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (AF488- or CY3-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG, 1:400; Affinity, China) for 40 min under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; Beyotime, China). Images were acquired using a Leica DFC350 FX system. Three representative fields per section were analyzed using ImageJ to quantify lamina propria macrophages and calculate the percentages of iNOS⁺ or CD206⁺ cells relative to CD68⁺ cells.

For immunohistochemistry, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Beyotime, China). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD86 (91882S, CST; 1:500) and CCR1 (TA374392, Origene; 1:500), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Affinity, China). Signal development was performed using DAB substrate (Beyotime, China), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, China). Images were captured using a Leica DFC350 FX system for histopathological evaluation.

Patients sample collection

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. II2024-221-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all ethical regulations relevant to human research were followed. Biological sample collection adhered to BRISQ guidelines. Clinical diagnosis of IC/BPS was based on standardized East Asian protocols and ESSIC diagnostic criteria, including chronic bladder-related pain lasting more than 6 months with at least one additional lower urinary tract symptom48,49. Symptom severity was assessed using the Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Index (ICSI) and Interstitial Cystitis Problem Index (ICPI).

Bladder biopsy samples from IC/BPS patients were obtained via cystoscopy from Hunner lesions or glomerulation/post-distension bleeding areas using cold cup forceps. Biopsies consisted of mucosal tissue, including the urothelium and superficial lamina propria. Control bladder tissues were obtained from normal-appearing mucosa of patients with non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), collected from sites distant from the tumor. Inclusion criteria included age 18–75 years and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included concurrent urological diseases, recent intravesical therapy, severe systemic illness, or recent antibiotic use. A total of 12 IC/BPS patients and 5 control subjects were enrolled. Urine samples were collected from IC/BPS patients and control subjects with stress urinary incontinence (SUI). Baseline characteristics of all participants are summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

Elisa

Urine samples and supernatants from TNF-α-induced bladder epithelial cell models of IC/BPS were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were then transferred into fresh polypropylene tubes for further analysis. CCL7 chemokine levels were measured using a human CCL7 ELISA kit (ELK1167, ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. Optical density (OD) was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and CCL7 concentrations were determined by interpolating absorbance values against a standard curve. Finally, urinary CCL7 levels were normalized to urinary creatinine concentrations.

Cell migration assays

SV-HUC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells per well) and cultured to ~90% confluency. A linear scratch was created using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. Cells were then incubated with macrophage-conditioned medium. Wound closure was imaged at 0, 12, and 24 h using phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus, Japan). Migration was quantified by measuring wound width using ImageJ software.

WB analysis

Bladder tissues (rat and human) were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, China). After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (CWBIO, China).

Protein aliquots (30–50 μg) were electrophoretically resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (0.1% Tween-20) prior to overnight incubation (4 °C) with specific primary antibodies: CCR1 (1:1000, Origene, TA374392, MD, USA), TNF-α (1:500, Bioworld, BS1857, Nanjing, China), IL-1β (1:1000, Abcam, ab283818, Cambridge, UK), ZO-1 (1:1000, Invitrogen, 33-9100, CA, USA), Occludin (1:1000, Invitrogen, 33-1500, CA, USA), UPK3 (1:1000, PROGEN, 690108S, Heidelberg, Germany), FOXO1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 18592-1-AP, Wuhan, China), p-FOXO1 (1:1000, Affinity, AF3416, Changzhou, China), PPARα (1:1000, Proteintech, 66826-1-Ig, Wuhan, China), PPARγ (1:1000, Proteintech, 81490-5-RR, Wuhan, China), PPARδ (1:1000, Proteintech, 60193-1-Ig, Wuhan, China), GAPDH (1:1000, CST, 2118S, MA, USA).

Post-TBST washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Affinity, Changzhou, China) for 1 h at 25 °C. Chemiluminescent detection employed ECL substrate (WBKLS0100; Millipore, MA, USA), with band densitometry analyzed using ImageJ software.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Bladder specimens from all experimental groups underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hr. Cryopreserved tissues were transversely sectioned into 5 μm slices using a CM1950 cryostat. Tissue morphology was evaluated through conventional hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) histochemistry, with particular focus on leukocyte infiltration patterns and vascular abnormalities. The slides were examined under a 20× objective lens using an upright microscope Leica DFC350 FX camera (Wetzlar, Germany).

Computational docking simulations

Molecular docking simulations were conducted to investigate the interaction between FOXO1 (PDB ID: 6QZS) and PPARγ (PDB ID: 4Y29). Crystal structures of both proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, with PPARγ designated as the receptor and FOXO1 as the ligand. Docking calculations were performed using the GRAMM-X platform on a high-performance computing cluster, employing a 20 Å grid search space centered on the PPARγ ligand-binding domain. Resultant binding poses were analyzed through PDBePISA to calculate binding free energy (ΔG) and interface contact areas. Structural visualizations were generated using PyMOL, with hydrogen bonds annotated and key functional residues highlighted to characterize the interaction interface.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used when data satisfied normality assumptions. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied. When normality assumptions were not met, non-parametric tests were used (Mann–Whitney U test for two groups; Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons for multiple groups). Statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05.

Sample sizes (n) represent biologically independent samples. For clinical samples, n denotes independent patients. For in vivo experiments, n denotes individual animals. For in vitro experiments, n denotes independent experiments performed on separate biological samples. All experiments were independently replicated at least three times unless otherwise indicated. Exact n values for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Post hoc statistical power analyses were performed using GPower (v3.1.9.7) to assess the adequacy of sample sizes. For two-group comparisons, effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d based on the corresponding t statistics and sample sizes. For one-way ANOVA analyses, effect sizes were expressed as Cohen’s f, derived from η² calculated as the ratio of between-group to total sum of squares. Statistical power (1−β) was then computed based on the calculated effect sizes, sample sizes, and corresponding test types using two-tailed assumptions. A power threshold of 0.80 was considered indicative of adequate statistical power.
