Assessment of marginal bone loss, distal caries, and external root resorption in second molars adjacent to impacted third molars: a retrospective CBCT and panoramic study
Authors: Vahide Hazal Abat, Gaye Keser, Hakan Yülek, Nimet Gencoglu, Filiz Namdar Pekiner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpacted third molars (M3s) are frequently associated with pathological changes in adjacent second molars (M2s), including marginal bone loss (MBL), distal caries (DCs), and external root resorption (ERR). Accurate radiographic assessment and identification of risk factors are crucial for timely intervention and informed clinical decision-making. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between anatomical and positional risk factors of impacted M3s and pathological changes in adjacent M2s using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Additionally, the diagnostic performance of panoramic radiography (PAN) was compared to CBCT in detecting early and advanced pathological conditions.MethodsCBCT and PAN radiographs obtained from 129 patients were evaluated, comprising a total of 301 impacted M3s and their adjacent M2s. The presence and severity of MBL, DCs, and ERR in M2s were systematically evaluated in relation to the anatomical characteristics of impacted M3s. Statistical analyses involved chi-square and Cohen’s kappa tests (p < 0.05).ResultsPathological changes were observed in 69.1% of M2s adjacent to impacted M3s. MBL was most common (63.5%), with 31.9% classified as severe. ERR occurred in 21.3% of M2s, mostly mild (53.1%), while DCs was least frequent (4.3%). M3s with mesioangular, horizontal, or transverse angulation, Class C impaction, and a contact point length > 1 mm were significantly associated with higher M2 pathology risk. CBCT demonstrated higher sensitivity compared to PAN, with low agreement between modalities, highlighting PAN’s limitations in detecting early or subtle pathological changes.ConclusionsThese results emphasize the need for thorough radiographic evaluation of impacted M3s, given their potential risks to adjacent M2s. Although the superior diagnostic capability of CBCT over PAN is well-established, this study uniquely correlates specific anatomical risk factors with CBCT-detected pathologies, thereby enhancing risk stratification for clinical decision-making. Furthermore, the proposed risk-based algorithm integrating CBCT findings supports more timely interventions and improved management of impacted M3s.Keywords: Distal caries, Cone-beam computed tomography, Impacted third molar, Marginal bone loss, Panoramic radiography, External root resorption, Wisdom teeth

Introduction

The prevalence of impacted third molars (M3s) varies widely across different populations, ranging from 16.67% to 68.6% [1, 2]. These teeth often remain asymptomatic and undiagnosed for extended periods. However, when present, impacted M3s can adversely affect the distal aspect of adjacent second molars (M2s), potentially leading to pathological conditions such as marginal bone loss (MBL), distal caries (DCs), and external root resorption (ERR) [3].

MBL is one of the most significant sequelae associated with impacted teeth, resulting from both the mechanical pressure exerted by the impacted tooth and the progressive structural degradation of alveolar bone. The condition is characterised by its insidious progression, often remaining asymptomatic until it reaches an advanced stage. It is frequently detectable only through detailed radiographic assessments, highlighting the need for early and accurate diagnosis to prevent long-term damage [4].

The distal surface of M2s, which is particularly difficult to access and clean, is more susceptible to plaque accumulation and thus carries a higher risk of developing DCs. This risk is further exacerbated by factors such as bone loss and gingival recession [5]. The detection of DCs on M2 adjacent to impacted M3 can be challenging and in cases where the caries involves the radicular portion, the restoration becomes difficult, often necessitating extraction [6]. It is noteworthy that the carious involvement of the M2 and/or M3 is the second most common indication for surgical removal of impacted M3s [5].

ERR, often triggered by direct contact between the root surface of M2s and impacted M3s, is characterized by the loss of root structure on the distal surface of the second molar. The reported prevalence of ERR in M2s adjacent to impacted M3s ranges from 4.3% to 49.43% [7, 8]. The absence of distinctive symptoms and the concealed position of impacted M3s frequently result in a delayed diagnosis of ERR [9].

The early identification of these pathologies along with the potential risks associated with impacted M3s is of paramount importance in order to prevent the need for extensive treatments such as root canal therapy, root resection or tooth extraction [10]. Panoramic radiography (PAN), owing to its wide field of view, low radiation dose, and accessibility, is routinely used as the first-line imaging modality for the assessment of impacted M3s and their effect on adjacent M2s [11]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), on the other hand, provides high-resolution three-dimensional imaging that allows for superior visualization of hard tissue structures [12].

While the superior imaging capabilities of CBCT are well established, the clinical challenge lies in translating these capabilities into improved patient management by identifying specific anatomical and positional risk factors that predispose to pathology in adjacent M2s [4, 7, 8]. Although several studies have compared CBCT with PAN [4, 8, 10], none have systematically correlated specific anatomical and positional characteristics of M3s with CBCT-detected pathologies in adjacent M2s. Therefore, this study aims to bridge this gap by comprehensively assessing the association between defined risk factors of impacted M3s and radiographically detected pathological changes in adjacent M2s, while also comparing the diagnostic performance and agreement of PAN and CBCT. Our goal is to inform clinical decision-making by refining early detection strategies and guiding appropriate imaging use.

The hypotheses tested in this study are as follows:

H1: Impacted M3s are significantly associated with pathological changes, including MBL, DCs, and ERR in adjacent M2s.

H1: Impacted M3s are significantly associated with pathological changes, including MBL, DCs, and ERR in adjacent M2s.

H2: Specific characteristics of impacted M3s, such as mesioangular, horizontal, or transverse angulations; Class C impaction depth; and contact point length exceeding 1 mm, are significantly correlated with pathology occurrence in adjacent M2s.

H3: CBCT demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy compared to PAN in detecting MBL, DCs, and ERR in M2s adjacent to impacted M3s.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study, based on PAN and CBCT, was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Uskudar University (Protocol No: 2020/93) on February 26, 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Selection of study samples

This study included patients who underwent both PAN and CBCT imaging for various clinical indications, including orthodontic assessment, implant planning, or surgical evaluation.

This retrospective study initially evaluated the digital and radiographic records of approximately 1000 patients who visited the Marmara University Faculty of Dentisty, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology between March, 2024 - March, 2025. Consecutive sampling was applied, and patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: Age over 18 years, presence of at least one impacted M3 in close proximity to the adjacent M2, availability of both PAN and CBCT images taken within a six-month interval, no prior restorative or endodontic treatment of the involved teeth, and radiographic images of adequate quality for interpretation. Exclusion criteria were as follows: poor image quality or image artifacts; absence of either the M3 or the adjacent M2; M3s with root development less than two-thirds; fully erupted M3s; a follicular diameter ≥ 5 mm (indicative of pathology); history of surgical intervention in the M3 region; M2s with extensive caries involving more than one surface; presence of crowns or distal restorations; and any high-density material obstructing the area of interest. To minimize potential interval bias, patient records were reviewed to confirm that no dental treatment or surgical intervention affecting the evaluated M2–M3 units occurred between PAN and CBCT acquisition.

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 129 patients were objectively selected for final analysis, resulting in the evaluation of 301 M2s in contact with adjacent impacted M3s. Among the 301 impacted M3s evaluated, 184 were mandibular and 117 were maxillary, indicating a slightly unbalanced distribution between the jaws. When more than one impacted M3 was present in the same patient, each M3–M2 pair was analyzed as an independent anatomical unit due to its unique position and contact characteristics.

All image evaluations were independently performed by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to each other’s assessments and to patient clinical histories. To minimize selection bias, consecutive patient records that met the predefined inclusion criteria were reviewed and included, without prior knowledge of radiographic findings.

Patient inclusion was based solely on the presence of both PAN and CBCT images obtained within the specified time interval, as well as the fulfillment of the eligibility criteria. Although the selection process was not randomized, the use of consecutive sampling aimed to reduce potential bias and enhance the representativeness of the study sample.

Data collection

Demographic data, including patient age and gender, were extracted from electronic database. Age was determined based on the CBCT acquisition date (Planmeca Romexis system).

CBCT images were utilized to comprehensively assess the anatomical characteristics of impacted M3s, including their mesiodistal angulation, buccal/lingual or palatinal inclination, depth of impaction relative to the occlusal plane, dimensions of the pericoronal follicular space, and the length and position of the contact area with the adjacent M2s. Additionally, pathological conditions such as MBL, DCs on M2s, and ERR were systematically evaluated using both PAN and CBCT scans to allow for comparative analysis of diagnostic efficacy and consistency between the two imaging modalities.

Image standardization and optimization

PAN images were acquired at 80 kVp, 12 mA, and 96 μm pixel size using the Planmeca Promax 2D (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland), resulting in a dose-area product (DAP) of approximately 90 mGy·cm² for an adult exposure. CBCT images were obtained using the Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid system (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) (90 kVp, 10 mA, 0.2 mm voxel, 16 × 9 cm FOV), which yielded an average computed-tomography-dose-index (CTDI) of about 6 mGy and a corresponding DAP of roughly 250 mGy·cm² per scan. These dose parameters adhere to the as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) principle, using the lowest exposure settings that still achieve diagnostic image quality. All image analyses were conducted using Romexis 2.92 software under standardized conditions on a calibrated high-definition 17-inch TFT LCD monitor (Global Opportunities, Kyoto, Japan).

All radiographic images were standardized prior to analysis following the protocol described by Yüksel et al. [13]. Although initial images were exported in JPEG format, they were immediately converted to TIFF to prevent data compression loss. Each image was resized to a uniform resolution of 1024 × 480 pixels at 600 DPI, ensuring consistent. Standardization and optimization were performed using PhotoScape X Pro 4.2.7. software (MOOII Tech, Seoul, South Korea), with uniform adjustments of brightness, contrast, and sharpness to enhance diagnostic clarity without altering the underlying anatomical content.

Evaluation of PAN and CBCT images

Two calibrated oral and maxillofacial radiologists (H.Y. and G.K., each with over six years of CBCT experience) independently analyzed the images. Final verification was performed by a senior radiologist (F.N.P., 15 years of experience). The observers were calibrated in sessions assessing 20 cases not included in the study. For intra-observer reliability, an eight-week interval was used to ensure sufficient time had elapsed to avoid memory effects, while maintaining consistency in evaluation conditions. The Cohen’s kappa coefficient was utilised in order to evaluate the reliability, with values greater than 0.90 indicating excellent agreement between both intra- and inter-observers.

Mesiodistal angulation of impacted M3s was classified according to Winter’s classification [14] encompasses the following categories: distoangular, horizontal, inverse, mesioangular, transverse, and vertical (Fig. 1).

Winter’s classification. a Distoangular; (b) Horizontal; (c) Inverse; (d) Mesioangular; (e) Transverse; (f) Vertical

Buccal/lingual or palatinal inclination was assessed using reference lines as described in prior studies [15, 16]. In the mandible, reference line 1 was drawn between the inferior borders of the jaw, and in the maxilla, along the midpalatal plane. Reference line 2 was aligned with the long axis of the M3 (through the central fossa and root midpoint at the apical third). The angle formed between the two lines determined inclination (Fig. 2): central: −10° to + 10°, buccal: > +10°, lingual/palatinal: < −10°.

The buccal/lingual or palatinal inclination of the indicated M3. a, b ‘central’ when the angle formed was between + 10° and − 10°, (c, d) ‘buccal’ when the angle formed was greater than + 10°, and (e, f) ‘lingual or palatinal’ when it was less than − 10°

Impaction depth was classified based on Pell and Gregory’s classification [17]: Class A (occlusal level above or at the M2 occlusal surface), Class B (between the occlusal plane and cementoenamel junction of M2, and Class C (below the cementoenamel junction of M2). Eruption status was categorized as soft tissue impaction (partially visible or covered by soft tissue) or bone impaction (fully surrounded by bone) [7]. Fully erupted M3s were excluded.

Root development was assessed via CBCT and categorized as complete (closed apical foramen), almost complete (open foramen), or developed more than two-thirds. Follicular space was measured in multiple CBCT planes. M3s with follicle diameters ≥ 5 mm were excluded, as this was deemed to be pathological. Contact point length between M3 and M2 was classified as: small (< 1 mm), medium (1–3 mm), or large (3–5 mm).

The presence of MBL was assessed using CBCT sagittal views and the severity of this pathology was classified as (Fig. 3): slight (involving the coronal third of the M2 root), moderate (reaching the middle third), and severe (extending to the apical third), based on the classification reported by Dias et al. [18].

Classification of marginal bone loss based on severity. a Slight; (b) Moderate; (c) Severe

DCs were evaluated using a modified version of the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) and the International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS™) [19], and classified as slight, moderate, or severe. ERR was identified when clear substance loss on the M2 root surface was observed, based on the criteria of Al-Khateeb and Bataineh [20]. The severity of resorption was determined using the Ericson and Kurol classification [21]: slight (less than half of dentin thickness affected), moderate (involvement up to or beyond the pulp chamber, and severe (pulp exposure due to resorption) (Fig. 4). Although this classification was originally developed for evaluating resorption caused by ectopic canines [21], it has subsequently been widely adopted in numerous studies assessing ERR of M2s associated with impacted M3s, as it provides a standardized and reproducible radiographic grading system [7, 15, 22–26].

Classification of external root resorption based on location and severity. Location of the lesion: (a) Cervical third, (b) Middle third, (c) Apical third. Severity of resorption: (d) Slight, (e) Moderate, (f) Severe

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22. Interobserver and intraobserver agreements were assessed with Cohen’s kappa. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies.

Each M3–M2 pair was considered a separate anatomical unit because of its unique angulation, position, and contact characteristics, even when multiple impacted molars were present in the same patient. To control for potential intra-patient dependency, analyses were verified using robust standard error adjustment accounting for repeated measures within patients, which did not alter the statistical significance of the results. Comparative analyses for categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s Exact test, Fisher-Freeman-Halton test, and Yates’ Continuity Correction, where appropriate. Diagnostic screening tests were employed to evaluate sensitivity and specificity, while the kappa coefficient assessed agreement between PAN and CBCT findings. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

This study analyzed 301 impacted M3s from 129 patients, with a mean age of 25.3 ± 8.6 years; more than half of the participants (56.8%) were between 14 and 24 years of age. The study sample consisted of 58 female and 71 male patients, indicating a balanced gender distribution.

Across demographic groups, the prevalence of MBL, DCs, and ERR showed similar distributions, with no statistically significant differences between male and female patients (p > 0.05). Age groups did not differ significantly for MBL or DCs; however, ERR was more frequent in older age (>25) groups (p = 0.012) (Table 1).

Prevalence of pathological findings

Among the 301 cases, MBL was the most prevalent pathology, affecting 191 M2s (63.5%), with 32% of those cases categorized as severe. ERR was observed in 64 M2s (21.3%), most frequently in the middle third of the root (45.3%), and most commonly classified as slight (53.1%). DCs were the least frequent pathology, occurring in 13 M2s (4.3%), with the majority of lesions classified as moderate (53.8%) and no cases deemed severe. Multiple pathological conditions were observed to co-occur in several cases. In total, at least one pathological finding was identified in 208 out of 301 s molars (69.1%) adjacent to impacted M3s. These results support the alternative hypothesis (H1), suggesting a significant association between impacted M3s and pathological changes in adjacent M2s.

Jaw-specific findings

Impacted M3s (n = 184) were 1.5 times more frequently located in the mandible than in the maxilla (n = 117). However, no statistically significant differences were observed based on jaw (p > 0.05).

Pathological findings differed slightly between maxillary and mandibular M3s; however, significant differences were limited to the prevalence of DCs. MBL was observed in 68.4% of maxillary M2s and 60.3% of mandibular M2s, with no statistically significant difference between the jaws (p = 0.157). ERR occurred at comparable rates in maxillary (19.7%) and mandibular (22.3%) M2s (p = 0.588). By contrast, DCs were significantly more common in mandibular M2s (6.5%) than in maxillary M2s (0.9%) (p = 0.039), indicating a jaw-dependent pattern for this pathology (Table 1). These findings suggest that while MBL and ERR occur at similar frequencies in both arches, mandibular M3s pose a disproportionately higher risk for DCs development in adjacent M2s.

A statistically significant association was found between the mesiodistal angulation of impacted M3s and the prevalence of MBL and ERR (p = 0.001). Mesioangular and horizontal impactions were associated with the highest prevalence of MBL (93.1% and 78.3%, respectively), whereas distoangular (34.6%), vertical (53.1%), and inverse (0%) positions exhibited significantly lower rates (p < 0.05). Similarly, ERR was most frequently observed in transverse (50%), horizontal (37.9%), and mesioangular (28.7%) angulations (p < 0.05). The buccal/lingual or palatinal inclination of the impacted M3s did not show a significant correlation with the prevalence of any pathological condition.

The depth of impaction of the M3 was also found to be significantly associated with the prevalence of adjacent pathological conditions. Class C impactions were associated with the highest prevalence of MBL (77.1%) and ERR (27.4%), while no DCs were observed in these cases (p < 0.05). Soft tissue impactions showed significantly higher rates of MBL, ERR, and DCs compared to bone-impacted M3s (p = 0.001, p = 0.001, and p = 0.002, respectively).

Root development stage and follicle size did not significantly correlate with any pathology (p > 0.05). Furthermore, a contact point length > 1 mm was significantly associated with both MBL and ERR (p < 0.05), but not with DCs (p > 0.05) (Table 2). These findings confirm H2, demonstrating that specific anatomical and positional characteristics of M3s significantly increase the risk of pathological changes in adjacent M2s.

Comparison between PAN and CBCT

The comparative analysis between PAN and CBCT revealed low levels of agreement in detecting all three pathologies. As shown in Table 3; the highest concordance between the two imaging modalities was found in the “absence” category for each pathology. For non-absent categories, agreement was minimal: between 3.3% − 7.6% for MBL, 0–1.3% for DCs, and 1.3–2.7% for ERR. Kappa statistics confirmed these findings, indicating slight agreement between the two modalities (MBL: κ = 0.167, p = 0.001; DCs: κ = 0.038, p = 0.019; ERR: κ = 0.079, p = 0.004). These results support H3, demonstrating the superior diagnostic performance of CBCT compared to PAN in detecting pathology in M2s adjacent to impacted M3s.

Discussion

The present study aimed not merely to reaffirm the well-known superiority of CBCT over PAN in image resolution, but also to utilise this advantage to clarify how specific anatomical and positional risk factors are associated with pathologies in adjacent M2s. Although CBCT is recognized to generate three-dimensional, distortion-free images, limited evidence exists on how these imaging benefits contribute to the early detection of MBL, DCs, and ERR in relation to factors such as angulation, impaction depth, and contact point length. This study therefore sought to fill this gap by analysing these relationships radiographically.

The higher prevalence of impacted M3s among individuals under 25 years likely reflects typical eruption patterns. Conversely, the lower frequency in older individuals may be attributable to prior extractions. No significant gender-related differences were found regarding the prevalence of MBL, DCs, or ERR, consistent with previous studies [18, 27]. Furthermore, age did not exert a significant influence on the occurrence of MBL or DCs (p > 0.05), aligning with previous findings [15, 26, 28]. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inference, underscoring the need for longitudinal investigations.

The mesiodistal angulation of the impacted M3s was found to play a critical role in pathology development on the adjacent teeth. Mesioangular, horizontal, and transverse angulations were significantly associated with both MBL and ERR, with horizontal impactions exhibiting the highest MBL rates. These results support prior studies advocating for prophylactic extraction of such M3s to prevent adjacent M2 damage [3, 7, 29]. In contrast, buccolingual inclination demonstrated no significant association with any pathology; however, limited data are available on this parameter [15], complicating direct comparisons.

Our results offer important insights into how impaction depth and eruption status influence the likelihood of pathological patterns. Deeply impacted M3s (Class C) were significantly associated with higher prevalence of MBL and ERR, whereas Class A and B impactions were more frequently associated with DCs, likely due to their closer proximity to the oral cavity and bacterial exposure. This observation is supported by a meta-analysis of 3,169 individuals, which found that M3s with Class A impaction depth were 3.45 times more likely to cause DCs in adjacent M2s than those with Class C impactions [30].

Soft tissue-impacted M3s also presented a higher risk for the evaluated pathologies compared to bone-impacted M3s, potentially attributable to bacterial accumulation and mechanical pressure associated with eruption, consistent with Oenning et al. [7]. These findings reinforce the argument for considering prophylactic extraction of soft tissue-impacted M3s to mitigate complications in adjacent M2s [3, 31].

While direct contact is known to promote ERR [32], the presence of a diastema between M2 and M3 may also contribute to plaque accumulation and subsequent distal caries formation [3]. Our data indicate that a contact point length exceeding 1 mm is a significant risk factor for both MBL and ERR, supporting literature that large contacts increase interfacial pressure and exacerbating hard-tissue damage [8, 26]. Collectively, these results highlight that precisely defined radiographic and anatomical risk indicators may serve as valuable adjuncts in stratifying patients for early intervention.

The elucidation of specific anatomical risk factors facilitates targeted prophylactic intervention, potentially improving patient outcomes and reducing unnecessary extractions. This risk-based approach supports more judicious clinical decision-making regarding the timing and necessity of impacted M3 extraction.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses underscore the enhanced diagnostic yield of CBCT compared to PAN for detecting mandibular canal proximity, ERR, and MBL [33, 34]. Additionally, Matzen and Wenzel’s hierarchical evidence review further supports CBCT’s superior accuracy in impacted lower third molar evaluation [35]. Based on our findings, we propose a stratified imaging protocol whereby PAN is employed as the initial screening modality, with selective use of CBCT reserved for cases exhibiting defined high-risk anatomical features. This approach optimises diagnostic accuracy while minimising unnecessary radiation exposure, consistent with radiological protection principles. A clinical decision-making algorithm summarising this stratified strategy is presented (Fig. 5), which may assist clinicians in personalised risk assessment and management of impacted M3s.

The comparative analysis of PAN and CBCT revealed only slight agreement between the modalities (p < 0.05), particularly in early or subtle lesions. The ability of CBCT to capture volumetric data in all three spatial planes without distortion enables more accurate detection of subtle details, such as differentiating ERR from carious lesions. PAN is affected by magnification, distortion, and projectional overlap between the M2 and M3, which may obscure the visualization of subtle pathological changes on the distal aspect of M2. In a retrospective study, nearly half of the PAN images exhibited superimposition of these teeth [36], which complicating prognostic assessments.

In addition, CBCT studies have demonstrated considerable anatomical variability in the mandibular canal region, which may influence both the interpretation of impacted M3 pathologies and the planning of surgical procedures. Such variations, as highlighted by Altındağ et al. [37], underscore the importance of recognizing these morphological differences to enhance diagnostic accuracy and reduce intraoperative complications.

Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that CBCT should not be indiscriminately employed in all cases of impacted M3s. From a clinical standpoint:

PAN is sufficient for the detection of advanced MBL, grossly evident ERR, and extensive DCs.

CBCT is most valuable in cases with high-risk anatomical features (e.g., mesioangular/horizontal/transverse angulation, Class C depth, contact > 1 mm) when early-stage pathology is suspected or when PAN interpretation is equivocal due to overlap or artefacts.

This stratified approach is congruent with the principles of justification and optimisation in radiological protection, ensuring maximal diagnostic efficacy while minimising unnecessary radiation exposure and resource utilisation.

Although this study included a total of 301 impacted M3s, the number of patients (n = 129) was relatively limited, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to validate these associations across broader populations.

Another limitation of the present study is that PAN and CBCT were not always acquired on the same day; the maximum interval was six months. While minor changes cannot be excluded, this period is generally considered short within radiographic recall frameworks (6–12 months) and most radiographic progression studies assess change over ≥ 12 months [38]. Moreover, progression of external resorptive processes is frequently detected beyond six months (often ≥ 8 months) [39, 40], reducing the likelihood that interval change materially biased our PAN–CBCT agreement estimates.

It is important to acknowledge that the present results are based solely on radiographic interpretation, without histopathological or surgical confirmation. This study did not include a definitive gold standard such as histopathological evaluation to the observed pathologies. Consequently, the superiority of CBCT over PAN should be interpreted within the scope of radiographic evaluation only, and not as a substitute for clinical diagnosis. This limitation underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the results and suggests that further studies incorporating histopathological validation are warranted.

Furthermore, given the retrospective nature of this study and the use of pre-existing radiographic records, a degree of selection bias may exist. To minimize this, all cases that met the inclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively included without preference for severity. Nevertheless, prospective multicentre studies with randomized sampling are needed to eliminate potential bias and strengthen the generalizability of the findings.

The final limitation of this study relates to the development of pathology, which may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as systemic health conditions and oral hygiene status. Due to the retrospective design, these variables could not be comprehensively assessed. Consequently, future prospective investigations incorporating clinical, histopathological, and patient-centered data including systemic diseases and oral hygiene measures are warranted to better clarify the complex interplay of these factors and their impact on pathology development.

Future research could further build upon these findings by investigating each pathological parameter through larger, focused prospective studies, thereby strengthening the evidence base for individualized diagnostic and treatment strategies. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study contribute valuable insight into the risk factors associated with impacted M3s, thereby informing clinical decision-making and guiding future research in this field.

Conclusion

This comprehensive assessment integrating both pathological associations and diagnostic provides a more realistic representation of clinical conditions and a broader understanding of the complex interactions between impacted M3s and adjacent M2s.

The findings of this retrospective analysis demonstrate that the presence and specific characteristics of impacted M3s, particularly mesioangular, horizontal, and transverse angulations, Class C impactions, and contact points exceeding 1 mm, are significantly associated with pathological changes such as MBL, DCs, and ERR in adjacent M2s.

Although PAN remains a common diagnostic tool, the results clearly indicate that CBCT provides superior accuracy in detecting these conditions, highlighting the importance of three-dimensional evaluation for precise clinical decision-making. Therefore, clinicians should carefully assess these radiographic and anatomical risk factors using the proposed decision-making algorithm (Fig. 5) when planning prophylactic or therapeutic interventions involving impacted M3s.
