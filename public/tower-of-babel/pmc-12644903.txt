MLM3 and MLM5 head and neck cancer models promote age-dependent musculoskeletal deterioration in mice
Authors: Alexander J. Jones, Marcos V. Esteca, Joshua R. Huot, Fernando M. Simabuco, Michael G. Moore, Fabrizio Pin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Head and Neck Cancer (HNC) patients are at high risk of developing cancer cachexia. While studies have focused on muscle, little is known regarding the consequences of non-bone metastatic HNC on the skeleton. Moreover, animal models used to date have only been evaluated in young, growing mice and not in fully developed adult animals. Therefore, this study focused on the effects of two murine HNC models, MLM3 and MLM5, on both muscle and bone in 2 and 12-month-old male mice. In young, but not adult mice, both tumors reduced growth as shown by shorter tibiae and altered growth plate size together with an increased bone remodeling. Tumor size was greater in the young compared to the adult mice, even though MLM3 tumors had comparable musculoskeletal effects in both ages. In both HNC models, muscle loss was accompanied by a corresponding loss in bone. The more cachectic MLM3 models induced severe bone deterioration at both microarchitecture and cellular levels. HNC had distinct growth inhibitory effects on the musculoskeletal system in young compared to adult mice. These observations encourage the use of mature, fully developed mice that better reflect the age of human HNC patients.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) includes a wide array of malignant pathologies which will account for 59,660 cases and 12,770 cancer deaths in 2025 in the United States. The cancer is more often diagnosed in middle-aged or older individuals and there is a male predilection1. Most disease involves squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) occupying the mucosal surfaces of the upper aerodigestive tract because of heavy tobacco and/or alcohol consumption. A substantial and increasing portion of oropharyngeal SCC is derived from human papilloma virus (HPV) and is seen in younger, healthier patients2. Due to location, HNC often results in significant morbidity both pre- and post-treatment due to impairment of speech, swallowing, and respiration.

Despite HNC accounting for < 5% of cancer diagnoses and deaths, patients with HNC are particularly susceptible to incurring cancer cachexia, having the third highest incidence amongst all malignancies3. This inflammatory condition produced by the tumor results in significant body wasting and unintentional weight loss through impaired anabolism, upregulated catabolism, anorexia, and overall energy wasting. Seen in up to 60% of advanced HNC cases, the cachectic phenotype results in sarcopenia, lipolysis, frailty, and fatigue. Its prevalence increases alongside the progression of tumor size, staging, and adverse pathologic features4,5. It has profound detrimental effects on post-operative and post-chemoradiation tolerance and overall survival, as a significant portion of patients will ultimately succumb to cachexia’s deterioration6–10. To date, there is unfortunately limited amelioration through standard nutritional methods, and no cure has been discovered other than through eradication of the malignancy11.

More recently, it has been shown that non-metastatic tumors not only induce cachexia but also extreme bone deterioration and bone loss12–15. Osteopenia, osteoporosis, and fractures have been identified in much higher rates in cachectic patients with localized breast, cervical, prostate, gastrointestinal, and lung cancers16–19. The bone density of HNC patients on CT imaging was also found to be lower than a matched healthy population20. Animal models of colorectal and ovarian cancer have shown concomitant deterioration of bone density with muscle mass without bony metastases12,14,15. Interestingly, there is paracrine interaction between muscle and bone whereby skeletal muscle loss can induce reduction in bone density; conversely, restoration of bone density has also demonstrated improvements in skeletal muscle mass and function12,21,22. This interaction clearly suggests that bone loss, a critical component of cancer cachexia which has been largely underestimated, is an important, potentially therapeutic topic of investigation.

To date, however, the direct effect that non-bone metastatic HNC has on the skeletal system has not been investigated. Animal models of HNC are particularly limited. Furthermore, only young mice have been utilized, which do not reproduce the typical HNC demographic, thereby limiting the translatability of animal studies. The present study therefore aimed to compare the musculoskeletal consequences of HNC in young growing mice to fully developed adult mice using the previously described MLM3 and MLM5 HNC models23. These findings emphasize the importance of selecting animal ages that more accurately reflect the human pathology and demonstrate particularly the detrimental effect of HNC cachexia on bone.

Results

Increasing HNC tumor burden causes significant body weight (BW) loss

In young mice, the MLM3 tumor caused reduction in BW when compared to the MLM5 or control mice (Fig. 1A). Conversely, no differences in BW were observed in the mature mice bearing either HNC tumors (Fig. 1A). Comparison of the BW change between the young and mature mice showed that MLM3, but not MLM5, decreases BW in both young and mature mice (Table S1,S2). The tumor-free BW change clearly shows that the MLM3 cells in both young and mature mice induced body wasting (Fig. 1B). No difference in tumor size was observed between MLM3 and MLM5 in either young or adult mice (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, in the 12-month-old mice, both MLM5 and MLM3 tumor masses were smaller when compared to the young mice (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that in mature mice the MLM3 tumor has more drastic effects on weight loss than the MLM5 and that HNC tumor growth over time is slower in adult mice.

HNC compromises body composition

To study the effects of HNC on body composition, the mice were assessed by means of DEXA on the day before sacrifice. Lean mass was reduced in the 2-month-old MLM3 bearers (Fig. 2A) as previously described23. Despite a smaller tumor mass in adult animals, (Fig. 1D) both clones induced lean mass wasting in the 12-month-old mice (Fig. 2A; Table S1-S2). Fat mass was reduced only in the young animals bearing MLM3 tumors (Fig. 2B; Table S2). This data was also confirmed by a reduction in epididymal white adipose tissue mass (WAT) measured the day of the sacrifice (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the 12-month-old mice bearing the MLM3 cells lost less fat mass when compared with the young tumor-bearing mice (Table S2). Heart and liver mass were only affected by MLM3 tumors in the young mice (Fig. 2D, E), while spleen mass was increased by both cell clones in the 2-month-old mice and only MLM3 in the 12-month-old mice (Fig. 2F). In both young and old mice, MLM3 tumor growth decreased BMD (bone mineral density) and BMC (bone mineral content) in respect to control animals (Fig. 2G-H). Reduced BMD was also observed in the young mice bearing the MLM5 tumor (Fig. 2G).

MLM3 is cachectic for both young and adult animals, while MLM5 is only cachexic for adult mice

As previously described23, MLM3 but not MLM5 cells induced muscle wasting when injected in young mice as suggested by reduced gastrocnemius (GSN), quadriceps (Quad) and tibialis anterior (TA) mass, (Fig. 3A). Although the 12-month-old mice developed a smaller MLM5 tumor mass compared to the 2-month-old mice, MLM5 induced muscle wasting only in the old mice (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the reduction in skeletal muscle mass, muscle fiber size decreased in both 2-month- and 12-month-old mice bearing the MLM3 tumor (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, young mice bearing the MLM5 clone also showed reduced fiber area despite unchanged TA muscle mass (Fig. 3B). Reduced protein ubiquitination was observed in the muscle of 2-month-old mice bearing MLM3 cells, whereas in 12-month-old mice it was unchanged compared to controls but reduced relative to age-matched MLM5-bearing mice. (Fig. 3C; Table S1,S2). No changes were detected in the mRNA expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligases Fbxo32 and Trim63 (Fig. S1). Consistent with the muscle atrophy, in vivo muscle strength was reduced in the 2-month and 12-month-old mice bearing the MLM3 tumor (Fig. 3D). Despite reduced skeletal muscle mass, the 12-month-old mice bearing MLM5 cells did not show significant reduction in plantarflexion strength (Fig. 3D).

Only the MLM3 tumor affects bone microarchitecture and mechanical properties

To determine whether the reductions in BMD and BMC (Fig. 2G-H) were also consistent with loss of bone mass, morphometry of tibiae were analyzed by means of µCT and trabecular and cortical bone quantified. MLM3 tumor induced severe trabecular bone loss in both young and mature mice (Fig. 4A,B). This was evident by reduced trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb. BV/TV), thickness (Tb.Th), and number (Tb.N) in young mice, as well as decreased Tb. BV/TV, reduced Tb.N, and increased trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in mature mice carrying MLM3 tumors (Fig. 4A-B; Table S2). The MLM3 tumors also reduced cortical bone volume fraction (Ct. BV/TV) in both young and old mice (Fig. 4C-D). However, no significant differences were observed in the cortical thickness (Fig. 4C,D), periosteal and endosteal perimeters, and bone marrow cavity area in all groups (Fig. S2A-C).

To determine if the changes observed in bone mass are also reflected changes in bone mechanical properties, the 3-point-bending to failure test was performed on fresh femurs. The MLM3 tumor negatively affected bone strength as shown by the reduced ultimate force in both young and adult mice (Fig. 5A). Femurs from both tumors in young mice, but not older mice, demonstrated reduced stiffness (Fig. 5B). Decreased energy to failure was only identified in the older MLM3 group (Fig. 5C; Table S2). Despite no differences in cortical bone mass, ultimate force and stiffness were highly reduced in the young mice bearing the MLM5 tumor (Fig. 5A,B).

Effects of non-bone metastatic HNC on osteoclast and osteoblast in vivo

To determine if the bone loss observed in HNC bearers was also associated with increased numbers of mature osteoclasts, TRAP-stained multinucleated cells in longitudinal sections from tibiae were quantified. In the 2-month-old MLM3 mice, a significant increase in osteoclasts in the trabecular bone was observed as shown by the increase of N.Oc/B.Pm (number of osteoclasts per bone perimeter) and Oc.S/BS (osteoclast surface per bone surface; Red arrows) (Fig. 6A-B). Although not statistically significant, a tendency for increased osteoclast numbers was observed in both young and adult mice bearing MLM5 cells as well as in adult mice bearing the MLM3 (Fig. 6A-B). As bone loss can result from changes in osteoblast number and /or function, longitudinal bone sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin, and osteoblasts were counted. The histomorphometry analysis revealed a reduction in number of osteoblasts per bone perimeter (N.Ob/B.Pm) and osteoblast surface per bone surface (Ob.S/BS; yellow arrows) in the trabecular bone of young MLM5 bearers (Fig. 6C-D). However, this reduction was not observed in young mice bearing the more cachectic MLM3 HNC (Fig. 6C,D). No difference in osteoblast number was observed between control and tumor-bearing mice in the 12-month-old group (Fig. 6C–D).

Both MLM3 and MLM5 tumors inhibit growth in young mice

To clarify if the bone deteriorations observed in the young, actively growing, tumor-bearing mice resulted from reduced growth and development, we measured the tibial length and epiphyseal growth plate thickness. Both the MLM5 and MLM3 HNC cells inhibited tibial bone growth in the young mice as represented by reduced tibia length (Fig. 7A; Tables S1 and S2), but no differences were observed in the adult, non-growing mice (Fig. 7A; Tables S1 and S2). Consistently, the growth plate thickness of young HNC bearers was reduced by almost half compared to controls (Fig. 7B-C). The typical proliferating and hypertrophic chondrocyte columnar organization was partially lost in the young mice bearing both MLM5and MLM3 cells (Fig. 7C). In the adult tumor bearing mice, a modest reduction in growth plate height was observed and only with the MLM3 tumor (Fig. 7B,C). Like young mice, older tumor-bearing mice displayed some altered chondrocyte organization compared to controls (Fig. 7B,C).

Effects of HNC on markers of bone formation and bone resorption

To determine systemic effects of bone loss and elevated osteoclast numbers, circulating serum levels of C-telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I, a marker of bone resorption) and protocollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP, a marker of bone formation) were quantitated. CTX-I levels were found to be markedly increased in both young and adult MLM5- and MLM3-bearing mice, supporting osteoclastic bone resorption (Fig. 8A). Circulating levels of P1NP were also drastically increased in young mice independent of the tumor type (Fig. 8A), potentially due to a growing skeleton compensating for elevated bone resorption. The levels of P1NP were unchanged in the older mice (Fig. 8A) but were reduced compared to those in young mice (Tables S1 and S2). Circulating levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, two important pro-resorptive and pro-inflammatory factors24,25 also shown to be elevated in osteocytes in non-bone metastatic tumors14 were also quantified. IL-6 was increased in both young and adult MLM5 bearers, whereas only the young MLM3 mice displayed increased IL-6 levels (Fig. 8B). Regarding TNF-α, only the adult MLM3-bearing mice showed increased levels (Fig. 8B).

Since the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL) is the most important and essential cytokine for osteoclast formation, we measured the circulating levels of soluble RANKL but observed no change in any of the groups (Fig. 8C). Since overall bone homeostasis is regulated by the ratio of RANKL to its decoy receptor, the anti-osteoclastogenic cytokine osteoprotegerin (OPG), we also measured circulating levels of OPG. Elevated levels of OPG were observed in all groups tested, resulting in a markedly reduced RANKL/OPG ratio (Fig. 8C; Tables S1 and S2). We also measured circulating sclerostin, a bone formation inhibitor26, but no major changes were observed between control and tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 8D). Since osteocytes are essential regulators of bone homeostasis27, we measured mRNA expression levels of RANKL and OPG in osteocyte-enriched samples. The mRNA for tnfsf11 (coding for RANKL) was unchanged across all groups, whereas the mRNA expression of tnfrsf11b (coding for OPG) was reduced in the 2-month-old mice but unchanged in the 12-month-olds (Fig. 8E). These suggest that the source of circulating OPG is not from the osteocyte. The decrease in tnfrsf11b in the young mice resulted in an increased RANKL/OPG ratio, which favors osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 8E) suggesting it is the osteocyte that is regulating osteoclast formation. Finally, we measured the mRNA expression levels of the Sost gene that codes for sclerostin and found significant reductions only in the young mice bearing both MLM3 and MLM5 cells (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

Cancer cachexia is a complex metabolic syndrome that severely compromises the clinical outcome of cancer patients28. The multisystemic nature of this condition is becoming clear; besides skeletal muscle, several other organs and tissues such as adipose, brain, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and bone are affected and are directly involved in the pathogenesis of cancer cachexia29. Ours and the work of others has identified bone loss as an important feature of cancer cachexia14,15,30,31. Increased osteoclastic bone resorption and reduced osteoblastic bone formation with consequent bone loss has been described in cachectic mice bearing lung, colorectal and ovarian cancer14. In the same models, we also showed significant osteocyte cell death along with greater numbers of empty osteocyte lacunae that together contributed to weaker bone14. Moreover, we have also shown that reducing bone loss by means of bisphosphonates can improve the cachexia phenotype in cachectic mice bearing ovarian cancer12. We have also shown that bone-derived factors can directly and synergistically perpetuate muscle deterioration associated with chemotherapy32. This growing body of evidence highlights the importance of studying bone deterioration associated with cancer cachexia and its involvement in cancer pathogenesis. Here we investigated whether two HNC tumor types, previously described to induce cachexia, can also affect bone health.

The majority of studies investigating cancer cachexia use actively growing mice as a model33,34, and only a few have utilized fully-developed adult mice as a preclinical model. Evans et al. defined cachexia as primarily weight loss in adults which is distinctly different from stunting growth in children35 and implies different pathogenic mechanisms in the growing compared to the mature individual. For this reason, we compared the musculoskeletal deteriorations associated with cachexia between the most commonly used model, two-month-old actively growing mice, with the rarely used twelve-month-old fully developed mice, both bearing cachexia-inducing HNC.

HNC in 2-month-old mice induced a delay in musculoskeletal growth, as indicated by the shorter tibia length. This is consistent with a previous study showing that tumor secreted factors can perturbate bone growth36. Similarly, it is known that several chemotherapy agents alter the growth plate thereby impairing skeletal maturation37. However, this study is the first to report non-bone metastatic tumor severely reducing the growth plate size and chondrocyte organization. Similarly, the differences in muscle mass between young and adult tumor-bearing mice could be due to stunted growth rather than skeletal wasting. We observed no changes in the levels of ubiquitin-labeled proteins in the old mice in respect to the controls, which contrasts with previous observations showing that ubiquitin expression increases with aging in muscle38. In contrast, the reduced ubiquitination observed in the young mice may reflect a compensatory response to impaired protein anabolism, which could represent the main driver of muscle atrophy in this group. This observation contrasts with previous data showing unchanged or increased levels of protein ubiquitination in other cachexia models using young mice39,40, this finding suggests that the molecular signature may be tumor specific.

Our data show that both MLM3 and MLM5 HNC cells grow slower in adults compared to young mice, consistent with previous studies using different preclinical cancer models33,34,41–43. Even though the 2-month-old mice had larger tumors, the 12-month-old mice bearing the MLM3 HNC developed musculoskeletal deterioration. The adult mice bearing the MLM3 clone, even though carrying smaller tumors, experienced a comparable degree of musculoskeletal deterioration to the young. These discrepancies could be due to different age-dependent tumor microenvironments. The tumor microenvironment is composed of different supporting tissues and several cellular entities including immune cells, stromal cells, and blood vessels which promote tumor growth, progression and invasion44. Using a mouse HNC model, Bojovic et al. demonstrated that tumors in older mice were less vascularized and had less cervical metastatic potential43. In the clinical setting, older patients overall have less aggressive HNC compared to early-onset HNC (< 50 years), indicating a distinct tumor microenvironment in the different age groups45.

Studies using colorectal cancer models have reported contradictory results regarding the severity of cancer cachexia between young and aged mice. Geppert et al. showed that aged mice develop a higher degree of cachexia severity33, while Talbert et al. found no differences34. This inconsistency may be explained not only by the different malignant tumor cell lines with unique tumor growth kinetics, but also by the use of older, 2 or 5 months to more aged mice of 15 to 20 months33. Our data demonstrates a similar impact of HNC type in young versus adult mice. White adipose tissue (WAT) seems to be a specific target only for young and not adult HNC mice. This youth-specific effect could be due to the greater metabolic rate and energy expenditure of young growing mice, which is only exacerbated by the metabolic demands of the malignancy. Moreover, WAT loses its plasticity during aging which contributes to WAT disjunction and hypertrophy46. In our study, young mice developed larger spleen sizes compared to old mice, consistent with other studies suggesting greater inflammation33. A stronger inflammatory and immune response could be responsible for more severe WAT wasting in young mice.

Bone loss can occur in cancer patients even in the absence of direct metastasis to bone. Unfortunately, only limited clinical data are available, as bone assessments are performed only in the circumstance of bone metastasis. However, it has been reported that HNC patients without bone metastases experience a generalized reduction in bone mass20. Osteopenia, defined as low bone mineral density (BMD), was also observed in localized esophageal cancer patients47. Interestingly, patients with both osteopenia and cachexia showed worse survival compared to those affected by either condition alone47. Congruent with this, the more cachexiogenic MLM3 clone induced severe alterations in bone microarchitecture and strength in both age groups while the MLM5 tumor only affected the bone of young mice. It is important to notice that in young mice bearing the MLM5, reduced BMD and reduced biomechanical properties were observed without a significant change in bone mineral content (BMC) nor bone microarchitecture. This shows that bone can be mechanically compromised even in the absence of structural changes.

The balance between osteoclast resorption and osteoblast bone formation maintains bone homeostasis48. We have previously shown that osteoclast number increases in different models of cancer cachexia14. Consistent with the altered bone microarchitecture, the young MLM3 bearers displayed increased numbers of TRAP+ osteoclasts. The reduced BMD and unchanged number of osteoclasts in the young MLM5 tumor bearing mice could be explained by alternative osteolytic mechanisms such as osteocytic osteolysis49. However, deeper analyses are needed to clarify if this mechanism occurs in this specific model. In older mice, the insignificant increase of osteoclast number in the trabecular bone combined with increased CTX-I levels, indicates a dissociation between osteoclast number and activity. This could be explained by timing, where osteoclasts were active earlier releasing CTX-I, by hyperactive osteoclasts that release more CTX-I, or by reduced number of osteoclasts in trabecular compared to cortical bone which is associated with aging50. In this context it has been shown that an increased number of TRAP+ osteoclasts does not always reflect increased activity51. As shown by the elevated circulating levels of the bone turnover markers CTX-I and P1NP52, bone remodeling is increased in the young tumor bearing mice. This could indicate that the elevated osteoclasts release factors which accelerate osteoclast to osteoblast coupling53. This is supported by the unchanged number of osteoblasts in the cancellous bone of the young MLM3 bearers. In the 12-month-old mice, the reduced levels of P1NP, together with the lower number of osteoblasts detected in the cancellous bone and the similar CTX-I levels observed in young mice, may indicate a disruption of osteoclast–osteoblast coupling. This is suggested from data showing that with aging, the capacity of the osteoclast to stimulate osteoblast activation is reduced50. In young mice, altered bone formation and bone resorption contribute to bone loss, whereas in adult mice, bone resorption appears to be the predominant factor.

RANKL is essential for osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption54. However, in our model, increased bone resorption did not correlate with the unchanged or even reduced levels of circulating RANKL nor with the increased circulating levels of OPG. Proinflammatory and procachectic cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, can stimulate osteoclast precursors and induce osteoclastogenesis55,56. The elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels observed in this study can partially explain the increased bone resorption in the HNC bearers. Less clear is the role of elevated OPG in association with bone loss in this model. Clinically, it has been reported that HNC patients suffering low BMD have elevated levels of circulating OPG20. However, the cause and implication of this observation are unknown but suggests that treatments such as denosumab may not be effective in this cancer model. Treatments with OPG-Fc, a decoy RANKL receptor, may not be effective inhibitors of bone resorption in HNC patients.

We next examined gene expression in osteocytes, the master regulator of osteoclasts and osteoblasts, as we have shown previously that RANKL, IL-6, and TNFa are elevated while OPG is decreased while the gene for sclerotin, Sost, is variable in osteocytes from different cancer tumor bearing mice14. No significant changes were observed with RANKL as coded by Tnfrsf11 in osteocytes from both young and adult mice; however, OPG coded by Tnfrsf11b was significantly reduced in the young but not adult osteocytes, resulting in an elevated RANK/OPG ratio in the young but not adult mice. It is possible that the HNC cells or another tissue are the source of OPG especially in the adult mice. This consideration is supported by studies showing that OPG/Tnfrsf11b is expressed by several types of cancer57. Also, Sost was decreased in the young but not the adult osteocytes from both HNC tumor models, whereas no differences were observed in circulating sclerostin except in the adult MLM-5 animals. This shows that while circulating cytokines can be an indicator of bone cell activity, the local actions of cytokines in the bone microenvironment are the regulators and are responsible for bone loss.

One limitation of this investigation and what cannot be specifically parsed out is the musculoskeletal deterioration because of simple malnutrition from nutrient deprivation versus directly from pro-cachectic, inflammatory tumor cytokines. Though hypothalamic dietary dysregulation is a known result of cachectic tumor cytokines58, the exact proportion of muscular and skeletal derangements reported in this study from the separate processes cannot be determined. Even if dietary intake is controlled, this does not mimic clinical cancer cachexia and masks the important appetite suppression the tumor induces. Furthermore, nutritional interventions have failed to result in any regression of cancer cachexia, and thus pair-matching the diet should be inconsequential in that regard. Finally, the cytokine profile and immune dysregulation differ between simple malnutrition and cancer cachexia, with the latter involving much higher levels of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, both of which were measured in this investigation and suggest the results are primarily due to cachexia.

Conclusions

Our study shows that the MLM3 and MLM5 HNC cells have severe detrimental effects on the entire musculoskeletal system. As previously described, MLM3 cells induce a more severe degree of skeletal muscle atrophy in the young than adult mice. In contrast, in adult mice, both clones have a comparable effect on the skeletal muscle. Only the MLM3 cells induced deterioration of both trabecular and cortical bone microarchitecture in both ages. These structural alterations are consistent with the reduced mechanical properties of the bone. The reduced bone strength observed in the young mice bearing the MLM5 cells suggests that changes in bone composition preceded microarchitectural damage. Furthermore, we show for the first time that these skeleton deteriorations could have age-dependent mechanisms in adult versus young mice. The reduced tibia length, altered growth plate size, accentuated bone remodeling, and chondrocyte disorganization in young mice clearly indicate that HNC induces stunted growth. This is unlike adult mice which clearly experience an actual loss of bone.

A more accurate selection of age for preclinical animal experiments studying cancer-induced musculoskeletal deterioration should be considered. This could prevent possible misinterpretation of the causes of musculoskeletal deterioration.

Methods

Cell culture

MLM3 and MLM5 HPV + squamous cell carcinomas were provided by Daniel L. Marks (Oregon, United States) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% minimum essential medium non-essential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 50 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained in a 5% CO2, 37 °C humidified incubator23.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were performed according to procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Indiana University School of Medicine and therefore performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations for animal research laid down in the 2010 Declaration of Basel and its later amendments. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All animals were maintained on a regular dark-light cycle (light from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.) with free access to food and water during the whole experimental period. For the experiments, 2-month-old male C57BL/6 (24,64 ± 1.5 g, Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and 12-month-old male C57BL/6 mice (36,64 ± 2.29 g) obtained from the NIH/NIA aging colony via Charles Rivers Laboratories were used. Both ages of mice were randomized in three groups: (1) control group injected with sterile saline (n = 7y & 6o), (2) MLM5 tumor-bearing mice and (n = 9y & 7o), (3) MLM3 tumor-bearing mice (n = 9y & 7o) inoculated subcutaneously (s.c.) and intrascapularly with 3 × 106 MLM5 or MLM3 cells in sterile saline. Twenty-six days following tumor injection, the mice were euthanized under light anesthesia (5% isoflurane in oxygen). The mice were monitored for the entire duration of the experiments. Several tissues were collected, weighed, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for further analyses.

Body composition assessment

Lean mass, fat mass, whole-body bone mineral density (BMD), and bone mineral content (BMC) were assessed by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scanning. Mice were scanned the day before euthanasia. According to the manufacturer’s guidelines, to calibrate and validate the apparatus for performance, a spine phantom was scanned using the Lunar PIXImus densitometer (PIXImus, Fitchburg, WI) before scanning the first mouse. Mice were placed in a prone position with the limbs outstretched and kept under anesthesia using a nose cone and isoflurane. From the whole-body scans, areal BMD, BMC, fat mass, and lean mass were calculated for the hindlimbs region using the Lunar ROI tools.

Assessment of the minimum feret diameter

To assess skeletal muscle atrophy, 10-µm-thick cryosections of tibialis anterior muscles taken at the mid-belly were processed for immunostaining as described previously40. Briefly, sections were blocked for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a dystrophin primary antibody [1:50, MANDRA1(7A10), Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, Iowa, USA], followed by a 1 h secondary antibody (AlexaFluor 488, 1:1000) incubation at room temperature. Entire dystrophin-stained sections were analyzed for CSA using a Lionheart LX automated microscope (BioTek Instruments).

Muscle contractility

On the day before sacrifice, all animals underwent in vivo plantarflexion torque assessment (Aurora Scientific Inc, Canada) to assess the hindlimb muscle torque15,59. Briefly, the right hind foot was taped to the force transducer and positioned to where the foot and tibia were aligned at 90°. The knee was then clamped at the femoral condyles, avoiding compression of the fibular nerve. Two disposable monopolar electrodes (Natus Neurology, Middleton, WI) were placed subcutaneously, posteromedial to the knee to stimulate the tibial nerve. Maximum twitch torque was first determined using supramaximal stimulations (0.2 ms square wave pulse). Peak plantarflexion torque was then assessed following a supramaximal square wave stimulation (0.2 ms) delivered at a frequency of 125 Hz.

Microcomputed tomography

Microcomputed tomography (µCT) scanning was performed to measure morphological indices of metaphyseal regions of tibiae as described previously60. After euthanasia, the left tibiae were dissected, cleaned from the soft tissue and fixed for 2 days in 10% neutral buffered formalin, transferred into 70% ethanol, and prepared for µCT scanning on a high throughput µCT specimen scanner. Bone samples were rotated around their long axes, and images were acquired using a Bruker Skyscan 1176 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) with the following parameters: pixel size = 9 µm3; peak tube potential = 50 kV; X-ray intensity = 500 µA; 0.5° rotation step. Raw images were reconstructed using SkyScan reconstruction software (NRecon; Bruker) to three‐dimensional cross‐sectional image data sets using a three‐dimensional cone beam algorithm. Structural indices were calculated on reconstructed images using the SkyScan CT Analyser software (CTAn; Bruker). Cortical and trabecular bone were separated using a custom processing algorithm in CTAn based on the different thicknesses of the structures. Cortical bone was analyzed by threshold 160–255 in the tibiae mid‐shaft. Cortical bone parameters included bone volume fraction (Ct.BV/TV), thickness (Ct.Th), periosteal perimeter, endosteal perimeter and marrow cavity area. Trabecular bone was analyzed between 1.0 and 2.0 mm under the tibial proximal growth plate using a threshold of 80–255. Trabecular parameters included bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), number (Tb.N), thickness (Tb.Th), and separation (Tb.Sp).

Biomechanical testing

Following euthanasia, the left femora were dissected, cleaned from the soft tissue, and three-point bending tests to failure were performed as previously described61. Briefly, the samples were placed on a custom-made three-point bending jig consisting of a bottom holder with two brass indenters that are 16 mm apart. The top indenter was connected to the 15-kN sensor of the Instron load frame (8511 biaxial model, High Wycombe, U.K) operated by MTS Flex Test 40 Controller (MTS TestSuite Multipurpose Elite Software, MTS, Eden Prairie, USA). An automated algorithm was defined on the controller program, which allowed to load the samples at a rate of 1 mm/s until fracture.

Bone histomorphometry

Tibiae were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin using previously established, standard histology method at the Indiana Center for Musculoskeletal Health, ICMH, Histology and Histomorphometry Core. Osteoclasts and osteoblasts in the cancellous bone of the tibiae were quantified in TRAP/Toluidine blue-stained or H&E-stained transverse tibiae sections, respectively. The proximal growth plate thickness was measured in paraffin embedded transverse tibiae sections stained with H&E. Histomorphometric analysis was performed using OsteoMeasure high resolution digital video system (OsteoMetrics Inc., Decatur, GA, USA). The terminology and units used are those recommended by the ASBMR Histomorphometry Nomenclature Committee26.

Quantification of circulating markers of bone and muscle health

The circulating levels of CTX-I (carboxy-terminal cross-linked telopeptide of type 1 collagen; #D6050; Bio-Techne Corporation, Minneapolis, MN, USA), P1NP (procollagen type I intact N-terminal propeptide; #ABIN1884501; Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor κB; #MTR00), OPG (osteoprotegerin; #MOP00), Sclerostin (#MSST00), IL-6 (interleukin 6; #M6000B) and TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor α; #MTA00B; all from Bio-Techne Corporation, Minneapolis, MN) were measured in mouse poor‐platelet plasma and ascites from control and HNC bearing mice by using a specific enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Reverse transcription and e quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Long bones were dissected, the epiphyses were removed, and the marrow cavity was flushed to remove bone marrow. Bones and quadriceps muscle were homogenized in TRIzol Reagent. RNA was isolated using the miRNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. RNA was quantified by using a Synergy H1 spectrophotometer (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA by using the Verso cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Transcript levels were measured by Real-Time PCR (Azure CieloqPCR system, Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA). Expression levels for Tnfsf11, Tnfrsf11b, Dmp1 and Sost were quantified in the flushed bone, whereas expression of Fbxo32 and Trim63 was quantified in whole muscle extracts. Gene expression was normalized to Actin levels using the standard 2−ΔΔCT methods.

Western blotting

Total protein extracts were obtained by homogenizing whole tibialis anterior muscle tissue in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer [150 mM NaCl, 1.0% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0] completed with protease (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, United States) and phosphatase (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, United States) inhibitor cocktails. Cell debris were removed by centrifugation (15 min, 14,000 g), and the supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay method (Thermo Scientific). Protein extracts (30 µg) were then electrophoresed in 4–15% gradient SDS Criterion TGX precast gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, United States). Gels were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with SEA BLOCK blocking reagent (Thermo Scientific) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by an overnight incubation with diluted antibody in SEA BLOCK buffer containing 0.2% Tween-20 at 4 °C with gentle shaking. After washing with PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 1 h with a secondary anti-rabbit IgG. Primary antibody used was ubiquitin (3933; Cell Signaling Technology).

Statistical analyses

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was performed to determine differences between age matched experimental MLM5 and MLM3 groups. Separate two‐way ANOVA tests were performed within MLM5 or MLM3 groups to determine age‐dependent effects. The nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used for data not following a normal distribution. Post‐hoc comparisons were accomplished via a Tukey’s test with statistical significance set a priori at P < 0.05. All statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.1 and data are presented as means ± SD.
