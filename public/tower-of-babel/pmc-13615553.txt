The Role of S100A8/A9 in Autoimmune Eye Diseases: From Pathogenesis to Targeted Therapy
Authors: Tingyu Zhang, Jiangqi Hu, Mei Sun, Xinyu Zhang, Hui Xu, Bei Du, Yankai Wei, Ruihua Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The S100 proteins are a group of calcium-binding proteins that are widely expressed in vertebrates and participate in various biological and pathological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, inflammation, infection, and autoimmunity. S100A8 and S100A9, members of the S100 family that predominantly exist as heterodimers (S100A8/A9), are primarily localized in myeloid cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and dendritic cells (DCs). Accumulating studies have identified S100A8/A9 as critical damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or alarmins. Upon engagement with their receptors, S100A8/A9 can drive the production of cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion factors, thereby initiating and amplifying immune responses. Current literature reports that S100A8/A9 is upregulated in various autoimmune diseases, and targeted inhibition of S100A8/A9 holds significant therapeutic potential for these diseases. Recently, emerging studies have demonstrated a close association between S100A8/A9 and the pathogenesis of autoimmune eye diseases. Elucidating the signaling pathways mediated by S100A8/A9 is critical for the prevention and treatment of these ocular disorders. Therefore, we here summarize the biological characteristics of S100A8/A9 and their roles in the onset and progression of three autoimmune eye diseases, including autoimmune uveitis, Sjögren's dry eye disease (SjDED), and thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO), with particular emphasis on their utility as diagnostic biomarkers. Furthermore, we discuss current therapeutic strategies targeting S100A8/A9 and propose promising future research directions for S100A8/A9 interventions in autoimmune eye diseases.

S100A8 and S100A9 are members of the S100 calcium-binding protein family and predominantly exist as a noncovalently bound heterodimer (S100A8/A9) because their monomers are relatively unstable. Under cellular stress or tissue injury, S100A8/A9 is released as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or alarmins into the extracellular space, where it is able to initiate and amplify immune and inflammatory responses.1 S100A8/A9 has been demonstrated to be upregulated in a wide range of autoimmune diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease.2–4 By shaping a pro-inflammatory tissue microenvironment, S100A8/A9 is able to promote the maturation and activation of antigen-presenting cells and thereby facilitate the differentiation of CD4+T cells into Th17 cells.5,6 Given the critical role of S100A8/A9 in orchestrating T cell-mediated autoimmune responses, it has emerged as an attractive therapeutic target, and strategies that inhibit S100A8/A9 are increasingly being explored for the treatment of autoimmune disorders.

The eye, an immune-privileged organ, uses many negative regulators to maintain an immunosuppressive microenvironment that suppresses inflammation and enhances immune tolerance.7 Disruption of this finely tuned immune privilege, driven by aberrant activation of effector immune cells (i.e. effector T cells) and excessive release of pro-inflammatory mediators, leads to the breakdown of ocular immune homeostasis and initiates autoimmunity.8 Accumulating evidence suggests that S100A8/A9 plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune eye diseases. For instance, in autoimmune uveitis and Sjögren's dry eye disease (SjDED), S100A8/A9 binds to the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) to activate nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, resulting in the elevated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.9 These increased pro-inflammatory mediators facilitate the migration of neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages to inflammatory foci in the iris, ciliary body, retina, and lacrimal glands (LGs), thereby exacerbating local inflammation and contributing to disease pathogenesis.10,11 In this review, we delineate the molecular characteristics of S100A8/A9 and dissect its pathogenic roles within three autoimmune eye diseases: autoimmune uveitis, SjDED, and thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy. In addition, we discuss the potential of S100A8/A9 as a diagnostic and therapeutic strategy for the effective management of autoimmune eye diseases.

Biological Properties of S100A8/A9

Molecular Characteristics and Subcellular Localization of S100A8/A9

S100A8/A9, also known as calprotectin, is a heterodimeric protein composed of S100A8 and S100A9 subunits, belonging to the S100 calcium-binding protein family12 (Fig. 1). The genes encoding S100A8 and S100A9 are located on human chromosome 1q21.3, a region that contains a cluster of S100 family genes.13 S100A8 comprises 93 amino acids and has a molecular weight of approximately 10.8 kilodalton (kDa), whereas S100A9 consists of 113 amino acids, including a unique C-terminal extension, and has a molecular weight of approximately 13.2 kDa.14,15 These 2 subunits associate via non-covalent interactions to form the S100A8/A9 heterodimer, which has a molecular weight of approximately 24 kDa.16 Structurally, S100A8/A9 contains 2 canonical EF-hand calcium-binding domains, located at the N-terminus (low-affinity site) and C-terminus (high-affinity site),17,18 which are linked by a hinge region.19 S100A8/A9 is abundantly expressed in neutrophils, accounting for approximately 45% of cytosolic proteins.20 It is also expressed in monocytes and upregulated in macrophages upon inflammatory activation.20 Intracellularly, S100A8/A9 functions as a Ca2+-responsive regulator of cytoskeletal remodeling and arachidonic acid metabolism.15,20,21 Elevated intracellular Ca2+ binds to the EF-hand motifs of S100A8/A9, inducing conformational rearrangements and promoting the formation of functional S100A8/A9 tetramers. These structural changes expose hydrophobic surfaces that facilitate interactions with target proteins, including cytoskeletal proteins such as tubulin/microtubules,22 thereby contributing to intracellular signaling and inflammatory cell activation. Upon cellular activation, S100A8/A9 can be released into the extracellular milieu,23 where it acts as a DAMP/alarmin and activates inflammatory signaling through pattern-recognition receptors such as TLR4 and RAGE.

Structural schematic of the S100A8/A9 heterodimer. The S100A8/A9 heterodimer is formed by the non-covalent association of S100A8 and S100A9 subunits, each containing two EF-hand calcium-binding domains linked by a hinge region. Calcium binding is able to enhance the stability of the heterodimer.

Extracellular and Intracellular Functions of S100A8/A9

As a DAMP and alarmin, extracellular S100A8/A9 is critical in regulating both innate and adaptive immune responses.24,25 Upon cellular injury, S100A8/A9 is actively secreted or passively released into the extracellular space, thereby contributing to the initiation and propagation of inflammatory responses. During the early phase of inflammation, S100A8/A9 binds to pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4 and RAGE on the surface of target cells,26–28 thereby activating downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways.2 This activation triggers innate immune responses through the release of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β and TNF-α) and chemokines (e.g., CXCL1 and CXCL2).20,29–31 Orchestrated by these inflammatory signals, S100A8/A9 stimulates the activation, adhesion, and chemotaxis of neutrophil and monocyte by inducing Mac-1 activation and L-selectin shedding, driving their targeted migration to inflammatory sites.32 Besides, S100A8 and S100A9 can induce the expression of various matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP-2, MMP-3, and MMP-9.33,34 It has been reported that MMP-3 deficiency or inhibition could reduce retinal leukocyte adhesion in a mouse model of endotoxin-induced uveitis.35 MMP-2 or MMP-9 was able to reduce monolayer electrical resistance and degrades occludin in retinal endothelial and retinal pigment epithelial cells, thereby compromising barrier integrity,36 suggesting that S100A8/A9-induced MMP expression may facilitate leukocyte recruitment and blood-retinal barrier breakdown.33,35,36 Furthermore, S100A8/A9 amplifies local inflammation and tissue damage by activating M1 macrophages to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.4 Crucially, S100A8/A9 bridges innate and adaptive immunity by activating antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs). By upregulating major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) and co-stimulatory molecules, S100A8/A9-stimulated APCs enhance antigen presentation and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that skew naïve CD4+T cell differentiation toward Th1 and Th17 lineages. IFN-γ and IL-17 produced by these effector T cells further exacerbate the immune response.37 Concurrently, this pro-inflammatory milieu suppresses the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and impairs their function by reducing the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which contributes to the breakdown of immune tolerance and culminates in chronic inflammation and tissue injury.38 However, S100A8/A9 can also exert context-dependent immunoregulatory effects. In a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model, endogenous S100A8/A9 promoted the accumulation and suppressive function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in joint-draining lymph nodes, thereby favoring Treg differentiation and limiting Th17 cell responses.39 Consequently, S100A8/A9 deficiency exacerbated CIA by reducing MDSC abundance in these lymph nodes.39

Recent studies have revealed that intracellular S100A8/A9 also plays crucial roles in regulating immune and inflammatory responses. In the cytoplasm, S100A8/A9 interacts with tubulin in a calcium-dependent manner to stabilize microtubules and promote actin reorganization,40 thereby regulating phagocyte trafficking and cell polarity. Recently, S100A8/A9 has been reported to promote NADPH oxidase (NOX2) activation in phagocytes such as neutrophils.13,41 Given the important role of NOX2 in the development of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis by affecting immune cell infiltration and oxidative stress, we speculate that intracellular S100A8/A9 may be implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases by regulating NOX2.42 Within the nucleus, S100A8/A9 could directly bind to the promoter region of the C3 gene, thereby enhancing C3 transcription and promoting complement activation, which drives immune cell recruitment and ultimately causes cutaneous inflammatory injury in psoriasis, an autoimmune skin disease.43

In summary, S100A8/A9 serves as a crucial immunoregulator within both extracellular and intracellular microenvironments. Elucidating its precise biological roles is therefore essential for deciphering the pathogenesis of autoimmune eye diseases, as well as for developing novel diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutic strategies.

The Role of S100A8/A9 in Autoimmune Eye Diseases

S100A8/A9 and Autoimmune Uveitis

Autoimmune uveitis represents a group of non-infectious intraocular inflammatory diseases characterized by autoreactive T cell-mediated damage to uveal and retinal tissues. Its diverse clinical manifestations and incompletely elucidated etiology pose substantial challenges for diagnosis and treatment, often resulting in irreversible photoreceptor injury and visual impairment or blindness.5,30,44 Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) recapitulates the pathogenesis of human uveitis, serving as an invaluable model for elucidating the pathological mechanisms of autoimmune uveitis and exploring novel therapeutic approaches.44 Researchers have found that the level of S100A8/A9 in the aqueous humor of EAU rats increases with disease progression.5 Consistent with animal model findings, Wang et al. reported markedly elevated plasma S100A8/A9 levels in patients with uveitis (1752 ± 113 ng/mL) compared to non-uveitis controls (517 ± 46 ng/mL), with significantly higher levels observed in active acute anterior uveitis than in inactive disease.30 Notably, plasma S100A8/A9 levels were further increased in patients with acute anterior uveitis with ankylosing spondylitis (an autoimmune disorder) relative to those without ankylosing spondylitis.30 Furthermore, Walscheid et al. found that aqueous humor S100A8/A9 levels were elevated in patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis-associated uveitis and idiopathic anterior uveitis (50.8 ± 91.9 and 134 ± 177.8 ng/mL, respectively) compared with non-uveitic controls (5.1 ± 0.5 ng/mL), whereas serum levels were higher in patients with active than inactive uveitis (1467.9 vs. 977.2 ng/mL), suggesting that S100A8/A9 may serve as a biomarker of intraocular inflammatory activity.45,46 In patients with HLA‑B27‑positive acute anterior uveitis without associated systemic diseases, elevated serum S100A8/A9 levels persist even during clinically inactive uveitis.47 Collectively, the above studies demonstrate that S100A8/A9 levels in body fluids are closely associated with the onset and progression of autoimmune uveitis, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for assessing uveitis activity (see the Table).

Paquinimod, a quinoline-3-carboxamide drug with immunomodulatory properties, can inhibit innate and adaptive immune responses by blocking the binding of S100A9, a subunit of calprotectin, to TLR4 or RAGE receptors.48 Paquinimod has exhibited excellent therapeutic efficacy for autoimmune diseases, including systemic sclerosis, liver fibrosis, and psoriasis.2,49 Over the past few years, paquinimod was applied orally to EAU rats, where it reduced vitreous inflammatory cell infiltration and retinal damage by inhibiting antigen-specific Th1 and Th17 cell responses while enhancing Treg function. The authors further demonstrated that paquinimod inhibited T cell activation and attenuated EAU recurrence by downregulating the expression of MHC II, CD80, and CD86 on APCs while upregulating the expression of CD200R20,50 (Fig. 2A), indicating that targeting S100A8/A9 with the small-molecule inhibitor paquinimod possesses a favorable therapeutic efficacy in autoimmune uveitis. However, it should be noted that current evidence is derived predominantly from animal models, and the therapeutic efficacy and safety of paquinimod for human autoimmune uveitis remain to be further verified. To advance its clinical translation, future studies could focus on developing organoids that recapitulate the intraocular immune microenvironment, or on conducting small-scale clinical trials in patients with autoimmune uveitis to evaluate the efficacy and pharmacokinetic profile of paquinimod.51 Furthermore, given the localized nature of autoimmune uveitis lesions and the risk of severe adverse effects, such as immunosuppression and hepatic or renal toxicity, associated with systemic medications, the development of topical drug delivery systems, such as nano-based eye drops, constitutes a promising approach to achieving autoimmune uveitis precision therapy. Such strategies are expected to increase intraocular drug concentrations, enhance therapeutic efficacy, and substantially reduce systemic toxicity, thereby holding great promise for clinical application.

S100A8/A9 and Sjögren's Dry Eye Disease

Sjögren's syndrome (SS) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by lymphocytic infiltration and progressive destruction of exocrine glands, particularly the lacrimal and salivary glands, with severe cases accompanied by multisystem involvement.52 As the principal ocular complication of SS, SjDED originates from immune-mediated LG impairment. The consequent deficiency in tear production causes tear film hyperosmolarity, which in turn precipitates a cascade of ocular surface inflammation and corneal injury.53 Through the analysis of the GSE176510 dataset, our team recently revealed a significant upregulation of S100A8/A9 expression in the conjunctival tissues of patients with Sjögren's disease keratoconjunctivitis sicca.6,54 Furthermore, we demonstrated that the expression of S100A8/A9 was significantly increased in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with SjDED. Notably, this elevated expression displayed a positive correlation with Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores and a negative correlation with tear secretion. Consistent with these clinical observations, the expression of S100A8/A9 in the LGs of SjDED mice increased significantly with disease progression, exhibiting close correlation with the severity of lymphocytic infiltration and corneal epithelial injury. Recently, an untargeted tear proteomic study demonstrated a significant upregulation of S100A8/A9 in patients with SjDED relative to healthy controls.55 Consistently, a targeted LC-MRM proteomic analysis confirmed that S100A8/A9 proteins were significantly increased in patients with SjDED compared with non-Sjögren’s dry eye patients and healthy controls, indicating the utility of S100A8/A9 as a specific biomarker to differentiate SjDED from other dry eye subtypes.56 Collectively, elevated S100A8/A9 levels in both tear fluid and PBMCs emerge as promising biomarkers for SjDED. Future large-scale clinical trials investigating their specificity and sensitivity in diagnosing SjDED will facilitate their translation into clinical practice.

Current clinical interventions for SjDED include artificial tears, corticosteroids, autologous serum, and immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclosporine A), which can relieve symptoms and suppress inflammation, but they are hindered by suboptimal responsiveness and significant adverse effects.57–60 Crucially, these conventional therapies fail to halt the LG lymphocytic infiltration, and thus patients often suffer from progressive tear deficiency and recurrent corneal epithelial damage. This highlights an urgent unmet clinical need for safe, effective, and etiology-targeted therapeutics. To address this, our team explored targeting S100A8/A9 as a novel therapeutic strategy for SjDED, demonstrating that administration of paquinimod could suppress DC-mediated Th17 cell responses to reduce lymphocytic infiltration in the LGs of SjDED mice, thereby increasing tear secretion and attenuating corneal epithelial injury.61 Mechanistic studies elucidated that S100A8/A9 upregulates aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1) expression in DCs to promote the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), which result in an enhancement in STAT3-mediated IL23A transcription and DC-induced Th17 generation6 (Fig. 2B). Therefore, our work demonstrated that targeted inhibition of S100A8/A9 ameliorates corneal injury and restores tear secretion by dampening the infiltration of Th17 cells within the LGs. Beyond this LG-mediated immunomodulatory effect, existing studies provide clues for an additional mechanism whereby S100A8/A9 inhibitors may directly suppress corneal inflammation, cell apoptosis, and barrier disruption. Recently, Liang et al. reported that S100A9 was increased in the corneal epithelium of the lacrimal gland excision (LGE) murine dry eye model, and administration of S100A9 inhibitor alleviated corneal epithelial damage, inflammation, and apoptosis.61 Additionally, studies have demonstrated that S100A8/A9 decreases junctional protein expression and induces apoptosis in human microvascular endothelial cells,9,62 indicating that inhibition of S100A8/A9 may help restore endothelial tight junction integrity and attenuate endothelial cell apoptosis. However, whether such a direct cornea-protective mechanism operates in SjDED requires validation in disease-specific experimental models. Moreover, to optimize the therapeutic efficacy of S100A8/A9 blockade in SjDED, future research should prioritize the development of sustained-release ocular drug delivery systems, such as nanocarriers, to maximize drug concentrations in the LGs while minimizing systemic adverse effects (see the Table).

S100A8/A9 and Thyroid-Associated Ophthalmopathy

Thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) is the major extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease and is characterized by autoimmune inflammation and remodeling of the orbital tissues.63 Autoreactive T cells and autoantibodies against thyrotropin receptor and insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor,64 contribute to the activation and proliferation of orbital fibroblasts.65 Activated fibroblasts in turn produce chemokines and pro-inflammatory factors that drive orbital adipose tissue hyperplasia,66 extraocular muscle enlargement, and the recruitment of immune cells to the orbital tissues,67 leading to chronic inflammation and fibrosis that contribute to the pathogenesis of TAO.64 S100A8/A9 has recently emerged as a pivotal pathogenic mediator in TAO. As reported by Kim and colleagues, the elevation of serum S100A8/A9 levels in patients with TAO is accompanied by markedly upregulated mRNA expression of S100A8 (32-fold) and S100A9 (6.7-fold) within the orbital adipose and connective tissues as compared with healthy controls.68 Exploring the underlying mechanisms, they found that S100A8/A9 activates NF-κB and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling in orbital fibroblasts to induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as IL-6, IL-8, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1. This cascade culminates in aggravated local tissue inflammation, wherein IL-6 promotes the generation of functional autoantibodies by B cells, and IL-8 directs the migration and infiltration of T lymphocytes into the orbit69 (Fig. 2C).

Although conventional treatments for TAO, such as glucocorticoids and radiotherapy, can effectively suppress early-stage inflammation, their clinical utility is frequently limited by significant long-term risks, including hyperglycemia (42%), osteoporosis (28%), and secondary malignancies.69–72 Consequently, the therapeutic landscape for TAO has shifted from nonspecific immunosuppression toward targeted therapies. Notably, the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R) antagonist teprotumumab has demonstrated remarkable efficacy, achieving a proptosis reduction of ≥2 mm in 83% of patients and alleviating inflammation in 59% of patients.73,74 Other biologic agents, including tocilizumab (an anti-IL-6 receptor antibody)75,76 and rituximab (an anti-CD20 antibody),77 have also shown promising therapeutic potential in recent clinical trials. Although serum S100A8/A9 levels have emerged as a promising biomarker for assessing disease activity and treatment response in TAO,68 the targeted inhibition of S100A8/A9 has not yet been investigated as a therapeutic strategy in TAO. Given the proven efficacy of S100A8/A9 small-molecule inhibitors in other autoimmune eye diseases, such as autoimmune uveitis and SjDED, we hypothesize that these inhibitors could similarly benefit TAO by suppressing orbital fibroblast activation and the subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines. To validate this hypothesis, future studies should evaluate the efficacy of S100A8/A9 inhibitors in animal models and patients with TAO, as well as their impact on orbital inflammation and fibrosis. Upon confirmation of therapeutic efficacy, local administration strategies such as intraorbital injection warrant further exploration, and optimizing the dosage, administration frequency, and delivery systems will be essential to ensure maximum efficacy at the target site while minimizing systemic adverse effects. Additionally, investigating the potential synergistic effects of S100A8/A9 inhibitors and existing therapies such as IGF-1R antagonists may offer more treatment options for patients with refractory TAO (see the Table).

Exploration of S100A8/A9-Targeted Therapy in Autoimmune Eye Diseases

As a key inflammatory mediator, S100A8/A9 has shown considerable promise as a therapeutic target in autoimmune eye diseases. Current research in this field has primarily focused on pharmacologically inhibiting S100A8/A9 activity using small-molecule compounds. Paquinimod has been shown to interfere with the interaction between S100A9 and TLR4/RAGE, thereby attenuating S100A8/A9‑mediated inflammatory signaling.78 In addition, evidence from clinical studies indicates that paquinimod demonstrates a favorable profile between safety and efficacy in systemic autoimmune disorders, such as systemic sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus, highlighting its translational potential in autoimmune diseases.49,78 Recent studies suggest that paquinimod may also have therapeutic potential in ocular autoimmunity. In a mouse model of SjDED, intraperitoneal administration of paquinimod significantly reduced corneal epithelial damage and lymphocytic infiltration in the LGs while increasing tear secretion.6 In a rat model of EAU, paquinimod was shown to reduce vitreous inflammatory cell infiltration and retinal injury, thereby slowing disease progression and lowering the likelihood of recurrence.5 Evidence from other ocular inflammatory diseases further supports the therapeutic rationale for targeting S100A8 and S100A9 in autoimmune eye diseases. For example, paquinimod treatment attenuated S100A8-associated neuroinflammation, retinal thinning, inner retinal neuronal loss, and retinal electrophysiological dysfunction exacerbated by microglia-specific Sfrp2 deletion following retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury.79 In an LGE murine dry eye model, paquinimod alleviated corneal epithelial damage, inflammation, and cell death by blocking the S100A9/TLR4 pathway.61 Furthermore, paquinimod may also hold therapeutic potential for TAO, although direct evidence remains limited. The effects may be mediated, at least in part, through suppression of aberrant orbital fibroblast activation and proliferation as well as reduction of Th17 cell infiltration in orbital tissues,80 thereby helping to mitigate orbital edema and eyelid erythema during the acute phase of TAO.

Beyond the use of inhibitors that prevent S100A8/A9-receptor interactions, blockade of its receptor may represent an alternative therapeutic strategy. The RAGE antagonist FPS-ZM1 has been demonstrated to reduce the colitis susceptibility of Dok3-deficient mice by blocking the binding of S100A8/A9 to RAGE in a dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis mouse model.81 In addition, the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 suppressed S100A8-induced TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, neuronal damage, cerebral edema, and neurological deficits in experimental traumatic brain injury.82 Evidence for this strategy has also emerged in ocular disease. For example, TAK-242 has been reported to alleviate clinical signs, corneal inflammation, and cell apoptosis by targeting the S100A9-TLR4 axis in a murine LGE dry eye model.61 Although direct evidence for TLR4 or RAGE inhibition in autoimmune eye diseases remains limited, these findings provide a rationale for investigating receptor-directed interventions in these diseases. Nevertheless, because TLR4 and RAGE recognize multiple ligands and may mediate context-dependent immune responses, the efficacy and safety of such approaches require disease-specific validation.

S100A8/A9 inhibitors and TLR4/RAGE inhibitors exert anti-inflammatory effects primarily by blocking the interaction between S100A8/A9 and its receptors,83–85 rather than by suppressing endogenous S100A8/A9 expression. In autoimmune eye diseases characterized by aberrant S100A8/A9 upregulation, therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing its expression may represent a valuable complement to receptor-blocking approaches. One such strategy is RNA interference (RNAi), which uses synthetic small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or vector-expressed short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) to degrade target mRNA and reduce protein expression.86–88 Although direct evidence about RNAi-mediated knockdown of S100A8/A9 in autoimmune eye diseases remains limited, this approach has demonstrated efficacy in other inflammatory conditions. For instance, knockdown of S100A8/A9 mediated by lentivirus-carrying shRNA alleviated renal interstitial fibrosis in a mouse model of diabetic nephropathy.89 Although this strategy holds promise for treating autoimmune eye diseases, challenges such as ocular delivery efficiency and biosafety remain to be addressed. Recently, He et al. developed an engineered macrophage membrane‑coated nanoparticles co-loaded with S100A9-targeting siRNA to reduce the area of myocardial fibrosis and the mortality of mice through the targeted delivery of S100A9 siRNA to the injured myocardial tissue, which provides an important reference for developing ocular delivery systems for S100A8- or S100A9‑targeting siRNA.90 Another promising strategy is the application of proteolysis‑targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology, which offers a powerful tool for selectively reducing pathogenic protein levels. PROTACs are heterobifunctional molecules composed of one ligand that binds the protein of interest and another ligand that binds an E3 ubiquitin ligase, such as von Hippel-Lindau or cereblon. By bringing these two components into proximity, PROTACs promote ubiquitination and degradation of the target protein.91 Although the targeted degradation of S100A8/A9 via PROTAC technology has not yet been reported, this therapeutic strategy has demonstrated considerable potential in a number of autoimmune and ocular diseases.92–96 For instance, Zhang et al. designed a PROTAC molecule to degrade aberrantly elevated leucine‑rich alpha‑2 glycoprotein 1 in the cornea of alkali burn-injured mice, resulting in a marked reduction in corneal neovascularization and lymphangiogenesis.96 More recently, Ismail et al. reported that the PROTAC molecule RGC-01-05-18 selectively targets S100A4, a member of the S100 protein family, for degradation, thereby reducing metastatic risk in triple‑negative breast cancer.97 Collectively, targeted degradation of S100A8/A9 using PROTAC technology may offer a novel therapeutic avenue for the treatment of autoimmune eye diseases in the future.

Summary and Prospect

S100A8/A9 plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis, SjDED, and TAO by promoting immune cell activation and amplifying inflammation. The aberrant expression of S100A8/A9 highlights its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for these autoimmune eye diseases. Therapeutic strategies targeting S100A8/A9 have achieved notable advances in the field of ocular autoimmunity. In particular, small-molecule inhibitors, such as paquinimod, have demonstrated great efficacy in animal models of autoimmune uveitis and SjDED. In the future, researchers should evaluate the therapeutic potential of RNAi and PROTAC technologies in targeting S100A8/A9 for autoimmune eye diseases. By integrating these technologies with nanotechnology, it is promising to develop more effective and safer therapeutic approaches. Furthermore, the pathogenic roles and underlying mechanisms of S100A8/A9 in other autoimmune eye diseases remain to be elucidated.
