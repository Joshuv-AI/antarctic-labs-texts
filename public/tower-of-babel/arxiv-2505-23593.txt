Position: Federated Foundation Language Model Post-Training Should Focus on Open-Source Models
Nikita Agrawal, Simon Mertel, Ruben Mayer

Abstract
Post-training of foundation language models has emerged as a promising research domain in federated learning (FL) with the goal to enable privacy-preserving model improvements and adaptations to users’ downstream tasks. Recent advances in this area adopt centralized post-training approaches that build upon black-box foundation language models where there is no access to model weights and architecture details. Although the use of black-box models has been successful in centralized post-training, their blind replication in FL raises several concerns. Our position is that using black-box models in FL contradicts the core principles of federation such as data privacy and autonomy. In this position paper, we critically analyze the usage of black-box models in federated post-training, and provide a detailed account of various aspects of openness and their implications for FL.



1
Introduction

The post-training of foundation language models has become increasingly important as they need to be adapted to specific downstream applications to ensure their effective use
(
Tie et al. 2025
)
. Traditional centralized machine learning approaches do not work well in privacy-sensitive settings, where data cannot be collected centrally and should remain private. Federated learning (FL) offers a promising decentralized paradigm for training machine learning models on private data across multiple clients without directly exposing the private data and only exchanging gradients
(
McMahan et al. 2023
)
. Thus, federated post-training emerged, which involves adapting foundation language models within the FL framework for downstream tasks.

Recent post-training approaches in centralized environments frequently leverage black-box models
(
Yuksekgonul et al. 2024
;
Khattab et al. 2023
)
, where interaction is limited to an API and internal parameters such as model weights and architecture are inaccessible. Researchers are now adapting this approach of using black-box models for federated post-training to leverage local data
(
Chen et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
. However, this adaption requires a critical reflection on whether black-box post-training approaches are suitable for privacy-preserving use cases of FL.

Our position is that research on federated foundation language model post-training should focus on open-source models.
Using black-box models for post-training in FL makes clients rely on third-party services, introducing significant drawbacks such as potential data leakage, reduced transparency, and diminishing client autonomy that undermine the advantages of using FL.
This is critical, as it contradicts the fundamental goals and principles of FL.
Moreover, black-box models can only be accessed via a provided API – mostly via text in natural language, making them difficult to optimize as required.
In contrast, open-source models provide access to model weights and architecture’s source code and documentation. These models offer transparency, control, and alignment with the privacy-preserving principles of FL. They enable the use of robust privacy mechanisms, effective optimization techniques, and promote ethical AI development. This enhances the autonomy and sovereignty of clients over their data.

Our position is in line with similar statements from various groups of academia and industry that argue in favor of using open-source models
in general

(
Manchanda et al. 2025
;
Floridi et al. 2025
)
. The particular focus of this position paper is on the combination of FL with black-box models, which is a new research trend that we think should be more critically assessed.
As there are different definitions of open-source foundation models, and there is no broad consensus on all the details of an open-source definition yet, we analyze this question in detail. In particular, we investigate various aspects of openness in light of their alignment with the goals of FL, such as privacy and autonomy. Our findings suggest that the most critical aspects are the openness of model weights and code, followed by restrictive licenses, while the openness of pre-training data is a less critical concern for federated post-training.

2
Federated Learning for Foundation Language Model Post-Training

FL involves training a shared machine learning model across multiple distributed clients. Each client owns local training data that it does not share during training. In each training round, a server shares the global model with selected clients that then train it locally on their own data. After training, they send their model updates to the server, which combines them according to an aggregation strategy, e.g., Federated Averaging (FedAvg)
(
McMahan et al. 2023
)
, to improve the global model. Training is complete after multiple rounds when the model reaches an acceptable performance level.

The implementation of FL in foundation language models is a significant problem given the large-scale and computational requirements of these models
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
. Post-training of foundation models on distributed datasets without collecting data centrally is essential in numerous real-world scenarios, particularly when handling sensitive information or complying with stringent data protection and governance regulations such as the GDPR
(
European Union 2016
)
or the EU AI act
(
European Union 2024
)
. To address the issues presented by the scale of foundation language models in FL, post-training techniques such as parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) have become increasingly significant
(
Woisetschläger et al. 2024b
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
. These strategies seek to reduce computational and communication overhead by training only a small subset of model parameters, while the majority of them remain frozen
(
Wu et al. 2024b
)
. Some popular PEFT strategies are Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), adapter tuning, and prompt tuning
(
Lialin et al. 2023
)
.
In addition to PEFT, personalized FL (PFL) represents a vital research domain with respect to foundation models
(
Lu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024a
)
. Recognizing the substantial variability in data distributions among diverse clients within a federated system (non-IID data), PFL aims to create models customized to the distinct requirements and data characteristics of individual clients. This typically entails a combination of global learning to learn fundamental knowledge from the entire federation and local adaptation to personalize the model for each client
(
Tan et al. 2022
)
.

The literature often distinguishes between
cross-device
and
cross-silo
FL
(
Huang et al. 2022
)
. In cross-device settings, model training is performed on end-user devices, such as mobile phones, severely restricting feasible model sizes and training speed
(
Woisetschläger et al. 2024a
)
. In cross-silo FL, one assumes that clients have access to powerful machines or data centers, and the focus is on bridging data silos in a privacy-preserving manner. Such deployments are shown to be capable of training very large models with billions of parameters
(
Sani et al. 2024
)
. As the focus of our position paper is on post-training of very large foundation language models, we mainly consider cross-silo settings where computational capacities are typically high enough to run large models.

Should we Fine-Tune Black Box Models with Federated Learning?

3.1
Open-Source Definitions

Although there is no consensus on what exactly an “open-source” foundation language model is, there are a couple of definitions proposed by various stakeholders
(
Alliance 2025
;
Open Source Alliance 2025
;
Staff in the Office of Technology 2024
;
IBM Data and AI Team 2023
)
. Not all open-source foundation models completely fulfill all requirements, i.e., there is an area between black-box and completely open models. To help clarify our position on such
gray-box
models, we analyze the various aspects of openness with respect to their impact on the main goals of FL (cf. Table
1
). This overview builds the basis for the following discussion of the risks of black-box models in FL.

•

Model weights.
An open-source model must provide the model weights in a usable format without deliberate obfuscation. Quantization of model weights must be documented.

•

Model architecture and code.
Beyond the weights, information on the architecture, training code and scripts, and other crucial data to successfully deploy and run the model is needed.

•

Open license.
The license shall not be restrictive. In particular, during post-training of open-source model, it should be allowed to redistribute the post-training result – be it updated model weights, adapter layers, or tuned system prompts. Generally, licensing is an important and hotly discussed topic in the open-source community. For further discussion, we refer to
(
Lerner and Tirole 2005
)
.

•

Open data.
The data used for training should be clearly documented and ideally be open as well to support reproducibility. Initiatives such as the Open Trusted Data Initiative
(
The AI Alliance 2025
)
aim to create trustworthy data catalogs for foundation model training.

3.2
Black-Box Post-Training Methods are Getting Adapted to Federated Learning

Modern foundation language models are designed as transformers for natural language processing (NLP) and demonstrate high performance in generating and understanding human-like text
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
. The development of extremely capable but privately owned models, such as the GPT series, has vastly influenced recent advances in centralized foundation model post-training
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
. These models have remarkable performance in natural language processing
(
Diao et al. 2022
;
Oh et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. However, users can only access these models through APIs, rendering them as a black-box. Users interact with the model, without knowledge of the internal architecture and weights of the model.

The reasons for the dominance of black-box foundation language models in centralized post-training are complex
(
Matarazzo and Torlone 2025
;
Sharma 2023
)
. Organizations that invest significantly in the development of these advanced models often claim and preserve ownership over their intellectual property and the technology itself. Facilitating access via APIs enables them to deliver their functionality as a service while concealing the intricate details of their models. Moreover, using these black-box models provides users with a convenient way to interact with advanced AI without requiring the substantial computing resources and expertise necessary to develop such extensive models independently. For that reason, we see a rapid increase of applications developed on these strong, yet non-transparent, foundation language models.

Recent research looks into how black-box foundation language models may be modified for the FL paradigm, given their popularity and success in centralized settings
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
. The incorporation of centralized black-box post-training methods to FL typically focuses on training smaller models to generate prompts or enhancing the input to the black-box model
(
Lin et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. This is because of the fundamental constraint that the internal parameters of a black-box foundation language model are inaccessible.

Federated Black-Box Prompt Tuning (Fed-BBPT)
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
is a notable example. Using their local private dataset, clients in an FL system train a lightweight model known as a prompt generator. When combined with local data, clients feed their prompts into the black-box model via the API. The output obtained from the black-box model allows the client to obtain a prediction and a task-specific loss value. The local training procedure, which involves updating the prompt generator’s parameters, is performed using zeroth-order optimization techniques because the black-box API does not provide first-order gradients. This process depends only on the received loss data. After local training, they send the parameters of the prompt generators to a central server, which aggregates them to form a globally learned prompt generator. This aggregated prompt generator model is then sent back to the clients for the next training round. This approach allows for collaborative learning and adaptation of the black-box model without requiring access to its parameters or sharing the raw training data.
Another set of approaches are Federated Discrete and Transferable Prompt Tuning (FedDTPT)
(
Wu et al. 2024b
)
and Federated Textual Gradient (FedTextGrad)
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. In both approaches, the clients optimize discrete prompts, as the use of black-box foundation language models impedes numerical gradient-based optimization. In FedDTPT, a feedback loop mechanism that leverages the Masked Language Model (MLM) API to guide prompt optimization is used during the client optimization phase. The server aggregates them using an attention method centered on semantic similarity to filter local prompt tokens, combined with embedding distance elbow detection and a DBSCAN clustering strategy to refine the filtering process. In FedTextGrad, clients make use of “textual gradients” for local prompt optimization. The server then aggregates the locally optimized prompts by summarizing them using the principle of uniform information density.

(
Lu et al. 2024
)
investigate the effect of using zeroth-order optimization methods in personalized FL to work with black-box foundation language models. These methods approximate gradients by querying the model using inputs incorporated with an auto-encoder with low-dimensional and client-specific embeddings, enabling post-training without requiring direct access to the model’s internal parameters.

3.3
Risks of using Black-Box Models in Federated Learning

Although there are arguments for using black-box foundation language models (see our
Alternative Position
in Section
4
), we argue that the combination of black-box foundation language models with FL is generally not a good fit. Relying on querying remote black-box models hosted by a third party counteracts the benefits and motivations of using FL in the first place.

We analyze the impact of violating the open-source principles on the fundamental goals of FL (cf. the overview in Table
1
). Firstly,
privacy
: One of the most important principles in FL is that clients keep their training data local and do not need to share it with any centralized training instance. Second,
autonomy
: Clients decide themselves what training data to use and how to contribute to the joint training process. Third,
heterogeneity
: Clients with different kinds of data distribution can participate in the training, with the goal of covering a wide variety of private training data in the joint training process. We are aware that FL has further goals and challenges (such as security), but we focus on these three for the following analysis. We first summarize our findings before we go into detailed discussion.

3.3.1
Main Findings

These are our main findings:

•

Without open weights, clients cannot directly access the foundation model for post-training. This means that they need to communicate with the model via an API of the model provider. This puts their privacy at risk. In the same way, clients depend on the foundation model provider to allow them to run their fine-tuning tasks, which constrains their autonomy. There may be restrictions on model use due to the provider’s safety and alignment policies. Heterogeneity is not directly affected if model weights are not open: Post-training is still possible with a variety of data. However, certain clients may be excluded from using the black-box models, which can have an indirect impact on heterogeneity. The question of architecture and code is tightly coupled to the openness of the weights. Without the corresponding information, using the model is much harder, or even impossible, so that clients have to rely on a model provider.

•

Restrictive licensing does not impede the privacy of the client’s post-training data. However, it limits their autonomy as they cannot freely decide how to use the fine-tuned models. In other words, an open-weight model with a restrictive license can still be used for privacy-preserving federated post-training, but must be handled with care. Clients need to carefully study the license and understand the impact of the restrictions on their intended use of the fine-tuned model. Licenses can provide a hurdle for certain participants to join the federated training, be it for financial reasons (i.e., the license may be costly), or for political reasons (e.g., the client comes from a sanctioned country and cannot get a license). This can affect heterogeneity.

•

The question of whether training data that is used for pre-training must be open has a lower impact on federated post-training. Post-training is largely independent of pre-training, i.e., clients can freely fine-tune the model with their private data regardless of whether the original pre-training data are open or not.

3.3.2
Detailed Discussion of Risks

Lack of privacy

FL aims to enhance user privacy by keeping raw training data on local devices. Using black-box models that are hosted by an untrusted third-party server puts the user’s private data at risk. All existing post-training methods for black-box models leak private query and training data
(
Hanke et al. 2024
)
. One can try to add additional privacy-preserving mechanisms such as differential privacy
(
Behnia et al. 2022
;
Zhao 2023
)
or use trusted computing (e.g., Flower Intelligence
(
Flower Labs
)
), but this can only mitigate the risk of data leakage and not completely remove it.

Lack of transparency

Unlike open-source foundation
models, black-box access restricts understanding of their internal architecture and the reasons behind their output
(
Rane et al. 2024
)
. Lack of transparency restricts systematic assessment and verification of the behavior and risks of these models according to predefined objectives. The lack of explanation for the outputs makes them unreliable for high-stake domains such as medical or finance. The black-box transparency issue combined with FL transparency issues in which stakeholders cannot verify the data of participating clients makes it a double black-box problem
(
Hatherley et al. 2025
)
.

Lack of autonomy

One of the key benefits of FL is that clients have control of their data and aspects of the training process. Outsourcing essential optimization decisions to a black-box model undermines this autonomy. Clients losing the ability to directly influence or monitor optimization results. If clients cannot retain complete control over the post-training process, one of the main motivations to use FL – maintaining local client control – is corrupted. It raises the question whether the partial control that is left for the clients is sufficient to motivate the use of FL in the first place
(
Hatherley et al. 2025
;
Lu et al. 2024
)
.

Inefficient natural language interface

Communicating with black-box models is often limited to natural language prompts. Arguably, natural language is not an effective way to program a computer system
Dijkstra 1997
due to its ambiguity, and the same arguments hold in the case of black-box foundation language models. Techniques that directly work on the model weights, such as post-training using Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models (LoRA)
(
Wu et al. 2024c
)
benefit from a rigorous mathematical explanation of the optimization process.

Lack of explainability

The lack of access to the parameters of black-box models inherently limits the explainability of the output. Initial promising approaches to explain the output of a language model require access to the model parameters
(
Ameisen et al. 2025
)
.
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
highlights that without access to the model parameters, it is almost impossible to explain black-box model in FL.

Ethical and societal risks

FL aims to promote the development of decentralized AI development by returning data ownership to the clients and reducing the dependency on a central entity
(
Flower AI 2025
;
Anelli et al. 2021
)
. Relying on black-box models can undermine this principle by providing access of data to centralized models. The introduction of a black-box model for participation in key steps of training a model can breach core FL principles of decentralized data ownership.

3.4
Advantages of Open-Source Foundation Language Models

Our position is that black-box foundation language models do not fit together with the core principles and goals of FL. In contrast, using open-source models has many advantages and aligns very well with FL.

Local deployment

Deploying open-source models locally removes the recurring need for API calls linked to black-box models that could add more communication overhead in FL systems. Although black-box models decrease the need for hardware maintenance, this argument is less convincing in FL systems, as the federated paradigm already requires clients to maintain local compute resources. Local deployment provides complete control over model training and data. When a model is deployed locally, several privacy-preserving mechanisms and security measures can be incorporated. These factors are essential to an FL system.

Soft prompts

Parameter access facilitates gradient-based optimization of continuous prompt vectors, a method that achieves performance equivalent to full fine-tuning while altering less than one percent of model parameters
(
Li and Liang 2021
)
. In contrast to discrete text prompts restricted by tokenization limits, soft prompts function directly on embeddings, allowing accurate control over model behavior
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
. Recent research indicates that prompt tuning, especially soft prompting, scales well with model size, achieving performance comparable to complete parameter fine-tuning in designs with 11 billion or more parameters
(
Lester et al. 2021
)
. Aggregation strategies such as FedSP
(
Dong et al. 2023
)
that facilitate information exchange between clients and the server through soft prompts show the effectiveness of soft prompts in FL. FedSP works with gray-box models that reveal their embeddings. When used with open-source models, it provides clients with complete authority over the local training process.

Broader Range of Fine-Tuning Approaches

Open-source models offer fine-tuning techniques such as adapter layers and LoRA beyond prompt-tuning that show that minimal parameter updates can yield task-specific performance improvements
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
. The adapter library
(
Poth et al. 2023
)
combines ten PEFT techniques under one framework, which preserves most of the base language model and enables multitask learning. The adapter layers and LoRA have been extended to FL
(
Wu et al. 2024c
;
Cai et al. 2023
)
. Decomposed Prompt Tuning (DePT) which combines soft prompting and LoRA, shows great promise
(
Shi and Lipani 2024
)
. Such approaches should be explored in FL, as they are lightweight and reduce communication overhead. However, these techniques require access to the model parameters which is restricted in black-box models.

Transparency and Reproducibility

Transparency in AI systems is essential for building trust, promoting fairness, and ensuring accountability as AI technologies become more integrated into daily life
(
Franzoni 2023
)
. Compliance with fair scientific standards demands complete transparency about the model structure and training process, which is in contrast to most black-box models. They lack the components required for complete understanding, reproducibility, and auditing of these models
(
White et al. 2024
)
. This lack of transparency hinders the independent verification of performance claims and meaningful comparisons among various studies in centralized and federated learning.

Ethical Considerations and Sensitive Data

AI systems and science must comply with both law and ethical guidelines such as the EU AI Act
(
European Union 2024
)
that demand the transparency, accountability, and safety of individuals and society while encouraging innovation and trust in AI systems. Such acts clearly state that the decisions made by an AI system need to be comprehensible and reproducible
(
Pavlidis 2024
;
European Commission 2024
)
. In addition to this, FL is often used in data-sensitive areas such as health and finance, where the traceability of decisions is important
(
Li et al. 2023
)
. Without access to the model weights, it is rather difficult to understand the reasons behind a certain model outcome. Both ethical and data-sensitivity concerns can be addressed by using open-source models over black-box models.

4
Alternative Position: Black-Box Foundation Language Models Have Many Advantages

Despite their risks, black-box foundation language models are in fact heavily used in research and practice. Here, we discuss common arguments in favor of using black-box models.

Black-box models have superior performance

Black-box foundation language models often demonstrate superior performance compared to open-source alternatives
(
Laskar et al. 2023
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
. Companies investing significantly in these proprietary models in terms of compute resources, data curation, model architecture innovation, or the scale of post-training, driven by market competition, to further contribute to their capabilities
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
. In particular, black-box models exhibit high accuracy in few- and zero-shot learning scenarios
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
, enabling rapid comprehension of task structures and facilitating their rapid adaptation across various natural language processing applications
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
.

Discussion:
Despite these capabilities of black-box models, open-source models show the potential to surpass them in post-training adaptation
(
Hanke et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Carammia et al. 2024
;
Wolfe et al. 2024
;
Irugalbandara et al. 2024
)
and even in zero-shot scenarios
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
The performance of foundation language models is evolving and the open-source community discusses ideas on how to close the performance gap
(
Kulkarni and Chakraborty 2025
)
.

Using black-box models is more economical

A key advantage of black-box models lies in their perceived lower operational overhead. The significant size of many open-source language models often makes local deployment impractical, necessitating dependence on external providers. Using black-box models via an API eliminates the need for users to acquire and maintain costly hardware and specialized software. Users benefit from updates deployed by the service providers, and hence can always have access to the latest generation of foundation language models. In addition, parametric fine-tuning conducted by well-resourced companies is often more efficient than fine-tuning a locally deployed open-source model.

Discussion:
The cost-benefit analysis of using black-box models depends on many factors. In fact, various studies show that deploying an open-source model locally can be more economical, e.g.,
(
Leduc 2023
;
Irugalbandara et al. 2024
)
, often providing higher throughput and lower costs
(
Ateia and Kruschwitz 2024
)
.

Black-Box models are easily accessible for everybody

Deploying open-source foundation language models on-premises of users poses significant technical challenges. Black-box models can be easily accessed without the need for any technical expertise. Beyond that, they shine with features such as built-in legal and guideline compliance and alignment, simplifying their safe usage for non-experts. The effectiveness of prompt engineering allows users to fine-tune the model performance without having to understand or modify the underlying model.

Discussion:

While the ease of use is a strong argument for many users to rely on black-box models, there are also limitations. Unpredictable response times and token limitations are possible burdens that restrict accessibility
(
Irugalbandara et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, the terms of use of black-box model providers restrict access, e.g., due to trade laws and sanctions
(
OpenAI 2024
)
.

Black-Box models are legally compliant and better aligned

Model providers supervise model deployment and maintenance in order to ensure security, alignment and legal compliance, implementing ethical and legal guidelines or safeguards, applying general terms and conditions, and privacy regulations
(
Hanke et al. 2024
;
Laskar et al. 2023
)
.

Discussion:
Black-box models can still be jailbreaked with only a few simple prompts
(
Lapid et al. 2024
;
Chao et al. 2024
)
.
With respect to privacy, one needs to trust the promises of the black-box model provider, as it is not possible to perform privacy-preserving post-training with existing methods
(
Hanke et al. 2024
)
.
Open models that are deployed locally offer more means to control them with respect to security, compliance, and privacy
(
Hanke et al. 2024
;
Achiam et al. 2023
)
.

5
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we discuss the following question: Should FL research on foundation language model post-training adopt black-box approaches? Our position is clear:
“No, it shouldn’t.”
We provided a comprehensive analysis that illustrates how black-box models undermine the fundamental goals of FL, such as privacy and autonomy. Based on a detailed analysis of aspects of openness in foundation models, we argue which of them are critical to which goal of FL. This allows for a more fine-grained assessment of ”gray-box” model that offer
partial
openness (e.g., open weights but restricted licenses). This way, our work provides a tool-box for a case-by-case analysis that helps researchers and industry to make an informed decision on which foundation models to consider for their work on federated post-training.

Outlook: Model Context Protocol (MCP) is Changing the Way of Interaction with foundation language models.

Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a standardized communication protocol that enables foundation language models to dynamically interact with external tools and services by defining a structured client-server architecture
(
Hou et al. 2025
)
. Since its inception, MCP has seen a steep rise in applications. Currently, research focuses on centralized architectures with black-box LLMs. Federated systems, in combination with open-source foundation models that are locally deployed, could help address security and privacy concerns.
