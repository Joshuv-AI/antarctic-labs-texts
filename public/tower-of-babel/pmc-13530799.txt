Poster 45. The Impact of Increased Posterior Tibial Slope on Survivorship in Meniscal Allograft Transplantation
Authors: Samy Shelbaya, Rushani K. Cameron, Emma Trasatti, Kobe Rodney, Eric J. Strauss, Laith Jazrawi, Kirk A. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) is a well-established surgical procedure for treating symptomatic meniscal deficiency in young and active patients. Despite the overall long-term success of the procedure, graft failure remains a significant clinical concern. Previous research has identified that anatomical factors, such as coronal alignment and tibial morphology, are substantial factors influencing outcomes following knee preservation procedures. Looking specifically at the posterior tibial slope (PTS), research has shown that an increased PTS has significant implications for altering knee biomechanics and is associated with higher revision rates after anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. Although the relationship between the posterior tibial slope and ACL revisions has been well established in the literature, its relationship with MAT graft survivorship remains largely unidentified. This study aimed to evaluate the influence of PTS on outcomes after MAT and to determine whether a threshold slope value is associated with increased risk of graft failure. We hypothesized that increased PTS would correlate with higher failure risk following MAT.Methods:Patients who underwent the MAT procedure between 2011 and 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. All included patients had preoperative standard or weight-bearing lateral knee radiographs and no history of concomitant realignment osteotomy. Patients were excluded if they underwent a concomitant osteotomy procedure or ligament reconstruction or later required arthrofibrosis debridement or meniscus retear debridement of the index knee. PTS was measured on standardized lateral radiographs of the proximal third of the tibial using the circle of best fit technique. Graft failure was defined using both clinical and imaging criteria. Primary failure included revision MAT, conversion to arthroplasty, or reoperation at the same site. Secondary failure was defined as imaging-confirmed structural graft compromise, subchondral collapse, meniscus fragmentation, or resorption, with corresponding clinical symptoms. Cox regression was performed to identify predictors of failure while controlling for age, sex, and BMI. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, using Youden's index, was employed to determine the optimal cutoff values for significant predictors. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and the log-rank test were used to compare graft survivorship above and below these thresholds. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.Results:A total of 134 MAT patients (63 medial, 71 lateral) were analyzed. This cohort had a mean posterior tibial slope (PTS) of 10.89 ± 2.78 mm (range 1.30-16.60) and 30 failures (22.4%). Cox regression demonstrated no significant association between PTS and failure in either univariate (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.93-1.19, p = 0.448) or multivariate models adjusting for age, sex, and BMI (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.93-1.20, p = 0.431), with poor model discrimination (c-index = 0.532) (Table 1). ROC analysis showed weak predictive capacity (AUC = 0.58), although an optimal PTS threshold of 11.9 mm was identified. This threshold was used to divide subjects into low PTS and high PTS groups. Subsequent Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank testing demonstrated significantly worse outcomes for patients with PTS >= 11.9 mm (log-rank χ²(1) = 11.40, p = 0.0007), with high PTS patients experiencing 21 observed failures versus 12 expected failures and low PTS patients experiencing 9 observed versus 18 expected (Figure 1). Stratified analysis confirmed reduced survival in both medial (p = 0.023) and lateral (p = 0.0079) MATs, though lateral grafts demonstrated slightly worse survival (Figure 2 and 3). While mean PTS did not differ between failures and non-failures (11.19 vs. 10.80 mm, p = 0.598), failure cases demonstrated broader distributions and higher extremes, suggesting that an elevated PTS may contribute to earlier graft failure despite overlapping averages.Conclusions:In this study, while PTS was not found to be an independent predictor for MAT survival time, further analysis identified a PTS threshold of 11.9 mm above which survivorship decreased significantly. This finding indicates there may be an association between increased posterior tibial slope and risk of meniscal graft failure in patients undergoing MAT, especially once a relevant threshold is exceeded. It also underscores the biomechanical importance of the PTS in postoperative outcomes for procedures of the knee. Clinically, this study supports the consideration of slope assessment in preoperative planning for MAT. Future studies should validate the proposed cutoff and determine whether tibial slope correction or alignment procedures in high-risk MAT patients could improve outcomes, reduce reoperation rates, and promote long-term graft survival.
