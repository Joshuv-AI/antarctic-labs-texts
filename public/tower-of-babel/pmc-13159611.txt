Evaluation of digital colorimetric analysis as an objective indicator representing microbial diversity and biological succession of dental plaque
Authors: Ji-Na Lim, Yunseok Oh, Kyudong Han, Seyoung Mun, Jae-Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisual assessment of dental plaque is limited by subjectivity and variability in imaging conditions.ObjectiveThis study aimed to objectively quantify plaque maturation using CIE L*a*b*-based color difference (ΔE*) and evaluate its association with microbial community structure and pathogenicity.DesignDental images were obtained from 30 participants after application of a plaque-disclosing agent. Plaque color was quantified as ΔE* relative to unstained tooth surfaces and classified into Low-ΔE* and High-ΔE* groups. Saliva and supragingival plaque samples from Low-ΔE* and High-ΔE* sites were collected, and full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using the PacBio Sequel IIe platform.ResultsSaliva showed significantly higher microbial diversity than plaque samples. The Low-ΔE* group was dominated by early colonizers, including Streptococcus sanguinis, whereas the High-ΔE* group showed enrichment of anaerobic and periodontal disease-associated taxa, including Prevotella, Treponema, Selenomonas, Campylobacter, and Porphyromonas endodontalis. The lightness value (L*) was negatively correlated with periodontal pathogens, including Treponema denticola and Tannerella forsythia. PICRUSt2 analysis indicated upregulation of inflammatory pathways, including NOD-like receptor signaling and apoptosis, in the High-ΔE* group.Conclusions ΔE*-based colorimetric analysis may serve as an objective indicator of dental plaque maturation and pathogenic potential, supporting personalized oral hygiene feedback.Keyword: Dental plaque, CIE L * a * b , color difference (ΔE *), supragingival microbiome, red complex, plaque maturation

Introduction

The oral cavity is recognised as one of the most microbially diverse ecosystems in the human body, second only to the gut, and distinct microbial communities colonise multiple microniches, including mineralised tooth surfaces, soft tissues, and saliva [1]. Approximately 600–700 bacterial taxa have been reported in the oral environment, and the total bacterial load in the adult oral cavity is estimated to be ~ 5 × 10¹⁰–10¹¹ (50–100 billion) [2]. Although the oral microbiome is relatively stable under healthy conditions, the accumulation and maturation of dental plaque can drive shifts in community structure and may be associated with inflammatory responses and an increased risk of periodontal disease [3]. Specifically, early colonisers such as Streptococcus sanguinis, Streptococcus mitis, and Actinomyces species primarily attach to the tooth surface to form a healthy early plaque, maintaining a stable symbiotic relationship with the host [4,5]. However, as the plaque matures and oxygen tension decreases, the environment shifts to favour anaerobes [6]. The colonisation and proliferation of the 'Red complex', comprising Porphyromonas gingivalis, Treponema denticola, and Tannerella forsythia, lead to a potent pathogenic consortium that triggers inflammatory responses, eventually resulting in periodontal diseases [7–9].

Dental plaque is a major risk factor for gingivitis and periodontal disease [10], and a key component of daily plaque control is to ‘make plaque visible’ in order to promote behavioural change. Plaque disclosing agents (plaque chequer; disclosing solution) provide immediate visualisation of plaque, thereby enhancing patients’/participants’ awareness and motivation and enabling targeted feedback on areas that are often missed during toothbrushing. Accordingly, disclosing solutions are needed not only as diagnostic adjuncts but also as behaviour-based tools that facilitate self-care behaviours [11]. In addition, two-tone/three-tone systems have been continuously explored to visually differentiate plaque states [12]. However, staining outcomes may vary depending on lighting and imaging conditions or examiner interpretation, highlighting the need to combine disclosing results with quantitative indices that can objectify and standardise the assessment. Importantly, plaque disclosing agents may interact differently with dental plaque depending on its structural and biochemical properties. As dental plaque matures, changes in plaque composition, thickness, and microbial metabolic activity may influence staining patterns, suggesting that observed colour variations may reflect underlying biological differences rather than merely serving as visual indicators. Therefore, an objective and standardised approach to quantify these colour differences is required [3,13].

When colour descriptors based on RGB or CIE L * a * b * are standardised and expressed as a colour difference (ΔE *), differences in stained colours can be converted into reproducible numerical values, enabling classification according to staining intensity and/or colour change [14]. Thus, ΔE *-based classification offers a potential framework for distinguishing plaque status using standardised indices while addressing the limitations of purely visual assessment [15]. Furthermore, quantification of colour information transforms visual signals into structured digital features, enabling objective comparison across samples and facilitating integration with microbiome data as well as future applications in artificial intelligence–based diagnostic tools [16,17].

Notably, red fluorescent plaque observed under 405 nm light has been reported as a signal associated with mature dental plaque [18], providing supportive evidence for the biological interpretation of colour-difference–based classification. In addition, a study that applied a three-tone disclosing gel and subsequently collected plaque according to colour categories, quantifying caries-associated bacteria by real-time PCR, suggested that bacteriological characteristics may differ across stained colour categories, thereby supporting the potential biological relevance of colour-based classification [12]. However, these lines of research have largely evolved independently. Fluorescence-based and optical studies have primarily focused on signal detection, whereas microbiome studies have focused on compositional changes without incorporating standardised quantitative colour metrics such as ΔE *. Therefore, direct evidence linking quantitatively defined colour differences with microbial community structure remains limited.

The transition from early to mature dental plaque may involve not only compositional changes in the microbiome but also reorganisation of interspecies interactions and overall community structure [19]. If colour changes stained by plaque disclosing agents are quantified as ΔE * values and classified into distinct colour profiles, and if these classifications are consistent with fluorescence signals characteristic of mature plaque, this would imply that a practical classification scheme could be established in which community characteristics differ according to colour-based indicators [20]. However, studies that directly compare plaque microbiome composition and community structure across ΔE *-defined colour-difference groups while simultaneously supporting biological plausibility using 405 nm light–based signals remain limited. Therefore, the present study aims to first establish the distinct microbial characteristics of supragingival plaque compared to saliva as a baseline, and subsequently elucidate the differences in microbial community structures between Low-ΔE * and High-ΔE * groups. In addition, by demonstrating the characteristic of microbiota of supragingival plaque of colour-difference-based classification, this study seeks to provide a scientific basis for personalised oral hygiene feedback and strategies for preventing periodontal disease.

Methods

Ethical approval and participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dankook University (IRB No. 2024-12-053-033). Participants were voluntarily recruited between May and November 2025 through recruitment notices distributed at the Dankook University Dental Hospital and local communities (e.g. community centres) around Cheonan, Republic of Korea from May to November 2025. Dental Hospital visitors (patients) and local community residents who expressed interest were screened for eligibility by a single researcher using consistent predefined criteria, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment.

Participants were excluded if they were pregnant or breastfeeding, had used antibiotics within the past 3 months, or had active cavitated dental caries. Periodontal status was assessed during the clinical examination; participants with moderate-to-severe periodontitis—characterised by deep periodontal pockets, clinical attachment loss, and/or generalised radiographic alveolar bone loss—were excluded, whereas those with gingivitis or mild periodontal inflammation (including bleeding on probing [BOP]) were eligible (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). The overall experiment was performed according to serial phase (Figure 1).

Calculation of absolute colour difference (ΔE *) and group definition

A total of dental images from 30 participants were included for analysis in this study using plaque disclosing agents (Plaque checker) (Denti-Check, Thomastone, KOREA). Dental images were captured in a standardised frontal view using a digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera (Nikon D3000, Nikon Corp., Japan). Prior to imaging, a plaque disclosing solution was applied to the facial surfaces of the teeth using a sterile micro-applicator. The solution was evenly applied to the tooth surfaces and allowed to react for approximately 15 seconds, followed by gentle rinsing with water according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plaque disclosing solution used in this study was a natural blue pigment derived from Gardenia jasminoides. The formulation does not contain antimicrobial agents and functions solely as a visual staining agent. Prior to the main experiment, a preliminary microbial viability assessment (Saliva-Check) was conducted to evaluate potential effects on microbial integrity, and no significant disturbance was observed.

No additional mechanical or chemical treatment of the tooth surface was performed prior to image acquisition. To minimise variability, participants were instructed to visit the clinic in the morning and to refrain from toothbrushing after waking until sample collection and imaging were completed. No strict fasting was required; however, participants were advised to avoid eating or drinking immediately prior to the visit.

For each participant, a total of 12 standardised regions of interest (ROIs) were defined on the facial surfaces of anterior teeth. Specifically, three anterior teeth from each quadrant (maxillary right, maxillary left, mandibular right, and mandibular left) were selected, resulting in 12 teeth in total. For each tooth, a single ROI was positioned at the central area of the facial surface to ensure consistent and reproducible measurements. Only facial surfaces were included in the analysis to minimise variability associated with anatomical differences and to maintain standardised imaging conditions.

Within each ROI, pixel-level RGB values were extracted from areas where dental plaque was observed. The extracted RGB values were averaged within each ROI to obtain representative RGB values. These averaged RGB values were subsequently converted to the CIE L * a * b * colour space using a standardised colour-space conversion procedure implemented in C# (Aspose SVG. NET Colour Converter; https://products.aspose.com/svg/net/color-converter/rgb-to-lab/).

The converted L * a * b * values were compared with those of an unstained reference surface within the same tooth (L * ₀ a * ₀ b * ₀), and the absolute colour difference (ΔE *) was calculated. To account for spatial variability in plaque distribution, ΔE * values were calculated at multiple regions of interest (ROIs) within each tooth surface. For each sample, the mean ΔE * value was obtained by averaging ΔE * values across all ROIs, providing a representative colour difference at the sample level.

To ensure reliable colour differentiation, only regions exhibiting clearly distinguishable staining patterns were included in the analysis. Based on staining intensity, samples were categorised into Low- ΔE * (representing minimal colour change; relatively bright) and High- ΔE * (representing significant colour change; relatively dark) groups. ΔE * values were calculated for each selected region, and group-level mean ΔE * values were used to represent the respective colour difference levels.

Here, L * ₀ , a * ₀, and b * ₀ represent the colour values of the unstained reference surface within the same tooth. This approach was adopted to minimise ambiguity associated with intermediate staining regions and to focus on clearly distinguishable colour differences. Group-wise means and standard deviations of RGB and L * a * b * values were then calculated, and tooth colour characteristics and microbial community features were compared between groups according to ΔE * level.

Sample collection and preparation of bacterial metagenomic DNA

Supragingival plaque was collected at the tooth level from representative sites exhibiting consistent staining characteristics, rather than from the whole mouth. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected using sterile collection tubes under standardised conditions. Three types of specimens (Saliva, Low-ΔE * supragingival plaque, High-ΔE * supragingival plaque) were collected from each participant. A total of 90 samples were collected and stored at −20 °C. Bacterial metagenomic DNA (mDNA) was extracted from each sample using the QIAamp PowerFecalTM Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and yield of the extracted mDNA were checked using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), Qubit 4 Fluorometer, and 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at −20 °C until the following process.

Full-length 16S rRNA gene library preparation and sequencing

SMRTbell libraries targeting bacterial full-length 16S rRNA genes were constructed from mDNA samples and sequenced using the PacBio Sequel IIe platform (Pacific Biosciences, USA). All mDNA samples were normalised to 2 ng and full-length 16S rRNA amplicons were amplified using the 2X KAPA HiFi HotStart readymix PCR Kit (Roche, Switzerland). For this purpose, a pair of 16S target-specific universal primers with different barcode combinations recommended by PacBio were used for each sample: 16S 27F primer sequence 5’ - GCATC/barcode/AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG - 3’ and 16S 1492 R primer sequence 5’ - GCATC/barcode/RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT - 3’. The PCR cycle condition was initiated with denaturation (95 °C, 5 min), followed by 25 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (57 °C, 30 s), extension (72 °C, 30 s), and storage (4 °C). PCR products were checked using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermofisher Scientific, USA), and samples were normalised to a total molecular weight of 1000 ng before pooling. The pooled samples were purified by AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA), and then the purified pooled sample (approximately 1500–1700 bp) was checked by Agilent TapeStation equipment (Agilent Technology, USA). The purified pooled sample was prepared as multiplexed amplicon libraries using the SMRTbell® Prep Kit v3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, USA). The following procedures were performed: DNA damage repair & A-tailing (37 °C for 30 min, 65 °C for 5 min) and adaptor ligation (20 °C for 30 min). After adaptor ligation, purification with AMPure XP beads was performed to discard residual adaptors. Nuclease treatment (37 °C for 15 min) was performed, followed by a final purification step. The multiplexed amplicon library was constructed with the Sequel® II Binding Kit 3.1 (Pacific Biosciences, USA) for primer annealing and polymerase binding. Finally, SMRTbell library was executed in the circular consensus sequence (CCS) mode on a PacBio Sequel IIe platform instrument. This process generated highly accurate single, continuous full-length long reads (HiFi reads) spanning the entire 16S rRNA gene, with a predicted accuracy of ≥Q30.

Bioinformatic processing and comprehensive microbiome analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed using SMRT Link (v13.1) to generate demultiplexed HiFi reads. The subsequent bioinformatic analysis largely followed the official PacBio HiFi-16S workflow. First, SeqKit was used to filter read, retaining only those with Q score > 20 [21]. Primers were trimmed from the quality-filtered reads using Cutadapt (v4.5), and reads outside the 1,000 - 1,600 bp range were discarded [22]. The adaptor trimmed reads were processed with the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 (v2023.9) to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) [23,24]. This step performed error correction and chimera removal. ASVs with a total frequency below 5 across all samples were excluded. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the A VSEARCH-based classifier plugin in QIIME 2 against the SILVA v138 (99% rRNA database). To ensure robust classification, the parameters were set with a minimum consensus fraction of 0.6. This consensus threshold (60%) is more stringent than the default setting (51%) of the VSEARCH algorithm, ensuring that a taxonomy was assigned only when at least 60% of the top hits agreed [25]. Afterwards, the sequences annotated with Bacteria were extracted, and those annotated with Chloroplast and Mitochondria were removed.

For ecological analysis, the ASV table was rarefied to a uniform depth. Alpha diversity (Chao1 index, Observed features, Shannon's index, Simpson’s index, and Pielou's evenness) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis, unweighted UniFrac) were calculated in QIIME 2. Chao1 and Observed features assessed microbial richness, Simpson's index and Pielou's evenness evaluated concentration of dominance and community evenness, and Shannon's index evaluated overall diversity including richness and evenness. Beta diversity was visualised with Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using the 'ape' R package v5.8.1. To assess statistical significance, differences in alpha diversity indices between groups were evaluated using non-parametric tests (e.g. Kruskal-Wallis test and Wilcoxon Rank Sum test (Mann-Whitney test)) from the scipy.stats library v1.10.1 in Python v3.10.14. For beta diversity, Permutational Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) was used to test for significant differences in community structure, implemented via the adonis2 function with 999 permutations in the ‘vegan’ R package v2.6.8. Differentially abundant microbial features between groups were identified using three distinct methods: ALDEx2 (Analysis of differential abundance taking sample and scale variation into account), DESeq2 (Differential expression analysis), and LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) [26,27]. To infer the functional potential of the microbial communities, the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2, v2.5.2) pipeline was utilised to predict KEGG pathway abundances [28]. ALDEx2 was then used to identify KEGG pathways that were differentially abundant between the groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and generation of figure of alpha, beta diversity and differentially abundant microbial features were performed using R v4.3.0. The Mann–Whitney U statistical test was applied to comparative statistical analysis among three comparison groups (Saliva, Low-ΔE * and High-ΔE * groups) established for this study. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis and visualisation were conducted to assess the relationship between the core microbiome abundance and ΔE * values using GraphPad Prism v10.4 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). For differential abundance analysis, thresholds were applied to identify significantly altered taxa. In LEfSe analysis, the alpha value for the factorial Kruskal-Wallis test was set to 0.05, and the threshold for the logarithmic LDA score was set to >2.0 for discriminative features. For DESeq2, taxa with a p of <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change of >1.0 were considered significant. In ALDEx2, features with a p of <0.05 and |Difference Between| of >1.0 were identified as differentially abundant. Differences in L * (lightness) values between the Low-ΔE * and High-ΔE * groups were evaluated using an independent samples t-test (or Welch’s t-test when variance inequality was assumed), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sample-level averaging of ΔE * across 12 tooth-surface regions and definition of representative colours

Representative group colours were defined based on the group-wise mean colour values (based on 12 tooth surface regions (ROIs) measured per participant). The Low-ΔE * group showed a lighter grayish blue/grey-blue appearance with a representative colour of HEX (sRGB) #5D666F, corresponding to RGB (mean ± SD) of R 93.08 ± 4.14, G 101.75 ± 3.49, and B 111.42 ± 6.17, and CIE L * a * b * (mean ± SD) of L * 42.596 ± 1.399, a * −1.409 ± 1.193, and b * −5.316 ± 1.977. In contrast, the High-ΔE * group exhibited a darker bluish grey/blue-grey appearance with a representative colour of HEX (sRGB) #293A47, corresponding to RGB (mean ± SD) of R 41.17 ± 4.43, G 58.25 ± 6.27, and B 71.17 ± 7.22, and CIELAB (mean ± SD) of L * 23.742 ± 2.409, a * −2.428 ± 3.660, and b * −7.882 ± 1.985 (Table 2, Supplementary Table 2). High-ΔE * (L *: 23.74) group was characterised by significantly lower L * values, indicating a darker and more intense staining profile compared to the Low-ΔE * (L *: 42.60) group.

Sequencing data processing

In this study, 90 mDNA samples of 3 types from 30 participants were extracted and sequenced as full-length 16S rRNA gene amplicon libraries. An average of 28,474, 20,380, and 20,255 raw reads were generated for each group, respectively. After filtering, error correction, and chimera removal, an average of 21,772, 13,285, and 13,021 > Q20 non-chimeric reads (HiFi reads) for microbiome analysis were generated (Table 3). Taxonomic classification identified a total of 10,736 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA v138 16S rRNA reference database with a confidence threshold of 60% (Supplementary Table 3).

Comparison of microbial diversity and community structure

To evaluate the overall microbial community characteristics, alpha and beta diversity indices were analysed across the three groups: Saliva, Low-ΔE *, and High-ΔE *. In the comparison of alpha diversity, we evaluated the microbial communities based on richness, evenness, and overall diversity. Regarding microbial richness (Chao1 index and Observed features), the Saliva group (Chao1: 477.92 ± 154.03; Observed features: 428.30 ± 130.09) exhibited significantly higher values compared to both the Low-ΔE * (Chao1: 185.74 ± 74.07; Observed features: 171.27 ± 68.72) and High-ΔE * (Chao1: 233.29 ± 96.02; Observed features: 214.80 ± 93.18) groups (Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.05) (Figure 2A, Table 4). However, when evaluating the concentration of dominance (Simpson's index) and microbial evenness (Pielou's evenness), the Saliva group (Simpson: 0.98 ± 0.02; Pielou: 0.83 ± 0.05) was only significantly higher than the Low-ΔE * group (Simpson: 0.93 ± 0.05; Pielou: 0.75 ± 0.09) (p < 0.05), with no significant differences observed between the Saliva and High-ΔE * groups (Simpson: 0.95 ± 0.06; Pielou: 0.78 ± 0.11) (p < 0.05). The Shannon index, which accounts for both richness and evenness, was significantly higher in the Saliva group (7.22 ± 0.77) compared to both the Low-ΔE * (5.47 ± 0.97) and High-ΔE * (6.01 ± 1.23) plaque groups (p < 0.05).

Taxonomic composition and differentially abundant taxa

Taxonomic composition analysis revealed distinct differences in microbial profiles and relative abundances between the saliva and supragingival plaque groups (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Table 4). For detailed comparison, taxa at the phylum, genus, and species levels were selected based on statistical significance among the groups (p < 0.05) and a relative abundance >1.00% across the three groups (Table 6). At the phylum level, Firmicutes (Saliva group: 31.10%, Low-ΔE *: 20.37%, High-ΔE *: 20.11%) was significantly enriched, while Actinobacteriota (8.32%, 22.27%, 18.85%) was depleted, in the saliva group relative to both the Low-ΔE * and High-ΔE * groups (Figure 3A, Table 6). At the genus level, the saliva group exhibited a significant enrichment in Streptococcus (19.68%, 13.71%, 12.49%), Prevotella (10.78%, 4.05%, 7.72%), Porphyromonas (6.48%, 2.88%, 3.37%), Haemophilus (5.65%, 3.91%, 1.83%), Alloprevotella (2.22%, 0.42%, 1.18%), and Gemella (2.44%, 0.26%, 0.55%), while showing a decreased abundance of Capnocytophaga (3.57%, 9.00%, 9.53%), Actinomyces (3.16%, 8.46%, 6.53%), Corynebacterium (1.56%, 8.15%, 7.10%), and Cardiobacterium (0.36%, 1.44%, 1.48%) ( Figure 3B, Table 6). At the species level, a significantly higher relative abundance of Haemophilus parainfluenzae (Saliva group: 4.40%, Low-ΔE *: 3.87%, High-ΔE *: 1.81%), Streptococcus salivarius (4.51%, 0.23%, 0.06%), Neisseria subflava (3.39%, 0.53%, 0.46%), Streptococcus mitis (3.52%, 0.41%, 0.14%), Prevotella melaninogenica (3.57%, 0.09%, 0.28%), and Streptococcus oralis (1.76%, 0.75%, 1.16%) was observed in the saliva group (Figure 3C, Table 6).

To investigate the relationship between the visual characteristics of dental plaque and its microbial composition, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the pairwise associations between the CIE L * a * b * colour coordinates and the relative abundances of 33 dental plaque-associated bacterial species [29,30]. The analysis was conducted on the total supragingival plaque group (n = 60) as well as within the Low-ΔE * (n = 30) and High-ΔE * (n = 30) groups (Figure 5, Supplementary Table S9, Supplementary Table S10).

In the Low-ΔE * group, 21 out of 32 species (Eubacterium sp. was excluded as it was not detected in the 30 samples of Low-ΔE * group) exhibited a negative correlation with the L * value. In the High-ΔE * group, 14 out of 33 species showed a negative correlation with L * . Notably, among these 14 species, Selenomonas noxia (rho = −0.44, p < 0.05) and Campylobacter gracilis (rho = −0.52, p < 0.01) met the criteria of p < 0.05 and |rho| > 0.20. Additionally, it was confirmed that 13 out of the 14 species exhibiting a negative correlation in the High-ΔE * group demonstrated a stronger negative correlation with L * compared to their correlations in the Low-ΔE * group.

Significant shifts in correlation patterns with the a * were observed between the two groups. In the High-ΔE * group, Streptococcus sanguinis (rho = −0.40, p < 0.05) and Haemophilus parainfluenzae (rho = −0.37, p < 0.05) showed significant negative correlations with a * values. Conversely, Campylobacter gracilis exhibited a significant positive correlation with a * in the High-ΔE * group (rho = 0.400, p < 0.05), whereas no significant correlation was found in the Low-ΔE * group (rho = 0.04, p > 0.05). In the Low-ΔE * group, Eikenella corrodens (rho = 0.40, p < 0.05) and Streptococcus gordonii (rho = 0.37, p < 0.05) were positively correlated with a * values.

Significant correlations with the b * were exclusively identified in the High-ΔE * group. Veillonella parvula demonstrated a strong negative correlation with b * values in the High-ΔE * group (rho = −0.62, p < 0.01), compared to a non-significant positive correlation in the Low-ΔE * group (rho = 0.08, p > 0.05). Additionally, Campylobacter concisus showed a significant negative correlation with b * only in the High-ΔE * group (rho = −0.42, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study introduced CIE L * a * b *-based colour difference (ΔE *) indices to overcome the inherent subjectivity of conventional dental plaque indices that rely on examiner interpretation. By quantifying the colour changes visualised by plaque disclosing agents, we minimised errors associated with lighting, imaging conditions, and subjective bias, thereby establishing an objective and standardised framework for plaque assessment. Our results demonstrated that the Low-ΔE * group (representing minimal colour change; relatively bright) exhibited significantly lighter coloration compared to the High-ΔE * group (representing significant colour change; relatively dark).

As a baseline validation, we compared the bacterial composition of saliva with that of supragingival plaque. Consistent with previous studies detailing the oral microbiome, our alpha and beta diversity analyses confirmed that saliva harbours a highly diverse and structurally distinct microbial community compared to the tooth surface [31–33]. The significant enrichment of Firmicutes (e.g. Streptococcus) in saliva and Actinobacteriota in plaque aligns with the known microbial niches of these taxa, affirming the integrity of our plaque sampling methodology.

The transition from early to mature dental plaque is classically described as an ecological succession from Gram-positive, aerotolerant saccharolytic bacteria to Gram-negative, obligate anaerobic proteolytic pathogens [6,34,35]. Our differential abundance analyses (LEfSe, ALDEx2, DESeq2) perfectly captured this successional shift across the ΔE * classifications. The High-ΔE * group exhibited a significant enrichment in major periodontal pathogens, including Prevotella, Campylobacter, Selenomonas, and Treponema, alongside key members of the 'Red Complex’ (Tannerella forsythia and Treponema denticola) [7]. As the plaque thickens and matures, oxygen depletion occurs in the deeper layers, creating an optimal microenvironment for these strict anaerobes [36]. The robust accumulation of these taxa in the High-ΔE * group provides strong bacteriological evidence that regions with profound colour changes are indeed hotspots for mature, pathogenic plaques.

This taxonomic shift in the High-ΔE * group was closely mirrored by alterations in the predicted functional potential of the microbiome. PICRUSt2 analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the ‘NOD-like receptor (NLR) signalling pathway’ and ‘Apoptosis’ in the High-ΔE * group. NLRs are critical intracellular sensors of microbial pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as peptidoglycan fragments from Gram-negative bacteria, and their activation is closely linked to the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines in periodontal disease [37]. Furthermore, the enrichment of pathways related to ‘Fatty acid metabolism’ and ‘Steroid hormone biosynthesis’ may reflect the structural maturation of bacterial cell envelopes (e.g. lipopolysaccharides) and complex metabolic cross-feeding within the mature plaque [38]. These functional predictions suggest that High-ΔE * plaque is not only compositionally mature but also possesses a heightened capacity to trigger host inflammatory responses.

One of the most striking findings of this study was the strong negative correlation between the lightness value (L *) and the relative abundance of periodontal pathogens. Species such as T. denticola, T. forsythia, F. nucleatum, and P. melaninogenica increased significantly as L * decreased (i.e. as the plaque appeared darker). The biological basis for this colorimetric change may be attributed to the accumulation of specific metabolic byproducts within the aging plaque. For instance, black-pigmented anaerobes, including Prevotella and Porphyromonas species, are known to accumulate dark-coloured porphyrin compounds (e.g. haem aggregates) as they utilise host-derived iron [39]. This pigment accumulation, combined with the increased physical density and structural complexity of the mature extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, likely contributes to the darker optical properties (lower L * and higher ΔE *) observed after disclosing agent application [20].

Moreover, network analyses highlighted distinct ecological interactions shaping plaque maturation. The strong positive correlation between T. denticola and T. forsythia across both groups highlights their well-documented synergistic co-aggregation within the pathogenic climax community [40]. Conversely, the significant negative correlation between Campylobacter gracilis (a later coloniser) and Streptococcus sanguinis (an early commensal coloniser) illustrates the competitive exclusion that occurs as the plaque environment shifts from health-associated to disease-associated states [41].

This study has some limitations. First, confounding variables could not be strictly considered due to insufficient collection of clinical data such as smoking history, gender, and age. Second, the functional profiles predicted via PICRUSt based on 16S rRNA gene sequences require further verification of more accurate metabolic pathways through whole metagenome shotgun sequencing. Third, while the analysis of beta diversity and taxonomic profiles revealed significant differences between salivary and supragingival plaque microbial communities, confirming the existence of distinct microbial habitats, these compositional differences alone cannot completely rule out the possibility of trace saliva contamination during the supragingival plaque collection process. Finally, due to the moderate sample size and the highly conservative compositional differential abundance tools (ALDEx2, DESeq2), strict FDR correction yielded limited significant features, necessitating reliance on nominal p-values for exploratory comparisons alongside the primary LEfSe results.

Despite these limitations, this study successfully bridged the gap between clinical visual assessments and molecular microbiology. By proving that ΔE * and L * values can serve as reliable, objective biomarkers for the pathogenic maturity of dental plaque, our findings provide a scientific foundation for integrating colour-difference-based classification into digital dentistry. Implementing this quantitative framework allows for the precise identification of high-risk plaque areas, enabling personalised, data-driven oral hygiene feedback and targeted preventive interventions in clinical settings.
