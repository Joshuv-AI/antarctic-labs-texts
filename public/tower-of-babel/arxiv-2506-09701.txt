TRIDENT: Temporally Restricted Inference via DFA-Enhanced Neural Traversal
Vincenzo Collura, Karim Tit, Laura Bussi, Eleonora Giunchiglia, Maxime Cordy

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) and other neural architectures have achieved impressive results across a variety of generative and classification tasks. However, they remain fundamentally ill-equipped to ensure that their outputs satisfy temporal constraints, such as those expressible in Linear Temporal Logic over finite traces ( LTL f \text{LTL}_{f} ). In this paper, we introduce TRIDENT : a general and model-agnostic inference-time algorithm that guarantees compliance with such constraints without requiring any retraining. TRIDENT compiles LTL f \text{LTL}_{f} formulas into a Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA), which is used to guide a constrained variant of beam search. At each decoding step, transitions that would lead to constraint violations are masked, while remaining paths are dynamically re-ranked based on both the model’s probabilities and the DFA’s acceptance structure. We formally prove that the resulting sequences are guaranteed to satisfy the given LTL f \text{LTL}_{f} constraints, and we empirically demonstrate that TRIDENT also improves output quality. We validate our approach on two distinct tasks: temporally constrained image-stream classification and controlled text generation. In both settings, TRIDENT achieves perfect constraint satisfaction, while comparison with the state of the art shows improved efficiency and high standard quality metrics.

Introduction

Machine-learning systems – propelled by deep neural networks, transformers, and large language models – have become the de-facto engines behind vision, language, and decision-making applications that impact millions of lives every day. The growing spread of these methods, however, poses several challenges. One is their oftentimes unexpected behavior, as they often offer little assurance that their predictions obey essential domain-specific requirements. This is an open problem that different communities have tried to tackle in orthogonal ways.

Neurosymbolic AI methods express requirements as logical constraints and introduce these constraints either in the loss function
(
Xu et al. 2018
;
Donadello et al. 2017
;
Diligenti et al. 2017
)
(to nudge the predictions to be compliant) or as an additional layer
(
Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz 2021
;
Ahmed et al. 2022
;
Manhaeve et al. 2018
)
(to restrict the output space to the space defined by the constraints, making the model compliant by design). A limitation of these methods, however, is that they do not support temporal constraints, which would enable specifying requirements over the temporality of output sequences. For example, in the text generation domain, temporal requirements may impose that not only a specific set of words should appear (a problem considered by previous methods
(
Lin et al. 2020
)
), but also that they should appear in a given order. The satisfaction of these requirements is essential to enhance consistency and, as later demonstrated in our experiments, it enables a general improvement of the quality of the generated sequences.

In the specific case of LLMs, constrained text generation methods were designed to either (i) constrain the beam search with logical constraints
(
Lu et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2022
)
, (ii) use auxiliary models to steer the generation
(
Krause et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, or (iii) treat constraints as conditioning and sample from the posterior
(
Miao et al. 2019
;
Loula et al. 2025
)
. While these methods can theoretically support temporal requirements, their capability in doing so has not been studied. However, our analyses reveal that there is no method that simultaneously (i) guarantees the satisfaction of the constraints, (ii) does not require finetuning/training an additional model, (iii) has low latency and, most importantly, (iv) retains a high quality of the generated text.

To overcome all these limitations, we propose
TRIDENT
: a model-agnostic method able to constrain any autoregressive model with temporal constraints. Specifically, we express constraints in Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces (
\text{LTL}_{f}
), which offers an adequate balance between expressivity and computational tractability. Given an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula, our method builds an equivalent Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA), which by design represents the set of sequences accepted by the formula. This DFA can be executed on the model outputs to determine whether or not the sequence satisfies the temporal constraint. More importantly, we compile the transition matrix of the DFA and develop a novel Beam Search process guided by this matrix. This enhanced Beam Search guides the model to avoid producing outputs that violate the temporal constraint and enforces its satisfaction by the end of the generation.

We demonstrate the wide applicability of our method through two different use cases: image sequence classification and constrained text generation.
The results on image classification show that we can improve the accuracy while also enforcing constraint satisfaction. We then test our method on the challenging constrained text generation task, and in particular on the CommonGen challenge, and we compare our results with all the state-of-the-art methods (e.g.,
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
). Our results confirm that not only are we able to guarantee the satisfaction of the constraints, but we also improve on or closely match state-of-the-art results on quality metrics. Finally, we show that
TRIDENT
has a low overhead compared to its competitors and produces high-quality results with a significantly lower computational footprint, as evidenced by our shorter runtimes for a fixed number of beams. The code for the experiments will be made available on github and is now in the supplementary materials.

Problem Statement and Notation

Let
f_{\theta}
be a neural network with learnable weights
\theta
. In this paper, we suppose that the network generates a finite sequence of
n
outputs of maximum length
T
(note that
T
might vary from one sequence to another), which we will denote by
x_{1:n}
, with
n\leq T
. This is a reasonable assumption, as in many tasks (including text generation), neural networks are trained with sequences of finite length. Similarly, we refer to the sequence produced up to the
t
-th time step as
x_{<t}
. Given a sequence
x_{1:n}
,
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
represents the
t
-th element of the sequence, while
\mathcal{X}
is the set of possible values that for every sequence and for every
t=1,\ldots,n
,
x_{t}
can take. Finally, we denote by
p_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})
the probability of emitting the output
x_{t}
given the already generated sequence
x_{<t}
.

Our objective is to ensure that, given a neural network
f_{\theta}
, every sequence
x_{1:n}
generated by
f_{\theta}
is compliant with specified temporal requirements. We assume these requirements to be expressed as an
\text{LTL}_{f}

(
De Giacomo and Vardi 2013
)
formula
\phi
over the set of atomic propositions
\mathcal{A}
. Each atomic proposition might correspond to one of the possibles values of the outputs, and hence given a sequence
x_{1:n}
, we have for example that
x_{t}\models a
iff
a\in x_{t}
. However, sometimes—like in the text generation case—we might not want to write the requirements over the outputs, i.e., the tokens, but rather on higher level concepts, i.e., the words. In these cases, each atomic proposition corresponds to a
concept

c\in\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is user-defined. Given the set of all the output sequences
\mathcal{X}^{*}
and the set
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, we assume that there exists an injective function
\nu:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\mathcal{C}^{*}
such that
\nu(x_{1:n})=c_{1:k}
. In the text generation case, indeed, given a sequence of tokens
x_{1:n}
we can always deterministically compute the set of words
c_{1:k}
. In the remaining of the paper, we will assume that each atomic proposition corresponds to a concept, and that if they directly correspond to the outputs then
\nu(x_{1:n})=x_{1:n}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set of atomic propositions.
\text{LTL}_{f}
formulas are generated by the grammar:

\phi::=\top\mid a\mid\lnot\phi\mid\phi\,\land\,\phi\mid\mathsf{X}\,\phi\mid\phi_{1}\,\mathsf{U}\,\phi_{2},\qquad\qquad a\in\mathcal{A}.

\text{LTL}_{f}
is interpreted on
finite traces
, namely finite words (or sequences)
w
on the alphabet of
2^{\mathcal{A}}
.
length(w)
is the length of the trace, with
length(w)\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
w(t)
the element
t
th of the sequence with
0\leq t\leq length(w)-1
.

Definition 2
.

The satisfaction of a formula
\phi
at a given instant
t
is defined via the following set of equations:

\begin{array}[]{l c l c l c l l}w,t\models\top\ \forall t.\ 0\leq t<length(w);\qquad\qquad\qquad\!\!\!\!w,t\models\lnot\phi\iff w,t\not\models\phi;\\
w,t\models a\ \text{ for }\ a\in\mathcal{A}\iff a\in w(t);\qquad\quad\quad w,t\models\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}\iff w,t\models\phi_{1}\ \text{ and }\ w,t\models\phi_{2};\\
w,t\models\mathsf{X}\phi\iff w,t+1\models\phi;\\
w,t\models\phi_{1}\,\mathsf{U}\,\phi_{2}\iff\exists j.\ j<length(w),\ w,j\models\phi_{2}\ \text{ and }\ \forall k.\ t\leq k<j\Rightarrow w,k\models\phi_{1}.\end{array}

We say that a formula
\phi
is
satisfied
in
w
(noted
w\models\phi
) iff
w,0\models\phi
.

More formally, given a neural network
f_{\theta}
, and a set of constraints expressed as an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula
\phi
over the atomic propositions
\mathcal{A}
, we target the problem of ensuring that for every sequence
x_{1:n}
generated by
f_{\theta}
, we have
\nu(x_{1:n})\models\phi
.

Now for a given sequence and its corresponding maximum length
T
, let
\mathcal{X}^{T}
be the set of all sequences of length
T
. Then, the mathematical formulation of the problem as an optimization program is the following:

\displaystyle x^{*}_{1:T}=\underset{x_{1:T}\in\mathcal{X}^{T},\nu(x_{1:T})\models\phi}{\text{argmin}}-\Sigma_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t}),

(1)

which corresponds to the
Maximum A Posteriori
(MAP) inference problem under
\text{LTL}_{f}
constraints.

3.1
DFA Compilation Step

The first step is to compute the DFA equivalent to the formula. Given an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula
\phi
, it is always possible to build a DFA
A_{\phi}
that accepts exactly the set of finite sequences satisfying
\phi

(
De Giacomo et al. 2022
)
. Each DFA can then be represented as a transition matrix.

Definition 3
.

A DFA is a 5-tuple
(Q,2^{\mathcal{A}},\delta,q_{0},F)
, where
Q
is the set of all states,
2^{\mathcal{A}}
is the alphabet of input symbols,
\delta:Q\times 2^{\mathcal{A}}\to Q
is the transition function,
q_{0}
is the initial state, and
F
is the set of accepting states.

Let
w=w_{0},...,w_{n}
be a sequence in the alphabet
2^{\mathcal{A}}
: we say that a DFA
accepts

w
iff there is a sequence of states
s_{0},...,s_{n}
such that
s_{0}=q_{0}
,
s_{i+1}=\delta(s_{i},w_{i+1})
for
0\leq i<n
,
s_{n}\in F
. On the contrary, we will say that a state is a
deadlock state
if it is a non-accepting state from which no accepting state is reachable via any sequence of transitions.

In our settings, we work on finite traces whose length
T
is not known at the time the temporal logic formula is compiled into an automaton. Instead,
T
is provided only later, at runtime, when the trace is generated and checked. As a result, the automaton must be constructed to operate correctly on traces of any finite length, without relying on a specific bound. This places our approach within the framework of Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces (
\text{LTL}_{f}
), rather than Bounded Linear Temporal Logic (B-LTL), where the bound
T
is assumed to be known and is used to constrain the semantics of temporal operators when compiling to an equivalent automaton. For each state
q
, we also calculate the set
\mathcal{R}_{q}
of states that are reachable from
q
, and then associate to each pair
(q,q^{\prime})
, a number representing the distance from
q
to the closest accepting state computed using the Dijkstra algorithm
(
Dijkstra 1959
)
. The full pipeline is summarized in (
2
).

3.2
DFA-Enhanced Beam Search

The intuition behind the DFA-Enhanced Beam Search is twofold: on one hand, we want to ensure that the model does not end up in a deadlock state, while on the other, we want to promote the exploration of the DFA towards accepting states.

At every step
k
, we fetch the current state
q_{k}
and then calculate the most promising next steps to take by simply taking all the pairs
(q_{k},q)
for
q\in\mathcal{R}_{q_{k}}
and checking which are associated with the lowest distance. For each state
q
we will refer to the distance from
q
to the closest accepting state as
d_{q}
. Notice that in the simplest cases we consider the cost of passing through an edge
=1
, but different applications might lead to different weights. For example, in the case of LLMs, we compute the cost of each edge as the minimum number of outputs required to build its transition label’s concepts. It is easy to see that since we only add an output to our beam if its distance to the closest accepting state
d_{q}
is
d_{q}\leq T-k
, then we will never add a deadlock state
q
as
d_{q}=+\infty
.

Recall that we defined an output match function
\nu:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\sl\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Such a function is not bijective, but we can assume the existence of a
canonical
function
\mu:\mathcal{C}^{*}\to\mathcal{X}^{*}
that, given a sequence of concepts
c_{1},...,c_{k}
returns a canonical sequence of
f_{\theta}
’s outputs
x_{1},...,x_{n}
such that
\mu(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We thus define a predicate:

\textsc{compatible}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},c)\iff\exists.\text{\sl suffix}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}.\ \mu(c)=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\text{\sl suffix}

We can now use this predicate to check if a sequence of outputs may lead to a transition in the future. We want to select the best candidate among states that are reachable from the current one. We assume to have a function
\textsc{last}(x_{<t})
that, given the output history, returns the last (possibly incomplete) output suffix. We can thus compute the best candidate quasi-next state as:

\textsc{BestQuasiNextState}(\text{\sl current},x,x_{<t})=

\text{arg}\!\min_{q\in Q}\{d_{q}\mid\exists c\in\mathcal{C}.\ \textsc{compatible}(\textsc{last}(x_{<t})x,c)\land\delta(\text{\sl{current}},c)=q\}

We now need a function that computes the actual next state. Note that, in the case of text generation, we are also interested in generating words that do not appear in the constraint, in order to get a sentence of common sense. We thus extend the set of atomic propositions
\mathcal{A}
with a special value
noMatch
, that represents the fact that the computed word does not match any concept, and we recompute the automaton accordingly. We define a function
\textsc{NextState}(\text{\sl{current}},x,x_{<t})
as follows:

\textsc{NextState}(\text{\sl{current}},x,x_{<t})=\begin{cases}\delta(\text{\sl{current}},c)&\text{if $\nu(\textsc{Last}(x_{<t})x)=c\in\mathcal{C}$}\\
\delta(\text{\sl{current}},\text{\sl{noMatch}})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Ramping Push Toward Accepting States

We introduce a dynamic mechanism for encouraging the model to follow transitions in the DFA that move it closer to an accepting state. At each decoding step
t
and for each beam

i
, we define a coefficient
\alpha_{i}\in[\alpha_{\min},1]
that controls how much we bias the logits of promising outputs toward the maximum logit. The coefficient
\alpha_{i}
increases as the number of decoding steps left
T-t
approaches the current DFA distance to acceptance
d_{i}
. Let:

\textsc{RampPushUp}(\alpha_{min},d_{i},T-t,\gamma)=\alpha_{\min}+(1-\alpha_{\min})\cdot\min\left(1,\left(\frac{d_{i}}{T-t}\right)^{\gamma}\right),

(3)

where
\gamma>0
controls the sharpness of the transition. This schedule ensures that the model explores freely when there is slack (i.e.,
T-t\gg d_{i}
) and ramps up pressure when approaching a deadline. We show the soundness of the resulting algorithm in the next section.

4
Theoretical Analysis

In this section we provide formal statements concerning the computational complexity of our inference tasks, soundness of our DFA-guided inference method, and comparison with existing work (Ctrl-G). Proofs of the stated theorems are reported in the Appendix.

4.1
Soundness and Complexity of DFA-Enhanced Beam Search

We first recall the nature of the problem at hand, explicted in Eq. (
1
), which is a constrained Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) inference problem over the output distribution of an auto-regressive neural model. MAP inference under logical constraints is known to be NP-hard in general, even when the model is a Bayesian network with treewidth 1
(
Roth 1996
)
. Our setting considers more expressive temporal constraints (LTL
f
) and long sequences over symbolic outputs, making exact inference intractable. Thus, we develop a structured approximate inference method that is sound and tractable in practice.

Theorem 1

(Soundness of DFA-Guided Beam Search with Ramping
\alpha
)
.

Let
\phi
be an LTL
f
constraint over a sequence of concepts,
q_{0}
the initial state of the associated DFA, and suppose that:

(i) At decoding step
0
, the initial DFA state
q_{0}
is at distance
d_{0}
from an accepting state with
d_{0}\leq T
, where
T
is the maximum length of the output sequence.

(ii) At decoding step
t
, the scheduler ramps up, for each beam
i
,
\alpha_{i}
as described in Section
3.2
.

(iii) At each step, we prune any transition to a DFA state
q_{k+1}
such that
d_{q_{t+1}}>T-t
.

Then the DFA-guided beam search produces a sequence
x_{1:T}
such that
x_{1:T}\models\phi
, whenever it exists.

While our approach does not guarantee optimality, it ensures that only feasible paths are explored and valid completions are prioritized, yielding a sound and efficient decoding algorithm.

Complexity of DFA Beam Search

The DFA-guided beam search procedure has polynomial-time complexity in the number of decoding steps
T
, beam width
B
, and output space of size
|\mathcal{X}|
, assuming a fixed-size DFA. Specifically, each decoding step processes at most
B\cdot|\mathcal{X}|
candidates, applies DFA filtering and scoring, and retains the top
B
. Thus, overall complexity is
O(T\cdot B\cdot|\mathcal{X}|)
.

Limitations: DFA Compilation Overhead

The LTL
f
formula
\phi
is first compiled into a deterministic finite automaton (DFA), using tools such as LTL2DFA
(
De Giacomo et al. 2022
)
. This compilation step may introduce an exponential blowup in the size of the automaton relative to the size of the formula, as shown by
De Giacomo and Vardi 2015
. While this step is performed only once (offline), it may limit scalability to highly complex constraints with deep temporal structures.

4.2
Theoretical Comparison with the State of the Art

Comparison with Ctrl-G

One way to address this problem, proposed by
Zhang et al. 2024
, is to distill the model
f_{\theta}
into Markov models to hopefully obtain a tractable representation. Unfortunately, the next result shows that even in the simplest case, the constrained MAP inference task is NP-hard.

Theorem 2

(Complexity of Markov Chains MAP Inference with Unary Constraint)
.

MAP inference on Markov Chains with a unary equality constraint is NP-hard.

Thus, even for the simplest class of probabilistic graphical models—first-order Markov Chains—and with minimal constraints (e.g., a constraint of the form
y_{\beta}=t
), MAP inference remains NP-hard. While MAP inference for Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) is solvable in polynomial time via the Viterbi algorithm, this tractability relies on the assumption that the emission matrix is dense. In practice, however, this is often not the case for LLMs, whose output distributions are extremely sparse. Consequently, approximating LLMs with HMMs
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
does not, in general, guarantee tractability for constrained decoding, even for very simple logical constraints.

Comparison with Sampling Methods

Sampling-based approaches
(
Miao et al. 2019
;
Ahmed et al. 2025
;
Loula et al. 2025
)
enforce constraints by modifying or resampling sequences guided by logic or learned distributions. While they offer flexibility and can handle complex, soft constraints, they often lack generality or require expensive sampling or approximations. In contrast, our method provides a structured and efficient solution for a wide range of constraints (including temporal logic). Finally, we note that while both MAP inference and exact constrained sampling are intractable in general, in many cases sampling is actually in the #P class of problems
(
Valiant 1979
;
Roth 1996
)
.

Related Work

Neurosymbolic AI.

Neurosymbolic AI

(
)

is a growing filed of interest in machine learning due to the ability of its methods to reconcile neural perception with symbolic reasonings
(
Raedt et al. 2020
;
d’Avila Garcez and Lamb 2023
)
. While the field is quite vast, the neurosymbolic solutions that are the closest to our method are those that try to incorporate symbolic background knowledge in the form of constraints into neural models
(
Dash et al. 2022
;
Giunchiglia et al. 2022
)
. These methods can be divided in two groups: those that give a probabilistic semantics to the constraints
(
Manhaeve et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2018
;
van Krieken et al. 2023
)
and those that give a fuzzy semantics
(
Donadello et al. 2017
;
Giunchiglia et al. 2024
;
Diligenti et al. 2012
)
. Another way to categorize the methods in the field is by whether they incorporate the constraints in the loss function
(
Donadello et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2018
;
Fischer et al. 2019
;
van Krieken et al. 2022
)
or they change the final output space of the model
(
Manhaeve et al. 2018
;
Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz 2021
;
Ahmed et al. 2022
;
Pryor et al. 2023
;
Hoernle et al. 2022
;
Misino et al. 2022
)
. The first can be used to nudge via the loss the network to satisfy the constraints, while the second can guarantee the constraints satisfaction.

Constrained Text Generation.

The work done in constrained text generation has been developing on multiple orthogonal axes.
1. Search based decoding.
These approaches act at inference time and constrain the beam search with logical constraints. For example, in
(
Lu et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2022
)
, the authors decide which beams to expand taking into account not only the LLM’s prediction but also the degree of satisfaction of the constraints reported for every sequence.

2. Auxiliary Classifier Guidance.
The works that follow this line aim to guide the base model with an auxiliary one. GeDi
(
Krause et al. 2021
)
, FUDGE
(
Yang and Klein 2021
)
and NADO
(
Meng et al. 2022
)
steer generation with class-conditional or token-level predictors. However, they provide no hard guarantees and might require task-specific supervision. On the contrary, GeLaTo
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
provides the guarantee as it distills the base LLM into a hidden-Markov model, which computes the probability that the remaining suffix can still contain a set of keywords and then multiplies this value into the LLM’s logits. Ctrl-G
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
is the recent extension of GeLato, which allows for any constraint that can be captured in a DFA (instead of propositional logic).
3. Probabilistic Sampling
treats constraints as conditioning and samples from the posterior. This line was pioneered by
(
Miao et al. 2019
)
with Metropolis–Hastings token edits that provide unbiased samples under lexical constraints. This idea has then been further explored in the works of
Ahmed et al. 2025
;
Loula et al. 2025
.

7
Conclusions

We introduced
TRIDENT
, a DFA-guided method to constrain the output of a Neural Network. By using
\text{LTL}_{f}
, we are able to address temporal requirements, thus widening the set of possible case studies w.r.t. other existing methods. Our algorithm is proved to be sound, while not optimal, and can easily be adapted to different applications. Comparison with the state of the art shows significant improvements in both efficiency and quality of the generated output. The introduced method can be used for multiple purposes, with a positive societal impact, like detoxification of LLMs’ output. On the other hand, we are also aware that our method may be misused and a potential issue could be its use to enforce instead the usage of toxic words by LLMs.

Appendix A
Proofs

A.1
Soundness of the
TRIDENT
algorithm

We prove here that our method is sound (Theorem
1
).

Proof of Theorem
1
. (Soundness of the
TRIDENT
algorithm).

We prove that the algorithm is sound by induction on the length
t
of the sequence.
More precisely, we propose the following inductive hypothesis

\mathcal{H}_{t}:\text{for all beams }(x_{<t},s,q)\in\mathcal{B}_{t},d_{q_{t}}\leq T-t.

The base case is
t=0
: the initial beam only contains the prompt
x_{0}
, the initial state of the DFA
q_{0}
, and the distance
d_{0}
from an accepting state. By the hypothesis of the theorem, we have
d_{0}\leq T
and thus
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is verified.

Let us now assume that
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is true, for
t\in[1,T-1]
.
At line 12 of the algorithm:

•

if a transition leads to a state
q^{\prime}
such that
d_{q^{\prime}}>T-t
then it is impossible to complete an accepting sequence within the remaining steps. But this choice is excluded by setting
Z[i,x]\leftarrow-\infty
.

This ensures that no sequence in
\mathcal{B}
is extended to a non-accepting sequence.

Indeed, at line 22:

•

we choose the top
k
beam extensions among the valid ones with respect to their score (denoted by
Z
in the pseudo-code), i.e. among those having distance
d_{q^{\prime}_{t+1}}\leq T-(t+1)
, since invalid outputs have
-\infty
as a score. In the case where we have less than
k
valid beam extensions, then we pad the remaining beams with copies of the valid beams. We know that at least one valid extension exists for each beam, since we assume that
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is true.

Thus we have shown that
\mathcal{H}_{t+1}
is true whenever
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is true. By induction,
\mathcal{H}_{T}
is true.

At time
T
, the algorithm returns the best sequence among those included in
\mathcal{B}_{T}
. But, by
\mathcal{H}_{T}
, every sequence in
\mathcal{B}_{T}
has distance
0
from an accepting state, and hence terminates in an accepting state. Thus the algorithm is sound.
∎

A.2
Complexity of MAP Inference on Markov Chains

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We show NP-hardness by providing a polynomial-time reduction from the
Constrained Shortest Path (CSP) problem with uniform arc lengths
to the
MAP inference problem on time-homogeneous Markov chains under a unary constraint
.

Step 1: CSP Formulation.

Consider a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where each arc
(i,j)\in E
has an associated cost
c(i,j)\geq 0
and a length
b(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}
. We focus on the special case where all arcs have the same length, specifically
b(i,j)=1
for all
(i,j)\in E
. Given vertices
s,t\in V
(source and target) and an integer bound
\beta\geq 1
, the CSP problem seeks a path of exact length
\beta
from
s
to
t
that minimizes total cost:

\min_{x_{1:\beta}\in V^{\beta},\;x_{1}=s,\;x_{\beta}=t,\;(x_{k},x_{k+1})\in E}\sum_{k=1}^{\beta-1}c(x_{k},x_{k+1}).

This uniform-length CSP variant is known to be NP-hard
[
Erzin et al. 2022
]
.

Step 2: MAP Inference Problem Formulation.

In the MAP inference setting, we have a time-homogeneous Markov chain over a state space
S
, an initial distribution
\mu
, and transition matrix
P
. Given a unary constraint specifying the state at time
\beta
, i.e.,
X_{\beta}=t
, the MAP inference problem is:

x_{1:\beta}^{*}=\argmax_{x_{1:\beta}\in S^{\beta},\;x_{\beta}=t}\mu(x_{1})\prod_{k=1}^{\beta-1}P(x_{k+1}\mid x_{k}).

Step 3: Reduction from CSP to MAP Inference.

Given a CSP instance
(G,c,s,t,\beta)
, construct a MAP inference instance as follows:

1.

State space.
Define the state space as
S=V\cup D
, where
D=\{d_{i}\mid i\in V\}
is a set of newly introduced dummy states, with one dummy state per original vertex.

2.

Initial distribution.
Set
\mu(x)=\delta_{x,s}
, ensuring the chain always starts at the source vertex
s
.

3.

Transition probabilities.
First, define an extended cost function
\tilde{c}:V\times S\to[0,+\infty]
by:

\tilde{c}(i,j)=\begin{cases}c(i,j),&(i,j)\in E\\[4.0pt]
-\log(Z_{\max}-Z_{i}),&j=d_{i}\\[4.0pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where for each vertex
i\in V
, we set:

Z_{i}=\sum_{j\in V}e^{-c(i,j)},\quad Z_{\max}=\max_{i\in V}Z_{i}.

With these definitions, we set the transition probabilities for all
i\in V
as:

P(j\mid i)=\frac{e^{-\tilde{c}(i,j)}}{Z_{\max}},\quad j\in S.

This ensures that all transitions from states in
V
are properly normalized, as:

\sum_{j\in S}P(j\mid i)=\frac{Z_{i}+(Z_{\max}-Z_{i})}{Z_{\max}}=1,\quad\forall i\in V.

4.

Absorbing states.
Explicitly set the target vertex
t
and all dummy states
d_{i}
as absorbing states:

P(t\mid t)=1,\quad P(j\mid t)=0\text{ for }j\neq t,\quad\text{and}\quad P(d_{i}\mid d_{i})=1,\quad P(j\mid d_{i})=0\text{ for }j\neq d_{i}.

Thus, any path entering a dummy state or
t
remains there indefinitely.

5.

Unary constraint.
Set the unary constraint as
\phi\equiv X_{\beta}=t
.

Step 4: Correctness and Equivalence.

Consider any path
x_{1:\beta}
that satisfies the constraint
X_{\beta}=t
. If the path ever enters a dummy state
d_{i}
, it will remain there indefinitely (absorbing), contradicting the constraint
X_{\beta}=t
. Thus, any feasible path must stay entirely within
V
.

For feasible paths
x_{1:\beta}\in V^{\beta}
, we have:

\displaystyle P(x_{1:\beta})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\beta-1}\frac{e^{-\tilde{c}(x_{k},x_{k+1})}}{Z_{\max}}=Z_{\max}^{-(\beta-1)}\cdot e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{\beta-1}\tilde{c}(x_{k},x_{k+1})}\cdot\delta_{x_{1},s}

\displaystyle\underset{(x_{1:\beta}\models\phi)}{\operatorname{=}}Z_{\max}^{-(\beta-1)}\cdot e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{\beta-1}c(x_{k},x_{k+1})}

Hence, the MAP objective is exactly equivalent to minimizing the total cost:

\argmin_{x_{1:\beta}\in S^{\beta},\;x_{\beta}=t}-\log P(x_{1:\beta})=\argmin_{x_{1:\beta}\in V^{\beta},\;x_{1}=s,\;x_{\beta}=t,\;(x_{k},x_{k+1})\in E}\sum_{k=1}^{\beta-1}c(x_{k},x_{k+1}),

recovering precisely the original CSP objective.

Step 5: Complexity and Conclusion.

The construction described above clearly runs in polynomial time (adding one dummy state per vertex and computing simple exponentials). Thus, we have demonstrated a polynomial-time reduction from the CSP problem (known NP-hard) to the MAP inference problem under unary constraints in a Markov chain.
∎
