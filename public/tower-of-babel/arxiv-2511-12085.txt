Explainable Transformer-Based Email Phishing Classification with Adversarial Robustness
Sajad U P

Abstract
Phishing and related cyber threats are becoming more varied and technologically advanced. Among these, email-based phishing remains the most dominant and persistent threat. These attacks exploit human vulnerabilities to disseminate malware or gain unauthorized access to sensitive information. Deep learning (DL) models, particularly transformer-based models, have significantly enhanced phishing mitigation through their contextual understanding of language. However, some recent threats, specifically Artificial Intelligence (AI)-generated phishing attacks, are reducing the overall system resilience of phishing detectors. In response, adversarial training has shown promise against AI-generated phishing threats. This study presents a hybrid approach that uses DistilBERT, a smaller, faster, and lighter version of the BERT transformer model for email classification. Robustness against text-based adversarial perturbations is reinforced using Fast Gradient Method (FGM) adversarial training. Furthermore, the framework integrates the LIME Explainable AI (XAI) technique to enhance the transparency of the DistilBERT architecture. The framework also uses the Flan-T5-small language model from Hugging Face to generate plain-language security narrative explanations for end-users. This combined approach ensures precise phishing classification while providing easily understandable justifications for the model’s decisions.

II
Related Work

Phishing attacks have evolved alongside the development of cybersecurity. Over time, these attacks have grown in sophistication in step with the evolution of the Internet. To improve the effectiveness of phishing email detection and prevention mechanisms, various researchers have suggested a wide range of techniques. Phishing email detection and classification has been an active research area since the mid-1990s
[
8
]
.

II-A

Evolution of Phishing Email Detection

Early approaches largely relied upon static blacklists and rule-based filtering systems
[
9
]
, which offered little flexibility in response to changing phishing tactics. Subsequently, researchers and businesses began using classical machine learning (ML) methods. Androutsopoulos et al.
[
10
]
provided one of the first thorough evaluations of the Naive Bayes classifier for anti-spam filtering. The authors conducted a pioneering experimental comparison of Naive Bayesian and keyword-based anti-spam filtering techniques.

The transition to DL marked a significant advancement in phishing detection capabilities. Kyaw et al.
[
11
]
provided a systematic review of DL techniques for malicious email detection. The authors highlighted the superior performance of neural network architectures compared to traditional ML. The review indicates that the ability of detection models to adapt to new phishing behaviors is a major area for improvement.

Lee et al.
[
12
]
introduced D-Fence, a flexible and comprehensive phishing email detection system. This system features a multi-modular architecture with specialized learning modules for structure analysis, text analysis, and URL analysis that collectively detect phishing attempts. This modular design allows this framework to maintain high detection accuracy and a robust approach to defending against diverse phishing attacks.

II-B

Transformer-Based Deep Learning Architectures

The introduction of transformer architectures
[
13
]
revolutionized natural language processing tasks, including phishing classification. Devlin et al.
[
14
]
introduced BERT, an innovative pre-trained language model that leverages the transformer architecture with a bidirectional attention mechanism. Unlike classic left-to-right or right-to-left models, BERT captures contextual information from both directions simultaneously.

Jamal et al.
[
15
]
advanced this line of research by designing improved transformer-based phishing detectors with DistilBERT and RoBERTa under an LLM framework. This work demonstrated the effectiveness of transformer-based models for detecting phishing, spam, and ham emails using LLM approaches. Similarly, Uddin et al.
[
16
]
proposed an explainable transformer-based model that combines LLM approaches with masked language modeling techniques. The authors utilized the RoBERTa transformer model for enhanced phishing email detection while maintaining interpretability using a hybrid explanation approach, LITA (LIME-TransformerAttribution).

Analyzing the high computational demands of large models like BERT, Sanh et al.
[
4
]
introduced DistilBERT. It is a compressed version of BERT that retains around 97% of BERT’s language-understanding performance while reducing its size by 40% and achieving approximately 60% faster inference. This lightweight approach is more practical for real-time applications. However, like BERT, they remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks that manipulate textual inputs.

II-C

Adversarial training has indeed become a crucial approach for strengthening the robustness of AI-based models. Liu et al.
[
17

introduced adversarial training methodologies designed for LLMs, establishing techniques to improve model robustness against malicious perturbations. The authors demonstrate that adversarial pre-training can simultaneously improve robustness in language models such as BERT. This work provided the theoretical foundation for applying adversarial training to transformer-based classification systems.

Gholampour et al.
[
3

[
18

[
19
]
conducted a thorough analysis of adversarial attacks against spam filters, showing that DL models are highly vulnerable to crafted perturbations. The authors highlighted the critical need for robust training methodologies in phishing mitigation systems.

II-D

Interpretability in Cybersecurity

A growing focus has been placed on XAI and LLM-based interpretability techniques that improve transparency in the cybersecurity domain. Charmet et al.
[
6
]
conducted a comprehensive literature survey on XAI for cybersecurity, establishing the theoretical framework and practical requirements for XAI in security applications. The survey discusses about how these methods can be used to enhance threat detection and response.

Al-Subaiey et al.
[
20

provided an interpretable web-based AI platform for phishing email detection. Their research addresses the critical need for user trust by integrating XAI techniques, specifically LIME, to provide clear justifications for the model’s predictions. This work demonstrated the practical implementation of XAI in email security systems. Recent advances in combining LLM with XAI were demonstrated by Lim et al.
[

II-E

Research Gaps and Motivations

Acknowledging the progress made by prior investigations and research in phishing detection, adversarial robustness, and model interpretability, there is a clear gap in the unified application of these methods. Most research either prioritizes detection or classification accuracy without considering adversarial resilience. Certain studies focus on interpretability but fail to provide clear, user-oriented explanations to support decision-making. This paper addresses this gap by proposing a unified and novel architecture for phishing email classification and explanation. The proposed system enhances reliability, adversarial robustness, and interpretability in the cybersecurity domain.

III
Methodology

III-A

Data Acquisition and Splitting

The experiments used the phishing email dataset available on Hugging Face (ID: zefang-liu/phishing-email-dataset)
[
22
]
. The dataset contains 18,650 email samples. Each sample includes the raw email text and a categorical label indicating the email type. The label distribution for email type is 60.71% for safe emails and 39.29% for phishing emails.

Labels:
The dataset consists of two primary fields with Email Text and Email Type, with Email Type further categorized into Safe Email and Phishing Email. For modeling purposes, these categories were encoded as 0 (Safe Email) and 1 (Phishing Email).

III-B

Preprocessing Pipeline

To prepare the data for the proposed model and ensure ethical handling of samples, a preprocessing pipeline was utilized. This began with text normalization, converting all email content to lowercase, followed by:

Class Imbalance Handling and Splitting:
The dataset exhibits a minor class imbalance. To mitigate biased model training and ensure reliable evaluation, stratified splitting was employed during the data partitioning process. This technique maintained the original class distribution (60.71:39.29 ratio) across the train, validation, and test splits, with 70% train, 15% validation, 15% test.

Sensitive Information Masking:
The system uses a hybrid PII (Personally Identifiable Information) masking approach for data privacy and enhanced model robustness. It relies on the spaCy NER library to accurately identify and anonymize context-sensitive data, backed by regular expressions (regex) for structured data. Using spaCy NER, PERSON entities (e.g., names) were replaced with [NAME] and CARDINAL entities (e.g., account numbers) were replaced with [ACCOUNT]. Regular expressions were used to mask PHONE and EMAIL entities as [PHONE] and [EMAIL].

Example transformations:

Original content: “Hi Alexis, your bank account 1234567890 has been suspended. Submit your PIN immediately to renew your access.”

Masked content: “Hi [NAME], your bank account [ACCOUNT] has been suspended. Submit your PIN immediately to renew your access.”

Tokenization:
The preprocessed text was tokenized using the DistilBertTokenizerFast, which is a fast, Rust-based tokenizer from the Hugging Face transformers library.

III-C

Model Architecture

The proposed framework has two main components. A transformer-based classification module and a user-centric natural-language explanation module. After data acquisition, the email contents undergo a preprocessing procedure. The token sequences are then concatenated to produce a unified input representation for the transformer encoder. The encoder outputs a unified text feature vector that is subsequently passed through a final classification layer to generate the prediction. The whole process is reinforced with an adversarial training component, which boosts the model’s resilience to slight malicious perturbations.

Following classification, the classified instance is directed to the XAI subsystem. This system initiates with LIME to extract numerical feature importance, which is then mapped to a language model-based short prompt tailored to a specific phishing tactic. The system leverages a dedicated Language model Hugging Face Transformers pipeline utility, which implements a lightweight, instruction-fine-tuned model to create the final, understandable explanation. This unified process supports transparent and interpretable decision-making. Fig.

III-C
1
DistilBERT for Phishing Email Classification

The core classification framework is the DistilBERT architecture, which is chosen for its robust performance, lightweight nature, and computational efficiency. This model served as the backbone for the sequence classification task, distinguishing between two output classes: Phishing and Legitimate. Input emails were first processed using the DistilBertTokenizerFast, which segments the text into subword tokens (or sub-lexical units) and adds necessary special markers. These tokens were transformed into contextualized embeddings, which, when augmented with positional encodings, were processed by DistilBERT’s transformer layers. The multi-head self-attention structure enabled the model to establish a strong bidirectional context for each token. It retained fine-grained word-to-word relationships needed to identify scattered phishing markers, regardless of their position in the text. This base model was subsequently strengthened using the FGM, an adversarial perturbation technique applied during adversarial training.

III-C
2
FGM-based Adversarial Training

Ensuring robustness against subtle textual perturbations was an important concern in the process of improving model resilience. For email content, these adversarial manipulations mimic real-world noise like character replacement, misspellings, and typos. In simple terms, minimal alterations can mislead standard classification systems. The FGM was chosen due to its effectiveness for BERT-family models in adversarial pre-training. FGM requires only one forward-backward pass per batch and perturbs embeddings directly rather than text. This avoids tokenization inconsistencies that affect text-level methods on limited GPU setups, making FGM the pragmatic choice for this study.

FGM was employed as a dynamic in-batch adversarial training technique for the DistilBERT model. This approach avoids the static generation of full adversarial text examples and focuses on strengthening the model’s decision boundary in the embedding space. Particularly, for every clean training batch, the total loss is computed as a combination of the standard classification loss (
L_{\mathrm{clean}}
) and an adversarial loss (
L_{\mathrm{adv}}
). The adversarial loss is calculated by applying a minimal, carefully computed perturbation (
\delta
) to the input word embeddings.

In plain terms,
\delta
is derived from the model’s sensitivity to small embedding changes. The system determines the direction in embedding space that most increases the classification loss, converts that direction to a unit vector, and scales it by a small scalar
\varepsilon
(the perturbation magnitude). This produces a tiny, directed change to the embeddings, which is then used to compute
L_{\mathrm{adv}}
by forwarding the model with the perturbed embeddings.

1.

The model computes
L_{\mathrm{clean}}
and the gradient with respect to the embeddings.

2.

An adversarial perturbation (
\delta
) is constructed from the gradient direction and scaled to be small.

3.

The adversarial loss,
L_{\mathrm{adv}}
, is computed using the perturbed embeddings.

4.

The final training step is optimized against the combined objective, the total loss

L_{\mathrm{total}}=L_{\mathrm{clean}}+\lambda\,L_{\mathrm{adv}},

where
\lambda
is a weighting factor controlling the contribution of the adversarial loss.

This procedure makes the model equally confident in its prediction for the clean and minimally perturbed input embeddings, thus significantly increasing its resilience to minor input noise and decreasing its vulnerability to character-level adversarial attacks.

III-C
3
Hybrid Explainability Framework

To attain both model transparency and practical user insights, a two-stage hybrid explainability framework was integrated into the classification pipeline. This framework leverages the local XAI explanation method, LIME, to identify the most important features. Following this, the system utilizes a Hugging Face language model to convert these feature importance scores into a concise security narrative.

Model’s Decision using LIME

LIME identifies the most important words or tokens that caused the model to classify the email as either ”PHISHING” or ”LEGITIMATE”. This provides a plain, token-level breakdown of the model’s complex decision, enhancing the trust of the security tool.

Plain Language Explanation Generation

LIME output was used to generate clear, human-readable explanations through the integration of a Flan-T5-small language model. The key steps in this process are as follows:

Feature Extraction:
The top 8 most influential tokens identified by LIME were extracted, along with the prediction label and confidence score.

Text summary Generation:
The extracted information was structured and provided as input to a locally deployed lightweight Hugging Face model, Flan-T5-small. The language model’s role was to transform the structured input into a brief, non-repetitive natural-language explanation referencing common phishing cues for clarity. Deterministic decoding (temperature=0) was used for Flan-T5-small, and to prevent hallucination, each generated explanation is grounded by including the most influential tokens as supporting evidence.

III-D

Experimental Setup

All experiments used DistilBERT (distilbert-base-uncased) fine-tuned on the zefang-liu/phishing-email-dataset. Training was performed on Google Colaboratory (free tier) using a GPU accelerator (often an NVIDIA Tesla T4) with PyTorch and CUDA. A per-device batch size of 4 was used with gradient accumulation set to 8, resulting in an effective batch size of approximately 32, and the model was trained for 5 epochs with a learning rate of
3\times 10^{-5}
.

For adversarial robustness, the Fast Gradient Method (FGM) was applied with a perturbation magnitude of
\varepsilon=0.001
, which offered the best trade-off between stability and robustness. The adversarial loss weight
\lambda
was set to 0.5, balancing contributions from clean and adversarial losses as:

L_{\mathrm{total}}=L_{\mathrm{clean}}+0.5\,L_{\mathrm{adv}}

The baseline model converged slightly faster, while the FGM variant required extra gradient computations but yielded improved robustness. For the explainability component, local feature attributions were produced with LIME, and effortless plain language explanations were generated using the lightweight Flan-T5-small model via the Hugging Face pipeline.

III-E

Model Evaluation and Assessment

III-E
1
Evaluation of Standard Classification Performance

Models were evaluated on the independent test set using five standard binary classification metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). Precision, Recall, and F1-Score were specifically computed for the Phishing class (label 1).

Evaluation Metric Equations:

Phishing is considered the positive class (
P=1
) for this binary classification task. The performance is evaluated using the following formulas.

Let
TP
(True Positives),
TN
(True Negatives),
FP
(False Positives), and
FN
(False Negatives) represent the counts derived from the confusion matrix.

The total number of evaluated samples, denoted as
N_{\text{Total}}
, is given by:

N_{\text{Total}}=TP+TN+FP+FN

(1)

The evaluation metrics are defined as follows:

•

Accuracy:

The proportion of correctly classified samples among all evaluated samples.

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N_{\text{Total}}}

(2)

•

Precision:

The ratio of correctly predicted positive instances to all predicted positives.

\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

(3)

•

Recall (Sensitivity):

The ratio of correctly predicted positive instances to all actual positives.

\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

(4)

•

F1-Score:

The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, balancing both metrics.

\text{F1-Score}=2\times\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(5)

•

Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC):

Measures the overall ability of the model to distinguish between classes across all thresholds.

III-E
2
Evaluation of Robustness Against Noise

The improved resilience afforded by the FGM adversarial training was quantified by measuring the performance of both the Baseline and the DistilBERT + FGM models against synthetic character-level adversarial perturbations. This dedicated robustness evaluation was performed to determine the model’s ability to withstand common phishing evasion tactics. The analysis was conducted by creating three distinct adversarial test sets from the original clean test data. Instead of utilizing a predefined external attack model, a custom, heuristic noise injection methodology was employed to corrupt the test set synthetically. This approach simulates realistic typographical errors and deliberate evasion tactics.
For each test sample, noise was introduced by randomly applying a combination of character-level operations to a predefined percentage of the email text. This selection process involved choosing an n% of characters in each email and applying one of the operations:

•

Deletion: Removing the character (e.g., “account” to “acount”).

•

Substitution: Replacing the character with a visually similar homoglyph (e.g., “o” to “0”).

•

Insertion/Swap: Adding a random character adjacent to the selected position or performing a swap with an adjacent character (e.g., “financial” to “finanxcial” or “fianncial”).

In this project, the model’s resilience was evaluated by introducing random character perturbations at three distinct noise levels: 5%, 10%, and 20% of the total character count.

Example transformations:

Original: ”Urgent: Verify Your Account Immediately!”.

10% noise: ”Urgent: Ver1fy Your Acount Immedaitely!” (3 characters affected).

V
Discussion

V-A

Interpretation of Results

The comparison proves that incorporating adversarial training with FGM substantially improves the performance and resilience of DistilBERT. The proposed model achieved higher accuracy, F1-score, and AUC, indicating improved detection of deceptive emails with fewer false positives and negatives. Under increasing input noise, the adversarially trained model achieves significantly better accuracy, showing strong resilience to both accidental typos and intentional alterations. Explainability further validated the model’s decisions. The plain language explanations align with known phishing indicators, providing understandable insights for end users, and this helps them make better decisions regarding how to act upon the email received.

V-B

Challenges and Limitations

This study shows promise, but has several limitations to address:
While BERT-style models improve text detection accuracy, they remain computationally heavy and complex due to their large number of parameters. Training and deploying such models on large-scale, real-time email traffic may require substantial hardware resources, which could limit adoption in organizations that have limited infrastructure and computational capacity.

Next, cyber adversaries are continually advancing, and systems might need to be regularly revised and retrained in order to remain effective in real-world use cases. Also, generating adversarial instances in a controlled setting differs from the complexity of real-world attacks, which might limit practical applicability.

In addition, though human-readable explanations improve interpretability, they also pose several challenges, such as ambiguity, potential linguistic bias, and lack of contextual understanding. These factors can impact the clarity of explanations and user trust in the system.

Finally, the developed framework depends on publicly available datasets, which is advantageous for reproducibility, yet may not fully capture the diversity and sophistication of cutting-edge phishing campaigns. Also, the dataset is limited to English text and does not include multilingual and image-based attacks.

V-C

Ethical Considerations

Ethical responsibility is very important in the cybersecurity domain, particularly in the case of phishing, where sensitive user data is usually involved. In this study, I relied on a publicly available phishing email dataset from Hugging Face (zefang-liu/phishing-email-dataset), which is shared under the GNU LGPL v3.0 license
[
22
]
, to ensure transparency and reproducibility. All sensitive information in these datasets was masked and processed in accordance with existing privacy regulations and laws. The adversarial attacking method FGM used in this study is strictly for defensive purposes to enhance resilience against malicious phishing attacks and must not be repurposed for harmful activities. While the proposed adversarial training enhances defensive robustness, it raises the dual-use risk. Adversaries could apply similar hardening techniques to malicious LLMs, enabling them to generate more evasive phishing content that bypasses detection systems. Besides, the explainability mechanism aims to enhance transparency and trust. The developed framework allows users to understand why an email has been flagged as suspicious or legitimate by providing reasoning. However, these results must be verified for accuracy and reliability.

V-D

Failure Case Analysis and Error Patterns

Analysis of the system’s performance reveals two primary error patterns. False negatives (FN) arise with highly refined spear-phishing or AI-generated messages that mimic benign language and avoid obvious trigger words, allowing malicious content to pass undetected. Conversely, false positives (FP) occur when genuine urgent communications, such as legitimate alerts or financial notifications, that share high-urgency keywords are misclassified as suspicious.

VI
Conclusion

The email phishing classification and explanation framework created in this study represents a significant breakthrough in the evolving cybersecurity domain. By intentionally combining adversarially hardened transformer-based classifiers with explainable plain language outputs, the framework ensures robust classification and human-interpretable decision making. The current working version was tested using thorough experiments. The system effectively identifies various types of email phishing attempts, maintains robustness against adversarial manipulations, and produces understandable explanations for users. These justifications from the system assist users in making better decisions and reducing the challenges presented by conventional ”black-box” security alerts. This framework provides a useful entry point for creating transparent and robust threat detection and classification systems. The design principles proposed here extend beyond email phishing classification and explanation. Future studies could adapt this approach to various security domains.

VI-A

Future Work

One potential direction is to integrate a continuous adaptive learning approach, such as an adaptive adversarial-training loop. This would provide durable robustness in real-world settings through the ability of the proposed model to evolve dynamically to respond to sophisticated cyber threats.

Another approach is to generalize the proposed framework to other high-priority domains like cloud/IoT security, healthcare cybersecurity, critical infrastructure protection, financial fraud security, etc.

Future research should focus on developing user-specific interactive explanation mechanisms to further improve clarity and utility for better decision-making.

Finally, Federated Learning (FL) enables multiple organizations to collaborate and train models without exchanging sensitive data, ensuring privacy while improving scalability and flexibility.
