Consensus statement for the diagnosis and management of optic neuritis
Authors: Swati Phuljhele, Rohit Saxena, Rohit Bhatia, Dheeraj Khurana, Taru Dewan, Suman K Meena, Lavanya Gandepalli, Virendra Sachdeva, Jaspreet Sukhija, Digvijay Singh, Ankur Sinha, Rebika Dhiman, Ambika Selvakumar, Mahesh Kumar, Gazella B Warjri, Akanksha Mulay, Rashmin Gandhi, M V Padma Srivastava, Navin Jayakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This consensus statement provides a practical, step-by-step framework designed by 19 national specialists to standardize the care of optic neuritis. Recognizing that clinicians handle these cases differently depending on local resources, the guidelines offer a clear roadmap for distinguishing true optic nerve inflammation from its common clinical mimics. The primary focus is on identifying early “red flags,” optimizing neuroimaging protocols, and using targeted cell-based antibody testing to pinpoint the exact cause of the disease. Beyond immediate diagnosis, the paper outlines clear strategies for managing acute episodes with high-dose therapies, handling patients who do not respond to initial steroids, and establishing structured, long-term monitoring plans to track visual recovery. Because delayed or incorrect treatment can lead to permanent, irreversible nerve damage, this document serves as a reliable tool to help ophthalmologists make quick, safe, and effective clinical decisions for patient care.

Management of optic neuritis requires a structured, guideline-based approach due to its clinical complexity. In India, limited access to neuroimaging, serological testing, and subspecialty care—coupled with evolving insights into disease subtypes—has resulted in variability in diagnosis and treatment. This often creates uncertainty about the scope of investigations, therapy choices, and follow-up duration.

To address these gaps, an expert panel convened under the Indian Neuro-Ophthalmology Society (INOS) developed consensus recommendations. This document provides comprehensive guidance on clinical evaluation, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, investigations, management, and follow-up, drawing from current evidence and expert opinion. Timely recognition and management are essential to prevent permanent visual loss and enable early intervention for underlying systemic conditions. While diagnosis is often clinical, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and antibody testing clarify etiology and subtypes. These recommendations aim to standardize optic neuritis care across India.

Methodology

INOS hosted a hybrid meeting of neuro-ophthalmology and neurology experts on May 18, 2025. The panel included specialists from hospitals nationwide, with experience ranging from 5 to 35 years (median: 18 years).

A team of four experts conducted a premeeting literature review and presented findings before the experts. The full panel then deliberated each aspect—clinical presentation, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, investigations, treatment, and follow-up—integrating published data, clinical experience, and protocols to forge consensus recommendations.

Post meeting, the core team drafted the document and circulated it for review. All panelists read and approved the final version.

Consensus Statement

Definition of Optic Neuritis

Optic neuritis refers to inflammation of the optic nerve due to diverse inflammatory or demyelinating processes.[1] It typically manifests with acute or subacute visual loss, periocular pain (especially on eye movement), and impaired color vision.[12]

Most cases link to central nervous system demyelinating diseases, including multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD).[13] Optic neuritis is often the initial presentation of these systemic conditions. Less commonly, it arises from central nervous system or systemic infections or as a feature of autoimmune diseases.

Clinical Presentation of Optic Neuritis

Background

In routine practice, optic neuritis diagnosis relies primarily on clinical assessment through detailed history and structured ophthalmic examination. Presentation varies by age, laterality, and etiology, so recognizing these patterns is essential for accurate diagnosis.

Consensus recommendations

The panel recommends actively assessing these features in all suspected cases:

Age at onset: Optic neuritis can occur at any age but reconsider the diagnosis at extremes (childhood or elderly) due to higher odds of alternative causes.[2]

Vision loss: Acute or subacute onset, peaking over 7–10 days; unilateral or bilateral; ranges from mild blur to severe impairment. Vision should be quantified with Snellen or logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) chart at distance and near; document best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA).

Pain: Is common in retrobulbar neuritis (RBN) (especially MS-related), triggered by eye movements; may feel like dull ache or headache.[12] Technique: Ask about pain onset, severity (e.g., VAS scale), and provocation (e.g., gaze direction).

Visual function deficits: Reduced brightness sense, color desaturation using Ishihara plates or Farnsworth-Munsell 100-hue test, and contrast sensitivity using Pelli-Robson chart.[1] Visual field assessment using confrontation or automated (e.g., Humphrey 24-2) for scotoma mapping often reveal central or centrocecal scotoma.[4]

Pupillary response: Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) in unilateral/asymmetric cases; sluggish responses in bilateral involvement.[12] Technique: Perform swinging flashlight test in dim light; grade RAPD (mild/moderate/severe) by constriction asymmetry.

Fundus examination: Optic disc edema in papillitis; normal disc in retrobulbar neuritis. Assess using a 90D/78D lens via slit-lamp for stereoscopic view; assess disc hyperemia, elevation, peripapillary hemorrhages, or exudates; photograph if possible.

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy: Essential to exclude anterior segment inflammation or vitritis; check for retrolental cells. Scan anterior chamber (cells/flare grading), lens, and vitreous with narrow beam; use + 28D lens for posterior vitreous assessment.

Additionally, the consensus panel recommends broadening the clinical history to identify potential triggers and underlying risks. Clinicians should specifically investigate any recent infections or vaccinations that may have preceded the onset of symptoms. Additionally, a thorough review of the patient’s medical history is essential, with particular emphasis on any known systemic autoimmune diseases or prior, transient neurological events. Finally, the history-taking process must evaluate potential environmental or occupational toxic exposures, alongside a detailed family history of autoimmune disorders.

Differential diagnoses of optic neuritis

Consensus and recommendations

The panel notes that certain atypical features should immediately trigger an investigation into alternative diagnoses. Red flags include extreme ages of presentation (under 3 or over 70) and a sudden profound loss of vision (no perception of light (PL) at the onset of symptoms). Clinicians should also look out for progressive worsening of vision past 2 weeks, severe headaches, or normal pupillary reactions. Finally, findings like arcuate or altitudinal field defects, sectoral disc edema, anterior segment inflammation, neuroretinitis atypical for demyelination, or initial disc pallor (unless recurrent) strongly warrant further evaluation.

Investigations in optic neuritis

The diagnosis of optic neuritis is primarily based on a detailed clinical history and thorough ophthalmic examination. However, a structured investigative approach is essential to confirm the diagnosis, identify the underlying etiology, detect associated systemic or neurological disorders, and guide long term.

Serological Investigations

Background

Antibody testing is crucial for identifying immune-mediated optic neuritis subtypes.

Consensus and recommendations

Testing for aquaporin-4 (AQP4) and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibodies using a cell-based assay is recommended in all patients with suspected optic neuritis as the presence of the antibodies has diagnostic and therapeutic implications.[3] It may be reasonable to get other autoimmune workup including antinuclear antibody (ANA), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs), and extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) profiles, especially if systemic features suggest broader autoimmunity.

Ocular Investigations

Background

The ocular investigations may have a limited role in diagnosis of optic neuritis; they are imperative to document visual deficits, track recovery, and aid prognostication. Central or centro-cecal scotoma is the most common field defect pattern seen in optic neuritis, though altitudinal, arcuate defects, or generalized depression may also be seen.[4] Visual field testing also helps in quantifying visual field deficits and monitoring disease progression or recovery, although the pattern of defects can vary considerably.[4] In the acute phase, optical coherence tomography (OCT) may demonstrate optic disc swelling, while chronic stages often show thinning indicative of axonal loss. An inter-eye difference greater than 4% or more than 4 µm in macular ganglion cell – inner plexiform layer (mGCIPL), or greater than 5 µm in peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), supports a diagnosis of unilateral optic neuritis.[1516171819202122232425]

In clinical settings where neuroimaging is not readily available, empirical treatment should not be attempted. Instead, primary eye care providers must immediately refer the patient to a higher eye care or neurology center equipped with imaging facilities.

Management of Optic Neuritis

Acute management

Background

Highdose intravenous corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of treatment for acute optic neuritis and are known to hasten visual recovery.

Consensus and recommendations

Adults with acute optic neuritis should receive intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) 1 g/day for five consecutive days as a single dose or divided doses.[228] Infusion should be given slowly over 30–45 minutes duration under observation.In pediatric patients, the recommended dosage of IVMP at 30 mg/kg/day.[29]In cases where optic neuritis is secondary to systemic conditions such as sarcoidosis or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), acute management remains the same and long-term treatment must be tailored to the underlying etiology.Infectious causes of optic neuritis require appropriate antimicrobial therapy directed at the causative pathogen.

Role of Oral Steroids

Consensus and recommendations

The panel strongly discourages the use of oral corticosteroids alone at standard doses in the acute management of optic neuritis, reported the increased risk of recurrence.[28] In seropositive optic neuritis (NMOSD or MOGAD), a prolonged oral steroid taper following intravenous therapy is advised to reduce the risk of relapse. Tapering schedules should be individualized based on clinical response and overall recovery.

Management of steroidnonresponsive optic neuritis

Background

In the panel’s experience, a subset of patients—particularly those with NMOSD or MOGAD—may show suboptimal response to corticosteroids.

Consensus and recommendations

If there is no significant visual improvement after the initial course of intravenous corticosteroid therapy, early referral for consideration of plasma exchange (PLEX) or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is recommended. Given the often suboptimal or unpredictable response to steroids in NMOSD and MOGAD, second-line therapy is generally warranted and should be considered early in the treatment course following steroid administration.

FollowUp of Optic Neuritis

Consensus and recommendations

The panel recommends close monitoring of visual recovery during the acute phase. In cases of severe visual impairment or poor recovery at 1 week, urgent neurological consultation is advised.

Further evaluations should be conducted in 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year, with the frequency adjusted according to the patient’s clinical course. At the 3-month visit, a comprehensive assessment apart from visual acuity should include visual field testing, contrast sensitivity, color vision evaluation, and optionally OCT to assess structural recovery of the optic nerve and retina.[1]

Recurrent Optic Neuritis

Consensus and recommendations

The panel defines recurrent optic neuritis as a new clinical episode occurring more than 30 days after the onset of a previous attack.[27] All the essential investigations mentioned for the first attack should be repeated in case of every recurrent episode. Simultaneously extensive investigations should be done to look for other causes of optic neuropathy.

Acute management generally follows the same principles as the initial episode. Long-term immunomodulators in consultation with neurologists will be required in cases of recurrent attack.

Optic Neuritis in Pregnancy

Consensus and recommendations

The panel agreed that during pregnancy, contrast during MRI may be avoided; however, the standard treatment protocol of IVMP for optic neuritis generally remains applicable.[3031] However, special consideration should be given in cases of NMOSD, where close coordination with obstetrics is critical to ensure maternal and fetal safety while initiating or maintaining immunosuppressive therapy.

Prognosis of Optic Neuritis

Background and consensus

The panel acknowledged that optic neuritis associated with MS-related optic neuritis generally carries a favorable visual prognosis. Long-term visual recovery in MS-related optic neuritis is typically good, with approximately 72% of patients maintaining a visual acuity of 20/40 or better at 15 years following disease onset.[32]

In contrast, among other demyelinating disorders, NMOSD is associated with the poorest visual outcomes, largely due to more severe axonal injury. Early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is therefore critical to preserve vision and reduce relapse rates. MOGAD in comparison may follow either a monophasic or relapsing course. Although patients often show a good initial response to corticosteroids, relapses are common and may require long-term immunomodulatory treatment.

Conclusion

Optic neuritis presents a complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in ophthalmic practice. Its varied etiologies, phases of presentation, and potential for long-term disability necessitate a structured, multidisciplinary approach. These guidelines aim to streamline evaluation and management for ophthalmologists by incorporating current evidence and expert consensus, ultimately optimizing patient outcomes.
