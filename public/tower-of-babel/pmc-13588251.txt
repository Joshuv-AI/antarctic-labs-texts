Human Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells and Their Extracellular Vesicles in Neurological Disorders: Mechanisms, Delivery Strategies, and a Translational Roadmap
Authors: Zhihao Zhao, Xiaobing Jiang, Wenwen Gao, Xin Man, Fei Li, Zhiwei Ding, Fan Yi, Kaiming Xie, Liang Zhang, Haikang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurological disorders—including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, neurodegenerative disease, chronic neuropathic and inflammatory pain, peripheral nerve injury, and selected psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders—remain major causes of death and disability. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (hUC-MSCs) and their extracellular vesicles (EVs) are being investigated as regenerative and immunomodulatory products, but translation is constrained by product heterogeneity, limited direct clinical evidence, uncertain CNS exposure, and incompletely validated potency assays.Main BodyThis narrative review critically compares hUC-MSC and hUC-MSC-EV evidence by indication, model, route, dose, mechanism, outcome, and clinical stage. It distinguishes direct product-specific findings from contextual evidence involving other MSC products. Mechanistic signals include innate immune reprogramming, attenuation of inflammasome activity and oxidative stress, neurovascular-unit protection, and support of angiogenesis, synaptic remodeling, and axonal repair. We evaluate delivery-route limitations, biomaterial-assisted retention, manufacturing and characterization requirements, and a staged translational roadmap aligned with established MSC and EV standards.ConclusionThe evidence base is promising but predominantly preclinical, heterogeneous, and weighted toward cell-based studies. Direct clinical evidence is currently concentrated in spinal cord injury and is insufficient to establish efficacy. Progress requires product-specific reporting, dose-normalized biodistribution, validated mechanism-linked potency assays, manufacturing comparability, multicenter replication, and randomized trials with prespecified target-engagement and functional endpoints.

Introduction

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (hUC-MSCs) can be obtained from post-partum tissue without an invasive harvest and can be expanded efficiently. Comparative studies indicate that umbilical cord or Wharton’s jelly MSCs often proliferate more rapidly and display lower expression of selected histocompatibility markers than adult bone marrow MSCs, although source-specific functional differences and donor effects preclude assuming universal superiority.1,2 MSC identity should therefore be established using accepted stromal-cell criteria and product-specific functional testing rather than tissue source alone.3

A substantial component of MSC activity is paracrine. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) carry proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that can alter recipient-cell phenotypes. In accordance with MISEV2023, EV is used here as the umbrella term; “small EV” is used when size is the demonstrated property, and “exosome” is retained only when the cited study used that term or supported an endosomal origin.4 EV products are non-replicating and may offer practical advantages for storage, filtration, and engineering, but they do not automatically eliminate immunologic, biodistribution, off-target, or manufacturing risks.5,6 The cell-free rationale is therefore a product-development hypothesis, not evidence of clinical equivalence to hUC-MSCs.

This review first compares disease-specific cell and EV evidence, then evaluates route-dependent CNS access and engineering, product definition and manufacturing, evidence quality, and clinical trial design. Review Approach and Evidence Selection

This is a narrative, evidence-mapping review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis. The update used targeted PubMed searches and backward citation checking through 31 July 2026 with combinations of “human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells”, “extracellular vesicles”, “exosomes”, individual neurological indications, “biodistribution”, “manufacturing”, “potency”, and “clinical trial”. Priority was given to primary studies directly evaluating hUC-MSCs or hUC-MSC-derived EVs, first-in-human studies, randomized trials, and consensus or position documents. Studies of other MSC sources were retained only when they informed delivery, trial design, or regulatory interpretation and are labeled as indirect contextual evidence. Cell and EV findings were extracted separately by indication, model, route, dose when reported, mechanism, outcome, and clinical stage (Table 1). Because heterogeneous models and outcome measures precluded valid quantitative pooling, the synthesis emphasizes product attribution, study design, consistency, and translational limitations. Therapeutic Applications in Neurological Disorders

Representative preclinical and early clinical evidence across major neurological indications is summarized in Table 1.

Neuropathic and Inflammatory Pain

hUC-MSC-derived exosomes reduced neuropathic and inflammatory pain in rodent models, but the evidence remains preclinical. Exosomal miR-146a-5p regulated the TRAF6/NLRP3 axis in microglia, suppressing pyroptosis and supporting autophagy.8 In a separate cell-product study, intravenous hUC-MSCs reduced the microglial marker Iba-1, promoted sciatic-nerve repair, and attenuated mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity.7 These findings should not be treated as interchangeable because route, dose unit, biodistribution, and product composition differ.

A second EV study implicated Rsad2-dependent inhibition of TLR2/MyD88/NF-κB signaling in spinal microglia.9 Together, References 7 and 9 support microglial regulation by hUC-MSC-derived EV preparations, whereas Reference 8 supports a cell-based effect. None establishes durability, a clinically scalable EV dose, or comparative efficacy between cells and EVs.

Brain Injury and Cerebrovascular Diseases

Anti-Inflammatory and Anti-Pyroptotic Mechanisms

VCAM-1-positive hUC-MSCs reduced NLRP3-associated pyroptosis in a rat cerebral-infarction model.10 MG53 was evaluated as an exogenous adjunct protein that protected hUC-MSCs from inflammatory injury and enhanced the cell therapy; it was not identified as endogenous hUC-MSC or EV cargo.11 This distinction is made explicit in Figure 2.

Antioxidative and Mitochondrial Repair

Combination therapy with curcumin and hUC-MSCs modulates the AKT/GSK-3β/β-TrCP/Nrf2 axis to promote anti-inflammatory microglial polarization, exerting both anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects. This strategy significantly reduces cerebral edema and infarct volume following acute ischemic stroke.12 Furthermore, mitochondrial transplantation from hUC-MSCs rescues neuronal apoptosis, attenuates astrocyte proliferation and microglial activation, restores ATP production, and alleviates oxidative stress, thereby improving motor function in traumatic brain injury (TBI) models.13

Angiogenesis and Blood–Brain Barrier Repair

hUC-MSC-derived exosomal circDLGAP4 promotes angiogenesis after ischemia via the miR-320/KLF5 axis, enhancing the viability of cerebral microvascular endothelial cells.20 In addition, secreted TNFR1 upregulates tight junction protein ZO-1, decreases IL-6 levels, and restores blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, reducing cerebral edema.14

Epigenetic and Neuroregenerative Regulation

hUC-MSCs modulated BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling in an aged perioperative neurocognitive-disorder model,15 while dedifferentiated hUC-MSCs were linked to SDF-1α-dependent neural repair in hypoxic-ischemic brain injury.16 The EPO-engineered MSC study used a modified MSC product and an in vitro ischemia/hypoxia model; it is therefore contextual engineering evidence rather than direct evidence for unmodified hUC-MSCs or their EVs.17

Clinical Translation

In a rodent subacute-stroke model, intracerebral delivery of GMP-grade hUC-MSCs provided local neurorestorative evidence but does not establish superiority over intravenous delivery in humans.18 A compassionate-use cerebral-palsy study reported motor outcomes after hUC-MSC treatment but lacked randomization and blinding.19 These studies support feasibility signals rather than efficacy conclusions.

Taken together, hUC-MSCs and their exosomes regulate inflammation, oxidative stress, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis through multi-target mechanisms, offering translational potential for ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative disorders. Combination therapy or genetic engineering may further enhance therapeutic efficacy.10–20

Importantly, despite the rapidly expanding preclinical literature for hUC-MSCs/EVs, high-quality human evidence in many CNS indications remains limited. Therefore, lessons from rigorously conducted MSC-based trials in stroke, multiple sclerosis (MS), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) provide a pragmatic template for designing hUC-MSC/EV studies that can withstand regulatory and peer-review scrutiny. Across neurological cell-therapy trials, safety signals have generally been more reproducible than efficacy. The MASTERS randomized trial evaluated multipotent adult progenitor cells rather than hUC-MSCs.49 The early open-label SB623 study reported improvements after stereotactic implantation of modified bone marrow stromal cells,50 but the subsequent 163-participant randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled phase 2b study (NCT02448641) did not meet its prespecified primary efficacy endpoint.51,52 The negative phase 2b result limits inference from the uncontrolled early study and illustrates why external MSC trials are used here only as design context.

Collectively, these trials underscore several design principles that are directly transferable to hUC-MSC/EV programs: (i) predefine a biologically coherent treatment window (acute immune modulation vs chronic repair), (ii) match delivery route to the target compartment (systemic immunomodulation vs local neurorestoration), (iii) incorporate target-engagement biomarkers (CSF, imaging, or circulating immune signatures) alongside functional endpoints, and (iv) mitigate heterogeneity through stratification (severity, lesion location/volume, inflammatory phenotype) and responder analyses.

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)

Anti-Inflammatory and Immunomodulatory Effects

Although the MFGE8 study used a neuromyelitis optica model rather than traumatic SCI, it provides contextual evidence that hUC-MSC-secreted MFGE8 can attenuate inflammation through the ITGB3/NF-κB axis.55 Direct SCI evidence includes EVs from IL-4-preconditioned hUC-MSCs enriched in miR-21-5p, which targeted PDCD4, promoted M2 macrophage polarization, suppressed secondary injury, and enhanced recovery.30 Intrathecal hUC-MSC transplantation also downregulated lactylation-related genes in a preclinical SCI model.56

Neural Circuit Reconstruction and Excitatory/Inhibitory Balance

hUC-MSC-derived BDNF upregulates GABA_A receptor β3/γ2 subunits and the KCC2 transporter, restoring excitatory/inhibitory balance in injured neurons and facilitating functional recovery.21 Exosomal miR-199a-3p and miR-145-5p inhibit Cblb/Cbl-mediated TrkA ubiquitination, activating the NGF/TrkA/Akt/Erk pathway to promote axonal growth and enhance locomotor recovery.31

Angiogenesis and Tissue Repair

hUC-MSC sheets increased local cell retention and were associated with angiogenesis, reduced glial scarring, and neuronal regeneration in experimental SCI.22 This finding is consistent with biomaterial-assisted strategies: dual-cross-linked gelatin hydrogels supported neural differentiation and functional recovery,23 whereas ROS-responsive nanoparticles were used with hUC-MSCs to suppress ferroptotic injury.24 These approaches may improve residence time at focal lesions, but their added value must be established by matched controls, material-degradation studies, and scalable manufacturing.

Optimization Strategies

Route and dose studies suggest that local or intrathecal delivery can increase spinal exposure relative to systemic infusion, but the apparent optimum is model- and product-dependent.25,26 Curcumin, hydrogels, and ROS-responsive nanoparticles have been used to improve cell survival, retention, or complementary pathway coverage.23,24,27 For EV products, 2-mercaptoethanol pretreatment increased yield in one preclinical study, but altered production conditions require comparability testing.32 Direct human evidence is limited to a first-in-human, single-arm Phase I intrathecal hUC-MSC-EV study focused on safety and a small phase 1/2 pilot study of repeated hUC-MSC administration.28,33 Reference 39 is a rodent safety/efficacy study and should not be described as a multicenter clinical trial.29

Neurodegenerative Diseases

Targeting Pathological Protein Aggregates

Engineered exosomes loaded with BACE1 siRNA and berberine (BBR) reduce amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, improve cognitive function, and attenuate neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) models.38 Similarly, neprilysin (NEP)-modified hUC-MSC-derived exosomes enhance Aβ degradation and restore cognition.39 Hybrid exosome–nanoliposome particles suppress α-synuclein fibrillation and toxicity, showing promise as a delivery platform for Parkinson’s disease (PD) therapeutics.40

Neuroprotection and Regeneration

MG53 protein activates the Nrf2 pathway, reversing senescent phenotypes in hUC-MSCs, reducing Aβ deposition and Tau phosphorylation, and promoting neurogenesis in AD models.34 hUC-MSC-derived hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) downregulates hyperphosphorylated Tau, restores spine density, and promotes synaptic plasticity.35 Fe3O4@PDA nanoparticle-labeled hUC-MSCs enhance brain targeting, increase BDNF expression, and promote hippocampal neurogenesis, improving cognitive deficits.36

Anti-Inflammatory and Antioxidative Effects

hUC-MSC-derived exosomes carrying AP2A1/AP2B1 modulate synaptic vesicle cycling and inhibit neuroinflammation. Cytokine cooperation is observed: HGF primarily drives Aβ/Tau clearance, while BDNF and TNFR1 contribute to anti-inflammatory and antioxidative responses, collectively ameliorating cognitive dysfunction.37,41 Cell-free strategies, such as engineered exosomes combined with nanocarriers, overcome BBB limitations and provide low-immunogenicity alternatives for AD and PD therapy.34–41

Psychiatric Disorders

Anti-Inflammatory and Immunomodulation

In a chronic unpredictable mild stress model, hUC-MSCs modulated astrocyte–microglia crosstalk through complement C3 signaling and promoted an anti-inflammatory microglial phenotype.42 A separate 2025 study also reported reduced neuroinflammation in depression- and anxiety-like behavior.43 These are cell-based preclinical findings and do not establish a C3a–C3aR mechanism for hUC-MSC-derived EVs.

Synaptic and Barrier Repair

Exosomes from heat-shock-preconditioned hUC-MSCs are enriched in HSP70, suppressing TLR4/p65 signaling, reducing neuroinflammation, enhancing synaptic protein expression, and improving sleep-deprivation-induced cognitive deficits.46 hUC-MSCs also preserve BBB integrity, promote cortical neuron growth, and reverse social deficits in valproic acid (VPA)-induced autism models.44

Cell-Free Strategies

Intranasal EV delivery is mechanistically attractive because labeled MSC-derived small EVs can reach multiple brain regions in rodents, including along perivascular pathways.47 Direct preclinical hUC-MSC-exosome evidence in a valproic-acid autism model also reported brain delivery and improvements in autism-like behaviors after intranasal administration.48 However, fluorescence-based distribution does not by itself establish intact-vesicle delivery, a clinically scalable dose, or efficacy of a standardized human product.

Engineering and Delivery Strategies for CNS Targeting

Route Selection, Biodistribution, and Neuroanatomical Access

Delivery route changes both exposure and plausible mechanism. Intravenous MSCs are practical for systemic immunomodulation but can be transiently retained in the pulmonary circulation, whereas invasive intrathecal or intraspinal delivery increases local access at the cost of procedure-related risk. For EVs, a mouse biodistribution comparison using 2.0 × 10^10 particles found predominant liver and spleen signal after intravenous administration and relatively greater brain-region signal after intranasal administration; labeling limitations and species scaling prevent direct conversion of these observations into a human dose.57 Intranasal hUC-MSC small EVs were detected across rodent brain regions within hours, but absolute recovered dose, intact-vesicle confirmation, and inter-batch reproducibility remain incompletely resolved.47

Accordingly, delivery studies should report administered particle number, protein mass, producer-cell equivalent where relevant, labeling chemistry and free-label controls, organ-level recovery over time, and a dose-normalized brain-to-peripheral-organ exposure metric. These are reporting targets rather than universal release thresholds; neither MISEV2023 nor current ISEV position documents endorse a single numerical brain-delivery cutoff.4,5

Targeting and Engineering to Improve CNS Tropism and Potency

Engineering can alter tropism or potency through parental-cell preconditioning, EV surface modification, or cargo loading. The reviewed examples include BACE1 siRNA/berberine-loaded EVs, neprilysin-modified hUC-MSC EVs, exosome–nanoliposome hybrids, and nanoparticle-labeled hUC-MSCs.36,38–40 These interventions are product modifications, not evidence for the native product. Each requires controls for loading efficiency, unbound cargo, altered biodistribution, and whether the engineered attribute remains stable after storage. In focal SCI, cell sheets and hydrogels may improve retention, but matched head-to-head comparisons are needed to separate scaffold effects from cell or EV effects.22,23

Dose, Schedule, and Combination Strategies

Reported doses are expressed as cells/kg, total cells, particle number, protein mass, or producer-cell equivalents, which prevents simple cross-study comparison. Timing also reflects different objectives: early administration may target secondary inflammation, whereas repeated or delayed treatment may target repair. Future studies should compare routes and schedules within the same product lot, prespecify primary outcomes, and report dose–exposure–response rather than dose alone.6,25,26 A functional-unit concept based on a quantitative response can support EV potency comparisons, but acceptance limits must be validated for the intended mechanism and manufacturing process rather than borrowed from another product.6

Product Definition, Manufacturing, and Characterization

hUC-MSC Identity and Release Criteria

For hUC-MSC products, the ISCT criteria provide a baseline identity framework—plastic adherence, a characteristic surface-marker profile, and trilineage differentiation—but are not sufficient as clinical release criteria.3 A development package should additionally define donor eligibility, cord region, passage and population-doubling limits, culture medium, ancillary materials, viability, sterility, mycoplasma, endotoxin, genomic stability as appropriate, and a mechanism-linked potency assay. Lot comparability should be demonstrated after manufacturing changes rather than inferred from matching identity markers.

EV/Exosome Isolation, Purity, and Stability

For EV products, terminology and characterization should follow MISEV2023: describe the source and separation method, quantify particles and relevant non-vesicular material, demonstrate positive and negative markers, and use orthogonal methods for size, morphology, and composition.4 Clinical translation additionally requires a defined manufacturing process, raw-material controls, sterility strategy, storage and freeze–thaw stability, dose definition, and qualified release tests for identity, purity, quantity, and biological activity.5,58 Particle-to-protein ratio is informative but is not a universal purity or potency threshold. A matrix of orthogonal functional assays may be needed when one assay cannot represent the proposed mechanism.6

Concrete release specifications should therefore be set prospectively during process development. Examples include validated ranges for particle concentration and size distribution, residual host-cell or process impurities, sterility/endotoxin, and a quantitative mechanism-relevant bioassay such as suppression of stimulated microglial cytokine release, improvement of endothelial-barrier resistance, or neurite outgrowth. The manuscript does not propose universal numerical cutoffs because such limits require product-specific analytical validation and clinical correlation.5,6,58

Evidence Quality, Reporting Standards, and Translational Roadmap

Mechanism-Linked Evidence Grading

The proposed evidence grade was derived from recurring limitations in the reviewed studies: product mixing, single-pathway association without perturbation, variable dose units, limited biodistribution, and sparse replication. Tier 1 denotes association (phenotype plus pathway change); Tier 2 requires product-specific target engagement with an appropriate negative control; Tier 3 requires loss-of-function or blocking evidence; and Tier 4 adds rescue, dose–exposure–response, independent replication, and a qualified potency assay. This hierarchy complements rather than replaces MISEV2023 reporting, ISCT identity criteria, and ISEV/EVOLVE manufacturing guidance.3–6,58

From Bench to Bedside: Patient Stratification and Trial Design Considerations

Patient selection should match a prespecified mechanism and treatment window. Acute injury studies may prioritize safety, inflammation, and early target engagement, whereas chronic repair studies require durable functional endpoints and control of rehabilitation exposure. External MSC trials illustrate why uncontrolled improvement cannot establish efficacy: the favorable phase 1/2a SB623 signal was not confirmed on the primary endpoint in the later sham-controlled phase 2b study.50–52

Trial readiness improves when the product, route, and biomarker are linked. Immunomodulatory programs can prespecify circulating and cerebrospinal-fluid immune markers; neurovascular programs can combine perfusion or barrier imaging with serum markers; EV programs should pair quantitative biodistribution or pharmacokinetics with pharmacodynamic readouts. A biomarker is supportive only if its assay, timing, and relationship to the proposed mechanism are specified in advance.

Endpoints should be hierarchical: safety and feasibility first, then target engagement, then clinically meaningful function. Randomization and blinded assessment are essential; sham control may be required for invasive administration when ethically and operationally justified. Small early-phase studies should report uncertainty and avoid efficacy language unless designed and powered for efficacy.

Product consistency is inseparable from trial interpretation. Release criteria, stability, batch comparability, administered dose, and deviations must be recorded for every lot. Potency assays should reflect the intended mechanism and be prospectively linked to exposure and clinical endpoints, while post hoc responder analyses should remain explicitly exploratory.5,6,58

A Practical Roadmap for the Next Phase of the Field

Figure 3 operationalizes a staged roadmap derived from the evidence gaps identified in Tables 1 and 2. Stage 1 fixes indication, injury window, and product definition. Stage 2 locks manufacturing and analytical release methods. Stage 3 establishes route-specific biodistribution, safety, and a mechanism-linked potency assay. Stage 4 requires independent or multicenter replication with harmonized outcomes. Stage 5 advances to a randomized clinical study with prespecified target engagement and functional endpoints. Progression is conditional: a failure in identity, comparability, exposure, safety, or replication returns the program to the relevant earlier stage. The added value over existing standards is this neurological, decision-gated linkage of product attributes to CNS exposure and indication-specific trial design; it does not replace the standards themselves.3–6,58

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

hUC-MSCs and hUC-MSC-derived EVs show convergent preclinical signals in neuroinflammation, neurovascular protection, and repair, but the certainty of evidence is limited by heterogeneous products, small studies, inconsistent dose units, incomplete biodistribution, and frequent mixing of cell and EV findings. Direct human evidence is sparse and concentrated in early SCI studies; it supports feasibility and short-term safety, not efficacy.28,33 Evidence from other MSC products is informative for trial design but cannot be used as proof of hUC-MSC or hUC-MSC-EV benefit, as illustrated by the discordance between early and later SB623 trials.50–52

The central translational conclusion is therefore conditional: a candidate should advance only when its identity and manufacturing process are locked, route-specific CNS exposure and safety are quantified, a mechanism-linked potency assay predicts lot activity, and the effect replicates independently before efficacy testing. MISEV2023, ISCT, and ISEV/EVOLVE documents already define much of the reporting and product-development foundation; the proposed roadmap adds a neurological decision sequence that links those requirements to injury window, target engagement, and clinical endpoint selection.3–6,58 Future updates should expand direct comparative evidence, include negative and inconsistent animal findings, and avoid class-level claims when the supporting experiment used a specific cell, engineered EV, scaffold, or adjunct product.
