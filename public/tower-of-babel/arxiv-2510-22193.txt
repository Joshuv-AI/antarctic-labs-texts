(Approximate) Matrix Multiplication via Convolutions
Kevin Pratt, Yahel Uffenheimer, Omri Weinstein

Abstract
A longstanding open question in algorithm design is whether “combinatorial" matrix multiplication algorithms—avoiding Strassen-like divide-and-conquer—can achieve truly subcubic runtime n 3 − δ n^{3-\delta} . We present an O ⁡ ( n 2.89 ) O(n^{2.89}) -time exact algorithm, which only sums convolutions in ℤ m k \mathbb{Z}_{m}^{k} (multivariate polynomial multiplications) via FFT, building on the work of Cohn, Kleinberg, Szegedy and Umans (CKSU’05). While the algorithm avoids recursion, the asymptotic speedup arises only for impractically large matrices. Motivated by practical applications, we use this baseline to develop a new framework for fast approximate matrix multiplication (AMM), via low-degree approximations of the CKSU polynomials. We show that combining the aforementioned algorithm with black-box linear sketching already breaks the longstanding linear speed-accuracy tradeoff for AMM (Sarlos’06, Clarkson-Woodruff’13, Pagh’11, Cohn-Lewis’00), achieving 1 r 1.1 ​ ‖ 𝐀 ‖ F 2 ​ ‖ 𝐁 ‖ F 2 \frac{1}{r^{1.1}}\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2} error in O ⁡ ( r ​ n 2 ) O(rn^{2}) -time. Our main result is a low-degree approximation scheme for the CKSU polynomials, based on a Fourier-concentration lemma, yielding substantially smaller error in the distributional setting where 𝐀 , 𝐁 \mathbf{A},\mathbf{B} come from an i.i.d product-distribution ; For random Gaussian matrices, this practical AMM algorithm attains smaller error than the best rank- r r SVD of the output matrix 𝐀𝐁 \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} , in time O ⁡ ( r ​ n 2 ) O(rn^{2}) . This is a substantial improvement over iterative Krylov subspace methods for low-rank approximation. Our theoretical and empirical results suggest the possibility of replacing MatMuls with sums of convolutions in LLM training and inference.

Introduction

Matrix multiplication (
MatMul
) is a fundamental operation across all fields of science and technology, and underlies most industry-scale applications. Above all,

The discovery of

Alas, FMM algorithms are unlikely to be practical on any imaginable hardware, as they

[

]
and lead to highly asymptotic runtimes, earning them the infamous name “galactic algorithms"
[

]
. Another drawback of FMM algorithms in the context of data-driven applications, is that they are

In attempt to capture practical algorithms, a concurrent line of work has focused on “combinatorial" MatMul algorithms

While the term “combinatorial" is open to interpretation, it is ultimately meant to capture practical algorithms (more on this below).
An outstanding open question in the field is whether combinatorial algorithms can achieve truly subcubic runtime, even for multiplying

A recent breakthrough result by Abboud et al.

Bypassing

Technical Overview

Below we provide a high-level streamlined overview of the ideas behind the proofs of the main results.

Matrix Multiplication via Convolutions

The idea of using group convolutions for computing Matrix Multiplication was first presented in the seminal work of Cohn and Umans
[
CU03
]
. Instead of directly multiplying
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, they proposed embedding the matrices as polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x]_{/(x^{n^{3}}-1)}
(identify
x^{n^{3}}
with
1
), choosing a specific set of monomials. In particular, set
P_{\mathbf{A}}(x)=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\mathbf{A}_{i,j}x^{-n^{2}i+nj}
and
P_{\mathbf{B}}(x)=\sum_{j,k\in[n]}\mathbf{B}_{j,k}x^{k-nj}
(all powers are taken modulo
n^{3}
). Let
P_{\mathbf{C}}=P_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot P_{\mathbf{B}}
denote the polynomial product. Suppose
P_{\mathbf{C}}(x)=\sum_{s\in[n^{3}]}c_{s}x^{s}
, then it is easy to see that

c_{k-n^{2}i}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\mathbf{B}_{j,k}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{i,k}.

(2.1)

Hence, by computing a single polynomial multiplication via the
Fast Fourier Transform
(FFT), which is equivalent to the cyclic convolution of the polynomials’ coefficient vectors, one can recover the product matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. This is true because the group-embedding used in (
2.1
) satisfies the property that, for any
(i,k)
, the equation
(k^{\prime}-k)+n(j-j^{\prime})+n^{2}(i-i^{\prime})=0
has
exactly

n
solutions, given by
i^{\prime}=i,k^{\prime}=k
and
j^{\prime}=j\in[n]
. In other words, only the desired
“diagonal"
terms
\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{B}_{jk}
in the convolution are aggregated to the monomial
x^{k-n^{2}i}
, whereas the undesired “cross-terms" (
i^{\prime}\not=i,k^{\prime}\not=k,j\neq j^{\prime}
) are aggregated into other “garbage" monomials which we simply ignore when recovering
\mathbf{C}
from
c
. Cohn and Umans termed this property the
Triple Product Property
(TPP).

Unfortunately, the convolution algorithm in (
2.1
) is quite useless, as the polynomial
P_{\mathbf{A}}
has degree
n^{3}
, hence the convolution (via FFT) requires
\Omega(n^{3}\log n)
time, slower than naïve matrix multiplication(!).
As such,
[

generalized the group-convolution approach (

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
.
2
2

2

(\sum_{g\in G}\mathbf{A}_{g}g)\cdot(\sum_{h\in G}\mathbf{B}_{h}h)=\sum_{f\in G}(\sum_{gh=f}\mathbf{A}_{g}\mathbf{B}_{h})f

For general matrices

5

5

For example, a polynomial which has exactly one non-zero monomial will have a perfectly uniform spectrum (uniform in magnitude).

Nevertheless, we prove that for random matrices coming from “nice enough" product distributions

2.2

3

is supported on the monomials
X_{1}=\left\{x^{i}y^{j}\mid 0<i,j\leq m-1\right\}

X_{2}=\left\{y^{i}z^{j}\mid 0<i,j\leq m-1\right\}
. The indicator of the support,
\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{supp}(a)}

\mathbf{1}_{X_{1}}+\mathbf{1}_{X_{2}}
(because
X_{1}
and

are disjoint). These are polynomials obtained by summing all monomials in
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
respectively. It is easily shown that the Fourier transform of
\mathbf{1}_{X_{1}}
at a frequency

\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{X_{1}}(\xi)=m^{-3/2}\times\begin{cases}(m-1)^{2}&\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=0,\\
-(m-1)&\xi_{1}=0\text{ xor }\xi_{2}=0,\\
1&\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\not=0.\end{cases}

The proof of this fact uses the geometry of complex roots of unity, which are the basic building block of Fourier analysis of finite groups (see
section 3
). A symmetric result holds for
X_{2}
and for the indicator of
\mathrm{supp}(b)
. Thus
almost all of the Fourier mass of
\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{supp}(a)}
resides on facets of the cube
, where
\xi_{i}=0
for some coordinate

the error bound in
Theorem 1.4
on
r
, as most of the energy is already captured by the thinnest slabs (of width
1
). It is also important to note that this specific structure of slabs lends itself to fast computation of the partial transform and its inverse.

To formalize this intuition, we need to bound the expected ratio between the
\ell^{2}
-mass (energy) outside of the retained frequencies, and the total energy product
\|a\|_{2}^{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}
. The first step of the proof is a concentration bound of the denominator
\|a\|_{2}^{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}
, which reduces the problem to bounding the ratio of expectations instead. The concentration bound is just an application of Hoeffding’s inequality, since
\|a\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|b\|_{2}^{2}
are just sums of i.i.d. random variables. We pay for this with an added exponentially small error term.

Given this reduction, the proof proceeds as follows. Our goal is to compute the algorithm’s expected
non-normalized
error. The first step is showing that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}(\xi)\cdot\overline{\hat{a}(\eta)}]=C_{a}(\xi-\eta)
for some function
C_{a}
which depends on the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{A}
(similarly, for
b
), i.e., that the above expectation is essentially a function of the difference between the frequencies. The functions
C_{a},C_{b}
are directly determined by the
structure
of the polynomials (i.e., their support sets), and can be estimated using a
mean
and
variance
part, where the variance part is sensitive to
\delta
and usually overwhelms the mean part.

The second step, is to express the expected error as a sum of the form
\sum_{\delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}}S\cdot C_{a}(\delta)\cdot C_{b}(\delta)\cdot T(\delta)
, where
S=(m-r)^{3}
is a constant, and
T(\delta)=\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Output}}\omega^{x\cdot\delta}
(summing over the output coordinates, from which we read off the result). From here, we continue with a straightforward case analysis and estimate the sum. The obtained upper bound is then used to conclude the theorem.

This bound simplifies for
\left\{0,1\right\}
random Bernoulli matrices and folded Gaussian matrices (where the values distribute as the absolute value of a Gaussian). This is sufficient to deduce a very strong result for
\left\{\pm 1\right\}
Rademacher and Gaussian matrices, by splitting the positive and negative parts of the matrices (which distribute like Bernoulli matrices), and using the distributivity of the convolution. In other words, observe that if
a=a_{1}\pm a_{2}
, then
\left\lVert a*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert a_{1}*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left\lVert a_{2}*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
(and the same for
b
). The formal details are presented in
section 9
.

3.1
Fourier Analysis of Abelian Groups

Every finite group
G
is accompanied with an algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
, which is just the linear span of
\left\{\delta_{g}\mid g\in G\right\}
(or formal sums
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}\delta_{g}
, with
a_{g}\in\mathbb{C}
), together with the convolution operation, which can be defined on the basis by
\delta_{g}*\delta_{h}\mapsto\delta_{gh}
and extended linearly. Elements in
\mathbb{C}[G]
are often referred to as signals and we can also treat them as functions
G\to\mathbb{C}
where the value at
g
is just the coefficient of
\delta_{g}
. We will often switch between different notations of elements in
\mathbb{C}[G]
, using
\delta_{g}
notation, plain
g
notation, or function notation. When
G
is abelian,
\mathbb{C}[G]
can be interpreted as the space of polynomials modulo some monomial. For example, take
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, then
\mathbb{C}[G]
is just polynomials in
X
modulo
X^{n}
, and convolution is just polynomial multiplication (
X^{i}\cdot X^{j}\mapsto X^{i+j}
) modulo
X^{n}
(meaning
X^{i}=X^{i\pmod{n}}
). For another example, take
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, then
\mathbb{C}[G]
is just polynomials with variables
X,Y
modulo
X^{n},Y^{m}
, with polynomial multiplication modulo
X^{n}Y^{m}
.

The group algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
, can be endowed with an inner product
\left\langle{f,f^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\cdot\overline{f^{\prime}(g)}
. The Artin-Wedderburn theorem states that the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{G}
of
G
is an isomorphism of algebras that diagonalizes the algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
into a direct sum of matrix algebras
\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}\times d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
, where
\left\{d_{i}\right\}
are called the character degrees of
G
. This provides a bound on the tensor rank of the structure tensor of
G
6
6

6

The structure tensor
\mathcal{T}_{G}
of a group
G
is a tensor describing the bilinear convolution operator
*:\mathbb{C}[G]\times\mathbb{C}[G]\to\mathbb{C}[G]
. Formally, we have variables
\left\{X_{g}\right\}_{g\in G},\left\{Y_{g}\right\}_{g\in G},\left\{Z_{g}\right\}_{g\in G}
and
\mathcal{T}_{G}(X_{g},Y_{h},Z_{f})=1
if
gh=f
and
0
otherwise, exactly matching the definition
\delta_{g}*\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh}
. See
Appendix B
for more details.
,
\mathcal{T}_{G}
, which is
\sum_{i}d_{i}^{3}
. In case
G
is abelian, all degrees are
1
, since all linear representations of
G
are one-dimensional, and so
\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}\times d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\left|G\right|}
, where the multiplication in the latter algebra is just the
element-wise product of vectors
. In other words,
\mathcal{F}_{G}
transforms convolution into element-wise multiplication. This is known as the
convolution theorem
.

Assuming
G
is abelian, the dual group
\hat{G}
, which is the set of characters (one dimensional representations, i.e., group morphisms
G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
), is
non-canonically
7
7

7

It requires us to choose a primitive root of unity of order
\left|G\right|
. Our choice will always be
\exp(\frac{2\pi i}{|G|})
.
isomorphic to
G
. Thus
\mathbb{C}[G]\cong\mathbb{C}[\hat{G}]
, and so the Fourier transform can be viewed as a change of basis, from the
\delta
-functions
\delta_{g}(h)=\mathbf{1}_{g=h}
, to the basis of characters. To ensure it is unitary, we choose the definition of the Fourier transform to be

f\mapsto(\hat{f}_{\chi})_{\chi\in\hat{G}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|G\right|}}\left(\left\langle{f,\chi}\right\rangle\right)_{\chi\in\hat{G}}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|G\right|}}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\chi(g^{-1})\right)_{\chi\in\hat{G}}.

Under this normalization, we have Parseval’s theorem
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}=\|\hat{f}\|_{2}
, and the convolution theorem is then stated as
\widehat{f*f^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\left|G\right|}\cdot\hat{f}\odot\hat{f}^{\prime}
. The inverse Fourier transform is given by
\hat{f}\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{G}}\left(\sum_{\chi\in\hat{G}}\hat{f}_{\chi}\cdot\chi(g)\right)_{g\in G}
.

By the structure theorem for abelian groups,
G
can be written as
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{d}}
for some integers
n_{i},d
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, fix
\omega_{n}
to be a primitive root of unity of order

\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}=\left\{\chi_{m}\mid m\in[n]\right\}
where
\chi_{m}(x)=\omega_{n}^{a\cdot m}
. Moreover,
\hat{G}
then
\left\{\otimes_{i=1}^{d}\chi_{m_{i}}\mid m_{i}\in[n_{i}]\right\}
, where
\phi\otimes\psi
is the function
(x,y)\mapsto\phi(x)\cdot\psi(y)
given that
\phi,\psi
are functions into
\mathbb{C}
. We define the

\sum_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}

\hat{f}(\chi_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|G\right|}}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\cdot\omega_{n_{1}}^{-g_{1}\cdot m_{1}}\cdots\omega_{n_{d}}^{-g_{d}\cdot m_{d}}.

The tuple

Runtime Analysis

For a square matrix of size
n_{0}\times n_{0}
where
n_{0}=\left(m-1\right)^{N}\cdot 2^{N}
, we can use the notation of the blocking scheme as follows: define
n=\left(m-1\right)^{N}

k=2^{N}
. Write

\tau=\frac{3\log(m)}{\log(m-1)}\quad,\quad\eta=\frac{\log(2)}{\log(m-1)}.

Then
n^{\tau}=m^{3N}
,

, and so
n_{0}=nk=n^{1+\eta}
. Therefore the runtime of the algorithm is

O(k^{2}m^{3N}\log m^{3N})=O(n^{\tau+2\eta}\log n)=O\left(n_{0}^{\frac{\tau+2\eta}{1+\eta}}\log n_{0}\right).

For example, setting
m=8
we obtain
\frac{\tau+2\eta}{1+\eta}\approx 2.89
, and optimizing the expression over

f(x)=\frac{3\log(x)+2\log 2}{\log(x-1)+\log 2}
, we see that it has a local minima at

for which
f(x)\approx 2.85
, and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=3
, so the best possible algorithm from this approach has exponent
\approx 2.85
.

Framework For Approximate MM via Convolutions

Suppose we are given input matrices
\left\{\mathbf{A}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
\left\{\mathbf{B}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k}
, all of size
n\times n
. Let

\mathbf{a}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A}_{k})\end{bmatrix}\quad,\quad\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{k})\end{bmatrix}\quad,\quad\mathbf{c}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{B}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{B}_{k})\end{bmatrix}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{kn^{2}}.

Problem 6.1

(Group Theoretic Approximation)
.

Given
r\geq 1
and possibly
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, find an abelian group
G
of size
s
and matrices
\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times kn^{2}}
, which minimize

\left\lVert\Pi_{C}^{\top}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{G}\left(\mathcal{F}_{G}(\Pi_{A}\mathbf{a})\odot\mathcal{F}_{G}(\Pi_{B}\mathbf{b})\right)-\mathbf{c}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

under the
constraint
that the tensor
\mathcal{T}_{G,\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{B}}
represented by the
Definition B.1

(Normal Form)
.
has tensor rank
\mathsf{Trk}(\mathcal{T}_{G,\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}})\leq r
, and preferably can be computed in time
\tilde{O}(r)
. Here
\mathcal{F}_{G}
denotes the Fourier transform for
G
.

Note that the specific presentation of
\mathcal{T}_{G,\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}}
with the matrices above might have a large inner dimension (i.e., larger than
r
), and still might have another representation (an equivalent normal form) with inner dimension at most
r
. In other words,
s
is only an upper bound on the tensor rank. As we will see in later sections, this distinction is necessary to unify our approaches under this formulation.

Moreover, it is important to note that the tensor rank does
not
directly relate to the runtime of the bilinear algorithm. As highlighted before, recursion makes linear transformations done before and after the recursive step essentially “free”, in the sense that the running time is dominated by the tensor rank. This was first presented by
[
Str69
]
. In our setting, we wish to avoid recursion for the algorithm to be combinatorial. Under this view, one needs some extra conditions to hold in order for the runtime to match the tensor rank. Examples for this are low rank linear transformations, or structured linear transformations (FFT and the like). Even without the tensor rank directly determining the runtime, the question remains interesting on its own, as low tensor-rank approximations of the matrix multiplication tensor are not well understood (these are “low-accuracy” approximations).

Of course this problem cannot be truly solved using the current tools, so we fall back to heuristics. It is also important to consider two different variations of this problem:

1.

Oblivious
Setup:
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
are unknown. A
data-dependent
solution will use the input compute the matrices
\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}
online, with running time that does not exceed the bilinear operation running time. A
data-agnostic
solution will pre-compute the matrices, meaning they are fixed for every
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
(this will be useful if we have some extra assumptions, like some distribution which
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
are taken from).

2.

Deep Learning
Setup:
\mathbf{a}
is known (we can always switch the roles of
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
by computing the transposition), and we can pre-compute the matrices
\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}
based on
\mathbf{a}
in advance. This is relevant in deep learning, since the model’s weights are known.

Example 6.2
.

For the “full” budget
r=n
and
k=1
, we can take
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n^{3}}
(using the
4.1
Vanilla TPP
construction) and set
\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B},\Pi_{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{\left|G\right|\times n^{2}}
to be the indexing matrices. More precisely, for
X\in\left\{A,B,C\right\}
, we note that each row of
\Pi_{X}
corresponds to an element of the group, while each column of
\Pi_{X}
corresponds to a position in the matrix, and so by setting
1
iff this position is embedded into the element matching the row, and
0
otherwise, we obtain
\Pi_{A}\mathbf{a}=a
and
\Pi_{B}\mathbf{b}=b
(here
a,b:G\to\mathbb{R}
are the signals, viewed as vectors). We give these indexing matrices special notation, by writing
I_{X}:=\Pi_{X}
as defined above (we will reference them later).

7
Sketch & Solve Approximation

The first approach we will present for solving the approximation problem is to reduce the runtime complexity by sketching the input and then applying the algorithm presented in
section 5
. This is a “black-box” approach, combining known techniques to obtain a slight improvement. Instead of applying the algorithm on the input matrices, we first sketch the input matrices to lower rank matrices and then compute their product using the algorithm. Note that while the algorithm was presented for square matrices, it can be easily adapted to rectangular matrices, by changing the partitioning scheme. Although this presents no major or practical advancements, it is worth laying out explicitly.

A simple formulation of sketching results (see
[
Woo14
]
and references within) for our needs is the following:

Lemma 7.1

(JL-Sketch)
.

Set
m=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
, draw
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
where each coordinate is a random normal Gaussian scaled by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}SS^{\top}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}]=O(\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}).

In fact, this inequality holds with high probability.

In particular, in order to reach an error of
O(\frac{1}{r}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2})
we need
m=r
. Computing
\mathbf{A}S
and
S^{\top}B
takes
rn^{2}
using the naive implementation, and so does computing their product. Note that this process inherently reduces the rank of the output matrix in order to achieve faster computation.

Theorem 7.2
.

Suppose we are given two square matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
of size
n
and a parameter
r\leq n
which is not insignificant (compared to
n
). If
n
is large enough, there exists a sketching algorithm that approximates the multiplication of
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, by computing a matrix
\mathbf{C}
in time
O\left(n^{2}\cdot r^{\delta+o(1)}\right)
where
\delta\leq 0.9
and
r
bounds the rank of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
while also satisfying

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{C}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{1}{r}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right).

In fact, this inequality holds with high probability.

By saying that
n
and
r
are large enough, we mean that the combinatorial STPP algorithm achieves savings for
r\times r
matrices.

Proof.

Fix
m
and
N
such that
r=2^{N}(m-1)^{N}
. Write
p=\lceil n/(m-1)^{N}\rceil
(so
p\geq 2^{N}
because
n\geq r
). Viewing
S
as a
p\times 2^{N}
block matrix, and
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
as
p\times p
block matrices (padding them if
p>n/(m-1)^{N}
), the products
\mathbf{A}S,S^{\top}\mathbf{B}
and
(\mathbf{A}S)(S^{\top}\mathbf{B})
can all be computed by
p^{2}\cdot 2^{N}
block products. In each invocation of the STPP algorithm we compute
2^{N}
block products, so the overall number of algorithm invocations is
p^{2}
. The runtime of each invocation is
O(3Nm^{3N}\log m)
. Thus our overall runtime is

O(p^{2}\cdot 3N\cdot m^{3N}\log m)=O\left(\frac{n^{2}\cdot 3N\cdot m^{3N}\log m}{(m-1)^{2N}}\right).

Denoting
s=\frac{3N\cdot m^{3N}\log m}{(m-1)^{2N}}
we observe that (
\log
is base
2
)

\log_{r}(s)=\frac{\log(3N)}{N(1+\log(m-1))}+\frac{3\log(m)-2\log(m-1)}{1+\log(m-1)}.

The dominating part is
f(m)=\frac{3\log m-2\log(m-1)}{1+\log(m-1)}
that approaches
1
from below as
m\to\infty
and has a minimum at
m\approx 20
(for which
f(m)\approx 0.85
). For
m\in[8,480]
, we have
f(m)\leq 0.9
. The second part
g(N)=\frac{\log(3N)}{N(1+\log(m-1))}
vanishes with
N
. Therefore, we can compute the sketch in time
O(n^{2}r^{\delta+o(1)})
, where
\delta\leq 0.9
(we can always use small
m
and take
N
to be large). The claim the follows from the JL sketch theorem.
∎

This theorem shows that for large enough matrices, we can achieve a better bound on the error, in the same runtime, thus beating the state of the art. In the formulation of
6.1
, we can write the Sketch & Solve algorithm as follows:

\Pi_{A}=I_{A}\vec{S}\quad,\quad\Pi_{B}=I_{B}\vec{S^{\top}}\quad,\quad\Pi_{C}=I_{C},

where
I_{X}
are the indexing matrices (see
6.2
) and
\vec{S}
is the natural embedding of the linear sketch
S
, seen as a linear morphism
S:\mathcal{M}_{kn\times kn}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{M}_{kn\times r}(\mathbb{R})
, into the space of linear morphisms
\mathbb{R}^{kn^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{knr}
(and similarly
\vec{S^{\top}}
is the embedding of
S^{\top}
). Therefore
\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B}
act as linear sketch “compressions” of the input vector into a lower dimensional group.

Remark 7.3
.

The Sketch & Solve scheme is rather unique, in the sense that the dimensions of the
\Pi
matrices correspond to the underlying tensor rank, which is exactly the group size.

8
Low Degree Embedding

The second approach we will present for solving
6.1
is a non-black box approach. The idea is to embed the matrices into sets which are approximately TPP/STPP sets. For the sake of simplicity of presentation we will focus on TPP, but a similar argument could be made for STPP constructions. Relaxing the TPP condition allows for a smaller group size, and therefore assuming the group has an FFT algorithm, we obtain a fast algorithm to compute an approximate matrix multiplication.

8.1
Approximate Triple Product Quantity

We work with square matrix multiplication, although the case of rectangular matrices is very similar, up to some minor adjustments.

Definition 8.1

(Indexing Triplet)
.

Let
G
denote a group with size at least
n^{2}
. We say that three subsets
S,T,U\subseteq G
are
an indexing triplet
for
G
and
n
, if
\left|S\right|=\left|T\right|=\left|U\right|=n
and
\left|S^{-1}T\right|=\left|T^{-1}U\right|=n^{2}
. We denote the collection of indexing triplets by
\mathcal{I}(G,n)
.

Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.

Let
G
be a group of size at least
n^{2}
, then for any indexing triplet
(S,T,U)\in\mathcal{I}(G,n)
we define the
approximate triple product quantity
for
(S,T,U)
, denoted by
\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U)
, as the number of solutions to the equation

s_{1}s_{2}^{-1}t_{1}t_{2}^{-1}=u_{1}u_{2}^{-1}\qquad,s_{1},s_{2}\in S,t_{1},t_{2}\in T,u_{1},u_{2}\in U.

We then define

\rho_{n}(G)=\min_{(S,T,U)\in\mathcal{I}(G,n)}\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U),

i.e. the minimal number of solutions to the equation, and call this quantity the
Approximate Triple Product Quantity
of
G
with respect to
n
.

Note that
\rho_{n}(G)\geq n^{3}
, and equality holds if and only if
G
satisfies the TPP. This follows from the fact that there are always
n^{3}
trivial solutions to the equation, obtained by setting
s_{1}=s_{2},t_{1}=t_{2}
and
u_{1}=u_{2}
. Furthermore, a simple argument shows that for an abelian group
G
to satisfy the TPP for size
n
, we must have
\left|G\right|\geq n^{3}
(to see this, note that
\phi:S\times T\times U\to G
given by
(s,t,u)\mapsto s+t+u
is an injection, using the TPP and the commutativity of
G
).

Remark 8.3
.

The
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
can be related to the notion of
Efficacy
presented in
[
AZ23
]
. In particular, letting
\mathcal{T}_{G,S,T,U}
denote the tensor obtained by the above approximate embedding scheme and convolution, and using the definition of buckets (
C.2
) presented in
Appendix C
, we have

\mathbf{Eff}(\mathcal{T}_{G,S,T,U})^{2}=\sum_{s\in S,u\in U}\frac{n^{2}}{\left|\mathrm{Bucket}_{G}^{s,u}\left(S,T,U\right)\right|}\quad\text{where}\quad\sum_{s\in S,u\in U}\left|\mathrm{Bucket}_{G}^{s,u}\left(S,T,U\right)\right|=\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U).

In certain constructions, the ATPQ is balanced, in the sense that buckets have almost constant sizes, which implies the (non-squared) efficacy is
\approx n^{4}/\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U)
. Using the efficacy notion might provide a natural generalization to the ATPQ that easily carries over to the
Definition B.2

(STPP)
.
case.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
S,A\subset G
, then

\left|\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},a_{1},a_{2})\in S^{2}\times A^{2}\mid x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}=a_{1}a_{2}^{-1}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{\left|A\right|^{2}\cdot\left|S\right|^{2}}{\left|G\right|}.

Proof.

Note that
x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}=a_{1}a_{2}^{-1}
if and only if
a^{-1}_{1}x_{1}=a_{2}^{-1}x_{2}
, so we can rewrite the LHS by -

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}

\displaystyle\left|\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},a_{1},a_{2})\in S^{2}\times A^{2}\mid a_{1}^{-1}x_{1}=g=a_{2}^{-1}x_{2}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G}\left|\left\{(x,a)\in S\times A\mid a^{-1}x=g\right\}\right|^{2}=(*)

By Cauchy-Schwarz we have

(*)\geq\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\left(\sum_{g\in G}\left|\left\{(x,a)\in S\times A\mid a^{-1}x=g\right\}\right|\right)^{2}.

The proof is complete by noting

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}\left|\left\{(x,a)\in S\times A\mid a^{-1}x=g\right\}\right|=\left|A\right|\cdot\left|S\right|.

∎

Corollary 8.5
.

Let
G
be a finite
abelian
group, then the
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
satisfies

\rho_{n}(G)\geq\frac{n^{6}}{\left|G\right|}.

Proof.

Let
S,T,U\subset G
be subsets of size
n
. Recall that
\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U)
is the number of solutions to the following equation, using additive notation,

s_{1}-s_{2}+t_{1}-t_{2}+u_{1}-u_{2}=0,

which is equivalent to (by commutativity),

s_{1}-s_{2}=(t_{2}-u_{1})-(t_{1}-u_{2}).

Denote
A=TU^{-1}=T-U=\{t-u\mid t\in T,u\in U\}
, and apply
8.4
) on
S,A
to obtain that the number of solutions to the last equation (equal to
\rho_{n,G}(S,T,U)
) is at least
\left|A\right|^{2}\cdot\left|S\right|^{2}/\left|G\right|
. We finish by noting that
\left|A\right|
must equal
n^{2}
, or more precisely that
n^{2}=\left|T^{-1}U\right|=\left|TU^{-1}\right|
by definition of an indexing set, due to the fact that
t_{1}u_{1}^{-1}=t_{2}u_{2}^{-1}
if and only if
u_{1}t_{1}^{-1}=u_{2}t_{2}^{-1}
if and only if (using the fact
G
is abelian)
t_{1}^{-1}u_{1}=t_{2}^{-1}u_{2}
.
∎

For an abelian group with
\left|G\right|=rn^{2}
, we conclude that
\rho_{n}(G)\geq\frac{n^{4}}{r}
. Note that the corollary doesn’t hold for non-abelian groups, because the proof uses commutativity to obtain the expression used in
8.4
.

8.2
PolyForm Algorithm

Given an indexing triplet
S,T,U\subset G
, we index
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
using these sets (while implicitly defining an order on
S,T,U
) and embed the matrices into
\mathbb{C}[G]
, by setting
a_{s^{-1}t}=\mathbf{A}_{s,t}
and
b_{t^{-1}u}=\mathbf{B}_{t,u}
. We then compute their convolution
c=a*b
and decode
\mathbf{C}_{s,u}=c_{s^{-1}u}
, which takes
O(\left|G\right|\log\left|G\right|)
assuming
G
has an FFT algorithm (for example, if
G
is abelian). The
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
determines the number of noise terms, which we call “
collisions
”, since

c_{s_{1}^{-1}u_{1}}=\sum_{s_{2},t_{1},t_{2},u_{2}:\ s_{2}^{-1}t_{1}t_{2}^{-1}u_{2}=s_{1}^{-1}u_{1}}a_{s^{-1}_{2}t_{1}}b_{t_{2}^{-1}u_{2}},

and a term is summed into
\mathbf{C}
if and only if it is a solution to the equation
s_{1}s_{2}^{-1}t_{1}t_{2}^{-1}=u_{1}u_{2}^{-1}
. There are
n^{3}

valid
solutions, which are
s_{2}=s_{1},t_{2}=t_{1},u_{2}=u_{1}
, which encode the matrix multiplication, while all other terms are
noise terms
. In the formulation of
6.1
, the matrices
\Pi_{X}
are just indexing matrices
I_{X}\in\mathcal{M}_{\left|G\right|\times n^{2}}
which embed into the sets
S^{-1}T,T^{-1}U,S^{-1}U
, and the rank of the resulting tensor is just the tensor of the structure tensor of
G
.

The actual Frobenius error of this algorithm then depends on the squared sum of the
coefficients
corresponding to these error terms. Since the collisions are pre-determined by the indexing triplet, they might be skewed, meaning that certain positions (
s^{-1}u
) might suffer from relatively large numbers of collisions. Moreover, big error coefficients might end up together under certain orderings of
S,T,U
, but not under different orderings. To mitigate the first effect, we should be mindful of how skewed a certain indexing triplet is. Moreover, to mitigate the latter effect, we can randomly choose the ordering of the indexing sets.

Another randomness we can add to the algorithm is adding structured random signs to
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
. The goal here is to promote error terms cancellations, as we’ll show next. Take
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
to be independent random vectors
\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
which are drawn uniformly at random. Define
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{A}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{B}
, where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product of matrices. Then
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t,u}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\mathbf{B}_{t,u}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
are independent, we have that

\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t^{\prime},u})^{2}]=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{t,u})^{2}&t=t^{\prime},\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

This is exactly what we are interested in – canceling collisions which can be characterized by the condition
t\not=t^{\prime}
.

This defines a randomized oblivious algorithm, which we denote by
\mathrm{RPF}
(stands for Randomized PolyForm). Our goal is to understand the worst case error of this algorithm, meaning

\mathcal{E}=\max_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\not=0}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathrm{RPF}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]}{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}\right\}.

Note that the algorithm implicitly uses some indexing triplet, thus we define
\mathcal{E}_{G}=\min_{(S,T,U)\in\mathcal{I}(G,n)}\mathcal{E}_{S,T,U}
as the best algorithm error achieved by the group
G
. The error can be related with the
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
, as described in the next theorem. The proof is given in
Appendix C
. The general idea of the proof is using Yao’s principle with a distribution over matrices with i.i.d. values in
\left\{\pm 1\right\}
, and showing that in expectation the RPF error is related with
\rho_{n}(G)
.

Theorem 8.6
.

If
G
is a finite group with
\left|G\right|\geq n^{2}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{G}=\Omega\left(\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}\right)
.

8.3
Tight Results In The Abelian case

Next, we show that for a specific abelian group
\mathbb{Z}_{rn^{2}}
, where
r\leq n
, there is a setup achieving this error. The proof is provided in
Appendix C
.

Theorem 8.7
.

For
G=\mathbb{Z}_{rn^{2}}
, where
r\leq n
, there exist an indexing triplet
(S,T,U)
based on arithmetic progressions, such that
\mathcal{E}_{(S,T,U)}=O\left(\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}\right)
. The runtime of PolyForm in this setup in
O(rn^{2}\log n)
.

It is worth laying out the construction explicitly. The indexing triplet is given by

S=\left\{rn\cdot i\mid i\in[n]\right\},\ T=\left\{(r-1)\cdot i\mid i\in[n]\right\},\ U=\left\{r\cdot i\mid i\in[n]\right\}.

The set
A=(S+U)-(S+U)
is the arithmetic progression with difference
r
, and
t-t^{\prime}\in A
(for
t,t^{\prime}\in T
) if and only if
t-t^{\prime}=kr(r-1)
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore
\rho_{n,\mathbb{Z}_{rn^{2}}}(S,T,U)=n^{3}\cdot\lfloor n/r\rfloor
. By
Theorem 8.6
and
Theorem 8.7
, this is tight for abelian groups using the PolyForm framework.

Remark 8.8
.

This simple randomized algorithm recovers state of the art results for AMM achieved using linear sketching techniques. Although the error is the same, there might be other qualitative differences. An important difference in the context of deep learning, is the rank of the output matrix. Our empiric trials suggest that the rank of the output matrices in this approach is generally full, even for small
r
. Compared with sketching techniques, where
r
is also the upper bound on the rank, this is a major difference.

9
Fourier Domain Truncation

The third approach we will present for solving
6.1
is based on the following simple idea: Given
r
, choose (randomly or deterministically) a set
K\subset\hat{G}
(where
G
is an abelian group) of
r
frequencies of the Fourier transform of the convolution
c=a*b
, compute
c^{\prime}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{G}(\hat{c}|_{K})
, and decode
c^{\prime}
, i.e., return
I_{C}^{\top}c^{\prime}
where
I_{C}
is the indexing matrix for
\mathbf{c}
.

In order to show this is a useful approach we must show two things:

1.

Bounded Error
: We need to bound
\left\lVert I_{C}(c^{\prime})-I_{C}(c)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
in terms of
\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2},\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}
and
r
.

2.

Low Complexity
: We need to show that
\hat{a}|_{K},\hat{b}|_{K}
and
c^{\prime}
can be computed in time
\tilde{O}(r)
.

Remark 9.1
.

A trivial bound on the runtime complexity of computing
\hat{a}|_{K},\hat{b}|_{K}
and
c^{\prime}
is
O(rkn^{2})
. This follows from observation that the algorithm can be written down as follows: Given inputs
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{kn^{2}}
and a set of
r
frequencies
K\subset\hat{G}
, computing
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
is the same as computing

f(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=I_{C}^{\top}\left(\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\left(P_{K}([\mathcal{F}_{G}(I_{A}\mathbf{a})]\odot[\mathcal{F}_{G}(I_{B}\mathbf{b})]\right)\right),

where
P_{K}
is the idempotent projection onto
K
(meaning
(P_{K}x)_{\chi}=x_{\chi}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\chi\in K}
). Note that
P_{K}(x\odot y)=P_{K}(x)\odot P_{K}(y)
, and so by pre-computing some of the matrix products (which are fixed), we obtain smaller matrices and vectors, leading the complexity to
O(rkn^{2})
.

In order to achieve low complexity, we will thus need
K
to admit some extra structure. We will see examples for this later in the section.

9.1
Basic Properties

Let
c=a*b
denote the convolved signal and
c^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{c}|_{K})
denote the obtained signal. We always assume some underlying abelian group
G
, so we drop the notation of
G
where it is clear. By the convolution theorem, we know that
\hat{c}_{\chi}=\sqrt{\left|G\right|}\cdot\hat{a}_{\chi}\cdot\hat{b}_{\chi}
, and so it is intuitive to think that large magnitude coefficients in
\hat{c}
must originate from large magnitude coefficients in
\hat{a},\hat{b}
. We would have liked to say that
\left\lVert c-c^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\hat{a}|_{K^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\|\hat{b}|_{K^{c}}\|_{2}
, but unfortunately this is not correct. A correct statement would be:

Lemma 9.2
.

It holds
\left\lVert c^{\prime}-c\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\hat{c}|_{K}-\hat{c}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left|G\right|\cdot\left\lVert\hat{a}|_{K^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\|\hat{b}|_{K^{c}}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Proof.

The first equality follows from Parseval’s identity. The inequality follows from viewing
c-c^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{c}|_{K^{c}})
as the convolution of two signals
a^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{a}|_{K^{c}})
and
b^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{b}|_{K^{c}})
(we can do this using the convolution theorem), and so by Young’s inequality (which says
\left\lVert x*y\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}
) and the
\ell^{1}
-
\ell^{2}
inequality (
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
for
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
), we obtain

\left\lVert c-c^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert a^{\prime}*b^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert a^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert b^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left|G\right|\cdot\left\lVert a^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert b^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}\overset{\text{Parseval}}{=}\left|G\right|\cdot\left\lVert\hat{a}|_{K^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\hat{b}|_{K^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

∎

The
\ell^{1}
-
\ell^{2}
is tight when the coordinates of the vector are uniform in magnitude, and loose when the coordinates are highly concentrated. So in some cases the bound might be improved upon.

The actual error in this approach is not given by the vector
c^{\prime}-c
, but rather by the projection of this vector onto the embedding set of the output matrix. Suppose
I_{C}
denotes the indexing matrix of the output, then we care about
\left\lVert I_{C}^{\top}(c^{\prime}-c)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Note that
I_{C}^{\top}
is a contraction (because it simply drops coordinates), so the above expression is an upper bound of the error. Essentially, this shows that the error can be bounded by
\ell^{2}
-concentration of
\hat{a},\hat{b}
on the set
K
.

Remark 9.3
.

The convolution
c=a*b
usually has large support (almost
\left|\mathrm{supp}(a)\right|\cdot\left|\mathrm{supp}(b)\right|
), but in general we cannot assume anything about the ratio
\left\lVert I_{C}^{\top}c\right\rVert_{2}/{\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{2}}
. For example, in the
4.1
Vanilla TPP
construction, we can take
\mathbf{A}
to be all zeros except for
\mathbf{A}_{1,1}=M>0
and similarly take
\mathbf{B}
to be all zeros except for
\mathbf{B}_{2,1}=1
. Then
a
and
b
are all zeros except for two coordinates, whose product is not a desired term, leading to
c=a*b
satisfying
I_{C}^{\top}c=0
while
\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{2}=M
. Thus the ratio may be
0
, or very close to it, in case there is some dominating non-desired term. In such cases, an approximation of
c
might essentially be an approximation of the non-desired terms. This is the regime of the problem where our algorithm under performs, and is more suitable for
output-sensitive
algorithms, like that of
[
Pag13
,
BN20
,
Lin09
]
.

The Fourier transforms for the different constructions presented here have very rigid structures:

1.

4.2
CKSU STPP
: Consider the case of
N=1
(for
N>1
the idea is the same, just with more notation and dimensions), i.e., the group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}
. We can write
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
where
a_{1},a_{2}
correspond to
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2}
respectively. Then
a_{1}
is supported on
\left\{(x,y,0)\mid x,y\not=0\right\}
, which implies

(\hat{a}_{1})_{\xi}=C\cdot\sum_{x=1}^{m-1}\sum_{y=1}^{m-1}a_{x,y,0}\omega^{\xi_{1}x+\xi_{2}y},

(where
\omega=\exp(-2\pi i/m)
) showing that the spectrum of
\hat{a}_{1}
is actually
2
-dimensional and fixed on the third axis (independent of
k_{3}
). Similarly,
a_{2}
is supported on
\left\{(0,x,y)\right\}
and so the spectrum of
\hat{a}_{2}
is fixed on the first axis. The same idea is true for
b
, just with different axes.

2.

4.1
Vanilla TPP
: Recall that
a
is supported on an arithmetic progression, with step size
n
. We have

\hat{a}_{\xi}=C\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}a_{sn^{2}+nt}\omega^{\xi(sn^{2}+tn)},

(where
\omega=\exp(-2\pi i/n^{3})
) thus if
\xi_{1}\equiv\xi^{\prime}_{1}\pmod{n^{2}}
we obtain that
\omega^{\xi_{1}(sn^{2}+tn)}=\omega^{\xi_{1}^{\prime}(sn^{2}+tn)}
for every
s
and
t
. Thus,
\hat{a}
is
n^{2}
-periodic. Therefore, to estimate the top-
r
Fourier coefficients of the convolution (assuming
r\geq n^{2}
), it suffices to estimate the top coefficients of
\hat{b}
, because we have an implicit representation of size
n^{2}
for
\hat{a}
which can be computed in time
O(n^{2}\log n)
. Sadly,
\hat{b}
is not periodic. However, in the
Deep Learning
context, the weights matrix is fixed, and so using this construction one can treat the weights matrix (or its transpose) as
\mathbf{B}
, and compute the full spectrum of
\hat{b}
offline. Then, assuming it is concentrated, the heavy set can be pre-computed and compared with
\hat{a}
in real time.

9.2
Concentration In Product Distributions

This subsection is devoted to proving a concentration results for the special case in which we are given matrices whose entries are i.i.d. from a real product distribution. Throughout this subsection we will work with the
4.2
CKSU STPP
construction.

Notation:
Let us start by fixing some notation. We are working in the group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}
. Define

X_{1}=\left\{(x,y,0)\mid x,y\not=0\right\}\quad,\quad X_{2}=\left\{(0,x,y)\mid x,y\not=0\right\}\quad,\quad X_{3}=\left\{(x,0,y)\mid x,y\not=0\right\}.

Recall that these sets form the STPP sets in the construction. In particular, given matrices
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2},
we embed
\mathbf{A}_{1}
on
X_{1}
,
\mathbf{A}_{2}
and
\mathbf{B}_{1}
on
X_{2}
, and
\mathbf{B}_{2}
on
X_{3}
. Let
f_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}}
denote the indicator function of
X_{i}
. Our
a
signal, is constructed by two signals
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
, such that
a_{1},a_{2}
are encoding
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2}
, respectively. Then
a
will be supported on
X_{1}\cup X_{2}
, so we define
f_{a}:=\mathbf{1}_{X_{1}\cup X_{2}}=f_{1}+f_{2}
(using the fact the set
X_{i}
are pairwise disjoint). Similarly,
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
will be supported on
X_{2}\cup X_{3}
, so we define
f_{b}:=f_{2}+f_{3}
. The output coordinates, which we read off to obtain our output, are
X_{1}\cup X_{3}
.

The dual group
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}^{3}
, i.e., the
frequencies
group, is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}
, so we can just treat frequencies
\xi\in\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}^{3}
as elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}
. Thus we write
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})
where
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, and for convenience we let
\xi_{4}:=\xi_{1}
denote the first coordinate (instead of writing
\pmod{3}
). We fix
\omega=\exp(-\frac{2\pi i}{m})
.

Given a real
sub-Gaussian
distribution
\mathcal{D}
we denote the distribution’s parameters as:

\mu=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x],\quad\nu=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x^{2}],\quad\sigma^{2}=\mathsf{Var}(\mathcal{D})=\nu-\mu^{2},

and we let
p
denote the variance proxy of the
squared
distribution,
x^{2}
for
x\sim\mathcal{D}
. The variance proxy of a distribution
\mathcal{D}
is the smallest real number to satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[e^{\lambda(x-\mathbb{E}x)}]\leq e^{p^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In case
\mathcal{D}
is a bounded in
[a,b]
,
p\leq\frac{(b-a)}{2}
(Hoeffding’s lemma). We assume all the parameters are bounded.

We will also need the following useful fact:

Fact 9.4
.

Let
1<n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\zeta
denote a primitive root of unity of order
n
. Then for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, it holds:

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{j\cdot k}=\begin{cases}0&k\not\equiv 0\pmod{n},\\
n&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The most basic lemma we need is the spectrum structure of the indicator functions:

Lemma 9.5
.

For
i\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}
, we have

\hat{f}_{i}(\xi)=\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\times\begin{cases}(m-1)^{2}&\xi_{i}=\xi_{i+1}=0,\\
-(m-1)&\xi_{i}=0\text{ xor }\xi_{i+1}=0,\\
1&\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1}\not=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

A direct computation shows:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}\right|}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}}f_{i}(x,y,z)\cdot\omega^{\xi_{1}x+\xi_{2}y+\xi_{3}z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in X_{i}}1\cdot\omega^{\xi_{1}x+\xi_{2}y+\xi_{3}z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\sum_{0\not=x,y\in[m]}\omega^{\xi_{i}x+\xi_{i+1}y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{0\not=x\in[m]}\omega^{\xi_{i}x}\right)\left(\sum_{0\not=y\in[m]}\omega^{\xi_{i+1}y}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\left(-1+\sum_{x\in[m]}\omega^{\xi_{i}x}\right)\left(-1+\sum_{y\in[m]}\omega^{\xi_{i+1}y}\right),

and so the claim follows from
9.4
.
∎

9.2.1
Main Theorem Statement

Now, we can state the main concentration theorem, i.e., the exact version of
Theorem 1.4
:

Theorem 9.6

(Concentration Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
and
\mathcal{D}_{B}
be two real sub-Gaussian distributions, with non-zero variance and second moments. Suppose
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1\times m-1}
take independent values in distributions
\mathcal{D}_{A},\mathcal{D}_{B}
. Let
r\leq m
be a parameter, and define
K=K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup K_{3}
where
K_{i}=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}\mid\xi_{i}<r\right\}
. Consider the convolution of the embedded signals
a=a_{1}+a_{2},b=b_{1}+b_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{3}]
(using the
4.2
CKSU STPP
construction), denoted by
c=a*b
. Let
c^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\widehat{a*b}|_{K})
denote the approximation of
c
. Then the expected normalized error of the algorithm satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\|I_{C}^{\top}(c^{\prime}-a*b)\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{(m-r)^{3}}{(m-1)^{4}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{A}^{2}\sigma_{B}^{2}}{\nu_{A}\nu_{B}}\right)+8(m-1)^{2}\cdot\exp(-\gamma(m-1)^{2}),

where
\gamma=(6-4\sqrt{2})\cdot\min\left(\frac{\nu_{A}^{2}}{p_{A}^{2}},\frac{\nu_{B}^{2}}{p_{B}^{2}}\right)
.

We call the sets
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
,
slabs
of width
r
, as depicted in
Figure 2
.

In the same setup of

Lemma 9.10
.

In the conditions and notations of

, the random variable

satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\nu_{A}\nu_{B}\cdot 4(m-1)^{4}

Proof of
Theorem 9.6
.

First we note that
\sum_{x\in X_{1}\cup X_{3}}\left|e(x)\right|^{2}=\left\lVert I_{C}^{\top}(c^{\prime}-a*b)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, by definition of the STPP sets. Define
X=\sum_{x\in X_{1}\cup X_{3}}\left|e(x)\right|^{2}
and
Y=\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Our goal is to bound
\mathbb{E}[X/Y]
. The first step is to reduce this to a bound on
\mathbb{E}[X]/\mathbb{E}[Y]
, which is easier handle, since
\mathbb{E}[Y]=4(m-1)^{4}\cdot\nu_{A}\cdot\nu_{B}
and
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathcal{E}
.

9.10
, with probability at most

\mathbb{E}[X/Y]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X}{Y}\mid Y<\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y]\right]\cdot 4\exp(-\gamma(m-1)^{2})+\frac{2\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]}\cdot 1.

Since

is a contraction, we have
X\leq\left\lVert c^{\prime}-a*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert e\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\|{\widehat{a*b}|_{K^{c}}}\|_{2}^{2}
, where we used the linearity of the Fourier transform and Parseval. Since the projection onto
K^{c}
is also a contraction, we have
X\leq\|\widehat{a*b}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert a*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
which by Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequality satisfies
\left\lVert a*b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2(m-1)^{2}\cdot\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Therefore
X/Y\leq 2(m-1)^{2}
always, concluding

\mathbb{E}[X/Y]\leq 8(m-1)^{2}\cdot\exp(-\gamma(m-1)^{2})+\frac{2\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]},

so we have reduced the problem to the ratio of expectations.

By
9.9

\frac{2\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]}=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{2(m-1)^{4}\cdot\nu_{A}\cdot\nu_{B}}=\frac{O((m-r)^{3}\cdot\sigma_{A}^{2}\cdot\sigma_{B}^{2})}{2(m-1)^{4}\cdot\nu_{A}\cdot\nu_{B}}=O\left(\frac{(m-r)^{3}\cdot\sigma_{A}^{2}\cdot\sigma_{B}^{2}}{(m-1)^{4}\cdot\nu_{A}\cdot\nu_{B}}\right).

The hidden constant is in the vicinity of
16
(again, depending on the size of
m
, but for

which is not too small, this holds).
∎

The added exponential factor is what we pay for choosing this type of analysis, but it is negligible for large enough
m
(assuming the distribution is “nice” enough).

9.2.3
Applications of the Theorem

An immediate corollary is the application of the theorem for the Rademacher and Gaussian distributions.

Corollary 9.11
.

Given random Rademacher or Gaussian matrices
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}
of size
(m-1)\times(m-1)
, and a parameter

, computing the set of frequencies
K
defined in
Theorem 9.6
results in matrices

Conclusions & Open Questions

The paper presents very simple and practical algorithms for approximate MM that potentially have better qualities compared with standard sketching techniques. It provides a theoretical foundation and tools to analyze the theoretical guarantees of the algorithms. A few open questions that we would like to note:

•

Is there a simpler, more elegant proof for
Theorem 1.4
? Can this result can be generalized for larger classes of distributions over matrices? In particular, the empiric evidence suggest that the same approach in the TPP case yields good results too.

•

What is the relationship between input and output rank of the matrices in the algorithm in
section 8
? We see empiric evidence (see
Figure 3
) that the algorithm produces high rank outputs.

•

Studying the
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
in the case of non-abelian groups. Can we prove general lower bounds?

•

Finding approximate STPP constructions (in the sense of
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
). We believe such constructions will lead to new state-of-the-art trade-off in the worst case, using similar ideas to
section 8
.

•

Are there other low-degree polynomial representations that might be useful? For example, using the theory of Chebyshev approximation, which deals with uniform approximation of polynomials over compact domains, sounds useful. In our attempt, it did not yield good results.

•

On the practical side, it is interesting to test this framework on real life matrices, specifically in the context of DL.

Appendix B
More Definitions

For completeness we present here a few definitions used in the paper.

Definition B.1

(Normal Form)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
denote a field and let
f:\mathbb{F}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{p}
denote a bilinear map. Then
f
can be represented in Normal Form, via three matrices
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times r},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times p}
, such that for every
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}^{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
it holds

f(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{W}^{\top}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{V}\mathbf{b}),

where
\odot
denotes the element-wise multiplication of vectors. The quantity
r
is called the rank of this representation.

Furthermore, a trilinear tensor
\mathcal{T}
is a formal sum over three sets of variables
\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
,
\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i\in[m]}
,
\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i\in[p]}
, given as
\sum_{i,j,k}T(i,j,k)\cdot X_{i}Y_{j}Z_{k}
, where
T:[n]\times[m]\times[p]\to\mathbb{F}
is a function. Then
\mathcal{T}
represents a bilinear map
f_{\mathcal{T}}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{p}
defined by

[f_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})]_{k}:=\sum_{i,j}T(i,j,k)\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{j}.

The normal form shows that the converse is also true, i.e., given a bilinear form and a representation
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}
as above, we set

\mathcal{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{i}\mathbf{U}_{t,i}X_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j}\mathbf{V}_{t,j}Y_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k,t}Z_{k}\right),

and obtain
f_{\mathcal{T}}=f
. The tensor rank of a tensor is the minimal number
r
for which
\mathcal{T}
can be represented as the sum of simple tensors, which are products as in the previous expression. Thus, the rank of the representation is an upper bound on the tensor rank. We denote the tensor rank by
\mathsf{Trk}(\mathcal{T})
.

For completeness, we present the definition of the simultaneous triple product property (
STPP
) capturing when the group structure tensor
\mathcal{T}_{G}
(defined via
T_{G}(g,h,f)=1
iff
gh=f
, where
g,h,f\in G
are our variables) can be restricted to a direct sum of matrix multiplication tensors. This definition was given in
[
Coh+05
]
.

Definition B.2

(STPP)
.

We say that a finite group
G
satisfies the
STPP
for
\left\langle{n_{1},m_{1},p_{1}}\right\rangle,\ldots\left\langle{n_{\ell},m_{\ell},p_{\ell}}\right\rangle
, if there exist triplets of subsets in
G
,
\left\{(S_{i},T_{i},U_{i})\right\}_{i=1,\ldots,\ell}
, of sizes
\left|S_{i}\right|=n_{i},\left|T_{i}\right|=m_{i},\left|U_{i}\right|=p_{i}
, such that
(S_{i},T_{i},U_{i})
satisfy the TPP, and they don’t mix:

\forall i,j,k\in\left\{1,\ldots,\ell\right\}:\quad s_{i}\cdot(s_{j}^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot t_{j}\cdot(t_{k}^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot u_{k}\cdot(u_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}=1_{G}\implies i=j=k,

assuming
x_{a},x_{a}^{\prime}\in X_{a}
(with
X\in\left\{S,T,U\right\},a\in\left\{i,j,k\right\}
).

An important and useful property is:

Lemma B.3
.

If
\left\langle{S,T,U}\right\rangle
satisfy the TPP in
G
and
\left\langle{S^{\prime},T^{\prime},U^{\prime}}\right\rangle
in
G^{\prime}
, then
\left\langle{S\times S^{\prime},T\times T^{\prime},U\times U^{\prime}}\right\rangle
satisfies the TPP in the product group
G\times G^{\prime}
. Similarly, if
\left\{\left\langle{S_{i},T_{i},U_{i}}\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{k}
satisfy the STPP in
G
and
\left\{\left\langle{S_{i}^{\prime},T_{i}^{\prime},U_{i}^{\prime}}\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}
in
G^{\prime}
, then
\left\{\left\langle{S_{i}\times S_{j}^{\prime},T_{i}\times T_{j}^{\prime},U_{i}\times U_{j}^{\prime}}\right\rangle\right\}_{i\in[k],j\in[k^{\prime}]}
satisfy STPP in
G\times G^{\prime}
.

Definition C.1

\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}\left(S,T,U\right)

In the same conditions as above, we define the set of
Collisions
for an index

, where

\mathrm{Bucket}_{G}^{x,y}\left(S,T,U\right)=\left\{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\mid s^{-1}t{t^{\prime}}^{-1}u=x^{-1}y\right\}.

We note the following useful characterization of the error.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
G
denote a finite group,
\left|G\right|\geq n^{2}

.
. Let

Proof of
Theorem 8.6
.

For brevity, we let
\mathcal{R}
denote a randomized PolyForm setup, composed of an indexing triplet
(S,T,U)\in\mathcal{I}(G,n)
, permutations of the indexing sets and sign vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. We denote the deterministic error from this setup by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}
, where
\mathbf{C}
is the output matrix of the algorithm with setup
\mathcal{R}
.
By Yao’s principle, for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
over inputs of the algorithm (i.e. couples of matrices), it holds

\mathcal{E}_{G}\geq\min_{\mathcal{R}}\underset{(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\right].

Set
\mathcal{D}
to be the uniform distribution on Gaussian matrices (all entries are independently drawn uniformly from
\{\pm 1\}
). We can assume without loss of generality that the permutations are all the identity permutations, because
\mathcal{D}
is invariant under permutation. Moreover,
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=n^{2}
when
(A,B)\sim\mathcal{D}
. Thus, by
C.3
and the fact that
(\mathbf{A}\odot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top})_{s,t}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{s,t}
(and similarly for
\mathbf{B}\odot\boldsymbol{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}
), we have

\displaystyle n^{4}\cdot\underset{(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in S,y\in U}\left(\sum_{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in S,y\in U}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\right)^{2}\right].

(1)

Fixing
x\in S,y\in U
, we note that

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\right)^{2}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\\
\not=\\
(p,r,r^{\prime},q)\end{subarray}\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{p,r}\mathbf{B}_{r^{\prime},q}\right)\right].

(3)

Since
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
all take values in
\pm 1
, we obtain

(2)=\sum_{(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[1\right]=\left|\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right|.

Regarding
(3)
, the assumption
(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\not=(p,r,r^{\prime},q)
implies that
(s,t)\not=(p,r)

or

(t^{\prime},u)\not=(r^{\prime},q)
, hence at most one couple of random variables in the set
\{\mathbf{A}_{s,t},\mathbf{A}_{p,r},\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u},\mathbf{B}_{r^{\prime},q}\}
are not independent. Using the multiplicity of the expectation, we obtain

(3)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,t,t^{\prime},u)\\
\not=\\
(p,r,r^{\prime},q)\end{subarray}\in\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}_{s,t}\mathbf{B}_{t^{\prime},u}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{p,r}\mathbf{B}_{r^{\prime},q}\right]=0,

where we used the fact that a multiple of independent Rademacher RVs (
\pm 1
with equal probability) is
0
in expectation.

To sum up, we have shown

(1)=\sum_{x\in S,y\in U}\left|\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}^{x,y}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right|=\left|\mathrm{Collisions}_{G}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\right|\geq\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}.

Since
\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{E}_{G}\geq\min_{\mathcal{R}}\underset{(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})}\right]\geq\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}.

Define the following polynomials -

\displaystyle P_{A}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k\in\left[n\right]}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i,k}\cdot x^{S\left(i\right)+T\left(k\right)}

\displaystyle P_{B}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,j\in\left[n\right]}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{l,j}\cdot x^{-T\left(l\right)+U\left(j\right)}

(the addition and subtraction are group operations, i.e. modulo
D
). Compute their convolution (using the cyclic group FFT algorithm), and denote the result by
\sum_{r\in[D]}\mathbf{c}_{r}x^{r}
. Now, define
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by -

\left(\mathbf{C}\right)_{i,j}:=\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\right)\cdot\mathbf{c}_{i\cdot S\left(i\right)+U\left(j\right)}\qquad\left(\forall i,j\in\left[n\right]\right).

Constructing the polynomials is done in
O\left(D\right)
time, computing the convolution in
O\left(D\log\left(D\right)\right)
time and constructing
\mathbf{C}
in
O\left(n^{2}\right)
time. Overall we reach
O\left(rn^{2}\log\left(n\right)\right)
running time.

Analysis
. Fix
i,j\in\left[n\right]
. We have by definition of
\mathbf{C}
:

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}\right)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{S\left(i\right)+U\left(j\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}\in\left[n\right]\\
\left[S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+T\left(k\right)\right]+\left[U\left(j^{\prime}\right)-T\left(k^{\prime}\right)\right]\\
=S\left(i\right)+U\left(j\right)\end{subarray}}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

We can expand and simplify this expression to obtain

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k=k^{\prime}\in\left[n\right]\\
S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)=S\left(i\right)+U\left(i\right)\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\mathbf{B}_{k,j^{\prime}}}_{*}+\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k\not=k^{\prime}\in\left[n\right]\\
S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)+T\left(k\right)-T\left(k^{\prime}\right)\\
=S\left(i\right)+U\left(i\right)\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}_{**}.

By definition of indexing triplets, we know that
\left|S+U\right|=n^{2}
. Thus, for the first sum
*
, we have that
S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)=S\left(i\right)+U\left(j\right)\iff\left(i^{\prime}=i\land j^{\prime}=j\right)
. This implies that we can rewrite the first sum as

*=\sum_{k\in\left[n\right]}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i,k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k,j}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})\cdot\sum_{k\in[n]}\mathbf{A}_{i,k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k,j}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})\cdot(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{i,j},,

using the definition of matrix multiplication. This sign can be canceled by multiplying it by itself as in the definition of
\mathbf{C}
.

The second sum
**
, is thus the sum of "noise" terms. Formally -
\left|\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)_{i,j}\right|=\left|**\right|
. As we wish to minimize the squared Frobenius norm
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
, we end up minimizing the squares of these noise terms. We further observe that -

\displaystyle\forall i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in\left[n\right],k\not=k^{\prime}:\quad S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)+T\left(k\right)-T\left(k^{\prime}\right)=S\left(i\right)+U\left(j\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle T(k)-T(k^{\prime})+S(i^{\prime})-S(i)+U(j^{\prime})-U(j)=0.

Thus when summing over all
i,j\in[n]
we obtain that the number of items in the sum is exactly the number of
non-trivial
solutions to the equation defining the
Definition 8.2

(Approximate Triple Product Quantity)
.
- which is
\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}
.

Bound
. We continue to bound the error. By linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{AB}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,j}\left(\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right].

Let
\mathbf{1}_{i,j}
denote an indicator over quad-tuples
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}),k\not=k^{\prime}
that obtains
1
if
T(k)-T(k^{\prime})+S(i^{\prime})+U(j^{\prime})=S(i)+U(j)
. For brevity we denote such quad-tuples by
t=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime})
and
t^{\prime}=(p,q,l,l^{\prime})
. We denote
f_{t}=:\mathbf{1}_{i,j}(t)
to be a random variable which relies on the ordering permutation
\pi
applied independently on
S,T,U
. We denote
g_{t}:=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
to be a random variable which relies on the sign vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. We note that
f_{t},g_{t}
are independent. Thus, opening up the error terms while fixing
i,j\in\left[n\right]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}_{i,j}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t}f_{t}\cdot g_{t}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}f_{t}\cdot f_{t^{\prime}}\cdot g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}\cdot f_{t^{\prime}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right].

Note that for the random signs in
g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}

not
to cancel out in expectation, we must have that
i^{\prime}=p,j^{\prime}=q
and
\left(k=l,k^{\prime}=l^{\prime}\right)\lor\left(k=l^{\prime},k^{\prime}=l\right)
. In all other cases we have two independent signs that cancel out in expectation. For the first case, where
t=t^{\prime}
, we note that

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right]=\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{A}_{i^{\prime},k}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{2}\geq 0.

For the second case, where
t^{\prime}=\left(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime},k\right)
, we note that
f_{t}
and
f_{t^{\prime}}
are fully-correlated. Assuming
f_{t}=1
(i.e.
t
is an error term in
\mathbf{C}_{i,j}
) then
f_{t^{\prime}}=1
. Thus

\displaystyle S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+T\left(k\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)-T\left(k^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=S\left(i^{\prime}\right)+T\left(k^{\prime}\right)+U\left(j^{\prime}\right)-T\left(k\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle T\left(k\right)=T\left(k^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle k=k^{\prime}

which contradicts the assumption that
t\not=t^{\prime}
. In other words, in the second case, we must have
f_{t}=0
, which implies

\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}\cdot f_{t^{\prime}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right]=0\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right]=0.

To conclude the discussion, we note that each
t\in I
can only fall into a single “bucket” (be an error term of a single index in
\mathbf{C}
). Thus the sum is
disjoint
for each
i,j
. Per the analysis above, our sum simplifies to:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{t=t^{\prime}\in I}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}\cdot f_{t^{\prime}}\cdot g_{t}\cdot g_{t^{\prime}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\left(\text{disjoint sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{k\not=k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{2}\cdot g_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left(\text{independence}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j,k,k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}\right]\text{ since }f_{t}\sim\text{Ber}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}\cdot\sum_{i,j,k,k^{\prime}}a_{i,k}^{2}\cdot b_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{B}\right\|_{F}^{2}

where we used the fact that the number of non-trivial collisions is
\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}
by hypothesis, and thus the probability of a quad-tuple
(i,j,k,k^{\prime})
being an error term is
\frac{\rho_{n}(G)-n^{3}}{n^{4}}
(because of the random shifts, this probability is uniform). This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of
9.7
.

The proof is symmetric in the case of
b
, so without loss of generality we focus on
a
. By definition of the Fourier transform (and using the fact
a
takes real values):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}(\xi)\cdot\overline{\hat{a}(\eta)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}a(x)\cdot\omega^{-x\cdot\xi}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{m^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}a(y)\cdot\omega^{y\cdot\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\mathbb{E}[a(x)\cdot a(y)]\cdot\omega^{-x\cdot\xi+y\cdot\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3}}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[a(x)^{2}]}_{\nu_{A}}\cdot\omega^{x\cdot(\eta-\xi)}+\frac{1}{m^{3}}\sum_{x\not=y\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[a(x)]\cdot\mathbb{E}[a(y)]}_{\mu_{A}^{2}}\cdot\omega^{-x\cdot\xi+y\cdot\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3}}\cdot(\nu_{A}-\mu_{A}^{2})\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\omega^{x\cdot(\eta-\xi)}+\frac{1}{m^{3}}\cdot\mu_{A}^{2}\cdot\sum_{x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\omega^{-x\cdot\xi+y\cdot\eta}.

Let us examine the first sum of roots of unity. Denote
\delta=\xi-\eta
and let
\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{0}
denote the Hamming weight (number of non-zero coordinates). By
9.5
, we can easily compute the sum:

\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\omega^{x\cdot(\eta-\xi)}=m^{3/2}\cdot\hat{f}_{a}(\delta)=\begin{cases}2(m-1)^{2}&\delta=0,\\
(m-1)^{2}-(m-1)&\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0\lor\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=0,\\
-2(m-1)&\delta_{1}=\delta_{3}=0,\\
-(m-1)+1&\delta_{1}=0\lor\delta_{3}=0,\\
2&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Similarly, we compute the second sum:

\sum_{x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\omega^{-x\cdot\xi+y\cdot\eta}=\left(\sum_{x}\omega^{-x\cdot\xi}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{y}\omega^{y\cdot\eta}\right)=m^{3}\cdot\hat{f}_{a}(\xi)\cdot\hat{f}_{a}(-\eta).

Since
\xi,\eta\notin K
we know that
\hat{f}_{a}(\xi)=\hat{f}_{a}(-\eta)=\frac{2}{m^{3/2}}
(note that
\eta\notin K
implies
-\eta
is not contained in any of the
heavy
planes or axes), hence
m^{3}\cdot\hat{f}_{a}(\xi)\cdot\hat{f}_{a}(-\eta)=4.

We conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}(\xi)\cdot\overline{\hat{a}(\eta)}]=\frac{4\mu_{A}^{2}}{m^{3}}+\underbrace{(\nu_{A}-\mu_{A}^{2})}_{=\sigma_{A}^{2}}\times\begin{cases}\frac{2(m-1)^{2}}{m^{3}}&\delta=0,\\
\frac{(m-1)^{2}-(m-1)}{m^{3}}&\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0\lor\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=0,\\
\frac{-2(m-1)}{m^{3}}&\delta_{1}=\delta_{3}=0\\
\frac{-m+2}{m^{3}}&\delta_{1}=0\lor\delta_{3}=0,\\
\frac{2}{m^{3}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This shows
\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}(\xi)\cdot\overline{\hat{a}(\eta)}]
is just a function of
\delta
, which we denote by
C_{a}
. The estimates now follow directly.
∎

Proof of
9.10
.

Let
Y=\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
and denote
W=\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{2}^{2},Z=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Since
W,Z
are independent random variables, we have
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[W]\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z]
. By definition,

\mathbb{E}[W]=\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\mathbb{E}[a_{x}^{2}]=\left|\mathrm{supp}(a)\right|\cdot\nu_{A}=2(m-1)^{2}\cdot\nu_{A},

and similarly
\mathbb{E}[Z]=2(m-1)^{2}\cdot\nu_{B}
. Thus

\mathbb{E}[Y]=4(m-1)^{4}\cdot\nu_{A}\nu_{B}.

Moreover, since
W,Z
are both sums of independent random variables, with variance proxies
p_{A}^{2},p_{B}^{2}
, we obtain that
W,Z
have variance proxies
2(m-1)^{2}\cdot p_{A}^{2}
and
2(m-1)^{2}\cdot p_{B}^{2}
respectively. Therefore, by Hoeffding’s inequality (stated as: for i.i.d.
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
sub-Gaussians with variance proxy
p^{2}
, it holds
\forall\varepsilon\geq 0
,
\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{i}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/(2n\cdot p^{2})}
):

\displaystyle\Pr(W<\delta\mathbb{E}[W])

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(|W-\mathbb{E}[W]|\geq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}[W])

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}\mathbb{E}[W]^{2}}{4\cdot(m-1)^{2}\cdot p_{A}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}\cdot\nu_{A}^{2}\cdot(m-1)^{2}}{p_{A}^{2}}\right).

A similar bound holds for
Z
, using
B
-parameters instead. Since
Y=W\cdot Z
, we obtain that
Y<\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y]
implies
W<\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{E}[W]
or
Z<\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z]
. Hence

\displaystyle\Pr(Y<\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[Y])

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(W<\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[W])+\Pr(Z<\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[Z])

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}\cdot\nu_{A}^{2}\cdot(m-1)^{2}}{p_{A}^{2}}\right)+2\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}\cdot\nu_{B}^{2}\cdot(m-1)^{2}}{p_{B}^{2}}\right).

Set
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
, then
(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}=\frac{3}{2}-\sqrt{2}
. Therefore setting
\alpha=(6-4\sqrt{2})\approx 0.34
we have

\Pr\left(Y<\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-(m-1)^{2}\cdot\alpha\cdot(\nu_{A}/p_{A})^{2}\right)+2\exp\left(-(m-1)^{2}\cdot\alpha\cdot(\nu_{B}/p_{B})^{2}\right)\leq 4\exp(-\gamma\cdot(m-1)^{2}),

where
\gamma=\alpha\cdot\min\left(\frac{\nu_{A}^{2}}{p_{A}^{2}},\frac{\nu_{B}^{2}}{p_{B}^{2}}\right)
.

Lemma D.1
.

Suppose we have an algorithm that given matrices
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
computes an approximation
\mathbf{C}_{1},\mathbf{C}_{2}
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{B}_{1}-\mathbf{C}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{B}_{2}-\mathbf{C}_{2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\cdot\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

Then approximating the product of two matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
can be done with error
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{C}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
.

Proof.

Partition each matrix into
4
blocks, of size
d\times d
, writing

\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1,1}&\mathbf{A}_{1,2}\\
\mathbf{A}_{2,1}&\mathbf{A}_{2,2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\quad,\quad\mathbf{B}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{1,1}&\mathbf{B}_{1,2}\\
\mathbf{B}_{2,1}&\mathbf{B}_{2,2}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Then
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
can be written as

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,1}+\mathbf{A}_{1,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,1}\right)&\left(\mathbf{A}_{1,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,2}+\mathbf{A}_{1,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,2}\right)\\
\left(\mathbf{A}_{2,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,1}+\mathbf{A}_{2,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,1}\right)&\left(\mathbf{A}_{2,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,2}+\mathbf{A}_{2,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,2}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right).

For each
i,j\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, apply the given algorithm on
\mathbf{A}_{i,1},\mathbf{A}_{i,2},\mathbf{B}_{1,j},\mathbf{B}_{2,j}
to obtain two approximate products
\mathbf{D}^{1}_{i,j},\mathbf{D}^{2}_{i,j}
and set the
(i,j)
-block of the result matrix
\mathbf{C}
to be
\mathbf{C}_{i,j}=\mathbf{D}_{1}+\mathbf{D}_{2}
. Since the blocks are disjoint, we have

\left\lVert\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j\in\left\{1,2\right\}}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{i,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

and by the parallelogram identity, it holds

\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{i,j}-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{i,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert(\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{1}-\mathbf{A}_{i,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,j})+(\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{2}-\mathbf{A}_{i,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,j})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{1}-\mathbf{A}_{i,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+2\left\lVert\mathbf{D}^{2}_{i,j}-\mathbf{A}_{i,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Applying the algorithm’s error guarantee we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i,j\in\left\{1,2\right\}}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{1}-\mathbf{A}_{i,1}\mathbf{B}_{1,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{D}^{2}_{i,j}-\mathbf{A}_{i,2}\mathbf{B}_{2,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\sum_{i,j\in\left\{1,2\right\}}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{i,1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{i,2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{1,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{2,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i\in\left\{1,2\right\}}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{i,1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}_{i,2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)\right)}_{=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}\cdot\underbrace{\left(\sum_{j\in\left\{1,2\right\}}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{1,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{2,j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)\right)}_{=\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

∎

2

\hat{a}_{2}
on the
y,z
-plane can be done by a single
2
-dimensional FFT of size
m\times m
. Both have runtime of
O(m^{2}\log m)
. Now, computing the frequencies in
K
is just a matter of accessing these
m\times m

O(rm^{2})=O(\left|K\right|)

2.

Recall that
K=K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup K_{3}
, where

It is convenient to think of
