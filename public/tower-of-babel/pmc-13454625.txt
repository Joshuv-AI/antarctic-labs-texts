Dynamic path optimization and multi-objective decision-making for photovoltaic tracking systems using deep reinforcement learning
Authors: Zhenzhen Qu, Qing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the core challenge of synergistically optimizing power generation efficiency, equipment wear, and environmental adaptability in photovoltaic (PV) tracking systems under dynamic conditions, this study proposes a deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based intelligent optimization framework. A 37-dimensional state-space multi-objective decision process model is constructed, integrating spatiotemporal solar irradiance, PV panel status, environmental parameters, and historical decisions. An entropy-weighted multi-objective reward function balances conflicting objectives, while an improved Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (CDDPG) algorithm, incorporating periodic decay learning rate, prioritized experience replay, and parallel training, resolves traditional issues of convergence oscillation, trade-off imbalance, and poor real-time performance. Validated via a dual-axis tracking platform using 2024 full-year field data from Xiaojin County, Sichuan, results show about 12.3% average efficiency gain vs. PID, Q-learning, and original DDPG, 18.7% lower equipment wear with 0.08 mm gear wear after 6 months, decision latency <8ms and 3.2% performance degradation under ± 5% noise, and 2.1% 6-month generation decay with 3.5% cross-season decay. This framework enables intelligent PV tracking upgrades with enhanced efficiency, reduced costs, and improved stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55002-0.

Introduction

In the global transition toward clean and sustainable energy systems, photovoltaic (PV) power generation has emerged as a cornerstone of the renewable energy sector. Over the past decade, the installed capacity of solar power has expanded rapidly, achieving an average annual growth rate of approximately 28%. Despite this impressive expansion, recent projections indicate a potential deceleration in future growth. In particular, BloombergNEF forecasts that the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of new global solar installations may decline to ~ 5% during the period from 2025 to 2030, signaling a maturing market and intensifying competition for efficiency gains1–4. Photovoltaic tracking systems dynamically adjust the azimuth and tilt angles of solar panels to maintain optimal alignment with sunlight, maximizing solar energy capture5,6. Research indicates that single-axis tracking systems yield about 20% higher annual power output than fixed-tilt installations, whereas dual-axis tracking systems can achieve over 30% greater annual output7. However, traditional photovoltaic tracking control methods face significant limitations: First, fixed trajectory tracking based on astronomical calendars relies solely on preset solar paths, making it vulnerable to dynamic environmental disturbances like cloud cover, gusty winds, and localized shadows—resulting in over 20% power loss during cloudy conditions8,9. Second, feedback control methods using photoelectric sensors (e.g., PID control) can respond to light variations but suffer from response delays, sensor noise interference, and struggle to balance multiple objectives including power efficiency, equipment wear, and environmental adaptability10–14. With advancements in artificial intelligence, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers new solutions for complex optimization challenges in dynamic environments through its ability to autonomously learn optimal strategies via real-time environmental interaction, eliminating the need for prior models15–19.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) technology has been successfully applied in fields such as robotic path planning and smart grid dispatching, yet its implementation in photovoltaic tracking systems remains in the exploratory phase20–24. Current research predominantly focuses on optimizing single objectives (such as power generation efficiency), while overlooking mechanical wear caused by frequent equipment adjustments (statistical data shows that traditional tracking systems experience 3–5 years shorter lifespan than theoretical estimates due to excessive adjustments) and environmental factors like high temperatures and strong winds that negatively impact system performance25–29. Therefore, developing DRL-based dynamic path optimization and multi-objective decision-making methods for photovoltaic tracking systems holds significant theoretical and engineering value for enhancing power plant generation efficiency, reducing operational costs, and improving system stability30.

To address this challenge, researchers have implemented multiple approaches. For instance, dual-axis photovoltaic tracking systems based on fuzzy control have been developed to adjust panel angles using light-sensor feedback and rule-based control strategies31. In sunny conditions, its power generation increased by 22% compared to fixed-mount systems. However, studies indicate that dual-axis tracking systems may underperform in cloudy weather. Research shows that while the system can achieve 35–45% power generation improvement under specific conditions, its tracking accuracy may drop to 5–8° in cloudy scenarios, with angle deviations reaching 5–8°32–34. Second, a PID-controlled tracking system using photodiode arrays for light sensing was developed, achieving a response time of approximately 50ms35–37. Yet when sensors are covered with dust or exposed to intense light, control performance fluctuates significantly, causing power generation efficiency to decrease by over 10%38,39. Third, an Actor-Critic framework-based intelligent photovoltaic tracking control method was proposed. By incorporating attention mechanisms to optimize state feature extraction, the algorithm’s accuracy in perceiving light variations was enhanced by 15%40–42.

Despite the notable advancements, current intelligent control methods lack a comprehensive multi-objective reward function system, and equipment degradation issues require further investigation for effective resolution43,44. Fourth, a distributed photovoltaic tracking system based on multi-agent reinforcement learning has been developed45,46. Through coordinated control of photovoltaic arrays by multiple agents, the system achieved a 10% improvement in power generation efficiency under large-scale power station scenarios47. However, it suffers from significant communication latency (approximately 50ms) and high algorithm training complexity, making it difficult to apply in small and medium-sized photovoltaic power stations48–50. In summary, existing research still falls short in dynamic adaptability, multi-objective collaborative optimization capabilities, real-time responsiveness, and system robustness of photovoltaic tracking systems. Dynamic path optimization techniques and multi-objective decision-making methods for photovoltaic tracking systems based on deep reinforcement learning (DRL) urgently require further breakthroughs.

Although DRL has been increasingly explored in photovoltaic and energy-control applications, existing studies rarely integrate mechanical wear modeling, environmental adaptability, and real-time decision guarantees within a unified PV tracking framework under complex and variable weather conditions. Compared with recent intelligent PV tracking and DRL-based energy-control studies, existing methods still show important limitations. Fuzzy-control and PID-based tracking methods improve solar alignment but remain sensitive to parameter tuning and environmental disturbances. Q-learning-based methods introduce learning capability but usually rely on discrete action spaces, which may cause stepwise adjustment and mechanical wear. Standard DDPG-based methods support continuous control but may suffer from training instability, weak multi-objective balancing, and embedded real-time limitations. Moreover, many multi-agent or multi-objective reinforcement-learning studies in energy systems focus mainly on dispatching or power optimization, while mechanical degradation and real-time motion constraints of PV tracking systems are rarely integrated together. Therefore, the novelty of this study lies in formulating PV tracking as a real-time multi-objective decision-making problem that jointly considers power generation efficiency, equipment wear, environmental adaptability, and decision latency within one CDDPG-based framework.

To address these gaps, this study developed a multi-objective decision-making process model that integrates spatiotemporal light intensity distribution, photovoltaic panel operational status, environmental parameters, and historical decision-making information into a unified system state space. A multi-objective reward function was designed to balance power generation efficiency, equipment wear, and environmental adaptability. This provides a rigorous theoretical modeling approach for multi-objective optimization of photovoltaic tracking systems, aligning with the multi-objective optimization research on solar photovoltaic systems mentioned in Reference (1) Additionally, we proposed an enhanced Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (CDDPG) algorithm addressing limitations of traditional DDPG in photovoltaic tracking scenarios. The improved version incorporates a periodic decay learning rate (CDLR) mechanism, entropy-based multi-objective weighted fusion strategy, prioritized experience replay (PEX), and parallel training architecture, significantly enhancing both training efficiency and optimization performance. These enhancements demonstrate similarities with the multi-objective optimization configuration methods for photovoltaic energy storage systems described in Reference (2) Furthermore, a comprehensive experimental verification platform was established through physical experiments and simulation analyses. Utilizing annual operational data from real photovoltaic power stations, comparative experiments were conducted across multiple dimensions including power generation efficiency, equipment wear, real-time responsiveness, and robustness against traditional PID control, Q-learning, and original DDPG methods. These rigorous comparisons fully validate the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed approach.

Experimental platform and validation setting

To validate the effectiveness of the CDDPG algorithm in dynamic path optimization and multi-objective decision-making for photovoltaic tracking systems, this study established a hybrid experimental platform combining physical testing and simulation validation. The platform was developed using real-world PV plant data for comparative experiments. Detailed methodologies for constructing the experimental platform and dataset are provided in supplementary materials. Through comprehensive evaluations using multiple comparison methods, we assessed the algorithm’s performance as follows: 1. Photovoltaic Tracking System Prototype: The physical test platform was deployed at Xiaojin County PV Plant in Sichuan Province (3200 m elevation, annual sunshine duration 2650 h). Core components included: a high-precision dual-axis tracking system (azimuth-90°~90°, pitch 0°~90°, resolution ± 0.1°), 280 W monocrystalline silicon solar panels (22% conversion efficiency), irradiance sensors with 1 Hz sampling frequency, NVIDIA Jetson Nano controller (algorithm deployment), MySQL database, and Python for data processing and training. Xiaojin County’s subtropical monsoon climate provides abundant annual sunshine (2214 h), ensuring optimal photovoltaic conditions.

In this study, model training and real-time deployment were distinguished to avoid overloading the edge controller. The multi-agent parallel training process was conducted offline using the collected field data and simulation environment, whereas the NVIDIA Jetson Nano was used mainly for real-time inference and control-command deployment after model compression. The lightweight actor network deployed on the Jetson Nano generated control actions from the current 37-dimensional state vector, while replay-buffer updating, batch training, and multi-agent parallel interaction were performed in the offline training stage. Therefore, the reported embedded latency reflects online inference and control decision time rather than full parallel training time on the Jetson Nano.

Xiaojin County was selected because it provides a challenging test environment for PV tracking control. The site has high-altitude irradiance conditions, obvious seasonal variation, and frequent transitions among sunny, cloudy, and weak-light weather conditions, which are useful for evaluating tracking accuracy, dynamic adaptability, and environmental robustness. However, because the field dataset was collected from a single geographical site, the results should not be interpreted as fully representative of all PV deployment environments. To partially examine generalization, additional simulation tests were conducted by varying irradiance fluctuation intensity, wind speed, temperature, and sensor-noise conditions. These tests were used to evaluate whether the learned policy remained stable under environmental conditions different from the original field setting.

Figure 5a illustrates the installation layout with strategically positioned sensors and RS485 bus communication between control units. 2. Data Set Composition: The experimental data originates from the power station’s 2024 annual operational measurements (8.76 × 10⁶ records, 1-second interval), encompassing three operational scenarios: sunny conditions (153 days, 42% sunshine stability), cloudy conditions (128 days, 35% sunshine fluctuation), and overcast conditions (84 days, 23% reduced sunlight). All operational variables were synchronized to a 1-second sampling interval before model training. Sensor readings with abnormal values were removed using the 3σ rule, short missing intervals were filled by linear interpolation, and all state variables were normalized to the [0,1] range before the dataset was divided into 70% training and 30% testing subsets. Preprocessing procedures included: 3σ rule-based outlier detection (approximately 1.2%), linear interpolation for missing values (0.3%), data normalization, and 7:3 training/test set partitioning.

Baseline Method Comparison: Three baseline approaches were selected for comparison: traditional PID control (incremental, aiming to maximize irradiation), Q-learning algorithm (using discrete action space with 15 preset levels), and the original DDPG algorithm (without CDDPG improvement strategy). Results and discussion

The construction of a comprehensive system state space serves as the core prerequisite for deep reinforcement learning agents to attain multi-objective optimization within ray tracing systems52,53. Limitations of traditional DDPG in PV tracking

Figure 2a illustrates the loss function evolution curve of the traditional DDPG in photovoltaic tracking tasks, sampled at 100-step intervals from 0 to 10,000 steps. The data reveals three distinct phases: Initially (0–1000 steps), the loss rapidly decreases from 4.8 to 3.2 before rebounding to 4.1 between 1000 and 2000 steps. During the intermediate phase (2000–5000 steps), while approaching the low loss target of 2.5, the loss fluctuates between 0.8 and 1.2 every 200–300 steps (e.g., 2.9 at 3000 steps → 3.8 at 4000 steps). In the final phase (5000–10,000 steps), reduced volatility persists with recurring “decline-rebound” patterns (e.g., 2.0 at 9000 steps → 2.8 at 10,000 steps). Photovoltaic tracking scenarios present challenges due to dynamic environmental changes like shifting light intensity and cloud coverage, which require adaptive learning rate adjustments. Fixed learning rates struggle to meet the demands for rapid convergence and stable optimization during training, potentially preventing effective global optimum attainment and compromising both algorithm stability and control precision. For instance, the application of intelligent algorithms such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithm (GA) in maximum power point tracking (MPPT) technology enables faster and more precise identification of the maximum power point through dynamic learning rate adjustments, thereby enhancing the energy conversion efficiency of photovoltaic power generation systems. Figure 2b illustrates the impact of frequent light intensity fluctuations under cloudy conditions on the photovoltaic panel angle adjustments controlled by the traditional DDPG algorithm. The study recorded time-series data (0–60 min, with 5-minute sampling intervals) of photovoltaic panel angle adjustments, revealing dynamic responses to light intensity variations. The data shows that the adjustment values exhibit “high-frequency alternating positive/negative movements with significant overshoots”: fluctuating violently between-18° and 20°, and predominantly reversing direction across adjacent time steps (e.g., + 12° at 5 min versus-8° at 10 min; +20° at 35 min versus-15° at 40 min), without maintaining consistent adjustment trends for three consecutive time steps. This characteristic directly reflects the limitations of discrete action spaces: the traditional DDPG algorithm employs discrete angular intervals (e.g., 5°,10°,15°) as action outputs, making it difficult to achieve continuous and fine-grained angle adjustments. Under cloudy conditions, minor variations in light intensity (e.g., ± 10,000 lx) require photovoltaic panels to make small adjustments within a narrow range of ± 1° to ± 3°. However, the discrete action space only allows fixed adjustment levels, forcing the algorithm to frequently resort to “over-adjustment” to compensate for errors. This not only wastes energy (due to frequent motor startups/stoppages) but also reduces power generation efficiency (as the angle consistently deviates from optimal values). Figure 2c shows the trade-off curve data of traditional DDPG in PV tracking, illustrating the trade-off between power generation efficiency (20%−100%) and equipment losses (5%−20%). The curve exhibits characteristics of “non-smooth fluctuations and no stable Pareto optimization trend”: When efficiency increased from 50% to 60%, equipment losses decreased from 12% to 11% instead of increasing; when efficiency rose from 80% to 90%, losses jumped from 16% to 18%, with the increase significantly outpacing efficiency gains (10% vs. 2%). The overall curve lacks a clear linear or smooth nonlinear relationship between “efficiency improvement-gradual loss growth”. This pattern reveals the limitations of traditional reward function design: Although conventional DDPG algorithms focus on “maximizing power generation efficiency”, their reward functions are overly simplistic. They merely introduce penalty terms for equipment losses (e.g., deducting fixed rewards when losses exceed thresholds) to handle efficiency-loss relationships, lacking dynamic balancing mechanisms between efficiency and losses. In photovoltaic tracking systems, improving power generation efficiency relies on high-frequency adjustments of solar panels (increasing losses), while reducing losses requires decreasing adjustment frequency (sacrificing efficiency). Traditional reward functions fail to quantify the marginal benefits of these two factors, causing algorithms to fall into a chaotic “alternating priority between efficiency and loss” during decision-making, making it impossible to output stable multi-objective optimization strategies. Figure 2d shows statistical data of delay time with decision counts (1–100, continuous sampling) under the traditional DDPG algorithm’s 10ms decision interval requirement. According to industry research reports, photovoltaic tracking systems exhibit high sensitivity to delay time in practical applications with strict real-time requirements. Data reveals that delays generally exceed system thresholds (≤ 10ms) and show “unstable fluctuations”: delay values are distributed between 12ms and 20ms, with 20% occurring between 19ms and 20ms, and only 30% in the 12ms-15ms range. Notably, no continuous five-decision sequence maintained a delay below 15ms. The average delay across 10 repeated data sets reaches 16.1ms, far exceeding photovoltaic tracking systems’ real-time requirement of “delay ≤ 10ms” (otherwise unable to respond promptly to light changes), which may adversely affect photovoltaic power generation efficiency and overall system performance.

The core challenge stems from the single-threaded architecture of traditional DDPG, which results in over 70% of forward inference time being consumed when processing high-dimensional inputs like eight-dimensional state features (including light intensity, wind speed, and temperature) in photovoltaic scenarios, leading to decision-making delays. However, DeepSens Semiconductor’s grid-connected inverter control method based on the DDPG algorithm demonstrates its potential for real-time analysis and optimized control strategies, potentially addressing the latency limitations inherent in conventional DDPG implementations.

Design and training behavior of the CDDPG algorithm

Figure 3a illustrates the periodic decay learning rate (CDLR) proposed by CDDPG, which is sampled every 5 cycles from cycle 0 to 50 in training. The figure demonstrates the learning rate’s decaying trend over training cycles, exhibiting a “staged stepwise decay” pattern: maintaining a high initial value (0.01) from cycle 0–10, decreasing to 0.005 from cycle 10–20, further reducing to 0.0025 from cycle 20–30, then halving every 10 cycles until reaching 0.0003125 at cycle 50. Within each decay phase (e.g., 0–10 cycles or 10–20 cycles), the learning rate remains constant without fluctuations. This mechanism effectively balances convergence speed and training stability: The high learning rate in early stages (0–10 cycles) accelerates parameter updates to rapidly reduce loss function values, addressing the “slow initial convergence” issue depicted in Fig. 3a; while the low learning rate in later stages (30–50 cycles) prevents excessive parameter updates and suppresses loss function oscillations, resolving the “late-stage oscillation” problem. Compared to traditional fixed learning rates, CDLR’s periodic decay dynamically adapts to training phases, which proves particularly crucial in photovoltaic tracking scenarios with complex dynamic environments, as it ensures more stable training processes. Figure 3b illustrates the dynamic weight adjustment strategy for CDDPG based on the entropy-based method, documenting how weights of three key objectives—generation efficiency, equipment losses, and real-time performance—evolve with iterations (0–200,25-sampling intervals). The weight changes shown in Fig. 3b were obtained using the entropy-weighting formulation described in “Methods and experimental setup” section. The weights exhibit “differentiated dynamic adjustment and adaptive priority optimization”: In power industry data analysis contexts, the generation efficiency weight increases from 0.3 to 0.5, reflecting optimized dispatching and improved generation efficiency through data-driven approaches. Meanwhile, the equipment losses weight decreases from 0.4 to 0.2, demonstrating the effectiveness of equipment condition monitoring and preventive maintenance in reducing power losses. The real-time performance weight remains stable at around 0.3 with fluctuations under 0.05, highlighting the stability of power quality data analysis in ensuring electricity safety and service efficiency. Notably, the total weights sum to 1 across all three objectives, satisfying normalization constraints and ensuring comprehensive and balanced optimization of power system operations. This pattern reflects the scientific nature of entropy-based weighting: CDLR calculates information entropy (lower entropy indicates greater objective fluctuation, requiring higher weighting) during training, dynamically adjusting priorities through entropy weighting. During the initial photovoltaic tracking phase (0–50 iterations), equipment losses fluctuated significantly due to frequent motor adjustments, thus assigning a high weight (0.3–0.4). As training progressed, loss fluctuations diminished while power generation efficiency became the core performance indicator, gradually increasing the weight to 0.5. Real-time performance remained stable due to hardware limitations, maintaining consistent weights. This mechanism resolved the “confusion in multi-objective trade-offs” observed in Fig. 3c, achieving adaptive Pareto optimization. Figure 3c compares the sample utilization rates of CDDPG’s priority experience sampling (PEX) with traditional random sampling, showing their evolution over training steps (0–10,000, with 1000-step intervals). PEX, an enhanced reinforcement learning technique, optimizes experience sampling strategies to improve learning efficiency and convergence speed. For instance, in DQN, it prioritizes samples with larger TD errors to maximize Q-value updates. The curves demonstrate PEX’s sustained improvement: utilization rates increased from 45% to 82%, maintaining a 5%−8% growth rate every 2000 steps without stagnation. In contrast, random sampling fluctuated between 30% and 45%, dropping to 40% after 8000–10,000 steps. At 10,000 iterations, PEX’s utilization rate outperformed random sampling by 42% points. This feature validates the advantages of PEX sampling: Traditional random sampling uniformly draws samples from the experience pool without distinguishing their “value” (e.g., high-error samples contribute more to network updates), whereas PEX prioritizes samples based on “TD error” (time difference of error), with high-error samples (e.g., those deviating from PV panel angles by over 5°) being sampled first, significantly improving utilization efficiency. For instance, the SA-TD3 algorithm enhances training convergence speed and stability through its TD error-based prioritization module. This optimization addresses the “inefficient use of training data” issue shown in Fig. 2a, enabling the network to learn optimal tracking strategies more rapidly in complex scenarios. Figure 3d illustrates how CDDPG’s multi-agent parallel training architecture demonstrates varying training throughput (samples/second) with agent count (1–10, continuous sampling). The throughput exhibits “approximate linear growth with significant scale effects”: At 1 agent, throughput reaches 120 samples/second; at 2 agents, it increases to 230 samples/second (nearly double); at 5 agents, it hits 500 samples/second (approximately 4.2× growth); at 8 agents, it surges to 816 samples/second (around 6.8x growth); and at 10 agents, it further climbs to 950 samples/second (about 7.9× growth) without noticeable diminishing marginal returns.

The adoption of a parallel architecture has significantly enhanced real-time performance, as demonstrated in CDDPG. This system achieves decoupling and parallel processing between modules through multi-agent synchronization for collecting illumination scenario data, parallel training of Actor-Critic networks, and concurrent output adjustment commands. Compared to the 16.1ms average latency of traditional DDPG in Fig. 2d, the eight-agent parallel architecture reduces decision-making latency to 8.2ms—fulfilling the PV tracking system’s real-time requirement of “≤10ms” —while expanding algorithmic generalization capabilities through multi-agent coverage of diverse scenarios (sunny, cloudy, overcast). Notably, the application of multi-agent systems in distributed sensor networks has already validated their generalization performance across various environmental conditions.

Performance under sunny and cloudy conditions

In subsequent performance comparisons of sunny scenarios, the illumination intensity remained relatively stable. Meteorological research data indicates that direct sunlight reaching the ground can reach approximately 60,000–100,000 lx (summer outdoor direct sunlight), averaging 920 W/m². The regularity of the sun’s orbital path provides an ideal testing environment for evaluating algorithm tracking accuracy and equipment wear control capabilities. Figure 4a compares the angle adjustment trajectories of CDDPG and baseline methods during midday (11:00–13:00). The results show: (1) Fixed Mounting Frame: Maintains constant azimuth and elevation angles at 0° and 45° respectively, unable to adjust according to solar position changes. Deviations from ideal angles gradually increase, reaching a maximum of 15°; (2) PID Control: Adjustments follow stepwise changes every 5 min, showing noticeable lag. Deviations remain 3–5° from ideal angles post-adjustment; (3) Q Learning: Discontinuous action space causes frequent minor fluctuations with 25 adjustments per minute; (4) CDDPG: Displays continuous smooth adjustment curves with deviations under 1°, maintaining ideal angles at 8 adjustments per minute, demonstrating superior tracking precision and operational efficiency. Figure 4b presents hourly power generation bar charts for different methods. Experimental results show: The fixed bracket system achieved an average hourly power output of 0.92 kWh; PID control demonstrated 1.05 kWh per hour with a 14.1% efficiency improvement; the Q-learning algorithm generated 1.08 kWh per hour, showing 17.4% efficiency gain; while the CDDPG algorithm outperformed by 13.3% (1.19 kWh vs. PID) and 10.2% (1.08 vs. Q-learning) in efficiency. The CDDPG’s superior performance stems from its continuous motion control system and precise angle adjustment, which maintains photovoltaic panels perpendicular to sunlight, thereby maximizing light capture efficiency. Figure 4c presents a Pareto frontier comparison of power generation efficiency and equipment losses obtained through specific algorithms. The far-right point represents the maximum number of coverage points achieved with a certain redundancy level, while leftward shifts indicate reduced coverage points and increased redundancy. The analysis reveals: PID control: 14.1% efficiency improvement and 28.5% equipment loss rate, positioned at the lower end of the Pareto frontier; Q-learning: 17.4% efficiency improvement but with a high equipment loss rate of 35.2% (due to frequent adjustments caused by discrete actions); Original DDPG algorithm: 22.6% efficiency improvement and 22.8% equipment loss rate, outperforming the other two methods but showing scattered Pareto points; CDDPG algorithm: 29.3% efficiency improvement and only 12.9% equipment loss rate, achieving optimal balance between power generation efficiency and equipment losses with concentrated Pareto points at the frontier optimal position. Figure 4d compares the convergence rates between CDDPG and the original DDPG algorithm. The results show that after 1500 epochs of training, the original DDPG algorithm maintained a loss function stabilized around 1.5 with minor fluctuations. In contrast, the CDDPG algorithm, leveraging periodic decay learning rate and priority experience replay mechanisms, achieved a stable loss function below 0.8 after just 800 epochs—a 46.7% improvement in convergence speed—with no noticeable fluctuations during training. This fully demonstrates the significant enhancement of the improved strategy in maintaining training stability.

The oscillation observed in the original DDPG during sudden irradiance changes can be attributed to the limited use of rare but important transition samples. Cloud-induced irradiance drops create high-error state transitions, but random experience replay may sample these events insufficiently, causing unstable value estimation and repeated over-correction of panel angles. In CDDPG, the PEX mechanism assigns higher sampling probability to transitions with larger temporal-difference errors, allowing the actor-critic networks to learn more effectively from abrupt light-change events. This reduces angle overshoot and improves the stability of the response under cloudy weather.

The superior performance of CDDPG can be explained by its closer alignment with the physical operating behavior of PV tracking systems. Under sunny conditions, irradiance changes smoothly and the solar path is relatively predictable; therefore, the continuous action space of CDDPG enables gradual azimuth and pitch adjustments, keeping the PV panel close to the optimal incidence angle while reducing unnecessary motor start-stop operations. Under cloudy conditions, irradiance fluctuates rapidly due to intermittent cloud cover. In this case, PID control suffers from response delay, Q-learning is limited by discrete action levels, and original DDPG may produce unstable adjustments due to fixed weighting and training oscillation. By contrast, CDDPG uses prioritized replay to learn more effectively from high-error irradiance-transition samples and entropy-based weighting to dynamically balance energy capture and mechanical wear. Under weak-light or environmentally stressful conditions, frequent tracking may provide limited energy gain but can increase actuator load and gear wear. The dynamic reward mechanism of CDDPG therefore helps suppress unnecessary movements, which explains its lower equipment wear and more stable long-term performance.

Using operational data spanning six consecutive months (April-September), we analyzed long-term performance variations, cumulative equipment wear, and cost-effectiveness of different methods. Conclusions and prospects

This study proposes an intelligent optimization framework based on the improved depth deterministic strategy gradient (CDDPG) algorithm for path optimization and multi-objective decision making of photovoltaic tracking system in dynamic environment. Through theoretical modeling, algorithm design and experimental verification, the following core conclusions are obtained:A multi-objective model was developed to comprehensively characterize system states. By integrating spatiotemporal light intensity distribution, photovoltaic panel operational status, environmental parameters, and historical decision-making data, a 37-dimensional state space was constructed. A multi-objective reward function balancing power generation efficiency, equipment wear, and environmental adaptability was designed, with weights dynamically adjusted through entropy-based optimization to resolve conflicting objectives. Experimental results demonstrate that this model accurately captures the dynamic characteristics of photovoltaic tracking systems, providing reliable interactive data support for algorithm training.The CDDPG algorithm is proposed, effectively overcoming the core limitations of traditional DDPG: By leveraging the cyclic decay learning rate (CDLR) mechanism, training convergence speed is improved by 46.7% with a 72% reduction in oscillation amplitude; based on priority experience replay (PEX), the utilization rate of critical samples increases by 300%; through parallelized training and model lightweighting strategies, decision latency decreases from 14.2 ms to 7.8 ms, meeting real-time requirements. Comparative experiments demonstrate that CDDPG significantly outperforms traditional methods in training stability, decision accuracy, and real-time performance.
