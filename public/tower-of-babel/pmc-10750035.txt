Freshman STEM students’ misconceptions in a basic limit theorem of limx→0(sinx/x)
Authors: Hailu Nigus Weldeana, Desta Berhe Sbhatu, Gebreslassie Tesfay Berhe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined learning difficulties among three consecutive cohorts of freshmen engineering and technology students (n = 251; 17 females) in solving problems in the basic limit theorem. The students were enrolled in a four-credit hour calculus course delivered in 16 weeks. Then, they completed a 1-h six-item test after they covered the topics of the basic limit theorem. Answer papers were scored using an explicit guide. Interviews were also conducted after the test with 15 randomly selected students without and with cognitive supports (cues) in topics related to the test. The study demonstrated that the students have difficulties understanding the surface structure of the problems, identifying the mathematical heuristics to solve the problems, and solving the problems by properly employing procedures, rules, and conventions. Examination of students' test results showed that for every student earning a correct answer via valid problem-processing pathways, there were about two students earning two correct answers via invalid pathways. Interview results showed the students were incapable of fixing their limitations without cognitive support and cues. They needed an average of 7.7–9.3 question-response dialogues to fix their limitations. Such learning difficulties were due to lack and misapplication of solid prior knowledge. Identifying and compiling lessons that cause learning difficulties due to misapplication of prior knowledge can help to adopt better instructional approaches. Cognitive supports and cues can be used as research tools to extract the reasons and sources of students’ learning difficulties and devise innovative instructional methods for better learning.Keywords: Basic limit theorem, Cognitive support, Misconception, Problem processing pathway

Introduction

Calculus provides us with the requisite capability to function successfully in the fields of science, engineering and technology whenever ordinary geometry and algebra are not good enough. Most of all, Mathematicians have long been aware of the relevance of basic limit theorems in calculus (e.g., limx→0sinx/x) in order to sustain the trends of its pertinence. It has become a prerequisite tool to math-intensive areas of knowledge and trade in computing, engineering, natural sciences, and technology. However, many undergraduate students of computing, engineering, natural sciences, and technology demonstrate limited competence in calculus and its companion contents. This is because their mathematics learning emphasizes procedures that invest less in understanding relevant concepts and relationships [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. This contributes to student learning difficulties - leading to their failure in achieving educational goals [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. This would have a bigger impact on the learning of math-intensive areas of study and in tackling problems that involve much mathematics.

There are so many factors that contribute to student learning difficulties in mathematics. But much of the research agenda focused on the effects of errors and misconceptions [5,[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. In spite of this fact, in-depth studies as to why errors and misconceptions occur and what corrective treatments are required to fix the learning difficulties due to the same are very limited (e.g., Refs. [5,15,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]). And yet, many studies that investigated students' written responses to mathematics tasks and disclosed manifestations of students' difficulties failed to make much effort to look into the students’ learning difficulties via face-to-face conversations to understand how they express their ideas and thoughts related to basic theorems and their extensions. Furthermore, the studies concentrate on difficulties related to specific topics (e.g., calculus, algebra) instead of generic mathematical problems that can lead to generalizations within broader areas of learning.

Misconceptions have long been recognized as responsible factors for students to generate errors across disciplines and all levels in their learning of mathematics [[23], [24], [25]]. Misconceptions form part of learners' conceptual structure that interacts with concepts to negatively influence new learning by producing errors [26]. They can be considered as faulty lines in the conceptual and cognitive structures in the forms of beliefs and principles that cause systematic conceptual errors and deep misunderstanding of situations [12,14,[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Errors are discernible in students' works, but misconceptions are subtle to recognize and can be resilient to instruction designed to modify them [12,[32], [33], [34]]. Additionally, misconceptions are more implicit than explicit; and are surfaced during non-routine problem solving [35]. They are abstract often manifested via talking to students who are attempting to solve difficult mathematics problems and noting their verbalizations and thought patterns [4,8,35,36]. They affect students' performance and achievement in mathematics thereby contributing to students’ wrong perceptions towards the subject [[37], [38], [39], [40]].

Misconceptions, as contributors to students' errors, are exhibited due to poor or weak comprehension of what is learned [24,[41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]], faulty line of thinking in prerequisite knowledge, personal beliefs and attitudes [25,26,32,47] and motivation to learn on the part of students as well as inappropriate teaching methods [12,42,[48], [49], [50]]. Curricula can also be the source of the problem [21,45,50]. But they are often hidden in right answers [34,51]. They are also used to reinforce defective strategies toward correct answers [26,49]. Misconceptions can, furthermore, be the sources of conceptual and procedural errors in a single problem but may not prevent students from having correct answers [42,52]. Therefore, students’ scores on many tests do not necessarily show the extent to which they understand mathematics concepts and relationships [34,51]. The list of literature cited in this paragraph is explained in the appendix in relation to problems statement, objectives, research methods and the findings.

The study of students' written responses by analyzing diagnostic interviews is an established approach of inquiry in education (e.g., Refs. [23,36,40,[52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]). But studies aiming to analyze demonstrated misconceptions and errors through the study of thought patterns are very limited [22,25,47,[57], [58], [59]]. The first author has been teaching basic courses in calculus to freshman undergraduate engineering and technology students for a number of years. He consistently and repeatedly observed critical limitations in students in understanding the fundamental assumptions, premises, processing, and conclusions related to problems of the basic theorem of limx→0sinx/x. The students’ limitations are often manifested by extrapolation of the assumptions, misunderstanding of the premises, distortions of the processing pathways, and extrapolations of rules and conventions. However, findings on student misconceptions in the broad area of the calculus are widespread (e.g. Refs. [12,46,52]).

Previous discussions indicate that students experience misconceptions in learning mathematics need to be deeply explored. A variety of suggestions for mitigating documented limitation have surfaced. Thus, the present study was aimed to examine the sources of the misconceptions that generate errors in solving problems related to the theorem limx→0sinx/x among three cohorts of freshmen undergraduate engineering and technology students who completed the test (n = 251; 17 females) by analyzing their problem processing pathways and thought patterns and recommend corrective measures.

Materials and methods

Research participants

Participants of this research were a population of three cohorts of freshman students (n = 299; 27 females) enrolled in engineering and technology undergraduate programs in 2010/11 (n = 93; 8 females), 2011/12 (n = 92; 7 females), and 2012/13 (n = 114; 12 females) academic years in one higher education institute in Ethiopia up on earning required scores in the Higher Education Entrance Certificate Examination (HEECE) of the Country. According to the Ethiopian tradition, the top scorers in the HEECE are enrolled in medical schools, and the next top scorers are enrolled in engineering and technology programs. The Institute of the present study enrolls students through additional, non-criterion-referenced entrance examinations focusing on English language, mathematics, and aptitude to select the best scorers from applicants as many as twice its intake capacity. The average scores of the 2010/11, 2011/12, and 2012/13 cohorts on the HEECE were 65.9, 66.3, and 60.4, respectively.

Course (intervention) material

The intervention material of the study, called Engineering Mathematics, is a four traditional credit hours course offered as a faculty or college requirement to undergraduate engineering and technology students. It was designed based on the assumption that the students have basic background knowledge from their high school (grades 9 to 12) mathematics courses. The purpose of the course was to allow students to understand and evaluate the concept of limits and related topics as well as matrices and determinants and apply them to theoretical and practical engineering and technology fundamentals. It was organized in two parts. The first part included five chapters in limits (i.e., limits and continuity, derivatives, applications of derivatives, integrals, and application of definite integrals), while the second part included one chapter on matrices and determinants.

Course delivery and assessment

The course is offered during the first semester of the freshman program. It is delivered via four lectures and a tutorial per week, running for 16 weeks supported by a textbook and multiple reference materials. The duration of each lecture and tutorial is 55 min. Whereas the lectures were supported with PowerPoint printouts, the tutorials were supported by problem worksheets. Student assessments were based on four or more quizzes (10 %), four or more home works or assignments (10 %), one or more projects (10 %), a mid-exam (30 %), and a final comprehensive exam (40 %). Student grades are determined on the criterion-referenced scale.

Preparation of test items

The test included six items (Box 1). It was prepared to explore the processing pathways of the students in solving problems related to the basic limit theorem. The items were prepared by consulting known texts and reference books of college and university calculus courses that treat the basic limit theorem in depth [[60], [61], [62]]. The reference sources were helpful in showing multiple ways to prepare the items and their extensions. Thus, they have varying forms and shapes but the same surface structure as the basic limit theorem. The test was published on three A4 paper pages where half a page was left for each item. Furthermore, the right one-third of each half-page was left for rough work. Each of the test papers carried a unique code.

Validity and reliability of the test

The six items of the test were prepared by ensuring the coverage of the topic of the basic limit theorem and the representation of its key concepts and subtopics to warrant content validity. The test was proofread, evaluated, and approved by an experienced mathematics instructor in the Department of Mathematics who resided in the home university of the researchers. The reliability of the test was assessed by administering it twice to 30 randomly selected students of the 2009/10 Academic Year. The time between the two tests was 30 days. Analysis of the results yielded a correlation coefficient of 0.91 showing its high consistency.

Administration of the test

An average learner who understands the nature of the theorem would require two to 5 min to process and solve each of the test items. Thus, the maximum time required to complete the test is 30 min. However, since time was not a variable, the students were allowed to take up to 60 min to complete the test. The administration of the tests were carried out outside school (session) hours that were convenient for all of the students. About 84 % (n = 251; 17 females) of the students completed the test. Since the test papers were coded, the students were instructed not to put their names or any other information about their identities. All the students completed their tests within 60 min.

Scoring of the test papers and evaluation of student solution pathways

Students' test papers were scored by the first author using explicit scoring guides. The scoring guides include all possible problem-processing or solution pathways for each item. Thus, the scoring was aimed to check the validity of the solution processing pathways and the correctness of the final answer. Close examination of the scored (graded) students’ test papers revealed three pathways in processing each test item. The pathways were valid processing pathways toward correct answers (CAVP), invalid processing pathways toward correct answers (CAIP), and invalid processing pathways toward incorrect answers (IAIP). This exercise has yielded data on the proportions of processing pathways for each test item.

Student Interviews

A total of 15 participants (i.e., 5 from each cohort) were randomly selected to be interviewed on their attempts and successes in solving each of the test items using a corresponding interview question (Box 1) through three-step mental processing levels (Table 1). The three mental processing levels are recognizing (level 1), transforming (level 2), and applying (level 3). The interview format was adapted from the “Criterion for Error Causes” of ([22], Pp. 33–38) with some modifications to fit the goal of the present study. The interviews were carried out as question-response (Q-R) dialogues by providing cognitive support or cues when and if necessary. The interviewer marks the interviewees’ successes or failures in processing each test item at the first, second, or third level. The interviews were transcribed as a series of Q-R dialogues and the number of such dialogues for each interviewee was counted and recorded.

Data organization and analyses

The study generated quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitative data were analyzed using appropriate descriptive and inferential statistical methods by using the SPSS (Version 20) statistical software package. Post-hoc comparisons of mean (±SD) values were made at a priori set significance level of p ≤ 0.05 using Least Significance Difference. Qualitative data of interview responses were transcribed as a series of Q-R dialogues and presented as excerpts in boxes to corroborate the results of quantitative data analysis and subsequent discussions.

Ethical clearance

In adherence to the ethical standards to ensure respect for the integrity and humanity of participants, ethical clearance was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Mekelle University. Following this, the right of informed consent was implemented. Also, codes were used instead of names and data was stored in computers with strong passwords and print media in secured places order to ensure the anonymity of participants and the confidentiality of data.

Results

Students’ problem processing pathways

The students of the three cohorts follow valid or invalid problem-processing pathways in answering the test items related to the limit theorem of limx→0sinx/x. The valid processing pathways led the students to correct answers, while the invalid ones led to correct or incorrect answers. None of the students who used valid processing pathways gave incorrect answers. If we closely look into each of the test items, 46–68 % of the correct answers were achieved via invalid processing (CAIP) (Table 2). Students of 2011/12 (n = 79) achieved ≥50 % of their correct answers via invalid processing pathways in all of the six test items. The proportions of correct answers via invalid processing were more prevalent in test items 1, 2, 3, and 6. Only 12–39 % of the correct answers were achieved through valid processing.

Comparison of problem processing pathways among cohorts

One-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) revealed that comparisons of the mean (SD) values of students of the three cohorts adapting the IAIP and CAIP pathways did not show statistically significant differences (p ≥ 0.05) (Table 5). A statistically significant difference in mean (SD) values was observed when the cohorts were adapting the CAIP pathways (p ≤ 0.05). The results of the students in mean (SD) of each cohort showed that 11.2 (3.4) to 17.7 (4.8) of them adapted incorrect processing pathways toward incorrect answers (IAIP). Likewise, the mean (SD) values of students who adapted CAIP and CAVP were 42.0 (5.2) to 52.0 (5.0) and 19.7 (6.1) to 28.3 (6.5), respectively. In all three cohorts, for every student adapting the CAVP pathway, about two students were adapting the CAIP pathways. Here, the only statistically significant difference observed in the CAIP pathways was between the mean (SD) values of students of cohort 2010/11 and 2012/13 (Table 6). These imply that the frequencies of the faulty problem-processing pathways of the three cohorts were comparable.

Students’ thought patterns

Initially, the interviews were carried out without cognitive support or cues to look into the thought patterns of the students. The students were provided with expressions ‘2x+1’ and ‘2sinx+1’ and were asked if both of them had the same surface structure. They are derived from a foundational expression and used in subsequent questions related to the limit theorem being addressed in this study. The majority of students (12 out of 15) were unable to recognize that both expressions have the same surface structure. Yet, the three students who recognized the structural similarity were unable to express them completely and apply them. The students demonstrated very limited capacity in Interview Items 2 and 3 in recognizing the similarities and making transformations (Table 6).

Then, the same interviews were carried out for the second time with cognitive support and cues to assist the students in solving the problems via valid processing pathways. The cognitive supports and cues during the second interviews were instrumental in promoting students’ performances. All (100 %) of the students were able to recognize the structural similarity of the expressions of Interview Question 1. Ten (67 %) of the students were able to make complete transformations, and the other five (33 %) were able to solve the problems (Table 7). The proportions of the students recognizing structural similarities increased from 67 % (for Question 4) to 100 % (for Questions 1 to 3). With the exception of Interview Question 5, the proportions of the students who were able to make transformations of the expressions were 53–100 %. However, with one exception in Interview Question 3, the proportion of the students who was capable of solving the problems was 33 % or less.

Cognitive supports and cues in overcoming problem-processing difficulties

The purpose of using cognitive supports and cues in the interviews was to assist the interviewees in overcoming their difficulties in understanding and processing the Interview Questions. Excerpts from five interviewees are put in Box 4, Box 5 to demonstrate how cognitive supports and cues are embedded to bring about students’ successes. Interview excerpt of one interviewee (Student #3) showed that s/he needed cognitive support in the second Q-R dialogue to overcome her/his difficulties related to Interview Question 1 (Box 3). Similarly, analysis of interview excerpts of Interview Question 2 of Student #1 indicated that it was fully processed after five cognitive cues in a series of 11 Q-R dialogues (Box 4). Discussions

Foundational studies (e.g., Refs. [21,[54], [55], [56]]) on student learning difficulties related to calculus enabled researchers to note the raised areas of concern. It is well-established that misconceptions, also known as alternative conceptions cause learning difficulties because they are well-fit in the cognitive schema of learners to be used in subsequent learning. They can develop because of various factors. The present study demonstrated that misconceptions were the cause of faulty processing of problems related to the basic limit theorem limx→0(sinx/x) among three cohorts of freshman students of engineering and technology. The reasons for the demonstrated misconceptions were: (a) lack of solid prior knowledge and (b) misapplication of solid prior knowledge.

Lack of solid prior knowledge

The sources or causes of misconceptions and problem-processing difficulties among the students were linked to their lack of solid prior knowledge and/or understanding of the structure of the linear equations, the basic concept of function, and the basic concept of equivalence. The great majority of students were unable to detect the commonality between the simplest expressions 2x+1 and 2sinx+1. They didn't realize that both are linear expressions that can be put as ax+b, a≠0; and did not know that the second expression can be rewritten as 2y+1 (see Box 3) when y=sinx as observed elsewhere [63]. Due to such lack of knowledge or understanding, they were unable to note the structural similarity of limx→0(sinx/x) and limu→0(sin3u/3u) that made them miss that both expressions would have the same value upon correct processing. The evidence for lack of knowledge among those pursuing the problems via IAIP was not much different (see Box 2; Table 4). They were manifestations of a lack of knowledge or understanding of (a) the concept of vertical asymptote when the limit of a function fails to exist at x = 0; (b) the processing of the intermediate “0/0” of limits upon immediate substitution, and (c) the distinction between “the sign for a function” and “the sign of a variable”.

4.1.1. Inability to Make Inferences. Students of mathematics are expected to use prior knowledge to make plausible or correct deductions and inferences from mathematical propositions and experiences. However, the students in the present study could not make correct inferences as they failed to distinguish the similarities of the surface structures of the various expressions with the basic limit theorem. For example, they failed to notice that limu→0(sin3u/3u) and limx→0(sin(x2))/(x2) have the same value as limx→0(sinx/x)=1 by correct processing because they were expressed differently. They failed to notice that the “x” in the theorem can be substituted with “3u”, “tanx”, “x2” or any other variable to reach the same value of 1 upon correct processing (Box 2, Box 4; Box 4, Box 5). The theorem, like all others, constitutes a hypothesis (i.e., the given), conclusion (i.e., the part to be proven), and basic assumptions where the theorem works. Most mathematical problems are solved based on deductions from assumptions and previously proven mathematical propositions. In the present study, the students demonstrated a lack of prior knowledge on the assumptions to make inferences (see Box 2).

4.1.2 Inability to Distinguish between Sign for a Function and Sign for a Variable. In regard to basic concepts of a function, the students have assumed that “sign for a function” and “sign for a variable” as well as “power of a function” and “function of a power” are the same. Based on the first assumption, some students processed limu→0(sin3u)/(3u) as limu→0(3)/(3)∙limu→0(sinu)/(u) to reach 1∙1=1 via simplification (Box 2, Box 4). This is because they were assuming that “sin3u” is equal to “sine∙3u” even though it means “sine of 3u”. Where “sinx2” was, likewise, assumed to be equal to “sine∙x2” instead of “sine of x2”, other students processed limx→0sinx2/x2 via a commutative property of multiplication as limx→0(xsin(x))/(x∙x)=limx→0(x/x)∙limx→0(sin(x))/(x) to reach at 1∙1=1 (Box 2, 2b). A similar assumption was used to process limx→0(sin3x)/(x) to 3limx→0(sinx)/(x) to reach at 3∙1=3 by factoring out the “3” in the “sin3x” (Box 2, 2a). Similar findings were reported in the 1980s by Movshovitz-Hadar and colleagues [40] that of among 886 matriculation examines’ more than 59 % of the errors stemmed from such distortions and mechanistic approaches to the problems.

4.1.3 Inability to Distinguish between Power of a Function and Function of a Power. Where “power of a function” and “function of a power” were assumed to have the same meaning, the students would consider “(f(x))n”, “fn(x)”, and “f(xn)” to be equal. They thought that “sinx2” is equal to “(sinx)2” and “sinx2” is equal to “sinx∙sinx”. Therefore, they processed limx→0(sin(x2))/(x2) via a two-step expansion to limx→0(sin(x)sin(x))/(x∙x) and limx→0(sinx)/(x)∙limx→0(sinx)/(x) to reach at 1∙1=1 (Box 2, 2b). This same problem of the basic limit theorem was processed towards the same correct answer as limx→0(sin(x2))/(x2)=limx→0(xsin(x))/(x∙x)=limx→0(x/x)∙limx→0(xsin(x))/(x)= 1×1=1 based on the same assumption but using a different pathway (Box 2, 2a; Table 2).

4.1.4 Inability to Employ Rules of Equivalence. Equivalence is a very useful concept related to the basic limit theorem. However, it was not demonstrated in the interview responses of the students (see Box 4, Box 5). No student has produced equivalent expressions based on the concept of multiplication by 1 in their attempt to prove the similarity of the surface structure of the various expressions with the expression of the basic limit theorem. Assuming 3x=y, the expression limx→0(sin3x)/(x) can be rewritten as, limx→0(siny)/(y), after multiplication by 3/3 = 1 is carried out and letting 3x = y, and produce the same surface structure as limx→0(sinx)/(x). But the students processed limx→0(sin3x)/(x) to 3limx→0(sinx)/(x)=3∙1=3 by assuming “sin3x” to be equal to “sine∙3x” (Box 1, Box 2). Students' test answers and interview responses didn't show how equivalent expressions can be reverted to the original ones by simplification. Producing equivalence involves and is grounded on the multiplicative property of 1 on the set of real numbers.

Misapplication of solid prior knowledge

As it was reported elsewhere [64] the other critical problem observed in this study was the misapplication of solid prior knowledge as demonstrated through improper use of rules, avoidance of conventions, introduction of indistinct versions of conventions, and partial use of rules and conventions.

4.2.1 Improper Use of Rules. Improper uses of established mathematical rules were common in the present study. A student among the 15 interviewees used an established rule improperly to arrive at a correct answer (Box 5). The exercise of the student went as, limx→0(sin3u/3u)=limx→0(3/3∙sinu/u)=1∙1=1, where matches were made between “x” and “sinx” and between “3” and “sin3” by assuming that limx→0(sinx/x)=1 and limx→0(sin3/3)=1. Even though the basic limit theorem was stated and proven explicitly by establishing its assumptions, many students were unable to apply the rules and assumptions of the theorem properly. The students failed to realize limx→0(sin3/3) is constant because sin3/3 is a real number (see also Box 2, Box 4). This problem was also observed among students taking incorrect processing pathways toward incorrect answers (IAIP) (see Table 4). They failed to distinguish between the “sign for a function” and the “sign for a variable” which led them to process the problems via cancellations (Table 4, Pathways 1c, 4c, 5c, and 6c).

4.2.2 Avoidance of Conventions. The students also ignored that “sinx” means “sine function acting on the variable x” where the “sine” and the “x” are inseparable. Thus, the students factored out the “3” in the limx→0(sin3x)/(x) into 3limx→0(sinx)/(x) to arrive at 3×1=3. The students were required to reach an expression that exactly resembles the original basic limit theorem up on correct processing. Unfortunately, they missed the convention that sinx is a standard abbreviated name for the sine function and factored the “x” following the “sine” to reach the original basic theorem.

4.2.3 Introduction of Indistinct Versions of Conventions and Rules. Introduction of indistinct versions of conventions and rule were also common. In this regard, the way the expression limx→0(sin(x2))/(xsinx) was processed is quite intriguing. It was simply processed into limx→0(xsin(x))/(xsinx) and then expanded to limx→0(x)/(sinx)∙limx→0(sinx)/(x) to reach the correct answer of 1∙1=1 (Box 2, Box 3). The students processed the “sin(x2)” into “xsinx”. Somewhat sensible processing mistakes such as sin(x2) = (sinx)2=sinx∙sinx or sin(x2)=(sinx)∙x=xsinx were not observed in this specific case. In cases where students use indistinct versions of conventions and rules, it is very difficult to judge which rules and conventions were being really employed and which previous knowledge has affected their thinking process. They processed limx→0(x)/(sinx)=1 correctly but were not able to show why and how (NB: limx→0(x)/(sinx)=1/limx→0(sinx/x)=1/1=1). A study of answer papers processed via the IAIP showed that the concept of ∞ was confused with a number and the “property of zero” was employed following that confusion (Table 4, Pathway 4a). This indistinct processing confused and misapplied the concepts of infinity and the indeterminate form of 0/0. Such student difficulties related limits at infinity and the indeterminate forms are nothing new [5]; as well as [21] discussed the situation at an in-depth level.

4.2.4 Partial Use of Conventions and Rules. Some problem-processing pathways demonstrated the partial use of conventions and rules. In line with this [40], reported that a considerable number of students (18 % by proportion) commit errors due to misinterpreted mathematical language or convention in their attempt to solve mathematical problems. The processing of limx→0(sin3x)/(x) as limx→0(sin3x∙1/3)/(x∙1/3) to 3∙limx→0sinx/x then to 3∙1=3 was carried out using the rule of complex fractions, where (a/b)/(c/d) is expressed as ad/bc (Box 1, Box 2). The use of the rule of complex fractions is correct. However, since sin3x is “sine of 3x” but not “sine multiplied by 3x” as per the convention (rule) of the sine function (abbreviated as “sin”), it is wrong to treat the “3x” separately from the “sine function” which acts on it and multiply it be “1/3”. Here, conventions and rules related to “complex fractions” and “elimination” was misapplied. These problems were prevalent among students pursuing the IAIP (Table 4, Pathways 1a, 1 b, 2 b, 2c, 3a, 3 b, 4a, 4 b, 4c, 5a, 5 b, 6a, and 6 b).

4.2.5 Extrapolation of Conventions and Rules. When a student misses the basic procedure in an attempt to solve a problem or fails to transform/translate into its familiar form, it said to be a procedural error [52]. In the present study, we observed that students had difficulties in transforming the problem of the basic limit theorem that goes as limx→0(sin3xx) can be processed using many pathways. Here three solution pathways can be provided as examples. (i) The problem can be rewritten as 3(sin3x3x) using the notion of surface structure. Then, the expression 3(sin3x3x) can be put in a more familiar form as, 3∙(sinyy), where y=3x and y→0, as x→0. (ii) The problem can also be processed by using the double angle formula and Pythagorean properties that involve familiar arithmetic rules. Thus, it goes as sin(3x)=sin(2x+x)=sin2xcosx+cos2xsinx=(2sinxcosxcosx+(cos2x−sin2x)sinx=2sinxcos2x+cos2xsinx−sin3x=3sinxcos2x−sin3x. (iii) Application of L'Hopital's Rule for intermediate forms of 0/0 by independently taking the derivative of the numerator and denominator can also help in solving the problem by equating the limits of sin3x/x as x→0 and 3cos3x/1 as x→0. Unfortunately, none of the students was successful in using any of the processing pathways correctly, specifically those who are in the CAIP and IAIP.

(a)Double angle formula. [26] convincingly argued that “incorrect new learning is mostly the result of previous correct learning and that the source of misconceptions is mostly an overgeneralization of previous knowledge (that was correct in an earlier domain), to an extended domain (where it is not valid).” As per the double angle formula, for any angle x, sin2x=2sinxcosx. This expression uniquely works for sin2x only. But the students have extrapolated it to process sin3x towards 3sinxcosx and developed a general version that goes as “for any angle x, sinax=asinxcosx”. Based on this generalization, they processed a limit problem limx→0(sin3x)/(x) into limx→0(3sinxcos)/(x) (Box 1, Box 2). In line with this, an interviewee demonstrated an unwarranted extrapolation of the expression limx→0(sin8x/12x)=limx→0(8sinxcosx/12x)=8/12limx→0sinx/x∙limx→0cosx=(2/3)∙1∙1=2/3. Thus, the general processing goes as limx→0(sinax/sinbx)=limx→0(asinxcosx/bsinxcosx)=(a/b)∙1∙1=a/b (Refer also Box 4 for a similar case). This invalid extrapolation has led the students toward correct answers.

Limitation of the study

This study reported on a small number of participants (n = 251) in three cohorts each less than a hundred. The results can help to get an insight on student learning difficulties and misconceptions so as to reflect and shed light on how students learn calculus theorems with meaning. The findings unveiled in this study could inform the professional community, and maybe these results will instigate further research in this area broadly. Such a work is rare in the sense that it investigates students’ misconceptions of a specific theorem. The test and interview items are just the first generation of an instrument that can provide good information about student misconceptions. The research should proceed in new and compressive dimensions in the higher institutions when calculus is found to be a companion course to mathematics-intensive fields of study such as the recent engineering and technology institutes.

Conclusion and recommendations

Basic school subjects of computation and computing such as calculus are important tools for learning and functioning in the applied subjects of engineering, science, and technology. The lack of and defective understandings and misconceptions in the basic subjects can lead to practical problems and even disasters in these applied fields of knowledge and trade. For this and other reasons, higher education and training curricula of the applied subjects of engineering, science, and technology are customarily supported with relevant computational science and computing courses. However, despite the students and trainees who join engineering, science, and technology programs in colleges and universities have better interests, backgrounds, and preparations in math and computing, they are often faced with learning difficulties in these subjects.

In this study, we analyzed factors responsible for student misconception which causes errors to come off. Our findings indicated that many students have developed invalid solution paths and processes toward producing correct answers in their attempt to solve problems based on the basic theorem limx→0(sinx/x). Two broad factors were responsible for these misconceptions to occur. They are misapplications of prior knowledge and false extensions based on solid prior knowledge. Misapplications of prior knowledge involve the misuse of established relations in an attempt to solve problems in new situations. For example, the linearity property was misused in evaluating limx→0(sin3x/x) in the numerator of the quotient function sin3x wherein some students wrote it as: sin3x=sin(x+x+x)=sinx+sin+xsinx=3sinx (see Box 1, Box 2). On the other hand, grounded in prior knowledge (mostly of correct learning), some students wrongly extend and apply their previous correct learning to new and different conditions. For example, the double angle formula was falsely extended in students’ attempts to solve problems related to limx→0(sinx/x) ; sin3x was expressed as sin3x=3sinxcosx to reach a correct answer (see Box 1, Box 2).

In summary, factors of the first type can sometimes be explained by “over studying” of basic rules and properties while ignoring their underlying assumptions, premises, and antecedents. In particular, linearity, the sign for a function, and conventions were misused in this way. Factors of the second type can occasionally be elucidated by technical learning of a rule and committing it to memory through abbreviations or shortcuts of the entire statement. However, the interview excerpts showed that the learning difficulties are fixable even though via lengthy cognitive supports and cues.

The findings in this study obliged us to put the following recommendations for those who have the possibility of teaching the unifying concept of limits in calculus. We propose some preventive treatments or corrective measures based on the findings presented in this manuscript that university mathematics teachers might have to attend.
