A tropical framework for using Porteous formula
Andrew R. Tawfeek

Abstract
. Given a rational polyhedral space X X (a tropical cycle with boundary, in the sense of Mikhalkin–Rau), one can define tropical vector bundles on X X having real or tropical fibers. By restricting attention to bounded rational sections of these bundles, one obtains characteristic classes that behave as expected classically. We develop further properties of these classes and use them to prove a tropical analogue of the splitting principle, which allows us to establish the foundations for Porteous’ formula in this setting: a determinantal expression for the fundamental class of degeneracy loci in terms of Chern classes. The boundary framework is essential, as it allows the rank of a bundle morphism to drop at sedentary strata, giving degeneracy loci their expected codimension.



1.
Introduction

In classical algebraic geometry, Porteous’ formula describes the
fundamental class
of degeneracy loci: the locus of points where the rank of a bundle morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
over
X
falls below an integer
k
.

Theorem 1.0.1

(Porteous Formula)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
be a morphism of vector bundles of ranks
e
and
f
on a smooth variety
X
. If the scheme
D_{k}(\varphi)\subseteq X
has codimension
(e-k)(f-k)
, then its class is given by

[D_{k}(\varphi)]=\Delta_{f-k}^{e-k}\left[\frac{c(\mathcal{F})}{c(\mathcal{E})}\right].

The right-hand side of the Porteous formula is a Sylvester determinant whose entries depend only on the Chern classes of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{E}
. In other words, by expressing a subspace of interest as a degeneracy locus of a bundle morphism, one can deduce topological information from knowledge of the bundles alone.

We adapt this statement to the tropical setting using the language of tropical vector bundles and Chern classes, largely building on the work of
[
2
]
. We prove a tropical analogue of Porteous’ formula in the rank-
0
case (Theorem
6.4.1
), with the resulting fundamental class residing in the tropical Chow ring:

Theorem 1.0.2

(Tropical Porteous Formula in Rank
0
)
.

Consider a morphism of tropical vector bundles
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
with bounded matrix entries over a rational polyhedral space
X
, where the bundles have ranks
e
and
f
, respectively. Suppose
D_{0}(\varphi)
has the expected codimension
ef
. Then

[D_{0}^{\varphi}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})]=\Delta_{f}^{e}\left(\frac{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})}{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})}\right),

where
c_{[t]}(-)
denotes the Chern polynomial of the vector bundle.

Along the way, we develop the necessary constructions for tropical vector bundles, including Chern classes, projectivization, intersection products, and a tropical splitting principle.

2.1.
Rational Polyhedral Spaces

We take the polytopal perspective following
[
16
,
1
]
. The key departure from the setup of
[

]
is that we work in the
extended tropical affine space

\mathbb{T}^{n}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{n}
rather than

. This allows polyhedra to have faces meeting the boundary of
\mathbb{T}^{n}

Definition 2.1.1

(Sedentarity)
.

For a point
p\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the
sedentarity
of
p
is the set
I(p)=\{i\in[n]\mid p_{i}=-\infty\}
. The
sedentarity order

\text{sed}(p)=|I(p)|
counts the number of coordinates equal to
-\infty
. For each subset
I\subseteq[n]
, the
sedentary stratum

\mathbb{T}^{n}_{I}=\{p\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\mid I(p)=I\}
is naturally identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n-|I|}
, and we have

\mathbb{T}^{n}=\bigsqcup_{I\subseteq[n]}\mathbb{T}^{n}_{I}.

The open stratum
\mathbb{T}^{n}_{\emptyset}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the
interior
.

Definition 2.1.2

(Rational polyhedral complex in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
)
.

A
rational polyhedral complex

X
in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is a weighted, pure-dimensional, rational, finite polyhedral complex in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
(with underlying lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
) that satisfies the
balancing condition
for every
\tau\in X^{(k-1)}
:

\sum_{\sigma:\tau<\sigma}\omega(\sigma)v_{\sigma/\tau}=0

where
v_{\sigma/\tau}
denotes the primitive integer generator of the ray obtained from projecting
\sigma
to the vector quotient
\mathbb{R}^{n}/V_{\tau}
, with
V_{\tau}
denoting the linear vector space spanned by
\tau
. Faces of
X
may be
sedentary
, meaning they are contained in a boundary stratum
\mathbb{T}^{n}_{I}
for some
I\neq\emptyset
. When a ridge
\tau
is sedentary with
\tau\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{n}_{I}
, the balancing condition is formulated within the stratum
\mathbb{R}^{n-|I|}
in which
\tau
has its interior; see
[
16
]
Ch. 4 for details.

We call the top-dimensional polyhedra in a pure-dimensional
X
the
facets
and the codimension one polyhedra the
corners
(or
ridges
) of
X
. We denote by
|X|
the
support
, which is the union of all facets of
X
having non-zero weight. Two rational polyhedral complexes are
equivalent
if they admit a common refinement with the same induced weights. A
rational polyhedral space
(or
tropical cycle
)
X
is an equivalence class of rational polyhedral complexes. When
X
has no sedentary faces (i.e.,
|X|\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
), this recovers the framework of
[
1
]
.

Remark 1
.

The framework of rational polyhedral spaces in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
was developed by Mikhalkin–Rau
[
16
]
. In this setting, the boundary strata of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
give the ambient space the structure of a manifold with corners. When a polyhedral complex
X
has faces meeting these boundary strata, continuous functions on
|X|
(including matrix entries of a bundle morphism) can degenerate to
-\infty
as one approaches the boundary, enabling rank to drop. This is precisely the mechanism by which degeneracy loci acquire positive codimension.

3.
Tropical Vector Bundles

3.1.
Tropical Linear Algebra

Definition 3.1.1

(Tropical matrices)
.

A
tropical matrix
will be a matrix with entries in the tropical semi-ring
(\mathbb{T},\oplus,\odot)
where
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and

a\oplus b:=\max\{a,b\}\quad\text{ and }\quad a\odot b:=a+b.

We will be using the following notation:

•

\text{Mat}(m\times n,\mathbb{T})
for the set of tropical
m\times n
matrices,

•

\text{G}(r\times s)\subseteq\text{Mat}(r\times s,\mathbb{T})
for the subset having at most one finite entry in each row,

•

\text{G}(r)\subseteq\text{G}(r\times r)
for the subset with exactly one finite entry in every row and column.

Lastly, when multiplying matrices
A\in\text{Mat}(m\times n,\mathbb{T})
and
B\in\text{Mat}(n\times p,\mathbb{T})
, we take their tropical product
A\odot B:=(c_{ij})\in\text{Mat}(m\times p,\mathbb{T})
where
c_{ij}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\odot b_{kj}
.

Remark 3
.

A matrix
A\in\text{G}(r\times s)
, for an
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, may send
x
to
A\odot x\not\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
(i.e. contain entries that are
-\infty
). If we need to interpret such a matrix as a map
f_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
, we redefine any entries that are
-\infty
in
A\odot x
to be zero.

For a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we denote by
E_{\sigma}\in\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})
the matrix

e_{ij}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $j=\sigma(i)$}\\
-\infty&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

which plays a role analogous to the permutations matrices, but in the tropical setting. Furthermore, for choices of
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
D(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\text{G}(n)
the matrix with
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
along the diagonal and
-\infty
elsewhere.

Remark 4
.

Using this, we may then write any matrix
M\in\text{G}(n)
as

M=E_{\sigma}\odot D(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

for some
\sigma\in S_{n}
and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. This makes it evident that
\text{G}(n)
is a group under tropical matrix multiplication, having identity element
D(0,\dots,0)
, which we denote by
E_{n}
to distinguish it from
I_{n}\in\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 3.1.2
.

The group
\text{G}(n)\subseteq\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})
is precisely the collection of invertible tropical matrices:

\text{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{T})=G(n)=\{A\in\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})\ |\ (\exists B\in\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})):\ A\odot B=B\odot A=E_{n}\}.

The proof is a short computation (resembling those in the proof of Lemma
3.1.8
below), as these matrices behave similarly to permutation matrices. In fact,
\text{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{T})
is isomorphic to
S_{n}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so one may think of invertible tropical matrices as permutation matrices with
0
’s replaced by
-\infty
and
1
-entries replaced by elements of
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3.1.3

(Tropical determinant)
.

Consider a tropical matrix
A\in\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})
. We define the
tropical determinant
to be

\text{tropdet}(A)=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(A_{1,\sigma(1)}\odot A_{2,\sigma(2)}\odot\cdots\odot A_{n,\sigma(n)})

Lemma 3.1.4
.

Consider a tropical matrix
A\in\text{G}(n\times n)
. We then have that

\text{tropdet}(A)\neq-\infty\iff A\in\text{G}(n).

That is to say, the only tropical matrices with “non-trivial” determinants are precisely those that are invertible.

Proof.

The reverse direction is clear, so we focus on the forward direction. Recall that if
A\in\text{G}(n\times n)
, then
A
has
at most
one finite entry in each row. Without loss of generality, assume the finite entries (if they exist) of
A
lie along the diagonal, i.e.

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&-\infty&\cdots&-\infty\\
-\infty&a_{2}&\cdots&-\infty\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\infty&-\infty&\cdots&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}

where
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. Observe then for any
\sigma\in S_{n}
,

A_{1,\sigma(1)}\odot A_{2,\sigma(2)}\odot\cdots\odot A_{n,\sigma(n)}=-\infty\iff(\exists i\in[n]):\ A_{i,\sigma(i)}=-\infty.

Therefore we may conclude that

\displaystyle\text{tropdet}(A)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(A_{1,\sigma(1)}\odot A_{2,\sigma(2)}\odot\cdots\odot A_{n,\sigma(n)})

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\{A_{1,\sigma(1)}+A_{2,\sigma(2)}+\cdots+A_{n,\sigma(n)}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{-\infty,\dots,-\infty,\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i},-\infty,\dots,-\infty\}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n}.

And so if we write a tropical matrix
A\in G(n\times n)
as
A_{\sigma}\odot D(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
for
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{T}
, we have that

\text{tropdet}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}

which will be finite (not
-\infty
) if and only if each
\beta_{i}
is finite, i.e.
A\in G(n)\subseteq G(n\times n)
, thus
A
is an invertible tropical matrix.
∎

The above statement is reminiscent of
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
; the reader interested in pursuing the tropical linear algebra framework further is directed there.

Remark 5
.

Observe that this does not hold for a general tropical matrix in
\text{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{T})
:

\text{tropdet}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\infty\end{pmatrix}=(0\odot-\infty)\oplus(0\odot 0)=-\infty\oplus 0=0,

but the above matrix has more than one finite entry in its first row, so it is not in
\text{G}(2)
.

Definition 3.1.5

(Tropical rank)
.

We now define for
A\in\text{G}(n\times m)
its
tropical rank
, which is crafted in analogy with the classical case:

\text{troprank}(A)=k\iff\begin{cases}\text{all $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ minors vanish, and}\\
\text{there exists a non-vanishing $k\times k$ minor.}\end{cases}

Where we say a minor
vanishes
if it equals
-\infty
.

The “correct” notion of rank for tropical matrices is a matter of debate, but the definition above satisfies the properties we require. Other variants, such as
Barvinok rank
and
Kapranov rank
, are related as follows:

Theorem 3.1.6

(
[
5
]
)
.

For every matrix
M
with entries in the tropical semiring,

\text{troprank}(M)\leq\text{Kapranov rank}(M)\leq\text{Barvinok rank}(M).

Both of these inequalities can be strict.

Geometrically, every
(d\times n)
-matrix has an image that is a tropical polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}
(see
[
15
, §5.2]
). The tropical rank of a matrix is the dimension of this tropical polytope
plus one
.

Example 3.1.7
.

Unlike working over
\mathbb{R}
, (tropical) rank is not generally additive over
\mathbb{T}
:

\text{troprank}\begin{pmatrix}2&-\infty&-\infty\\
-\infty&-\infty&3\end{pmatrix}=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{troprank}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\infty&-\infty\\
-\infty&0&-\infty\\
-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\end{pmatrix}=2

since
\left|\begin{smallmatrix}2&-\infty\\
-\infty&3\end{smallmatrix}\right|=5\neq-\infty
and
\left|\begin{smallmatrix}0&-\infty\\
-\infty&0\end{smallmatrix}\right|=0\neq-\infty
, yet

\begin{pmatrix}2&-\infty&-\infty\\
-\infty&-\infty&3\end{pmatrix}\odot\begin{pmatrix}0&-\infty&-\infty\\
-\infty&0&-\infty\\
-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-\infty&-\infty\\
-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\end{pmatrix}

but the resulting matrix has tropical rank
1
. Thus for matrices
A\in\text{G}(n\times m)
and
B\in G(m\times s)
, generally

\text{troprank}(A\odot B)\neq\text{troprank}(A)+\text{troprank}(B).

However, repositioning the finite entries via a tropical change of basis restores equality. We make this precise below.

Lemma 3.1.8
.

Let
A\in\text{G}(n\times m)
and take
N\in\text{G}(n)
and
M\in\text{G}(m)
. Then

\text{troprank}(A)=\text{troprank}(N\odot A\odot M)

and
\text{troprank}(A)
is the number of columns having at least one finite entry.

Proof.

First we prove that
\text{troprank}(A)
is the number of columns having at least one finite entry. The matrices in
G(n\times m)
are only subject to having at most one finite entry in each row. As such, there may be empty (all
-\infty
) columns or rows, which do not contribute the rank. Assume then without loss of generality that
A
has no such empty rows or columns. Then the dimension of
A
is
k\times\ell
with
k\geq\ell
, and since a minor is a determinant of a square matrix, we must have that
\text{troprank}(A)=\ell
. But as
\ell
was the number of columns having a finite entry, we are done.

To prove the second statement, by Remark
4
, we may write

N=E_{\sigma}\odot D(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})\quad\text{ and }\quad M=E_{\tau}\odot D(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m})

where
\beta_{i},\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. This allows us to express the product as

N\odot A\odot M=E_{\sigma}\odot D(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})\odot A\odot E_{\tau}\odot D(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}).

We may assume without loss of generality that
\beta_{i},\gamma_{j}
are all zero, as the only aspect that would distinguish rank is whether entries are infinite. But as
D(0,\dots,0)
is the identity matrix, this simplifies this expression immediately:

E_{\sigma}\odot E_{n}\odot A\odot E_{\tau}\odot E_{m}=E_{\sigma}\odot A\odot E_{\tau}.

This situation is now analogous to multiplying with permutation matrices, where
E_{\sigma}
is permuting the rows of
A
and
E_{\tau}
the columns. It follows that
\oplus_{\alpha\in S_{k}}\odot_{i=1}^{k}a_{i,\alpha(i)}\neq-\infty
if and only if
\oplus_{\alpha\in S_{k}}\odot_{i=1}^{k}a_{\sigma(i),\tau(\alpha(i))}\neq-\infty
, hence
\text{troprank}(A)=\text{troprank}(N\odot A\odot M)
.
∎

Many common matrix operations carry over to the tropical setting, but multiplication and addition must be replaced by their tropical counterparts.

Definition 3.1.9

(Direct sum and tensor products of tropical matrices)
.

Consider two tropical matrices
A=(a_{ij})\in\text{Mat}(m\times n,\mathbb{T})
and
B=(b_{st})\in\text{Mat}(k\times\ell,\mathbb{T})
, we then define

•

the
direct sum
to be the
(m+k)\times(n+\ell)
tropical matrix

A\oplus B=\begin{pmatrix}A&-\infty\\
-\infty&B\end{pmatrix},

•

the
tensor product
to be the
mk\times n\ell
tropical matrix

A\otimes B=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}\odot B&\cdots&a_{1n}\odot B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}\odot B&\cdots&a_{mn}\odot B\end{pmatrix}.

It follows that the tensor product of tropical matrices expands over their direct sum, i.e.

\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)\otimes B=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}(A_{i}\otimes B),

which can be seen by expanding out the entries.

Remark 6
.

In the original source

There has been considerable work with the above definition of tropical vector bundles (introduced by Allermann

, the authors studied the case where

Adopting this language, we may then interpret tropical vector bundles of rank

Proposition 3.2.2

(
[

)

The category of tropical vector bundles of rank

That is, a tropical vector bundle of rank

In perhaps a different flavor,

generalizes this definition of tropical vector bundles to apply more broadly to tropical/monoidal schemes, proving in Proposition 5.1 that for a pair

These definitions are straightforwardly consistent with one another, i.e. every short exact sequence of tropical vector bundles is split.

Definition 3.2.10
.

With notation as above, we define the

given by

Lemma 3.2.11

Let

be local trivializations of a tropical vector bundle morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
. We denote by

the associated transition maps. Then for

where we have the transition maps

Proof.

This statement is equivalent to the content that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(U\cap V)\times\mathbb{T}^{e}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(U\cap V)\times\mathbb{T}^{e}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(U\cap V)\times\mathbb{T}^{f}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(U\cap V)\times\mathbb{T}^{f}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{id}\times A_{U}{\Large|}_{U\cap V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{id}\times A_{V}{\Large|}_{U\cap V}}

The above allows us to establish what is one of the most important tools for our subsequent work.

When the base space
X
has sedentary strata (boundary), the entries of
A_{i}(p)
that are regular invertible functions on the interior of a face can approach
-\infty
as
p
approaches a sedentary face of
X
. At such boundary points, the induced stalk map
\varphi_{p}
has strictly lower rank than at interior points. This is the mechanism by which degeneracy loci acquire positive codimension.

Corollary 3.2.12
.

Given a tropical vector bundle morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
over a rational polyhedral space
X
, we have an induced rank function

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{rank($\varphi$)}:\hskip-28.90755pt}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{|X|}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})}}

which is locally constant on the interior of each top-dimensional face, but may drop at sedentary strata. In particular,
\text{rank}(\varphi)
is upper semicontinuous on
|X|
.

Proof.

Well-definedness follows from Lemma
3.2.11
and Lemma
3.1.8
. On the interior of a top-dimensional face
\sigma
of
X
, the entries of the local matrix
A_{i}(p)
are continuous functions valued in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
whose non-
(-\infty)
entries are regular invertible (hence piecewise-linear on
\mathbb{R}
). On connected open subsets of the interior, the set of non-
(-\infty)
entries is constant, so
\text{rank}(\varphi)
is locally constant there.

At a sedentary face
\tau\subseteq|X|
(where some coordinates of the ambient
\mathbb{T}^{n}
equal
-\infty
), regular invertible functions on the interior that extend continuously to
\tau
may take the value
-\infty
at
\tau
, causing additional entries of
A_{i}(p)
to become
-\infty
. Since additional entries becoming
-\infty
can only decrease the tropical rank,
\text{rank}(\varphi)
is upper semicontinuous.
∎

Remark 7
.

Upper semicontinuity of the rank function means that the sublevel sets
D_{k}(\varphi)=\{p\in|X|\mid\text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})\leq k\}
are closed in
|X|
for each
k
. On the interior, the locally constant behavior implies
D_{k}(\varphi)\cap X^{\circ}
is a union of connected components of
X^{\circ}
. The genuine rank drop occurs at sedentary strata, giving
D_{k}(\varphi)
additional faces of positive codimension.

Lemma 3.2.13
.

Consider a morphism of tropical vector bundles
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
over a rational polyhedral space
X
, with
\text{rank}\ \mathcal{E}=e
and
\text{rank}\ \mathcal{F}=f
. Then the sublevel sets

D_{k}(\varphi)=\{p\in|X|\mid\text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})\leq k\}

for
0\leq k\leq\min(e,f)
are closed subsets of
|X|
that inherit the structure of rational polyhedral subspaces of
X
.

Proof.

That
D_{k}(\varphi)
is closed follows from the upper semicontinuity of
\text{rank}(\varphi)
(Corollary
3.2.12
). To see that
D_{k}(\varphi)
inherits a rational polyhedral structure, pass to a refinement of
X
on which
\text{rank}(\varphi)
is constant on the interior of each top-dimensional face
\sigma
(this is possible since the rank function is locally constant on the interior). Then
D_{k}(\varphi)
is a union of closures of those top-dimensional faces
\sigma
on which
\text{rank}(\varphi)|_{\sigma^{\circ}}\leq k
, together with any sedentary faces where the rank drops further. It remains to verify balancing. Let
\tau
be a codimension-one face (ridge) of
D_{k}(\varphi)
. If
\tau
lies in the interior of
|X|
, then upper semicontinuity forces
\text{rank}(\varphi)|_{\tau}\leq k
, and since rank is constant on each adjacent facet interior, every facet
\sigma>\tau
in
X
with
\text{rank}(\varphi)|_{\sigma^{\circ}}\leq k
is in
D_{k}(\varphi)
. But since
\text{rank}(\varphi)|_{\tau}\leq k
and rank is locally constant on each
\sigma^{\circ}
, all facets adjacent to
\tau
satisfy
\text{rank}(\varphi)|_{\sigma^{\circ}}\leq k
(otherwise
\tau
would not be a codimension-one face of
D_{k}(\varphi)
, but rather a boundary face). Hence
\tau
has the same adjacent facets and weights in
D_{k}(\varphi)
as in
X
, so the balancing sum is inherited. For sedentary ridges, the balancing condition is likewise inherited within the appropriate stratum.
∎

4.

Definition 4.0.1

(Rational sections of vector bundles)
.

Let
\pi:F\to X
be a vector bundle of rank

. A

of

is a continuous map

such that

(a)

\pi\circ s=\text{id}_{|X|}
, i.e.

is a section of
\pi
, and

(b)

there exists a cover of

by local trivializations and with associated homeomorphisms

such that for each

the components of

are rational functions on

, i.e.

\Phi\circ s=(\text{id}_{U},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}):U\rightarrow U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
s_{j}\in\mathcal{K}(U)
for each
1\leq j\leq n
.

We say a rational section
s:X\to F
is

if the components are bounded over each local trivialization.

Remark 8
.

For the remainder of the paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise, all the tropical vector bundles we discuss will be assumed to admit bounded rational sections.

An important fact we use routinely is the following: if
\mathcal{L}\to X
is a line bundle, then a rational section
s:X\to\mathcal{L}
naturally corresponds to a Cartier divisor
\{(U_{i},\widehat{s}_{i})\}
, which we denote by

. We state the following result, directing the reader to the source for the proof.

Lemma 4.0.2

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\pi:L\to X
be a line bundle and let
s_{1},s_{2}:X\to L
be two bounded rational sections. Then

[\mathcal{D}(s_{1})]=[\mathcal{D}(s_{2})]\in\text{Div}(X).

Remark 9
.

Most importantly, the above lemma lets us now associate to any line bundle
\mathcal{L}
that admits a bounded rational section

a well-defined Cartier divisor class

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}):=[\mathcal{D}(s)]\in\text{Div}(X).

Theorem 4.0.3

(

]
)

Let

be a vector bundle of rank

on a simply connected rational polyhedral space

. Then

is a direct sum of line bundles, i.e. there exist line bundles

Definition 4.0.4

(Global intersection cycles)
.

Let

Theorem 4.0.8

(Tropical Whitney’s Formula)
.

If we have a short exact sequence of finite rank tropical vector bundles over

]

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\geq 0}\left(\sum_{i+j=p}c_{i}(\mathcal{E})c_{j}(\mathcal{G})\right)=\sum_{p\geq 0}c_{p}(\mathcal{F})

∎

An important consequence of Whitney’s formula is that it expresses higher Chern classes of a splitting vector bundle in terms of first Chern classes.

Corollary 4.0.9
.

If a finite rank tropical vector bundle splits as a direct sum of tropical line bundles, i.e.

By definition,

∎

This decomposition of higher Chern classes into first Chern classes is a highly desirable property. Even when a vector bundle does not split, one can construct a space where its pullback does, perform computations with the resulting “virtual” Chern roots, and then push the result back down. This is the core principle behind the splitting construction, which we prove below in the tropical setting.

Definition 5.1.1

(Tropical projective space)

The

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\sim(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\iff(\exists\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{\times}):\ (x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=(\lambda\odot y_{0},\dots,\lambda\odot y_{n}).

Note that
\mathbb{T}^{\times}=\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-\infty\}
and the above condition asks that

for all
0\leq i\leq n

Remark 11

We may cover

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{x_{0}-x_{i}}_{y_{1}}:\cdots:\underbrace{x_{i-1}-x_{i}}_{y_{i}}:0:\underbrace{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}_{y_{i+1}}:\cdots:\underbrace{x_{n}-x_{i}}_{y_{n}}]

for each choice of

, having an inverse given by

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{\pi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{\varphi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

The pullback
\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{E})
is also of rank
n

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}
, i.e. we associate to the point
(p,[L])\in{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})

. Let us make this precise. Letting

Lemma 5.1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\to X
be a rank
n
tropical vector bundle. Then

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{E})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{E}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}

is a short exact sequence of tropical vector bundles over
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})
.

Proof.

We show that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}
is a rank
1
subbundle of
\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{E})
in the tropical sense, from which it follows that
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{E}}
is the corresponding quotient and the sequence is exact. Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{s}
be an open cover by trivializations of
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
. As
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})

\Phi_{i}|_{\hat{\pi}|_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}(U_{i})}:(\hat{\pi}|_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1})(U_{i})\rightarrow U_{i}\times\langle e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

for

\mathbb{R}

, by taking

∎

Lemma 5.1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\to X
be a rank
n+1
tropical vector bundle. Then the induced pullback map

\varphi^{*}:A_{k}(X)\rightarrow A_{k+n}(\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}))

is a split monomorphism.

Proof.

We show that
\beta\mapsto\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot\beta)
for
\beta\in\text{im}\varphi^{*}\subseteq A_{k+n}({\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}))
is an inverse of
\varphi^{*}
. It suffices to prove that
\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot[{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})])=[X]
.

The key input is the
projection formula
(
[
9
]
; cf. Theorem
4.0.10
(2) for the special case of pullback Chern classes): for
\alpha\in A({\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}))
and
\gamma\in A(X)
,

\varphi_{*}(\alpha\cdot\varphi^{*}(\gamma))=\varphi_{*}(\alpha)\cdot\gamma.

It therefore suffices to show that
\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot[{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})])=[X]
, as then for
\beta=\varphi^{*}(\gamma)
we have

\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot\varphi^{*}(\gamma))=\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot[{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})])\cdot\gamma=[X]\cdot\gamma=\gamma.

To establish this, we use fiber integration. Each fiber of
\varphi:{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})\to X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
as a rational polyhedral space (with boundary given by the sedentary strata of
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
). On
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, Theorem
5.1.3
gives
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}}}(1))=1\in A_{n-1}({\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n})
, and iterating,

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}}}(1))^{n}\cdot[{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}]=[\text{pt}]\in A_{0}({\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}),

where the intersection takes place in the Chow ring of
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
as a rational polyhedral space. Since the restriction of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1)
to each fiber is
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(1)
, the pushforward
\varphi_{*}
performs fiberwise integration, yielding

\varphi_{*}(c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(1))^{n}\cdot[{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})])=[X],

as needed. The intersection theory on
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
(including its sedentary strata) is that of rational polyhedral spaces
[
16
]
; see also
[
9
]
for the projection formula and pushforward in the tropical setting.
∎

Corollary 5.1.9

(Tropical Splitting Principle)
.

For a tropical vector bundle
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to X
of rank
n
over a rational polyhedral space
X
, there is an associated rational polyhedral space
Y
and a morphism
f:Y\to X
such that

(1)

the pullback bundle
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
is a direct sum of tropical line bundles, and

(2)

the induced pullback map
f^{*}:A(X)\to A(Y)
is injective.

Proof.

We proceed with the classical
splitting construction
adapted to our situation. At each step,
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{i})
is a rational polyhedral space (with boundary from both the base and the fibers), and Lemma
5.1.8
applies via the projection formula. We construct
f
by induction on the rank of
\mathcal{E}
. Projectivization provides

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{E})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

where the pullback
p^{*}\mathcal{E}
fits into the short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})}(-1)\longrightarrow p^{*}\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}\longrightarrow 0.

If
\mathcal{E}
has rank
2
, then the quotient bundle
\mathcal{Q}=p^{*}(\mathcal{E})/\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})}(-1)
is a line bundle. Therefore we may take
Y={\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})
as
p^{*}\mathcal{E}\cong\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})}(-1)\oplus\mathcal{Q}
and
p^{*}
is injective by Lemma
5.1.8
. Otherwise, we may iterate this construction by repeated projectivization and quotienting:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overbrace{\varphi_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{E}_{n-1})/\mathcal{O}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n-1}}(-1)}^{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overbrace{\varphi_{2}^{*}(\mathcal{E}_{1})/\mathcal{O}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}(-1)}^{\mathcal{E}_{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overbrace{\varphi_{1}^{*}(\mathcal{E}_{0})/\mathcal{O}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}(-1)}^{\mathcal{E}_{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{E}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underbrace{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})}_{Y_{n}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underbrace{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})}_{Y_{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\underbrace{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{0})}_{Y_{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{n}}

Then taking
Y=Y_{n}
, we have that the pullback decomposes into a direct sum
f^{*}\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{j}
, and that
f^{*}:A(X)\to A(Y)
is the desired injective map.

∎

This allows us to write

c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{j})t)

when computing with Chern classes. We call
c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{1}),\dots,c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{n})
the
virtual Chern roots
of
\mathcal{E}
; they live in
A(Y)
rather than
A(X)
, but the injectivity of
f^{*}
ensures that identities proved in
A(Y)
descend to
A(X)
.

6.
Porteous formula

6.1.
Sylvester determinants

The goal of Porteous’ formula is to express the class of the
k
th degeneracy locus of a morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
of tropical vector bundles over
X
,
[{D}_{k}^{\varphi}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})]\in A_{(e-k)(f-k)}(X)
, in terms of the Chern classes of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
alone. This expression involves the Sylvester determinant, which we now define.

Definition 6.1.1

(Sylvester determinant)
.

Let
\mathbf{c}=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of elements in a commutative ring
R
. For integers
e,f
, we define the
Sylvester determinant
of
\mathbf{c}
of order
f
and degree
e
to be the element

\Delta_{f}^{e}(\mathbf{c})=\det\begin{bmatrix}c_{f}&c_{f+1}&\cdots&c_{f+e-1}\\
c_{f-1}&c_{f}&\cdots&c_{f+e-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{f-e+1}&c_{f-e+2}&\cdots&c_{f}\end{bmatrix}

in the commutative ring
R
. We write
S_{f}^{e}(\mathbf{c})
for the
e\times e
-matrix above.

Let
a(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{e}a_{i}t^{i}
and
b(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{f}b_{j}t^{j}
be two polynomials of degrees
e
and
f
over a polynomial ring
R[t]
. Assume that
a(0)=a_{0}=1
and
b(0)=b_{0}=1
. We then have over the appropriate ring extension
R\subseteq S
that we may factor these polynomials into linear terms

a(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{e}(1+\alpha_{i}t)\quad\text{ and }\quad b(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{f}(1+\beta_{j}t)

for coefficients
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in S
.

Lemma 6.1.2
.

In this setting,

\Delta_{f}^{e}\left(\frac{b(t)}{a(t)}\right)=\prod_{\begin{smallmatrix}1\leq i\leq e\\
1\leq j\leq f\end{smallmatrix}}(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i})

where
\frac{b(t)}{a(t)}
is identified with its sequence of coefficients in the ring of power series.

Proof.

This is proven as Proposition 12.2 of
[
6
]
.
∎

6.2.
Degeneracy Loci

Now given a morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
of tropical vector bundles over a rational polyhedral space
X
, we have by Corollary
3.2.12
that the rank function
\text{rank}(\varphi):|X|\to\mathbb{Z}
is upper semicontinuous. By Lemma
3.2.13
, the sublevel sets inherit a rational polyhedral structure from
X
. We now define degeneracy loci and exhibit them as subcycles of
X
.

Definition 6.2.1
.

Let
X
be a rational polyhedral space and
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
a morphism of tropical vector bundles of ranks
e
and
f
over
X
. We define the
k
th tropical degeneracy locus
to be

D_{k}(\varphi)=\{p\in X\ |\ \text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})\leq k\}.

Proposition 6.2.2
.

With notation as above,
D_{k}(\varphi)
is a rational polyhedral subspace of
X
. On the interior
X^{\circ}
,
D_{k}(\varphi)\cap X^{\circ}
is a union of connected components. At sedentary strata of
X
, the rank can drop, giving
D_{k}(\varphi)
additional faces of positive codimension. The expected codimension of
D_{k}(\varphi)
is
(e-k)(f-k)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2.13
,
D_{k}(\varphi)
inherits the structure of a rational polyhedral subspace of
X
. On the interior, rank is locally constant by Corollary
3.2.12
, so
D_{k}(\varphi)\cap X^{\circ}
is a union of connected components. At sedentary strata, entries of the local matrix
A_{i}(p)
can degenerate to
-\infty
, causing the tropical rank to drop below that of nearby interior points. These sedentary faces give
D_{k}(\varphi)
faces of positive codimension. The expected codimension
(e-k)(f-k)
is the tropical analogue of the classical expectation: at a generic point of
D_{k}(\varphi)
, exactly
(e-k)(f-k)
independent conditions are imposed by requiring the rank to be at most
k
.
∎

Throughout this section, let us assume we have a morphism of tropical vector bundles
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
over a rational polyhedral space
X
, where
\text{rank}\ \mathcal{E}=e
and
\text{rank}\ \mathcal{F}=f
.

6.3.
The Hom bundle

We now establish the key link between morphisms and sections: a morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
is equivalently a bounded rational section of the Hom bundle
\textsf{Hom}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})\cong\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 6.3.1
.

Given a morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
of tropical vector bundles over
X
whose matrix entries are bounded (i.e., the finite entries of each local representative
A_{i}:U_{i}\to\text{G}(f\times e)
are bounded functions on
U_{i}
), there is a canonical bounded rational section
\sigma_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
.

Remark 14
.

The bounded-entries hypothesis holds automatically when
|X|
is compact, since continuous functions on compact sets are bounded. In particular, it is satisfied in all applications where the base space is a compact rational polyhedral space (e.g., tropical curves, tropical toric varieties).

Proof.

On a local trivialization
U_{i}
, the morphism
\varphi
is represented by
A_{i}:U_{i}\to\text{G}(f\times e)
whose entries are regular invertible functions on
U_{i}
or constantly
-\infty
. We define
\sigma_{\varphi}
locally by viewing
A_{i}(p)
as an element of
\mathbb{T}^{ef}
(listing the
ef
entries of the matrix). The transition behavior of
\varphi
(Lemma
3.2.11
),

A_{j}(p)=M_{ij}^{\mathcal{F}}(p)\odot A_{i}(p)\odot(M_{ij}^{\mathcal{E}}(p))^{-1},

is precisely the transition law for sections of
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
, since the transition maps of
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
are
(M_{ij}^{\mathcal{E}})^{-T}\otimes M_{ij}^{\mathcal{F}}
, which acts on a matrix
A\in\mathbb{T}^{ef}
as
M_{ij}^{\mathcal{F}}\odot A\odot(M_{ij}^{\mathcal{E}})^{-1}
. The entries of
A_{i}
that are not constantly
-\infty
are bounded by hypothesis, so
\sigma_{\varphi}
is a bounded rational section.
∎

Proposition 6.3.2
.

D_{0}(\varphi)=Z(\sigma_{\varphi})
, where
Z(\sigma_{\varphi})
denotes the zero locus of
\sigma_{\varphi}
(the locus where all
ef
components equal
-\infty
).

Proof.

By definition,
D_{0}(\varphi)=\{p\in X\mid\text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})=0\}
, which is the set of points where
A_{i}(p)
has all entries equal to
-\infty
. This is precisely the vanishing locus of
\sigma_{\varphi}
.
∎

Proposition 6.3.3
.

If
D_{0}(\varphi)
has the expected codimension
ef
in
X
, then

[D_{0}(\varphi)]=c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})\cdot[X].

Proof.

By definition of Chern classes (cf. the definition of
c_{k}
above),
c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})\cdot[X]=[\sigma^{(ef)}\cdot X]
for any bounded rational section
\sigma
of
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
. Since
\sigma_{\varphi}
is such a section (Proposition
6.3.1
), by the independence of Chern classes from the choice of section (Theorem
4.0.6
):

c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})\cdot[X]=[\sigma_{\varphi}^{(ef)}\cdot X].

We claim that
[\sigma_{\varphi}^{(ef)}\cdot X]=[D_{0}(\varphi)]
. For support: on the complement
|X|\setminus D_{0}(\varphi)
, at least one component
(\sigma_{\varphi})_{j}
is a regular invertible function in each local trivialization (since
p\notin D_{0}(\varphi)
means not all entries of
A_{i}(p)
are
-\infty
). But the Weil divisor of a regular invertible function vanishes, so the iterated intersection
\sigma_{\varphi}^{(ef)}\cdot X
receives no contribution from
|X|\setminus D_{0}(\varphi)
, and is therefore supported on
Z(\sigma_{\varphi})=D_{0}(\varphi)
. For the cycle structure: the expected-codimension hypothesis ensures
\dim D_{0}(\varphi)=\dim X-ef
, matching the dimension of
\sigma_{\varphi}^{(ef)}\cdot X
, so the intersection is proper and
[\sigma_{\varphi}^{(ef)}\cdot X]=[D_{0}(\varphi)]
as cycles in
A_{\dim X-ef}(X)
.
∎

6.4.
The rank-
0
case

In the base case of Porteous’ formula, we have

D_{0}(\varphi)=\{p\in X\ |\ \text{troprank}(\varphi_{p})=0\}.

The
0
th degeneracy locus consists of all points
p\in X
where
\varphi_{p}
is an
(f\times e)
-matrix with all entries equal to
-\infty
(the tropical analogue of a zero matrix). Equivalently,

D_{0}(\varphi)=\{p\in X\ |\ \varphi_{p}:\mathcal{E}_{p}\to\mathcal{F}_{p}\text{ is identically $-\infty$}\}.

Note, this
is not
the same as asking that
f_{\varphi_{p}}:\mathbb{R}^{e}\to\mathbb{R}^{f}
is identically zero! Above, we are specifically referencing the stalk map, so
\mathcal{E}_{p}=\mathbb{T}^{e}
and
\mathcal{F}_{p}=\mathbb{T}^{f}
.

Theorem 6.4.1

(Tropical Porteous Formula in Rank
0
)
.

Consider a morphism of tropical vector bundles
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
with bounded matrix entries over a rational polyhedral space
X
, where the bundles have ranks
e
and
f
, respectively. Suppose
D_{0}(\varphi)
has the expected codimension
ef
. Then

[D_{0}^{\varphi}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})]=\Delta_{f}^{e}\left(\frac{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})}{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})}\right),

where
c_{[t]}(-)
denotes the Chern polynomial of the vector bundle.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.3.3
, the expected-codimension hypothesis gives
[D_{0}(\varphi)]=c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})\cdot[X]
. It remains to show that
c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})
equals the Sylvester determinant
\Delta_{f}^{e}(c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})/c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E}))
. By the splitting principle (Theorem
5.1.9
), there exists a rational polyhedral space
Y
and a morphism
f:Y\to X
such that
f^{*}:A(X)\to A(Y)
is an injective map. Naïvely identifying
A(X)
with its image in
A(Y)
, as we have that

c(\mathcal{E})=f^{*}(c(\mathcal{E}))=f^{*}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{e}c_{i}(\mathcal{E})\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{e}c_{i}(f^{*}\mathcal{E})=c(f^{*}(\mathcal{E})),

and
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
splits into a direct sum of line bundles, say
f^{*}\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{L}_{i}
, we may conclude from Whitney’s formula that

c(\mathcal{E})=c(f^{*}(\mathcal{E}))=c\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{L}_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{e}(1+c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{i})).

So, moving forward, with even greater naïvity, we write
\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{L}_{i}
(respectively
\mathcal{F}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{f}\mathcal{M}_{j}
) with
\alpha_{i}=c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{i})
and
\beta_{j}=c_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{j})
. From this, it follows that

\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}=\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\vee}\right)\otimes\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{f}\mathcal{M}_{j}\right)=\bigoplus_{ij}(\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{j})

and since
c_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{j})=-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}
by Theorem
4.0.10
, we finally have that

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=c\left(\bigoplus_{ij}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{j}\right)

by the splitting principle

\displaystyle=\prod_{ij}(1+\underbrace{(-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})}_{\gamma_{ij}})

by Whitney’s formula.

We then have that the top Chern class
c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})
is the highest degree term in the above expression, namely the product
\prod_{ij}\gamma_{ij}
. Lastly, we have that the Chern polynomials of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
factor as

c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})=\prod_{i=1}^{e}(1+\alpha_{i}t)\quad\text{ and }\quad c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})=\prod_{j=1}^{f}(1+\beta_{j}t).

So we can then conclude by Lemma
6.1.2
that

c_{ef}(\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F})=\prod_{ij}(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i})=\Delta_{f}^{e}\left(\frac{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})}{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})}\right),

as we had desired.
∎

Example 6.4.2
.

Let us use the above formula for a simple instance of a computation. Consider a vector bundle morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}
from a rank
e
bundle to the trivial line bundle over the tropical projective line
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. By Proposition
5.1.4
, the bundle
\mathcal{E}
splits over
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}
into a direct sum
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(d_{i})
for some tuple of integers
(d_{1},\dots,d_{e})\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}
. Carrying out Porteous formula, we have that

[D_{0}^{\varphi}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})]=\left[D_{0}^{\varphi}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(d_{i}),\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}\right)\right]=\Delta_{1}^{e}\left(\frac{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})}{c_{[t]}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{e}\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(d_{i})\right)}\right).

Expanding out the Chern polynomials, we have that this may be expressed as

\Delta_{1}^{e}\left(\frac{c_{0}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})t}{\prod_{i=1}^{e}(1+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(d_{i}))t)}\right)

where the splitting into products downstairs is through Corollary
4.0.9
. We know that
c_{0}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})=1
,
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})=c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(0))=0
, and
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(d_{i}))=d_{i}
(Theorem
5.1.3
). Using Lemma
6.1.2
, we may conclude that

[D_{0}^{\varphi}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}^{1}})]=\Delta_{1}^{e}\left(\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{e}(1+d_{i}t)}\right)=(-1)^{e}d_{1}\cdots d_{e}.

7.
Future Directions

We conclude by addressing two questions: what is needed to extend the rank-
0
result to the full Porteous formula for arbitrary
D_{k}(\varphi)
, and what motivates a tropical Porteous formula? The first leads to a discussion of the tropical Grassmannian and its role in reducing higher-rank degeneracy loci to the rank-
0
case. The second returns to classical algebraic geometry and a conjecture that was the primary motivation for this work.

7.1.
Higher Rank Porteous

In the classical setting, the full Porteous formula for
[D_{k}(\varphi)]
is deduced from the rank-
0
case by a
Grassmann bundle reduction
: one replaces the original morphism
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
with a new morphism on a larger space whose rank-
0
locus maps onto
D_{k}(\varphi)
. We outline how this strategy would proceed tropically, and identify the key ingredients that remain to be constructed.

The classical strategy.
Given
\varphi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
over
X
with
\text{rank}\mathcal{E}=e
and
\text{rank}\mathcal{F}=f
, and a fixed integer
0\leq k<\min(e,f)
, the classical proof proceeds as follows:

(1)

Form the Grassmann bundle
\rho:\text{Gr}(e-k,\mathcal{E})\to X
, whose fiber over
x\in X
parametrizes
(e-k)
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{E}_{x}
. Over
\text{Gr}(e-k,\mathcal{E})
there is a
universal exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{S}\to\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Q}\to 0,

where
\mathcal{S}
is the tautological subbundle of rank
e-k
, with
\mathcal{S}_{(x,K_{x})}=K_{x}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{(x,K_{x})}=\mathcal{E}_{x}/K_{x}
.

(2)

Restrict
\rho^{*}\varphi
to
\mathcal{S}
to obtain
\psi=\rho^{*}\varphi|_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{S}\to\rho^{*}\mathcal{F}
, a morphism of ranks
e-k
and
f
.

(3)

Observe that
D_{0}(\psi)=\{(x,K_{x})\mid K_{x}\subseteq\ker\varphi_{x}\}
maps onto
D_{k}(\varphi)
under
\rho
, since
\text{rank}(\varphi_{x})\leq k
if and only if
\ker\varphi_{x}
contains an
(e-k)
-plane.

(4)

Apply the rank-
0
formula:
[D_{0}(\psi)]=c_{(e-k)f}(\mathcal{S}^{\vee}\otimes\rho^{*}\mathcal{F})\cdot[\text{Gr}(e-k,\mathcal{E})]
.

(5)

Push forward via
\rho_{*}
and compute using the Giambelli formula to obtain

[D_{k}(\varphi)]=\rho_{*}[D_{0}(\psi)]=\Delta_{f-k}^{e-k}\left(\frac{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})}{c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E})}\right).

Note that the projectivization
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})
used in the splitting principle is precisely the case
k=e-1
of this construction, i.e.
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})=\text{Gr}(1,\mathcal{E})
.

Tropical ingredients needed.
To carry out this program tropically, one would need:

(I)

A
tropical Grassmann bundle

\rho:\textsf{trop}(\text{Gr}(d,\mathcal{E}))\to X
whose fiber over
x
is
\textsf{trop}(\text{Gr}(d,\mathcal{E}_{x}))
, realized as a rational polyhedral space. The Speyer–Sturmfels bijection between
\textsf{trop}(G(d,n))
and tropical
d
-planes
[
17
]
gives candidates for the fibers, but assembling them into a bundle with the correct sedentary structure remains open.

(II)

A
universal exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{S}\to\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Q}\to 0
over
\textsf{trop}(\text{Gr}(e-k,\mathcal{E}))
, with
\mathcal{S}_{(x,K_{x})}=K_{x}
the tautological subbundle of rank
e-k
.

(III)

A
projection formula
for
\rho_{*}
, generalizing Lemma
5.1.8
from
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})=\text{Gr}(1,\mathcal{E})
to the higher Grassmann bundle.

(IV)

A
Giambelli-type computation
verifying
\rho_{*}(c_{(e-k)f}(\mathcal{S}^{\vee}\otimes\rho^{*}\mathcal{F}))=\Delta_{f-k}^{e-k}(c_{[t]}(\mathcal{F})/c_{[t]}(\mathcal{E}))
, which is formal given
(I)
–
(III)
.

Of these,
(I)
is the essential obstacle: it requires globalizing the tropical Grassmannian into a fiber bundle compatible with the Mikhalkin–Rau framework.

Question 7.1.1
.

Can one construct a tropical Grassmann bundle
\textsf{trop}(\text{Gr}(d,\mathcal{E}))\to X
as a rational polyhedral space satisfying
(I)
–
(II)
above?

An alternative: iterated projectivization.
Since the projectivization
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})=\text{Gr}(1,\mathcal{E})
is already available, one could attempt to reduce
D_{k}
to
D_{0}
through iterated projectivization rather than a single Grassmann bundle construction. Concretely: over
{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})
, the tautological sequence
0\to\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})}(-1)\to p^{*}\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Q}\to 0
and the composition
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})}(-1)\hookrightarrow p^{*}\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{p^{*}\varphi}p^{*}\mathcal{F}
relate
D_{e-1}(\varphi)
to a zero locus upstairs. Iterating this on the successive quotients
\mathcal{Q}
reduces
D_{k}
to
D_{0}
after
e-k
steps, at the cost of working over a flag bundle rather than a Grassmann bundle. This avoids ingredient
(I)
entirely — the splitting principle already constructs the relevant flag bundle — but requires tracking iterated pushforwards and verifying the resulting expression simplifies to the Sylvester determinant.

7.2.
Towards the Brill–Noether Conjecture

Let
W^{r}_{d}\subseteq\text{Pic}^{d}(\Gamma)
denote the locus of divisor classes of
degree

d
and
rank
at least
r
(see, e.g.,
[
3
,
14
]
).

Conjecture 7.2.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

Assume
\rho=g-(r+1)(g-d+r)\geq 0
. Then there exists a canonical tropical subvariety
Z^{r}_{d}\subseteq W^{r}_{d}
of pure dimension
\rho
such that

[Z^{r}_{d}]=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{r}\frac{i!}{(g-d+r+i)!}\right)[\Theta]^{g-\rho}\ .

modulo tropical homological equivalence.

This conjecture has been proven in the case
r=0
, where
W_{d}^{0}=W_{d}
is pure-dimensional (
[
8
]
) and
Z_{d}^{0}=W_{d}
. This echoes the classical statement for subvarieties
W_{d}\subset\text{Pic}^{d}(C)
parameterizing effective divisor classes of degree
d
on a curve
C
, i.e., images of the Abel–Jacobi maps
u_{d}:C_{d}\to\text{Jac}(C)\cong\text{Pic}^{d}(C)
.

Question 7.2.2
.

In the classical setting, the Brill–Noether theorem is proven by expressing
W_{d}^{r}
as a degeneracy locus of an evaluation map of vector bundles over
\text{Pic}^{d}(C)
and then applying Porteous’ formula (see
[
6
]
). Could one approach this conjecture in an analogous way?
