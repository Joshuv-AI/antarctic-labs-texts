Recent advances in stimuli-responsive nanomaterials for the treatment of acute kidney injury
Authors: Sheng Chen, Leilei Yu, Lingling Wu, Shuang Liang, Ping Yu, Xiaohan Ma, Ting Zhang, Peng Jiang, Taisheng Liang, Hongjun Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury (AKI) can be triggered by multiple insults, including ischemia–reperfusion, sepsis, and drug-induced nephrotoxicity. It is characterized by abrupt onset, rapid progression, and rapidly amplifying pathological cascades. Clinically, AKI often manifests as a reduced glomerular filtration rate, elevated serum creatinine or blood urea nitrogen, and oliguria; in severe cases, anuria may occur. Despite substantial heterogeneity in etiology, the pathological evolution of AKI converges on shared, therapeutically actionable hubs. Early microcirculatory dysfunction and an imbalance between oxygen supply and demand precipitate an energy crisis in renal tubules. Subsequent mitochondrial injury amplifies reactive oxygen species (ROS) and H2O2, creating a positive feedback loop with inflammatory and immune responses. Sustained oxidative stress and inflammation can trigger multiple cell death programs, such as apoptosis, necrosis, and ferroptosis. These processes result in epithelial barrier breakdown, tubular lumen obstruction, and rapid deterioration of renal function. If acute-phase injury is not promptly interrupted, persistent low-grade inflammation and chronic hypoxia may promote fibrotic remodeling, significantly increasing the long-term risk of AKI-to-CKD transition. In recent years, stimuli-responsive nanomaterials have been designed to exploit microenvironmental signals within AKI lesions, such as ROS/H2O2, pH, hypoxia, enzymes, and reductive molecules, as well as exogenous physical triggers such as ultrasound. These systems follow a paradigm of circulatory quiescence, lesion activation, and intracellular or organelle-targeted release/catalysis, thereby enabling spatiotemporally controlled therapy that balances effective renal exposure with minimal off-target effects. This review is guided by the key pathological hubs of AKI. It systematically summarizes structural designs and activation mechanisms of several types of responsive platforms. These include ROS/oxidative stress responsive systems such as TK, PBAP, and non-classical physicochemical state-switching or self-consuming platforms. They also encompass H2O2-activatable strategies such as gas-releasing, nanomotor, and nanozyme-based approaches with pathway-selective catalysis and visualization. Additional platforms include pH-responsive release, ultrasound-triggered carriers, and hypoxia-responsive systems. We further distill shared principles of stepwise activation in multi-stimulus synergistic systems. In these systems, tissue-level pH-mediated presentation and penetration are coupled with organelle-level ROS/H2O2-enabled therapeutic unlocking. Finally, we critically examine key translational challenges, including safety and biodegradability, dose windows, stratification by AKI subtype and disease course, endpoint evaluation frameworks, and scalability and batch-to-batch manufacturing consistency. These considerations provide a framework for the rational design and clinical translation of precision nanotherapeutics for AKI.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is among the most common and challenging forms of acute organ failure encountered in intensive care medicine, perioperative management, and cancer chemotherapy [1, 2]. AKI not only markedly increases in-hospital mortality, prolongs length of stay, and elevates the risk of multi-organ complications, but also frequently results in persistent renal dysfunction rather than constituting a merely transient event [3]. Accumulating evidence indicates that, if inadequately controlled, acute injury can promote renal fibrosis and structural remodeling through mechanisms such as persistent inflammation, capillary rarefaction, and chronic hypoxia, thereby substantially increasing the long-term risk of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD), i.e., the AKI-to-CKD transition [4–6]. However, AKI evolves rapidly, the effective therapeutic window is narrow, and the triggers are complex. Current clinical management therefore remains largely etiologic and supportive, with a lack of precise interventions capable of “cutting off” pathological cascades at critical times and sites. Consequently, developing strategies that enable early intervention, act in a site-directed manner, and reduce systemic toxicity by targeting shared pathological hubs of AKI has become a major focus for both research and translation.

Although AKI can be initiated by distinct insults, such as ischemia–reperfusion (I/R), sepsis, and drug toxicity, its histological and molecular events often progress rapidly through a relatively stereotyped pathological sequence [7]. Early heterogeneous microvascular perfusion and imbalance in oxygen supply and demand in the renal microcirculation lead to ATP depletion and disruption of ion homeostasis in tubular cells [8]. Reperfusion and inflammatory activation further induce endothelial adhesion, leukocyte infiltration, and microcirculatory impairment such as the “no-reflow” phenomenon, resulting in sustained local hypoxia and concomitant metabolic acidosis [6, 9]. Proximal tubules rely heavily on energy metabolism, so mitochondrial injury becomes a central pathological hub in AKI. Dysfunction of the respiratory chain causes electron leakage and triggers bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS oxidize membrane lipids, proteins, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), creating a positive feedback loop of mitochondrial damage, increased ROS, and additional mitochondrial injury [10]. Notably, compared with short-lived free radicals, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is more stable and diffusible, and can accumulate in tissues [11]. It acts not only as a mediator of oxidative injury but also as a reactive substrate for generating more aggressive species, thereby linking mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and downstream death pathways. In parallel, injured tubular cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating innate immunity and inducing a cytokine cascade (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) [12]. Infiltrating inflammatory cells generate additional ROS/reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which in turn aggravate mitochondrial injury and cell death, driving injury propagation from focal lesions to widespread tubular and interstitial damage [12]. As injury deepens, several cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necrosis/necroptosis, and ferroptosis, proceed simultaneously [13, 14]. These pathways lead to terminal events such as brush border loss, disruption of the epithelial barrier, tubular obstruction, and interstitial edema, ultimately resulting in rapid deterioration of renal function. These features of “cascade amplification coupled with positive feedback” imply that downstream rescue alone is often insufficient to reverse rapidly expanding injury; a more feasible approach is early interception of upstream hubs (the mitochondrial ROS/H2O2 axis, inflammatory nodes, lipid peroxidation/iron dyshomeostasis, etc.), with spatial prioritization toward injured tubules and the inflammatory microenvironment to broaden the therapeutic window between efficacy and safety [10].

Importantly, AKI lesions exhibit a set of predictable microenvironmental features that can be engineered as triggers: elevated oxidative stress (increased ROS/H2O2), local acidification and intracellular acidic compartments (endosomal/lysosomal pH gradients), hypoxia with metabolic reprogramming, inflammation-associated cellular and molecular markers (e.g., ICAM-1/VCAM-1, injury biomarkers such as kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and immune cell homing signatures), as well as disease-relevant enzymatic and reductive signals (e.g., upregulated proteases and altered glutathione (GSH) levels) [15–17]. These cues provide a natural activation window for precision therapy based on “activation upon reaching the lesion.” Unlike conventional dosing that entails continuous release and systemic exposure, carriers that remain stable with low background reactivity during circulation but undergo microenvironment-triggered remodeling, de-shielding, or disassembly at the lesion site can concentrate pharmacological effects within injured renal tissue while reducing off-target toxicity. In this context, stimuli-responsive nanomaterials have developed rapidly in recent years. Their core value lies not merely in “delivering drugs to the kidney,” but in using responsiveness to connect “delivery–activation–intervention” into a programmable mechanistic sequence, thereby reconciling a central contradiction in AKI therapy: the need for quiescence and safety in circulation versus rapid, efficient, and multi-pathway intervention within lesions. Several representative design paradigms have emerged. First, ROS/oxidative stress responsive systems use controlled release and structural switching, such as thioketal (TK) cleavage or phenylboronic acid pinacol ester (PBAP) oxidation. These reactions trigger linker breakage, de-PEGylation, charge conversion, and nanostructure disassembly, establishing a causal loop from circulatory stability to lesion activation and drug release [18]. (ii) H2O2-activatable strategies that exploit H2O2 as a trigger substrate to enable gas-releasing therapy, nanomotor-driven enhanced delivery, or pathway-selective catalysis by nanozymes, aiming to reduce oxidative burden while minimizing secondary oxidative injury; these approaches can further integrate imaging outputs to support treatment monitoring and prognostic evaluation [19]. (iii) pH-responsive release and intracellular escape, which harness protonation-driven charge/hydrophobicity switching or acid-labile coordination/framework dissociation for endosomal/lysosomal site-specific release, and can serve as an “escape engine” particularly in nucleic acid delivery. (iv) Synergistic use of endogenous cues (hypoxia, enzymes, GSH) and exogenous ultrasound triggering: the former improves lesion selectivity, whereas the latter provides a focusable and controllable spatiotemporal window, well suited to AKI scenarios characterized by rapid progression and the need for dosing at critical time points [20]. (v) Multi-stimuli systems centered on “stepwise activation,” in which tissue-level presentation/penetration is coupled with organelle-level therapeutic unlocking, enabling end-to-end optimization of delivery and multi-pathway synergistic intervention. Collectively, these strategies establish a clear causal chain of “trigger signal, material behavior, and therapeutic output”: the trigger defines when and where activation occurs, the material structure determines how release/catalysis is executed, and the therapeutic module maps onto key pathological hubs of AKI (e.g., mitochondrial protection, anti-inflammation, anti-ferroptosis, metabolic repair, and long-term anti-fibrotic effects).

Based on this background, this review summarizes recent advances in stimuli-responsive nanomaterials for AKI in a mechanism-guided manner. We first outline key events spanning microcirculatory dysfunction, mitochondrial ROS/H2O2 amplification, inflammatory/immune cascades, parallel cell-death programs, and the AKI-to-CKD transition [1, 3, 6, 10, 13]. We then categorize representative design logics according to lesion-associated triggering cues, including ROS/oxidative stress responsive systems (alongside H2O2-as-substrate activatable strategies), pH-activatable systems, hypoxia-responsive platforms, and ultrasound-triggered systems [15, 20]. Next, we synthesize how multi-responsive platforms integrate tissue-scale delivery with intracellular/organelle-level intervention through stepwise activation, thereby constructing a programmable mechanistic chain that couples lesion-activated targeting with on-demand release/catalysis (Scheme 1). Finally, we discuss major translational challenges and opportunities, including biosafety and biodegradability, indication matching, and stratification across AKI subtypes (e.g., I/R-AKI, SA-AKI, and DI-AKI). We also cover the definition of clinically meaningful dose windows and endpoint evaluation frameworks, such as functional indices, injury biomarkers, imaging readouts, and long-term fibrotic outcomes. Pathological features of AKI and engineering entry points for stimuli-responsive nanotherapies

AKI is not driven by a single dysregulated pathway. Instead, it results from multi-level cascades initiated by ischemia/reperfusion, drug toxicity, and infection-associated systemic inflammation (e.g., sepsis) [1, 2, 7]. Impaired energy metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction in tubular epithelial cells lead to excessive production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), which in turn amplifies inflammation, microcirculatory disturbance, and cell death, ultimately manifesting as rapid loss of renal function and elevated injury biomarkers [12, 21, 22]. For stimuli-responsive nanotherapy, the value of AKI lies not only in its mechanistic complexity but also in the lesion microenvironment, which provides a set of triggerable cues that can be exploited by material engineering (e.g., ROS/H2O2, pH, upregulated inflammatory receptors, metal ions, and ferroptosis-related events) [15, 23, 24]. These cues can switch nanoplatform behavior from “silent and stable during circulation” to “activated and intensified within lesions,” thereby increasing local effective exposure while reducing off-target risk.

To make the subsequent discussion easier to follow, this section organizes AKI lesion features into four practical questions. These questions are: (1) which signals act as triggers; (2) which amplification loops contribute to injury progression; (3) which pathological nodes can be therapeutically targeted; and (4) which readouts are most informative for evaluating treatment outcomes.

Triggerable signal landscape in the lesion microenvironment

Oxidative stress signals: hub roles of ROS and H2O2

Elevated oxidative stress is among the most prevalent and consistent features across AKI models. Compared with short-lived radical ROS species with limited diffusion distances, H2O2 is relatively stable and diffusible, making it more likely to form exploitable concentration gradients in injured tubular microenvironments [25]. Accordingly, H2O2 plays a dual role in stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms. On the one hand, it serves as a hub molecule within the ROS network and can act as a trigger substrate to drive material responses (e.g., gas generation, catalytic decomposition, pathway modulation, or structural switching). On the other hand, H2O2 is a key precursor for generating more aggressive oxidants and promoting lipid peroxidation; if uncontrolled, it can further amplify oxidative injury [26, 27].

At the design level, two engineering routes should therefore be distinguished. The first is a “broad ROS-driven” strategy, which emphasizes programmed changes in material physicochemical properties (state switching, structural rearrangement) or self-consuming antioxidant behavior under highly oxidative conditions to enhance drug release or enable carrier-intrinsic therapy. The second is an “H2O2-as-substrate” strategy, which leverages the stability and diffusibility of H2O2 as a specific trigger substrate to enable gas therapy, motion-enhanced delivery, nanozyme pathway-selective catalysis, and closed-loop treatment monitoring. The former focuses on programmable material behavior, while the latter highlights substrate-driven functional outputs along with a verifiable feedback loop. This distinction serves as an important boundary for categorization in later sections.

Acidic signals: tissue microacidification and endosomal/lysosomal acidification

AKI, particularly ischemia-associated forms, often involves metabolic dysregulation and lactate accumulation. This contributes to mild interstitial acidification within affected lesions [7, 21]. Meanwhile, once internalized, nanocarriers almost invariably traffic into endosomes/lysosomes and experience stepwise acidification, representing a more predictable and stable intracellular pH gradient [23, 24]. Therefore, pH signals have two layers of engineering relevance for responsive delivery: (i) lesion-level microacidification can support “activation after localization,” reducing premature release in circulation; and (ii) endosomal/lysosomal acidification provides an “inevitable intracellular switch” that can trigger disassembly-based release, framework dissociation, unmasking of active sites, and endosomal escape. Overall, pH-responsive strategies help reconcile a key delivery challenge: maintaining circulatory quiescence and stability while enabling rapid intracellular activation and payload release.

Inflammatory microenvironment and receptor upregulation: basis for lesion targeting and secondary locking

AKI lesions are characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration and elevated cytokines and are often accompanied by upregulation of multiple receptors, adhesion molecules, and injury markers that provide molecular entry points for “localization and secondary locking” of nanoplatforms [28]. For example, hyaluronan-related receptors (e.g., CD44) can be exploited under injury/inflammation to promote adhesion and receptor-mediated uptake [29]. Injured tubular epithelial cells frequently upregulate biomarkers such as KIM-1, which can serve as secondary anchoring targets [16]. In addition, proximal tubular reabsorption pathways (e.g., megalin-mediated uptake) offer structural and physiological bases for tubular-oriented delivery [30].

From an engineering perspective, the value of receptor upregulation lies in enabling a layered logic of “localize first, activate later.” Nanoplatforms can initially enrich and retain at lesions via inflammation tropism, biomimetic coatings, or receptor recognition, and are then locally activated or triggered to release by ROS/H2O2/pH cues. This dual control reduces background reactivity and off-target effects at both the spatial and temporal levels.

Metal ions and ferroptosis-related amplification: fenton chemistry and lipid peroxidation

Oxidative injury in AKI is driven not only by excessive ROS generation but also by amplification mediated by labile metal ions, particularly iron [26, 31]. Ferrous ions (Fe2+) participate in Fenton reactions that convert H2O2 into highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals, thereby accelerating lipid peroxidation and membrane damage. When lipid peroxidation exceeds a threshold, ferroptosis and other death programs can be triggered, further aggravating tubular injury. Accordingly, when systems discussed later incorporate iron chelators (e.g., deferoxamine, DFO) or explicitly target ferroptosis, including the “metal ions–Fenton amplification–lipid peroxidation–cell death” axis within the triggerable signal spectrum helps explain why intercepting iron-driven amplification confers upstream protection, and supports evidence-chain organization linking labile iron pools, lipid peroxidation readouts, death endpoints, and functional improvements [14, 31].

Pathological cascades and key amplification loops

Mitochondrial dysfunction and the mtROS amplification loop

Tubular epithelial cells have high energy demand and rely heavily on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [21, 22]. Ischemia, drug toxicity, or inflammation-mediated injury can reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, impair the respiratory chain, and collapse energy metabolism, leading to excessive mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production. In turn, mtROS oxidizes mitochondrial proteins and lipids, disrupts membrane integrity, and worsens respiratory chain dysfunction, creating a positive feedback loop of “mitochondrial injury–mtROS elevation–further injury.” This loop explains a hallmark of AKI: even transient early insults may be sustained and amplified through mitochondrial circuitry, driving progressive deterioration.

Accordingly, stimuli-responsive nanotherapies typically adopt two complementary entry points: one reduces upstream ROS/H2O2 burden to weaken the trigger intensity for ongoing mitochondrial damage; the other directly protects mitochondria or restores energy metabolism, shifting cells from an unstable state of persistent ROS generation toward a recoverable steady state, thereby improving the durability of structural and functional recovery.

Mutual amplification of oxidative stress and inflammation

Oxidative stress is both a byproduct of cellular injury and a key driver of inflammatory amplification [32]. RONS can activate multiple inflammatory pathways, promoting cytokine release and immune cell recruitment. Conversely, infiltrating immune cells and injured parenchymal cells can generate additional ROS via mechanisms such as NADPH oxidases, forming an amplification loop of “oxidative stress drives inflammation–inflammation exacerbates oxidative stress.” This loop directly shapes secondary injury and the tempo of AKI progression, and represents a stage where stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms can best leverage their advantages of local high efficiency and rapid interception. Rapid lesion-confined reduction of ROS/H2O2 or localized delivery of anti-inflammatory agents can simultaneously lower both ends of the loop, expanding the net therapeutic window while mitigating systemic side effects.

Crosstalk between calcium overload and oxidative stress

In certain AKI contexts, disrupted intracellular calcium homeostasis can mutually reinforce mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation [33]. Calcium overload may induce mitochondrial permeability changes and increase ROS production, whereas ROS can perturb ion channels and calcium-regulatory systems, further aggravating calcium overload. If later systems incorporate calcium chelation or restoration of calcium homeostasis as part of the mechanistic chain, highlighting this amplification relationship here helps rationalize why combined “ROS clearance + calcium overload relief” can yield synergistic protection and maintains consistency of evidence chains across sections.

Engineering-actionable nodes and corresponding therapeutic strategies

ROS scavenging and H2O2 decomposition: upstream interception

Because ROS/H2O2 stress occupies an upstream position in multiple pathological cascades, reducing this burden can simultaneously alleviate lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial injury, and inflammatory amplification, making it the most general engineering objective for stimuli-responsive nanotherapy in AKI [1]. Common strategies include: direct radical/ROS scavenging by the material or building blocks (reflecting “carrier-as-therapy” or self-consuming antioxidant behavior); substrate-directed consumption or catalytic decomposition of H2O2 (with emphasis on pathway selectivity to avoid generating more aggressive oxidative byproducts); and triggered release of antioxidants or anti-inflammatory drugs to indirectly downregulate ROS production. Across representative systems, a shared mechanistic logic can be identified: the trigger determines when and where activation occurs, the resulting material transformation reduces H2O2/ROS burden, and this reduction maps onto improvements in inflammation, mitochondrial function, and cell death outcomes.

Mitochondrial protection and metabolic repair: restorative intervention

ROS reduction alone does not always explain durable recovery, particularly when mitochondria have already undergone structural and functional damage [21, 22]. Restoration of energy metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis is therefore a decisive determinant of prognosis and repair. A second key actionable node is mitochondrial protection and metabolic repair, achieved by delivering mitochondria-targeted/protective agents, restoring membrane potential and respiratory chain function, strengthening mitochondrial antioxidant defenses, or replenishing key metabolic factors to restore pools such as NAD + and activate energy metabolism–related axes. These approaches naturally complement ROS/H2O2 clearance: the former reduces damage-trigger intensity, whereas the latter improves intrinsic recovery capacity, facilitating coordinated restoration of tissue structure and function and potentially reducing the risk of AKI-to-CKD transition.

Anti-inflammation and immunomodulation: limiting secondary injury and promoting resolution

Inflammation governs both injury expansion and the trajectory of repair in AKI. Stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms can achieve lesion-confined anti-inflammatory effects through targeted delivery of glucocorticoids or other anti-inflammatory agents, attenuate inflammatory drive upstream by lowering oxidative stress, or modulate immune cell behavior using biomimetic membranes and immunoregulatory modules to promote a pro-repair phenotype. In later sections, it is helpful to present “anti-inflammatory” and “antioxidant” actions as parallel intervention routes, and to clarify how they jointly act on amplification loops: decreased oxidative stress weakens inflammatory activation, and reduced inflammation limits secondary ROS generation, yielding a stabilized therapeutic feedback loop.

Inhibition of cell death: apoptosis, ferroptosis, and necrosis-related endpoints

Histopathological injury in AKI ultimately converges on tubular epithelial cell death and structural disruption, with apoptosis, ferroptosis, and necrosis (including regulated necrosis) often coexisting across models and stages [14, 27, 31]. For review organization, the key value of cell death lies in serving as a verifiable downstream endpoint of the mechanistic chain: upstream interventions (ROS/H2O2 clearance, inflammation suppression, mitochondrial protection, or interception of iron-driven amplification) should ultimately produce consistent improvements in cell-death markers, histological injury, and renal functional outcomes, thereby closing the loop of “trigger–material behavior–pathological node–outcome.” For each platform discussed later, at least one set of death-related evidence and one functional endpoint are recommended as anchors for this closed loop.

Quantifiable evaluation metrics for mechanistic closure

To enhance comparability across platforms and maintain alignment between design descriptions and evidence chains, this review adopts a three-layer evaluation framework comprising function, injury biomarkers, and mechanistic evidence. These components together provide a unified set of quantitative readouts [17].

First, renal function endpoints form the basis for assessing therapeutic efficacy. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen reflect overall filtration and metabolic clearance and can serve as shared endpoints for cross-study comparisons. Second, injury biomarkers more sensitively report the extent and recovery dynamics of tubular injury, including KIM-1, NGAL, and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase (NAG) [34]. These markers can support efficacy and also align with targeting/localization mechanisms. Third, mechanistic evidence validates the causal chain of “material activation and pathological intervention,” including oxidative stress readouts (ROS probe signals, lipid peroxidation indices, changes in antioxidant enzyme activities), inflammatory readouts (cytokine profiles, immune cell infiltration, and related signaling pathways), mitochondrial readouts (membrane potential, ultrastructure, and respiratory/energy metabolism indicators), and cell-death readouts (e.g., assays relevant to apoptosis and ferroptosis) [14, 22, 31]. When platforms are H2O2-substrate-driven or involve nanozyme catalysis, it is additionally useful to emphasize the correspondence among H2O2 consumption, product profiles, and imaging signals, extending evaluation from endpoint observation to process monitoring and thereby strengthening comparability and reproducibility across studies.

In summary, AKI lesions display a highly engineerable microenvironmental signal spectrum (ROS/H2O2, pH, inflammatory receptor upregulation, and metal ion/ferroptosis-related amplification), and disease progression is propelled by mitochondrial dysfunction and the oxidative stress–inflammation amplification loop. These features provide clear entry points for stimuli-responsive nanomaterials. By translating lesion triggers into structural switching, release behaviors, motion enhancement, or catalytic functions, and by building verifiable evidence chains based on renal function endpoints, injury biomarkers, and mechanistic readouts, responsive platforms can achieve higher lesion selectivity and reduce off-target effects within the short therapeutic window of AKI.

Building on these engineering entry points, the following sections classify and discuss responsive nanoplatforms according to trigger types and response modes. These include broad ROS-driven physicochemical or structural switching and self-consuming antioxidant systems, H2O2-substrate-driven gas generation, motion enhancement, and nanozyme pathway-selective catalysis, as well as pH-triggered disassembly-based release, coordination or framework dissociation, and nucleic acid delivery systems [24, 25]. Across sections, we consistently apply a unified mechanistic chain: trigger signal, material behavior, pathological node intervention, and quantitative readouts. This approach enables cross-platform comparison.

Although ROS/H2O2-responsive systems are covered in more detail due to the abundance of mechanistic studies and translational evidence, hypoxia- and ultrasound-responsive strategies are emerging fields with fewer reported examples. Nevertheless, they provide complementary triggers that can enhance lesion selectivity and spatiotemporal control.

To further improve accessibility for interdisciplinary readers and to avoid a purely study-by-study reading of the subsequent sections, the main practical implications of the major platform classes are briefly summarized in Box 1. Rather than repeating detailed mechanistic descriptions, this summary highlights how different trigger types address different therapeutic bottlenecks, what their main translational caveats are, and how their potential clinical value may be interpreted in the context of AKI.

Box 1. Practical take-home messages for interdisciplinary readers

1. Not all “responsive” systems solve the same problem.

Different trigger types address different bottlenecks in AKI nanotherapy. ROS/H2O2-responsive systems are mainly used to couple oxidative injury signals to drug release or catalytic detoxification; pH-responsive systems are often more useful for intracellular release and endosomal/lysosomal escape; hypoxia-responsive systems are more context-dependent and may be particularly relevant in ischemic injury or maladaptive repair; ultrasound-triggered systems provide external spatiotemporal control when endogenous signals are heterogeneous or difficult to predict.

2. Strong renal efficacy in animals is an important proof-of-concept outcome. However, it does not by itself establish clinical feasibility.

Improved serum creatinine, BUN, histology, or oxidative-stress readouts in rodent models are important proof-of-concept outcomes, but they do not by themselves establish that a platform is clinically feasible. Translational relevance also depends on whether the trigger threshold is physiologically plausible, whether biodistribution is quantitatively validated, whether the material is biodegradable, and whether the formulation remains reproducible across batches.

3. Targeting efficiency should be interpreted cautiously.

Many studies infer kidney targeting mainly from fluorescence imaging. Although useful, this approach may overestimate true nanoparticle accumulation because dyes can dissociate from carriers, signal readouts vary across studies, and AKI itself can increase vascular permeability. Accordingly, renal fluorescence enrichment should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of kidney-selective delivery unless confirmed by more quantitative methods.

4. A trigger is useful only if it matches the real disease microenvironment.

The translational value of a responsive system depends not simply on whether activation can be demonstrated in vitro, but on whether the activation window is compatible with the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of AKI lesions in vivo. This issue is particularly important for ROS/H2O2-responsive systems, many of which are validated under oxidant concentrations that may exceed likely bulk intrarenal levels.

5. Simpler platforms may sometimes be more translatable than more elaborate ones.

Multicomponent or biomimetic systems can improve lesion enrichment, immune evasion, or intracellular delivery, but they may also introduce added complexity in manufacturing, characterization, quality control, and mechanism attribution. From a translational perspective, the most promising platforms may be those that combine clear mechanistic logic with acceptable safety, scalable production, and robust reproducibility, rather than those with the greatest structural sophistication.

6. The key question is not only whether a system works, but how and under what conditions it works.

Throughout this review, platforms are interpreted using a common mechanism chain: trigger signal → material behavior → pathological-node intervention → measurable outcome. This framework is intended to help readers compare different systems more directly and to distinguish between descriptive efficacy claims and more testable, clinically interpretable design logic.

With these practical considerations in mind, the following sections discuss representative stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms in greater mechanistic detail. Across categories, emphasis is placed not only on trigger chemistry and material design, but also on how each system aligns with lesion biology, therapeutic timing, and translational feasibility.

A methodological issue that applies across the reviewed platforms concerns the evidence used to support kidney-targeted delivery. In most studies, biodistribution is inferred from fluorescence imaging of labeled nanoparticles. Although practical and widely adopted, this approach has several important limitations. First, hydrophobic dyes commonly used for nanoparticle labeling, such as ICG, Cy5, and IR780, may dissociate from carrier structures and redistribute independently, thereby overestimating actual nanoparticle accumulation in the kidney. Second, substantial heterogeneity in reporting formats across studies, such as %ID/g, kidney-to-liver signal ratios, and absolute organ fluorescence, limits the ability to perform direct quantitative comparisons between platforms. Third, because AKI itself increases vascular permeability, an enhanced renal signal under injury conditions may reflect, at least in part, passive leakage or nonspecific retention rather than responsive design per se. Future studies would therefore benefit from more rigorous quantitative standards, including ICP-MS-based %ID/g measurements for metal-containing systems and radiolabeled tracer approaches for absolute uptake quantification, to improve cross-study comparability and strengthen mechanistic claims regarding kidney-targeted delivery.

A second cross-cutting consideration concerns the gap between in vitro validation conditions and the in vivo ROS/H2O2 microenvironment. Across the studies reviewed here, responsiveness is commonly evaluated in vitro using H2O2 concentrations in the 1–10 mM range. By contrast, physiological and pathological H2O2 levels in tissues are generally reported within the nanomolar-to-micromolar range, indicating a substantial mismatch between simplified validation settings and the in vivo context. Although subcellular compartments such as mitochondria and lysosomes may exhibit locally elevated oxidative stress, and although AKI can acutely amplify ROS generation, the concentration range actually accessible to responsive nanomaterials within injured tubular cells remains difficult to define with precision and is likely to vary across AKI subtypes, disease stages, and measurement methods. This discrepancy should therefore be recognized as a general translational constraint rather than a platform-specific limitation. From a materials-design perspective, strategies to narrow this gap may include lowering activation thresholds through more sensitive chemistries, incorporating local signal-amplification mechanisms, and integrating orthogonal exogenous triggers such as ultrasound to complement endogenous oxidative cues. Future studies would also benefit from standardized reporting that explicitly relates in vitro activation windows to the best available estimates of the in vivo oxidative microenvironment [7, 35]. At present, precise cross-study quantification of ROS/H2O2 levels across AKI subtypes and disease stages remains difficult because reported values are strongly influenced by model type, sampling compartment, probe chemistry, and temporal resolution. Therefore, current evidence is more suitable for defining an approximate oxidative landscape than for establishing a single universal activation benchmark. The translational value of ROS/H2O2-responsive systems depends on whether their activation thresholds, amplification mechanisms, and delivery kinetics are compatible with the heterogeneous in vivo microenvironment of injured renal tubules. To clarify the terminology used throughout this review, the core design concepts are summarized in Box 2.

Box 2. Core design concepts used throughout this review

Trigger: a lesion-associated endogenous cue or an externally applied physical input that initiates material activation, such as ROS/H2O2, acidic pH, hypoxia, enzymatic activity, or ultrasound.

Material behavior: the physicochemical transformation induced by the trigger, including de-shielding, disassembly, charge conversion, catalytic activation, gas generation, or framework dissociation.

Pathological node: the disease-relevant process targeted by the activated platform, such as oxidative stress amplification, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory signaling, ferroptosis, tubular cell death, or fibrotic remodeling.

Mechanism-chain verification: the stepwise evaluation of whether trigger engagement, material transformation, intrarenal or subcellular localization, pathway modulation, and functional benefit occur in a causally linked manner.

Stepwise activation: a hierarchical activation process in which lesion-level localization, de-shielding, or penetration is followed by intracellular or organelle-level release, catalysis, or therapeutic unlocking. This design logic links circulatory stability with local therapeutic intensity and is particularly relevant for multi-stimulus nanoplatforms.

ROS/oxidative stress–responsive nanosystems for AKI therapy

TK–cleavable ROS-responsive systems

TK linkers are a prototypical ROS-cleavable module that remains stable during circulation but undergoes oxidative scission in ROS-enriched AKI lesions, converting systemic stability into lesion-activated disassembly [36–39]. This trigger-to-behavior coupling enables on-demand de-shielding and nanostructure breakup, thereby accelerating payload release through a sequential mechanism of delivery, lesion activation, and triggered release [18, 40]. Accordingly, this section summarizes TK cleavage–mediated ROS-responsive controlled-release systems and compares them by carrier morphology and construction strategy (e.g., synthetic polymeric/micellar prodrugs and self-assembled nanoparticle systems).

In this review, we categorize ROS/H2O2-responsive nanosystems into two operational classes based on their predominant activation logic: broad ROS-driven physicochemical switching (sects. "TK–cleavable ROS-responsive systems"–"Non-TK/Non-PBAP ROS-responsive strategies") and H2O2-as-substrate functional output (sect. "H2O2-activatable nanosystems for AKI therapy"). In the former category, elevated oxidative stress primarily acts as a bulk chemical stimulus that perturbs material stability or physicochemical state. This perturbation can induce linker cleavage, de-shielding, charge conversion, disassembly, or carrier-intrinsic ROS consumption. In the latter category, H2O2 serves as a function-defining substrate, whose consumption directly determines specific therapeutic or diagnostic outputs, such as gas generation, motion enhancement, or pathway-selective catalysis. These categories represent a mechanistic continuum rather than mutually exclusive classes. For example, several TK-cleavable systems validated in vitro at 1–10 mM H2O2 may functionally implicate H2O2 as a major reactive substrate, whereas PBAP-based boronate systems occupy an intermediate position: they are more H2O2-biased than generic ROS-switching platforms but do not always involve a fully pathway-defining catalytic output. Thus, this classification should be used as an operational framework for comparing platform behavior and design intent, rather than as a strict chemical taxonomy; PBAP/boronate systems should be described as H2O2-biased cleavage/release systems when H2O2 is the predominant oxidant, but not as pathway-selective H2O2-output systems unless substrate consumption is directly linked to a defined functional output [41, 42].

Synthetic TK-containing polymeric nanocarriers

In synthetic polymeric/micellar TK platforms, TK motifs are typically embedded within hydrophobic cores, crosslinks, or prodrug backbones to preserve nanostructural integrity in blood while enabling ROS-driven cleavage once the carrier enters injured tubules. As TK bonds break, amphiphilicity and network cohesion collapse, translating lesion ROS signals into rapid micelle/nanoparticle disassembly and on-demand drug liberation.

Peptide/small-molecule delivery

For peptides and small molecules, TK-triggered breakup is frequently used to reconcile the need for prolonged circulation with the requirement for prompt intracellular availability at the lesion. In practice, ROS-mediated TK scission unlocks encapsulated or conjugated therapeutics in situ, increasing local exposure in tubules while reducing off-target release. Peptide therapeutics (e.g., mitochondria-targeting protective peptides and anti-inflammatory peptides) have advantages such as clear targets and good biocompatibility, but commonly face delivery bottlenecks including insufficient in vivo stability, rapid clearance, limited tissue penetration, and limited intracellular delivery efficiency [43, 44]. In contrast, small-molecule antioxidant/anti-inflammatory drugs are often constrained by nonspecific distribution and systemic toxicity. Therefore, conjugating peptides/small molecules to polymeric carriers and using TK linkers to achieve ROS-triggered cleavage and release is a representative strategy for prolonging systemic exposure, minimizing off-target toxicity, and enhancing lesion-localized and intracellular bioavailability [18]. Liu et al. [45] reported a ROS-responsive, kidney-targeted “nano/macromolecular prodrug” system (SC-TK-SS31), in which L-serine–modified chitosan (SC) and the mitochondria-targeting antioxidant peptide SS31 were connected via a ROS-sensitive TK linker for AKI treatment (Fig. 1A). The positive charge of chitosan facilitated crossing kidney-related anatomical barriers and supported tubular delivery. Meanwhile, serine moieties in SC can recognize KIM-1, which was highly expressed in injured proximal tubular cells in AKI (Fig. 1B and C), and promoted endocytosis. After entering lesion cells, the sulfur atom(s) in the TK linker were oxidized by ROS, leading to cleavage of the C–S bond in TK and release of SS31 from the carrier in the high-ROS lesion environment (Fig. 1D). The released SS31 accumulated in the inner mitochondrial membrane through electrostatic/hydrophobic interactions with cardiolipin, alleviated mitochondrial swelling, and protected cristae structure. At the same time, SS31 decreased mitochondrial ROS, maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, and downregulated HO-1, thereby suppressing mtROS cascade injury at its source and further reducing inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 in renal tissue. Accordingly, after tail-vein injection, SC-TK-SS31 preferentially localized to the renal tubular region, showed strong kidney signals at 4 h, and maintained relatively high retention up to 12 h (Fig. 1E). In synergistic therapies combining a small-molecule anti-inflammatory drug and a nanozyme, TK linkers can be used to construct hybrid nanosystems that maintain circulation stability while enabling lesion-specific drug release, following the TK cleavage–triggered release mechanism described above [18, 37, 46]. Yu et al. [47] reported a ROS-responsive, kidney-targeted nano-prodrug system (Atv/PTP-TCeria NPs), composed of mPEG-TK-PLGA–coated, mitochondria-targeting triphenylphosphonium (TPP)-modified ceria nanoparticles (TCeria) and loaded atorvastatin (Atv), for treatment of LPS-induced AKI (Fig. 1G). mPEG coating improved the stability of Atv/PTP-TCeria NPs and reduced rapid phagocytic clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), thereby increasing in vivo exposure and the chance of reaching the kidney. Inflammation-associated increased vascular permeability in AKI and a particle size of 43 nm enabled passive enrichment of Atv/PTP-TCeria NPs in the kidney. After cellular internalization, the sulfur atoms in TK were first oxidized by ROS, leading to C–S bond cleavage and breakage of the mPEG-TK-PLGA chains, thereby releasing Atv in the ROS-rich lesion environment (Fig. 1H). TCeria NPs accumulated in mitochondria driven by mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial targeting was achieved due to the presence of the lipophilic cationic TPP moiety. Ceria exhibited SOD/CAT-like ROS-scavenging activity via reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ cycling (Ce3+ sites scavenged superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, whereas Ce4+ sites catalyzed the decomposition of H2O2), thereby reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress at the ROS source and protecting mitochondrial ultrastructure (Fig. 1I). In addition, released Atv, a small molecule with reported renoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in AKI models, exerted anti-inflammatory effects and acted synergistically with the antioxidant activity of TCeria, ultimately reducing TNF-α and IL-6 and resulting in increased SOD and decreased MDA. This was accompanied by further downregulation of caspase-3 (Fig. 1J), inhibition of apoptosis, and improvement of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 1K). Accordingly, after tail-vein injection, fluorescence signals of Atv/PTP-TCeria NPs were detectable at all monitored time points from 3 to 36 h and persisted to 36 h, and renal signals in the AKI group were significantly stronger than those in healthy controls (Fig. 1L). After treatment, SCr and BUN levels were significantly reduced, indicating improved renal function. H&E staining showed reduced tubular necrosis and casts with better brush-border preservation (Fig. 1M), indicating effective alleviation of overall renal dysfunction and tissue injury.

Nanozyme delivery and activation

When nanozymes are incorporated into TK platforms, ROS responsiveness is used not only to trigger drug release but also to induce functional reconfiguration. This includes exposing catalytic sites or dispersing aggregates to restore enzymatic activity within the oxidative lesion [48, 49]. This design couples lesion activation with amplified ROS scavenging, forming a closed mechanism chain in which the trigger simultaneously initiates delivery and therapeutic catalysis [50]. In addition to releasing small molecules or peptides, TK cleavage can also activate functional nano-cores (nanozymes) in a stimulus-gated manner. ROS-triggered de-PEGylation and micelle disassembly, often accompanied by charge reversal, expose catalytic sites and allow the system to maintain circulatory stability while providing catalytic activity at the lesion site. In this way, while reducing systemic exposure, ROS-scavenging efficiency is concentrated in the inflammatory microenvironment. Choi et al. [48] reported a ROS-responsive nanozyme micellar system (PTC-M) composed of ROS-sensitive PEG-TK-C18 micelles (PTC) and encapsulated 1-dodecanethiol–stabilized Mn3O4 nanoparticles (dMn3O4), for treatment of renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI)-induced AKI (Fig. 2A). PEG, as the hydrophilic head, maintained micelle stability, prolonged blood circulation, and reduced nonspecific protein adsorption; meanwhile, PEG shielded surface charge, rendering the micelles overall negatively/weakly charged under PEG shielding. After inflammation-mediated passive enrichment, when PTC-M entered ROS-rich lesion cells, responsive cleavage of the TK linker decoupled PEG-TK from the hydrophobic C18 segment, resulting in de-shielding and micelle disassembly (Fig. 2B). This in turn caused rapid destabilization and exposure of hydrophobic amines (stearamine), leading to decreased particle size and charge reversal (ζ-potential shifting from negative to positive), accompanied by morphological distortion and release of the loaded dMn3O4 nanozyme. Due to reversible redox cycling of multivalent Mn on the surface, dMn3O4 catalyzed disproportionation of H2O2 and generated O2, thereby exhibiting catalase (CAT)-like activity. Therefore, the CAT-like dMn3O4 nanozyme decomposed H2O2 into H2O and O2 (Fig. 2C), significantly reduced local renal cellular ROS, downregulated HO-1 (Fig. 2D), attenuated Bax/Bcl-2 imbalance and caspase-3–related apoptosis (Fig. 2E), and decreased phosphorylation levels of ERK/JNK/p38. Accordingly, after tail-vein injection, PTC-M reached a peak in the kidney at 6 h (Fig. 2F). Biomimetic/cell-derived TK systems

Biomimetic TK systems add an additional layer to the mechanism chain by combining ROS-cleavable unlocking with inflammation-guided homing and immune evasion [51, 52]. Here, membrane/vesicle interfaces improve lesion accumulation and retention, whereas TK cleavage inside ROS-rich microenvironments converts localization into on-demand release.

Membrane-coated nanozyme–anti-inflammatory platforms

Membrane coating confers “circulation compatibility and inflammatory tropism,” while the TK module provides the lesion switch that converts oxidative stress into carrier opening. This dual logic enables simultaneous suppression of ROS burden and inflammatory amplification precisely where microvascular and tubular injury converge. Biomimetic/cell-derived delivery systems “biologize” the surface interface of nanocarriers via membrane cloaking: on the one hand, they inherit immune-cell adhesion and homing tendencies in inflammatory microenvironments, thereby improving lesion enrichment and local retention; on the other hand, they retain key membrane proteins and receptors, which can act as cytokine “decoys” to neutralize pro-inflammatory signals, and can reduce nonspecific recognition and clearance, thereby providing immune-evasion effects and extending the effective circulation window [53]. On this basis, introduction of ROS-cleavable TK linkers can further couple “arrival at the lesion” with “lesion-triggered release/activation”: the material remains stable during circulation but cleaves and disassembles in the high-ROS inflammatory lesion, enabling spatiotemporally controlled anti-inflammatory/antioxidant intervention. Deng et al. [51] reported macrophage membrane–camouflaged ROS-responsive platinum nanozyme clusters (MM-PtNCs), composed of TK-crosslinked platinum nanoparticle clusters (PtNCs) and macrophage membrane (MM), for treatment of I/R-induced AKI (Fig. 3A). In AKI, elevated pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, etc.) can continuously recruit immune cells and amplify inflammatory cascades. In this context, macrophage membrane cloaking, on the one hand, enhanced retention and accumulation of MM-PtNCs in inflamed renal tissue; on the other hand, MM functioned as a cytokine “decoy” because the membrane retained multiple receptors for pro-inflammatory cytokines, allowing direct binding and neutralization of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Fig. 3B), thereby weakening the inflammatory cascade upstream. EVs/membrane vesicles for mitochondria-protective peptides or nucleic acids

EV- or vesicle-based TK systems are particularly suited for fragile bioactives, because their native-like interfaces protect cargo during transit and facilitate cellular entry, whereas ROS-triggered TK scission governs intracellular release [54]. The resulting cascade enables organelle-oriented intervention, with a particular focus on mitochondrial rescue, within a lesion-activated delivery framework [22, 55]. Based on the above biomimetic strategy, EVs/membrane vesicles, due to their natural origin and cargo compatibility, have also been used to deliver mitochondria-protective peptides or nucleic acids [56]. In disease processes such as AKI that are dominated by tubular injury, mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS generation are often key events: they not only directly cause collapse of energy metabolism and cell death, but also further amplify inflammation, forming a pathological cycle that is difficult to reverse [57]. Therefore, precision interventions centered on “mitochondrial protection” and “suppression of oxidative stress/inflammatory signaling” have gradually become important approaches. Compared with free peptides or nucleic acids, mitochondria-protective peptides and nucleic acid molecules such as RNAi face problems including insufficient in vivo stability, limited targeted delivery efficiency, and difficulty establishing effective doses at lesion sites [58]. In recent years, EV/membrane vesicle delivery systems, by virtue of good biocompatibility, protection of active molecules, and the ability to achieve lesion enrichment and controllable release via membrane proteins or microenvironment responsiveness, have provided a new solution for improving in vivo effectiveness of such therapeutics. In addition to delivery of mitochondria-protective peptides, this platform has also been extended to RNAi therapy [56, 58]. The concept involves using an inflammation-homing shell to achieve accumulation in injured kidneys, combined with a TK-based ROS-responsive module to trigger siRNA release. This sequence establishes a therapeutic chain comprising targeted delivery, responsive release, gene silencing, and downstream inhibition of pathological processes.

Recent studies outside the AKI field have also expanded the design repertoire of enzyme-responsive and antioxidant nanomaterials and provide useful transferable concepts for renal nanotherapy. For example, Basak et al. [60] developed glutathione peroxidase-loaded polymeric nanogels to improve enzyme stability and antioxidant activity, illustrating how fragile antioxidant enzymes can be protected within soft polymeric networks. Basak and Das reported a bioinspired phenolic hydrogel for inflammatory cardiovascular therapy through oxidative-stress reduction and macrophage-related modulation [61]. In addition, enzyme-responsive PEG nanogels have been used to reduce low-density lipoprotein oxidation through matrix metalloproteinase-responsive release of paraoxonase-1, highlighting the broader utility of enzyme-responsive cross-linked polymer networks in controlling oxidative processes [62]. Although these systems were not designed specifically for AKI, they are mechanistically relevant to oxidative-stress modulation and suggest design principles that may be adapted for AKI-responsive platforms, provided that future studies validate renal accumulation, activation thresholds, biodegradation, and safety under impaired renal-clearance conditions.

To exemplify biomimetic ROS-responsive platforms, Zhang et al. [63] reported a ROS-responsive cellular vesicle nanomedicine, RNAi@ROS-CVs, formed by fusing neutrophil-derived cellular vesicles (Neu-CVs) loaded with anti-ACMSD siRNA and ROS-responsive liposomes (ROS-Lip), for AKI treatment (Fig. 4F). Because early AKI is characterized by intense inflammation, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to injured tubular regions; therefore, the Neu-CV biomimetic shell inherited inflammation-homing features of neutrophil membranes, enabling RNAi@ROS-CVs to adhere and retain more readily in inflammatory lesions and achieve renal enrichment in AKI. After entering the high-ROS microenvironment at AKI lesions, the TK structure within the ROS-responsive module DSPE-TK-PEG2000 underwent ROS-mediated cleavage, thereby loosening the carrier structure and accelerating release of ACMSD siRNA from vesicles (Fig. 4G). The released siACMSD was taken up by tubular cells and knocked down ACMSD (Fig. 4H), thereby attenuating its adverse impact on TCA metabolism and NAD + /redox homeostasis at the metabolic source (Fig. 4I). This ultimately resulted in reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation and suppressed ferroptosis-related injury (e.g., PTGS2↓, GPX4↑, decreased MDA and labile iron, etc.) (Fig. 4J), thereby achieving tubular protection and renal function improvement in CP- or I/R-induced AKI. Accordingly, after tail-vein injection, RNAi@ROS-CVs rapidly accumulated in AKI kidneys within 2 h, peaked at approximately 8 h, and persisted up to 48 h (Fig. 4K). After treatment, RNAi@ROS-CVs showed clear efficacy in both CP-AKI and I/R-AKI models: on the one hand, ACMSD was downregulated in renal tissue; on the other hand, renal function and histological injury were significantly improved (decreased SCr; alleviated tubular injury on H&E/PAS) (Fig. 4L). Meanwhile, ferroptosis-related pathology was suppressed (PTGS2 downregulated, GPX4 upregulated, decreased MDA and labile iron), thereby reducing tubular injury markers such as KIM-1 and NGAL and overall alleviating AKI. These findings illustrate the potential of biomimetic and enzyme-responsive platforms for precise AKI therapy, complementing other ROS/H2O2-triggered nanomaterials discussed in subsequent sections.

Taken together, TK-cleavable systems are attractive primarily because of their architectural versatility [18]. The same thioketal chemistry can be incorporated into polymeric prodrugs, micelles, biomimetic vesicles, and hybrid nanoassemblies while preserving a common design logic of circulatory stability followed by oxidative lesion-triggered disassembly [64]. This modularity facilitates adaptation to different cargos and targeting interfaces and partly explains the widespread use of TK linkers in AKI-responsive nanoplatforms. Nevertheless, their limitations should also be recognized. In many studies, TK-triggered activation is validated under comparatively high H2O2 concentrations in vitro, which may overestimate the efficiency and sharpness of activation achievable in vivo. Consequently, the translational robustness of TK-based systems is likely determined not only by their intrinsic oxidative responsiveness but also by the inclusion of additional layers of biological selectivity, such as tubular injury markers, mitochondrial targeting, or inflammation-homing interfaces, which support renal accumulation and intracellular delivery [47]. Accordingly, TK-based platforms may be more appropriately regarded as versatile lesion-activated release frameworks than as intrinsically precise H2O2-selective sensors.

PBAP–cleavable ROS-responsive systems

PBAP motifs are relatively stable under physiological conditions but undergo rapid oxidative conversion, primarily by H2O2, within AKI lesions. This property makes them widely used as lesion switches for ROS-responsive remodeling [65–67]. Oxidation of PBAP drives boronate cleavage and hydrophilization, which disrupts amphiphilic balance or crosslinking, triggering disassembly and on-demand release. Among ROS-responsive motifs, phenylboronic acid pinacol ester/boronate (PBAP) shows a dual characteristic: stability under physiological conditions and susceptibility to ROS-mediated oxidation and cleavage, especially by H2O2, under oxidative stress. Consequently, PBAP motifs are commonly employed as lesion-triggered switches in polymer side groups, crosslinking sites, or hydrophobic cores [68]. Compared with nanodelivery relying solely on EPR and/or inflammation-associated permeability, incorporation of PBAP modules couples “arrival at the lesion” with “in situ structural reconstruction and drug release”: the nanostructure remains stable in circulation by maintaining hydrophilic–hydrophobic balance, whereas in ROS-rich lesion microenvironments PBAP undergoes oxidative transformation, leading to boronate cleavage and hydrophilization. This disrupts amphiphilic balance and triggers polymer degradation, crosslink dissociation, or disassembly of micelles/nanoparticles, thereby enabling spatiotemporally controlled release. Based on this logic, this subsection focuses on PBAP cleavage–mediated ROS-responsive controlled-release systems (PBAP-cleavable boronate systems) and further classifies and compares them according to carrier morphology and construction strategies (e.g., boronate-derivatized polymer systems, self-assembled micelles, or nanoparticles).

In synthetic PBAP polymers, boronate units are frequently positioned as side chains, crosslinkers, or hydrophobic-core elements to maintain self-assembly during circulation. Upon exposure to lesion ROS/H2O2, PBAP oxidation converts hydrophobic domains into more polar fragments, causing network loosening, micelle breakup, or backbone degradation and thereby enabling spatially confined drug release. Accordingly, PBAP-containing amphiphilic block copolymers (e.g., PPBAE-derived systems) can embed PBAP units as ROS-triggered switches within the hydrophobic core, establishing a mechanistic chain from stable self-assembly/long circulation to lesion disassembly and payload liberation [69]. Guo and colleagues reported ROS-responsive curcumin-loaded polymeric nanoparticles, NPSBG@Cur, self-assembled from the amphiphilic block copolymer PAGA-b-PPBAE (a hydrophilic glycosylated shell, PAGA, and a hydrophobic PPBAE segment containing phenylboronic acid pinacol ester motifs) and encapsulating curcumin (Cur) for AKI therapy (Fig. 5A) [70]. In the high oxidative-stress microenvironment of AKI renal tubules, the hydrophobic core PPBAE, as a ROS-responsive “switch,” was oxidized, causing chemical transformation of boronate-related structures and oxidative degradation of polymer side chains/backbones (Fig. 5B and 5C). Meanwhile, methylene groups adjacent to the boronate ester were oxidized into hydroxymethyl small molecules, which compromised the structural integrity of the hydrophobic core and led to gradual disassembly of NPSBG@Cur, thereby promoting Cur release. Released Cur decreased ROS and promoted lipophagy by regulating autophagy-related markers (e.g., p62 was decreased and LC3II/I was increased), facilitating lipid droplets to enter the autophagy–lysosome degradation pathway (lipophagy) (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, lipid droplet deposition decreased, mitochondrial ultrastructure and cellular injury were improved, and apoptosis-related indices declined, ultimately alleviating lipid deposition and tubular injury in AKI. After treatment, tubular necrosis and structural disorder were alleviated and cellular morphology became more regular; lipid droplet deposition was markedly reduced; mitochondrial cristae structure was restored, and lipid droplets entering double-membrane autophagy-related structures/lysosomal degradation were observed. Beyond synthetic PBAP polymers, cell-derived or membrane-mimetic PBAP carriers integrated inflammation homing/immune camouflage with ROS-triggered unlocking, thereby favoring lesion accumulation followed by microenvironment-driven release rather than premature leakage [71, 72]. Biomimetic/cell-derived carriers provide “interfacial biological advantages” that are difficult for synthetic materials to achieve simultaneously. By retaining immune-cell membrane proteins, these carriers can enable immune evasion and inflammation homing, thereby improving lesion enrichment and local retention [73, 74]. Meanwhile, introducing PBAP oxidation into the carrier framework or hydrophobic core can further couple “arrival at the lesion” with “lesion-triggered disassembly/drug release,” allowing the material to remain relatively stable during circulation while enabling on-demand release and enhanced therapeutic effects in ROS/RONS-rich inflammatory lesions [65, 66, 75, 76]. Cao and colleagues reported M2FPPF@Cur, composed of M2 macrophage membrane (M2M) and a ROS-responsive carrier FPPF@Cur (loading Cur and incorporating Fer-1–related structures), for treating cisplatin (Cis)-induced AKI (Fig. 5G) [77]. IL-4–induced M2M coating conferred a “self-membrane” interface; CD47 retained on the particle surface bound SIRP-α on phagocytes (Fig. 5H), transmitting a “don’t-eat-me” signal, thereby reducing macrophage phagocytosis and reticuloendothelial clearance and prolonging circulation. Further, adhesion molecules on M2M (e.g., integrin α4/β1, accompanied by enrichment of LFA-1/VLA-4) formed adhesion axes with ICAM-1/VCAM-1 that were upregulated on immune cells and tubular epithelium (HK-2) under inflammatory stimulation, thereby enhancing adhesion/retention and cellular uptake in inflamed endothelium and injured renal tissue. This promoted lesion enrichment and was reflected by increased uptake under inflammatory conditions and stronger renal signals with longer retention in vivo. In the ROS/RONS-rich microenvironment of AKI lesions, PBA units in FPPF underwent oxidative transformation under H2O2, leading to reduced amphiphilicity and structural stability and triggering disassembly (Fig. 5I and 5J), thereby accelerating local Cur release (and this ROS-triggered release was retained after membrane coating). At the same time, the platform itself (including the Fer-1–related anti-ferroptosis module) scavenged excessive ROS/RNS as a sacrificial RONS “quencher,” rapidly lowering oxidative-stress background. Subsequently, released Cur and Fer-1 acted synergistically: they reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation (LPO), decreased 4-HNE/MDA and alleviated Fe2+ burden; they also restored anti-ferroptosis defenses (upregulation of the GSH/GPX4 axis and Nrf2-related antioxidant pathways, and recovery of FTH1) (Fig. 5K), thereby intercepting the “iron ion–lipid peroxidation–cell death” cascade and suppressing ferroptosis amplification. At the immune/inflammatory level, Cur inhibited NF-κB activation and downstream NLRP3 inflammasome signaling (caspase-1/IL-1β/IL-18 were attenuated), and promoted macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 (CD86↓, CD206↑; TNF-α↓, IL-10↑), thereby weakening the mutually reinforcing cycle of “oxidative stress–ferroptosis–inflammation amplification” and promoting lesion repair. After intravenous injection, M2FPPF@Cur maintained a prominent signal at 8 h, and renal fluorescence remained observable at 12 h (Fig. 5L). After treatment, renal tissue injury was markedly improved, with reduced epithelial detachment/necrosis and fewer protein casts (Fig. 5M); renal function indicators BUN and SCr were significantly decreased; renal apoptosis was reduced, accompanied by decreased renal ROS generation, downregulated KIM-1 expression, and an increased Bcl-2/Bax ratio, indicating alleviation of oxidative stress and apoptosis-related injury. Meanwhile, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 were downregulated, whereas IL-10 was upregulated, further supporting mitigation of the Cis-induced inflammatory renal microenvironment.

Taken together, PBAP- or boronate-based systems offer a comparatively clearer H2O2-biased activation logic than many broadly ROS-responsive platforms [78]. In these systems, oxidation typically induces boronate conversion, hydrophilization, and subsequent destabilization or disassembly, thereby providing a relatively transparent mechanistic link between lesion-associated oxidative cues and material behavior. This feature facilitates cross-platform comparison and partly explains the appeal of PBAP chemistry in responsive nanocarrier design. Nevertheless, PBAP-based systems should not be viewed as a completely discrete class separate from other H2O2-responsive strategies. Rather, they occupy an intermediate position along the ROS–H2O2 mechanistic continuum defined in this review [64]. Moreover, despite their chemically attractive activation mode, these platforms still face an important translational challenge, namely the uncertainty regarding whether in vivo oxidative levels are sufficient to reproducibly trigger the intended response [23]. In practice, PBAP-based formulations appear more convincing when oxidative responsiveness is integrated with additional layers of lesion selectivity, such as biomimetic targeting, ferroptosis modulation, or inflammatory microenvironment regulation. Accordingly, the value of PBAP chemistry lies not only in its H2O2-biased responsiveness, but also in how effectively it is coupled to broader biological targeting and therapeutic logic.

Non-TK/Non-PBAP ROS-responsive strategies

Beyond canonical TK and PBAP cleavage, multiple ROS-responsive designs achieve lesion activation through oxidation-induced conversion and hierarchical structural reorganization, rather than single-bond scission [40, 46, 79]. These approaches typically map ROS signals to polarity/charge switching, self-consuming antioxidant attenuation, or supramolecular interaction remodeling, ultimately enhancing on-demand release or function exposure along a transparent trigger–behavior–efficacy chain [19]. Accordingly, in addition to the two classical ROS-responsive linkers, TK cleavage and PBAP oxidation–deprotection, several alternative ROS-responsive strategies have emerged in recent years for AKI therapy [80]. In these systems, the key is not a one-step rupture of a specific chemical bond, but rather oxidation-driven transformation of material building blocks and hierarchical structural remodeling within oxidative-stress microenvironments. First, ROS can oxidize carrier components into more polar/more hydrophilic products, inducing hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity switching or surface charge reversal; this weakens the cohesive forces that sustain self-assembly such as hydrophobic aggregation and π–π stacking, thereby loosening/disassembling nanostructures and accelerating release [81]. Second, some materials can directly engage in redox reactions and be self-consumed to eliminate ROS. Structural attenuation or degradation into metabolizable products occurs simultaneously, naturally linking lesion activation, therapeutic output, and in vivo clearance [49, 82, 83]. Third, ROS can perturb weak interaction networks such as host–guest interactions, electrostatic/hydrophobic interactions, or metal–ligand coordination, leading to redistribution of supramolecular loading states and interfacial interactions; thus, drug release can be enhanced or functional sites can be exposed without relying on TK/PBAP [84, 85]. Overall, Non-TK/Non-PBAP strategies provide greater flexibility in chemical design and facilitate coupling of multiple pathological nodes (e.g., ROS scavenging, inflammation suppression, and restoration of ionic homeostasis). Meanwhile, reviews should clearly articulate the causal chain linking the responsive event, material behavior, and therapeutic output. This clarity helps prevent confusion with classical bond-cleavage systems. Accordingly, this section focuses on Non-TK/Non-PBAP ROS-responsive systems for AKI and discusses them along three routes: (i) oxidation-conversion–driven hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity switching and carrier disassembly to promote release; (ii) self-consuming antioxidant materials with ROS-triggered structural attenuation (“material-as-drug”); and (iii) ROS-perturbed supramolecular/coordination interactions causing loading-state rearrangement and enhanced release.

Oxidation-driven switching and disassembly

In oxidation-driven switching systems, ROS converts carrier building blocks into more polar products, weakening hydrophobic aggregation and π–π stacking that sustain assembly. Once the cohesive forces fall below a stability threshold, nanostructures loosen or collapse, accelerating payload escape specifically in oxidative lesions. In this mechanism, ROS does not trigger “linker bond cleavage,” but instead induces oxidation and chemical conversion of carrier building blocks, thereby changing polarity/hydrophilicity, packing mode, or interfacial energy. In amphiphile self-assembled systems, oxidation of building blocks directly weakens the aggregation driving force of the hydrophobic core (e.g., hydrophobic interactions and π–π stacking), leading to loosening/disassembly or remodeling of nanoparticles and enabling “structural collapse–type” release at lesions. Importantly, if the oxidized motif itself has antioxidant activity, a multi-layer output can be achieved, integrating “carrier response, intrinsic carrier efficacy, and cargo release” [86, 87]. Endogenous oxidizable small molecules represented by bilirubin constitute a typical paradigm of this design: bilirubin undergoes oxidation conversion in ROS environments (often described as conversion to more polar products, such as biliverdin), which destabilizes amphiphilic structures and accelerates drug release [88, 89]. Wang and colleagues reported ROS-responsive HA–Br nanoparticles (BA-loaded HA–Br NPs) composed of a hyaluronic acid–bilirubin amphiphilic carrier and a loaded Ca2+-chelating prodrug, BAPTA-AM, for treating I/R-induced AKI (Fig. 6A) [90]. On the surface of BA-loaded HA–Br NPs, HA binds to HA receptors such as CD44 on injured tubular cells and triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis, enabling preferential uptake and enrichment in lesion cells under AKI oxidative-stress/inflammatory conditions. After cellular entry, high ROS at lesions oxidizes bilirubin (Br) in the hydrophobic core, converting it from a hydrophobic moiety to a more polar/more hydrophilic oxidation product (e.g., biliverdin). This weakens the self-assembly core cohesive forces driven by Br-mediated hydrophobic aggregation and π–π stacking. When the hydrophobic driving force decreases below a threshold, HA–Br nanoparticles loosen/disassemble, promoting rapid escape of BAPTA-AM trapped in the hydrophobic core, manifested as markedly accelerated release under ROS conditions (Fig. 6B). Released BAPTA-AM, as a membrane-permeable prodrug, is deprotected by intracellular esterases to form BAPTA, which rapidly chelates free Ca2+ and alleviates intracellular Ca2+ overload (Fig. 6C). Meanwhile, the Br motif continuously scavenges ROS and reduces oxidative stress. Given that Ca2+ overload promotes mitochondrial damage and ROS generation, and ROS further disrupts Ca2+ homeostasis to form a positive-feedback amplification loop, “BA chelating Ca2+ + Br scavenging ROS” achieves dual-pathway interception within the same cell, thereby cooperatively blocking ROS–Ca2+ cascade amplification and alleviating cellular injury. Thus, after intravenous administration of BA-loaded HA–Br NPs, renal fluorescence was observed at 1 h and rapidly increased, remaining at relatively high levels at 3–6 h (Fig. 6D). After treatment, typical tubular injury phenotypes (cast formation, tubular dilation/vacuolar degeneration, epithelial swelling, and nuclear pyknosis) were alleviated and the overall structure approached normal (Fig. 6E). In parallel, renal oxidative stress was corrected, with a marked decrease in MDA and recovery of SOD activity (Fig. 6F and G), consistent with the synergistic mechanism of “Br scavenging ROS + BA alleviating Ca2+ overload,” supporting overall protection of BA-loaded HA–Br NPs against I/R-induced AKI.Fig. 6HA-bilirubin (HA-Br) nanoparticles for AKI therapy. Self-consuming antioxidant platforms

Self-consuming antioxidant materials act as both carriers and therapeutics: they directly react with ROS in lesions and are progressively oxidized into degradable/metabolizable products. This intrinsic coupling naturally links lesion activation to therapeutic ROS removal and material attenuation/clearance, helping balance efficacy with biodegradability [19, 49, 91]. In self-consuming antioxidant materials, the material itself directly participates in redox reactions and is consumed to remove ROS in lesions with elevated oxidative stress. This process occurs alongside structural degradation into metabolizable products, naturally linking lesion activation, therapeutic output, and in vivo clearance.

BP nanosheets exemplify the “material-as-drug” paradigm, where oxidative lesions drive rapid ROS adsorption and redox conversion on the phosphorus lattice. ROS consumption and concomitant oxidation-induced fragmentation jointly achieve therapeutic action and in situ degradation [92–94] For AKI, oxidative stress induced by factors such as ischemia/reperfusion is a key upstream event that drives amplification of tubular injury and apoptosis [95, 96]. Therefore, if a material can rapidly enrich in the kidney and efficiently reduce ROS locally, “interception-type” protection may be achieved early in the pathological cascade. Hou and colleagues reported black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs), composed of mono-/few-layer elemental phosphorus, for ROS scavenging and treatment of AKI (Fig. 7A) [92]. After reaching AKI renal tissue, high levels of H2O2/•OH/O2•− in lesions rapidly adsorb onto BPNS surfaces and undergo oxidation reactions with the P-atom framework, gradually oxidizing P from lower valence states into PxOy/phosphate products. In this process, ROS acts as the oxidizing species and is progressively consumed through the formation of P–O bonds (Fig. 7B and C). With continuous surface oxidation, the oxide layer thickens and drives progressive fragmentation/dissolution of nanosheets, ultimately converting into metabolizable phosphate-related products, achieving a synchronized response of “ROS clearance and self-degradation” (Fig. 7D). After intravenous injection, BPNSs entered the kidney rapidly within 5 min; the strongest renal uptake signal appeared at 1 h and then gradually decreased, with a marked reduction by 12 h (Fig. 7E). After treatment, serum creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) significantly decreased, and renal function recovery was better than that of controls. In HA–rGO composites, oxidative modification of sp2 carbon domains attenuates hydrophobic interactions with loaded drugs and reshapes the local binding microenvironment [97, 98]. This ROS-driven weakening of noncovalent retention promotes lesion-biased release while leveraging HA-mediated renal injury recognition [99, 100]. AKI is renal damage caused by multiple factors such as I/R, and oxidative stress is one of the key mechanisms in its initiation and progression [39, 95, 101]. Conventional therapeutic approaches are often insufficient to effectively alleviate oxidative-stress–induced cellular injury and functional failure [40]. Therefore, developing nanomaterials that respond to high oxidative environments and act at renal injury sites has become an important research direction. In recent years, ROS-responsive nanomaterials, as an emerging therapeutic strategy, have shown substantial potential in AKI therapy. Seungjun Lee and colleagues reported P/HA/rGO, composed of reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and hyaluronic acid (HA), for treating the transition from AKI to CKD (Fig. 7G) [99]. The targeting mechanism of P/HA/rGO relies on HA binding to CD44 receptors that are highly expressed in injured renal regions, enabling selective accumulation at the lesion site and ensuring that the drug is released in the target region. Under oxidative conditions, sp2 carbon atoms in rGO are oxidized, reducing hydrophobic interactions with the drug (paricalcitol), thereby triggering paricalcitol release (Fig. 7H). Released paricalcitol inhibits activation of TGF-β and reduces phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, thereby decreasing expression of fibrosis-related proteins (ACTA2 and fibronectin), alleviating tubular injury and fibrotic progression, and ultimately promoting recovery of renal function (Fig. 7I). After intravenous injection, P/HA/rGO rapidly entered the kidney within 5 min; the strongest renal uptake signal appeared at 1 h and then gradually decreased, with a marked reduction by 12 h (Fig. 7J). After treatment, serum creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) significantly decreased, and renal function recovery was better than that of controls (Fig. 7K). Meanwhile, tubular injury and cast formation were alleviated and renal apoptosis decreased, supporting a downstream effect of “ROS clearance and oxidative-stress alleviation leading to tubular protection” (Fig. 7L).

ROS-remodeled supramolecular/coordination release

Some platforms translate elevated ROS into changes in host–guest affinity, electrostatic/hydrophobic association, or metal–ligand coordination equilibria, thereby redistributing interfacial interactions and/or destabilizing prodrug linkers to promote cargo release [64, 102]. Such ROS-remodeled supramolecular/coordination states enable release enhancement and functional unmasking without relying on the canonical TK or PBAP cleavage motifs. Importantly, this category emphasizes “interaction-programmed loading and ROS-amplified liberation” rather than a single, predefined ROS-cleavable linker as the sole control element.

For AKI therapy, where lesion-confined delivery and dose-sparing pharmacology are often required, supramolecularly programmed carriers offer a two-stage scheme: kidney-biased localization followed by ROS-enhanced drug liberation at injured sites. In this context, CB [7]-based host–guest nanocarriers can stabilize drug loading during circulation, enrich at renal injury regions through peptide guidance, and then leverage locally elevated ROS to accelerate prodrug conversion and guest release, aligning targeting-driven localization with lesion-activated therapy in a stepwise manner [103, 104]. He and colleagues reported CB [7]/CRGD-AuNPs, ultrasmall gold nanocarriers consisting of an ultrasmall near-infrared II (NIR-II) luminescent gold nanoparticle core and a surface CB [7]/CRGD ligand layer, for treating cisplatin-induced AKI (Fig. 8A) [105]. When the proportion of CRGD increased, CB [7]/CRGD-AuNPs exhibited a smaller hydrated particle size, more negative surface charge, and weaker binding to serum proteins (protein corona), thereby reducing uptake by the hepatic reticuloendothelial system (RES) and liver retention. Accordingly, these particles more readily entered the renal filtration–tubular pathway and showed pronounced renal enrichment/renal clearance (increased urinary excretion fraction and significantly increased kidney/liver ratio) (Fig. 8B). After reaching the kidney, CRGD further enhanced interactions with renal cells, increasing local adhesion and retention, thereby supporting lesion delivery. Dexamethasone (DXM) was stably loaded on the carrier surface as an Ada-DXM prodrug via Ada–CB [7] host–guest interactions. After arriving at AKI lesions, elevated local ROS triggered cleavage of the succinate linker in Ada-DXM, decoupling DXM from Ada and converting it into diffusible free DXM, which was then released from the carrier surface (~ 80% release at 24 h) (Fig. 8C). Non-classical ROS-responsive systems represent a mechanistically distinct category in which the carrier material itself participates directly in therapeutic ROS elimination, rather than relying solely on triggered bond cleavage. These platforms enable lesion-specific activation through several mechanisms, including sacrificial ROS consumption, antioxidant surface reactions, and physicochemical state transitions [23]. In each case, the behavior of the carrier is directly coupled to therapeutic function through a clearly defined mechanistic pathway.

Nevertheless, the practical simplicity of this design logic also raises translational considerations that merit attention. In AKI, where glomerular filtration rate may already be compromised, the in vivo clearance and degradation kinetics of self-consuming or surface-reactive materials are not trivial concerns [106]. A carrier that effectively attenuates acute oxidative stress but undergoes incomplete renal clearance may introduce a secondary safety burden. Accordingly, the long-term biodegradability, degradation product profiles, and renal handling of non-classical ROS-responsive platforms deserve systematic evaluation alongside their antioxidant efficacy, particularly in the context of AKI-to-CKD transition models where chronic material retention could contribute to maladaptive outcomes [107].

H2O2-activatable nanosystems for AKI therapy

H2O2 is relatively stable and diffusible within AKI lesions, making it an attractive trigger for activatable nanotherapies that remain quiescent in circulation yet become functional in oxidative microenvironments [25, 108–110]. Accordingly, H2O2-activatable systems implement a deliver–activate–verify approach by converting elevated H2O2 levels into controllable functional outputs, including gas generation, motion-enhanced transport, or pathway-selective catalysis. These outputs are often linked to imaging readouts [111–113].

Within the oxidative-stress network of AKI, H2O2 features relative stability and diffusibility and can establish lesion-associated concentration gradients. Pathophysiologically, it serves as a key intermediate linking mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory amplification, and cell death [21, 114], and it is also a precursor for generating stronger oxidants and driving lipid peroxidation [115–117]. Rather than treating ROS only as harmful species to be eliminated, H2O2-activatable systems reframe H2O2 as a lesion-specific chemical substrate: materials remain structurally stable with low background reactivity during circulation, whereas elevated intralesional H2O2 triggers the unmasking of therapeutic factors or activation of material functions, thereby concentrating efficacy in injured tubules and reducing off-target risk.

On the basis of a mechanistic chain of “H2O2 (trigger/substrate)–material functional output–pathological-node intervention–verifiable readout,” this section organizes representative systems into three transferable paradigms: (i) H2O2-triggered gas generation combined with asymmetric architectures to form nanomotors, upgrading controlled gas release into motion-enhanced delivery [118]; (ii) membrane biomimicry/inflammation homing plus secondary lesion locking by injury markers, followed by H2O2-on-demand gas/drug release to strengthen lesion enrichment while lowering background release [119]; and (iii) pathway-selective regulation of H2O2-related nanozymes (e.g., favoring catalase-like disproportionation while reducing radical-generating routes), coupled with photoacoustic/fluorescence readouts to form a closed loop of therapy, monitoring, and evaluation [48]. Overall, H2O2 responsiveness is incorporated not only as a release switch but also as an integrated framework for delivery enhancement, mechanistic intervention, and efficacy quantification in AKI.

H2O2-triggered gas generation can be upgraded from controlled release to active transport, where asymmetric architectures convert localized CO production into propulsion. This strategy increases lesion contact efficiency and local exposure while retaining low background activity during circulation [118]. In this paradigm, H2O2-triggered gas release and gradient-driven motion jointly overcome two challenges in gas therapy: off-target exposure and limited lesion-site contact. This is achieved by combining on-site gas generation with active localization.

Tong and colleagues reported Au@MSN/CORM-401@HA (AMCH) nanomotors, in which an Au@MSN nanocarrier was loaded with the CO donor CORM-401, and one side was modified with HA while the other side was attached with Au to form an “asymmetrical” structure, for treating AKI-related tubular injury (Fig. 9A) [118]. AMCH first accumulated in injured kidneys through injury-associated vascular/permeability changes, and then HA bound with high affinity to CD44 on tubular cells to promote adhesion, retention, and uptake, thereby enabling secondary “locking” to the injured tubular microenvironment. After entering AKI lesions, oxidative stress increased H2O2, which served as a trigger to activate CORM-401 within the pores; CO release was accelerated while concomitantly consuming H2O2, and higher H2O2 led to stronger release (Fig. 9B and 9C). Due to the asymmetric single-side HA/Au modification, CO was generated non-uniformly, forming a local gradient that drove self-propelled motion of the nanomotor and increased contact with tubular cells and local effective exposure (Fig. 9D). Released CO further reduced oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury (ROS decreased and membrane potential improved), inhibited apoptosis, and restored tubular functional proteins including AQP1/AQP2 and Na+/K+-ATPase, thereby alleviating tubular injury and improving renal functional endpoints. Therefore, after intravenous injection, AMCH fluorescence was observed in the injured kidney region as early as 30 min, then gradually increased over time and reached a relatively high level at ~ 1 h, remaining detectable in the kidney up to 72 h (Fig. 9E). After treatment, tissue injury–related changes were alleviated (Fig. 9F), pathological injury was mitigated, and renal apoptosis markedly decreased. Meanwhile, HO-1 was upregulated and CD44 and NOX4 were downregulated; AQP1/AQP2 and Na+/K+-ATPase were further upregulated, and injury markers such as DcR2/NAG/NGAL were reduced. Membrane biomimicry provides inflammation-guided accumulation and immune-compatible circulation [120, 121], whereas KIM-1 recognition offers secondary lesion locking at injured tubules [122, 123]. H2O2 then serves as the final biochemical key to unlock CO locally, concentrating gaseous signaling at the lesion and reducing off-target exposure [124, 125]. In glycerol-induced AKI, tubular injury is often accompanied by inflammatory-cell recruitment and increased oxidative stress [126, 127]. The inflammatory microenvironment provides a driving force for membrane-biomimetic delivery; upregulation of KIM-1 on injured tubular epithelial cells provides a secondary locking target; and elevated intralesional H2O2 participates in injury amplification while also serving as a pathological trigger for on-demand release. Accordingly, the design logic is a hierarchical coupling of inflammation-directed delivery, lesion locking, and H2O2-triggered gas release: neutrophil membrane–mediated self-recognition and inflammation tropism enables preferential accumulation in inflammatory renal regions, PS–KIM-1 recognition further anchors the carrier to injured tubular microenvironments [128], and high H2O2 triggers local CO release while simultaneously lowering oxidative burden, thereby concentrating effective exposure at lesions, reducing off-target risk, and strengthening tubular protection. Zhou and colleagues reported an H2O2-responsive nanomaterial (MnCO@hMSN@NM-PS) composed of hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (hMSN) loaded with Mn2(CO)10 and coated with neutrophil membrane with externalized phosphatidylserine (NM-PS), for treating glycerol-induced AKI (Fig. 9G) [129]. NM coating conferred immune evasion and inflammation tropism, enabling preferential accumulation in inflammatory renal regions in glycerol-induced AKI. After reaching tubular injury sites, surface PS bound to highly expressed KIM-1, enhancing adhesion, retention, and uptake, thereby “locking” the carrier to lesion tubular microenvironments. Under low H2O2 levels in normal tissues, MnCO@hMSN@NM-PS maintained low background CO release. After entering AKI lesions, elevated H2O2 diffused through hMSN mesopores into the particle interior and triggered oxidation of Mn2(CO)10, thereby disrupting the Mn–CO coordination environment and releasing CO (Fig. 9H and I), with a clear dose dependence in which higher H2O2 produced stronger CO release. CO, as a gaseous signaling molecule, further suppressed inflammatory amplification and reduced cell death, with downregulation of renal pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1 and TNF-α) and decreased tubular apoptosis (reduced TUNEL), thereby alleviating renal pathological injury (Fig. 9J). Thus, after intravenous administration, MnCO@hMSN@NM-PS accumulated in injured tubular regions and achieved lesion localization (Fig. 9K). At the end of treatment, MnCO@hMSN@NM-PS markedly alleviated renal pathological injury (reduced tubular necrosis/dilation, etc., Fig. 9L) and improved renal functional endpoints (decreased SCr/BUN, with reduced kidney injury markers such as KIM-1 and urinary NAG), supporting improvement of glycerol-induced AKI through a chained mechanism of H2O2-triggered CO release with concurrent oxidative-burden reduction, leading to anti-inflammation/anti-apoptosis and tubular protection.

For H2O2-related nanozymes, the central design question shifts from whether to react to which catalytic pathway to bias, aiming to promote catalase-like detoxification while minimizing radical-generating peroxidase-like routes [130–132]. By coupling H2O2 consumption with optical/acoustic outputs, these platforms naturally fit a deliver–activate–verify paradigm for treatment monitoring and post-therapy evaluation [133]. In AKI, H2O2 not only accumulates as a relatively stable ROS intermediate but also serves as a substrate that can be diverted toward either detoxifying (CAT-like) or radical-generating (POD-like) pathways [134, 135]. Therefore, compared with non-selective ROS removal, directing H2O2 reactions toward CAT-like disproportionation without free-radical byproducts, while avoiding POD-like •OH-generating pathways, is more favorable for reducing oxidative burden without introducing secondary oxidative injury [136–138]. Meanwhile, because AKI evolves rapidly and dynamically, coupling therapy with imaging readouts can enable a closed loop for treatment monitoring and post-treatment assessment [139]. Accordingly, this section focuses on two transferable strategies for H2O2-related nanozymes: regulating H2O2 pathway selectivity via valence/electronic-structure control and coupling with signals such as photoacoustics for visualization-based evaluation [140]. Li and colleagues reported a MoO3₋x-based nanozyme system (MF-0) composed of a MoO3₋x nanozyme with Pluronic F127 coating, for treating ROS-related AKI and enabling “post-treatment evaluation” via ROS-activatable photoacoustic imaging [141]. In blood and normal tissues, where H2O2 levels are low, MF-0 remained relatively stable with weak background reactivity; after entering AKI lesions, local oxidative stress markedly increased H2O2, and H2O2 accessed surface active sites on MoO3₋x driven by concentration gradients. Because MF-0 has a lower average Mo valence state and a more electron-rich Mo–O coordination environment, Mo centers preferentially followed a CAT-like pathway rather than a POD-like radical-generating pathway. H2O2 was adsorbed and activated at Mo active centers and then underwent two-electron disproportionation to generate H2O and O2 through reversible Mo redox cycling (electron transfer among Mo4+/Mo5+/Mo6+), enabling continuous turnover. Here, the ROS responsiveness arises from dynamic valence-state cycling under substrate availability, rather than covalent bond cleavage, translating higher intralesional H2O2 into faster decomposition and a lower oxidative burden. Accordingly, MF-0 removed H2O2 on demand at lesions, reduced oxidative burden and downstream reactive species and lipid/protein/mitochondrial damage, alleviated tubular injury and tissue structural disruption, and improved renal functional endpoints (e.g., SCr/BUN) with a survival benefit. Meanwhile, its photoacoustic readout tracked ROS status; after therapy, ROS reduction restored a PA-on signal, enabling discrimination between AKI and recovered kidneys and forming a therapy–evaluation closed loop. Thus, after intravenous administration, renal fluorescence of IR780@MF-0 was observed at 10 min, rapidly increased, and reached a peak at 30 min. After treatment, CRE/BUN markedly decreased and approached healthy levels, and the survival rate reached 100%.

Although gas-releasing systems, nanomotors, and nanozymes can all be grouped under the H2O2-responsive category, they address distinct therapeutic bottlenecks and differ substantially in translational maturity. Gas-releasing systems provide a relatively direct substrate-to-output relationship, in which H2O2-triggered gas release mediates vascular, anti-inflammatory, or cytoprotective effects, but local dose control remains challenging. Nanomotors offer an innovative strategy by converting chemical gradients into active transport, yet their scalability, standardization, and manufacturability remain unresolved. By contrast, pathway-selective nanozymes, particularly those biased toward CAT-like detoxification rather than POD-like radical generation, currently provide a stronger balance between mechanistic traceability and translational plausibility because their activity can be evaluated through substrate consumption, catalytic products, and imaging-linked readouts.

It is tempting to group gas-releasing systems, nanomotors, and nanozymes under one H2O2-responsive umbrella and leave it there. Such grouping, however, would obscure important mechanistic distinctions [142]. They do not solve the same problem in the same way, and they are not equally close to clinical translation [143].

Gas-releasing systems offer a relatively transparent substrate-to-output relationship. H2O2 triggers release, and the released gas then exerts vascular, anti-inflammatory, or cytoprotective effects. From a translational perspective, these H2O2-responsive subclasses differ substantially in feasibility. Gas-releasing systems have a clear substrate-to-output relationship but require improved control over local gas dose and release kinetics. Nanomotor systems provide proof-of-concept evidence for chemically driven active transport, yet their scalability, reproducibility, and manufacturing standardization remain unresolved [144]. Pathway-selective nanozymes currently appear more readily comparable across studies because their activity can be evaluated through H2O2 consumption, catalytic product profiles, and downstream biological outcomes. Therefore, future development should prioritize platforms that combine mechanistic traceability with reproducible activation under physiologically relevant oxidative conditions [48]. pH-activatable nanosystems for AKI therapy (pH-activatable systems)

AKI, particularly I/R–associated AKI, is characterized by amplified oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades, which drive tubular injury and rapid loss of renal function [148, 149]. In parallel, AKI lesions frequently exhibit local acidification due to metabolic dysregulation, and internalized nanocarriers inevitably traffic through acidic endosomal/lysosomal compartments [150–152]. These pH features create spatially and temporally predictable triggers that can be exploited to improve activation selectivity at lesion sites. Accordingly, pH-activatable nanosystems have been developed to use acidic cues as triggers. They remain relatively quiescent in blood and normal tissues but are activated in lesions or intracellular acidic compartments, thereby enhancing on-target efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity [152, 153].

This section summarizes representative pH-responsive strategies for AKI therapy: (i) protonation-driven charge and/or hydrophilicity–hydrophobicity switching that induces rapid disassembly and release; (ii) acid-labile coordination or framework dissociation (e.g., metal–natural product coordination) that accelerates release while strengthening antioxidative/anti-inflammatory outputs; (iii) ultrasmall carriers designed for kidney/tubule-relevant delivery with acid-triggered release; and (iv) pH-facilitated gene delivery, in which endosomal acidification promotes decomplexation and endosomal escape to mitigate intracellular trapping [154–156]. Overall, pH-activatable systems can connect “localization–activation–intracellular delivery–pharmacological output–safety” into a programmable mechanism chain, providing a translationally oriented design route for precision AKI therapy.

Protonation-driven “charge/hydrophilicity switching–disassembly–release” systems

In the lesion microenvironment of AKI and along intracellular trafficking routes, acidity is among the most common and predictable triggers. On the one hand, I/R injury perturbs energy metabolism and promotes lactate accumulation, leading to mild acidification in the tubulointerstitium and inflamed regions. On the other hand, once nanocarriers are internalized by injured tubular cells, they almost inevitably experience progressive endosomal/lysosomal acidification, creating a physiological pH gradient from the extracellular milieu to endolysosomal compartments. Therefore, compared with ROS-responsive systems that may require a threshold oxidative intensity for full activation, protonation-based activation often behaves as an “entry-coupled switch,” remaining quiescent during circulation but releasing/activating rapidly after internalization.

Mechanistically, these systems maintain nanostructural stability at physiological pH (often via charge shielding, a compact hydrophobic core, or tight complexation). Under acidic conditions, ionizable groups such as amines become protonated, inducing charge redistribution and hydrophilicity–hydrophobicity switching, and driving micelles/complexes from compact assembly toward loosening, disassembly, and payload release or functional-site exposure [157]. This process can increase intracellular effective exposure; in some designs, it also unmasks intrinsic catalytic/antioxidant activity in a switch-like manner [158, 159]. Below, two representative routes are highlighted: (i) amine-protonation systems in which disassembly enhances intrinsic antioxidative activity (material-as-therapy, switch-like); and (ii) polyelectrolyte complexes in which endosomal/lysosomal acidification loosens electrostatic interactions to release peptide/small-molecule therapeutics with downstream organelle-level repair.

Amine-protonation type: acid-triggered disassembly with enhanced antioxidant activity

In amine-protonation designs, protonatable amines are incorporated into the nanocarrier core so that the structure remains stably assembled at physiological pH and sequesters active moieties to reduce off-target effects, whereas protonation under acidic conditions induces charge/hydrophilicity switching, triggers rapid disassembly, and increases antioxidant activity [158]. Yoshitomi and colleagues reported pH-responsive nitroxide radical–containing polymeric nanoparticles (RNPpH) for I/R-induced AKI (Fig. 10A) [160]. RNPpH was constructed from an amphiphilic PEG-b-PMS–type block copolymer with TEMPO groups covalently attached to side chains in the hydrophobic segment. After injection, the PEG shell stabilizes a core–shell micellar structure and prolongs circulation, while TEMPO is confined within the hydrophobic solid core in a restricted conformation (broad single-peak ESR), which reduces redox activity in blood/normal tissues (OFF). Compared with free TEMPO, RNPpH exhibited longer circulation and showed prolonged retention in I/R-injured kidneys (> 6 h), supporting sustained lesion exposure. Upon entering the acidified microenvironment in injured kidneys (pH < 7), amine groups in the core become protonated, converting the core from a compact hydrophobic state to a more hydrophilic, positively charged state. This disrupts hydrophobically driven assembly and induces rapid disintegration of the core–shell structure, exposing/releasing TEMPO into the aqueous phase and local tissue (Fig. 10B). Consistently, TEMPO mobility increases (ESR transitions toward clearer triplet features), enhancing contact with ROS and improving scavenging efficiency (ON) (Fig. 10C). Polyelectrolyte complexation: endosomal/lysosomal acid–triggered “complex loosening–peptide release”

For pH-activatable AKI nanosystems, protonation-driven charge redistribution and disassembly can be implemented not only in amphiphiles but also in polyelectrolyte complexes, which provide a practical route to reversible encapsulation [161]. AKI lesions (especially I/R-AKI) often exhibit local acidification alongside oxidative stress and inflammation [95, 162], and internalized nanocarriers inevitably traffic through endosomal/lysosomal compartments (pH ~ 3.8–6.0) [163–165]. Introducing multiple ionizable groups and forming electrostatic complexes enables pH-dependent stabilization: the assembly remains stable at physiological pH to reduce off-target exposure, but becomes destabilized in acidic compartments due to protonation of carboxyl and/or amine groups [166, 167]. The resulting disruption of charge balance loosens the complex and enables rapid intracellular release. This process integrates circulation stability, lesion enrichment or targeted uptake, intracellular acid-triggered release, and organelle-level efficacy into a programmable therapeutic chain. Liu and colleagues reported pH-sensitive CD44-targeting nanoparticles (HA/CS/SS-31 NPs) formed by electrostatic complexation of hyaluronic acid (HA) and chitosan (CS), encapsulating the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant peptide SS-31 for AKI therapy (Fig. 11A) [158]. In AKI, CD44 is upregulated and vascular permeability increases (Fig. 11D), enabling HA-modified HA/CS/SS-31 to enrich in injured kidneys and bind CD44, followed by receptor-mediated internalization. After uptake, endosomal/lysosomal acidification (pH ≈ 3.8–6.0) protonates HA (–COO⁻ → –COOH), weakening negative charge, while increasing protonation of CS/SS-31 amines (–NH2 → –NH3+), strengthening positive charge. Together, these shifts weaken net interpolymer attraction and increase intracomplex repulsion, disrupting the original charge balance and loosening/disassembling the nanocomplex to enable rapid intracellular release of SS-31(Fig. 11E). For small-molecule antioxidants/anti-inflammatory agents with poor solubility and limited exposure, electrostatic self-assembly into reversible nanocomplexes can similarly achieve an “inactive in blood, activated intracellularly” release profile [166, 168, 169]. Huang and colleagues reported HA-coated liposomal nanocomplexes (HALP), assembled from curcumin-loaded cationic liposomes (LP) and anionic HA, for AKI therapy (Fig. 11H) [170]. HA coating improves circulation stability and reduces rapid clearance; lesion permeability changes and the overall negative surface favor renal accumulation in injured kidneys, while HA–CD44 binding promotes adhesion and receptor-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 11I). In acidic endosomal/lysosomal compartments, low pH disrupts the electrostatic balance maintaining the “HA shell–liposome” assembly, shifting the complex from a compact, stable state to a loosened/dissociated state and accelerating curcumin release (Fig. 11J). Released curcumin reduces ROS and lipid peroxidation, lowers LDH release, restores intracellular antioxidant capacity (e.g., GSH), suppresses inflammatory cytokines and macrophage infiltration, and alleviates mitochondrial depolarization and apoptosis (JC-1 recovery; reduced Annexin V/PI and TUNEL signals), ultimately improving histology and renal-function readouts (BUN/creatinine). After injection, HALP showed stronger renal enrichment in AKI mice (Fig. 11K and L). Treatment alleviated tubular necrosis/structural disruption and reduces fibrotic tendency (Fig. 11M), with decreased BUN and creatinine (Fig. 11N and O).

Acid-labile coordination/framework “dissociation–release” systems

Compared with protonation-driven disassembly, which mainly relies on changes in charge state and hydrophilic–hydrophobic balance, acid-labile coordination/framework systems convert pH signals into a more explicit structural gatekeeping event [171]. At low pH, ligand protonation and/or weakening of coordination bonds induces de-coordination, framework collapse, or core dissociation, thereby producing a threshold-like transition from a stable carrier to rapid payload release [172, 173].

For rapidly progressing diseases with narrow therapeutic windows, such as acute kidney injury (AKI), this acid-triggered “dissociation–release” behavior helps minimize premature leakage during circulation while enabling rapid establishment of effective local concentrations in acidic lesions or intracellular acidic compartments [95, 101, 174]. Such kinetics are particularly valuable for early interruption of the ROS–inflammation–apoptosis/cell-death cascade.

Acid-labile coordination systems also support integration of multiple therapeutic mechanisms within a single platform. Metal centers can contribute redox-cycling and nanozyme-like antioxidant activity, while natural-product ligands (e.g., polyphenols, carotenoids) provide radical-trapping and anti-inflammatory effects [51, 175, 176]. Upon acid-triggered dissociation, small-molecule release can occur simultaneously with exposure/activation of metal sites, yielding coordinated outputs (“release + catalytic scavenging”). In addition, MOF/MOF-like porous architectures with tunable coordination chemistry are well suited for protein/peptide delivery: they protect fragile biomacromolecules under neutral conditions yet degrade rapidly under acidic conditions, thereby improving intracellular bioavailability.

The following sections discuss (i) metal–natural product coordination nanoparticles and (ii) acid-sensitive MOF/MOF-like protein/peptide carriers, with emphasis on trigger location (extracellular lesion acidity vs. endosomal acidity), release kinetics, mechanistic synergy, and biodegradability/safety.

Metal–natural product coordination nanoparticles

Because AKI lesions are often locally acidified and nanocarriers typically traffic through acidic endo/lysosomal compartments after cellular uptake, acid-labile coordination systems can use acidity as a direct trigger for metal–ligand destabilization and dissociation. This design enables rapid lesion/intracellular release while reducing off-target exposure. In such systems, the intrinsic antioxidant properties of natural products can synergize with metal valence-cycling activity, enabling programmable intervention across the ROS–inflammation–apoptosis axis [174]. Zhang and colleagues reported pH-responsive biomimetic targeted nanoparticles (CeAst@MK) consisting of a Ce3+–astaxanthin (Ast) coordination core (CeAst), coated with macrophage cell membrane (MCM) and further modified with a kidney-targeting peptide (KTP), for AKI treatment (Fig. 12A) [119]. MCM retained CD47, which suppresses phagocytic clearance via CD47–SIRP-α interactions and prolonged circulation (Fig. 12B). Membrane-associated adhesion/homing features promoted accumulation in inflamed tissue, while KTP enhanced uptake by injured tubules via recognition of KIM-1, which is upregulated in AKI (Fig. 12C). After arrival at AKI lesions, extracellular acidity and endosomal/lysosomal acidification weakened Ce3⁺–ligand coordination involving Ast hydroxyl groups (FT-IR changes in C–OH-associated vibrations support coordination; Fig. 12D). Acid-sensitive MOF/MOF-like carriers: acid-triggered release of proteins/peptides

For protein/peptide therapeutics, short half-life, limited membrane permeability, and insufficient organ-specific targeting often prevent sustained lesion accumulation and limit efficacy [177, 178]. Acid-sensitive MOF/MOF-like carriers address these issues by loading and protecting proteins under mild conditions and releasing them upon acid-induced protonation/de-coordination [172, 179, 180]. This provides a “stable at neutral pH, release in acidic lesions/compartments” logic that balances stability with intracellular effective exposure. Zhang and colleagues reported MCM@MOF@irisin, comprising a macrophage membrane–coated MOF nanocarrier (MCM@MOF) loaded with irisin protein, for I/R-induced AKI (Fig. 13A) [181]. In neutral environments (pH ≈ 7.4), the MOF remained relatively stable and released irisin slowly (< 20% cumulative release at 28 h), reducing premature leakage during circulation. The MCM retained inflammation/chemotaxis-related proteins (e.g., CD18, CCR2, CD40, CD47) (Fig. 13B), enabled multivalent interactions with adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1) upregulated on inflamed endothelium after I/R injury, thereby improving vascular adhesion/retention and renal accumulation. In the more acidic lesion microenvironment, 2-methylimidazole ligands became protonated, which weakened Zn2⁺–imidazole (Zn–N) coordination and induced pH-driven MOF collapse/degradation. This process disrupted pore structure and triggered rapid irisin release (~ 80% within the first 2 h) (Fig. 13C). Released irisin promoted mitochondrial protection (e.g., SOD2 upregulation and recovery of SOD activity), reduced ROS, stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential, improved mitochondrial ultrastructure, and restored respiratory-chain complex I/II function. It also suppressed mitochondrial apoptosis signaling (decreased cytochrome c and cleaved caspase-3) and downregulated inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, CCL-2), resulting in reduced tubular injury (decreased KIM-1) and improved renal function (decreased SCr/BUN). In longer-term evaluations, fibrotic progression was reduced, as reflected by decreases in α-SMA, COL-1, and collagen deposition. In vivo, renal fluorescence was detectable at 2 h and increased over time; at 24 h, retention in injured kidneys was approximately 1.8-fold higher than that of free irisin (Fig. 13D). In mildly acidic lesion-associated environments (e.g., interstitial pH ≈ 6.5) [162, 182] or acidic lysosomal compartments [163, 183], protonation of polymer networks [184], weakened electrostatic crosslinking, and carrier loosening/degradation can accelerate the release of drugs or chelators [185] and synergize with nanozyme-like ROS-scavenging modules [49, 186]. This process can simultaneously interrupt the “excess ROS–lipid peroxidation–cell death/inflammatory amplification” loop in I/R injury [95, 187, 188]. Ding and colleagues reported neutrophil membrane–inspired nanorobots (N − Cu5.4O@DFO), consisting of a neutrophil membrane coating, an ultrasmall copper-based nanoparticle core (Cu5.4O), and a CS/PGA@DFO polymer loading layer for I/R-induced AKI (Fig. 13H) [189]. The neutrophil membrane proteins (e.g., LFA-1, TNF-αR, IL-1R) confer inflammatory-tissue recognition (Fig. 13I); LFA-1 supported adhesion/retention on inflamed endothelium, while TNF-αR/IL-1R can bound and neutralized inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), further enhancing inflammatory-site enrichment (Fig. 13J). After cellular uptake, N − Cu5.4O@DFO mainly trafficked to lysosomes (Fig. 13K). Under lysosome-like acidic conditions (pH ≈ 6.0), protonation weakened electrostatic interactions within the CS/PGA@DFO layer, shifting the network from compact to more porous and significantly accelerating DFO diffusion/release (at pH 7.4, ~ 13% release at 24 h; release rate and amount increased at pH 6.0) (Fig. 13L). Released DFO chelated labile iron, reducing Fe2+-driven Fenton chemistry (Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + ·OH + OH⁻) and thereby lowering ·OH generation and lipid peroxidation burden, which suppressed iron-driven ferroptotic amplification. In parallel, the Cu5.4O core continuously scavenged ROS in a nanozyme-like manner and catalyzed H2O2 decomposition with O2 generation (Fig. 13M), thus reducing oxidative load while also decreasing H2O2 availability for Fenton reactions. Together, DFO-mediated iron sequestration and Cu5.4O-mediated ROS removal constituted a dual-interception mechanism that mitigated oxidative damage and ferroptosis in I/R microenvironments. In vivo, renal fluorescence signals were stronger for N − Cu5.4O@DFO than for uncoated Cu5.4O@DFO, indicating enhanced inflammatory-kidney accumulation conferred by the neutrophil membrane (Fig. 13N). Therapeutically, renal histological injury was alleviated, renal ROS signals decreased, inflammatory markers (IL-1β, TNF-α) were reduced, and survival improved.

pH-adaptive release/degradation in kidney-clearable systems

In kidney-targeted acute injury models, therapeutic nucleic acids often face substantial delivery challenges, particularly in reaching lesion-rich regions such as proximal tubules [190, 191]. Hydrophilic macromolecules, including NAD⁺, proteins, and nucleotides, often exhibit poor in vivo stability, limited membrane permeability, and non-specific biodistribution [177, 192], which hampers rapid attainment of effective intrarenal concentrations. Consequently, a “renal-filtration-prioritized + microenvironment-adaptive release/degradation” strategy has emerged as an approach that more closely aligns with renal delivery physiology.

A central design principle is to engineer ultrasmall carriers that preferentially pass glomerular filtration and become enriched in proximal tubules [193, 194], and then exploit acidic features of injured renal tissue and acidic organelles (e.g., lysosomes) after tubular uptake to trigger pH-dependent disassembly/degradation [162, 163]. This enables accelerated release only after renal access and cellular entry, thereby balancing circulation stability with effective lesion exposure. Kong and colleagues reported ultrasmall pH-responsive nanoparticles (GA–NAD NPs) formed by self-assembly of a gallic acid (GA)–NAD+ conjugate and Fe3+–polyphenol coordination for treating I/R-induced AKI and suppressing AKI-to-CKD transition (Fig. 14A) [195]. GA–NAD NPs showed an average diameter of ~ 3.65 nm (Fig. 14B), consistent with an ultrasmall size range favorable for glomerular filtration (often considered advantageous below 6 nm). Accordingly, GA–NAD NPs became concentrated in proximal tubules and were internalized by tubular cells via endocytosis (Fig. 14C). At physiological pH (7.4), GA–NAD NPs predominantly existed as a more stable tris-complex (Fe3+:GA ≈ 1:3), forming a dense coordination network that limited premature leakage. After intracellular trafficking into acidic lysosomal compartments (pH ~ 5–6) (Fig. 14D), Fe3+–GA coordination shifted from tris- to bis-complex, which weakened Fe–O coordination and reduced crosslinking density. The network became looser and underwent partial pH-triggered disassembly/degradation, substantially accelerating NAD+ release (release at pH 5.0 was faster than at pH 7.4; partial disassembly was evident after 24 h incubation under acidic conditions; Fig. 14E and F). Released NAD+ rapidly replenished renal NAD pools and activated NAD+–Sirt1-associated deacetylation signaling, which promoted PGC-1α/TFAM-driven mitochondrial biogenesis and restored respiratory-chain/energy metabolism (e.g., increased ATP5a, COX IV, and ATP production; Fig. 14G). These effects reduced oxidative stress, stabilized mitochondrial function, and thereby alleviated tubular cell injury and death. In parallel, oxidative-stress relief and Sirt1-linked immunomodulation suppressed inflammatory amplification, decreased cytokine expression and inflammatory cell infiltration, and ultimately improved tubular injury phenotypes and kidney function indices. During AKI-to-CKD transition, GA–NAD NPs further suppressed TGF-β/Smad fibrotic signaling via the Sirt1 axis, reduced collagen deposition, and downregulated fibrosis markers such as α-SMA and fibronectin, thereby lowering long-term CKD-transition risk. In vivo imaging showed rapid renal fluorescence appearance followed by gradual enhancement after intravenous administration (Fig. 14H). pH-facilitated nucleic acid/gene delivery

Compared with small molecules or peptides, the major bottleneck for nucleic-acid therapeutics (e.g., siRNA) in AKI is often not intrinsic bioactivity but efficient cytosolic delivery to engage the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) and achieve gene knockdown [196]. Delivery is constrained by serial barriers, including degradation and rapid blood clearance, off-target biodistribution, limited renal penetration, inefficient cellular uptake, and intracellular trafficking obstacles [56]. The most critical barrier is endo/lysosomal sequestration after endocytosis, which can inactivate siRNA before RISC loading [197].

Accordingly, nucleic-acid delivery systems should be designed around the post-internalization stage: carriers should undergo acid-triggered structural transitions in endosomes, release nucleic acids from condensed complexes, and simultaneously promote endosomal escape (via proton-sponge-driven osmotic imbalance and/or membrane perturbation), thereby enabling siRNA access to the cytosolic RISC pathway [153, 156, 198]. In this framework, pH triggering has dual roles: a release switch and an escape engine. A representative architecture uses polyamine/cationic materials to condense nucleic acids into a core, while a shell layer (e.g., HA or kidney-targeting ligands/peptides) masks surface charge at neutral pH, reduces protein adsorption, and promotes directed tubular uptake [199–201]. After endosomal entry, acidification drives core disassembly/degradation and activates proton-sponge effects that facilitate membrane destabilization, executing sequential “release + escape” steps. This design links three essential stages for nucleic-acid therapy, from renal localization and uptake to intracellular release and then endosomal escape, into a programmable mechanistic chain, enabling more reliable gene silencing and downstream suppression of oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction in tubular injury-dominant settings such as drug-induced AKI (DI-AKI) [202].

In DI-AKI and related tubular epithelial injury settings, siRNA therapeutics face a coupled delivery bottleneck, including rapid systemic clearance, non-specific distribution, limited renal penetration, and endo/lysosomal trapping, making concurrent cytosolic delivery and robust target silencing difficult. This subsection focuses on a combined strategy integrating a spermidine-derived polyamine/cationic carrier with a renal-targeting shell: the former condenses siRNA and provides endosomal-escape driving force, whereas the latter enhances tubular-specific uptake while reducing non-specific hepatic accumulation, thereby amplifying the local renal delivery-release-effect cascade. Gu and colleagues developed a renal-targeted, pH-responsive siRNA nanodelivery system (HA-KTP/PSPD/siRNA) for treating vancomycin-induced DI-AKI [203]. The platform consisted of a PSPD/siRNA core coated with an HA-KTP shell. PSPD, a cationic polymer synthesized from spermidine, enabled efficient siRNA condensation, protection from degradation, and pH-responsive release; in addition, PSPD retained spermidine-derived biofunctions that synergized with gene silencing to attenuate DI-AKI. The HA-KTP shell masked the positive core charge, yielding a near-neutral, highly hydrated surface that reduced protein adsorption/opsonization and prolonged circulation. The resulting mesoscale hydrated size (approximately 313 nm) supported passive enrichment in tubular regions (as reported by the authors), and kidney targeting was further enhanced via dual-receptor-mediated uptake: KTP bound megalin, which is highly expressed on renal tubular epithelial cells, and HA engaged CD44, which is upregulated in injured tubular epithelium. This cooperative recognition increased tubular uptake while reducing non-specific liver retention. After endocytosis into endo/lysosomes, acidic pH conditions trigger disassembly of polymeric or peptide-based nanosystems, enabling rapid release of the encapsulated nucleic acids. At the same time, the carriers induce a proton-sponge effect, reducing lysosomal acidification and destabilizing the membrane. This combination facilitates efficient endo/lysosomal escape and cytosolic delivery. The released siRNA efficiently silenced Arg-2, thereby suppressing mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive RONS generation at the source and reducing oxidative/nitrosative damage. In addition, PSPD’s spermidine-like pleiotropy cooperated with gene knockdown to activate Pink1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, upregulate Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant defenses, and inhibit apoptosis via the Bcl-2/Bax axis, thus achieving multipathway intervention in DI-AKI. In vivo, the system showed kidney-targeted pharmacokinetics, characterized by rapid renal accumulation (strong bilateral renal fluorescence at approximately 4 h), a peak around 12 h, and gradual decline by 24 h, consistent with enrichment followed by clearance. Therapeutically, under the prophylactic regimen used in the study (intravenous administration before vancomycin challenge), HA-KTP/PSPD/siRNA significantly lowered SCr, BUN, KIM-1, and NGAL and improved tubular histology and apoptosis-related phenotypes, supporting robust renoprotection in DI-AKI.

A principal advantage of pH-activatable systems is that they take advantage of an intracellular trigger that is usually more predictable than freely diffusible extracellular ROS or H2O2. For nucleic acid cargos, endosomal escape, or drugs that must act inside the cell, that is a meaningful advantage [204]. In many cases, the core problem is not getting the formulation into the kidney. It is getting the payload released at the right intracellular location once it is there.

A more nuanced limitation is that extracellular acidification in AKI lesions is typically modest and spatially heterogeneous [7]. Therefore, pH responsiveness may be most convincing when used to promote endosomal/lysosomal release rather than as a stand-alone tissue-scale trigger. In this context, pH-sensitive systems are particularly valuable when intracellular release is the major delivery bottleneck, whereas their benefit may be limited if lesion localization has not first been adequately achieved.

Ultrasound-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

In addition to ROS/H2O2 and hypoxia-responsive strategies, ultrasound-responsive nanoplatforms have emerged as a powerful tool for achieving spatially controlled drug activation and enhanced tissue penetration in the treatment of AKI. These carriers include gas‑filled microbubbles, liposomes with mechanosensitive linkers, and polymeric nanoparticles designed with ultrasound‑cleavable bonds. Upon ultrasound exposure, nanocarriers undergo structural disruption or enhanced penetration, allowing localized payload release. In the context of acute kidney injury, recent work has demonstrated that ultrasound‑responsive liposomes integrating thylakoid fragments and encapsulating l‑ascorbic acid can restore energy metabolism and antioxidant capacity in injured renal tubular cells, improve renal function, and ameliorate histological damage in animal models [205].

Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is characterized by rapid progression, fulminant inflammation, and a narrow therapeutic window [206, 207]. In addition, the spatiotemporal distribution of renal lesions is highly dynamic, which often leads to a mismatch between systemic drug exposure and effective intrarenal concentrations [208]. Owing to its noninvasive nature, deep tissue penetration, and focal controllability, ultrasound (US) enables spatially localized and temporally controlled triggering in vivo [209]. This feature provides a distinct advantage for imaging-guided, stimulus-controlled drug release [210, 211].

Accordingly, US-responsive nanoplatforms are commonly conceptualized as a closed-loop delivery sequence: circulatory stability, renal enrichment, ultrasound-triggered unlocking, rapid local drug release, and pathway-specific intervention. Within this framework, nanocarriers maintain colloidal stability and hemocompatibility in circulation, thereby preserving the time window required for renal delivery. When needed, lesion-preferential cellular or tissue uptake can be incorporated to improve local retention [20]. Subsequently, focused ultrasound induces phase transition or structural perturbation, which rapidly releases payloads at the lesion site while simultaneously enhancing ultrasound imaging contrast [212]. This strategy enables targeted suppression of inflammatory and cell-death pathways with reduced off-target exposure. Guo et al. [20] developed an ultrasound-triggered nanoplatform (LIPs-S@TAK/PFP) composed of L-serine (S)-modified liposomal nanoparticles co-encapsulating the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 and a phase-change core perfluoropentane (PFP) for the treatment of SA-AKI (Fig. 15A). The liposomal formulation remained stably dispersed in blood and showed favorable hemocompatibility, providing sufficient circulation time for kidney access. Under SA-AKI conditions, injured proximal tubular epithelial cells exhibited enhanced binding and uptake of L-serine-modified particles, establishing the basis for subsequent local triggering and controlled release (Fig. 15B). After low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) was applied to the renal region, intraliposomal PFP underwent acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV; liquid-to-gas phase transition) and generated microbubbles. This process markedly increased acoustic scattering and enhanced B-mode and contrast-enhanced ultrasound signals (Fig. 15C). Concurrently, phase-transition-associated volume expansion and cavitation-related mechanical effects (e.g., shear stress and microstreaming) loosened the liposomal membrane and increased permeability, thereby accelerating carrier unlocking and enabling rapid, on-demand local release of TAK-242 at lesion sites (Fig. 15D). Mechanistically, released TAK-242 acted as a TLR4 inhibitor and blocked an upstream inflammatory switch, thereby suppressing activation of the TLR4/NF-κB axis (Fig. 15E). This reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and alleviated oxidative stress (decreased ROS and MDA, increased SOD). Upstream inhibition further attenuated the NF-κB/NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling cascade, restraining inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. Hypoxia-responsive platforms are conceptually well suited for AKI. Ischemic injury, maladaptive repair, and persistent oxygen mismatch represent core features of the disease, particularly in specific subtypes and stages [213]. Nevertheless, the evidence supporting hypoxia-triggered systems is currently more limited than that for ROS/H2O2- or pH-responsive platforms. This imbalance explains why these systems are often discussed more briefly in current reviews.

The shorter coverage should not be interpreted as a dismissal. Hypoxia-triggered systems may be particularly valuable not only in the earliest acute injury window, but also in stage-specific contexts, such as sustained post-ischemic hypoxia or the AKI-to-CKD transition [6, 214]. The potential therapeutic value remains significant. However, broader validation and comparative benchmarking against well-established ROS-responsive platforms are still needed [23]. Until such studies are performed, the field remains promising but underexplored.

The concise discussion of ultrasound-triggered systems reflects the limited literature in AKI [215]. These platforms face a dual optimization challenge, which requires integrating material design with trigger-device parameters. Unlike endogenous cue-responsive systems, their performance depends on carrier architecture, acoustic settings, exposure protocols, and reproducibility of trigger delivery in vivo [216]. Consequently, the current evidence base is narrower. Nevertheless, the spatiotemporal controllability of ultrasound-responsive platforms may offer substantial advantages in clinically heterogeneous AKI settings, particularly when endogenous trigger intensity is variable or difficult to quantify.

Hypoxia-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

In addition to ROS/H2O2- and ultrasound-responsive systems, hypoxia-responsive platforms exploit low-oxygen microenvironments in AKI lesions, providing complementary mechanisms and enabling controlled payload release under hypoxic conditions. However, the current literature on hypoxia-responsive systems for AKI remains sparse, and these platforms face challenges related to precise lesion targeting and reproducibility. Relative to other platform categories, hypoxia-responsive systems are represented by fewer published examples but provide mechanistically complementary triggers, particularly relevant in ischemic injury contexts, where local oxygen depletion and reductive stress occur.

These platforms often incorporate hypoxia-sensitive moieties, such as nitroaromatic derivatives or azobenzene gatekeepers, which remain stable under normal oxygen levels but undergo enzymatic or reductive cleavage in low-oxygen environments, releasing the therapeutic payload. For example, nitroreductase-responsive gated mesoporous silica nanocarriers have been developed for targeted drug delivery in hypoxic tissues [217]. Similarly, azobenzene-capped mesoporous silica nanoparticles have shown hypoxia-triggered doxorubicin release [218].

This hypoxia-trigger design provides a mechanistic blueprint adaptable to pathological contexts such as acute kidney injury, where sustained low oxygen and reductive stress exacerbate tubular damage and maladaptive repair. While hypoxia-responsive systems hold significant potential, further validation and comparative studies are needed to establish their efficacy relative to ROS/H2O2- or pH-responsive platforms.

A hallmark of acute kidney injury (AKI), particularly ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI)-associated AKI, is the persistence and regional heterogeneity of the hypoxic lesion microenvironment [219]. Ischemia causes an abrupt decline in oxygen supply, and although reperfusion restores blood flow, microcirculatory dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment can keep proximal tubular regions in a relatively hypoxic state [220]. This sustained hypoxia drives metabolic imbalance, lipotoxicity accumulation, and inflammatory amplification, thereby exacerbating tubular cell injury and impairing renal functional recovery [221].

For nucleic-acid therapeutics, systemic administration is commonly limited by inadequate in vivo stability, inefficient renal delivery, and suboptimal intracellular release, which makes it difficult to achieve precise gene silencing in the lesion-relevant tubular cells [197]. Hypoxia-responsive nanoplatforms address these limitations by coupling proximal tubule–biased delivery with hypoxia-triggered structural transformation and payload release. These systems remain relatively stable in circulation and normal tissues, yet undergo disassembly under hypoxic conditions within IRI-AKI lesions to enable on-demand intracellular siRNA release, thereby concentrating gene-silencing effects in injured tubular cells while reducing off-target risk [222]. Hu et al. [222]reported a hypoxia-responsive siRNA delivery system (CS-Hex-IEN/siCD36) composed of a chitosan-grafted hexyl-IEN polymer (CS-Hex-IEN) complexed with siCD36 for the treatment of IRI-induced AKI (Fig. 16A). By tuning the IEN alkyl-chain length, nanoparticles in the 43–105 nm range were obtained, and the smallest formulation (~ 43 nm) exhibited the strongest renal accumulation and high colocalization with the proximal tubule marker LTL. Delivery was primarily driven by size-mediated proximal tubular tropism (particles > 20 nm tend to enter PTECs via the peritubular capillary–basolateral route), and cellular uptake proceeded mainly through clathrin/caveolae-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 16B). After internalization, nanoparticles showed transient colocalization with endo/lysosomal compartments followed by endosomal escape (Fig. 16C). Under hypoxic conditions, nitroreductase (NADPH-dependent) reduces the nitro group on the surface IEN moiety (–NO2 → –NH2), converting /IEN into a more hydrophilic AIE species. This redox conversion increases molecular polarity and induces a hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic inversion, rapidly weakening the hydrophobic interactions that stabilize the nanoparticles (Fig. 16D–F). As a result, CS-Hex-IEN/siCD36 disassembles and releases siCD36 on demand within cells. Once released into the cytosol, siCD36 is loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) and guides sequence-specific degradation of CD36 mRNA, thereby downregulating CD36 expression. This suppresses fatty-acid uptake and lipid-droplet accumulation, improves mitochondrial respiration and ATP production (OCR increase), reduces ROS generation, and dampens the NF-κB inflammatory axis, leading to reduced IL-1β/IL-6/TNF-α and decreased kidney injury markers (KIM-1/NGAL; Fig. 16G). In vivo, after tail-vein injection, renal fluorescence from CS-Hex-IEN/siCD36 (~ 43 nm) was detectable at 2 h, peaked at 4 h, and gradually declined by 12 h (Fig. 16H). Therapeutically, CS-Hex-IEN/siCD36 significantly reduced Scr and BUN (Fig. 16I and J), alleviated brush-border loss and vacuolar degeneration with lower injury scores, and markedly decreased KIM-1 staining. Ultrasound-triggered platforms occupy a rather different niche because they do not depend entirely on the strength of endogenous signals [216]. This feature represents a major advantage, allowing activation to be imposed externally at selected times and locations. In AKI, where the therapeutic window is narrow and the pathology evolves rapidly, such spatiotemporal control is particularly valuable [215]. However, this introduces additional complexity. Platform performance depends not only on material properties but also on device settings, trigger protocols, acoustic safety margins, and workflow integration. Therefore, the research question shifts from merely whether the nanoplatform functions to whether the platform and triggering hardware operate reproducibly under realistic conditions [223]. This added complexity partly explains why ultrasound-responsive systems are less prevalent in the literature. Rather than being a limitation, addressing both material optimization and trigger control simultaneously provides rich avenues for further exploration.

Combined stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

The pathological evolution of AKI, particularly ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI)-associated AKI, is characterized by pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity [224]. Local bursts of oxidative stress, inflammatory amplification, and organelle dysfunction often occur in a staged manner across distinct renal compartments and intracellular organelles [10]. Accordingly, single-stimulus systems frequently struggle to satisfy the entire delivery cascade, including circulatory stability, lesion enrichment, intracellular release, and multi-pathway intervention [225]. In contrast, combined stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms integrate two or more disease-relevant triggers (e.g., ROS, pH, enzymes, and reducing signals) in a stepwise logic, enabling a closed-loop paradigm of “stealth in circulation, unlocking at the lesion, and precise activation inside cells.” This design can increase effective renal exposure while minimizing off-target toxicity [101].

This section summarizes design strategies and mechanistic principles of representative dual-/multi-responsive systems for AKI, emphasizing transferable rules linking structure, trigger, delivery behavior, and therapeutic endpoints.

ROS and pH dual-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

Within the AKI microenvironment, excess ROS and local/organellar acidification are two highly representative and exploitable stimuli [188]. ROS directly drives lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial injury, and cell death, whereas acidic cues act as a practical “gate” for lesion entry and intracellular delivery [163]. Therefore, ROS and pH dual-responsive systems commonly follow a hierarchical logic: pH-guided delivery (e.g., size shrinkage, charge reversal, or deshielding to enhance penetration and endocytosis) followed by ROS-triggered activation (e.g., carrier degradation and on-demand drug release) [226].

Combining multiple stimuli offers the advantage of tailoring treatment to complex pathological environments, enhancing therapeutic precision and reducing off-target effects. Here, we outline three representative design routes: (i) lesion pH–triggered size/charge transformation (“presentation” strategy) coupled with ROS-triggered degradation/release; (ii) inflammation-related targeting (HA–CD44) combined with ROS-triggered micellar disassembly and pH-facilitated co-drug release [99, 227, 228]; and (iii) lysosomal pH–triggered gas therapy to promote active delivery, further paired with mitochondrial high-ROS–triggered cleavage of ROS-labile linkers to release mitochondria-targeted antioxidants [45].

Lesion pH–guided size/charge remodeling with ROS-activated degradation for on-demand drug release

In cisplatin-induced AKI, the renal tissue typically exhibits early mild acidification and elevated ROS levels, accompanied by altered vascular permeability at the injury site and changes in cellular uptake behavior [229, 230]. These features create an exploitable therapeutic window for “stepwise activation” delivery. Therefore, an ideal dual-responsive system should remain stealthy in systemic circulation with minimal nonspecific interactions; after reaching the lesion, pH-triggered size shrinkage and charge reversal should enhance presentation, thereby improving tissue penetration, tubular epithelial endocytosis, and local retention. Subsequently, high ROS should induce carrier degradation and on-demand drug release, enabling synchronized coupling of therapeutic output with pathological signals. Lan and colleagues reported a ROS/pH dual-responsive curcumin nanoplatform (NPS@Cur), which was self-assembled from two block copolymers, PEG-b-PAGA and (PDMAEMA-r-PAAPBA)-b-PPBAE, and loaded with curcumin for the treatment of cisplatin-induced AKI (Fig. 17A) [231]. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), NPS@Cur comprises an outer PEG/PDMAEMA layer, a PAGA/PAAPBA boronate-ester crosslinked layer, and a hydrophobic PPBAE core. This architecture maintains a relatively large particle size and a negative surface charge, supporting circulation stability while reducing nonspecific interactions. After reaching the AKI kidney, its accumulation may be associated with early structural disruption and increased permeability of the glomerular filtration barrier, which facilitates entry into injured renal tissue. Upon exposure to the slightly acidic microenvironment at the lesion (pH ≈ 6.0), the dynamic covalent boronate ester bonds formed between PAGA (diols) and PAAPBA (phenylboronic acid) dissociate, triggering de-shielding/shedding of the PEG-b-PAGA shell and leading to particle shrinkage (140.9 → 99.36 nm; Fig. 17B). Meanwhile, protonation of PDMAEMA (DMAEMA segments) induces charge reversal (− 4.9 → + 12.6 mV; Fig. 17C), thereby enhancing tissue penetration as well as tubular epithelial uptake and local retention. Subsequently, in a ROS-rich microenvironment, the PPBAE core undergoes ROS-responsive degradation, enabling on-demand curcumin release (Fig. 17D). The released curcumin acts primarily through a “ROS scavenging–mitochondrial protection” mechanism. It attenuates oxidative stress (DHE↓, MDA↓, SOD↑; Fig. 17E) and improves mitochondrial function (restored membrane potential and improved ultrastructure; Fig. 17F), thereby suppressing mitochondria-dependent apoptosis (Bax↓, BCL-2↑, cleaved caspase-3↓; Fig. 17G and H). In parallel, it promotes reparative autophagy (LC3II/I↑, p62↓) and alleviates ER stress (BiP/ATF4/CHOP↓), ultimately improving tubular pathology and renal function (improved PAS staining; decreased SCr and BUN) and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). After intravenous administration, ex vivo kidney fluorescence was detectable as early as 2 h and remained robust at 12 h (Fig. 17I). Renal tissue sections further indicated predominant localization in tubular regions rather than glomeruli. Inflammation-targeted delivery coupled with ROS-triggered disassembly and pH-accelerated co-drug release

Unlike the cisplatin model, which is dominated by tubular epithelial injury, LPS-induced AKI is driven more prominently by innate immune activation and systemic inflammation [206]. In this setting, recruitment and activation of macrophages amplified renal inflammation through the release of mediators such as TNF-α and NO [232], and were accompanied by elevated intracellular ROS as well as the emergence of an acidic microenvironment [233]. Therefore, integrating inflammatory-cell targeting with a ROS/pH cascade-responsive release program may enable more precise intervention within the immunoinflammatory milieu: HA–CD44 interactions facilitate preferential localization [234], ROS promotes carrier disassembly for rapid curcumin release, and acidic pH further accelerates the release of the co-therapeutic rhein to reinforce anti-inflammatory efficacy [235]. Liu et al. [236] developed a hyaluronic acid (HA)-based ROS/pH dual-responsive nanomicelle system, RHP@Cur, composed of an amphiphilic carrier (RHE-HA-PBAP, denoted RHP) loaded with curcumin, for the treatment of LPS-induced AKI (Fig. 18A). During AKI-associated inflammation, macrophages infiltrated the kidney and contributed to inflammatory amplification. Because HA can specifically bind the CD44 receptor on macrophage surfaces, RHP@Cur associated more readily with macrophages and was internalized (Fig. 18B), thereby enabling targeted delivery to inflammation-relevant cells. After entering the intracellular environment, ROS oxidized aryl boronate esters into more hydrophilic products (boronic acids/borates). This chemical conversion of the PBAP segment, which originally provides hydrophobicity and structural stabilization, disrupted amphiphilicity, destabilizes the micellar core–shell architecture, and consequently promoted micelle disassembly, which accelerated curcumin leakage and release (Fig. 18C). The released curcumin exerted anti-inflammatory activity largely through inhibition of NF-κB signaling, as supported by in vitro and in vivo readouts including NO, TNF-α, and NF-κB pathway proteins (p-p65 and p-IκBα). In addition, RHE was linked to HA via ester bonds; under acidic conditions, these ester linkages underwent hydrolysis, enabling pH-triggered release of RHE (Fig. 18D). Notably, released RHE itself exhibited anti-inflammatory activity. Consequently, RHP@Cur reduced inflammatory mediators (NO and TNF-α) and suppressed aberrant NF-κB activation at the molecular level (decreased p-IκBα and p-p65; Fig. 18E), thereby interrupting the sequence of IκBα phosphorylation and dissociation, followed by p65 nuclear translocation, culminating in pro-inflammatory gene transcription. In vivo, these effects translated into attenuated renal histopathological injury, reduced deposition of the kidney injury marker KIM-1, and improved renal function (lower BUN and SCr), with overall efficacy superior to that of free curcumin. Lysosomal pH-activated H2S propulsion coupled with mitochondrial ROS-triggered thioketal cleavage for SS31 release

During the progression of cellular injury in AKI, delivery systems often face two concurrent bottlenecks. First, intracellular trafficking is constrained: after internalization, nanocarriers tend to be trapped within endosomes/lysosomes, limiting effective cytosolic distribution [237]. Second, a major amplification node resides in mitochondria, where a ROS-rich microenvironment promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death; thus, organelle-level precision intervention is critical for improving therapeutic efficacy [22].

Accordingly, a closed cascade can be established by using the acidic lysosomal environment to trigger rapid gas-prodrug decomposition and propulsion [238], while exploiting high mitochondrial ROS to cleave ROS-labile linkers and release an antioxidant peptide on demand [45]. This cascade links active intracellular delivery with mitochondria-targeted therapy, thereby improving both penetration and precision. Tong et al. [238] reported a pH/ROS dual-responsive nanomotor, Au@SS31-TK-MSN/GYY4137@S (ASMGS), for AKI therapy (Fig. 18H). ASMGS consisted of a gold-decorated mesoporous silica nanoparticle (MSN) carrier, the H₂S prodrug GYY4137 loaded within the mesopores, an L-serine (S) targeting moiety, and the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant peptide SS31 conjugated via a ROS-cleavable TK linker. Following systemic administration, ASMGS accumulated gradually in renal lesions through an EPR-like effect associated with increased vascular permeability in injured regions. Subsequently, L-serine (S) presented on one side of the particle surface recognized and bound Kim-1, which was markedly upregulated on tubular epithelial cells during AKI, thereby enhancing selective adhesion to injured tubules and promoting Kim-1–mediated endocytosis. After cellular entry, particularly within acidic lysosomal compartments, the encapsulated GYY4137 decomposed more rapidly at low pH. This resulted in accelerated H2S generation and release (Fig. 18I–K). Because ASMGS possesses an asymmetric (Janus) architecture, H2S release is spatially uneven, creating a local concentration gradient that drives self-propulsion via a self-diffusiophoretic mechanism (Fig. 18L), thereby enhancing penetration and retention within diseased tissue and inside cells. The released H2S also exerted renoprotective effects. In parallel, under ROS-rich conditions in AKI lesions and mitochondria, the TK linker tethering SS31 was oxidatively cleaved, which disrupted the linkage and enabled SS31 detachment and release from the carrier surface (Fig. 18M). The liberated SS31 then provided mitochondria-localized antioxidant intervention to mitigate oxidative stress–mediated injury. After intravenous injection, fluorescence signals appeared predominantly in the kidney region as early as 2 h, further increased by 6 h, and remained detectable at 24 h (Fig. 18N and O). At the end of treatment, the ASMGS group showed an overall attenuation of renal injury, reduced cell death, and effective suppression of apoptosis. LAMP1 was upregulated, whereas Kim-1, HMGB1, and CRT were downregulated, suggesting restoration of lysosomal function accompanied by reduced injury-associated and inflammatory signaling. Moreover, GSDMD and caspase-11 were decreased, together with reduced urinary IL-18, supporting inhibition of pyroptosis. Collectively, these coordinated effects contributed to more pronounced mitigation of AKI.

ROS and enzyme dual-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

In AKI, excessive ROS is not only a direct driver of cellular injury but also functions as an upstream switch that amplifies inflammation [239]. Accordingly, beyond “precise drug release,” a readily translatable alternative is to engineer nanoplatforms in which the material itself serves as the therapeutic core by broadly scavenging ROS [240]. By leveraging oxidases enriched in the inflammatory microenvironment (e.g., human myeloperoxidase, hMPO) to intensify local oxidative reactions [241], such platforms may enable a lesion-confined closed loop of ROS scavenging, followed by self-oxidation and eventual degradability/metabolizability, thereby balancing efficacy with long-term biosafety. Zhao et al. [242] reported poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP)-modified MXene nanosheets (TPNS; Ti3C2–PVP nanosheets) for AKI therapy (Fig. 19A). TPNS consisted of Ti3C2 MXene nanosheets coated with PVP. PVP modification maintains good dispersion in circulation, and TPNS preferentially accumulates in the kidney under AKI-associated pathological conditions characterized by increased renal permeability and enhanced uptake/retention. After reaching the injured kidney, TPNS exhibited dual responsiveness to the ROS- and enzyme-enriched inflammatory microenvironment. Under elevated ROS levels, TPNS continuously scavenged reactive species such as H2O2, ·O2⁻, and ·OH via redox reactions, which was accompanied by an upward shift in titanium valence states (decreased Ti(II)/Ti(III) and increased Ti(IV) (Fig. 19B). In the presence of inflammation-associated hMPO, hMPO catalyzed H2O2 to create a more strongly oxidative milieu, which in turn accelerated oxidative damage and degradation of the MXene layers (Fig. 19C). Functionally, this cascade first alleviated local oxidative stress (reduced ROS/H2O2 and MDA, with restoration of antioxidant capacity such as SOD and GSH). It then suppressed ROS-driven inflammatory amplification (decreased TNF-α and IL-1β; inhibited NF-κB activation; reduced immune-cell infiltration) (Fig. 19D and E), and mitigated mitochondria-associated apoptosis and tissue injury (downregulated Bax, cytochrome c, and cleaved caspase-3; fewer TUNEL-positive cells), ultimately improving tubular pathology and renal functional endpoints (lower BUN and creatinine). Consistent with rapid renal accumulation, the strongest fluorescence signal in kidney tissue was detectable as early as 5 min after intravenous injection of TPNS (Fig. 19F). At the end of treatment, TPNS markedly improved overall outcomes: body-weight loss was alleviated (Fig. 19G) and survival was significantly increased (Fig. 19H). Renal function indices (BUN/creatinine) were substantially reduced, and H&E staining showed pronounced attenuation of casts and tubular injury. In parallel, renal H2O2 and MDA levels decreased while SOD and GSH recovered; DHE staining indicated suppressed superoxide generation. Kidney injury–related markers, including LDH, 8-OHdG, HO-1, and KIM-1, were also reduced. Moreover, TNF-α and IL-1β levels decreased in both kidney tissue and serum, accompanied by fewer TUNEL-positive cells. ROS and MMP2 dual-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

In AKI, excessive ROS is not only a direct driver of cellular injury but also functions as an upstream switch that amplifies inflammation [239]. Accordingly, beyond “precise drug release,” a readily translatable alternative is to engineer nanoplatforms in which the carrier simultaneously (i) responds to lesion-associated cues and (ii) contributes to therapy by buffering oxidative stress. In I/R-AKI in particular, ROS elevation often coincides with protease remodeling in the injured microenvironment (e.g., MMP2 upregulation), creating an opportunity to implement dual-triggered, lesion-confined release with built-in safety through stimulus-driven disassembly and degradability [49]. Zhang et al. [243] reported a vitamin E (VitE)–loaded ROS/MMP2 dual-responsive kidney injury–targeted nanoparticle (NP) system, self-assembled from two amphiphilic polymers (Gel-SH and DSPE-Se-Se-PEG2k-NH2) with encapsulated vitamin E, for the treatment of I/R-induced AKI and for delaying progression from AKI to CKD (Fig. 19I). PEGylation improved systemic stability, while I/R-AKI–induced disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) together with MMP2 upregulation, enabled injury-selective entry coupled with lesion retention, resulting in preferential accumulation in injured renal tissue without reliance on ligand–receptor active targeting (Fig. 19J). After reaching the AKI lesion, elevated ROS oxidatively cleaved the Se–Se (diselenide) bonds in the carrier (Fig. 19K), thereby loosening/disrupting the DSPE-Se-Se-PEG outer layer and weakening micellar stability, which promoted VitE release from the hydrophobic core. Meanwhile, because MMP2 was upregulated in the injured microenvironment, Gel-SH (a shell component) underwent MMP2-mediated proteolysis/degradation, inducing particle size/structural changes and further accelerating release kinetics (Fig. 19L). Upon trigger-induced release, VitE terminated PUFA lipid peroxidation propagation by scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals (LOO•), reduced oxidative damage burden (e.g., MDA↓, SOD↑), and globally suppressed ferroptosis-related pathways. RNA-seq and pathway enrichment analyses indicated downregulation of ferroptosis, accompanied by protective normalization of key molecules (e.g., GPX4↑; ACSL4/ALOX5↓) and reduced lipid peroxidation markers such as 4-HNE (Fig. 19M). After tail-vein injection, a distinct renal fluorescence signal was detectable at 3 h and remained stable up to 48 h (Fig. 19N). At the end of treatment, once ferroptosis was effectively intercepted, downstream tubular cell death and tissue injury were concurrently alleviated (TUNEL↓; KIM-1/NGAL↓; improved H&E histology; decreased SCr and BUN) (Fig. 19O). Over the longer term, fibrosis progression was further attenuated (reduced fiber deposition in Masson/PSR staining; decreased collagen I and α-SMA), supporting an intervention effect on the AKI-to-CKD transition.

GSH and pH dual-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI therapy

In I/R–associated AKI, the burst of ROS during reperfusion can initiate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial injury, which in turn disrupt Ca2+ homeostasis and drive inflammatory amplification and apoptotic cascades [21]. This establishes a vicious cycle in which ROS and Ca2+ mutually reinforce each other, thereby exacerbating tubular damage. Accordingly, a rational design is to couple lesion-redox–triggered carrier “unlocking” with pH-accelerated activation/release of therapeutic factors, enabling a lesion-confined synergistic intervention that simultaneously suppresses ROS and reduces Ca2+ overload, thereby intercepting upstream amplification loops and promoting renal recovery [23]. Yan et al. [33] reported a nanoplatform termed mSiO2@BA-AM/BAC@LTH for the treatment of I/R-associated AKI (Fig. 20A). This system comprised a mesoporous silica-based nanocarrier with a disulfide (S–S)–rich framework, co-loaded borane amino complex (BAC) and BA-AM (BAPTA-AM), and a tubular-targeting peptide (PEG-LTH). The surface-conjugated LTH peptide (LTHVVWL) specifically recognized the Kim-1 receptor, which was highly expressed on injured proximal tubular cells in AKI, thereby promoting receptor-mediated binding and cellular uptake. As a result, the nanoparticles selectively accumulated in the diseased kidney and showed markedly enhanced renal retention. After reaching injured proximal tubules, elevated GSH in the early lesion reduced and cleaved disulfide bonds within the carrier framework, thereby inducing collapse of the mesoporous architecture and initiating cargo release (Fig. 20B). Subsequently, within the mildly acidic inflammatory microenvironment, BAC decomposed more rapidly and generates bioactive H2, which efficiently scavenged ROS (Fig. 20C) and alleviated oxidative stress. In parallel, following cellular internalization, BA-AM was deprotected by intracellular esterases to yield BAPTA (BA), which directly chelates cytosolic free Ca2+ and corrects Ca2+ overload (Fig. 20D). Through the combined actions of H₂-mediated ROS suppression and BA-mediated Ca2+ reduction, the ROS–Ca2+ self-amplifying loop was interrupted at its source. This resulted in inhibition of ER stress–related apoptotic signaling (PERK/ATF4/CHOP–Bax and caspase-12–caspase-3 cascades), blockade of the mitochondrial cytochrome c–cleaved caspase-3 pathway, and downregulation of TNF-α/NF-κB inflammatory signaling (Fig. 20E), culminating in reducing tubular apoptosis and inflammatory injury and facilitating renal functional recovery. Consistent with enhanced targeting, after intravenous injection of mSiO2@BA-AM/BAC@LTH following reperfusion, kidney fluorescence signals at 1, 3, and 6 h were approximately 1.6-, 1.8-, and 1.7-fold higher, respectively, than those of the non-targeted formulation, as quantified by in vivo/ex vivo near-infrared imaging (Fig. 20F and G). At the end of treatment, the intensity of the tubular injury marker Kim-1 was markedly reduced (the model group was 23-fold higher than normal, whereas the treatment group showed a pronounced decline). Renal histopathological injury (including necrosis and tubular structural disorganization) was substantially alleviated with a markedly lower injury score (Fig. 20H). TUNEL-positive apoptosis was significantly decreased (≈54.3% in the model group, markedly reduced in the treated group and approaching normal levels). Conclusions and perspectives

Overarching rationale and scope

Despite encouraging progress, several barriers continue to limit the clinical translatability of stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms for AKI. Responsive nanomaterials address a central delivery paradox: carriers must remain inert during circulation to reduce off-target exposure, yet activate within injured renal niches to enable restricted release, de-shielding or disassembly, catalytic ROS removal, or organelle-specific intervention [244]. As a result, kidney exposure, particularly within the vulnerable proximal tubules, can be enhanced without causing a corresponding increase in systemic toxicity [245]. Current barriers to clinical translation

Technical barriers

At the technical level, the specificity of activation is limited by the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of intrarenal cues. This is particularly true for ROS/H2O2-responsive systems, where in vitro trigger thresholds may not accurately reflect the in vivo oxidative environment. Structural complexity, incomplete biodegradation, inorganic-component retention, and insufficiently validated kidney-targeting mechanisms further complicate mechanistic interpretation and safety assessment [106, 193, 244].

Practical barriers

At the practical level, many reported systems have been evaluated predominantly in young animals under simplified single-insult and immediate-treatment designs. These conditions do not adequately capture the delayed intervention, recurrent injury, or comorbidity burden typical of clinical AKI [246]. Human AKI is rarely a single-insult disorder; it frequently arises from interacting factors such as infection, hemodynamic instability, nephrotoxic exposure, aging, diabetes, and pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD). These differences alter vascular permeability, tubular uptake, inflammatory cell recruitment, complement activity, and nanoparticle clearance kinetics. Consequently, a platform that performs well in an early-treated murine model may fail in the clinic because the trigger intensity, therapeutic window, and safety margins are substantially different in human disease [247].

Translational and safety barriers

At the translational level, the absence of standardized efficacy endpoints, limited pharmacokinetic comparability, manufacturing complexity, batch-to-batch variability, and unresolved regulatory requirements collectively hinder progression beyond proof-of-concept studies. Favorable efficacy readouts in preclinical AKI models do not in themselves constitute evidence of translational readiness.

The safety profile of renal nanomedicines remains incompletely defined for many material classes. Polymeric, lipidic, inorganic, and biomimetic systems each have distinct toxicological liabilities. These include oxidative injury, lysosomal stress, complement activation, altered macrophage uptake, and prolonged tissue retention, which are particularly concerning when renal clearance is impaired. Immune compatibility should not be assumed even for clinically advanced nanocarrier chemistries. In the broader clinical nanomedicine literature, infusion-related hypersensitivity reactions have been documented for certain liposomal and PEGylated formulations, underscoring that apparent biocompatibility in animals may not fully predict human immunoreactivity. AKI-oriented nanoplatforms that incorporate PEG, membrane coatings, metallic cores, or multicomponent hybrid architectures should therefore not be evaluated solely on the basis of short-term efficacy and histology [248].

Biomimetic or cell membrane-derived systems may reduce rapid clearance, yet their source heterogeneity, membrane-protein composition, and batch-dependent immunological behavior can introduce additional uncertainty rather than inherently conferring immunological compatibility. For inorganic systems, prolonged retention of nondegradable or slowly cleared components is a particular concern under reduced glomerular filtration. These considerations suggest that the most promising AKI nanoplatforms are not necessarily the most structurally complex ones, but rather those that combine a clear activation logic with verifiable biodegradation, disease-relevant immune safety, and reproducible manufacturing.

Future directions

Mechanism-chain design and activation verification

A critical next step is to shift from “trigger inclusion” to testable mechanism-chain design, in which cue engagement, material transformation, intrarenal and subcellular distribution, pathway modulation, and functional outcomes are evaluated as a causal sequence [244]. In practice, this approach necessitates activation verification and falsifiable controls, such as trigger-insensitive analogs, blocked catalytic sites, and non-targeting shells, along with time-resolved readouts that align with the kinetics of AKI [193]. Platforms with a clearly verifiable mechanism chain provide stronger translational value than those supported mainly by endpoint improvement alone [246].

Subtype-aware platform design

Subtype-aware matching should be treated as an explicit design constraint rather than a secondary consideration. Mitochondrial and ferroptosis-focused strategies are best suited to ischemia–reperfusion AKI (I/R-AKI) [249]; anti-inflammatory and endothelial-protective approaches, together with externally controllable activation such as ultrasound, are particularly relevant for sepsis-associated AKI (SA-AKI); and pH-enabled intracellular release with efficient endosomal escape is most appropriate for nucleic-acid modalities in drug-induced AKI (DI-AKI). Although AKI is etiologically diverse, its progression commonly involves self-reinforcing loops linking microvascular dysfunction, tubular energy failure, mitochondrial impairment, redox imbalance, and inflammatory activation. Responsive nanomaterials provide a framework to operationalize these lesion cues into controllable therapeutic behaviors.

Systems that combine lesion-level enrichment with intracellular or organelle-level activation are more mechanistically convincing than those relying on a single trigger, because they better reconcile circulation stability with local therapeutic intensity. Although ROS/H2O2-responsive systems remain the most extensively studied, their apparent maturity should not obscure persistent uncertainties regarding activation thresholds, oxidative heterogeneity, and cross-model reproducibility. Hypoxia- and ultrasound-based strategies are less mature in AKI but may prove particularly useful in settings where endogenous biochemical triggers are weak, variable, or difficult to quantify.

Degradability-aware safety and immunotoxicity assessment

Future studies should prioritize degradability-by-design as a core principle, with a detailed assessment of the long-term retention and clearance of all components, particularly inorganic or metal-containing moieties, in conditions where glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is reduced [106]. Catalytic platforms must be evaluated for secondary oxidative liabilities and unintended immune activation. Gas-releasing or motion-enhanced systems require careful assessment of local concentration spikes and vascular effects. Immunotoxicity testing under disease-relevant conditions is essential, as nanoparticle formulations can activate innate immune pathways including complement and phagocyte-driven responses whose behavior in the injured kidney may differ substantially from the healthy state [250].

Preclinical model refinement and standardized endpoints

Future studies should incorporate delayed-dosing designs, recurrent injury models, and comorbidity-relevant animal cohorts to more faithfully reflect the heterogeneous clinical syndrome. Cross-study inconsistencies in model severity, trigger definition, biodistribution metrics, and endpoint selection make apparently positive results difficult to compare directly, and may partially explain why strong protection in animal models has not yet translated into approved AKI nanotherapies. Standardized, multidimensional endpoint frameworks, including functional, histological, molecular, and pharmacokinetic readouts, should be systematically adopted. Whenever feasible, confirmation in large-animal or human-relevant ex vivo systems should precede clinical escalation.

Scalable manufacturing and regulatory readiness

A clinically relevant development pathway should include reproducible GMP-compatible manufacturing, with predefined critical quality attributes such as particle size distribution, drug loading, trigger sensitivity, colloidal stability, sterility, and endotoxin control. Additionally, pharmacokinetic and biodistribution characterization under conditions of impaired renal function should be integrated. Alignment with regulatory expectations for chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) and indication-specific risk–benefit assessment should be integrated early in platform development rather than addressed post hoc. Recent advances in enzyme-responsive nanomaterials and biomimetic antioxidant platforms illustrate design strategies that integrate intrinsic antioxidant function with stimuli-responsive release, potentially complementing these translational requirements [61, 62, 251].

A more balanced evaluation framework should consider not only maximal therapeutic effect under optimized experimental conditions, but also therapeutic window, reversibility of material fate, and reproducibility across clinically heterogeneous AKI contexts. Taken together, responsive nanomedicines could become clinically interpretable interventions for AKI if developed with activation-verified mechanism chains, subtype-aware logic, quantitative delivery rules, and degradability-by-design principles. These approaches would support both acute rescue and longer-term risk modification within a “deliver, activate, verify” framework.

Broader design trends emerging from the reviewed evidence

Beyond cataloguing trigger types and carrier architectures, the evidence reviewed here suggests several broader trends relevant for future platform design. First, systems combining lesion-level enrichment with intracellular or organelle-level activation appear more convincing than those relying on a single trigger, because they better reconcile circulation stability with local therapeutic intensity [244]. Second, platforms with a clearly verifiable mechanism chain, which links trigger engagement, material transformation, pathway modulation, and functional outcomes, offer stronger translational value than those primarily supported by endpoint improvement. Third, while ROS/H2O2-responsive systems remain the most extensively studied, their apparent maturity should not obscure persistent uncertainties regarding activation thresholds, oxidative heterogeneity, and cross-model reproducibility [193].

Failure to resolve self-reinforcing pathological loops in time predisposes the kidney to persistent inflammation, chronic hypoxia, and fibrotic remodeling, increasing the likelihood of AKI-to-CKD transition [6]. Addressing AKI’s etiology-dependent heterogeneity therefore requires activation-threshold matching to pathophysiological cues, rigorous quantitative biodistribution methods, and subtype- and stage-aware preclinical models [246]. Taken together, the current field is defined not only by expanding material diversity, but also by the urgent need to move from proof-of-concept responsiveness toward subtype-aware, quantitatively testable, and clinically interpretable platform design.

Limitations of the present review and distinction from prior work

Limitations

Several critical limitations merit direct acknowledgment. First, the potential toxicity of nanomaterials, including inorganic components, cationic polymers, and reactive catalytic species, is not yet fully characterized in the context of AKI, where reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) may lead to their retention and accumulation [106, 244]. Second, immune responses to nanoparticle surfaces vary substantially across formulations and are rarely assessed in AKI-specific models [250]. Third, most preclinical data derive from young, otherwise healthy animals subjected to a single AKI insult, whereas clinical AKI typically occurs in older patients with comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and pre-existing CKD. This translational gap limits the direct applicability of reported efficacy data. Although none of the responsive AKI nanoplatforms reviewed here has progressed to clinical trials, valuable translational insights can be drawn from clinically advanced nanomedicines in oncology and inflammatory disease [246]. Another important limitation is that the current evidence base is strongly biased toward positive proof-of-concept outcomes. True failure cases and contradictory findings are rarely reported in detail, which makes it difficult to determine whether limited efficacy reflects inadequate renal accumulation, insufficient stimulus intensity, premature cargo leakage, poor intracellular release, or material-related toxicity. Moreover, apparently favorable results across studies should be interpreted cautiously because AKI models differ substantially in etiology, severity, timing of intervention, animal age, comorbidity status, and endpoint selection. A nanoplatform that performs well in a young and otherwise healthy mouse model of ischemia–reperfusion injury may not show the same benefit in septic AKI, drug-induced AKI, delayed-treatment settings, or patients with pre-existing CKD. In addition, some responsive systems rely on cationic polymers, inorganic catalytic cores, metal-containing nanozymes, or biomimetic membranes, each of which may introduce distinct safety concerns, including membrane disruption, complement activation, macrophage uptake, inflammatory amplification, or long-term tissue retention. Therefore, future studies should report both positive and negative outcomes more transparently, include trigger-insensitive or non-targeted controls, evaluate immunotoxicity and long-term clearance under impaired renal function, and test representative platforms in delayed-treatment and comorbidity-relevant models before making strong claims of clinical applicability.

Distinction from prior reviews

Unlike earlier AKI nanomedicine reviews that mainly categorize by material type or therapeutic target, this review uniquely positions AKI lesion microenvironmental signals as programmable engineering inputs [193]. It also maps each responsive platform onto a causal sequence of “trigger, material behavior, pathological node, and quantitative outcome”. This mechanistic-chain logic allows for meaningful cross-platform comparisons and assists in identifying the platform architectures best suited to specific AKI subtypes and disease stages [246]. Furthermore, this review explicitly examines the mechanistic continuum between broad ROS-responsive and H2O2-specific strategies, a distinction often overlooked in prior reviews. It also highlights the importance of integrating safety, immunotoxicity, and manufacturing readiness as primary design considerations rather than secondary factors [250].

In summary, advancing the field toward clinical translation will depend not only on structural innovation but also on mechanistic rigor, reproducibility, scalable manufacturing, and thorough safety evaluation. These principles should guide every stage of responsive AKI nanoplatform development.
