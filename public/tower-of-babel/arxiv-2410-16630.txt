Nonlinear semiclassical spectroscopy of ultrafast molecular polariton dynamics
Michael Reitz, Arghadip Koner, Joel Yuen-Zhou

Abstract
We introduce a theoretical framework that allows for the systematic and efficient description of the ultrafast nonlinear response of molecular polaritons, i.e., hybrid light-matter states, in the collective regime of large numbers of molecules 𝒩 \mathcal{N} coupled to the cavity photon mode. Our approach is based on a semiclassical, mean-field evolution of the molecular Hamiltonian and the cavity field, which is complemented by a perturbative expansion of both light and matter counterparts in the input fields entering the cavity. In addition, expansion in terms of the pulse phases enables us to disentangle different excitation pathways in Liouville space, thereby distinguishing contributions to the nonlinear response. The formalism extends traditional free-space nonlinear spectroscopy by incorporating the feedback of matter onto the light field via the induced polarization. We demonstrate the utility of the framework by applying it to the calculation of pump-probe polariton spectra and show how, by storing the pulses, the cavity facilitates additional excitation pathways, which can be used to isolate purely bright state contributions . Our method, which does not scale with 𝒩 \mathcal{N} , is broadly applicable and can be extended to model a wide range of current experiments investigating the dynamical nonlinear response of hybrid light-matter states.

Mean-field approach.
—We consider an ensemble of
\mathcal{N}
molecules collectively coupled to the single confined mode of an optical cavity at frequency
\omega_{c}
[see schematics in Fig.
1
(a)]. Each molecule
j
is described by an (in principle arbitrary) Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{j}
with a corresponding dipole operator
\hat{\mu}_{j}
. The interaction between the molecules and the cavity field is described by a Dicke-type Hamiltonian such that the total Hamiltonian describing the full quantum evolution of the coupled light-matter system expresses as (we set
\hbar\equiv 1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\omega_{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{j}+E_{0}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\hat{\mu}_{j},

(1)

with the cavity zero-point amplitude
E_{0}=\sqrt{\omega_{c}/(2\epsilon_{0}\mathcal{V})}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the cavity mode volume and
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity.
To facilitate the understanding of the formalism, we initially assume that the cavity field is driven by only a single input field with amplitude
\eta
at a central carrier frequency
\omega_{\ell}
and with a Gaussian pulse shape described by the temporal envelope function
f(t)
with a temporal pulse width
\tau_{w}
.

The limit of large
\mathcal{N}
allows us to apply a mean-field approximation (exact for
\mathcal{N}\to\infty
), which amounts to setting
\expectationvalue{\hat a}=\alpha(t)

[
38
]
. Additionally, for illustration, we take all molecules to be identical, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{j}=\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\hat{\mu}_{j}=\hat{\mu}
, although extension to disordered ensembles offers no conceptual difficulty. The simplified mean-field Hamiltonian then expresses as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+E_{0}\left(\alpha(t)+\alpha^{*}(t)\right)\hat{\mu}.

(2)

The dynamical evolution of the
(now single-molecule)
density matrix
\rho
is governed by the master equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathrm{i}[\rho,\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}]+\mathcal{D}[\rho]
. Here, the dissipator
\mathcal{D}[\rho]
represents possible additional loss channels of the molecule (e.g., dephasing at rate
\gamma_{\phi}
, non-radiative decay,…) that may be added, e.g., in Lindblad form. However, a complete description of the mean-field problem also involves solving the classical equation for the cavity field amplitude which undergoes damping at rate
\kappa
(photon loss)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha(t)-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{N}E_{0}P(t)-\eta f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\ell}t},

(3)

containing the induced molecular polarization
P(t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}\rho(t)]
of all
\mathcal{N}
molecules. Altogether, this describes a self-consistent, semiclassical evolution of the coupled light-matter system, where the matter component evolves quantum mechanically, while the part describing the electromagnetic field undergoes classical evolution and is driven by both the input field as well as by the collective molecular polarization feeding back into the cavity field.
While Eq. (
3
) assumes a simplified, single-mode description of the electromagnetic field, we discuss the straightforward extension to multimode cavities in the Supplementary Material (SM)
[
49
]
.

Perturbative expansion.
—While the above set of mean-field equations can be readily solved numerically, there is generally no closed analytical solution due to the nonlinearity of the molecular transitions. To gain more insight into the structure of the nonlinear response, we therefore proceed, akin to standard nonlinear spectroscopy, with a perturbative expansion of both cavity field
and
matter density matrix in terms of the input field amplitude, i.e.,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\eta^{n}\alpha^{(n)}(t),\quad\rho(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\eta^{n}\rho^{(n)}(t).

(4)

From this, also other perturbative quantities can be derived such as, e.g., the
n
th order polarization
P^{(n)}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}\rho^{(n)}(t)]

Separating excitation pathways.
—The nonlinear signal stemming from the
n
th order density matrix generally contains many possible excitation pathways. For instance, even for free-space spectroscopy of a 2LS, there are eight Feynman diagrams contributing to the third-order nonlinear response
[
26
]
. In experiments, it is therefore highly desired to isolate certain excitation pathways and thereby understand the different contributions to the nonlinear signal, which here, owing to the collinear geometry in question, can be achieved by phase cycling.
This method is equivalent to the more standard phase matching procedure with oblique pulses, which, however obfuscates our formalism as it requires the description of a multimode cavity
[
28
,
29
,
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
]
.

To this end, we will consider the effect of additional phases applied to the pump (
\Phi_{p}
) and probe (
\Phi_{p^{\prime}}
) pulses. These phases are subsequently also imprinted onto the cavity field and the molecular density matrix, enabling us to disentangle different excitation pathways characterized by specific phase sequences
[
26
,
28
]
. The cavity field and density matrix are now expanded as

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m,\vec{v}}\eta_{p}^{n}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\Phi}}\alpha^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}(t),

(9a)

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m,\vec{v}}\eta_{p}^{n}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\Phi}}\rho^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}(t),

(9b)

where
\vec{\Phi}=(\Phi_{p},\Phi_{p^{\prime}})^{\top}
denotes the vector of pump and probe phases, and
\vec{v}=(v_{p},v_{p^{\prime}})^{\top}
with
v_{p},v_{p^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}
denotes the phase coefficients which also depend on the pump and probe orders
v_{p}(n)
,
v_{p^{\prime}}(m)
(see SM
[
49
]
). The raw DT signal from Eq. (
8
) can then be separated into different phase contributions

\displaystyle\Delta T_{\vec{v}}(\omega)\approx\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}\eta_{p}^{2}\frac{2\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha^{(0)(1)*}_{(0,-1)}(\omega)\alpha^{(2)(1)}_{\vec{v}}(\omega)\right]}{f_{p^{\prime}}(\omega)^{2}},

(10)

where
(0,-1)
is the only phase contribution for the conjugate probe field
[
49
]
and the total signal is obtained as
\Delta T=\sum_{\vec{v}}\Delta T_{\vec{v}}
.

Two phase combinations contribute to
\alpha^{(2)(1)}
:
(0,1)
and
(2,-1)
[along with the complex conjugates
(0,-1)
and
(-2,1)
], corresponding to pump–probe and double-quantum coherence (DQC) spectroscopies, respectively [Figs. 4(a,b)]. The pump–probe pathway
(0,1)\equiv\pm\Phi_{p}\!\mp\!\Phi_{p}\!+\!\Phi_{p^{\prime}}
creates populations and zeroth-order quantum coherences via two pump pulses of opposite phase [Fig.
4
(c)]. The DQC pathway
(2,-1)\equiv 2\Phi_{p}\!-\!\Phi_{p^{\prime}}
is negligible outside the cavity because it requires the two-level system to be excited twice. However, inside the cavity, the probe can act on the pump-induced coherence within the lifetime of the bright state
(\kappa+\gamma_{\phi})^{-1}
[left diagram in Fig.
4

(2,-1)
pathway isolates the bright state only, in contrast to the
(0,1)
pathway, which accesses both bright and dark states (and probes only dephased dark states in the long time limit). Eq. (

[
28
]
.

Discussion and conclusions.
—We have presented an efficient and broadly applicable framework for nonlinear polariton spectroscopy, based on a mean-field evolution of the coupled light-matter system. The formalism extends conventional free-space spectroscopy by accounting for the feedback of the molecular polarization onto the cavity field and allows for an understanding of the polariton response beyond the stationary limit
[
33
]
. Expanding the cavity field and molecular density matrix in terms of the input fields’ amplitudes and phases
provides
a systematic understanding of the various contribution to the nonlinear polariton response,
and allows to separate purely bright state contributions from dark reservoir states
.

The formalism offers potential for extension and generalization via multiple avenues. For instance, while our current treatment relies on a semiclassical mean-field description, valuable extensions could involve capturing nonclassical photon statistics
or many-body correlations among particles
, e.g., by using cumulant
or cluster
expansion techniques
[
66
,
67
]
.
Other areas of interest are the study of spatially-resolved nonlinear polariton spectroscopy, which would involve generalizing the zero-dimensional cavity Eq. (
3
) to the 3D set of cavity modes afforded by classical electromagnetic simulations
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
]
, or cavities exhibiting inherent nonlinearities
[
73
]
.
Furthermore, while we have focused on pump-probe spectroscopy involving two pulses, the formalism can be readily applied to 2D or multidimensional coherent spectroscopy,
which may also help to disentangle pure electronic dephasing from vibronic relaxation channels
.
Finally, let us emphasize that our approach is not limited to molecular polaritons, but could also be extended to other strongly coupled light-matter platforms, such as exciton-polaritons in inorganic semiconductor microcavities
[
74
]
or atomic
ensembles
.

(S4b)

from which the transmitted intensity can be straightforwardly derived as

\displaystyle T=\left|\frac{\expectationvalue{\hat b_{\mathrm{out}, R}(t)}}{\expectationvalue{\hat b_{\mathrm{in}, L}(t)}}\right|^{2}=\frac{\kappa_{R}\kappa_{L}}{\eta^{2}f(t)^{2}}|\alpha|^{2}.

(S5)

For a balanced cavity
\kappa_{L}=\kappa_{R}=\kappa/2
, this becomes

\displaystyle T=\frac{(\kappa/2)^{2}}{\eta^{2}f(t)^{2}}|\alpha|^{2},

(S6)

which holds in both linear and nonlinear regimes, as well as in the Fourier domain.
It is important to note, however, that in the ultrastrong coupling regime, the input-output relations are significantly modified due to the breakdown of the rotating wave approximation (RWA). In this regime, where the collective coupling strength
g\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}
becomes comparable to the cavity or matter transition frequencies, non-trivial corrections to the input-output relations must be incorporated that account for counter-rotating terms and the hybrid eigenmodes of the light-matter system
[
60
,
61
]
.

S3
General form of pump-probe equations

For a cavity driven by two pulses, the
(n)(m)
-th order equations for the field and molecular density matrix are given by (assuming initial conditions
\alpha(0)=\alpha^{(0)(0)}=0
)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(n)(m)}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(n)(m)}-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}\rho^{(n)(m)}]-\delta_{1n}\delta_{0m}f_{p}(t-\tau_{p})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p}t}-\delta_{0n}\delta_{1m}f_{p^{\prime}}(t-\tau_{p^{\prime}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p^{\prime}}t},

(S7a)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(n)(m)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[\mathcal{H}_{0},\rho^{(n)(m)}]-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{m-1}\left(\alpha^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})}+\alpha^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})*}\right)\left[\hat{\mu},\rho^{(j)(j^{\prime})}\right],

(S7b)

where we will generally assume identical pulse envelopes
f_{j}(t-\tau_{j})=\mathrm{e}^{-(t-\tau_{j})^{2}/\tau_{w}^{2}}
. In Liouville space, the equation for the density vector can then be expressed in compact form as

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{\rho}}^{(n)(m)}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{0}\vec{\rho}^{(n)(m)}(t)-\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})}(t)\vec{\rho}^{(j)(j^{\prime})}(t),

(S8)

where we defined the interaction Liouvillian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})}(t)=E_{0}[\alpha^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})}(t)+\mathrm{c.c.}]\mathcal{L}_{\mu},

(S9)

and the vectorized commutators are given by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{0},\rho]\to\mathcal{L}_{0}\vec{\rho}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{H}_{0}\otimes I-I\otimes\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\top}]\vec{\rho},

(S10a)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mu},\rho]\to\mathcal{L}_{\mu}\vec{\rho}

\displaystyle=[\hat{\mu}\otimes I-I\otimes\hat{\mu}^{\top}]\vec{\rho}.

(S10b)

S4
Differential transmission

We define the differential transmission (DT) as the difference in the probe transmission with and without the presence of the pump pulse

\displaystyle\Delta T(\omega)=T_{p^{\prime}}^{\text{pump\, on}}(\omega)-T_{p^{\prime}}^{\text{pump\, off}}(\omega).

(S11)

Considering only the lowest (third) order correction of the probe field due to the pump, the normalized transmitted probe intensities can be expressed from input-output relations in Fourier domain (see section
S2
) as

\displaystyle T_{p^{\prime}}^{\text{pump\, off}}(\omega)=\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{|\alpha^{(0)(1)}(\omega)|^{2}}{f_{p^{\prime}}(\omega)^{2}},\qquad T_{p^{\prime}}^{\text{pump\, on}}(\omega)\approx\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{|\alpha^{(0)(1)}(\omega)+\eta_{p}^{2}\alpha^{(2)(1)}(\omega)|^{2}}{f_{p^{\prime}}(\omega)^{2}}.

(S12)

From this, we can approximate the DT as

\displaystyle\Delta T(\omega)\approx\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}\eta_{p}^{2}\frac{2\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha^{(0)(1)*}(\omega)\alpha^{(2)(1)}(\omega)\right]}{f_{p^{\prime}}(\omega)^{2}}.

(S13)

This quantity is not the usual pump-probe signal but contains different phase contributions (see main text, paragraph ‘Differential transmission’).

Analytical solution of pump-probe dynamics for a 2LS

For a two-level system, let us consider the
(2)(1)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(2)(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(2)(1)}-\mathrm{i}g\mathcal{N}\rho_{21}^{(2)(1)},

(S14a)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}g\alpha^{(1)(0)}(\rho_{22}^{(1)(1)}-\rho_{11}^{(1)(1)}),

where we considered dissipation affecting the molecules in the form of dephasing (

(2)(0)

S9
Bright and dark states in mean-field theory

We show here how molecular dark and bright states are arising in the mean-field approach (for a 2LS)
[
38
]
. The total population is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\expectationvalue{\s_j^\dagger\s_j},

(S23)

which in mean field theory becomes

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathcal{N}\expectationvalue{\s^\dagger\s}.

(S24)

Using that the general population of the
k
-th collective mode is given by
p_{k}=\expectationvalue{\s_k^\dagger\s_k}
, the population of the bright mode (corresponding to
k=0
) is given by

\displaystyle p_{B}=p_{k=0}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\expectationvalue{\s_j^\dagger\s_{j'}^{\phantom{\dagger}}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\expectationvalue{\s_j^\dagger\s_j^{\phantom{\dagger}}}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\expectationvalue{\s_j}^{*}\expectationvalue{\s_{j'}},

(S25)

which in mean field theory becomes

\displaystyle p_{B}=\expectationvalue{\s^\dagger\s}+(\mathcal{N}-1)|\expectationvalue{\s}|^{2}

(S26)

The dark state population
p_{D}=p_{\mathrm{tot}}-p_{B}
is therefore given

\displaystyle p_{D}=(\mathcal{N}-1)\expectationvalue{\s^\dagger\s}-(\mathcal{N}-1)|\expectationvalue{\s}|^{2}.

(S27)

In the large
\mathcal{N}
limit, this can be expressed as

\displaystyle p_{B}

\displaystyle\approx\expectationvalue{\s^\dagger\s}+\mathcal{N}|\expectationvalue{\s}|^{2},

(S28a)

\displaystyle p_{D}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\expectationvalue{\s^\dagger\s}-|\expectationvalue{\s}|^{2}\right).

(S28b)

Therefore, the bright state corresponds to molecular coherence in the large
\mathcal{N}
limit.

S10
Details on phase expansion

For an arbitrary multilevel system described by a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and a dipole operator
\hat{\mu}=\hat{\mu}^{(+)}+\hat{\mu}^{(-)}
, the mean-field Hamiltonian in the rotating wave approximation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+E_{0}\left(\alpha^{*}(t)\hat{\mu}^{(-)}+\alpha(t)\hat{\mu}^{(+)}\right).

(S29)

We expand the cavity field and density matrix
in terms of the input fields’ amplitudes and phases
as

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=\sum_{n,m,\vec{v}}\eta_{p}^{n}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\Phi}}\alpha^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}(t),\quad\rho(t)=\sum_{n,m,\vec{v}}\eta_{p}^{n}\eta_{p^{\prime}}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\Phi}}\rho^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}(t),

(S30)

where
\vec{\Phi}=(\Phi_{p},\Phi_{p^{\prime}})^{\top}
denotes the vector of pump and probe phases and
\vec{v}=(v_{p},v_{p^{\prime}})^{\top}
denotes the coefficients of the phases, respectively. Possible values for the phase coefficients in
(n),(m)
-th order are
v_{p}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}
,
v_{p^{\prime}}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}
, where each
s_{j}=\pm 1
. Note that complex conjugation corresponds to flipping the sign of the phase, i.e.,
\left[\alpha^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}\right]^{*}=\alpha^{(n)(m)*}_{-\vec{v}}
.

The equation for the
(n)(m)
-th order cavity field including phases on the pump and probe input fields reads

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(n)(m)}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(n)(m)}-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}^{(-)}\rho^{(n)(m)}]-\delta_{1n}\delta_{0m}f_{p}(t-\tau_{p})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{p}}-\delta_{0n}\delta_{1m}f_{p^{\prime}}(t-\tau_{p^{\prime}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p^{\prime}}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{p^{\prime}}}.

(S31)

It is now straightforward to see that, at first order, the only phase contributions of the cavity field which are driven by the input pulses are
(1,0)
for the field created by the pump and
(0,1)
for the field created by the probe

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}^{(-)}\rho^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}]-f_{p}(t-\tau_{p})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p}t},

(S32a)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}^{(-)}\rho^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}]-f_{p^{\prime}}(t-\tau_{p^{\prime}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{p^{\prime}}t},

(S32b)

while the other possible phase combinations at first order
(-1,0)
and
(0,-1)
are contained in the complex conjugate cavity fields. All other phase components are not driven at first order and are therefore zero. The positive-phase cavity fields can create positive-phase density matrices in first order via the application of
\hat{\mu}^{(+)}
to a ket (creation of excitation) or to a bra (annihilation of excitation):

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[\mathcal{H}_{0},\rho^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}]+\mathcal{D}[\rho^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}]-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\alpha^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)}[\hat{\mu}^{(+)},\rho(0)],

(S33a)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[\mathcal{H}_{0},\rho^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}]+\mathcal{D}[\rho^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}]-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\alpha^{(0)(1)}_{(0,1)}[\hat{\mu}^{(+)},\rho(0)],

(S33b)

while the complex conjugate processes correspond to the application of
\hat{\mu}^{(-)}
, thereby imprinting negative phase contributions onto the density matrix.

Beyond first order, the cavity field is not driven directly anymore. Instead, the density matrix is now driven by lower orders of
\alpha
, which determines the possible phase contributions in higher orders. The phase combinations for the
(n)(m)
-th order density matrix driven by lower orders of cavity field and density matrix are given by

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}=-\mathrm{i}[\mathcal{H}_{0},\rho^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}]+\mathcal{D}[\rho^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}]-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\vec{u}+\vec{w}=\vec{v}}\left(\alpha_{\vec{u}}^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})}[\hat{\mu}^{(+)},\rho^{(j)(j^{\prime})}_{\vec{w}}]+\alpha_{\vec{u}}^{(n-j)(m-j^{\prime})*}[\hat{\mu}^{(-)},\rho^{(j)(j^{\prime})}_{\vec{w}}]\right),

(S34)

where the possible values for
\vec{u}
,
\vec{w}
are determined by the phase contributions in lower orders. This drives the phase contributions for the cavity field at nonlinear orders
n+m>1
as

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}(t)=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{c}\right)\alpha^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}-\mathrm{i}E_{0}\mathcal{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\mu}^{(-)}\rho^{(n)(m)}_{\vec{v}}].

(S35)

S11
Explicit phase expansion for three-level systems

\omega_{2}
,

-
\ket{2}
and
\ket{2}
-
\ket{3}
transitions
to the cavity field

(S38)

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the anticommutator.

First order.

Assuming identical frequencies for pump and probe
\omega_{p}=\omega_{p^{\prime}}
and an initial state
\rho(0)=\ket{g}\bra{g}

\omega_{p}
are given by

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{21,(1,0)}^{(1)(0)}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\gamma_{\phi}}{2}-\mathrm{i}\Delta_{12}\right){\rho}_{21,(1,0)}^{(1)(0)}-\mathrm{i}g_{12}\alpha^{(1)(0)}_{(1,0)},

(S39a)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(0)(1)}_{21,(0,1)}

S12
Extension to multimode cavities

For many scenarios and applications (e.g., studies of polariton transport, polariton condensation,…), the picture of a single cavity mode does not suffice and instead many photonic modes inside the cavity are required. We sketch here how the mean-field treatment can be extended in a straightforward way to also account for such more complicated electromagnetic environments, namely by grouping the molecular system into subsystems
[
39
]
.

To this end, we consider a planar cavity which extends a width
W
perpendicular (transverse) to the cavity axis. To enable a tractable numerical treatment, one can coarse-grain the transverse extent into
N_{k}
discrete points at distance
\Delta r
,
W=N_{k}\Delta r
. Assuming the total number of molecules
\mathcal{N}
inside the cavity to be uniformly distributed along the transverse direction, every one of the grid points is assigned
N_{E}=\mathcal{N}/N_{k}
molecules interacting with
N_{k}
photon modes. This is described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\omega_{k}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{E}}\left[\mathcal{H}_{0}^{nj}+E_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\left(\hat{a}_{k}\hat{\mu}_{nj}^{(+)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr_{n}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\right],

(S44)

where the index
n=1,\ldots,N_{k}
labels the position while
j=1,\ldots N_{E}
labels the particular molecule at a certain position. For the cavity, one may assume a quadratic dispersion
\omega_{k}=\omega_{c}+k^{2}c^{2}/(2\omega_{c})
, with
\omega_{c}
the cavity frequency at
k=0
. This parabolic approximation is well‐known to accurately capture the mode structure of planar cavities near normal incidence. Within the mean-field approximation, the cavity field is assumed to be classical and all molecules at a position
r_{n}
are assumed to be identical, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{nj}=\mathcal{H}_{0}^{n}
,
\hat{\mu}_{nj}=\hat{\mu}_{n}
, leading to the simplified Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{MF}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{n}+E_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\left(\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{\mu}_{n}^{(+)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr_{n}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right).

(S45)

The classical evolution of the
N_{k}
cavity modes driven by pump and probe input pulses with wavevectors
k_{p}
and
k_{p^{\prime}}
transverse to the cavity axis is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}_{k}(t)=-\left(\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\alpha_{k}(t)-\mathrm{i}E_{0}N_{E}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\expectationvalue{\hat{\mu}_n^{(-)}(t)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kr_{n}}-\sum_{j=p,p^{\prime}}\eta_{j}\delta_{k_{j},k}f_{j}(t-\tau_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{j}t},

(S46)

where only the modes corresponding to
k=k_{p},k_{p^{\prime}}
are driven by the input pulses and we assume an equal decay rate
\kappa
for all cavity modes. This set of equations can now, e.g., be used to model pump-probe spectroscopy with pulses entering the cavity at oblique angles.
