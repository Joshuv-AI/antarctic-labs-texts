Physics-guided Emulators Reveal Resilience and Fragility under Operational Latencies and Outages
Sarth Dubey, Subimal Ghosh, Udit Bhatia

Abstract
Reliable hydrologic and flood forecasting requires models that remain stable when input data are delayed, missing, or inconsistent. However, most advances in rainfall–runoff prediction have been evaluated under ideal data conditions, emphasizing accuracy rather than operational resilience. Here, we develop an operationally ready emulator of the Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) that couples long- and short-term memory networks with a relaxed water-balance constraint to preserve physical coherence. Five architectures span a continuum of information availability: from complete historical and forecast forcings to scenarios with data latency and outages, allowing systematic evaluation of robustness. Trained in minimally managed catchments across the United States and tested in more than 5,000 basins, including heavily regulated rivers in India, the emulator reproduces the hydrological core of GloFAS and degrades smoothly as information quality declines. Transfer across contrasting hydroclimatic and management regimes yields reduced yet physically consistent performance, defining the limits of generalization under data scarcity and human influence. The framework establishes operational robustness as a measurable property of hydrological machine learning and advances the design of reliable real-time forecasting systems.

1.3
Performance under data latency and outages

We evaluated the emulator under conditions that mimic real-time forecasting, where near-term meteorological inputs are delayed or temporarily unavailable. Together, these analyses convert operational robustness from a qualitative concept into a measurable property of model behavior. Quantifying how skill declines with information loss provides a benchmark for comparing alternative architectures and establishes a reproducible metric for evaluating the resilience of hydrologic machine learning under real forecasting constraints.

1.5
Limits of transfer learning in data-scarce, managed basins

To assess how physics-guided emulators behave under distributional shifts, we examine their transfer from data-rich and minimally managed basins to data-scarce and strongly regulated ones. The zero-shot evaluation of models pre-trained in CAMELS-US provides the reference performance under domain transfer. To isolate the effect of adaptation, we also retrain emulators from scratch on each target domain, HYSETS and CAMELS-IND, and then fine-tune the pre-trained models on the same data. Together, these three configurations (zero-shot, retrained, and fine-tuned) establish the baselines to diagnose transfer learning behavior in contrasting hydroclimatic and management regimes.

Retraining in HYSETS produces a median Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) of approximately 0.7, indicating that models trained from scratch can recover skill comparable to that of regional basins in the source domain. Fine-tuning of CAMELS-US pre-trained models on HYSETS improves performance further, achieving a median NSE of about 0.6, while preserving spatial coherence. However, localized negative NSE values appear in highly intermittent and heavily regulated basins, underscoring that the degree of human modification remains a limiting factor even in data-rich settings.

In contrast, CAMELS-IND exhibits a markedly different behavior. Both retraining and fine-tuning lead to catastrophic failure, with training losses diverging to negative infinity and validation performance collapsing well below the zero-shot baseline. This degradation reveals the limits of transfer learning under the combined effects of data scarcity, strong regulation, and sparse gauge placement. Extending the zero-shot evaluation to 1,409 subcatchments nested within the 191 CAMELS-IND basins confirms this pattern (see Figure
S10
for a comparative view): the median NSE of approximately 0.5 aligns with the expected skill drop under a larger domain shift relative to HYSETS.

Diagnostic analysis further highlights the structural differences between the two target domains. On average, the CAMELS-IND catchments contain twice as many reservoirs as HYSETS despite being roughly six times smaller, and the density of the gauge stations is substantially lower. These attributes, limited observation networks, high intermittency, and intensive regulation, characterize hydrologically data-scarce regions globally and compound the difficulty of learning consistent rainfall–runoff relationships, exposing the limits of current transfer learning approaches in managed environments.

Conclusion and Discussion

Operational hydrology has long judged progress by accuracy in curated datasets
gauch2023defense
;
krajewski2021real
. Our results redefine this criterion. We show that rainfall–runoff emulation can be assessed under the conditions in which forecasts are actually made, not under ideal data availability
cloke2009ensemble
;
demargne2014hefs
. The emulator developed here reproduces the physical logic of a process-based system when information is complete and maintains credible behavior when data are delayed or missing
harrigan2020glofas
;
alfieri2013glofas
;
hoedt2021mc
;
frame2023strictly
. By analyzing five data-availability regimes, lead-time dependence, and cross-domain transfer, we make operational robustness—a property once treated qualitatively—both measurable and designable. Reliability under data latency and scarcity must become the benchmark of hydrological machine learning.

Three strands of evidence support this shift. (1) With complete information, the emulator captures the spatial pattern of GloFAS skill and scales from regional to national domains, setting the upper bound of emulation fidelity. (2) When inputs degrade through latency or outage, performance declines smoothly rather than collapsing; architectures that retain any consistent information sustain nearly constant skill across a ten-day horizon. (3) Cross-domain transfer exposes the limits of current practice: while zero-shot application preserves spatial coherence, naïve fine-tuning on short, shifted records can destabilize learning. Together, these findings show that robustness can be quantified and that failure modes can be diagnosed prior to deployment.

This redefinition challenges the prevailing paradigm of evaluation. The accuracy of synchronized data cannot represent readiness for operation. Models should instead be profiled for how their skill decays as information thins and how gracefully it recovers. Such profiling offers a reproducible language for comparison and a metric that matters to agencies that must act before all data arrive. In practice, the implications are direct. A latency-sensitive, mask-explicit emulator produces forecasts that fade gently rather than abruptly fail, maintaining operational continuity. Its soft water-balance regularization anchors learning in hydrologic consistency without imposing rigidity, avoiding the brittleness of strict conservation in managed systems. These design features make robustness an engineered, testable property rather than a by-product of scale.

Despite these advances, clear boundaries of applicability remain. The robustness of the emulator has been demonstrated across continental domains and moderate data latency, but its stability under distributed routing, human-managed regulation, and uncertain observations remains to be tested. Extending the framework to include reservoir operations, demand–release dynamics, and explicit uncertainty propagation will allow systematic evaluation of these boundaries and advance toward a coupled, end-to-end forecasting system.

By defining and quantifying robustness within these boundaries, this work provides a reproducible basis for evaluating hydrologic emulators under real forecasting constraints. It shows that physically guided machine learning can sustain performance when information degrades, offering a foundation for forecasting systems that remain credible in operation rather than only in calibration. Incorporating robustness diagnostics into forecasting practice could help agencies assess model readiness more systematically—valuing stability, interpretability, and physical coherence alongside accuracy. Ultimately, the study underscores that meaningful progress in hydrologic machine learning lies not in marginal gains on idealized tests, but in sustained, physically consistent behavior under the imperfect data streams that define real-world forecasting.

A rainfall–runoff (RR) model acts as a transfer function,

The emulator

employs an encoder-decoder LSTM architecture with a 365-day lag and 10-day lead window, similar to models that have repeatedly achieved predictive performance comparable to, or exceeding, that of state-of-the-art physically based models (Kratzert et al., 2019; Nearing et al., 2024). \displaystyle+\lambda_{1}NSE\left(Q_{\textit{GloFAS}},Q_{\phi_{\textit{hist}}}\right)

D\in\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}\}
and configuration
\phi_{\star}\in\{\phi_{\textit{hist}},\phi_{\textit{oper}}\}
is

\hat{y}^{(D)}_{\textit{lead}}\;=\;\mathcal{F}^{(D)}_{\phi_{\star}}\!\left(x_{\textit{lag}}\odot\mathcal{M}^{\text{enc}}_{\textit{lag}},\,x_{\textit{lead}}\odot\mathcal{M}^{\text{dec}}_{\textit{lead}},\,\mathcal{M}^{\text{enc}}_{\textit{lag}},\,\mathcal{M}^{\text{dec}}_{\textit{lead}}\,;\,\theta\right),

where for
\phi_{\textit{hist}}
we set
\mathcal{M}^{\text{enc}}_{\textit{lag}}=\mathbf{1}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\text{dec}}_{\textit{lead}}=\mathbf{1}
(complete, synchronized inputs), and for
\phi_{\textit{oper}}
we apply latency-aware masks as specified earlier. The training loss follows the hybrid objective defined in the Problem Setup.

Scenario 1: Zero-shot transfer.

Use the pretrained source model
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\phi_{\star}}
directly on
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
without any parameter updates:

\hat{y}^{(\mathcal{T}_{k})}_{\textit{lead}}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\phi_{\star}}(\cdot;\,\theta_{\mathcal{S}}),\quad k\in\{1,2\}.

This quantifies out-of-distribution generalization from minimally influenced basins to (i) mixed human influence (
\mathcal{T}_{1}
) and (ii) strong regulation/data scarcity (
\mathcal{T}_{2}
), under both
\phi_{\textit{hist}}
and
\phi_{\textit{oper}}
.

Scenario 2: Retraining on target (from scratch).

Train domain-specific models on each target to obtain
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{T}_{1})}_{\phi_{\star}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{T}_{2})}_{\phi_{\star}}
with randomly initialized
\theta
and identical temporal splits. This establishes in-domain upper bounds and benchmarks the value of source pretraining relative to fully target-trained models.

Scenario 3: Fine-tuning on target only.

Initialize with
\theta_{\mathcal{S}}
and fine-tune all weights on the target using reduced learning rates to produce

\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{1})}_{\phi_{\star}}
and

\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{2})}_{\phi_{\star}}
.
For
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, we additionally vary the effective record length to emulate data scarcity comparable to
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, thereby assessing sensitivity of adaptation to training duration.

Scenario 4: Mixed-source fine-tuning (rehearsal).

Adapt while preserving source generalization by jointly optimizing on source and target:

\min_{\theta}\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\!\big[\ell_{\phi_{\star}}(x,y;\theta)\big]}_{\text{target adaptation}}\;+\;\lambda\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}}\!\big[\ell_{\phi_{\star}}(x,y;\theta)\big]}_{\text{source retention}},\quad k\in\{1,2\},

where
\ell_{\phi_{\star}}
is the hybrid loss (historical or operational) and
\lambda{>}0
balances adaptation against retention. The resulting models

\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}+\lambda\mathcal{T}_{1})}_{\phi_{\star}}
and

\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}+\lambda\mathcal{T}_{2})}_{\phi_{\star}}

explicitly trade off target gains with avoidance of catastrophic forgetting on
\mathcal{S}
.

Together, these scenarios measure (i) zero-shot generalization, (ii) in-domain upper bounds, and (iii) adaptation–retention trade-offs across historical and latency-aware operational configurations, spanning minimally influenced to heavily managed river systems.

3.4
Statistical Analysis

All hypothesis tests were framed as within-unit (paired) repeated-measures at the level of HUCs/Basins for each domain (CAMELS-US, HYSETS, CAMELS-IND) and metric (NSE, F1). For lead-wise model differences, we applied a Friedman omnibus test across the five configurations hist or H1, oper or H2-H5 separately at Lead 1 and Lead 10 (reporting
\chi^{2}
,
df
, and p-value). When the omnibus was significant (
\alpha=0.05
, two-sided throughout), we performed paired Wilcoxon signed-rank post-hoc tests for all 10 pairs, using the Pratt treatment of zero differences; p-values were adjusted by Holm within each (region × metric × lead) family. For each contrast we report the Wilcoxon statistic (W), adjusted p, and two paired effect sizes: rank-biserial
r_{rb}
computed from signed ranks, and the common-language effect size (CLES) interpreted as P(X¿Y). In addition to exhaustive pairwise testing, we used a planned paired contrast (H1 vs the per-HUC mean of H2-H5) to summarize overall operational impact at each lead with a single test and effect estimate.

To characterize latency sensitivity across leads, we aligned observations by (HUC, lead) and computed, for each operational case,
\Delta(lead)
as difference between an operational case to the historical case. For every HUC we estimated a Theil–Sen slope of
\Delta
versus lead (robust to non-normality and outliers), then summarized per-case trends by the median slope and percentile bootstrap 95% CIs. Differences in trend magnitude among H2-H5 were evaluated with a Friedman test on slopes followed, when significant, by Holm-adjusted paired Wilcoxon post-hoc comparisons. Because cross-region samples are independent (distinct HUC sets), Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for region-level comparisons of slopes, with Brunner–Munzel pairwise follow-ups (Holm-adjusted) where applicable.

All analyses were implemented in Python (SciPy/statsmodels); results are presented as medians with 95% CIs and accompanying nonparametric test statistics and adjusted p-values.

S1
Theoretical Details

S1.1
Rainfall-Runoff (RR) Modeling

A Rainfall-Runoff (RR) model is a transfer function,
\mathcal{F}
, that maps input to output signals.
\mathcal{F}
treats the contributing catchment as the system at a lumped, semi-distributed, or distributed scale. In this work, the lumped scale representation is utilized. The state of the system is defined by the catchment stores and fluxes: Stores representing the water content within the surface, sub-surface, and groundwater layers; Fluxes representing the linkages between the stores and out of the system. The dynamic meteorological forcings (
x_{1:T}
, where
1:T
refers to time period of analysis) subject to static catchment characteristics/attributes (
\theta_{attr}
) form the input, and the output is represented by hydrological response timeseries (
y_{1:T}
) that include fluxes like discharge and stores like soil moisture.

The complexities are introduced when we add structural priors to our model, which can take the form of simplified abstractions for bucket-like water content stores and fluxes (Conceptual hydrological models, CHMs), equations utilizing the physical laws and hydrological process understanding (Physically-based hydrological models, PHMs), and a universal function approximator through neural networks (Data-driven hydrological model, DHMs). This summarizes the design landscape of the RR modeling.

A simple mathematical representation for a generic RR model is as follows:

y_{1:T}=\mathcal{F}\left(x_{1:T};\theta\right)

Based on the solver underlying
\mathcal{F}
:

•

CHMs:
\theta\rightarrow\theta_{attr}\cup\theta_{calibrated}

•

PHMs:
\theta\rightarrow\theta_{attr}\cup\theta_{calibrated}\cup\theta_{donor}

•

DHMs:
\theta\rightarrow\theta_{attr}\cup\theta_{trained\;weights}

CHMs usually require a limited number of meteorological input variables and a small set of tunable yet interpretable calibration parameters (
\theta_{calibrated}
). PHMs can capture our most detailed understanding of hydrological processes and water use by incorporating a large number of meteorological input variables, a comprehensive set of parameter maps (
\theta_{maps}
) from
\theta_{attr}
, and tunable parameter maps which can be calibrated where data is available (
\theta_{calibrated}
) or taken from donor catchments where data is scarce (
\theta_{donor}
).

In CHMs and PHMs, the search space of the learnable transfer function is restricted by the underlying processes being considered. Further, the learning through calibration is time and computationally intensive. An answer to these two concerns is using neural networks (NNs) as a structural prior, which leads us to DHMs. As a universal approximator, NNs will capitalize on the unrestricted information mixing through the dense matrix multiplications to learn more generalizable transfer functions, which is popularly known as data synergy in the literature. Though a large variety of NNs have found their use in RR modeling, we restrict our focus in this work to encoder-decoder LSTMs that have repeatedly demonstrated state-of-the-art predictive prowess on large-sample benchmarks such as CAMELS datasets and Caravan.

S1.2
Emulation of PHMs: A GloFAS example

Let
\mathcal{F}_{GloFAS}
denote the underlying operator implemented on the Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) grid, which utilizes the open-source LISFLOOD (a PHM) as its hydrological core. GloFAS (version 4.0) is a PHM that utilizes ECMWF forcings to provide publicly available long-term daily (UTC-0) fields of discharge, total runoff, snow-water equivalent, and soil wetness at fine spatial resolutions of 0.05 degrees on a consistent global grid. A simple mathematical representation of the same is as follows:

y_{1:T}=\mathcal{F}_{GloFAS}\left(x_{1:T};\theta_{maps}\cup\theta_{calibrated}\cup\theta_{donor}\right)

Despite these merits, GloFAS faces challenges due to (a) significant computational demands, (b) over-parameterization, (c) requiring extensive parameter maps (
\sim 100
), (d) expert calibration using local information and donor catchments, and (e) ill-suitedness as a near-real-time operational framework - making it onerous to produce timely, correct and consistent simulations across the basins globally.

We define an emulator
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}
that approximates
\mathcal{F}_{GloFAS}
for discharge at the catchment outlet under the same input conditions, trained against GloFAS discharge rather than heterogeneous in-situ gauge records. Since the models considered here have an encoder-decoder LSTM backbone, this can be described using the following mathematical representation:

\hat{y}_{lag+1:lead}=\mathcal{F}_{\phi}\left(x_{t:t+lag},x_{lag+1:lead};\theta_{attr}\right),t\in[1,T-\left(lag+lead\right)]

Unlike PHMs that operate in persistence, DHMs predict the output per snapshot of input. This leads to the splitting of the input into the lag and lead parts of the snapshot window. Further, the choice of GloFAS discharge as the training target against heterogeneous gauge records yields several practical merits for large-scale cross-region assessment: (a) physically consistent targets with complete spatiotemporal coverage, avoiding gaps and uneven quality in in-situ archives; (b) uniform time standard (UTC-0) across all grid cells, simplifying synchronization for sequence models; (c) mitigation of basin-specific gauge bias/measurement error, enabling fairer comparison across basins with different instruments, water-use practices, and regulation regimes; and (d) minimal data preparation for
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}
, because globally consistent forcings and targets can be ingested directly, allowing us to focus on generalization, robustness under near-real-time data latency, and physics-compatibility.

Conceptually, the emulation of GloFAS discharge is intended to approximate the hydrologic core under input conditions similar to GloFAS simulations. This surrogacy framing preserves the strengths of GloFAS (global consistency, physically credible fields) while enabling systematic tests of robustness to data scarcity and latency, zero-shot transfer, and failure-mode attribution across minimally influenced to heavily managed river systems.

S1.3
Latency-aware RR Modeling

The emulator described above is trained and evaluated in historical mode, i.e., with complete and synchronized inputs, which is different from how operational systems receive data. In practice, each forcing stream arrives on its latency schedule, while also subjected to brief outages: for example, ERA5 offers a preview with
\sim
5-day latency; GPM IMERG provides Early (
\sim
4 hours), Late (
\sim
12-14 hours) and Final (
\sim
3 months) runs; and ECMWF HRES forecasts become available several hours after synoptic times. Development of a realistic operational emulator, therefore, requires explicit latency modeling and not generic missing data handling.

Consider an input snapshot/sample, for a near-real time operational setting, we need to define data availability masks, say
\mathcal{M}
, such that it can mimic realistic data streaming conditions, assuming the end of lag to be the present day. It is important for the model to understand the data availability and not confuse the no-data timestamps as zero or NaN data by utilizing masked inputs, which can be achieved as depicted in the following mathematical representation:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{lag+1:lead}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{\phi}\Big(x_{t:t+lag}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{t:t+lag},\,x_{lag+1:lead}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{lag+1:lead},

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\,\mathcal{M}_{t:t+lag},\,\mathcal{M}_{lag+1:lead}\,;\,\theta_{attr}\Big),t\in[1,T-(lag+lead)]

We have proposed the following four cases that vary in the inputs that are fed to the model’s decoder, spanning the spectrum from pseudo-real time to near-real time, and permit comparison against complete historical inputs. Such an examination allows us to characterize the graceful degradation of performance as data availability shifts from complete to pseudo-real time to near-real time. This reveals not only the average loss of skill but also which regimes (snow, regulated, arid) are most sensitive to asynchrony.

Baseline Case (Historical)
:
This is the baseline case where complete and synchronous inputs are used in both the encoder and decoder.

Case 1 (Filter ERA5 variables to match HRES availability; Pseudo-real time)
:
In this case, the encoder inputs are not masked, and the decoder only inputs those ERA5 variables that are also available through HRES. This examination shows the degradation in performance as less detailed, though of the same quality, inputs are used in the decoder.

Case 2 (GPM-Final; Pseudo-real time)
:
In this case, the encoder inputs are not masked, and the decoder only inputs GPM-Final. Since precipitation is the most critical input for RR modeling, we investigate the performance retained when it is the only input fed into the decoder.

Case 3 (No Meteorological; near-real time)
:
Here, the encoder inputs are masked, and the decoder receives no meteorological inputs. This case examines the deterioration in performance when the decoder can only utilize the latent encoder representations and feedback from the last decoder timestep.

Case 4 (HRES; near-real time)
:
Here, the encoder inputs are masked, and HRES forecasts are fed as decoder inputs. Since it is not always convenient to scrape historical or fetch real-time HRES forecasts, the proposed cases have incorporated both having and not having access to meteorological forecasts.

S1.4
Transfer Learning Scenarios

We formalize transfer from a data-rich, minimally influenced source domain (CAMELS-US or
\mathcal{S}
) to two targets with increasing human influence (HYSETS or
\mathcal{T}_{1}
with mixed human influence, and CAMELS-IND or
\mathcal{T}_{2}
with data-scarcity and heavily influenced). For both historical (
\phi_{hist}
) and operational (
\phi_{oper}
) emulators, we first set up pre-training over the
\mathcal{S}
domain, which can be represented mathematically as follows:

\hat{y}_{lag+1:lead}=\mathcal{F}_{\phi_{hist}}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\left(x_{t:t+lag},x_{lag+1:lead};\theta_{attr}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{lag+1:lead}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{\phi_{oper}}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\Big(x_{t:t+lag}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{t:t+lag},\,x_{lag+1:lead}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{lag+1:lead},

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\,\mathcal{M}_{t:t+lag},\,\mathcal{M}_{lag+1:lead}\,;\,\theta_{attr}\Big)

Scenario 1 (Zero-shot transfer)
:

We utilize
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}
(In general, to denote both
\mathcal{F}_{\phi_{hist}}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\phi_{oper}}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}
) to directly evaluate on
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
without any retraining or fine-tuning.
This quantifies out-of-distribution generalization from minimally influenced basins to (i) mixed human influence (
\mathcal{T}_{1}
) and (ii) strong regulation/data scarcity (
\mathcal{T}_{2}
).

Scenario 2 (Re-training)
:

Next, we retrain from scratch on each target domain to get
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{T}_{2}\right)}
.
This establishes upper-bound performance for domain-specific models under identical temporal splits and allows comparison against the zero-shot baseline and fine-tuned transfer.

Scenario 2 (Fine-tuning using only Target data)
:
We can fine-tune (full model unfreeze under small learning rates) the pre-trained emulator
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}
to yield
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{2}\right)}
.
Fine-tuning for
\mathcal{T}_{\textit{1}}
can be performed with varying record lengths to mimic data-scarcity conditions similar to
\mathcal{T}_{\textit{2}}
.

Scenario 3 (Fine-tuning using both Target and Source data)
:
We can also fine-tune such that we jointly optimize on both source and target to preserve source generalization while adapting to the target. Here, we need to tune
\lambda
to balance retention with adaptation to yield
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}+\lambda\mathcal{T}_{1}\right)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}^{\left(\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}+\lambda\mathcal{T}_{2}\right)}
.

S2
Catchment Data Generation

S2.1
Snap-to-Grid Procedure

Hydrological emulation at large scales requires that each gauge-based catchment be satisfactorily matched to a gridded hydrological model framework, such as GloFAS. Since model outputs are inherently tied to a fixed grid structure, this step is crucial to ensure spatial alignment, maintain hydrological consistency, and enable reliable surrogate modeling.

Each catchment in the CAMELS-US, HYSETS and CAMELS-IND datasets contains metadata for outlet gauge coordinates and a reported upstream contributing area. Additionally, these datasets also provide the delineated catchment boundaries. To associate each catchment outlet with the appropriate GloFAS grid cell, we adopt the method described by Burek and Smilovic (2022), originally designed for calibrating large-scale hydrological models using GRDC stations. This approach minimizes spatial mismatch by evaluating both geometrical overlap and hydrological plausibility.

The procedure involves the following steps:

1.

Search Window
: For each gauging station, a 5×5 window (i.e., 25 neighboring cells) centered on the GloFAS grid cell nearest to the gauge coordinate is defined. Within this search window, two matching criteria are computed for each candidate cell.

2.

Intersection over Union (IoU)
: Measures the spatial congruence between the contribution maskmap of the candidate grid cell and the delineated catchment boundary. IoU is computed as the ratio of the intersection area to the union area of the two polygons.

3.

Upstream Area accordance (UPA)
: Evaluates hydrological consistency by comparing the reported upstream area and the contributing area for the candidate grid cell. UPA is computed as the ratio of the smaller area to the larger area.

4.

Objective function
: The overall fitness of the candidate grid cell is defined using a dual-objective function which ensures both spatial and volumetric agreements. It is computed as Euclidean Distance (ED) using
ED=\sqrt{\left(1-UPA\right)^{2}+\left(1-IoU\right)^{2}}
. The gauge is snapped onto the candidate grid cell with the lowest ED, with preference given to higher UPA in case of multiple candidates with equal minimum ED.

5.

Rejection Criteria
: If the UPA ratio deviates by more than 10
\%
, the catchment is flagged as unmatched and excluded from further analysis. This ensures that only catchments with sufficiently close correspondence in contributing area are used.

6.

Catchment Maskmap
: Once a catchment is successfully snapped to a grid cell, its contributing upstream cells are derived using the local drainage direction (LDD) of GloFAS. These binary maskmaps define the spatial domain over which both dynamic and static variables are prepared.

S3
Curated Dataset

All meteorological inputs from
ERA5
,
GPM IMERG
(Late and Final runs), and
ECMWF HRES
were first regridded to the GloFAS grid using bilinear interpolation from the Python package ’xesmf’. Catchment time series were constructed by area-weighted averaging over the intersection of each catchment with the target grid. Variables were then aggregated to daily resolution using either a sum (fluxes) or a mean (states and intensive variables).

S3.1
Dynamic Inputs

ERA5 reanalysis.

We used precipitation, evaporation and potential evaporation (summed to daily totals); net shortwave and net longwave radiation at the surface (daily totals); 2-m air temperature and dew point, 10-m wind components, and surface pressure (daily means); snowfall rate, snow depth, and snowmelt (daily means); volumetric soil water content (four standard layers; daily means); and total, surface, and subsurface runoff (daily totals).

GPM IMERG.

Gauge-adjusted precipitation from the Late-Run and Final-Run products was used as alternative precipitation forcings (daily totals).

ECMWF HRES.

Deterministic forecasts (10-day lead time) provided daily-accumulated precipitation (24-h total), and daily means of 2-m air temperature, surface pressure, and 10-m wind components.

GloFAS (Target).

For each catchment outlet, discharge was taken from the snapped cell location. The discharge normalized by catchment area (converted to mm d
-1
) was used in the analysis.

S3.2
Static Inputs

Time-invariant descriptors include: (i) catchment morphometry (upstream contributing area; grid-cell area for normalization), (ii) terrain statistics (mean and standard deviation of MERIT-Hydro elevation, slope, aspect, curvature using Python package ’richdem’), (iii) soils (surface and sub-surface fractions of clay, silt, sand, and gravel; soil class encoded as categorical indicators), (iv) land cover (fractions of high and low vegetation; vegetation functional types encoded categorically), (v) hydrographic/channel attributes from GloFAS (bankfull characteristics, channel width and slope, length, roughness; fractional land cover), and (vi) climatic summaries (aridity and moisture indices from energy- and PM-based formulations; long-term means of precipitation and PET; frequency/duration of high- and low-precipitation spells; fraction of snow).

S3.3
Space–Time Encodings

To aid spatial and seasonal generalization, we appended (i) smoothly transformed geographic coordinates (normalized latitude; sine transform of longitude), (ii) daily solar insolation at local solar noon, and (iii) cyclical encodings of day-of-year, week-of-year, and month-of-year (sine transformed).

Model inputs were standardized using the mean and standard deviation computed on the
CAMELS-US training window
(water years 1999–2009). The same scaling parameters were applied unchanged to HYSETS and CAMELS-IND. Unless otherwise noted, all results are reported for the
test window
(water years 2009–2019).

Within the curated datasets used here,

Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}
,
Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}
are observed and simulated streamflow at time
t
, respectively.

•

\bar{Q}^{\text{obs}}
,
\bar{Q}^{\text{sim}}
are mean observed and simulated flows.

•

T
is the total number of time steps.

•

\mathcal{H}
is the set of top 2% high-flow days (used for FHV);
\mathcal{L}
is the set of lowest 30% flow days (used for FLV).

•

r
is the Pearson correlation coefficient.

•

\alpha=\frac{\sigma^{\text{sim}}}{\sigma^{\text{obs}}}
is the ratio of standard deviations.

•

\beta=\frac{\bar{Q}^{\text{sim}}}{\bar{Q}^{\text{obs}}}
is the bias ratio.

•

t^{\text{obs}}_{\text{peak}},t^{\text{sim}}_{\text{peak}}
are the days of the main peak flow event in observation and simulation, respectively.

•

Precision = TP / (TP + FP); Recall = TP / (TP + FN); peaks are counted as TP if they occur within ±1 day of an observed peak above the 80th percentile.

•

\hat{Q}_{R}
and
Q_{R}
are estimated and reference return levels for return period
R
(e.g., 2, 5, or 10 years), obtained via annual maxima.

•

P_{t}
is precipitation at time
t
.

•

\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function (1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise).

Metric Name (Abbreviation)

Formula

Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE)

\displaystyle\text{NSE}=1-\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-Q_{t}^{\text{obs}})^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}-\bar{Q}^{\text{obs}})^{2}}

Kling-Gupta Efficiency (KGE)

\displaystyle\text{KGE}=1-\sqrt{(r-1)^{2}+(\alpha-1)^{2}+(\beta-1)^{2}}

Percent Bias (PBIAS)

\displaystyle\text{PBIAS}=100\cdot\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-Q_{t}^{\text{obs}})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}}

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

\displaystyle\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-Q_{t}^{\text{obs}})^{2}}

Pearson Correlation Coefficient (
r
)

\displaystyle r=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}-\bar{Q}^{\text{obs}})(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-\bar{Q}^{\text{sim}})}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}-\bar{Q}^{\text{obs}})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-\bar{Q}^{\text{sim}})^{2}}}

High Flow Bias (FHV)

\displaystyle\text{FHV}=100\cdot\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{H}}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-Q_{t}^{\text{obs}})}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{H}}Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}}

Low Flow Bias (FLV)

\displaystyle\text{FLV}=100\cdot\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{L}}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}-Q_{t}^{\text{obs}})}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{L}}Q_{t}^{\text{obs}}}

Timing Error (Peak Lag)

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\text{peak}}=t^{\text{sim}}_{\text{peak}}-t^{\text{obs}}_{\text{peak}}

F1 Score of Peak Capture

\displaystyle\text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

Return Level Error (2/5/10-year)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{RL}=\hat{Q}_{R}-Q_{R}

Runoff Ratio (RR)

\displaystyle\text{RR}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}P_{t}}

No-Flow Fraction - Strict

\displaystyle f_{=0}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}=0)

No-Flow Fraction - Threshold

\displaystyle f_{<1}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(Q_{t}^{\text{sim}}<1)
