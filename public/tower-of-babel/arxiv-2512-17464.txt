Solution concepts for a model of visco-elasto-plasticity with slight compressibility
Thomas Eiter ,

Abstract
We study a model for the deformation of a visco-elasto-plastic material that is nearly incompressible. It originates from geophysics, is given in the Eulerian description and combines a Kelvin–Voigt rheology in the spherical part with a Jeffreys-type rheology in the deviatoric part. Despite a constant density, the model allows for non-isochoric deformation and the propagation of pressure waves. An additive decomposition of the strain rate into elastic and inelastic parts leads to an evolution equation for the small elastic strain, which is coupled with an adapted momentum equation. As plasticity is modeled through a non-smooth dissipation potential, we introduce a weak formulation in terms of a variational inequality. Since the well-posedness in such a weak setting is out of reach, we study two possible modifications: the regularization in terms of stress diffusion, and the relaxation of the solvability concept by transition to energy-variational solutions. In both cases, solutions are constructed by the same time-discrete scheme, consisting of solving a saddle-point problem in each time step.

Introduction

The motion of tectonic plates
due to convective processes within the Earth’s mantle
is a well-accepted theory in geology.
One way to describe this deformation of the Earth’s lithosphere,
which happens on large time scales,
is to model rock as
a visco-elasto-plastic material
[
30
,
21
,
23
,
32
]
.
Since there seems to be no reasonable choice for the reference configuration
in this geophysical framework,
a description in terms of the current configuration is natural,
that is, an Eulerian description.
The density varies merely on small scales,
which motivates to treat
it as a constant during the evolution.
However, this simplification would lead to an incompressibility constraint,
which does not allow the model to feature the propagation of longitudinal waves.
To combine both characteristics, we follow an approach first employed by Témam
[
38
,
39
]
,
and consider an additional internal force in the momentum equation.
In summary, the studied model combines this setting of slight compressibility
with a visco-elasto-plastic rheology.

An analogous approach was applied in

where a compressible visco-elastic rheology was studied.
More precisely, (

and coupled with the classical continuity and momentum equations.
The existence of weak solutions was shown after introducing a viscous hyperstress
in the momentum equation.
This improves the regularity of the velocity field,
whereby higher-order spatial regularity of the density and the elastic strain
is inherited from the initial values.

Therefore, the existence of weak solutions to (

also seems to require a modification

Here we follow the idea

To regularize a viscoelastic flow in terms of stress diffusion
is a nowadays common approach
[
7
,
4
,
16
,
9
]
,
and it can be derived from thermodynamic considerations

]
.

One regards the regularized system (
1.3
)
for small
\gamma>0

as an approximation of the original system (
1.1
).
Therefore, it is natural to ask
what happens to solutions to (
1.3
) as
\gamma\to 0
.
Suitable a priori bounds indicate the convergence towards a limit object,
at least along a subsequence,
and the question is in which way
this limit is connected to the original problem (
1.1
).
Usually, the limit is not a weak solution,
but one arrives at a solution in a further generalized sense.

The study of such generalized solvability concepts,
which further relax the notion of weak solutions,
is nowadays quite common, in particular in fluid dynamics.
For instance,
DiPerna and Majda
[
11
]
introduced
the concept of
measure-valued solutions
to the incompressible Euler equations.
A different approach goes back to P.-L. Lions
[
27
, Sec. 4.4]
,
who defined
dissipative solutions
to the Euler equations
in terms of a relative energy inequality.
In both cases,
those solutions were constructed as the inviscid limit of weak solutions
to the Navier–Stokes equations.
In
[
15
]
and
[
9
]
,
the approach by Lions was adapted to the aforementioned systems of
incompressible visco-elasto-plasticity with one and two phases, respectively,
There, the solvability concept was based on a relative energy inequality,
and solutions were constructed as limits of weak solutions to
the regularized system.
While such solutions are also called dissipative solutions in
[
9
]
,
the name
energy-variational solutions
was used in
[
15
]
.
However, the latter notion of solution
has been further refined in various works
since then
[
17
,
1
,
26
,
24
,
25
,
19
,
18
]
.
We follow this approach and introduce a corresponding notion of energy-variational solutions to problem (
1.1
),
see Subsect.
3.3
,
where we adapt this refined solvability concept to our framework.

To show the existence of energy-variational solutions to (
1.1
),
we could proceed similarly to
[
15
,
9
]

and pass to the limit
\gamma\to 0
with weak solutions to (
1.3
).
Instead, we follow the approach
from
[
17
,
1
]

and obtain solutions
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
to (
1.1
)
as the limit
N\to\infty

of time-discrete approximate solutions
(\boldsymbol{v}_{N},\mathbb{E}_{N})
,
which are constructed by iteratively solving
a saddle-point problem at each time step.
To this end, one introduces a certain
regularity weight

that gives sufficient convexity properties to render the saddle-point problem solvable
and to pass to the limit by weak convergence.
As it was observed in
[
18
]
,
both steps are, to some extent, independent of each other,
which allows to use two different regularity weights.

In this article, we apply this iterative scheme
for the regularized and the original system simultaneously.
Indeed, we use the same scheme to construct approximate solutions
for
\gamma=0
and for
\gamma>0
.
On the one hand,
the passage to the time-continuous limit
only yields a weak solution in the case
\gamma>0

due to the availability of stronger a priori estimate.
On the other hand,
the existence of energy-variational solutions
can be shown under more general assumptions on
\mathcal{P}
and
\varphi
.
In particular, can allow for non-quadratic
\varphi

if
\gamma=0
.

To derive the model (

2.1
Visco-elasto-plastic rheology

The main motivation for the model (
1.1
)
arises from geophysics,
where related models have been used to study
the rock deformation in the Earth’s lithosphere
[
30
,
21
,
32
]
.
In this context, the deformation is
considered on very long time scales
and with very slow speeds of only millimeters per year.
Due to the lack of a plausible reference configuration,
an Eulerian description is preferred
over a Lagrangian description
[
37
]
.
Therefore, we consider the continuity equation and the momentum equation
given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\rho(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\dv(\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathbb{T})+\bf

(2.2)

for the density
\rho
, the Eulerian velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}
and an external force ,
where the total stress is the sum of two parts
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})
and
\mathbb{T}
.
Here,
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})
is a classical linear viscous stress tensor.
A standard choice for isotropic viscous behavior
would be the Newtonian stress tensor

\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})=2\mu_{1}\Big(\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{1}{3}\dv\boldsymbol{v}\mathbb{I}\Big)+\mu_{2}\dv\boldsymbol{v}\mathbb{I}=2\mu_{1}\dev\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})+3\mu_{2}\sph\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}),

where

denote shear and bulk viscosity coefficients.

To describe the deformation of tectonic plates, the development of faults,
and the occurrence of aseismic creep,
such geophysical models combine viscous, elastic and plastic effects
[

,

,
23
,

,
2

,

]

which we incorporate in terms of the stress tensor

We consider different rheologies for volumetric and the shear deformation.
While the spherical stress
\sph\mathbb{T}=\frac{1}{3}(\tr\mathbb{T})\mathbb{I}
is purely elastic,
the deviatoric part
\dev\mathbb{T}
is subject to
a Maxwell-type visco-elasto-plastic rheology.
We consider the case of small strain and
employ the Green–Naghdi-type additive decomposition into
the elastic strain
\mathbb{E}
and the inelastic strain
\mathbb{P}

For the strain rate
\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\top})
,
this gives

\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathring{\mathbb{E}}+\mathring{\mathbb{P}}
.
Here, the
\mathring{(\cdot)}
denotes an objective tensor rate,
which we discuss below, see (

).

The elastic strain
\mathbb{E}
is associated with the stress
\mathbb{T}

through a stored energy potential
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}\to[0,\infty)

such that

\mathbb{T}=D\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\mathbb{I}.

(2.4)

Here the second term is an ‘internal’ pressure that
is due to the transport of the elastic energy.
By allowing for a non-quadratic stored energy

which is relevant in the geophysical context.
Note that this does not contradict the above assumption of small strain,

37

The inelastic strain

is assumed to be purely isochoric
and governed by the stress-strain relation

,
where

Invoking the dual potential

Mathematically, it can be incorporated in

with elastic bulk modulus
K>0
and elastic shear modulus
G>0
,
and the (dual) dissipation potential
\mathcal{P}
is given by (
1.2
).
Moreover, the present model generalizes the large-deformation
small-strain viscoelastic model from
[
37
]

by including plastic effects.

In summary,
we consider a Kelvin–Voigt rheology
in the volumetric/spherical part,
combined with a Jeffreys (or anti-Zener) rheology
enriched with plasticity
in the isochoric/deviatoric part.
In particular, the model allows for isochoric creep
and isochoric plastic deformation.

2.2
Slightly compressible approximation

In many situations,
like the flow of water or the deformation of rock,
the material compression is negligible compared to other effects during the evolution.
On the one hand, this motivates to simplify the model
by directly imposing incompressibility as an assumption,
and to work with the Navier–Stokes equations
for incompressible flow.
Such models were analytically studied
in
[
16
,
15
,
9
]
.
On the other hand, such a restriction to divergence-free velocity fields
would not allow for the propagation of pressure waves,
corresponding to sound waves or longitudinal seismic waves.

Therefore, we consider a model for slight compressibility,
which is an approximation of (
2.1
), (
2.2
) that
assumes a constant density
without restricting to the divergence-free velocities.
Clearly, this contradicts the continuity equation (
2.1
),
and simply omitting it would destroy the energetic structure of the evolution.
In particular, the kinetic energy-dissipation balance

\partial_{t}\Big(\frac{\rho|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}{2}\Big)+\dv\Big(\frac{\rho|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\Big)+\Sigma:\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\dv(\Sigma^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})=\bf\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\hskip 22.99988pt\Sigma=\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathbb{T},

(2.10)

would no longer apply.
To compensate this, one introduces an additional internal force in the momentum balance (
2.2
).
More precisely, for a homogeneous material with constant density
\rho
,
we consider

\rho(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})=\dv(\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathbb{T})-\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}+\bf.

(2.11)

This approach goes back to Témam
[
38
,
39
]
,
see also
[
40
, Ch. III, Sect. 8]
.
The new term ensures that the kinetic energy
is transported properly,
and that multiplying (
2.11
) with
\boldsymbol{v}

leads to the energy-dissipation balance (
2.10
).
Note that there exist other possible modifications
of the momentum balance
that preserve (
2.10
),
compare
[
35
, Remark 1]
.
The choice (
2.11
)
has the advantage
that one arrives at the model for incompressible flow by setting
\dv\boldsymbol{v}=0
.
In several works, a combination of (
2.11
)
with an evolution equation for the pressure
is used to approximate the incompressible Navier–Stokes flow
numerically or
analytically
[
8
,
10
,
33
,
13
,
12
,
5
]
.
For further discussion and references of such slightly compressible approximations,
we refer to the recent article
[
35
]
.

Combining the adapted momentum equation (
2.11
)
with the visco-elasto-plastic stress-strain relations (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)
and suitable initial and boundary conditions,
we finally arrive at the full continuum model (
1.1
).
Observe that (
1.1
) generalizes the model for
semi-compressible
viscous flow from
[
35
]
,
where a pure spherical stress
\mathbb{T}=-(\pi+\frac{\pi^{2}}{2K})\mathbb{I}
with elastic bulk modulus
K>0
and pressure
\pi
is considered, which are subject to

\frac{1}{K}\partial_{t}\pi+\frac{1}{K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\pi+\dv\boldsymbol{v}=0.

Indeed, this particular case can be obtained from (
2.11
), (
2.4
), (
2.5
)
by choosing
\varphi
as in (
2.7
) with
G=0

and by setting
\pi=-\frac{1}{3}\tr(\mathbb{T})
.

2.3
Energy dissipation

To get further insight into the energetics of the system (
1.1
),
we consider
\Xi\in\partial\mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})
and multiply (
1.1c
)
with
D\varphi(\mathbb{E})
.
Using (
2.8
) and integration by parts,
we calculate

\displaystyle 0=\big(\mathring{\mathbb{E}}+\Xi-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\big):D\varphi(\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\Xi:D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}):D\varphi(\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\dv\big(\varphi(\mathbb{E})\boldsymbol{v}\big)+\Xi:\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{T},

where we used (
1.1b
) and that
\tr\Xi=0
.
Due to the boundary conditions (
1.1d
) and (
1.1e
),
combining this identity with (
2.10
)
and integrating over
\Omega
,
we conclude

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}{2}+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})+\Xi:\mathbb{T}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\bf\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.12)

for
\Xi\in\partial\mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})
.
This shows that the total energy
is given by

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.13)

as the sum of the kinetic energy and the stored elastic energy.
It is dissipated
by the Newtonian viscous stress
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})

and the deviatoric stress associated with the (dual) dissipation potential
\mathcal{P}
,
and it is subject to the power of the external force .

As
\Xi\in\partial P(\dev\mathbb{T})
, we have
\Xi=\dev\Xi
,
and we can use the Fenchel equivalence
to write
\int_{\Omega}\Xi:\mathbb{T}\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})+\mathcal{P}^{*}(\Xi)
.
Employing this identity in (
2.12
)
and omitting
\mathcal{P}^{*}(\Xi)\geq 0
, which follows from
\mathcal{P}(0)=0
,
leads to the energy-dissipation inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})\leq\int_{\Omega}\bf\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.14)

Although this inequality is not equivalent to (
2.12
),
it has the advantage that the subdifferential element

\Xi\in\partial P(\dev\mathbb{T})
is not present in (
2.14
).
Moreover, a weak form of (
2.14
)
is directly included in the generalized solution concepts discussed in this article.

2.4
Towards a weak formulation

To derive a weak formulation of (
1.1
),
we follow the approach from
[
34
,
16
,
15
]
.
Instead of simply testing (
1.1c
) and integrating,
we express the subdifferential in terms of the inequality

\Xi\in\partial\mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})\iff\forall\Psi:\ \mathcal{P}(\dev\mathbb{T})-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)\leq\int_{\Omega}\Xi:(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\Psi
belongs to a suitable class of
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
-valued
test functions.
Here we used
\dev\mathbb{T}=\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E})

and that
\mathcal{P}
is defined on deviatoric tensors,
so that
\tr\Xi=0
.
With this characterization,
we can express (
1.1c
) as

\int_{\Omega}\mathring{\mathbb{E}}{\,:\,}D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}){\,:\,}D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))-\int_{\Omega}\mathring{\mathbb{E}}{\,:\,}\Psi-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}){\,:\,}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)\leq 0.

This corresponds to formally testing (
2.5
) with
D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi
.
We proceed similarly with the momentum equation (
1.1a
)
and multiply it with
\boldsymbol{v}-\psi
for a test function
\psi
and integrate.
Adding up both equations, integrating over a time interval
(s,t)
for
s<t

and using integration by parts,
where we make use of the boundary conditions (

) and (

),
we arrive at

\displaystyle\Big[\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})-\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi+\mathbb{E}:\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\bf\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

(2.15)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi)+\rho(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}):\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}-\big(\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\big):\mathbb{D}(\psi)-\varphi(\mathbb{E})\dv\psi+\bf\cdot\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}:\partial_{t}\Psi+(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}:\Psi+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Psi:\mathbb{E}-(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\leq 0

for all test functions
\psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})

and
\Psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}_{\sym}^{3\times 3})

with
\psi=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
and

\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}
.
The calculations are similar to the derivation of (
2.12
),
where we tested (
1.1a
) and (
1.1c
)
with
\boldsymbol{v}
and
D\varphi(\mathbb{E})
, respectively.
If we ignore
\mathcal{P}
, then (
2.15
)
is merely the sum of an energy inequality
and the standard weak formulations of (
1.1a
) and (
1.1c
).
Observe that for

,
we rediscover a time-integrated version of the energy-inequality (
2.14
).
Moreover, the initial conditions (

) are satisfied in a weak sense
if (

) holds for

with

and

.

For a reformulation of (

.

3
The generalized solution concepts

We first collect the main assumptions
to develop the theory.
Subsequently, we introduce the concepts of
weak solutions to (
1.3
)
and of energy-variational solutions to (
1.1
),
and we state the main results on
the global-in-time existence of such solutions.

3.1
General assumptions

To perform a rigorous existence analysis, we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
,
and let
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega
be measurable.
If
\Gamma_{D}
has vanishing surface measure, assume in addition that
there is no rigid motion that leaves
\Omega
invariant.

(A2)

Let
\bf\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.

(A3)

Let the linear viscous stress
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})
be given by
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})]

for a linear mapping
\mathbb{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}

such that there exists
\mu_{*}>0
with

\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{A}]:\mathbb{A}\geq\mu_{*}|\mathbb{A}|^{2}
.

(A4)

Let
\mathcal{P}\colon L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0})\to[0,\infty]
be nonnegative,
convex and lower semicontinuous, and let
\mathcal{P}(0)=0
.

(A5)

Let
\varphi\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym};[0,\infty))
be a nonnegative, convex function
such that

(i)

\varphi
is strongly convex, that is, there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\varphi-\frac{\kappa}{2}|\cdot|^{2}
is convex,

(ii)

\varphi
has at least quadratic growth, that is,
there exists
\beta>0
such that it holds
1+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\geq\beta|\mathbb{E}|^{2}

for all
\mathbb{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
,

(iii)

there exists
M>0
such that
|D^{3}\varphi(\mathbb{E})|\leq M
for all
\mathbb{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
,

(iv)

it holds
D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}\,D\varphi(\mathbb{E})
for all
\mathbb{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
.

(A6)

Let the function
\mathcal{P}\circ[\dev D\varphi]\colon L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})\to[0,\infty]
,
given by

\mathbb{E}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))
, be convex and lower semicontinuous.

While these assumptions are sufficient to show the existence of energy-variational solutions to the original system (
1.1
),
we need to restrict to quadratic stored energy potentials
\varphi

and to require an additional property of
\mathcal{P}

to obtain weak solutions for the regularized system (
1.3
):

(A5’)

Let
\varphi
be quadratic and strictly convex, that is,

\varphi(\mathbb{E})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{K}[\mathbb{E}]\mathbin{:}\mathbb{E}

for some linear function

\mathbb{K}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
that satisfies

\mathbb{K}[\mathbb{A}]\mathbin{:}\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{K}[\mathbb{B}]\mathbin{:}\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{K}[\mathbb{A}]\mathbin{:}\mathbb{A}\geq\kappa{\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert}^{2}

for all
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}
and some
\kappa>0
.

(A6’)

There exists
R_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{P}(\Psi)<\infty

for all
\Psi\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathbb{E}}_{\gamma}
with
\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}^{\mathbb{E}}}\leq R_{0}
.

The additional geometric condition in
(A1)

has to be understood in combination with
(A3)
.
Both assumptions together imply the Korn-type inequality

\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\mu\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.1)

for some constant
\mu>0
and
all
\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})

with
\boldsymbol{v}=0
on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Indeed, by
(A3)
we ensure that

\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathbb{L}[\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})]:\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq\mu_{*}|\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})|^{2},

which allows to conclude (
3.1
) by a classical compactness argument.
However, to this end,
we have to exclude that
\boldsymbol{v}
is a rigid motion,
which results from the boundary conditions if
(A1)
holds.

The bound on
D^{3}\varphi
in
(A5)
(A5)(iii)

will be used at several points in the argument.
Firstly, it ensures that
D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})

for
\mathbb{E}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})
by Taylor’s theorem.
Therefore, the term
D\varphi(\mathbb{E}):\mathbb{D}(\psi)
in the weak formulation is integrable,
and this explains the domain of
\mathcal{P}
in
(A4)
.
Moreover,
we can ‘convexify’ this term
with the help of the energy,
which will be crucial for the proposed construction of approximate solutions
and for the derivation of energy-variational solutions,
see Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
5.4
below.

Assumption
(A5)
(A5)(iv)
means that the elastic strain
\mathbb{E}
commutes with the stress
\mathbb{T}=D\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\mathbb{I}
.
In particular, this is the case for an isotropic material,
which comes along with
\varphi(\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{Q}^{\top})=\varphi(\mathbb{E})

for all rotational matrices

\mathbb{Q}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
.
Indeed,
\varphi
can be expressed as a function of the principal
invariants of
\mathbb{E}
in this case, so that

D\varphi(\mathbb{E})=a\mathbb{I}+b\mathbb{E}+c\mathbb{E}^{2}
for scalars
a,b,c

that are functions of the principal invariants of
\mathbb{E}
,
compare
[
22
, Sect. 37]
.

Clearly, the potential from (
1.2
)
modeling visco-plasticity satisfies
(A4)
.
Note that
we do not impose any growth assumptions on
\mathcal{P}
in
(A4)
,
and we shall not use
\mathcal{P}
to derive additional bounds on the solutions.
Therefore, even the choice
\mathcal{P}=0
is possible,
which would lead to pure elastic strain
and reduce (
1.1
) to a model of Kelvin–Voigt type.

Assumption
(A6)
concerns the interplay between the
energy potential
\varphi
and the dissipation potential
\mathcal{P}
.
Notice that in the case of a quadratic stored elastic energy
\varphi
as in
(A5’)
,
which satisfies
(A5)
trivially,
assumption
(A6)
is a direct consequence of
(A4)
.
The most relevant example for such a quadratic
\varphi
is
given in (
2.9
),
which allows for different elastic shear and bulk modulus.
We shall only use the strict assumption
(A5’)

if
\gamma>0
,
where it renders the stress diffusion a linear term.

The technical assumption
(A6’)
is used
to derive a suitable incremental bound for the elastic strain
\mathbb{E}

in the case
\gamma>0
, see Lemma
4.6
below.
This estimate will lead to strong convergence of the approximate sequence for
\mathbb{E}
,
which allows to pass to the limit in a weak formulation.
As strong convergence cannot be expected for
\gamma=0
,
this estimate is not necessary in this case,
and the limit object is identified as an energy-variational solution.

3.2
Weak solutions to the system with stress diffusion

We introduce the notion of weak solutions to the regularized system (

)
with
\gamma>0
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})

such that
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{0})<\infty
.
We call
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})),

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbb{E})\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),\quad D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})),

if
\boldsymbol{v}=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}

For the original system (

Instead, we study energy-variational solutions.
For their definition, we do not follow

from
[

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})

such that
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{0})<\infty
.
We call a triple
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E},E)

an energy-variational solution

to (
1.1
)
if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})),

\displaystyle E\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T]),

if
\boldsymbol{v}=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
,
if
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}
,
if
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\leq E
a.e. in
(0,T)
,
and if there exists a function
\mathcal{K}\colon C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})\to[0,\infty)

such that

\displaystyle\Big[E-\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi+\mathbb{E}:\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\bf\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi)+\rho(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}):\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}-\big(\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\big):\mathbb{D}(\psi)-\varphi(\mathbb{E})\dv\psi+\bf\cdot\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}:\partial_{t}\Psi+(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}:\Psi+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Psi:\mathbb{E}-(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)\big(E-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

for a.a.
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t

and all
\psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})

and
\Psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}_{\sym}^{3\times 3})

such that
\psi=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
and

\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
(0,T)\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}
.

Compared to the weak formulation (
2.15
),
there are two major differences to (
3.5
).
Firstly, there occurs the new energy variable
E
, which dominates
the state-dependent energy
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
and
replaces it on the left-hand side of the inequality.
Secondly, there is a non-zero term on the right-hand side,
composed of the product of the
energy defect

E-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\geq 0

and the
regularity weight

\mathcal{K}
.
In other words, the energy-variational formulation (
3.5
)
coincides with the weak formulation (
2.15
)
up to an error that is controlled by the energy defect.
In particular, if

, then
both formulations coincide,
so that every weak solution is also an energy-variational solution.

Invoking Lemma
2.1
,
we obtain directly from the definition that
the state variables
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\mathbb{E}
of an energy-variational solution are weakly* continuous with respect to time.
Here, duality is to be understood with respect to
the space of test functions.
Moreover,
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
attains the initial values in this sense,
compare Remark
2.2
.

Clearly, the set of energy-variational solutions depends on
\mathcal{K}
,
and it becomes larger if we increase
\mathcal{K}
.
It thus seems reasonable find
\mathcal{K}

as small as possible such that solutions exist.
To this end,
we consider the regularity weight
defined by

\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)=\frac{M}{\kappa}\lVert\nabla\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\lVert\dv\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\frac{5}{\kappa\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(3.6)

As we shall see in Lemma
5.4
below,
this choice is appropriate to allow for a limit passage in (
3.5
).
Note that for the construction of approximate solutions
in Section
4
,
we will use a different regularity weight
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}

that plays a similar role and yields suitably convexity properties,
see Lemma
4.1
below.

3.4
Functional framework

We introduce some abbreviations.
For
\gamma\geq 0
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}\mid\psi,\Psi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi)+\rho(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}):\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}:\Psi+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Psi:\mathbb{E}-(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}):\Psi-\gamma\nabla[D\varphi(\mathbb{E})]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox to4.30554pt{\hbox{$\displaystyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\displaystyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\displaystyle.$}}}{\vbox to4.30554pt{\hbox{$\textstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\textstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\textstyle.$}}}{\vbox to3.01389pt{\hbox{$\scriptstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\scriptstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\scriptstyle.$}}}{\vbox to2.15277pt{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}\vss\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}\nabla\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x,

Moreover, we set
\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
,
which is independent of
\mathbb{E}
.
As we only consider quadratic elastic energies as in
(A5’)
if
\gamma>0
,
the functional
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
is actually given by

\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})+\gamma{\bigl\lvert\nabla\mathbb{K}[\mathbb{E}]\big\rvert}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

and thus quadratic and strictly convex.
We further introduce the function spaces

\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{X}^{\boldsymbol{v}}\times\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}^{\mathbb{E}},\qquad\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}^{\boldsymbol{v}}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathbb{E}}

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle={\bigl\{\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}},\ \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}=0\bigr\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{E}}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})&\text{if }\gamma=0,\\
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})&\text{if }\gamma>0,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle={\bigl\{\psi\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})\,\big|\,\psi=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{D},\ \psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{N}\bigr\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle=C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym}).

3.5
Consistency

From the derivation of the solution concepts,
we have seen that strong solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.3
)
with sufficient regularity
are generalized solutions in the sense of Definition
3.1

and Definition
3.4
, respectively.
It is a natural question if also the converse it true,
that is if generalized solutions with increased regularity
satisfy the problem in the classical sense.

We first show this consistency property for weak solutions to (
1.3
).
To this end, we assume

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\forall\,\mathbb{A}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0})\ \ \forall\,\mathbb{G}\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0})\ \ \exists\,(\Phi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0}):

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}\big|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbb{G},\qquad\Phi_{j}\mathrel{\rightharpoonup}\mathbb{A}\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0}),\qquad\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{j})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{A})\text{ as }j\to\infty.

One readily shows that this approximation property
is satisfied for integral functionals of the form

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{A})=\int_{\Omega}P(\mathbb{A}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

if
P\colon\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0}\to[0,\infty]
is a convex function
with
P(0)=0
and with a suitable upper bound.
In particular, the visco-plastic dissipation potential (
1.2
)
is suitable.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
satisfy (
3.8
).
Let
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
be a weak solution to (
1.3
)
in the sense of Definition
3.1
.
Assume

\boldsymbol{v}\in C^{1}([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})),\qquad\mathbb{E}\in C^{1}([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})).

Then
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
is a strong solution, that is,
the system (
1.3
) is satisfied pointwise.

Proof.

We use the abbreviations introduced in (
3.7
).
Due to the regularity assumptions, we can write

\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})=\int_{\Omega}\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}:D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using integration by parts in time,
we can thus reformulate (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}:D\varphi(\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})+\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))-\mathcal{B}(\tau;\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}-\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi-\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}:\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}\mid\psi,\Psi)-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)+\mathcal{B}(\tau;\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq 0

for all
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
,
and
for all test functions

(\psi,\Psi)\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
.
In particular, we can omit the time integrals and consider
time-independent test functions
(\psi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{Y}
.
We set
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v})+D\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\mathbb{I}
.
Employing integration by parts
and, in some sense, reverting the algebra that led to the weak formulation (
2.15
),
we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}-\dv\Sigma-\bf\Big)\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{v}-\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathring{\mathbb{E}}-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\gamma\Delta[D\varphi(\mathbb{E})]\Big):\big(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{N}}\Sigma\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{v}-\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla[D\varphi(\mathbb{E})]:\big(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)-\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))

in
(0,T)
for all
(\psi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{Y}
.
On the one hand, the choice
\Psi=D\varphi(\mathbb{E})
yields

\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}-\dv\Sigma-\bf\Big)\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{v}-\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{N}}\Sigma\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{v}-\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq 0

for all
\psi\in\mathcal{Y}^{\boldsymbol{v}}
.
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
this implies the momentum equation (
1.3a
)
and the boundary conditions (
1.3e
)
as
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
.
On the other hand, the choice
\psi=\boldsymbol{v}
leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathring{\mathbb{E}}-\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})-\gamma\Delta[D\varphi(\mathbb{E})]\Big):\big(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla[D\varphi(\mathbb{E})]:\big(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\Psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)-\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))

for all
\Psi\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathbb{E}}=C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})
.
If we assume
\Psi=D\varphi(\mathbb{E})
on
\partial\Omega
,
the boundary integral vanishes,
and by approximation property (
3.8
),
the resulting inequality extends to all
\Psi\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym})
,
which gives (
1.3c
)
by the definition of the convex subdifferential.
A combination with the previous inequality further yields (
1.3f
).
The other boundary conditions are satisfied by assumption.
Since the initial values are attained in a weak* sense
for any weak solution,
compare Remark
2.2
,
the assumed regularity yields (
1.3g
)
pointwise.
In summary, we have shown that
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
is a classical solution to (
1.3
).
∎

An analogous statement holds for energy-variational solutions to (

Here the approximation property (

) can be relaxed to

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\forall\,\mathbb{A}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0})\ \ \exists\,(\Phi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0}):

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}\mathrel{\rightharpoonup}\mathbb{A}\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\sym,0}),\qquad\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{j})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{A})\text{ as }j\to\infty.

Observe that the consistency result is independent of the regularity weight

.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
satisfy (

).
Let
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E},E)
be an energy-variational solution to (

)

Assume

Then

, and

is a strong solution, that is,

Proof.

In virtue of the regularity assumptions, we can use

a test function in the energy-variational formulation (

A straightforward calculation leads to

\Big[E-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\Big]\Big|_{s}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\leq\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v},D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))\big(E-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

for all a.a.

By Gronwall’s inequality, this implies

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\gamma\geq 0

–

\gamma>0

the functional
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}\mid\psi,\Psi)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1},\mathbb{E}^{n-1})+\tau\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})-\tau\mathcal{B}^{n}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}{\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\rvert}^{2}+\beta|\mathbb{E}|^{2}-1\,\mathrm{d}x-\tau\lVert\bf^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where we employed
(A5)
(A5)(ii)

see
[
3

we thus obtain the existence of

The ensure the inequality (

\displaystyle-\phi(0)\Big[\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{0})-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\psi(0)+\mathbb{E}_{0}:\Psi(0)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\leq 0

for all
(\psi,\Psi)\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{Y})

and
\phi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T))
with
\phi\geq 0
.
In virtue of Lemma
2.1
,
this means that
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})
is a weak solution in the sense of Defintion
3.1
,
compare Remark
2.2

∎

5.3
Limit passage for the original system

Now we consider the case
\gamma=0
,
where we want to obtain an energy-variational solution in the sense of
Definition
3.4
.
As
we have neither strong convergence of
\mathbb{E}

nor the identity
E=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

for
\gamma=0
,
we make use of the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
from (
3.6
)
to pass to the limit in (
4.13
) by weak lower semicontinuity.

We define
E_{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N},\mathbb{E}_{N})
in
(0,T)

and
E_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{0})
,
and we write (
4.13
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{T}-\partial_{t}\widehat{\phi}_{N}(t)\Big[E_{N}(t)-\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}_{N}(t)\cdot\psi(t+\tau)+\mathbb{E}_{N}(t):\Psi(t+\tau)\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{\tau}^{T}\phi_{N}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}_{N}\cdot\delta_{\tau}\psi+\mathbb{E}_{N}:\delta_{\tau}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{N}))-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{N}\mathcal{B}_{N}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N}-\psi)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{N}\Big(\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N})+\mathcal{N}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N},\mathbb{E}_{N}\mid\psi,\Psi)+\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)\big(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N},\mathbb{E}_{N})-E_{N}\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\phi(0)\Big[E_{0}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)+\mathbb{E}_{0}:\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\leq 0.

Observe that in the forth line,
we introduced a term that equals zero and is related to
\mathcal{K}
.
Taking
\mathcal{K}
as in (
3.6
)
ensures convexity of certain terms,
so that we can pass to the limit
N\to\infty
in inequality (
5.9
)
by lower semicontinuity.
More precisely, we pass to the limit in the second part of this new term
by using the weak* convergence of
(E_{N})
in
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
,
while the first term combines with the certain terms
to ensure weak lower semicontinuity
by the following convexity property.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be as in (
3.6
). For each
(\psi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{Y}
, the functional

\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}\colon\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-(D\varphi(\mathbb{E})+\varphi(\mathbb{E})\mathbb{I}):\nabla\psi+(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}:\Psi-(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}):\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

is convex and lower semicontinuous, and thus weakly lower semicontinous.

Proof.

We split the functional
\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}

into several parts.
Firstly, from
(A5)
we obtain the
convexity of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\mapsto-\varphi(\mathbb{E})\dv\psi+\lVert\dv\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\varphi(\mathbb{E})=\big(\lVert\dv\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}-\dv\psi\big)\varphi(\mathbb{E}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\mapsto-D\varphi(\mathbb{E}):\nabla\psi+\frac{M}{\kappa}\lVert\nabla\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\varphi(\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle=-D\varphi(\mathbb{E}):\nabla\psi+\frac{M}{\kappa}\lVert\nabla\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\frac{\kappa}{2}|\mathbb{E}|^{2}+\frac{M}{\kappa}\lVert\nabla\psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big(\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\frac{\kappa}{2}|\mathbb{E}|^{2}\Big),

which implies convexity and lower semicontinuity of the associated integral functional.
Secondly, by Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\dv\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}:\Psi+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{1}{\kappa\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\,\varphi(\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle\geq-{\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rvert}\,{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}\,\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\mu}{2}{\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{\kappa\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big(\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\frac{\kappa}{2}{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}^{2}\Big)\geq 0,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle\mapsto-(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathbb{E}):\Psi+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}):\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{4}{\kappa\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\,\varphi(\mathbb{E})

\displaystyle\geq-2{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}\,{\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rvert}\,\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\mu}{2}{\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rvert}^{2}+\frac{2}{\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}^{2}+\frac{4}{\kappa\mu}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\Big(\varphi(\mathbb{E})-\frac{\kappa}{2}{\lvert\mathbb{E}\rvert}^{2}\Big)\geq 0.

Therefore, both functions are quadratic and nonnegative,
and thus convex.
This implies the weak lower semicontinuity of the associated integral functional.
Summing up and recalling the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}

from (
3.7
)
with
\gamma=0
,
wee conclude that
\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}

is also convex and lower semicontinuous.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

As
\varphi
is non-negative, the function

\mathbb{E}\mapsto-\varphi(\mathbb{E})\dv\psi+\lVert\max{\{\dv\psi,0\}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\varphi(\mathbb{E})

is also convex. Therefore, one could improve
\mathcal{K}
from (
3.6
)
by taking into account only the non-negative part of
\dv\psi
.
Such an optimized regularity weight
would be in line with the choices considered in
[
17
,
19
]

for instance.
However, a similar improvement seems to be unavailable for the other terms in (
3.6
),
which is why we omit it here.

We can now finalize the proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We use the convergence properties established in Lemma
5.1

(for
\gamma=0
)
and Lemma
5.3

in order to pass to the limit in (
5.9
).
The first, third and fifth line in (
5.9
)
are linear in
\boldsymbol{v}_{N}
and
\mathbb{E}_{N}
,
and we can directly pass to the limit.
For the second line of (
5.9
),
we combine a similar argument with assumption
(A6)
,
which yields

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\int_{\tau}^{T}\phi_{N}\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{N}))\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{T}\phi\,\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))\,\mathrm{d}t.

To pass to the limit in the forth line of inequality (
5.9
),
we first use the strong convergence from (
5.3
).
A combination with Lemma
5.3

and the weak and weak* convergence from (
5.2
) and (
5.4
)
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{N}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v}_{N})(\boldsymbol{v}_{N}\cdot\psi)+\rho(\boldsymbol{v}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{N}){:}\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{N}\int_{\Omega}-\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N}){:}\mathbb{D}(\psi)+\boldsymbol{v}_{N}\cdot\nabla\Psi{:}\mathbb{E}_{N}+\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N}){:}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}(\dv\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi)+\rho(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}){:}\nabla\psi-\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{v}){:}\mathbb{D}(\psi)+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Psi{:}\mathbb{E}+\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{v}){:}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

Combining the convexity and lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}

by Lemma
5.4

with Fatou’s lemma,
we also deduce weak lower semicontinuity of the time-integrated functional,
which implies

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{N}\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}(\boldsymbol{v}_{N},\mathbb{E}_{N})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{T}\phi\,\mathcal{J}_{(\psi,\Psi)}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Moreover,
from (
5.5
) we conclude

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}-\phi_{N}\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)E_{N}=\int_{0}^{T}-\phi\,\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)E.

Recalling the definition of
and
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}
from (
3.7
) (with
\gamma=0
),
we can thus pass to the limit inferior in the forth line of (
5.9
).
We thus obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}-\partial_{t}\phi\Big[E-\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\psi+\mathbb{E}:\Psi\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.10)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\Big(\int_{\Omega}\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{t}\psi+\mathbb{E}:\partial_{t}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{P}(\dev D\varphi(\mathbb{E}))-\mathcal{P}(\dev\Psi)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\Big(-\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{v}-\psi)+\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{N}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E}\mid\psi,\Psi)+\mathcal{K}(\psi,\Psi)\big(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E})-E\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\phi(0)\Big[E_{0}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\psi(0)+\mathbb{E}_{0}:\Psi(0)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big]\leq 0.

Employing Lemma
2.1
,
we deduce that
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{E},E)
is an energy-variational solution
in the sense of Definition
3.4

with
E(0)=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{0})
.
∎
