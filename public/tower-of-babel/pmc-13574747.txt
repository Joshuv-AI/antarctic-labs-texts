Autophagy-lysosomal pathway in neurodegeneration
Authors: Samantha R. McDonough, Aditya K. Rao, Jacqueline M. McAleer, Bergan M. Babrowicz, Manu Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autophagy-Lysosomal Pathway (ALP) dysfunction has emerged as a prominent mechanism underlying neurodegenerative disease. Given its central role in facilitating cellular clearance of misfolded proteins, damaged organelles and other cellular debris, the ALP is vital for cellular health and survival. In mature neurons, whose post-mitotic state renders them unable to dilute such materials through cell division, this waste removal pathway takes on an even greater importance. It is therefore unsurprising that ALP dysfunction is increasingly implicated in the context of age-associated neurodegenerative disease, where impaired proteostasis and pathological protein aggregation are common hallmarks. The focus of this review is how dysregulation of ALP-associated processes, including autophagic flux, chaperone-mediated autophagy, endolysosomal trafficking, lysosomal pH and ion balance, and lysosomal metabolic signaling, is implicated in both the pathogenesis and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, with an emphasis on Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease. A more detailed understanding of how these processes, both independently and cooperatively, contribute to the development and progression of neurodegenerative disease will improve our understanding of these debilitating disorders and inform the development of ALP-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The importance of the autophagic and endolysosomal pathways in maintaining proteostasis and cellular health has become increasingly evident in recent years. In the context of age-related neurodegeneration, there is growing recognition that autophagic and lysosomal dysfunction are early and significant events underlying pathology and disease progression (Fig. 1). The post-mitotic state of neurons makes the central nervous system (CNS) especially vulnerable to degeneration, as damaged neurons cannot be readily replaced. Furthermore, the neuronal accumulation of aggregated proteins and debris cannot be diluted through cell division. The significant energetic demands and elongated morphology of neurons provide additional challenges in maintaining proteostasis throughout their extraordinarily long lifespan, making neurons particularly susceptible to toxic protein buildup. A detailed understanding of the cellular mechanisms preceding cascading neurodegenerative processes is therefore necessary to ameliorate neuronal dysfunction and death. The autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP) is the primary cellular system for degrading protein aggregates and damaged organelles and thus plays a central role in mediating mechanisms of neurodegeneration. Although ALP dysfunction is broadly observed in neurodegeneration, we focus here on two of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), where ALP dysfunction is increasingly recognized as contributing to both disease pathogenesis and progression. Macroautophagy in neurodegeneration

One of the defining features of lysosomes is their ability to degrade cellular materials via autophagy. There are several subtypes of autophagy, including microautophagy, in which the lysosomal membrane directly invaginates to engulf and degrade substrates, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), in which specific proteins are recognized by chaperones and translocated across the lysosomal membrane for degradation [1, 2]. This section will focus on macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) which accounts for the majority of cellular autophagy. During autophagy, a double membrane structure known as a phagophore nucleates around cellular cargo, elongates, and seals to form an autophagosome [3, 4] (Fig. 2). The autophagosome can then fuse with a lysosome to form an autolysosome, where the lysosomal acidic environment and activated hydrolases [5] enable the degradation of substrates [2]. The resulting breakdown products are then transported to the cytoplasm for cellular reuse [6] or released via autolysosomal exocytosis in certain cases [7, 8]. Bulk autophagy occurs nonspecifically in response to nutrient needs, but selective autophagy also takes place and can include targeted degradation of protein aggregates (aggrephagy) or entire organelles, such as mitochondria (mitophagy) and endoplasmic reticulum (ER-phagy) [9, 10]. Importantly, the formation of autophagosomes or autolysosomes alone does not indicate completion of autophagy; rather, the entire process from phagophore nucleation to substrate degradation and nutrient recycling must occur for autophagy to be fully functional. The term autophagic flux describes the rate at which substrates cycle through this entire process, and maintaining a balance between autophagosome formation and clearance is critical for cellular health [11]. Despite basal levels of constitutive autophagy, it is not common to observe a high number of autophagic vacuoles such as autophagosomes and autolysosomes in healthy brains due to their transitory nature [12]. Buildup of visible autophagic vacuoles can therefore indicate increased induction of autophagy and/or defective autophagic flux, resulting in the accumulation of undigested materials.

Autophagy is a complex, multistep process that requires the coordinated action of numerous proteins. Autophagy-related (ATG) proteins play a central role in autophagosome nucleation and maturation [13], while a separate set of Endosomal Sorting Complexes Required for Transport (ESCRT) proteins are essential for autophagosome sealing, autolysosome formation, and membrane remodeling [14, 15]. Autophagy induction is tightly regulated at multiple levels, with the transcription factor EB (TFEB) upregulating expression of many autophagy-related genes, including ATG genes that initiate autophagosome formation and mediate substrate trafficking to lysosomes [16, 17]. Additional TFEB targets include subunits of the vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase) proton pump, the lysosomal cation channel mucolipin-1 (MCOLN1), and lysosomal hydrolases, collectively upregulating lysosomal biogenesis, maturation, and degradative capacity [18, 19]. The ability of TFEB to undergo nuclear translocation and induce gene expression is dependent on cellular metabolism, with mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) sequestering TFEB in the cytosol via phosphorylation in times of cellular nutrient abundance [20]. Autophagic flux is thus typically greater when cells are depleted of energy, promoting replenishment of cellular nutrients by “self-eating”. In low energy states, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylates mTORC1 pathway components, inhibiting its activity, while also directly phosphorylating FNIP1, enabling TFEB nuclear translocation [21–23]. Some evidence suggests that AMPK also directly phosphorylates TFEB itself at sites that promote its nuclear translocation [24], although this mechanism has been disputed and requires further validation [25]. In a mechanism distinct from the canonical nutrient-sensing pathway, TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation can also be promoted by Atg8 proteins such as GABARAP, together with the innate immunity regulator of autophagy IRGM [26].

In neurons, elongated morphology renders trafficking of distal autophagic substrates both challenging and critical [27, 28]. As most neuronal lysosomes reside near the soma, autophagosomes formed in distal axonal and dendritic processes must undergo long-distance retrograde trafficking to achieve fusion with lysosomes and undergo degradation [29, 30]. Distal autophagosomes can also fuse with local endosomes to form amphisomes prior to lysosomal fusion, which improves retrograde trafficking efficiency by enhancing recruitment of molecular motors [31, 32]. These trafficking demands render neurons particularly vulnerable to autophagic dysfunction, especially as autophagic flux declines naturally with age, and may help explain the observation that synaptic and axonal degeneration typically precede somal loss in neurodegeneration [9, 33, 34].

Failure to maintain effective autophagic flux gives way to severe cellular stress and downstream pathology. Strikingly, neuron-specific knockout of essential autophagy genes Atg5 or Atg7 results in neurodegeneration characterized by protein aggregate accumulation and cell death, highlighting the critical role of autophagy in neuronal survival [35, 36]. Chronic autophagy impairments can compromise lysosomal membrane integrity, leading to cytosolic leakage of lysosomal enzymes and subsequent activation of cell death pathways [37, 38]. Further, impaired mitophagy causes the buildup of damaged mitochondria, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can cause oxidative stress and accelerate cell death pathways if left unchecked [39]. Additionally, impaired autophagic flux can trigger a compensatory increase in autophagy induction, amplifying the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles and undigested substrates, which further impairs lysosomal degradative function in a toxic feedback loop [40]. Moreover, enhanced autophagy induction can increase lysosomal delivery of protein aggregates, promoting lysosomal membrane damage [41]. In neurodegenerative disease contexts, these considerations complicate autophagy-targeted therapeutic approaches; evidence suggests that autophagy induction may be a useful strategy early in neurodegenerative models but may exacerbate autophagic burden if administered too late in disease progression once degradative capacity is severely compromised [42–44].

Autophagic dysfunction is a common feature shared across many neurodegenerative disorders (Fig. 2). In neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorders, autophagy impairments are a key contributor to severe disease pathology [45]. In Huntington’s disease (HD), autophagy dysfunction is observed, and mutant huntingtin protein has been shown to decrease autophagic flux via degradation of the autophagy-regulator Beclin-1, reducing trafficking of autophagosomes to lysosomes [46]. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), autophagic dysfunction is implicated and disease-associated risk genes have been linked to key autophagy and mitophagy functions [45, 47–51]. These examples, along with the following links to AD and PD, highlight the importance of maintaining proper autophagic flux and demonstrate how autophagic dysregulation leads to an array of severe neurodegenerative disorders.

Autophagic dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease

AD is the most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, affecting between 4–8% of the elderly population globally, and is characterized by progressive memory loss and cognitive decline [52]. The accumulation of misfolded protein aggregates form the histopathological hallmarks of AD in the brain and include extracellular amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular inclusions of hyperphosphorylated microtubule-associated protein tau, termed neurofibrillary tangles [53].

Increasing evidence suggests that disruption of autophagy is a core characteristic of AD pathology [54]. Severe autophagic vacuole accumulation in AD patient brains and animal models, particularly within dystrophic neurites, has been consistently reported, implicating defective autophagic flux in AD pathogenesis [12, 55]. This buildup of autophagic vacuoles observed in AD is markedly more robust than in other age-related neurodegenerative disorders, and is more similar to that seen in lysosomal storage disorders which display severe lysosomal dysfunction and autophagic impairments [56]. Interestingly, several lysosomal storage disorders, including Niemann-Pick disease type C and mucopolysaccharidosis type III B, share key features of AD such as neurofibrillary tangles and Aβ deposits [57, 58], highlighting the mechanistic possibility that ALP dysfunction is sufficient to drive AD pathology.

The accumulation of autophagic vacuoles seen in AD is likely due to a combination of factors, including impaired lysosomal degradative capacity, impaired retrograde transport of axonal autophagosomes and amphisomes, and impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion, which together contribute to impaired autophagic vacuole clearance [42]. Supporting the hypothesis that defective degradative capacity is driving this phenotype, similar patterns of autophagic vacuole accumulation to those found in AD are observed when lysosomal enzymes are inhibited or deleted [42, 59–61]. The role of autophagy induction in AD remains unclear, but it has been proposed that overactive induction of autophagy can worsen disease progression by exacerbating autophagic vacuole accumulation without addressing defects in autophagic vacuole clearance [12]. Increased delivery of autophagic substrates may also exacerbate lysosomal membrane permeabilization in AD where lysosomal membrane integrity is compromised, likely due in part to the presence of tau aggregates which have been shown to directly damage lysosomal membranes [62].

Genetic findings highlight additional evidence for autophagy dysregulation in AD. The gene PSEN1 encodes presenilin 1 (PS1), a key component of the γ-secretase complex which produces Aβ via the enzymatic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP). In addition to autosomal dominant mutations in APP, mutations in PSEN1 are directly responsible for a subset of early-onset familial AD cases and result in increased Aβ42 production and Aβ plaque pathology. Aside from its role in APP cleavage, PS1 contributes to lysosomal acidification by facilitating maturation, trafficking, and assembly of the V-ATPase, and PS1 loss-of-function (LoF) mutations directly result in autophagic dysfunction [63, 64]. Severe ALP impairments, including defective autolysosome maturation and accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, have been observed in various PS1-knockout (PS1−/−) or mutant models [64–66]. Transgenic mice expressing familial AD-causal mutant APP also display autophagy defects [67, 68], and in the mutant PS1/APP mouse model, PS1 was found to have amyloid-independent effects on autophagy, with autophagy dysregulation preceding amyloid deposition [44, 69]. These findings that familial AD mutations impair autophagy in addition to their Aβ-specific effects support the idea that dysregulation of autophagic flux is a key mediator of AD pathology.

Furthermore, the greatest genetic risk factor for sporadic AD, apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4), disrupts autophagic flux by accumulating within lysosomes, dysregulating lysosomal trafficking, promoting lysosomal membrane leakage, and suppressing TFEB-modulated autophagy induction [70–74]. Another strong risk gene for sporadic AD is PICALM, which encodes phosphatidylinositol-binding clathrin assembly protein (PICALM) and has ties to both Aβ and autophagy. PICALM regulates endocytosis, including the endocytosis of soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins needed for membrane fusion in amphisome and autolysosome formation. Mutations in this gene may contribute to autophagic vacuole accumulation by lowering rates of autophagosome fusion with endosomes and lysosomes. PICALM expression has also been found to modulate tau clearance, likely via these effects on autophagy [75]. Together, these findings demonstrate that both familial, AD-causal mutations and sporadic, AD-associated gene variants impair autophagy.

Growing evidence indicates that tau turnover and spread are dependent on autophagy. Although the ubiquitin–proteasome system was previously considered the primary method of tau clearance, autophagy has since been confirmed as another pathway by which tau is degraded [76]. In support of this finding, treatment with the autophagy inhibitors chloroquine and 3-methyladenine resulted in tau accumulation and the formation of tau aggregates in M1C cells [77], while the autophagy inducer rapamycin was shown to reduce aggregated tau in P301S mice [78] and in triple transgenic (3xTg)-AD mice [79]. Furthermore, chemical induction of autophagy has been found to enhance secretion of total tau and phosphorylated tau (p-tau), whereas treatment with autophagy inhibitors or knockdown of the core autophagy-regulator Beclin-1 reduced tau secretion [80], pointing to autophagy-mediated secretion as a potential mechanism for tau propagation in AD.

In addition to clearing intracellular pathological tau, autophagy has shown to be a prominent pathway for clearance of toxic intracellular Aβ42 aggregates, which are a key feature of AD along with extracellular amyloid plaques [81, 82]. For instance, autophagy induction via rapamycin reduced Aβ burden in 7AP2 cells, which produce high levels of Aβ oligomers [79]. Similarly, small molecule autophagy enhancers decreased Aβ peptide levels whereas autophagy inhibition caused Aβ accumulation in primary neurons [83]. As further evidence, deficiencies of key autophagy genes cause increased intraneuronal amyloid accumulation in APP transgenic mice [84, 85]. Interestingly, autophagy deficits caused by excitatory neuron-specific deletion of Atg7 in the APP mouse forebrain led to reductions in extracellular amyloid secretion and plaque burden [86]. In this model, neurodegeneration originating from autophagic impairment was worsened by amyloidosis, possibly due to toxicity stemming from intracellular amyloid accumulation, which has been reported to contribute to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [87, 88]. This highlights a potential dichotomy by which induction of autophagy may help clear intracellular amyloid burden while also contributing to increased extracellular Aβ secretion and plaque deposition. These observations in tau and Aβ42 studies further emphasize that simply enhancing autophagy induction may be insufficient to restore autophagic flux, and may instead promote secretion of amyloid seeds left undigested by lysosomes with impaired degradative capacity.

Together, these findings implicate autophagy as a central mechanism mediating the clearance and secretion of toxic intraneuronal tau and Aβ aggregates. Importantly, the relationship between these pathological aggregates and autophagic flux is also bidirectional; while tau and Aβ aggregates are broken down via aggrephagy, they have also been shown to directly impair autophagic and lysosomal machinery [89–91], as well as promote lysosomal membrane permeabilization [62, 74, 92]. This produces a vicious cycle throughout disease progression, as worsening autophagic clearance gives way to increased protein aggregation, further inhibiting ALP.

Autophagic dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, is characterized histopathologically by intracellular inclusions referred to as Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites composed primarily of aggregated phosphorylated α-synuclein (αSyn) [93]. The selective vulnerability and degeneration of dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra leads to four cardinal motor symptoms of PD (bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural instability), with patients also displaying non-motor symptomology and cognitive impairment [93].

ALP dysfunction plays a key role in the development and progression of PD, with impaired autophagic flux causing a buildup of autophagic vacuoles, promoting intracellular αSyn accumulation, and contributing to dopaminergic neuron loss. Mutations in GBA, the gene encoding β-glucocerebrosidase, a lysosomal enzyme crucial for lipid metabolism, are found in approximately 5–15% of PD patients and are the most common genetic risk factor for sporadic PD [94, 95]. These mutations, likely through lysosomal accumulation of lipid substrates and subsequent lysosomal dysfunction such as increased lysosomal pH, impair autophagy and lead to αSyn accumulation [95, 96]. Consistent with these observations, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived dopaminergic neurons from PD patients carrying GBA mutations also show autophagy defects and increased αSyn accumulation [97]. Furthermore, postmortem brain tissue from PD patients carrying GBA mutations showed elevated levels of autophagosome marker LC3-II, reductions in Beclin-1, and defective mitophagy, together indicating impaired autophagic flux and clearance [98].

Familial PD mutations provide additional insight into autophagic mechanisms contributing to PD pathogenesis. PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) and parkin (PRKN) familial mutations have been shown to directly disrupt mitophagy, resulting in inefficient clearance of damaged mitochondria and accumulation of ROS [99, 100]. This oxidative stress impairs lysosomal degradative capacity and membrane integrity, leading to eventual cell death. The most common cause of familial PD is mutations in leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), which phosphorylates Rab GTPases to regulate endolysosomal trafficking, autophagy, and lysosomal damage repair [101, 102]. These familial mutations cause LRRK2 hyperactivity and are associated with reduced autophagosome transport and fusion, decreased autophagic flux, and increased levels of αSyn phosphorylation and secretion [103–105]. LRRK2 kinase inhibitors could therefore be a promising therapeutic approach and have demonstrated autophagy-restoring effects in the LRRK2 (G2019S) mutant cell line [106]. In addition, biallelic mutations in VPS13C, which encodes vacuolar protein sorting 13 homolog C (VPS13C), cause early onset parkinsonism [107], and VPS13C has also been linked to sporadic PD risk via genome-wide association studies [108]. As VPS13C serves an essential role in lysosomal membrane repair pathways by transporting ER lipids to sites of lysosomal membrane damage [109], this genetic evidence underscores the importance of maintaining lysosomal membrane integrity in PD.

Aggregated αSyn can impede autophagic flux, disrupt lysosomal membrane integrity, and impair overall degradative capacity. One study found that overexpression of αSyn resulted in defective autophagy both in vitro and in vivo via inhibition of Rab1A, a protein critical for autophagosome formation [110]. It has also been demonstrated that αSyn aggregates promote vesicle and lysosomal rupture in neuronal cell lines [111, 112]. Additionally, in a human dopaminergic neuron model of synucleinopathy, αSyn accumulation was found to disrupt trafficking of lysosomal hydrolases, leading to impaired degradative capacity and reduced autophagic flux [113]. Furthermore, in cultured primary neurons, fibril-induced αSyn aggregates were resistant to lysosomal degradation and found to reduce autophagosome clearance [114]. From these results, it seems possible that the defective autophagic clearance seen in PD is caused by the presence of toxic intracellular αSyn aggregates; however, it appears more likely that autophagy defects precede αSyn accumulation. In mouse models, dopaminergic neuron-specific knockout of Atg7 was sufficient to cause axonal dystrophy, locomotor impairments, Lewy body formation, and neurodegeneration [115, 116], providing evidence that αSyn aggregation and Lewy body formation occur downstream of perturbations in autophagic flux. Similarly, neuron-specific knockout of Atg5 led to neurodegeneration in mice, which was preceded by protein aggregates, neuronal inclusion bodies, and progressive motor deficits [35]. The observation that PD patient Lewy bodies contain a high concentration of mitochondria further suggests that defective mitophagy occurs early in the course of disease [117, 118].

Considering these findings, restoring autophagic flux represents an attractive therapeutic strategy for PD. Several groups have shown that upregulation or activation of TFEB, or pharmacological activation of AMPK, results in enhanced autophagy induction, reduced burden of αSyn aggregates, and improved neuronal survival in PD models [119–123]. However, it is important to consider that TFEB is a master transcriptional regulator of many genes and thus its broad activation could have numerous off-target impacts. Another concern is that enhancing autophagy initiation through TFEB or AMPK pathways may worsen autophagic vacuole accumulation in dopaminergic neurons, even in early-stage PD, if lysosomal degradative capacity or membrane integrity are already impaired. If degradative capacity in affected neurons is not improved in addition to increasing autophagy induction, or if lysosomal membrane repair mechanisms are not robust enough to process increased substrate delivery, autophagic flux will likely not be improved and may instead be worsened by these interventions. Furthermore, as lysosomal exocytosis has been implicated in the release of seeding-competent pathogenic αSyn species from neurons [124], enhancing autophagy induction without fully restoring autophagic flux may exacerbate the spread of αSyn pathology throughout the brain, introducing another caveat to this approach. It remains to be determined whether prolonging an individual neuron’s longevity at the expense of spreading pathology to neighboring neurons is more harmful or beneficial in the context of PD.

Chaperone-mediated autophagy in neurodegeneration

Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is a selective form of autophagy distinguished from other autophagic mechanisms by the involvement of a chaperone protein which targets individual protein substrates and guides them to the lysosome for degradation [125]. The selectivity of CMA for a specific subset of intracellular, primarily soluble proteins is dictated by the presence of a pentapeptide motif within target proteins that is biochemically related to Lys-Phe-Glu-Arg-Gln (KFERQ) [126]. Such motifs are present in approximately 30% of soluble cytosolic proteins, though it is likely that post-translational protein modifications dramatically increase the number of potential CMA substrates [125, 126].

The presence and accessibility of a KFERQ-like motif is required for a target protein to be degraded via CMA, as it serves as a recognition and binding site for the cytosolic chaperone heat shock cognate protein 70 kDa (Hsc70) [126, 127] (Fig. 2). Several co-chaperones, including carboxyl terminus of Hsc70-interacting protein (CHIP), heat shock protein 40 kDa (HSP40), and HSP70/HSP90 organizing protein (HOP) have been shown to participate in CMA by modulating substrate targeting to lysosomes [128, 129]. However, the activities of these co-chaperones during CMA are proposed to be Hsc70-dependent, as Hsc70 is the only chaperone known to bind directly to the KFERQ-like motif of CMA substrates, thereby initiating the multi-step process of CMA [127]. Once formed, the substrate-chaperone complex is transported to the lysosome where it binds to lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2 A (LAMP2A) on the lysosomal membrane [130]. This triggers the multimerization of LAMP2A into a large protein translocation complex through which CMA substrates are unfolded and enter the lysosomal lumen where they are degraded by lysosomal proteases [131, 132]. This process cannot take place without the presence of additional Hsc70 within the lysosomal lumen which binds to the substrate and facilitates translocation [133].

While most mammalian cells exhibit basal levels of CMA [134], this process is upregulated in response to an array of stressors including starvation, hypoxia, oxidative stress, and DNA damage [135–139]. These observations demonstrate the importance of CMA in maintaining protein quality control under healthy and stress conditions via targeted degradation of damaged, improperly folded or superfluous proteins. Evidence suggests that while CMA is unable to degrade protein aggregates, an adequate level of CMA is necessary to maintain proteostasis and prevent the accumulation and aggregation of damaged proteins [140, 141]. As such, functional CMA plays a vital role in protecting cells against proteotoxicity, whereas defective CMA can contribute to proteostatic dysregulation and proteotoxic stress.

Failure to effectively maintain neuronal proteostasis via CMA has been implicated in age-associated cognitive decline and neurodegenerative disease [142] (Fig. 2). Several studies report an age-dependent decline in CMA across various human and rodent tissues and cell types, contributing to the accumulation and aggregation of CMA substrate proteins in senescent cells even in the absence of disease [125, 143–145]. Strikingly, nearly one third of all genes encoding chaperone proteins are repressed in the aging human brain, with HSPA8 (Hsc70) significantly repressed in normal aging as well as in AD and HD patient brains [146]. In addition to general repression of chaperone proteins in the aging brain, age-associated reductions in LAMP2A have been observed and likely contribute to the decline in CMA activity as LAMP2A expression, availability, assembly, and disassembly govern the rate of CMA [147, 148].

Given that ineffective protein clearance and subsequent protein buildup is common to normal aging and neurodegenerative disease, it is not surprising that age-related decline in CMA has been proposed to contribute to the pathogenesis of age-associated neurodegenerative disease [143, 149–151]. Several neurodegenerative disease-linked proteins are substrates of CMA, including proteins implicated in PD (PARK7 [152, 153], αSyn [140, 154, 155], LRRK2 [156], UCH-L1 [157]), HD (huntingtin [158, 159]), and tauopathies including AD and FTD (tau [160, 161], TDP-43 [162]). While these proteins are degraded by CMA under normal conditions, their disease-linked forms often exhibit decreased or otherwise aberrant degradation by CMA, implicating CMA disruption in their pathological accumulation.

CMA dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease

Neuronal CMA decline is proposed to contribute to AD pathology. Blocking neuronal CMA via LAMP2A deletion in otherwise wildtype (WT) mice resulted in proteome alterations, protein aggregation, and cognitive defects, and when CMA was blocked in in 3xTg AD mice, it exacerbated Aβ pathology [150]. Furthermore, disruptions in CMA can trigger or worsen AD related pathology, as both tau and Aβ are CMA targets [44]. Additionally, mutations or post-translational modifications of tau can impair its recognition by Hsc70 and interfere with its translocation to the lysosomal lumen for CMA-mediated degradation [160, 163]. For example, tau acetylation is upregulated in AD [164, 165] and has been shown to inhibit CMA-mediated tau clearance by preventing proper disassembly of the multimeric LAMP2A complex, thereby reducing effective CMA flux of other substrates and placing a greater burden on the ubiquitin–proteasome system and macroautophagy to degrade tau [160, 166].

Another CMA substrate is regulator of calcineurin-1 (RCAN1), which functionally inhibits tau dephosphorylation via calcineurin, causing increased tau phosphorylation [163, 167]. AD patients show elevated levels of RCAN1 [167], likely in part due to CMA impairments, contributing to tau pathology. Restoring CMA therefore represents a promising therapeutic strategy for AD to reduce pathological tau aggregation by both enhancing CMA-mediated tau clearance and increasing CMA-mediated RCAN1 degradation, promoting tau dephosphorylation.

Transmembrane protein 59 (TMEM59) negatively regulates CMA by decreasing the expression of LAMP2A, the primary limiting factor for CMA flux [168]. TMEM59 gene expression is upregulated in AD patients compared to healthy controls [169] which likely contributes to decreased CMA activity. Notably, TMEM59 overexpression was sufficient to cause memory defects in WT mice, and TMEM59 haploinsufficiency was shown to mitigate tau and amyloid pathologies in PS19 and 5xFAD mouse models, respectively [168, 170].

Importantly, APP also contains a CMA motif within its C-terminus, and selective removal of this motif impairs APP processing, leading to increased toxic APP C-terminal fragments which contribute to AD pathogenesis by amplifying tau phosphorylation, synaptic toxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction [171–173]. Additionally, chemically enhancing CMA alleviated tau and Aβ pathology in the PS19 and 3xTg mouse models, respectively [150]. Taken together, these findings suggest that perturbations in CMA are crucial upstream events that contribute to AD pathogenesis, with activation of CMA demonstrating potential as a therapeutic approach.

Defective CMA in Parkinson’s disease

Growing evidence also points to defective CMA as a contributing factor in both familial and sporadic PD. Typically, CMA facilitates the lysosomal degradation of soluble αSyn; however, this mechanism is compromised in PD, leading to the buildup of pathogenic αSyn species [140, 174]. In familial PD, αSyn mutants A30P and A53T exhibit strong binding to the CMA receptor LAMP2A but fail to translocate into the lysosomal lumen, remaining bound to the receptor at the lysosomal membrane [140]. The resulting LAMP2A blockage not only prevents the degradation of these αSyn mutants, but also impairs the degradation of additional CMA substrates by decreasing LAMP2A availability. In a similar mechanism, familial PD-causal LRRK2 mutants, most commonly the G2019S variant, are poorly degraded by CMA despite WT LRRK2 being a CMA substrate. These mutants bind monomeric LAMP2A but prevent LAMP2A multimerization and thus fail to translocate while simultaneously blocking other CMA substrates from binding, further aggravating proteostatic dysfunction in PD [156].

Alterations of CMA are also observed in sporadic PD. Similar to mutant forms of αSyn and LRRK2, αSyn modified by interactions with oxidized dopamine exhibits decreased susceptibility to CMA degradation, and blocks the breakdown of other CMA substrates by disrupting LAMP2A assembly at the lysosomal membrane [141]. Other PD-associated post-translational modifications such as nitration, phosphorylation, and oxidation, are similarly reported to impair αSyn degradation by CMA [141]. Notably, a postmortem analysis of advanced-stage PD patient brains found reduced levels of LAMP2A and Hsc70 in the substantia nigra pars compacta and the amygdala, areas which are highly susceptible to αSyn pathology. These alterations in CMA components were found to be dependent on disease progression and directly correlated with αSyn pathology, with increased levels of LAMP2A in the early stages of PD suggesting a potential compensatory response [175]. These observations underscore the complex and progressive nature of CMA dysfunction in PD and suggest that therapeutic strategies aimed at upregulating the CMA pathway in PD would need to account for disease stage-dependent effects.

Endolysosomal trafficking in neurodegeneration

The endolysosomal system is a dynamic, highly coordinated network which plays a central role in cellular recycling, trafficking, and degradation to maintain cellular homeostasis [176]. This system consists of five primary subclasses: early endosomes (EEs), recycling endosomes, late endosomes (LEs), multivesicular bodies (MVBs), and lysosomes [177, 178] (Fig. 2). Early and recycling endosomes function as principal sorting hubs, directing cargo internalized via clathrin-dependent or -independent endocytosis to be released back to the extracellular space, trafficked to the trans-Golgi network via the Retromer complex, or sent to the lysosome for degradation [179]. The Rab5 GTPase tightly regulates the transition of internalized cargo from the plasma membrane into EEs by coordinating vesicle fusion and tethering following endocytosis [180, 181]. Rab5-to-Rab7 conversion acts as a critical molecular switch that drives EE maturation into LEs and MVBs, where ESCRT-mediated intraluminal vesicle formation sequesters ubiquitinated cargo destined either for lysosomal degradation or extracellular release [182, 183]. SNARE-mediated fusion of MVBs with the plasma membrane is also regulated by Rab GTPases and results in release of exosomes to the extracellular space, which serve important roles in cell-to-cell communication. LE or MVB fusion with lysosomes results in endolysosome formation, where LE/MVB cargo is degraded. Mature lysosomes function as major degradative organelles, breaking down endocytic, phagocytic and autophagic cargo via their highly acidic luminal environment and abundant acid hydrolases [184–186]. Vesicular transport between endolysosomal compartments relies on evolutionarily conserved proteins including several Rab GTPases [187], tethering complexes such as homotypic fusion and protein sorting complex (HOPS) and class C core vacuole/endosome tethering complex (CORVET) [188], and SNARE proteins to ensure accurate directional transport and membrane fusion [189].

Lysosomal trafficking of both intraluminal enzymes and integral membrane proteins is essential for proper lysosomal function. Soluble luminal enzymes are first synthesized as inactive, N-glycosylated precursors, ensuring their degradative activity is limited to the confines of the lysosomal environment. Most lysosomal enzymes rely on the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tag for proper lysosomal targeting through the endosomal pathway [190, 191], although several M6P-independent mechanisms also exist [192–194]. In the M6P-dependent pathway, mannose residues within high-mannose-type oligosaccharide modifications added to proenzymes in the ER are converted to M6P groups through consecutive enzymatic reactions in the Golgi apparatus [190, 195]. M6P-tagged proenzymes can then be recognized by two distinct M6P receptors (MPRs), the cation-dependent MPR and the cation-independent MPR [196]. These MPRs package lysosomal proenzymes into clathrin-coated vesicles which are trafficked to endosomes, where the acidic environment promotes dissociation of proenzymes from MPRs, which are recycled by the Retromer complex [196]. The tagged proenzymes are eventually delivered to lysosomes via endolysosomal fusion, where the strong acidity activates enzymes and acid phosphatases remove the M6P tag [190, 195].

In contrast to lysosomal enzyme synthesis, integral lysosomal transmembrane proteins contain cytoplasmic sorting signals that recruit adaptor proteins for vesicular transport [196]. Lysosomal transmembrane proteins can either be transported directly through the trans-Golgi network or indirectly by insertion into the plasma membrane and subsequent reinternalization into the endosomal pathway [196]. These membrane proteins are heavily glycosylated, protecting their luminal-facing sides from degradation by lysosomal hydrolases [197].

Due to their highly polarized architecture, extended morphology, and postmitotic state, neurons are particularly vulnerable to perturbations in endolysosomal trafficking. Neuronal autophagosomes form continuously in distal axons and dendrites and must undergo motor protein-mediated retrograde transport along microtubules to reach the soma, where lysosomal number and degradative capacity are enriched [29, 30]. Given the complexity of this system, dysfunction may arise at many possible steps, such as hydrolase misrouting, altered endolysosomal lipid composition, disrupted endosome trafficking, impaired vesicle fusion, and accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, all of which are increasingly recognized as early and potentially causative events in neurodegenerative disease [46, 198–200] (Fig. 2). There are also several changes in endolysosomal trafficking which occur naturally with aging, including changes in endocytosis rates, clathrin dynamics, and endocytosis-related protein expression, which may have consequences for neurodegeneration [201–204].

Endolysosomal trafficking defects in Alzheimer’s disease

In the context of AD, several perturbations in endolysosomal transport have been observed. Increased rates of endocytosis and enlargement of early endosomes are early features of AD, indicating that defective endosomal trafficking could contribute to AD pathogenesis [205, 206]. Furthermore, electron microscopy has revealed that extracellular amyloid plaques in AD patient brains are surrounded by dystrophic neurites that contain high numbers of putative lysosomes [207], a finding that is recapitulated in mouse models of AD, where axonal swellings containing an abnormally high concentration of lysosomes are observed around extracellular Aβ aggregates [208–210]. These observations underscore defects in retrograde endolysosomal trafficking at dystrophic neurites driven by Aβ pathology. Furthermore, these accumulated axonal lysosomes contain lower levels of several lysosomal proteases than would normally be found in somatic lysosomes, suggesting additional disruptions in endolysosomal maturation [209]. These effects appear bidirectional; while treatment with Aβ42 oligomers directly impairs microtubule architecture and disrupts endolysosomal trafficking, dystrophic neurites surrounding Aβ plaques also accumulate Aβ producing beta-secretase 1 (BACE1) and are sites of increased Aβ deposition, exacerbating both Aβ plaque pathology and axonal trafficking defects [210].

This abnormal distribution of neuronal lysosomes raises questions about the precise mechanisms governing perturbed lysosomal trafficking in AD. In neurons, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)-interacting protein (JIP3) couples lysosomes to microtubule motor proteins including kinesin and dynein [211], thereby contributing to effective anterograde and retrograde lysosomal transport. JIP3−/− mice exhibit lysosomal accumulation within axonal swellings resembling that observed in AD, and haploinsufficiency of JIP3 has been shown to worsen amyloid pathology in the 5xFAD mouse model [212]. This data, along with similar phenotypes in JIP3-depleted human iPSC-derived neurons, suggests that JIP3 is essential for proper lysosomal trafficking in neurons, preventing the buildup of axonal lysosomes and preserving proteostasis, particularly that of Aβ [213, 214].

Sortilin-related receptor 1 (SORL1) is an endosomal sorting receptor which, in addition to having strong genetic associations with AD [215, 216], has reduced expression in AD patient brains [217]. Notably, SORL1 has been reported to traffic Aβ peptides to lysosomes [218], and manipulating SORL1 expression impacts APP processing in human iPSC models [219]. Depletion of SORL1 in human iPSC-derived neurons resulted in endosome enlargement and alterations in APP trafficking [220]. While in this study endosome enlargement caused by SORL1 depletion was specific to derived neurons and not observed in derived microglia, a more recent study found that SORL1 deficiency also caused enlarged lysosomes and impaired trafficking of lysosomal enzymes from the trans-Golgi network in human-derived microglia-like cells [221]. Interestingly, SORL1 deficiency in iPSC-derived neurons, but not astrocytes or microglia, was also shown to increase p-tau levels and reduce both intracellular and secreted APOE [222], highlighting both the cell-type specificity of endolysosomal trafficking defects in AD as well as the interconnected nature of key AD-related pathways and proteins.

Bridging integrator-1 (BIN1) is an integral regulator of neuronal endocytosis via interactions with endocytic machinery including dynamin [223], adaptor protein-2 complex (AP2) [224], clathrin [225, 226], synaptojanin [227], and endophilin [228]. BIN1 has consistently been associated with increased AD risk in genome-wide association studies, second only to APOE [229–232]. RNA analyses from postmortem AD patient brain tissue demonstrated that lower BIN1 expression was associated with earlier disease onset [233], and BIN1 isoforms have been linked to cognitive decline in AD patients [234]. In human iPSC-derived neurons, overexpression of different BIN1 isoforms resulted in differential changes in endosome size, highlighting the role of BIN1 in the regulation of early endosome formation and function [235]. In other reports, depletion of BIN1 in rodent cortical and hippocampal neurons resulted in enlarged early endosomes, causing increased tau propagation via upregulation of endocytosis [236], as well as impaired lysosomal trafficking and subsequent degradation of BACE1, leading to higher production of amyloid beta [237]. Although the precise role of BIN1 isoforms and expression levels in AD pathogenesis is not fully defined, these studies implicate BIN1 as a central regulator of the endolysosomal network, specifically the processes involved in early endosome formation, size, and maturation, which has consequences on the cell-to-cell propagation of protein pathology in AD.

Endolysosomal trafficking defects in Parkinson’s disease

Dysfunction of the endolysosomal trafficking system is also a key characteristic of PD, with the protein products of several genetic risk factors for PD functioning within endocytosis, endosome maturation, and lysosomal protein trafficking pathways [113, 238]. PD-associated mutations in key trafficking genes, including VPS35, LRRK2, RAB7L1, and sorting nexins, disrupt endolysosomal trafficking and promote αSyn aggregation [239–243]. Familial mutations in VPS35, which encodes a component of the retromer sorting complex, vacuolar protein sorting 35 (VPS35), are causal for late-onset PD and impair the recycling of the cation-independent MPR, leading to reduced delivery of lysosomal hydrolases, accumulation of αSyn, and dopaminergic neuron loss [242, 244]. Additionally, familial PD-causal mutant LRRK2 is hyperactive, leading to increased phosphorylation of Rab GTPases, disrupting their role in endolysosomal trafficking and increasing αSyn propagation [239, 240, 245]. PD mutant LRRK2 has also been demonstrated to bind microtubules with much higher affinity than WT LRRK2, decreasing motility of motor proteins and acting as a roadblock for microtubule-reliant trafficking [246]. RAB7L1 is one of the genes within the sporadic PD-associated PARK16 locus and has been shown to interact with LRRK2 to modulate PD risk, with PD-associated alterations in RAB7L1 causing sorting defects in the Golgi-endolysosomal system [247]. Interestingly, expression of functional VPS35 was sufficient to rescue both LRRK2 variant and RAB7L1 variant-induced defects, highlighting the interconnected nature of the Retromer complex, Rab GTPases, and the Rab kinase LRRK2 in modulating PD mechanisms [247].

Additionally, mutations in synaptojanin 1 (SYNJ1), a phosphatase essential for clathrin coat removal during endocytosis and synaptic vesicle recycling, are linked to early-onset parkinsonism [248]. In neuronal and non-neuronal cells, depletion of SYNJ1 caused enlargement of early endosomes similar to observations in fibroblasts from PD patients with SYNJ1 mutations, implying these mutations cause SYNJ1 LoF [249]. Interestingly, this SYNJ1 depletion also led to defective transferrin trafficking, linking endolysosomal defects to iron dyshomeostasis [249]. Together, evidence from AD and PD demonstrates that impaired endolysosomal trafficking, which is inexorably linked to disruptions in the ALP, is an important factor in the development of progressive, age-associated neurodegeneration.

Lysosomal pH and ion homeostasis in neurodegeneration

Maintenance of a highly acidic lysosomal pH (4.5–5.5) is essential for efficient lysosomal degradation and cellular clearance [250, 251]. Lysosomal acidification promotes the proteolytic cleavage and activation of luminal hydrolase pro-forms and creates the optimal conditions for mature acid hydrolase activity [252, 253]. In addition to this central function, the acidic lysosomal pH is important for proton-coupled transport of amino acids, secondary active transport, nutrient sensing, signaling, and membrane trafficking [254, 255]. Lysosomal pH is also essential for driving dissociation events that promote recycling, such as dissociating lipids from carrier proteins [256], metal ions from carrier proteins [257], and ligands from receptors [258].

Lysosomal pH is maintained by the V-ATPase proton pump, which uses ATP hydrolysis to drive the movement of protons across the lysosomal membrane and into the lumen [259] (Fig. 3). V-ATPase is a large, multi-subunit complex composed of a peripheral, catalytic V1 domain that hydrolyzes ATP and a membrane-embedded Vo proton core required for proton translocation [260]. The Voa1 subunit tethers V1 to Vo, with the reversible association and dissociation of V1 and Vo regulating V-ATPase activity, as subunit dissociation inhibits ATP hydrolysis and proton flow [261, 262]. This dynamic regulation enables V-ATPase to respond to environmental cues such as nutrient availability and pH, adjusting the lysosomal pH in accordance with cellular needs [263, 264]. Although V-ATPase is the primary driver of lysosomal acidity, there is evidence that lysosome-associated membrane proteins (LAMPs) also contribute to lysosomal pH regulation by preventing proton leak through the transmembrane protein 175 (TMEM175) channel [265]. The lysosomal membrane contains many ion pumps, transporters, and channels. Several of these transmembrane proteins serve as ion exchangers, using the proton concentration gradient to drive transport of calcium, sodium, and chloride ions into the lysosomal lumen while releasing protons into the cytosol [255]. The lysosomal lumen has high concentrations of calcium, sodium, and chloride ions relative to the cytosol, with regulated ion release through lysosomal channels serving numerous functions [269, 270]. For instance, lysosomal calcium release via the TRPML1 channel regulates membrane trafficking and lysosomal exocytosis [271], and can induce autophagy by activating TFEB via the protein phosphatase calcineurin [272, 273], with alternative calcineurin-independent mechanisms of TFEB activation also proposed. Additionally, lysosomal sodium stores are essential for amino acid transport [274], and lysosomal chloride, mediated by Cl−/H+ exchangers ClC-6 and ClC-7, acts as a proton counterion to support lysosomal acidification, activation of digestive enzymes, and regulation of calcium release [275, 276].

Defective acidification can have severe consequences for lysosomal and cellular health. Disrupted lysosomal acidity reduces hydrolase activity, impairing the lysosomal degradative capacity and leading to the buildup of damaged organelles and protein aggregates, which can cause oxidative damage and long-term cellular stress [277–279]. Disrupted lysosomal acidification and ion homeostasis are observed even in healthy aging [280], and it is unsurprising that many neurodegenerative diseases also exhibit these abnormalities [277] (Fig. 3).

Increasing evidence implicates deacidified luminal pH as an early feature of age-related neurodegeneration [253], likely arising before additional lysosomal dysfunction such as buildup of autophagic vacuoles. Of note, TRPML1, like other lysosomal ion channels, is regulated by pH and has higher activity in deacidified states [281, 282], which could cause higher cytosolic calcium and iron levels and contribute to oxidative stress and cytotoxicity [283]. Impaired acidification also disrupts mitophagy, leading to accumulation of damaged, ROS-producing mitochondria [278]. These ROS directly activate TRPML1 and further impair lysosomal acidification, which amplifies cytotoxic calcium dysregulation and autophagic stress [253, 284]. Interestingly, inhibition of calpains, calcium-dependent proteases involved in apoptotic pathways, has shown neuroprotective effects in AD, PD, and HD models [285–287], highlighting the significance of aberrant cytosolic calcium signaling in neurodegeneration. Further, LoF mutations in CLN1, which encodes palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1), a lysosomal enzyme that facilitates V-ATPase trafficking, cause a form of the neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorder neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis [288]. Additionally, polymorphisms in TMEM106B, which encodes transmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B), a lysosomal membrane protein that regulates lysosomal pH by binding to V-ATPase, have been identified as a major risk factor for frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), with additional ties to ALS, AD, and PD [289]. Mutations in V-ATPase subunits or regulators also directly underlie rare neurodegenerative disorders such as autosomal recessive osteopetrosis and Wolfram syndrome [290, 291].

Further, lysosomal ion dyshomeostasis is a key feature of many neurodegenerative disorders. Disruptions to the PIKfyve-Vac14-Fig4 complex, which regulates synthesis of the endolysosomal signaling lipid PI(3,5)P₂, causes lysosomal ion imbalance and osmotic swelling that drives neurodegeneration across multiple diseases [292]. For example, prion infection has been shown to deplete PIKfyve in Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease [293], resulting in endolysosomal enlargement. LoF mutations in VAC14 and FIG4 have also been identified as causal for ALS [294], Yunis-Varón syndrome [295, 296], and Charcot-Marie Tooth type 4 J [297]. Thus, perturbations in lysosomal pH and ion balance converge through many pathways to cause neurodegeneration or amplify neurodegenerative risk.

Lysosomal pH and ion homeostasis in Alzheimer’s disease

Defective lysosomal acidification and ion imbalance is strongly implicated in AD, based on evidence from a combination of human genetic data, AD mouse models, and cells derived from AD patients. Mutations in the PSEN1 gene encoding PS1 are the most common cause of early-onset AD [298]. PS1 is a transmembrane protein subunit of the γ-secretase complex responsible for cleaving APP to form Aβ, while also facilitating the maturation of the Voa1 subunit of V-ATPase by acting as a molecular chaperone in the ER [299]. In addition to increasing production of aggregation-prone Aβ42 species, AD-causal PSEN1 mutants exert Aβ-independent effects by reducing functional lysosomal Voa1 levels, thus impairing lysosomal acidification [64]. PS1 mutant fibroblasts from AD patients display reduced levels and trafficking of V-ATPase, increased lysosomal pH, altered expression of key lysosomal pathway genes, and autophagic impairments [64, 300]. Additionally, in PSEN1−/− mouse blastocysts and in PS1 hypomorphic mouse neurons, impaired glycosylation, trafficking, and maturation of Voa1 subunits were observed alongside reduced functional Voa1 levels, impaired V-ATPase proton-pumping activity, and diminished maturation and activity of acid hydrolases [64, 281]. In addition to impaired Voa1 trafficking, PSEN1−/− or mutations have been shown to impair lysosomal trafficking of ClC-7, leading to markedly reduced lysosomal chloride content, in both murine PSEN1−/− cell lines and fibroblast lines from familial AD patients with PSEN1 mutations [301]. PSEN1−/− or mutations have also been demonstrated to cause ER calcium overload via impaired ER calcium leak [302] and lysosomal-to-cytosolic calcium dysregulation via elevated pH-induced hyperactivation of TRPML1 [281]. These findings highlight the interconnected nature of lysosomal pH disturbance and ion imbalance in PSEN1-associated familial AD.

In fibroblasts from Down syndrome patients, who are afflicted with early onset AD due to APP triplication [303], as well as in the 5xFAD and Tg2576 AD mouse models, APP was found to directly impair V-ATPase function, thereby increasing lysosomal pH, via its cleavage product β-C-terminal fragment (βCTF) binding to Voa1 and decreasing V-ATPase complex formation [304]. Mature Voa1 levels are reduced in 5xFAD mouse brains [305], and lysosomal V-ATPase levels are decreased in PS/APP mouse brains [306]. Reduced Voa1 and V1B2 expression was also reported in the hippocampi of young 3xTg-AD mice [307]. Additionally, experimentally impairing lysosomal acidification or proteolysis was shown to cause similar phenotypes to AD [12, 308], and knocking down the Voa1 subunit in Drosophila neurons caused neurodegeneration under normal conditions and increased vulnerability to toxic human amyloid or tau protein perturbations in aging and stress contexts [309, 310]. Further, conditional knockout of the V-ATPase accessory protein ATP6AP2 leads to cortical and hippocampal neurodegeneration as well as cognitive impairment in mice [311]. In several AD mouse models, defective lysosomal acidification was observed much earlier than extracellular deposition of amyloid [312]. Together, these studies suggest that impaired lysosomal acidification is an early and potent driver of neurodegenerative processes in AD.

Importantly, in primary mouse neurons and HT22 cells treated with oligomeric Aβ, Aβ-mediated neurotoxicity was rescued by nanoparticles that restore lysosomal acidity, supporting the idea that lysosomal deacidification is an early mediator of Aβ-induced cytotoxicity [313]. This study also found that intralumenal Aβ directly disrupted ATP hydrolysis and proton pumping by V-ATPase, highlighting the bidirectional and cascading impacts of lysosomal deacidification and protein accumulation.

Intracellular tau aggregates also directly impact lysosomal acidity, with tau overexpression causing increased lysosomal pH in primary murine hippocampal neurons [313]. Tau seeds from AD brains impair lysosomal degradative capacity, manifesting as increased lysosomal size, in primary human fibroblasts [314], indicating that toxic tau species directly perturb lysosomal acidification. Moreover, primary human astrocytes treated with tau pre-formed fibrils display lysosomal swelling and impaired lysosomal acidification [62], highlighting the relevance of neurodegeneration-associated lysosomal impairments in glia in addition to those seen in neurons.

Interestingly, it has been observed that impaired lysosomal acidification can cause increased release of lysosomal calcium stores through TRPML1, which can further impair V-ATPase function [281]. PSEN1−/−-induced TRPML1 overactivation has also been implicated in JNK activation and subsequent reduction of dynein activity, leading to endolysosomal trafficking dysfunction and accumulation of autophagic vacuoles in dystrophic axons [315]. Inhibiting TRPML1 activation or JNK rescued these effects in PSEN1−/− mouse neurons [315]. TRPML1 overactivation can also activate Ryanodine receptors in the ER, triggering the release of ER calcium stores and exacerbating calcium-associated cytotoxicity [316, 317]. However, it remains unclear whether elevated intracellular calcium is purely a downstream effect of lysosomal dysfunction. Blocking Ryanodine receptors rescued lysosomal dysfunction in iPSC-derived neurons from AD patients carrying PSEN1 mutations [307], whereas restoring the lysosomal calcium balance failed to rescue autophagy defects in PSEN1−/− blastocysts. Similarly, reacidifying the lysosome with acidic nanoparticles was effective in correcting PSEN1−/−-induced autophagy defects, TRPML1 overactivation, and calcium dysregulation in murine blastocysts, whereas restoring lysosomal calcium balance alone was insufficient to restore lysosomal acidification or autophagy defects [281]. These studies highlight the complex interplay between calcium homeostasis, lysosomal pH, and broader lysosomal impairments in AD.

In addition to calcium dysregulation in AD, iron dysregulation and ferroptosis have emerged as key contributors to AD pathology [318, 319]. Iron deposition in the brain increases with age and is correlated with cognitive decline, with Aβ and phosphorylated tau shown to amplify iron deposition and ferroptotic stress [320]. Further, APOE4 was found to confer greater iron-dependent AD risk [321] and may lower cellular defense to ferroptosis pathways.

Lysosomal pH and ion homeostasis in Parkinson’s disease

Dysregulated lysosomal acidification and ion homeostasis are also hallmarks of PD. Early-onset genetic forms of PD, such as X-linked parkinsonism with spasticity (XPDS) and Kufor-Rakeb syndrome (KRS), are caused by mutations that impair lysosomal acidification and ion balance, highlighting a central role of lysosomal pH and ion regulation in PD pathogenesis. XPDS-causal mutations in ATP6AP2, which encodes ATPase H+ transporting accessory protein 2 (ATP6AP2), cause lysosomal alkalization and substrate accumulation through impaired V-ATPase subunit assembly [322]. As seen in KRS-casual mutations, LoF of ATP13A2, which encodes the lysosomal transporter ATPase cation transporting 13A2 (ATP13A2), causes dysregulated cation and polyamine transport, leading to lysosomal alkalization and αSyn accumulation [323–325]. Notably, reduced protein levels of ATP13A2 are also observed in dopaminergic nigral neurons from sporadic PD patients, where ATP13A2 accumulates inside Lewy bodies [326]. Interestingly, an upregulation of ATP13A2 mRNA is observed in surviving dopaminergic neurons in PD patient brains, suggesting a potential protective compensatory response to lysosomal stress [325].

As further genetic evidence, LRRK2 familial PD mutations have been shown to cause defective autophagic vacuole acidification [103]. Recent studies have also shown that LRRK2 is recruited to the lysosomal surface when lysosomal pH rises or membranes are damaged, where it activates its kinase activity to phosphorylate Rab GTPases. This process is amplified in PD-linked LRRK2 mutations, suggesting that chronic lysosomal deacidification and membrane damage could drive pathogenic LRRK2 hyperactivation in a feed-forward cycle [327–330]. Additionally, variants in TMEM175, which encodes the lysosomal membrane channel TMEM175, have been associated with both PD risk and protection. TMEM175 is a potassium-permeable channel and plays an essential role in the regulation of lysosomal pH, with neuronal TMEM175 deficiency causing increased lysosomal pH, impaired cathepsin activity, accumulation of αSyn, degeneration of dopaminergic neurons, and motor impairments [331–333]. Whether TMEM175 also exhibits direct proton permeability and acts as a proton leak channel to regulate lysosomal pH remains an area of debate [265–268].

Further along in PD progression, αSyn aggregates directly disrupt lysosomal membranes and neutralize the proton gradient, causing reduced hydrolase activation and impaired degradation of αSyn, among other substrates [334, 335]. αSyn aggregation can also induce iron dysregulation and ferroptosis, which growing evidence implicates as a key player in PD progression [336]. In support of an iron-centric hypothesis, PD patients have been found to have differential expression of iron regulatory proteins [337] and increased iron deposition in the brain, which can exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [338]. These pathways occur simultaneously with calcium dysregulation in PD, as impaired mitochondrial function and oxidative stress can promote overactivation of lysosomal calcium release, further impairing lysosomal acidification in a self-reinforcing cycle. Toxin-based models of PD, which cause oxidative stress, have also been shown to decrease lysosomal acidification [339], highlighting the vicious cycle of cellular stress, ion imbalance, and lysosomal pH defects in PD.

Several groups have demonstrated promising therapeutic benefits of acidic nanoparticles in PD models to restore lysosomal pH and degradative capacity. In a variety of genetic fibroblast models and dopaminergic cell models of PD, acidic nanoparticles have been shown to re-acidify lysosomes and enhance their degradative capacity, among other measures of lysosomal function, conferring increased protection against cytotoxicity [340]. Acidic nanoparticles have also shown protective effects against dopaminergic cell death in vivo in several PD mouse models [340, 341]. Recently, in both neuroblastoma cells expressing A30P mutant αSyn and A30P transgenic Drosophila, acidic nanoparticles were similarly found to effectively re-acidify lysosomes, improve αSyn clearance, and reduce cytotoxicity [342]. Nanoparticles also improved Drosophila motor function when administered in vivo [342]. These findings underscore the importance of lysosomal degradative functions in neurodegeneration and suggest that direct restoration of lysosomal pH may be a viable therapeutic approach for PD.

Lysosomal metabolic signaling in neurodegeneration

In addition to their degradative capacity, lysosomes serve as central metabolic signaling hubs that sense and respond to cellular nutrient and energy states, thereby supporting proteostasis, mitochondrial quality control, and cell survival. V-ATPase is central to this role and responds to nutrient availability via sensing lysosomal luminal amino acid abundance. V-ATPase is constitutively bound to the Ragulator complex at the lysosomal membrane at its V1 cytosolic subunit [343, 344] (Fig. 3). This V-ATPase-Ragulator complex regulates both AMPK and mTORC1, serving as a metabolic switch between catabolic and anabolic processes [345]. V-ATPase can also respond to nutrient states by undergoing reversible subunit dissociation. Under states of glucose [346] or amino acid [347] starvation, V-ATPase subunit assembly rates increase, promoting its activity and enhancing lysosomal acidification as the need arises for more autophagic substrates to undergo degradation.

mTORC1 is a serine/threonine kinase complex recruited to the cytosolic surface of the lysosomal membrane in nutrient-replete conditions. This recruitment is mediated by Rag GTPases, whose activation is promoted by the guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) activity of the Ragulator complex, which is regulated by V-ATPase in response to luminal amino acid availability. Active Rag heterodimers bind the Raptor subunit of mTORC1, tethering mTORC1 to the lysosomal surface, where it can be activated by Rheb-GTP [348, 349]. In this activated state, mTORC1 suppresses autophagy pathways while promoting protein synthesis and cell growth pathways via numerous phosphorylation events [350–352]. Importantly, lysosomal amino acid abundance sensed by V-ATPase reflects both overall nutrient availability and lysosomal proteolysis, allowing mTORC1 to dynamically respond to cellular metabolic needs through its lysosomal recruitment, activation, and modulation of lysosomal degradative capacity [353]. In nutrient-depleted states, mTORC1 dissociates from the lysosomal membrane and is inactivated, allowing autophagy and other catabolic processes to predominate [354, 355]. Additionally, lysosomal cholesterol has been shown to activate mTORC1, expanding its regulatory response to readouts of lipid availability [356]. Interestingly, lysosomal positioning also influences mTORC1 activity, with perinuclear clustering reducing mTORC1 activity relative to peripheral lysosomal localization [357].

Under nutrient rich conditions, TFEB is phosphorylated by mTORC1, restricting its localization to the cytoplasm and preventing its nuclear translocation [20], thus preventing the upregulation of autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, and lysosomal acidification gene expression [358, 359]. Alternatively, low cellular energy states, in the form of increased AMP:ATP or ADP:ATP ratios, activate AMPK, which functions via a combination of direct and indirect phosphorylation events to inhibit mTORC1 and promote TFEB nuclear translocation, thereby upregulating autophagy and restoring energy balance by fueling catabolism [24, 360].

There are numerous ways in which these nutrient-sensing lysosomal functions can be dysregulated. Metabolic diseases such as obesity and type II diabetes are rising in global prevalence [361] and are associated with increased risk for neurodegenerative disease [362, 363], especially in combination with age and genetic risk factors which further impair lysosomal pathways. Mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, glucose metabolism changes, insulin resistance, and impaired lipid metabolism are common features of metabolic and neurodegenerative disease, and can impede homeostatic lysosomal functions [364]. Consistent with this, there is ample evidence of dysregulation in the mTORC1, AMPK, and TFEB pathways in neurodegenerative disease [364, 365] (Fig. 3), which are further impacted by broader metabolic perturbations [366]. Notably, aging itself is associated with decreased AMPK function, decreased ATP production, and increased neuronal vulnerability to oxidative stress. mTORC1 dysregulation is observed in HD [367], although there are contrasting findings regarding the directionality of this dysregulation, with enhanced mTORC1 activity [368] and impaired mTORC1 activity [369] both reported. AMPK and TFEB dysregulation also appear to be a common feature in neurodegeneration, with aberrations observed in both HD and ALS [370, 371]. Additionally, stimulating TFEB has been effective in mouse models of some neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorders [372]. Dysregulation of metabolic signaling and the lysosomal functions dependent on such signaling provide a valuable framework for understanding neurodegenerative disease progression and introduce numerous therapeutic approaches.

Lysosomal metabolic signaling defects in Alzheimer’s disease

Growing evidence suggests that AD-associated lysosomal dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation are highly interconnected [373, 374]. Lysosomes engage in extensive bidirectional crosstalk with other metabolic organelles, including the mitochondria, ER, and Golgi, such that lysosomal dysfunction can disrupt metabolic pathways in these compartments, while metabolic perturbations reciprocally compromise lysosomal function [375, 376]. For instance, oxidative stress, a hallmark of AD, accelerates disease progression by compromising the lysosomal response to the metabolic needs of the cell, in addition to impairing mitochondrial integrity and metabolic homeostasis [377]. As lysosomal degradative capacity is diminished with age [378], nutrient sensing may become decoupled from lysosomal degradation, placing an additional metabolic burden on neurons which already have high energetic demands [379]. While the precise mechanisms of metabolic-lysosomal dysfunction in AD are not fully elucidated, converging evidence implicates dysregulation of mTORC1, TFEB, and cholesterol metabolism pathways as factors underlying AD progression.

Mounting data suggests that mTORC1 is hyperactive in AD, including findings that active, phosphorylated mTORC1 is increased in AD patient brains [380, 381] alongside markers of mTORC1 activity [380, 382–384], which correlate with tau pathology [380, 382]. As such, there has been significant interest in therapeutically targeting the mTORC1 pathway in AD, primarily via pharmacological mTORC1 inhibition, which has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in AD mouse models [385, 386]. For instance, the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin has been found to prevent cognitive decline [387], reduce Aβ pathology [387–389], increase vascular density [388], and preserve blood-brain barrier integrity [390] in PDAPP mice. Rapamycin has also been reported to rescue cognitive decline [79], reduce Aβ and tau pathology [79, 391], and decrease microglial activation [391] in 3xTg-AD mice, among other AD mouse models. However, these beneficial effects appear to be highly dependent on timing, as they are not seen when rapamycin is administered after plaque and tangle formation. This may reflect lysosomal membrane damage and impaired degradative capacity at later disease stages, when autophagy induction approaches are less likely to effectively restore autophagic flux in the absence of a sufficient pool of functional lysosomes. Additionally, despite promising results in animal models, rapamycin increased biomarkers of inflammation and AD when administered to patients with mild cognitive impairment during a phase 1 clinical trial [392], flagging concerns for the continuation of this approach.

In addition to directly inhibiting mTORC1, several studies have taken the approach of activating existing mTORC1 inhibitors such as AMPK. Notably, the type II diabetes medication metformin is a known activator of AMPK, and is associated with decreased risk of cognitive impairment [393] and neurodegenerative disease [394] in elderly diabetes patients. While AMPK activation has been shown to reduce Aβ production in rat cortical neurons [395], and to reduce tau hyperphosphorylation and apoptosis in palmitate-treated neuroblastoma cells [396], this type of intervention may share similar timing-related concerns with rapamycin as it primarily acts upstream to initiate autophagy rather than directly repairing lysosomal degradative functions. Additionally, some evidence suggests prolonged exposure to AMPK activators may be neurotoxic [397], marking a major caveat of this approach.

As an alternative approach to mitigate metabolic dysregulation in AD, upregulation or activation of TFEB has yielded promising results in animal models. Crossing the P301S tauopathy mouse model with a TFEB overexpression model produced several beneficial effects, including decreased filamentous tau, increased autophagy, restored cortical and hippocampal neuron count, reduced lipofuscin signal, and restored spatial and working memory [398]. Increasing TFEB expression also increased lysosomal activity and enhanced uptake of extracellular tau by WT primary murine astrocytes, reducing overall tau spread, while TFEB−/− yielded opposite results [399]. These observations were reproduced in vivo in the PS19 mouse model, where astrocytic AAV-mediated TFEB overexpression reduced hippocampal tau pathology and spread [399]. Importantly, AAV-mediated TFEB overexpression in the rTg4510 tauopathy mouse model selectively reduced hyperphosphorylated and misfolded tau species without impacting physiological tau, resulting in reduced neurofibrillary tangle pathology as well as improvement of synaptic and behavioral outcomes [400]. Similarly, viral-mediated TFEB expression in the hippocampal astrocytes of APP/PS1 mice reduced Aβ burden by increasing Aβ degradation [401], suggesting that enhancing autophagy induction can stimulate glial uptake and degradation of extracellular Aβ.

Somewhat counterintuitively, elevated levels of TFEB are found in brain tissue from dementia patients with tau pathology [399], possibly as a compensatory response triggered by the accumulation of protein aggregates to stimulate lysosomal degradation. This raises the question of whether further TFEB activation would remain beneficial in late-stage disease, particularly given evidence that downstream lysosomal function is already compromised. Although TFEB directly upregulates expression of V-ATPase subunits and lysosomal hydrolases in addition to its autophagy-inducing effects, thereby increasing availability of lysosomal degradative machinery, these transcriptional changes do not guarantee the rescue of functional degradation, as many converging facets of lysosomal dysfunction are present at later disease stages, including lysosomal membrane permeabilization. The temporal dynamics of TFEB regulation throughout AD progression is not well-defined, and establishing whether TFEB upregulation is an early adaptive response to perturbations in metabolic-lysosomal pathways or a late-stage response to protein aggregation will be critical to inform therapeutic strategies. Alongside these considerations, TFEB acts as a broad regulator of many lysosomal, metabolic, and immune genes [18], which presumably have a wide range of cellular impacts, complicating its utility as a therapeutic target for neurodegenerative disease.

In addition to mTORC1, AMPK, and TFEB pathways, cholesterol metabolism is another important facet of AD risk and pathology [402, 403]. Cholesterol accumulation within both neurons and glia can drive lysosomal dysfunction, mitochondrial stress, and inflammation, and may interact with aging-related metabolic alterations to amplify AD risk [402–404]. Notably, AMPK activation reduces cholesterol levels, and AMPK inactivation can cause cholesterol accumulation [395], relating the AMPK/mTORC1 axis to lipid metabolism.

The E4 allele of APOE is the greatest genetic risk factor for sporadic AD. APOE is primarily expressed in astrocytes and functions to transport cholesterol and other lipids to neurons [405, 406]. On average, APOE4 carriers have higher cholesterol levels [407], suggesting potential disease mechanisms driven by lipid dysregulation. In primary mouse neurons, cholesterol enrichment impaired lysosomal clearance and led to an accumulation of damaged mitochondria [408]. Interestingly, brain tissue from AD patients also showed high levels of mitochondrial cholesterol [408]. The authors propose a mechanism whereby cholesterol accumulation-induced defects in the recruitment of autophagy receptor optineurin lead to mitophagy impairment. Consistent with this, APOE4-mediated cholesterol accumulation in AD patient-derived astrocytes reduced mitophagic flux, with cholesterol-reducing treatments restoring autophagy levels and mitochondrial dynamics [409]. In mice expressing human APOE4, cholesterol accumulation was associated with cellular senescence, a key feature of AD, and resulted in mTORC1 activation, lysosomal dysfunction, and increased lipofuscin staining [381]. Reducing cholesterol levels was effective in reversing senescence markers, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficiencies in these APOE4-TR mice [381]. These findings highlight the effects of APOE allele-specific cholesterol metabolism and may eventually inform targeted AD therapeutics for high-risk APOE4 carriers.

Lysosomal metabolic signaling dysregulation in Parkinson’s disease

Metabolic dysregulation is a key feature of PD. Midbrain dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra, which degenerate in PD, are particularly susceptible to metabolic stress due to high energy demands, long sparsely myelinated axons, extensive arborization, limited astrocytic support, and low spare respiratory capacity [410–413]. In PD, mTORC1 activity is dysregulated [367], and mTORC1 protein levels are increased in postmortem PD brain tissue [414]. However, mTORC1 signaling is still largely undefined in the context of PD, and mTORC1 modulation has demonstrated conflicting neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects in various PD models [415]. Several studies report that mTORC1 is upregulated in PD models. For instance, paraquat, a toxic herbicide and environmental risk factor for PD, increased mTORC1 levels in WT mice, reducing autophagic flux and inducing αSyn accumulation [414]. Additionally, pathologic species of αSyn have been shown to sequester a negative regulator of mTORC1, resulting in mTORC1 hyperactivity and decreased protein clearance in αSyn pre-formed fibril mouse models, whereas inhibition of mTORC1 ameliorated dopaminergic neuron loss [416]. Consistent with the seemingly pathological role of upregulated mTORC1 in PD, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 has demonstrated beneficial effects in multiple in vitro and in vivo PD models, improving clearance of autophagic vacuoles and reducing αSyn pathology [415, 417–419]. Importantly, these results suggest mTORC1 inhibition may successfully improve autophagic flux rather than autophagy induction alone; however, defining the temporal dynamics of this intervention will be important for better understanding the progression of metabolic dysregulation in PD and identifying an effective therapeutic window.

Conversely, other studies have reported that mTORC1 activity is downregulated in PD models. In this framework, activating mTORC1 could provide neuroprotective effects through upregulation of cell survival pathways such as protein synthesis and neuronal repair [420]. The MPTP toxin model of PD has been reported to decrease mTORC1 activity [421, 422], likely through oxidative stress-mediated inactivation of protein kinase B [423]. Similarly, in PD mimetic toxin models, mTORC1 activity was decreased, and mTORC1 overexpression protected neurons from cell death [423]. Another study found that mTORC1 activation had protective effects on oxidative stress-induced dopaminergic cell death, seemingly through the depletion of accumulated autophagic vacuoles [424]. In this model, the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin exacerbated cell death. mTORC1 activity was also found to be decreased in a model of nucleolar damage in dopaminergic neurons [421], and genetic deletion of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) activated mTORC1 and protected dopaminergic neurons against neurotoxic insults in both genetic and toxin-based PD mouse models [425].

These seemingly conflicting findings may be reconciled by recognizing that mTORC1 is regulated by a diverse set of post-translational modifications that differentially impact its nutrient responsivity and overall activity. mTORC1 likely serves as a metabolic switch between promoting pro-survival pathways or neurodegenerative pathways depending on the cellular environment. Limitations of the PD models used in these studies must also be considered, with toxin-based models failing to recapitulate the slow, progressive nature of human PD and often omitting the effects of aging entirely, and genetic or preformed fibril models also failing to capture the complexity of sporadic disease. Thus, evidence from postmortem PD patient brains should be weighed as stronger evidence for mTORC1 overactivation in PD. However, the lack of consensus among these findings, together with the multifaceted role of mTORC1 as a master regulator of essential cellular functions, makes mTORC1 modulation a challenging therapeutic approach.

Alternatively, therapeutic AMPK activation is an active area of PD research. AMPK functions to promote antioxidant pathways, anti-inflammatory pathways, mitochondrial biogenesis, mTORC1 inhibition, and autophagy/mitophagy, which together may help neutralize the chronic metabolic, oxidative, and inflammatory stress observed in PD [360, 426]. AMPK activity decreases with age, likely becoming insufficient to buffer the growing oxidative stress that occurs with PD progression [410]. AMPK activation is also decreased in postmortem PD patient brain tissues, and conditional deletion of AMPK in mice has been shown to promote age-dependent neurodegeneration of dopaminergic neurons and enhance vulnerability to 6-hydroxdopamine toxicity [427].

In agreement with these findings, pharmacological or genetic AMPK activation has been shown to improve dopaminergic neuron survival in PD models by reducing cellular energy consumption through inhibition of anabolic pathways, enhancing autophagic clearance of αSyn aggregates, and increasing ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation [428]. Metformin activates AMPK and has consistently been found to increase survival of dopaminergic neurons, conferring protection against MPTP-induced neurodegeneration in vivo [123]. However, there is conflicting evidence as to whether AMPK activation is necessary for the neuroprotective effects of metformin [429, 430], and the mechanisms underlying metformin-induced neuroprotection have not been fully elucidated. Interestingly, the green tea derivative epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) has also been shown to ameliorate dopaminergic and mitochondrial dysfunction via activation of AMPK pathways in PD mutant Drosophila, with ablation of AMPK activity exacerbating neuron loss [431].

It is important to note that hyperactive or prolonged AMPK activation can also be harmful, leading to energy depletion and activation of cell death pathways [432], introducing a key consideration for therapeutic modulation. These effects may partially explain how oxidative stress, an activator of AMPK and hallmark of PD, contributes to neurodegenerative pathways [433]. Similarly, αSyn aggregates have been shown to overactivate AMPK via the sequestration of an AMPK inhibitor, triggering cell death pathways [434]. Based on these findings, it is possible that AMPK is depressed throughout much of PD progression, but becomes upregulated in later stages and switches to promoting apoptotic pathways. This would make the timing of AMPK-modulating therapeutic interventions imperative to avoid further harmful effects of AMPK overactivation.

In PD models, TFEB activity appears to be blunted, impairing aggregated protein clearance [122]. TFEB overexpression is widely considered neuroprotective, with one study demonstrating that it prevents dopaminergic cell death in the MPTP mouse model of PD [435], and additional studies producing similar results in other PD models [121, 122, 436]. Furthermore, enhancing TFEB activity via genetic overexpression or small-molecule activators has been shown to reduce αSyn accumulation, improve lysosomal function, and diminish dopaminergic neuron loss [120]. Moreover, TFEB activation may compensate for GBA1- or LRRK2-associated lysosomal impairments by promoting autophagic lysosome reformation and rebalancing metabolic flux [437, 438]. Despite these promising results in mouse models, the nonspecific and widespread functionality of TFEB as a master transcriptional regulator may make TFEB a challenging therapeutic target. However, this same functional breadth may also make TFEB an attractive target, given that a diverse array of lysosomal, autophagic, and metabolic pathways are dysregulated in PD. Notably, the ability to concurrently enhance expression of lysosomal degradative components and autophagosome formation components positions TFEB modulation as a promising strategy to restore lysosomal clearance, particularly in early-to-middle-stage PD, when lysosomal dysfunction is not yet too severe to respond effectively.

At the molecular level, lysosomes in neurons, astrocytes and microglia share conserved core machinery, including V-ATPase-dependent acidification [439], lysosomal hydrolases [5], ion channels [440] and dynamic fusion cycles with endosomes and autophagosomes [441, 442]. As mentioned previously, the post-mitotic nature of neurons necessitates highly efficient lysosomal clearance of substrates. Neuronal morphology, characterized by extensive arborization, large cytoplasmic area, and distinct polarization, also produces a greater dependence on efficient transport of lysosomes, endosomes, and autophagic vacuoles between compartments. The exceptionally long distances organelles and cargo must travel render neurons particularly susceptible to perturbations in the ALP. Under normal conditions, neuronal lysosomes are concentrated around the soma and additionally present in dendrites, with lysosomal positioning determined by anterograde and retrograde microtubule-based transport facilitated by molecular motors including kinesin and dynein [443]. Of note, lysosomes more distal from the soma appear to have less efficient acidification and degradative capacity [444], possibly due to inefficiencies in distal endolysosomal maturation or lysosomal protein transport from the perinuclear Golgi network. However, consistent anterograde transport of mature, highly degradative lysosomes to distal processes including axons, has been observed [30]. Thus, the buildup of axonal endosomes and autophagic vacuoles observed in neurodegenerative diseases is likely due to a combination of defective anterograde transport of mature lysosomes and defective retrograde transport of endosomes and autophagic vacuoles.

Compared to neurons, astrocytes have less polarized morphology and contain a more even distribution and higher abundance of lysosomes, which are slightly less acidic compared to mature neurons [445]. Astrocytes provide homeostatic and metabolic support to the neural environment, and their lysosomes are optimized for processing endocytosed extracellular material, including neurotransmitters, lipids, and neuron-derived waste, in addition to their own intracellular waste products [446–448]. The astrocytic endolysosomal system also contributes to the recycling and secretion of factors that support nearby neuronal and endothelial cells via lysosomal exocytosis, exosome release, and constitutive exocytosis. Of note, alterations in these processes have been associated with the onset of neurodegenerative disease [449].

Although all cells in the CNS are thought to undergo lysosomal exocytosis, astrocytes use this process for unique functions such as releasing molecules that regulate oxidative stress [450], inflammation [451], and synaptic plasticity [452], providing a means to dynamically respond to the needs of surrounding cells. Astrocytic lysosomal exocytosis-mediated ATP release has been linked to stress signaling, with astrocyte-specific depletion of lysosomal exocytosis shown to mediate depression-like symptoms in mice [453]. As such, loss of lysosomal function within astrocytes can have severe downstream effects. For instance, astrocyte-specific lysosomal storage defects cause neurodegeneration of healthy cortical neurons and downstream behavioral defects in mice [454]. As astrocytic lysosomes play important roles in the clearance of protein aggregates, they are also susceptible to the toxic effects that protein aggregates have on autophagic flux. In one study, αSyn overexpression in an immortalized astrocyte line resulted in autophagy inhibition [455]. Notably, pharmacological enhancement of astrocytic autophagic flux has been demonstrated to have neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in models of PD and AD [456, 457].

Microglia, as the immune-effector cells of the CNS, are optimized for the phagocytic degradation of extracellular material and exhibit lysosomal functions that distinguish them from other neural cell types. At baseline, microglial lysosomal pH is typically higher than that of other cell types, becoming more acidic upon “activation” such as exposure to protein aggregates and proinflammatory stimuli, resulting in enhanced lysosomal degradative capacity [458, 459]. These qualities enable efficient clearance of protein aggregates, debris, apoptotic cells, pathogens, and engulfed synapses as microglia conduct immune surveillance and synaptic pruning, among other functions [460–463]. Importantly, microglia are highly heterogeneous and dynamically adopt different states that exhibit unique morphology, gene expression and functions, making microglial lysosomal activity highly state-dependent [464, 465].

Microglial lysosomal exocytosis plays an important role in synaptic pruning. Cathepsin S, a lysosomal cysteine protease primarily expressed in microglia and released extracellularly via lysosomal exocytosis, has been shown to degrade components of the extracellular matrix that affect synapse formation [466], and CTSS−/− mice display significantly higher neuronal spine densities than WT controls [467]. Furthermore, microglia have been found to perform digestive exophagy, a process by which they create extracellular acidic digestive compartments, termed lysosomal synapses, in order to break down large molecules that are difficult to phagocytose [468]. This mechanism relies on microglial lysosomal exocytosis to deliver acid hydrolases into the specialized extracellular space.

Dysfunction of microglial lysosomes has been proposed to underlie the pathology of several neurodegenerative diseases. Microglia degrade extracellular Aβ by either phagocytosis and autophagy or digestive exophagy, both of which may become impaired in AD [312, 468]. However, intracellular processing of Aβ can itself disrupt autophagic flux and activate an inflammasome complex, leading to neuronal damage [469]. This establishes a positive feedback loop in which neuronal apoptosis further challenges microglial clearance mechanisms. As extracellular Aβ accumulation precedes neuronal degeneration and triggers a rapid microglial inflammatory response [470], early enhancement of microglial phagocytosis and autophagy, or digestive exophagy, could be a promising therapeutic strategy for slowing AD progression. In PD, αSyn released by neurons is engulfed by microglia and degraded by autophagy [471], but αSyn overload can cause microglial lysosomal stress and membrane leakage. In both AD and PD, neuroinflammation is a key disease feature which is largely mediated by microglia [472, 473]. Thus, microglial lysosomal dysfunction contributes to neuroinflammation, and inflammatory stress itself can further exacerbate lysosomal dysfunction.

In summary, lysosomes serve as central hubs for the maintenance of cellular homeostasis in the CNS, with functions tailored to the specific needs of each cell type. In neurons, lysosomes are essential for the long-range transport and degradation of intracellular cargo, supporting proteostasis and neuronal survival over the course of decades, and their dysfunction makes neurons particularly vulnerable to toxic protein aggregation and degeneration. Astrocytic lysosomes, in contrast, are optimized for metabolic support, extracellular clearance, and regulated secretion, enabling astrocytes to maintain neuronal homeostasis and respond to insults such as stress, injury, and infection. Microglial lysosomes mediate the clearance of extracellular debris, such as in synaptic pruning and aggregate clearance. These digested materials contribute to proinflammatory signaling seen in neurodegenerative disease. There are several additional CNS cell types not discussed in depth in this review including oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes, in which lysosomal dysfunction can disrupt myelin integrity, vascular clearance, and blood–brain barrier homeostasis. Across these cell types, disruptions in key lysosomal functions are increasingly recognized as contributors to neurodegenerative disease. Understanding the cell type-specific roles of lysosomes not only provides important insight into CNS mechanisms under physiological and pathological conditions but also highlights potential lysosome-targeted therapeutic approaches to slow or prevent neurodegeneration.

Lysosomal storage disorders and neurodegeneration

Lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) are a group of more than 70 inborn errors of metabolism broadly characterized by lysosomal dysfunction resulting in excessive accumulation, or storage, of cellular components and/or substrates within lysosomes [474]. These diseases stem from mutations in lysosome-associated genes which disrupt normal lysosomal degradation, leading to the buildup of undegraded or incompletely degraded macromolecules within lysosomes. Regardless of the specific materials accumulated, this lysosomal storage causes cellular dysfunction, ultimately leading to cell death. The post-mitotic state of neurons leaves them particularly vulnerable to lysosomal storage defects, as they are unable to dilute their storage material through cell division. It is therefore unsurprising that most LSDs lead to progressive neurodegeneration, in addition to varied effects on other organ systems [474–477]. As most LSDs present with infantile or childhood onset, taken together, LSDs are the most common cause of neurodegeneration among pediatric patients [478, 479]. While symptoms vary by disorder, common neurological manifestations of LSDs include progressive cognitive and motor impairment, developmental and psychomotor regression, seizures, severe sensory deficits such as vision loss, and psychiatric and behavioral abnormalities [474].

While the genetic defects underlying individual LSDs are heterogenous, they can be generally categorized as leading to either (i) defects in lysosomal protein synthesis and trafficking, which can be enzymatic or non-enzymatic, or (ii) defects in lysosomal biogenesis, such as enzyme activation or endosomal trafficking to lysosomes [477]. LSD-causing mutations commonly occur in genes that encode lysosomal enzymes, causing enzymatic loss of function that leads to abnormal lysosomal accumulation of that enzyme’s substrate(s). As such, many LSDs are grouped based on shared biochemical characteristics of their associated lysosomal storage materials [474]; (i) sphingolipidoses (e.g., Gaucher disease, Niemann-Pick disease types A and B) lead to accumulation of sphingolipids [480, 481], (ii) mucopolysaccharidoses (e.g. Sanfilippo syndrome, Morquio syndrome) are characterized by accumulation of glycosaminoglycans [482–484], (iii) glycoproteinoses (e.g., Galactosialidosis, α- and β-Mannosidosis) result in glycoprotein accumulation [485–487], (iv) glycogen storage disease II (also known as Pompe disease) is aptly named for the abnormal storage of glycogen [488], and (v) lipid storage diseases (e.g., Wolman disease, cholesterol ester storage disease) lead to excessive storage of lipids [489]. Enzymatic mutations can also cause LSDs by disrupting a lysosomal enzyme’s ability to perform post-translational modifications of target substrates, as occurs in Mucolipidosis II/III and multiple sulfatase deficiency [490, 491]. The neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses (NCLs) are another group of LSDs which, despite arising from mutations in distinct genes, all result in lysosomal accumulation of the autofluorescent storage material referred to as ceroid lipofuscin [492–494]. Alternatively, some LSDs are caused by mutations in genes that encode for lysosomal integral membrane proteins (e.g., LAMP2 (Danon disease) [495] and MCOLN1 (Mucolipidosis IV) [496]).

Most LSDs are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and are individually rare in the population. However, when taken together, LSDs affect approximately 1 in 5000 live births [497]. The number of heterozygous carriers for LSD-causing mutations is therefore far greater in the general population. While carriers for LSD mutations are asymptomatic for the associated LSD, these heterozygous mutations may confer greater risk for common neurodegenerative diseases [498].

In perhaps the most prominent example of this phenomenon, PD patients exhibit an increased frequency of mutations in the GBA gene, encoding the lysosomal enzyme β-glucocerebrosidase, relative to healthy controls [94, 499, 500]. Biallelic LoF mutations in GBA are responsible for Gaucher disease, a recessively inherited neurodegenerative LSD [481], and relatives of Gaucher disease patients carrying heterozygous GBA mutations have an increased incidence of PD [501, 502]. Furthermore, PD patients carrying GBA mutations are more likely to have affected family members, demonstrate atypical clinical presentations, and present with earlier disease onset [94, 499–501].

The glycoprotein progranulin (PGRN) is another LSD-related protein implicated in more common neurodegenerative diseases. Biallelic LoF mutations in the GRN gene encoding PGRN are associated with the CLN11 subtype of NCL, a rare neurodegenerative LSD that primarily impacts patients in early adulthood [503–505]. Alternatively, heterozygous LoF mutations in GRN are known to cause a familial version of the common neurodegenerative disorder frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 inclusions (FTLD-TDP), which presents later in life than CLN11-type NCL [505]. The clinical implications of GRN mutations are varied, and this field is actively expanding with mounting associations reported between GRN variants and other neurodegenerative diseases, including primary progressive aphasia, limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE), and subsets of ALS, PD, and AD [506–508].

Biallelic LoF mutations in MCOLN1, the gene encoding lysosomal ion channel TRPML1, cause the neurodegenerative LSD Mucolipidosis type IV (MLIV) [509, 510]. Interestingly, studies have also implicated TRPML1 dysregulation in pathogenic mechanisms underlying several neurodegenerative diseases including AD, ALS, and muscular dystrophy, as well as HIV-associated cognitive decline [281, 511–515].

These examples highlight the genetic similarities between LSDs and common neurodegenerative diseases including AD, PD, ALS and FTD. With these areas of overlap in mind, further analysis of lysosomal gene variants in patients with age-associated neurodegenerative diseases will help to elucidate the complex factors contributing to the development of common neurodegenerative diseases. Deeper understanding of the etiologies of lysosomal storage disorders, especially those which primarily impact neurons, will also enhance our knowledge of the lysosomal mechanisms contributing to neurodegenerative processes. Such knowledge will create new possibilities for lysosome-targeted therapeutic interventions for common age-associated neurodegenerative diseases, in addition to advancing the limited treatment options for LSDs.

Conclusions

Mounting evidence implicates perturbations within the ALP in the development and progression of numerous age-associated neurodegenerative diseases. While this review focuses primarily on the role of ALP dysfunction in the pathogenesis of AD and PD, as these are two of the most prevalent age-associated neurodegenerative diseases, ALP dysfunction is also a key feature of many other neurodegenerative diseases. The primary neurodegeneration-associated mechanisms of ALP dysfunction include autophagic flux dysregulation, CMA decline, disrupted endolysosomal trafficking, lysosomal deacidification and ion imbalance, metabolic signaling dysfunction, and impaired clearance of lysosomal contents. Alterations in these essential cellular processes contribute to the proteostatic failure and pathological protein aggregation that are characteristic of many neurodegenerative diseases. The post-mitotic state of mature neurons, coupled with their distinct polarization, highly branched and elongated morphology, and high energetic demands, makes them especially susceptible to ALP dysfunction and the resulting buildup of protein aggregates and cellular debris, highlighting the particular importance of proper ALP function in maintaining neuronal health.

Integration of the various neurodegenerative mechanisms that stem from ALP dysregulation, as delineated in this review, leads to the overarching conclusion that ALP dysfunction is not merely a downstream consequence of neurodegeneration but is instead an early and active driver of neurodegenerative disease. Common age-associated neurodegenerative diseases, including the myriad subtypes of AD and PD, converge on shared autophagic and lysosomal vulnerabilities despite significant differences in causal mutations, initiating events, pathological hallmarks, cell type-specific and regional vulnerabilities, and disease symptomology. Furthermore, several disease-associated ALP defects have been shown to result in self-perpetuating feedback loops in which they both contribute to pathogenesis and exacerbate disease progression, as discussed here in the contexts of autophagic impairments, aberrant endolysosomal trafficking, metabolic dysregulation, lysosomal deacidification and ion imbalance, oxidative stress, and proinflammatory signaling. ALP dysfunction has therefore emerged as a central node linking numerous disease-associated processes that collectively drive neurodegeneration.

Despite these converging elements, differences between distinct neurodegenerative diseases, and between disease subtypes, may arise in part from regional and cell type-specific variations in ALP function, in combination with distinct genetic variants and environmental insults. Thus, additional research is needed to clarify why certain individuals, brain regions, and cellular populations are more susceptible to ALP dysfunction and neurodegeneration across a wide variety of disease contexts.

The recognition of ALP dysfunction as an early and central mechanism underlying neurodegeneration makes it an attractive therapeutic target for neurodegenerative disease. However, the complex interplay between cellular and molecular mechanisms governing autophagic clearance, endolysosomal trafficking, metabolic signaling, lysosomal acidification, and ion balance poses a significant challenge to the identification of effective and targetable intervention points. This task is further complicated by the presence of compensatory mechanisms which may alter the directionality of observed dysfunction throughout the course of disease. There are additional concerns regarding differential effects of modulating ALP-related mechanisms at different time points in disease progression, as noted in the context of autophagy induction where poorly timed interventions have the potential to worsen pathology if lysosomal degradative capacity is already severely compromised. These obstacles highlight the need for further research to better distinguish individual ALP contributions to neurodegenerative disease processes, and to map out how these distinct functions interact and change over the course of disease progression. Such work will support the development of enhanced ALP-targeted therapeutic strategies for the slowing or prevention of neurodegenerative disease.
