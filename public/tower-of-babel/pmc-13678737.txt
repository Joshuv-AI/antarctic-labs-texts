Anti-PLA2R positivity does not exclude malignancy in membranous nephropathy: lessons from the course after tumor treatment
Authors: Saide Elif Güllülü Boz, Abdülmecit Yıldız
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Anti-phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) positivity is known to occur in malignancy-associated membranous nephropathy (MN) [1, 2]. Less is known about the course of these patients over time. It is unclear whether MN activity parallels that of the tumor, or what happens when immunosuppression is increased before the cancer is found. We report three anti-PLA2R-positive patients whose courses after tumor treatment differed (Table 1).

The first patient had an anti-PLA2R level of 106 RU/ml. MN remitted with corticosteroids and cyclosporine, and treatment was stopped. Thirteen months after diagnosis, MN relapsed, and testicular seminoma was diagnosed at the same time. He underwent orchiectomy and radiotherapy, and positron emission tomography showed no residual disease. However, the relapse persisted, and anti-PLA2R rose to 760 RU/ml. Complete remission was achieved only with rituximab. The tumor may have triggered the relapse, but MN remained active after the tumor had been eradicated.

The second patient was a 67-year-old smoker with an anti-PLA2R level of 839 RU/ml. Because of his age and mesangial expansion on biopsy, a malignancy work-up was started. Computed tomography was normal, but he declined endoscopy. MN did not respond to corticosteroids with cyclosporine, rituximab, or two courses of cyclophosphamide. During treatment, he developed chronic diarrhea, and colonoscopy revealed early-stage colon adenocarcinoma, 9 months after the diagnosis of MN. Immunosuppression was stopped. After resection and chemotherapy, a partial remission occurred without immunosuppression. Nephrotic-range proteinuria recurred later, although the cancer remained in remission. In this patient, the timing supported malignancy-associated MN [3], whereas the very high anti-PLA2R titer favored primary MN. The course suggests that tumor-related and PLA2R-driven activity overlapped. A delayed response to rituximab appears unlikely, because there was no response at 3, 6, and 9 months, but it cannot be excluded.

The third patient had an anti-PLA2R level of 90 RU/ml, and biopsy showed focal segmental cellular proliferation. Kidney function was declining, so cyclophosphamide was started before the malignancy work-up was completed. The screening tests performed until then were unremarkable. Within 3 months, he presented with headache, and cranial magnetic resonance imaging showed a metastatic mass. On re-evaluation, a cutaneous melanoma was found in the inguinal region. The patient died 9 months after the diagnosis of MN.

Three observations deserve emphasis. First, all three patients were anti-PLA2R positive, and the titer was very high (839 RU/ml) in the second patient. This titer favored a diagnosis of primary MN and contributed to the escalation of immunosuppression while the tumor remained undetected. Second, two patients received an alkylating agent before the cancer was diagnosed. In one, endoscopy had been declined; in the other, treatment could not wait for the work-up. Third, the course after tumor treatment was more informative than antibody status or timing. MN persisted despite tumor eradication in the first patient. In the second, remission occurred without immunosuppression and was followed by relapse. These courses suggest that PLA2R-associated autoimmunity and a tumor-related contribution can coexist and interact. Each patient also had a feature that is unusual in primary MN: low serum C3, mesangial expansion, and segmental proliferation, respectively.

Antigen-based classification, including glomerular PLA2R staining and mass spectrometry, could clarify such relationships [2, 4]. Neither these techniques nor serial anti-PLA2R measurements were available at our center, and oncological care was provided elsewhere. In such settings, the clinical context and the disease course guide the diagnosis. We therefore suggest that anti-PLA2R positivity should not replace age- and risk-appropriate cancer screening. Screening should be completed as early as possible, ideally before immunosuppression is intensified, although this is not always feasible. It should be repeated when MN is unexpectedly resistant to treatment [5].
