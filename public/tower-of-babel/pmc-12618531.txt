Systems biology modelling based evaluation of omega-3 formulation in managing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk
Authors: Sanjaay Balakrishnan, Abha Saxena, Madhusmita Mahapatra, Aparna Damle, Shyam Ramakrishnan, Palaniyamma Durairaj, Venkatesh Kareenhalli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Natural therapies for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease management often use nutraceuticals and herbal supplements to improve lipid profiles. This in-silico study examined the effects of Omega-3 fatty acids, specifically Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), on lipid metabolism using systems biology-based models. The pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of EPA (1 g) and DHA (1 g) were analysed to understand their lipid-lowering mechanisms. The study also assessed the impact of omega-3 fatty acid-based formulation, (EPA 180 mg and DHA 120 mg), on lipid biomarkers in a simulated disease population. Over six months, the model predicted a 14.7% reduction in triglycerides, 1.7% reduction in total cholesterol, 3.7% increase in LDL, and a 22.38% increase in HDL. Non-traditional markers improved markedly, with triglyceride-to-HDL ratio reduced by 31%, total cholesterol-to-HDL by 21%, and LDL-to-HDL by 16%. A population-level subgroup analysis showed that individuals with high baseline lipid ratios (triglyceride-to-HDL > 8, total cholesterol-to-HDL > 12, LDL-to-HDL > 8) could achieve > 35% reductions within six months. These in-silico findings highlight subgroups with better predicted efficacy, guiding inclusion–exclusion criteria for future trials. Further, the study highlights the therapeutic potential of EPA and DHA in improving lipid profiles and managing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23677-6.

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain predominant causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide1. Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are the risk factor of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) necessitating urgent diagnosis and treatment. AMI, commonly known as a heart attack, occurs due to prolonged ischemia caused by sudden and complete blockage of a coronary artery by a thrombus or plaque. Conversely, AIS occurs in the cerebellum and results from arterial obstructions that directly hinder blood and oxygen delivery to this brain region2,3.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2019, an estimated 17.9 million people died from CVDs, constituting 32% of all global deaths4. The majority of these deaths (85%) were attributable to heart attacks and strokes. By 2021, the global mortality due to CVDs rose to 20.5 million deaths, accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths globally5. AMI affects nearly 3 million people worldwide, while AIS cases and related deaths increased to 7.86 million and 3.15 million, respectively, in 20206,7.

Patients diagnosed with CVDs exhibit elevated levels of lipids, including triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), accompanied by decreased levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL)8,9. High serum total cholesterol, especially in association with LDL, is recognized as a primary contributor to CVDs9,10. According to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, optimal LDL cholesterol levels should be 100 mg/dL or lower, while levels between 100 and 129 mg/dL are considered near optimal, 130–159 mg/dL are borderline high, and levels of 160 mg/dL or higher is considered as a high risk. Triglyceride levels below 150 mg/dL are ideal, with levels over 200 mg/dL considered high risk; values in between are considered borderline. For total cholesterol, the optimal level for a healthy individual is less than 200 mg/dL. Similarly, healthy HDL (good) cholesterol levels should exceed 40 mg/dL for men and 50 mg/dL for women; levels below these thresholds are considered risk factors for various metabolic diseases10,11.

While traditional lipid biomarkers are independent risk factors for CVD and stroke, non-traditional lipid indices also play crucial roles12. The LDL-to-HDL ratio has been identified as a superior predictor of these diseases13. AMI, characterized by a higher triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, demonstrates stronger predictive power compared to total cholesterol-to-HDL and LDL-to-HDL ratios.

The association between elevated triglycerides and HDL levels in increasing CVD risk arises from elevated plasma levels of triglyceride-rich very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL). These lipoproteins facilitate the formation of small, dense LDL particles during lipid exchange as well as lipolysis. Thus, accumulation of these LDL particles forms HDL particles, closes the atherogenic process, thereby elevating the risk of CVD14. While total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio is primarily associated with ischemic stroke risk in men, triglyceride is a significant predictor in women12. Among non-traditional lipid variables, a high triglyceride-to-HDL ratio and an increased total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio are linked to increase vascular risk post-stroke, with only the elevated triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, associated with stroke recurrence risk15.

Recent years have witnessed a growing emphasis on identifying natural compounds with cardiovascular health benefits and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms16,17. Nutraceuticals like omega-3 (ω−3) polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) has gained importance due to their numerous advantages in promoting health and reducing disease risks. Omega 3 fatty acids such as Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) are available in the fatty fish, white lean fish and other marine mammals and are considered safe to use18,19. These nutraceuticals exhibit cardioprotective properties by addressing lipid dysregulation. These nutraceuticals reduce triglyceride levels primarily by inhibiting hepatic synthesis through inhibition of 1,2-diglyceride acyltransferase, which converts diacylglycerides into triglycerides. Additionally, n-3 PUFAs have a high affinity for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor - alpha (PPAR-α), a transcription factor, when activated, promotes the synthesis of enzymes involved in lipid catabolism. Apart from this, these active ingredients enhance fatty acid oxidation in peripheral tissues. Furthermore, ω −3 PUFAs suppress the activity of Sterol regulatory element binding protein-1c (SREBP1c), a regulator of genes responsible for fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis. Beside lipid regulation, these compounds possess anti-inflammatory, antiarrhythmic, antithrombotic, and blood pressure-lowering properties20.

While numerous health benefits of omega-3 PUFAs have been reported in the literature, there are also some controversies surrounding their efficacy and certain advantages to human health21. Thus, the current study aims to narrow down the gap by investigating how the formulation of EPA and DHA collectively influences lipid parameters that impact individual’s health. Systems biology-based mathematical modelling and simulation studies facilitate integration and provide a perspective of the mechanisms that contribute to lipid imbalance. Thus, the study aims in identifying the roles and impact of EPA and DHA on lipid metabolism, their influence on lipids and the efficacy of EPA and DHA based supplementation in enhancing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular health. The results of this in-silico study can form a basis for clinical trial design and planning.

Results

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic model benchmarking

The pharmacokinetics profile of 560 mg of EPA and 362 mg of DHA22 were obtained from the literature. These profiles along with their dosage were used to fit the parameters of two compartment model with Michaelis Menten and linear rate of elimination (Supplementary Figure S1 online). The predicted profiles of these two dosages were within the standard errors reported in literature as shown in Fig. 1a and b. The optimized pharmacokinetic parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S1 online. For validation, the EPA and DHA profiles were simulated at longer time periods to identify the saturation points. The overall half-life of EPA and DHA were identified to be 18.56 h and 69.65 h respectively (Supplementary Figure S2 online). Similarly, the EPA starts to reach steady-state concentrations by ~ 30 days and DHA reaches by ~ 50 days, which aligns with literature reported physiological timelines. In the pharmacodynamic liver model, the model parameters were altered to reproduce cardiovascular conditions by mimicking unhealthy lipid levels (Supplementary Figure S3 online). Pharmacodynamic parameter tuning for the effects of EPA and DHA on liver model was guided by lipid profile changes reported across multiple clinical trials evaluating EPA, DHA, and their combined administration23–27. Individual PK profiles for EPA and DHA were first generated for literature reported doses and these profiles were integrated into the liver model. Model parameters were then iteratively adjusted to reproduce the characteristic lipid responses for EPA and DHA when administered separately, as well as their synergistic effects when co-administered. This benchmarking against outcomes from five independent clinical studies ensured that the model captured both compound-specific effects and interaction dynamics, enabling physiologically consistent simulation of lipid metabolism under varied dosing conditions. The predicted and experimental fold changes were in close agreement as depicted in Fig. 1c. The points close to the diagonal indicate good prediction and the error increases as they move away from the diagonal. Almost all the biomarkers are predicted in all three simulations with less than 5% error, except for triglycerides from experiment 3 (Fig. 1c). Still, the combination effects of EPA and DHA were predicted good.

Influence of EPA and DHA on liver metabolic processes

The models were simulated with 1 g of EPA, 1 g of DHA and combination of both for a duration of 1 month on a diseased population. The study observed a significant decrease in the concentration of triglycerides, total cholesterols, a significant increase in LDL levels and an insignificant increase in HDL-C with the use of the EPA, DHA individually and as well as combination (Fig. 2a and d, Supplementary Table S2 online). The concentration of triglycerides which was 1.46-fold higher than healthy levels, reduced to 1.37, 1.31 and 1.22-fold due to EPA, DHA and their combination respectively (Fig. 2e). Overall, on average the combination was able to reduce the triglyceride levels by 37 mg/dL (Fig. 2a). Compared to the changes in triglycerides, the changes in total cholesterol were significantly less, where the combination of EPA and DHA reduced the cholesterol levels only by 3.5 mg/dL (Fig. 2b). The HDL was the next best improved lipid due to this intervention. The diseased HDL levels increased from 0.82-fold with respect to healthy to 0.91, 0.86, and 0.95-fold due to EPA, DHA and combination respectively (Fig. 2e). LDL levels had an average increase of 7.6 mg/dL due to the combination. Of all the lipids, triglycerides improved significantly and of the two omega fatty acids, DHA was the major contributor.

The flux dynamics analysis provides a deeper understanding on the roles of these omega fatty acids on alterations in liver metabolism (Fig. 3). The simulation revealed that the administration of 1 gram each of EPA and DHA led to reductions and activations of various genes and metabolic pathways in our mathematical model. Majorly affected pathways include fat and triglyceride synthesis. The conversion of Malonyl-CoA to FFA has reduced to 0.56-fold and further the fat oxidation has been increased to 1.04-fold. Also, there has been reduced fat uptake. All these processes have reduced FFA in the blood and has led to 0.88-fold decrease in triglyceride synthesis. The conversion of acetyl-CoA to HMG-CoA, the rate limiting step in cholesterol synthesis, is reduced to 0.9-fold. The LDL synthesis production remains unchanged, whereas uptake of LDL into liver is reduced by 0.96-fold. The conversion of LDL-C to HDL-S also increases by 1.29-fold. These processes led to a slight increase in LDL-C and HDL-C levels. All these effects were predominantly produced by DHA as indicated by individual flux analysis simulations of EPA and DHA (See Supplementary Figure S4 and S5 online).

Efficacy of an EPA and DHA-based supplementation in enhancing cardiac and cerebrovascular health

An in-silico population with biomarkers denoting the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease condition was simulated with an intervention of 180 mg of EPA and 120 mg of DHA for a duration of six months. The combination of EPA and DHA led to significant reductions in triglycerides, and total cholesterols, while LDL-C and HDL-C levels increased. Over the initial one-month period, the observed reductions in biomarkers were minimal and insignificant except for triglycerides (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S4 online). Whereas, over a six-month timeframe, mean triglyceride levels decreased from 198 mg/dL to 169 mg/dL, reflecting a 14.7% reduction. Similarly, total cholesterol decreased by approximately 1.7%, whereas LDL-C levels increased by 3.8%. Notably, HDL levels increased insignificantly by 22.4%, resulting in 23% of the population transitioning to a healthier state. Approximately 9% of the population had their triglyceride levels shifted to healthy state, whereas the total cholesterol and LDL were still in the diseased range for most of the population. Though these two lipids showed minimal changes, their ratios with HDL had a significant change. The ratio of triglycerides-to-HDL had 31% reduction; total cholesterol-to-HDL had 21% reduction and LDL-to-HDL had 16% reduction approximately. Significant reductions in the ratio of triglyceride- to-HDL indicate the potential of this supplement in improving the cardiac health. A good efficacy in improving cerebellum health, especially in treating ischemic stroke, can be expected from the EPA and DHA combination as indicated by reduction of total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio. These lipid ratio levels seem to saturate over a period of time.

The percentage change observed in six months for these ratios are almost in linear relationship with their initial levels (Fig. 5). The rates of change in biomarker levels were consistent across all lipid concentrations, although the rates varied depending on the initial state of lipid ratios. For example, in a subpopulation with an initial triglyceride-to-HDL ratio greater than 10, there was an average decrease of 43% over six months, whereas a population with this ratio between 8 and 10 showed an average decrease of 36% (Fig. 5a and d). Similar trends were observed for other lipid ratios. The subpopulation with a total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio greater than 12 showed an average decrease of 36%, with the percentage drop gradually decreasing at lower ratios (Fig. 5b and e). Likewise, individuals with LDL-to-HDL ratios between 8 and 10, and greater than 10, exhibited average percentage drops of 28% and 36%, respectively (Fig. 5c and f).

To evaluate the comprehensive efficacy of the supplementation in a disease-specific context, a scoring algorithm was developed that integrates lipid levels and their respective ratios, allowing for a nuanced assessment across these biomarkers (Supplementary Table S5 online). The data presented in Supplementary figure S6 online shows a steady and significant improvement in both cerebellum and cardiac health scores over a 6-month period, with the mean scores increasing from 14.67 to 28.33 for cardiac health and from 21.66 to 30.66 for cerebellum health. This consistent increase in health scores indicates that the supplement may have a beneficial effect on these aspects of health.

Discussion

The prevalence of stroke and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is on the rise, primarily due to lifestyle modifications that lead to fat accumulation in the body. Elevated lipid concentrations serve as early indicators of cardiac and cerebrovascular disorders. A systems biology-based mathematical model has been employed to elucidate the dynamics of liver metabolism associated with these diseases. This model provided quantitative insights into the effects of omega fatty acids on disease progression and was used to evaluate the efficacy of a formulation containing eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA).

The flux analysis of liver metabolic reactions following combined intervention with 1 g of EPA and 1 g of DHA revealed reductions in the rates of key steps in fat metabolism pathways. The decrease in the Malonyl-CoA to free fatty acid (FFA) conversion rate can be attributed to reduced activity of the fatty acid synthase (FAS) enzyme, which is regulated by the expression of SREBP1c. This reduction in conversion rates is further influenced by decreased SREBP1c levels and increased AMPK levels28,29. The rate of Palmitoyl-CoA transport across mitochondria via the carnitine shuttle also increased, indicating enhanced fat oxidation. Studies by Kersten et al. have similarly reported an increase in carnitine shuttle activity due to EPA and DHA supplementation30. The reduction in triglyceride synthesis flux observed in this study can be linked to the decreased expression of genes such as SREBP1c and PPARγ, as well as reduced FFA levels in the liver. Despite increased fat oxidation rates, cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA levels remained stable, while mitochondrial acetyl-CoA levels increased. This is due to the reduced flux of the citrate shuttle, which is regulated by genes involved in fat synthesis and oxidation, particularly PPARγ and PPARα. Notably, DHA was found to significantly decrease PPARγ and slightly increase PPARα gene expression (See Supplementary Figure S7 online) as reported by different clinical studies31,32. These perturbations-maintained acetyl-CoA homeostasis while controlling fat metabolism. Additionally, the net fluxes of all reversible reactions in glycolysis were negative, favouring reverse reactions and indicating an overall increase in gluconeogenesis. However, the glucose-6-phosphate to glucose conversion was not significantly altered, thereby maintaining stable blood glucose levels. The urea cycle rates also increased by approximately 3%. Overall, EPA and DHA specifically target fat metabolism, altering lipid levels while keeping other major metabolic processes intact.

The intervention with 180 mg of EPA and 120 mg of DHA demonstrated a steady reduction in all considered cardiovascular and cerebrovascular biomarkers. The efficacy of the intervention improved over longer durations, with HDL levels reaching healthy levels in a significant portion of the population by the end of six months. In the case of non- traditional markers, the lipid ratio’s rate of reduction decreased as they shifted towards healthy. This could be attributed to the varying rates of change of all four lipids. HDL had a faster increase compared to change in all other three lipids. HDL levels can be observed to reach a slight saturation as they reach healthy state, whereas the other lipids are still in the diseased state and continue to decrease. Overall, the individuals with triglyceride-to-HDL ratio over 8, total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio above 12 and LDL-to-HDL ratio more than 8, have the potential to achieve over a 35% decrease in lipid ratios within six months. This suggests a greater efficacy of this combination in improving lipid levels for those with higher initial lipid ratios. For individuals with mild to moderate raise in biomarker levels, the rate of reduction was steady and gradual. Prolonged consumption of the supplement could help maintain healthy lipid ratios, thereby improving AMI and AIS.

The results from the in-silico population analysis using EPA and DHA supplementation, which showed steady improvements in both cerebellar and cardiac health scores over a six-month period, are strongly supported by existing literature on the benefits of these omega-3 fatty acids. For instance, a recent study by Peña-de-la-Sancha et al. reported a 17% increase in HDL levels following supplementation with higher doses of EPA and DHA, indicating the potential lipid-modulating effects of these fatty acids33. Similarly, the combination of EPA and DHA with statin therapy has been shown to further reduce triglycerides and total cholesterol, which may enhance the efficacy of lipid management strategies34. A study by Asztalos et al. conducted on healthy individuals demonstrated reductions in triglyceride levels by approximately 8 mg/dL and 42 mg/dL over a six-week period following daily interventions with 600 mg of EPA and DHA, respectively. Despite the observed decrease in triglycerides, other lipid profiles remained significantly unaffected, highlighting the safety and tolerability of these dosages35. Continuous intake of EPA and DHA over extended periods has consistently been shown to reduce and maintain lipid levels in patients with CVD36. This is consistent with the observed reduction in variability and the consistent increase in mean health scores in the current study, suggesting that the supplement’s effects become more predictable and effective over time​.

Furthermore, the neuroprotective effects of DHA, as highlighted by Hongjian Pu et al., suggest that the observed improvement in cerebellar health scores may not only be due to lipid management but also to DHA’s ability to reduce tissue loss and cognitive deficits post-stroke37. This aligns with the significant improvement in cerebellar health scores seen in the current study, indicating that the EPA and DHA combination may offer additional benefits beyond cardiovascular health, particularly in maintaining and improving cerebrovascular health. These findings underscore the potential of this EPA and DHA formulation to serve as a valuable tool in the management of both cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, especially when integrated into a comprehensive treatment regimen that includes statins or other standard therapies.

However, the clinical evidence for omega-3 supplementation—particularly EPA and DHA—remains mixed. Large, randomized trials and meta-analyses have reported variable outcomes: some found no significant effect on coronary heart disease or major vascular events38, whereas others reported modest but significant reductions in cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, and major adverse cardiovascular events, particularly with EPA-only formulations39. Safety concerns have also been raised, including a 25% increased risk of atrial fibrillation reported in meta-analyses, with higher risk at doses above 1 g/day40. Observational data have also linked fish oil use to increased AF and stroke risk in individuals without established CVD41.

The difference between these mixed clinical findings and our predictions arises from a fundamental difference in focus. Most large trials evaluate hard clinical endpoints (e.g., MACE, mortality), whereas our model quantifies biomarker-driven changes—specifically lipid levels and ratios—that are strongly mechanistically linked to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk. These predictions are anchored in well-established hepatic pathways by which EPA and DHA modulate lipid synthesis, transport, and clearance. This mechanistic and biomarker integration allows us to identify subpopulations most likely to benefit (e.g., those with high baseline lipid ratios) while also informing the need for careful safety monitoring.

The current simulation study also has several important limitations. Dietary intake was fixed for calories and macronutrients, without accounting for physical activity, medications, comorbidities, or genetic variation affecting lipid metabolism and cardiovascular outcomes. While the model captures lipid dynamics and health-score trends, it cannot simulate clinical events or account for differences in supplement formulation, absorption, or bioavailability. These constraints are inherent to in-silico approaches, as noted in health economic and physiological modeling guidelines42, and highlight the need for external validation against diverse real-world datasets before clinical application. Thus, large, well-controlled clinical trials remain essential to confirm both the benefits and potential risks suggested by our model.

Conclusion

A predictive mathematical model designed to capture the intricate mechanisms of liver metabolism offers a powerful tool for understanding the mechanism of action of nutraceuticals, optimizing dosage regimens, and identifying populations that may experience enhanced effects. In this study, a pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic model was benchmarked to simulate the effects of key Omega-3 fatty acids, EPA and DHA. The validated model revealed the interactions of these fatty acids with liver metabolism in an in-silico population with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Interventions with 1 g of EPA and 1 g of DHA demonstrated significant reductions in triglycerides and increases in HDL levels over one month. Subsequent model simulations for a supplementation containing 180 mg of EPA and 120 mg of DHA showed a 15% decrease in triglycerides and a 26% increase in HDL levels within the in-silico population. Additionally, key biomarkers—triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio, and LDL-to-HDL ratio—decreased by 34%, 27%, and 34%, respectively, over six months intervention. The model also identified a subpopulation that responded more favourably to the EPA and DHA supplement. Overall, this systems-based mathematical approach demonstrated the gradual and sustained in-silico efficacy of an EPA- and DHA-based supplements in improving cardiac and cerebrovascular health. Thus, further controlled clinical trials are needed to establish the true clinical impact of omega-3 supplementation at different concentrations on cardiovascular diseases.

Methods

Description of the computational model

The systems biology tools used for the analysis contains pharmacokinetic equations and pharmacodynamic model. A two-compartment pharmacokinetic (PK) model incorporating both linear clearance and a saturable Michaelis–Menten elimination term was selected to describe EPA and DHA kinetics (Supplementary Figure S1). This structure was supported by literature-derived concentration–time profiles demonstrating biphasic elimination and gradual systemic accumulation over several weeks, necessitating a distribution compartment. Compared with a one-compartment model, the two-compartment framework more accurately captured both the rapid initial distribution phase and the slower long-term accumulation, improving simulation fidelity for short-term (1-day) and long-term PK profiles. The inclusion of a Michaelis–Menten elimination pathway was guided by early-phase saturation-like behaviour in plasma concentration curves, while linear clearance dominated at steady state. Model parameters were optimized by fitting to published PK datasets22, with validation confirming that simulated terminal half-life, time to steady state, and steady-state concentrations under various dosing regimens fell within physiologically reported ranges. This PK model was later used to determine the pharmacokinetic profile of EPA and DHA at any required dose and duration. To establish pharmacodynamic results, a mathematical framework of liver metabolism created by Tomar et al., was used43. The framework encompasses a range of key metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogen metabolism, the TCA cycle, fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, protein synthesis and degradation, the urea cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway, cholesterol biosynthesis, and the hexosamine pathway. The model connects the pathways to molecular signaling pathways of insulin, glucagon, and mTOR and also to key genetic factors like SREBP1c, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), and PPARα. The current framework has been expanded to include the metabolism of HDL and LDL. Owing to its genetic regulation and signaling mechanisms, the framework captures the effect of different diets, categorized by macronutrients amount, on liver metabolism. Furthermore, Kevin D. Hall’s mathematical framework44, is integrated into the model for additional refinement.

Simulation of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease pathophysiology in the liver model

To define a disease environment, the model parameters were perturbed to reach a steady state with unhealthy levels of triglycerides, total cholesterols, LDL, HDL and their ratios. The parameters associated with fatty acid uptake, regulation of triglyceride synthesis, cholesterol synthesis and transport were perturbed such that these pathways are enriched resulting in higher levels of lipids. The detailed information about the parameters perturbed to obtain disease state and resulting lipid levels are reported in supplementary file online.

Integration of PK-PD components and benchmarking against clinical data

To ensure physiologically realistic, time-resolved responses, the pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles of EPA and DHA were dynamically integrated into the pharmacodynamic (PD) liver model. The primary targets of these supplements include key transcriptional regulators such as SREBP1c, PPARγ, and ChREBP, which are involved in fatty acid synthesis, triglyceride synthesis, and cholesterol biosynthesis. The parameters governing the activity of these regulators were modeled as functions of the plasma concentration–time profiles of EPA and DHA at specific doses and durations.

This PK-PD coupling enabled the regulatory influence of EPA and DHA on hepatic metabolic pathways to evolve over time, allowing the model to simulate dynamic changes in lipid metabolism and blood lipid profiles in response to systemic exposure. For benchmarking, clinical study data reporting the effects of EPA and DHA on lipid profiles were curated from published literature23–27. The corresponding dose and duration information from these studies was used to generate PK profiles based on previously benchmarked equations. These simulated PK profiles served as inputs to the model, while the lipid profile changes reported in the literature were treated as objective targets. The parameters within the PK-PD linkage functions were then optimized to reproduce the observed clinical lipid responses.

Modelling the effects of EPA, DHA, and their combination on liver metabolism

Post benchmarking of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic models, to identify the effects of EPA and DHA on the liver metabolism, the models were simulated under three different dosages: 1 g of only EPA, 1 g of only DHA, 1 g of EPA and 1 g of DHA for a duration of 1 month. An in-silico population that resembles cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases was created by varying the parameters mentioned earlier. The effect on all the lipids were studied. Further, the effect on all the reactions, pathways and genes in the model were captured and comparative analysis of those results were made.

Assessing the potential of EPA and DHA-Based supplements in enhancing cardiac and cerebrovascular health

The EPA and DHA are recognized for their capability of reducing CVD’s biomarkers. A combination of EPA and DHA with dosages 180 mg and 120 mg was analyzed through the benchmarked models for its efficacy in reducing the cardiac diseases and stroke related disorders. The study comprises of a 180-day simulation trial designed to evaluate the effect of combination on various lipid profile and their ratios especially triglyceride-to-HDL, total cholesterol-to-HDL and LDL-to-HDL. This in-silico study involves simulation of a population size of 1000 individuals, of both genders, aged 18–60 years with body mass index (BMI) ranging from 23 to 33 kg/m². The dietary intake was standardized to a fixed calorie range (1600–2500 kcal) and macronutrient distribution (carbohydrate: 45–60%, protein: 10–20%, fat: 20–35%) to provide a controlled baseline for simulation. Other lifestyle and medication variables were not incorporated, as the aim was to isolate the effects of EPA and DHA supplementation under standardized dietary conditions. Comparative analysis of these results was then carried out.
