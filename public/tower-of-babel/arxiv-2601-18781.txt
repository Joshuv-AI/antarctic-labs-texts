Another relation among the neutrino mass-squared differences?
I. Alikhanov

Abstract
Determining absolute neutrino masses remains a central challenge in particle physics. Relations among the neutrino mass-squared differences could facilitate this determination and shed additional light on the underlying mass-generation mechanism. Inspired by recent global fits of neutrino oscillation parameters, we propose a simple algebraic relation between Δ ​ m 21 2 \Delta m^{2}_{21} and Δ ​ m 31 2 \Delta m^{2}_{31} . It provides a framework to analytically manipulate these parameters and admits specific physical interpretations. In particular, the results may point toward a vanishing ν 1 \nu_{1} mass.

A simple ratio

\lambda\left(\Delta m^{2}_{21},\Delta m^{2}_{31}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{31}}+\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}}{\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{31}}-\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}}.

(1)

de Salas et al. 2021

1

\lambda\left(7.50\times 10^{-5}~\text{eV}^{2},\,2.55\times 10^{-3}~\text{eV}^{2}\right)=1.4140,

reported to five significant digits.

\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41421356

Inspired by these observations, we assume that

\frac{\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{31}}+\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}}{\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{31}}-\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}}=\sqrt{2}

Relation (

One might speculate that the emergence of this special number on the right-hand side is a manifestation of the underlying quantum physics, such as state normalization or mixing angles. It would be valuable to find an explanation for (

III
Implications and Interpretation

We proceed to investigate the implications of the assumed validity of (
2
), which may be far-reaching.

An immediate nontrivial conclusion is that a single neutrino mass-squared difference suffices to determine the remaining two (because we also have the condition
\Delta m^{2}_{31}-\Delta m^{2}_{21}=\Delta m^{2}_{32}
). In other words, the entire mass-squared difference spectrum is determined by just two neutrino masses. This may suggest that the lightest neutrino mass eigenstate is massless, thereby contributing nothing to the spectrum.

From (
2
), we also find

\frac{\Delta m^{2}_{21}+\Delta m^{2}_{31}}{\left(\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}+\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{31}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{3}{4},

(4)

which is consistent with the global fits as well. For example, with the data from
(
de Salas et al. 2021
)
, the left-hand side is
0.7501
, in excellent agreement with the predicted value of 3/4.
Moreover, (
4
) mirrors the structural form of the Koide formula for charged lepton masses
Koide 1982
;
Koide 1983a
;
Koide 1983b
,

\frac{m_{e}+m_{\mu}+m_{\tau}}{\left(\sqrt{m_{e}}+\sqrt{m_{\mu}}+\sqrt{m_{\tau}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{2}{3},

(5)

if interpreting the neutrino mass-squared differences as the structural components. It is relevant to recall that the Koide formula demonstrates a precision of
\sim 0.001\%
with the pole masses. At the same time, it can deviate by approximately 0.2% when applied to running charged lepton masses at higher energy scales, such as the
Z
pole
Xing and Zhang 2006
.

Beyond the intuitively appealing similarity to (
5
), it follows from (
4
) that

32\,\Delta m^{2}_{21}\Delta m^{2}_{31}=\left(\Delta m^{2}_{31}-\Delta m^{2}_{21}\right)^{2}.

(6)

On the other hand, (
6
) can also be written as

\Delta m^{2}_{21}\Delta m^{2}_{31}=\frac{1}{32}\left(m^{2}_{3}-m^{2}_{2}\right)^{2}.

(7)

The product on the left-hand side is, by definition, a function of
m_{1}
, yet it is determined solely by
m_{2}
and
m_{3}
. Here, we attribute this observation to the masslessness of the
\nu_{1}
state (
m_{1}=0
). This aligns with our earlier projections.

It may also be interesting to represent (
7
) in the form

\frac{(\Delta m^{2}_{32})^{2}}{\Delta m^{2}_{21}\Delta m^{2}_{31}}=32.

(8)

The global fits
(
de Salas et al. 2021
;
Capozzi et al. 2017
;
Capozzi et al. 2025
;
Esteban et al. 2024
;
Esteban et al. 2026
)
confirm that (
8
) is consistent with the data within
\sim 1\sigma
. For instance, substituting the corresponding best-fit values from
Capozzi et al. 2025
into the left-hand side yields 31.88. The other independent fits from
de Salas et al. 2021
lead to 32.03.

Thus, relation (
2
) provides a simple algebraic framework for manipulating these oscillation parameters and deriving physically meaningful interpretations. The uncertainty that we neglect by introducing (
2
) does not substantially perturb the results. Future oscillation measurements and global fits will further test the validity of this framework.

IV
Discussion and Conclusions

It is a challenging problem to determine absolute neutrino masses from currently available data. However, additional algebraic relations among the neutrino mass-squared differences could facilitate solving this problem.
We have proposed a relation between
\Delta m^{2}_{21}
and
\Delta m^{2}_{31}
inspired by recent global fits of neutrino oscillation parameters. From the beginning, we restrict our analysis to the normal mass ordering in which
\Delta m^{2}_{31}>0
. Thus, there is a simple ratio that approximates
\sqrt{2}
to within 0.3% when the best-fit values for the mass-squared differences from the global analysis are substituted. We have assumed that the ratio is exactly equal to
\sqrt{2}
. This provides an algebraic framework for the manipulation of the mass-squared differences. Then, we have investigated the implications of this ratio and proposed an interpretation of the results obtained. In particular, it follows that specifying just two neutrino masses allows for the reconstruction of the entire mass-squared difference spectrum. This may indicate that the lightest neutrino is massless. For example, it turns out that the product
\Delta m^{2}_{21}\Delta m^{2}_{31}
can be represented as a function of solely two neutrino masses,
m_{2}
and
m_{3}
. We have attributed this observation to the masslessness of the
\nu_{1}
state (
m_{1}=0
). In this case, (
2
) reduces to a simple neutrino mass relation

\frac{m_{3}+m_{2}}{m_{3}-m_{2}}=\sqrt{2}.

(9)

Furthermore, there appears an expression that mirrors the structural form of the Koide formula
Koide 1982
;
Koide 1983a
;
Koide 1983b
. It may be appealing to view this similarity as complementary, albeit indirect, support for the assumptions made. It should be noted that there have been various proposals to extend the Koide formula to neutrino masses
Koide 2007
;
Xing and Zhang 2006
;
Li and Ma 2005
;
Cao 2012
;
Rodejohann and Zhang 2011
;
Gao and Li 2016
.

One could hypothesize that the emergence of the specific numbers on the right-hand sides of the presented relations, such as
\sqrt{2}
in (
2
), reflects the quantization inherent in the underlying physics. These values typically arise from state normalization or specific mixing angles. Deriving these relations within a quantum theoretical approach would provide a rigorous foundation for the presented framework.

The proposed ratio remains applicable even under slight deviations from
\sqrt{2}
. Based on Table
1
(rightmost column) as well as on (
3
), the ratio is expected to be stable to at least two decimal places (being approximately 1.41). In these non-ideal scenarios, such a deviation can be treated as perturbation, justifying the use of (
2
) as an unperturbed equation. Notably, the values in Table
1
approximate
\sqrt{2}
with a maximum relative error of 0.27%, which represents very good agreement given the typical precision of neutrino oscillation experiments. At this point, it is worth noting that measuring an absolute zero mass in a neutrino experiment is impossible, as
m_{1}=0
and
m_{1}\ll\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}
are, in practice, indistinguishable
Formaggio et al. 2021
. Therefore, the results derived here also hold for a vanishing
\nu_{1}
mass, specifically when
m_{1}
is on the order of the experimental uncertainty in
\sqrt{\Delta m^{2}_{21}}
.
