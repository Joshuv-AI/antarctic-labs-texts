Parametric Mean-Field Empirical Bayes in High-dimensional Linear Regression
Seunghyun Lee, Nabarun Deb

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of parametric empirical Bayes estimation of an i.i.d. prior in high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression, with random design. We obtain the asymptotic distribution of the variational Empirical Bayes (vEB) estimator (motivated from [ 10 ] and [ 27 ] ) which approximately maximizes a variational lower bound of the intractable marginal likelihood. We characterize a sharp phase transition behavior for the vEB estimator — namely that it is information theoretically optimal (in terms of limiting variance) up to p = o ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) p=o(n^{2/3}) while it suffers from a sub-optimal convergence rate in higher dimensions. In the first regime, i.e., when p = o ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) p=o(n^{2/3}) , we show how the estimated prior can be calibrated to enable valid coordinate-wise and delocalized inference, both under the empirical Bayes posterior and the oracle posterior. In the second regime, we propose a debiasing technique as a way to improve the performance of the vEB estimator beyond p = o ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) p=o(n^{2/3}) . Extensive numerical experiments corroborate our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Consider a canonical linear regression model

(1)

\displaystyle y\mid X,\beta\sim N(X\beta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),

where
y\in\RR^{n}
denotes the responses,
X\in\RR^{n\times p}
denotes the design matrix,
\beta\in\RR^{p}
is the unknown regression coefficients, and
\sigma^{2}>0
is the error variance (potentially unknown). We work under a high-dimensional asymptotic setting, where both the sample size
n
and the number of parameters
p
grow to infinity.
Here, we additionally posit an i.i.d. prior on the coefficient vector
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})
, where the (random) coefficients (
\beta_{i}
) are generated i.i.d. from an
unknown
prior distribution
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
, and
\theta_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a (deterministic) finite-dimensional parameter. These models arise naturally in estimating effect size distributions and in applications involving genetic associations; see e.g.
[
63
,
41
,
65
]
.
Our goal in this paper is two-fold:

1.

To
estimate the prior

\mu_{\theta}
in a data-driven manner, and

2.

To use the
estimated prior for downstream inference
under the
oracle posterior
on
\beta
.

While traditional Bayesian inference assumes that the prior
\mu_{\theta}
is specified by the statistician, this can be problematic for high-dimensional noisy data. For example, assume the simple high-dimensional setting where
p=n
and
X
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. This is the classical Gaussian sequence model

y_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(\beta_{i},\sigma^{2}).

In this case, the posterior distribution of
\beta\mid y,X
, witnesses proportional contribution from the prior and the likelihood, which implies that there is no posterior contraction. In other words, all posterior based inference depends heavily on the prior, even asymptotically as
n\to\infty
. A misspecified prior from the statistician would therefore result in inconsistent results downstream. This motivates the development of procedures that can consistently estimate
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
.
Empirical Bayes
(EB) addresses this problem by using the observed data to estimate the prior. For simplicity, suppose the variance
\sigma^{2}
is known. A natural strategy for estimating
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
is to maximize the marginalized likelihood

(2)

\displaystyle m_{\theta}(y):=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\int\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|{y}-{X}{\beta}\|^{2}\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{p}d\mu_{\theta}(\beta_{i})

with respect to the ‘hyperparameter’
\theta
. However, except in extreme cases (such as when
\mu_{\theta}
is a normal distribution or
X^{\top}X
is diagonal), the integral in (
2
) does not have an explicit form. The problem is particularly challenging under the high-dimensional setting with
p\to\infty
, as the marginal likelihood in (
2
) is extremely challenging to compute by standard numerical integration or sampling-based approaches.

Recent works (see
[
10
,
27
,
38
,
20
]
) have addressed this issue by maximizing a more tractable
variational lower bound
, also commonly known as the evidence lower bound (ELBO), instead of (
2
), to obtain a
variational empirical Bayes
(vEB) estimator. Necessary conditions for the consistency of this estimator was recently established in
[
38
]
. However, to our knowledge, the explicit convergence rates, asymptotic distributions, and asymptotic efficiency of vEB estimators have not been studied in the literature. In this work, we take a first step towards this direction by analyzing an approximate vEB estimator in the parametric setting (see
[
10
,
21
,
22
]
). We provide a complete characterization of its asymptotic behavior when
p=o(n)
, under appropriate scaling of the design matrix. We then use the estimated prior for coordinate-wise and delocalized inference under the oracle posterior.

1.1
Organization and summary of main results

This work first analyzes
frequentist
inferential guarantees of the vEB estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
that maximizes a variational approximation of the marginal likelihood
m_{\theta}(y)
, assuming a random design matrix
X
and regularity conditions for the prior parametric family. Interestingly, the optimality of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
exhibits a phase transition depending on the asymptotic scaling of the problem. When
p\ll n^{2/3}
, the vEB estimator is
\sqrt{p}
-consistent, asymptotically normal, and attains the information-theoretically optimal variance. In contrast, all of these properties fail when
p\gg n^{2/3}
. This is formally stated in the following theorem, which combines the separate upper and lower bound claims in
Section

3
. While the following result is stated under a correctly specified parametric family for the prior, we also derive analogous limit distributions under misspecified priors (see
3.4
).

Theorem
.

Suppose
y
is generated from the Bayesian regression model (
1
) with a true prior parameter
\theta_{0}
. Assume a random design matrix with a low signal to noise ratio (SNR) scaling, alongside standard regularity conditions on the prior (see Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
).
Let
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
be the vEB estimator (see (
11
)). Under the asymptotic regime
p=o(n^{2/3})
,
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
is
\sqrt{p}
-consistent with a limiting normal distribution, and attains the information-theoretic optimal limiting variance. However, when
p\gg n^{2/3}
,
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
is no longer optimal and is only
n/p
-consistent with a non-normal limit.

Moving beyond frequentist recovery of the prior hyperparameter
\theta
, we illustrate in
Section

4
that the vEB estimator can be used for downstream
Bayesian
inference on the regression coefficients
\beta
, under the asymptotic regime
p=o(n^{2/3})
. First, we bound the total variation distance between coordinate-wise distributions under the
EB posterior
(constructed using the estimated prior
\mu_{\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}}
) and the
oracle posterior
(constructed using the oracle prior
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
). This in particular implies that the mean/variance computed under the variational approximation of the EB posterior are consistent.
Next, we address uncertainty quantification under the two posteriors. We mainly focus on the one-dimensional projection of the posterior, that is
q^{\top}\beta\mid y,X
, for some fixed vector
q\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Contrary to previous studies in high SNR settings which show that inference under the oracle posterior and the EB posterior are asymptotically equivalent (the so-called higher-order merging phenomena in
[
45
]
), we illustrate that this is not guaranteed in the low SNR setting. More specifically, the one-dimensional projection of the oracle posterior and the EB posterior merge if and only if the projection direction is an approximate contrast vector (i.e.
\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}}{\|q\|}=o(\sqrt{p})
), illustrating the need for caution for utilizing the EB posterior. Nonetheless, we provide EB adjusted credible intervals for such one-dimensional projections which provide valid marginal coverage under the
oracle posterior
, using the estimated prior hyperparameter
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
.

In
Section

5
, we verify satisfactory finite-sample performance of our theoretical results, under various priors that include common choices such as spike-and-slab and Gaussian mixtures. In
Section

5.4
, we also illustrate that the slower convergence rate of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
in higher-dimensions can be improved by debiasing. To elaborate, we show that the debiased vEB estimator relaxes the requirement
p\ll n^{2/3}
for the aforementioned normal limit to
p\ll n^{3/4}
. We conclude the paper in
Section

6
by discussing avenues for future work.

1.2
Related works

Standard EB in denoising.

The standard EB setup for normal means
[
46
,
24
,
25
,
51
, this corresponds to taking
X=I_{n}
in (
1
), see e.g.,]
lead to a product posterior, and hence results in
independent observations
under the posterior. This allows the marginal likelihood to factorize, and does not require a high-dimensional integral computation as in (
2
). Additionally, the exact factorization of the posterior results in tractable representations for individual coordinates of the posterior mean and variance, using Tweedie’s formula (see e.g.
[
46
]
). In contrast, our regression setup does not have any such representations of the posterior moments due to the intractable likelihood, creating additional theoretical challenges for downstream inference.

Statistical efficiency of MML for EB.

As stated earlier in (
2
), maximizing the marginal likelihood is a natural approach to empirical Bayes. Consequently, asymptotic properties of parametric MML was previously studied in the literature
[
42
,
53
,
47
,
45
]
.
In particular,
[
42
]
established consistency of the MML estimator to an “oracle set” as well as the consistency of the EB posterior. The recent work
[
45
]
additionally shows a higher-order merging of the EB posterior to the oracle posterior, which indicates that the EB posterior credible intervals enjoy coverage under the oracle posterior.
However, this line of work considered the more classical finite-dimensional asymptotic scaling with high SNR. More importantly, they posit a frequentist true model that corresponds to fixing any true parameter
\beta^{\star}
in the regression case, thus making the Bayesian model formulation to be misspecified. This is in sharp contrast to our high-dimensional low SNR scaling with correct model specification. Indeed, we will show that such higher-order merging may not happen under our setup. We also stress that going beyond consistency, no asymptotic normality or efficiency results have previously been established for the MML estimator, beyond simple settings.

Gradient flow based methods.

Another direction for empirical Bayes methods in regression involves using gradient flows to solve the complex optimization problem in (
2
)
[
31
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. In particular, the recent line of works
[
21
,
22
]
consider a very similar problem setup as ours, but instead analyze an adaptive Langevin dynamics estimator under proportional asymptotics
p\asymp n
. We believe these results complement ours, as we analyze the vEB estimator, consider the scaling
p=o(n)
, and impose different assumptions for priors. In
Section

5
, we compare our vEB estimator with the adaptive Langevin algorithm from
[
21
,
22
]
. We also note that in the parametric EB setup, no consistency guarantees have been established for the gradient flow estimator.

vEB for general latent variable models.

To our knowledge, vEB dates back to
[
9
]
, which first proposed the notion of maximizing the variational approximation lower bound of the intractable likelihood in the context of topic modeling. This idea was also utilized for unsupervised learning in the context of variational autoencoders (VAEs,
[
28
]
) and item response theory
[
14
]
as well as various modern machine learning problems such as matrix factorization
[
60
,
64
]
, due to their excellent scalability. However, the theoretical understanding of such vEB procedures is limited, in particular for precise convergence rates and limiting distributions, which is precisely the gap we aim to fill in this paper.

Notation

For two measures
\PP,\mathbb{Q}

\mathrm{D_{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\mid\PP):=\begin{cases}\EE_{\mathbb{Q}}\log{\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\PP}}&\mbox{if }\mathbb{Q}\ll\PP,\\

\mathrm{D_{TV}}(\PP,\mathbb{Q}):=\sup_{E\subseteq\mathbb{R}}\left|\PP(E)-\mathbb{Q}(E)\right|.

Let

. Given a matrix/tensor

\{M_{p}\}_{p\geq 1}
(with fixed dimensions), write
M_{p}=O(c_{p})

\|M_{p}\|_{F}=O(c_{p})
, and write
M_{p}=o(c_{p})
when
\|M_{p}\|_{F}=o(c_{p})

, and in particular write

The notations

For a vector

v^{2}:=v\otimes v
and

, where

, define its sub-Gaussian norm

as the smallest

that satisfies

\EE e^{t(Z-\EE Z)}\leq e^{K^{2}t^{2}/2},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

Throughout the paper, we work on an asymptotic setting where both
n,p\to\infty
. Hence, we understand
n\equiv n(p)
as a function of

and write limits in terms of

. All probabilities and expectations are conditioned on

unless specified otherwise. When the expectation is respect to

Definition 1.1

(Linear-quadratic tilt)
.

Given a base-measure
\mu_{\theta}
and constants
d_{i}
for each
i=0,1,\ldots,p
(defined below in (
3
) and
3.1
), let
\mu_{i,\theta}
be a quadratic tilt of

by setting the likelihood ratio as

\frac{d\mu_{i,\theta}}{d\mu_{\theta}}(b):=e^{-\frac{d_{i}}{2}b^{2}-C_{\theta}(d_{i})},\quad\text{where}\quad C_{\theta}(d_{i}):=\log\int e^{-\frac{d_{i}}{2}b^{2}}d\mu_{\theta}(b).

Next, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mu_{i,t,\theta}
be the linear tilt of
\mu_{i,\theta}
, i.e.,

\frac{d\mu_{i,t,\theta}}{d\mu_{i,\theta}}(b):=e^{tb-\psi_{i,\theta}(t)},\quad\text{where}\quad\psi_{i,\theta}(t):=\log\int e^{tb}d\mu_{i,\theta}(b).

By properties of exponential families, the mean and variance of
\mu_{i,t,\theta}
are
\psi_{i,\theta}^{\prime}(t)
and
\psi_{i,\theta}^{\prime\prime}(t)
, respectively.

2
Variational empirical Bayes

For our goal of estimating
\theta
, the natural strategy is to maximize the marginal likelihood
m_{\theta}(y)
(see (
2
)) with respect to
\theta
. We first define some transformations of the data
(X,y)
, which will be useful in simplifying
m_{\theta}(y)
.

Definition 2.1

(Transformed variables)
.

Define
p
-dimensional vectors
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{p})
and
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})
as follows:

(3)

w:=\frac{X^{\top}y}{\sigma^{2}},\quad d_{i}:=\frac{(X^{\top}X)(i,i)}{\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,p.

Also, let
A_{p}\in\RR^{p\times p}
be the off-diagonal part of the matrix
-\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}X
(with diagonal entries set to zero), that is

A_{p}(i,j)=-\frac{(X^{\top}X)(i,j)}{\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq p\quad\mbox{and}\quad A_{p}(i,i)=0\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i\leq p.

As
d
and
A_{p}
respectively denote the diagonal and off-diagonal parts of
\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}X
, we can write

\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}X=\text{{Diag}}(d)-A_{p}.

Using these notation, we expand the quadratic term in the exponent of (
2
):

\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|y-X\beta\|^{2}\Big)=\exp\Big(-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}d_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{\top}A_{p}\beta}{2}+w^{\top}\beta\Big).

Now, by absorbing each summand
d_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}
to the base measure
\mu_{\theta}
and using the notations
C_{\theta}(d_{i}),\mu_{i,\theta}
in
1.1
,
we can re-write the scaled log-likelihood as

(4)

\displaystyle\ell_{p}(\theta):=\frac{1}{p}\log m_{\theta}(y)=-\frac{n}{2p}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2p\sigma^{2}}\lVert y\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}C_{\theta}(d_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\log Z_{p}(w,\theta),

where

(5)

\displaystyle Z_{p}(w,\theta):=\int\exp\Big(\frac{\beta^{\top}A_{p}\beta}{2}+w^{\top}\beta\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{p}d\mu_{i,\theta}(\beta_{i}).

We motivate the vEB (variational empirical Bayes) estimator, initially assuming a known variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. While maximizing
\ell_{p}(\theta)
as a function of
\theta
is tempting, the normalizing constant
Z_{p}(w,\theta)
in (
5
) involves an intractable high-dimensional integral which makes the maximization computationally challenging. To remedy this issue, we build our estimator of
\theta_{0}
using ideas from Mean-Field variational inference. To wit,
first observe that the Gibbs variational formula implies the following dual representation of
\log Z_{p}(w,\theta)
(c.f.
[
59
,
39
]
):

\displaystyle\log Z_{p}(w,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})}\left(\EE_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}A_{p}\beta+w^{\top}\beta\right]-\mathrm{D_{KL}}\big(\mathbb{Q}|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu_{i,\theta}\big)\right),

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
denotes the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Now, by restricting
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
to the set of product measures
\mathbb{Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}
, we get a lower bound for
\log Z_{p}(w,\theta)
:

(6)

\displaystyle\log Z_{p}(w,\theta)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\mathbb{Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}}\left(\EE_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}A_{p}\beta+w^{\top}\beta\right]-\mathrm{D_{KL}}\big(\mathbb{Q}|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu_{i,\theta}\big)\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{u^{\top}A_{p}u}{2}+w^{\top}u-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}I_{i,\theta}(u_{i})}_{=:M_{\theta}(u)}\Bigg),

where
I_{i,\theta}(t):=\mathrm{D_{KL}}(\mu_{i,(\psi_{i,\theta}^{\prime})^{-1}(t),\theta}|\mu_{i,\theta})
denotes the KL divergence between a linear tilt (with mean
t
) and the base measure of the
i
th coordinate.
Here (
7
) follows from the fact that the supremum in (
6
) is attained by linear tilts
\mu_{i,\cdot,\theta}
, which allows reducing the optimization over measures to that over reals (see
[
39
,
62
]
). Note that the vector
u
effectively denotes the coordinate-wise mean of the product measure
\mathbb{Q}
.

Hence, by plugging-in (
7
) to (
4
), the re-scaled marginal log-likelihood
\ell_{p}(\theta)
can be lower bounded by the “Mean-Field” log-likelihood
\ell_{p}^{\text{MF}}(\theta)
(equivalently, a re-scaled ELBO):

(8)

\displaystyle\ell_{p}(\theta)\geq\ell_{p}^{\text{MF}}(\theta):=-\frac{n}{2p}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2p\sigma^{2}}\lVert y\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}C_{\theta}(d_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}M_{\theta}(u).

The supremum in (
8
) is still an implicit function of
\theta
. We therefore approximate it further to reduce to a more tractable optimization problem in
\theta
.

Definition 2.2

(Mean-Field optimizers)
.

Under
3.1
, by
[
34
, Lemma 2.1]
, the optimizer attaining the supremum in (
8
) is unique. Let us denote it by
u_{\theta}=(u_{1,\theta},\ldots,u_{p,\theta})
. Then
u_{\theta}
satisfies

{u}_{i,\theta}=\psi_{i,\theta}^{\prime}({s}_{i,\theta}+w_{i})
, where
s_{i,\theta}:=\sum_{j\neq i}A_{p}(i,j){u}_{i,\theta}
, for
i\leq p
.

For heuristics, assume that
\mu_{\theta}
is compactly supported. Then under our random design condition (see
3.1
below), direct computations show that the empirical measure
p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{s_{i,\theta}}
converges weakly to
0
. It is then natural to define the approximate optimizer
\tilde{u}_{\theta}=(\tilde{u}_{1,\theta},\ldots,\tilde{u}_{n,\theta})
as
\tilde{u}_{i,\theta}:=\psi^{\prime}_{i,\theta}(w_{i}).
As a result, by spelling out the KL divergences in
I_{i,\theta}
, we can write

\frac{1}{p}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}M_{\theta}(u)\approx\frac{\tilde{u}_{\theta}^{\top}A_{p}\tilde{u}_{\theta}}{2p}+w^{\top}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}I_{i,\theta}(\tilde{u}_{i,\theta})\approx\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\psi_{i,\theta}(w_{i}),

where
\psi_{i,\theta}
is defined in
1.1
. Now, define functions
F_{i}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
for each
i\leq p
as

(9)

\displaystyle F_{i}(\theta):=\log\int e^{w_{i}\beta_{i}-\frac{d_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}}{2}}d\mu_{\theta}(\beta_{i}).

Note that
F_{i}(\theta)=C_{\theta}(d_{i})+\psi_{i,\theta}(w_{i})
. By plugging in the above observations into the lower bound (
8
), we have

(10)

\displaystyle\ell_{p}(\theta)\geq\ell_{p}^{\text{MF}}(\theta)\approx-\frac{n}{2p}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2p\sigma^{2}}\lVert y\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{p}F_{i}(\theta).

Therefore maximizing the above (approximate) lower bound, we get our vEB estimator

(11)

\displaystyle\boxed{\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}=\argmax_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}(\theta).}

Remark 2.1

(Unknown variance)
.

So far, the vEB estimator has required the knowledge of the variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. Fortunately, even though consistent estimation for
\beta
is impossible under the low SNR scaling with
\sigma^{-2}\|X^{\top}X\|=O(1)
, it is straightforward to consistently estimate
\sigma^{2}
using the usual MSE estimator:

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}:=\frac{y^{\top}(I_{n}-X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top})y}{n-p}.

Thus, by plugging-in the above variance estimate, the vEB estimator can be further extended. Alternatively, one may choose to maximize the approximate likelihood in (
10
) with respect to both
(\theta,\sigma^{2})
, as suggested in
[
27
]
.

3
Prior recovery via vEB and Fisher optimality

Now, we illustrate our main results regarding the statistical performance of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
. We discuss its limiting distribution in
Section

3.1
, extension to the misspecified setting in
Section

3.2
, and its information-theoretic optimality in
Section

3.3
. To present our theoretical results, we require several assumptions. First, to understand dimension dependence, we work under the following random design matrix with independent entries.

Assumption 3.1

(Random design)
.

For some distribution
P
with mean zero, variance one, and sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
K>0
, suppose that
X_{k,i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}P
for all
k\leq n,i\leq p
. We will work under the asymptotic scaling
p=o(n)
.

Here the
n^{-1/2}
scaling for
X
is chosen so that
\|X^{\top}X\|/\sigma^{2}=\Theta(1)
, placing the model in a low signal to noise ratio (SNR) regime where individual regression coefficients
\beta
are not consistently estimable. In contrast, when the prior belongs to a low-dimensional parametric family, its hyperparameters remain identifiable through aggregation across coordinates. This makes our high-dimensional scaling particularly natural for empirical Bayes analysis. Such regimes are standard in theoretical studies of high-dimensional Bayesian regression
[
44
,
11
,
3
,
33
,
48
]
, and in particular appealing for EB problems that analyze the consequences of estimating the prior
[
38
,
20
,
22
]
. For simplicity, we also assume that the regression variance
\sigma^{2}
is known; we expect that all following results will go through when this is replaced by the plug-in estimator in (
12
) (since it is consistent at a much faster
\sqrt{n}
-rate; also see
Remark

2.1
).

Next, we impose the following regularity conditions on the prior, which are standard assumptions for efficient estimation in parametric models
[
35
, e.g. see Theorems 2.6 or 5.1 in]
. For example, exponential families satisfy these conditions.

Assumption 3.2

(Prior regularity)
.

Suppose that the prior
\mu_{\theta}
is a parametric family with log-likelihood
\ell(\theta;\beta)
with respect to a non-degenerate base measure
\nu
, such that satisfies the following regularity conditions:

R0.

\mu_{\theta}
is identifiable and has common support.

R1.

The parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a compact set for some fixed integer
k
, and the true parameter
\theta_{0}
lives in the interior of
\Theta
.

R2.

For all
\beta
and
\theta\in\Theta
, the log-likelihood
\ell(\theta;\beta)
is three-times differentiable.

R3.

The Bartlett’s identities for
\nabla\ell:=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta}
and
\nabla^{2}\ell:=\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\theta^{2}}
hold (see conditions (g), (h) in Theorem 2.6,
[
35
]
), and the Fisher information
I(\theta):=\Var_{B_{\theta}\sim\mu_{\theta}}[\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta})]
is positive definite for all
\theta\in U
.

In passing, we mention that R2 requires the derivatives to exist for all
\theta
, compared to the usual regularity condition that assumes differentiability only near
\theta_{0}
. This is mainly for technical convenience.

Finally, in this Section, we assume a well-specified model, in the sense that the prior parametric family
\mu_{\theta}
and the regression variance
\sigma^{2}
are both correct. We will discuss extensions to misspecified priors later in
Section

3.2
.

Assumption 3.3

(Well-specified model)
.

Assume that the data
y
is generated from a correctly specified model with true prior parameter
\theta_{0}\in\textnormal{int}(\Theta)
. In other words, assume that
y
is generated from the same model used to construct the posterior:

\beta_{i}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu_{\theta_{0}},\quad y\mid X,\beta^{\star}\sim N(X\beta^{\star},\sigma^{2}I_{n}).

3.1
Upper bound

To analyze
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
, we first introduce additional notations that will be used to describe the limiting distribution.

Definition 3.1
.

1.

Recalling the regression variance
\sigma^{2}
, set
d_{0}:=\sigma^{-2}
.

2.

For any
\theta\in\Theta
, define a pair of random variables
(B_{\theta},W_{\theta})
by setting

B_{\theta}\sim\mu_{\theta},\quad W_{\theta}\mid B_{\theta}\sim N(d_{0}B_{\theta},~d_{0}).

Also define a deterministic mapping
V:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
by setting

(13)

V(\theta):=I(\theta)-\EE\Big[\Var\big(\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta})\mid W_{\theta}\big)\Big]=\Var\Big[\EE\big(\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta})\mid W_{\theta}\big)\Big]\succ 0_{k\times k}.

3.

For
t\in\mathbb{R},~d>0
, let
B_{t,d,\theta}
be a linear-quadratic tilted probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
defined as
\frac{dB_{t,d,\theta}}{dB_{\theta}}(b)\propto e^{tb-\frac{db^{2}}{2}},
so that
B_{\theta}\mid(W_{\theta}=t)\sim B_{t,d_{0},\theta}
.
Finally, set
h_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
as
h_{\theta}(t):=\EE\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{t,d_{0},\theta})
and define a mapping
\kappa:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
as

\kappa(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\EE[B_{\theta}^{2}]\EE[h_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(W_{\theta})].

We motivate the notation
W_{\theta}
and
V(\theta)
in part (b) above, which will later play a crucial role as the Fisher information. First, recall from R3 in
3.2
that
I(\theta)=\Var[\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta})]
is exactly the Fisher information for a single
observed
coefficient
B_{\theta}\sim\mu_{\theta}
. However, under the hierarchical model, we cannot directly observe the regression coefficient
\beta
and we instead observe
y
. This is roughly equivalent to observing the transformed data
w
in (
3
):

(14)

w=\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}y\mid X,\beta^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N(d_{0}X^{\top}X\beta^{\star},d_{0}X^{\top}X).

Approximating
X^{\top}X\approx I_{p}
, we can understand each coordinate
w_{i}
as i.i.d. random variables with distribution
W_{\theta}
.
Then,
V(\theta)
can be understood as the Fisher information of the single sample
W_{\theta}
, which is formalized in the following lemma. Note that
V(\theta)\prec I(\theta)
since
W_{\theta}
has less information than
B_{\theta}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Fisher information)
.

Let
\tilde{\ell}(\theta;\cdot)
be the log-likelihood of the random variable
W_{\theta}
. Then, the corresponding Fisher information satisfies:

-\EE[\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}(\theta;W_{\theta})]=V(\theta).

The vector
\kappa(\theta)
in part (c) is inevitably more technical. While we refrain from displaying the details, this quantity is crucial for understanding the mean of
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{0})
, which is the gradient of the objective function in (
11
). We will further motivate
\kappa(\theta)
after presenting the main result (see the proof overview that precedes
Remark

3.3
).

To analyze the limiting distribution, we require additional technical assumptions for the prior in addition to the usual ones in
3.2
.

Assumption 3.4

(Additional prior regularity)
.

Assume the following conditions on the prior likelihood
\ell(\theta;\beta)
and tilted random variables
B_{t,d,\theta}
(see part (iii) in
3.1
).

R4.

The first three derivatives of the log-likelihood satisfy the following polynomial growth condition, where
r>0
is some positive constant:

\|\nabla\ell(\theta;\beta)\|+\|\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;\beta)\|_{F}+\|\nabla^{3}\ell(\theta;\beta)\|_{F}\lesssim 1+|\beta|^{r},\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

R5.

All finite moments of
B_{\theta_{0}}
exist.

R6.

The tilted random variables
B_{t,d,\theta}
satisfy:

\sup_{d\in B_{\epsilon}(d_{0})}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\big|\EE[B_{0,d,\theta}]\big|\lesssim 1,\quad\sup_{d\in B_{\epsilon}(d_{0})}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\|B_{t,d,\theta}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim 1+|t|,

where
B_{\epsilon}(d_{0}):=(d_{0}-\epsilon,d_{0}+\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon<\frac{d_{0}}{2}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
denotes the sub-Gaussian norm.

Remark 3.1

(On Conditions R4 and R5)
.

R4 is a technical polynomial growth condition for the first three derivatives of the log-likelihood. Note that this can be easily satisfied since this is a requirement for functions which are already in the log-scale. This is required to control the remainder term in the Taylor expansion for analyzing the first-order condition of (
11
), and is reminiscent of the usual integrability assumption for
\nabla^{3}\ell(\theta;\beta)
. Here we need the additional regularity for
\nabla\ell(\theta;\beta)
and
\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;\beta)
as the third derivative of
F_{i}(\theta)
involves all three derivatives. Also, R5 is a standard moment assumption for the prior. Note that it is weaker than a sub-Gaussian assumption on the prior.

Remark 3.2

(On Condition R6)
.

R6 requires sub-Gaussian tails for all linear and quadratic tilts of
B_{\theta}
. This is weaker than assuming sub-Gaussianity directly on the prior
\mu_{\theta}
. Here, as
\Theta
and
B_{\epsilon}(d_{0})
are bounded sets, the uniform control over all
\theta,d
is natural.
On the other hand, the linear tilt parameter
t
is unbounded, so instead of taking a supremum over
t
, we allow the sub-Gaussian norm to depend on
|t|
. This flexibility allows us to cover log-concave as well as non log-concave mixture priors (see
Lemma

3.2
below). We note that our results go through with minor modifications if we replace the linear growth of the sub-Gaussian norm in R6 with a general polynomial order growth condition. The linear growth is primarily for expositional simplicity.

The following lemma illustrates concrete instances where conditions R5, R6 in
3.4
hold, which already generalizes existing assumptions in the literature. In particular, we can accommodate priors with unbounded support, mixture priors, and sparsity inducing priors. On the other hand, existing works on low SNR high-dimensional Bayesian regression require the prior to have (a) bounded support
[
38
,
33
,
20
]
, (b) log-concave densities
[
32
,
49
]
, or (c) densities that satisfy the log-Sobolev inequality alongside a stronger version of our R4
[
21
]
. These assumptions in the existing literature exclude common choices in practical EB methods, such as the adaptive shrinkage priors (this is essentially scale mixtures of Gaussians, see
[
52
]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Conditions R5, R6 in
3.4
hold when the prior
\mu_{\theta}
satisfies any one of the following conditions.

1.

\mu_{\theta}
has bounded support.

2.

\mu_{\theta}
is a strongly log-concave density.

3.

\mu_{\theta}
is a finite mixture of
\delta_{0}
and Lebesgue densities which satisfy (R5), the first condition in (R6), and the stronger second condition

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{d\in B_{\epsilon}(d_{0})}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\|B_{t,d,\theta}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim 1.

In particular, these conditions for the densities are satisfied by distributions from parts (a) and (b) above.

Now, we state our main result on limiting distributions of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
,
3.4
hold. Then, for general
p=o(n)
, we have
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0}=O_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\frac{p}{n}\Big)
.
In particular, the following limits hold.

1.

When
p\ll n^{2/3}
, we have

(15)

\displaystyle\sqrt{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0})\mid X\xd N\Big(0,V(\theta_{0})^{-1}\Big).

2.

When
\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}\to\delta\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\sqrt{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0})\mid X\xd N\Big(\delta V(\theta_{0})^{-1}\kappa(\theta_{0}),V(\theta_{0})^{-1}\Big).

3.

In contrast, when
n^{2/3}\ll p\ll n
, we have

\frac{n}{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0})\mid X\xp V(\theta_{0})^{-1}\kappa(\theta_{0}).

We elaborate on each part of
3.3
. First consider the setting of part (a) which shows
\sqrt{p}
-consistency of the estimator, with a normal limit. This is the typical parametric rate expected in this problem, as justified in
Remark

3.3
below.
In our hierarchical model, as we only have indirect measurements of
\beta^{\star}=(\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu_{\theta_{0}}
, the limiting variance
V(\theta_{0})^{-1}
is strictly larger than
I(\theta_{0})^{-1}
, the limiting variance for estimating
\theta_{0}
directly from
\beta^{\star}
. We will further discuss the optimality of this limiting variance by deriving a matching lower bound in
Section

3.3
.

Part (b) considers higher dimensions with
p\propto n^{2/3}
. While the convergence rate and limiting variance is same as that in part (b), an additional bias appears in the limit. Note that this bias grows as
\delta=\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}
increases. In part (c), we further increase the dimension so that
\delta\to\infty
, or equivalently
n^{2/3}\ll p\ll n
. The resulting limiting distribution exhibits a
phase transition
and results in a slower rate of convergence (since
\frac{n}{p}\ll\sqrt{p}
under this regime). This is because now the bias term in part (b) dominates the variance.

To better understand this phase transition, we briefly discuss the proof strategy when
k=1
. Assuming that
\theta
is an one-dimensional parameter, the first-order conditions for (
11
) gives

\sqrt{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0})\approx\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})}{\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{0})}.

We show that the denominator converges to
V(\theta_{0})
as long as
p=o(n)
. To analyze the numerator, we additionally condition on the true coefficients
\beta^{\star}
and show that

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})\mid X,\beta^{\star}\approx N\big(\mu_{p}(X,\beta^{\star}),T_{2}\big),\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{p}(X,\beta^{\star}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\EE\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})\mid X,\beta^{\star}\Big]

and
T_{2}>0
is some constant variance (see (
51
)). The phase transition on
p
arises from analyzing this mean term, where we use second-order Taylor expansions to write

(17)

\displaystyle\mu_{p}(X,\beta^{\star})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Phi(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})+\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}\kappa(\theta_{0})

for some function
\Phi
with
\EE\Phi(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})=0
(see (
52
), (
53
)). When
p\ll n^{2/3}
, the first summand in (
17
) dominates and gives the limit
\mu_{p}(X,\beta^{\star})\mid X\xd N(0,T_{1})
. Combining this with the conditional normal limit (
16
) using the fact that
T_{1}+T_{2}=V(\theta_{0})
, we get
\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})\mid X\xd N(0,V(\theta_{0}))
. The conclusion in part (a) follows by Slutsky’s theorem.

In contrast, suppose
p\gg n^{2/3}
. Then, as the first term in the RHS of (
17
) is
O_{P}(1)
, the second term dominates. The resulting non-degenerate limit of
\mu_{p}
requires a slower convergence rate:

\frac{n}{p^{3/2}}\mu_{p}(X,\beta^{\star})\mid X\xd\kappa(\theta_{0}).

Consequently, we have
\frac{n}{p^{2}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}F_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{0})\mid X\xd\kappa(\theta_{0}),
and the conclusion in part (c) follows again by Slutsky’s theorem.

Remark 3.3

(
\sqrt{p}
is the optimal rate of convergence)
.

The convergence rate
\sqrt{p}
is information-theoretically tight. To see this, consider the setting where one directly observes all regression coefficients
\beta^{\star}=(\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu_{\theta_{0}}
. The resulting convergence rate for estimating
\theta_{0}
is
\sqrt{p}
, and hence a faster convergence rate is impossible when one is only given
y
.

We can also show that under our general asymptotic scaling
p=o(n)
, the method of moments estimator for
\theta
is
\sqrt{p}
-consistent, which illustrates that the slower rate in part (c) is sub-optimal. For illustration, assume a simple setting with
k=1
and suppose that the mean-mapping
M(\theta):=\EE B_{\theta}
is bijective. Then, by setting
\hat{\beta}:=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y
as the usual least squares estimator, we can define the first moment-based estimator as:

\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{MoM}}_{p}:=M^{-1}\Big(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\hat{\beta}_{i}\Big).

By summing up the separate limiting distributions of
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\hat{\beta}_{i}-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}{\beta}^{\star}_{i}\mid y,X
,
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}{\beta}^{\star}_{i}-M(\theta_{0})\mid X
and applying the Delta method, we can show the following limit:

\sqrt{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{MoM}}_{p}-\theta_{0})\xd N\Big(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}+\Var(B_{\theta_{0}})}{M^{\prime}(\theta_{0})^{2}}\Big).

While this limit generally holds under
p=o(n)
, the limiting variance is sub-optimal and strictly greater than
V(\theta_{0})^{-1}
.

Remark 3.4

(Faster rate of convergence at singularity)
.

In part (c) of
3.3
, note that the
k
-dimensional vector
\kappa(\theta_{0})
may be zero. While this singularity is typically a measure-zero event (see Figure
1
below for a visual illustration), the convergence rate at such points is
faster
than the
\frac{n}{p}
-rate in part (c). In particular, assuming
\kappa(\theta_{0})=0
, we can extend the proof of
3.3
to show that
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0}=O_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\big(\frac{p}{n}\big)^{3/2}\Big)
. Consequently, (
15
) extends to the higher-dimensional asymptotic regime of
p=o(n^{3/4})
, and the phase transition no longer happens at
p\asymp n^{2/3}
.

Fig 1
:
Visualization of
\kappa(\theta)
under various priors: (first)
\textsf{Ber}(\theta)
, (second) Normal with unknown variance:
N(0,1/\theta)
, (third/fourth) Gaussian mixtures
\frac{1}{2}N(\theta_{1},1)+\frac{1}{2}N(\theta_{2},0.25)
with
\theta_{2}=-1
fixed throughout the visualization. For the mixture prior,
\kappa(\theta)=(\kappa_{1}(\theta),\kappa_{2}(\theta))
is a two-dimensional vector, and each component is plotted in the third/fourth figure. We see that the singularity
\kappa(\theta)=0
is atypical. In particular, for the Gaussian mixture case, the singularity occurs when the two mixture components share a common mean
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=-1
.

To illustrate the limits in
3.3
, we consider a toy example of the Gaussian prior with unknown mean. Here, the conjugacy allows directly computing the limit distribution of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
.

Example 3.1

(Gaussian mean)
.

As a toy example, suppose that the prior is a Gaussian with unknown mean and known variance:
\mu_{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N(\theta,1)
. Then, the integral for defining the functions
F_{i}
becomes tractable (see (
11
)), and we can write

F_{i}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(\frac{2\pi}{d_{i}+1}\Big)-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+\frac{(w_{i}+\theta)^{2}}{2(d_{i}+1)}.

As this is a quadratic function in
\theta
, the vEB estimator in (
11
) can be explicitly written as

\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}=\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}w_{i}/(d_{i}+1)}{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}d_{i}/(d_{i}+1)}=\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}w_{i}}{pd_{0}}+O_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),

where the second equality used
d_{i}=d_{0}+O_{P,X}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Using the Gaussianity
w\mid X,\beta^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N(\Sigma\beta^{\star},\Sigma)
where
\Sigma:=d_{0}X^{\top}X
(where
d_{0}=\sigma^{-2}
is as in
3.1
), we can write

\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}\mid X,\beta^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}\frac{1}{pd_{0}}N\Big({1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma\beta^{\star}},1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma 1_{p}\Big).

Then, using the Gaussianity of
\beta^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N(\theta_{0}1_{p},I_{p})
, we can spell out the limit of the mean as

1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma\beta^{\star}\mid X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N\Big(\theta_{0}{1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma 1_{p}},{1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma^{2}1_{p}}\Big).

By combining the two displays above and noting
\frac{1_{p}^{\top}\Sigma 1_{p}}{p}=d_{0}+O_{P,X}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)
(standard mean and variance computations using the design matrix randomness), we have obtained the limit distribution of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
:

\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}\mid X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}N\Big(\theta_{0},\frac{1+d_{0}}{pd_{0}}\Big)+O_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Under the asymptotic scaling
p=o(n)
, the error terms are negligible and we can write:

(18)

\sqrt{p}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\theta_{0})\mid X\xd N(0,1+\sigma^{2}).

To see that this matches
3.3
, note that the corresponding log-likelihood derivative can be written as
\ell^{\prime}(\theta;b)=b-\theta
and
\Var(B_{\theta}\mid W_{\theta})=\frac{1}{1+1/\sigma^{2}}
.
We first compare with part (a) by computing the limiting variance under
3.3
. Direct computations give

I(\theta)=\Var(B_{\theta})=1,\quad\EE\Var(B_{\theta}\mid W_{\theta})=\frac{1}{1+1/\sigma^{2}},

so we have
V(\theta)=\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}}
. This is exactly inverse of the limiting variance computed in (
18
).

To check consistency with parts (b) and (c) in
3.3
, note that
h_{\theta}(t)=\EE\ell^{\prime}(\theta;B_{t,d_{0},\theta})=\EE[B_{t,d_{0},\theta}]-\theta=t
is exactly linear. Thus,
h_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}\equiv 0
for all
\theta
, and
\kappa\equiv 0
. In other words, every
\theta
is a singular point in the sense of
Remark

3.4
. The limit in part (c) becomes degenerate, and does not reflect the tight order as the actual limit in (
18
).

3.2
Extension to misspecified priors

One natural question would be to go beyond the well-specified requirement in
3.3
by allowing
\beta^{\star}
to be generated from a misspecified prior, say
\mu^{\star}\notin\{\mu_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
. Standard finite-dimensional Bayesian asymptotics
[
6
,
29
]
consider a
misspecified likelihood
and showed that the resulting posterior contracts around a KL minimizer. Compared to such results, we consider a
misspecified prior
while still assuming a correctly specified likelihood (see (
1
)). Interestingly, as we shall illustrate, our results more resemble model misspecification in the frequentist literature
[
61
]
.

To formalize the setup, instead of requiring a well-specified class of priors as in
3.3
, assume that
\beta_{i}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu^{\star}
. Here,
\mu^{\star}
is a true “data-generating” measure, which may not be parametrized as
\mu_{\theta}
.
We also modify the notations in parts (b) and (c) of
3.1
accordingly as follows.

Definition 3.2

(Extension of
3.1
to misspecified priors)
.

(a)

For any
\theta\in\Theta
, define random variables
(\beta^{\star},W^{\star},B_{\theta}^{\star})
as

\beta^{\star}\sim\mu^{\star},\quad W^{\star}\mid\beta^{\star}\sim N(d_{0}\beta^{\star},d_{0}),\quad B_{\theta}^{\star}\mid W^{\star}\sim B_{W^{\star},d_{0},\theta}.

Here, recall the notation
B_{t,d,\theta}
from part (c) in
3.1
. Set
V(\theta)
as in (
13
), and also define

V^{\star}(\theta):=V(\theta)-\EE\Big[\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta;B_{\theta}^{\star})+\big(\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta}^{\star})\big)\big(\nabla\ell(\theta;B_{\theta}^{\star})\big)^{\top}\Big].

(ii)

Define a mapping
\kappa^{\star}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
as

\kappa^{\star}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\EE[(\beta^{\star})^{2}]\EE[h_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(W^{\star})].

Recalling the discussion in (
14
),
\beta^{\star}
denotes a sample coefficient, and
W^{\star}
denotes the approximation of each
w_{i}
. Also recalling from
3.1
that
\frac{dB_{W^{\star},d_{0},\theta}}{dB_{\theta}}(b)\propto e^{-\frac{(W^{\star}-d_{0}b)^{2}}{2d_{0}}}
,
B_{\theta}^{\star}
denotes a draw from the posterior
\beta\mid W^{\star}
(while using
\mu_{\theta}
as the prior). Note that these modified notations reduce to that in
3.1
under correct specification, in the sense that we have
B_{\theta}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}B_{\theta}
and
V^{\star}(\theta)=V(\theta)
(by Bartlett’s second identity) when
\mu^{\star}=\mu_{\theta}
. However such reductions are not possible for a general
\mu^{\star}\notin\{\mu_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
.

Finally, define
\theta^{\star}
as the parameter value that minimizes the KL divergence between
W^{\star}
and
W_{\theta}
, i.e.

(19)

\theta^{\star}\in\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathrm{D_{KL}}(W^{\star}\mid W_{\theta}).

Here, viewing
W^{\star}
as an approximation of each
w_{i}
,
\theta^{\star}
is the parameter value that best approximates this “data-generating” distribution
W^{\star}
. We formally state limit distributions of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
under misspecified priors below.

Corollary 3.4

(Generalization of
3.3
to misspecified priors)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
hold (where we replace all instances of
\theta_{0}
to
\theta^{\star}
in
3.2
), and suppose that (
19
) has a unique minimizer. Assume R4,R6 for the class of priors
\mu_{\theta}

Lower bound

We show that the limiting variance in (

Definition 3.3

Thus, understanding the fundamental limits of estimation boils down to evaluating the Fisher information

Below we state our main lower bound result, under a technical bounded support assumption.

4
Consequences of EB in downstream posterior inference

Moving on from the frequentist guarantees for recovering the prior
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
discussed in
Section

3
, now we investigate consequences of EB (Empirical Bayes) in downstream
posterior inference
for the regression coefficients
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})
. To set notation, we formally define the oracle posterior and the EB posterior.

Definition 4.1

(Oracle and EB posterior)
.

Given a prior
\mu_{\theta}
, let
\PP_{\theta}(\beta)
denote the posterior distribution under the likelihood in (
1
):

\PP_{\theta}(\beta)=\PP_{\theta}(\beta\mid y,X)\propto\PP(y\mid X,\beta)\prod_{i=1}^{p}d\mu_{\theta}(\beta_{i}).

In particular, we say that
\PP_{\theta_{0}}(\beta)
is the
oracle posterior
. For
any estimator

\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}=\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}(y,X)
of
\theta_{0}
, we say that
\PP_{\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}}(\beta)
is the
EB posterior
.

Here note that the EB posterior
\PP_{\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}}
depends on the estimated prior
\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}
. So, the data
y
is double-dipped to construct
\PP_{\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}}
; once to write the likelihood
\PP(y\mid X,\beta)
and second to estimate the prior
\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}
. In this section, the notation
\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}
will denote any generic estimator, and
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
will continue to denote the vEB estimator (
11
). Also,
\beta
will denote a sample from the posterior (or its variational approximation), as opposed to the true regression coefficients
\beta^{\star}
(see
3.3
).

Our main goal is to understand whether the EB posterior is a good approximation of the oracle posterior. In this section, we answer this question in terms of
posterior inference
for (a) each coordinate, and (b) an one-dimensional projection. Due to technical reasons (mainly to use dependent concentration inequalities in
[
34
]
), this section will work under
3.5
and the asymptotic scaling
p=o(n^{2/3})
.

4.1
Estimating marginal distributions

Here, note that even understanding the oracle posterior itself is a nontrivial task due to its dependent and high-dimensional structure. In fact, it is extremely challenging to even compute the posterior mean and variance due to the intractable normalizing constant. Fortunately, the variational approximation turns out to be useful for understanding these intractable quantities. Below, we introduce the notion of
variational posteriors
, which are product measure approximations of the corresponding posterior.

Definition 4.2

(Variational posteriors)

Recall from

that
\PP_{\theta}
denotes the usual posterior given a prior
\mu_{\theta}

as the Mean-Field variational approximation of

\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}(\beta):=\argmin_{\mathbb{Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}}\mathrm{D_{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\mid\PP_{\theta}),

\mu_{\theta}

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose

1.

(20)

\displaystyle\EE_{\PP_{\theta_{0}}}[\beta_{i}]

\displaystyle=u_{i}+O_{P,X}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big),\quad\Var_{\PP_{\theta_{0}}}[\beta_{i}]=\tau_{i}^{2}+O_{P,X}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big),

\displaystyle\EE_{\PP_{\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}}}[\beta_{i}]

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{i}+O_{P,X}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big),\quad\Var_{\PP_{\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}}}[\beta_{i}]=\hat{\tau}_{i}^{2}+O_{P,X}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big).

(22)

\displaystyle\EE_{\PP_{\theta_{0}}}[\beta_{i}]

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{i}+O_{P}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}+\big(\frac{p}{\sqrt{n}}+1\big)\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}-\theta_{0}\|\Big),

\displaystyle\Var_{\PP_{\theta_{0}}}[\beta_{i}]

Here, part (a) illustrates that the variational approximations give meaningful estimates of the coordinate-wise mean and variance, for both the oracle posterior and EB posterior. Note that this claim justifies the variational approximation of the posterior, but does not discuss the effect of estimating the prior. Part (b) addresses this, by providing error bounds for moments of the vEB posterior and corresponding oracle quantities. For illustration, take

Thus, the vEB posterior constructed under
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
consistently estimates the marginal mean and variance.

4.2
Uncertainty quantification for linear statistics

Now we move beyond pointwise estimation and discuss the consequences of vEB on uncertainty quantification. We first consider the oracle case. Due to our low SNR scaling where the posterior crucially depend on the choice of prior, one cannot establish a normal limit for each coordinate of the posterior
[
48
]
. Recently, jointly with Sumit Mukherjee, the authors established normal limiting distributions for
one-dimensional projections
of the posterior
[
33
]
. We restate part (a) of Theorem 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 in
[
33
]
below.

Proposition 4.2

(CLT under the oracle posterior)
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a deterministic vector normalized as
\|q\|=1
, satisfying
\|q\|_{\infty}=o(1)
. Assume that the prior
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
is compactly supported. Set
\upsilon_{p}=\upsilon_{p}(y):=\Var_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta_{0}}}(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i})=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\tau_{i}^{2}
. Under the scaling
p\ll n^{2/3}
, we have

(23)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{p}\mid X\xp\upsilon(\theta_{0}):=\EE\Var(W_{\theta_{0}}\mid B_{\theta_{0}}),

and the following limit holds under both the oracle and variational posteriors:

\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(\beta_{i}-u_{i})}{\sqrt{\upsilon_{p}}}\mid y,X\xrightarrow[\PP_{\theta_{0}}]{d}N(0,1),\quad\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(\beta_{i}-u_{i})}{\sqrt{\upsilon_{p}}}\mid y,X\xrightarrow[\mathbb{Q}_{\theta_{0}}]{d}N(0,1).

In other words, under the oracle posterior, a CLT with a normal limiting distribution holds for the statistic
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i}
. Interestingly, the above result shows that one may replace the intractable mean and variance of the oracle posterior
\PP_{\theta_{0}}
with the tractable counterparts that arise from the Mean-Field approximation
\mathbb{Q}_{\theta_{0}}
. This allows one to perform posterior inference, such as constructing credible intervals with explicit expressions. Note that establishing limit distributions of the oracle posterior requires the asymptotic requirement
p\ll n^{2/3}
.

Armed with this result, our goal is to perform inference for one-dimensional projections
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i}
when
\theta_{0}
is unavailable. Given an EB estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}
, two immediate questions are:

•

Does
4.2
generalize to the EB posterior?

•

Can one modify the centering and variance in
4.2
to data-driven quantities (for example the plug-in counterparts using
\hat{\theta}_{\mathnormal{p}}
)?

The following result answers both questions, where the first question is true (see part (b)) but the plug-in part of the second question is generally false (see part (c)).
Recalling the notation
B_{t,d,\theta}
and

\displaystyle J(\theta):=\EE\left[\frac{\partial\EE[B_{W_{\theta},d_{0},\theta}\mid W_{\theta}]}{\partial\theta}\right],\quad\forall\theta\in\textnormal{int}(\Theta).

5.4
Improving the naive vEB estimator by debiasing

As the vEB estimator exhibits a sharp phase transition at
p\sim n^{2/3}
, a natural question arises: can one modify the vEB estimator to recover a
p^{-1/2}
convergence rate beyond
p\sim n^{2/3}
? To this end, one natural strategy is debiasing, especially noting that the limiting distribution in part (c) of
3.3
is essentially a degenerate bias. The following proposition shows that debiasing the vEB estimator can allow the normal limit (
15
) to extend to higher dimensions.

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume the usual assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
, and consider the debiased estimator

\tilde{\theta}^{\mathsf{d}}:=\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}-\frac{p}{n}V(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}})^{-1}\kappa(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}).

Then, the limit (
15
) holds for
\tilde{\theta}^{\mathsf{d}}
as long as
p\ll n^{3/4}
.

The following
Figure

2
considers the one-dimensional symmetric Gaussian mixture prior
\mu_{\theta}\equiv\frac{1}{2}N(\theta,1)+\frac{1}{2}N(-\theta,1)
, and illustrates that bias correction leads to better finite sample performance, which is more apparent for larger
p
. Our debiasing formula arises from correcting the second-order expansion of the likelihood. More specifically, noting that the slower rate of convergence of
\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}}
arises from the second term in the RHS of (
17
), we correct for this bias by replacing
\kappa(\theta_{0})
with
\kappa(\hat{\theta}^{\mathsf{vEB}})
. This is in contrast to the more common first-order debiasing in the frequentist literature for estimating high-dimensional regression coefficients
\beta

[
55
,
4
,
5
,
37
]
. We conjecture that it may be possible to extend the limit (
15
) to higher dimensions
p\gg n^{3/4}
by additionally correcting for higher-order error terms in the likelihood expansion (
17
).

6
Discussion

We discuss limitations of the current paper and avenues for future research.

1.

Technical conditions.
It would be important to improve upon various technical conditions. One immediate direction involves our design matrix assumption (see
3.1
), which requires entry-wise independence. While this is a natural starting point for theoretical understanding, it would be important to further relax this assumption to design matrices with less randomness, such as rotationally invariant matrices or fixed designs with correlated columns that were considered in the AMP literature
[
36
,
18
]
. We believe that the definition of the vEB estimator (see (
11
)) as well as the limit distributions and debiasing formula should change appropriately. Additionally, some parts of our analysis require a bounded prior (see
3.5
). As this restriction is inevitable due to limitations in existing literature for RFIMs
[
34
]
, it would be important to further understand RFIMs with relaxed conditions on the base measure.

2.

Singular or hierarchical priors.
One common choice for practical empirical Bayes procedures is to use finite (Gaussian) mixtures to model the prior
\mu_{\theta}
. While our results allow such mixtures, we require standard regularity assumptions (see
3.2
), which rules out singular models such as overfitted mixtures.
Indeed, it is well-known that overfitted mixtures have a slower
p^{1/4}
-rate of convergence for parameter estimation, even under the non-hierarchical setting with
p
i.i.d. observations
[
23
]
. It would be interesting to explore whether our phase transition for the vEB estimator persists under such singular priors.
On a related note, it would be interesting to view mixture priors as another layer of hierarchy and consider inference for each mixture component (for example, suppose
\beta_{i}\mid Z_{i}\sim P_{Z_{i}}
and
Z_{i}\sim\textsf{Categorical}(\pi)
, where
P_{1},\ldots,P_{M}
denotes each mixture distribution,
Z_{i}
denotes the mixture component, and
\pi
is a
M
-dimensional weight vector). Related hierarchical modeling approaches were recently studied under the classical normal means EB problem
[
26
]
as well as oracle structured variational inference in regression
[
50
]
, and it would be interesting to explore additional hierarchical modeling under our setup.

3.

Going beyond naive MF.
Finally, it would be interesting to utilize higher-order variational approximations (as opposed to the naive Mean-Field) such as the TAP free energy in statistical physics
[
54
,
30
]
to estimate the prior.
It is known that the TAP free energy can capture the leading-order asymptotics of the log-partition function under a proportional scaling
[
43
]
, but its tightness compared to NMF has not been investigated when
p=o(n)
. It would be interesting whether using the TAP free energy can improve prior recovery, and whether it has connections with the debiasing strategy mentioned in
5.1
.

\psi_{d,\theta}(0)=\log\int e^{-\frac{db^{2}}{2}}d\mu_{\theta}(b)\leq 0.

It remains to show the claims regarding
B_{t,d,\theta}
. Again by the sub-Gaussianity of
B_{t,d,\theta}
and (
54
), we have

\displaystyle\EE|B_{t,d,\theta}|^{r}\lesssim_{r}(1+|t|)^{r}+[\EE B_{t,d,\theta}]^{r}\lesssim_{r}1+|t|^{3r}.

Now, by taking

and using the tower property, we can further write

\EE\big[|B_{w_{i},d,\theta}|^{r}\mid X,\beta^{\star}\big]\lesssim_{r}1+\EE\big[|w_{i}|^{3r}\mid X,\beta^{\star}\big]\lesssim_{r,d_{0}}1+|\beta_{i}^{\star}|^{3r}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{3r},\quad\forall d,\theta.

Hence, using
Lemma

A.7
and the assumption R5 that
\beta_{i}^{\star}
has finite moments, we have
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\EE|B_{w_{i},d,\theta}|^{r}=O_{P}(p).

∎

Set

The last bound used

To show the second claim, for some

We start by showing the first claim. Fix any constant

\displaystyle\EE\left(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}e^{K|\mathcal{F}_{i}|}\mid\beta^{\star}\right)\xp 1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\EE\left(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}e^{K|\mathcal{F}_{i}|}\mid\beta^{\star}\right)^{2}\xp 1.

We will only prove the first display of (

Proof of
Lemma

A.8
.

We start by deriving general results that will be used to prove both parts of the statement.
Let
g:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
be a continuous function.
Define

T_{p}=T_{p}(B):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Big(g(B_{i})-\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,s_{i}+c_{i},\theta}}\Big).

Given an RFIM sample
B\sim\tilde{\PP}
, generate
B^{\prime}
as follows: let
I
be a randomly sampled index from
\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
. Given
I=i
, replace
B_{i}
with an independently generated
B^{\prime}_{i}
drawn from the conditional distribution of
B_{i}
given
(B_{j},\ j\neq i)
.

Recalling the notation
m_{i}
from
Lemma

A.10
, we can write

(56)

\displaystyle B_{i}\mid(B_{j},\ j\neq i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}\mu_{i,m_{i}+c_{i},\theta}.

For notational convenience, define

h_{i}(t):=\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,t,\theta}}=\EE_{B_{i}\sim\mu_{i,t,\theta}}g(B_{i}),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},

and note that (
56
) implies
\EE[g(B_{i})\mid B_{j}:j\neq i]=h_{i}(m_{i}+c_{i}).
Also, observe that

(57)

\displaystyle h_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\Cov_{\mu_{i,t,\theta}}(B_{i},g(B_{i})),\quad h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\Cov_{\mu_{i,t,\theta}}\Big((B_{i}-\psi_{i,\theta}^{\prime}(t))^{2},g(B_{i})\Big)

are uniformly bounded.
By a Taylor’s series expansion of around
m_{i}\approx s_{i}
, we get that for some
\{\xi_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
:

\displaystyle\EE(T_{p}-T_{p}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mid B)=\frac{1}{p\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(g(B_{i})-h_{i}(m_{i}+c_{i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\left(g(B_{i})-h_{i}(s_{i}+c_{i})-(m_{i}-s_{i})h_{i}^{\prime}(s_{i}+c_{i})-\frac{1}{2}(m_{i}-s_{i})^{2}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}+c_{i})\right)

(58)

\displaystyle={\frac{T_{p}}{p}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{p\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(m_{i}-s_{i})h_{i}^{\prime}(s_{i}+c_{i})}_{:=H_{1}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2p\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(m_{i}-s_{i})^{2}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}+c_{i})}_{:=H_{2}}.

We bound the error terms
H_{1},H_{2}
in the above expression.

•

For
H_{1}
, we expand
m_{i}-s_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}A_{p}(i,j)(B_{j}-u_{j})
and write

H_{1}=\frac{1}{p\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{j}(B_{j}-u_{j}),

where
\gamma_{j}:=\sum_{i\neq j}A_{p}(i,j)h_{i}^{\prime}(s_{i}+c_{i})
.
Then, by part (a) of
Lemma

A.10
, we have

p^{2}\EE H_{1}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{p}\big(\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{j}^{2}\big)\Big(1+\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big)=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\frac{p}{n}\Big).

The final inequality follows from using the operator norm bound for
A_{p}
in
Lemma

A.11
to write
\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{j}^{2}\lesssim p\|A_{p}\|^{2}=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}(p^{2}/n)
.

•

For
H_{2}
, using the fact that
h_{i}^{\prime\prime}
are uniformly bounded followed by part (b) of
Lemma

A.10
, we have

p^{2}\EE H_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{p}\EE\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(m_{i}-s_{i})^{2}\Big]^{2}=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big).

Now, we are ready to prove each claims.

(a) We prove the first moment bound. Since
(T_{p},T_{p}^{\prime})
form an exchangable pair, we have
\EE T_{p}=\EE T_{p}^{\prime}
. Hence, taking an outer expectation on (
58
) gives the identity

\EE T_{p}=p\EE H_{1}+p\EE H_{2}.

The earlier error bounds for
H_{1},H_{2}
implies that the RHS is
\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}}\right)
.

To prove the second conclusion, using a Taylor expansion followed by the operator norm bound of
A_{p}
(see
Lemma

A.11
) gives

(59)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Big[h_{i}(s_{i}+w_{i})-h_{i}(w_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Big[s_{i}h_{i}^{\prime}(\xi_{i}+w_{i})\Big]\sum_{1\mathnormal{\leq i\neq j\leq p}}A_{p}(i,j)s_{i}h_{i}^{\prime}(\xi_{i}+w_{i})u_{j}=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\frac{p\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Here, we have used the fact that
h_{i}^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded, which follows from the compact support assumption.

(b) Next, we prove the second moment bound.
Let
\Delta_{p}:=T_{p}-T_{p}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}(g(B_{I})-g(B_{I}^{\prime}))
and note that
|\Delta_{p}|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{p}}
. Multiplying (
58
) by
pT_{p}
and taking an outer expectation gives

p\EE[\Delta_{p}T_{p}]=\EE[T_{p}^{2}]-(p\EE[T_{p}H_{1}]+p\EE[T_{p}H_{2}]).

On the other hand, by exchangeability of
(T_{p},T_{p}^{\prime})
, we can also write

p\EE[\Delta_{p}T_{p}]=\frac{p\EE[\Delta_{p}^{2}]}{2}\leq 2.

Hence, by upper bounding

(60)

\displaystyle|p\EE[T_{p}H_{a}]|\leq\sqrt{p^{2}\EE H_{a}^{2}}\sqrt{\EE[T_{p}^{2}]},\quad a=1,2,

we get the inequality

\EE T_{p}^{2}\leq 2+\sum_{a=1}^{2}\sqrt{p^{2}\EE H_{a}^{2}}\sqrt{\EE[T_{p}^{2}]}.

By plugging-in the earlier bounds for
p^{2}\EE H_{a}^{2}
, we get a preliminary bound
\EE[T_{p}^{2}]=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}(1)
.

To compute the precise constant for
\EE[T_{p}^{2}]
, we expand the term
p\EE[\Delta_{p}^{2}]
as follows:

\displaystyle p\EE[\Delta_{p}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\EE\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}[g(B_{i})-g(B_{i}^{\prime})]^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Big[\EE g(B_{i})^{2}-\EE[g(B_{i})\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,m_{i}+c_{i},\theta}}]\Big],

where the last line follows by the tower property (by conditioning on
(B_{j}:j\neq i)
and using (
56
)) and using exchangeability.
Now, using the two conclusions of part (c) in
Lemma

A.10
(with
h\equiv g^{2},g
, respectively) gives the following moment bounds:

\displaystyle\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}g(B_{i})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\langle g^{2}\rangle_{\mu_{i,m_{i}+c_{i},\theta}}\Big]+O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\Big),

\displaystyle\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}g(B_{i})\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,\theta}(m_{i}+c_{i})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\big(\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,m_{i}+c_{i},\theta}}\big)^{2}\Big]+O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\Big).

We additionally simplify the RHS expressions using the following fact: for any continuous function
h:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
, we have

\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}h(m_{i}+c_{i})\Big]=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}h(c_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}|m_{i}|=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}h(c_{i})+\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big({\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}}\Big),

which follows from bounding
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}|m_{i}|\leq\sqrt{p}\sqrt{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}m_{i}^{2}}=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{n}}\Big).

By taking
h
appropriately, we can further simplify the individual moment bounds as:

\displaystyle\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}g(B_{i})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\langle g^{2}\rangle_{\mu_{i,c_{i},\theta}}+\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big),

\displaystyle\EE\Big[\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}g(B_{i})\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,\theta}(m_{i}+c_{i})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\big(\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,c_{i},\theta}}\big)^{2}+\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\Big).

By combining all computations above, we have the following:

\EE[T_{p}^{2}]=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Big[\langle g^{2}\rangle_{\mu_{i,c_{i},\theta}}-\big(\langle g\rangle_{\mu_{i,c_{i},\theta}}\big)^{2}\Big]+(p\EE[T_{p}H_{1}]+p\EE[T_{p}H_{2}])+\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big({\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}}\Big).

Using (
60
) along the preliminary bound
\EE T_{p}^{2}=O(1)
, we have

|p\EE[T_{p}H_{1}]+p\EE[T_{p}H_{2}]|\leq\sqrt{\EE T_{p}^{2}}\Big(\sqrt{p^{2}\EE H_{1}^{2}}+\sqrt{p^{2}\EE H_{2}^{2}}\Big)=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},X}\Big({\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}}}\Big).

This completes the proof. Note that the term
\sqrt{p/n}
is omitted in the final bound since

\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}.

∎

m\leq M
. Then, by using Hoeffding’s inequality, we can write

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\pi_{m}e^{\lambda\mu_{m}}=\EE e^{\lambda Y}\leq e^{\lambda\EE Y+\frac{\lambda^{2}(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min})^{2}}{8}}.

Finally, noting that
\EE X=\EE Y
, we have

\EE e^{\lambda(X-\EE X)}\leq\exp\Big[\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\Big(K_{\max}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min})^{2}\Big)\Big]

and the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

B.2
.

Recall that
X_{p}\mid Y_{p}\xp 0
if and only if
\PP(|X_{p}|>\epsilon\mid Y_{p})\xp 0
for any
\epsilon>0
. The claim is immediate after using the tower property to write
\PP(|X_{p}|>\epsilon)=\EE\PP(|X_{p}|>\epsilon\mid Y_{p})
, and using the bounded convergence theorem (for the if part) / Markov inequality (only if part).
∎

Proof of
Lemma

B.3
.

It suffices to prove the claim under two cases: (1)
m=m_{0}
, (2)
d=d_{0}
. First, suppose
m=m_{0}=0
.
Noting that
\EE h(V)=\EE h(\sqrt{\frac{d}{d_{0}}}V_{0})
, we can write

\displaystyle|\EE h(V)-\EE h(V_{0})|

\displaystyle=|\EE h(\sqrt{\frac{d}{d_{0}}}V_{0})-\EE h(V_{0})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\EE\Big|\big(\sqrt{\frac{d}{d_{0}}}-1\big)V_{0}\Big|(1+|V_{0}|^{r})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{r}\big|\sqrt{\frac{d}{d_{0}}}-1\big|(d_{0}^{1/2}+d_{0}^{(r+1)/2})\lesssim_{d_{0},r}|d-d_{0}|.

Here, the second line follows from a Taylor expansion alongside the polynomial growth bound
|h^{\prime}(v)|\lesssim 1+|v|^{r}
, where
v\leq\max(V_{0},\sqrt{d/d_{0}}V_{0})\leq\sqrt{2}V_{0}
.
The square root term in the final line is simplified using
\sqrt{\frac{d}{d_{0}}}-1=\frac{d-d_{0}}{\sqrt{d_{0}}(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{d_{0}})}.

Next, suppose
d=d_{0}=1
. Then, by similar computations using the bound

\sup_{v\in[v_{0}+(m-m_{0}),v_{0}]}|h^{\prime}(v)|\lesssim\sup_{v\in[v_{0}+(m-m_{0}),v_{0}]}\big[1+|v|^{r}\big]\lesssim_{r}1+|v_{0}|^{r}+|m-m_{0}|^{r},

we have

\displaystyle|\EE h(V)-\EE h(V_{0})|

\displaystyle=|\EE h(V_{0}+(m-m_{0}))-\EE h(V_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|m-m_{0}|(1+\EE|V_{0}|^{r}+|m-m_{0}|^{r})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{r}|m-m_{0}|(1+m_{0}^{r}+|m-m_{0}|^{r}).

∎

Lemma

B.4
using another technical moment bound as a separate lemma, which will be proved at the end of this appendix.

Lemma C.1
.

Assuming
\EEB(\beta_{1}^{\star})^{4}<\infty
and
\EEB g(\beta_{1}^{\star})^{2}<\infty
, it holds that

\EE\Big[\frac{n}{p^{2}}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})-\EEB g(\beta_{1}^{\star})\EEB(\beta_{1}^{\star})^{2}\Big]^{2}=O\Big(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{p}{n}\Big).

Proof of
Lemma

B.4
.

(a) Let
\Phi
be any
\mathcal{C}^{3}
function such that
|\Phi^{\prime}(m)|,|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(m)|,|\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(m)|=\mcp(r)
for some constant
r>0
.
By a Taylor expansion, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Phi(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}-\mathcal{F}_{i})-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Phi(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}\Phi^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{3}\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}+\xi_{i}),

where
\xi_{i}\in(-\mathcal{F}_{i},0)
. We separately control the second moment of each term in the RHS.

We start by control the first term in (
63
). By computing the moment (c.f. Lemma A.9(a) in
[
33
]
), we have

\EE_{X}\Big[\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}\Phi^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})\Big]^{2}\mid\beta^{\star}\Big]\lesssim\frac{\|\beta^{\star}\|^{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\Phi^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})^{2}}{n}\lesssim\frac{\|\beta^{\star}\|^{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}|\beta_{i}^{\star}|^{2r}}{n}=O_{P,\beta^{\star}}\Big(\frac{p^{2}}{n}\Big).

The last bound used assumption R5 that
\beta_{i}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\equiv}}B_{\theta_{0}}
has finite moments. Hence, we have

\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}\Phi^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})=O_{P}\Big(\frac{p}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Next, we move on to the second term in (
63
). By the moment bound in
Lemma

C.1
, we can write

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})=\frac{p^{2}}{n}\kappa_{\Phi}(\theta_{0})+O_{P}\Big(\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}+\frac{p^{5/2}}{n^{3/2}}\Big).

Finally, we control the third term in (
63
). By Holder’s inequality (with exponents
4/3,4
), using the growth condition on
\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}
, and recalling
|\xi_{i}|\leq|\mathcal{F}_{i}|
, we have

\displaystyle\EE_{X}\Big|\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{3}\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}+\xi_{i})\Big|\leq\Big[\EE_{X}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{4}\Big]^{3/4}\Big[\EE_{X}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\big[|d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}|^{4r}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{4r}\big]\Big]^{1/4}=O_{P,\beta^{\star}}\Big(\frac{p^{5/2}}{n^{3/2}}\Big).

Here, the last bound follows as (i)
\EE_{X}\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{4}\Big]=O_{P,\beta^{\star}}(p^{3}/n^{2})
by Lemma A.9(b) in
[
33
]
, and (ii)
\EE_{X}\Big[\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}|d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}|^{4r}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{4r}\Big]=O_{P,\beta^{\star}}(p)
by the moment assumption for
\beta_{i}^{\star}
and the moment bounds for
\mathcal{F}_{i}
in
Lemma

B.6
.

The final bound follows by combining the above bounds and noting that

\frac{p}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{p^{5/2}}{p^{3/2}}\gg\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}.

Note that the final form in
Lemma

B.4
, which is conditional on
X
, follows from
Lemma

B.2
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

B.6
.

Throughout the proof, all expectations will be over
X
, and always be conditional on
\beta^{\star}
.
Fix any
i\leq p
. For
k\leq n
, let
Z_{k,i}:=\sqrt{n}X_{k,i}
and recall from
3.1
that
Z_{k,i}
are mean zero sub-Gaussian random variables with sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
K>0
. Let us assume
By definition, we can write
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq p}Z_{k,i}Z_{k,j}\beta_{j}^{\star}
. Setting
Y_{k,i}:=\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq p}Z_{k,j}\beta_{j}^{\star}
,
Y_{k,i}
is a sub-Gaussian random variable whose norm is bounded by
K\|\beta^{\star}\|
by Hoeffding’s inequality.

Now, the product property for sub-Gaussians (e.g. Lemma 2.8.6 in
[
58
]
) implies that
Z_{k,i}Y_{k}
is sub-exponential with the corresponding sub-exponential norm bounded by
K^{2}\|\beta^{\star}\|
. This can be equivalently written out as

\EE e^{\lambda^{\prime}Z_{k,i}Y_{k}}\leq e^{K^{4}\|\beta^{\star}\|^{2}\lambda^{\prime 2}},\quad\forall\,\,|\lambda^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{K^{2}\|\beta^{\star}\|}.

Now, by spelling out
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k,i}Y_{k,i}
and setting
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}n
, we have

\EE e^{\lambda\mathcal{F}_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\EE e^{\frac{\lambda}{n}Z_{k,i}Y_{k}}\leq e^{K^{4}\|\beta^{\star}\|^{2}\lambda^{2}/n}

as long as
|\lambda|\leq\frac{n}{K^{2}\|\beta^{\star}\|}
. The final conclusion follows by noting that
\|\beta^{\star}\|=\mathnormal{O}_{\mathnormal{P},\beta^{\star}}(\sqrt{p}).

∎

Proof of
Lemma

C.1
.

For notational convenience, define a
n\times p
matrix
Z
with entries
Z_{k,i}:=\sqrt{n}X_{k,i}
, so that each
Z_{k,i}
are independent sub-Gaussians with mean 0 and variance 1.
We will write
\EE_{\bZ}
and
\EEB
for expectations with respect to the matrix
Z
and
\bbeta
. Observe that

(64)

\displaystyle\EE_{\bZ}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}=\EE_{\bZ}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\neq i}Z_{k_{1},i}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}}\beta_{j_{1}}^{\star}\beta_{j_{2}}^{\star}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}(\beta_{j}^{\star})^{2}.

By using (
64
), we get:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{p^{2}}\EEB\EE_{\bZ}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\EEB\sum_{i\neq j}(\beta_{j}^{\star})^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{p-1}{p}\EEB(\beta_{1}^{\star})^{2}\EEB g(\beta_{1}^{\star})\to\EEB(\beta_{1}^{\star})^{2}\EEB g(\beta_{1}^{\star}).

Having established the limit for the mean, we proceed to show the variance converges to
0
. Using the law of total variance, we will separately show that

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{p^{4}}\EEB\Var\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})\mid\bbeta\right)\to 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{n^{2}}{p^{4}}\Var\EE_{\bZ}\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})\mid\bbeta\right)\to 0.

By (
64
), we have:

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\frac{n^{2}}{p^{4}}\Var\EE_{\bZ}\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})\mid\bbeta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{4}}\Var\left(\sum_{i\neq j}(\beta_{j}^{\star})^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{4}}\sum_{i\neq j}\Var(g(\beta_{i}^{\star})(\beta_{j}^{\star})^{2})+\frac{1}{p^{4}}\sum_{i_{!}\neq j_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}\neq j_{2}}\Cov(g(\beta_{i_{1}}^{\star})(\beta_{j_{1}}^{\star})^{2},g(\beta_{i_{2}}^{\star})(\beta_{j_{2}}^{\star})^{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim p^{-2}+p^{-1}\to 0,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the covariance term is
0
if all the indices
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}
are distinct. For the other term in (
65
), let us define

A(i,j_{1},j_{2}):=\beta_{j_{1}}^{\star}\beta_{j_{2}}^{\star}g(\beta_{i}^{\star}),\,\,\,\,1\leq i,j_{1},j_{2}\leq p.

We then note that

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\EEB\Var\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}g(\beta_{i}^{\star})\mid\bbeta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\neq i}\EEB[A^{2}(i,j_{1},j_{2})]\Var(Z_{k_{1},i}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\neq i_{1},\\
j_{3},j_{4}\neq i_{2}\end{subarray}}\EEB[A(i_{1},j_{1},j_{2})A(i_{2},j_{3},j_{4})]\Cov(Z_{k_{1},i_{1}}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i_{1}}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}},

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad Z_{k_{3},i_{2}}Z_{k_{3},j_{3}}Z_{k_{4},i_{2}}Z_{k_{4},j_{4}}).

As
\EE A^{2}(i,j_{1},j_{2})<\infty
uniformly, for
j_{1},j_{2}\neq i
, we have:

\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\neq i}\EEB[A^{2}(i,j_{1},j_{2})]\Var(Z_{k_{1},i}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}})=\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}=o(p^{4}/n^{2}).

To bound the covariance part, we will first isolate terms for which

\displaystyle\Cov(Z_{k_{1},i_{1}}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i_{1}}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}},Z_{k_{3},i_{2}}Z_{k_{3},j_{3}}Z_{k_{4},i_{2}}Z_{k_{4},j_{4}})

is not equal to
0
.
If the set
\{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\}
consists of
3
or more distinct elements, then clearly the above covariance term equals
0
. If
|\{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\}|=2
, once again if
k_{1}=k_{2}
,
k_{3}=k_{4}
, but
k_{1}\neq k_{3}
, then the covariance term will be
0
. So let us consider the case
k_{1}=k_{3}
,
k_{2}=k_{4}
. In that case, the covariance term reduces to

\Cov(Z_{k_{1},i_{1}}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i_{1}}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}},Z_{k_{1},i_{2}}Z_{k_{1},j_{3}}Z_{k_{2},i_{2}}Z_{k_{2},j_{4}}).

Now if
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, then simple calculation shows that the above covariance term can be non-zero only when
j_{3}=j_{4}=i_{1}
and
j_{1}=j_{2}=i_{2}
. The contribution of such terms is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k_{1}\neq k_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}\EEB[A(i_{1},i_{2},i_{2})A(i_{2},i_{1},i_{1})]\Var(Z_{k_{1},i_{1}}Z_{k_{1},i_{2}}Z_{k_{2},i_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i_{2}})\lesssim\frac{p^{2}}{n^{2}}=o(p^{4}/n^{2}).

Next suppose
i_{1}=i_{2}
. Then we can check that the covariance term will be
0
if either
j_{1}\neq j_{3}
or
j_{2}\neq j_{4}
. Therefore, to get non-zero covariance we must have
j_{1}=j_{3}
and
j_{2}=j_{4}
. The contribution of such terms is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k_{1}\neq k_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\neq i}\EEB[A^{2}(i,j_{1},j_{2})]\Var(Z_{k_{1},i}Z_{k_{1},j_{1}}Z_{k_{2},i}Z_{k_{2},j_{2}})=\frac{p^{3}}{n^{2}}=o(p^{4}/n^{2}).

The final remaining case is when
|\{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\}|=1
. In this case, it is not hard to check that unless
|\{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\}|\leq 4
, the covariance term will be
0
. The contribution of such terms is given by

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},(j_{1},j_{2})\neq i_{1},(j_{3},j_{4})\neq i_{2},\\
|\{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\}|\leq 4\end{subarray}}\EEB[A(i_{1},j_{1},j_{2})A(i_{2},j_{3},j_{4})]\Cov(Z_{k,i_{1}}^{2}Z_{k,j_{1}}Z_{k,j_{2}},Z_{k,i_{2}}^{2}Z_{k,j_{3}}Z_{k,j_{4}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{p^{4}}{n^{3}}=o(p^{4}/n^{2}).

This establishes (
65
) and completes the proof.
∎

Simulation details

Parameter space

For each parametric family defined in
Section

5
from the main text, we consider the following compact parameter space
\Theta
:

1.

for
\textsf{Ber}(\pi)
, consider

2.

for

for

Implementation details

The three methods are implemented as follows.

•

Variational EB:
We computed the vEB estimator by using the
optimize
(one-dimensional) and
optim
(higher-dimensional) functions in
R
to solve the minimization (
11
). For the latter, we used the “L-BFGS-B” method with the above parameter space constraints.

•

Method of Moments:
For each parametric family, we solve the following moment equations using the LSE
\hat{\beta}=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y
:

\displaystyle\EE_{\textsf{Ber}}\beta=\pi,\qquad\EE_{\textsf{Spike-Slab}}\big[\beta^{2},~\beta^{4}\big]=(1-\pi)\big[\tau^{2},~3\tau^{4}\big],

\displaystyle\EE_{\textsf{location-GMM}}\big[\beta,~\beta^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{2}\big[\theta_{1}+\theta_{2},~\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}+1.25\big].

The final estimates are thresholded to take values in the compact parameter space.

•

Langevin diffusion:
We adaptively sample the prior parameters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
and regression coefficients
\beta

4000
times via the discretized Langevin algorithm in
[
21
]
. The algorithm is initialized under the constraints
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}
, with the default learning rate
10^{-2}
(for both
\theta,\beta
). The final estimates are thresholded to take values in the compact parameter space.

The Mean-Field approximation in (
7
) is computed by iterating the fixed point equation for
u
in
2.2
.
To evaluate coverage guarantees under the Oracle posterior
\PP_{\theta_{0}}
, we draw samples from
\PP_{\theta_{0}}
as follows.

•

Bernoulli

IsingSampler
package in
R

[

•

Spike-Slab, GMM

\beta^{\star}
