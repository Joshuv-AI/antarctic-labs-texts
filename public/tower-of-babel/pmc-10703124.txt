Sensory Outcomes after Neurotization in Nipple-sparing Mastectomy and Implant-based Breast Reconstruction
Authors: Anne Warren Peled, Rie von Eyben, Ziv M. Peled
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Mastectomy and breast reconstruction techniques continue to evolve to optimize aesthetic and reconstructive outcomes. However, the loss of sensation after mastectomy remains a major limitation. This article describes our evolution of a novel approach that we first described in 2019, combining recent advances in breast oncologic, reconstructive, and peripheral nerve surgery to optimize sensory outcomes.Methods:Nipple-sparing mastectomy was performed in all patients and involved preservation of lateral intercostal nerves when anatomy was favorable. When nerves could not be preserved without compromising oncologic safety, nipple–areolar complex neurotization was performed using allograft or intercostal autograft from a transected T3, T4, or T5, lateral intercostal nerve to identified subareolar nerve targets. Immediate, prepectoral, direct-to-implant reconstruction was then performed. Acroval one-point moving and one-point static pressure thresholds established baseline sensibility values, which were then repeated at multiple time points postoperatively.Results:Outcomes from 47 women (79 breasts) were assessed prospectively. Mean follow-up was 9.2 months (range 6–14 months). At 6 months postoperatively, over 80% of patients had good-to-excellent one-point moving as well as one-point static sensibility scores averaged across all areas tested. None of the patients developed persistent dysesthesia or clinical evidence of neuroma.Conclusions:This study represents the largest series reported to date of sensibility outcomes after nipple-sparing mastectomy and implant reconstruction with concurrent neurotization. Sensibility results show that this approach allows for preservation of high degrees of breast and nipple–areolar complex sensation in most patients.

Takeaways

Question: How does neurotization impact sensory outcomes after nipple-sparing mastectomy and implant breast reconstruction?

Findings: Performing neurotization led to high rates of sensory return in the breast and nipple–areolar complex as measured by postoperative neurosensory testing.

Meaning: Performing neurotization during nipple-sparing mastectomy and implant breast reconstruction improves postmastectomy breast and nipple–areolar complex sensation.

INTRODUCTION

With the evolution of mastectomy techniques1–4 and improved reconstruction options, excellent aesthetic outcomes after mastectomy are now possible, particularly with newer, less-invasive reconstruction approaches.5 However, limitations related to quality-of-life outcomes after mastectomy still exist, particularly with the loss of sensation that typically occurs after mastectomy. The relatively poor sensation typically seen after mastectomy has been well described, with many studies documenting suboptimal patient-reported sensory outcomes after these operations.6–8 Still, others have found meaningful breast skin and nipple–areolar complex (NAC) sensation rates in the range of 0%–47%.9–11 These low rates of sensory return combined with associated patient dissatisfaction6 have prompted a number of surgeons to develop approaches to improve sensibility following mastectomy.

Use of autologous reconstruction to restore postmastectomy sensation loss was first reported by Slezak et al in 1992,12 with use of a neurotized, pedicled transverse rectus abdominis musculocutaneous flap. Since that time, other surgeons have described techniques for re-innervation of DIEP flaps with promising results.13,14 Not surprisingly, innervated autologous reconstruction patients reported improved quality of life compared with their denervated counterparts.15,16 Refinements in technique have allowed for preservation of motor nerve branches to minimize donor site morbidity while still allowing for flap neurotization.17 However, widespread applicability of these approaches in breast reconstruction is limited, as many patients are not (or choose not to be) candidates for autologous reconstruction, consistent with data showing that approximately 80% of all current breast reconstruction procedures in the United States are implant-based.18 Moreover, studying sensory return after breast reconstruction has been challenging given the myriad reconstruction techniques used in different studies, and the various outcomes metrics studied and the tools used to study them.19

Consideration for sensory reconstruction with implant-based reconstruction has become possible because of improvements in peripheral nerve surgical techniques. Use of acellular nerve allografts has shown promising results with motor, sensory, and mixed motor/sensory nerves.20,21 Specifically, results comparable to using autografts, considered by many to be the gold standard for nerve reconstruction, have been shown in defects up to 7 cm.22,23 In addition, use of connector-assisted repairs has potential advantages at nerve coaptation sites that may further improve outcomes.24 All the developments noted above have arrived at a fortunate time because the past few years have also seen increased public awareness of the toll of breast cancer treatment, specifically the loss of sensation postoperatively.25

Leveraging all these advances in breast oncologic, reconstructive, and peripheral nerve surgical techniques, we previously reported our early results with a novel surgical procedure aimed at optimizing sensibility following nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) and implant-based breast reconstruction.26 Similar approaches and promising results have subsequently been reported by other groups in smaller studies.27,28 This study expands upon our prior work in a larger patient population with a longer follow-up and more rigorous outcome metrics incorporating sensibility assessments using the Acroval (Axogen, Jacksonville, Fla.) pressure-specified sensory device (PSSD).

METHODS

Between May 2019 and December 2021, 207 women underwent immediate, NAC neurotization during NSM and prepectoral, direct-to-implant acellular dermal matrix-assisted reconstruction. NSM and reconstruction were performed by a single plastic surgeon formally trained in breast surgical oncology, whereas nerve dissection and grafting were performed by a plastic surgeon formally trained in peripheral nerve surgery. The details of this procedure have been described in our pilot study.26 For all patients, the dominant lateral intercostal nerve was dissected as far distally as oncologically feasible and then transected following a gentle tug test to determine its point of insertion at the NAC. Connector-assisted nerve reconstruction for NAC neurotization was then performed using an Avance nerve allograft (Axogen, Jacksonville, Fla.) from the transected intercostal nerve to a subareolar nerve. The cost of the allografts was covered by insurance and billed through the surgery center or hospital in nearly all cases, with a prior authorization process that included preapproval of allograft materials for nerve reconstruction.

Pre- and postoperative neurosensory testing data were collected in an institutional review board–approved protocol. Patients were tested preoperatively for baseline sensibility scores using the Acroval PSSD. This device allows for quantitative measurements of both one-point static (1PS) and one-point moving (1PM) pressure thresholds. During testing, a 1 mm2 blunt prong attached to a force transducer is applied with gradually increasing pressure to each area tested. With 1PS testing, the prong is applied in a stationary location with increasing pressure whereas with 1PM testing, the prong slides along the skin within the location of interest (eg, the superior breast quadrant) with increasing pressure. As soon as the patient perceives the touch of the prong, they indicate that sensation by pressing a button held within their dominant hand. The force applied in grams per mm2 is then recorded as the pressure threshold. Testing was performed in each of the following five locations: the superior breast skin, the lateral breast skin half the distance between the lateral areolar border and the breast interface with the thoracic cage, the superior areola, the lateral areola, and the nipple. It was repeated on each involved breast at each time point and measured preoperatively, and then repeated postoperatively at 6 months and 12 months after mastectomy/reconstruction.

Patient and Treatment Characteristics

Static and moving pressure threshold values were further analyzed as continuous outcomes in a mixed effects model to account for the within-subject correlation. Given that each patient could potentially have results from bilateral mastectomies, each breast was measured at five locations, and each location was measured at multiple time points. In the mixed effects model, time was included as a random effect and the fixed effects included location tested, baseline pressure, and age as well as the interactions between location and time, baseline pressure and time, and age and time. Three-way interactions were tested as well, though none were included in the final reporting because they were not statistically significant. All statistical tests were two-sided with an alpha level of 0.05, and all analyses were performed in SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, N.C.).

RESULTS

Of the patients eligible during the study period, 47 women (79 breasts) completed neurosensory testing a minimum of 6 months postoperatively and constitute the study group for analysis. Mean follow-up was 9.2 months (range 6–14 months). Patient and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Overall major complication rate (including unplanned return to the operating room, implant infection requiring intravenous antibiotics or implant exchange/removal, wound dehiscence or skin flap/nipple necrosis requiring intervention, and seroma) was 2.5%.

At six months postoperatively, 66% of the 1PM pressure thresholds were in the excellent range, and a further 20% were in the good range. At 12 months, 75% of the moving pressure measurements were in the excellent range with an additional 21% in the good range (Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

As oncologic and reconstructive treatments for breast cancer have fortunately improved and many women are living longer with their diagnosis, we need a stronger focus on the long-term sequelae of those treatments. In this study, we report on the largest series to date of a novel surgical procedure intended to optimize postmastectomy sensation in the setting of immediate, implant-based reconstruction.

Our clinical results with this operative technique demonstrate higher levels of breast and nipple–areolar complex sensation than prior reports of NSM and immediate reconstruction using standard mastectomy approaches. For example, Dossett et al reported that only 26% of patients had measurable sensation after NSM and that the degree of that sensation was relatively poor.9 Although we cannot perform a formal, direct comparison, because the outcome metrics in this and similar studies were different from ours, PSSD results are more precise than those obtained using Semmes Weinstein monofilaments, as the data are linear, not logarithmic, and the device can be routinely calibrated while the monofilaments cannot. The PSSD has been validated and used in numerous published works to measure sensibility following nerve repair both in the extremities and the breast for two decades.29,30 Several investigators have compared the various assessment methods for skin sensory receptors and found the PSSD not only valid, but superior to many methods currently in use.19,31 Santanelli et al have used this device to measure sensory recovery following both reduction mammaplasty and DIEP reconstruction.32,33 Still other authors have used it to assess sensation following breast augmentation, comparing results with respect to implant size and surgical approach.34,35 Finally, Djohan et al have used this same sensibility testing device in this same clinical setting in their prior work assessing sensory recovery after NAC neurotization and implant-based breast reconstruction.27

Mixed effect analyses allow one to assess trends over time, considering that measurements at multiple time points for each individual are correlated. The results of the mixed effects model show that sensibility improves at a rate consistent with that which would be expected with this type of nerve graft reconstruction. The return of sensation improved markedly over the first 6 months with a less incremental improvement between 6 and 12 months during which a few more patients transitioned from the good to the excellent sensibility groups. In addition, return of sensation was similar in all the breast and NAC areas tested, although it was consistently highest in the superior breast skin region, likely as a result of secondary innervation from the supraclavicular nerves. These findings would be less consistent with spontaneous neurotization which often occurs haphazardly and typically over a much longer period of time.36 Furthermore, the fact that 1PM sensation was generally better than 1PS sensation, especially at the earlier, postoperative time points tested, is quite significant because this pattern of pressure threshold recovery is consistent with that seen in other nerve reconstruction settings.37 Moreover, moving sensibility is more clinically relevant compared with static sensibility as the former represents the manner in which any part of the body interacts with the outside world, whether it be with regard to the sensation of clothing against your skin or during intimacy. It is also worth noting here that 1PS pressure threshold measurements likely represent a higher/finer threshold of sensibility measurements, and these improved commensurate with 1PM sensibility measurements, which is an encouraging sign.

None of the patients in this study reported chronic, postmastectomy pain at their most recent follow-up visit (minimum 6 months postoperative). This finding is important in that the reported incidence of chronic, postmastectomy pain is 20%–50%.38 Although the etiology of postmastectomy pain is likely multi-factorial, it is reasonable to assume that in a fair number of those patients, the pain is neuropathic in nature, likely from an intercostal neuroma. If so, then our results highlight one possible way of mitigating this postoperative complication. Indeed, recent work by peripheral nerve surgeons has highlighted the importance of giving any transected/injured nerve a downstream target as a means of controlling neuropathic pain, and our approach represents one method to do so.39,40 We believe that the absence of any cases of postmastectomy chronic pain in our cohort is a strong argument and indication for neurotization.

There are some limitations to this study. Most significantly, there is no control group of patients not undergoing neurotization. We understand and appreciate this limitation, but based on our encouraging pilot study results after we first started performing the procedure in 2018,26 we felt strongly that all future patients should be offered the option of neurotization to allow for postmastectomy sensation preservation. Our techniques have evolved over time, including the amount of native nerve length preserved and use of nerve autografts in addition to allografts, which we did not formally analyze in this study. We plan to look at subgroup analysis in future studies to better understand these nuances in techniques. Additionally, unfortunately, many of the patients included in this study period underwent surgery during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic and traveled from out of the area for surgery, and so overall in-person follow-up for neurosensory testing was limited as a result. Finally, the peripheral nerve surgeon (Z.P.) was the same person performing the postoperative sensibility measurements, and this fact represents a potential source of bias. However, the nature of PSSD testing is such that the patients themselves report to the evaluator how much sensibility they have by responding with the click of a button when they feel a stimulus. Given this test trait, we feel that any bias here is minimal because the evaluator is not personally commenting on how much the patients feel; rather, the patients themselves are reporting these data.

In conclusion, this approach continues to demonstrate good efficacy and safety with regard to postmastectomy sensibility. Novel approaches combining nerve preservation and reconstruction during NSM and implant-based reconstruction can significantly improve postoperative return of breast and nipple–areolar complex sensation. Additionally, these techniques also prevent the development of postmastectomy pain syndrome. Ongoing adoption of these techniques gives patients undergoing mastectomy both the potential for sensory return and avoidance of chronic pain, which can be transformative for postoperative quality of life.
