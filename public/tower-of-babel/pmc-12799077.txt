Exploring the Impact of an Oral Health Education Program on School teachers’ Knowledge and Behaviors in Iraq: A Quasi-Experimental Study
Authors: Hanan Fadhil Alautry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:School teachers play a fundamental role in modeling a child’s overall development, including their health habits. Considering that schools occupy a large share of children’s daily lives, teachers have a beneficial opportunity to foster daily dental care practices.The current investigation aimed to explore the influence of an oral health education program on school teachers’ knowledge and behaviors related to oral health problem prevention.Materials and Methods:The study subjects in this experimental study included 154 primary school teachers in Kut, Iraq. Six primary schools were randomly selected via a lottery method out of 42 elementary schools situated in the city to constitute the study participants. Exhaustive information about school teachers was obtained using thorough questionnaires that involved sociodemographic properties, oral health behaviors, and an Arabic validated oral health preventive knowledge assessment. The statistical analysis included an independent sample t test, McNemar test, and paired t test.Results:The mean age of school teachers was 39.6 (±9.93). Study subjects were almost equally distributed between the age groups, with more than half having ≤10 years of teaching experience. An increase in school teachers’ oral health preventive knowledge was observed after the education program, regardless of their demographic characteristics (P < 0.001). The number of teachers with correct responses about all oral health preventive statements increased after the education program. A significant improvement in teachers’ oral health behaviors was observed following the educational program (P < 0.001, P < 0.05).

INTRODUCTION

Oral health constitutes an integral part of overall health,[1] impacting a person’s physical, mental, and social well-being, in addition to appearance and social relationships.[2] Early implementation of health education is crucial as habits formed early in life often continue into adulthood.[3] The importance of educating children on oral health has long been recognized.[4] The period of school-age represents an optimal window for instilling behaviors and attitudes that may endure throughout a child’s lifetime.[5] Promoting oral health can be effectively achieved through initiatives delivered within schools since children can be accessed from childhood to adolescence.[6]

School teachers play a key role in shaping a child’s overall development, including their health habits. Since children occupy a significant portion of their day at school, teachers have a valuable opportunity to promote daily dental care practices.[7] Teachers’ knowledge of oral health is fundamental to the success of oral health programs, especially since schools often serve as the primary setting where children can consistently learn and practice healthy behaviors.[8]

Previous reports suggested encouraging school teachers to become involved in promoting the oral health of school children as part of the teaching system.[9,10,11] Several studies demonstrated a positive role of oral health education (OHE) programs for school staff in improving the knowledge and behaviors of teachers[12,13,14] and their students.[5,15] We consider the shortage of knowledge about the correlation of unfavorable oral health behaviors with the increased likelihood of dental disease.[16] Students acquire knowledge through their teachers, who significantly influence their behaviors and attitudes.[17] Due to a high prevalence of oral diseases[18,19] and limited information regarding the influence of OHE programs on school staff’s knowledge and practices regarding oral health in Iraq, the current investigation aimed to explore the influence of the OHE program on the knowledge and behaviors of school teachers. This study’s outcomes will aid in understanding the degree of teachers’ preparedness to execute a coordinated school oral healthcare program and tackle potential challenges. We hypothesized that school teachers would show improved oral health preventive knowledge and positive behaviors following participation in the OHE program.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This experimental study was performed between February and April 2025. To explore the influence of the OHE program, our investigation adopted a one-group pretest–posttest experimental approach. The required sample size was calculated using the paired-sample t test method, which is appropriate for comparing the mean scores before and after the experiment within the same group. The calculation was based on an effect size of 0.25, a power of 0.80, and an α level of 0.05. Using G*Power software edition 3.1.9.7.[20] The minimum number of participants needed was determined to be 128. To account for potential dropouts, an extra 20% was added. The final sample size was 154 school teachers. Six primary schools were randomly selected via lottery out of 42 elementary schools in Kut city to serve as the study subjects. Figure 1 presents the recruitment process. Inclusion criteria specified Iraqi nationality and no prior participation in a similar program within the last 3 months. Exclusion criteria included teachers on extended leave or who declined to provide consent.

Flow chart of the study

The ethical committee of Wasit College of Dentistry approved this study (reference number: 132025) on February 11, 2025. The necessary permissions were granted by the General Directorate of Education in Kut city and by the principals of the involved schools. Verbal consent was obtained from each participant.

The questionnaires used in this study comprised three distinguished parts. The first part collected information regarding demographic characteristics of teachers, including age, gender, and teaching experience years. The second part concentrated on aspects of the teachers’ oral health behaviors, examining variables such as frequency of toothbrushing, use of fluoridated dentifrices, use of dental floss, consumption of sugar-containing snacks, and the purpose of their most recent dental consultation. The third part drew from Arabic validated questionnaires used in a prior study[18] and included 20 statements concerning oral health knowledge and caries preventive measures. In this part, schoolteachers were instructed to explicit their opinion on each statement using a 2-point Likert scale; disagree and agree (disagree = incorrect, agree = correct) with corresponding scores of 0 and 1. The final scores, theoretically ranging from 0 to 20, constituted the total knowledge score for each participant, in which higher scores signal a higher degree of knowledge.

To assure the validity and reliability of the oral health preventive knowledge questionnaire, several measures were implemented. First, a forward–backward translation approach was applied to translate the English version into Arabic.[21] Then, the content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR) were calculated for all items. Eight experts from the departments of pediatric and preventive dentistry assessed the necessity, simplicity, clarity, and relevance of each item, resulting in a CVI of 0.94 and CVR of 0.92, indicating an acceptable level of validity.[22,23] As a final step, the reliability of this questionnaire section was evaluated, in which 20 school teachers were enlisted to fill the questionnaire on two occasions, separated by a 2-week interval, utilizing the test–retest technique.[24] The analysis of this process yielded an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.83, which is considered within the standard range for reliability assessment.

During the research baseline and before starting the education program, participants’ oral health knowledge and behaviors were assessed. The author (HA) provided an OHE program through holding two educational sessions with a 2-week interval in each specified school.

The instructional sessions involved the favorable tooth brushing method (modified bass technique) presented on a dental model, the significance of tooth brushing frequency and times, flossing at least once per day, eating healthy food, periodic dental visits, and stressing the importance of mouth rinsing following each meal. Moreover, the author clarified the importance of fluoride and fissure sealant application in preventing dental caries and other behaviors in preserving the oral health status. In addition to the educational sessions, the teachers were provided with free packages containing a toothbrush, a fluoridated toothpaste, interdental floss, and an oral health educational brochure. One month subsequent to the initial assessment, the author attended the schools to gather data concerning teachers’ oral health-related knowledge and behaviors using the same questionnaires. The outcome data in this study were any positive changes in teachers’ oral health knowledge and behaviors at the end of the education program.

Statistical analysis included a paired t test to compare the mean of teachers’ oral health knowledge pre- and post the educational program. The McNemar test was used to evaluate changes in the frequency of each knowledge statement and positive behaviors before and after the intervention. An independent-sample t test was conducted to compare the mean oral health knowledge of teachers between groups. All statistical analyses adhered to a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05. The dataset was analyzed utilizing the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences program version 25 (Armonk, NY, USA).

DISCUSSION

The results of our investigation showed a beneficial impact of the educational program, as reflected by a rise in the percentage of teachers providing correct responses to oral health statements after the program. Moreover, the percentage of teachers exhibiting positive oral health behaviors increased after the educational program. OHE serves as a foundational element for promoting behavior change, achieving better health outcomes, and supporting overall health promotion efforts. A systematic review study has demonstrated that OHE effectively enhances individuals’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding oral health.[25] The improvement observed in teachers’ oral health knowledge following the OHE program in this study aligns with the results of previous research.[5,12,26] These findings further support the role of OHE as an effective tool in promoting oral health.

Our findings showed a significant improvement in teachers’ oral health mean scores following the educational program. This result is in line with findings from prior research carried out in India.[9] Moreover, the beneficial effects of OHE on favorable oral health behaviors of schoolteachers in the present study is in line with the findings of a similar study,[5] which supplies further evidence regarding the effectiveness of OHE in the promotion of oral health. Improvement in knowledge resulting from OHE has been shown to positively affect self-reported oral health behaviors and practices, as well as to improve clinical indicators such as oral hygiene, gingival status, and dental caries.[27] The baseline information of the present study revealed that male and female teachers had almost equal oral health knowledge. This finding aligns with a previous study in India,[12] while another study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that female participants exhibited greater knowledge than their male peers.[9] In the present research, 66% of respondents identified frequent sugar consumption as a contributing factor to dental caries, consistent with a previous study performed in India[17] and higher than that of a study conducted in India that showed a lower percentage (52%).[28]

In the current study, the percentages of teachers with positive responses regarding the effectiveness of water containing fluoride and adding pit and fissure sealant are below than those for periodic dental consultation and decreasing sugary food consumption in the prevention of dental decay. This is in the same line as the previous study in the USA.[29] Evidence from the study shows that teachers’ awareness of oral health and preventive methods is limited. The inclusion of a training program for teachers within the national oral healthcare program is a commendable initiative. To support its implementation, a guidebook has been developed in the regional language.

In the present investigation, as baseline information, a quarter of schoolteachers reported brushing their teeth two times daily. In contrast with other studies implemented in India[7] and China,[30] twice-daily tooth brushing was reported by more than 75% of the participating schoolteachers. This disparity may be attributed to socioeconomic and cultural contexts, as well as differences in the infrastructure and educational system across countries. A statistically significant improvement in teachers’ knowledge regarding the correct duration for cleaning teeth was observed after the OHE program. This was in agreement with prior studies undertaken in India[31] and Saudi Arabia.[32]

After the program, 96% of school teachers believed that frequent tooth brushing prevents tooth decay, which aligns with findings from previous studies carried out in India.[7,12] In our experimental study, awareness of the importance of fluoridated toothpastes and the correct time to replace a toothbrush was significantly increased after the educational program. This was in accordance with previous studies conducted in China[33] and India.[5]

This study possesses specific advantages. It is noteworthy that this experiment is considered the first attempt to explore the impact of an educational program on primary school teachers in Iraq. The application of random sampling warranted that the selected teachers adequately represented the target population, thereby increasing the generalizability of the findings to schoolteachers in Kut City, Iraq. Furthermore, the study experienced a relatively low rate of loss to follow-up. On the other hand, one limitation of the current investigation is the quasi-experimental design, in which the absence of a control group weakens the ability to draw causal inferences. Although the statistical analyses used (paired t test and McNemar test) were appropriate for the chosen design and demonstrated significant within-group improvements, they cannot establish causality in the absence of a comparison group. Additionally, inherent bias (over-reporting of positive oral health behaviors) can be expected. However, the respondents were asked to be honest in their responses, and there was no pressure on their answers. Another limitation of the present study was that oral health-related responses were self-reported; there is a possibility of inaccuracies or biased reporting. Future studies are recommended to include a control group to strengthen the causal inference and more rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the OHE program on teachers and their students’ oral health status.

CONCLUSION

The findings of our investigation demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in schoolteachers’ knowledge and behaviors following the oral health promotion program, although the quasi-experimental design without a control group limits the ability to definitively attribute these changes to the implemented program. These results suggest that the educational approach may be effective and well-received. Engaging professional teachers in OHE can serve as a strategic component in community-wide oral health promotion initiatives and could enable teachers to contribute actively in promoting oral health among young children, contributing toward the broader goal of achieving optimal oral health for all. Future studies employing more strict designs, such as randomized controlled trials, are required to confirm these findings.
