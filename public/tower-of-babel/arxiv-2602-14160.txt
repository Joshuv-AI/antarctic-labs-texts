Process-Supervised Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Reliable Clinical Reasoning
Chaeeun Lee, T. Michael Yates, Pasquale Minervini, T. Ian Simpson

Abstract
Clinical decision-making requires nuanced reasoning over heterogeneous evidence and traceable justifications. While recent LLM multi-agent systems (MAS) show promise, they largely optimise for outcome accuracy while overlooking process-grounded reasoning aligned with clinical standards. One critical real-world case of this is gene–disease validity curation, where experts must determine whether a gene is causally implicated in a disease by synthesising diverse biomedical evidence. We introduce an agent-as-tool reinforcement learning framework for this task with two objectives: (i) process-level supervision to ensure reasoning follows valid clinical pathways, and (ii) efficient coordination via a hierarchical multi-agent system. Our evaluation on the ClinGen dataset shows that with outcome-only rewards, MAS with a GRPO-trained Qwen3-4B supervisor agent substantially improves final outcome accuracy from 0.195 with a base model supervisor to 0.732, but results in poor process alignment (0.392 F1). Conversely, with process + outcome rewards, MAS with GRPO-trained supervisor achieves higher outcome accuracy (0.750) while significantly improving process fidelity to 0.520 F1. Our code is available at https://github.com/chaeeunlee-io/GeneDiseaseCurationAgents .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in biomedical applications, but their deployment in real-world clinical settings remains limited due to the need for domain expertise and the heterogeneity of biomedical reasoning tasks.
Recent works on LLM agents
(
33
;
21
;
25
;
11
)
have extended LLM reasoning through tool use, self-reflection, and retrieval, while multi-agent systems
(
29
;
2
;
32
, MAS;)
further expand this paradigm by enabling coordination of specialised agents.
These advances provide a promising pathway for underexplored biomedical domains where process fidelity and auditability are essential
(
9
;
26
;
28
)
.
In particular, many such settings require not only correct conclusions through complex reasoning but also structured, traceable evidence aligned with formal clinical standards.
The medical specialty of Clinical Genetics provides a representative real-world case. Rare genetic diseases (RD) affect over 300 million people worldwide
(
10
)
, and diagnosis of these conditions relies on establishing valid gene–disease relationships from literature.
With an increasing number of reported gene–disease relationships, developing a shared framework for consistent assessment has become increasingly important.
This need is exemplified by the Gene Curation Coalition (GenCC), an international coalition of groups engaged in gene–disease validity evaluation
(
4
)
, including the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen).
ClinGen has developed an internationally recognised standard operating procedure (SOP) that defines formal criteria for evaluating evidence supporting gene–disease relationships
(
19
;
1
)
.
These criteria decompose evidence into modular categories, providing a structured basis for decision-making.
In this work, we introduce an agent-as-tool reinforcement learning (RL) framework designed to align LLM reasoning with rigorous clinical standards.
We propose a hierarchical multi-agent architecture where a supervisor agent directs specialised sub-agents to follow valid reasoning pathways.
Each sub-agent is responsible for a distinct evidence category and produces structured outputs relevant to that category.
By training the supervisor agent with a hybrid approach that combines outcome-based and process-based rewards, we improve outcome accuracy while enforcing adherence to the clinical SOP.
In addition, the multi-agentic system produces evidence-specific reasoning traces and structured outputs that mirror clinical curation practices, providing the traceability and auditability that are crucial in real-world clinical decision-making.

Process-Supervised Multi-Agent RL

We frame the gene–disease validity curation task as a hierarchical MAS, in which a
Supervisor Agent

\pi_{\theta}
orchestrates the reasoning process by invoking a set of
K=6
specialised
sub-agents

\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{K}\}
.
Each sub-agent specialises in a single evidence category (e.g.,
Model Systems
,
Rescue
,
Expression
) via prompt-based role assignment. When invoked, a sub-agent evaluates a single article for evidence of that type.

4.1
Task Definition and Agent Trajectories

An input instance is defined as a global context
x=(g,d,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}})
, consisting of a gene
g
, a disease
d
, and a set of relevant scientific abstracts
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{N}\}
.
The supervisor delegates the evaluation of specific evidence categories on full-text articles to LLM sub-agents through discrete tool calls:

c=(a_{k},\text{id}_{j})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}},

(1)

where each call links a specific expert agent
a_{k}
to a target document identifier
\text{id}_{j}
.

Supervisor Trajectory.

Given a global input context
x=(g,d,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}})
, the supervisor generates a trajectory:

\tau_{\text{sup}}=\Big(x,\,z_{\text{plan}},\,\mathbf{C},\,\mathbf{O},\,z_{\text{synth}},\,y\Big).

(2)

Here,
z_{\text{plan}}
is the model’s reasoning before invoking the tools
\mathbf{C}
.
\mathbf{C}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\}
denotes a batch of parallel tool calls, while
\mathbf{O}=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{m}\}
represents the resulting structured
observations
returned by the sub-agents. Finally, the supervisor produces
z_{\text{synth}}
to generate the final validity classification
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. In this study, we constrain our experiments to a single round of tool usage. While the supervisor reasons in two stages (planning
z_{\text{plan}}
and synthesis
z_{\text{synth}}
), all tool calls
\mathbf{C}
are generated as a single parallel batch.

2
illustrates the supervisor trajectory and its interaction with specialised sub-agents as tool environment.

Supervisor Rollout with Ground-Truth Agent Observations.

During supervisor training, rolling out all sub-agents to obtain structured observations
\mathbf{O}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{m})
can incur substantial computational overhead, particularly under Group Relative Policy Optimisation
(
23
, GRPO;)
, where the number of sampled trajectories scales with the group size.
To reduce this cost, we adopt a strategy in which the supervisor
\pi_{\theta}
is conditioned on ground-truth sub-agent observations rather than on sampled LLM sub-agent outputs.
Formally, for a given input instance
x=(g,d,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}})
and a set of tool calls
\mathbf{C}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
, where each
c_{i}=(a_{k},\text{id}_{j})
routes agent
a_{k}
to document
D_{j}
, we construct a synthetic observation

\tilde{\mathbf{O}}=\big(\tilde{o}_{1},\dots,\tilde{o}_{m}\big)

(3)

by retrieving from ground-truth subtype labels.
Each injected
\tilde{o}_{i}
includes the correct evidence subtype label for
(a_{k},D_{j})
as well as natural-language evidence summary by a clinician. This allows the supervisor to generate training time trajectories

\tilde{\tau}_{\text{sup}}=\big(x,\,z_{\text{plan}},\,\mathbf{C},\,\tilde{\mathbf{O}},\,z_{\text{synth}},\,y\big),

(4)

reducing training costs and mitigating the noise introduced by incorrect or incomplete sub-agent predictions during training.

Sub-Agent Trajectory.

During inference, each invoked sub-agent
a_{k}
operates on a specific document instance with full-text visibility.
Upon receiving a call
c=(a_{k},\text{id}_{j})
, the sub-agent’s trajectory is defined as:

\tau_{\text{sub}}=\Big((g,d,D^{\text{full}}_{j}),\,z_{\text{reason}},\,e\Big),

(5)

where
D^{\text{full}}_{j}
is the full text of the article specified by the supervisor in the tool argument.
Each sub-agent generates a local reasoning trace
z_{\text{reason}}
and returns a structured evidence object
e
which forms the observation
o\in\mathbf{O}
, where
e=(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{M})
.

\mathcal{S}
is the set of predicted evidence subtypes for document
D_{j}
(possibly empty), and
M
is an evidence-specific rationale summarising the reasoning behind the sub-agent’s conclusion, mirroring the real-world clinical curation workflow in which clinicians record natural-language summaries together with evidence annotations.
We emphasise that providing such rationales is a crucial prerequisite for real-world deployment, where evidence assessment must be transparent and incrementally updated as new studies emerge.

4.2
Reward Design

We train
\pi_{\theta}
to predict the correct validity class while invoking specialised sub-agents to ground decisions in evidence categories defined by the SOP.
We optimise this with GRPO
(
23
)
using a reward
R
that combines outcome reward with tool call-level process reward.

Outcome Reward (
R_{\text{out}}
).

Outcome reward evaluates the correctness of the final validity classification
y
.
Given the ordinal nature of the ClinGen scale (Definitive
>
Strong
>
Moderate
>
Limited
>
No Known Disease Relationship), we map the label set
\mathcal{Y}
to integer ranks
\text{rank}(\cdot)\in\{0,\dots,4\}
(where
0=

No Known Disease Relationship
and
4=

Definitive
).
We employ a centered distance-based reward:

R_{\text{out}}(y,y^{*})=\sigma\cdot\left(1-\alpha\cdot|\text{rank}(y)-\text{rank}(y^{*})|\right),

(6)

where
\alpha=0.5
centers the base metric to
[-1,+1]
(penalising catastrophic errors while rewarding exact matches), and
\sigma=4.0
scales the final reward signal.
This results in a value range of
R_{\text{out}}\in[-4,+4]
, incentivising the model to predict labels that are
ordinally
close to the ground truth.

Process Reward (
R_{\text{proc}}
).

The process reward
R_{\text{proc}}
evaluates the quality of agent invocations.
Given the set of predicted tool calls
\mathbf{C}
and ground-truth tool calls
\mathbf{C}^{*}
, we compute the base alignment score
s
as the F1 score:

s=\frac{2\cdot|\mathbf{C}\cap\mathbf{C}^{*}|}{|\mathbf{C}|+|\mathbf{C}^{*}|},

(7)

where the intersection
\cap
denotes exact matches on both agent type and input arguments.
To discourage suboptimal agent invocation strategies and accelerate convergence, we apply a cubic shaping function to the base score. Since we leverage foundational models with native tool-calling capabilities, we do not introduce complex format rewards.
Instead, we include a minimal penalty term for malformed tool blocks.
We define a tool block as the text segment enclosed within
<tool_call>
and
</tool_call>
tags. A block is penalised if its content fails to parse as valid JSON or if the parsed object lacks required argument keys (i.e., PMID, PMCID, gene, and disease) required by the agent schema. Denoting
n_{\text{err}}
as the number of malformed tool blocks, the final process reward is:

R_{\text{proc}}=\text{clip}\left(\gamma\cdot s^{3}-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\lambda\cdot n_{\text{err}},-4,4\right),

(8)

where
\gamma=8.0
scales the base score
s\in[0,1]
to
[-4,+4]
, and
\lambda=0.5
is the penalty coefficient for each malformed tool calls.

Hybrid Process + Outcome Reward (
R_{\text{Hybrid}}
).

To incentivise the ”right answer for the right reasons” trajectories, we employ a hybrid reward configuration.
The total reward
R_{\text{hybrid}}
is computed as the weighted sum of the process and outcome components:

R_{\text{hybrid}}=\sigma\cdot\left[\beta R_{\text{out}}+(1-\beta)R_{\text{proc}}\right].

(9)

We set the balancing coefficient
\beta=0.5
, ensuring that both the final diagnosis and the supporting evidence path contribute equally to the combined reward within the global range of
[-4,+4]
.

4.3
Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO)

We optimise the supervisor policy
\pi_{\theta}
using Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO).
As described in
Section

4.1
, the supervisor is conditioned on ground-truth observations during training. For each input
x
, the model samples a group of
G
trajectories
\{\tilde{\tau}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\tau}_{G}\}
following
Equation

4
from the policy
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
.
GRPO uses the group mean reward as a baseline, removing the need for a learned value function. We exclude the KL divergence term from the loss.
The objective is:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=&\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\{\tilde{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\Big(\rho_{i}A_{i},\\
&\text{clip}(\rho_{i},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{i}\Big)\bigg],\end{split}

(10)

where the importance sampling ratio is defined over the generated tokens of the trajectory:

\rho_{i}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\tilde{\tau}_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i}\mid x)}

(11)

and the group-relative advantage normalises the hybrid reward
R_{\text{hybrid}}
in
Equation

9
against the group statistics:

A_{i}=\frac{R_{\text{hybrid}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})-\mathrm{mean}(\{R_{\text{hybrid}}(\tilde{\tau}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{R_{\text{hybrid}}(\tilde{\tau}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})+\delta}.

(12)

Single-Agent Baseline.

We implement a single-agent pipeline as a baseline for comparison with our hierarchical multi-agent framework.
In this setting, a single agent is responsible for both identifying relevant evidence, including subtype distinctions, and producing the final validity classification
y
.
Given an input context
x=(g,d,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{abs}})
, the agent produces a trajectory
\tau_{\text{single}}=(x,\mathbf{C}_{\text{ft}},\mathbf{D}_{\text{full}},z_{\text{reason}},J_{\text{out}})
, where
\mathbf{C}_{\text{ft}}
denotes optional tool invocations to the function
get_full_text()
that retrieves the full text of an article given its identifier, and
\mathbf{D}_{\text{full}}
denotes any retrieved full-text articles.
The response concludes with a structured JSON object
J_{\text{out}}
that contains (i) a list of evidence items
E_{\mathrm{fine}}
predicted at the subtype level for individual articles, and (ii) a single predicted validity label
y
.
While the process reward for the supervisor in a multi-agentic system targets high-level agent routing, the process reward in this setting directly targets fine-grained subtype predictions.
For training, we apply corresponding outcome, process, and hybrid reward formulations to
J_{\text{out}}
, with the distinction that the process component computes the base F1 score
s
at the evidence subtype level:

s=\text{F1}(\mathbf{E}_{\text{fine}},\mathbf{E}^{*}_{\text{fine}}).

(13)

2
illustrates the single-agent trajectory and compares it with that of the MAS supervisor.

Sub-Agent Supervised Fine-Tuning.

A key difference between the single-agent and multi-agent settings lies in the granularity of the process reward.
In the single-agent setup, evidence subtypes are predicted directly, whereas the supervisor in the MAS is trained only to route queries to broader agent categories.
We additionally evaluate the effect of training both the supervisor and sub-agents.
To this end, we train a shared sub-agent LLM via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on high-quality traces generated by DeepSeek-V3.2
(
3
)
to fulfill all agent roles.
Although all sub-agents share the same model weights, each invocation uses a role-specific prompt and constrains the output space to the evidence subtypes relevant to that category.

4.4
Experimental Settings

We implement all RL and SFT experiments using the veRL framework
(
24
)
.
To support tool use and multi-agentic topology, both single- and multi-agent settings are implemented using LangGraph, leveraging veRL’s integration to enable graph-structured agentic workflows. We use Qwen3 models
(
30
)
at three scales (1.7B, 4B, and 8B) for our experiments.
All models support native tool use and strong instruction-following behaviour. Experiments with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B were conducted on a compute node with four NVIDIA H100 (80GB) GPUs, while experiments with Qwen3-8B used a node with four NVIDIA H200 (141GB) GPUs.

Training Configuration.

All GRPO training utilises multi-turn rollouts with a global batch size of 16 and 8 responses per query, 5 epochs. We set the sampling temperature to 0.8 and remove KL regularisation.
For SFT, we used a micro-batch size of 1, a global batch size of 4, and a maximum sequence length of 32,600 tokens over 4 epochs.
See Appendix
D
for full training details.

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate performance at three levels of granularity: final validity classification, evidence category, and evidence subtypes.
Given a test set
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each input is
x_{i}=(g_{i},d_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i})
,
outcome accuracy
measures whether the predicted validity classification (e.g.,
Moderate
) matches the ground truth on a per-input basis.

Agent call accuracy
and
evidence accuracy
are exact-set metrics averaged over inputs:

\displaystyle\text{Agent Call Accuracy}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[C_{\text{pred}}^{(i)}=C_{\text{true}}^{(i)}]

\displaystyle\text{Evidence Accuracy}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[S_{\text{pred}}^{(i)}=S_{\text{true}}^{(i)}]

Here
C_{\text{pred}}^{(i)}
and
C_{\text{true}}^{(i)}
denote the predicted and ground-truth sets of tool calls for input
i
. Each tool call consists of a tool name (e.g., “ExperimentalEvidence_BiochemicalFunction_agent”) and the required arguments (PMID, PMCID, gene, disease) supplied to the agent.
S_{\text{pred}}^{(i)}
and
S_{\text{true}}^{(i)}
denote the predicted and ground-truth evidence profiles for input
i
.

S_{\text{pred}}^{(i)}
is obtained from the outputs of the invoked sub-agents, whereas
S_{\text{true}}^{(i)}
is defined by gold-standard annotations mapping each document in
\mathcal{D}
to evidence subtypes (e.g., Functional Alteration
Patient cells
, Biochemical Function
B
).
Note that false-positive agent calls are not penalised at the evidence accuracy level if those sub-agents correctly predict no evidence.

We additionally report
macro F1
at both the agent and subtype levels, averaged across inputs, where F1 is computed over predicted versus gold agent calls and evidence subtypes, respectively.
For the
single-agent setting
, we report only
outcome accuracy
and
evidence accuracy/F1
, since the model directly predicts the final validity class and document-level subtypes.

5.1
GRPO with Outcome-Only Reward

In this setting, the model is trained using an outcome-based reward derived solely from the final validity class. While outcome-only training substantially improves outcome accuracy (from 0.314 to 0.786 for Qwen3-1.7B in the multi-agent setting), agents trained with outcome-only rewards exhibit poor process alignment. For example, for Qwen3-1.7B, multi-agent Agent Call accuracy drops from 0.186 in the base model to 0.114 under outcome-only GRPO, and Evidence F1 decreases from 0.353 to 0.184. Consistently across both single-agent and multi-agent configurations, outcome-only GRPO degrades process metrics (Agent Call Acc./F1, Evidence Acc./F1) relative to the untrained baseline. This suggests that outcome accuracy alone is an unreliable proxy for reasoning quality in SOP-governed tasks, where coarse outcome labels fail to capture the complexity of the task or promote adherence to the clinically defined criteria.

5.2
GRPO with Hybrid Reward

GRPO with a hybrid reward combines outcome and process-level signals to encourage strong outcome accuracy while maintaining procedural validity.
We observe that this hybrid reward consistently improves process metrics while maintaining high outcome accuracy (
Figure

4
).
For example, in the multi-agent Qwen3-4B setting, Agent Call accuracy increases from 0.105 (base) and 0.082 (outcome-only) to 0.455, while Evidence F1 improves from 0.417 to 0.520, with outcome accuracy remaining stable at 0.732.
Similar trends are observed for Qwen3-8B, where Agent Call F1 improves from 0.436 (base) to 0.611 and Evidence F1 from 0.469 to 0.542. Reward trajectories also reflect that hybrid training improves both reward components (

3
).

We further analyse multi-agent performance by comparing prompt-based sub-agents with sub-agents trained via SFT.
Fine-tuned sub-agents improve evidence accuracy and F1 for the 1.7B and 8B models, and increase evidence accuracy for the 4B model despite a slight decrease in F1 (

6
Discussion

Interpretability

Although our evaluation focuses on outcome and process-level prediction performance, a crucial advantage of MAS lies in its ability to produce structured, evidence-specific intermediate outputs.
These intermediate rationales are indispensable for real-world clinical deployment. While a single agent can be prompted to produce both the final validity class and supporting evidence, its reasoning is not easily decomposable by evidence type, limiting interpretability at the level required for clinical curation.
By contrast, MAS yields structured outputs for each agent, including
explanation
field that mirrors how clinicians record natural-language evidence summaries.
Moreover, MAS allows reasoning traces to be inspected at the level of individual evidence categories.
This is crucial in clinical curation settings, where evidence assessments accumulate over time as new studies are published, and where intermediate evidence annotations are essential both for arriving at a final validity class and for enabling clinicians to revisit past decisions and integrate emerging evidence
(
1
)
.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a process-supervised multi-agent RL framework for gene–disease validity curation.
Our approach trains a supervisor to coordinate specialised evidence agents using GRPO with a hybrid reward that combines outcome correctness and process-aligned agent invocation.
This hybrid approach substantially improves process fidelity while maintaining strong outcome accuracy.
We show that comparable gains can be achieved by training only the supervisor without updating sub-agent parameters, reducing the computational overhead of multi-agent learning while preserving the benefits of structured coordination.
Although a single-agent pipeline achieves higher quantitative performance, the MAS produces evidence-specific intermediate outputs that better reflect clinical curation practice.
Together, our study proposes a multi-agent solution to the gene–disease validity curation task and positions multi-agent RL as a promising direction for a broader class of SOP-governed clinical tasks
(
9
;
26
)
that have received limited attention in prior LLM studies.

Impact Statement

This work aims to improve the reliability of LLM-based decision support in healthcare by aligning training objectives with the procedural requirements of a real-world clinical task. We introduce a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for gene–disease validity curation, a real-world clinical task that underpins rare disease diagnosis and directly impacts both clinicians and patients. This task exemplifies a broader class of clinical reasoning problems that require transparent evaluation of heterogeneous evidence under strict clinical SOPs. By incorporating process-aligned rewards framed as agent invocation supervision, our approach improves process fidelity while producing structured, auditable intermediate outputs. These evidence-specific rationales are a critical requirement for real-world deployment, enabling clinicians to inspect, revise, and update decisions as new literature emerges. The system is designed to assist rather than replace clinicians’ curation efforts, and was developed in close collaboration with a clinician with directly relevant domain expertise. Beyond gene–disease curation, we view this work as a first step toward tackling a broader class of SOP-governed clinical tasks, encouraging more trustworthy and clinically grounded biomedical AI systems.
