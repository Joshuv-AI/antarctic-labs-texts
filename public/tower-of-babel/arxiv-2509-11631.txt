Hurst Index of Gamma-Ray Burst Light Curves and Its Statistical Study Journal: Journal of High Energy Astrophysics
Ruo-Yu Guan, Feifei Wang, Yuan-Chuan Zou

Abstract
Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) rank among the most powerful astrophysical phenomena, characterized by complex and highly variable prompt emission light curves that reflect the dynamics of their central engines. In this work, we analyze a sample of 163 long-duration GRBs detected by the Burst and Transient Source Experiment (BATSE), applying detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) to derive the Hurst index as a quantitative descriptor of temporal correlations in the light curves. We further explore statistical correlations between the Hurst index and 12 other observational parameters through regression and correlation analyses. Our results reveal anti-correlations between the Hurst index and the burst durations (T50, T90), and a negative trend with the low-energy spectral index α \alpha . We also find that correlations with peak photon flux are strongest at the shorter timescale (64 ms) and systematically weaken at longer timescales (256–1024 ms), indicating that the persistence of temporal correlations is most evident in the rapid variability component of GRB emission. The results offer new perspectives on the temporal structure of the GRB emission and its potential link to the underlying physical mechanisms driving these bursts.



1
Introduction

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the most powerful and luminous transient events in the universe, believed to originate from relativistic jets produced by catastrophic astrophysical processes. Observationally, GRBs are broadly classified into two categories—long and short—based on a characteristic duration threshold of approximately two seconds
(
Kouveliotou et al. 1993
)
. Long GRBs (
T_{90}>2\,\mathrm{s}
) are generally associated with the core collapse of massive, rapidly rotating stars
(
Woosley 1993
;
Paczyński 1998
)
and are frequently accompanied by broad-lined Type Ic supernovae
(
Woosley and Bloom 2006
)
. In contrast, short GRBs (
T_{90}<2\,\mathrm{s}
) are commonly linked to the mergers of compact binary systems
(
Li and Paczyński 1998
)
, such as neutron star–neutron star
(
Abbott et al. 2017
;
Goldstein et al. 2017
;
Paczynski 1986
)
or neutron star–black hole systems
(
Meszaros and Rees 1992
;
Paczynski 1991
)
.

The light curves (LCs) of the GRBs serve as a vital diagnostic of the underlying physical processes governing these extreme astrophysical explosions and the conditions in the vicinity of their central engines. Characterized by highly variable, non-thermal emission that spans timescales from milliseconds to several minutes
(
MacLachlan et al. 2012
)
, GRB light curves display a remarkable range of temporal structures, including single-peaked components, multi-episodic pulses, and intricate patterns of variability across different energy bands. This morphological diversity indicates the complex nature of relativistic outflows and energy dissipation mechanisms, which may involve internal shocks, magnetic reconnection, or cascades driven by turbulence within the jet
(
Zhang and Yan 2011
)
.

Over the years, numerous indicators have been developed to quantify the temporal variability of GRBs. These include pulse decomposition analysis
(
Norris et al. 1996
)
, variability indices
(
Fenimore and Ramirez-Ruiz 2000b
)
, Fourier power density spectra
(
Beloborodov et al. 2000
)
, autocorrelation functions
(
Borgonovo 2004
)
, and minimum variability time scales
(
MacLachlan et al. 2012
)
. Crucially, some studies have found significant correlations between variability indicators and other properties such as luminosity
(
Reichart et al. 2001
;
Guidorzi et al. 2005
)
,
suggesting that light curve structure may provide key diagnostics of jet composition and energy dissipation.

Originally introduced by
Peng et al. 1994
;
Peng et al. 1995
, the detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) is a fundamental method to examine the scalar properties in a variety of time series datasets. Later,
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
expanded DFA to analyze multifractal processes, resulting in the multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis (MFDFA) technique. A generalized Hurst index was defined to quantify the long-range dependence of a time series, which was initially introduced by
Hurst 1951
in the study of the long-term storage capacity of reservoirs. This method effectively addresses correlation issues in time-series data and is applicable to both discrete and continuous stochastic processes.
It has been used successfully in a wide range of applications spanning several fields, such as music
(
Jafari et al. 2007
)
, heartbeat dynamics
(
Ivanov et al. 1999
)
, electroencephalogram sleep data
(
Pavlov et al. 2020b
;
Pavlov et al. 2021
)
, arterial pressure
(
Pavlov et al. 2020a
)
, cosmic microwave radiation
(
Movahed et al. 2011
;
Movahed et al. 2013
)
, atmospheric turbulence effects on stellar images
(
Zunino et al. 2014
)
, sunspot fluctuations
(
Sadegh Movahed et al. 2006
;
Hu et al. 2009
)
, solar flares
(
Lee et al. 2020
)
, and gravitational wave detection
(
Eghdami et al. 2018
)
.
In this context, DFA and its extension to MFDFA offer powerful tools to characterize temporal correlations and long-range memory in GRB light curves. Unlike traditional variability measures, DFA quantifies the scaling behavior and persistence of non
-
stationary time series, making it particularly suited to GRB data
(
Peng et al. 1994
;
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
;
Zunino et al. 2014
)
.

Since its launch in 1991 onboard NASA’s Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, the Burst and Transient Source Experiment (BATSE) has been instrumental in shaping current understanding of GRBs
(
Band et al. 1993
)
. One of BATSE’s most impactful achievements is its systematic collection of high-resolution light curves in four well-defined energy bands: 20-50 keV, 50-100 keV, 100-300 keV and above 300 keV, which has facilitated detailed multiband temporal and spectral investigations of GRB prompt emission. Using this extensive data set,
Hakkila 2021
found that most GRB pulses can be well characterized by a smooth, single-peaked emission profile accompanied by a temporally symmetric residual component, shedding new light on the structure and physical origin of GRB emission mechanisms. We also chose the BATSE data for our analysis because of the high sensitivity.

In this paper, we analyze the detrended fluctuations in the light curves of 163 long GRBs detected by BATSE, obtain a characteristic index (Hurst index) consisting of the light curves as a time series, and perform regression and correlation analyses between this index and 12 different physical parameters that have been observed for these long GRBs. The paper is arranged as follows. In Section
2
, we present our data sample and describe the 12 physical parameters for which we performed the correlation analysis. The detrended fluctuation analysis model and its parameter selection and statistical analysis are presented in Section
3
. The results and conclusion are reported in Sections
4
and
5
, respectively.

3
Methods

3.1
Detrended Fluctuatioin Analysis

The DFA method was originally developed by
Peng et al. 1994
and was later extended to multifractal analysis by
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
. Following
Peng et al. 1994
and
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
, the DFA procedure consists of the following steps:

1. For a given light curve represented as a time series
x(i)
of length
N
, we first construct the integrated series:

y(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}[x(i)-\langle x\rangle],

(1)

where
\langle x\rangle
is the mean value of the time series.

2. The integrated series is divided into non-overlapping segments of equal length
s
. In each segment
\nu
, we fit a polynomial trend
y_{\nu}(k)
of the order
n
(typically linear or quadratic) to remove local trends.

3. The detrended fluctuation function
F(\nu,s)
for each segment is calculated as:

F^{2}(\nu,s)=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=(\nu-1)s+1}^{\nu s}[y(k)-y_{\nu}(k)]^{2}.

(2)

4. The overall fluctuation function
F(s)
is obtained by averaging across all segments:

F(s)=\left[\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{2N_{s}}F^{2}(\nu,s)\right]^{1/2},

(3)

where
N_{s}=\text{int}(N/s)
.

5. The process is repeated for different time scales
s
to examine the scaling behavior. For fractal processes,
F(s)
follows a power law:

F(s)\sim s^{H},

(4)

where
H
is the Hurst index, which serves as our primary measure of temporal correlation. The Hurst index ranges between 0 and 1, with
H=0.5
indicating uncorrelated (white noise) behavior,
H>0.5
indicating persistent long-range correlations, and
H<0.5
indicating anti-persistent correlations.

Following
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
and
Rydin Gorjão et al. 2022
, for multifractal analysis, we generalize the fluctuation function to the
q
-th order:

\mathcal{F}(q,s)=\left(\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{2N_{s}}\left[F^{2}(\nu,s)\right]^{q/2}\right)^{1/q}.

(5)

For
q=0
, we use logarithmic averaging:

\mathcal{F}(0,s)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4N_{s}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{2N_{s}}\ln\left[F^{2}(\nu,s)\right]\right).

(6)

The Hurst index
H
is particularly relevant for GRB light curves, as it provides a quantitative measure of “memory” in the emission process. A high Hurst index suggests that the central engine activity exhibits long-term correlations, possibly indicative of sustained accretion or magnetic processes, while a low Hurst index might imply more stochastic behavior dominated by short-timescale phenomena.

3.2
Implementation and Parameter Optimization

Our analysis was implemented using the MFDFA Python package
(
Rydin Gorjão et al. 2022
)
, which provides efficient computation of both standard DFA and multifractal extensions
4
4

4

https://github.com/LRydin/MFDFA
. The key parameters in our implementation include:

1. Scale selection: We used logarithmically spaced segment sizes (scales) ranging from a minimum of 3 data points (
0.192
s) to a maximum of one-tenth the length of each light curve, with 200 scale points. This range ensures adequate statistics while avoiding both undersampling at small scales and excessive averaging at large scales.

2. Detrending: We used first-order polynomial detrending (linear detrending, order=1) to remove local trends while preserving the intrinsic variability of the light curves. This choice represents the standard approach in DFA implementations for astrophysical time-series analysis.

3. Fluctuation order: For the monofractal analysis presented here, we used
q=2
, which corresponds to the conventional DFA implementation and provides the Hurst index as a measure of long-range correlation.

4. Data processing: Each gamma-ray burst light curve was analyzed within the astrophysical relevant time window from trigger time (
t=0
) to duration
T_{90}
(the time interval containing 90% of the total burst emission). This ensures that the analysis focuses on the most significant emission phase while excluding background-dominated regions. The counts were analyzed in both their total (integrated across all channels) and individual BATSE energy channels (channels 1-4) to investigate potential energy-dependent correlations.

5. Hurst index calculation: The Hurst index
H
was derived from the slope of the fluctuation function
F(s)
versus the size of the segment
s
in log-log space using ordinary least squares regression. Following the standard DFA convention for nonstationary time series
(
Kantelhardt et al. 2002
)
,
H=\alpha-1
, where
\alpha
is the fitted slope of
\log(F(s))
versus
\log(s)
.

6. Uncertainty estimation: The standard errors of the Hurst indices were estimated using the ordinary least-squares regression process. For cases where regression-based error estimation failed (typically due to very short data segments), we used resampling techniques to ensure reliable uncertainty quantification.

Following the methodology described above, we computed the Hurst indices for all four energy channels of 163 GRBs from the BATSE catalog. In this work, we choose the combined light curves of all four channels. It should be noted that we fixed the parameter
q
to 2, which means that we performed DFA rather than MFDFA. However, the whole method described above could be used as a general treatment for further research in the future. Table

exhibits a strong negative correlation with
H
(
r=-0.57
), while the high-energy index
\beta
shows a weaker positive correlation (
r=0.32
). The peak energy
E_{peak}
shows a moderate positive correlation (
r=0.35
), suggesting that bursts with more correlated light curves tend to have harder spectra.

Temporal parameters show particularly strong correlations:
T_{50}
has the strongest negative correlation (
r=-0.71
), indicating that bursts with longer duration tend to have less correlated variability patterns. Similarly,
T_{90}
shows a negative correlation (
r=-0.54
), although weaker than for
T_{50}
. The variability index also shows a negative correlation (
r=-0.39
), consistent with the interpretation that more variable bursts have less persistent correlations in their light curves.

The flux-related parameters exhibit mixed behavior: the peak photon fluxes (
P_{pk1-3}
) all show strong positive correlations (
r\in\left[0.62,0.71\right]
), with the strongest correlation for the shortest time bin (64 ms). In contrast, the energy fluence
F_{g}
shows essentially no correlation (
r=-0.09
), while the peak energy flux
F_{pk}
shows a moderate negative correlation (
r=-0.41
).

The hardness ratio shows a weak positive correlation (
r=0.18
), suggesting a slight tendency for more correlated light curves to have harder spectra. This is consistent with the positive correlation seen for
E_{peak}
.

5
Conclusions and Discussion

In this work, we applied detrended fluctuation analysis to the prompt emission light curves of 163 long-duration GRBs detected by BATSE, extracting the Hurst index as a quantitative measure of temporal correlation. By systematically correlating the Hurst index with twelve key physical parameters, including spectral, temporal, and flux-related variables, we revealed significant and interpretable trends.

The strong negative correlation between Hurst index and duration parameters (
T_{50}
,
T_{90}
) suggests that longer bursts tend to exhibit less persistent correlations in their light curves, possibly reflecting more stochastic central engine activity or increased importance of external shock components at late times. Conversely, the positive correlations with peak photon fluxes indicate that the most intense emission episodes tend to show more correlated variability patterns.

The spectral correlations are particularly intriguing. The negative correlation with
\alpha
and the positive correlation with
E_{peak}
suggest that bursts with more correlated light curves tend to have harder spectra. This could imply that the physical processes responsible for spectral hardness (possibly related to electron acceleration or magnetic field strength) are connected to those governing the temporal correlation structure. The weak correlation with the hardness ratio suggests that this relationship may be more complex than a simple dependence on the spectral hardness. If we look at Person’s
r
and adjusted
R^{2}
, it is interesting that only the spectral index
\alpha
of the Band function has a strong correlation with
H
, while
\beta
and
E_{peak}
have much weaker correlations with
H
. If such a phenomenon is true, it may indicate that
\alpha
is independent of
\beta
. In other words, the radiation mechanism for the lower band (dominated by
\alpha
) is not correlated with the one for the higher band (dominated by
\beta
).

As shown in table
3
, the correlation between H and peak photon flux is strongest on the shortest timescale (64 ms,
P_{pk1}
) and gradually weakens at longer timescales (256–1024 ms,
P_{pk2}
and,
P_{pk3}
).
This implies that the persistence of correlations is most evident in the rapid variability components of GRB light curves. This finding supports models in which the shortest timescale variability originates from fundamental processes in the emission region, whereas longer timescale variations may be influenced by additional factors such as jet geometry or the circumburst environment.

Several caveats must be taken into account in interpreting these results. First, our analysis is limited to the BATSE energy band (20-2000 keV), and different correlations might emerge at higher or lower energies. Second, the DFA method assumes stationarity in the correlation structure, which may not hold for the entire duration of some complex GRBs. Third, while the Hurst index (
H
) obtained by conventional DFA theoretically satisfies
0<H<1
, empirical computations may produce values beyond this bound. Cases with
H\leq 0
typically reflect either excessive trend elimination or pronounced antipersistent behavior in the signal, whereas
H\geq 1
often emerges from inadequate detrending or intrinsic non-stationarities. In our analysis, we systematically employed linear DFA across all gamma-ray burst (GRB) light curves, acknowledging that this uniform approach might inaccurately characterize long-range dependencies in all GRBs that exhibit multiscale variability. Crucially, even when
H
falls outside its nominal range, it retains utility as a quantitative scaling parameter for comparative statistical analysis among GRBs, although without the standard interpretation as a persistence metric. This approach allows for the retention of all observational data while maintaining methodological consistency across the sample.

Future work could address these limitations by: (1) extending the analysis to broadband spectral data from Fermi/GBM and other telescopes, while overcome the bias between different instruments, (2) examining time-dependent Hurst indices to track evolution within bursts, and (3) comparing with detailed simulations of various emission mechanisms.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the Hurst index provides a valuable quantitative measure of the structure of the GRB light curve, which correlates with several key observable properties. These correlations offer new insights into the connection between temporal variability and physical processes in GRBs, potentially helping to distinguish between different emission models and central engine scenarios.
