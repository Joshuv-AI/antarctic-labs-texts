A Technique to Avoid Endograft Lumen Narrowing during Endovascular Aortic Arch Repair in Chronic Dissections
Authors: Michal Proczka, Mohammad Zagzoog, Stéphan Haulon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe use of retrograde branches during endovascular aortic arch repair in chronic dissections has been associated with endograft lumen compression in the distal arch true lumen. This results in a functional pseudocoarctation, which carries a risk of cardiac failure.TechniqueThis exemplar case presents a patient treated with a three-branch arch endograft for an anastomotic false aneurysm and a residual chronic arch dissection extending to the supra-aortic trunks. To overcome device compression, a kissing angioplasty of the endograft and the left common carotid artery branch was performed after achieving wire access to the left subclavian artery. This manoeuvre successfully disrupted the distal arch lamella while securing the left common carotid artery bridging stent and maintaining access to the left subclavian artery.DiscussionThis technique is now routinely performed in all patients presenting with chronic aortic dissections. In the authors’ limited recent experience with short-term follow up, no instances of cardiac failure secondary to endograft lumen narrowing have been observed since implementing this manoeuvre. It appears to be a feasible and effective method for overcoming endograft lumen compression and its associated complications.

INTRODUCTION

Endovascular aortic arch repair has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to open surgery for managing aortic arch aneurysms and dissections.1 Arch branched custom made devices can be designed and manufactured with either antegrade or retrograde inner branches connected to the supra-aortic trunks (SATs).2 While these repairs were initially exclusively performed with antegrade branches, retrograde branches have recently been evaluated and demonstrated favourable early outcomes.3 Retrograde branches can be accessed and stented via a femoral approach, eliminating the need for direct SAT access, and they facilitate future interventions on the visceral aorta from a cranial approach.4 Because early experiences utilising two antegrade inner branches were associated with morbidity related to open left subclavian artery (LSA) revascularisation,4 the unit’s practice has evolved from a hybrid approach to a fully percutaneous endovascular repair. This contemporary strategy incorporates a retrograde branch to the LSA, a retrograde left common carotid artery (LCCA) branch, and an antegrade or retrograde branch to the brachiocephalic artery.5,6

However, the implantation of endografts for arch chronic dissections in patients with a collapsed true lumen carries a risk of endograft lumen narrowing, which may prevent the graft from expanding to its nominal diameter.7 This predominantly occurs at the distal arch angulation, resulting in a pseudocoarctation.5 This narrowing is exacerbated by retrograde inner branches because they are positioned within the endograft lumen. This specific configuration causes spatial crowding at the branch level, thereby reducing the functional flow lumen to the descending aorta and potentially increasing systemic resistance. Furthermore, these branches are often located distal to the SAT origins, frequently at the distal arch angulation, where the graft curve further limits the stent graft lumen. True lumen narrowing can result in an increased cardiac afterload and, ultimately, cardiac failure; this specific complication has been reported in the recent literature.5,6 This Technical Note aims to describe a novel endovascular technique designed to overcome endograft lumen compression in the distal arch true lumen.

TECHNIQUE

This Technical Note presents an exemplar case demonstrating the application of this technique. A 54 year old man presented with an anastomotic false aneurysm and a residual chronic arch dissection extending into the SAT. He underwent a mechanical valve replacement and an ascending aorta tube graft repair (Bentall procedure) in 2018 for an acute type A aortic dissection. Anatomically, the patient had a type two aortic arch, with the dissection membrane protruding into the brachiocephalic artery and LCCA. To avoid a redo sternotomy, an endovascular aortic arch repair using a Zenith arch branch graft was selected (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA; 40 mm proximal and 28 mm distal diameters) designed with an antegrade branch to the brachiocephalic artery and retrograde branches to the LCCA and LSA. The endograft was successfully deployed under rapid ventricular pacing (180 beats/minute). A self- expandable bridging limb was then implanted into the brachiocephalic artery via axillary access. A right femoral approach was used for the aortic arch endograft implantation, as well as for the cannulation and stenting of both retrograde branches. A 9 x 75 mm Viabahn bridging stent (WL Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ, USA) was deployed in the LCCA. Wire access was securely maintained in the LCCA, while the LSA branch and vessel were cannulated through a 20 F DrySeal sheath (WL Gore & Associates) using a parallel wire and catheter. The hydrophilic guidewire was subsequently exchanged for a stiffer Rosen wire (Cook Medical); this LSA wire secured access to the LSA true lumen.

At this stage, a kissing balloon angioplasty was performed to mechanically rupture the dissection membrane. This was achieved by simultaneously inflating an 8 x 40 mm Mustang angioplasty balloon (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) within the LCCA branch and a 46 mm compliant Coda balloon (Cook Medical) within the endograft lumen at the level of the retrograde branches. This inflation was performed under rapid pacing at 130 beats/minute and under continuous fluoroscopy until visual estimation confirmed full expansion of the endograft to its nominal diameter (Fig. 2B and 2C). The rupture of the dissection membrane was clearly visualised during this manoeuvre. The LSA Viabahn bridging stent was then implanted (WL Gore & Associates).

The outcome of this mechanical septotomy was consistently verified at the end of the procedure using cone beam computed tomography to confirm full endograft expansion and the absence of a pseudocoarctation at the distal arch. This patient's post-operative recovery was uneventful. A post-operative day 3, CTA demonstrated that the true lumen had expanded, and the endograft was fully expanded to its 28 mm nominal diameter (Fig. 3). DISCUSSION

The mechanical septotomy of the dissected arch performed via the kissing angioplasty described in this Technical Note appears to be an effective solution to avoid endograft pseudocoarctation in narrow true lumens crossed by retrograde branches. This risk is notably high in type three aortic arches due to severe distal arch angulation, which was initially a contraindication for arch retrograde branches in the authors’ practice. In the unit’s recently published experience, new onset post-operative congestive heart failure was observed in 5.9% (two patients) of the cohort treated with retrograde LCCA branches.5 Consequently, this kissing technique has been incorporated into standard practice for all chronic arch dissections, regardless of branch direction (anterograde or retrograde). It is a safe and reliable method for mitigating the risk of cardiac failure associated with the pressure gradient and its cardiac afterload impact associated with pseudocoarctation. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, cardiac failure and thrombus formation within the endograft are the primary complications reported following endograft lumen narrowing.

The feasibility and safety of ballooning stent grafts deployed for chronic type B aortic dissections in the thoracic aorta is supported by the experience of Gennai et al.8 However, it is critical that the kissing angioplasty is performed only after access to the LSA true lumen from its endograft branch has been secured, as catheterisation of the LSA may become extremely challenging, if not impossible, following intimal disruption.

Technically, the 46 mm Coda balloon is advantageous over the 32 mm because it can be inflated up to 46 mm using 60 mL of saline, seamlessly adapting to the varying diameters of aortic arch endografts. Additionally, its 140 cm shaft length is compatible with the distance from the common femoral artery access to the aortic arch through long femoral sheaths.

While electrosurgical (thermal) septotomy before graft implantation represents an alternative method to avoid endograft lumen compression,9 its applicability is heavily limited by the risk of false lumen thrombus embolisation into the SAT and downstream of the aorta. Furthermore, it can cause uncontrolled opening of the dissection flap, making subsequent true lumen cannulation of the supra-aortic trunks very challenging. Thus, thermal septotomy is not routinely performed.

In the present experience, this kissing angioplasty has not been associated with adverse events. Because it is performed strictly within an endograft lumen that is sized smaller than the native arch diameter, there is no risk of direct injury to the aortic wall. Moreover, as it is performed remotely from the proximal and distal sealing zones, the risk of a stent graft induced new entry tear is negligible.9 Neither device migration nor branch instability during balloon inflation or follow up has been observed.

From this early experience, mechanical septotomy via kissing balloon angioplasty is safe, feasible, and does not appear to be associated with false lumen pressurisation, thrombus embolisation, or uncontrolled tear propagation. Long-term clinical follow up is mandatory to further confirm these favourable early outcomes.
