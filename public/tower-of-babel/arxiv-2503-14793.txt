Tracking time-varying signals with quantum-enhanced atomic magnetometers
Júlia Amorós-Binefa ID, Morgan W. Mitchell ID, Jan Kołodyński ID

Abstract
Quantum entanglement, in the form of spin squeezing, is known to improve the sensitivity of atomic instruments to static or slowly-varying quantities. Sensing transient events presents a distinct challenge, requires different analysis methods, and has not been shown to benefit from entanglement in practically-important scenarios such as spin-precession magnetometry (SPM). Here we adapt estimation control techniques introduced in [PRX Quantum 6, 030331 (2025)] to the experimental setting of SPM and analogous techniques. We demonstrate that real-time tracking of fluctuating fields benefits from measurement-induced spin squeezing and that quantum limits dictated by decoherence are within reach of today’s experiments. We illustrate this quantum advantage by single-shot tracking, within the coherence time of a spin-precession magnetometer, of a magnetocardiography signal overlain with broadband noise.

We show that such a system, when appropriately polarized by optical pumping, can be used as a quantum-enhanced magnetometer to track time-varying fields. We consider the dynamical model of Ref.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}=

\displaystyle-i\left\{\omega(t)+u(t)\right\}\sqrt{N}[\!\,\hat{Z\;}\!\!,\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{\kappa_{\text{loc}}N}{2}\!\!\sum_{j=1}^{N}\!\mathcal{D}[\hat{z}^{(j)}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\mathrm{d}t\,+\kappa_{\text{coll}}N\mathcal{D}[\!\,\hat{Z\;}\!\!]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\mathrm{d}t

Conclusions.—

With help of a novel dynamical model capable of accurately describing quantum measurement backaction and decoherence in orientation-based (spin-1/2) atomic magnetometers
[
15
]
, we simulate experimental conditions (magnetic-field range, optical pumping/probing strength and spin-relaxation rate) of Ref.
[
33
]
, while disregarding effects of spin-exchange collisions, in order to show that an estimation and control scheme based on
the EKF and LQR opens doors for tracking time-varying magnetic fields at the quantum limit. For fluctuating fields, we derive a fundamental bound on sensitivity applicable to any setup involving measurement-based feedback, and show it to be attainable by the EKF+LQR scheme. Moreover, we demonstrate that the EKF+LQR solution naturally accommodates tracking magnetocardiograms, in which case the EKF reproduces accurately the underlying waveform of a cardiac signal while filtering out the noise. Our findings provide an important step in the development of theory for atomic magnetometers to leverage spin-squeezing in real-time applications. Although we assume idealised, infinitely fast measurement—adapting our analysis to a realistic discrete-time readout is possible requiring a continuous-discrete formulation of the EKF
[
57
]
combined with numerical strategies allowing for precise integration of system dynamics over finite time increments
[
58
]
—we hope that our methods will soon be adapted to apply to orientation-based magnetometers in the Bell-Bloom configuration
[
59
,
13
,
34
]
, alignment-based magnetometers
[
60
,
61
,
62
]
; as well as to accommodate for spin-exchange interatomic collisions
[
63
,
64
]
and more general forms of atomic decoherence
[
65
]
.

Appendix A: Model

The non-linear function
f(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
depends on the control law
u(t)\coloneqq u(t|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
, the noise vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{at}\oplus\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
, and the state vector
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{at}\oplus\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
. These vectors include components
\boldsymbol{x}_{at}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{at}
, describing atomic evolution, and components
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
, accounting for the signal evolution. The components
\boldsymbol{x}_{at}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{at}
are given by our atomic sensor model:
\boldsymbol{x}_{at}=(\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{at}=\xi
, where
\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}
and
\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}
are the mean of the normalized collective angular momenta
\hat{X}=\hat{J}_{x}/\sqrt{N}
and
\hat{Y}=\hat{J}_{y}/\sqrt{N}
, and
\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},
and
\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}
are the variances and covariances of such operators, i.e.
\mathrm{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}_{\alpha\beta}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2N}\left(\big\langle\{\hat{J}_{\alpha}(t),\hat{J}_{\beta}(t)\}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}-2\big\langle\hat{J}_{\alpha}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\big\langle\hat{J}_{\beta}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\right)
with diagonal elements
\mathrm{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}_{\alpha}(t)\coloneqq\mathrm{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}_{\alpha\alpha}(t)
(
\alpha,\beta=X,Y,Z
). The signal components, however, depend on the specific signal being tracked and its dynamical equation.

Similarly, the state function
f(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
can also be decomposed into two terms: one corresponding to the evolution of the atoms, and another characterizing the field, respectively. Namely,
f(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=(f_{at}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi}),f_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi}))^{T}
. The state function describing the atomic evolution,
f_{at}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, is fixed by the model of the atomic magnetometer:

\displaystyle f_{at}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\hat{X}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\hat{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\end{pmatrix}

(7)

where the form of each component of the function
f_{at}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
can be derived from the SME in Eq. (
2
), following the steps described in
[
15
]
. Namely,

\displaystyle f_{\hat{X}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!-(\omega(t)\!+\!u(t))\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;-\!\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{\text{coll}}\!+\!2\kappa_{\text{loc}}\!+\!M)\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!2\sqrt{\eta MN}\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\,\xi

(8)

\displaystyle f_{\hat{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!(\omega(t)\!+\!u(t))\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;-\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{\text{coll}}\!+\!2\kappa_{\text{loc}})\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!2\sqrt{\eta MN}\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\,\xi

(9)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!-2(\omega(t)\!+\!u(t))\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!\kappa_{\text{coll}}\!\left(\!\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!+\!\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}\!-\!\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)\!

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!\kappa_{\text{loc}}\!\left(\!\frac{1}{2}\!-\!2\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)\!

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!M\!\left(\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!-\!\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!-\!4\eta N{\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}^{2}\,\right)\!

(10)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!2(\omega(t)\!+\!u(t))\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!\kappa_{\text{coll}}\!\left(\!\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!+\!\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}\!-\!\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)\!

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;+\!\kappa_{\text{loc}}\!\left(\!\frac{1}{2}\!-\!2\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\!\right)\!\!-\!4\eta MN{\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!M\!\left(\!\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!+\!\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}\!-\!\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)\!

(12)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!(\omega(t)\!+\!u(t))\!\left(\!\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!-\!\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)\!

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;-\!\kappa_{\text{coll}}\!\left(2\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!+\!\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;-\!2\kappa_{\text{loc}}\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\;-\!\frac{1}{2}M\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\left(1+8\eta N\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\right).

(13)

Given that the atoms are initially prepared in a coherent spin state, then the initial values for the first and second moments are
(\big\langle\hat{X}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\big\langle\hat{Y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{V}_{\!Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},\mathrm{C}_{\!XY}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}})=(\sqrt{N}/2,0,0,1/4,1/4,0)
.

A.1. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

When the magnetic field we aim to track follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process just like the one in Eq. (
5
), then the component of the state vector corresponding to the signal evolution is simply
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}=\omega(t)
, with a noise component
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}=\xi_{\omega}=\mathrm{d}W_{\omega}/\mathrm{d}t
. Moreover, the part of the non-linear state space function
f(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
modelling the dynamics of the signal becomes:

\displaystyle f_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\equiv f_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\!=\!-\chi\omega(t)+\xi_{\omega}

A.2. Filtered Van der Pol oscillator

For a MCG-like signal modelled by the filtered VdP oscillator, the state variables describing the signal evolution are
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}=(\nu(t),\omega(t),\upsilon(t))
, as the dynamical model of the filtered VdP consists of a system of three ODEs:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu(t)=-p\,\omega(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega(t)=\frac{k}{m}\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+2\frac{c}{m}(1-\upsilon(t))\,\omega(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\upsilon(t)=\frac{|\nu(t)|-\nu(t)}{2T}\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\upsilon(t)}{T}\,\mathrm{d}t

(17)

where
p,k,m,c,T>0
are all positive constants, and the second component, i.e.
\omega(t)
, is the variable we aim to track. The parameters specifying the MCG-like signal chosen throughout this work are:
p=10^{3}
,
k=1
,
m=0.00098
,
c=1
and
T=0.003
, with initial values:
\nu_{0}=\omega_{0}=\upsilon_{0}=0.0045
.
Since none of its components is assumed to fluctuate in time, the noise component is simply
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}=0
. It follows from Eq. (
15
) that
f_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})
has three terms:

\displaystyle f_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\\
f_{\upsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\end{pmatrix}

(18)

where

\displaystyle f_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=-p\,\omega(t)

(19)

\displaystyle f_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{k}{m}\nu(t)+2\frac{c}{m}(1-\upsilon(t))\,\omega(t)

(20)

\displaystyle f_{\upsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},u,t,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{|\nu(t)|-\nu(t)}{2T}-\frac{\upsilon(t)}{T}

(21)

Following the same steps as in
[
15
]
, our goal is to find a convex decomposition of the joint noisy map
\Omega\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\left[\;\cdot\;\right]\right]
which also accounts for the
\omega

54

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})=\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\;q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\}

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\ell}=\{\zeta^{\,(1)}_{\ell},\zeta^{\,(2)}_{\ell},\dots,\zeta^{\,(N)}_{\ell}\}

\ell

{\delta t}

\zeta^{\,(1)}

\zeta^{\,(2)}
etc.

While

represents the mixing distribution that crucially contains all the

\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})=

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}}\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\left[\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{k}}\!\dots\,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}}\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}\!\left[\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{0}}\rho_{0}\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{0}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\!\dots\!\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\right]\!\right\}\!.

\displaystyle\Omega[\Lambda_{\omega}[\;\cdot\;]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}}}\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\;f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\overline{\zeta}-\omega)^{2}}{2\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}[\;\cdot\;]

D.3. Upper bound on the Fisher Information

The main goal behind our proof is to bypass the calculation of the Fisher Information
F\,[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})]
, defined as

\displaystyle F\,[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})}\!\left[\left(\partial_{\omega_{k}}\log{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})}\right)^{2}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})}\!\left[-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\log{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})}\right],

(61)

in the Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound (BCRB) by upper-bounding it. However, the likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})
decomposed in Eq. (
55
) is not exactly the conditional distribution appearing in the BCRB. In particular, the BCRB, which is defined as the inverse of the Bayesian Information
J_{B}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq(J_{B})^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{F\,[p(\omega_{k})]+\int\mathrm{d}\omega_{k}\,p(\omega_{k})F\,[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})]},

(62)

depends on both the prior knowledge about the frequency
\omega_{k}
at time
k{\delta t}
and
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})
, and the probability of recording a measurement trajectory
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
given that the frequency at time
t
is
\omega_{k}
. Thus, we must find a way to relate the likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})
required to compute the BCRB to the likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})
appearing in Eq. (
55
), i.e. the likelihood of detecting a measurement record
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
given that the Larmor frequency has followed a trajectory
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\}
. To do so, we simply use the Bayes’ rule and rewrite

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\omega_{k})}{p(\omega_{k})}=\frac{1}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}\,p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}\,p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}).

(63)

Thus, now we can apply Eq. (
55
) to Eq. (
63
) and reveals a decomposition similar to Eq. (
55
) but for
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})
. Specifically,

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\!\left[\frac{1}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})\!\right]

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\!\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\left[\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\right]

(64)

where we identify
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\to\,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}[\;\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{$\,\bm{\cdot}\,$}}\;]=\int\!d\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\,p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\;\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{$\,\bm{\cdot}\,$}}\;
as a stochastic map independent of the parameter
\omega_{k}
, and the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})
as

\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})=\frac{1}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}\,p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}),

(65)

which contains the information on
\omega_{k}
within
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})
.
As the Fisher Information is generally contractive (monotonic) under the action of stochastic maps, we can now upper-bound
F\,[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})]
as

\displaystyle F\,[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega_{k})]=F\,[\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\left[\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\right]]\leq F\,[\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})].

(66)

Thus, the problem of lower-bounding the BCRB in Eq. (
62
) now reduces to evaluating the Fisher Information of
\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})
.

D.4. Analytical form of
\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

The probability distribution outlined in Eq. (
65
) consists of three different probability distributions: the marginal probability
p(\omega_{k})
, the prior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})
and the classically-simulated likelihood or mixing distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})
. To derive an analytical expression for Eq. (
65
), we first need to elaborate on the exact forms of each probability component.

D.4..1. Prior contribution

Tracking is a sensing task where we wish to monitor the evolution of a parameter, in this case a trajectory of frequencies
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\}
, with each element
\omega_{k}
drawn from a probability distribution
p(\omega_{k})
:

p(\omega_{k})=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}).

(67)

We choose
\omega_{k}
to be the time-discretized version of the OU process
\omega(t)
of Eq. (
5
). However, in the following proof, we omit the term
\bar{\omega}
since it has no impact on the aMSE of
\tilde{\omega}
. Specifically, shifting a process by a deterministic value
\bar{\omega}
, i.e.
\nu(t)\coloneqq\omega(t)-\bar{\omega}
, preserves the aMSE:
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\nu}(t)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}(t)}\right]
. Hence, the effective process we consider is:

\mathrm{d}\omega(t)=-\chi\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{q_{\omega}}\mathrm{d}W_{\omega}

(68)

where
\chi>0
and
q_{\omega}>0
parametrize the decay and volatility of the process, and
\mathrm{d}W_{\omega}
denotes the Wiener differential with mean
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\mathrm{d}W_{\omega}}\right]=0
and variance
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{\omega}}\right]=\mathrm{d}t
, then the probability of the process transitioning from
\omega_{k\shortminus 1}
at time
(k-1){\delta t}
to
\omega_{k}
at
k{\delta t}
is given by

p(\omega_{k}|\omega_{k\shortminus 1})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\dfrac{(\omega_{k}-\omega_{k\shortminus 1}\mathrm{e}^{-\chi\delta t})^{2}}{2\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}}}

(69)

with variance

\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}=\frac{q_{\omega}}{2\chi}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\chi{\delta t}}).

(70)

Since the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is a Markov process, the probability of the process
\omega(t)
of following a discrete trajectory
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\}
is given by

p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p(\omega_{i}|\omega_{i-1})p(\omega_{0}),

(71)

where we assume
p(\omega_{0})
to be a Gaussian prior with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, i.e.
p(\omega_{0})=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
. From that, we can compute the probability of the frequency taking the value
\omega_{k}
at time
k{\delta t}
, irrespective of the previous values of
\omega
:

\displaystyle p(\omega_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{2\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}}\right),

(72)

with variance

\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}=\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2k\chi{\delta t}}+\frac{q_{\omega}}{2\chi}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2k\chi{\delta t}}).

(73)

D.4..3. Integrated form of

Once each contribution to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}\,p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\overline{\zeta}}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k})

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})}{p(\omega_{k})}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k\shortminus 1}\!\!\prod_{j=1}^{k}\!p(\omega_{j}|\omega_{j\shortminus 1})\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{j}|\omega_{j})\,p(\omega_{0})\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{0}|\omega_{0})

\displaystyle\,\dots\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\,p(\omega_{2}|\omega_{1})\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{1}|\omega_{1})\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega_{0}\,p(\omega_{1}|\omega_{0})\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{0}|\omega_{0})p(\omega_{0}).

Since all the functions inside the integrals are Gaussian, this set of nested integrals has a recursive solution, as given in

]
. In particular, if we identify the variances of the recursive relation in the Lemma 2 of
[
42
]
,
\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}
and
\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}
, with Eq. (
70

\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}=\text{V}_{\!\text{coll}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{loc}}/N

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{f\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})}{p(\omega_{k})}\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{k}|\omega_{k})\mathcal{P}_{k}(\omega_{k})

(78)

where

\mathcal{P}_{k}(\omega_{k})=C_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\omega_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}}{2\mathrm{V}_{k}}}

(79)

\mathrm{V}_{k}=\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}+\frac{\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}V_{k\shortminus 1}}{\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+V_{k\shortminus 1}}

(80)

with initial value

D.5. Fisher Information of
\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

Now that we have a closed form for
\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})
, we can move to computing its Fisher Information using the form for the Fisher specified in Eq. (
61
). Namely,

\displaystyle F\,{[\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})]}=

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\!\left[\!-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\!\log{\!\left(\frac{f\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})}{p(\omega_{k})}\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{k}|\omega_{k})\mathcal{P}_{k}(\omega_{k})\!\right)}\!\right]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\left[-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\log{f\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})}\right]

(81)

\displaystyle-\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\left[-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\log{p(\omega_{k})}\right]

(82)

\displaystyle+\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\left[-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\log{\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\zeta}_{k}|\omega_{k})}\right]

(83)

\displaystyle+\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})\left[-\partial^{2}_{\omega_{k}}\log{\mathcal{P}_{k}(\omega_{k})}\right]

(84)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}}+\frac{1}{\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}_{k}},

(85)

where to reach the final expression of (
85
) we have used the fact that
f\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})
in Eq. (
81
) does not depend on
\omega_{k}
, and that in Eqs. (
82
-
84
),
-\partial_{\omega_{k}}^{2}\log{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\omega_{k}-\mu)^{2}}{2V}}}=V^{-1}
. We have a closed expression for both
\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}
and
\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}
, but not for
\mathrm{V}_{k}
. For that, we need to solve the recursive relation of Eq. (
80
). Fortunately, such a form for
\mathrm{V}_{k}
exists, even though lengthy:

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{k}=\frac{W_{+}V_{+}^{\,k}+W_{-}V_{-}^{\,k}}{U_{-}V_{-}^{\,k}+U_{+}V_{+}^{\,k}},

(86)

with terms
W_{+}
,
V_{+}
,
U_{+}
,
W_{-}
,
V_{-}
, and
U_{-}
given by

\displaystyle W_{+}

\displaystyle=2\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}})}

(87)

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=-2\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}-\sigma_{0}^{2}\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}}

(88)

\displaystyle U_{+}

\displaystyle=-\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}+2\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}})}

(89)

\displaystyle U_{-}

\displaystyle=\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}-2\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}})}

(90)

\displaystyle V_{+}

\displaystyle=2\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}+\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}})}

(91)

\displaystyle V_{-}

\displaystyle=2\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}-\sqrt{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(4\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}+\text{V}_{\!\text{P}})}

(92)

where
\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}
is the variance given in Eq. (
58
) and
\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}
is defined in Eq. (
70
).

D.6. Continuous-time limit

If now we take the continuous-time limit of
{\delta t}\rightarrow 0
, we can observe that the term
1/\text{V}_{\!\text{Q}}
in Eq. (
85
) goes to zero. The other terms become,

\displaystyle\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\delta t}\rightarrow 0}\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}=\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\chi t}+\frac{q_{\omega}}{2\chi}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\chi t})

(93)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{\sigma_{0}}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\delta t}\rightarrow 0}V_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{q_{\omega}\kappa}\,\sigma_{0}^{2}\cosh{\left(t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa}}\right)}+q_{\omega}\kappa\sinh{\left(t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa}}\right)}}{\sqrt{q_{\omega}\kappa}\cosh{\left(t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa}}\right)}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\sinh{\left(t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa}}\right)}},

(94)

where
\kappa=\kappa_{\text{coll}}+2\kappa_{\text{loc}}/N
. Therefore, the BCRB (
62
) can be now bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{F\,[p(\omega_{k})]+\int\mathrm{d}\omega_{k}\,p(\omega_{k})F\,[\mathbb{P}_{\omega_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})]}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\dfrac{1}{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}}-\dfrac{1}{\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}}+\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{V}_{k}}}=\mathrm{V}_{k},

(95)

where we have used the fact that
F\,[p(\omega_{k})]=1/\text{V}_{\!\text{P}}^{(k)}
.

Hence, in its most general form in the continuous time limit, the aMSE is lower-bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}(t)}\right]\!\geq\mathrm{V}_{\sigma_{0}}(t)

(96)

\displaystyle=\!\dfrac{\sqrt{q_{\omega}\kappa(N)}\,\sigma_{0}^{2}\cosh{\left(\!t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa(N)}}\right)}\!+\!q_{\omega}\kappa(N)\sinh{\left(\!t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa(N)}}\right)}}{\sqrt{q_{\omega}\kappa(N)}\cosh{\left(\!t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa(N)}}\right)}\!+\!\sigma_{0}^{2}\sinh{\left(\!t\sqrt{\dfrac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa(N)}}\right)}}

which in the limit
\sigma_{0}\rightarrow\infty
simplifies to

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}(t)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,N)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{q_{\omega}\kappa(N)}\coth{\left(t\sqrt{\frac{q_{\omega}}{\kappa(N)}}\right)},

(97)

and matches the result of Ref.
[
42
]
if further
\kappa_{\text{loc}}=0\,\implies\,\kappa(N)=\kappa_{\text{coll}}
. If we then further take the limit of
q_{\omega}\rightarrow 0
, it then becomes

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}(t)}\right]\geq\frac{\kappa(N)}{t}=\frac{\kappa_{\text{coll}}}{t}+\frac{2\kappa_{\text{loc}}}{Nt},

(98)

which now exhibits the standard quantum limit (SQL), as discussed in Ref.
[
15
]
.

D.7. Accounting for fluctuations of the atomic number
N

In typical atomic magnetometry experiments, the number of atoms
N
is not precisely known and changes from shot to shot. To model this, we assume that in each realization of the experiment, the actual atomic number is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean
\bar{N}=10^{13}
and standard deviation
\sigma=10^{11}

[
33
]
, i.e.:

N\sim p(N)=\mathcal{N}(\bar{N},\sigma^{2}).

(99)

While the system evolves with the specific drawn value of
N
, the estimation is performed assuming that
N=\bar{N}
, since the experimenter does not have access to the exact number of atoms in the ensemble.
Consequently, the aMSE must be further averaged over the probability distribution of
N
,
p(N)
.

As a result, the bound on the aMSE given in Eq. (
97
) becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(N)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}(t)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{p(N)}\!\left[\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,N)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,\mathbb{E}_{p(N)}\!\left[N\right])=\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,\bar{N})

(100)

where we have applied the Jensen’s inequality since
\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,N)
is a convex function of
N
. Its convexity can be established by computing its second derivative w.r.t.
N
and showing that it is positive
\forall N,t,q_{\omega},\kappa_{\text{loc}},\kappa_{\text{coll}}
. Although computing the second derivative is lengthy, the positivity of all parameters (
N
,
t
,
q_{\omega}
,
\kappa_{\text{loc}}
,
\kappa_{\text{coll}}
) assures that all the summands forming
\mathrm{V}^{\prime\prime}(t,N)
are also positive, and hence
\mathrm{V}^{\prime\prime}(t,N)\geq 0
. To further support this analytical result, the convexity of
\mathrm{V}_{\infty}(t,N)
is also illustrated in Fig.
6
.
