Artificial intelligence literacy and associations with thriving at work among nurses in Anhui Province, China: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Zhenni Xie, Jiangying Han, Dalin Kuang, Jinxiu Hu, Chenxia Liao, Ruiqi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies into clinical nursing is changing nursing practice and creating new competency requirements for nurses. Some nurses may still face difficulties in technical adaptation, ethical judgment, and practical use of AI tools in intelligent healthcare environments. Understanding nurses’ AI literacy and its relationship with thriving at work may help hospitals design more targeted support strategies.AimsThis study aimed to investigate the current status of nurses’ artificial intelligence literacy, identify latent profiles of self-reported AI literacy, analyze factors associated with profile membership, and examine differences in thriving at work across AI literacy profiles.DesignA cross-sectional study.MethodsIn January 2026, 1000 nurses from 62 hospitals in Anhui Province, China were recruited by convenience sampling. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale, the Chinese version of the Thriving at Work Scale, a nine-item measure of attitudes toward AI in nursing, and the General Self-Efficacy Scale. Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into clinical nursing practice, including data analysis, risk warning, and decision support. These applications may improve nursing service efficiency and quality when used appropriately [1]. As AI becomes more embedded in nursing scenarios, nurses are expected not only to maintain traditional professional competencies but also to develop the ability to understand, evaluate, and use AI in intelligent healthcare environments. This competency is commonly referred to as AI literacy.

The term “AI literacy” was used by Agre [2] in an early technical guide for AI practitioners, although it referred mainly to familiarity with foundational AI literature rather than the broader competency-based concept used today. Ng et al. [3] defined AI literacy as the ability to understand and apply AI technologies without requiring prior technical expertise. Long et al. [4] further described it as a comprehensive capability that includes critical evaluation, effective collaboration, and appropriate application of AI technologies. In nursing, AI literacy can therefore be understood as nurses’ cognitive understanding, evaluative reflection, ethical judgment, and human-machine collaboration abilities required for delivering high-quality care in intelligent healthcare environments. Previous research suggests that higher AI literacy may help nurses use AI technologies more safely and effectively and support clinical decision-making and care quality [5]. However, current AI applications in nursing remain largely technology-driven, with relatively insufficient attention to nurses’ competency development. This may create a mismatch in which technological implementation advances faster than nurses’ AI-related preparedness. Nurses also differ in their awareness and understanding of AI technologies, and some may have difficulty recognizing how AI can support clinical work [6]. In addition, AI use in healthcare raises concerns about data privacy, algorithmic bias, and professional accountability, highlighting the need for nurses’ ethical awareness and judgment [7].

Thriving at work refers to a positive psychological state characterized by the joint experience of vitality and learning and is regarded as an important psychological resource for nurses’ professional development [8]. A recent three-wave longitudinal study found that AI literacy positively predicted career growth among new nurses through job crafting, indicating its potential relevance to nurses’ positive growth and development at work [9]. In AI-assisted healthcare environments, nurses with stronger AI literacy may be better prepared to engage with new technologies and may report greater professional confidence, creativity, and problem-solving capacity. Among nursing students, AI literacy has also been found to be closely related to academic adaptation and psychological resilience [10].

Existing research on nurses’ AI literacy remains at an early stage. Previous studies have relied on total scale scores, which may overlook within-group heterogeneity [5, 11]. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered approach that identifies unobserved subgroups according to response patterns across multiple observed indicators. Compared with traditional grouping methods, LPA estimates profile membership probabilistically and uses model fit indices to guide the selection of the optimal profile solution [12]. This approach is therefore useful for identifying nurses with similar AI literacy patterns and for revealing heterogeneity that may be hidden behind similar total scores. Accordingly, this study used latent profile analysis to identify latent profiles of AI literacy among nurses in Anhui Province, China, and to compare thriving at work across profiles. The findings may provide empirical evidence for developing stratified educational, training, and managerial strategies to support nurses’ AI literacy development.

Methods

Subjects of investigation

In January 2026, convenience sampling was employed to recruit nurses from 62 hospitals in Anhui Province, China. Inclusion criteria were registered nurses working on-site for ≥ 6 months. Exclusion criteria included visiting or intern nurses. Sample size was calculated as 5–10 times the number of items [13]. With 52 questionnaire items and allowing for 20% invalid responses, the required sample was 325–650. In addition, according to Nylund’s recommendation [14], the minimum sample size for latent profile analysis should be 200 to ensure model stability. Ultimately, 1,026 questionnaires were collected, which met the sample size requirement.

Survey instruments

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire captured participants’ sociodemographic, professional, institutional, and AI-related behavioral characteristics. It was developed through literature review and group discussion. Items included gender, age, marital status, educational level, professional title, hospital grade, hospital administrative affiliation, years of work experience, key nursing position/department management role, AI training experience, and AI usage frequency in the past 6 months.

Artificial intelligence literacy scale

The Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS), developed by Wang et al. [15] to assess artificial intelligence literacy in the general population, was used in this study. The scale comprises four dimensions: awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics. The awareness dimension reflects an individual’s ability to recognize and understand AI-related technologies when encountering AI products; the usage dimension refers to the ability to efficiently complete tasks with the assistance of AI tools; the evaluation dimension reflects the ability to analyze, select, and critically judge AI applications and their outputs; and the ethics dimension focuses on users’ awareness of the potential risks and related responsibilities associated with AI technologies, as well as their adherence to relevant ethical principles. Each dimension contains three items, yielding a total of 12 items. The scale uses a 7-point Likert response format ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” scored from 1 to 7, with total scores ranging from 12 to 84; higher scores indicate higher levels of artificial intelligence literacy. The original scale showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.944. This scale was selected because its four dimensions correspond to foundational competencies required for the safe use of AI. However, as the AILS was not developed specifically for clinical nursing contexts, it may not fully capture more specific AI competencies required in nursing practice, such as integrating AI outputs into clinical decision-making, identifying patient safety-related risks, and adapting to AI-enabled nursing workflows. Therefore, in this study, AILS scores were interpreted as nurses’ self-reported general AI literacy rather than as an objective assessment of nursing-specific AI competence.

Thriving at work scale

The Chinese version of the Thriving at Work Scale (TWS), originally developed by Porath et al. [16] and psychometrically evaluated among Chinese employees by Zeng et al. [17], was used to assess thriving at work. The scale comprises 10 items across two dimensions: learning and vitality, with five items in each dimension. Items 4 and 8 are reverse-scored. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), yielding a total score of 10–70. Higher scores indicate greater thriving at work. Cronbach’s α was 0.827 in the present study.

Attitudes toward AI in nursing

The nine-item section on attitudes toward AI in nursing from the questionnaire developed by Wang et al. [18] was used to assess nurses’ attitudes toward AI applications in nursing. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 9 to 36, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward AI applications in nursing. The original study reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.860, and Cronbach’s α was 0.921 in the present study.

General self-efficacy scale

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), developed by Schwarzer et al. [19] and adapted into Chinese by Wang et al. [20], was used to assess nurses’ self-efficacy. The GSES is a unidimensional instrument consisting of 10 items, each scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all true) to 4 (exactly true). Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The Chinese version demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.87; Cronbach’s α was 0.813 in the present study.

Data collection

Thirty nurses meeting the inclusion criteria participated in a pilot test before formal data collection. The pilot test was not intended to revalidate the standardized scales. Instead, because the General Information Questionnaire was developed for this study and included context-specific sociodemographic, occupational, AI training, and AI use items, the pilot test was used to assess the clarity and appropriateness of its wording and response options. It also assessed the comprehensibility of item instructions after scale integration, the estimated completion time, and the usability of the online Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn) survey process. Minor wording and layout adjustments were then made to improve readability. Participants in the pilot test were not included in the formal survey. Subsequently, with assistance from head nurses across departments, questionnaires were distributed and guidance was provided. The survey landing page explained the study purpose, questionnaire content, voluntary nature of participation, anonymity, confidentiality, and intended use of the data. It further clarified that the AI-related data collected in this study were limited to participants’ self-reported AI training experience, AI usage frequency in the past 6 months, AI literacy, and attitudes toward AI in nursing, rather than patient information, clinical records, or AI-system log data. Participants were informed that their responses would be used only for aggregate statistical analysis and academic research, and that no individual nurse or hospital would be identifiable in any report or publication. Participants proceeded to the questionnaire only after indicating informed consent online and could stop before submission. To ensure data integrity, all items were mandatory; incomplete submissions triggered automatic prompts preventing submission, and single-account restrictions prevented duplicate submissions. Two researchers monitored the data in real time and exported the dataset after one week with no new responses. After data collection, two researchers independently screened the submitted questionnaires for data quality by jointly considering completion time, response patterns, and logical consistency across related items. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.3. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test with unrotated exploratory factor analysis.

Latent profile analysis was conducted in Mplus 8.3 using the four AILS dimension scores as indicators to identify nurses’ AI literacy profiles. Models were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR). One- to four-profile models were estimated and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicated better model fit; entropy values closer to 1 indicated higher classification precision; and significant LMR-LRT and BLRT results indicated that the k-profile model fitted better than the k-1 profile model. Because the four AILS dimensions were derived from the same scale, each contained three items, and all used the same 7-point response format, the raw dimension scores were considered comparable. In this study, measurement invariance testing was not conducted because LPA was used to identify latent subgroups within a single sample rather than to compare measurement structures across predefined groups. An indicator-scaling check and optimization-stability analyses were performed for the LPA. First, LPA was repeated using z-score standardized AILS dimension scores to examine whether the classification depended on indicator scaling. Second, each candidate model was estimated using 2,000 initial-stage random starts and 500 final-stage optimizations (STARTS = 2000 500), with 20 initial-stage iterations (STITERATIONS = 20) and a fixed random seed (STSEED = 20260712). Convergence and replication of the best log-likelihood were examined using TECH8. The selected three-profile model was then independently re-estimated using a different random seed (STSEED = 20260713).

Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics before analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were described as mean±standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were described as median [P25, P75]. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons across latent profile groups, normally distributed continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance, non-normally distributed continuous variables using Kruskal-Wallis H tests, and categorical variables using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The distributional characteristics of the learning, vitality, and total scores on the Thriving at Work Scale were examined using skewness and kurtosis, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Because the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was violated for all three outcomes, differences in scores on the Thriving at Work Scale across the three AI literacy profiles were examined using Welch’s one-way analysis of variance, followed by Games-Howell post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For multinomial logistic regression, independent variables were selected using a comprehensive strategy that combined theoretical evidence with univariate results in parallel. Specifically, variables previously reported in the literature to be associated with artificial intelligence literacy [11, 21], together with variables showing statistically significant differences across latent profile groups in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05), were jointly included in the candidate variable pool. These two sources of evidence were considered simultaneously rather than through a stepwise screening procedure; that is, variables were not retained only after reaching statistical significance in univariate testing. Based on this strategy, age, educational level, work experience, key nursing position/department management role, AI training experience, AI usage frequency in the past 6 months, attitude toward AI in nursing, and self-efficacy were entered simultaneously into the multinomial logistic regression model using the enter method. Age, educational level, work experience, and AI usage frequency in the past 6 months were entered into the model as ordinal variables; their ORs therefore represent the change in the odds of profile membership associated with a one-category increase. Key nursing position/department management role and AI training experience were entered as binary categorical variables, with Yes as the reference category. Multicollinearity among the candidate predictors was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Serious multicollinearity was considered present when the tolerance value was < 0.10 and the VIF was > 10.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval no. KY2026-10-76). Data were collected anonymously through an online questionnaire. All participants provided online informed consent before completing the survey. All collected data were anonymized and used only for research purposes.

Results

Common method bias test

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using unrotated exploratory factor analysis of the 41 items. The results showed that eight factors had eigenvalues greater than 1 and collectively explained 65.18% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 19.91% of the variance, which was below the recommended threshold of 40% [22]. These findings indicate that no single dominant factor accounted for most of the covariance among the measured items, suggesting that serious common method bias was not evident in this study. Nevertheless, because all key variables were collected using self-reported questionnaires, the possibility of residual common method bias should still be considered when interpreting the findings.

Characteristics of participants

After dual-review data-quality screening, 26 responses were excluded because of obvious random or patterned answering (n = 14), unusually short completion times combined with inadequate response quality (n = 7), or logical inconsistencies in core items (n = 5). Consequently, 1000 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 97.47%.

Participants were predominantly female (96.10%). The largest age group was nurses older than 30 and up to 40 years (52.00%), and most participants were married (79.90%). Educational level was relatively high, with 84.10% holding a bachelor’s degree or above. Regarding professional characteristics, the largest professional title category was nurse-in-charge (56.00%), and 52.40% of participants had more than 5 years of work experience. Most participants did not hold a key nursing position/department management role (72.70%). In terms of hospital characteristics, 58.20% of participants were from tertiary hospitals and 41.80% were from secondary hospitals; 45.50% were from provincial-level hospitals, 40.30% from prefecture-level hospitals, and 14.20% from county-level hospitals. Regarding AI training experience, 24.90% of participants reported having received AI training. For AI usage frequency in the past 6 months, weekly use was the most commonly reported category (39.50%), followed by monthly use (36.60%), occasional use (13.40%), and daily use (10.50%). The AILS total scores for 1000 nurses ranged from 42 to 80 (58.92 ± 9.28). Mean dimension scores were: awareness (14.59 ± 2.89), usage (14.93 ± 2.47), evaluation (15.09 ± 2.25), and ethics (14.32 ± 3.21).

The primary LPA was conducted using the raw scores of the four AILS dimensions. Model fit indices for the one- to four-profile models are presented in Table 2. As the number of profiles increased, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased consistently, and the LMR-LRT and BLRT P values were < 0.001 for the two- to four-profile models, indicating better fit than the model with one fewer profile. Therefore, entropy, likelihood ratio tests, profile proportions, parsimony, classification certainty, and substantive interpretability were considered together.

Fit indices for latent profile models of nurses’ AI literacy

The two-profile model had relatively higher AIC, BIC, and aBIC values and mainly provided a broad low-versus-high distinction, which was insufficient to capture heterogeneity in nurses’ AI literacy across the four dimensions. The three-profile model yielded the highest entropy (0.942), indicating the clearest classification among the candidate models. It also produced interpretable profile patterns, with acceptable profile proportions and practical relevance for nursing management. Although the four-profile model showed lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, its entropy declined to 0.884, suggesting reduced classification certainty. In addition, the four-profile solution increased model complexity but did not provide a clearer or more theoretically meaningful classification pattern. Considering model fit indices, entropy, likelihood ratio tests, profile proportions, parsimony, classification certainty, and substantive interpretability, the three-profile solution was selected as the optimal model.

After the three-profile solution was selected, additional checks were conducted to assess its stability. Each candidate model was estimated using 2,000 initial-stage random starts and 500 final-stage optimizations (STARTS = 2000 500; STITERATIONS = 20; STSEED = 20260712), and all models terminated normally. An independent run using a different random seed (STSEED = 20260713) reproduced the same log-likelihood, AIC (16693.322), BIC (16781.662), aBIC (16724.493), entropy (0.942), and profile sizes. After profile labels were matched according to the four AILS dimension means, all 1,000 participants had the same most likely profile assignment in the two runs. The four AILS dimension scores were then z-score standardized in Mplus 8.3, and LPA was repeated using the same random-start settings to examine whether the classification depended on indicator scaling. The standardized analysis also supported a three-profile solution. After profile labels were matched by the four AILS dimension means, agreement between the raw-score and standardized classifications was 100.00% (Cohen’s kappa = 1.000; adjusted Rand index = 1.000), and the profile sizes were identical: C1 = 332 (33.20%), C2 = 538 (53.80%), and C3 = 130 (13.00%). These results show that indicator rescaling did not change the classification in this dataset. Because z-score standardization is a linear transformation of the same indicators, this analysis was treated as a scaling check rather than independent evidence of model robustness.

Characteristics and naming of nurses’ AI literacy profiles

Profile labels were assigned based on the relative score patterns across the four AILS dimensions, as shown in Fig. 1. For visualization, raw dimension scores were divided by three to obtain mean item scores; this linear transformation did not alter the relative profile patterns. Profile 1 included 332 nurses (33.20%) and showed the lowest overall scores, with a relatively lower ethics score; therefore, it was labeled the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile. Profile 2 included 538 nurses (53.80%) and showed intermediate overall scores, with relatively higher awareness but lower usage and evaluation scores, suggesting a gap between cognitive awareness and practical application; therefore, it was labeled the Cognition Practice Gap profile. Profile 3 included 130 nurses (13.00%) and showed consistently higher scores across all four dimensions, indicating a relatively balanced and advantageous literacy pattern; therefore, it was labeled the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile. These labels were used to summarize the observed patterns of self-reported AI literacy across the four AILS dimensions and should be understood as descriptive profile names.

Thriving at work across AI literacy profiles

Scores on the Thriving at Work Scale differed significantly across the three AI literacy profiles. Welch’s one-way ANOVA showed significant between-profile differences in the learning score, Welch’s F(2, 436.225) = 17.425, P < 0.001; vitality score, Welch’s F(2, 480.272) = 26.726, P < 0.001; and total score, Welch’s F(2, 482.282) = 28.093, P < 0.001. Games-Howell post-hoc comparisons showed that nurses in the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile had significantly higher learning, vitality, and total scores on the Thriving at Work Scale than those in the Cognition Practice Gap and Ethical Awareness Deficit profiles. Nurses in the Cognition Practice Gap profile also scored significantly higher than those in the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile on all three outcomes (Table 3).

Multinomial logistic regression analysis of factors associated with nurses’ AI literacy profiles

Using AI literacy profile membership as the dependent variable, a multinomial logistic regression model was constructed. Independent variables were selected using a comprehensive strategy that combined theoretical evidence with univariate results in parallel. Specifically, variables previously reported in the literature to be associated with artificial intelligence literacy, together with variables showing statistically significant differences across latent profile groups in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05), were jointly included in the candidate variable pool. Based on this strategy, eight candidate variables were entered simultaneously into the model using the enter method: age, educational level, work experience, key nursing position/department management role, AI training experience, AI usage frequency in the past 6 months, attitude toward AI in nursing, and self-efficacy. Discussion

Nurses’ AI literacy showed heterogeneous profiles and requires targeted support

The mean total AILS score among the 1,000 nurses was 58.92 ± 9.28, indicating that their self-reported AI literacy was above the scale midpoint, a finding similar to that reported among pediatric nurses [23]. Latent profile analysis further showed clear heterogeneity in nurses’ self-reported AI literacy across the awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics dimensions. The Ethical Awareness Deficit profile showed the lowest overall pattern, with a particularly low ethics dimension score. This pattern is consistent with previous concerns that nurses may need stronger preparation for patient data confidentiality, algorithmic bias, accountability, and patient safety in AI-assisted healthcare settings [24]. The Cognition Practice Gap profile showed basic awareness of AI but relatively lower usage and evaluation scores, suggesting that some nurses may have conceptual familiarity with AI but still face difficulties in applying AI tools or critically appraising AI outputs in clinical scenarios. In contrast, the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile showed higher scores across the awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics dimensions, although it represented the smallest subgroup. These findings suggest that AI literacy support in nursing should not remain limited to general awareness. An integrated framework covering AI knowledge, practical skills, and ethical judgment may be considered. Educational innovation, clinical empowerment, and talent development could be coordinated to incorporate critical appraisal and ethical reasoning into routine training and practice.

Differences in thriving at work across AI literacy profiles

In this study, nurses’ scores on the Thriving at Work Scale were in the moderate range, suggesting that further support for thriving at work may be needed. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Li et al. [25] among psychiatric nurses and by Terry et al. [26] among newly qualified nurses. Thriving at work is a positive psychological concept focused on work experience and derived from well-being psychology, incorporating both eudaimonic and hedonic perspectives of well-being [27]. In the present study, scores on the Thriving at Work Scale differed across the three AI literacy profiles, with higher scores observed in profiles characterized by stronger self-reported AI literacy. In intelligent nursing contexts, nurses with higher self-reported AI literacy may feel more able to adapt to AI-related tools, interpret AI-assisted information, and engage with technology-enabled workflows, which may be reflected in more favorable work experiences. This interpretation is consistent with Conservation of Resources theory [28], which emphasizes that individual resources are closely related to stress coping and positive psychological states. A study among undergraduate nursing students similarly reported associations between AI literacy, psychological empowerment, anxiety, and overall well-being [29]. Research involving university faculty has also found that higher AI literacy is associated with greater satisfaction with work autonomy, professional competence, interpersonal relationships, and work-life balance [30]. Future interventions could provide stratified AI literacy support according to profile characteristics. For nurses in the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile, hospitals could provide short 2- to 4-hour modules covering data privacy, algorithmic bias, patient safety, accountability, and basic AI operation, followed by case-based exercises using common clinical scenarios. For nurses in the Cognition Practice Gap profile, quarterly workshops or simulation-based training could focus on interpreting AI outputs, checking clinical relevance, identifying automation bias, and integrating AI suggestions with nursing judgment. For nurses in the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile, hospitals could encourage peer mentoring, ward-level demonstrations, and participation in AI-related quality-improvement projects. These measures may provide practical ideas for narrowing gaps in AI literacy across subgroups; however, whether such stratified support is associated with improvements in nurses’ thriving at work should be examined in future longitudinal or interventional studies.

Factors associated with nurses’ AI literacy profiles

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics

Higher educational level was associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to the Cognition Practice Gap and Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profiles, which is consistent with the findings of Li et al. [31]. This association may be related to stronger knowledge reserves, more learning opportunities, and better information-processing abilities among nurses with higher educational level. It may also reflect greater exposure to emerging digital technologies and more opportunities to consider their integration into nursing work. In addition, nurses with shorter work experience were more likely to belong to the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile. Although longer work experience may indicate richer clinical experience, one possible explanation is that nurses with shorter tenure may be more receptive to emerging technologies. A study among midwives also showed that practitioners with 0–5 years of work experience held more positive attitudes toward AI [32]. Nurses without a key nursing position or department management role were more likely to belong to the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile. This association may reflect differences in opportunities to participate in AI training, quality-improvement activities, or decision-making processes, as well as differences in exposure to ethical and practical issues related to AI use. Similarly, Zeng et al. [33] reported higher AILS scores among teaching nurses, which may be related to routine training, instructional responsibilities, and continuing professional development opportunities. Nursing managers may therefore consider differentiated strategies, such as broadening continuing education opportunities for nurses with lower educational level, supporting senior nurses in updating digital mindsets, and providing standardized AI practical-skills training for general clinical nurses.

AI training experience and usage frequency

Nurses without AI training were more likely to belong to the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile. This association may reflect fewer opportunities to learn basic AI concepts, algorithmic logic, clinical applicability, data-related risks, and ethical principles. A recent systematic review suggested that AI education and training may support the development of healthcare workers’ AI literacy by improving their attitudes and perceptions, AI-related knowledge and skills, and some behavioral outcomes [34]. The Technology Acceptance Model proposes that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are core factors related to technology adoption [35]. Specialized AI training may help strengthen nurses’ perceptions of technological usefulness, and hands-on training may support perceived ease of use and be related to the translation of use intentions into practical behavior. According to competency development theory, skill formation usually involves knowledge acquisition, internalization, and practice transfer. Differences in cognitive understanding and practical experience may therefore be reflected in group-level differences in AI literacy. However, most related studies have used cross-sectional designs, making it difficult to capture changes in literacy over time. Future longitudinal cohort studies, combined with structural equation modeling or latent growth modeling, could further examine how AI training, AI use, and AI literacy profiles are related over time.

Higher AI usage frequency was also associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to the Cognition Practice Gap and Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profiles. With the increasing implementation of big data and AI in healthcare, intelligent tools have received growing attention for their clinical reference value, and nurses may have more opportunities to encounter these tools in clinical work. In this study, 50.00% of nurses reported weekly or daily AI tool use. In a study of nursing students, Lan et al. [36] reported that frequent AI users had higher scores in AI cognition, data interpretation, and clinical application. However, without standardized guidance, frequent AI use may be accompanied by technology dependence or automation bias. Therefore, while promoting AI tools, clinical managers should also provide critical thinking training and AI ethics education to balance technology use with independent clinical judgment.

Attitude toward AI in nursing and self-efficacy

Nurses with more positive attitudes toward AI in nursing were more likely to be classified into the Cognition Practice Gap and Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profiles. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology suggests that technology adoption is influenced not only by individual attitudes but also by performance expectancy and effort expectancy [37]. Nurses with positive attitudes toward AI in nursing may be more likely to recognize its value in clinical decision support and workflow optimization and to show a stronger willingness to learn about AI. Self-efficacy was also associated with profile membership: higher self-efficacy was associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to the Cognition Practice Gap and Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profiles, which is consistent with previous research among undergraduate nursing students [38]. Social Cognitive Theory states that self-efficacy is related to confidence in handling complex tasks and persistence in learning [39]. Nurses with stronger self-efficacy may report greater confidence and willingness to try when facing new AI-related tasks, as well as greater engagement in learning and practice. Previous studies have also reported an association between attitude toward AI and self-efficacy [40]. However, how attitudes toward AI in nursing, self-efficacy, and AI literacy profiles change together over time remains to be examined. Based on the associations observed in this study, AI literacy development should not be limited to single-skill training. Instead, an integrated cognitive, emotional, and behavioral intervention approach may be useful. Nursing managers could use clinical case demonstrations and real-world application examples to support nurses’ recognition of AI value, while providing tiered training and long-term support to reduce learning barriers and concerns about use. Such support may provide conditions for nurses to gradually build digital operation confidence and support the more standardized integration of AI into clinical nursing practice.

Conclusions

This study identified three profiles of self-reported AI literacy among nurses in Anhui Province, China: the Ethical Awareness Deficit profile (33.20%), Cognition Practice Gap profile (53.80%), and Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile (13.00%). Educational level, key nursing position/department management role, years of work experience, AI training experience, AI usage frequency in the past 6 months, attitude toward AI in nursing, and self-efficacy were associated with profile membership. Scores on the Thriving at Work Scale also differed significantly across the three profiles. These findings may inform differentiated educational and managerial strategies to support nurses’ AI literacy and thriving at work in similar clinical contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study used convenience sampling and recruited nurses only from hospitals in Anhui Province, China. Although the sample included 1,000 nurses from 62 hospitals, selection bias may still exist, and the high proportion of female participants may limit generalizability. Anhui Province may also differ from other regions in per capita GDP, hospital digitalization, AI infrastructure, and nursing education resources. Therefore, the stability of the identified profiles and the transferability of the findings should be verified in multi-regional studies. Second, the profile labels were based on quantitative score patterns across the four AILS dimensions and should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of self-reported AI literacy rather than externally validated categories of nursing-specific AI competence. Self-reported AI literacy may not fully reflect actual AI-related ability and may be influenced by overestimation or social desirability. Although common method bias was assessed, residual bias cannot be completely excluded because all key variables were collected using self-report questionnaires. Future studies should incorporate objective indicators, such as AI knowledge tests, simulated clinical AI tasks, peer or supervisor assessments, and scenario-based evaluations. Third, the Comprehensive Literacy Advantage profile included 130 nurses, accounting for 13.00% of the sample. Parameter estimates involving this smaller subgroup may be less stable, especially when multiple predictors are examined simultaneously. Findings related to this profile should therefore be interpreted cautiously and verified in larger independent samples. Fourth, this study did not include organizational-level variables, such as hospital AI infrastructure, institutional support, department culture, leadership support, and the degree of AI integration into clinical workflows. Future studies should collect hospital- and department-level data and use multilevel models to examine how organizational context is related to nurses’ AI literacy profiles and work-related outcomes.
