Artificial intelligence in hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis and AI-assisted treatment support: Current landscape and future directions
Authors: Quan-Kai Lei, Tong-Xv Wu, Chu-Long Zhang, Yun-Wen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains challenging to diagnose and manage, particularly at an early stage when curative treatment is most feasible. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers tools for integrating histopathology, radiology, laboratory testing, demographics, and electronic health records, while also creating opportunities for clinician-supervised AI-assisted procedural support. Deep learning applied to digitized pathology slides can support tumor subtype classification, grading, and microvascular invasion assessment. AI-driven imaging methods may support liver and lesion segmentation, small-nodule detection in cirrhotic livers, and non-invasive risk stratification when combined with radiomics and clinical variables. Emerging foundation models, large language models (LLMs), and agentic approaches may help structure evidence, summarize longitudinal records, and support guideline-aligned decision workflows. In AI-assisted treatment-support settings, robotic surgery, endoscopic platforms, and image-guided interventions should be viewed as environments for AI-assisted planning, navigation, documentation, and quality control rather than autonomous treatment systems. Most evidence remains retrospective, single-center, and vulnerable to domain shift. Other limitations include LLM/agentic reliability failures, limited interpretability, privacy constraints, computational burden, incomplete workflow integration, workforce readiness gaps, and uncertain clinical benefit. Future progress will require diverse multimodal datasets, prospective multicenter validation, explainable and lightweight algorithms, standardized data alignment, rigorous governance, patient-centered workflow implementation, staff training, and careful separation of technical feasibility from clinically meaningful outcomes. Responsibly deployed AI may support diagnostic consistency, workflow efficiency, and human–AI collaboration in HCC care.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality among all cancers worldwide.1,2 Its development is mainly associated with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection and long-term liver injury, which lead to fibrosis and cirrhosis.3,4

Early and accurate diagnosis of HCC is essential for treatment. Conventional approaches—such as liver biopsy, serum biomarkers like alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and imaging techniques including ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—have notable limitations. Although biopsy remains an important diagnostic tool in oncology, it is not routinely used for HCC surveillance because of its invasiveness and potential complications. AFP exhibits limited sensitivity and specificity, while imaging methods are often challenged by cirrhosis, small or atypical lesions, and indistinct tumor margins, which complicate early detection and accurate characterization.5,6

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) provide complementary tools for HCC care across pathology, imaging, structured clinical data, and AI-assisted surgical robotics, endoscopic robotics, and minimally invasive interventional therapy. Deep learning on whole-slide histopathology may improve diagnostic consistency; AI-based radiology may enhance lesion detection, segmentation, and characterization; and clinical models may support non-invasive risk stratification.7, 8, 9, 10 Multimodal fusion can link these evidence streams to better capture tumor heterogeneity and progression.11 Emerging large language models (LLMs) and agentic frameworks extend this paradigm to evidence structuring, longitudinal summarization, retrieval-supported question answering, and guideline-aligned workflow support.12, 13, 14 In parallel, AI-assisted treatment support is increasingly discussed in surgical robotics, endoscopic robotics, and image-guided interventional therapy, where its realistic near-term role is clinician-supervised planning, navigation, documentation, and quality control rather than autonomous treatment.15, 16, 17

Clinical translation, however, remains constrained by cross-site robustness gaps, LLM reliability failures (e.g., hallucination and overconfidence), and structural risks in multi-step agentic pipelines, in addition to persistent barriers such as multimodal fusion, interpretability, computational cost, privacy, and fragmented large-scale datasets.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 For AI-assisted treatment-support technologies, an additional challenge is to distinguish technical feasibility from clinically meaningful benefit, including treatment completion, complications, recurrence, survival, and workflow efficiency.16,17

Existing reviews often emphasize either broad cross-cancer overviews or single HCC modalities, leaving fewer integrative analyses spanning pathology, radiology, clinical multimodal modeling, LLM-enabled systems, and AI-assisted surgical robotics, endoscopic robotics, and minimally invasive interventional therapy. This review addresses that gap by synthesizing progress across these domains, summarizing methodological and translational challenges, and outlining future directions for clinically responsible deployment. It also considers human-centered implementation, including patient acceptance, accountability, workflow readiness, and redistribution of clinical tasks. Looking ahead, multimodal AI may generate hypothesis-level insights into latent risk patterns and disease trajectories, while also assisting clinicians in decision-making. However, such findings require rigorous multicenter prospective validation before clinical adoption. AI-assisted pathological diagnosis of HCC

Histopathological examination remains an important modality for HCC diagnosis, particularly in cases with atypical imaging findings or in research settings requiring precise tissue characterization. It provides critical insights into cellular morphology, tumor grading, and histological subtype classification.23 In clinical practice, pathologists frequently evaluate specimens from patients with chronic liver diseases, such as fibrosis and cirrhosis. In these settings, the background liver tissue often exhibits regenerative nodules, fibrous septa, and architectural distortion. Within this context, the diagnosis of HCC depends on the recognition of malignant features including trabecular thickening, pseudoglandular architecture, and cytological atypia.5,24 Traditionally, these assessments rely on manual interpretation of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides, focusing on nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, and tumor growth patterns.24,25 However, manual evaluation is time-consuming, subjective, and prone to inter-observer variability.26

Recent advances in digital pathology have enabled the large-scale digitization of histological slides as whole-slide images (WSIs), creating a foundation for computational analysis. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models can process these high-resolution images to extract quantitative morphological patterns that are difficult to assess consistently by eye, thereby complementing conventional pathology assessment.27,28 In the HCC setting, AI-assisted pathological approaches are often organized according to clinical objectives across the disease continuum, from precancerous risk stratification in chronic liver disease to biological characterization of established tumors and their microenvironment.

Across this continuum, AI-based pathology can be grouped into two task families: (i) precursor risk assessment in fibrosis/cirrhosis, and (ii) tumor characterization, including subtype classification, grading, invasion status, and tumor microenvironment (TME) profiling.29,30 Accordingly, this section focuses on models for early risk stratification and pathological characterization of tumor progression and the TME.28,31

Early detection, risk stratification, and lesion-level categorization

WSIs enable large-scale digitization of tissue sections and provide a practical foundation for AI-assisted pathology.27 Compared with routine qualitative review, DL models trained on WSIs can capture subtle risk-relevant histomorphologic patterns that are difficult to assess consistently by visual inspection alone, thereby supporting prediction of future HCC development in chronic liver disease settings.28

Patient-level risk prediction

In chronic liver disease and early hepatocarcinogenesis research, patient-level prognostic modeling aims to estimate future HCC risk from baseline biopsies obtained before overt malignancy develops. HCC commonly arises in a background of chronic injury, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, where precursor lesions along the dysplastic continuum, such as high-grade dysplastic nodules, as well as early HCC may be observed.29 Translating these early morphological signals into patient-level risk estimates is therefore of substantial clinical interest.

For example, Nakatsuka et al.28 developed a model based on H&E-stained biopsy WSIs to predict 7-year HCC risk in patients with steatotic liver disease, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.80. The model identified high-risk individuals even at relatively mild fibrosis stages, suggesting that computational pathology may capture prognostic signals beyond fibrosis burden alone. Although the interpretability findings remain exploratory and longitudinal WSI-to-outcome studies are still limited, this study illustrates the potential of such AI-based approaches for early risk assessment.

Lesion-level categorization

Building on patient-level risk prediction, lesion-level categorization shifts the question from who may develop HCC to what a sampled nodule represents at biopsy. It focuses on diagnostically difficult distinctions, including high-grade dysplastic nodules (HGDNs) versus early HCC, hepatocellular adenoma versus well-differentiated HCC, and triage of borderline lesions. In practice, such categorization may affect downstream management, including intensified surveillance, repeat biopsy, local treatment, or follow-up.

Cheng et al.32 proposed a hybrid neural attention model (HnAIM) that integrated multiple convolutional neural networks and achieved an AUC of 0.935 for classifying hepatocellular nodular lesions in their evaluation setting. Xia et al.33 developed an interpretable ML framework based on quantitative nuclear morphology features to distinguish normal liver, hepatocellular adenoma, and well-differentiated HCC with high diagnostic performance. Together, these studies illustrate complementary strategies for lesion triage: high-capacity DL models that maximize discrimination and feature-based models that offer greater interpretability. Clinically, more reliable lesion-level categorization may help reduce equivocal diagnoses and better align pathological interpretation with surveillance and treatment pathways, although broader external validation remains necessary.

For these lesion-level tasks, immunohistochemistry (IHC) biomarkers are best framed as complementary quantitative cues for differentiating early HCC from dysplastic or benign hepatocellular lesions, resolving equivocal H&E morphology, and supporting lesion triage rather than as standalone AI classifiers. Established markers, including glypican-3 (GPC3), heat-shock protein 70 (HSP70), glutamine synthetase (GS), and Arginase-1, may augment H&E-based WSI models when digitized as staining-intensity or spatial features.34,35 In this context, Zaidi et al.’s36 AI-assisted quantification of transforming growth factor beta receptor 1/2 (TGFBR1/TGFBR2) expression illustrates that quantitative IHC biomarkers can be converted into AI-compatible computational features, with future potential to support lesion-level categorization when combined with established diagnostic biomarkers and morphology. Although this example should not currently be regarded as a patient-level carcinogenesis-risk prediction tool or as a clinically validated standalone classifier for distinguishing HGDN, early HCC, and hepatocellular adenoma, future integration of quantitative IHC features with established biomarkers and H&E morphology may strengthen AI-assisted lesion-level categorization.36

AI-assisted characterization of established HCC

Building on Section 2.1, once HCC has been identified, the pathological objective shifts from early detection and risk assessment to biological characterization of established tumors, where differentiation status, invasive potential, and microenvironmental context jointly inform prognosis and management in HCC.30,37 Because conventional assessment is affected by inter-observer variability and limited sensitivity to subtle, spatially heterogeneous patterns, AI-enabled computational pathology is increasingly being used to support more objective and reproducible characterization across the tumor progression continuum and within the TME.38,39

Tumor differentiation and grading

A core stage-associated pathological readout is Edmondson-Steiner (E-S) grade, which reflects differentiation from well-differentiated to poorly differentiated states and is associated with tumor aggressiveness and clinical decision-making.40 Deshpande et al.41 developed a hybrid DL framework based on H&E sections, incorporating color normalization, data augmentation, and pretrained convolutional neural network (CNN) backbones, and reported strong performance for E-S grading across cohorts. Feature-based nuclear morphometry has also been used for HCC classification and prognostic modeling, providing a more interpretable complement to end-to-end DL.42 While these findings are promising, they require cautious interpretation and robust multi-center validation given model complexity, potential domain shift, and the risk of overfitting.

Invasive phenotypes and tumor microenvironment

Beyond tumor grade, AI has also been applied to invasion-related phenotypes and microenvironmental signals that are closely associated with progression and prognosis in HCC.43,44 As an AI-analyzable immunostaining modality, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) enables simultaneous quantification of immune-cell distribution, marker expression, cell-cell spatial interactions, and tumor microenvironment architecture. Using this modality, Dievernich et al.45 developed a DL score for identifying HCC regions while incorporating microenvironmental information, representing a step toward spatially informed TME assessment beyond tumor-only morphology. A related AI-ready restained multiplex IHC/mIF dataset in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma illustrates how co-registered staining resources can support more reproducible immune-cell annotation and virtual staining workflows, and these strategies may be transferable to HCC TME assessment, although HCC-specific validation is still required.46 In parallel, Zhang et al.’s31 MVI-AIDM pipeline extracted microvascular invasion (MVI)-related patterns from H&E WSIs with high accuracy, suggesting that computational pathology may support more consistent recognition of high-risk invasive features that are variably reported in routine practice. IHC markers such as Ki-67 and cytokeratin 19 (CK19) are already well established in pathological assessment of proliferative activity and aggressive tumor biology, although they do not directly demonstrate improved histologic grading by AI. Future models may use these mature biological readouts as quantitative pathology anchors; integrating them with WSI morphology, immunostaining, and imaging-derived features could support AI-based characterization of tumor aggressiveness and multimodal risk stratification.35,47

Despite these advances, several barriers remain, including limited and imbalanced representation of less common histologic subtypes, such as steatohepatitic, macrotrabecular-massive, scirrhous, and fibrolamellar HCC, insufficient interpretability for high-stakes clinical deployment, and the risk of inappropriate reliance on model outputs without rigorous governance, calibration, and external validation.48,49 Future work should prioritize prospective multi-center evaluation, reliability reporting, including calibration and uncertainty estimation, and multimodal integration to link histology-derived grade, invasion, and TME signatures to clinically actionable endpoints such as recurrence, survival, and therapy response.28,32

AI-assisted imaging diagnosis of HCC

The preceding section reviewed AI applications in HCC pathological diagnosis. While histopathology provides high-resolution cellular and tissue-level information, its invasive nature limits its use in early screening and longitudinal surveillance.50,51 Imaging modalities, by contrast, are noninvasive, repeatable, and form the backbone of routine HCC detection and follow-up. Importantly, these two evidence streams are complementary: pathology clarifies microscopic phenotype and invasion biology, whereas imaging captures whole-liver spatial distribution and temporal evolution for surveillance and treatment planning. However, AI-based imaging analysis remains challenged by the liver's anatomical complexity, limited tissue contrast, and dependence on multi-phase acquisition, which impair reliable lesion localization, characterization, and temporal consistency.52, 53, 54 These limitations are especially consequential in atypical or clinically ambiguous HCC scenarios, including subcentimeter lesions and other nodules less than 2 cm in cirrhotic backgrounds and focal lesions with normal AFP levels.55, 56, 57, 58

From a clinical workflow perspective, effective AI-assisted imaging must support three distinct diagnostic functions. First, imaging features can stratify at-risk patients before a definite tumor is identified. Second, segmentation and detection models localize the liver and suspicious lesions for quantification, follow-up, and treatment planning. Third, after lesion detection, multiphasic CT (MPCT) and hepatobiliary MRI may help predict tumor biological behavior through vascular enhancement patterns and hepatocyte-function-related imaging signals.59, 60, 61 Accordingly, this section reviews representative DL models addressing these three components, with emphasis on clinical relevance, robustness, and translational feasibility.

Early risk identification and prediction

The rising burden of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), including fatty liver and fibrosis, has expanded the population entering HCC surveillance.3,62 In this setting, fibrosis and cirrhosis mark a high-risk surveillance substrate; imaging AI should therefore stratify risk in these patients rather than treat fibrosis itself as the endpoint.55,62,63

Along this line, Fan et al.63 introduced the age-male-albumin-bilirubin (ALBI)-platelet-CT (aMAP-CT) model for HCC risk stratification in cirrhosis patients. The model did not aim to diagnose fibrosis itself; instead, it integrated liver and spleen CT signatures with the aMAP clinical score to capture risk-relevant correlates such as cirrhotic morphology, portal hypertension, and systemic inflammatory changes. It achieved an AUC of up to 0.869 and separated high-vs. low-risk groups with 3-year HCC incidence of 26.3% vs. 1.7% across multicenter validation cohorts.63 CT acquisition variability still raises radiomic stability concerns, supporting harmonization methods such as ComBat.

Atypical cases, including AFP-negative HCC and small, hypovascular, or LI-RADS-indeterminate cirrhotic nodules, often require imaging-based longitudinal risk estimation beyond biomarker-based triage alone.55, 56, 57, 58 In high-risk patients with focal liver lesions, Zhang et al.58 showed that a B-mode US DL model could help identify AFP-negative HCC, suggesting that AI-assisted imaging may complement biomarker-based surveillance and guide closer follow-up or multiphasic imaging. As a complementary MRI-based pathway, Li et al.64 developed an explainable DL model using gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI (EOB-MRI) that achieved strong external diagnostic performance and improved reader sensitivity, underscoring the value of multiparametric MRI-based AI when biomarker or US findings are insufficient.

More broadly, robust imaging-only risk models remain challenging because early progression can be subtle and nonspecific on a single modality. Multimodal integration—combining imaging with longitudinal laboratory results and clinical risk factors—may support model-level accuracy but also increases operational complexity and demands rigorous prospective validation to demonstrate clinical utility.65,66

Future work should prioritize: (i) longitudinal, multi-center cohorts; (ii) harmonization and calibration to support dependable risk estimates; and (iii) prospective evaluation embedded in surveillance programs rather than retrospective performance alone. Once risk-oriented imaging models identify patients or nodules requiring closer surveillance, the next task is precise lesion localization and boundary delineation for measurement, follow-up, and treatment planning.

Precise liver tumor segmentation and sensitive detection of small lesions

Accurate liver and lesion segmentation supports tumor quantification, surgical and radiotherapy planning, and longitudinal treatment monitoring.53,67 However, reliable boundary delineation remains difficult because HCC often occurs in heterogeneous fibrotic or steatotic parenchyma, has irregular or infiltrative margins, and may show limited contrast from adjacent organs and background liver.54 Scanner, protocol, and reconstruction variability further reduce cross-site transportability, echoing broader multicenter radiomics reproducibility concerns.68

To address these segmentation challenges, most contemporary models build on U-Net-style encoder-decoder frameworks with stronger multi-scale and contextual feature learning. DEMF-Net uses dilated convolutions and multi-scale fusion for low-contrast CT segmentation,54 attention-based variants such as CC-DenseUNet target tumor boundary delineation,69 and 3D or multi-organ frameworks can leverage volumetric context but remain more computationally demanding and sensitive to protocol shift.68,70

Small-lesion detection is a distinct clinical bottleneck. Lesions below 2 cm are often obscured by heterogeneous cirrhotic parenchyma, have low lesion-to-liver contrast, and may lack typical early-stage imaging features.71 Public benchmarks such as LiTS include heterogeneous tumor sizes, but aggregate metrics can mask poor performance on small lesions.72 This is particularly relevant for subcentimeter and other small cirrhotic nodules, where regenerative or dysplastic nodules may mimic early HCC and remain indeterminate.55, 56, 57

To address these challenges, models increasingly combine local texture preservation with global context. Zheng et al.73 developed a pattern-matching and DL MRI pipeline for automatic detection of small HCC measuring 2 cm or less in cirrhotic liver; in the external test cohort, the model achieved 89.74% per-lesion sensitivity and a Dice coefficient of 0.77 ± 0.16, illustrating a targeted AI strategy for this clinical bottleneck.73 U-Net variants with residual pathways, multi-scale extraction, and attention modules have also shown model-level performance for segmentation of small or indistinct lesions.74,75 CAFCT-Net further integrates contextual aggregation, attention gating, and CNN-Transformer fusion to preserve weak lesion signals and sharpen boundaries on LiTS.75 Collectively, these approaches suggest that coupling detailed texture learning with global contextual awareness is critical for reliable small-lesion detection and boundary modeling.

Despite these advances, small-lesion sensitivity can increase computational cost and inference latency, while multicenter domain shift remains a major barrier.68 Parameter-efficient strategies such as prompt-based tuning may support adaptation, but HCC-specific evidence remains limited.76 Thus, clinical utility should be evaluated not only by average Dice scores, but also by lesion-size-stratified sensitivity, false-negative rates, stability across cirrhotic backgrounds, and integration into LI-RADS-oriented workflows.57

Dynamic and functional imaging integration: from lesion to biological characterization

After lesion localization, imaging analysis should move from detection to characterization. MPCT and hepatobiliary MRI provide complementary biological phenotypes: MPCT captures vascular remodeling, whereas EOB-MRI captures hepatocyte-specific function; AI can integrate these signals to support lesion characterization and assessment of biological aggressiveness.

Vascular phenotype in multi-phase CT images

The clinical need in MPCT is not only to confirm lesion presence, but also to quantify vascular phenotypes that reflect tumor biology. During hepatocarcinogenesis, arterial neovascularization and reduced portal venous supply produce arterial phase hyperenhancement and portal venous or delayed phase washout, which are core LI-RADS features.57 Phase-wise enhancement, peritumoral signals, and texture heterogeneity may therefore provide non-invasive clues to angiogenesis and invasive behavior such as MVI.43,53,60

The main challenge is that these vascular cues depend on accurate cross-phase correspondence; respiratory motion, patient repositioning, variable injection timing, and fuzzy lesion boundaries can blur enhancement trajectories and weaken feature extraction.77,78 Registration methods such as Mok et al.’s79 modality-agnostic structural representation learning help establish accurate cross-phase anatomical correspondence, providing a foundation for subsequent feature fusion and enhancement-pattern analysis. More directly, Xia et al.80 extracted tumor and peritumor radiomics from registration-aligned or subtraction-based multiphase CT images to predict MVI; their hybrid model achieved AUCs of 0.86 and 0.84 in internal and external test sets and linked imaging-derived scores to survival stratification and MVI-associated gene-expression differences. This supports the value of well-aligned multiphase CT for AI-assisted biological characterization, although prospective validation for recurrence and treatment-response prediction remains necessary.

CT-invisible functional and aggressive phenotypes in hepatobiliary MRI

Unlike multiphase CT, hepatobiliary MRI provides functional information on hepatocyte activity and tumor heterogeneity. Hepatobiliary-phase signal reflects organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP)-mediated contrast uptake, and Kitao et al.81 linked hepatobiliary-phase intensity with transporter expression and histopathologic phenotype, supporting EOB-MRI as a biological imaging biomarker. Future HCC AI models may use such functional MRI features to predict aggressive biology, recurrence, and treatment response.

Beyond functional characterization, hepatobiliary MRI-based AI may help evaluate aggressive tumor phenotypes that are incompletely characterized on CT. Existing imaging studies provide candidate imaging biomarkers rather than direct evidence of AI performance. Wu et al.61 described characteristic CT/MRI findings of HCC with bile duct tumor thrombus (BDTT), including HCC lesions and localized intrahepatic bile duct dilation that may facilitate preoperative recognition of biliary invasion. As an AI-specific proof of concept, Liu et al.82 developed a CT-based Faster R–CNN (region-based convolutional neural network) pipeline to identify dilated bile ducts and diagnose BDTT, achieving a patient-level AUC of 0.94 in a small matched cohort of 32 HCC patients. This case supports the feasibility of BDTT-oriented AI, but hepatobiliary MRI-based AI applications remain prospective and require dedicated development, external validation, and outcome-linked testing.

Beyond imaging-visible invasive phenotypes, EOB-MRI may also support noninvasive assessment of molecularly aggressive HCC subtypes. CK19-positive HCC is associated with poor differentiation, invasive growth, early recurrence, treatment resistance, and unfavorable prognosis.83 Consistent with this biology, radiomics and DL radiomics studies using EOB-MRI, including hepatobiliary-phase features, have reported preoperative prediction of CK19 status.84, 85, 86, 87 These findings suggest a potential bridge between functional MRI biomarkers and precision oncology, although most evidence remains retrospective and requires standardized acquisition, external validation, calibration, and outcome-linked testing before clinical translation. Despite advances in AI-based imaging for HCC, clinical translation remains constrained by computational burden, retrospective evidence, and protocol heterogeneity. State-of-the-art models such as DEMF-Net, PVTFormer, and CAFCT-Net can be computationally intensive, limiting real-time deployment.54,75,88 Broader external validation across heterogeneous imaging protocols is still needed to establish reliable generalizability.65,66,68 Small lesions (<2 cm) and tumors with fuzzy boundaries remain difficult, and Transformer-based methods may improve feature learning at the cost of added architectural complexity.75,88 These bottlenecks motivate a shift from modality-specific imaging models to integrated reasoning with longitudinal clinical and laboratory context, which is the focus of the next section.

The evolution and challenges of AI in HCC diagnosis: leveraging clinical data and multimodal fusion

In clinical practice, early HCC suspicion often begins with risk assessment based on patient history, symptoms, and routine laboratory findings, before definitive pathological or imaging confirmation. AI models operating on structured clinical data may support early risk stratification and triage, with potential to help identify high-risk individuals and inform longitudinal follow-up. However, these benefits depend on setting-specific calibration, data quality, and external validation.

This section reviews the evolution of such models—from traditional ML to DL and multimodal frameworks—and emphasizes translational limitations that motivate complementary workflow-oriented approaches. Fig. 1 illustrates the multimodal HCC diagnostic workflow, including clinical history, laboratory testing, imaging, pathology, and AI-based integration.

AI-assisted diagnosis based on laboratory biomarkers and clinical history

In routine clinical practice, evaluation of patients at risk for HCC is usually initiated through surveillance context and baseline clinical/laboratory assessment rather than immediate biopsy. Contemporary guidance recommends risk-based surveillance (e.g., US with or without AFP) and stepwise diagnostic confirmation using multiphasic CT or MRI when indicated.55,56 Because early-stage HCC is frequently asymptomatic, symptoms and routine clinical impressions alone are insufficient for reliable early diagnosis.55,56 Consequently, clinicians integrate laboratory biomarkers with imaging findings to improve diagnostic confidence and management decisions.55,56

Clinical and laboratory variables documented in routine care (e.g., liver function tests, platelet counts, AFP, and comorbidity history) are pragmatic inputs for AI-based risk modeling; recent HCC AI baselines operationalize such variables in retrospective tabular pipelines.89 Although these signals are individually limited by disease specificity, they can be complementary when modeled jointly. From an AI perspective, models that infer disease risk from clinical history and laboratory profiles are therefore particularly attractive. On one hand, such models may help mitigate inter-physician variability in interpreting biochemical parameters and clinical histories, which often depends on individual experience, diagnostic thresholds, and subjective judgment.90,91 On the other hand, they may capture complex patterns associated with MASLD-related HCC—an increasingly prevalent subtype that frequently arises in the absence of cirrhosis and is challenging to identify using conventional clinical heuristics alone.62

Together, these capabilities suggest that AI-based clinical models could help prioritize high-risk individuals for further evaluation and improve the efficiency of diagnostic workflows. Nevertheless, given the biological complexity of liver disease and the high stakes of oncologic decision-making, these systems should be treated as decision-support tools rather than standalone diagnostic determinants.

From traditional ML to DL: single-modality AI models for HCC risk prediction

Early AI applications in HCC risk prediction relied primarily on conventional ML methods, including logistic regression, random forest (RF), and support vector machines (SVM), applied to structured biochemical and clinical data.92,93 These approaches align naturally with routine clinical inputs, such as laboratory panels and baseline patient characteristics, and depend on handcrafted features informed by clinical expertise. As a result, they perform adequately on small, tabular datasets and are computationally efficient.

Among these methods, RF is comparatively robust due to bootstrap aggregation and random feature sampling, which reduce variance and mitigate overfitting, rendering it relatively tolerant to noise and missing values common in routine laboratory data. In a cohort of 550 patients, an RF model based on six standard biomarkers achieved AUCs of 0.969 in training and 0.858 in validation, outperforming traditional regression models and the ASAP framework.93 Because this evidence remains retrospective and internally validated, these models are best viewed as candidates for preliminary risk screening that require prospective and external validation before broad deployment.

However, the clinical applicability of traditional models remains constrained by their limited representational capacity. These methods primarily capture linear or low-order interactions and rely on static feature definitions, making them ill-suited to model the hierarchical, nonlinear, and temporally evolving processes underlying HCC development. As clinical datasets grow in scale and heterogeneity, these limitations become increasingly pronounced, motivating a transition toward DL-based approaches.92,94

DL for structured clinical data: longitudinal representation learning and risk stratification

The adoption of DL in HCC risk prediction reflects the increasing scale, heterogeneity, and incompleteness of real-world clinical datasets. Unlike traditional ML pipelines, DL architectures reduce reliance on manual feature engineering by learning multi-level representations directly from raw or minimally processed inputs.9,10,95 For structured electronic health record (EHR) and laboratory data, this shift is most clinically useful when models move beyond isolated baseline variables toward representations that can capture longitudinal, multifeatured patient trajectories over time.94,96

Variational autoencoder (VAE) models, for example, are commonly used for latent representation learning on heterogeneous tabular data and may support downstream handling of missingness, but this should be viewed as a methodological capability rather than direct HCC-specific clinical proof.97 Singh et al.9 demonstrated that VAE-based modeling of routine blood tests, including complete blood count (CBC) and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP), enabled cancer risk stratification across multiple malignancies, achieving AUCs of approximately 0.76 for colorectal cancer, 0.85 for liver cancer, and 0.78 for lung cancer. This is relevant in routine care, where records are often fragmented and incomplete, and where HCC risk unfolds across years of chronic liver disease progression.

Beyond cross-sectional representation learning, longitudinal clinical information is particularly important because HCC usually develops gradually during chronic liver disease. Clinically, serial AFP, protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II; also known as des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin, DCP), platelet count, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST)/gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) may track tumor signal, hepatic reserve, liver injury, and fibrosis burden over time.55,98, 99, 100 Complementing this biomarker rationale, Ren et al.101 validated combined AFP, lens culinaris agglutinin-reactive AFP fraction (AFP-L3), and PIVKA-II testing in Chinese patients and developed an age-AFP-AFP-L3-PIVKA-II (AALP) diagnostic model, supporting the clinical relevance of these serum markers without establishing a slope-based longitudinal prediction framework. In MASLD, Li et al.102 developed DL strategies using time-varying EHR covariates from sequential records, although AFP/PIVKA-II/LSM slopes were not specifically modeled. Dynamic HCV prediction models have demonstrated that incorporating longitudinal laboratory trajectories improves discrimination compared with baseline-only Cox models.103 Consistent with this concept, the SMART-HCC model identified baseline LSM as its most influential predictor, and the authors acknowledged that future incorporation of longitudinal measurements could further enhance HCC risk prediction, suggesting serial LSM as a promising dynamic biomarker.100 Collectively, future DL models may integrate longitudinal biomarkers, repeated LSM assessments, and sequential EHR records to identify elevated HCC risk earlier, but prospective validation remains required.

Specialized DL architectures have also been introduced to further enhance modeling capacity. TabNet incorporates attention-based feature selection to prioritize clinically relevant variables while retaining partial interpretability.104 Transformer-based models extend this framework by capturing long-range dependencies in longitudinal EHR sequences.96 More recently, Sun et al.95 reported CATCH-FM, a foundation-model approach pre-trained on large-scale longitudinal EHR code sequences to identify high-risk patients from historical medical records. In the preprint evaluation, the model achieved approximately 50% sensitivity at a 99% specificity operating point for broader cancer pre-screening tasks.95 These results are encouraging but should be interpreted cautiously, because they are preprint findings and may not directly translate to HCC-specific diagnosis without peer-reviewed external and prospective validation.

Multimodal AI in HCC: from data fusion to workflow-level limitations

Despite these advances, models based solely on structured non-imaging data face persistent translational limitations. Most studies remain retrospective and single-center, limiting generalizability.105 DL models are sensitive to label noise and inconsistent coding, which are prevalent in EHR systems.106 Moreover, although approaches such as TabNet and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-enhanced LightGBM partially address interpretability, most DL models remain black boxes, constraining clinical trust in high-stakes applications such as early HCC surveillance.107 Modeling longitudinal disease trajectories also remains challenging, as many approaches treat clinical data as static snapshots despite the dynamic evolution of HCC involving liver function, metabolic status, and comorbidities.108

These constraints have driven increasing interest in multimodal AI frameworks that integrate complementary data sources. Although imaging-based AI models have demonstrated strong performance in HCC detection, clinical decision-making depends on the joint interpretation of imaging, pathology, laboratory biomarkers (e.g., AFP), and longitudinal EHR data. Integrating these heterogeneous inputs within a unified framework is therefore potentially valuable for improving diagnostic reliability and translational relevance, but requires robust data harmonization and workflow-level validation.11,109

Multimodal integration has been shown to improve predictive performance across selected cohorts and tasks. In liver tumor detection, the AUCs of conventional US, contrast-enhanced US, and shear wave elastography were 0.760, 0.897, and 0.829, respectively, whereas multimodal integration increased AUC to 0.968.110 Similarly, Hu et al.60 developed a clinical-radiomics model that combined clinical variables with multiphase MRI-derived tumor/peritumoral features to predict MVI in 501 HCC patients, achieving an AUC of 0.852 in the testing cohort. Newer CT fusion and distillation strategies, including LIDIA and lightweight teacher-student segmentation models, further illustrate efforts to combine multiphase information with deployment efficiency.111, 112, 113 However, multimodal modeling introduces additional challenges, including heterogeneous data representations, modality-specific noise, missing channels, and temporal or spatial misalignment.110 Existing fusion and model-efficiency strategies can improve task-level performance and computational feasibility, but integrating multimodal evidence into longitudinal, guideline-driven clinical workflows remains difficult and requires prospective multicenter evaluation.11,109,114

Taken together, these limitations suggest that even advanced multimodal AI systems remain largely task-centric. This gap between model-level performance and workflow-level clinical reasoning motivates the exploration of LLMs and agentic AI as complementary frameworks for integrating evidence, guidelines, and decision logic in HCC care, which is the focus of the following section.

LLMs and agentic AI for HCC diagnosis and clinical decision support

Building upon previous sections, this section shifts from modality-specific prediction to language-and workflow-oriented AI for HCC care. Conventional ML pipelines usually address well-defined tasks (e.g., classification, segmentation, and risk prediction) from structured inputs, whereas real-world HCC diagnosis and management are longitudinal, guideline-dependent, and documentation-heavy.89,115,116 Clinicians must synthesize laboratory values, imaging reports, pathology narratives, prior treatments, complications, and evolving guideline logic while producing auditable decisions. LLM systems, especially when paired with retrieval augmentation and tool use, can reduce this “information friction” by supporting guideline-linked question answering, narrative information extraction, and longitudinal case summarization with explicit provenance.12,117,118 Agentic AI extends this role from isolated responses to supervised workflow execution, while AI-assisted knowledge discovery remains exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Importantly, the near-term goal is human-in-the-loop decision support rather than autonomous diagnosis.

Evidence-grounded LLM task mappings in the HCC diagnostic chain

A central limitation of standalone LLMs in medicine is that their knowledge is encoded in model parameters, which can be incomplete, outdated, or expressed with unwarranted confidence.119 Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mitigates this limitation by conditioning outputs on external evidence such as guideline passages, systematic reviews, and institutional protocols.120,121 In HCC, benchmark studies have reported incomplete cirrhosis/HCC answers, weaker performance for diagnosis or preventive medicine, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC)-stage errors, and low concordance of LLM-generated treatment recommendations with physician decisions.122, 123, 124 Therefore, HCC-facing LLM outputs should expose the guideline or imaging basis for each answer—for example, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD)/European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) recommendation or LI-RADS feature used—and flag missing or discordant essentials such as imaging phases, AFP trends, liver-function status, or unsupported claims.55,56,125, 126, 127 The value of LLMs in HCC is best defined at the task level. We emphasize three text-centric task families aligned with routine hepatology and oncology workflows.

Guideline- and literature-aligned question answering

When clinicians face LI-RADS-indeterminate nodules or suspected AFP-negative HCC, retrieval-enabled LLMs could help identify whether continued surveillance, repeat multiphasic CT/MRI, diagnostic review, or tumor-board discussion is supported by current HCC guidance.55, 56, 57, 58 Benchmark or question-answering performance alone is insufficient for clinical readiness; HCC modules should retrieve concrete sources such as AASLD/EASL guidance, LI-RADS criteria, and BCLC treatment-staging recommendations, and return citation-linked outputs for physician verification.12,13,55, 56, 57

Information extraction and structuring from narratives

Many HCC-relevant signals are embedded in free-text reports, including lesion descriptors, enhancement patterns, capsule invasion, and background cirrhosis. LLMs can transform finalized clinician-authored narratives into structured templates by extracting findings, normalizing terminology, and flagging missing fields.128,129 In HCC workflows, these fields can support audit of lesion size, number, enhancement pattern, vascular invasion, AFP status, and cirrhotic background for LI-RADS/BCLC-oriented staging, tumor-board preparation, follow-up planning, and treatment-eligibility review.55, 56, 57

Longitudinal patient summarization and case tracking

HCC care is longitudinal, requiring synthesis of evolving liver function, surveillance imaging, treatments, and complications across time.55 LLMs can generate timeline- or problem-oriented summaries linked to source dates and report locations, and can surface open questions such as missing AFP values or insufficient imaging phase information.130 Because summarization systems may omit or overemphasize details, performance should be assessed with clinician-led frameworks such as QUEST.131

Agentic AI: workflow-executable decision support for HCC

Agentic AI refers to LLM-centered systems augmented with planning, tool invocation, and state tracking, so that they can execute multi-step workflows rather than produce isolated text.14,132 An “agent” is primarily an orchestration layer: it constrains model behavior to approved actions such as retrieving records, calling calculators or rule engines, populating structured drafts, and logging intermediate states under governance constraints. For HCC, this orchestration should be anchored to the actual care pathway—surveillance recall, diagnostic imaging adequacy, LI-RADS-oriented lesion review, liver-reserve assessment, BCLC-linked treatment preparation, and post-treatment follow-up—rather than to generic medical chatbot tasks.55, 56, 57

For HCC diagnosis, the core value of agentic systems is not autonomous interpretation, but operationalization of sequential guideline logic across fragmented data streams. Clinical work-up is conditional: interpretation depends on imaging adequacy, lesion evolution, liver reserve, biomarkers, and comorbidity context.56 Real-world studies also show that guideline adherence can vary, creating opportunities for omission and inconsistency.133,134 Agentic pipelines can reduce these gaps by coupling reasoning to executable steps: flagging overdue US-AFP surveillance or poor-visualization US, retrieving prior CT/MRI and AFP/PIVKA-II trajectories, extracting lesion size, number, arterial enhancement, washout, capsule, threshold growth, and tumor-in-vein from finalized reports, and checking whether liver reserve and performance status are documented before tumor-board review.55, 56, 57,101 These outputs should remain traceable decision-support artifacts for clinician review.55,57,135, 136, 137

tasks.120,138,139 HCC-specific multimodal LLM-agent frameworks have also been proposed, supporting the feasibility of integrated workflows across imaging, pathology, and clinical data.140 Accordingly, Potential role of AI in future diagnostic knowledge discovery

Beyond improving performance within predefined clinical tasks, AI may also contribute to knowledge discovery from large-scale multimodal datasets that integrate imaging, pathology, clinical variables, and molecular information. Recent multimodal foundation-model work in oncology shows that jointly modeling pathology images with report-level and molecular signals can improve representation quality and downstream prediction, thereby providing a technical basis for discovery-oriented analysis.145 In parallel, clustering- and representation-learning studies in other diseases have identified clinically meaningful latent strata, including tumor-immune microenvironment subtypes in lung adenocarcinoma, Parkinson's disease severity subtypes from multimodal sensing, and EHR-derived progression trajectories from patient embeddings across complex conditions.146, 147, 148 For HCC, the analogous opportunity is to search across serial US/CT/MRI, AFP/AFP-L3/PIVKA-II trends, cirrhosis etiology, liver stiffness, WSI morphology, and recurrence outcomes for latent trajectories such as biomarker-rising but imaging-negative disease, poor-visualization surveillance failure, or steatotic-liver morphology with high-risk pathology patterns.28,55,101,102

At present, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory, hypothesis-generating outputs rather than definitive disease classes. Still, combining HCC biology studies with AI representation learning could generate testable bridges between hepatobiliary-phase MRI, peritumoral radiomics, WSI invasion patterns, CK19/MVI risk, BDTT, and early recurrence, potentially refining which indeterminate lesions warrant biopsy, closer follow-up, or more aggressive treatment planning.59, 60, 61,84,86 Their clinical relevance depends on reproducibility across cohorts, interpretability, and prospective validation against meaningful patient outcomes. Recent multisite implementation evidence also indicates that model performance and clinical impact can vary across institutions, underscoring the need for rigorous external validation before routine deployment.149 If such criteria are met, AI-assisted discovery could support iterative refinement of diagnostic frameworks and enable more precise stratification for surveillance, treatment selection, and longitudinal management.

AI-assisted treatment support technologies for HCC

In AI-assisted HCC treatment workflows, AI should be framed as support embedded within a clinical procedure, not as a standalone therapy.16,17 Surgical, endoscopic, and interventional platforms can generate procedural data for AI-assisted planning, navigation, documentation, and quality control, but platform performance, AI decision support, and autonomy require separate validation.15, 16, 17

AI-assisted surgical robotics

Robotic liver surgery offers three-dimensional magnified visualization, a stable camera platform, motion scaling, tremor filtration, and wristed or articulated instruments, which may support surgeon ergonomics and maneuverability during selected hepatectomies.150,151 These platform advantages are particularly relevant when tumors are centrally located or close to the hepatic hilum, major hepatic veins, the inferior vena cava, or portal pedicles.152 Contemporary HCC-focused reviews similarly frame robotic resection as a selected surgical approach for technically demanding cases, including complex anatomical resections, and operations in cirrhotic livers with distorted anatomy.153 Current evidence therefore supports robotic resection as a selected surgical approach in HCC, but this should not be conflated with validated AI-assisted treatment support; AI-enabled intraoperative functions remain early, assistive, and insufficiently validated as independent outcome-improving or autonomous systems.15,153,154

The robotic operating environment is nevertheless well positioned for AI integration because it can produce synchronized operative video, instrument-motion or kinematic signals, device events, and workflow-level procedural labels.155 When linked with preoperative CT/MRI, intraoperative navigation, and postoperative outcomes, these data streams can support model development for procedural phase recognition, anatomy localization, and auditable quality assessment.16 In practical terms, near-term applications include liver, tumor, and vascular segmentation for operative planning, anatomy and phase recognition during the case, and margin- or safety-zone prompts during parenchymal transection.16 Image-to-patient registration and augmented-reality overlays have been explored to project preoperative tumor and vascular anatomy into the operative field, which may help surgeons maintain spatial orientation around major vessels when the liver is mobilized or deformed.156 AI-enhanced navigation for early identification of the inferior vena cava and major hepatic-vein roots may further help highlight critical vascular landmarks during robotic hepatectomy, where loss of these landmarks can complicate dissection and margin planning.157

These applications may be most useful in high-risk HCC, but they are also where validation is most difficult.152,153 Liver deformation, bleeding, cirrhotic parenchymal distortion, complex inflow/outflow anatomy, and strict oncologic margin requirements can degrade registration accuracy, obscure visual cues, and reduce the reliability of AI-generated guidance.152,156 For HCC, validation should therefore distinguish platform performance metrics from AI-derived guidance and any steps approaching autonomy, with emphasis on surgeon-supervised use in carefully selected complex resections.15, 16, 17

AI-assisted endoscopic robotics

For patients with HCC, relevant endoscopic scenarios include portal-hypertension-related variceal bleeding, for which endoscopic management is central to portal-hypertension care.158 Other relevant scenarios include biliary strictures after liver transplantation or locoregional therapy, for which endoscopic management is frequently required.159,160 In patients with HCC and underlying cirrhosis, endoscopic assessment may contribute to portal-hypertension/variceal management and, indirectly, to transplant evaluation within guideline-directed care.55,56

Robotic endoscopic systems may add instrument stability, enhanced reach, distal dexterity, tele-manipulation, precise navigation, and access to difficult anatomy.161,162 AI-assisted endoscopy is distinct: it provides algorithmic support for lesion or bleeding-source recognition, anatomical orientation, navigation cues, workflow guidance, structured reporting, and procedural documentation.163,164 More broadly, AI and automation in endoscopy are being developed as workflow-support technologies rather than as evidence-free substitutes for endoscopist judgment.165 Clinical risk-prediction tools operate upstream by identifying patients who may need endoscopy, whereas autonomous intervention would imply machine-controlled cannulation, hemostasis, banding, dilation, or stenting and is not supported by current evidence.

Robotic platforms are important AI integration environments because they can generate synchronized endoscopic video, instrument-position and navigation data, device events, and workflow streams.161,162 These streams can be linked to imaging, laboratory findings, procedural events, and outcomes for downstream quality assessment and model validation.165 A practical example is an HCC patient with cirrhosis who develops portal-hypertension-related variceal bleeding: a robotic endoscopic platform could help maintain a stable view during suction, irrigation, and band-ligation-oriented maneuvers, while AI could flag the bleeding source, key esophagogastric landmarks, and incomplete visualization for operator review and documentation.158,163,164 A second example is suspected biliary stenosis or leakage after transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), hepatic surgery, or liver transplantation, where robot-assisted endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)-like control could support cannulation and accessory manipulation while AI tracks anatomical orientation, fluoroscopic or endoscopic landmarks, and report completeness.159,160,166 These examples illustrate plausible workflow benefits, including stabilized visualization, more consistent landmark recognition, structured documentation, and richer procedural data capture, but they do not establish HCC-specific improvements in safety, treatment success, or outcomes.165,166

These roles remain extrapolated largely from robotic gastrointestinal surgery, robotic flexible endoscopy, and early robotic ERCP or advanced endoscopy, not HCC-specific trials.162 Evidence from other robot-assisted microsurgical fields is even less directly transferable to HCC care.167 Similarly, AI-assisted gastrointestinal endoscopy supports the general concept of algorithmic endoscopic support but does not establish HCC-specific benefit.164 Technical feasibility therefore does not establish clinical benefit, workflow readiness, or generalizability to patients with impaired liver reserve, portal hypertension, coagulopathy, or altered anatomy.55,56 Robotic platform performance, AI decision support, and autonomous action should be evaluated separately; near-term use should remain clinician-supervised augmentation, not autonomous intervention or a validated HCC AI-assisted treatment platform.15,17

AI-assisted support for minimally invasive interventional therapy

AI-assisted support for minimally invasive interventional therapy is clinically relevant to HCC because image-guided ablation, including radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA), is already used for selected small or unresectable tumors.168,169 Potential applications include lesion segmentation, preoperative-intraoperative image registration, needle or antenna trajectory planning, ablation-zone estimation, and margin verification; these functions may support planning and intraprocedural review, but they do not by themselves establish improved tumor control, recurrence, survival, or treatment efficacy.168,169

The interventional environment is suitable for AI integration because ablation depends on translating preprocedural CT or MRI information into real-time procedural guidance using US, CT, fluoroscopy, or fused imaging.169 Earlier augmented-reality systems for RFA explored fused preoperative and intraoperative imaging to guide probe navigation, showing how image-overlay tools may help operators maintain spatial orientation during minimally invasive targeting.170

A transferable example comes from robot-assisted RFA for multiple spinal metastases, where robotic percutaneous guidance was used to support technically demanding needle placement in vulnerable oncology patients.171 For HCC, the analogous use case would be selected ablations requiring careful trajectory control, such as lesions near major vessels, the diaphragm, bile ducts, or other structures where registration, targeting, and margin assessment are especially demanding; this remains a hypothesis for HCC rather than proven clinical benefit.168,169,171

Future HCC studies should therefore move beyond targeting accuracy alone and evaluate complete ablation, local tumor progression, recurrence, complications, procedure time, retreatment, workflow efficiency, and survival.168,169 Robotic assistance, AI decision support, and autonomous intervention should be assessed separately, and near-term use should remain clinician-supervised augmentation rather than autonomous ablation or an independently validated AI-assisted treatment-support platform.16,17

Challenges and solutions

In the preceding sections, we have reviewed AI models spanning pathology, imaging, clinical data, biochemical examinations, LLM/agentic approaches, and AI-assisted treatment-support workflows in surgical robotics, endoscopic robotics, and interventional therapy. Despite promising results, adoption is limited by robustness, LLM/agent reliability and safety, computational burden, interpretability, multimodal data fusion and alignment, patient-centered AI-assisted treatment-support workflow redesign, ethics, governance, and clinical-labor redistribution. This section therefore separates technical infrastructure barriers from human-centered ethical and organizational barriers. The main challenges and corresponding solution pathways are summarized in Fig. 2.

Performance robustness and deployment limits

Robust deployment of HCC AI is limited less by algorithm design alone than by distribution shift across devices, acquisition protocols, tissue processing, patient mix, and clinical workflows.18,172 CT/MRI protocol differences, US operator dependence, and pathology variation in fixation, staining, sectioning, scanner calibration, and WSI digitization can alter feature distributions across sites.50,68 These shifts interact with HCC etiology, cirrhotic remodeling, AFP trends, liver function, and longitudinal history, creating missing or discordant inputs that reduce real-world reliability.55,56

Evidence from HCC and broader medical imaging shows that models may lose accuracy under hospital, vendor, protocol, or population shifts.18,173 In US-based HCC screening, acquisition conditions vary across machines and sonographers, making small, poorly visualized, or artifact-obscured lesions a major failure mode; in a representative screening model, lesions smaller than 1 cm were missed more frequently, and HCC detection sensitivity increased as lesion size increased, highlighting a clinically important limitation for early diagnosis.174 AFP-negative HCC is another clinical bottleneck because normal AFP may falsely reassure clinicians despite focal-lesion risk, so imaging AI must remain robust to biomarker-negative presentations and be paired with clinician review.58 This pattern is not limited to imaging: in structured-clinical modeling, an RF-based HCC risk-prediction model using six routine biomarkers showed strong internal performance, with AUCs of 0.969 in training and 0.858 in validation, but its retrospective, single-center design still leaves major uncertainty about cross-site transportability and real-world readiness.93,105 Practical mitigation should combine heterogeneous multicenter training, domain-shift simulation, lesion-size-stratified reporting, external and temporal validation, prospective silent deployment, and impact testing on workflow and outcomes.105,149,175

LLM reliability concerns

LLMs pose distinct risks for medical diagnosis. Chief among these is hallucination: LLMs often produce fluent but incorrect or unsupported outputs. In clinical contexts, hallucinations can lead to fabricated patient details or other unsupported clinical assertions.19 LLMs may also provide outdated or incomplete medical information, because their responses depend on the scope and recency of their training and updating.176

Critically, LLMs tend to be overconfident: they give answers in authoritative language even when unsure. Models frequently prioritize coherence and confidence over factuality, leading to errors delivered with high certainty.177 In medicine this is dangerous, since clinicians might trust a confident LLM output despite lacking evidence. Taken together, LLM-based tools for HCC diagnosis and care raise three connected concerns: they may generate unsupported diagnostic or therapeutic claims, fail to keep pace with current clinical evidence, and present uncertain conclusions with unwarranted confidence.

To mitigate these risks beyond RAG, as discussed in previous sections, developers can combine confidence calibration and uncertainty estimation with curated domain-specific fine-tuning and factual-consistency prompting or self-evaluation techniques, encouraging models to check claims or defer when uncertain and thereby improving reliability for high-stakes medical use.178,179

Risk control should also include explicit clinician-AI collaboration rules. LLM outputs should provide source-linked evidence, calibrated confidence signals, and an “abstain/escalate” option when uncertainty is high; clinicians should independently verify key claims before acting on recommendations, especially for high-impact decisions such as biopsy indication, surgery, or systemic therapy selection.127,177,180

Agentic AI structural risks

“Agentic” AI systems that chain multiple steps or tools introduce additional failure modes. In long reasoning chains, dependencies between intermediate steps can make error tracing difficult, so an early flawed premise may propagate through later steps unless explicit verification is built into the workflow.20 Such multi-step workflows also foster automation bias: users may over-rely on the AI's intermediate suggestions. Studies of decision aids show users often accept incorrect automated advice uncritically, skipping vital checks. In HCC care, a doctor might defer too quickly to an AI agent's multi-stage analysis, even if one step was mistaken.181

In summary, improving agentic AI reliability can involve uncertainty communication, structured oversight, and human-AI mixed-initiative workflows with explainable decision layers to reduce error propagation, automation bias, and integration failures.138,182 A practical safeguard is checkpointed collaboration: (i) agent-generated draft reasoning, (ii) clinician verification of retrieved evidence and rule execution, and (iii) multidisciplinary sign-off with auditable logs before final documentation or action.138,144,182

Computational capacity and deployment burden in HCC workflows

Computational burden in HCC AI is modality- and workflow-specific rather than merely a function of model size, particularly when deployment must accommodate computationally intensive pipelines under resource-constrained conditions.183 Clinical consequences are especially relevant when rapid assessment is needed for difficult diagnostic targets, including AFP-negative HCC or small HCC lesions.58,73

Several representative workflows show where these costs arise. Multiphase CT/MRI adds phase inference, enhancement fusion, and registration for LI-RADS-oriented review.79,111 WSI pathology adds tile-level H&E or mIF processing for MVI and TME tasks.31,45 Longitudinal models repeatedly update AFP/PIVKA-II, LSM, MASLD-related EHR covariates, and post-direct-acting antiviral (DAA) HCV trajectories, whereas foundation-scale or LLM-agent systems add memory and orchestration costs.100, 101, 102, 103,140,184

Solutions should be grouped by deployment function. Lightweight architectures, pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, mixed precision, and edge deployment reduce inference load.114,185,186 For example, G-MBRMD (Guided teaching with Multi-head Boundary Reconstruction Mapping Distillation) used guided-teaching knowledge distillation with multi-head boundary reconstruction mapping to produce a lightweight liver segmentation model that preserved boundary-sensitive segmentation while reducing computational cost, improving deployability in resource-constrained CT imaging settings.113 Moreover, region-of-interest (ROI) selection and tile-based sampling can reduce the amount of WSI data processed in pathology workflows, whereas structured biomarker and EHR-based models may lower analytic overhead for longitudinal risk updating without removing the need for repeated clinical data collection.31,45,100, 101, 102

Future HCC AI studies should report latency, hardware, memory, throughput, failure modes, and fallback strategies alongside AUC, Dice, or reader performance, and should test these metrics in the intended clinical workflow.183

Interpretability and clinical trust in HCC diagnosis

Clinically meaningful interpretability in HCC should connect predictions to decision-relevant concepts and reviewable evidence. Imaging explanations should indicate whether attribution supports established diagnostic or risk pathways, using representative cues such as LI-RADS enhancement patterns, peritumoral MVI signal, or BDTT-related ductal findings.57,59, 60, 61,82 Pathology explanations should provide WSI regions that pathologists can interrogate for lesion classification, invasion, or TME scoring,31, 32, 33,45 whereas biomarker and EHR models should distinguish signal families such as AFP/PIVKA-II dynamics, liver-stiffness or MASLD-related EHR features, and antiviral-treatment history.100, 101, 102, 103

Poor alignment between model explanations and clinical evidence may pose safety concerns in HCC, particularly in settings already characterized by diagnostic ambiguity, including cirrhotic perfusion artifacts, AFP-negative disease, small lesions, and biologically informative endpoints such as MVI or CK19 risk.47,57,58,60,73 Post-hoc explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, including gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM), saliency maps, and related attribution methods, should therefore remain localization aids, not proof of disease-relevant reasoning.187 Evaluation should test use-case-specific explanations, including phase-aware MRI reader studies, pathologist-reviewable WSI regions for nodule or MVI tasks, and focused error summaries for small or AFP-negative lesions.31,32,58,64 Intrinsically interpretable or domain-constrained models should be adopted only when clinically acceptable performance is retained; the interpretability-performance trade-off must be evaluated, not assumed.107,188

Multimodal data fusion and alignment in HCC diagnosis

In HCC, multimodal fusion is primarily an alignment problem, not feature aggregation. Imaging defines lesion location and enhancement context, WSI provides lesion-specific invasion biology, and biomarkers or EHRs encode longitudinal liver disease and treatment history.31,55,59,100, 101, 102 Although missingness, modality imbalance, and implementation heterogeneity are general fusion risks, HCC adds clinically consequential lesion- and time-level misalignment.21,22 Post-ablation AFP paired with pretreatment MRI can distort recurrence assessment; a resection WSI linked to the wrong lesion can invalidate imaging-pathology supervision; and, when imaging evidence is equivocal, inappropriate weighting of EHR-derived risk information may bias multimodal inference, particularly in AFP-negative disease or benign cirrhotic nodules.22,31,56, 57, 58

Fusion should be anchored to HCC decision points. Multiphase CT/MRI should be registered before cross-phase imaging analysis.79,111 Lesion identifiers should link nodules across reports, masks, specimens, and outcomes to keep labels lesion-matched.22,189 Biomarker/EHR trajectories should be indexed to surveillance, diagnosis, treatment, and recurrence because the clinical interpretation and predictive value of AFP/PIVKA-II, LSM, antiviral history, and cirrhosis-related events depend on disease stage and temporal context.100, 101, 102, 103 Only then should latent-space alignment, attention/contrastive fusion, and dynamic weighting be treated as clinically meaningful.63,111,140 Evaluation should include subgroup-specific analyses for small cirrhotic lesions, AFP-negative disease, post-ablation anatomy, and imaging-pathology discordance in addition to aggregate metrics such as AUC or Dice.31,56,58,73

Human-centered and ethical challenges in AI-assisted HCC care

Human-centered risks in HCC extend beyond diagnostic accuracy because AI can reshape expectations, autonomy, responsibility, and physician-patient trust.190 Direct evidence for patient-facing AI in HCC remains limited: ChatGPT responses on cirrhosis and HCC were often factually reasonable but incomplete and sometimes inconsistent with staging or guideline-based care, supporting use for education or question preparation rather than independent decisions.122 Broader oncology evidence is consistent, with approximately one-third of ChatGPT treatment responses at least partially non-concordant with National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidance.191

HCC care is also preference sensitive. Patients may face surveillance anxiety, recurrence risk, transplant eligibility, locoregional or systemic therapy, and end-of-life decisions; patient-journey and preference studies show that goals shift over time and that some patients would trade survival benefit to preserve daily function or avoid burdensome adverse events.192,193 Acceptance is conditional on accuracy, privacy, bias mitigation, and physician involvement, and cancer-specific evidence highlights concerns about depersonalized care and unclear responsibility.194, 195, 196 Patient-facing HCC AI should therefore remain narrow, clinician-supervised, and value-sensitive, limited to explaining basic disease or staging terms, preparing visits, and correcting misconceptions with clinician verification.122,197 Clinicians should disclose material AI use, communicate uncertainty, preserve patient choice, and remain accountable for final decisions.198,199

Data governance and privacy are translational barriers, not abstract safeguards. Multicenter HCC AI must coordinate multiphase imaging, WSI pathology, serial biomarkers, liver stiffness, antiviral history, cirrhosis events, and transplant records; fragmentation can separate evidence needed to distinguish early HCC, dysplastic nodules, AFP-negative disease, or post-treatment recurrence.200 Reidentification remains a practical privacy concern because de-identified genomic data can be linked with auxiliary external information, showing that conventional de-identification alone may be insufficient.201 Data handling should align with applicable privacy frameworks, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), when relevant. Federated and privacy-preserving approaches reduce raw-data transfer, but non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) etiologies, scanner protocols, staining pipelines, AFP testing frequency, and annotation rules still require harmonized standards, transparent governance, fairness safeguards, external validation, and prospective testing.149,202,203

Regulatory compliance should be anchored to intended use. HCC AI may support surveillance recall, LI-RADS-oriented triage, ablation-planning segmentation, MVI or CK19 risk prediction, recurrence monitoring, or treatment-support documentation; these functions may enter U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), European Medical Device Regulation (MDR), or European Union (EU) AI Act pathways, but require different evidence.204 Segmentation tools need boundary accuracy and workflow safety, whereas surveillance or biology-risk models require calibration, false-negative analysis, and follow-up outcomes; retrospective AUC or Dice alone rarely establishes safety in cirrhotic livers, small lesions, AFP-negative cases, post-ablation anatomy, or transplant-candidate populations.204 After deployment, institutions should maintain intended-use documentation, accountable clinical ownership, audit trails, post-market surveillance, drift monitoring, and recalibration as data sources or patient mix change.205 Governance committees should monitor performance, bias, failures, and escalation rules across tumor-board and transplant workflows so AI remains supervised support rather than a substitute for clinical judgment.206

Workforce transformation and redistribution of clinical labor in AI-assisted HCC care

AI in HCC care should be treated as a sociotechnical implementation tool that redistributes selected tasks rather than replacing clinical staff.207,208 Because HCC management in patients with cirrhosis or chronic viral hepatitis depends on liver reserve, AFP trends, prior imaging, transplant eligibility, and patient goals, AI outputs should be interpreted within this clinical context.55 Guideline-based management still requires clinicians to decide whether a lesion changes management and whether ablation, resection, transplantation, locoregional therapy, systemic therapy, or observation is appropriate.56

Under clinician supervision, AI can automate selected imaging-analysis and reporting tasks within clinical workflows, while physicians review, correct, and approve AI-generated outputs before they are incorporated into patient care.209 Pathology-facing tools may also estimate invasion-related risk from WSIs, but these outputs still require expert review.31 However, atypical lesions, discordant imaging and laboratory trends, poor liver reserve, possible transplant eligibility, or unclear treatment sequencing should trigger physician reassessment.56 AI cannot make these final judgments because they require explaining uncertainty to patients, weighing survival benefit against liver decompensation or quality of life, discussing transplant candidacy, and resolving disagreements across hepatology, radiology, surgery, oncology, and transplant teams.116,192,210

AI-enabled workflow integration may streamline selected reporting steps under clinician review;209 if this creates operational capacity, it should be redirected toward surveillance outreach, patient follow-up, and navigation, because mailed outreach and patient navigation were shown in a randomized trial to improve HCC surveillance uptake.211 HCC surveillance remains underused in cirrhosis, and nurse-led surveillance models provide clinical precedent for structured monitoring and outreach.212,213 Nurses and coordinators can therefore focus on arranging surveillance, scheduling follow-up imaging, contacting patients after missed visits, and flagging abnormal AFP results for clinician review, while any AI reminder or registry function should remain institutionally validated and supervised. HCC programs also need informaticians, data managers, information-technology staff, quality-improvement personnel, and AI governance teams to link radiology, pathology, laboratory, EHR, and transplant data and monitor performance, bias, and failures.205 Training should include basic AI literacy so staff can interpret outputs, recognize bias, and identify failure cases; implementation experience also shows that clinical AI adoption requires practical workflow training and staff support.172,214

At the organizational level, AI implementation may require redesign of HCC care pathways rather than simple software adoption.144,207,208 Institutions should define standardized protocols specifying when AI outputs can enter documentation or tumor-board deliberation, who verifies them, and how conflicts between AI recommendations and clinical judgment are resolved.144,205,214 Multidisciplinary tumor boards may increasingly review AI-generated imaging, pathology, and risk-stratification reports as structured inputs, creating workflow checkpoints for human validation, auditability, and accountability.116,144,210

Conclusions and future perspectives

AI is reshaping HCC care most clearly as a set of clinician-supervised tools for diagnosis, risk stratification, evidence synthesis, and AI-assisted procedural support. In pathology and imaging, AI can assist subtype classification, MVI assessment, segmentation, small-lesion detection, and non-invasive risk modeling. In clinical data and LLM-enabled workflows, AI may help structure reports, summarize longitudinal records, retrieve evidence, and support guideline-aligned review. In AI-assisted treatment-support settings, robotic surgery, endoscopic robotics, and image-guided interventions should be interpreted as platforms for AI-assisted planning, navigation, documentation, and quality control rather than as autonomous treatment systems or independently validated outcome-improving therapies.

Translation remains limited by retrospective and single-center evidence, domain shift, incomplete multimodal datasets, limited interpretability, privacy constraints, computational cost, and uncertain workflow readiness. Future studies should prioritize prospective multicenter validation, privacy-preserving collaboration, standardized multimodal data alignment, transparent evaluation, and clinically meaningful endpoints, including diagnostic accuracy, decision quality, workflow efficiency, complete ablation, complications, recurrence, retreatment, survival, and patient-centered outcomes. Implementation should also address accountability, patient acceptance, staff training, and redistribution of clinical labor. The main barriers and mitigation strategies are summarized conceptually in Fig. 2. Ultimately, AI is best positioned as collaborative intelligence that augments clinician expertise while preserving human judgment, auditability, and patient-centered decision-making.
