Cavity-enhanced optical readout and control of nuclear spin qubits
Authors: Alexander Ulanowski, Johannes Früh, Fabian Salamon, Adrian Holzäpfel, Andreas Reiserer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTheir exceptional coherence makes nuclear spins in solids a prime candidate for quantum memories in quantum networks and repeaters. Still, the direct all-optical initialization, coherent control, and readout of individual nuclear spin qubits have been an outstanding challenge. Here, this is achieved by embedding 167Er dopants in yttrium orthosilicate in a cryogenic Fabry-Perot cavity, whose linewidth of 65 MHz is much smaller than the 0.9 GHz separation of neighboring hyperfine levels. Frequency-selective emission enhancement thus enables a single-shot readout fidelity of 91(2) %. Furthermore, a large magnetic field freezes paramagnetic impurities, leading to coherence times exceeding 0.2 s. The combination of nuclear-spin qubits with frequency-multiplexed addressing and lifetime-limited photon emission in the minimal-loss telecommunications C-band establishes 167Er as a leading platform for long-range, fiber-based quantum networks.Subject terms: Quantum information, Qubits

Introduction

Quantum networks will enable their users to perform tasks and interact in ways that are impossible using classical devices1, enabling numerous applications in quantum information processing and distributed quantum sensing. First prototypes2 have demonstrated the entanglement of stationary spin qubits over up to tens of kilometers3–5. Increasing this to even larger distances, however, is hindered by photon loss in optical fibers6. It has been proposed that this obstacle can be overcome using quantum repeaters, which require highly efficient interfaces between stationary qubits and optical photons in the minimal loss-band of optical fibers7, as well as memories with storage times approaching the scale of seconds8.

Owing to their low sensitivity to electric and magnetic field noise, nuclear-spin qubits are ideally suited to reach this timescale. Previous experiments used atoms trapped in vacuum9,10 or embedded as dopants in crystals with a low density of magnetic moments, such as diamond11–15, silicon16, silicon carbide17, and silicate crystals18,19. In the latter, the current record coherence—more than 10 h—has been achieved using ensembles of rare-earth dopants20.

Still, combining such exceptional storage times with efficient and coherent single-photon generation, as required for quantum repeaters8, is an outstanding challenge. Earlier attempts used nuclear spins around color centers in diamond11,12,21 and silicon carbide22,23, which were read out indirectly via a controlled coupling to their electronic spin. This way, up to 27 individual nuclear spins have been resolved24. However, in this approach, random flips of the electron reduce the nuclear spin coherence13,14, and the indirect nuclear-spin readout via the electronic spin ancilla requires additional electron-nuclear spin quantum gates11,12,21–24 that are typically implemented by microwave pulses. This reduces the achievable rates and induces heating and imperfections that deteriorate the fidelity. These limitations have been overcome with ensembles of nuclear spins by applying a large magnetic field. When the induced Zeeman splitting strongly exceeds the thermal energy, the electronic spins are frozen to the ground state and decay to it on very fast timescales via the direct spin-lattice relaxation process25. Still, it has been shown that hyperfine interaction can enable all-optical control of nuclear spin ensembles exhibiting second-long coherence26.

Similar to the pioneering work with erbium dopant ensembles26, we study erbium dopants in yttrium orthosilicate (YSO) crystals. This combination of emitter and host is particularly promising for quantum networking: First, erbium dopants can emit photons in the telecommunications C-band7, where loss in optical fibers is minimal. Second, exceptional optical coherence times have been observed in Er:YSO, up to several ms27, achieving the lifetime-limit in suited resonators28. Finally, frequency-selective addressing allows coherent control of many individual spin qubits in the same optical mode29,30—up to several hundreds31—paving the way to multiplexed entanglement generation32 over long distances.

While previous experiments in Er:YSO implemented qubits in the electronic spins of the even isotopes29,31, here we study individual nuclear-spin qubits. In the following, we demonstrate the initialization, coherent control, and optical single-shot readout of the nuclear spin of 167Er dopants.

Results

In our experiment, we study Er:YSO at temperatures < 1.8 K in a closed-cycle 4He cryostat and apply a large magnetic field of 6.8 T to fully polarize the electronic spins of both the emitters and of all paramagnetic impurities. As shown in Fig. 1a, this reduces intrinsic spin noise and—even in materials with significant impurity concentrations—allows for nuclear spin coherence exceeding seconds26 and optical coherence exceeding milliseconds27. At the same time, it eliminates optical dephasing caused by superhyperfine interactions33,34.

At zero magnetic field, site 1 of Er:YSO exhibits an optical transition frequency of 1536.4 nm between ∣g≡Z1—the lowest crystal-field level of the 4I15/2 ground state—and ∣e≡Y1—the lowest level of the 4I13/2 optically excited state27. When applying 6.8 T along the b-axis of the crystal, the nuclear spin levels are separated by up to 0.9 GHz35, as shown in Fig. 1b. This large separation exceeds the inhomogeneous broadening in Er:YSO, which —even in ensembles26— enables frequency-selective spin initialization by optical pumping, i.e., repeated excitation on the spin-flip transitions with ΔmI = ± 1. Here, mI denotes the nuclear spin quantum number projection along the magnetic field direction, which ranges from − 7/2 to + 7/2 for the I = 7/2 nuclear spin of 167Er. Note that the effective S = 1/2 electronic spin may decay optically to mS = 1/2, but in this case returns immediately to the thermal equilibrium state − 1/2 because of direct spin-lattice relaxation at a rate ∝ B525.

For optical readout, the nuclear spin quantum number mI needs to be preserved. Albeit this can be ensured upon frequency-selective optical excitation, mI can change with a significant probability when decaying optically on the Y1 → Z1 transition (see Supplementary Information Sec. II A), as indicated by the blue and red dotted arrows in Fig. 1b. Similar to recent experiments with color centers in diamond15, the low cyclicity of the transition hinders an all-optical readout with high fidelity. We overcome this challenge by embedding the emitters into an optical Fabry-Perot resonator with a high quality factor of Q = 3 × 106, as shown in Fig. 1c. The resulting linewidth of 65 MHz (full-width-half-maximum) is much narrower than the separation of the optical transitions between neighboring nuclear spin levels. Thus, as shown in Fig. 1d, the decay rate of the detuned spin-flip transitions exhibits no enhancement as P ≪ 1; it is thus unchanged from its bulk value. In contrast, the cavity selectively enhances the resonant nuclear-spin-preserving transitions (ΔmI = 0), with a measured Purcell factor2 of up to 95(10). The resulting increase in cyclicity paves the way for all-optical nuclear-spin qubit readout demonstrated in this work.

Detection of single 167Er dopants

Our experiments use a 10.9 μm thin membrane of YSO that is integrated into a frequency-tunable Fabry-Perot resonator, as described in our earlier works31,34. The technical details of the experiment are summarized in the Supplementary Information Sec. I. The crystal contains Er in natural isotopic abundance, such that ~ 23 % of the emitters are of the isotope 167Er. At temperatures below 2 K, individual erbium dopants in this crystal exhibit narrow optical transitions down to 0.2 MHz 30, whose center frequencies differ because of local modifications in the crystal environment, e.g., strain, defects, or other dopants. These random frequency shifts allow frequency-multiplexed addressing of individual emitters29,30. Co-doping with Eu allows for a tailored decrease of the spectral density31.

To identify a suited 167Er emitter, we employ pulsed fluorescence spectroscopy with laser pulses of 8 μs duration that are frequency-chirped over 2 MHz. Using superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors, we detect the emitted fluorescence photons after the laser is turned off. We then scan the excitation laser frequency while keeping the cavity on resonance. While the even isotopes without nuclear spin lead to a strong fluorescence at a single resonance frequency, one expects eight smaller peaks—one for each nuclear spin ground state—for the spin-preserving transitions of 167Er. An example spectrum showing this signature can be seen in Fig. 2. The assignment of the individual spin-preserving lines (green) of a single dopant is described in the Supplementary Information Sec. II C. The spin-flip transitions, which are detuned by a few hundred MHz, do not give a measurable signal (not shown) because their Purcell enhancement is reduced in proportion to the branching ratio (see Supplementary Information Sec. II A); in addition, in the case of repeated probing at the same frequency, the signal will decay because of unintended optical spin pumping.

Purcell-enhanced optical spin initialization

In the following, we will focus on the optical transition between ∣−7/2g and ∣−7/2e. First, as described in the Supplementary Information Sec. V B, the transitions between the outermost spin states (mI = ± 7/2) are expected to exhibit the largest cyclicity, which is favorable for spin readout. Second, the spin can be initialized in ∣−7/2g by driving all the ΔmI = − 1 optical transitions, as demonstrated previously with atomic ensembles26. However, the use of a single emitter in an optical resonator calls for an adaptation of the protocol, which we will describe in the following.

To initialize the spin in ∣−7/2 starting from a random state, one needs to de-populate all other levels—a procedure termed optical pumping. To this end, one can selectively excite all seven transitions of the red sideband, i.e., all spin-flip transitions that decrease the quantum number of the nuclear spin by one, ΔmI = − 1. The subsequent decay from the excited state can randomly change the spin. Under continuous or repeated driving, the system will undergo many optical excitations and decays until it ends up in ∣−7/2g, from which no excitation on the red sideband is possible.

The timescale of the optical pumping process is governed by the emitter lifetime and by the branching ratio of the spin-flip versus the spin-preserving decays. Thus, the presence of the resonator enables a speed-up2, as the Purcell effect enhances the branching of the spin-preserving lines and leads to a faster optical decay, reducing the excited-state lifetime of 11.4ms36 down to 0.12 ms.

The spectral dependence of the relative Purcell factors P/Pmax of the individual transitions are shown in Fig. 1d for a situation in which the resonator is tuned precisely to ∣−7/2e→∣−7/2g, such that the decay of this transition is enhanced. The Purcell factors are smaller for the higher spin states, and negligible for the ∣+7/2e→∣+7/2g transition. To achieve the fastest initialization, all seven red-sideband transitions could be excited simultaneously. Instead, for technical reasons, we choose to apply them sequentially, such that the required pulses can be generated from a single laser that is shifted in frequency by an electro-optical modulator.

In the experiment, we use pulses of 20 μs duration that are frequency-chirped over 10 MHz. We start at the ∣+7/2g→∣+5/2e frequency, then proceed with ∣+5/2g→∣+3/2e and continue analogously until ∣−5/2g→∣−7/2e. Repeating this sequence leads to a strong increase of the population in ∣−7/2g, as can be seen in Fig. 2, where the fluorescence signal on the corresponding spin-preserving transition is strongly increased (orange). The initialization fidelity can be characterized by autocorrelation measurements (see Supplementary Information Sec. V D). To achieve the highest value, up to 97.3(9) %, we repeat the pumping sequence 500 times in the experiments on single-shot readout that will be described below.

While owing to the branching ratios (see Supplementary Information Sec. II A), the initialization in the outermost spin states, such as ∣−7/2g, is expected to be most efficient, the spin can also be initialized in other states by replacing red-sideband transitions with the corresponding ones on the blue sideband. This allows us to identify the precise frequencies of all ΔmI = 0, ± 1 optical transitions of the studied dopant, 22 in total, and thus to fully characterize the energy levels of the ground and excited state, as detailed in the Supplementary Information Sec. II C. The assigned spin-preserving transitions in the spectrum are marked with green dots at the bottom of the panel in Fig. 2.

Single-shot nuclear spin qubit readout

After implementing a pulse sequence that allows for efficient initialization, we now turn to the optical readout of the nuclear spin state. To this end, we define the qubit in the two lowest levels in the ground state, ∣−7/2g and ∣−5/2g, and apply optical excitation pulses on resonance with the spin-preserving transition ∣−7/2g→∣−7/2e. As this transition is far detuned from all other optical transitions, this allows for a spin-selective excitation, such that the detection of a photon after a narrowband excitation pulse unambiguously heralds the ∣−7/2g state. However, absence of a photon does not herald the ∣−5/2g state because of the finite excitation and detection probabilities. Thus, several optical excitations are needed to achieve an unambiguous readout of the nuclear spin. This is only possible if the transition exhibits a high cyclicity, which means that the spin state is not changed in the optical decay. In our experiment, this is enabled by the selective resonator enhancement, as detailed in the Supplementary Information Sec. V B. Furthermore, the resonator improves the photon detection efficiency to 11(1) % and reduces the optical lifetime, which leads to a speed-up of the readout process. Only this combination of advantages makes a single-shot readout feasible.

For a first experimental demonstration, after spin initialization, we repeatedly excite the spin-preserving ∣−7/2g→∣−7/2e transition and measure the fluorescence. We use pulses of 8 μs duration that are frequency-chirped over 2 MHz. If the spin is in the ∣−7/2g state, several photons can be detected in each readout attempt. If, however, the spin is in ∣−5/2g, or in another state outside of the qubit manifold, one expects to detect only a small number of fluorescence photons that can originate from off-resonant driving or from detector dark counts. The histograms obtained after 110 excitation pulses are shown in Fig. 3a. Clearly, the distribution obtained when initializing the spin in ∣−7/2g (orange) is different from that of ∣−5/2g (blue), which both are close to binomial distributions with average values of 10.69(5) and 2.356(24) photons, respectively. Setting the discrimination threshold at the optimal value of n = 5 detected photons gives a single-shot readout fidelity of 91(2) %. Here, we define fidelity as the minimum probability of correctly assigning the nuclear spin states ∣−7/2g and ∣−5/2g, and we do not correct for initialization errors (see Supplementary Information Sec. V D).

The histograms in Fig. 3a were measured at the optimal pulse number and discrimination threshold. This is the result of a systematic optimization, shown in Fig. 3b. From the histograms, it can be seen that the main factor that limits the achieved fidelity is detector dark counts at a rate of 43.9(1) Hz, as can be seen from the distribution of detection events in the absence of optical excitation pulses (black open bars), which is very close to the distribution in the dark state ∣−5/2g (blue bars). As the dark counts can be reduced by more than an order of magnitude at comparable efficiency in state-of-the-art devices37, we expect that it will be possible to increase the single-shot-readout fidelity above 98 % (see Supplementary Information Sec. V C), surpassing common thresholds for topological quantum error correction38.

All-optical coherent control

After demonstrating the single-shot readout of individual nuclear spins, we now turn to their coherence properties. We start by measuring the lifetime, which is 33(3) s at the used temperature of 1.72(1) K and magnetic field of 6.8 T (see Supplementary Information Sec. II D). Further improvement may be achieved at lower temperatures.

To determine the coherence time, we implement coherent control over the nuclear spin states. Because of their small gyromagnetic ratio, direct driving with radio-frequency fields would require powers of many Watts to achieve Rabi frequencies in the kHz regime, leading to heating and resonator instability. Therefore, we instead implement all-optical control. Specifically, we drive Raman transitions with two control fields, as sketched in Fig. 4a. To avoid that scattering from the excited state limits the spin control fidelity, while still operating at moderate drive powers of 10 mW, we choose a detuning of Δ = −90 MHz from the ∣−7/2g↔∣−7/2e transition.

All-optical coherent control and hyperfine coherence.

Then, Raman transitions are driven using square laser pulses with Rabi frequencies Ω1 and Ω2 that are irradiated simultaneously with a frequency difference that matches the qubit transition when δ = 0. Scattering is avoided by choosing a detuning of Δ = − 90 MHz from the readout transition. b When varying the Raman pulse duration, Rabi oscillations are observed at δ = 0. A cosine function with a stretched-exponential envelope (solid line) fits the data well. c In a Hahn-Echo experiment, consisting of a pulse sequence of π/2 − π − π/2 (see inset), a coherence time of THahn = 14.8(9) ms is obtained from a fit to a stretched-exponential function (solid line). d The coherence time can be further extended by dynamical decoupling, in which N equidistant π-pulses are applied sequentially. To reduce the influence of pulse errors, the phase of the pulses is altered between rotations around the X and Y axes, forming an XY(N) sequence. With this, the coherence time increases with the number of pulses (purple data) according to TDD ∝ N0.82(2) (black fit curve) up to a value of TDD = 0.28(8) s. The dotted gray line shows the expected increase for a slowly varying spin bath, ∝ N2/3. Error bars: 1 SD.

To ensure that both fields have a comparable and constant amplitude, and to make the Rabi frequency insensitive to residual frequency fluctuations of the resonator, we tune the latter such that both fields exhibit equal detunings of opposite sign from the cavity resonance (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI B). Then, we irradiate pulses of varying duration to observe Rabi oscillations between the states of the qubit manifold, as shown in Fig. 4b. The oscillations are damped because of power and/or polarization fluctuations of the Raman laser pulses. Dephasing can be excluded for pulse durations below 0.1 ms, as the dephasing time is measured as T2*=0.62(3)ms in a Ramsey sequence (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI C 2). This value exceeds the 56(9) ns coherence of the electronic spins of erbium in the same host39 by more than four orders of magnitude, demonstrating the low sensitivity of the nuclear spin qubits to magnetic field fluctuations.

Nuclear-spin qubit coherence

To further increase the coherence, we use a Hahn-echo sequence. As shown in Fig. 4c, we find a Hahn-echo time of THahn = 16.7(9) ms. This value is much smaller than that found in ensemble measurements on site 2 in 167Er, exceeding one second26. This reduction, and the revival of THahn around 30 ms, are attributed to fluctuations of the bias magnetic field that can be reduced in the future by active damping (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI C 3).

Alternatively, the coherence can be extended by dynamical decoupling. To this end, we apply π-pulses, alternating around the X and Y axes to reduce the sensitivity to pulse errors40. With this, we find a coherence time up to TDD = 0.28(8) s after 64 pulses. Eliminating residual pulse imperfections will likely enable extending this even further. The scaling of the coherence time with the number of applied pulses N follows a power law, TDD ∝ N0.82(2), as shown in Fig. 4d, in good agreement with the expectation from the fluctuating magnetic field in the setup, and different from the power law ∝ N2/3 expected for a slowly varying spin bath (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI C 3).

Discussion

The above measurements demonstrate the all-optical initialization, coherent control, and single-shot readout of nuclear spin qubits in a situation where all electronic spins in the sample are frozen to the ground state. Our technique eliminates three main sources of decoherence that have plagued earlier experiments on erbium and other Kramers rare-earth dopants: First, their large sensitivity to fluctuating magnetic fields caused by paramagnetic impurities in the host material. Second, their anisotropic interaction with other dopants of the same type, which cannot be decoupled effectively41. Finally, the superhyperfine interaction of erbium dopants with the Y nuclei of the host, which leads to a fast collapse of the optical coherence33 that can be avoided at large magnetic fields27,34.

While the achieved coherence is already sufficient for the implementation of quantum repeater protocols8, it can be further extended by eliminating the primary limitation, classical magnetic-field noise in our cryostat (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI C 4). Alternatively, the fidelity of the Raman pulses may be improved by active pulse-area stabilization, which would facilitate the application of more pulses to increase the coherence. However, a limit may be reached because of the small probability of spontaneous decay after populating the optically excited state during the Raman pulses. This may hinder achieving the lifetime limit of 66(6) s. If such ultralong coherence times are desired, it will be advantageous to switch to a crystal with a low abundance of nuclear spins, such as calcium tungstate, in which the coherence of the electronic spin at mK temperatures is four orders of magnitude longer42 than that achieved in YSO41.

In spite of the optical scattering, our demonstrated use of Raman pulses for optical control of individual spins has the advantage that it enables comparably low drive powers, on the order of 10 mW, most of which ( > 90 %) is reflected because of the detuning from the cavity resonance. This results in much lower heating compared to the tens of Watts that are required for dynamical decoupling using radio-frequency fields applied via external coils43,44. In addition, the Raman fields induce an AC Stark shift to the dopants that depends on both the detuning and the field amplitude, and thus the emitter location in the resonator mode (see Supplementary Information Sec. VI A). Therefore, the technique could enable selective addressing of individual spins, similar to earlier experiments with electronic spins in Er:YSO that used microwave driving and an additional AC Stark shift laser29.

While the achieved fidelity of the single-shot readout is still below that required for state-of-the-art quantum error correction protocols45, our analysis reveals that fidelities exceeding 98 % could be directly achieved by photon detectors with lower dark counts, which in turn can be realized when efficiently filtering out remaining blackbody radiation46. In addition, a higher cyclicity can be obtained by increasing the cooperativity of the system via a reduced resonator mode waist, or—potentially—by using a vector magnet to apply the magnetic field in a direction in which the nuclear-spin-flip probability is reduced. At the same time, tuning the magnetic field to a specific operating point can also make the ground-state or optical transition frequencies insensitive to magnetic field fluctuations47, which would further extend their coherence times.

Already without these improvements, our system is well-suited for the optically-mediated generation of entanglement between remote erbium dopants. Compared to recent realizations with ytterbium32, the increased outcoupling efficiency of our setup (up to 76(5) %, see Supplementary Information Sec. I A), its operation at telecommunications frequencies, and its combination of lifetime-limited optical28 and 0.2 s long ground-state coherence even without 3He refrigerators make nuclear spins of erbium a prime candidate for quantum repeater experiments8. This could be based on the presented implementation of a nuclear spin qubit combined with time-bin encoding of photonic qubits48,49. Alternatively, one could make use of the larger Hilbert space of the nuclear spin manifold to encode quantum information in small Schrödinger cat states. In contrast to recent implementations of this idea with donor qubits50, erbium allows for optical interfacing of such states, opening a path towards efficient error-correction protocols based on spin-cat codes51.

However, our approach is not restricted to 167Er:YSO, the studied combination of host and dopant; it may also be implemented in other physical systems, e.g., Er:Si52–54, Er:Y2O3, or color centers in diamond, silicon, and silicon carbide. This requires resonators with a linewidth that is smaller than the hyperfine coupling and the spectral diffusion of the emitters. Not only Fabry-Perot cavities, as studied in this work, but also nanophotonic resonators53,55 may be suited to this end. Thus, we expect that the demonstrated combination of long memory time, efficient photon extraction, photon coherence, spectral multiplexing capacity, and telecom operation opens unique perspectives for large-scale quantum networks in several hardware platforms.

Methods

Resonator fabrication

The optical Fabry-Perot resonators used in this work have a plano-concave geometry with a mirror separation of 27(1) μm and a radius of curvature of 65(2) μm. The curved mirror is fabricated by ablating a superpolished quartz glass substrate with short CO2 laser pulses, producing nearly Gaussian-shaped depressions on an initially flat surface. These depressions are arranged in a regular grid pattern on a single substrate. Combined with a second, flat mirror substrate, each depression forms an individual cavity structure. For the experiments, two of these resonators are coupled to single-mode polarization-maintaining optical fibers—one for stabilization of the mirror distance, the other one for the experiments. Both the curved and flat mirrors are coated (Laseroptik) with 27 or 31 alternating layers of a high- (niobium pentoxide) and low-refractive index (silicon dioxide) material to form distributed Bragg reflectors. These coatings are optimized for a single target wavelength, λ0 = 1.54 μm, which corresponds to the telecom-compatible optical transition of erbium in YSO. A piezo-electric tube (Physik Instrumente) is inserted between the mirrors for active stabilization and tuning of the mirror separation.

Sample preparation

The YSO crystal was obtained from Scientific Materials Corp. and contains trace amounts of Er owing to the finite purity of the starting material. A tailor-made spectral density of emitters is achieved by doping with 100 ppm of Eu31. To minimize scattering losses at the crystal interfaces and enable a small mode waist, the initially millimeter-thick crystal is polished down28, before being transferred to the flat mirror substrate. The root-mean-square surface roughness extracted from optical linewidth measurements of the final resonator is 0.38(2) nm, assuming no absorption loss in the crystal and negligible scattering at the mirror surfaces.

Cryostat setup

Experiments were performed in a 4He pulse-tube based closed-cycle cryostat (Attocube Systems, attoDRY2100). This system provides direct optical access to the sample space and is equipped with a one-axis superconducting solenoid magnet up to 9 T. A custom-built vibration-isolation system is used to enhance the passive stability of the setup as described in Supplementary Information Sec. I C. Separate cryostat systems operating at 0.8 K from PhotonSpot or ID Quantique were used for superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPDs) of the same companies.

Optical setup

Each of the two optical resonators is coupled to a single-mode polarization-maintaining optical fiber. The free-space beam path contains several lenses inside the cryostat and dielectric mirrors, λ/2 and λ/4 waveplates outside the cryostat as shown in detail in Supplementary Information Sec. I C. Additional color filters are inserted into the free-space path to reduce leakage from the cavity-stabilization laser (Toptica CTL or DLpro) into the single-photon detectors. Overall, the efficiency of extracting an intracavity photon into the optical fiber is 60 %.

Single-tone and two-tone optical pulses are generated from a continuous-wave laser source (NKT Photonics, Koheras BASIK X15) using cascaded acousto-optical and electro-optical modulators (iXblue/exail and Gooch & Housego) driven by arbitrary waveform generators (Zurich Instruments, SHFSG and HDAWG). Further details are provided in the Supplementary Information Sec. I E.
