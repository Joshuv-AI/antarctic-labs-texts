A Noncommutative Szegő-Type Theorem on the Row-Ball
Connor J. Gauntlett, David P. Kimsey

Abstract
. In this paper we leverage the recently developed theory of noncommutative (nc) measures to prove a free noncommutative analogue of many known equalities extending the weak Szegő limit theorem, by applying Constantinescu’s theory of Schur parameters to an appropriate kernel on the free monoid on d d generators, where d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 ; in particular, we show that our nc Szegő entropy depends only upon the absolutely continuous part of the associated nc measure. We obtain a correspondence between nc measures and multi-Toeplitz kernels arising from considering the moments of the nc measure, and apply this correspondence to study orthogonal polynomials associated to an nc measure. Finally, we study the determinantal zeros of those polynomials and obtain a noncommutative row-ball analogue of the so-called Zeros Theorem for orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle.

1.
Introduction

The primary purpose of this paper is to prove a noncommutative analogue of Szegő’s theorem, in different forms variously referred to as the Szegő–Verblunsky theorem or the first, or weak, Szegő limit theorem. In so doing, we introduce and study a number of tools reminiscent of the classical Szegő theory including Verblunsky coefficients (or Schur parameters), orthogonal polynomials, and the Christoffel function associated to a measure.

The first form of Szegő’s theorem we consider herein was first given by Verblunsky and hence is sometimes referred to as the Szegő–Verblunsky theorem. This result links the absolutely continuous part of a (non-finitely atomic) measure
\mu
on the unit circle to the Verblunsky coefficients
(\gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
\mu
: let the Lebesgue decomposition of
\mu
with respect to the normalised Lebesgue measure
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}
be
\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)=w(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}+\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\theta)
, and let
(\gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
be the Verblunsky coefficients of
\mu
. Then

(1.1)

\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\lvert\gamma_{j}\rvert^{2})=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\big(w(\theta)\big)\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\right),

and hence in particular

(1.2)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lvert\gamma_{j}\rvert^{2}<\infty\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\big(w(\theta)\big)\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}>-\infty;

we stress that while the Verblunsky coefficients of
\mu
depend on all of
\mu
, the condition on the right-hand side of (
1.2
) — the
Szegő condition
— explicitly depends only upon the absolutely continuous part of
\mu
.

In another form more often called the first, or weak, Szegő limit theorem, one considers the Fourier coefficients
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mu
, recalling that positivity of
\mu
implies that
\overline{c_{n}}=c_{-n}
, and defines the Toeplitz determinants of
\mu
via

(1.3)

D_{n}:=\det\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n}\\
c_{-1}&c_{0}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{-n}&c_{-(n-1)}&\cdots&c_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

One formulation of the weak Szegő limit theorem then says that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{n}}{D_{n-1}}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\lvert\gamma_{j}\rvert^{2})\left(=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\big(w(\theta)\big)\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\right)\right),

providing another equivalent form for the quantity
\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\lvert\gamma_{j}\rvert^{2})
.

Given the recent introduction of absolutely continuous nc measures, it becomes natural to ask whether an analogue of Szegő’s theorem in some form holds with the noncommutative measures of Jury and Martin in place of a measure on the unit circle. Some work in this direction exists already: a phrasing of Szegő’s theorem in terms of factorisations of symbols of Toeplitz operators admits a full generalisation to the setting of multi-Toeplitz operators on the full Fock space

Meanwhile, in

In this setting, Popescu is able to obtain a weak Szegő limit theorem relating the multi-Toeplitz determinants to these generalised Schur sequences; in

The Verblunsky coefficients (or sometimes
Schur parameters
)
(\gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
associated to a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
are perhaps the most ubiquitous tool in Szegő theory. One way to generate this association is via the
Schur algorithm
(or
coefficient stripping
), a recursive algorithm utilising the power series representation of the Schur function associated to
\mu

[
2
]
develops a Schur algorithm for Schur functions on the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
where the Verblunsky coefficients are now elements of the unit ball, while in several noncommutative variables the aforementioned work
[
26

discusses generalised Schur sequences associated to a multi-Toeplitz kernel, realised as sequences of tuples of matrices; notably, in both of these cases, the multivariate analogue of the Verblunsky coefficients are vector- or operator-valued, rather than complex-valued and inside the unit disc.

In order to produce a complex-valued (in particular,
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
-valued) analogue of Verblunsky coefficients for nc measures, then, a different method is required, and we discover our solution in
[
8

[
6
]

[
8

d

\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\times\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}

d

\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
rather than
\mathbb{N}

Classically, of course, positive measures on the unit circle correspond bijectively with positive kernels on the natural numbers by defining
K(n,m):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(n-m)\theta}\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)
: this is part of the Carathéodory–Toeplitz theorem (see, e.g., Theorem 11.3,
\mathrm{(i)}\iff\mathrm{(v)}

[

We shall see that such a correspondence does hold, with the caveat that we need to slightly modify one of the axioms of Constantinescu and Johnson’s definition — however, this modification does not affect the theory thereafter, so we retain their results for the setting with which we proceed to work.

We shall also require study of the
Christoffel function
of a measure. If
\mu
is a non-finitely atomic measure on
\mathbb{T}
,
[
33
]
defines what we shall call the Christoffel approximates of
\mu
to be

(1.4)

\lambda_{n}(\zeta,\mathrm{d}\mu):=\min\left\{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lvert\pi(e^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta):\pi\in\mathbb{C}[z],\deg(\pi)\leq n,\pi(\zeta)=1\right\}

and the Christoffel function of
\mu
is then

\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta,\mathrm{d}\mu):=\lim_{n}\lambda_{n}(\zeta,\mathrm{d}\mu)=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lvert\pi(e^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta):\pi\in\mathbb{C}[z],\pi(\zeta)=1\right\}.

This function is linked to the Christoffel-Darboux kernel of the measure,

K_{n}(z,\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\overline{\varphi_{j}(\zeta)}\varphi_{j}(z)\quad\text{ and }\quad K(z,\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\overline{\varphi_{j}(\zeta)}\varphi_{j}(z)

where
(\varphi_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
are the orthonormal polynomials associated to
\mu
, by the formula

(1.5)

\lambda_{n}(\zeta,\mathrm{d}\mu)=\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}

and it follows that

\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta,\mathrm{d}\mu)=\frac{1}{K(\zeta,\zeta)}.

A noncommutative Christoffel function is discussed in
[
5
]
in the context of bounded tracial states on the algebra of noncommutative polynomials equipped with a particular involution with respect to which the monomials
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\}
are self-adjoint. In
[
5

1.5

nc Christoffel approximates

[
5
]

Once this is done, we are able to prove a noncommutative version of

Finally, we pivot to a related problem, inspired by the

[

Indices and Notation

Herein we list the main items of notation we refer to throughout the paper. We begin with a discussion of the free monoid on
d
generators,
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, which appears often in place of
\mathbb{N}
in the free noncommutative setting. We use the convention of denoting the generators
1,\ldots,d
and the identity by
\emptyset
; elements of
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
are called
words
, and the monoid product is concatenation of words. The
length
of a word
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, denoted
\lvert\sigma\rvert
, is the number of generators used to form
\sigma
: for example,
\lvert 112\rvert=3
, and by convention we use
\lvert\emptyset\rvert=0
. Given a tuple
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
, of operators or of formal variables, we shall use the notation

Z^{\sigma}:=Z_{\sigma_{1}}\cdot\cdots\cdot Z_{\sigma_{n}}

whenever we write the word
\sigma
as
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
with
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. In other words, we write out the word
\sigma
with
Z_{j}
in place of each generator
j
appearing in
\sigma
— for example,
(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{112}=Z_{1}Z_{1}Z_{2}=Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}
. We take the convention that
Z^{\emptyset}=1
.

We shall also have cause to refer to the
short lexicographical
(shortlex) ordering on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
; when
\sigma
precedes
\tau
in this ordering, we write
\sigma\prec\tau
. Explicitly, this is the ordering obtained by declaring for generators that
1\prec\cdots\prec d
, that
\sigma\prec\tau
if
\lvert\sigma\rvert<\lvert\tau\rvert
, and that if
\lvert\sigma\rvert=\lvert\tau\rvert=n
, we say that
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\prec\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}=\tau
if there exists
j=1,\ldots,n
such that

\sigma_{1}=\tau_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j-1}=\tau_{j-1}\text{ and }\sigma_{j}\prec\tau_{j}.

For example, when
d=2
, the shortlex ordering on the first few elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+}
is

\emptyset\prec 1\prec 2\prec 11\prec 12\prec 21\prec 22\prec 111\prec 112\prec\ldots.

We denote by
\sigma(n)
the final word under the shortlex ordering of length
n
, which is the generator
d
repeated
n
times; the immediate predecessor of
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
with respect to the shortlex ordering is denoted
\sigma-1
. For example, when
d=2
,
\sigma(1)=2
,
\sigma(3)=222
,
112-1=111
and
11-1=2
.

Of course, when
d=1
, we have
\mathbb{F}_{1}^{+}=\{\emptyset,1,11,\ldots\}=\{1^{j}:j=0,1,2,\ldots\}\cong\mathbb{N}
, the shortlex ordering is the usual ordering on
\mathbb{N}
, and
\sigma(n)=n
is the only word of length
n
.

\Lambda_{n}(\cdot;\mu)
; see
Remark

5.6
.

•

\mathcal{Z}(P)
- the set of determinantal zeros of an nc polynomial
P
; see
Definition

7.3
.

•

0^{(d)}_{1\times 1}
- the

2.
Background: NC Measures

2.1.
Motivation and Definitions

Herein we briefly recall some relevant notions and definitions; much more detailed exposition on this topic can be found in, for example, Sections 2 and 3 of
[
19
]
, which we follow for much of this section.

We begin with a particular construction of some classical objects. Let
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
be the Hardy space on the disc, that is, the holomorphic function space

H^{2}(\mathbb{D})=\{f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}:\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lvert c_{n}\rvert^{2}<\infty\},

and let
S:H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
be the forward shift operator given by
(Sf)(z)=zf(z)
. Furthermore, let
A(\mathbb{D})
, the disc algebra, be the Banach algebra

A(\mathbb{D})=\left\{f:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{C}:f\text{ is coontinuous}\;{\rm and}\;f|_{\mathbb{D}}\text{ holomorphic}\right\}

equipped with the supremum norm.

Firstly, note that
S
does not satisfy any polynomial identities, and has operator norm
\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=1
, so that the map

\mathbb{C}[z]\to\{p(S):p\in\mathbb{C}[z]\},\quad z\mapsto S

is a bijective isometry. It is well-known that the disc algebra is the supremum-norm closure of
\mathbb{C}[z]
, and therefore can be realised as the operator-norm closure of the set of polynomials in
S
:

A(\mathbb{D})\cong\overline{\left\{p(S):p\in\mathbb{C}[z]\right\}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}},

that is,
A(\mathbb{D})
is the operator-norm-closure of the algebra generated by the identity and
S
.

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, any continuous, complex-valued function on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
can be uniformly approximated by polynomials in
z
and
\overline{z}
and therefore by elements of
A(\mathbb{D})+A(\mathbb{D})^{*}
(where
A(\mathbb{D})^{*}=\{\overline{f}:f\in A(\mathbb{D})\}
). From this, it follows that

C(\mathbb{T})=\overline{A(\mathbb{D})+A(\mathbb{D})^{*}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}.

By the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem, any (positive) finite, regular Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
induces a (positive) bounded linear functional on
C(\mathbb{T})
and vice versa. Hence elements of the dual of
\overline{A(\mathbb{D})+A(\mathbb{D})^{*}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}
and finite, regular Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}
are in bijection.

The noncommutative setup generalises this construction. We begin with the
d
-variate noncommutative stand-in for the complex plane, the
nc space

\mathbb{C}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}

consisting of tuples of square matrices of arbitrary size, and for the unit disc, the
row-ball

\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}:Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}<I_{n}\right\},

consisting of tuples of strict row contractions. The row-ball is a noncommutative set in the sense of
[
23
]
with matrix “levels” or “windows”

\mathbb{B}^{d}_{n}=\left\{Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}:Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}<I_{n}\right\}.

A foundational result of Popescu
[
28
]
says that any formal power series in
d
noncommuting variables with square-summable complex coefficients, that is any

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}\quad\text{ such that }\quad\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\lvert c_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}<\infty,

converges absolutely (in operator norm) on the row-ball, and uniformly on the subset
r\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
for any
0<r<1
. Such a power series can therefore be viewed as a locally bounded (hence holomorphic) nc function on the row-ball
[
23
, Chapter 8]
. On the other hand, any locally bounded nc function on the row-ball has such a power series representation (the
Taylor-Taylor series
)
[
23
, Chapter 7]
, so that the Hilbert space

\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}:=\{f(Z)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}:\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\lvert c_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}<\infty\}

of all such power series is a noncommutative version of
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Indeed,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{1}\cong H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
so that in one variable the noncommutative function theory coincides with the classical function theory.

Remark 2.1
.

The Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
arises variably as the space of holomorphic functions
\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
with square-summable Taylor series coefficients, the reproducing kernel Hilbert space corresponding to the Szegő kernel

k_{S}(z,w):=\frac{1}{1-z\overline{w}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\overline{w^{n}},\quad z,w\in\mathbb{D},

and as the holomorphic function space isomorphic as a Hilbert space to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
. The nc Hardy space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
similarly arises as the space of holomorphic nc functions described above, as the noncommutative reproducing kernel Hilbert space (in the sense of
[
4
]
) corresponding to the nc Szegő kernel

K_{S}(Z,W)[\cdot]:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}Z^{\sigma}[\cdot](W^{\sigma})^{*},\quad Z,W\in\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}},

and as the full Fock space on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

F^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d}):=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)^{\otimes k},

which is naturally isomorphic to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+})
by sending the basis element
e_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\sigma_{n}}\in F^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
to
e_{\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+})
for
\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. A detailed discussion of the different origins of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
with further references can be found in, for example,
[
22
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
be the nc Hardy space. The
left free shift
is the
d
-tuple

L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}),

where for
j=1,\ldots,d
,
L_{j}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
(left) shift operator on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
given by

(L_{j}f)(Z)=Z_{j}f(Z).

Remark 2.3
.

The left free shift’s components
L_{j},j=1,\ldots,d,
are each isometries on
\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}
, with pairwise orthogonal ranges:

L_{j}^{*}L_{k}=\delta_{jk}I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},\quad 1\leq j,k\leq d;

these conditions together make
L
a
row-isometry
, an isometry
(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})^{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
. This property will be central to our study in particular in Section 3.

Emulating the construction outlined above, the
nc disc algebra
is the operator norm-closure of the space of noncommutative polynomials evaluated on the left free shift:

\mathcal{A}_{d}:=\overline{\{p(L):p\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}\rangle\}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}};

similarly, this is the norm-closure of the algebra generated by the identity operator and the components of
L
.

We next construct the
nc disc system
from
\mathcal{A}_{d}
and the space of adjoints of elements of
\mathcal{A}_{d}
: defining
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{*}:=\{f^{*}:f\in\mathcal{A}_{d}\}
where
f^{*}:Z\mapsto f(Z)^{*}
, the nc disc system is the operator system

\mathscr{A}_{d}:=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{d}+\mathcal{A}_{d}^{*}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}.

Notice that
\mathscr{A}_{1}\cong C(\mathbb{T})
and recall that positive finite regular Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}
are in bijection with positive elements of
C(\mathbb{T})^{\dagger}
, the dual of
C(\mathbb{T})
. Motivated by this, we define an nc measure as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

A
noncommutative (nc) measure

\mu
is a positive functional on the nc disc system, i.e. a linear map

\mu:\mathscr{A}_{d}\to\mathbb{C}

such that
\mu(f)\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathscr{A}_{d}
such that
f\geq 0
. We write
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
.

2.2.
The Lebesgue Decomposition and the Radon–Nikodym Derivative

Identify
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with the subspace
H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
by identifying
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with the function
\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
given by

\widetilde{f}(\zeta)=\lim_{r\to 1}f(r\zeta),

which is defined almost everywhere
[
24
, Theorem 3.3.8]
.

An operator
T:H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is called
Toeplitz
with symbol
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
if

T=PM_{g}|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where
P
is the projection onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
and
M_{g}
is the multiplication operator induced by
g
. A famous theorem of Brown and Halmos
[
12
, Theorem 6]
says that
T
is Toeplitz if and only if the
Brown–Halmos identity
,

S^{*}TS=T

holds, where
S
is the forward shift operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This has a natural extrapolation to
d
noncommuting variables: the following definition was given by Popescu and has subsequently been studied extensively, e.g. in
[
27
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
T:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
be a bounded positive operator. We say
T
is
multi-Toeplitz
if

L_{j}^{*}TL_{k}=\delta_{jk}I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},\quad 1\leq j,k\leq d,

i.e. if
T
satisfies
d
separate Brown–Halmos identities.

In
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
, Jury and Martin study a related class of operators.

Definition 2.6

(
[
20
]
, Definition 4.4)
.

A closed, positive, densely-defined operator
T:\mathcal{D}(T)\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is called
left-
or
L
-Toeplitz
if

(1)

\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})
is
L
-invariant, i.e.
L_{j}\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, and

(2)

the associated closed quadratic form

q_{T}(g,h):=\langle\sqrt{T}g,\sqrt{T}h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},\quad g,h\in\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})

satisfies

q_{T}(L_{j}g,L_{k}h)=\delta_{jk}q_{T}(g,h),\quad g,h\in\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T}).

Remark 2.7
.

The connection between these notions can be found in
[
19
, Remark 6]
: if
T:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is bounded, then
T
is
L
-Toeplitz if and only if
T
is multi-Toeplitz. In this sense, one may consider
L
-Toeplitz operators an unbounded generalisation of multi-Toeplitz operators.

Building on the Lebesgue decomposition for (positive semi-definite) quadratic forms of Simon
[
32
]
, Jury and Martin prove a decomposition (with respect to the nc Lebesgue measure) for nc measures
[
19
, Theorem 4]
: if
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
, there exists a unique decomposition

\mu=\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}

where
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}},\mu_{\mathrm{s}}\leq\mu
are nc measures,
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
is the maximal absolutely continuous (with respect to the nc Lebesgue measure) nc measure bounded above by
\mu
, and
\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
is a singular (again with respect to the nc Lebesgue measure) nc measure; here a functional
\nu
is absolutely continuous (resp. singular) if the quadratic form
q_{\nu}(a_{1},a_{2}):=\nu(a_{1}^{*}a_{2})

[
19
, Equation (4.3)]
on
\mathcal{A}_{d}\times\mathcal{A}_{d}
is absolutely continuous (resp. singular) in the sense of
[
32
]
.

Moreover,
[
19
, Theorem 5, Lemma 2]
if
\mu
is an absolutely continuous nc measure, then there exists a unique closed, densely-defined positive
L
-Toeplitz operator
T:\mathcal{D}(T)\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
such that
\mathcal{A}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})
and
[
19
, Equation (6.1)]

(2.1)

\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}(a_{2}^{*}a_{1})=\langle\sqrt{T}a_{1},\sqrt{T}a_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},\quad a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be an nc measure. The
nc Radon–Nikodym derivative
of
\mu
is the closed, densely-defined positive
L
-Toeplitz operator
T
given by (
2.1
), which will in general be unbounded.

As Jury and Martin elaborate in
[
19
, Section 6]
, this operator
T
can be thought of as a noncommutative Radon–Nikodym derivative for the nc measure
\mu
, see the discussion preceding Theorem 6.2. In this way, we may think of closed, densely-defined
L
-Toeplitz operators on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
as corresponding to absolutely continuous nc measures; hence the theory e.g. surrounding the factorisation results relevant to Szegő’s theorem in
[
27
]
can be re-cast in terms of absolutely continuous nc measures.

In the next section we offer another relationship along these lines, associating to any nc measure a multi-Toeplitz
kernel
on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, though this kernel may no longer define an unbounded operator on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
.

2.3.
The Univariate Setting

We stress that this function theory is a genuinely multivariate phenomenon.

When
d=1
, the nc Hardy space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{1}
coincides with the usual Hardy space on the disc:

\mathbb{H}^{2}_{1}=\{f(Z)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{1}^{+}}c_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}:\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{1}^{+}}\lvert c_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}<\infty\}=\{f(Z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}Z^{n}:\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lvert c_{n}\rvert^{2}<\infty\}=H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

It follows that any nc function on the row-ball in one variable,
\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\mathrm{nc}}
, is completely determined by its behaviour on the scalar level
\mathbb{B}^{1}_{1}=\mathbb{D}
, and the holomorphic function theory in this case reduces to the classical theory.

Moreover, in a similar fashion, recall that by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem
\mathscr{A}_{1}\cong C(\mathbb{T})
. Hence nc measures in one variable are simply positive functionals on
C(\mathbb{T})
, and by the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem, such functionals correspond precisely to finite, positive, regular Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}
so that again we recover the classical theory.

We remark that Verblunsky coefficients, orthogonal polynomials, and Szegő limit theorems have all been studied in the univariate, matrix-valued setting, see e.g.
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. However, in this setting, the candidate measures are no longer scalar-valued but rather matrix-valued, and so this theory generalises the classical Szegő theory in a different direction to that of nc measures.

3.
The Correspondence Between NC Measures and Multi-Toeplitz Kernels

Definition 3.1
.

Generalising the case where
d=1
, we say that a kernel
K:\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\times\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is
positive
if the matrix
K_{\sigma_{0}}:=[K(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is positive semi-definite for all
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. For example, if
d=2
, then

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+}=\{\emptyset,1,2,11,12,21,22,111,112,121,\ldots\}

(here we have listed the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{+}
according to the shortlex ordering) and so e.g.:

\displaystyle K_{\emptyset}=\begin{bmatrix}K(\emptyset,\emptyset)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle K_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}K(\emptyset,\emptyset)&K(\emptyset,1)\\
K(1,\emptyset)&K(1,1)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle K_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}K(\emptyset,\emptyset)&K(\emptyset,1)&K(\emptyset,2)\\
K(1,\emptyset)&K(1,1)&K(1,2)\\
K(2,\emptyset)&K(2,1)&K(2,2)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\text{and }K_{11}=\begin{bmatrix}K(\emptyset,\emptyset)&K(\emptyset,1)&K(\emptyset,2)&K(\emptyset,11)\\
K(1,\emptyset)&K(1,1)&K(1,2)&K(1,11)\\
K(2,\emptyset)&K(2,1)&K(2,2)&K(2,11)\\
K(11,\emptyset)&K(11,1)&K(11,2)&K(11,11)\end{bmatrix}.

In
[
8
]
, the authors study a class of positive kernels
K
indexed by elements of
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, satisfying two additional axioms:

(3.1)

K(\tau\sigma,\tau\sigma^{\prime})=K(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),\quad\tau,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{+}_{d},

(3.2)

K(\sigma,\tau)=0\quad\text{ if there is no $\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}$ such that $\sigma=\alpha\tau$ or $\tau=\alpha\sigma$.}

The former, (
3.1
), is a generalisation of the notion of Toeplitzness, or of shift-invariance: in one variable, this becomes

K(n+k,m+k)=K(n,m)

so that our kernel is constant along each superdiagonal. On the other hand, the condition for (
3.2
) (and indeed for (
3.4
) below) never occurs in one variable, as there is always some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n=m+k
or
m=n+k
, so this phenomenon is genuinely multivariate.

Given a positive kernel satisfying (
3.1
) and (
3.2
),
[
8
]
goes on to derive sets of Verblunsky coefficients and polynomials which are orthonormal with respect to the kernel, both of which are also indexed by noncommutative words. This approach, based in the matrix theory of
[
6
]
, reproduces the classical theory of Verblunsky coefficients and orthogonal polynomials when
d=1
.

Meanwhile, Jury and Martin (in, e.g.,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
) recently established a theory of noncommutative measures which also reduces to the classical theory in one variable. When
d=1
, kernels and measures enjoy a natural one-to-one correspondence, which leads one naturally to question whether this still holds in several noncommuting variables. As we shall see, nc measures and the kernels studied by
[
8
]
do not correspond directly, but nc measures
do
correspond to a very similar class of kernels. Motivated by
Theorem

3.8
, we shall be interested instead in so-called
multi-Toeplitz
kernels.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a positive kernel
K:\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\times\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is
multi-Toeplitz
if
K
satisfies the axioms

(3.3)

K(\tau\sigma,\tau\sigma^{\prime})=K(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),\quad\tau,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{+}_{d},

(3.4)

K(\sigma,\tau)=0\quad\text{ if there is no $\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}$ such that $\sigma=\tau\alpha$ or $\tau=\sigma\alpha$.}

Definition 3.3
.

Let
K
be a multi-Toeplitz kernel. The
multi-Toeplitz determinants
of
K
are the determinants

D_{\sigma_{0}}:=\det\begin{bmatrix}K(\sigma,\tau)\end{bmatrix}_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}=\det K_{\sigma_{0}}

for
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
.

Multi-Toeplitz kernels are, of course, very similar to those of
[
8
]
, with only a “twisted” version of (
3.2
), and so almost all of the theory established therein will apply also to these modified kernels. Critically, though, we shall see that this class corresponds precisely to that of nc measures in exactly the way measures and kernels correspond in one variable (indeed, in one variable, our correspondence reduces precisely to the classical one).

Remark 3.4
.

As discussed across the previous two sections, multi-Toeplitz kernels have been studied by a number of authors: they are the focus of numerous works of Popescu such as
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
, and (when they define bounded operators on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
) they appear in Jury and Martin’s works
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
, where they are called
L-Toeplitz
. Constantinescu and Johnson have also studied multi-Toeplitz kernels, mentioning them explicitly in
[
7
]
(though defined via (
3.2
) rather than (
3.4
)).

Remark 3.5
.

We recall that
[
26
]
also considers objects associated to a multi-Toeplitz kernel analogous to the classical Verblunsky coefficients — there dubbed
generalised Schur sequences
— but that this construction differs from the perspective presented herein in several key ways. In particular, the generalised Schur sequences of
[
26
]
are genuine sequences (that is, indexed by
\mathbb{N}
) of tuples of matrices, whereas the Verblunsky coefficients we consider here are more generally a family (indexed by
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
) of single complex numbers inside the unit disc.

We formulate the first direction of our correspondence as follows.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be an nc measure, and define

K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau):=\mu\big((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma}\big)\text{ for words $\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}$,}

where
L
is the left free shift (see
Definition

2.2
).
Then
K_{\mu}
is a multi-Toeplitz kernel.

Proof.

For
k,j=1,\ldots,d
, we have the identity
L_{k}^{*}L_{j}=\delta_{k,j}I
from
[
19
]
, so for
\tau,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
where
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
,
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we see that

\displaystyle K_{\mu}(\tau\sigma,\tau\sigma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mu\big((L^{\tau\sigma^{\prime}})^{*}L^{\tau\sigma}\big)=\mu\big((L^{\tau}L^{\sigma^{\prime}})^{*}L^{\tau}L^{\sigma}\big)=\mu\big((L^{\sigma^{\prime}})^{*}(L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\tau}L^{\sigma}\big)

\displaystyle=\mu\big((L^{\sigma^{\prime}})^{*}L_{\tau_{m}}^{*}\cdots L_{\tau_{1}}^{*}L_{\tau_{1}}\cdots L_{\tau_{m}}L^{\sigma}\big)=\mu\big((L^{\sigma^{\prime}})^{*}L^{\sigma}\big)

\displaystyle=K_{\mu}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),

so
K_{\mu}
satisfies (
3.3
).

Now, suppose that
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
are such that there is no
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
such that
\sigma=\tau\alpha
or
\tau=\sigma\alpha
. If we write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}
and
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}
for
\sigma_{j},\tau_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
(supposing without loss of generality that
n\leq m
), then this means that
\sigma_{j_{0}}\neq\tau_{j_{0}}
for some
j_{0}=0,\ldots,n
: otherwise, we would have
\sigma=\tau\sigma_{n+1}\cdots\sigma_{m}
, contradicting the hypothesis. It follows that
\delta_{\tau_{j_{0}},\sigma_{j_{0}}}=0
, and we then have from
L_{k}^{*}L_{j}=\delta_{k,j}I
that

\displaystyle K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mu\big((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma}\big)=K_{\mu}\big(L_{\tau_{n}}^{*}\cdots L_{\tau_{1}}^{*}L_{\sigma_{1}}\cdots L_{\sigma_{m}}\big)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\tau_{1},\sigma_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}}\mu\big(L_{\sigma_{n+1}}\cdots L_{\sigma_{m}}\big)

\displaystyle=0,

so
K_{\mu}
satisfies (
3.4
).

It remains to show that
K_{\mu}
is positive, i.e. that the square matrix
[K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is positive semi-definite for any
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. Fix
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
and let
n
be its position under the (graded) lexicographical (shortlex) ordering, so that
[K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is
n\times n
, and let
x=[x_{\emptyset},\cdots,x_{\sigma_{0}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus{\{0\}}
. We see that

\displaystyle x^{*}[K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}x

\displaystyle=x^{*}\left[\sum_{\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)x_{\tau}\right]_{\sigma\preceq\sigma_{0}}=\sum_{\sigma\preceq\sigma_{0}}\overline{x_{\sigma}}\sum_{\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)x_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)\overline{x_{\sigma}}x_{\tau}=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}\mu\big((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma})\overline{x_{\sigma}}x_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\sum_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}(\overline{x_{\tau}}L^{\tau})^{*}(\overline{x_{\sigma}}L^{\sigma})\right)=\mu\left(\sum_{\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}(\overline{x_{\tau}}L^{\tau})^{*}\sum_{\sigma\preceq\sigma_{0}}\overline{x_{\sigma}}L^{\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\left(\sum_{\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}\overline{x_{\tau}}L^{\tau}\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{\sigma\preceq\sigma_{0}}\overline{x_{\sigma}}L^{\sigma}\right)\right),

so that setting
y:=\sum_{\sigma\preceq\sigma_{0}}\overline{x_{\sigma}}L^{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
(as a polynomial in
L
) we have that
y\neq 0
and therefore

x^{*}[K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}x=\mu(y^{*}y)\geq 0,

simply since
\mu
is positive by definition. Thus
[K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)]_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is positive semi-definite and hence
K_{\mu}
is multi-Toeplitz.
∎

Classically this association is bijective, and we can show that this remains the case in the noncommutative theory.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
K
be a multi-Toeplitz kernel. For
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, define

\mu_{K}(L^{\sigma}):=K(\sigma,\emptyset)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{K}\big((L^{\sigma})^{*}\big):=K(\emptyset,\sigma)=\overline{K(\sigma,\emptyset)}.

Then
\mu_{K}
extends to a unique positive nc measure in
(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
, which we also denote by
\mu_{K}
.

Proof.

This argument hinges on a result of
[
17
]
, namely, Lemma 4.6, that the positive elements of the nc disk system are norm limits of sums of (Hermitian) squares; though we do not apply it directly, it is also worth mentioning the foundational
[
13
]
at this point as a canonical Positivestellensatz in the free noncommutative setting.

By definition, we have
\mathscr{A}_{d}=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{d}+\mathcal{A}_{d}^{*}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{d}=\overline{\mathrm{Alg}\{I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\}}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}
. Extension by linearity and continuity therefore yields continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{A}_{d}
and
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{*}
, and then extending by linearity and continuity a second time we obtain a continuous linear functional on
\mathscr{A}_{d}
; we denote this functional by
\mu_{K}
.

It remains to show that
\mu_{K}
is positive, so let
f\in\mathscr{A}_{d}
be a positive element. Then by the aforementioned
[
17
, Lemma 4.6]
, there exists
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{d}
such that
f=\sum_{k}a_{k}^{*}a_{k}
, where by the infinite sum we mean the norm-limit of the partial sums. Since each
a_{k}
is in
\mathcal{A}_{d}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
polynomials

(p^{(k)}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{k}=\lim_{j}p^{(k)}_{j}
. Each of these polynomials can be written in the usual way as

p^{(k)}_{j}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}L^{\sigma}

where the coefficients
c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}
are eventually all zero. It follows that

\displaystyle\left(p^{(k)}_{j}\right)^{*}p^{(k)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}L^{\sigma}\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}L^{\sigma}\right)=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\overline{c}^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}(L^{\sigma})^{*}\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}L^{\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\overline{c}^{(k)}_{j,\tau}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}(L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma}.

For some fixed
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, if we write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}
and
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
, then
L_{k}^{*}L_{j}=\delta_{k,j}I
implies that

(L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma}=\begin{cases}\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}(L^{\tau_{n+1}\cdots\tau_{m}})^{*},\quad\text{ if $m>n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}I,\quad\text{ if $m=n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{m},\tau_{m}}L^{\sigma_{m+1}\cdots\sigma_{n}},\quad\text{ if $m<n$.}\end{cases}

Applying
\mu_{K}
yields

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu_{k}((L^{\sigma})^{*}L^{\tau})&=\begin{cases}\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}\mu_{K}\big((L^{\tau_{n+1}\cdots\tau_{m}})^{*}\big),\quad\text{ if $m>n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}\mu_{K}(I),\quad\text{ if $m=n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{m},\tau_{m}}\mu_{K}(L^{\sigma_{m+1}\cdots\sigma_{n}}),\quad\text{ if $m<n$.}\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}K(\emptyset,\tau_{n+1}\cdots\tau_{m}),\quad\text{ if $m>n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}K(\emptyset,\emptyset),\quad\text{ if $m=n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{m},\tau_{m}}K(\sigma_{m+1}\cdots\sigma_{n},\emptyset),\quad\text{ if $m<n$.}\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}K(\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n},\tau),\quad\text{ if $m>n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}}\cdot 1,\quad\text{ if $m=n$;}\\
\delta_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\sigma_{m},\tau_{m}}K(\sigma,\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}),\quad\text{ if $m<n$.}\end{cases}\end{split}

where the final line holds by (
3.3
). Notice now that this is zero if, for some
j=1,\ldots,\min\{m,n\}
, we have
\delta_{\sigma_{j},\tau_{j}}=0
, or in other words, if there does not exist
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
such that
\sigma=\tau\alpha
or that
\tau=\sigma\alpha
; by (
3.4
), this is also the value of
K(\sigma,\tau)
.

Alternatively, suppose such an
\alpha
does exist: by comparing successive letters of the words
\sigma
and
\tau
, we have
\tau=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}
if
m<n
or
\sigma=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}
if
m>n
; if
m=n
then
\sigma=\tau
, and hence
K(\sigma,\tau)=1
. In this case (
3.5
) transforms into

\displaystyle\mu_{k}((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\cdot K(\sigma,\tau),\quad\text{ if $m<n$;}\\
1,\quad\text{ if $m=n$;}\\
1\cdot K(\sigma,\tau),\quad\text{ if $m>n$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=K(\sigma,\tau).

Hence in every case we have

\mu_{k}((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma})=K(\sigma,\tau).

Now, denote the highest
\sigma
such that
c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}\neq 0
by
\sigma^{(k)}_{j}
. Applying linearity of
\mu_{K}
, we now return to our previous calculation to see that

\displaystyle\mu_{K}\left(\left(p^{(k)}_{j}\right)^{*}p^{(k)}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\overline{c}^{(k)}_{j,\tau}c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}K(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{col}\left\{c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}\right\}_{\sigma\preceq\sigma^{(k)}_{j}}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}K(\sigma,\tau)\end{bmatrix}_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma^{(k)}_{j}}\mathrm{col}\left\{c^{(k)}_{j,\sigma}\right\}_{\sigma\preceq\sigma^{(k)}_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

by virtue of
K
being positive. Finally, since
\mu_{K}
is continuous by construction, we can see that
\mu_{K}(f)
is positive:

\displaystyle\mu_{K}(f)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\sum_{k}a_{k}^{*}a_{k}\right)=\sum_{k}\mu\left((\lim_{j}p^{(k)}_{j})^{*}(\lim_{j}p^{(k)}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\lim_{j}\mu\left(\left(p^{(k)}_{j}\right)^{*}p^{(k)}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0

as a limit of a sum of limits of positive objects. Therefore
\mu_{K}
is a positive functional on
\mathscr{A}_{d}
, i.e.
\mu_{K}\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
.
∎

To summarise, we have the following correspondence between multi-Toeplitz kernels and nc measures.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
K
be a multi-Toeplitz kernel and define a functional
\mu_{K}
on
\mathscr{A}_{d}
via

\mu_{K}(L^{\sigma}):=K(\sigma,\emptyset)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{K}\big((L^{\sigma})^{*}\big):=K(\emptyset,\sigma)=\overline{K(\sigma,\emptyset)}

and by extending by linearity and continuity. Then
\mu_{K}
is an nc measure.

Symmetrically, let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be an nc measure and define a kernel on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
by

(3.6)

K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau):=\mu\big((L^{\tau})^{*}L^{\sigma}\big).

Then
K_{\mu}
is a multi-Toeplitz kernel.

Thus the formula (
3.6
) defines a bijection between the class of multi-Toeplitz kernels on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
and
(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from
Lemma

3.7
and the second follows from
Lemma

3.6
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

When
d=1
, (
3.6
) reduces to precisely the classical formula linking a measure on the unit circle and a kernel indexed by
\mathbb{N}
, i.e.

K(n,m)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(n-m)\theta}\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta),

since the forward shift
S
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
satisfies
S^{*}S=I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
.

4.
Verblunsky Coefficients and Orthogonal Polynomials

Constantinescu and Johnson’s paper
[
8
]
associates to a positive kernel satisfying (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) a set of
Schur parameters
,
(\gamma_{\sigma,\tau})_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, though we prefer the nomenclature
Verblunsky coefficients
following Simon
[
33
]
.

This two-parameter family of complex numbers in the unit disk is constructed via a process outlined in detail in
[
6
]
which relies on the theory of integer-indexed kernels, which we can connect to a noncommutatively-indexed kernel via the short lexicographical ordering. It should be noted that this construction is completely different to the classical derivations of Verblunsky coefficients corresponding to a measure on the unit circle (i.e. the Schur algorithm or the Szegő recurrences); however, when
d=1
they coincide as remarked upon by Constantinescu
[
6
]
, and as such this process is an appealing generalisation to apply to our setting.

Crucially, constructing the Verblunsky coefficients
(\gamma_{\sigma,\tau})_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
of a positive kernel on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
does not at all rely on the axioms (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), and as such, we can derive Verblunsky coefficients for a multi-Toeplitz kernel in an identical fashion. Moreover, these Verblunsky coefficients can be reduced to a one-parameter family via (2.9) in
[
8
]
:

(4.1)

[\gamma_{\sigma,\tau}]_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=j,\lvert\tau\rvert=k}=([\gamma_{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}}]_{\lvert\sigma^{\prime}\rvert=j-1,\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert=k-1})^{\oplus d},\quad j,k\geq 1;

using this, one can build any
\gamma_{\sigma,\tau}
from the base, single-indexed data
\gamma_{\sigma}:=\gamma_{\emptyset,\sigma}
(and the fact that
\gamma_{\tau,\sigma}=\overline{\gamma_{\sigma,\tau}}
). Now, (
4.1
) does rely on (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), but we can perform the same procedure using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), so that the same notion holds for multi-Toeplitz kernels.

Combined with our correspondence in Section 2, this construction allows us to define the Verblunsky coefficients of a positive nc measure with any
d\geq 1
:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mu
be an nc measure and let its corresponding multi-Toeplitz kernel be
K_{\mu}
. Denote by
(\gamma_{\sigma,\tau})_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
the Verblunsky coefficients arising from
K_{\mu}
by considering
K_{\mu}
as a kernel on
\mathbb{N}
via the shortlex ordering, applying
[
6
, Theorem 1.5.3]
, and then pulling back again via the shortlex ordering. The
Verblunsky coefficients
of
\mu
are the family
(\gamma_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
where
\gamma_{\sigma}:=\gamma_{\emptyset,\sigma}
is the one-parameter family determining all of
(\gamma_{\sigma,\tau})_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
via (
4.1
).

In parallel to Verblunsky coefficients,
[
8
]
uses a kernel
K
to define an inner product on nc polynomials just as one would in the commutative univariate setting: given nc polynomials
P(Z)=\sum_{\sigma}a_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}
and
Q(Z)=\sum_{\sigma}b_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}
, define

\langle P,Q\rangle_{K}:=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}K(\sigma,\tau)a_{\sigma}\overline{b_{\tau}}.

A quick calculation, very similar to one in our proof of
Lemma

3.6
, shows that
\langle P,Q\rangle_{K}
is also equal to
\mu_{K}(Q^{*}P)
; this is in some sense the expected inner product arising from an nc measure — indeed, a very similar construction using linear functionals appears in the related work
[
5
]
. Constantinescu and Johnson
[
8
]
then construct the Hilbert space
L^{2}(K)
by factoring out the subspace
\{P\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle:\langle P,P\rangle_{K}=0\}
from the space
\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
of nc polynomials in
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}
with complex coefficients and completing with respect to the resulting norm

\left\lVert P\right\rVert_{K}=\sqrt{\langle P,P\rangle_{K}}=\sqrt{\mu_{K}(P^{*}P)}.

If we assume that
\begin{bmatrix}K(\sigma,\tau)\end{bmatrix}_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is invertible for all
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, then this subspace is zero, in which case we can view nc polynomials as elements of
L^{2}(K)
directly, and the set
\{Z^{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\}
is linearly independent in this Hilbert space. Again we emphasise that none of this relies on axioms (
3.1
) or (
3.2
), only positivity of
K
.

Remark 4.2
.

In this remark we motivate this assumption via the univariate setting.

In the case
d=1
, set
K_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}K(k,l)\end{bmatrix}_{k,l\leq n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
K_{n_{0}}
is not invertible for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, then it has a nonzero vector in its kernel, say
x
. As noted before, when
d=1
axiom (
3.1
) is simply that
K
is Toeplitz, so we have

K_{n+1}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{n}&b\\
b^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}

and by assumption
K_{n+1}
is positive semi-definite. Smuljan’s Lemma
[
39
]
now implies the existence of some matrix
W
such that
b=K_{n}W
and
c\succeq W^{*}K_{n}W
. Positive semi-definite matrices are in particular Hermitian, so we have

K_{n+1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{n}&b\\
b^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{n}x\\
b^{*}x\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{n}x\\
W^{*}K_{n}^{*}x\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{n}x\\
W^{*}K_{n}x\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}

and hence
K_{n+1}
is also not invertible. Indeed, we could repeat this process indefinitely; hence once
K_{n}
is not invertible for some
n
, it turns out that
K_{m}
is not invertible for all
m\geq n
.

In the commutative theory, this implies that the measure
\mu_{K}
on the unit circle is finitely atomic
[
31
, Theorem 11.3]
(and hence singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure); moreover, finitely atomic measures have finitely many Verblunsky coefficients strictly inside the unit disc
[
33
, Section 1.3.6]
. This is a situation we wish to avoid: for example, such a measure has no absolutely continuous part, so statements we are interested in (such as Szegő’s theorem itself) are built around measures where this does not happen, and as another, if some Verblunsky coefficient has
\lvert\gamma_{n}\rvert=1
, then
\lvert\gamma_{m}\rvert=1
for all
m\geq n
, so statements about the square-summability of the Verblunsky coefficients become trivial and the product in
Theorem

1.1
(iv) becomes zero. The condition that an nc measure is
non-trivial
in the sense we shall define shortly, then, is a noncommutative generalisation of a measure not being finitely atomic, which avoids this less desirable setting.

From here on, we shall assume that our multi-Toeplitz kernels satisfy this invertibility property, and introduce the corresponding term for nc measures, motivated by the terminology of
[
33
]
in the classical setting.

Definition 4.3
.

We say that an nc measure
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
is
non-trivial
if its corresponding multi-Toeplitz kernel
K_{\mu}
has that
\begin{bmatrix}K_{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)\end{bmatrix}_{\sigma,\tau\preceq\sigma_{0}}
is invertible for all
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
.

In the classical setting, the measure
\mu
must often be a probability measure. We have a corresponding notion in the free noncommutative setting.

Remark 4.4
.

Let
K
be a multi-Toeplitz kernel. By (
3.3
), we have that

K(\sigma,\sigma)=K(\emptyset,\emptyset)

for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, so that multi-Toeplitz kernels are constant along the “diagonal” of
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\times\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. Morever, encoded in the setting of
[
8
]
is the implicit assumption that their kernels are
normalised
, i.e. that
K(\emptyset,\emptyset)=1
and hence
K(\sigma,\sigma)=1
for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
— this assumption can be seen in, e.g., the example following Theorem 3.1 of
[
8
]
, and arises from the application of the Schur parameter theory of
[
6
]
. In particular,
[
6
, Theorem 1.5.3]
makes this assumption, and it is with this theorem that
[
8
]
associates the Verblunsky coefficients to a kernel.

For this reason, we also assume from here on that our multi-Toeplitz kernels are normalised. Indeed, in some places, such as
[
26
]
, this is part of the definition of a multi-Toeplitz kernel. Taking into account our
Theorem

3.8
, this has a natural analogue in the setting of nc measures: if
\mu_{K}
is the nc measure corresponding to
K
, then

K(\emptyset,\emptyset)=\mu_{K}\left(\big(L^{\emptyset}\big)^{*}L^{\emptyset}\right)=\mu_{K}(I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}).

Recall that when
d=1
,
\mu_{K}
is the integration functional on
C(\mathbb{T})
with respect to some measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{K}
, so that
\mu(1)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}_{K}
. Hence
K
being normalised is equivalent to
\widetilde{\mu}_{K}
being a probability measure.

This motivates the next definition.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be an nc measure. We say that
\mu
is a
probability nc measure
if
\mu(I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}})=1
.

As
K
is implicitly always normalised in
[
8
]
, we shall assert this condition in much of the remainder of the paper.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial nc measure. The space
L^{2}(\mu)
is the Hilbert space gained by completing the space
\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
of nc polynomials with respect to the norm

\left\lVert P\right\rVert_{\mu}:=\sqrt{\mu(P^{*}P)},

and has inner product

\langle P,Q\rangle_{\mu}:=\mu(Q^{*}P).

Next, the authors of
[
8
]
go on to apply Gram-Schmidt to the (linearly independent) set of monomials
\{Z^{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{K}
, ordered by the short lexicographical ordering. They obtain nc polynomials
\{\varphi_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\}
which are orthonormal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{K}
; much of the remainder of the paper is devoted to exploring consequences of these nc orthonormal polynomials and their relationship to the Verblunsky coefficients of
K
. In particular, they derive recurrence relations for the orthonormal polynomials in Theorem 3.2 that compare well with the classical Szegő recurrences, in the process acquiring a set of “reverse polynomials”, though these are defined via these recurrence relations rather than through an explicit formula, as one has in the commutative case.

An exploration of commutative Szegő theory, as in e.g.
[
33
]
, quickly finds a heavy reliance both on orthonormal polynomials and on monic orthogonal polynomials, which are themselves the orthonormal polynomials rescaled to be monic. However,
[
8
]
focuses on other topics, omitting any consideration of noncommutative monic orthogonal polynomials. The work already present in
[
8
]
does, however, lead quickly to several observations if we
do
define these polynomials.

Definition 4.7
.

Given a non-trivial probability nc measure
\mu
, we perform Gram-Schmidt in
L^{2}(\mu)
on the set
\{Z^{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\}
with respect to the shortlex ordering. Omitting normalisation associates to
\mu
by definition a family of
orthogonal monic polynomials
, which we denote
(\Phi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, while normalising gives a family of
orthonormal polynomials
, which we denote
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure and let
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
be the orthonormal polynomials associated to
\mu
. For
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, the coefficients of
\varphi_{\sigma}
shall be denoted by
(a_{\sigma,\tau})_{\emptyset\preceq\tau\preceq\sigma}
so that we may write

\varphi_{\sigma}=\sum_{\emptyset\preceq\tau\preceq\sigma}a_{\sigma,\tau}Z^{\tau}.

Remark 4.9
.

By considering the normalisation at each stage of Gram-Schmidt, the monic orthogonal polynomials are related to the orthonormal polynomials via
\Phi_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\varphi_{\sigma}
.

This observation allows us to note a number of facts about these polynomials. Firstly, since
\varphi_{\sigma}
has
\mu
-norm 1, the
\mu
-norm of
\Phi_{\sigma}
is
\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}
. We then have by
[
8
, Theorem 3.1(2)]
that (for
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}
)

\left\lVert\Phi_{\sigma}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}^{2}}=\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma_{j}\cdots\sigma_{k}}\rvert^{2}).

An equality just like this appears in
[
33
]
for the norm of the monic orthogonal polynomials. However, a subtle point to note is that in one variable, this product is in fact over
all
Verblunsky coefficients up to a certain index, whereas for us, this product is over only a particular subset of the Verblunsky coefficients preceding
\sigma
.

Perhaps more importantly, we can use the recurrence relations for
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
of
[
8
, Theorem 3.2]
, i.e.

(4.2)

\varphi_{k\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lvert\gamma_{k\sigma}}\rvert^{2}}(Z_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}-\gamma_{k\sigma}\varphi_{k\sigma-1}^{\#})

and

(4.3)

\varphi_{k\sigma}^{\#}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lvert\gamma_{k\sigma}\rvert^{2}}}(-\overline{\gamma_{k\sigma}}Z_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}+\varphi_{k\sigma-1}^{\#}),

to derive corresponding ones for the monic polynomials.

Remark 4.10
.

These recurrences reduce to the classical relations one finds in
[
33
]
in the case
d=1
due to the fact that
k\sigma-1=\sigma
in this case. However, there is a subtlety when
d>1
not present in the
d=1
setting: as
k\sigma-1
no longer equals
\sigma
in general, on the right hand side of both (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) we note that the index of the orthonormal polynomial differs from the index of the reverse polynomial, which may in some sense be surprising from the univariate theory.

Remark 4.11
.

It should be noted that we have some freedom to make a definition here: as the reverse polynomials of
[
8
]
are defined via recurrence relations rather than a general direct formula as we have in the classical theory, the noncommutative reverse polynomials of the monic orthogonal polynomials are not immediately determined by those of the orthonormal polynomials. The most natural thing to do would be to follow the behaviour observed classically, that is, if
\varphi_{n}(z)=\sum_{j}a_{j}z^{j}
then
\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{a_{n}}\varphi^{*}_{n}(z)
. Making a definition analogous to this phenomenon, we can obtain recurrence relations for these orthogonal polynomials from those of the orthonormal ones.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with monic orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
and orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
and let the leading coefficient of
\varphi_{\sigma}
be
a_{\sigma,\sigma}
. The
reverse polynomials
of the orthonormal polynomials are the polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma}^{\#})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
appearing in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), and for
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, we define the
reverse polynomial
of
\Phi_{\sigma}
to be
\Phi_{\sigma}^{\#}:=\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\varphi_{\sigma}^{\#}
.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with associated monic orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. We have the following analogue of the Szegő recurrence relations:

(4.4)

\Phi_{k\sigma}=Z_{k}\Phi_{\sigma}-\gamma_{k\sigma}\frac{a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}

and

(4.5)

\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma}=-\overline{\gamma_{k\sigma}}Z_{k}\Phi_{\sigma}+\frac{a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}.

Proof.

This follows readily from substitution of our definitions into the recurrence relations (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) and some algebraic observations. Recall from an equation in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
that for
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{N}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+},k=1,\ldots,d
we have

a_{\sigma,\sigma}=\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma_{j}\cdots\sigma_{N}}\rvert^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}};

for
\tau=k\sigma
we can write
\tau=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{N+1}=k\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{N}
, so that

\displaystyle a_{k\sigma,k\sigma}=a_{\tau,\tau}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq j\leq N+1}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\tau_{j}\cdots\tau_{N+1}}\rvert^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=(1-\lvert\gamma_{k\sigma}\rvert^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\prod_{2\leq j\leq N+1}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\tau_{j}\cdots\tau_{N+1}}\rvert^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{k\sigma}}\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma_{j}\cdots\sigma_{N}}\rvert^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}{d_{k\sigma}}.

Now, substituting
\Phi_{\sigma}=a_{\sigma,\sigma}\varphi_{\sigma}
and
\Phi_{\sigma}^{\#}=a_{\sigma,\sigma}\varphi_{\sigma}^{\#}
into the Szegő recurrences for the orthonormal polynomials, we obtain from (
4.2
) that

d_{k\sigma}a_{k\sigma,k\sigma}\Phi_{k\sigma}=a_{\sigma,\sigma}Z_{k}\Phi_{s}-\gamma_{k\sigma}a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}\Phi_{k\sigma-1}^{\#}

and with the above computation it follows that

\Phi_{k\sigma}=Z_{k}\Phi_{\sigma}-\gamma_{k\sigma}\frac{a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}.

Similarly, (
4.3
) becomes

d_{k\sigma}a_{k\sigma,k\sigma}\Phi_{k\sigma}^{\#}=-\overline{\gamma_{k\sigma}}a_{\sigma,\sigma}Z_{k}\Phi_{\sigma}+a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}\Phi_{k\sigma-1}^{\#}

which reveals the identity

\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma}=-\overline{\gamma_{k\sigma}}Z_{k}\Phi_{\sigma}+\frac{a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\Phi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}.

∎

Remark 4.14
.

The factor of
\frac{a_{k\sigma-1,k\sigma-1}}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}
appearing in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) is trivially always 1 when
d=1
, and in that case these compare precisely to the Szegő recurrence relations (see
[
33
]
with a relabelling of the Verblunsky coefficients).

Remark 4.15
.

The structure of the recurrence relations (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) (and similarly (
4.4
) and (
4.5
)) are twofold. Firstly, for
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
we obtain a relationship between
\varphi_{\sigma}
and
\varphi_{\sigma+1}
, i.e. between immediately shortlex-successive orthonormal polynomials, and secondly, we see a relationship between
\varphi_{\sigma}
and
\varphi_{k\sigma}
for each
k=1,\ldots,d
. In this way these recurrences encode a relationship that intertwines the monoidal and order structures on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
.

What we now have is a way to associate Verblunsky coefficients and families of both monic orthogonal and orthonormal polynomials to any non-trivial probability nc measure, up to and including recurrence relations for both families of polynomials. As these polynomials are used extensively in the classical theory, this puts us in a strong position to explore analogues of those classical results in
d
noncommuting variables.

Finally, in the commutative theory, Verblunsky’s theorem (or Favard’s theorem on the unit circle) provides the converse of this statement: given a candidate sequence
(\eta_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
in the unit disc, there exists a measure
\mu
on the unit circle whose Verblunsky coefficients are
\gamma_{n}=\eta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
, and when all of these lie strictly inside the disc,
\mu
is unique. In
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
we see the equivalent result for our setting, which we restate here in the language of nc measures.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
(\gamma_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
be a family of complex numbers with
\gamma_{\emptyset}=0
and
\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert<1
for
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. Then there exists a unique non-trivial probability nc measure
\mu
such that the polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
defined by the recurrences (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) with
\varphi_{\emptyset}=\varphi_{\emptyset}^{\#}=1
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product induced by
\mu
.

This combined with
Theorem

4.13
provides a full correspondence between noncommutatively-indexed families of complex numbers inside the unit disc on the one side and non-trivial probability nc measures on the other, directly analogous to the commutative theory.

5.
The NC Christoffel Function

In the univariate setting, a central role is played by the Christoffel-Darboux kernel, and accordingly the Christoffel function associated to a measure. In this section we use the theory of orthogonal polynomials to introduce a noncommutative analogue that will play a similarly central role to our setting.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. The
n^{\mathrm{th}}

nc Christoffel-Darboux kernel
associated to
\mu
is the map sending
k\times k
matrices
A,B
to

(5.1)

\kappa_{\mu,n}(A,B):=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(B)^{*}=I_{k}+\sum_{\emptyset\prec\sigma\preceq\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(B)^{*}

and the
n^{\mathrm{th}}

nc Christoffel approximate
is

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu):=\kappa_{\mu,n}(A,A)^{-1}.

We use the same Christoffel-Darboux kernels as
[
8
]
, whence we see an alternate formula analogous to the Christoffel-Darboux formula
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Remark 5.2
.

A very similar construction appears in
[
5
]
, but our setting requires some differences to this construction as
[
5
]
imposes that the generators
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
be self-adjoint, which is not a property we impose for polynomials in the disk system.

Remark 5.3
.

Classically, the Christoffel approximates
\lambda_{n}(\cdot;\mathrm{d}\mu)
are minima of a certain integral quantity over polynomials of degree up to
n
which map the origin to 1, and it is a theorem that these minima are equal to the inverse of a sum of the form (
5.1
). We next prove an analogue of this result in the noncommutative setting, though for convenience we proceed in the opposite direction, showing that our
\Lambda_{n}(\cdot;\mu)
defined in terms of orthogonal polynomials is in fact equal to a certain minimum. We proceed by mimicking the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
, which is a very similar result, but our setting has some minor differences that require slightly different computations — for example, while the involution
\star
of
[
5
]
appears on the right in
PP^{\star}
, the structure of the
d
-shift on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
encourages us to consider quantities with an adjoint on the left in
Q^{*}Q
; this allows us to bring the machinery of Jury and Martin to bear. Nevertheless, the spirit of the proof in
[
5
]
carries through.

In
[
5
, Section 3]
, two tensor products between complex algebras are described. Given algebras
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}
over
\mathbb{C}
, one has a choice of two product operations on the algebraic tensor product
\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathcal{W}
: either
\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathcal{W}
, the algebra with product

(5.2)

\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}\otimes w_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i}\widetilde{v_{i}}\otimes\widetilde{w_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i,j}v_{i}\widetilde{v_{j}}\otimes w_{i}\widetilde{w_{j}}

or
\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{op}}
, the algebra with product

(5.3)

\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}\otimes w_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i}\widetilde{v_{i}}\otimes\widetilde{w_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i,j}v_{i}\widetilde{v_{j}}\otimes\widetilde{w_{j}}w_{i}.

Belinschi, Magron and Vinnikov consider
(\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle)^{k\times k}\cong\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}\otimes\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle
with the
opposite product
(
5.3
), as this is of interest in the study of operator spaces and has applications down the line in their work. This interest arises as one can identify
P(Z)=\sum_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}\otimes Z^{\sigma}\in(\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle)^{k\times k}
as taking values in linear maps on matrices: for a tuple of
k\times k
matrices
A
,
P(A)=\sum_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\sigma}
sends
C\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
to
\sum_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}CA^{\sigma}
, and the opposite product on the tensor product corresponds to the usual composition of these operators. See the in-depth discussion at the beginning of
[
5
, Section 3.2]
for further details.

On the other hand, we shall choose the tensor product algebra structure
(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle)^{k\times k}\cong\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
with the product (
5.2
), as this structure will allow us to have the adjoint on the left as discussed in
Remark

5.3
. In this algebra, one may still consider
P(A)=\sum_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\sigma}
as a linear map on
\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
, however, this embedding of
\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}\hookrightarrow B(\mathbb{C}^{k\times k},\mathbb{C}^{k\times k})
is no longer a homomorphism as the product (
5.2
) no longer corresponds to the composition of the relevant linear operators. This will suffice for our setting as we need not evaluate a product of two matrix-valued nc polynomials as a linear map.

Using this choice of product, one may work with a polynomial expressed in terms of the orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}+}
directly, rather than in terms of their adjoints — this is beneficial to us as, unlike the setting of
[
5
]
where
P^{\star}
is a polynomial when
P
is, the adjoint of
\varphi_{\sigma}
is no longer itself a polynomial.

Remark 5.4
.

In the sequel, we in fact only need to evaluate the linear operator
P(A)
, where
P\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle)^{k\times k}
and
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
, on the
k\times k
identity matrix
I_{k}
. For this reason, we shall suppress this secondary dependence, so that for us
P(A)
shall mean not the linear operator, but rather the matrix-valued quantity
P(A)[I_{k}]
in the notation of
[
5
]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with Christoffel approximates
(\Lambda_{n}(\cdot;\mu))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in(\mathbb{C}^{k\times k})^{d}
, we have

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)=\min\{(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q^{*}Q)\;:\;Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}.

Proof.

For
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
c_{\sigma}(A)=\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
and define the
k\times k
matrix of nc polynomials
Q_{n}
by

Q_{n}:=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}\otimes c_{\sigma}(A).

Note that, unlike the corresponding step in
[
5
]
, this is expressed in terms of the basis
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, rather than in terms of
(\varphi_{\sigma}^{*})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
in analogy to
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
: this is because while
(P^{\star}_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
is also a basis of
\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle
in their setting,
\varphi_{\sigma}^{*}
is not even a polynomial in
Z
for us.

Now,

\displaystyle Q_{n}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)c_{\sigma}(A)=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{-1}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)

\displaystyle=I_{k},

so that
Q_{n}
is admissible for the minimum of interest. Next, we see from our choice of the tensor structure (
5.2
) that

Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n}=\sum_{\sigma,\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}^{*}\varphi_{\tau}\otimes c_{\sigma}^{*}(A)c_{\tau}(A)

and subsequently we calculate that

\displaystyle(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\mu(\varphi_{\sigma}^{*}\varphi_{\tau})c_{\sigma}(A)^{*}c_{\tau}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\langle\varphi_{\tau},\varphi_{\sigma}\rangle_{\mu}c_{\sigma}(A)^{*}c_{\tau}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\delta_{\sigma,\tau}c_{\sigma}(A)^{*}c_{\tau}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}c_{\sigma}(A)^{*}c_{\sigma}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\big(\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\big)\big(\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)\big)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\right)\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{*}

\displaystyle=I_{k}\cdot\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\right)^{-1}\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\right)^{*}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu).

Hence
\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
is in the set we want to minimise over.

Now let
P=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}\otimes a_{\sigma}\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k}
be arbitrary and suppose that
P(A)=I_{k}
, i.e. that
\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)a_{\sigma}=I_{k}
. In terms of the natural Hilbert module structure over
\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
, this means precisely that
\langle(\varphi_{\sigma}(A))_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)},(a^{*}_{\sigma})_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\rangle=I_{k}
. Considering the matrix

X=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{\emptyset}(A)&\cdots&\varphi_{\sigma(n)}(A)\\
a_{\emptyset}^{*}&\cdots&a_{\sigma(n)}^{*}\end{bmatrix}

and applying
[
25
, Theorem 14.10]
, we obtain positivity of the matrix

Y=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\langle\varphi_{\sigma}(A),\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\rangle&\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\langle\varphi_{\sigma}(A),a_{\sigma}^{*}\rangle\\
\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\langle a_{\sigma}^{*},\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\rangle&\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\langle a_{\sigma}^{*},a_{\sigma}^{*}\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}&\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)a_{\sigma}\\
\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*}&\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}a_{\sigma}\end{bmatrix},

and on the other hand,
P(A)=I_{k}
implies that

Y=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{-1}&I_{k}\\
I_{k}&\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}a_{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}.

We note that

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{-1}=\kappa_{\mu,n}(A,A)=I_{k}+\sum_{\emptyset\prec\sigma\preceq\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(A)\varphi_{\sigma}(A)^{*},

which is evidently positive definite. Via the Schur complement (e.g. see
[
3
, Remark 2.4.6]
), then,
Y
is positive semi-definite if and only if

\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}a_{\sigma}-I_{k}^{*}\left(\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)^{-1}\right)^{-1}I_{k}\succeq 0,

or equivalently,

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)\preceq\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}a_{\sigma}.

Finally, note that

(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(P^{*}P)=\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}a_{\sigma}^{*}a_{\sigma}.

As a result we now see that, for any
P\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k}
, it holds that

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)\preceq(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(P^{*}P),

and so
\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
is the minimum claimed.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The proof of the preceding lemma is constructive, that is, not only do we show that
\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
is the minimum claimed, but for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
we in fact construct an nc polynomial
Q_{n}
such that
(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n})=\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
. In addition to the lemma, the existence of this explicit minimiser shall prove useful to us later. We emphasise that this sequence is dependent upon the choice of input
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
.

We can use this to obtain an nc Christoffel function, analogous to the function
\lambda_{\infty}(\cdot;\mathrm{d}\mu)
of
[
33
]
.

Theorem 5.7
.

The pointwise limit of the Christoffel approximates,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu),

exists for all
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and is given by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)=\inf\{((\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k}))(Q^{*}Q)\;:\;Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle)^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}.

Proof.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>n
, we have the inclusion
(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k}\subset(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(m)})^{k\times k}
. Hence the minimiser
Q_{n}
from
Remark

5.6
is also an element of the set minimised by
Q_{m}
, so that

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)=(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n})\succeq\Lambda_{m}(A;\mu)=(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q_{m}^{*}Q_{m}).

This gives us a decreasing sequence of matrices
Z_{n}:=(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n})
which are all themselves positive semi-definite, i.e.

Z_{1}\succeq Z_{2}\succeq\cdots\succeq Z_{n}\succeq Z_{n+1}\succeq\cdots\succeq 0_{k\times k}.

Each of these matrices has an associated positive semi-definite sesquilinear form given by
q_{n}(x,y):=y^{*}Z_{n}x
; for each
x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, we can hence associate a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
via
a_{n}(x):=x^{*}Z_{n}x
. Each of these sequences is decreasing: since
Z_{n}-Z_{n+1}\succeq 0_{k\times k}
, we have

a_{n}(x)-a_{n+1}(x)=x^{*}(Z_{n}-Z_{n+1})x\geq 0

and hence
a_{n}(x)\geq a_{n+1}(x)
.

Again by positive semi-definiteness, these sequences are bounded below by zero, so each one converges in
\mathbb{C}
. Denote by
a(x)
the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(x)
. We claim that there exists a unique positive semi-definite
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
such that
a(x)=x^{*}Zx
.

Consider the polarisation identity for
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

q_{n}(x,y):=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3}a_{n}(x+i^{k}y).

Since the limits
a(x+i^{k}y)
all exist (from the above), taking the limit, we obtain

q(x,y):=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3}a(x+i^{k}y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(x,y).

The limit of sesquilinear forms must be itself a sesquilinear form, and so
q
is sesquilinear; it follows that there exists some matrix
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
such that
q(x,y)=y^{*}Zx
. Furthermore, we have that

q_{n}(x,x)-q(x,x)=x^{*}Z_{n}x-x^{*}Zx=x^{*}(Z_{n}-Z)x;

since the sequences
a_{n}(x+i^{k}x)
are decreasing, so too are the sequences
q_{n}(x,x)
, and it follows that
q_{n}(x,x)-q(x,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
. From this we can see that
x^{*}(Z_{n}-Z)x\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, i.e.
Z_{n}\succeq Z
. On the other hand, since each
a_{n}(x)\geq 0
, taking limits we have
a(x)\geq 0
and hence
q(x,x)\geq 0
, so that
Z
is positive semi-definite, and the sequence
(Z_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a positive semi-definite matrix
Z
.

With this
Z
in hand, we then have for any
x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that

x(Z_{n}-Z)x^{*}=xZ_{n}x^{*}-xZx^{*}=a_{n}(x)-a(x)\geq 0;

this shows that
xZ_{n}x^{*}\geq xZx^{*}
and therefore
Z_{n}\succeq Z
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence the limit
Z=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)
exists. This allows us to write

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\min\{(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q^{*}Q):Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q^{*}Q):Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle)^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}.

To elaborate on this last equality:
Z
may not be in the set

\{(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q^{*}Q):Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)})^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}

for any particular
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but nevertheless, for any
Y
in this set, there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Z_{j}\preceq Y
, and as we have just seen, we then have
Z\preceq Z_{j}
. Hence
Z
provides the desired notion of infimum.
∎

This existence result allows us to make the following definition.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial probability nc measure. The
nc Christoffel function
associated to
\mu
, denoted
\Lambda_{\infty}(\cdot;\mu)
, is the pointwise limit of the nc Christoffel approximates, and is given by

\Lambda_{\infty}(A;\mu):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)=\inf\{(\mu\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k})(Q^{*}Q):Q\in(\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle)^{k\times k},\;Q(A)=I_{k}\}

for any
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The final result of this section relies on the Szegő–Verblunsky theorem (see (
1.1
)) to see that singular nc measures in the sense of Jury and Martin
[
20
]
have zero nc Christoffel function at zero. Let
0^{(d)}_{1\times 1}
be the
d-
tuple of scalar zeros
(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{B}^{d}_{1}
.

Lemma 5.9
.

If
\mu
is a singular nc measure, then
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is positive, the infimum form of
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)
shows that
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)
is non-negative, and it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)\leq\inf\{\mu(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z_{1}\rangle,\;Q(0)=1\},

using the canonical embedding of
\mathbb{C}\langle Z_{1}\rangle
into
\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
. We thus restrict
\mu
to the case
d=1
, and as
[
20
, Corollary 8.5]
demonstrates, in this setting the nc Lebesgue decomposition coincides with the classical one. When we restrict
\mu
to the copy of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
generated by
Z_{1}
, then, we get a functional
\mu|_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}:\mathcal{A}_{1}\to\mathbb{C}
; since
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is isomorphic to the space
C(\mathbb{T})
of continuous functions on the circle
\mathbb{T}
, we can further use the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem
[
29
, Theorem 2.17]
to see that this corresponds to integration with respect to a finite, regular Borel measure on the circle. Moreover, due to the nc and classical Lebesgue decompositions coinciding, this measure will be singular; we shall denote this singular measure by
\widetilde{\mu}
.

This provides the computation

\displaystyle\inf\{\mu(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z_{1}\rangle,\;Q(0)=1\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{2\pi}Q(e^{i\theta})^{*}Q(e^{i\theta})\;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}(\theta):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z_{1}\rangle,Q(0)=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lvert Q(e^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}(\theta):Q\in\mathbb{C}[z],Q(0)=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\infty}(0;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}),

where
\lambda_{\infty}(\cdot;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu})
is the Christoffel function associated to
\widetilde{\mu}
in the sense of
[
33
]
. Now, since
\widetilde{\mu}
is singular, its (classical) Lebesgue decomposition
\widetilde{\mu}=\widetilde{\mu}_{ac}+\widetilde{\mu}_{s}
satisfies
\widetilde{\mu}_{ac}=w(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}=0
, i.e.
w=0
. Applying the Szegő–Verblunsky theorem in the classical setting to
\widetilde{\mu}
demonstrates the equality

\lambda_{\infty}(0;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu})=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log(w(\theta))\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\right)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log(0)\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\right)=0,

so that

0\leq\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)\leq 0,

i.e.
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)=0
.
∎

In the commutative univariate setting, the Christoffel function at zero depends only upon the absolutely continuous part
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
of the measure
\mu
. One can see this e.g. via
Theorem

1.1
:
\mu
and
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
have the same Radon–Nikodym derivative
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, so that

\lambda_{\infty}(0;\mathrm{d}\mu)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\big(w(\theta)\big)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\right)=\lambda_{\infty}(0;\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}).

In the free noncommutative setting, we can use
Lemma

5.9
to establish an analogous fact.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
be the nc Lebesgue decomposition of a non-trivial probability nc measure
\mu
. Then

\Lambda_{\infty}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu)=\Lambda_{\infty}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}).

Proof.

Recall that for positive real-valued functions
F,G
on a set
X
, we have that
\inf_{X}\{F+G\}=\inf_{X}\{F\}+\inf_{X}\{G\}
. Since nc measures are by definition positive and
Q^{*}Q
is a positive element of
\mathscr{A}_{d}
for any nc polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q)+\mu_{s}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle,Q(0)=1\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle,Q(0)=1\}+\inf\{\mu_{s}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle,Q(0)=1\};

since
\mu_{s}
is singular, however, the second term is zero by
Lemma

5.9
, so that

\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)=\inf\{\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle X\langle,Q(0)=1\}=\Lambda_{\infty}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}).

∎

Remark 5.11
.

It is easy to check that
\Lambda_{\infty}(\cdot;\mu)
respects direct sums (though not similarities, and hence is not an nc function). For this reason, it is sufficient to evaluate on
0_{1\times 1}^{(d)}
in the above results: any tuple of larger zero matrices, say
0_{k\times k}^{(d)}\in(\mathbb{C}^{k\times k})^{d}
, decomposes as a direct sum of
k
copies of
0_{1\times 1}^{(d)}
and hence

\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{k\times k}^{(d)};\mu)=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{k}\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)=\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)I_{k}.

In other words,
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{k\times k}^{(d)};\mu)
is determined solely by the value of
\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{k\times k}^{(d)};\mu)
.

6.
A Noncommutative Szegő-Type Theorem

This section will unite the work done in the previous three sections to prove the main result of the paper,
Theorem

6.3
.

First, we recall some notation. For
d\geq 2
, let
\mu\in(\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with absolutely continuous part
\mu_{ac}
and nc Radon–Nikodym derivative
T
, Verblunsky coefficients
(\gamma_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, monic orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, multi-Toeplitz determinants
(D_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, nc Christoffel approximates

\Lambda_{n}(A;\mu)=\left(\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\tau}(A)\varphi_{\tau}(A)^{*}\right)^{-1}

and nc Christoffel function
\Lambda_{\infty}(\cdot,\mu)
. Let the leading coefficient of
\varphi_{\sigma}
be
a_{\sigma,\sigma}
. We denote by
(\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
the polynomials satisfying the nc Szegő recurrence relations with
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
in
[
8
, Theorem 3.2]
, and similarly to the commutative theory we define
\Phi^{\#}_{\sigma}:=\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}}\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma}
. Finally, by
\sigma(n)
we mean the last word of length
n
under the shortlex ordering of
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, i.e.
\sigma(n)=d\cdots d
with
n
copies of
d
, with
\sigma(0)=\emptyset
, and by
\sigma-1
and
\sigma+1
we mean the immediate predecessor and successor of
\sigma
in this ordering.

Of relevance shortly will be the following well-known lemma (see, e.g., the remark following
[
24
, Lemma 3.3.5]
) and its consequence.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers with
0\leq a_{n}\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-a_{n})

converges (and is nonzero) if and only if

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}

converges. In particular, if
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}=+\infty
then
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-a_{n})=0
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with Verblunsky coefficients
(\gamma_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. The Verblunsky coefficients are square-summable, i.e.
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}<+\infty
, if and only if
\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})
converges and is nonzero.

Proof.

Using the shortlex ordering on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, let
a_{n}
be the
n^{\rm{th}}
element of
(\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. The corollary is immediate from
Lemma

6.1
with this choice of
a_{n}
.
∎

We are now in a position to formulate and prove our main result; let
0^{(d)}_{1\times 1}
be the
d-
tuple of scalar zeros. Recall the sequence of minimisers
Q_{n}
from
Remark

5.6
with the choice
A=0_{1\times 1}^{(d)}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure. The following quantities are equal.

(i)

\kappa:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert\Phi_{\sigma(n)}\right\rVert^{2}
;

(ii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{\sigma(n),\sigma(n)}^{-2}
;

(iii)

\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma(n)}\rvert^{2})
;

(iv)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{\sigma(n)}}{D_{\sigma(n)-1}}
.

Moreover, the following quantities are equal:

(v)

\widetilde{\kappa}:=\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})
;

(vi)

\Lambda_{\infty}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu)
;

(vi)’

\Lambda_{\infty}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu_{\mathrm{ac}})
;

(vii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lvert\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{-2}
;

(viii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\lvert\varphi_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}\right)^{-1}
;

(ix)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle TQ_{n},Q_{n}\rangle
for some
Q_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\langle X\langle_{\sigma(n)},n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(x)

\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{\mu})=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}(t)
for some positive Borel measure
\widetilde{\mu}
on
[0,\infty)
,

and by comparing (iii) and (v) we see that

\kappa=\widetilde{\kappa}\cdot\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}\setminus\{\sigma(n):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}).

Proof.

The proof is broken into steps.

Step 1:

Proof that
(i) = (ii):

This item is straightforward. Since the polynomials
\Phi_{\sigma}
are the (monic) results of Gram-Schmidt, and the polynomials
\varphi_{\sigma}
are normalisations of those, equating coefficients of
Z^{\sigma}
we can use

\varphi_{\sigma}:=\frac{\Phi_{\sigma}}{\left\lVert\Phi_{\sigma}\right\rVert}

to see that

a_{\sigma,\sigma}=\frac{1}{\left\lVert\Phi_{\sigma}\right\rVert},

or in other words, that
\left\lVert\Phi_{\sigma}\right\rVert=a_{\sigma,\sigma}^{-1}
for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, and the equality follows.

Step 2:

Proof that
(ii) = (iii) = (iv):

In the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
appears the equation (for arbitrary
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
)

\frac{1}{a_{\sigma,\sigma}^{2}}=\frac{D_{\sigma}}{D_{\sigma-1}}=\prod_{1\leq j\leq m}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma_{j}\cdots\sigma_{m}}\rvert^{2});

taking specifically
\sigma=\sigma(m)
, we have
\sigma_{j}=d
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, so the product on the right-hand side becomes (writing
j
copies of
d
as
d^{j}
)

\prod_{1\leq j\leq m}(1-\lvert\gamma_{d^{j}}\rvert^{2})=\prod_{n=1}^{m}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma(n)}\rvert^{2}).

The equation from
[
8
]
under these choices is then

\frac{1}{a_{\sigma(m),\sigma(m)}^{2}}=\frac{D_{\sigma(m)}}{D_{\sigma(m)-1}}=\prod_{n=1}^{m}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma(n)}\rvert^{2}),

and recalling from
[
8
]
that
\gamma_{\sigma(0)}=\gamma_{\emptyset}=0
, we can take this product from
n=0
without changing the value. Taking the limits as
m\to\infty
shows the desired equalities.

Step 3:

Proof that
(vii) = (v):

Recall the recurrence relation (
4.3
) for
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+},k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
:

\varphi_{k\sigma}^{\#}=\frac{1}{d_{k\sigma}}(-\overline{\gamma_{k\sigma}}Z_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}+\varphi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}),

and also that
\varphi_{\emptyset}=\varphi^{\#}_{\emptyset}=1
are constant polynomials. First, notice that

\varphi^{\#}_{1}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}(-\overline{\gamma_{1}}Z_{1}\varphi_{\emptyset}+\varphi^{\#}_{\emptyset})=\frac{1-\overline{g_{1}}Z_{1}}{d_{1}}

which gives

\varphi^{\#}_{1}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})=\frac{1}{d_{1}}=\frac{1}{d_{\emptyset}}\frac{1}{d_{1}},

recalling again that
d_{\emptyset}=1-\lvert 0\rvert^{2}=1
.

With this as our base case, suppose now for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
that
\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma}(0)=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}d_{\tau}^{-1}
. The same recurrence relation as above shows us that

\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma+1}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})=\frac{1}{d_{\sigma+1}}\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})=\frac{1}{d_{\sigma+1}}\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}d_{\tau}^{-1}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}d_{\tau}^{-1};

by induction on the place of
\sigma
in the shortlex ordering of
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
then,

\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}d_{\tau}^{-1}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\left(\sqrt{1-\lvert\gamma_{\tau}\rvert^{2}}\right)^{-1}

for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. It is now quick to see that

\lvert\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{-2}=\left\lvert\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\left(\sqrt{1-\lvert\gamma_{\tau}\rvert^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\rvert^{-2}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\tau}\rvert^{2});

since this holds for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, in particular it holds for
\sigma(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and then taking the limit as
n\to\infty
completes the proof.

Step 4:

Proof that
(viii) = (v):

First, notice that (
4.2
) implies that

\varphi_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})=\frac{1}{d_{\sigma}}(-\gamma_{\sigma}\varphi^{\#}_{\sigma-1}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1}))=\frac{-\gamma_{\sigma}}{d_{\sigma}}\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma-1}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}=-\gamma_{\sigma}\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}},

where the second equality is from our proof of “(vii) = (v)”. Next, as a base case, consider that

\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(0)}\lvert\varphi_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}=\lvert\varphi_{\emptyset}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}=1=\frac{1}{d_{\emptyset}^{2}};

with this in mind, suppose for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
that

\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\lvert\varphi_{\tau}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}.

Then direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}\lvert\varphi_{\tau}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}+\lvert\varphi_{\sigma+1}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}+\lvert\gamma_{\sigma+1}\rvert^{2}\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\sigma+1}^{2}+\lvert\gamma_{\sigma+1}\rvert^{2}}{\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}}=\frac{1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma+1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\gamma_{\sigma+1}\rvert^{2}}{\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma+1}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2},

so by induction we have

\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\lvert\varphi_{\tau}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}^{2}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})^{-1}

for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
. Hence in particular

\left(\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\lvert\varphi_{\tau}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}\right)^{-1}=\prod_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})

and taking the limit as
n\to\infty
completes this proof.

Step 5:

Proof that
(vi) = (viii):

This item also follows almost immediately. Taking
k=1
, we have from our definition of
\Lambda_{n}(\cdot;\mu)
that

\Lambda_{n}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1};\mu)=\left(\sum_{\sigma=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\varphi_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\overline{\varphi_{\sigma}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})}\right)^{-1}=\left(\sum_{\tau=\emptyset}^{\sigma(n)}\lvert\varphi_{\tau}(0^{(d)}_{1\times 1})\rvert^{2}\right)^{-1},

and once more taking
n\to\infty
proves the equality.

Step 6:

Proof that
(vi) = (vi)’:

This was proved in
Corollary

5.10
.

Step 7:

Proof that
(vi)’ = (ix):

Recall that
T
is the nc Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu

[
19
]
, that is, the (generally unbounded) positive, closed, densely-defined linear operator
\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}
such that

\mu_{ac}(a^{*}b)=\langle\sqrt{T}b,\sqrt{T}a\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}.

Since
T
is closed, densely-defined, and positive,
T
has a unique positive square root
\sqrt{T}
with
\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{T})=\mathcal{D}(T)
, so for
Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
, we have
\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q)=\langle\sqrt{T}Q,\sqrt{T}Q\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}=\langle TQ,Q\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}
. Moreover, recall the minimiser
Q_{n}
for
\min\{\mu(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)},Q(0)=1\}
from
Remark

5.6
. Combine this with the above to see that

\displaystyle\inf\{\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle X\langle,Q(0)=1\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\min\{\mu_{ac}(Q^{*}Q):Q\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle_{\sigma(n)},Q(0)=1\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{ac}(Q_{n}^{*}Q_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle TQ_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}

as claimed.

Step 8:

Proof that
(ix) = (x):

First, suppose that the Verblunsky coefficients of
\mu
are not square-summable, i.e. that
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}=+\infty
. Then by
Corollary

6.2
, we have that the product
\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})=0
. Taking
\widetilde{\mu}=0
to be the zero measure we immediately obtain a candidate measure:

\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2})=0=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}=\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{\mu}),

and we are done.

On the other hand, suppose that the Verblunsky coefficients are square-summable, i.e. that the sum
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}
is finite. Once again, the operator
T
of
[
19
]
is densely-defined, closed, and positive, so we can apply the spectral theorem for an unbounded self-adjoint operator (see e.g.
[
30
, Theorem 5.8]
) to obtain a projection-valued spectral measure
E
on the spectrum
\sigma(T)
, such that
E(\sigma(T))=I_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}
. This measure will satisfy

T=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}t\;\mathrm{d}E(t)

and — to us more importantly —

\langle Tf,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}t\;\mathrm{d}\langle E(t)f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}},

where
\langle E(\cdot)f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}
is a positive scalar measure on
\sigma(T)
determined by
f,g\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
. Following on from the previous item, then, we see that

\Lambda_{\infty}(0_{1\times 1}^{(d)};\mu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}t\;\mathrm{d}\langle E(t)Q_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}

where
(\langle E(\cdot)Q_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive measures on the positive half-line. For ease of notation we shall write
\nu_{n}
in place of
\langle E(\cdot)Q_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}
.

For
j=0,1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the moments

a_{j}^{(n)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{j}\;\mathrm{d}\nu_{n},

so that
\lim_{n}a_{1}^{(n)}=\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}(\nu_{n})=\Lambda_{\infty}(0;\mu)
and

a_{0}^{(n)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}=\langle E(\sigma(T))Q_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\langle Q_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\left\lVert Q_{n}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}.

We show that this cannot have a limit of zero. Suppose to the contrary that
\lim_{n}\left\lVert Q_{n}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=0
, and notice that
\left\lVert\sqrt{T}Q_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\langle\sqrt{T}Q_{n},\sqrt{T}Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\langle TQ_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}
converges, so
(\sqrt{T}Q_{n})_{n}
converges in
\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}
. Then
[
30
, Proposition 1.4]
says that

0=\sqrt{T}(0)=\sqrt{T}\left(\lim_{n}Q_{n}\right)=\lim_{n}\sqrt{T}Q_{n}

and, iterating this calculation, that

0=\sqrt{T}(0)=\sqrt{T}\left(\lim_{n}\sqrt{T}Q_{n}\right)=\lim_{n}TQ_{n}.

Thus
\Lambda_{\infty}(0;\mu)=\lim_{n}\langle TQ_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=0
, but by
Corollary

6.2
, when
\sum_{\sigma}\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}<+\infty
this is not the case. By contradiction we thus demonstrate that
\lim_{n}a_{0}^{(n)}\neq 0
and hence that
\inf_{n}\{a_{0}^{(n)}\}>0
.

By
[
30
, Theorem 1.8]
, the closure of
T
is
T
itself; Equation (1.6) of the same source then means that we can decompose
\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}
as

\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}=\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(T)}\oplus\mathrm{Ker}(T).

The operator
T
is then unitarily equivalent to the operator matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{T}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
, where

Notice that, if

\begin{bmatrix}Q_{n}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}Q_{n}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}

We have already seen that the right hand side converges, and so the left hand side cannot diverge to

Recall that the space of continuous functions on

We have already shown that this quantity is bounded above; if we let some upper bound be

Let

\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{\mu})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}t\;\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}(t)=\psi(f)=\lim_{j}\widehat{\mu}_{n_{j}}(f)=\lim_{j}\mathbb{E}(\mu_{j})\\
=\lim_{j}a_{1}^{(n_{j})}=\lim_{j}\langle TQ_{n_{j}},Q_{n_{j}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\lim_{n}\langle TQ_{n},Q_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{d}^{2}}=\prod_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}+d^{+}}(1-\lvert\gamma_{\sigma}\rvert^{2}),

Finally, the relation between

7.
Zeros of Orthonormal Polynomials

Section 1.7 of
[
33
]
studies the location of the zeros of the monic orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
associated to a measure
\mu
with an aim towards proving
Verblunsky’s theorem
, that if a sequence of complex numbers lies entirely within the open unit disc then there exists a unique measure on the unit circle with that sequence as its Verblunsky coefficients. In particular, Simon provides the following result
[
33
, Theorem 1.7.1]
:

Theorem 7.1

(Zeros Theorem)
.

Let
\Phi_{n}(z)
be an OPUC polynomial. Then all the zeros of
\Phi_{n}
lie in
\mathbb{D}
.

Remark 7.2
.

In the proof of this result named
Third Proof of Theorem 1.7.1
,
[
33
]
also proves that the reverse polynomials, there called
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)
, are non-vanishing on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. In other words, Simon shows that if
z\in\mathbb{D}
then
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)\neq 0
, and if
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}
, then
\Phi_{n}(z)\neq 0
. It follows that if
z\in\mathbb{T}
then both
\Phi_{n}(z)
and
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)
are nonzero.

Indeed,
[
33
]
provides six proofs of Theorem 1.7.1, with an historical discussion of the providence of the different ideas involved in each at the end of the section — we direct the interested reader there for more details.

In the noncommutative setting, the Favard’s theorem of
[
8
]
provides the analogous result to Verblunsky’s theorem — see
Theorem

4.16
for this theorem in terms of nc measures. Nevertheless, the location of the zeros of the monic orthogonal polynomials — and of the orthonormal polynomials, as
\varphi_{\sigma}
and
\Phi_{\sigma}
are proportional by definition — associated to an nc measure remains an interesting question, which we study in this section.

An immediate question presents itself: what is the appropriate notion of “zero” of a multivariate nc polynomial? The most immediate candidate may be to define a zero of an nc polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
as a tuple of matrices
A
lying in either the row-ball or the entire nc space
\mathbb{C}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}
such that
P(A)=0
.

The notion of zero sets of nc polynomials appears already in the literature, in the burgeoning field of noncommutative real algebraic geometry. One such consideration is in
[
14
]
, which discusses noncommutative generalisations of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, and where tuples
A
such that
P(A)=0
are called
hard zeros
of
P
; also in that paper we find the notion of
determinantal zeros
, which shall prove the more useful concept for our study. In
[
14
]
we find the following definition.

Definition 7.3
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{C}\langle Z\rangle
be a noncommutative polynomial. A
determinantal zero
of
P
is a tuple
Z\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}
, for any
n
, such that
\det P(Z)=0
. The set of all determinantal zeros of
P
is denoted by
\mathcal{Z}(P)
and is given by

\mathcal{Z}(P)=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}_{n}(P)\quad\text{ where }\quad\mathcal{Z}_{n}(P):=\left\{Z\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}:\det P(Z)=0\right\}.

Remark 7.4
.

As discussed in Section 4,
[
8
]
formulates the same Christoffel-Darboux kernel as we defined above, though we note that (when
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
) the kernel of
[
8
]
is the restriction of ours to the
n^{\text{th}}
level of the row-ball. We recall that
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
is an analogue of the Christoffel-Darboux formula for these kernels; though we do not use this formula directly, we shall use the calculations involved in the proof of this result as motivation for our own.

Remark 7.5
.

Though
[
33
]
works primarily in terms of the monic orthogonal polynomials, since the orthonormal polynomials are simply rescalings of the monic orthogonal polynomials we have that
\Phi_{n}(z)=0
if and only if
\varphi_{n}(z)=0
and one can easily rephrase everything in terms of the orthonormal polynomials. On the other hand, the theory we wish to exploit in the noncommutative setting of
[
8
]
is entirely in terms of the orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
, so in this section we choose to work with
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
rather than
(\Phi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
as one might expect from a direct analogy.

Furthermore, the calculations motivating our starting point involve summing over all words
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
of a fixed length
n
; as such, the results on
\varphi_{\sigma}
and
\varphi_{\sigma}^{\#}
we obtain are not for arbitrary
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
but rather for the shortlex-final words of arbitrary length,
(\sigma(n))_{n=0}^{\infty}
.

Let
Z,W\in(\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})^{d}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
, and
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Motivated by a calculation in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
, the recurrence relations for orthonormal polynomials ((
4.2
) and (
4.3
)) provide us with

\displaystyle\varphi^{\#}_{k\sigma}(Z)^{*}\varphi^{\#}_{k\sigma}(W)-\varphi_{k\sigma}(Z)^{*}\varphi_{k\sigma}(W)=\varphi_{k\sigma-1}^{\#}(Z)^{*}\varphi^{\#}_{k\sigma-1}(W)-\varphi_{\sigma}(Z)^{*}Z_{k}^{*}W_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}(W)

after some elementary algebra. Following the motivating calculation of
[
8
]
, if we sum these equations (which are distinct for each
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}
) over
k=1,\ldots,d
and
0\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1
we form the equality

(7.1)

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z)^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W)-\sum_{0\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z)^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(W)=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{0\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z)^{*}Z_{k}^{*}W_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}(W).

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We are now in a position to discuss the locations of certain determinantal zeros.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with associated orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
Z\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
is such that
\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=0
, then
Z
lies outside the row-ball.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
. Setting both
Z
and
W
in the above to be
Z^{*}
, (
7.1
) becomes

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})-\sum_{0\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{0\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}Z_{k}^{*}W_{k}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*}).

We can combine the sums on left and right over
\lvert\sigma\rvert=1,\ldots,n-1
, leaving the terms where
\sigma=\emptyset
and
\lvert\sigma\rvert=n
; recalling that
\varphi_{\emptyset}=1
, the result of this manipulation is

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})+(I-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*})\\
+\sum_{1\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}(I-Z_{1}Z_{2}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*})\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*}).

Now,
Z
is in the row-ball, so
Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}<I
and hence
I-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}
is a positive definite matrix; meanwhile, both

\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{1\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}(I-Z_{1}Z_{2}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*})\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})

are sums of positive semi-definite matrices, so are themselves positive semi-definite. It follows that
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})
is positive definite, and therefore has a positive determinant on the row-ball. Finally,

\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\overline{\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})}\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\lvert\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})\rvert^{2}>0

so that

\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})>0,

and hence any determinantal zero must lie outside the row-ball.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

In one variable,
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})
is simply
\varphi_{n}^{*}(\overline{z})
(where
\varphi_{n}^{*}
is the reverse polynomial of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
orthonormal polynomial
\varphi_{n}
), and in that situation this polynomial has no zeros inside the unit disk. Indeed, this map’s zeros are inside the unit disk if and only if those of
\varphi_{n}^{*}
are, as
\lvert\overline{z}\rvert=\lvert z\rvert
;
Lemma

7.6
is then our noncommutative analogue of the statement that reverse polynomials have no zeros inside the disk, with
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})
taking the role of the modulus-squared of
\varphi_{n}^{*}
in our investigation of determinantal zeros.

Our next consideration is a quantity that reduces to the (modulus-squared of the)
n^{\mathrm{th}}
orthonormal polynomial in one variable, and we shall see that it has no zeros
outside
the row-ball; however, to obtain our result we must use a more specialised notion of “outside the row-ball” than simply
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with associated orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
Z\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
is such that
\det\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})=0
, then
Z
lies outside the set

\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}):=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}:Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}>I_{n}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}})
. Again setting
Z
and
W
in the preamble to be
Z^{*}
, when
Z\in\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}})
we instead have that
Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}-I>0
, so separating out the
\lvert\sigma\rvert=n
and
\sigma=\emptyset
terms as before we move from (
7.1
) to

\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{s}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})=\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})+(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}-I)\\
+\sum_{1\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}-I)\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*}).

Now,
Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}-I
is positive definite, and the other two terms on the right hand side here are positive semi-definite, so for
Z\in\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}})
we have that
\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})
is positive definite and therefore has nonzero determinant, that is, its determinantal zeros must lie outside
\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}})
.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

As before, notice that when
d=1
,
\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})
is simply
\lvert\varphi_{n}(\overline{z})\rvert^{2}
, and that this (and equivalently
\varphi_{n}
) has no zeros outside the unit disk, so this quantity is our analogue of (the modulus squared of)
\varphi_{n}
in the free noncommutative setting.

Our analogue of the unit circle, as one might imagine after
Lemma

7.8
, is the distinguished boundary of the row-ball, the set

\partial\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{d}:Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}=I_{n}\right\}

of

, and this turns out to be a well-behaved set to consider: on
\partial\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
, the previous two quantities discussed coincide, so that if one is non-singular on the entirety of this set, we automatically see that the other is too. The argument we provide for non-singularity broadly mimics that of
[
33
]
, though some additional care is needed to handle determinantal zeros of a multivariate nc polynomial compared to the zeros of a scalar univariate polynomial.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with associated orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

, we have that

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})

and this quantity is non-singular.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
; then
I-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}+\cdots+Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*}=0
, so the same manipulations as in
Lemma

7.6
and
Lemma

7.8
this time yield the stated equality immediately. It remains to show that this quantity is non-singular on
\partial\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
.

To see this, suppose to the contrary that

\det\left(\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W^{*})\right)=0

for some
W\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
. Since for any matrix
A
we have
\det AA^{*}=(\det A)(\overline{\det A})=\lvert\det A\rvert^{2}
, this then implies that

\det\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W^{*})=0,

so in particular if
W
is
m\times m
then there exists some
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W^{*})\xi=0
.

Recall from the proof of
Lemma

7.6
that if
Z\in\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
then

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})+(I-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*})\\
+\sum_{1\leq\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n-1}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}(I-Z_{1}Z_{2}^{*}-\cdots-Z_{d}Z_{d}^{*})\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*}),

so that

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})\geq I-\sum_{k=1}^{d}Z_{k}Z_{k}^{*}.

Notice that if
r\in(0,1)
then
rW\in\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
, providing us with the matrix inequality

\displaystyle\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})

\displaystyle\geq I-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(rW_{k})(rW_{k})^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(I-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}(rW_{k})(rW_{k})^{*}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(I+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}(rW_{k})(rW_{k})^{*}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I-\left(r^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}W_{k}W_{k}^{*}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(I+\left(r^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}W_{k}W_{k}^{*}\right)^{1/2}\right);

recalling that
W\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}}
, this becomes

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})\geq(I-rI^{\frac{1}{2}})(I+rI^{\frac{1}{2}})=(1-r)(1+r)I.

Thus, we have

\frac{\xi^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})\xi}{1-r}\geq(1+r)\xi^{*}\xi>\xi^{*}\xi>0.

Write the polynomial
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}
as
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}\alpha_{\sigma}Z^{\sigma}
; with this notation,
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(W^{*})\xi=0
implies that

\alpha_{\emptyset}\xi=-\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi.

Substituting this in for the constant coefficient in
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})\xi
we obtain

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(rW^{*})\xi=-\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi+\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(rW^{*})^{\sigma}\xi=\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(r^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}-1)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi,

which in turns transforms our inequality into

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\xi^{*}\xi<\frac{\left(\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(r^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}-1)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}(r^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}-1)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)}{1-r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r}\left((1-r)\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lvert\sigma\rvert-1}r^{j}\right)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)^{*}\left((1-r)\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lvert\sigma\rvert-1}r^{j}\right)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)

\displaystyle=(1-r)\left(\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lvert\sigma\rvert-1}r^{j}\right)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{\sigma\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lvert\sigma\rvert-1}r^{j}\right)(W^{*})^{\sigma}\xi\right)

\displaystyle\to 0<\xi^{*}\xi

as
r\to 1
, a contradiction. Hence no such
W
can exist.
∎

The following corollary summarises our results on the determinantal zeros of these quantities associated to an nc measure.

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
\mu\in(\mathscr{A}_{d}^{\dagger})_{+}
be a non-trivial probability nc measure with associated orthonormal polynomials
(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{+}}
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we have that

(i)

if
Z\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
, then
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})
is non-singular, i.e.

\mathcal{Z}\left(\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})\right)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}=\emptyset;

(ii)

if
Z\in\mathrm{Ext}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{\mathrm{nc}})
, then
\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})
is non-singular, i.e.

\mathcal{Z}\left(\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})\right)\cap U=\emptyset;

(iii)

if
Z\in\partial\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{nc}}^{d}
, then
\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})
and
\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})
are non-singular, and furthermore

\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma(n)}^{\#}(Z^{*})=\sum_{\lvert\sigma\rvert=n}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*})^{*}\varphi_{\sigma}(Z^{*}).

Proof.

The first claim is
Lemma

7.8
, the second claim is
Lemma

7.6
, and both parts of the final claim are
Lemma

7.10
.
∎
