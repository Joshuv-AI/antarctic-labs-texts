Exploration of the molecular mechanism of Wufu Yin in the treatment of knee osteoarthritis based on network pharmacology and experimental validation
Authors: Zhengcong Ye, Miaomiao Wang, Guoan Qi, Tuo Wang, Guoping Cao, Canfeng Wang, Minlong Wang, Qinrong Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Wufu Yin (WFY) exhibits significant clinical effectiveness in knee osteoarthritis (KOA) treatment, yet its therapeutic mechanisms are still unclear. This study aimed to explore the active ingredients and potential mechanism of WFY in the treatment of KOA. The network pharmacology-based approach was adopted to investigate the underlying mechanism of WFY in treating KOA. Molecular docking analysis was performed using Auto Vina software. An in vitro model of KOA inflammation was established by inducing chondrocyte cultures with interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Cell viability was quantified through the cell counting kit-8 assay, inflammatory cytokine levels were measured via ELISA, and protein expressions were assessed by Western blot analysis. A total of 225 active ingredients and 265 targets of WFY were identified, of which 88 were identified as potential targets against KOA. Enrichment analysis showed that these targets were associated with oxidative stress, cell proliferation and apoptosis, and inflammatory response, and were involved in the regulation of Th17 cell differentiation, IL-17 signaling pathway, tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway, and other signaling pathways. Topology analysis showed that PTGS2, NOS2, ESR11, PPARG, and MAPK14 had higher degree values and were key targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA. Molecular docking analysis showed that these key targets and active ingredients had low binding energies, indicating that they had potential binding activity. Furthermore, IL-1β-induced elevation of inflammatory cytokines, PTGS2 protein expression, and phosphorylated p38/p38 ratios in chondrocytes were significantly attenuated upon WFY intervention. Our study systematically elucidated the pharmacological basis and molecular mechanism underlying WFY’s therapeutic effects in KOA, substantiating its ability to suppress inflammation and regulate PTGS2 expression and p38 phosphorylation.

1. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a major public health concern affecting millions of people worldwide. Epidemiological studies have shown that approximately 30% of adults have radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis, and 8.9% have clinically significant knee or hip osteoarthritis, with KOA being the most common type.[1] The likelihood of developing osteoarthritis increases with age. For instance, a nationwide Chinese population study reported an overall incidence of symptomatic KOA of 8.1%, which increases with age.[2] Knee and hip joints are the 2 most commonly affected joints. Risk factors for KOA include age, sex, race, and BMI, among others.[3] KOA not only reduces patients’ quality of life but also leads to significant healthcare costs and increased mortality rates.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been shown to be effective in relieving symptoms and improving quality of life. TCM treatments, such as acupuncture and herbal medicine, have been used for centuries to treat KOA, and recent studies have provided scientific evidence to support their use. With the growing interest in complementary and alternative therapies, TCM has gained popularity as a safe and effective treatment option for KOA. Wufu Yin (WFY) is a traditional herbal formula made up of RENSHEN (Panax ginseng), DIHUANG (Rehmannia glutinosa), DANGGUI (Angelica sinensis), BAIZHU (Atractylodes macrocephala), and GANCAO (Glycyrrhiza uralensis). It originated in the Ming Dynasty in China and was used to treat qi and blood deficiency. Recent clinical studies have demonstrated that WFY can effectively improve clinical symptoms associated with KOA.[4] However, the lack of systematic research on its active constituents has left the underlying mechanisms behind its therapeutic effects on KOA unclear.

Network pharmacology is an emerging discipline that integrates systems biology, pharmacology, and network analysis to identify the complex interactions between drugs and biological systems. By using a network-based approach, network pharmacology allows researchers to explore the multi-target mechanisms of drugs, identify novel therapeutic targets, and predict adverse drug reactions.[5] This study systematically investigated the active constituents of WFY and their key targets and mechanisms in the treatment of KOA, utilizing a combination of network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data collection

The Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP) contains 499 Chinese herbs registered in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, with 29,384 components, 3311 targets, and 837 related diseases. This database uses the SysDT algorithm from the HIT database to obtain the relationship between drug targets. We searched for chemical components using the Chinese names of the herbs in the TCMSP database and selected active ingredients based on their drug-likeness index and oral bioavailability index. We obtained the corresponding target information by importing the names of active ingredients and standardized the target symbols using the Uniprot database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and removed nonhuman target genes. KOA-related genes were obtained by searching the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (https://www.omim.org/), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/), and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) databases using “knee osteoarthritis” as the keyword. The intersection of the search results from the 3 databases was taken as the KOA-related genes.

2.2. Protein–protein interaction analysis

The potential targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA were obtained by taking the intersection of its targets and KOA-related genes, and their protein–protein interaction information was obtained from the STRING database. We set the minimum required interaction score to medium confidence (0.4) and downloaded all interaction data, and then imported them into Cytoscape software for network reconstruction and topology analysis.

2.3. Enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis can reveal the biological processes and pathways that drug targets regulate to control diseases. Therefore, we used the ClusterProfiler package to perform ID conversion and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the potential targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA, and drew bubble charts to filter significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways based on gene numbers.

2.4. Network construction and topology analysis

Cytoscape software was used for all network construction and topology analysis. In this study, we constructed 5 networks, including the herb–active ingredient network, active ingredient–target network, pathway–gene network, and herb–active ingredient–target–pathway network. The topological structure parameters of the networks were calculated using the Analyze Network tool, and the networks were integrated using the Merge tool. The key targets were screened using the MCODE algorithm with default parameters.

2.5. Molecular docking

Molecular docking analysis was used to evaluate the binding ability of active ingredients and key targets. The structural data of the key targets were obtained from the Protein Data Bank database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and preprocessed using PyMOL software, including removal of solvent molecules, metal ions, and water molecules, and obtaining the docking grid using the GetBox plugin. The structure of the target protein was further processed in Autodock software, including hydrogenation, charge calculation, and format conversion. The structural data of the active ingredients were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted using Open Obabel software. Batch docking was performed using Auto Vina software, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal binding pose of the active ingredient and key target. The binding energy heatmap was drawn, and the average binding energy was calculated.

2.6. Preparation of WFY

The botanical components for the formulation of WFY were obtained from Zisun Chinese Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. The preparation involved meticulous weighing of each herb: 30 g RENSHEN, 30 g DIHUANG, 20 g BAIZHU, 20 g DANGGUI, and 10 g GANCAO processed. Following the weighing, the herbs were subjected to a preliminary soaking in distilled water for a duration of thirty minutes. Subsequently, the herbs underwent aqueous extraction through boiling, which was then concentrated to a density of 4 g/mL and subsequently used as a crude drug.

2.7. Cell culture and treatment

Primary human articular chondrocytes were procured from Procell Technology (Catalog No. CP-H096, Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured using complete growth medium formulated for human articular chondrocytes (Catalog No. CP-H096, Procell Technology, Wuhan, China) under standard incubation conditions at 37 oC in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells at the third passage were utilized for subsequent experiments. To establish an in vitro model of KOA, they were exposed to interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; PeproTech EC, London, United Kingdom) at a concentration of 10 ng/mL for 24 hours, thereby inducing a state mimicking osteoarthritic inflammation.

2.8. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

To evaluate the impact of WFY on chondrocyte viability, a CCK-8 assay was conducted. Chondrocytes were seeded onto 96-well plates and allowed to adhere. Following adherence, cells were subjected to pharmacological intervention with WFY. Posttreatment, CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the plates were returned to the incubator for an additional 2-hour incubation period. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured for each well using a Cytation 3 microplate reader (ThermoFisher Scientific) to quantify cell viability.

2.9. Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from samples using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Protein concentration was determined by the bicinchoninic acid assay. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on polyacrylamide gels (ACE Biotechnology, Changzhou, China) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 hours at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding, followed by overnight incubation at 4 oC with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The primary antibodies used were against PTGS2 (1:1000, Abcam), phosphorylated p38 (p-p38, 1:1000, Abcam), total p38 (1:1000, Abcam), and β-actin (1:2000, Abcam) as a loading control. After thorough washing to remove unbound primary antibodies, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Detection of target proteins was accomplished using an imaging system (Bio-Rad) for subsequent analysis.

2.10. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) were determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Jingmei Biotechnology, China). The optical density of each sample was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm employing a Cytation 3 microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

2.11. Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 18.0 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL). Differences between groups were evaluated using the independent-samples t test, and a P < .05 was considered statistical significance.

3. Results

3.1. Identification of active ingredients and targets of WFY

Utilizing TCMSP data, we conducted a search for the chemical constituents of the 5 herbs in WFY and screened for potential active ingredients based on DL ≥ 0.1 and OB ≥ 20. The results revealed 225 potential active ingredients in the 5 herbs, with RENSHEN containing 66, DIHUANG containing 19, DANGGUI containing 10, BAIZHU containing 14, and GANCAO exhibiting the most with 125 active ingredients identified (Fig. 1A). Among these active ingredients, 7 were present in multiple herbs, including diisobutyl phthalate (MOL000057) in GANCAO and BAIZHU, dibutyl phthalate (MOL000676), and kaempferol (MOL000422) in GANCAO and RENSHEN, sitosterol (MOL000359) in GANCAO and DIHUANG, stigmasterol (MOL000449) in DIHUANG, DANGGUI, and RENSHEN, beta-sitosterol (MOL000358) in DANGGUI and RENSHEN, and alloaromadedrene (MOL000066) in REHSHEN and BAIZHU. Moreover, we analyzed the targets of all active ingredients, with 152 active ingredients identified as regulating 265 human targets (Fig. 1B). On average, these active ingredients could regulate 11 targets, with quercetin (MOL000098) exhibiting the most with 151 targets.

3.2. Potential targets of WFY in treating KOA

To identify potential targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA, KOA-related genes were acquired from various databases. As depicted in Figure 2A, the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database contained 63 KOA-related genes, the DisGeNET database contained 368, and GeneCards retrieved 981. After eliminating duplicates, a total of 1226 KOA-related genes were obtained, with 88 of these genes identified as potential targets of WFY for treating KOA through intersection with the targets of WFY (Fig. 2B). The protein interactions of these targets are illustrated in Figure 2C, with 1555 interaction records present among the targets. The average number of interactions per gene was 18, and TNF exhibited the most interactions with 75 records. These targets provide a foundation for further elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of WFY in KOA.

KOA = knee osteoarthritis, WFY = Wufu Yin.

3.3. Key pathway of WFY in treating KOA

To gain further insights into the cellular processes and pathways involved in the therapeutic effects of WFY in KOA, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the potential targets identified previously. The results indicated significant enrichment of these potential targets in multiple biological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammatory response, cell proliferation, and apoptosis (Fig. 3A). Moreover, these targets were associated with various signaling pathways, such as the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation pathway (Fig. 3B). By constructing the key pathway–target network (Fig. 3C), we could obtain a better understanding of the significance of the related genes. Topological analysis revealed that MAPKs series proteins and several inflammatory factor genes possessed high degree values, signifying their close association with these key signaling pathways, and their potential roles as key regulatory proteins or ultimate effectors.

GO = Gene Ontology, KEGG = Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes.

3.4. Key active ingredients and targets of WFY in treating KOA

The pathway–target network described above failed to account for interactions between active ingredients, herbs, and proteins. Consequently, we constructed an integrated network comprising herb–ingredient, ingredient–target, and protein interaction networks, depicted in Figure 4. This network comprised 5 herb nodes, 126 active ingredient targets, 56 target nodes, and 10 pathway nodes. Topological analysis revealed that the mean degree value of targets in this network was 19, with PTGS2 (degree = 171) exhibiting the highest degree value, followed by ESR1 (degree = 125), PPARG (degree = 110), NOS2 (degree = 99), and MAPK14 (degree = 96). These degree values not only reflected the regulatory effects of active ingredients on targets but also indicated the influence of other proteins on targets, as well as the role of targets in key pathways. Therefore, targets with high degree values were considered as key targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA.

The herb–compound–target–pathway network of WFY in the treatment of knee osteoarthritis. The outermost green nodes represent herbs, the red font nodes are 10 non-disease related pathways, the inner nodes of the pathway nodes are targets, and the outer nodes are active ingredients. WFY = Wufu Yin.

3.5. Affinity analysis of key active ingredients and targets of WFY

Molecular docking was utilized to assess the binding affinity between key targets and active ingredients. The top 9 key targets and top 10 active ingredients were selected for molecular docking based on their degree values, and the binding energies between them were represented in a heatmap (Fig. 5A). The results indicated a low binding energy between the active ingredients and targets, signifying their potential binding activity. Notably, CDK2 and glabridin (-10 kcal/mol) and 4’-methoxyglabridin (-10 kcal/mol) exhibited the lowest binding energy. Additionally, we calculated the average binding energy of each active ingredient with all targets, with results highlighting that llicochalcone had the lowest average binding energy, followed by glabridin and 4’-methoxyglabridin, thus implying that these active ingredients had more stable binding with the targets (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the average binding energy of all active ingredients with targets showed that CDK2 had the lowest average binding energy, suggesting that it may be the target with the most stable binding energy with all active ingredients (Fig. 5C).

Binding affinities among bioactive compounds and hub targets. (A) The heatmap of the binding energy of the selected targets and bioactive compounds. (B) The average binding energy of bioactive compounds to all targets. (C) The average binding energy of targets to all bioactive compounds.

3.6. WFY reduces IL-1β-induced elevated levels of inflammation

To determine the optimal concentration of WFY for our experimental conditions, we assessed the viability of chondrocytes exposed to varying concentrations of WFY (0, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 μg/mL) using the CCK-8 assay. Our data revealed a significant decline in cell viability at the highest concentration of WFY (1600 μg/mL), whereas no remarkable changes were observed at lower concentrations (Fig. 6A). Consequently, an 800 μg/mL concentration of WFY was selected for subsequent experiments. Co-administration of 800 μg/mL WFY with 10 ng/mL IL-1β did not significantly alter IL-1β-induced proliferation or viability of chondrocytes (Fig. 6B). ELISA analysis demonstrated that IL-1β significantly elevated the extracellular levels of IL-6 and TNF-α compared to the control group. Remarkably, treatment with WFY significantly attenuated these increases in IL-6 and TNF-α levels (Fig. 6C and D). These observations suggest that WFY effectively reduces the secretion of inflammatory cytokines in OA chondrocytes without affecting their proliferation or survival.

Effect of WFY on the cell viability and inflammatory response of chondrocytes. (A) Cell viability of chondrocytes after 24 hours of action of different concentrations of WFY. (B) Effect of WFY on cell viability of IL-1β-induced chondrocytes. (C and D) The effect of WFY on TNF-ɑ and IL-6 secretion by chondrocytes was assayed using ELISA. All data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). WFY = Wufu Yin.

3.7. Suppression of IL-1β-Induced PTGS2 upregulation and p38 activation by WFY

Network pharmacology identified PTGS2 and MAPK14 (p38) as key targets of WFY in the treatment of KOA, with relevance to the disease pathology. We thus investigated whether WFY modulates the expression of PTGS2 and p38. Specifically, Western blot analysis was employed to measure the expression levels of p-p38, total p38, and PTGS2. The results showed that IL-1β markedly increased the relative expression of p-p38/p38 and PTGS2 proteins; however, this effect was notably diminished following WFY treatment (Fig. 7A–C). Interestingly, there were no significant differences in the expression levels of total p38 among the groups (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these findings indicate that WFY is capable of inhibiting the upregulation of PTGS2 and the phosphorylation-activation of p38 in chondrocytes induced by IL-1β.

4. Discussion

The management of KOA entails a range of interventions, including pharmacotherapy, physical rehabilitation, and surgery. TCM has also been widely used as an alternative treatment modality, featuring approaches such as acupuncture, herbal fumigation, and cupping therapy. TCM has been found to offer symptomatic relief, pain reduction, and improved joint function. WFY, a TCM formulation originating from the Ming Dynasty in China, comprises P ginseng, R glutinosa, A sinensis, A macrocephala, and G uralensis, and is specifically intended to address deficiencies in qi and blood. Clinical investigations have demonstrated its significant therapeutic potential in the management of KOA.[4] The knee joint comprises cartilage, which is susceptible to damage and degeneration that underlie the pathophysiology of KOA. Research has revealed that A sinensis polysaccharides can stimulate the expression of IGF-1 and IGF-1 receptor genes, thereby promoting the synthesis of UDP-sugars and GAG, and further upregulating the expression of USSs genes, ultimately conferring anti-osteoarthritis activity.[6] Cartilage degeneration is linked to multiple factors, including cell apoptosis, inflammatory reactions, and oxidative stress, and the active ingredients of these herbs can also exert anti-osteoarthritis activity through these mechanisms. Notably, bone hyperplasia and osteoporosis are critical mechanisms underlying KOA, with bone hyperplasia leading to bone spur formation, which impairs joint mobility and the normal function of soft tissues, whereas osteoporosis renders bones fragile and vulnerable to fractures and other injuries.[7] Inflammatory reactions are also a common manifestation of KOA, whereby joint inflammation can cause damage to the cartilage and bone, thereby exacerbating progression of the disease.[8] The active ingredients of herbs such as P ginseng have been shown to possess anti-inflammatory properties.[9] Nonetheless, these scattered studies do not fully elucidate the therapeutic efficacy of WFY in KOA. Our cellular functional assays demonstrate that WFY markedly attenuates IL-1β-induced cellular inflammation, suggesting a potential avenue for alleviating KOA through its anti-inflammatory properties. Building upon these findings, this study employs network pharmacology and molecular docking technology to systematically investigate and interpret the potential mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of WFY in KOA.

The primary characteristic of Chinese medicine’s efficacy lies in the synergistic effect of multiple active ingredients, regulating multiple targets, pathways, and even organs to achieve clinical symptom relief or cure. Therefore, exploring the active ingredients of Chinese medicine is of paramount importance as it serves as the basis for subsequent systematic pharmacological analysis. In the absence of broad-spectrum detection techniques, such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry and high-performance liquid chromatography, it is especially critical to use reliable public database platforms for data retrieval and integration. In this study, the TCMSP analysis platform was employed to identify the active ingredients and their targets by searching for the chemical composition of each herb in WFY. This platform comprises over 5000 Chinese medicines and more than 20,000 compounds, encompassing various aspects of Chinese medicine, such as pharmacological effects, chemical composition, targets, and diseases. Based on network analysis, quercetin, kaempferol, licochalcone a, isoliquiritigenin, formononetin, and other targets were found to play important roles in the treatment of KOA with WFY. Some of these ingredients have been proven to possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities,[10] which are of significant value in relieving KOA inflammation. Studies have demonstrated that, in addition to its anti-inflammatory effects, licochalcone a can also inhibit matrix metalloproteinases and ADAMTSs in chondrocytes, which are considered potential therapeutic strategies for osteoarthritis.[11] Isoliquiritigenin attenuates osteoarthritis by inhibiting bone resorption and subchondral bone vascularization.[12] Formononetin can inhibit inflammation in human chondrocytes, slow down the progression of osteoarthritis in rat models, and its nano-drug development has also recently made progress in osteoarthritis.[13] Thus, these backgrounds not only confirm the reliability of the active ingredients based on TCMSP but also lay a solid foundation for subsequent target and mechanism research.

Moreover, this study identified several key targets and pathways for treating KOA with WFY through cross-analysis of drug targets and disease targets. Network topology analysis revealed that PTGS2, CDK2, PPARG, ESR1, and MAPKs are important targets or effector molecules that play crucial roles in the treatment of KOA with WFY. PTGS2, also known as COX2, is a critical enzyme in prostaglandin biosynthesis and possesses dual cyclooxygenase and peroxidase activities. Downregulation of PTGS2 can alleviate osteoarthritis since the pre-secretion of osteoclasts in aging promotes metabolic syndrome-related osteoarthritis.[14] Our investigation further corroborates the upregulation of PTGS2 protein expression in chondrocytes induced by IL-1β, while concurrently validating the capacity of WFY to counteract this phenomenon. This finding underscores the characteristic of PTGS2 as a potential therapeutic target for WFY in the context of KOA treatment. CDK2, as a cell cycle regulatory protein, has significantly reduced methylation levels in KOA cartilage tissue, suggesting its possible involvement in the pathological process. Isoliquiritigenin has been shown to increase CDK2 activity, which may play an important role in WFY treatment of KOA.[15] Chondrocyte autophagy is involved in osteoarthritis pathogenesis, and PPARG can participate in improving KOA by activating the Akt/MTOR signaling pathway and inducing chondrocyte autophagy. The incidence of female KOA is significantly higher than that of male, and ESR1 is believed to play a pivotal role due to significant gender differences in its expression; furthermore, polymorphism studies have shown that ESR1 mutations are associated with KOA.[16] MAPKs, acting as an integration point for multiple biochemical signals, participates in various cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, transcriptional regulation, and development, and has been shown to be regulated by active ingredients such as quercetin and kaempferol.[10,17] Evidence has demonstrated that elevated expression of p38 plays a pivotal role in facilitating chondrocyte demise in KOA.[18] Modulation of p38 phosphorylation and its downstream signaling cascade has been shown to promote autophagy in chondrocytes subjected to IL-1β-induced osteoarthritis, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses.[19] Although our findings did not reveal a significant alteration in total p38 levels within chondrocytes exposed to IL-1β, a marked increase in its phosphorylated form was observed, a condition that was effectively mitigated by WFY. This suggests that the phosphorylation of p38 and the consequent activation of the signaling cascade may represent crucial targets for the intervention of KOA.

By performing enrichment analysis of potential targets for treating KOA with WFY, we discovered that these targets are primarily concentrated in biological processes such as oxidative stress, inflammatory response, cell proliferation, and apoptosis, which aligns with existing treatment strategies for KOA. In addition, multiple signaling pathways contribute to the WFY treatment of KOA, including the TNF, IL-17, and NF-kappa B signaling pathways, which all play essential roles in mediating inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and other processes. Moreover, these targets also participate in Th17 cell and osteoclast differentiation processes. While peripheral blood Th17 cells are significantly increased in patients with osteoarthritis, Treg cells exhibit the opposite trend. Therefore, the imbalance of Th17/Treg cell ratio in the peripheral blood of osteoarthritis patients and the regulation of Th17 cell differentiation may be a potential treatment strategy for improving KOA.[20] Osteoclast-induced sensory nerve innervation and osteoarthritis pain, as well as abnormal subchondral bone remodeling, can exacerbate osteoarthritis. Hence, inhibiting osteoclast differentiation may represent another potential mechanism for WFY to treat KOA. These findings not only provide promising targets and pathways for treating KOA with WFY, but they also explain the therapeutic effect of WFY from the perspective of possible target cells.

Despite the exciting discoveries our study has made regarding the treatment of KOA with WFY, these findings are presently limited to conclusions based on established facts or software predictions and cannot fully elucidate the true mechanism of action. Notwithstanding the supplementation of our findings with cellular experiments and target validation, future research endeavors are warranted to conduct in vivo studies for the comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic efficacy and the corroboration of the underlying mechanisms.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study leveraged network pharmacology and molecular docking methodologies to elucidate the potential targets and molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of WFY in KOA. Our in vitro experimentation not only substantiated the anti-inflammatory properties of WFY by demonstrating its capability to suppress IL-1β-induced inflammation in chondrocytes, but also revealed its efficacy in inhibiting the upregulation of PTGS2 expression and the phosphorylation-activation of p38 triggered by IL-1β. Nonetheless, to robustly validate and advance the insights garnered from this study, a comprehensive series of both in vitro and in vivo experiments are indispensable.
