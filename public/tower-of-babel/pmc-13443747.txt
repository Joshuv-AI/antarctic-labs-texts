Evaluation of the impact of Comprehensive Medication Management on health outcomes in people living with hypertension: a pragmatic clinical trial
Authors: Taiana de Alencar, Maria Paula Debona Vieira, Mayra Cardozo Mendes, Elisabeth Maria López De Prado, Emilly Velasquez Nicolini, Andressa Carlos Barbosa, Bárbara Brambila-Manso, Maria Eduarda Silva Pinto, Larissa Couto-Rosa, Alfredo Dias de Oliveira-Filho, Dyego Carlos Araújo, Kérilin Stancine Santos Rocha, Genival Araujo dos Santos-Júnior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension is a chronic condition frequently requiring long-term pharmacotherapy, which may increase the risk of medication-related problems. Comprehensive Medication Management (CMM) has been associated with improvements in health outcomes in patients with hypertension; however, evidence from pragmatic randomized clinical trials in primary care settings remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the association between pharmacist-led CMM on clinical, anthropometric, humanistic, and cardiovascular risk outcomes among patients with hypertension treated within the public health care network of Alegre, Espírito Santo, Brazil. A pragmatic, single-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial with a 12-month follow-up was conducted. A total of 128 adults with hypertension were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention group (CMM) or the control group (usual care). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for clinical, anthropometric, or humanistic parameters; cardiovascular risk was the only outcome showing a statistically significant time × group interaction (p = 0.039; η2p = 0.026), indicating a trend toward a more favorable trajectory in the CMM group. Intention-to-treat analyses showed significant time effects for systolic blood pressure, lipid profile components, quality of life, hypertension-related knowledge, and cardiovascular risk in both groups. In exploratory intragroup analyses, the intervention group showed significant changes in systolic blood pressure (− 9.47 mmHg), total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, and cardiovascular risk, along with improvements in quality of life and hypertension-related knowledge, whereas the control group showed improvements limited to knowledge scores and self-rated quality of life. Per-protocol sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistency of these findings. Although between-group comparisons did not demonstrate clear statistical superiority of CMM over usual care for most outcomes, the results suggest that CMM may contribute to clinically relevant improvements in cardiovascular risk and patient-centered outcomes in real-world practice. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, and larger, adequately powered, multicenter pragmatic trials are warranted to confirm the effectiveness of CMM in hypertension management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54614-w.Keywords: Hypertension, Pharmacists, Pharmacy practice, Clinical TrialSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Hypertension represents an important public health concern worldwide given its complex etiology and significant consequences, including cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and renal impairment1–4. Estimates indicate that about 1.4 billion people currently live with hypertension worldwide5,6, contributing to about 10.9 million deaths in 20217. This condition affects more than 38.1 million people in Brazil, making it one of the most prevalent chronic health conditions in the country8. In Espírito Santo, a state in southeastern Brazil, an estimated 700,000 people live with hypertension9. Although no data on the prevalence of this health condition are available for the municipality of Alegre/ES, a study in the region has reported a high prevalence10.

The treatment of hypertension involves lifestyle modifications (primarily related to changes in health behaviors) and medication1,4. These are important tools in managing hypertension as monotherapies or in combination, depending on their degree, associated risk factors, and each patient1–4,11. Although management options include lifestyle changes and medications, the use of antihypertensive medications alone has been associated with suboptimal blood pressure control rates, despite strong evidence supporting the efficacy of drug treatment12,13.

This scenario requires multidisciplinary interventions to control individuals’ hypertension and improve their quality of life. This context highlights the contribution of pharmacists as essential members of reference health teams. Their clinical services play a strategic role in finding, preventing, and solving medication-related problems to better clinical outcomes, reduce risks, and improve effectiveness and quality of health care provided to individuals14.

A randomized clinical trial in primary health care in the state of São Paulo, Brazil, evaluated the cost-effectiveness of Comprehensive Medication Management (CMM) for patients with hypertension and diabetes over three years15. The results demonstrated significant reductions in systolic (SBP) (23 mmHg) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (14.8 mmHg), as well as a 0.7% reduction in hemoglobin A1C (p < 0.001). Other studies have reported positive impacts of pharmacists on other outcomes, such as improved quality of life, increased knowledge about the disease, and satisfaction with CMM16–18.

Despite the growing evidence, high-quality studies evaluating the impact of Comprehensive Medication Management (CMM) on hypertension outcomes remain limited, particularly those adopting pragmatic designs. This gap is relevant in the Brazilian Health System, where organizational, resource, and population characteristics differ from controlled research environments. Considering that pragmatic clinical trials more closely reflect real-world practice, it is important to generate high-impact scientific evidence related to the effects of this service on the health outcomes of patients with hypertension in this context19. Thus, this study aims to evaluate the impact of CMM on the health outcomes of patients with hypertension who received treatment in the health care network in Alegre/ES.

Methods

Study design

This study is a pragmatic, randomized, single-blind, and controlled clinical trial that lasted 12 months. It consisted of two groups, a control group (CG), in which patients received usual care, and an intervention group (IG), in which patients received the pharmacotherapeutic regimen. This study was carried out according to the research protocol developed before the start of data collection, in accordance with the recommendations of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials20 and Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials21.

We assessed the degree of pragmatism using the PRagmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary 2 (PRECIS-2) tool (Supplementary Material), scored from 1 (very explanatory) to 5 (very pragmatic)22.

Study setting

This study was carried out within the Brazilian Health System of the municipality of Alegre, located in southern Espírito Santo, Brazil. This municipality has an area of about 778.6 km2 and an estimated population of 29,177 inhabitants23​. Estimates suggest that Alegre has about 30 private health establishments, one hospital, one municipal emergency room, 10 primary care establishments, one mental health unit, and one medical specialties center. It also has one community pharmacy.

Selection and recruitment

This project was disseminated to managers and the health team (cardiologists, nutritionists, nursing team, community health workers, and social workers) through meetings with the project coordinator and the pharmacists. Dissemination also occurred within the community through active outreach and health education activities. Patients were recruited by: (i) active searches of medical records of patients linked to the Hiperdia (National Program of Arterial Hypertension and Diabetes Mellitus) and direct approach of patients attending the Community Pharmacy of the municipality and those who attended the cardiology service of the municipality of Alegre; (ii) referral from community health agents.

The researchers contacted the patients by phone and in person to verify eligibility criteria, briefly explained the project, and invited them to participate after obtaining written informed consent. Recruitment started in January 2022 and ended in October 2022. Previously trained undergraduate students, under the supervision of the research team, completed the selection and recruitment stages. The informed consent form and the patient recruitment protocol are provided as Supplementary Information.

Research team training

Training consisted of a theoretical-practical course in which interview techniques, measurement of clinical-anthropometric parameters, and application of research instruments were addressed. Data were collected by external evaluators (undergraduate students in pharmacy) who had no knowledge of the nature of this study. A standardized form (Supplementary Information) and implementation protocols were used to ensure standardization and consistency in the collection of information at the three chosen time points. Additionally, the entire team who collected data underwent periodic training to ensure the quality and reliability of the results (Supplementary Information).

Sample calculation

The study sample was estimated at 128 patients, divided into two equal groups: 64 patients in the intervention group and 64 patients in the control group. The expected reduction in SBP totaled 10 mmHg (Δ = − 10). A 5% significance level (α = 0.05) and an 80% statistical power (β = 0.80) were adopted. In general, a 10-mmHg reduction in SBP is associated with a decrease in the risk of major cardiovascular events such as heart attack (20%), coronary heart disease (17%), stroke (37%), heart failure (28%), and all-cause mortality (13%)24​. The sample size was calculated on Stata®, version 16.1, considering the difference in means between independent samples and a sample loss of up to 20%.

Collected variables

Sociodemographic data

The sociodemographic data were collected using the Google Forms® platform, following the standardization of collection and approach provided in the theoretical-practical training. During patients contact, identification documents were requested and the following variables were collected: gender, ethnicity, place of birth, age, years of schooling, and marital status.

Humanistic parameters

The following humanistic parameter was collected:

Quality of life

Evaluation was carried out by the European Quality of Life 5 dimensions (EQ-5D-3L) and the mini-questionnaire on quality of life in arterial hypertension (Minichal)25–27.

The EQ-5D-3L constitutes a generic questionnaire validated for the Brazilian context that evaluates various health conditions26. It consists of two parts: a descriptive system composed of five dimensions: mobility, personal care, usual activities, pain/malaise, and anxiety/depression, each with three response options according to level of severity (e.g.: no problems—level 1, some problems—level 2, and extreme problems—level 3). This part of the questionnaire has 243 health statuses, ranging from 1111 (one, no problems in any dimension) and 3333 (three, severe problems in all dimensions). The interpretation of the 243 possible health states followed the numerical predictors in the literature26. The questionnaire also includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from zero (worst imaginable health status) to 100 (best imaginable health status).

Minichal, in turn, configures a specific questionnaire for hypertension that comprises 17 multiple-choice questions into two domains: mental state (10 questions) and somatic manifestations (six questions) and an additional question to assess how patients perceive the influence of hypertension and its treatment on their quality of life27.

Satisfaction with the service

This was evaluated by an adaptation of the “Patient Satisfaction with Pharmacist Services Questionnaire” that Correr et al. (2009) validated for Brazilian Portuguese and that was developed by Larson, Rovers, and Mackeigan (2002)28,29. This questionnaire has 12 items to be answered on a five-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction.

Satisfaction was also evaluated by the Net Promoter Score, which uses a 10-point Likert scale to determine to which extent patients are willing to recommend pharmacists’ services to others. Patients were asked to rate their recommendation on a scale of zero to 10, in which zero indicates that they would not recommend it at all and 10 indicates a highly likely recommendation. Based on this classification, patients were categorized as follows: (i) promoters, if they assigned a score from 9 to 10; (ii) neutral, if they assign a score of 7 to 8; (iii) detractors, if they assign a score between 1 and 630,31. The outcome of satisfaction with the service provided was evaluated only for patients allocated to the IG.

Other variables

Knowledge about hypertension: It was evaluated by the Hypertension Knowledge-Level Scale32, which has been translated and cross-culturally adapted to Brazilian Portuguese, showing evidence of reliability and validity regarding its content, appearance, construct, and criteria to measure knowledge about hypertension in Brazilians33,34. It consists of 22 statements divided into six sub-dimensions. Each statement is answered by a Likert scale with “right, “wrong,” and “I don’t know” answer options, the maximum score of which totals 22 points. Each sub-dimension has a different maximum score: definition (2 points), medical treatment (4 points), drug compliance (4 points), lifestyle (5 points), diet (2 points), and complications (5 points).

Cardiovascular risk: Cardiovascular risk was estimated using the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), which predicts the 10-year risk of general cardiovascular events. Risk was categorized as low (< 10%), intermediate (10–20%), or high (> 20%), according to established guidelines1.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible patients underwent initial evaluation (T0) and were then randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the CG and IG. A computer-generated sequence of random numbers from Research Randomizer (http://www.randomizer.gov) was used for randomization. Randomization was performed by a research pharmacist who was not involved in the recruitment or selection processes, nor in the interventions or data analysis. Allocation concealment was ensured through a centralized web-based randomization system managed by an external coordinating center (considered the gold standard). Only this researcher had access to the randomization list, which was kept confidential. Allocation was disclosed only after each participant was enrolled in the study, ensuring that the professionals involved in recruitment and intervention were unaware of the sequence, thus preventing selection bias. No stratified randomization was performed for allocating patients to the groups.

Then, the researchers responsible for the intervention received information about IG patients. Due to the nature of the intervention, it was impossible to mask the pharmaceutical drugs. Patients were not informed about the group to which they were assigned. The researchers in charge of collecting the primary and secondary outcomes and the researcher responsible for analyzing the data were also blinded to the allocation of patients. Patients’ data were given to the researcher in charge of the statistical analysis as “group 1” and “group 2” to obscure information about which group was the intervention or control.

Interventions

Intervention group

Patients randomly assigned to the intervention group received a pharmacotherapeutic regimen35–37. In it, the pharmacist takes responsibility for the patient’s pharmacotherapeutic needs and the care process adopted in this service involves the following steps in a cyclical manner:

Initial patient assessment: this step aims to collect, analyze, interpret, and understand patients’ complaints and needs to identify Medication-related problems (MRP). For this, the data collection forms available in the Supplementary Information were used;

Elaboration of the care plan: this stage aims to prevent, control, and solve the identified MRP to achieve the established therapeutic goals. It is essential that the plan is developed in collaboration with patients and/or healthcare teams.

Elaboration of the care plan: this stage aims to prevent, control, and solve the identified MRP to achieve the established therapeutic goals. It is essential that the plan is developed in collaboration with patients and/or healthcare teams.

Evaluation of results: this stage aims to evaluate the pharmaceutical interventions and determine whether the therapeutic goals established in the previous stage have been achieved.

The patients were followed up for one year. An average of six consultations were carried out with an interval of about two months between them (depending on individual demands). Consultations took place in a private environment in face-to-face meetings that had been scheduled with the patients. The meetings were held by four pharmacists who were studying for their master’s and PhD degrees.

First consultation (C1): the pharmacist introduced herself and the objective of the service to the patient and carried out the initial evaluation process. Sociodemographic, clinical, lifestyle, and pharmacotherapeutic profile data were collected in this consultation. Therapeutic goals and personalized interventions were established based on this information according to the needs of each patient, resulting in a care plan.

Subsequent consultations (C2, C3, C4, C5, and C6): the pharmacist established patients’ care plan. The pharmaceutical interventions in the care plan covered areas such as guidance on health conditions (nature, causes, diagnosis, pharmacotherapy, and monitoring), lifestyle changes, training for self-care and prevention of acute and chronic complications, guidance on the rational use of medications, identification of signs and symptoms related to the medication (efficacy and safety), provision of educational materials (such as dose schedules, pictograms, and booklets), prescription of medicines and/or supplements without medical prescription, and referral to other health professionals and services if necessary.

Results were also evaluated in these consultations to verify if the goals were achieved and to identify new needs. A new care plan and interventions were adopted if these goals were unmet. The protocol with a detailed description of how the pharmacists carried out the consultations can be found as Supplementary Information.

Control group

CG patients received the usual care, which involved the health care offered by the municipal health care network, which may involve the delivery of prescribed medications in public or private community pharmacies, which may include guidance on the use of medications.

Evaluation time points

IG and CG patients’ outcomes were evaluated over a 12-month follow-up at three time points: baseline (T0), 6 months (T1), and 12 months (T2). Study variables were collected in these three moments, except for satisfaction with service, which was performed in T1 and T2 for the IG. The 6-month assessment was included to capture intermediate changes in blood pressure and other outcomes, allowing evaluation of the trajectory and early effectiveness of the intervention. The 12-month follow-up was chosen to assess the sustainability of the intervention effects, as this time frame is commonly used in hypertension studies to evaluate long-term blood pressure control and clinically meaningful outcomes38,39.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JASP (version 0.95.4) and SPSS (version 29.0). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with 95% confidence intervals.

Intention-to-treat and per-protocol approaches

The primary analyses followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, whereby all participants were retained in their originally allocated groups. Missing data at 6 and 12 months were imputed using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm, assuming data missing at random. A per-protocol (PP) analysis was additionally conducted as a sensitivity analysis including only participants who completed the full 12-month follow-up.

Repeated-measures analysis

To evaluate changes in clinical, anthropometric and humanistic outcomes over time (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was applied for normally distributed variables. When the sphericity assumption was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. For non-normally distributed outcomes, the Friedman test was used. Effect sizes for RM-ANOVA were reported using partial eta squared (η2p).

Multivariable models

Two complementary analytical strategies were used. First, repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) was applied to evaluate within-group changes over time (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months) and time × group interactions, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction when sphericity was violated. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared (η2p).

Second, adjusted between-group differences at 12 months (T2) were estimated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). In these models, the T2 value of each outcome was entered as the dependent variable, treatment group was included as the main factor, and the corresponding baseline value (T0) was included as a covariate. Additional covariates included age, sex, and years of education. Adjusted mean differences between groups were reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Mean changes (Δ T2–T0) are presented for descriptive purposes only and were not used for hypothesis testing.

These ANCOVA models are mathematically equivalent to multivariable linear regression and were used to provide clinically interpretable adjusted estimates of intervention effects.

Intragroup Analyses

Intragroup analyses (T0, T1, T2) were conducted to explore temporal changes within each group. These analyses were considered descriptive, as between-group differences were primarily assessed through RM-ANOVA and multivariable models.

Statistical significance

All tests were two-sided and a significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η2p) for repeated-measures analyses and as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals for ANCOVA models, to facilitate clinical interpretation.

Intention-to-treat analysis: longitudinal trajectories

The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis included all 128 randomized participants (64 in the intervention group [IG] and 64 in the control group [CG]). Repeated-measures analyses conducted under the intention-to-treat principle demonstrate significant time effects for several clinical and humanistic outcomes, indicating improvements over the 12-month follow-up period in both groups (Table S1). Significant reductions over time were observed for SBP, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, and Framingham cardiovascular risk score. Significant time effects were also identified for quality of life measures (EQ-5D-3L index and VAS), HK-LS, and Minichal domains.

No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for clinical, anthropometric, or humanistic parameters; cardiovascular risk was the only outcome showing a statistically significant time × group interaction (p = 0.039; η2p = 0.026), indicating a trend toward a more favorable trajectory among participants in the intervention group.

Adjusted between-group comparisons at T2

Adjusted between-group differences at 12 months were estimated using ANCOVA-equivalent multivariable models, controlling for baseline values of each outcome and relevant covariates (Table 2). These analyses revealed no statistically significant between-group differences for most clinical, anthropometric, or humanistic outcomes.

Intragroup analyses

Intragroup analyses were conducted for descriptive and exploratory purposes to characterize changes over time within each group. Within the IG, clinically relevant changes from T0 and T2 were observed for: SBP (∆ = − 9.47 mmHg, p = 0.014); total cholesterol (∆ = − 18.93 mg/dL, p = 0.001), LDL (∆ = − 15.70 mg/dL, p < 0.001), and non-HDL cholesterol (∆ = − 19.76 mg/dL, p < 0.001); and cardiovascular risk (∆ = − 1.82 points, p < 0.001).

IG also showed an increase in the knowledge score (∆ = 1.39 points, p < 0.001) and improved quality of life in the mental state subdimension (∆ = − 2.76 points, p = 0.020) and somatic manifestations (∆ = − 1.5 points, p = 0.012) (Table 3).

Discussion

This clinical trial was designed to evaluate the effect of a CMM in a real-world setting. Thus, this research can be considered a pragmatic study as it was conducted in an uncontrolled environment. Pragmatic studies reflect the realities of local communities, enabling the evaluation of CMM in routine health services. Pragmatic clinical trials show some advantages over traditional clinical trials, including lower costs, reduced potential for selection bias, and greater generalizability to broader populations19.

In this study, the intervention and control groups were considered homogeneous at baseline across all assessed variables, as recommended in the literature20,40, allowing for valid comparisons between groups regarding key characteristics41. The results showed that women composed most individuals in both groups. This finding corroborates the evidence in the literature that indicates a greater demand for health services by women than by men42,43. Nevertheless, the predominance of women in the sample may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly to male populations with hypertension. The mean age of the patients agrees with other studies that have described the profile of patients with hypertension receiving primary care in Brazil43. These observations reflect the demographic composition of Brazil, characterized by a predominance of women and an aging population44.

No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for clinical, anthropometric, or humanistic parameters. Intention-to-treat analyses showed significant time effects for systolic blood pressure, lipid profile components, quality of life, hypertension-related knowledge, and cardiovascular risk in both groups. Overall, several factors can explain the absence of significant differences between groups despite their homogeneity after randomization: (i) the mean baseline SBP and DBP at the initial evaluation were already close to the therapeutic goal, hindering the detection of a statistical difference, as in other studies45–47; (ii) within the context of pragmatic trials, these parallel improvements over time likely reflect Hawthorne effects, better-than-usual care, and potential spillover into the control group48. Repeated check-ins, extra clinical attention, and off-the-record advice provided during routine follow-up visits may have contributed to these improvements; (iii) additionally, the predicted rate of loss to follow-up may also have affected our ability to detect differences between groups49–52. About 31% of participants dropped out, which is higher than expected53 and likely reduced the study’s ability to spot real differences. These losses can also stem from the characteristics of the service, since although the model of pharmaceutical care practice is known35–37, the general population often does not perceive pharmacists as clinical professionals54,55.

In this study, cardiovascular risk was the only outcome showing a statistically significant time × group interaction, indicating a trend toward a more favorable trajectory in the CMM group, however, adjusted between-group differences at 12 months did not reach conventional statistical significance. Firmino (2013), a study conducted in a northeast state in Brazil, also demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular risk following CMM47. The Cardiovascular Prevention Guideline of the Brazilian Society of Cardiology56, points to a relationship between a reduction in these risk factors and cardiovascular risk in the life expectancy of patients over the next 10 years. As cardiovascular diseases configure the leading cause of death in the world, the decrease in cardiovascular risk directly contributes to preventing these diseases and improving quality of life and increased life expectancy1,2. In this context, the cardiovascular risk may represent a clinically relevant parameter for pharmacists to assess, monitor, and support clinical management of patients with hypertension, contributing to comprehensive care and to reducing the negative consequences of cardiovascular events.

The intragroup analysis showed statistically significant differences. The reduction in SBP represents a clinically relevant finding, in agreement with meta-analyses of pharmacist-led interventions16,57. Considering that a 10 mmHg decrease in SBP is associated with the prevention of cardiovascular events and a reduction in cardiovascular-related mortality1,58, the results of this study suggest that CMM may contribute to clinically meaningful reductions in blood pressure, with a reduction in SBP similar to that achieved by some antihypertensive medications59, although the findings did not demonstrate clear statistical superiority over usual care. CMM also significantly reduced total cholesterol, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which have reported significant reductions in total and LDL cholesterol among patients receiving pharmacist-led or similar CMM interventions60,61. Reductions in lipid profile components, especially LDL cholesterol, together with blood pressure control, are associated with improved cardiovascular health, a lower risk of cardiovascular events, and stroke prevention62,63.

Still within the intragroup analysis, improvements in quality of life and knowledge about hypertension highlight relevant humanistic outcomes of the intervention. Significant improvements were observed in specific quality-of-life dimensions, suggesting enhanced subjective well-being and a potential reduction in the severity of health-related complaints, particularly reflected by transitions from level 3 to levels 1 and 2 in EQ-5D-3L dimensions. These findings are consistent with evidence indicating that individuals with hypertension without severe comorbidities tend to report good quality of life64 and that maintaining it is essential for adopting healthy lifestyle behaviors and improving overall well-being65. The increase in hypertension-related knowledge observed after one year of follow-up aligns with previous studies involving pharmacist-led care and is clinically relevant, as improved knowledge has been associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality66–68. In the control group, improvements in quality of life measured by the EQ-5D-3L VAS and in hypertension knowledge were also observed. These findings may be explained by the subjective nature of the VAS, which is influenced by emotional and psychosocial factors69, as well as by the potential positive effects of perceived attention and usual care, even in the absence of structured pharmacotherapeutic follow-up70. Additionally, the increase in knowledge in the control group may reflect a learning effect related to repeated questionnaire administration or increased curiosity prompted by the assessment itself, as previously described in the literature70–72.

Overall, the per-protocol (PP) sensitivity analysis confirmed the principal findings of the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. Improvements were consistently observed over time for SBP, lipid profile components, quality of life measures, hypertension-related knowledge, and cardiovascular risk. Importantly, the consistency in the direction and magnitude of effects between ITT and PP analyses supports the robustness and reliability of the intervention’s impact, despite the methodological limitations inherent to pragmatic trials.

Finally, improvements were observed in humanistic outcomes related to satisfaction with CMM. Patients in the intervention group reported high satisfaction levels and were willing to recommend the service, reinforcing the perceived value of pharmacotherapeutic follow-up18,73,74.

The main limitation of this study refers to the higher-than-expected loss to follow-up (31%), which likely impacted the statistical power to detect differences between the groups. This higher attrition rate may be attributed to factors such as voluntary withdrawal and the perception of pharmacists not seen as clinical professionals by the general population. Subgroup analyses (e.g., according to baseline risk or adherence level) could not be performed due to insufficient data to reliably classify participants. Although researchers and data analysts were blinded, the intervention could not be masked to pharmacists or patients, which may have introduced performance bias, particularly for subjective outcomes such as quality-of-life measures. This risk was mitigated through use of objective clinical endpoints, standardized intervention delivery, and blinded data processing and analysis. Follow-up loss reached 31%, higher than the 20% assumed during power estimation, which may have reduced precision and statistical power and threatened internal validity. To address this issue, intention-to-treat analyses with EM-based imputation were conducted, alongside per-protocol sensitivity analyses. The consistency of results across both approaches supports the robustness of the findings, although residual attrition bias cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the possibility of contamination of the control group cannot be ruled out, as participants continued to receive usual care within the same health system. The absence of detailed data on medication adherence and safety also limits a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed effects. Finally, the single-center design and the predominance of women in the sample may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings and populations.

Despite these limitations, the randomized design strengthens the internal validity of the study and supports cautious inferences regarding the association of CMM with hypertension management. The consistency between intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses further reinforces the robustness of the findings. Moreover, as this pragmatic study was carried out in a real-world setting, it provides practical and contextually relevant evidence on the implementation and impact of CMM in patients with hypertension within the Brazilian Health System. In this context, the implementation of CMM may be facilitated by its alignment with multidisciplinary team-based care and the expanding role of pharmacists within public health services. However, structural and organizational barriers, such as limited physical infrastructure, insufficient workforce availability, and competing demands within routine consultations, pose key challenges to the integration of clinical pharmacy services75. Addressing these factors is essential to ensure the sustainable integration of CMM into primary care settings. From a health systems perspective, CMM may represent a scalable and potentially cost-effective strategy for hypertension management, by reducing medication-related waste, avoidable hospitalizations, and emergency department visits associated with poor blood pressure control76. Future studies should incorporate formal economic evaluations to better assess its value in optimizing public health resources.

Conclusion

No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for clinical, anthropometric, or humanistic parameters; cardiovascular risk was the only outcome showing a statistically significant time × group interaction. Intention-to-treat analyses showed significant time effects for systolic blood pressure, lipid profile components, quality of life, hypertension-related knowledge, and cardiovascular risk in both groups. CMM was associated with improvements over time within the intervention group, although no clear superiority over usual care was demonstrated. CMM was associated with clinically relevant changes in SBP, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and non-HDL cholesterol, as well as improvements in hypertension knowledge scores and participants’ quality of life.

These findings reinforce that CMM is a feasible and potentially effective intervention to manage patients with hypertension in real-world settings, contributing to improvements in cardiovascular risk factors and health outcomes. However, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and limited by the study design and sample characteristics. Larger, adequately powered, multicenter pragmatic randomized clinical trials are needed to confirm these results and to determine whether CMM offers superiority over usual care in hypertension management.
