Background

Tuberculosis remains a major global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries and regions affected by healthcare limitations [1]. Tuberculous pleuritis is among the most frequent manifestations of extrapulmonary tuberculosis and typically presents with nonspecific symptoms such as pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, fever, and weight loss [2].

Radiologically, tuberculous pleuritis most commonly manifests as unilateral pleural effusion, which may be free-flowing or loculated and is sometimes associated with pleural thickening [3]. However, in certain cases, these findings may closely resemble pleural malignancies, including malignant mesothelioma or metastatic pleural disease, leading to diagnostic uncertainty [4].

Chest wall involvement, including rib or vertebral destruction, is an uncommon manifestation of tuberculosis but may occur through direct extension of infection. Such findings are more typically associated with malignancy and may further complicate the diagnostic process [5].

In resource-limited settings, access to advanced diagnostic tools such as pleural fluid biomarkers and molecular testing may be restricted, necessitating reliance on invasive procedures such as thoracoscopy for definitive diagnosis [6].

Case presentation

A 41-year-old male with a significant smoking history (30 pack-years) presented with a two-month history of progressive right-sided pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, night sweats, and unintentional weight loss of approximately 8 kg. There was no history of prior tuberculosis, malignancy, or occupational asbestos exposure.

On physical examination, decreased breath sounds were noted over the right hemithorax. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP: 81.7 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR: 84 mm/h).

Diagnostic thoracentesis was performed and demonstrated an exudative pleural effusion with lymphocyte predominance. However, due to limited diagnostic resources, further biochemical and microbiological analyses, including adenosine deaminase (ADA), cytology, molecular testing (GeneXpert MTB/RIF), and mycobacterial cultures, were not available. Sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli was negative. Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) and tuberculin skin testing were not performed due to resource limitations.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest demonstrated a large loculated right-sided pleural effusion with diffuse pleural thickening and compressive atelectasis. Additionally, cortical irregularity and destruction of the posterior segment of the right 10th rib with extension toward the adjacent vertebral body were observed, raising strong suspicion for pleural malignancy (Fig. 1).

Given the diagnostic uncertainty and limited availability of advanced investigations, the patient underwent diagnostic video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).

Thoracoscopic exploration revealed diffusely thickened and inflamed parietal pleura with multiple whitish plaque-like lesions and areas of subpleural bulging over the involved rib. No discrete tumor mass was identified. Multiple targeted pleural biopsies were obtained (Fig. 2).

Histopathological examination demonstrated well-formed epithelioid granulomas with caseating necrosis and Langhans-type multinucleated giant cells. No evidence of malignancy was identified. Intraoperative frozen section analysis was not performed due to institutional limitations.

The patient was started on standard anti-tuberculous therapy consisting of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months, followed by isoniazid and rifampicin for four months.

The patient showed marked clinical improvement, with complete resolution of symptoms and significant radiological regression on follow-up imaging over several months.

Discussion

Tuberculous pleuritis remains a common cause of exudative pleural effusion, particularly in endemic regions, yet it continues to present a diagnostic challenge due to its ability to mimic pleural malignancy [2, 4].

In this case, the presence of loculated pleural effusion, diffuse pleural thickening, and especially rib and vertebral destruction strongly suggested malignancy. Such features are more commonly associated with malignant pleural mesothelioma or metastatic disease; however, they may rarely occur in tuberculosis due to chronic inflammatory extension [5].

The pleural fluid analysis revealed a lymphocyte-predominant exudative effusion, which is highly suggestive of tuberculous pleuritis in the appropriate clinical context [4]. However, the absence of advanced microbiological testing represents a recognized limitation, although such constraints are common in resource-limited settings.

The differential diagnosis included malignant mesothelioma, metastatic pleural disease, chronic empyema, fungal infections, and other granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis. The absence of a detectable primary tumor and the presence of systemic symptoms supported an infectious etiology, although definitive differentiation required tissue diagnosis [6].

Thoracoscopic pleural biopsy played a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. Thoracoscopy has a high diagnostic yield in undiagnosed pleural effusion and allows direct visualization and targeted biopsy of abnormal pleural areas [7].

Histopathological findings of caseating granulomas with Langhans-type giant cells strongly supported tuberculosis. Although microbiological confirmation was not obtained, the combination of histopathology, clinical presentation, pleural fluid characteristics, and favorable response to therapy is widely accepted as sufficient for diagnosis in resource-limited settings [8].

This case is notable for the presence of chest wall involvement, which is an uncommon manifestation of tuberculous pleuritis and contributed to the initial suspicion of malignancy. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of atypical pleural disease.

Conclusions

Tuberculous pleuritis should always be considered in the differential diagnosis of pleural effusion with features suggestive of malignancy, particularly in endemic and resource-limited settings.

Unusual findings such as rib or vertebral involvement may further obscure the diagnosis and mimic malignant disease.

Thoracoscopic pleural biopsy is a highly valuable diagnostic tool when non-invasive investigations are inconclusive or unavailable.
