CAR T cells against the B cell receptor to precisely eliminate pathogenic B cells
Authors: Audrey C. Bochi-Layec, Ivan J. Cohen, Jean Lemoine, Marco Ruella
Published: 2026

Main text

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, monoclonal antibodies, and bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) have transformed the treatment of B cell malignancies and are emerging as an exciting therapeutic option for autoimmune diseases. In particular, CAR T cells targeting CD19 (CART19) and BsAbs targeting CD3/CD19 and CD3/CD20 are now approved for B cell malignancies and are undergoing clinical trials for B cell-mediated autoimmune diseases. However, while potent, these therapies target pan-B cell markers and are, therefore, associated with a significant B cell aplasia and hypogammaglobulinemia.1,2,3 Indeed, following treatment, a significant portion of patients with B cell malignancies experience prolonged immune suppression, leading to the inability to mount effective immune responses even following vaccination. As a consequence, these patients face potentially life-threatening infections for years afterward.1,4

In contrast to an approach that targets a pan-B cell marker, next-generation immunotherapeutic strategies that precisely target neoplastic or self-reactive B cells while sparing the otherwise healthy B cell compartment would ensure long-term immune protection for treated patients. The B cell receptor (BcR) repertoire is highly diverse and polyclonal in healthy B cells, as it is composed of multiple immunoglobulin variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) regions that can further be mutated to achieve incredible breadth against foreign antigens. Interestingly, however, a small number of BcRs carrying specific immunoglobulin heavy chain or light chain regions have been shown to be enriched across B cell malignancies and autoimmune diseases due to their propensity toward antigen-independent signaling and self-reactivity, respectively.5,6

Prior studies have similarly developed therapies to target the BcR for B cell malignancies and autoimmune diseases. However, both the specificity of the therapy toward only neoplastic or autoimmune B cells and its applicability to a broader population varies (Figure 1). For the treatment of B cell malignancies, examples include anti-pan-κ or pan-λ CAR T cells (targets 40%–60% of healthy B cells), anti-mutated IGLV3-21 CAR T cells (chronic lymphocytic leukemia [CLL] subset specific), and monoclonal antibodies or vaccines against B cell idiotypes (patient specific).5,7,8,9 In the context of autoimmune disease therapeutics, chimeric autoantibody receptor T cells have also been developed to target the BcRs responsible for specific autoimmune diseases, such as pemphigus vulgaris.10Figure 1Patient immunosuppression risk due to precision capabilities of immunotherapy targets for B cell malignancies and autoimmune diseasesImmunotherapies range from targeting nearly 100% of B cell populations to only targeting specific BcR clones.

Our group found that, in the context of B cell malignancies, the immunoglobulin heavy variable 4-34 (IGHV4-34) BcR represents a potentially safer and more precise target for CAR T cell therapy when compared with the current standard CD19 and is more broadly applicable than prior anti-BcR studies.6 Using next-generation sequencing, we determined that across healthy and malignant B cells, the IGHV4-34 gene was only expressed in ∼5% of a healthy individual’s B cells, but was highly expressed in patients with B cell malignancies, including diffuse large B cell lymphoma, hairy cell leukemia, and CLL. In patients defined to have IGHV4-34+ B cell malignancies, the IGHV4-34 BcR represented the dominant clone and was homogenously expressed, suggesting the IGHV4-34 BcR is an ideal CAR T cell target.

We then developed an anti-IGHV4-34 CAR T cell (CART4-34) using the rat monoclonal antibody 9G4 to form the single-chain variable fragment. Compared with CART19, CART4-34 demonstrated specificity to IGHV4-34+-only tumor cell lines and primary samples. However, CART4-34 demonstrated subpar long-term in vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity. We hypothesized that this weak cytotoxicity may have been because CART4-34 cells target a membrane-distal portion of the BcR antigen, while standard CART19 targets a membrane-proximal epitope of the CD19 antigen. CAR T cells targeting membrane-proximal epitopes may result in a more compact immune synapse, a critical requirement for CAR T activation. Since CART4-34 specifically targets the heavy variable chain of the BcR, it cannot target an epitope closer to the plasma membrane. Thus, to address this issue, we replaced the original 44-amino acid CD8a hinge of the CAR (CART4-34 [CD8]) with either a short 12-amino acid IgG4-based hinge (CART4-34 [IgG4]) or a 5-amino acid G4S linker (CART4-34 [G4S]) to promote strong immune synapse formation. We found that shortening the CART4-34 hinge, in particular the CART4-34 (G4S) construct, led to improved enhanced anti-tumor effects in vitro and in vivo against tumor cell lines as well as primary malignant B cells.

The IGHV4-34 BcR and its corresponding antibodies have also been implicated in autoimmune diseases. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), B cells and IgG antibodies expressing the IGHV4-34 gene are highly enriched, autoreactive to nuclear antigens, and associated with SLE severity.11 We thus focused on the therapeutic potential of CART4-34 for SLE. To better define the significance of IGHV4-34 BcR as a target, we first aimed to deepen the field’s understanding of how IGHV4-34+ immunoglobulins (Igs) contribute to the SLE autoantibody repertoire. Upon depleting IGHV4-34 from SLE patient sera, we observed reductions of SLE-associated autoantibodies (anti-RO60, anti-SM, anti-Ribo P0, and anti-DNAse1L3). Higher levels of IGHV4-34+ Ig in the serum have been associated with lupus nephritis (LN). Therefore, we assessed the presence of IGHV4-34+ Ig via immunohistochemistry in 11 kidney biopsies from adult and pediatric LN patients. Remarkably, 10/11 LN biopsies and 0/2 glomerulonephritis controls had IGHV4-34+ antibodies in the affected glomeruli. These results indicated that IGHV4-34 autoantibodies may indeed be a pathogenic antibody specific to SLE and not to other immune complex-driven diseases. CART4-34 was also observed to specifically target IGVH4-34+ SLE B cells and plasmablasts and reduce IGHV4-34+ IgG production while simultaneously preserving total IgG. Taken together, these results further support the hypothesis that IGHV4-34+ Ig plays a role in SLE pathogenesis, as well as that CART4-34 could be used to generate a potent and targeted therapy for SLE.

Lastly, we sought to assess the specificity and safety of CART4-34 against normal IGHV4-34-negative B cells. In an assay against healthy donor B cells, all CART4-34 constructs specifically eliminated IGHV4-34+ B cells and reduced IGHV4-34+ IgG while preserving the overall B cell viability and IgG repertoire. Furthermore, next-generation sequencing on the isolated genomic DNA demonstrated that CART4-34 led to a significant reduction of only the IGHV4-34 gene out of 46 IGHV genes. We, therefore, concluded that CART4-34 is highly specific to only IGHV4-34+ B cells.

There are three primary challenges that require further investigation following this study. One such challenge is whether the ability of CART4-34 to specifically target IGHV4-34+ pathogenic B cells merits its selection for the treatment of IGHV4-34+ B cell malignancies over the well-documented and efficacious CART19. A second challenge is whether IGHV4-34+ B cells and antibodies drive severe SLE, and whether their depletion is sufficient for the treatment of SLE, both of which can be determined through in vivo studies. Finally, as demonstrated in the study, circulating IGHV4-34+ IgG may competitively inhibit the efficacy of CART4-34. As such, the third challenge is whether strategies can be developed to moderate this inhibitory effect, potentially through the modification of the CAR4-34 construct or through the depletion of IGHV4-34+ Ig. This challenge is particularly relevant with regard to autoimmune diseases where the autoantibodies play a major role in pathogenesis.

We developed a CAR T cell approach that specifically and efficaciously depletes neoplastic and autoimmune B cells while sparing the healthy B cell and immunoglobulin repertoire by targeting IGHV4-34+ B cells. This approach could be applied not only to IGHV4-34+ B cell malignancies and SLE but also potentially to other IGHV4-34-driven autoimmune diseases.
