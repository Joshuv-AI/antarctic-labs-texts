Association of plant-based diet indices with all-cause and cause-specific mortality among Korean adults in the KNHANES
Authors: Heejin Lee, Jung Eun Lee, Minji Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA plant-based diet is associated with all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, but the associations might differ by plant qualities. We examined the associations between the plant-based diet index (PDI) and mortality. Adults with 24-hour dietary recall from the 2007–2015 Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (KNHANES), who were eligible for and linked to national mortality records through 2019, were included (n = 44,291). Three PDIs were calculated: overall PDI (all plant foods), healthful PDI (hPDI, healthful plant foods), and unhealthful PDI (uPDI, unhealthful plant foods), with higher scores for respective plant foods and animal foods reverse-scored. A weighted Cox proportional hazards model estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mortality. During follow-up (median 8.3-year), 2,302 deaths were identified. For all-cause mortaltiy, HRs (95% CIs) were 0.77 (0.64–0.93) for overall PDI, 0.82 (0.68–0.99) for hPDI, and 1.17 (0.98–1.39) for uPDI in the fifth versus first quintile (P-trend: <0.001, 0.035, 0.031). For CVD mortality, corresponding HRs were 0.60 (0.40–0.90), 0.63 (0.42–0.94), and 1.90 (1.32–2.75). Introduction

Adopting a plant-based diet is expected to transform the current food systems, enhancing both human health and environmental sustainability1, and has been associated with a lower risk of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, total cancer, and certain types of cancer2–5. However, previous studies might overlook the quality of plant foods; less healthful plant foods, such as refined grains and sugar-sweetened beverages, are known to increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and cancer6–10. To address this issue, a plant-based diet index (PDI) has been proposed to assess plant foods quality while accounting for animal food consumption: the overall PDI, healthful PDI (hPDI), and unhealthful PDI (uPDI)11. Healthful plant foods typically include whole grains, fruits, and vegetables. By incorporating animal food consumption, the PDI provides a more accurate reflection of real-world mixed-diet eating habits, thereby enhancing the evaluation of the impact of plant-based diet11.

Previous studies have shown that higher adherence to overall PDI and hPDI is associated with lower risks of incident CVD as well as all-cause and CVD mortality12–17, whereas uPDI has been associated with higher risks of all-cause and CVD mortality12,13, highlighting the importance of the types of plant foods consumed. While overall PDI has been found to be inversely associated with cancer mortality, findings for hPDI and uPDI regarding cancer mortality have been mixed12,13,15,18.

However, there are limited data to draw generalizable insights on plant-based diets and mortality in Asian populations. Among middle-aged Koreans and Chinese, overall PDI or hPDI was found to be associated with lower all-cause mortality, CVD, and/or cancer mortality, although some studies have reported null findings, resulting in mixed evidence overall19–21. Due to differing food consumption patterns in Asia—namely, higher plant food consumption—compared to Western countries22–24, accumulating epidemiological evidence in Asia is crucial. Thus, we aimed to investigate the associations between PDI and all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality risk among adults aged 19 years or older, using national representative data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) linked to national mortality data.

Methods

Study population

The KNHANES is an annual, nationally representative survey conducted by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) that uses a stratified multistage clustered probability sampling design25. The survey collects information on socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, laboratory results, clinical information, anthropometric measurements, dietary intakes, and diet-related behaviors. Utilizing data from the 2007–2015 KNHANES, we conducted prospective analyses on participants aged 19 or older. Participants were excluded if they did not consent to linkage with mortality data (n = 21,778), did not have information on dietary recall data (n = 6,122), died within the initial three years of follow-up (n = 811), or had implausible energy intake, defined as a deviation of more than three standard deviations (SD) from the mean energy intake after logarithmic transformation (n = 351). Analytic sample includes 44,291 adults (men: n = 17,822, women: n = 26,469) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The KDCA approved the KNHANES protocol26–28, and all participants provided written informed consent. This study was waived by the Institutional Review Boards of Seoul National University (IRB No.E2303/002–002). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Mortality linkage

Mortality data from the Cause of Death Statistics, provided by the Microdata Integrated Service (MIDS) of Statistics Korea, were linked to participants aged 19 years or older who consented to the linkage of Cause of Death Statistics29. Participants were enrolled from 2007 to 2015 and followed up until December 2019. We calculated the person-years of follow-up from the date of entry to the date of death, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. We identified all-cause death, death from CVD (I00–I99), and death from cancer (C00–D48) according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10).

Dietary assessment and calculation of plant-based diet indices

Dietary data was collected using the one-day 24-hour dietary recall (24HDR) method. Participants were interviewed face-to-face using the Pen-and-Paper Interviewing (PAPI) method up until 2013, and the Computer-Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) system was adopted in 201430,31. A trained dietitian conducted a 24HDR at the participants’ house, a week after the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) visit32. We categorized food items into 19 food groups based on their nutrient and culinary similarities, which were further aggregated into three categories: healthy plant foods, less healthy plant foods, and animal foods11. Healthy plant foods include whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, vegetable oils, and tea and coffee. Less healthy plant foods consist of fruit juices, refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets and desserts, and salty food group. We included salty plant food groups to reflect the high consumption of pickled and salted vegetables in Asian populations. In Korea, for example, the average intake of kimchi, a fermented and salted vegetable dish commonly consumed across the country, is 122 g/day22. Animal foods include animal fats, dairy products, eggs, fish and seafood, meats, and miscellaneous animal-based foods (Supplementary Table 1). Using the residual method, we estimated energy-adjusted food intake for each of the 19 food groups in the total analytic sample and ranked them into quintiles. Based on this quintile classification, we then calculate the overall PDI, hPDI, and uPDI. For the overall PDI, in each plant food category, participants who consumed higher amounts of plant food were assigned higher scores, ranging from 1 for those in the lowest quintile of consumption to 5 for those in the highest quintile. Conversely, participants with higher animal food consumption received lower scores, ranging from 5 for those in the lowest quintile of consumption to 1 for those in the highest quintile. For the hPDI, only the healthy plant food groups received a score 5 for the highest quintile of consumption, while less healthy plant food groups and animal food groups were scored inversely. For the uPDI, less healthy plant food groups were scored positively, while healthy plant food groups and animal food groups were scored inversely. We then summed the scores across all food groups to calculate the total scores for the overall PDI, hPDI, and uPDI, respectively, and categorized these scores into quintiles in a sex-specific manner. The detailed scoring scheme is presented in Supplementary Table 2. In addition, we calculated the top ten foods contributing to total energy intake, as well as the total energy intake for each PDI quintile. We determined the percentage contribution of each food item by dividing its energy intake by the relevant total energy intake.

Assessment of health-related factors

A trained interviewer collected information on demographics (age, sex), socioeconomic status (education level, household income, and marital status), lifestyle behaviors (smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and vigorous physical activity), and self-reported medical history. Smoking history included current and past smoking status, frequency, and duration of smoking, with pack-years of smoking calculated as (daily number of cigarettes smoked/20) Χ years smoked. Information on alcohol consumption, including drinking status, frequency, and quantity, was collected. Participants who self-reported having had a physician-diagnosed condition or medication use for diabetes, stroke, myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, heart attack, or cancer were considered to have a disease at baseline. Trained medical staff took a single measurement for both height (in cm) and weight (in kg) using the stadiometer and weight scale, reading to one decimal point33. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight (in kg) by the square of the height (in m2).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses of participants presented weighted means and standard errors (SE) for continuous variables, and numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Pearson test assessed the correlations between each PDI score. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, according to the PDI score, were estimated using weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models to account for the complex multistage probability sampling design of the surveys. We confirmed the assumption of proportional hazards was not violated, using Schoenfeld residual methods. The Multivariablemultivariable models were stratified by age at baseline (< 40, 40–< 60, ≥ 60 years) and adjusted for age (continuous, years), sex (men, women), and energy intake (continuous, kcal). In Multivariablemultivariable model 2, we additionally adjusted for BMI (< 18.5, 18.5–< 23, 23–< 25, 25–< 30, ≥ 30 kg/m2), smoking (never smoker, < 10, 10–< 20, ≥ 20 pack-years), alcohol drinking (non-current drinker, current drinker: < 3, ≥ 3 cups/week), and vigorous physical activity (no, < 3, ≥ 3 times/week). Multivariable model 3 was further adjusted for educational level (under elementary school, middle school, high school, college or above), household income (low, medium-low, medium-high, high), marital status (single; married or living with the partner; divorced, separated, or widowed), and history of CVD, cancer, and diabetes at baseline (yes, no). We also analyzed the men and women separately, adjusting menopausal status only in women. For missing covariate data, we assignted median values to continuous variables and the most frequent categories to categorical variables. To test for a linear trend, we included the median PDI scores within each quintile as a continuous variable in the model. Restricted cubic splines with three knots were used to test for possible non-linear associations between PDI scores and mortality. The knots were placed at PDI scores of 47, 57, and 66 for the overall PDI; 47, 57, and 68 for the hPDI; and 45, 57, and 70 for the uPDI. We stratified participants by baseline age and BMI to test for effect modification. The likelihood ratio test estimated the significance of interaction by comparing models with and without the interaction terms. We did not take into account the sampling weights in the subgroup analyses due to statistical complications when implementing the likelihood ratio test. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with a history of disease at baseline were performed to examine the robustness of our findings. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All statistical analyses were two-sided and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Participants with higher adherence to the overall PDI were more likely to be older, married or living with a partner, less educated, never smokers, non-current drinkers, have a history of disease, be postmenopausal women, and have a lower energy intake than those in the first quintile (Table 1, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). BMI and the weekly frequency of vigorous activity were similar across the overall PDI quintiles. Similar baseline characteristics were observed across quintiles of hPDI, except for household income, vigorous physical activity, and energy intake. Meanwhile, participants with higher scores on the uPDI were more likely to be older, less educated, have lower household incomes, be heavy smokers, be postmenopausal women, and consume more energy across the quintiles. They were also less likely to have a history of disease than those in the first quintile. The correlations between each PDI were all low: 0.28 for overall PDI and hPDI, −0.21 for hPDI and uPDI, and − 0.07 for overall PDI and uPDI (P < 0.001 for all).

Contribution analyses

The top ten foods contributing to energy intake across the quintiles of PDI type were identified. White rice was the top contributor, with its contribution increasing with higher adherence to overall PDI and uPDI, but decreasing with higher adherence to hPDI: overall PDI (Q1: 27.06%, Q5: 37.77%), hPDI (Q1: 37.00%, Q5: 29.47%), uPDI (Q1: 27.19%, Q5: 40.44%) (Supplementary Tables 5–7). For overall PDI and hPDI, fruits like apples and legumes like soybeans were top contributors in the higher adherence groups, while in the higher uPDI groups, refined grains and its product were the major contributors to energy intake. Of note, alcoholic beverages were among the top contributors in the higher uPDI adherence groups compared to the lower adherence group, particularly among men, and their proportion of energy contribution was higher as well.

Plant-based diet indices and mortality

During the median follow-up of 8.3 years, we identified 2,302 deaths. Among those, 22.5% died from CVD and 31.7% died from cancer. The associations between each PDI score and all-cause mortality are shown in Table 2. Overall PDI was associated with lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality. Compared to the lowest quintile (Q1) of overall PDI score, the weighted HRs (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality were 0.77 (0.64–0.93) for the highest quintile (Q5) in the fully adjusted model (P for trend < 0.001). Each 5-point increment in overall PDI score was associated with an 8% (95% CI: 0.88–0.96) reduction in the risk of all-cause mortality. HRs (95% CIs) of CVD mortality was 0.60 (0.40–0.90) for the same comparison (P for trend = 0.001; HR and 95% CI for 5-point increment 0.83, 0.76–0.92). Similarly, hPDI was inversely associated with all-cause and CVD mortality. For hPDI, HRs (95% CIs) for Q5 vs. Q1 was 0.82 (0.68–0.99) for all-cause mortality (P for trend = 0.035), and 0.63 (0.42–0.94) for CVD mortality (P for trend = 0.020). A 5-point increase in hPDI was associated with a 4% (95% CI: 0.91–1.00) marginally lower risk of all-cause mortality and a 13% (0.79–0.96) lower risk of CVD mortality. In contrast, uPDI increased the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality. HRs (95% CIs) for Q5 vs. Q1, were 1.17 (0.98–1.39) for all-cause mortality (P for trend = 0.031) and 1.90 (1.32–2.75) for CVD mortality (P for trend < 0.001). Per a 5-point increase in uPDI, the associated risk was 1.05-fold higher (95% CI: 1.01–1.09) for all-cause mortality and 1.16-fold higher (95% CI: 1.06–1.26) for CVD mortality. There were no significant associations between any type of PDI and cancer mortality (Supplementary Table 8). We did not find any non-linear associations between all types of PDI and all-cause mortality in the restricted cubic spline analysis (for overall PDI: P for curvature 0.27, P for linearity < 0.001; for hPDI: P for curvature 0.86, P for linearity 0.11; for uPDI: P for curvature 0.11, P for linearity 0.01) (Fig. 1).

Plant-based diet indices and sex-specific mortality

We further analyzed men and women separately. The associations between all types of PDI and both all-cause and CVD mortality remained similar in both sexes. However, the association between overall PDI and all-cause mortality was stronger among men: HR (95% CI) for the fifth vs. first quintile was 0.72 (0.55–0.94; P for trend = 0.002) (Table 3). Among women, the corresponding HR was 0.85 (0.65–1.10; P for trend = 0.061). The associations between hPDI and all-cause mortality did not reach statistical significance in men but were more pronounced in women (HR 0.72 [0.53–0.98]; P for trend = 0.085). For CVD mortality, the associations between overall PDI and uPDI became more pronounced among men (Table 4). HRs (95% CIs) for the fifth vs. first quintile were 0.52 (0.28–0.98) for overall PDI (P for trend = 0.026), and 1.99 (1.17–3.41) for uPDI (P for trend = 0.003). Among women, the corresponding HRs were 0.68 (0.40–1.15; P for trend = 0.018) for overall PDI and 1.73 (1.05–2.86; P for trend = 0.044) for uPDI. Sensitivity analyses

In the sensitivity analyses, when excluding participants with a disease history of CVD, cancer, and diabetes at baseline, the results were attenuated but similar (Supplementary Table 11). HRs (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality of the fifth quintile compared to the first quintile of overall PDI, hPDI, and uPDI were 0.82 (0.66–1.04), 0.83 (0.65–1.06), and 1.22 (0.97–1.53), respectively. HRs (95% CIs) for CVD mortality of the top versus bottom quintile of overall PDI, hPDI, and uPDI were 0.80 (0.49–1.30), 0.85 (0.50–1.45), and 2.01 (1.22–3.33), respectively.

Discussion

We found that overall PDI and hPDI were associated with a lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality among Korean adults in a nationally representative prospective cohort. Conversely, adherence to the uPDI was associated with an increased risk of both all-cause and CVD mortality by 1.05-fold and 1.16-fold per 5 points increment, with the associations for CVD mortality being more pronounced in men. However, all types of PDI were not associated with cancer mortality. Of note, the association between uPDI and all-cause and CVD mortality was more pronounced among adults with a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or higher.

Our findings were consistent with previous studies, showing that improving overall PDI and hPDI over a prolonged period was associated with a lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, while higher adherence to uPDI over a longer period was associated with a higher risk of all-cause and CVD mortality12. Meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies similarly demonstrate that adherence to overall PDI and hPDI have been associated with a reduced risk of all-cause and CVD mortality18, while adherence to the uPDI is linked to a higher risk of CVD mortality17,18. However, research on PDI in Asian populations remains limited, particularly regarding their associations with mortality. In our study, we observed a significant reduction in both all-cause and CVD mortality risk among Korean adults with higher adherence to overall PDI. Consistent with our results, one Korean prospective cohort study also reported inverse associations between overall PDI and both all-cause and CVD mortality risk among middle-aged Koreans19. Similarly, an overall PDI was associated with a 19% lower risk of all-cause mortality among Chinese centenarians20. However, another study in China, the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), reported no significant association between overall PDI and all-cause mortality21.

Our findings about overall PDI and hPDI in relation to all-cause and CVD mortality risk are consistent with other well-established diet quality indices, such as the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, the planetary health diet, and the Mediterranean diet. While these indices differ in the scoring system and specific components, they all prioritize the inclusion of plant-based food, including whole grains, vegetables, and fruits. These high-quality diets, which emphasize plant-based diets, are widely associated with lower all-cause and CVD-specific mortality risk34–37. The potential underlying mechanisms may involve compounds found in healthy plant foods, such as those in vegetables and fruits. These compounds, which include dietary fiber, polyunsaturated fat (PUFA), and monounsaturated fat (MUFA), may reduce the risks of diabetes, CVD, and mortality38–42, by preventing oxidative damage, improving insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis, promoting gut microbiota diversity, and lowering inflammation39,42–48.

A few studies have reported no association between uPDI and all-cause mortality or CVD mortality14,20. However, several studies, similar to our findings, indicate that uPDI increases all-cause mortality and CVD mortality12,13,19,21. This might be due to the high consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) and added sugars, which are largely classified as less healthful plant foods, along with the simultaneous lower consumption of fruits and vegetables. Specifically, in this study, participants with higher uPDI scores primarily obtained their energy intake from UPF such as ramyun, instant coffee (3-in-1 mixed coffee), as well as from sugars. UPFs that are high in saturated fat, additives, and added sugar49, are all known to increase obesity and weight gain, deteriorate lipid profile, worsen insulin resistance that can thus increase CVD and mortality risk50–56.

Our findings of null associations between all plant-based diet indices and total cancer mortality are in line with previous mixed results12,13,15,18. These null findings may partly reflect the heterogeneity of cancer types included in this composite outcome, with liver and lung cancers being the major causes of cancer death in Korea57. When aggregated across different cancer types with diverse etiologies and prognoses, the associations could have been attenuated.

Food preferences and the amount of food consumed can differ between men and women58, so we conducted separate analyses for men and women. The association for hPDI showed no significant difference between men and women. Conversely, the associations between overall PDI and uPDI with all-cause mortality attenuated in women but were more pronounced in men, although the overall results were similar. The underlying reasons for the stronger associations observed in Korean men remain uncertain, though variations in food choices may contribute. In this population, we observed that among men, alcoholic beverages like soju and beer were among the top contributors to uPDI, which may partly explain the stronger associations between uPDI and mortality. To ensure robustness, we excluded participants who had pre-existing diseases at baseline, and the associations remained similar, albeit did not reach significance.

The lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality observed among participants with a higher hPDI, particularly pronounced in those aged 65 and younger in this study, underscores the potential benefits of targeting dietary interventions on younger populations. These individuals may experience more pronounced benefits from adhering to healthful plant-based dietary patterns, which could contribute to maintaining and improving health, ultimately leading to better long-term health outcomes. Furthermore, although the interaction did not reach statistical significance, we observed a marginally significant interaction for the age group in the overall PDI. The association between greater adherence to the overall PDI and a lower risk of all-cause mortality among older adults aged 65 or older suggests that following a plant-based diet may also support healthy aging in older populations.

The unfavorable associations between uPDI and all-cause mortality and CVD mortality were stronger among participants with obesity. Adherecne of uPDI was reported to associated with metabolically unhealthy obesity in adolescents59. In the French prospective cohort study, BMI mediated the associations between uPDI and type 2 diabetes and hypertension60, which are known risk factors for CVD and mortality. These factors may account for the more pronounced associations with mortality observed among individuals with obesity. Considering that diet is a modifiable lifestyle factor, public health efforts should encourage individuals, especially those with higher BMI, to reduce their intake of unhealthful plant foods.

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, the large sample size drawn from a nationally representative cohort, and the availability of comprehensive dietary data along with detailed information on a wide range of potential confounding factors. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, PDI was scored based on a one-day 24-hour recall, which may not reflect participants’ usual diet and might introduce potential measurement error. Second, dietary assessment was conducted only once at recruitment, limiting the capturing of participants’ lifetime dietary changes. Further research reflecting long-term and changing-over-time dietary habits is warranted to better understand the relationships between plant-based diets and health outcomes. Third, we did not use multiple imputations for missing covariates. However, since exposure and outcome distributions appeared comparable between participants with and without missing covariates, the potential for bias in the associations is likely minimal. Fourth, despite adjusting for potential confounders, there might still be residual confounding factors that could not be ruled out. Fifth, we used a nationally representative sample of Korea; the associations observed in this study may not be generalized to populations with different ethnic or cultural backgrounds. Future research with a more diverse ethnicity is warranted to generalize the findings to other populations.

Conclusion

In a representative sample of Korean adults, we found that healthy plant-based diet are associated with lower CVD mortality and all-cause mortality. In contrast, diets with a higher proportion of unhealthy plant foods and animal foods, coupled with lower consumption of healthy plant foods, were associated with increased all-cause and CVD mortality risk. However, no associations were found for cancer mortality. Current Korean dietary guidelines emphasize consuming vegetables, fruits, and grains. Our findings reinforce these recommendations and suggest that future updates could highlight the importance of plant-food quality and the need to limit less-healthful plant foods to better distinguish the health effects of different plant-based diets and to promote cardiovascular health and overall health.
