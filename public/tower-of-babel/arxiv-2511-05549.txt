AGRAG: Advanced Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation for LLMs
Yubo Wang, Haoyang Li, Fei Teng, Lei Chen

Abstract
Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (Graph-based RAG) has demonstrated significant potential in enhancing Large Language Models (LLMs) with structured knowledge. However, existing methods face three critical challenges: Inaccurate Graph Construction, caused by LLM hallucination; Poor Reasoning Ability, caused by failing to generate explicit reasons telling LLM why certain chunks were selected; and Inadequate Answering, which only partially answers the query due to the inadequate LLM reasoning, making their performance lag behind NaiveRAG on certain tasks. To address these issues, we propose AGRAG, an advanced graph-based retrieval-augmented generation framework. When constructing the graph, AGRAG substitutes the widely used LLM entity extraction method with a statistics-based method, avoiding hallucination and error propagation. When retrieval, AGRAG formulates the graph reasoning procedure as the Minimum Cost Maximum Influence (MCMI) subgraph generation problem, where we try to include more nodes with high influence score, but with less involving edge cost, to make the generated reasoning paths more comprehensive. We prove this problem to be NP-hard, and propose a greedy algorithm to solve it. The MCMI subgraph generated can serve as explicit reasoning paths to tell LLM why certain chunks were retrieved, thereby making the LLM better focus on the query-related part contents of the chunks, reducing the impact of noise, and improving AGRAG’s reasoning ability. Furthermore, compared with the simple tree-structured reasoning paths, our MCMI subgraph can allow more complex graph structures, such as cycles, and improve the comprehensiveness of the generated reasoning paths.

•

We present the AGRAG framework, which consists of three steps: Data Preparation, Graph Retrieval, and Hybrid Retrieval, aiming at addressing the Inaccurate Graph Construction, Poor Reasoning Ability, and Inadequate Answer issues of current Graph-based RAG models.

•

We design a statistic-based entity extraction method, avoiding the hallucination caused by LLM entity extraction, and improving the pipeline effectiveness.

•

We propose the MCMI subgraph generation problem, prove it to be NP-hard, and design a greedy algorithm, ensuring AGRAG’s generalizability to complex tasks.

•

According to our experiments,
AGRAG achieve better effectiveness and efficiency compared with current state-of-the-art RAG models on six tested tasks.

II-B

Community-based Models

Early Graph-based RAG models typically retrieve specific neighborhoods from the graph based on the input query.
The pioneering work in this category is GraphRAG
[
25
]
, as illustrated in
Figure 1
, which constructs a graph and aggregates nodes into communities, followed by generating community summaries to assist the LLM in making predictions. However, GraphRAG requires re-computing the graph communities after each index update, leading to significant computational overhead.

To address this limitation, LightRAG
[
26

eliminates the explicit detection of graph communities, as well as the computation of community summaries. Nevertheless, this approach can still incur computational costs and may lead to hallucinations, as the 1-hop neighborhoods can be large and often contain noisy information.

Moreover, all these community-based models are limited in their ability to capture structural information beyond a small local neighborhood, thereby restricting their ability for long-range reasoning.

In this paper, we propose a novel graph-based and efficient RAG framework, termed AGRAG. AGRAG first constructs a KG using a statistics-based entity extraction method, and then assigns graph’s node weights based on both the KG structure and the input query. Framework Overview

As shown in

Step 1: Data Preparation.

In this step, AGRAG constructs a Knowledge Graph (KG)

based on the given text corpus
\mathcal{T}

Here, we substitute other Graph-based RAG baselines’ LLM entity extraction to a statistics-based method based on TFIDF

.
This method can avoid LLM hallucination, and hence reduce the noise in the constructed graph, leading to better effectiveness.
After entity extraction, we apply LLM to detect relation edges w.r.t. the extracted entity nodes and the text chunk.
Furthermore, we also construct text chunks into KG as passage nodes, and link them to all their extracted entity nodes with node-to-passage edges.
The detailed pseudo code of Step 1 is shown in

III-B

Step 2: Graph Retrieval.

In this step, we firstly map the query
q
to KG fact triples considering their semantic similarities, after that, we calculate the PPR score
[
33
]
based on these mapped fact triples
F
as the node influence score
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}

On the other hand, we calculate the edge costs
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
considering the semantic similarity between query and facts’ vector representations.
the higher their similarity is, the lower the cost is.
Compared with solely applying PPR to score nodes for retrieval,
the edge cost further provides constraints on the involved nodes based on the semantic correspondence between query and facts,

After that, we initialize the MCMI subgraph with the generated MCST, and apply our greedy algorithm by iteratively expanding the MCMI subgraph by selecting its neighboring node with the highest influence score-edge cost ratio (i.e.,

If the highest ratio is smaller than the average ratio of the current MCMI, the iteration stops and the current MCMI subgraph is returned.

Step 3: Hybrid Retrieval.

In this step,
we perform hybrid retrieval and obtain text chunks based on their BM25 sparse similarity score, and dense vector representation similarity score with the query.
These chunks will be input together with the MCMI subgraph to the LLM for the final query answering, providing extra contexts.

Next, we introduce the Minimum Cost Maximum Influence (MCMI) subgraph generation problem, which
can provide more comprehensive reasoning chains and result in more comprehensive answers in tasks that require knowledge summarization.
According to
Figure 3
, the MCMI subgraph can include cyclic graph structures to cover key query related triples (colored in
orange
), which are not covered by simple node matching or Steiner tree, hence address the Poor Reasoning Ability and Inadequate Answer issue of current Graph-based RAG models.
Here, we give a formal definition of the MCMI subgraph generation problem:

Definition 1

(Minimum Cost Maximum Influence Subgraph Generation Problem)
.

Given an node and edge weighted connected graph
\mathcal{KG}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}})
, a set of mapped triples
\mathcal{F}
. The aim of this problem is to generate a subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{\text{MCMI}}
with the maximum average node score and minimum total edge cost that contains all the mapped triples.

Next, we prove our MCMI Subgraph Generation problem can be reduced from the NP-hard Steiner Tree problem
[
70
]
.

Theorem 1
.

The Minimum Cost Maximum Influence Subgraph Generation problem is NP-hard.

Proof.

We define the decision version of MCMI as follows:
Given a weighted knowledge graph
\mathcal{KG}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}})
,
entity node set from mapped triples (considered as terminal nodes)

\mathcal{V}_{\text{term}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, and two thresholds
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, does there exist a connected subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{KG}
such that:

(i)

\mathcal{V}_{\text{term}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, which means all mapped triples are included,

(ii)

the average node influence score satisfies
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}^{\prime}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}s_{v}\geq\alpha
, and

(iii)

the total edge cost satisfies
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}c_{e}\leq\beta
?

Now, consider an arbitrary MCST instance with graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, terminal node set
\mathcal{V}_{\text{term}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, and total cost
\gamma
. We construct an MCMI instance as follows:

1.

Use the same graph
\mathcal{G}
as the KG
\mathcal{KG}
.

2.

Set
s_{v}=1
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, hence any subgraph has average node score exactly 1.

3.

Keep edge costs
c_{e}
unchanged for each
e\in\mathcal{E}
.

4.

Set
\alpha=1

and
\beta=\gamma
.

Because all nodes have score 1, condition (ii) holds for any non-empty subgraph. Therefore, a Steiner tree of cost
\leq\gamma
exists in the MCST instance
if and only if
a feasible subgraph exists in the MCMI instance. This establishes a polynomial-time reduction, proving that MCMI is NP-hard.
∎

Since the MCMI Subgraph Generation problem is the NP-hard, it is infeasible to obtain the optimal result in polynomial time.
Therefore, we propose a greedy algorithm (
algorithm 3
) based on the Mehlhorn’s algorithm
[
71
]
to solve this problem.

To begin with, we initialize the MCMI as the Minimum Cost Steiner Tree (MCST) through all mapped triplet facts
\mathcal{F}
in the weighted KG
\mathcal{KG}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}})
(line 1 to line 10 of
algorithm 3
), where score
s_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
of each node
v
, and cost
c_{e}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
of each edge
e
are obtained with
Equation 12
and
Equation 13
, respectively:

\mathcal{G}_{\text{MCMI}}(\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}})=\text{MCST}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{KG}),

(14)

where
\text{MCST}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{KG})
denotes the Minimum Cost Steiner tree generation algorithm.

Next, we calculate the ratio of the nodes’ influence score and their connected edges’ cost (i.e.,
s/c
), and check this ratio of all nodes in the current MCMI’s 1-hop neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{MCMI}})
, if this ratio is greater than that the current MCMI subgraph’s average score-cost ratio, we add this node (entity) and the corresponding edge (relation) to the MCMI, recalculate the MCMI’s score-cost ratio and continue the iteration, until all neighbor node’s score-ratio is less or equal than the MCMI subgraph’s average score-cost ratio. That is, for each edge
e_{(u,v)}
, we have (line 10 to line 16 of
algorithm 3
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}}\cup v,&\text{if }\frac{s_{v}}{c_{e_{(\text{MCMI},v)}}}>r_{\text{MCMI}},\\
\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}\cup e_{(\text{MCMI},v)},&\text{if }\frac{s_{v}}{c_{e_{(\text{MCMI},v)}}}>r_{\text{MCMI}},\\
\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
r_{\text{MCMI}}
denotes the average score-cost ratio of the current MCMI subgraph;
\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}
denotes the node and edge set of current MCMI subgraph;

e_{(\text{MCMI},v)}
denotes the outgoing edges from the MCMI subgraph to its 1-hop neighborhood; And
r_{\text{MCMI}}
is calculated as follow:

r_{\text{MCMI}}=\sum_{e_{(u,v)}\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}}\frac{s_{u}+s_{v}}{2\times c_{e_{(u,v)}}\times|\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}|}.

(16)

After generate MCMI subgraph generation, we serialize the generated MCMI subgraph into a text graph string
g
:

g=\text{StrConcat}(\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{MCMI}}).

(17)

III-E

Complexity Analysis

III-E
1
Time Complexity

For the entity extraction procedure, the time complexity would be
O(|\mathcal{V}||\mathcal{T}_{c}|)
;
Adding passage nodes also requires
O(|\mathcal{V}||\mathcal{T}_{c}|)
time.
For graph weighting, calculate the PPR score requires
O(|\mathcal{T}_{c}|(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|))
time, and calculate the edge cost requires
O(|\mathcal{E}|)
time.
For AGRAG’s Step 3,
the time complexity of AGRAG’s MCST generation is
O(|\mathcal{E}|+|\mathcal{V}|log|\mathcal{V}|)
using the Mehlhorn algorithm
[
71
]
;
The expansion from MCST to MCMI would cost

O(|\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}}|\cdot|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}})|)
Hence, the time complexity of AGRAG’s MCMI generation at the worst case when
\mathcal{V}_{\text{MCMI}}=\mathcal{V}
and the graph is fully connected would be:

O(|\mathcal{V}|^{2}+|\mathcal{E}|).

Hence, the total time complexity of AGRAG is:

O(|\mathcal{T}_{c}|(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|)+|\mathcal{V}|^{2})
.
Since the number of text chunk
|\mathcal{T}_{c}|
is less or equal to the number of nodes
|\mathcal{V}|
in graph, in the worst case, the total time complexity of AGRAG is:

O(|\mathcal{V}|\cdot|\mathcal{E}|+|\mathcal{V}|^{2})
.

III-E
2
Space Complexity

The graph of AGRAG’s Step 1 and Step 2 is stored with the dense weighted adjency matrix, which needs
O(|\mathcal{E}|)
space; Storing the text chunk would need
O(|\mathcal{T}_{c}|)
space;
Storing the entity nodes require
O(|\mathcal{V}|)
space. Hence the total space complexity of AGRAG’s Graph Indexing step would be:

O(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|+|\mathcal{T}_{c}|)
.
In AGRAG’s Step 3, we only need
O(|\mathcal{E}|)
to store the graph.

Hence, the total space complexity of AGRAG is:

O(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|+|\mathcal{T}_{c}|)
.

Evaluation Metrics

In this paper, we apply 5 metrics to evaluate AGRAG’s performance on 5 different tasks:

a.

Accuracy (ACC.)
: For text classification, the proportion of exact matches between LLM and gold labels. For other tasks, sample is scored 0, 0.5, or 1 by the LLM for inaccurate, partially accurate, or exactly accurate responses.

b.

Recall (REC.)
: Quantifies the model’s ability to identify all actual positive instances by calculating the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false negatives, we calculate the macro recall score and apply it to the text classification task, where we have a fixed label set.

c.

ROUGE-L (ROG.)

[
76
]
: Evaluates the answer quality of by comparing the longest common subsequence (LCS) between the generated answer
G
and the gold answer
Y
.

\text{ROUGE-L}=\frac{\text{LCS}(G,Y)}{|G|},

(21)

we apply ROUGE-L to the fact retrieval and the complex reasoning tasks, as the query answer of these two tasks are usually a fixed entity or relation.

d.

Coverage (COV.)

[
34
]
: Evaluates answer completeness by assessing whether all necessary reference evidences
E
are included in the LLM’s response
R
, computed as the proportion of covered evidences to the number of required evidences
|E|
:

\text{Cov}=\frac{|\{e\in E\ |\ M(e,R)\}|}{|E|},

(22)

where
M(e,R)
is a boolean function indicating whether evidence
e
is covered by generated response
R
. We apply the coverage metric to contextual summarization and creative generation tasks, as the their answers are usually passages that conclude multiple information points.

e.

Faithfulness (FS.)

[
34
]
: Measures the percentage of answer claims
A
directly supported by retrieved context
C
, calculated as the ratio of claims aligned with evidence to the number of total claims
A

\text{FS}=\frac{|\{a\in A\ |\ S(a,C)\}|}{|A|},

(23)

where
S(a,C)
is a boolean function indicating whether a claim is supported by contexts in
C
. We apply the faithfulness metric to the creative generation task to evaluate the quality of the hypothesis in the LLM’s response.

Traditional RAG Models

These models directly retrieve text chunks based on the semantic similarity between chunks and the query.

•

NaiveRAG

[
15
]
: NaiveRAG acts as a foundational baseline of current RAG models.
It encodes text chunks into a vector database and retrieve the Top-k text chunks based on the vector similarities between queries and text chunks.

•

L.LLMLingua

[
69
]
:
LongLLMLingua utilizes LLM’s generation perplexity to filter out unimportant LLM input tokens.
In this paper, we apply it to compress the retrieved text chunks of NaiveRAG w.r.t. the query and task instruction.

Community-based Models

These models retrieve one or multi-hop neighborhood of certain graph nodes that correspond to the query.

•

GraphRAG

[
25
]
: It employs LLM for graph construction. When retrieval, it aggregates nodes into communities w.r.t. the query, and generates community reports to encapsulate global information from texts.

•

LightRAG

[
26
]
:
It calculates the vector similarity between query extracted entities and graph nodes, achieving a one-to-one mapping from entities to graph nodes, then retrieve these nodes’ graph neighborhood.

Walking-based Models

These models apply walking-based graph algorithms to score graph nodes, then retrieve text chunks w.r.t. their contained entity nodes’ score.

•

F.GraphRAG

[
32
]
:
Fast-Graphrag employs LLM for graph construction.
During retrieval, it applies PPR to assign node scores based on the query,
and retrieve the Top-k passages based on the sum of their contained nodes’ scores.

•

HippoRAG2

[
31
]
:
It employs LLM for graph construction, and also add the text chunks as nodes in the graph.
As a result, it directly retrieves Top-k text chunks based on their respective nodes’ PPR score.

V
Conclusion

This paper presents AGRAG, an Advanced Graph-based Retrieval Augmented Generation framework addressing the Inaccurate Graph Construction, Poor Reasoning Ability, and Inadequate Answer issues of current RAG frameworks. AGRAG introduces a statistics-based entity extraction method to replace the LLM entity extraction method applied by current baselines, avoiding hallucination, improving effectiveness, and having better efficiency. By formulating retrieval as a Minimum Cost Maximum Influence (MCMI) subgraph generation problem, AGRAG captures further query-relevant semantic dependencies by allowing more complex graph structure,
and the reasoning paths can be more comprehensive and include more query-related graph nodes. By providing explicit reasoning paths to help LLM inference, AGRAG makes the LLM better focus on query-related contexts within the retrieved context. These can lead to better performance on complex tasks that require content summarization.
Experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate the effectveness and efficiency of AGRAG over state-of-the-art RAG models.
