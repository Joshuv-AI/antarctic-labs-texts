Spin-quenching cobalt center for efficient ammonia synthesis derived from oxide mixology
Authors: Shuairen Qian, Yuhan Wang, Tianying Dai, Xiaohang Sun, Kaiqi Nie, Maoxian Wang, Tie Wang, Jinyu Yang, Yu Ji, Kai Feng, Dong Su, Binhang Yan, Yi Cheng, Ning Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spin polarization plays a critical role in heterogeneous catalysis by influencing the properties of active sites and thereby altering catalytic performance. However, precise construction of active sites with well-tuned spin states remains challenging. Here we demonstrate to manipulate the spin polarization of ferromagnetic cobalt (Co) centers in nitride catalysts by regulating the oxide mixology of precursors. The oxide mixology modulated the interaction between CoOx and MoO3 from local interfacial bonding to remote contacting. Strong and weak interactions induce the generations of Co3Mo3N and Co/γ-Mo2N phases with low-spin and high-spin polarized Co centers, respectively, and Co centers with weakened spin polarization can facilitate the ammonia synthesis catalytic performance. Through the theoretical verification, we demonstrate that the spin-quenching configuration mitigates exchange splitting of Co centers, enabling efficient d-π* back-donation and enhancing N2 activation. These findings open new perspectives for understanding the spin effect in heterogeneous catalysis and constructing well-defined spintronic catalysts.

Introduction

The nature of active sites plays a pivotal role in determining the activity, selectivity, and stability of heterogeneous catalysts through the intimate interactions between the active sites and reactant species1,2. The active sites are responsible for activating the substrates and stabilizing the transition state (TS) configurations, thus facilitating reactant conversion in disbalance3. From the microscopic perspective, the properties of active sites are determined by the electronic characteristics of the catalytic materials, i.e., orbital occupation and electron spin arrangement4–9. For ferromagnetic materials and spin-unconserved reactions, spin polarization of the active sites can drastically change the catalytic reaction behaviors10–12. Recently, ammonia synthesis catalyzed by iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), and nickel (Ni)-based catalysts has been demonstrated to be a spin-dependent reaction by Chorkendorff et al.7,13, and a more efficient N2 activation was achieved when the magnetic moments of surface atoms are quenched. Additionally, a partially demagnetized Co catalyst was considered as high-effective ammonia synthesis catalyst by theoretical calculations9,14. These reports highlight the significant role of spin effect in regulating the catalytic performance of the ammonia synthesis process.

Typically, many top-down strategies have been proposed to modulate the spin polarization of magnetic metals (Fig. 1a). The introduction of promoters (alkali or alkaline earth metals) can induce a localized charge redistribution, which quenches the magnetic moments of surface-bound ensembles7,8,13,15. Curie phase transition, the collapse of magnetic ordering through thermal fluctuations, is another effective strategy to regulate the spin polarization of ferromagnetic materials9,16. Despite considerable efforts, these spin engineering strategies are still not widely applied. Adsorbents or promoters can only influence the adjacent atoms on the surface; thus, the demagnetization degree and region cannot be precisely controlled17. Curie phase transition enables a complete flip of spin polarization; however, the sample must be heated above the Curie temperature, which obviously does not achieve an adequate adaptation to the optimal reaction conditions, especially for Fe and Co metals with high Curie temperatures. Therefore, more precise strategies are required to achieve the manipulation of spin polarization to further enhance the catalytic activity.Fig. 1Schematic representation of the strategies for regulating the spin polarization of magnetic metals.a Post-modification strategy by promotion induction or thermal-induced Curie phase transition. b The proposed strategy by regulating the oxide mixology of precursors.

Herein, we demonstrate that Co centers with well-tuned spin states in nitride-based catalysts can be constructed by regulating oxide mixology of precursors (Fig. 1b). Molybdenum (Mo) is chosen as the spin-quenching additive, which can adjust the magnetic properties of Co centers by valence orbital hybrid interactions. The traditional volcano curve has identified the Co–Mo system as a promising candidate for ammonia synthesis. The optimal performance of the Co–Mo system is attributed to the linear combination of N binding energies associated with Co and Mo elements, while the potential spin effect of Co centers is not emphasized18. Oxide mixology modulates the oxide-oxide interactions between CoOx and MoO3 from local interfacial bonding to remote contacting19–22. Strong interactions induce the confinement of Co active sites to generate the Co3Mo3N phase during nitridation, weakening the spin polarization. In contrast, weak interactions induce the construction of an unconfined Co/γ-Mo2N heterogeneous structure, maintaining the high spin polarization characteristics. Compared with high-spin polarized Co centers, low-spin polarized Co active sites enhance ammonia synthesis activity, and the spin-quenching configuration promotes N2 activation and subsequent reactions. Based on the relationship between oxide mixology and phase evolution, we prepare the Co–Mo biemetallic nitride catalyst by the ball milling method, which delivers a further enhanced activity of 14.1 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 400 °C and 0.9 MPa. This work provides an effective approach to construct Co centers with well-tuned spin polarization and reveals the spin-related interplay of magnetic metal active sites and adsorbed species, which is pivotal for the rational design of spintronic catalysts.

Results

Oxide mixology regulation and structure characterizations

As a proof of concept, the Mo species was chosen to modulate the spin polarization of Co centers. The Co–Mo system is a classical bimetallic system as the candidate for efficient ammonia synthesis, and the Co3Mo3N phase has also been reported experimentally18,23,24. However, the potential role of Co spin polarization in the Co–Mo system has not been emphasized (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 1)25. To this end, a series of oxide precursors with different oxide-oxide interactions were prepared by modulating the proximity between Co and Mo oxides (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 2–7). The strong interaction of Co–Mo ensemble enables the formation of monophase bimetallic oxides19,21, and the lattice confinement (denoted as CoMoO4) is closer in affinity compared to traditional chemical deposition (denoted as CoO/MoO3-WI). In addition, both Co3O4 and CoO phases were considered for physical mixing with MoO3 powder (denoted as Co3O4/MoO3-PM and CoO/MoO3-PM, respectively) due to their chemical valences with different reduction potentials26. The Co/Mo molar ratio of all samples was maintained constant to avoid the loading effect (Supplementary Table 1). The differences in distance proximity have been investigated in a previous report21. Different nitride structures were generated after nitridation through an identical protocol, ensuring that the evolution of spin polarizations of Co centers is governed by the oxide-oxide interactions without introducing additional variables. Typically, oxide precursors with strong (lattice confinement and chemical deposition) and weak (physical mixing) interactions were transformed into Co3Mo3N pure phase and Co/γ-Mo2N heterogeneous structure, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8). Intuitively, Co centers are located on the confined Mo–N framework and the open Mo–N surface, respectively. Thus, the detailed coordination environment of Co centers was investigated by extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 2). The Co K-edge EXAFS spectra of Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 samples exhibit similar trends to that of Co foil with a main peak of 2.18 Å (Co–Co scattering path), indicating that the Co centers exhibit the metallic characteristics, which is in agreement with X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. In contrast, for CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 samples, the spectra exhibit broad characteristic peaks, which may be attributed to the Co–O/N scattering path (1.40 Å) and additional Co–Mo scattering path (2.79 Å). Furthermore, quantitative EXAFS fitting analysis was performed to clarify the coordination structure (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 9). The broad peaks of CoMoO4-NH3 (and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3) sample were deconvoluted into Co–N, Co–Co, and Co–Mo scattering paths with the coordination numbers of 1.9 (2.1), 4.1 (3.9), and 4.2 (3.8), respectively. Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 samples exhibit typical bulk metallic properties, and the coordination numbers of Co–Co scattering are 12.0 and 11.9, respectively. The nitride structures and spatial distribution of Co active sites at the atomic level were further investigated by electron microscopy combined with high-resolution energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 2c–j and Supplementary Figs. 11–14). As shown in Fig. 2c, e, the high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images of CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 samples capture the atomic arrangements of the Co3Mo3N phase along the [111] direction. EDS mapping demonstrates a uniform distribution of Co and Mo elements, further evidencing the confined growth of Co centers within the Mo–N skeleton (Fig. 2d, f). In contrast, the annular bright field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ABF-STEM) images of Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 samples clearly illustrate that Co nanoparticles are embedded on the γ-Mo2N surface (Fig. 2g, i). Unconfined Co nanoparticles, with a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure (Supplementary Fig. 15), are stabilized on the open support surfaces by moderate metal-support interactions instead of reconstruction (Fig. 2h, j)28. These observations provide ample evidence that oxide-oxide interactions can determine the growth mode of crystalline phases, which potentially have a dramatic effect on the spin polarization of Co active sites.

Electronic and magnetic properties of well-established Co centers

Then we examined the differences in electronic structure and magnetic properties of these well-established Co active sites. The catalyst surfaces are passivated to form an oxide overlayer due to the sensitivity of nitrides to air (Fig. 2c–j), posing difficulties in obtaining electronic structure information from the surface-science technology (e.g., X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, XPS)29,30. Thus, the oxidation states were investigated by X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) results (Fig. 3a–c). The XANES spectra of Co K-edge for a series of samples and references were acquired. As shown in Fig. 3a, the white-line intensities of all samples are close to that of the Co foil, indicating the Co centers of all these samples exhibit metallic properties. The adsorption edges of CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts slightly shift to higher energies, which is attributed to the weak charge transfer from the Co center to the Mo–N framework31. The first derivative of XANES spectra also exhibits the fingerprints of metallic Co (7709 eV) species for these catalysts (Fig. 3b). EELS results of Co L2 & L3 edges collected from the central regions also reveal the higher average valence states for CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts based on the shifts of L3 peaks towards higher energy loss (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 16). More importantly, the ratio of Co L3 and L2 peak intensities can reflect the electron arrangement and spin state of Co centers since L3 and L2 peaks represent the electron transitions of 2p3/2 → 3d and 2p1/2 → 3d, respectively32. We observed that CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts exhibit lower L3/L2 relative intensities, implying that these Co centers may possess lower spin polarization degrees (Supplementary Fig. 17). Therefore, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectra were employed to further unravel the electron spin configuration and magnetism of Co centers (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 18). The catalysts with the same electronic structures exhibit similar ESR signals (i.e., CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts, Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalysts), implying the phase transition and charge redistribution are accompanied by the transition of electron spin configuration33. For ease of presentation, CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalysts are chosen as representations of confined and unconfined Co centers, respectively, to investigate the spin states. The ESR spectra reveal a feature with a characteristic line shape at a magnetic field of ~3510 G, and the CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst exhibits a higher signal intensity (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, the magnetic properties of Co centers were reflected by field-dependent magnetization curve (M–H) measurements (Fig. 3e). CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst exhibits strong ferromagnetic behavior with high saturation magnetism (44 emu/g). In contrast, CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalyst displays weak ferromagnetic behavior with a narrow hysteresis loop and low saturation magnetism (only 1 emu/g). Coercivity, the resistance of a ferromagnetic material to demagnetization, exhibiting a trend inversely proportional to ferromagnetic properties (Fig. 3f). The influences of other factors derived from morphological evolution during nitridation were excluded (Supplementary Note 2). The spin density diagram near the Fermi level based on DFT calculations provides an atomic-scale perspective of spin polarizations (Fig. 3g). Specifically, Co3Mo3N (111) and Co fcc (111) surfaces were used to simulate CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalysts, respectively30,34–36. The Co fcc structure possesses a stronger spin polarization feature. Conversely, the spin-related channel of the Co center in the Co3Mo3N structure is narrowed. The magnetic moments also exhibit the same trend with both the magnetic properties and spin polarization feature (Supplementary Fig. 19). These results demonstrate that the Co3Mo3N structure displays a weaker spin polarization compared to the Co/γ-Mo2N structure, and the nitride derived from the oxide precursor with strong interaction achieves the construction of low-spin Co centers. All these structures can be regarded as metallic Co centers modified by Mo–N species, thereby preserving similar structural and electronic features. The differences in magnetic properties originate from the controlled modulation of spin polarization instead of changes in chemical compositions.

Structure evolutions of precursors with different oxide-oxide interactions

Furthermore, we investigate the relationship between oxide mixology and the spin state of Co centers through the structure evolution of oxide precursors. The spin polarization degrees of Co centers depend on the crystal phases, which are derived from precursors with different oxide-oxide interactions. Specifically, close distance proximity of oxide precursors induces localized solid-phase reaction; thus, the CoMoO4 phase, instead of the CoO and MoO3 mixed phases, was observed in the CoO/MoO3-WI sample. Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed to precisely describe the dynamic behaviors between chemical deposited CoOx and MoO3 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 20). The Co8O8 cluster migrated and propagated on the MoO3 surface, and the relative distances between dispersed Co and surface Mo (O) atoms decrease rapidly, indicating the formation of Co–Osurface and Co–Osurface–Mo bonds. Radial distribution functions relative to the surface Co atom also reveal the generation of CoMoO4 phase (Supplementary Fig. 21). To measure the strength of oxide-oxide interactions quantitatively, H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) experiments were conducted (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Note 3). The onset temperatures, reflecting the reducibility of Co species, follow the order of CoMoO4 > CoO/MoO3-WI > Co3O4/MoO3-PM > CoO/MoO3-PM, consistent with the more positive O vacancy formation energies of CoMoO4 than CoO structures (Fig. 4c). To support our hypothesis, we performed quasi in situ XPS to visualize the transitions of Co species (Fig. 4d, e). Similar to the above discussion, CoO/MoO3-WI and CoO/MoO3-PM samples are chosen as representations of strong and weak oxide-oxide interactions, respectively (see Supplementary Figs. 23–26 for details). Metallic Co species were preferentially observed on CoO/MoO3-PM surface at ~400 °C, subsequently found on CoO/MoO3-WI surfaces at ~500 °C. Based on the deconvolution of Co 2p spectra, the proportions of metallic Co for CoO/MoO3-PM precursors increase more dramatically in the low-temperature regions, suggesting that the enhanced reducibility facilitates the generation of high-spin Co species (Supplementary Fig. 26). The phase transitions of oxide precursors were further proved by in situ XRD during nitridation process, which can accurately distinguish the phase structure of Co centers (Fig. 4f–i and Supplementary Fig. 27). For CoO/MoO3-WI sample, the diffraction peak intensity of CoMoO4 phase gradually decreases and disappears at 425 °C, while low-spin polarized Co3Mo3N phase (40.02°, 42.57° and 46.55°) generates at 600 °C. For the CoO/MoO3-PM sample, the diffraction peak intensity of the CoO phase disappears at the lower temperature of 410 °C, while the diffraction signal of the MoO3 phase is partially preserved. As the temperature elevates, high-spin polarized metallic fcc Co phase (44.23°) generates accompanied by the transition from MoO3 to γ-Mo2N. Then the normalized diffraction peak intensities of Co3Mo3N [511] (42.57°) and Co [111] (44.23°) were employed to compare the relative contents of low-spin and high-spin polarized Co species, and CoO/MoO3-WI and CoO/MoO3-PM samples exhibit continuously increasing signals of low-spin and high-spin polarized Co species, respectively (Fig. 4f, h). Overall, these results confirm that oxide mixology determines the reducibility of precursors, which plays a crucial role in phase transition and construction of Co centers with targeted spin polarizations.

Spin-mediated enhancement of ammonia synthesis reaction activity

The ammonia synthesis performance of these nitride catalysts was evaluated to ascertain the spin effect of Co centers on catalytic reactions. As shown in Fig. 5a, the CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst is almost inactive under the test condition (300 °C and 0.1 MPa), and while the catalytic activities of the other samples follow the order of CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 > CoMoO4-NH3 > Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3. Furthermore, the turnover frequency (TOF) of these catalysts was calculated to measure the intrinsic activity of Co active sites (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts exhibit TOF values of 0.211 and 0.205 s−1, respectively. In contrast, the TOF value for Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst is 0.059 s−1, while for the CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst is nearly 0. The similar TOF values of CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts indicate that both samples possess identical intrinsic active sites, i.e., low-spin polarized Co center, and the observed difference in reaction rates may be attributed to varying site accessibilities caused by preparation methods (Supplementary Table 3). CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst is inactive, and the catalytic activity of Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst originates from the partial Co3Mo3N phase (low-spin polarized center) due to the moderate reducibility of its precursors. The apparent activation energies of these samples increase with the spin enhancements, exhibiting a trend similar to intrinsic activity (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 30). These catalysts can achieve continuous ammonia production for 50 h without loss of activity (Supplementary Fig. 31), and the magnetic properties of Co centers were preserved after long-term reaction, demonstrating the stability of the spin center under reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 32). Furthermore, the spin polarization of Co centers was modulated based on high-spin polarized CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst to provide evidence that changes in spin polarization directly influence catalytic performance (Supplementary Note 5). Joule heating treatment was employed to induce demagnetization while minimizing Co sintering (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). An excellent linear relationship between saturation magnetisms (as the descriptor of the degree of spin polarization) and TOF values of these Co centers is obtained, demonstrating that the activity originates from the spin polarization of Co centers (Supplementary Fig. 35). DFT calculations further reveal the spin effect of Co active sites for ammonia synthesis. N–N bond dissociation is considered as the rate-determining step to measure the ammonia synthesis activity for Co–Mo nitride systems, which is confirmed by 14N2-15N2 isotope exchange experiments and our previous reports (Supplementary Fig. 36)30,37,38. Co3Mo3N (111) surface represent the Co centers derived from strong oxide-oxide interactions (CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts), and Co fcc (111) and γ-Mo2N (111) surfaces are individually constructed to represent the potential active sites for Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalysts as the Co nanoparticles supported on γ-Mo2N surface are large in size (~20 nm) (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 37–46). Co3Mo3N surface exhibits stronger N–N dissociation capacities (0.85 eV) compared with the Co fcc surface, as expected from the classical volcano curve and previous reports18,34,36. Although the γ-Mo2N (111) surface exhibits a low energy barrier for N2 dissociation (0.56 eV), the subsequent hydrogenation steps are associated with prohibitively high energy barriers (1.80 eV), leading to surface poisoning by N-containing species (Supplementary Fig. 37). Therefore, the γ-Mo2N surface is ruled out as the potential active site. In comparison, we also investigated the reaction pathways on the Co hcp (0001) surface, which shows a higher N2 dissociation energy barrier of 1.22 eV. The potential role of Co/γ-Mo2N intersurfacial site with a similar coordinate environment and different spin polarization was also considered (Supplementary Fig. 38). The interface structure exhibits an energy barrier of 1.02 eV for N2 dissociation, which is slightly higher than that of the Co3Mo3N structure (0.85 eV). To understand the substantial role of spin polarization on ammonia synthesis activity, we performed the non-spin-polarized DFT calculations, though these non-magnetic structures are not the thermodynamically stable phases for Co-based materials (Supplementary Figs. 39 and 40). The N2 dissociation energy barriers on non-spin polarized Co fcc and hcp surfaces are lower than on the corresponding spin polarized Co surfaces, while the energy barriers on non-spin and spin polarized Co3Mo3N surfaces are almost similar. We define spin promotion effect (SPE) as the difference between the energy barriers on spin and non-spin polarized surfaces (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Note 6). The SPE of Co3Mo3N structure is only 0.03 eV, which is significantly lower than that of Co fcc and hcp structures (0.71 and 0.69 eV respectively), demonstrating that spin polarization suppresses the N2 dissociation step, and the Co3Mo3N structure with a low spin polarization in its ground state exhibits greater potential for the ammonia synthesis process. The N2 dissociation energy barrier over non-spin polarized Co/γ-Mo2N interfacial site was reduced to 0.90 eV, approaching that of Co3Mo3N structure (Supplementary Fig. 40), further supporting that the spin polarization of Co centers plays a key role in governing the reaction performance.

Atomic-scale insights into the spin-quenching configuration of Co centers

Densities of states analysis were conducted to elucidate the spin effect by orbital interactions of Co centers and adsorbed N2 species from an atomic-scale perspective (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Figs. 47–49). Due to spin splitting, the Co d orbitals of both Co3Mo3N and Co fcc structures exhibit asymmetric characteristics. The energy levels of α-spin orbitals decrease due to the exchange stabilization energy, while β-spin orbitals are closer to the Fermi level39. For a high-spin polarized Co center, the energy levels between the α-spin Co d orbital and the N2 π* antibonding orbital are severely mismatched, and only β-spin orbitals can interact with the N2 β-π* orbital with the same orientation to form d-π* interaction. For a low-spin polarized Co center, weak spin polarization delivers a smaller energy gap between the α-spin Co d orbital and the N2 π* orbital, and both spin channels can form d-π* interactions while reducing the overall spin polarization of the system. Therefore, the low-spin polarized Co center possesses an enhanced efficiency in N2 activation. The spin-quenching Co configuration effectively stabilizes N2 adsorption and reduces the N2 dissociation energy barrier. The orbital interactions indicate that the N2 activation process typically involves Co centers that facilitate electron back donation from d orbitals to the N2 π* antibonding orbital, which is visualized by charge density difference (Fig. 6c)40. The electrons accumulate near adsorbed N2 species for both Co3Mo3N and Co fcc surfaces, demonstrating that the electrons are partially transferred to the adsorbed N2 antibonding orbital. The changes of spin polarization can be quantitatively measured by d band centers and magnetic moments before and after N2 adsorption (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Note 8)41,42. After N2 adsorption, the difference of spin-up and spin-down d band center (Δεd) and magnetic moment of Co3Mo3N structure are significantly reduced, whereas those of Co fcc structure are nearly unchanged due to the limited electronic perturbation. Δεd can serve as a descriptor of the splitting degree between different orbitals, i.e., the degree of spin polarization and magnetism (Fig. 6e). The origin and mechanism of spin-quenching enhancement can then be elucidated based on Stoner theory, which provides a natural explanation for the magnetic properties (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Note 9)43–45. Both Co3Mo3N and Co fcc structures exhibit Stoner criterion values greater than 1, indicating their ferromagnetic characteristics. For the Co3Mo3N structure, the Stoner criterion value is 1.32, which is significantly lower than the value of Co fcc (2.36). This result indicates that the Co3Mo3N structure exhibits weaker spin polarization. The spin polarization degree depends on the competition between the unstable splitting kinetic energy and the stable exchange energy. The radial distribution of confined Co centers at the Fermi level for the Co3Mo3N structure is narrower, resulting in inefficient electron exchange and low-spin polarized characteristics. In comparison, the high-density free electrons of unconfined Co centers near the Fermi level facilitate a higher degree of spin splitting (Supplementary Figs. 50 and 51). The bonding interaction of adsorbate and low-spin polarized Co center overcomes the electron exchange energy, and thus N2 activation is kinetically favorable by enhancing the electron donation into π* antibonding orbital through the spin quenching of Co centers (Fig. 6g).

Extension of constructing highly efficient Co centers by regulating oxide mixology

The low-spin polarized Co centers can be constructed by the nitridation of precursors with strong oxide-oxide interactions. The extensibility of the oxide mixology has also been demonstrated in other magnetic metal systems (Supplementary Figs. 52–54 and Supplementary Note 10). Besides the spin effect, the reaction performances of CoMoO4-NH3 and CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalysts with similar low-spin polarized characteristics also show variability. As discussed above, these two samples possess the same intrinsic active sites but different site accessibilities, which accounts for the difference in apparent reaction rates. Higher site accessibilities, beyond phase composition, further enhance the catalyst activity at the microscopic scale. This inspires us to search for the oxide precursor with moderate interactions that can simultaneously achieve both high site accessibilities and low-spin polarized Co centers (Fig. 7a). High-energy ball milling provides a convenient and efficient method to achieve structure engineering and nanoparticle dispersion, especially the preparation of materials with high specific surface area46,47. The ball-milling conditions and the Co sources were rigorously optimized (Supplementary Fig. 55 and Supplementary Note 11). With CoO and MoO3 as raw materials, the high-energy ball milling can provide stronger mixing strength, inducing the generation of moderate oxide-oxide interaction and high-dispersion CoMoO4 phase (denoted as CoO/MoO3-BM, Supplementary Fig. 56). The corresponding nitride catalyst (denoted as CoO/MoO3-BM-NH3) reach a higher ammonia synthesis reaction rate of 0.282 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 0.1 MPa with a similar TOF of 0.21 s−1 (Fig. 7b). The high-pressure ammonia synthesis activity of CoO/MoO3-BM-NH3 catalyst remains relatively excellent compared to the conventional Co-based catalysts (14.1 mmol gcat−1 h−1), indicating that constructing the Co active sites with both low-spin intrinsic characteristics and high-accessibility structure by modulating oxide mixology has the potential applications (Supplementary Table 4).Fig. 7Rational design and application of the strategies based on regulating precursor interactions.a The potential volcano curve between oxide precursor interactions and nitride reaction performance. b The comparison of the ammonia synthesis performance of various Co-based catalysts under 300 °C and ambient pressure with WHSV of 36,000 mL/(gcat h). Discussion

The origin of ammonia synthesis activity in the Co–Mo system is widely considered to be attributed to the electronic synergistic effect between Co and Mo by modulating the N binding strength. We demonstrate that low-spin polarized Co centers quenched by Mo–N species facilitate the efficient ammonia synthesis process. A series of spin-tunable Co–Mo nitride catalysts can be directionally prepared by regulating the oxide mixology of precursors. Local interfacial bonding induces the confined growth of low-spin polarized Co centers within the Mo–N skeleton, whereas remote contacting leads to high-spin polarized Co nanoparticle supported on γ-Mo2N surface. The linear relationship between the saturation magnetisms and Co center intrinsic activities confirms the promoting effect of low-spin polarized centers on ammonia synthesis. Theoretical studies demonstrate that the spin-quenching configuration efficiently enhances N2 activation over low-spin polarized Co centers. These findings reveal a methodology for constructing spin-tunable Co centers and a spin-dependent reaction mechanism for ammonia synthesis, inspiring the development of a novel Co–Mo biemetallic nitride catalyst derived from the precursor prepared by ball milling, which reaches an excellent ammonia synthesis activity. This work also provides insights into the potential spin effect in specific reaction processes and the rational design of corresponding spintronic catalysts.

Methods

Catalyst preparations

Preparation of CoMoO4-NH3 catalyst

CoMoO4 precursor was synthesized by the hydrothermal method. Typically, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O (2471.7 mg) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (4074.4 mg) were dissolved in 90 mL deionized water to form solution A and solution B, respectively. Solutions A and B were mixed homogeneously for 10 min and transferred into a stainless-steel reactor. After hydrothermally synthesizing at 160 °C for 6 h, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and then the solid was collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and dried at 80 °C overnight. After calcination at 500 °C for 4 h in air, the CoMoO4 precursor was obtained. Subsequently, the precursor sample was nitridated from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 2 h in pure NH3 flow. The sample was then cooled down to room temperature and passivated in 0.5% O2/Ar to yield CoMoO4-NH3 catalyst.

Preparation of CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalyst

CoO/MoO3-WI precursor was synthesized by the wet co-impregnation method. Typically, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O (529.6 mg) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (873.1 mg) were dissolved together in 50 mL deionized water to form a homogeneous solution. Then the mixture was evaporated under vigorous stirring at 60 °C and dried at 80 °C overnight. After calcination at 500 °C for 4 h in air, CoO/MoO3-WI precursor was obtained, and WI denotes wet impregnation. Subsequently, the precursor sample was nitridated from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 2 h in pure NH3 flow. The sample was then cooled down to room temperature and passivated in 0.5% O2/Ar to yield CoO/MoO3-WI-NH3 catalyst.

Preparation of Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst

Co3O4/MoO3-PM precursor was synthesized by the mechanical grinding method. Typically, 191.9 mg MoO3 and 107.0 mg Co3O4 powders were put together in a mortar and then ground for 20 min to mix thoroughly. Then, Co3O4/MoO3-PM precursor was obtained, and PM denotes physical mixing. Subsequently, the precursor sample was nitridated from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 2 h in pure NH3 flow. The sample was then cooled down to room temperature and passivated in 0.5% O2/Ar to yield Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst.

Preparation of CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalysts

CoO/MoO3-PM precursor was synthesized by the mechanical grinding method. The synthetic procedure of CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst was the same as that of Co3O4/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst except for the mass of CoO powder (99.9 mg).

Preparation of CoO/MoO3-BM-NH3 catalysts

CoO/MoO3-BM precursor was synthesized by the ball milling method. Typically, 2878.8 mg MoO3 and 1498.6 mg CoO powders were put together in a mortar and then ground for 20 min to mix thoroughly. Then the mixture was transferred into milling jars, and 20 g zirconia grinding balls (3 mm in diameter) and 5 mL deionized water were added. The powder was then milled for 6 h at 800 rpm in a planetary miller (Fritsch, PULVERISETTE 7). The sample was cooled to room temperature, and then the solid was collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and dried at 80 °C overnight. Then CoO/MoO3-BM precursor was obtained, and BM denotes ball milling. Subsequently, the precursor sample was nitridated from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 2 h in pure NH3 flow. The sample was then cooled down to room temperature and passivated in 0.5% O2/Ar to yield CoO/MoO3-PM-NH3 catalyst.

Catalytic reaction performance measurements

The catalytic performance measurement was conducted in a homemade fixed-bed reactor. The reactor inlets were equipped with a de-oxidation equipment to keep a low oxygen content in the feed gas. Typically, 100 mg of catalyst (40–60 mesh) was loaded into the quartz tube and fixed with quartz wool. Subsequently, the catalysts were activated under 600 °C for 1 h to remove the surface oxidation layers at a ramping rate of 10 °C/min under N2/H2 (N2/H2 = 15/45 mL/min) gas mixture. Then the reactor was set at the specific temperatures and pressures for reaction evaluation. For each temperature point, the catalyst beds were maintained for 1 h to ensure stable outlet gas compositions. The ammonia was detected by an online conductivity meter (Mettler Toledo SevenMulti). The ammonia produced was trapped in dilute sulfuric acid solution (1 mM), and the change of ion conductivity was correlated with ammonia production quantitatively. The principle of ammonia quantification has been described in previous work48.

Catalyst characterizations

Electron microscopy characterizations

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was performed on a JEM-2100 (JEOL) microscope operating at 200 kV. The high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and annular bright-field (ABF) images were collected on a probe aberration-corrected JEOL NEOARM-200F electron microscope operating at 200 kV. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping and EELS spectra were obtained using the JED-2300 Analysis Station Plus and the Gatan Imaging Filter Quantum Model 1077 spectrometer, respectively.

In situ X-ray diffraction characterizations

In situ XRD was carried out on a Bruker D8 Advance equipment with a high-temperature reaction cell for a range of 20–60° at a rate of 0.2°/min. The flow rate was set as 20 mL/min with a pure NH3 flow, and the reaction temperature was ramped from 30 to 700 °C. The specified temperature was kept for 30 min for the diffraction spectra measurements.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy characterizations

XPS data were collected on a Shimadzu AXIS Supra+ using monochromatic Al Kα radiation (λ = 0.8339 Å). The binding energy was calibrated to the C 1s peak of environmental carbon at 284.8 eV. Quasi in situ XPS was carried out in the high-temperature reaction chamber with a homemade gas supply system. The flow rate was set as 20 mL/min with a pure NH3 flow, and the reaction temperature was ramped from 30 to 700 °C for 30 min. Then the samples were transferred into the ultra-high vacuum chamber for analysis.

X-ray absorption characterizations and analysis

X-ray absorption spectroscopy was performed at beamline 4B9A of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility in a transmission mode under room temperature and atmospheric pressure. Data were collected from 200 eV below the Co K edge to ~800 eV above the edge. Co foil, CoO, and Co3O4 were taken as a reference sample for energy calibration (E0 = 7709 eV). The EXAFS data were analyzed via the Athena/Artemis program integrated within the IFEFFIT software packages49. Radiative length (R), Debye-Waller factor (σ2), amplitude factor (S02), and energy shift (ΔE0) were used to optimize the EXAFS fitting results. The WT analysis was performed with MATLAB code by Munoz et al.50.

Computational methods

Spin-polarized calculations with the dispersion correction of the DFT-D3 method were performed with the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 5.4.4)51,52. The exchange-correlation energy was described by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation53. The interaction of the atomic cores and valence electrons was described by the projector augmented wave method, and the valence states of all atoms were expanded in a plane-wave basis set with a cut-off energy of 450 eV54,55. The self-consistent electron loops and the structural optimizations were converged to 10−5 eV and 0.05 eV/Å, respectively. The k-point grid of (2 × 2 × 1) was chosen to sample the reciprocal space. The p (2 × 2) supercells were modeled with four atomic layers to investigate the catalytic reaction. The top two atomic layers and adsorbates were relaxed, and other layers were fixed during structural optimizations. A 15 Å vacuum region was set between the periodically slabs to avoid the interaction. The structures of the TS were determined by both the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method and dimer method56,57. The electron energies of all structures were corrected by zero-point energy.

AIMD simulations were carried out with Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4)51. The simulations were sampled by the canonical ensemble (NVT) using Nosé-Hoover thermostats with a target temperature of 773 K58,59. The time step was chosen as 1 fs. The self-consistent electron loops were converged to 10−5 eV, and dispersion correction was also considered. The DFT + U approach was employed to correct the correlation effects of localized 3d electrons, and the U values were set as 3.0 eV for Co and 6.0 eV for Mo, as in the previous literature60. The Co8O8 cluster was built on the MoO3 (110) surface to simulate the dispersion behavior of cobalt oxides on the MoO3 surface. Before the AIMD simulations, the spin-polarized static DFT calculations were performed to get the local optimum structure, and this structure was used as the initial structure for AIMD calculations.
