Automated zonal-rotor CsCl ultracentrifugation with ÄKTA-based fractionation removes empty and intermediate AAV capsids
Authors: Kosuke Horita, Ryuki Miyauchi, Hideaki Yamamoto, Yasuhiro Yamazaki, Kentaro Nagatomo, Takahide Aburatani, Shuji Takeda, Satoshi Baba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) preparations contain heterogeneous full, empty, and intermediate capsids. Although anion-exchange chromatography is scalable, it does not always resolve these populations, particularly intermediate capsids. A workflow integrated zonal-rotor cesium chloride (CsCl) density-gradient ultracentrifugation with ÄKTA-based loading, inline monitoring, unloading, and fraction collection. SW41Ti swing-bucket experiments defined the empirical 1,240–1,360 mg/cm3 AAV density range and identified a two-layer CsCl configuration for transfer to a 1.65-L Ti-15 zonal rotor. The same process was demonstrated with AAV8-X and AAV9-Y. The AAV8-X full fraction contained 95.5% full and 0.4% empty capsids; the AAV9-Y full fraction contained 91.8% full capsids, with no empty capsids detected by sedimentation velocity analytical ultracentrifugation (SV-AUC). Vector-genome-based full-fraction recoveries were 78.1% and 93.4%, respectively. On an equal-capsid basis, the AAV8-X intermediate fraction showed an SV40-targeted quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) signal equivalent to 48.2% of the full fraction. Separately, a Huh-7 assay tested the intermediate fraction at a higher capsid input than the full fraction, yielding approximately 4% of full-fraction X-gene mRNA expression. Thus, the intermediate fraction retained genome-targeted qPCR signal but showed limited expression under the tested conditions. This automated ÄKTA-coupled zonal CsCl workflow provides a scalable, high-resolution polishing operation for separating AAV capsid populations.Keywords: adeno-associated virus, zonal rotor, cesium chloride, ultracentrifugation, empty capsid, intermediate capsid

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are a core delivery platform for in vivo gene therapy, and their clinical use continues to expand as approved products and development programs increase. Regulatory expectations for AAV manufacturing and quality are also becoming more stringent, with increasing emphasis on chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMCs), process consistency, and analytical characterization of critical quality attributes (CQAs).1,2,3

Recombinant AAV preparations typically contain full capsids encapsidating the intended genome, empty capsids lacking payload DNA, and intermediate capsids containing fragmented vector genomes, host-cell-derived DNA, or residual plasmid DNA.4,5 This heterogeneity can reduce the proportion of active drug substance, confound dose estimation, and increase the total capsid burden administered to patients. In systemic high-dose settings, product-related heterogeneity may contribute to immune-mediated toxicities, including complement activation and thrombotic microangiopathy.6,7,8,9,10

Anion-exchange chromatography (AEX) is widely used as a polishing step because it is scalable and compatible with manufacturing. However, the charge distributions of empty, intermediate, and full capsids can overlap. Intermediate capsids may contain near-full-length vector genomes and consequently display surface-charge properties close to those of full capsids; resolution can also vary with serotype, genome design, capsid heterogeneity, and operating conditions.11,12 These factors can limit robust separation by AEX alone and provide a rationale for an orthogonal density-based operation.

Intermediate capsids should not be assumed to be uniformly inactive. Studies of oversized AAV genomes have shown that AAV particles frequently package heterogeneous, incomplete genome fragments rather than a single intact genome. These fragments can nevertheless generate biological activity through intracellular complementation, whereby partial fragments anneal and are subsequently repaired by host-cell mechanisms. The extent of this activity depends on the target cell type and assay design, as well as on genome architecture and fragment composition.13 Accordingly, intermediate capsids can retain measurable potency even when they do not contain an intact vector genome.

Cesium chloride (CsCl) density-gradient ultracentrifugation separates capsids according to buoyant density and can provide high resolution across capsid populations. Conventional swing-bucket workflows are limited by low throughput, extensive manual handling, and lengthy processing. Zonal rotors extend CsCl processing to substantially larger volumes, including short-duration approaches, while comparative studies have confirmed the high capsid-resolution capability of CsCl gradients.14,15,16

The present study developed a scalable CsCl-based polishing workflow using a Ti-15 zonal rotor and integrated the operation with an ÄKTA pure system for controlled loading, unloading, inline monitoring, and automated fraction collection. A small swing-bucket rotor was evaluated as a practical scale-down model, and isolated intermediate capsids were characterized by sedimentation velocity analytical ultracentrifugation (SV-AUC) and a cell-based mRNA expression assay.

Results

High-resolution separation of AAV8-X using a small swing-bucket rotor

AAV8-X and AAV9-Y contained packaged vector genomes of approximately 4,300 and 3,400 bases, respectively. Both lengths include the two inverted terminal repeats (ITRs). Initial experiments evaluated whether AAV8-X subpopulations could be resolved by CsCl density-gradient ultracentrifugation using an SW41Ti swing-bucket rotor (Figure 1). AAV8-X (1 × 1013 vg) was centrifuged at 38,000 rpm for 20 h at 20°C in CsCl solution with an initial density of 1,330 mg/cm3. Sequential fractionation produced a continuous density profile and resolved peaks assigned to empty, intermediate, and full capsid populations by UV absorbance and density measurements.

AAV8-X purification by CsCl density-gradient ultracentrifugation using an SW41Ti rotor

AAV8-X (1 × 1013 vg) was centrifuged at 38,000 rpm for 20 h at 20°C with 5.70 mL of CsCl solution and 6.30 mL of sample. After centrifugation, the gradient was fractionated using a Piston Gradient Fractionator equipped with a Triax flow cell (BioComp Instruments, Tatamagouche, NS, Canada), and sequential fractions were collected into a 96-well plate at approximately 150 μL per fraction. Fraction number is shown on the x axis; A260 and A280 are shown as absorbance in arbitrary units (AU) on the left y axis, and density is shown in mg/cm3 on the right y axis. Density was determined gravimetrically by transferring 100 μL of each fraction to a pre-tared tube, measuring the net mass using a laboratory balance, and calculating the density from the measured mass and volume. The shaded region indicates the empirical AAV density range of 1,240–1,360 mg/cm3.

AAV8-X was recovered predominantly within 1,240–1,360 mg/cm3, and this interval was therefore used as the process-design window for AAV8-X and subsequently evaluated with AAV9-Y. Because capsid buoyant density depends on packaged DNA content, additional serotypes and genome designs may require adjustment of the gradient composition or fraction-collection window.

Optimization of CsCl gradient conditions for AAV subpopulation separation

The SW41Ti rotor has a K-factor of 124, whereas the Ti-15 zonal rotor has a K-factor of 481. Rotor speed and centrifugation time were fixed for scale-down development, while CsCl layer densities and volume ratios were adjusted. For the Ti-15 process, 30,000 rpm was the maximum permitted operating speed after accounting for the density of the CsCl solution and the applicable rotor safety relationship. A 20-h run was selected both to preserve resolving power and to provide an operationally manageable overnight cycle in which loading can be completed during the working day and unloading and post-processing can be performed the following day.

K-Factor equivalence was used as a practical, although approximate, reference for the different rotor geometries. A 1,200-min Ti-15 run corresponds to approximately 309 min in the SW41Ti rotor at 38,000 rpm (Table 1). Seven CsCl layer configurations were compared using the swing-bucket scale-down model (Figure 2A). For each condition, the distribution of collected fractions below 1,240 mg/cm3, within 1,240–1,360 mg/cm3, and above 1,360 mg/cm3 was evaluated.

Transfer of the optimized density window from swing-bucket to zonal rotors

The two-layer condition was transferred from 12 mL in the SW41Ti rotor to 1.65 L in the Ti-15 zonal rotor, representing a 137.5-fold increase in gradient volume (Figure 3). The Ti-15 rotor was loaded with 990 mL of 1,200 mg/cm3 CsCl solution and 660 mL of 1,400 mg/cm3 CsCl solution. Three independent Ti-15 gradient-formation runs generated similar profiles that encompassed the empirical AAV density range of 1,240–1,360 mg/cm3. The corresponding SW41Ti condition produced a comparable density window, supporting use of the swing-bucket rotor as a practical scale-down model for gradient design and loading studies.

Automated large-volume AAV polishing using an ÄKTA-connected zonal rotor

An automated workflow was established by connecting the Ti-15 zonal rotor to an ÄKTA pure 150 system (Figure 4A). CsCl solutions and samples were delivered through a closed fluid path. After ultracentrifugation, rotor contents were displaced with high-density CsCl solution while UV absorbance, conductivity, and pH were monitored inline, enabling chromatogram-guided fraction collection.

Automated large-volume AAV polishing using an ÄKTA-connected Ti-15 zonal rotor

(A) Schematic of automated loading, inline monitoring, unloading, and fraction collection. (B and C) Chromatograms for AAV8-X and AAV9-Y, respectively. Each run used 990 mL of 1,200 mg/cm3 CsCl solution and 660 mL of 1,400 mg/cm3 CsCl solution, with total loads of 6.4 × 1013–8.7 × 1013 vg, and was centrifuged at 30,000 rpm for 1,200 min at 20°C. The x axis is labeled “unloading volume (mL)”; UV absorbance and conductivity units are indicated on the corresponding axes. For AAV8-X, empty, intermediate, and full capsid pools were collected at unloading volumes of 884–980, 980–1,172, and 1,172–1,364 mL, respectively. For AAV9-Y, the corresponding pools were collected at 764–860, 860–980, and 980–1,196 mL.

One AAV8-X and one AAV9-Y preparative run were evaluated under the same two-layer and centrifugation conditions, with loads ranging from 6.4 × 1013 to 8.7 × 1013 vg per run (Figures 4B and 4C). Discrete empty, intermediate, and full capsid regions were resolved for both serotypes. For AAV8-X, the empty, intermediate, and full pools were collected over unloading-volume ranges of 884–980, 980–1,172, and 1,172–1,364 mL, respectively. The corresponding AAV9-Y ranges were 764–860, 860–980, and 980–1,196 mL. The resulting pools were used for subsequent SV-AUC and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)-based characterization; the AAV8-X pools were also used for the cell-based potency assay. Full-fraction recovery was defined as vector genomes measured by qPCR in the pooled full fraction divided by vector genomes loaded into the zonal-rotor operation. Full-fraction recoveries were 78.1% for AAV8-X and 93.4% for AAV9-Y. A separate repeat AAV8-X run reproduced the overall separation pattern (data not shown).

Characterization of separated AAV by analytical ultracentrifugation and functional testing

SV-AUC showed that the AAV8-X and AAV9-Y starting materials contained 58.0% and 71.9% full capsids, respectively (Figures 5A–5D). The empty fractions contained 94.4% empty capsids for AAV8-X and 95.7% for AAV9-Y. The intermediate fractions contained 61.0% intermediate capsids for AAV8-X and 95.5% for AAV9-Y. The pooled full fractions contained 95.5% full capsids for AAV8-X and 91.8% for AAV9-Y. The AAV8-X full fraction retained 0.4% empty capsids, whereas empty capsids were not detected in the AAV9-Y full fraction by the applied SV-AUC method.

To compare encapsidated SV40-targeted DNA on an equal-capsid basis, qPCR signals for the AAV8-X fractions were calculated at a common amount of 5.10 × 1010 capsids (Figure 5E). On this basis, the intermediate fraction showed an SV40-targeted qPCR signal equivalent to 48.2% of that in the full fraction. The fractions were evaluated separately in Huh-7 cells using triplicate wells (technical replicates) from one preparative process run (Figure 5F). Based on capsid titer measurements of retained fraction samples using the Gyrolab AAVX Titer Kit, the actual capsid inputs for the intermediate and full fractions were 3.44 × 1010 and 2.24 × 1010 capsids per well, respectively. Despite the approximately 1.54-fold higher intermediate-fraction capsid input, the intermediate fraction generated approximately 4% of the full-fraction X-gene mRNA expression. Because the transduction assay was not performed at matched capsid inputs, the 48.2% qPCR value and approximately 4% mRNA value should not be interpreted as a dose-matched quantitative ratio. Rather, these observations indicate that the intermediate fraction contained SV40-targeted DNA but showed limited X-gene expression under the tested conditions.

Discussion

This study establishes an automated, high-resolution AAV polishing workflow based on zonal-rotor CsCl ultracentrifugation. An ÄKTA system controls loading, unloading, inline monitoring, and fraction collection. Zonal CsCl ultracentrifugation is an established technique. The advances of the present work are the automated fluid-handling configuration, transfer of a defined density window from a practical scale-down model, and high-purity isolation and functional characterization of intermediate capsids.

The 20-h centrifugation condition complements short-duration zonal-rotor approaches such as that reported by Wada et al.14 and the AEX-zonal ultracentrifugation process described by Sheth et al.17 Shorter runs prioritize cycle time. The present workflow instead prioritizes resolution of intermediate and empty capsids. The overnight schedule also fits a one-unit-operation-per-day workflow. Material can be loaded during the working day and recovered the following day without compressing loading, centrifugation, unloading, and downstream handling into a single shift.

The Ti-15 configuration used a 1.65-L gradient and processed 6.4 × 1013–8.7 × 1013 vg per run. Use of a 1,800 mg/cm3 CsCl stock could maintain the two-layer density design while allowing approximately 1 L of affinity-purified material to be incorporated, although this maximum input volume was not experimentally evaluated. Practical loading capacity is therefore determined primarily by capsid concentration rather than liquid volume. In swing-bucket studies, excessive in-gradient concentration and aggregation were observed for some inputs (data not shown). These effects depended on the affinity-pool concentration, initial full-capsid proportion, and capsid-specific aggregation propensity. The SW41Ti scale-down model can define an acceptable loading concentration before transfer to the Ti-15 rotor.

The same two-layer gradient resolved capsid populations for AAV8-X and AAV9-Y, which carried genomes of approximately 4,300 and 3,400 bases, respectively. This cross-serotype demonstration supports further evaluation of density-based polishing, but does not establish that the same gradient will perform without optimization across other serotypes or constructs. Gradient composition and fraction-collection windows may require adjustment according to genome size, capsid properties, and process input to optimize resolution.

The AAV8-X intermediate fraction showed a capsid-normalized SV40-targeted qPCR signal equivalent to 48.2% of the full fraction. The cell-based assay was not conducted at matched capsid inputs: the intermediate fraction received approximately 1.54-fold more capsids than the full fraction but generated approximately 4% of the full-fraction X-gene mRNA expression. Because the intermediate fraction received more capsids, any simple arithmetic normalization by input capsid number would reduce rather than increase the relative value; however, no dose-normalized potency value was calculated because a linear relationship between capsid input and mRNA expression was not established. The residual signal may reflect full-capsid carryover or activity from a subset of partially packaged genomes, as reported for oversized AAV vectors that undergo intracellular complementation or repair.13

Empty and intermediate capsids affect product characterization in distinct ways. Empty capsids increase the total capsid burden but do not contribute vector-genome qPCR signal when they lack the assay target. Intermediate capsids may contain assay-targeted DNA and can therefore contribute to genome-based qPCR measurements without proportional transgene expression. Reducing both populations improves the compositional definition of AAV drug substance, although their analytical and biological implications should be considered separately.

The tested inputs had undergone affinity chromatography and AEX, so the present study establishes zonal CsCl ultracentrifugation as a polishing operation. In principle, an affinity eluate could be incorporated into the 1,200 and 1,400 mg/cm3 CsCl solutions using the same loading framework, but direct processing of affinity eluate was not evaluated here. Such implementation could simplify the downstream train; host-cell impurity clearance, loading concentration, aggregation, and serotype-dependent separation performance would need to be characterized.18

After fractionation, the pooled AAV fractions were buffer-exchanged and formulated by tangential-flow filtration. Diafiltration was used to reduce CsCl. For manufacturing implementation, residual CsCl should be quantified as a process-clearance attribute and linked to the diafiltration endpoint. This TFF step provides a direct transition from density fractionation to formulated bulk.

SV-AUC at 230 nm includes absorbance from encapsidated DNA, so DNA-containing particles can be overrepresented relative to empty capsids when no wavelength-specific correction is applied.19 Because intermediate capsids contain heterogeneous amounts and species of DNA, no single DNA-content-specific correction factor was applied. The resulting compositions provide a consistent, method-defined comparison across the resolved fractions, but the expected bias toward DNA-containing species should be considered when interpreting the values.

Taken together, the ÄKTA-connected Ti-15 workflow converts CsCl ultracentrifugation into a controlled 1.65-L polishing process with automated fractionation and high capsid resolution. It generated full-capsid pools exceeding 90% purity with vector-genome-based recoveries of 78.1%–93.4% for AAV8-X and AAV9-Y. In AAV8-X, the intermediate fraction retained substantial capsid-normalized SV40-targeted qPCR signal but showed limited mRNA expression under the tested conditions. These findings support characterization and removal of intermediate capsids as a population distinct from empty capsids and demonstrate the value of automated density-based fractionation for improving AAV capsid-population control.

Materials and methods

AAV vector samples

Recombinant AAV8-X and AAV9-Y vectors containing packaged genomes of approximately 4,300 and 3,400 bases, respectively, including both ITRs, were produced in monoclonal suspension cell lines derived from HEK293 or HeLa cells. The preparations were purified by affinity chromatography followed by AEX and were used as input materials for CsCl polishing studies. Samples were stored at −80°C before use, thawed before processing, and maintained at 2°C–8°C or on wet ice during preparative handling.

Small-scale CsCl ultracentrifugation using SW41Ti

Small-scale ultracentrifugation was performed using an SW41Ti rotor in an Optima XE-90 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). All preparative ultracentrifugation runs were performed at 20°C. Centrifugation conditions are specified in the corresponding results sections and figure legends. Gradients were fractionated using a Piston Gradient Fractionator equipped with a Triax flow cell for UV monitoring (BioComp Instruments, Tatamagouche, NS, Canada). Sequential fractions were collected into a 96-well plate at approximately 150 μL per fraction. Density was determined gravimetrically by transferring 100 μL of each fraction to a pre-tared tube, measuring the net mass on a laboratory balance, and calculating the density from the measured mass and volume.

Vector genome titer by qPCR

Vector genome titer was measured by qPCR. Samples were treated with Salt Active Nuclease (SAN; ArcticZymes Technologies, Tromsø, Norway) at 37°C for 30 min to remove non-encapsidated DNA, followed by Proteinase K digestion at 55°C for 30 min and heat inactivation at 95°C for 15 min. Processed samples were analyzed using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with a primer/probe set specific to the SV40 sequence in the AAV8-X and AAV9-Y genomes. qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio 6/7 Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, and 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Titers were quantified against an eight-point, 1:10 serial dilution of a linearized plasmid standard beginning at 2.0 × 1010 copies/mL and are reported as vg/mL. Capsid titers of the AAV8-X fractions were measured using the Gyrolab AAVX Titer Kit (Gyros Protein Technologies, Uppsala, Sweden). For the comparison shown in Figure 5E, the SV40-targeted qPCR signal for each fraction was calculated at a common capsid amount of 5.10 × 1010 capsids using the corresponding measured vector-genome-to-capsid ratio.

AAV separation using a zonal rotor under a two-layer CsCl condition

A Ti-15 zonal rotor (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was connected to an ÄKTA pure 150 chromatography system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) through a seal assembly. The rotor was operated at 2,000 rpm during loading. CsCl solutions containing AAV at 1,200 and 1,400 mg/cm3 were introduced sequentially through column valve port 1A to form a two-layer configuration. The optimized large-scale condition comprised 990 mL of 1,200 mg/cm3 solution and 660 mL of 1,400 mg/cm3 solution. A 1,500 mg/cm3 CsCl solution was introduced to remove residual air and complete rotor filling. The seal assembly was replaced with the rotor cap, and ultracentrifugation was performed at 30,000 rpm for 20 h at 20°C. The maximum operating speed was selected with consideration of CsCl solution density and the rotor safety relationship. After centrifugation, the seal assembly was reinstalled and the rotor was operated at 2,000 rpm while 1,500 mg/cm3 CsCl solution displaced the contents through port 1B. Fractions were collected with continuous monitoring of UV absorbance, conductivity, and pH. During unloading, the AAV8-X empty, intermediate, and full pools were collected at 884–980, 980–1,172, and 1,172–1,364 mL, respectively; the corresponding AAV9-Y pools were collected at 764–860, 860–980, and 980–1,196 mL. These pooled fractions were used for subsequent SV-AUC and qPCR-based characterization, and the AAV8-X pools were additionally used for the cell-based potency assay. After collection, the pooled fractions were buffer-exchanged into formulation buffer by tangential-flow filtration using diafiltration.

SV-AUC

SV-AUC was performed using an Optima AUC instrument (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Samples (390 μL) and reference buffer (400 μL) were loaded into 12-mm double-sector cells equipped with Rubicon ES2 sapphire windows and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 20°C. Absorbance data were collected at 230 nm. Data were analyzed using the continuous c(s) distribution model in SEDFIT v.16.1c over 0–200 S at a resolution of 200. The solvent density and viscosity were 1,008 mg/cm3 and 1.0 cP, respectively, and the partial specific volume was 0.7300 mL/g. The frictional ratio was optimized by dataset. For AAV8-X, the values were 1.742 for the empty fraction, 1.491 for the intermediate fraction, and 1.534 for the full fraction. For AAV9-Y, the values were 1.692 for the prefractionation sample, 1.785 for P1, 1.701 for P2, and 1.564 for P3. No DNA-content-specific absorbance correction factor was applied. Goodness-of-fit was assessed by root-mean-square deviation (RMSD); values below 0.01 were considered acceptable, and all datasets in this study had RMSD values below 0.004. Sedimentation coefficient distributions were visualized and integrated using GUSSI, a software program for displaying and processing analytical ultracentrifugation data.

Activity assay

AAV8-X fractions were evaluated in a Huh-7 cell transduction assay by measuring X-gene mRNA expression. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 4.0 × 104 cells/well in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 18–24 h, cells were transduced in triplicate in serum-free medium containing adenovirus type 5 at a multiplicity of infection of 800. Based on Gyrolab AAVX measurements of retained fraction samples, the actual capsid inputs in the transduction assay were 5.10 × 1010, 3.44 × 1010, and 2.24 × 1010 capsids per well for the empty, intermediate, and full fractions, respectively. After 18–24 h, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus 96 Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) with genomic-DNA elimination and on-column DNase digestion. Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). X-Gene and GAPDH expression were measured by TaqMan qPCR. X-Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method and expressed relative to the full-fraction response. The mRNA expression values were not normalized to capsid input. Data are presented as mean ± SD from triplicate wells (technical replicates).

Statistical analysis and replication

Figure 3A presents three independent Ti-15 gradient-formation runs. One AAV8-X and one AAV9-Y preparative run were used for the principal fraction-composition analysis, and a separate AAV8-X run reproduced the overall separation pattern (data not shown). The activity assay used triplicate wells from one AAV8-X preparative run; these were technical replicates and not independent process batches. Figure 5F data are presented descriptively as mean ± SD, and no inferential statistical testing was applied.
