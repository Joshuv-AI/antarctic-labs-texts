Pulsed Field Ablation with the Pentaspline Catheter vs Radiofrequency Ablation for Recurrent Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Wenbo Cheng, Xue Chen, Shiyi Wang, Mu Qin, Zonghan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulsed field ablation (PFA) is a non-thermal energy modality for catheter ablation. Although recent studies have demonstrated that PFA is noninferior to radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for the initial treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF), its comparative efficacy and safety in patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure remain unknown.MethodsFrom July 1, 2024 to September 30, 2024, 223 consecutive patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure were prospectively enrolled from 2 participating centres and underwent either PFA using the pentaspline catheter (PFA group, n = 77) or RFA (RFA group, n = 146), based on clinical judgment. The effectiveness endpoint was freedom from AF/atrial tachycardia (AT) recurrence at 12 months.ResultsAt 12 months, no significant difference was present in the rate of freedom from AF/AT recurrence between the PFA and RFA groups (71.4% vs 67.8%; log-rank P = 0.514; hazard ratio 0.85, 95% confidence interval 0.52-1.38). Similarly, the incidence of safety endpoints did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (2.6% vs 3.4%; P = 0.947).ConclusionsIn patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure, PFA using a pentaspline catheter demonstrated comparable effectiveness and safety to RFA, with similar rates of freedom from AF/AT recurrence and adverse events at 12 months.

Compared to antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy, catheter ablation has been shown to significantly improve the rate of freedom from atrial fibrillation (AF) and enhance quality of life.1, 2, 3 However, among patients with persistent AF, approximately 50% experience AF recurrence after the initial ablation procedure.4, 5, 6 Recurrent AF often leads to repeat procedures. Notably, repeat ablation provides only a modest, incremental improvement, of approximately 10%, in long-term sinus rhythm maintenance, compared to the initial procedure.5

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a novel non-thermal energy modality that has been adopted widely in clinical practice due to its unique tissue selectivity mechanism.7 Currently, a large number of clinical studies support the safety and efficacy of PFA for AF treatment.8, 9, 10 The Randomized Controlled Trial for Pulsed Field Ablation versus Standard of Care Ablation for Paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillation (ADVENT) demonstrated that PFA was noninferior to conventional thermal ablation in both efficacy and safety for patients with paroxysmal AF.11 The Single Shot Pulmonary Vein Isolation: Comparison of Cryoballoon vs. Pulsed Field Ablation in Patients With Symptomatic Paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillation (SINGLE SHOT CHAMPION) study demonstrated that PFA was noninferior to cryoballoon ablation with respect to the incidence of a first recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia, as assessed by continuous rhythm monitoring.12 The Multinational Survey on the Safety of the Postapproval Clinical Use of Pulsed Field Ablation in 17,000+ Patients (MANIFEST-17K study) indicated that a significant advantage of PFA is its ability to avoid most of the collateral damage associated with traditional thermal ablation.13

However, whether these findings can be generalized to more complex cases—specifically, patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure—is not known. Therefore, we conducted a prospective, multicentre study to compare the efficacy and safety of PFA using the pentaspline catheter vs radiofrequency ablation (RFA) in this patient population.

Methods

Study design

This multicentre, prospective, nonrandomized study was conducted between July 1, 2024 and September 30, 2024. A total of 223 consecutive patients who had AF recurrence following an initial ablation procedure were enrolled from 2 Chinese hospitals. Patients underwent either PFA using the pentaspline catheter (PFA group) or RFA (RFA group), based on clinical judgment. AF recurrence was defined as any episode of AF lasting > 30 seconds detected after the 3-month blanking period following the initial ablation procedure. No evidence of typical or atypical atrial flutter was documented on pre-procedural electrocardiography or Holter monitoring in any of the patients who underwent repeat procedures. Therefore, all included patients with recurrence had isolated AF recurrence rather than AF combined with atrial flutter. All ablation procedures were performed by experienced electrophysiologists, and the operators in the PFA group had completed substantial prior training with the system used. Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of the Ren Ji Hospital and the other participating hospital. Study population

Patients were eligible if they were aged 18-80 years, had symptomatic AF refractory to at least one antiarrhythmic medication, had undergone pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) alone as the first ablation procedure, and were undergoing a second ablation due to AF recurrence. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) congestive heart failure with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III or IV symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, or left ventricular ejection fraction < 35%; (ii) history of severe valve disease and/or prosthetic valve replacement; (iii) left atrial diameter > 60 mm; (iv) severe congenital heart disease; (v) left atrial thrombus confirmed by preoperative esophageal ultrasound; (vi) contraindications to anticoagulation therapy; (vii) contraindications for cardiac catheterization; (viii) any cardiac surgery within the preceding 2 months; and (ix) life expectancy < 12 months.

Ablation procedures

Repeat PVI was performed in patients with pulmonary vein (PV) reconnection. For patients with durable isolation of all PVs, direct current cardioversion was performed. The procedural details of RFA and PFA for PVI at the 2 participating centres are provided in the Supplementary Appendix S1. Briefly, radiofrequency ablation procedures were performed with the irrigated contact force–sensing ablation catheter Thermocool SmartTouchTM ST/SF (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) and the CARTO platform (Biosense Webster) using 45-50W (15-20 mL/min saline perfusion) with an ablation index of 450-550. PFA procedures were performed with the FARAPULSE catheter (Boston Scientific) and based on the 5S study. Each PV was treated with a minimum of 8 energy applications at 2.0 kV, 4 in the basket configuration and 4 in the flower configuration. PFA is guided by intracardiac echocardiography. (For more details, see Supplemental Appendix S1.)

For patients with paroxysmal AF, only PVI was performed, whereas for those with persistent AF, additional posterior wall ablation was conducted. In the PFA group, posterior wall ablation was achieved using the pentaspline catheter in its flower configuration, with the number of energy applications tailored to the anatomic size of the posterior wall and guided by an electroanatomic mapping system. Cavotricuspid isthmus ablation was performed if sustained atrial flutter developed spontaneously during the procedure; this was carried out using radiofrequency ablation in both groups. No burst pacing or active triggering maneuvers for flutters or non-PV triggers was performed. General anesthesia was administered for all PFA procedures, whereas RFA procedures were performed under local anesthesia. Comprehensive procedural details are provided in Supplemental Appendix S1.

Study endpoint

The effectiveness endpoint was freedom from documented AF/atrial tachycardia (AT) lasting more than 30 seconds (after 3-month blanking), at a minimum of 12 months of follow-up after a single ablation procedure without AADs. The safety endpoints were acute adverse events (< 7 days postprocedure) and chronic major adverse events (> 7 days postprocedure). Major adverse events included esophageal fistula, symptomatic pulmonary vein stenosis, acute heart failure, surgical drainage, stroke, coronary spasm, persistent phrenic nerve injury, and vascular complications requiring surgical intervention. Minor adverse events included pericardial effusion without intervention, pericarditis, air embolism, hemolysis, transient ischemic attack, transient phrenic nerve injury, vascular access complications, hematoma, arteriovenous fistula, pseudoaneurysm, deep vein thrombosis, and respiratory-related events. Hemolysis was defined as a haptoglobin level of ≤ 4 mg/dL.

Follow-up

The first 3 months after the procedure were designated as the blanking period, during which class I or III AADs were permitted. After the blanking period, AADs were discontinued in patients with no evidence of AF recurrence. Patients were followed for 12 months, with outpatient clinic visits scheduled at 3,6, 9, and 12 months after discharge. At each visit, all patients underwent a 12-lead surface electrocardiogram (ECG) and 24-hour Holter monitoring. Any symptoms suggestive of recurrence were promptly evaluated with an additional 12-lead ECG or 24-hour Holter monitoring. Recurrence was defined as any episode of atrial arrhythmia lasting longer than 30 seconds, as confirmed by electrocardiographic documentation (ECG or Holter monitor).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, or median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Comparisons between groups for continuous variables were made using the Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are compared using χ2 tests. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the freedom from AF/AT recurrence during the follow-up period, and groups were compared with the log-rank test. To identify independent predictors of AF recurrence, we used univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Variables entered into the multivariable model included age and those with a P value < 0.20 in the univariable analysis (male, persistent AF, and coronary artery disease). Results are presented as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 27.0, IBM, Armonk, NY).

Repeat PVI was achieved successfully in all patients with PV reconnection (Table 2). The total procedure time was significantly shorter in the PFA group than in the RFA group (109.9 ± 28.0 minutes vs 152.9 ± 33.2 minutes; P < 0.001). General anesthesia was used for all PFA procedures, whereas RFA procedures were performed under local anesthesia (Table 2).

Discussion

Main findings

In this prospective, multicentre study, we found the following: (i) no statistically significant difference was present in the rate of freedom from AF and/or AT recurrence between the PFA and RFA groups; and (ii) the incidence of adverse events was comparable between the 2 groups. These results indicate that PFA using the pentaspline catheter is an effective and safe treatment option for patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure, with outcomes comparable to those achieved with RFA.

Procedural factors influencing repeat ablation success

PVI remains the cornerstone of AF ablation. In patients undergoing repeat ablation for recurrent AF, PV reconnection is a critical determinant of procedural success. Previous studies have shown that among patients undergoing a third or more ablation procedures, the PV reconnection rate exceeds 90%.14 In a study of patients who underwent repeat AF ablation, those with more PV reconnections showed better clinical outcomes than those with fewer.15 In our study, only 25.6% of patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure had durable PV isolation, indicating that the mechanism of recurrence in the majority of cases remains related to the PVs. Previous studies have reported acute PVI success rates approaching 100% with PFA, with long-term maintenance of isolation exceeding 90%.9, 10,16, 17, 18 Similarly, in our study, all patients with PV reconnection underwent repeat PVI, which demonstrated that PFA was effective for repeat procedures.

The durability of the posterior wall isolation is another important factor. Although the Effect of Catheter Ablation Using Pulmonary Vein Isolation With vs Without Posterior Left Atrial Wall Isolation on Atrial Arrhythmia Recurrence in Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation (CAPLA trial) demonstrated that adjunctive posterior wall ablation did not significantly reduce atrial arrhythmia recurrence,4 the reconnection of a previously isolated posterior wall remains a crucial mechanism for recurrent AF. Previous studies have shown that the incidence of reconnection after posterior wall isolation using thermal energy can reach up to 85%.19,20 Whether this high reconnection rate contributes to an underestimation of the true impact of posterior wall isolation on arrhythmia recurrence remains speculative. Evidence comes from a chronic remapping study of patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure, which reported a 100% rate of durable posterior wall isolation when PFA was used.21 However, these findings are derived from a small, nonrandomized study and require confirmation in larger prospective trials. The Multi-National Survey on the Methods, Efficacy, and Safety on the Post-Approval Clinical Use of Pulsed Field Ablation (MANIFEST-PF study) found that adjunctive posterior wall isolation in patients with persistent AF did not significantly improve the rate of AF recurrence, compared with PVI alone.22 A point to note is that the present study did not perform posterior wall isolation during the initial procedure, nor did it systematically evaluate the durability of posterior wall isolation during repeat ablation. Therefore, the potential role of PFA in improving posterior wall isolation durability, as suggested by previous reports, remains hypothetical and was not tested directly in our study.

Efficacy and safety of PFA for recurrent AF

Patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure present greater procedural complexity than treatment-naive patients, which is characterized by more advanced atrial remodelling and complex substrates. Our study demonstrated that PFA significantly reduced total procedure time compared to RFA (109.9 ± 28.0 minutes vs 152.9 ± 33.2 minutes, P < 0.001). Despite the uniform use of general anesthesia in the PFA group, PFA still enhanced procedural efficiency. In addition, fluoroscopy time was significantly longer in the PFA group than in the RFA group (17.1 ± 3.3 minutes vs 5.2 ± 1.5 minutes, P < 0.001). Regarding effectiveness, the freedom from AF and/or AT recurrence was similar between the 2 groups, even though 56.5% of the patients had persistent AF. This finding is consistent with previous studies.13,23 The absence of high-density electroanatomic mapping in the PFA group may have influenced this outcome.

In addition, the incidence of adverse events was similar between the 2 groups. Only 2 cases of procedure-related hemolysis occurred in the PFA group. These events likely were due to an excessive number of PFA applications. A study on hemolysis after PFA showed that more than 70 PFA applications were significantly associated with perioperative hemolysis.24 Our retrospective review found that although the number of applications for these 2 patients did not reach 70, these numbers (54 and 62, respectively) were much higher than those for other patients. This difference may explain the hemolysis in these 2 cases. Moreover, no major complications occurred in the PFA group. Although the safety advantage of PFA could not be demonstrated fully in this study—likely due to the relatively small sample size and the low baseline incidence of such events—a larger-scale study, such as the MANIFEST-17K study, has shown that PFA reduces serious complications.12

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a nonrandomized controlled study in which patients underwent either PFA using the pentaspline catheter or RFA based on clinical judgment. Therefore, the study is susceptible to selection bias, and the level of evidence it provides is lower than that of a randomized controlled trial. In addition, the sample size is small and the follow-up period is short. Therefore, larger-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to draw definitive conclusions. Second, follow-up was based on 12-lead surface ECG and 24-hour Holter monitoring, which is less sensitive for detecting AF and/or AT recurrence compared with continuous invasive monitoring. In addition, in this study, age was significantly associated with AF and/or AT recurrence in the univariable analysis but was not an independent predictor after adjusting for male sex, persistent AF, and coronary artery disease. This finding suggests that the effect of age on AF and/or AT recurrence may be partially mediated by these factors or may be related to the limited sample size and insufficient statistical power. In addition, no significant difference in recurrence rate was observed between patients with paroxysmal and persistent AF in this study, which may be related to the small sample size and insufficient statistical power. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution, and larger-scale studies are warranted to further validate the impact of AF type on postablation recurrence.

In addition, no a priori power calculation was performed during the design phase of this study. Based on a post hoc analysis, the statistical power to detect the observed intergroup difference with the current sample size is low. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, considering the potential risk of a false-negative outcome. Nevertheless, the effect trend observed in this study provides valuable data for estimating the sample size in future larger-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials. In addition, the small sample size means that minor variations between groups can lead to substantial differences in the outcomes. In 25.6% of patients, the PVs were already isolated at the beginning of the procedure, which resulted in an even smaller cohort undergoing redo PVI. In addition, a point to note is that all included patients with recurrence had isolated AF recurrence rather than AF combined with atrial flutter, as no evidence of typical or atypical atrial flutter was documented on pre-procedural electrocardiography or Holter monitoring. Accordingly, our findings may not be directly generalizable to all patients undergoing repeat ablation. Finally, only one PFA device was used (the FARAPULSE system), which limits the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

In patients who had AF recurrence following the initial ablation procedure, PFA using a pentaspline catheter demonstrated comparable effectiveness and safety to RFA, with similar rates of freedom from AF and/or AT recurrence and adverse events at 12 months.
