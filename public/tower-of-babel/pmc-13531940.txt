Examining determinants of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and acceptance among Arab university students: A regional scoping review
Authors: Stephen F. Gambescia, Vivian Tran, Fatmah Almoayad, Basil H. Aboul-Enein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis scoping review consolidates survey research conducted among university students across the League of Arab States to understand the nature and extent of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and acceptance. Using PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a search was conducted across 10 databases covering publications from 2020 to 2024. Fifty-three studies met the inclusion criteria. Reported determinants of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy aligned with key constructs of the Health Belief Model, including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers. Concerns regarding vaccine safety, effectiveness, and side effects were among the most frequently reported reasons for hesitancy, while perceived protection of self and others, trust in health authorities, and institutional requirements were associated with vaccine acceptance. Findings suggest that vaccine decision-making among university students was influenced by an interaction of individual, familial, social, and informational factors. These results help inform future vaccination communication, education, and public health strategies in university settings across the region.

Introduction

Vaccine hesitancy, defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services,1 poses a significant threat to global health.2–4 It is a complex phenomenon influenced by a multitude of factors, making it challenging to address with a single intervention.4,5 The WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) further characterized vaccine hesitancy as a context-specific phenomenon influenced by factors related to confidence, complacency, and convenience, which remains one of the most widely used conceptual frameworks for understanding vaccination behaviors.1 More recent behavioral models conceptualize vaccine acceptance through the ‘5C’ framework, which includes confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility as key psychological antecedents of vaccination behavior.6

The rates of vaccine hesitancy vary greatly depending on context, time, vaccine, price, risk, and location.2–4 For instance, a Gallup poll revealed that during the initial stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, only 58–60% of Americans surveyed expressed willingness to receive the vaccine, even with FDA approval and free availability.7 A review of the literature in high-income countries showed that rates of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy ranged from 7% to 77.9%, with almost half of the studies in high-income countries reporting rates of 30% or more.8 Similar results were reported in lower-income countries; for example, in Ethiopia, rates ranged from 14.1% to 68.7%.5

The consequences of vaccine hesitancy can be severe, potentially leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burdens.9 Vaccine hesitancy is a significant barrier to achieving herd immunity, which is crucial for disrupting disease transmission and protecting the population from outbreaks.2,3,10 Herd immunity is achieved when a significant portion of the population is immunized, making it difficult for a disease to spread. This protects individuals who cannot be vaccinated, such as those with certain medical conditions.7

Mathematical models have shown that achieving herd immunity against COVID-19 is challenging, particularly with regard to vaccine hesitancy. To achieve herd immunity against COVID-19 with a vaccine that is 80% effective, it is estimated that 75–90% of the population would need to be vaccinated. This range depends on several factors, such as the virus’s basic reproduction number (the average number of people that one infected person will pass the virus to), the length of vaccine-induced immunity, and whether vaccines prevent transmission altogether.2 However, the reported rates of vaccine acceptance are often much lower than the required threshold, ranging from about 7% to 50% globally.2 Several factors can influence a person’s decision to vaccinate. Concerns about the safety and efficacy of vaccines often play a significant role, as individuals may fear potential short- to long-term side effects or doubt the effectiveness of immunizations.2,5,8,11 Misinformation, particularly when spread through social media, can exacerbate these fears by promoting false narratives or unverified claims.8 Similarly, distrust in corporate media complicates recommended actions, given that the ‘Fourth Estate’ continues to lose credibility with the public, especially in developed nations with robust media outlets.12,13 Distrust in governments and healthcare systems is another critical factor,14 as skepticism about authorities can lead to resistance against vaccination programs.5 Additionally, a lack of awareness about the benefits of vaccines, including their role in preventing diseases and protecting public health, can result in hesitancy. Finally, religious or cultural beliefs may shape attitudes toward vaccination, with some individuals perceiving it as incompatible with their values or traditions.5,15

While vaccine hesitancy has been studied across many countries, there is a notable absence of a review synthesizing the findings across the League of Arab States (22 countries), with a particular focus on Arab students in higher education. Despite the growing body of primary research conducted in the region, findings remain dispersed across countries and student populations, making it difficult to identify common determinants of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy. Students enrolled in health profession programs represent an especially important population because of their future roles in patient care and public health advocacy.

This gap is significant, given the unique sociocultural, economic, and political factors that may influence vaccine attitudes in the region. The consequences of vaccine hesitancy underscore the urgency of addressing this issue. Thus, this review was conducted to consolidate extant survey research conducted among Arab university students across the League of Arab States to understand the nature and extent of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and acceptance. The review also sought to identify recurring determinants of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy reported across studies.

Methods

Literature search

A scoping review of the literature was conducted using the Arksey and O’Malley framework16 and PRISMA-ScR guidelines.17 Inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed (Table 1). A search of the literature was conducted across 10 databases using a variety of search terms, keywords, and phrases (Table 2). The search strategy (Table 2) was adapted according to the indexing system of each respective database. Furthermore, the references of the articles were examined to identify any additional studies. Eligibility criteria

The search focused on studies published in English, Arabic, and French in peer-reviewed journals. The search was conducted in December 2024 and encompassed studies published between January 2020 and December 31, 2024. Only original peer-reviewed research articles were included in this review. The review excluded studies on Arabic-speaking communities or migrants of Arab origin residing outside the League of Arab States, such as the diaspora. It also excluded publications in languages other than English, French, and Arabic, along with brief communications, gray literature, dissertations, and theses not presented in traditional peer-reviewed journals (Table 2).

Study selection and data extraction

Two authors independently conducted the literature search and selected studies for the scoping review. Any differences were resolved through consensus. In instances where consensus could not be achieved, two additional authors arbitrated the discrepancies. Two authors were responsible for data extraction and organization, while two separate authors independently verified this process. The search strategy was tailored to fit the indexing system of each database. The Rayyan QCRI software was used to facilitate the screening and selection of studies.19 Titles and abstracts were initially screened for relevance, and those that appeared potentially relevant were further scrutinized by four authors for their suitability for inclusion (Table 2). All articles chosen for potential inclusion were reviewed by the lead author before making the final inclusion decision (Figure 1). Following data extraction, the authors conducted a narrative synthesis of factors reported to influence COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and hesitancy. During this process, recurring themes were found to align closely with constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers. Although use of the HBM was not an inclusion criterion and only a small number of studies explicitly referenced the model, it was adopted post hoc as an interpretive framework to organize and synthesize findings across studies. Study findings were mapped to HBM constructs based on the primary determinants reported by investigators. Not all studies addressed every HBM construct; therefore, the model was used to categorize recurring themes rather than to impose a uniform theoretical framework on all included studies.

Ethical statement

Approval by an institutional or human subject review board was not sought, as the review relied on previously published literature and required no human subject participation.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of studies reviewed

Authors and years of publication

The number of authors in the publications ranges from 1 to an outlier high of 31. The high outlier was an international student experience of the COVID-19 vaccine administration response in 9 countries.25 The average number of authors is 5.6 per article. Without a high outlier, the average was five authors per article. The mode of response was eight articles with a single author, followed by seven authors on articles with three, four, and five authors. The small number of authors per article makes sense given that the scale of these projects is manageable, such as a focused target for subjects (students), in an easy-to-reach place (university), and investigators most often familiar with the subjects’ site, a high motivation for subject participation, organizational cooperation with investigators, and easy to manage self-administered online questionnaires.

The literature search was conducted from 2021 to 2024. The greatest number of publications is from 2022, with 25 articles published. This is followed by 14 publications in 2021 and 14 publications in 2023. Only one article in this area of inquiry was published in 2024.

League countries involved in published studies

The highest number of studies published among the Arab League of States are from Saudi Arabia (13), followed by Egypt (10) and Iraq (8), Jordan and Palestine published four projects each, and three articles were from Lebanon, Sudan, and the United Arab Emirates. One article was published by students from Kuwait, Morocco, and Yemen. No articles were published on university students’ responsiveness to the COVID-19 vaccine in Algeria, Bahrain, Comoros, Djibouti, Libya, Mauritania, Oman, Somalia, Syria, and Tunisia. Thus, 45% of the League countries had no formal studies providing a sense of university student responsiveness to the COVID-19 vaccine and the health risks of the pandemic.

Types of students and number participating in studies

The highest number of students participating in these published studies was the general student body population in the university, i.e. not from a particular major or profession within the university, at 44.6% of the articles. The next highest were medical school students, representing 21.4% of the studies published. Seven studies involved nursing students and seven studies involved health science students. Pharmacy students were represented in two studies, and dentistry students were the same. One study included physical therapy students.

The range of participants in these published studies was from 20 medical students in a qualitative in-person interview with medical students at Al-Hikma University in the city of Sana’a, Yemen26 to 5,664 students in a study by Bardus, et al.27 examining the use of social marketing to promote COVID-19 vaccination uptake at the American University Beirut, Lebanon. Approximately one-third of the studies had up to just over 300 participants. There are 28% of the studies that recruited between 300 to 500 students. Nineteen percent of the studies had participants between 500 and 1,000 and about 19% between 1,000 and the highest of 5,664 student participants.

Type of study conducted

Naturally, to understand a subject’s knowledge, attitude, perceptions, and behavior regarding the area under study, the investigators would use some type of questionnaire. Except for four studies, the investigators used an online self-administered survey questionnaire. Investigators described these surveys in various ways, but all were electronic, wherein the investigators solicited student input and instructed them to go to an online survey about their experiences with the COVID-19 virus and subsequent availability of a vaccine. The electronic online mediated surveys used various formats such as web-based, Google Forms, social media, or simply stating “online survey.”

Forty-three (80%) of the studies conducted a cross-sectional analysis to learn about participants’ knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and actions taken in response to the COVID-19 virus and the subsequent offering of a COVID-19 vaccine, with a main interest in students’ acceptance of, hesitancy, or declining to take the vaccine. A range of common demographics was asked about the participants, such as age, sex, status in the program of study by year, and university affiliation. Other demographic questions were asked when the study involved a combination of students, faculty, and staff, such as employment status. Some surveys asked about the health status of the participant and some asked if the participant had contact with the COVID-19 virus or knowledge of someone in their family or someone they knew who had contact with the virus.

Four studies outside these cross-sectional or descriptive surveys included a semi-structured interview of 50 students from Palestinian universities in the West Bank, wherein the investigators conducted a thematic content analysis to identify students’ perceptions and concerns regarding the COVID-19 vaccine, perceived risks of the vaccine, experiences related to the vaccine, and causes of vaccine hesitancy.28 Al-Naggar, et al.26 conducted in-person interviews with 20 medical students from Al-Hikma University in Sana’a, Yemen, who addressed their willingness to accept future COVID-19 vaccines. In-depth interviews were conducted, transcribed, and manually analyzed. A third in-person interview was conducted with a small number of students (11) from a larger online survey (669) of students from the University of Sharjah in the United Arab Emirates29 to measure acceptance or hesitancy to take the COVID-19 vaccine, the reasons for and knowledge of the nature of the virus, the vaccine’s safety and effectiveness, and any outside influencers for them to take some action. One intervention study measured changes in students’ acceptance or hesitancy regarding the vaccine. Al-Turk et al. (2022) conducted a pre-post interventional study of 100 students using questionnaires after students from four pharmacy colleges (three in Amman, Jordan, and one in the United Arab Emirates) viewed a 90- minute videoconference covering the nature and extent of the COVID-19 virus.

Outcomes of studies conducted—nature and extent of vaccine hesitancy or acceptance and reasons

While only three of the 53 reviewed studies explicitly mentioned the Health Belief Model (HBM), many of the factors examined by investigators corresponded conceptually to established HBM constructs. Outcomes of using this classic health education model are essentially the aims of work done in this area of COVID-19 virus vaccine acceptance, hesitancy, or non-acceptance. Thus, following completion of data extraction, the authors conducted a thematic grouping of reported determinants of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy and mapped these determinants to the HBM. This process was undertaken to provide a coherent framework for synthesizing findings across studies that used different survey instruments and analytical approaches. Not all studies addressed all HBM constructs; therefore, the framework was used to organize recurring themes rather than to suggest that all studies were designed using the HBM.

Students’ reported experience with “catching the virus” (susceptibility) and seriousness of the infection for self or others

Students’ perception of the susceptibility and seriousness of the COVID-19 virus infection was shown to affect their acceptance of the vaccine. Among the reviewed studies, two explicitly stated that perceived susceptibility and controllability were factors that led to vaccine acceptance.30,31 Six studies discussed the influence of past COVID-19 infections on the participants themselves or on close family members and/or friends. Among these studies, three mentioned that past COVID-19 infections led to vaccine acceptance, while others demonstrated that past infections led to vaccine hesitancy. Khalafalla, et al.31 proposed an opposing finding, with perceived susceptibility being the least significant factor, based on the Health Belief Model that would predict vaccine acceptance.

The perceived seriousness of COVID-19 infection was briefly mentioned in one study as a factor leading to vaccine acceptance.32 Although students’ perception of the seriousness of COVID-19 infection was not clearly mentioned in other studies, their indication of past infection as leading to their vaccine acceptance or hesitancy suggests that perceived seriousness does play a role in their decision. For example, Khalafalla, et al.31 found that among vaccine-accepting students, including both health science and non-health science majors, past infection with a close family member or friend led to their decision as they wished to avoid having the same experience. Hamza, et al.,33 however, found that past COVID-19 infections did not impact vaccine acceptance among the American University of Beirut population in Lebanon. Their study mentioned that participants who were combating an unspecified infection or illness at the time of the survey reported themselves as vaccine hesitant. However, this may be the result of vaccine hesitancy because of current health status, rather than the overall attitude toward or perception of the COVID-19 vaccine. Ten studies revealed participants’ desire to take the vaccine to protect themselves, their family members, and friends and likely a sense of the perceived susceptibility and seriousness of the COVID-19 virus.30,34–42

Student’s knowledge/perception/attitude toward the makers of the vaccine (pharmaceutical companies) and the advice and administration by a government entity

Vaccine acceptance and hesitancy were also affected by students’ attitudes toward or perceptions of the makers or administrators of the COVID-19 vaccine, which included the pharmaceutical industry, WHO, Ministry of Health, and government entities. Specific factors for vaccine hesitancy included students’ perception of the COVID-19 vaccine development as being rushed or the process did not undertake a scientifically accepted period for testing, prior to being administered to the world’s population.26,38,43 In addition, many of the reviewed studies found that students were concerned about the safety and efficacy of the COVID-19 vaccine due to a lack of research done by the pharmaceutical industry, thus leading to students’ lack of trust in and consequent hesitancy to receive the vaccine. This ties into students’ knowledge of COVID-19 and the vaccine, which is discussed in a later section. Furthermore, two studies revealed that students’ vaccine acceptance was highly associated with trust in the pharmaceutical industry or the health system, and two studies revealed that students trusted COVID-19-related information and standards provided by the WHO or Ministry of Health. Interestingly, four studies questioned students about their perception of the conspiracy theories associated with COVID-19 and the vaccine, with three studies demonstrating that vaccine hesitancy was heavily impacted by students’ beliefs in conspiracy theories, thus leading to a lack of trust in the COVID-19 vaccine.44–46 Those that embraced or disregarded conspiracy theories were found to be more vaccine-accepting.45

Knowledge/perception/attitude toward the COVID-19 vaccine

Among the reviewed studies (N = 53), there were varying results regarding whether knowledge or perception of the COVID-19 vaccine had significant effects on vaccine acceptance or hesitancy. Only one study claimed that knowledge was not significantly associated with vaccine acceptance or hesitancy.47 Five studies explicitly mentioned that students possessed more knowledge or a better understanding of COVID-19, and that the vaccine demonstrated higher vaccine acceptance, thus emphasizing the importance of education targeting populations of lower education levels. Three studies revealed that students indicating vaccine hesitancy did so in part due to a lack of information about the COVID-19 vaccine.34,48,49 However, such measurements of knowledge levels did not include students’ attitudes toward or perceptions of the information provided to them. Some studies have revealed that the source of information, including the WHO, Ministry of Health, and social media, played a role in students’ trust in the COVID-19 vaccine. For example, Ali50 showed that misinformation presented on social media was a significant factor that led to vaccine hesitancy. Jabbour, et al.51 discussed similar findings, although distrust of COVID-19-related information circulating through social media served as a factor toward vaccine acceptance. However, Jabbour, et al.51 surveyed students on their attitudes toward generalized vaccinations nonspecific to only the COVID-19 vaccine, demonstrating that these findings apply widely to other preventative health interventions. Similarly, seven other studies questioned students regarding their perception of circulating COVID-19 and vaccine-related information in general without specifying the source of information. They revealed that students’ attitudes toward and degree of trust in circulating such information played a crucial role in their decisions on whether to vaccinate against COVID-19.

Concern for the possible safety, efficacy, and side effects of the COVID-19 vaccine was shown to have a significant impact on vaccination decisions among surveyed students in 34 out of 53 studies reviewed. This was a consistent finding among students from different disciplines, including nursing,52 medical,53 physical therapy,54 pre-doctoral and post-graduate students,43 and university students in general.37 Alzubaidi, et al.29 and26 were the only studies that mentioned only effectiveness, without discussion of concern for side effects or safety, as a factor impacting the decision to vaccinate. All other studies that mentioned these factors (n = 32) found that at least two of them contributed toward vaccine hesitancy. Eight studies demonstrated that some students, regardless of discipline, were concerned about the lack of research conducted or publicized on the COVID-19 vaccine.34,48,55–60 Two studies also found that some nursing and medical students were concerned about becoming infected with the vaccine, suggesting a concern for vaccine safety.61,62

Other external influencers on student acceptance or hesitancy toward the COVID-19 vaccine

Other factors were found to have a significant impact on students’ acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine. Among these factors was a strong desire to return to normal life or life prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and was mainly associated with vaccine acceptance.34,36,37 Four studies found that mandatory vaccines to return to campus influenced them to take the vaccine.27,34,52,63 Others mentioned the influence of logistical issues that led to vaccine hesitancy, including the inability to schedule an appointment or the uncertainty of vaccine accessibility.27,33,49 Also, the religious or cultural beliefs of students were among the many factors that influenced their decision to vaccinate. One study revealed that Jazan University students, of both health and non-health science majors and of different academic years, were vaccine-accepting in part due to the willingness of public figures, such as celebrities or government officials who apparently served as exemplars in taking the vaccine.58 This study also questioned whether willingness to complete the survey created bias in the results, as most participants indicated that they were vaccine accepting, while those who were more vaccine hesitant may have been less willing to complete the survey or answer questions truthfully. Khalis, et al.32 had similar findings, with family members’ and friends’ opinions having an impact on students’ attitudes toward the COVID-19 vaccine. Students also indicated that the factors influencing their acceptance of the vaccine included suggestions provided by their physician, their personal values, cost of the vaccine, and preference for natural immunity. Many of the reviewed studies briefly mentioned that male students demonstrated greater vaccine acceptance than did female students. However, there has been no discussion of the factors contributing to this finding.

Discussion

The consistent specific aims of studies examining the uptake of a COVID-19 vaccine among students attending universities in the League of Arab States clearly fit within the Health Belief Model of behavior mentioned in the Results section of this article. Researchers sought to examine students’ knowledge, perceptions, behaviors, and barriers related to the nature and extent of the COVID-19 virus and the subsequent vaccines that were forthcoming or arrived to prevent or protect people or mitigate the illness, including death, for which they would be at risk.

Studies sought information regarding: (a) students’ understanding of the COVID-19 vaccine and their perceived likelihood of contracting the virus; (b) their understanding of viral transmission to family members and communities; (c) their beliefs about the seriousness of infection; (d) their perceptions of vaccine safety, effectiveness, and side effects; (e) external influences on vaccine-related decision-making; (f) trust in information sources such as government entities, social media, traditional media, and university administrators; (g) vaccine acceptance or hesitancy; and (h) barriers to vaccine uptake.

Two other specific aims were external to students’ welfare. Investigators stated clearly in their articles that university students, and health profession students in particular, should be the early adopters to the uptake of a COVID-19 vaccine, given that they are expected to be well-educated exemplars, and health professionals should ascribe to best healthcare practice for themselves, their patients, and the university and wider community.

Mostoles Jr, et al.47 concluded that education programs for students help them “develop skills and encourage them to become accountable health professionals who can support current societal healthcare demands.”47 Al-Turk, et al.64 concluded that “pharmacy students as future healthcare providers could play an essential role in counteracting vaccine hesitancy and increasing COVID-19 vaccine acceptance.”64 Alshammari, et al.57 provided rationale to understand “healthcare students’ perceptions and beliefs regarding immunizations and immunization-preventable diseases will produce well-informed and confident healthcare professionals … that are knowledgeable and competent healthcare professionals [to work] in this important public health area.”57

Abu-Hammad, et al.65 that studied factors associated with vaccine acceptance and recommendation among clinical dental students and faculty in Saudi Arabia stated that “COVID-19 vaccines and awareness among dental personnel is a crucial factor in determining vaccine acceptance and recommending it to patients. Vaccine awareness campaigns can improve the attitudes of dental personnel in academic settings.”65

Note that more than 50% of the studies in this scoping review looked at health profession students (medicine, nursing, health science or pre health professional programs, pharmacy, and dental). Clearly, the investigators see these university students as a priority target for knowledge acquisition, given that they not only treat people when infectious disease epidemics or pandemics strike, but also know that the public views them as exemplars. Faculty who teach students in the healing arts formally and informally remind them that “they are in the business of improving the human condition.”66

Given that the aim of these studies was to query university students on their acceptance or hesitancy to take the COVID-19 vaccine, they had students complete self-administered surveys and subsequently conducted cross-sectional analysis among several characteristics of their subjects, such as gender, age, grade level, educational level, active lifestyle, belief about the virus and the vaccine, presence of chronic disease, exposure to social media, marital status, having contacted the COVID-19 virus, and knowing family members or friends who contacted the virus, among others, to understand the association with the uptake of the vaccine. These studies analyzed a healthy number of characteristics, depending on their specific aims, and all were able to discover useful information to make confident recommendations for improving the uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine. All studies aimed at improving the uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine among students recommended that the curriculum, campaigns, lessons, counseling, etc. should be implemented.

The reviewed studies generally approached vaccine acceptance as the preferred public health outcome and frequently focused on identifying barriers to vaccine uptake and strategies to increase acceptance. This sentiment has existed from the earliest studies to the latest (2021–2024). This position or healthcare recommendation was presented from the reading of the rationale and background of the studies through the discussion section and the conclusion of the articles published. While the recommendation to take the vaccine was given by what most would consider credible sources, such as the WHO; national, regional, and local health ministries in these countries; and healthcare and health professional organizations, among others, it is peculiar that such a strong normative posture is taken by these investigators who aimed to provide insight into an unprecedented public health action of all people. In other words, there is little evidence on the write-up of these reports of some consideration for why students may be hesitant to take the COVID-19 vaccine. Across many studies, vaccine hesitancy was frequently framed as a problem requiring correction through education, awareness campaigns, counseling, or institutional interventions designed to increase vaccine uptake among students. One major consideration in studying this behavior that was overlooked is the evolving timeline of the pandemic and the subsequent discovery, making, distribution, and administration of the COVID-19 vaccine to people. Subsequently, forming a response to whether one would take the vaccine or not had several variables, as these studies show, but such variables changed over time and had a reasonable effect on one’s enthusiasm to take the vaccine, and in most cases, ask people to take two or more vaccines. Several reviewed studies discussed vaccine mandates, institutional requirements, and promotional strategies intended to increase vaccine uptake. These findings highlight ongoing tensions between public health objectives and individual decision-making regarding vaccination.

Below are serious considerations during the COVID-19 outbreak and the subsequent development of a vaccine that on balance explains why students believed not taking the vaccine was prudent, and why such thinking may escape a rush to judgment as being ill-informed, unreasonable, and irresponsible.

Several factors may help explain students’ caution regarding COVID-19 vaccination. These included the unprecedented nature of the pandemic, uncertainty regarding disease transmission patterns, evolving public health recommendations concerning masking and distancing, differing experiences with disease severity, varying levels of trust in scientific experts, governments, media organizations, and pharmaceutical companies, concerns regarding the accelerated timeline of vaccine development and testing, and changing public health messages regarding the vaccine’s role in prevention, protection, and disease mitigation.

While the points above may not be the conventional thinking of those messaging out to groups of people to take the vaccine, it is unusual to find such a lack of reflection among research groups from many countries over a four-year period. One would expect some latitude to understand the public’s belief and subsequent action given evolving and conflicting information when considering the factors in the Health Belief Model, that is, perceived susceptibility, seriousness of the sickness, perceived benefits and barriers to recommended measures, usurping autonomy in making healthcare decisions, and conflicting cues to action.

The Health Belief Model depends on the information presented to an individual. In this regard, much has been said and written by several stakeholders concerning misinformation. While people generally understand what is meant by misinformation, the arguments against this may not distinguish the various forms of misinformation, as they can come from several areas with varying motives such as 1) people and groups that have a serious concern about vaccines in general, and this vaccine, in particular, does not comport with conventional vaccination recommendations, at least in part. 2) Trolls that push out misinformation for ideological or nefarious reasons. 3) Traditional, Corporate, or what is considered Mainstream Media. Note that the latter has been broadly criticized as no longer serving as objective news purveyors in many countries and, consequently, has lost favor in the public’s eyes. Furthermore, in some countries, media outlets have picked sides favoring political actors and political parties over objective, accurate, and responsible news reporting; consequently, healthy discussions on public policy issues suffer.

In their concluding remarks, three studies used the term misinformation directly.51,67,68 Surprisingly, of these 53 articles from 11 countries, little was said that mainstream media could be implicated in providing biased or misinformation at some level. More attention should be given by investigators to clarify the source of misinformation/disinformation and not conflate the several sources, which unfortunately include mainstream media.

Reporting the results of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy among a population must be nuanced when examining variables in an evolving phenomenon. For example, interpreting responses to students’ knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors toward taking the COVID-19 vaccine must be considered when the vaccine is produced and made available. For the latter, note that early distribution was phased in, as it took time for vaccines to be produced and mass distributed. Students in the health profession were likely a priority, given that they had contact with patients. Another variable in interpreting the administration of the vaccine was the varying policies on mandatory uptake by the schools, and certainly if out of clinical rotations. The nuance here is that students may have taken the vaccine, but do not agree with being forced to take it. This set up a hybrid category of takers in that they accepted the vaccine but ranged from hesitation to being against forced to take the vaccine.

Students in health professional programs had a high level of pressure to take the vaccine. For example, Wedaa, et al.69 concluded that: “Implementing vaccination-supportive strategies increases the level of vaccination uptake and decreases the level of hesitancy among students. The majority of vaccinated students were hesitant at first but changed their minds over time, potentially as a result of vaccination-supportive strategies at their institutions, particularly as a result of availability and ease of access to the vaccines. Most of the students accepted a mandatory vaccination strategy. Nevertheless, a quarter of students believe this strategy violates their privacy and human rights.”69

The multi-country study by Sitarz, et al.,25 considered the mandatory variable, writing: “Further, taking into account the vaccine policy in the investigated countries, it seems beneficial to make the COVID-19 vaccination obligatory, as the highest percentage of vaccinated students was found in those countries where the vaccination was mandatory. Such attitudes could potentially provide a safer environment for work and minimize the spread of the infection.”25

Shawqi, et al.70 wrote in their conclusion: “Additionally, the students supported the implementation of mandatory booster vaccines for both the staff and students. However, there is still a gap in vaccination practices. Therefore, we recommend initiating vaccination campaigns to raise awareness and enhance vaccination practices, particularly in medical education and training.”70 Alshehri and Sallam45 in a pilot study of Saudi Universities on COVID-19 vaccination side effects and negative attitude toward booster COVID-19, influenza, and monkeypox vaccines addressed students opinions of mandatory uptake, “show[ing] the tendency [of students] to disagree with mandatory vaccination resonating the previous divided opinion regarding this strategy which should be applied cautiously as a public health control measure aiming to reduce the burden of infectious diseases.”45 A recent systematic review of university students worldwide found that hesitancy among college students was primarily driven by concerns regarding vaccine safety, efficacy, misinformation, and accessibility barriers, although important regional variations remain insufficiently understood.71

The findings of this scoping review have several practical implications for universities, health ministries, and public health professionals across the Arab region. Given the concerns regarding vaccine safety, effectiveness, side effects, and trust in information sources were the most reported determinants of hesitancy, vaccination communication strategies should prioritize transparent, evidence-based messaging that directly addresses these concerns.

Universities are naturally well positioned to serve as trusted settings for vaccine education through curriculum integration, student-led health promotion initiatives, collaborations with healthcare professionals, and dissemination of accurate information through digital and social media platforms.

Given the influence of family members and peers on vaccine hesitancy and acceptance reported across several studies, interventions that engage these social influences may be more effective than approaches directed solely at individual students. Similarly, university faculty, healthcare providers, and institutional leaders should consider how campus policies, communication strategies, and vaccination requirements influence student decision-making. Efforts that foster trust, provide transparent information, and encourage informed health choices may be particularly valuable in addressing vaccine hesitancy among university populations.

Future research should expand beyond the countries most frequently represented in the literature and examine student populations in underrepresented Arab League states. Longitudinal and mixed-methods studies are also needed to better understand how vaccine attitudes evolve over time and how changes in public health policies, information environments, and emerging infectious disease threats influence vaccine decision-making among university students. Additional research evaluating the effectiveness of specific educational and communication interventions would further inform evidence-based strategies to address vaccine hesitancy in university settings. These findings suggest that vaccine acceptance and hesitancy among Arab university students are shaped by a complex interaction of perceived risks and benefits, trust in institutions and information sources, social influences, and other factors throughout a period of considerable uncertainty.

Strengths and limitations

Regarding strengths, to our knowledge it is among the first reviews to synthesize evidence on COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and hesitancy specifically among university students across the League of Arab States. The review followed the Arksey and O’Malley framework and PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines, using a comprehensive search strategy across 10 databases, and included studies published in English, Arabic, and French. The review consolidated findings from 53 studies conducted across multiple countries and diverse student populations, including both health profession and non-health profession students. The use of the Health Belief Model as an interpretive framework provided a structured approach for synthesizing determinants of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy reported across studies employing different survey instruments and methodologies.

Regarding, limitations, the evidence base was unevenly distributed across the Arab region, with most studies originating from a limited number of countries, particularly Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and Iraq, while many League of Arab States had no published studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Thus, the findings may not fully represent the experiences and perspectives of university students throughout the entire region. Second, most included studies employed cross-sectional designs and relied on self-reported survey data, limiting the ability to infer causal relationships and introducing the possibility of response and social desirability biases. Third, substantial variation existed among studies regarding survey instruments, timing of data collection, populations studied, and measures of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy, which limited direct comparisons across studies. Fourth, because this review was conducted as a scoping review, the methodological quality of included studies was not formally assessed. Finally, attitudes toward COVID-19 vaccination evolved rapidly throughout the pandemic as information regarding the virus, vaccine effectiveness, safety, public health recommendations, and vaccine mandates changed over time. Therefore, findings should be interpreted within the specific temporal context in which the studies were conducted.

Conclusion

This scoping review synthesized evidence from 53 studies examining COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and hesitancy among university students across the League of Arab States. The findings indicate that vaccine decision-making among students was influenced by factors consistent with the Health Belief Model, including perceived susceptibility to COVID-19, perceived severity of infection, perceived benefits of vaccination, and perceived barriers to vaccine uptake.

Across studies, concerns regarding vaccine safety, side effects, and effectiveness emerged as the most frequently reported reasons for vaccine hesitancy. Trust in vaccine manufacturers, health authorities, governments, and information sources also played an important role in shaping attitudes toward vaccination. Conversely, the desire to protect oneself and others, prior experiences with COVID-19, the desire to return to normal activities, and institutional requirements were commonly associated with vaccine acceptance.

The review further demonstrates that vaccine-related decisions among university students cannot be explained solely by knowledge deficits. Students’ attitudes were shaped by a complex interaction of personal experiences, social influences, cultural factors, trust in institutions, and perceptions of risk and benefit. These findings highlight the importance of tailored communication and educational strategies that address specific concerns regarding vaccine safety and effectiveness while fostering trust in credible health information sources.

Finally, the uneven geographic distribution of studies across Arab League countries highlights important evidence gaps and the need for further research in underrepresented settings. Understanding the diverse factors that influence vaccine acceptance and hesitancy among university students may help universities, healthcare organizations, and public health authorities develop more effective communication, educational, and public health responses to future infectious disease outbreaks and vaccination campaigns.
