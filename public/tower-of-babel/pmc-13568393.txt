From data to quality: the story of A-QUA, Switzerland's Anaesthesia QUAlity initiative and database
Authors: Christoph Karl Hofer, Sarah R. Haile, Remo Schmutz, Milo Alan Puhan, Michael Thomas Ganter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Anaesthesiology has long been a leader in advancing patient safety and clinical standardisation, with mortality due to anaesthesia in high-income countries now estimated at fewer than 1 in 100 000 procedures.1,2 Despite this success, peri-operative morbidity, complication rates, and hospital variation in process quality remain significant challenges, particularly for high-risk patients undergoing complex surgery.3 As such, national-level quality initiatives have emerged across Europe to promote continuous improvement, foster transparency and support data-driven decision-making in peri-operative care.4–6

Switzerland has a population of approximately 9 million and a decentralised healthcare system comprising 26 cantons, each with its own hospital network. It is estimated that more than 1.1 million anaesthesia procedures are performed annually by physician anaesthesiologists, supported by specialised nurse anaesthesia providers across around 170 public and private hospitals, including teaching hospitals with accredited anaesthesia training programmes, non-teaching hospitals and office-based anaesthesia providers.7 Switzerland is divided into three primary language regions, which exert a substantial influence on culture and social life. Approximately two-thirds of the population speak German, the German part of Switzerland. A quarter speak French, and less than 10% speak Italian.8

Historically, the country lacked a comprehensive infrastructure for collecting and evaluating quality indicators in anaesthesia practice. To fill this gap, the Swiss Society for Anaesthesiology and Peri-operative Medicine (SSAPM) launched the national Anaesthesia QUAlity (A-QUA) programme in 2014.9 The programme initially concentrated on structural data (A-QUA part 1). In 2017, it expanded to include detailed information on each anaesthetic, including processes and outcomes (A-QUA registry, part 2). As of the end of 2024, A-QUA part 2 has accumulated over 2 million anaesthesia cases, with nearly 450 000 cases reported in 2024 alone by more than 50 participating centres. Participation is formally mandatory for all SSAPM-accredited anaesthesia teaching hospitals and voluntary for other providers.

The A-QUA programme plays a central role in Switzerland's broader medical quality framework. It supports benchmarking peri-operative quality indicators across Swiss anaesthesia providers, informs political and strategic decisions within the SSAPM, and serves as a prerequisite for hospital accreditation through the new Swiss anaesthesia certification programme, A-CERT.10 Additionally, it forms the foundation for prospective outcome-based peri-operative research projects in Switzerland.

This manuscript presents the development, structure, and current status of the Swiss anaesthesia quality programme A-QUA, and, while providing an overview of the entire programme, considers its analyses on the registry, part 2 of the national database. The study aims to assess the database's quality by verifying accuracy, identifying potential issues, and analysing missing values through descriptive statistics and plausibility checks for consistency with the existing codebook. We further characterise the database, summarise coded procedures, and examine in detail the five most frequent standard A-QUA procedures to illustrate its scope and utility.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for the ethical conduct of human research. The Ethical Committee of Zurich, Switzerland (Kantonale Ethikkommission) together with all other national ethical committees reviewed the study and confirmed that it does not fall within the scope of the Swiss Human Research act (BASEC No: Req-2025–00775, 27 June 2025). Thereby, an approval by a Swiss Ethics Committee is not required. As this study used retrospective data from anonymised records, individual patient consent was not required and was formally waived by the committee.

A-QUA is classified under Swiss law as a quality assurance initiative using anonymised routine care data (HFG Art. 34 / HFV). The programme has been assessed and approved by the Swiss Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC) and adapted to meet the requirements of the revised Swiss data protection act in 2023.16 Data handling complies with national data protection regulations, and registry operations are periodically reviewed by SSAPM's legal and data governance committees.

Quality framework of SSAPM

The SSAPM's quality framework is based on the model of quality proposed by Donabedian, who identified three essential dimensions of quality in healthcare: structure, process and patient outcomes.11,12 The current quality management strategy was approved by the SSAPM's general assembly in 2019.13 This multifaceted approach combines data collection, analyses and benchmarking (programme A-QUA9) and the establishment of professional standards with a dedicated quality label (programme A-CERT10), ensuring and continuously improving the quality of anaesthesiology and peri-operative medicine in Switzerland.

A-QUA programme, structure and variables

A-QUA is the SSAPM's core quality assurance programme, launched in 2014. This national, multilevel programme systematically collects data to measure and improve the quality of anaesthesia and peri-operative care in Switzerland. The programme enables departments to analyse their own data and compare their performance with that of other anonymised departments. It forms the basis of the legally required quality assurance measures in Swiss healthcare. Furthermore, the data collected through A-QUA is used to identify areas for improvement and develop new standards and recommendations. The SSAPM Commission for Data and Quality (CDQ) provides governance and oversees the validity, operational standards and data security of the programme as a regulatory body. A-QUA is divided into two parts, A-QUA part 1 and 2, which together cover all quality-related aspects of anaesthesia and peri-operative services. An overview of the A-QUA areas and dimensions is shown in Fig. 1.

The landscape of the Swiss Anaesthesia QUAlity programme, A-QUA. The database consists of structural data (A-QUA part 1, collected annually) and detailed information on anaesthesia and surgical processes and outcomes (A-QUA part 2, recorded continuously for each anaesthesia case). The ClassIntra classification is a standardised tool used to grade the severity of intra-operative adverse events based on the level of intervention required to treat them. It ranges from Grade 0 (no deviation) to Grade 5 (death), providing a consistent way to report intra-operative complications.

The first part of A-QUA examines structural data of individual anaesthesia departments. The collection of data occurs on an annual basis, and since 2016, participation has been mandatory for all accredited anaesthesia training centres. The data set encompasses a range of metrics, including facility-related information such as the number of operating theatres, annual performance indicators such as the number of anaesthetics administered, and anaesthesia staff details such as the number of nurses, residents and consultants. Postoperative care metrics include the number of beds in the postanaesthesia care unit, intermediate care unit or ICU, while pain services are measured by the number of consultations provided. The data also includes information on operating theatre management and emergency medical services.

The second component of the A-QUA programme pertains to case-related data. This part encompasses the collection of data concerning individual patients who have undergone anaesthesia. Data are collected continuously for every anaesthesia procedure, and participation in this part is mandatory for all accredited anaesthesia training centres as of 2022. The dataset is both structured and comprehensive, consisting of approximately 200 well defined, standardised variables.14 It covers patient characteristics and preoperative risk, as well as intra-operative events and early postoperative outcomes including patient satisfaction with anaesthesia care. Other variables contain the type of anaesthesia and monitoring, a procedure code, the discipline and location, timestamps, urgency, in- or outpatient and further variables. The SSAPM developed a coding system for the procedures (A-QUA codes) with the objective of facilitating enhanced analysis and comparison of data, thereby ensuring greater clarity and significance.14 The Coding system consists of 177 A-QUA codes reflecting either procedure groups or well defined routine procedures (standard A-QUA procedures, n = 24). Each A-QUA code has its own definition of the surgical risk (1 to 4). Timestamps encompassing the peri-operative process are meticulously delineated and synchronised with the widely accepted definitions outlined in the German peri-operative procedural time glossary.15 In addition to anaesthesia-related activities, A-QUA part 2 also encompasses the collection of additional anaesthesia services. These services include blood sampling, catheter insertion not associated with a particular anaesthesia procedure, anaesthesia consultation and pain service provision.

Information technology architecture, data management and safety

The A-QUA platform and database have been developed by ProtecData AG (Boswil, Switzerland), an organisation with extensive experience in information technology solutions in healthcare. A-QUA is a web-based platform that operates on a Microsoft SQL server (Redmond, Washington, USA); client installations are not a prerequisite. The system is scalable and has a standard user interface that can be operated on any platform. A formal agreement between the SSAPM, as the proprietor of A-QUA, and each participating anaesthesia department specifies the technical and procedural standards for data protection and secure data transfer, including protocols for reception, plausibility checking, and validation of submitted datasets. In addition, the agreement outlines the financial framework for participation, covering costs associated with data processing, registry maintenance, and the provision of standardised benchmarking and feedback reports. The data for A-QUA part 1 is entered directly via a web-based application on an annual basis by the centre head or a responsible data quality manager. For A-QUA part 2, three modes of data entry are available. Firstly, individual patient data can be entered directly during anaesthesia via a secure web application. Secondly, data can be recorded within the centre's anaesthesia information management system (AIMS) and/or hospital information system (HIS) and subsequently exported to the central server through a defined interface, using a batch process after completion of the dataset. Thirdly, data can be transferred automatically via an URL call, with any missing information completed through the web application. It should be noted that there are two distinct databases: the source database and the report database. Initially, the data are stored in the source database. Following the processes of data validation and completion, the data are then anonymised prior to transfer to the report database (Fig. 2).

A considerable number of providers use a hybrid approach, integrating both exportation and transfer of data to the A-QUA source database, thereby combining automated core data submission with manual entry of supplementary quality indicators. Plausibility checks of A-QUA part 2 database

We defined 22 plausibility checks based on the codebook14 and data knowledge, addressing consistency between related variables, logical dependencies by anaesthesia type, completeness of mandatory fields, and range checks for demographic, procedural, and coded variables (Appendix A). Several checks ensured empty fields where inapplicable and that reported values matched allowed categories. Noncompliance was stratified by year, with results tabulated and common issues discussed. Anaesthesia practices were also screened for outliers to identify providers with markedly different values. The analysis was exploratory, using visualisation to detect implausibility and compare outcomes and baseline variables across hospitals, language regions, and centre categories, without formal hypothesis testing. Checks 1 to 15, 17 and 20 follow codebook rules.14 Errors were categorised into major, intermediate and minor. Cases with major errors were completely excluded from analysis, cases with intermediate and minor errors were included in data analysis, except for the variables concerned.

Statistical analysis of A-QUA part 2 database

Summary statistics are reported as absolute numbers n (%), % or median [IQR]. Boxplots were used for continuous variables and alluvial plots were used to visualise associations between two categorical variables. In this descriptive analysis, no statistical hypothesis test was performed, and therefore, no P-values are reported.

All analyses were performed in the R programming language.17 The plausibility checks were implemented using data.table.18

Results

Swiss anaesthesia landscape

By the end of 2024, anaesthesia care had 173 providers, including: Teaching hospitals (levels A-C) with accredited anaesthesiology training programmes. A1 (n = 11) and A2 (n = 14) centres are high-volume hospitals, typically university or major cantonal or private hospitals, performing at least 7500 (A2) or at least 12 500 (A1) anaesthesia procedures annually with a complex case mix. B centres (n = 22) are medium-sized hospitals (≥3500 procedures per year) and C centres (n = 5) are smaller regional hospitals (≥1000 procedures per year). In addition, there are non-teaching hospitals, including smaller regional (n = 39) and private clinics (n = 59) and ‘office-based’ anaesthesia providers (n = 23), mainly providing outpatient surgical services.

Participation in A-QUA

The part 1 was initiated in 2014 with 70 participating centres. Participation levels increased until 2017, after which point they remained stable, with approximately 100 participants. The former represent the larger and more established Swiss hospitals, collectively performing approximately 800 000 anaesthetics each year.

The more complex, second part of A-QUA was launched in 2017 with 10 participants from 7 cantons. As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the database has received cases from 54 out of a total of 173 providers across all language regions and 21 out of a total of 26 cantons by the end of 2024. In certain instances, some participated for one year but subsequently withdrew. This phenomenon may be attributed, for instance, to alterations in the information technology system of a given hospital. The re-establishment of data flow can be a time-consuming process, which may result in temporary non-participation in the programme.

Quality of A-QUA part 2 database

Plausibility checks of the A-QUA part 2 database identified 16 965 (0.8%) major, 144 162 (7%) intermediate and 496 640 (24%) minor errors (Appendix A). Major issues mainly involved invalid procedural codes and implausible durations, while intermediate errors included date inconsistencies, physiologically impossible patient measures and incompatible variable combinations. Minor errors largely reflected noncritical incomplete documentation. Error distribution was highly uneven, with a small number of participants responsible for a disproportionate share. Most errors could have been prevented through stricter input validation and automated cross-checks, reflecting a potential target for quality improvement.

Description of the data in A-QUA part 2 database

By the end of 2024, the A-QUA part 2 database comprised 2 045 026 anaesthetics (after exclusion of major erroneous entries) from all three Swiss language regions. Most cases originated from large teaching hospitals (74%), followed by small and medium-sized teaching hospitals (17%), nonteaching public hospitals (4.3%), and private clinics (4.5%). There are no cases from office-based providers so far. The median annual number of cases per participant was 20 952 [5444 to 46 124], with higher volumes in German-speaking institutions. There are relevant differences between the three language regions. Although the number of cases in the current registry (61% from German, 35% from French and 4% from Italian part) is similar to the previous survey on the anaesthesia landscape in Switzerland,7 most cases in the German and French part come from large teaching hospitals (G: 73%, F: 81%), whereas in the Italian part, most cases come from small and medium-sized teaching and non-teaching hospitals (I: 54%) (Table 1).

Patients had a median age of 56 years [35 to 71] and 52% were women. Two-third were classified as ASA physical status I to II and one third as III to VI. The median BMI was 25.3 kg m-2 [22.2 to 29.1]. Surgical risk categories were distributed as follows: lower risk 1 to 2, about two-thirds, and higher risk 3 to 4, about one-third, whereas highest risk accounted only for 2.3%. Emergency procedures accounted for 28% of all cases. Patient and surgical risk are comparable between the regions, however, there is a smaller number of high-risk procedures (surgical risk category 3 and 4) in the German part (25%; F: 33%, I: 30%) and emergency procedures were highest in the French part (35%; G: 24%, I: 23%) (Table 1).

General anaesthesia (including combined) was administered in 74% of cases, with total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) being more frequent (48%) than inhalational general anaesthesia (26%). Regional anaesthesia alone was used in 18% of cases, and monitored anaesthesia care (MAC) in 8.5%. TIVA was most common in the German-speaking region, while regional anaesthesia alone was most common in the French-speaking region, and MAC was most common in the Italian-speaking region. Median anaesthesia duration was 122 min [78 to 183] and median surgical duration 53 min [27 to 95], corresponding to a median anaesthesia-to-surgery time ratio of 2.20 [1.70 to 3.20]. Postanaesthesia care was most frequently delivered in a postanaesthesia care unit (64% of cases), followed by direct transfer to a ward (13%) or day clinic/outpatient setting (9.7%). Intermediate care units (3.0%) and ICUs (5.6%) were less frequent postoperative destinations. Intra-operative events occurred in 5.8% of cases and postoperative events in 1.8%. The lowest rate was reported in the Italian area (Table 1).

A total of 459 959 standard A-QUA procedures (24 predefined, well defined routine interventions) were reported between 2017 and 2024. Table 2 summarises anaesthesia and surgical times for these procedures, which ranged from short interventions such as carpal tunnel release (median surgical time 13 min) to long and complex operations such as coronary artery bypass graft with sternotomy (median surgical time 211 min). The median anaesthesia-to-surgery time ratio varied accordingly, from 1.5 for coronary artery by-pass graft to 4.7 for carpal tunnel release. Case studies of the top five standard A-QUA procedures

These case studies were conducted as illustrative examples to demonstrate the analytical scope and data granularity of the A-QUA registry and to highlight the types of quality-relevant questions that can be addressed using this national dataset, rather than to provide a comprehensive description of anaesthesia practice patterns.

The top five most frequently reported standard A-QUA procedures accounted for 203 966 cases, representing 44% of all standard A-QUA cases, and were performed across all Swiss language regions (Table 2). Not surprising, teaching hospitals had the longest and private nonteaching institutions the shortest procedure times. Event rate was higher in teaching institutions and for higher ASA physical status. In addition, for the latter, anaesthesia and surgical times were longer (Fig. 5, Appendix C).

Uncomplicated caesarean section was the single most common standard procedure, with 52 709 cases. Median anaesthesia and surgical durations were 88 min [76 to 106] and 45 min [35 to 56], respectively, yielding a ratio of approximately 2.0. Regional anaesthesia was used in the vast majority (over 90%), with general anaesthesia reserved for urgent or emergency deliveries. Intra-operative events occurred in 11% of cases and postoperative events in 2%. Most patients were either transferred to the delivery (German part) or recovery room (French and Italian part). Unilateral cataract surgery was reported in 34 380 cases and showed a markedly different profile. Median surgical duration was 16 min [12 to 23], but anaesthesia duration was 55 min [37 to 80], giving a ratio of 3.0. A high percentage of the cases were performed under MAC. Intra-operative events occurred in 10% and postoperative events in 1%. Most patients were outpatients in a day clinic. Total hip arthroplasty was reported in 46 452 cases. Median anaesthesia duration was 166 min [139 to 204] compared with a median surgical duration of 77 min [61 to 100], corresponding to a ratio of 2.1. Anaesthesia techniques were different in all three language regions using mostly general anaesthesia, TIVA in the German, mostly general anaesthesia with inhalational agents in the French and mostly RA in the Italian region. Intra-operative events were recorded in 12% and postoperative events in 4%. Most patients went to the PACU in all regions. Knee arthroscopy without cruciate ligament reconstruction comprised 35 229 cases. This was a relatively short procedure, with a median surgical time of 33 min [22 to 55] and anaesthesia time of 104 min [83 to 140], producing a ratio of 3.0. Again, the Italian region had the highest percentage of RA. Intra-operative events occurred in 4% and postoperative events in 1%. Postanaesthesia destinations were predominantly the PACU. Total knee arthroplasty accounted for 35 196 cases. Surgical duration was longer, median 90 min, [72 to 113] and anaesthesia duration was 173 min [148 to 213], giving a ratio of 1.9. Most procedures were performed under RA alone or combined with general anaesthesia, again with the highest percentage for regional anaesthesia in the Italian region. Intra-operative events were documented in 10% of cases and postoperative events in 3%. PACU was the most common postoperative destination (Fig. 5, Appendix C and D).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive description of the structure, participation, data quality, and analytical potential of A-QUA, Switzerland's national anaesthesia quality registry. With more than 100 participating anaesthesia providers and over two million anaesthesia procedures, A-QUA now delivers a broad national coverage, particularly among teaching hospitals, and captures anaesthesia practice patterns across all Swiss language regions. The registry demonstrates robust completeness for core variables and the ability to generate detailed, procedure-specific benchmarks, positioning it as a key instrument for national anaesthesia quality monitoring in Switzerland.

The evolution of A-QUA parallels that of other national anaesthesia and peri-operative registries, which have become central to quality improvement and research worldwide. In the Nordic countries, registry-based collaboration has shown that harmonising definitions and scoring systems enables multicountry benchmarking and outcome research despite differences in healthcare systems.6 In the United States, the Anesthesia Quality Institute's National Anesthesia Clinical Outcomes Registry (NACOR) and the Multicenter Perioperative Outcomes Group (MPOG) illustrate the potential of large, routinely collected datasets to drive continuous benchmarking and collaborative studies.19,20 Similarly, the long-standing German national anaesthesia quality project underscores the importance of a standardised minimal dataset, robust validation processes, and regular feedback to sustain participation, while the UK's Peri-operative Quality Improvement Programme (PQIP) integrates process, outcome, and patient-reported measures into a national feedback loop.15,21 Compared with these initiatives, A-QUA combines a broad case mix, a standardised core dataset and centralised governance within the Swiss Society for Anaesthesiology and Peri-operative Medicine (SSAPM). Its integration with the SSAPM's accreditation programme (A-CERT) provides a direct link between structural, process and outcome indicators, consistent with the Donabedian model of healthcare quality.11,12

Comparison with our 2019 national survey confirms stability in several core characteristics of Swiss anaesthesia practice.7 Our study estimated 1 071 054 anaesthetics annually (12 445 per 100 000 inhabitants), with 67% general anaesthesia, 20% regional anaesthesia and 12% MAC, with a mean anaesthesia duration of 2.03 h. The present registry data confirm the predominance of general anaesthesia but suggest a modest increase in the relative use of regional techniques, especially in the French and Italian speaking regions. Anaesthesia and surgical times remain comparable, and participant distribution is similar, although the registry captures a more granular spread of caseloads across smaller institutions and confirms regional variations in practice style (Table 1). This continuity reinforces the robustness of Swiss anaesthesia service structures, while modest shifts in practice patterns highlight the value of transitioning from periodic surveys to continuous, validated registry reporting for high-resolution monitoring, benchmarking, and iterative quality improvement.

Data quality is a critical pillar of any registry. A-QUA's detailed codebook defines mandatory variables, acceptable ranges and logical relationships for all data imports.14 In this study, we extended these routine import checks by applying 22 targeted plausibility rules to evaluate the database's internal consistency and validity in greater depth (Appendix A). This comprehensive assessment revealed preventable issues such as incorrect procedure codes, implausible anthropometrics and incomplete postanaesthesia care documentation that were not fully detected by existing import checks. In addition, the analysis demonstrated an uneven distribution of errors across providers, with a small number of centres accounting for a disproportionate share. This finding underscores the importance of targeted quality improvement and enables corrective actions to be directed toward specific participants and error categories. Most of the identified issues were readily preventable through stricter input validation and automated cross-checks, and these measures will be integrated into the registry's data management process. The insights gained will guide the ongoing revision of the codebook, the implementation of enhanced automated validation prior to data acceptance, and the introduction of structured, targeted feedback mechanisms for participants. Recurrent error patterns will also be addressed through peer-review processes, building on approaches successfully used in other national registries (German, Swedish and Nordic initiatives), where iterative data standard refinement and continuous feedback have led to measurable improvements in completeness and accuracy.6,15,22 These steps will further strengthen A-QUA's role as a reliable, high-quality data foundation for benchmarking, research and peri-operative quality improvement in Switzerland.

The descriptive analyses and case studies illustrate how A-QUA can benchmark procedure durations, intra-operative and postoperative event rates, anaesthesia techniques, and postoperative care pathways (Fig. 5, Appendix C and D). Differences in anaesthesia-to-surgery time ratios, for example, may indicate workflow variation or preparation inefficiencies that could be optimised. Similarly, regional differences in the choice between general anaesthesia and regional anaesthesia for standard procedures may prompt review against best practice guidelines. International experience shows that such benchmarking can inform targeted quality improvement projects and accelerate guideline adoption.21,23

Implementing A-QUA in Switzerland's multilingual and decentralised healthcare system demonstrated that national data harmonisation is achievable through a centrally co-ordinated yet flexible structure. Key success factors included the use of standardised and validated terminology, centrally managed translations and strong stakeholder engagement across regions and hospital types. Continuous feedback loops and peer review foster trust and participation, while maintaining local autonomy. These experiences highlight that even in complex healthcare environments, effective governance and transparent communication can ensure consistent data quality and sustained collaboration.

Beyond its role in quality assurance, A-QUA offers substantial research potential. Its breadth enables risk-adjusted outcome analyses, monitoring of rare adverse events, and trend assessment over time. Linkage to complementary datasets, such as the planned Swiss Outcome after Anaesthesia and Surgery (SOCAS) study, will facilitate postdischarge follow-up and long-term outcome research. On a strategic level, A-QUA data can support workforce planning, peri-operative service allocation and the development of national patient safety strategies, roles already demonstrated by other mature peri-operative registries.4,6,22

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Participation in A-QUA is high among teaching hospitals but remains limited among non-teaching and private providers and is currently absent among office-based providers, which may introduce selection bias and limit the representative nature of Swiss anaesthesia practice. Hospitals with more advanced digital infrastructures are therefore overrepresented. In addition, data entry processes differ across participants, which may affect data completeness and timeliness despite continuous validation efforts. Although variable definitions are standardised, local interpretation may vary, particularly within Switzerland's multilingual and decentralised healthcare system, posing an ongoing challenge to full harmonisation.

At present, A-QUA captures only intra-operative and early postoperative (≤24 h) events, and event reporting and severity classification are optional, limiting the granularity of outcome assessment and precluding the evaluation of longer-term outcomes such as in-hospital or 30-day mortality. Furthermore, given the scale of the dataset, the current analysis was restricted to descriptive assessments of participation, process indicators and data quality; more advanced benchmarking and risk modelling will require further national standardisation and will be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

A-QUA has evolved into a robust, nationwide platform for capturing high-quality anaesthesia data across Switzerland. Its comprehensive coverage, validated dataset and capacity for procedure-specific benchmarking, position it as a central tool for monitoring practice patterns, guiding quality improvement and supporting research. Beyond data collection, A-QUA directly contributes to patient safety by enabling the identification of practice variation and system-level errors that can be addressed through targeted interventions, automated feedback, and structured peer review within the A-CERT framework. By transitioning from cross-sectional surveys to continuous registry reporting, Switzerland now has the infrastructure to track trends, identify variation and implement evidence-based improvements in peri-operative care on a national scale. Remaining challenges include minimising potential selection bias, given the voluntary nature of centre participation, and further harmonising definitions across Switzerland's multilingual and decentralised healthcare landscape. Ongoing efforts to refine data standards, expand participation, and strengthen interoperability will ensure that A-QUA continues to evolve as a cornerstone of quality assurance, patient safety and peri-operative research.
