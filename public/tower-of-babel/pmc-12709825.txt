The Mediational Intervention for Sensitizing Caregivers: an evaluation of feasibility and acceptability among mothers with borderline personality disorder
Authors: Kiana Cano, Nabeeha Asim, Madeleine Allman, Carla Sharp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtant data suggest that children of mothers with borderline personality disorder (BPD) are at risk for a variety of poor psychosocial outcomes, including psychopathology. One avenue for interrupting intergenerational transmission of psychopathology is through scaffolding parenting. The Mediational Intervention for Sensitizing Caregivers (MISC) has been used with other high-risk groups and holds promise for this population. The objective of the current study was to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of MISC for mothers with BPD, and to identify key modifications for future adaptation of MISC for this population.MethodsThis objective was accomplished via three aims following a best-practice adaptation model for evidence-based psychosocial interventions. Aim 1 consisted of quantitative assessment (phase 1) of need for parenting support with n = 88 mothers with BPD and n = 195 without, and qualitative assessment (phase 2) of the lived experience of parenting among 15 mothers with BPD. Aim 2 consisted of integration of mixed methods results from Aim 1, and the decision to proceed with adaptation of MISC for mothers with BPD. Aim 3 consisted of focus groups and theatre testing of MISC, with n = 14 mothers with BPD.ResultsAim 1 findings showed significant group differences on quantitative measures between mothers with and without BPD. Qualitative themes provided information about perceived need and desire for parenting support from the patient perspective. Aim 2 integration provided preliminary support for the feasibility and utility of MISC for mothers with BPD. Results of Aim 3 focus groups identified qualitative evidence in support of the acceptability of MISC for mothers with BPD. Additionally, participant feedback identified feasible and actionable modifications to MISC that can be implemented in future work with this population.ConclusionsThe current study suggests that MISC is a potentially feasible and acceptable alternative for mothers with BPD who are interested in receiving professional parenting support services.Keywords: Borderline personality disorder, Parenting, Intervention, Intergenerational transmission, Mixed methods, Qualitative methods

Background

Extant data suggests that children of mothers with borderline personality disorder (BPD) are at particular risk for a variety of poor psychosocial outcomes [1–3] even compared to those whose parents have other personality disorders [4]. Consistent with etiological theories of BPD [5] these outcomes appear to be the result of a complex interaction between biological factors and environmental factors, including parenting behavior [6]. Specifically, the parenting behavior of mothers with BPD is often perceived as insensitive [7, 8] intrusive [9] less emotionally warm and empathetic [10, 11] and even hostile [12–15]. In most cases this behavior and negative emotionality is not intentional nor malicious. Rather, it is a manifestation of the parents struggle with symptoms of psychopathology, parenting without a strong support system, high psychosocial stress, and low feelings of self-efficacy related to parenting [16, 17]. Nonetheless, it ultimately does not match the needs of the child and can significantly contribute to the development of psychopathology and poor psychosocial outcomes.

However, parental behavior is a highly modifiable aspect of the parent-child relationship and may be the key to preventing intergenerational transmission of psychopathology within high-risk families. Existing interventions such as Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) have been evaluated among mothers with BPD to examine the interventions effects on parenting behavior, and subsequent child outcomes, indirectly via the remediation of parental psychopathology (e.g., Martin, et al. [18]). However, parenting interventions that directly target the parenting practices of patients with mental illness may be even more effective at improving child outcomes [19, 20]. As such, a number of parenting interventions have been created or adapted for use with mothers with BPD [21, 22]. These include Mother-Infant DBT [23, 24] Parenting Skills for Mothers with Borderline Personality Disorder [25] The Helping Families Programme [26] and the Project Air parenting intervention [27]. Research evaluating the efficacy and effectiveness of these diverse interventions for mothers with BPD is ongoing [28–31].

An exhaustive discussion of the strengths and limitations of existing parenting intervention options for mothers with BPD is beyond the scope of this paper. However, one general limitation of note is that they are typically brief (approximately 3 to 16 sessions). Parenting behaviors that are relevant to the parent-child attachment relationship are often deeply embedded, as they are rooted in the parent’s own attachment schema. This embedding means they are often difficult to change and may not be modifiable in the short term, or changes may not be long lasting if effects are superficial. Secondly, some parenting interventions available to mothers with BPD prioritize skills and psychoeducation, with content focused on topics such as safety planning, self-care, enhancing social support, and child development information. However, mothers with BPD do not appear to demonstrate a lack of knowledge about sensitive parenting behavior compared to healthy mothers or those with depression [32] although they may struggle to implement best practices. Beyond psychoeducation and skills training, cognitive-behavioral oriented interventions also extend principles of cognitive restructuring to the parenting context and provide behavioral strategies for managing children’s emotions. Although indeed important and effective at addressing some characteristics of the parents psychopathology or parenting behavior, the intervention strategies described above do not directly address the specific attachment-based interaction patterns known to characterize parent-child relationships of mothers with BPD (e.g., lack of warmth, hostility, lack of synchrony), nor do they clarify the moment-by-moment behaviors that mothers engage in and how they may behave differently. Rather than lack of knowledge or a general parenting skills deficit, poor maternal reflective function or mentalizing – the inability to consider the child’s thoughts, emotions, and intentions [33–35] and to then act helpfully in response [36] – may be driving the unhelpful parenting behavior of mothers with BPD. Thus, psychoeducational, skills-based interventions run the risk of over-reliance on what is called the teleological mode in mentalization-based treatment, where “quick-fix” behavioral approaches are expected to improve the relationship between parents and children. Such approaches may be effective in the short term but are unlikely to change implicit (unconscious) interaction patterns [37]. However, making implicit interaction patterns explicit is challenging. We identified a need for an intervention that implements concrete behavioral strategies and can bring interaction patterns to light so that more authentic curiosity and warmth – in short, a mentalizing stance [38] – can be developed.

It follows that an attachment-based orientation to intervention, which can remediate mentalizing, is critical to understanding and treating parents with BPD. Many attachment-based parenting interventions currently exist, albeit with varying degrees of support for their efficacy and effectiveness [39–41]. Further, to our knowledge, only one, the Video-Feedback Intervention to Promote Positive Parenting and Sensitive Discipline (VIPP), has been explicitly evaluated among with mothers with personality disorders [42, 43]. Mentalization-based parenting interventions, specifically, have similar variability in their evidence base [44] with few having been evaluated specifically for use with mothers with BPD [45].

Attachment- and mentalization-based parenting interventions have their own unique weaknesses in the psychotherapy landscape. Some are brief in nature, like a skills-based intervention. Others emphasize psychodynamic therapeutic strategies, such as reflecting on intergenerational experiences and the caregiver’s own attachment relationship. This form of reflection, especially early in therapy, can be contraindicated for patients with BPD. In addition, attachment-based interventions focus on the emotional, or affective expression, components of sensitive and responsive caregiving. However, to fully interrupt the intergenerational transmission of psychopathology from mothers to children, it is also important that children consider the mother as a trustworthy source of learning [37, 46]. When there is epistemic trust between a caregiver and a child, the epistemic (i.e. knowledge/learning) highway opens and the child can use the mother as a source of learning in navigating socio-emotional (and other) challenges. To this end, the emotional components of sensitive and responsive caregiving, targeted in attachment-based interventions, must be supplemented with learning (cognitive components [37, 47]).

The current study

Against this background, there is a critical need for development and evaluation of tailored interventions for mothers with BPD, including an empirically supported, manualized, attachment-based parenting intervention that focuses on the “here and now” and provides concrete guidance regarding the steps necessary to achieve a mentalizing stance and epistemic trust in interactions with their children. One such intervention is the Mediational Intervention for Sensitizing Caregivers (MISC). The objective of the current study was to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of MISC, described in detail in the next section, for mothers with BPD.

To appropriately implement any intervention for use with a population in which it was not originally developed, best practices require the use of an adaptation model such as ADAPT-ITT [48]. The current study utilized a convergent mixed-methods design [49] with simultaneous quantitative and qualitative data collection, consisting of four aims to accomplish the first three steps of ADAPT-ITT (Fig. 1).

Methods

Information about the Mediational Intervention for Sensitizing Caregivers (MISC)

The MISC is a semi-structured, manualized program that aims to support parents in helping children reach their cognitive and socio-emotional developmental goals. Dr. Pnina Klein [50, 51] originally developed the program in communities that have faced significant adversity and have a high likelihood of attachment disruption [47]. The theoretical foundation of MISC is grounded in Bowlby’s attachment theory [52–54] and Feuerstein’s theory of Cognitive Modifiability and Mediated Learning Experience [55, 56]. The overall premise of MISC is that emotional development takes place when a caregiver can “slow down” an interaction to facilitate reflection on a child’s needs, interests, and abilities, and in turn increase a child’s own reflective (mentalizing) capacity.

Figure 2 depicts the conceptual model of MISC. The foundation of MISC is based in secure attachment characterized by reciprocity, mutual attention, and engagement between the mother and child. This foundation is established behaviorally through physical touch, turn taking, closeness, eye contact, smiles, positive vocalizations, and sharing of joy (operationalized as the “emotional components” of MISC). These behaviors communicate basic messages to the child (e.g., I love you). However, as Klein [47] points out, attachment (emotional) components alone are not enough for optimal cognitive and socio-emotional development. While it opens the door for learning, it does not determine what passes through it. Therefore, to enhance learning, empirically defined caregiving behaviors operationalized as the “cognitive components” of MISC [47, 57] are essential. These include focusing, affecting, expanding, rewarding, and regulating. Together, the behavioral communication of love between mother and child and the mother’s mediation of the child’s subjective experience fulfills the child’s emotional and cognitive needs, resulting in positive social, emotional, and cognitive outcomes for the child (represented by the leaves of the tree in Fig. 2).

Conceptual model for the Mediational Intervention for Sensitizing Caregivers (MISC)

MISC relies on an implicit learning approach [37, 58]. These concepts, which result in behavior change and create a new, more responsive caregiving style, have been shown to result in positive psychosocial and cognitive outcomes for children [59–62]. Specifically, better mental health [59] receptive and expressive language [60] and memory [61] when compared to controls who did not participate in MISC. MISC has also been shown to increase mentalizing capacity in children [62]. Consistent with a slower-paced, implicit learning approach, MISC concepts are learnt over the course of one year using video feedback and everyday activities to break down parent-child interactions to a granular level. Specifically, one year of MISC is organized into three “modes”. In bi-weekly sessions during the first mode, the caregiver and MISC trainer review video-taped interactions between the parent and child and reflect on the parents’ thoughts and feelings that occurred during the interaction, using the MISC framework of emotional and cognitive components. The MISC trainer initially focuses on strengths only, but as trust and rapport are established between the MISC trainer and parent, the trainer begins identifying parts of the interaction where a parent could implement a MISC component, for the child’s benefit, and uses curiosity and reflective questions to encourage the parent to identify such opportunities for themselves. In the second mode of MISC, similar “learning moments” are identified so that the MISC components can be implemented. However, this feedback occurs during a real-time interaction between the parent and child, not via video tape. The third and final mode of MISC consists of group meetings where parents can reflect on everyday experiences using the MISC model with their peers.

Aim 1-phase 1 (Step 1 of ADAPT-ITT)

Participants and procedures

For Aim 1-phase 1, mothers were recruited from various sources, including non-profit BPD organizations, Reddit, and the ResearchMatch.org platform. Inclusion criteria required that participants were English-speaking, over the age of 18, and the parent of at least 1 child under the age of 18. All eligible participants completed questionnaire-based assessments about parenting and mental health online using Qualtrics. BPD group status was determined by self-report (yes or no) to the question “have you been diagnosed with BPD?” Participants were instructed to answer parenting measures with only one child in mind. At the end of the survey, participants had the opportunity to enter a raffle to win one of fifty $20 Amazon gift cards. Participants with BPD also indicated whether they consented to be contacted for participation in phase 2 of the study. Following standard approaches to ensuring data quality on platforms like ResearchMatch.org [63] participants were excluded from analysis if their response was identified as a duplicate (n = 19), they answered 3 or more check questions incorrectly (n = 23), or they completed the survey too quickly (n = 9).

After application of all inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final study sample consisted of N = 283 participants, specifically n = 88 mothers with BPD and n = 195 mothers without BPD. Participants were 36 years old on average (range: 20–60; SD = 7.50), mostly white (n = 228; 81%), and non-Hispanic/Latino (n = 256; 90%; see Table 1). On average, participants had 2 children (range: 1–11) and reported they first became parents at age 26 (range: 13–45). Participants diagnosed with BPD reported their symptoms onset at age 16 on average (range: 7–39; SD = 5.38), but the average age of formal diagnosis was 26 (range: 11–45; SD = 7.71).

Measures

Self-report measures assessed parenting-related constructs proposed to be potential mechanisms of intergenerational transmission of psychopathology, and subsequent parenting intervention treatment targets, in parent-child dyads where the mother has BPD [1, 6]. Specifically, we assessed parenting style, disorganized attachment, emotional responsiveness, and reflective function. We chose to assess a range of parenting constructs, that perhaps relate to different theoretical perspectives on the development of BPD, given the current paucity of research among mothers with BPD. To gain additional insight into participants’ parenting context, and to replicate and extend findings of previous research [16, 17] participants were also asked to respond to a measure of parenting self-efficacy, and life stress.

As part of the study demographics questionnaire, participants were asked to provide the following information about each of their children: age, gender, and relationship to the participant (i.e. biological, adoptive, or stepchild). Just prior to responding to the parenting questionnaires, participants were instructed to answer the following items only as they relate to their “most difficult child under the age of 18.” At this point in the survey, participants were also asked to provide that child’s age for later cross-referencing with the demographic questionnaire. Participants responded to the surveys by reporting on children ranging in age from 2 months to 21 years (mean age was approximately 8.5 years old), and with an even gender split (n = 137 female; n = 138 male). Two children were reported to be members of a gender minority, and 6 were missing gender data. Most children who were the focus of survey responses were the participants biological children (n = 245; 86.6%). The remaining were reported to be adoptive children (n = 6; 2.1%), stepchildren (n = 9; 3.2%), or had no data available (n = 23; 8.1%). Participants mostly reported on only children (n = 109; 38.5%), followed by first-borns (n = 78; 27.6%), second-borns (n = 58; 20.5%), third-borns (n = 21; 7.4%) or later (n = 14; 5.1%).

Alabama Parenting Questionnaire – short form (APQ-SF [64]). The APQ-SF is a 9-item self-report measure of parenting styles and behaviors. Participants rate each item (e.g., you praise your child if (s)he behaves well) on a 5-point scale ranging from “never” to “always.” In the current study, internal consistency of the measure subscales was acceptable (inconsistent discipline α = 0.77; poor supervision α = 0.76) and good (positive parenting α = 0.85).

Caregiving Helplessness Questionnaire (CHQ [65]). The CHQ is a 26-item self-report measure of parent-child attachment. Each item (e.g., when I am with my child, I often feel out of control) is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from “not characteristic at all” to “very characteristic.” The CHQ results in three subscales indicative of disorganized or dysregulated attachment behaviors: mother helpless, mother and child frightened, and role reversal. In the current study, the helpless subscale exhibited good internal consistency(α = 0.87)1, however the frightened and role reversal subscales did not (α = 0.66 and 0.68, respectively).

Coping with Children’s Negative Emotions Scale (CCNES [66]). The CCNES is a self-report measure that assesses parental responses to children’s negative emotions. The measure consists of 12 hypothetical scenarios (e.g., if my child falls off his/her bike and breaks it, and then gets upset and cries, I would…) depicting children experiencing common negative emotions, such as sadness or fear, and parents are asked to rate how likely it is that they would respond in 6 different ways (e.g., tell child they are over-reacting, validate emotions) on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “very unlikely” to “very likely.” Scoring of the CCNES results in 6 subscale scores. In the current study, internal consistency of the subscales ranged from good to excellent (α = 0.78 − 0.91).

Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire – adolescent version (PRFQ-A [67]). The PRFQ-A is an 18-item self-report measure of capacity for reflective functioning in the context of parenting. The PRFQ-A was chosen for the current study, instead of the PRFQ, as items are nearly identical and are generalizable to all youth. Participants rate each item (e.g., I can completely read my child’s mind) on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “completely disagree” to “completely agree.” The PRFQ-A results in three subscales: pre-mentalizing or non-mentalizing, certainty about mental states, and interest and curiosity. Internal consistency of each subscale in the current study was acceptable (α = 0.75 − 0.77).

Parenting Sense of Competence scale (PSOC [68]).The PSOC is a 16-item self-report measure of parental self-efficacy and satisfaction. Each item (e.g., being a parent is manageable, and any problems are easily solved) is rated on a 6-point scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. Higher total scores suggest greater feelings of efficacy and satisfaction. In the current study, the PSOC demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.86).

Difficult Life Circumstances scale (DLC [69]). The DLC is a 38-item self-report questionnaire designed to evaluate the presence of a variety of chronic life stressors or problems. Positive responses (yes) to stressor questions (e.g., do you have a hard time paying bills?) and negative responses (no) to resource questions (e.g., do you have access to a phone?) are summed to obtain a total score, with higher scores reflecting a greater number of chronic life stressors.

Data analytic strategy

Preliminary analyses were conducted to examine outliers, normative distribution, and missing data for each variable. Chi-square analyses (for categorical or dichotomous outcomes) and independent sample t-tests (for continuous outcomes) were conducted to compare mothers with BPD and mothers without BPD on all quantitative, self-report variables.

Aim 1-phase 2 (Step 1 of ADAPT-ITT)

Participants and procedures

Aim 1-phase 2 included a subset sample of N = 15 mothers with BPD who participated in phase 1. Participants who consented to continued participation completed a video-taped, semi-structured, qualitative interview of their parenting experience. All participants were provided with a $30 Amazon gift card. The sample size for aim 1-phase was determined by considering best practice recommendations for qualitative data analysis. Although sample sizes can vary widely across qualitative research, specific guidelines regarding interview-based research, like the current project, suggest that aims can be accomplished with a sample size of less than twenty [70]. For the current study, we also considered recommendations for phenomenological techniques, specifically, and the concept of saturation, or the point at which more cases in a qualitative sample do not contribute any additional or unique information to the research question. Empirical studies suggest that thematic code saturation can be reached with just 9 interviews but provide additional parameters for estimating an ideal sample size for a given project [71]. Based on these parameters (e.g., homogeneity of the sample population given criterion sampling, and utilization of a nested iterative sample), a sample size of N = 15 was determined to be adequate.

Measure

The aim 1-phase 2 qualitative interview was guided by a phenomenological approach. Phenomenology aims to describe the common meaning individuals attribute to their lived experience of a phenomenon [70]. This approach was chosen for the current study to “better understand the target population” (step 1 of ADAPT-ITT) by (1) capturing participant descriptions of phenomenon that researchers have documented quantitatively, and (2) improve researcher understanding of mother’s perceived need for support.

Data analytic strategy

Qualitative analysis of transcribed interviews was consistent with best-practice phenomenological techniques [70]. In depth details about qualitative procedures and analysis and results are beyond the scope of this manuscript but have been described elsewhere [72]. Generally, however, phenomenological analysis results in “meaning clusters,” or themes, that are apparent across all transcripts.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Qualitative research methods necessarily emphasize “the role of the researcher” in all aspects of study conception, design, and data analysis. Therefore, researcher interpretation of data will inherently be influenced by prior experience and knowledge. However, best-practice methodological guidelines for qualitative research also recommend that researchers engage in reflexivity, or examination of their own experiences, beliefs, and judgements about a topic to ensure that they do not unduly influence qualitative findings and to enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the results. In line with the epistemology of a phenomenological approach, researchers must acknowledge, and then “bracket off” any subjective experiences prior to data collection and analyses. In the current study, we aimed to ensure validity of qualitative results by engaging in reflexivity throughout data collection, and during data analysis and write-up. Researcher positionality statements for authors K.C. and M.A. are available elsewhere [72].

Aim 2 (Step 2 of ADAPT-ITT)

Procedures and data analytic strategy

Aim 2 was to decide whether the MISC is appropriate for adoption or adaptation for mothers with BPD and therefore did not involve study participants. Specifically, author K.C. conducted preliminary analyses of aim 1-phase 1 data (t-tests and chi-square tests on partial data), and author K.C. and M.A. engaged in memoing of aim 1-phase 2 qualitative interviews, during data collection. Memoing refers to “any writing…in relationship to the research other than actual field notes, transcription, or coding” [73] (p33). In practice, memoing can occur at any time during the research process. The purpose of memoing is to facilitate the researcher’s reflection and insight and to increase their understanding of their participants and research questions. During data collection, memos may reflect a researcher’s personal reaction to observations, interviews, documents, etc., or make note of emerging themes. These preliminary results and memos were integrated using best practices in mixed-methods research to inform the decision to adapt MISC for mothers with BPD.

Aim 3 (Step 3 of ADAPT-ITT)

Participants and procedures

Aim 3 was to “better understand how to adapt the intervention and determine refinements related to content, delivery style and materials that may need to be made” [48] and included N = 14 mothers with BPD who participated in aim 1. Specifically, n = 10 individuals had participated in both Aim 1-phase 1 (surveys) and Aim 1-phase 2 (interviews), and n = 4 had only participated in phase 1. In groups of 3–5 participants, mothers participated in video-recorded, open-ended focus groups where authors K.C. and N.A. elicited group feedback pertaining to preliminary study results and the content, delivery methods, goals, and materials of MISC. Upon completion of the study appointment, participants were provided with a $30 Amazon gift card and recordings were transcribed by undergraduate research assistants.

Data analysis and rigor

Thematic content analysis [74] was used to analyze the recorded focus-group sessions and participant feedback was summarized and described. Consistent with best practices in qualitative research, in addition to being a novel source of qualitative data, member checking during Aim 3 focus groups also functioned as a source of validation of Aim 1-phase 2 qualitative findings. At the beginning of each focus group, feedback from participants included reflection on how well emerging themes from Aim 1-phase 2 represented their experiences.

Aim 1-phase 2

In all, 7 themes emerged from the phenomenological analysis of the qualitative parenting interviews with mothers with BPD. Three themes illustrated the bidirectional relationship between BPD symptoms and parenting. Specifically, (1) the impact of overwhelming emotions while parenting, (2) profound difficulties with mentalizing while parenting, and (3) struggling with attachment needs while parenting. The fourth theme highlighted mothers’ heightened awareness of their family’s risk for intergenerational transmission. Themes five and six emphasized the importance of mental health care in supporting effective parenting for participants, and the substantial difficulty accessing and maintaining that care. Lastly, a theme that resonated strongly across many interviews was the experience that becoming a parent was a motivator to get, and stay, well. More detailed descriptions of the qualitative analysis and results of Aim 1-phase 2 have been described elsewhere [72].

In the context of MISC, qualitative findings are consistent with the MISC belief that most parents, regardless of circumstance and readily apparent ability, have a similar “deep objective” when it comes to parenting. That is, a deep desire to have the best for their children and, therefore, concern with being as good a mom as possible. Additionally, themes that emerged in aim 1-phase 2 suggested mothers with BPD are often actively participating in and benefitting from mental health services. Examples from participants’ lived experience with health care services suggested that MISC could be a good fit for this population. For example, regarding therapy, one mom shared that “it’s really easy to read a [parenting] book and be like, ‘Yeah, that makes sense to me’ and it’s totally different to actually be in the middle of a situation and be like, ‘let me remember what that person said I should do in these circumstances’” (Participant 83). This quote may highlight the benefit of the implicit learning model used by MISC through recorded and in-vivo feedback provided by MISC trainers. Additionally, the foundation of MISC, and mentalization-based therapies, is epistemic trust – trust that information provided by a “source” is reliable and in one’s best interest. Providers establish epistemic trust with their clients throughout therapy, modeling how patients may trust others and have others trust them in their personal lives. Epistemic trust in providers, particularly for mothers with BPD seeking support for parenting, is particularly important as they may struggle with trusting themselves:

“I don’t know if I trust myself. I don’t trust my gut. I don’t know if I trust my thoughts to come up with things by myself [in the context of parenting]. So yeah, I really defer to professionals.” (Participant 140).

The slow pace of MISC, and the importance MISC places on secure attachment and trust within close relationships, suggests the intervention would be a good fit for mothers with BPD.

Further, although individual therapy can provide critical emotion regulation and interpersonal skills, which mothers themselves report as beneficial to themselves and their parenting, personality disorders are long-term enduring patterns of behavior that can take a significant amount of time to change. Some mothers expressed hopelessness about this fact, and the potential impact their symptoms would have on their children over time.

“When I think about [my BPD symptoms] sometimes, I almost have a sense of hopelessness. Like, this is the way that I’ve experienced emotions for as long as I can remember. This is the way that I’ve experienced relationships as far as I can remember, and like, can I train myself out of this, one. And two, if I can’t, am I doomed to have a poor relationship with my kid?” (Participant 83)

As a parenting program, the MISC approach focuses solely on parent-child interactions. In theory, regardless of parental background, knowledge, or existing emotional or interpersonal skills, MISC can teach parents concrete ways to interact with their children in a way that is beneficial and simple. Because the MISC approach is slow-paced, a year-long, and strengths-based, mothers with BPD may benefit from the extended time to build trust with providers, develop feelings of competency and hope in their abilities, and make long-lasting change to interaction patterns with their children.

Aim 3

MISC fundamentals resonated with participants

In general, participant reactions and open-ended commentary after the MISC presentation (“theatre testing”) suggested that mothers with BPD understood and valued guiding principles of MISC. For example, regarding the mediational (cognitive) components, one participant elaborated on the utility of the focusing component for overcoming difficulties with impulsivity and hypomentalizing:

“I think a lot of the skills you talked about would definitely be helpful…especially with somebody that’s got BPD. Like one of the things you mentioned earlier about kind of prepping the child to grab their attention towards something…I don’t think to prep. I will think something, and I’ll just say it. Sometimes I catch myself that way with my daughter…[gives an example of beginning a conversation with no context or focusing for the child]”.

Participant feedback also spoke to the concept of “attachment is not enough” when discussing how useful the intervention would be. For example, one mom expressed her opinion that the “foundational stuff” (i.e., the emotional components) were “mostly intuitive for people,” suggesting that, at least for this mother with BPD, a parenting program only focused on the emotional components of parent-child interactions would miss the mark. Another mom elaborated on this idea and highlighted how the mediational components of MISC may be a unique strength of the program:

“I feel like the beginning, like the roots or whatever of MISC, um that I felt like I did. A lot of prenatal education covered a lot of that stuff. But the sort of like building the trunk…how you build off of that emotional basis to help your kid achieve um the outcomes that are desired, that was just where I was like, “okay what do I do now”…like I think I am doing the basic stuff but I really don’t know how to parlay [meaning translate/act on] that into affecting or helping my kid learn how to regulate or any of that stuff. So I feel that like um this program could fill a need there”.

The above example also shows that the tree metaphor of the MISC resonated with participants and provides a memorable metaphor for internalizing the MISC components (as intended by its developers). Participants also reported they felt MISC would be appropriate across development and mothers highlighted different developmental periods at which they thought MISC would be most helpful, namely toddlerhood and adolescence.

MISC weakness and recommendations

By and large, the major concern highlighted by participants as a weakness of MISC was the video-recording component. Some moms felt that recording “neutral interactions” would be easy – describing how they often recorded interactions, like any other mom, to share on social media or with friends and family. Others felt that they would certainly be willing to record themselves, however they felt the authenticity of the interactions would be reduced and they may inauthentically present as the “perfect mom” and defeat the purpose of the recording. Finally, others felt that recording interactions to share would be difficult to undertake due to self-judgement and negative emotions:

“I have mom guilt really bad and to be able to have the strength to come forward and be like “I am having an issue with this” is really hard for me. And then to have the strength enough to record an interaction of me and my child having this active problem? I don’t know about anyone else, but for me that would take a lot of strength”.

Of note, however, participants often engaged one another in lively conversations about this topic. There were many participants who dissented with this viewpoint, and reported no concerns, viewing in vivo and video recorded feedback as “opportunities” to have another individual help them enhance their self-awareness and “catch” teachable moments. Others proposed suggestions for how to mitigate the valid concerns of video recording. Suggestions included emphasizing that peer support was available for moms who might struggle with guilt or shame, and ensuring the camera is discreet when recording. Several moms also highlighted that, although initially intimidating, recording interactions would be easier if the patient felt as though they trusted their provider and their providers intentions:

“As I am looking at the MISC stuff, I am like well if you had a relationship with somebody where you trust them,they are not judgmental with you, and their goal is to help you with your kid…maybe having that interaction in front of them or having that video is a little easier. Like [the video recording is] intimidating in a theoretical sense”.

The second most common piece of negative feedback given about MISC was the program’s length. Participants anticipated experiencing difficulties with treatment attendance and fidelity due to variable motivation associated with mental health symptoms, and due to life stressors. Again, however, not all moms expressed this concern. Others asked what services were offered as part of MISC beyond one year, stating that due to changes in family situations, child development, or parental mental health, they believed new challenges would arise and they would want additional support at that time.

It’s not what you say, it’s how you say it

While giving feedback regarding many different components of MISC, participants emphasized the importance of delivering MISC in an effective way for this population. Participants suggested that delivery of the intervention needed to be non-judgmental, genuine, and “extra supportive” to overcome difficulties with trust, waning motivation throughout treatment, and emotional reactivity. When prompted to describe what would help ensure a therapeutic relationship felt this way, participants expressed a desire to go slow, avoid jargon when at all possible, emphasize peer support, have a provider with patience to build trust over time, and to at least begin the program in person as opposed to virtually.

Practical modifications or considerations

Some participants believed that virtual sessions could interfere with establishing therapeutic trust and rapport. However, others viewed telehealth options for MISC as a sustainable option for ensuring that patients could overcome barriers to care, maintain consistency, and be able to realistically participate in a support group consisting of similar “types” of moms even if they lived in a remote area. Regardless of modality, participants noted that for moms with BPD it would be best if the peer support groups consisted only of other moms with BPD, or related disorders/life circumstances (e.g., substance use, intimate partner violence), as opposed to having mothers with more diverse presentations (e.g., “just” anxiety, schizophrenia). If being completed in person, participants noted that the environment should be physically comfortable and conducive to having natural, authentic interactions both with the provider and the child (if present), citing previous experiences with mental health offices that felt “clinical,” or contributed to the power differential between the participant and provider.

MISC is an inherently low-resource treatment modality that does not require many materials. However, focus group participants recommended offering “visual representations” of MISC content in the form of example videos and images, like the MISC tree, to improve learning. Others also expressed concern that engaging in MISC would be difficult without emotion regulation skills being taught in tandem. Possible suggestions to remedy this included having simplified emotion regulation skills handouts to review or model during session at the outset of treatment.

Finally, many moms with BPD who participated in the current study reported having neurodiverse children. Although MISC is generalizable and transportable across contexts, cultures, etc., moms did highlight the need for MISC trainers working with this population to be aware of the differences in implementing MISC with children with autism and ADHD. For example, regarding the emotional components, one mom noted that eye contact, touch, and reciprocity are inherently different for her child with autism and should be considered when providing feedback and generating ideas for future interactions during MISC sessions.

Discussion

The current study utilized a mixed methods approach to explore the feasibility and acceptability of MISC for mothers with BPD. Results of aim 1 suggest there is both an objective, quantitative need for parenting support, and a deep concern about parenting and subjective desire for support from the patient perspective. Focus group feedback from aim 3 suggests that MISC would be an acceptable and feasible program to adapt for mothers with BPD. Participants resonated with foundational MISC premises, made reasonable recommendations for modifications, and negative feedback about the program was measured and actionable.

In line with prior studies, participants with and without BPD demonstrated significant differences on measures of parenting behaviors and constructs that are risk factors for intergenerational transmission of psychopathology and targets of treatment in parenting interventions. During aim 1-phase 1 interviews, and member-checking in aim 3 focus groups, participants confirmed and elaborated upon these results, giving credence to quantitative findings using examples from lived experience (Table 3). Of note, there were a few non-significant findings in aim 1-phase 1 that contrasted with expectations. Specifically, participants with BPD demonstrated less excessive certainty on the parental measure of reflective function (PRFQ-A). While the authors of the PRFQ [67] and the RFQ [75] from which the PRFQ derives, have suggested a linked between certainty and hypermentalizing in these measures, data is beginning to emerge that suggest the certainty subscales of the PRFQ and RFQ do not measure hypermentalizing [76]. Consequently, we are not sure how to interpret this non-intuitive finding. Our results concerning hypomentalization are consistent with the findings of other recent studies using the PRFQ in this population [77] thus we are more confident in interpreting the tendency of mothers in the current sample to hypomentalize, and reserve conclusions regarding hypermentalizing for when the certainty subscales of the PRFQ and RFQ are better understood. Participant feedback on these findings during aim 3 suggested that, from the patient perspective, individual differences or level of insight may be at play. Moms with BPD described experiences with both hyper- and hypomentalizing (Table 3), consistent with clinical formulations and observations by providers in the therapy context. This makes sense, given that all individuals have the capacity to, and indeed do, hyper-, hypo- and accurately mentalize across time, with psychiatric populations simply tending to exhibit impairment with greater frequency. Ultimately, it is beyond the scope of the current study to determine whether hypermentalizing or hypomentalizing is unique to BPD in mothers, however future studies may endeavor to examine how individual differences and level of insight explain variations in self-reported mentalizing ability.

The remaining non-significant findings in aim 1-phase 1 were found on the parental emotion socialization measure, the CCNES. Specifically, mothers with and without BPD did not differ on the emotion focused subscale of the CCNES, suggesting that all mothers were just as likely to engage their children in talking through their emotional experiences. Additionally, mothers with and without BPD did not differ on the minimizing or punitive subscales, suggesting that mothers with BPD were not more likely to punish their children or invalidate and minimize their child’s emotional experiences. To our knowledge, the CCNES has not been used among mothers with BPD specifically, however prior research has suggested that mothers with BPD generally demonstrate low levels of sensitivity and high levels of hostility towards their children [78]. This would suggest that mothers with BPD would be more likely to use negative strategies (e.g., punishing and minimization) in response to difficult interactions with their children. It is possible that negative impression management occurred throughout administration of the self-report measures and moms with BPD did not endorse use of these strategies due to feelings of guilt or discomfort. Alternatively, however, there may indeed not be a true significant difference between the two groups on these aspects of emotion socialization. As illustrated in Table 3, during aim 3 participants provided the feedback that they engaged in positive behaviors, such as validation of emotions, and did not engage in negative behaviors, such as punishment, due to negative childhood experiences with their own parents and the concern that they would negatively impact their children similarly. We therefore tentatively propose that the most traction in supporting mothers with BPD would not lie in increasing emotion-focused parenting, but indeed, increasing a mentalizing stance with intentional enhancement of epistemic trust.

Results of aim 1-phase 1 largely suggest that mothers with BPD and their children are, on average, high-risk dyads as compared to mothers without BPD. In aim 1-phase 2, themes consistent with this risk emerged. Almost all mothers noted that their prominent BPD symptoms (namely emotion dysregulation, impaired social cognition, and fears of abandonment) had a significant impact on their parenting, and vice versa. Mothers generally expressed a desire to participate in mental health services, often with a secondary goal of improving their parenting. Importantly, many participants reported that they were currently participating in therapy or had done so previously. Many provided examples of how interventions, particularly DBT, improved their mental health and supported their parenting. Future studies in this area, regardless of treatment orientation, must continue to evaluate the relative efficacy of individual treatment versus parenting-intervention, specifically, and their effect on child outcomes.

Mixed-methods integration of quantitative and qualitative findings in aim 2 suggested that sufficient need for parenting support existed in this population to warrant intervention. Additionally, when interpreting these results in the context of MISC, preliminary evidence emerged suggesting MISC may be a good fit for this population. Specifically, foundational components of MISC including recording review and in-vivo feedback, length, and theoretical orientation appeared to be in line with participant needs expressed in the qualitative interviews.

Aim 3 suggested that the fundamental approach of MISC resonated with and was acceptable to mothers with BPD. Additionally, participants provided practical recommendations for proceeding with the adaptation process of MISC using the ADAPT-ITT model. Constructive feedback about the intervention primarily centered around the use of video-recordings, trust and rapport with MISC trainers, and logistical considerations. Feedback regarding video-recordings was not surprising given recent literature suggesting that mothers with personality disorder participating in the VIPP were uncomfortable with having themselves recorded [43]. When administering the video-recording component of MISC with this population, MISC trainers will need to be sufficiently aware of, and sensitive to, difficulties with attachment and trust among individuals with BPD. Practically, providers will need to ensure that adequate rapport is built during one-on-one sessions prior to the introduction of video-recordings for those who appear hesitant. As a program, MISC already prioritizes slow, reflective, strengths-based feedback and therefore lends itself well to ensuring adequate rapport between trainers and patients. However, given participant feedback in this area, future modifications could also include additional time, more frequent initial visits (weekly instead of biweekly), or more in-depth conversations about the mother as an individual (rather than as a caregiver) than would be typical of a MISC session with a mother without BPD. Additionally, it will be important for MISC trainers working with this population to ensure that limits to confidentiality are very clear from the beginning of treatment. For some mothers with BPD, this may need to be a continued topic of discussion throughout the program to ensure that providers are ethically fulfilling their duty to report and that patients simultaneously feel entirely informed to prevent ruptures in rapport with individual providers and the mental health care system at large.

Logistically, based on participant feedback, it will be important during the adaptation and implementation of MISC with this population to consider how, and when, to administer this program via telehealth. Unfortunately, as illustrated by participants, there are many barriers to care for mental health treatment, particularly in the United States where insurance coverage is not universal and there are a significant number of rural communities. These concerns are often exacerbated for disorders such as BPD where stigma and the rarity of evidence-based interventions reduce the accessibility of services. Telehealth options for MISC would help improve the availability of the program.

Relatedly, peer support was a concept emphasized both in the qualitative interviews and in focus groups. Without prompting, participants in two focus groups who bonded quickly during participation shared contact information amongst themselves and requested follow-up from researchers about MISC’s availability. Therefore, for this population, it may be worthwhile to consider adapting the MISC structure to offer the group meeting component throughout the program rather than waiting until the end, or to consider adding a one-on-one peer mentorship component to the program. This is consistent with recent calls in the literature for “lighter touch” peer support interventions for BPD [79].

Finally, many moms who participated in the qualitative interviews and focus groups had neurodivergent children. Although the current study cannot speak to the statistical significance of this observation, it was a topic of discussion frequently enough that the adaptation process should be mindful of this consideration. MISC trainers working with this population should be cognizant of how some emotional components (e.g., eye contact and touch) or mediational components (e.g., expanding) may appear differently in some family systems. Given that MISC is inherently individualized, adaptable, and transportable across “contexts,” this is not a significant concern and instead speaks to the potential strength of MISC for this population. Specifically, MISC trainers attune to the reactions of the child during an interaction to determine whether a caregiver should continue engaging in a behavior or not. If a caregivers behavior (e.g., touching the child’s shoulder), that includes a MISC component (e.g., physical touch), is not successfully mediating the experience for the child and is instead interfering with the serve and return of the interaction (e.g., dysregulating or distracting the child), a trainer and caregiver would reflect on this together to generate alternative behaviors to achieve the desired outcome for the interaction.

Conclusions

This study makes significant contributions to the literature base concerning maternal BPD and began the process of adapting a new parenting program for this population. However, it is not without limitations. First, to maintain feasibility of recruitment, no age limits were placed on the necessary self-reported age range of participants’ children, except that at least one was under age 18. This meant that measures that were administered in aim 1-phase 1 had to be generally appropriate for children of all ages, limiting the extent to which we were able to assess parenting in the context of unique developmental windows such as infancy and toddlerhood. Future, more complex, analyses of these specific measures should control for the age of the child being reported on. Additionally, when we administered parenting items, we asked participants to think of their most difficult child under the age of 18. Therefore, results cannot be generalized to all children in the family and findings may have been impacted by how parents cope with parenting their most difficult child. It is possible that for some mothers, parenting their most difficult child results in their “worst” performance, while for others this may capture their “best” performance if they commit significant time and energy to being aware of their child’s heightened needs. Relatedly, we have limited information about the children of the mothers who participated in this study and lack detailed information about the parenting and psychiatric histories of the mothers. Additional information, such as child mental health symptoms, diagnoses, or school performance for example, would increase insight into the intergenerational effects of maternal constructs (e.g., parental reflective function) that were measured.

Although the purpose of qualitative research is not to widely generalize findings to all members of a population (thus not invalidating findings) it is still important to highlight that qualitative approaches do not “control” for extraneous variables of interest examined using quantitative methods. Future mixed methods and quantitative research should attempt to further investigate the qualitative themes that emerged from our work in Aim 1-pahse 1. For example, whether variables such as number of children, maternal age, treatment history, or mentalizing capability, or a source of selection bias, account for variability in the parenting experience. As noted previously when discussing parents’ experiences parenting neurodivergent children, it was beyond the scope and ability of the current study design to determine whether such experience systematically effected participant responses in Aim 1. We hope that the rich qualitative data captured as part of this project invigorates novel research questions about moderators of the parenting experience among those with BPD. Finally, the aim of the current study was to evaluate the potential feasibility and acceptability of MISC for mothers with BPD. Results were interpreted as being either supportive or contraindicative of the MISC approach only. Thus, we were unable to compare or contrast the propriety or utility of MISC with any other parenting intervention. Future studies concerning MISC, or other parenting interventions for mothers with BPD, should endeavor to compare and contrast available interventions in terms of patient acceptability as well as efficacy and effectiveness.

Notwithstanding the above limitations, the current study suggests that MISC is a potentially feasible and acceptable alternative for mothers with BPD who are interested in receiving professional parenting support services. The adaptation process using the ADAPT-ITT model can be continued to ultimately be implemented in clinical trials with this population.
