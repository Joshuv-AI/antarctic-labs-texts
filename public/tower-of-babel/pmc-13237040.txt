Channel estimation strategies for STAR-RIS-Aided NOMA: robustness to imperfections and complexity trade-offs for 6G wireless systems
Authors: Mahesh Babu Ammisetty, Madhu Ramarakula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simultaneously transmitting and reflecting reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (STAR-RIS) have attracted increasing attention as an effective means to improve coverage and spectrum utilization in future wireless systems. This paper examines channel estimation strategies for STAR-RIS-assisted non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) networks, highlighting both estimation accuracy and computational burden. Five representative schemes are considered: time-switching (TS), ON/OFF training, energy-splitting (ES) in ideal and practical forms, and a simplified two-phase method. The normalized mean square error (NMSE) expressions are derived and compared, together with their pilot overhead and arithmetic complexity. To reflect realistic deployment, the analysis is extended to scenarios with imperfect channel state information (CSI) due to pilot contamination and noise mismatch, as well as residual interference arising from imperfect successive interference cancellation (SIC). Numerical results show that ES-based approaches achieve substantially lower NMSE than TS and ON/OFF, maintaining up to an order-of-magnitude improvement under strong CSI errors. The two-phase ES method achieves a favorable balance, offering near-ideal performance with reduced complexity. System-level evaluations further reveal that NOMA-STAR-RIS provides higher ergodic sum rate and spectral efficiency than its OMA counterpart, and the relative gain becomes more pronounced when imperfections are present. These results demonstrate the robustness and efficiency of STAR-RIS channel estimation under NOMA and confirm its potential for beyond-5G and 6G networks.

Introduction

In recent years, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) have garnered attention because they may improve spectrum consumption and efficiency in next-generation wireless networks1. RISs use many low cost passive elements to direct incoming signals, changing the wireless environment. However, traditional RIS systems inherently reflect signals toward one side of the surface, limiting coverage to half-space2. In order to solve this problem, STAR-RISs, or simultaneous transmitting and reflecting RISs, have been proposed. These surfaces allow signal transmission and reflection throughout space, boosting geographical coverage, spectrum reuse, and signal propagation in complex conditions3,4.

STAR-RISs are promising, but they need accurate channel state information (CSI). The passive surface makes this difficult5. STAR-RISs function in both directions, therefore existing channel estimate approaches for traditional RISs, such as DFT-based training schemes and ON/OFF protocols, cannot be employed6,7. Energy splitting (ES) and time switching (TS) protocols restrict pilot training and CSI in STAR-RIS systems3,7,8. Non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) also helps share spectral resources. NOMA allows numerous users to access the same time-frequency resource using power-domain multiplexing. This considerably boosts spectrum efficiency9. NOMA and STAR-RIS designs enable ultra-dense and spectrally efficient wireless communication10.

Superposition coding and successive interference cancellation (SIC) allow STAR-RIS-NOMA systems to serve users with different channel gains6,11. This integration demands accurate separation of overlapping pilot signals and dynamic user power allocation, making channel estimation tougher. You must consider transmission and reflection coefficients, user interference, and ES and TS mode regulations while developing a pilot for STAR-RIS-aided NOMA12.

This research proposes novel channel estimation methods for STAR-RIS-assisted uplink NOMA systems. We examine a two-user NOMA system employing ES and TS protocols with simultaneous power-domain multiplexing. For the TS protocol, we train reflection and transmission patterns independently to estimate cascaded channels13. We employ a connected phase-shift model to modify the energy splitting ratio, pilot sequences, and training matrix simultaneously to minimize ES protocol channel estimation MSE. Our simulations show that power loss affects ES-based estimate14,15, yet a well-structured training strategy may approach the optimal performance.

Literature review

Researchers are integrating reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) with non-orthogonal multiple access. Conventional RIS structures are limited to half-space, complicating signal reflection management and the enhancement of wireless channels. They can accommodate a limited number of users throughout diverse regions9,16. introduce STAR-RIS, a system that transmits and reflects RIS concurrently17. provides a comprehensive overview of RIS-aided cell-free massive MIMO systems for 6G, demonstrating their ability to enhance coverage, spectral efficiency, and energy efficiency. Furthermore10, investigates joint AP–UE association and precoding in SIM-aided cell-free massive MIMO systems, showing that intelligent surfaces can effectively optimize network resource allocation. This enables comprehensive connection and bidirectional signal management. Extensive research has been conducted on the integration of STAR-RIS with NOMA18. investigated covert communication scenarios in NOMA systems utilizing STAR-RIS and identified methods to enhance communication privacy and obfuscate location.

19 investigated the influence of STAR-RIS settings on data transmission security and the probability of privacy loss in systems20. assert that intelligent surface tuning and user pairing in STAR-RIS-enhanced NOMA networks may boost physical layer security21,22. enhanced beamforming and power allocation to develop a secure STAR-RIS-NOMA system that maximizes the secrecy sum rate. These studies demonstrate that security is crucial in the design of STAR-RIS-NOMA systems, particularly in the face of real-world challenges such as channel state information (CSI) deficiencies or hardware malfunctions16. assert that phase noise and channel state information estimation mistakes diminish the efficacy of STAR-RIS-NOMA uplink systems, necessitating robust channel estimation techniques.

The contemporary low-complexity RIS beamforming and estimating techniques are attributed to23. In 202024, introduced a minimal variance unbiased estimator for RIS. It functioned effectively in STAR-RIS and low-pilot-resource environments15. conducted a thorough assessment of RIS channel prediction techniques at mmWave and THz frequencies, uncovering challenges associated with high-frequency implementation. This enables STAR-RIS technology to attain higher frequencies in the future25. introduced a discrete phase shift-based progressive refinement method for the linked reflection-transmission limitations of STAR-RIS systems.

This research equipped us for the challenging endeavour of channel estimation in STAR-RIS-NOMA systems. A significant amount of research has focused on beamforming and security at the physical layer or presumed that all conditions are satisfactory. This work addresses deficiencies by demonstrating a speedy and straightforward method for estimating channels using NOMA and TS/ES-based STAR-RIS protocol requirements and user superposition.

where\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{x}=[{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbf{t}},{\mathbf{q}}_{t}^{T},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{q}}_{r}^{T}{]}^{T}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{2M+2},\mathbf{n}=[{n}_{1},\dots\:,{n}_{\tau\:}{]}^{T}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:},\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}\:\mathbf{V}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:\times\:\left(2M+2\right)}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\:\mathrm{t}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{e}\:\mathrm{p}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}\:\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{x}.$$\end{document}16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}=\left[\begin{array}{llllllll}{s}_{t,1}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{\mathrm{1,1}}}{s}_{t,1}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{M,1}}{s}_{t,1}&\:{s}_{r,1}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{\mathrm{1,1}}}{s}_{r,1}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{M,1}}{s}_{r,1}\\\:{s}_{t,2}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{\mathrm{1,2}}}{s}_{t,2}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{M,2}}{s}_{t,2}&\:{s}_{r,2}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{\mathrm{1,2}}}{s}_{r,2}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{M,2}}{s}_{r,2}\\\:\vdots&\:\vdots&\:&\:\vdots&\:\vdots&\:\vdots&\:&\:\vdots\\\:{s}_{t,\tau\:}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{1,\tau\:}}{s}_{t,\tau\:}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{t}{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{M,\tau\:}}{s}_{t,\tau\:}&\:{s}_{r,\tau\:}&\:{\beta\:}_{1}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{1,\tau\:}}{s}_{r,\tau\:}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\beta\:}_{M}^{r}{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{M,\tau\:}}{s}_{r,\tau\:}\end{array}\right]$$\end{document}

The least-squares (LS) estimator of x for V with complete column rank is: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{2}{p}{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{y},$$\end{document}

and the mean squared error (MSE) becomes:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}=\frac{2{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}Tr\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)$$\end{document}

MSE derivation under ideal phase-shifts

Consider an M-element STAR-RIS-assisted NOMA system.Channel estimation uses orthogonal training matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:\times\:(2M+2)}$$\end{document}, constructed from a DFT or Hadamard matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document}, such that:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}=\alpha\:{\mathbf{I}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} is a scaling factor, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{I}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document} is the identity matrix. MSE of the least squares (LS) channel estimate is18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}=\frac{{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}^{2}$$\end{document} is the noise variance, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} is the transmit power.

The trace term can be expanded as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=\sum_{q=1}^{2M+2}\frac{1}{\sum_{p=1}^{\tau\:}\left|\right[\mathbf{V}{]}_{p,q}{|}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Under the assumption of unit-modulus training symbols (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|{s}_{k,i}\right|=1$$\end{document}) and symmetric power splitting (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{m}^{t}={\beta\:}_{m}^{r}=0.5$$\end{document}), the matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document} consists of:

Direct-link channels (2 columns): \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|\right[\mathrm{V}{]}_{p,q}{|}^{2}=1\sum_{p=1}^{\tau\:}\left|\right[\mathrm{V}{]}_{p,q}{|}^{2}=\tau\:.$$\end{document}

STAR-RIS-assisted channels (2 M columns): \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|\right[\mathbf{V}{]}_{p,q}{|}^{2}=0.25\sum_{p=1}^{\tau\:}\left|\right[\mathbf{V}{]}_{p,q}{|}^{2}=0.25\tau\:.$$\end{document}

Substituting these into the trace formula yields:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=2\cdot\:\frac{1}{\tau\:}+2M\cdot\:\frac{1}{0.25\tau\:}=\frac{2}{\tau\:}+\frac{8M}{\tau\:}$$\end{document}

Simplifying:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=\frac{2(4M+1)}{\tau\:}$$\end{document}

The minimum training length for orthogonal estimation is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:=2M+2$$\end{document}. Substituting this:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=\frac{2(4M+2)}{2M+2}=\frac{4M+1}{M+1}$$\end{document}

Thus, the ideal MSE for NOMA under the ES protocol is:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}=\frac{2{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}\cdot\:\frac{4M+1}{2\left(M+1\right)}$$\end{document}

Alternatively, this can be written as:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}=\frac{(4M+1)}{M+1}\cdot\:\frac{{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}$$\end{document}

This result provides a benchmark for evaluating practical ES-based STAR-RIS estimators.

Practical channel estimation under coupled phase-shift model

In Practical STAR-RIS hardware transmission and reflection phase-shifts cannot be independently programmed since implementations use a shared passive structure. The following characterizes a coupling constraint between reflection and transmission phase changes: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{c}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{s}({\theta\:}_{i,n}-{\varphi\:}_{i,n})=0\:\:\:\:\Rightarrow\:\:\:\:\:{\theta\:}_{i,n}-{\varphi\:}_{i,n}=\pm\:\frac{\pi\:}{2}.$$\end{document}

This constraint complicates the optimization of the pilot training matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document}, as independent design of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\boldsymbol{\Theta\:}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\boldsymbol{\Phi\:}}$$\end{document} is no longer feasible. To address this, we propose a suboptimal but effective training design that satisfies the phase coupling constraint while retaining near-orthogonality of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document}.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{(2M+2)\times\:(2M+2)}$$\end{document} can be either a Hadamard matrix or a discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix. Columns (M + 2)th of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document} are assigned the pilot symbols of the T-user and R-user, respectively. we set:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:{\mathbf{s}}_{t}={\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}[:,1],{\mathbf{s}}_{r}=[j,-j,j,-j,\dots\:{]}^{T}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{s}}_{r}$$\end{document} is constructed to introduce a quadrature phase shift relative to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{s}}_{t}$$\end{document} in each time slot. The training phase patterns for the STAR-RIS are defined by:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\theta\:}_{i,n}=\frac{[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n+1}}{{s}_{t,i}},$$\end{document}27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\varphi\:}_{i,n}=\frac{[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n+M+2}}{{s}_{r,i}},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{r,i\:}and\:\:{s}_{t,i}$$\end{document} are the pilot symbols of the R-user and T-user at time slot \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}, respectively.To verify that this design satisfies the coupling constraint, consider the ratio of the corresponding transmission and reflection coefficients:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\frac{{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{i,n}}}{{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{i,n}}}=\frac{[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n+1}}{[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n+M+2}}\cdot\:\frac{{s}_{r,i}}{{s}_{t,i}}$$\end{document}

If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document} is a DFT matrix, then it satisfies the property:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n+M+2}=(-1{)}^{i+1}\cdot\:[{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}{]}_{i,n\:+1}$$\end{document}

Substituting into (24), and using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{r,i}=j\cdot\:{s}_{t,i}\cdot\:(-1{)}^{i+1}$$\end{document}, we obtain:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\frac{{e}^{j{\theta\:}_{i,n}}}{{e}^{j{\varphi\:}_{i,n}}}=j\:\:\:\:\Rightarrow\:\:\:\:\:{\theta\:}_{i,n}-{\varphi\:}_{i,n}=\frac{\pi\:}{2}$$\end{document}

which confirms that the phase-coupling constraint in (1) is satisfied.

With the above configuration, the pilot matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document} has the form:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathbf{V}=\left[{\mathbf{s}}_{t},\:\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{g}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{t}\right)\cdot\:\stackrel{-}{\boldsymbol{\Theta\:}},\:\:{\mathbf{s}}_{r},\:\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{g}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{r}\right)\cdot\:\stackrel{-}{\boldsymbol{\Phi\:}}\right]$$\end{document}

Due to the quasi-orthogonal structure of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{D}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document} and the alternating pilot design, all columns of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document} remain orthogonal except possibly for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{s}}_{r}$$\end{document}. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{V}$$\end{document} remains full rank, and the MSE remains close to the ideal value. As a result, the effective Gram matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}$$\end{document} deviates from the identity matrix, leading to an increased trace of its inverse: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left[({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right]>2M+2$$\end{document}

To account for this degradation in orthogonality, a multiplicative penalty factor is introduced. The MSE is then approximated by:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}}\approx\:\eta\:\left(M\right)\cdot\:\frac{\left(2M+2\right){\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}$$\end{document}

Under ideal orthogonal training, the Gram matrix of the pilot matrix satisfies33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:{\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}=\alpha\:{\mathbf{I}}_{2M+2}$$\end{document}

which leads to the minimum LS estimation error. However, due to the hardware constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i,n}-{\varphi\:}_{i,n}=\pm\:\frac{\pi\:}{2}$$\end{document}, the transmission and reflection phase patterns cannot be independently optimized, resulting in a quasi-orthogonal training matrix. Consequently, the Gram matrix can be expressed as34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:{\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}=\alpha\:\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Delta\:}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\Delta\:}$$\end{document} represents the correlation introduced by the coupled phase shifts.

By evaluating the column energies of the ES pilot matrix, the trace of the inverse Gram matrix becomes35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=\frac{2}{\tau\:}+\frac{8M}{\tau\:}$$\end{document}

Using the minimum pilot length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:=2M+2$$\end{document}, we obtain36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{H}\mathbf{V}{)}^{-1}\right)=\frac{4M+2}{M+1}$$\end{document}

Substituting this result into the LS error expression yields37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:MS{E}_{ES}^{prac}=\frac{4M+2}{M+1}\frac{{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}$$\end{document}

Thus, the penalty factor can be written as38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\eta\:\left(M\right)=\frac{4M+2}{\left(2M+2\right)\left(M+1\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left(M\right)$$\end{document} is a function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} that captures the quasi-orthogonality degradation due to coupling. Based on analytical bounding and empirical fitting from simulation studies9,10,16, this penalty factor is chosen as:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\eta\:\left(M\right)=\frac{\left(4M+2\right)}{\left(2M+2\right)\left(M+1\right)}$$\end{document}

Substituting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left(M\right)$$\end{document} into the MSE expression gives:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}}\approx\:\frac{\left(4M+2\right)}{M+1}\cdot\:\frac{{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}$$\end{document}

This is based on matrix perturbation constraints (such as the Gershgorin Circle Theorem) and checked by analytical bounding.

Two-phase channel estimation under ES protocol

To address the limitations of coupled transmission and reflection phase-shifts in practical STAR-RIS hardware, we adopt a two-phase channel estimation strategy. Two separate stages, which do not overlap, make up the entire pilot time τ. We estimate the channel components associated with the transmission path in the first phase and the reflection path in the second.Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{y}}^{\left(1\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:/2}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{y}}^{\left(2\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:/2}$$\end{document} denote the received signal vectors at the base station (BS) during the first and second phases, respectively. In Phase 1, the BS receives:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:{\mathbf{y}}^{\left(1\right)}=\sqrt{\frac{p}{2}}\cdot\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}\cdot\:{\mathbf{x}}^{\left(1\right)}+{\mathbf{n}}^{\left(1\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{x}}^{\left(1\right)}=[{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{T},{\mathbf{q}}_{t}^{T}{]}^{T}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{M+1}$$\end{document} contains the T-user’s direct and STAR-RIS channels, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:/2\times\:(M+1)}$$\end{document} is the corresponding training matrix. Similarly, during Phase 2, the BS receives:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\mathbf{y}}^{\left(2\right)}=\sqrt{\frac{p}{2}}\cdot\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}\cdot\:{\mathbf{x}}^{\left(2\right)}+{\mathbf{n}}^{\left(2\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{x}}^{\left(2\right)}=[{\mathbf{h}}_{r}^{T},{\mathbf{q}}_{r}^{T}{]}^{T}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{M+1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{C}}^{\tau\:/2\times\:(M+1)}$$\end{document} is the training matrix associated with the R-user.

Assuming that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}$$\end{document} are full rank and built using orthogonal sequences, the least squares (LS) estimates are obtained as:43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\left(1\right)}=\frac{2}{p}{\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}{)}^{H}{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}\right)}^{-1}({\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}{)}^{H}{\mathbf{y}}^{\left(1\right)}$$\end{document}44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\left(2\right)}=\frac{2}{p}{\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}{)}^{H}{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}\right)}^{-1}({\mathbf{V}}^{\left(2\right)}{)}^{H}{\mathbf{y}}^{\left(2\right)}$$\end{document}

Under the orthogonal design with normalized pilot sequences, the MSE of each phase is:45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}^{\left(1\right)}=\frac{2{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}\cdot\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{r}\left({\left(({\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}{)}^{H}{\mathbf{V}}^{\left(1\right)}\right)}^{-1}\right)=\frac{4{\sigma\:}^{2}\left(M+1\right)}{p\tau\:}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}^{\left(2\right)}=\frac{4{\sigma\:}^{2}(M+1)}{p\tau\:}.$$\end{document}.

The total MSE of the two-phase ES estimation is given by:46\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}^{2-\mathrm{p}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}}=\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}^{\left(1\right)}+\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}^{\left(2\right)}=\frac{8{\sigma\:}^{2}(M+1)}{p\tau\:}$$\end{document}

By setting the pilot overhead \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:=2M+2$$\end{document}, as in the ideal case, we obtain:47\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}{\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}^{2-\mathrm{p}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}}=\frac{8{\sigma\:}^{2}(M+1)}{p(2M+2)}=\frac{4{\sigma\:}^{2}}{p}$$\end{document}

This result shows that the two-phase ES estimation achieves slightly higher MSE than the ideal case, but retains low overhead and satisfies the phase-coupled hardware constraint.

This section shows numbers that show how well the proposed TS- and ES-based channel estimation strategies work for STAR-RIS-assisted NOMA systems. There are M = 20 sub-surfaces in a STAR-RIS with M0 = 80 elements. We model all wireless channels as Rician fading with a Rician factor of 10 dB. We model the path loss as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l={\beta\:}_{0}{\left(\frac{d}{{d}_{0}}\right)}^{-\alpha\:}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{0}=-30$$\end{document} dB is the path loss at the reference distance d0 = 1 m. We consider of a two-dimensional coordinate system with the BS at the origin and the STAR-RIS in the middle at (50 m, 0). The T-user is at (54 m,3 m) while the R-user is at (46 m, -3 m). The path-loss exponents for the linkages between the user and the BS, the user and the STAR-RIS, and the BS and the STAR-RIS are 3.5, 2.8, and 2.2, respectively. The noise power is always set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}^{2}=-110$$\end{document} dBm, and the user transmit power is always set to p = 30 dBm, unless otherwise noted. We compare the various ways to estimate against two benchmarks. The first is the TS ON/OFF technique. In this plan, direct links are estimated with all sub-surfaces turned off, and cascaded links are estimated one at a time with one sub-surface turned on for either transmission or reflection. The second is the two-phase ES method. In Phase 1, users transmit orthogonal pilots with the STAR-RIS turned off to find out how things are directly connected. In Phase 2, structured transmission and reflection patterns are exploited to find cascaded channels.

Figure 8 shows the normalized mean square error (NMSE) in dB for different STAR-RIS channel estimation schemes with M = 20 subsurfaces, under varying levels of residual SIC error. As expected, the ES-based methods achieve significantly lower NMSE compared to TS and ON/OFF. For example, at − 20dB residual SIC, ES-Ideal achieves an NMSE of about − 44 dB, whereas TS and ON/OFF remain above − 38dB and − 40dB, respectively. As SIC imperfection increases toward 0 dB, all schemes exhibit degradation, but the ES-Ideal and ES-Practical approaches maintain roughly a 6–8 dB advantage over TS and ON/OFF. Figure 9 compares the robustness of different estimation schemes as the number of STAR-RIS elements increases from M = 20 to M = 30. Increasing the number of elements improves training diversity and effective channel strength, resulting in lower NMSE across all schemes. For instance, at − 15 dB SIC error, ES-Ideal improves from − 43 dB (M = 20) to nearly − 45 dB (M = 30). Similarly, ON/OFF reduces its NMSE from − 39 dB to about − 41 dB when moving from M = 20 to M = 30. The relative ordering among schemes remains unchanged, with ES-Ideal performing best, followed by ES-Practical, ES-Two-Phase, TS, and ON/OFF. This demonstrates that increasing RIS elements consistently enhances estimation accuracy, though the performance gap between schemes persists.

The presented results confirm that ES-based estimation schemes consistently outperform TS and ON/OFF in terms of NMSE, robustness, and throughput. Increasing the number of RIS elements improves estimation accuracy, while the performance hierarchy among schemes remains unchanged. Under imperfect CSI, ES-Ideal and ES-Practical maintain up to an order-of-magnitude advantage, and the two-phase method provides near-ideal performance with lower complexity. When residual SIC is considered, the ergodic sum rate degrades for all schemes, but NOMA-STAR-RIS still preserves a clear throughput gain over OMA. Overall, the study demonstrates that ES-based approaches achieve the best trade-off between accuracy, robustness, and complexity in practical STAR-RIS-aided NOMA systems.

Conclusion

This paper presented a detailed study of channel estimation techniques for STAR-RIS-assisted NOMA systems, emphasizing estimation accuracy, complexity, and robustness to practical imperfections. Analytical and simulation results showed that energy-splitting (ES) schemes, particularly the ideal and practical variants, consistently outperform TS and ON/OFF methods. For instance, at a CSI error level of − 20 dB, ES-Ideal achieves nearly an order-of-magnitude lower NMSE compared to TS and ON/OFF. The proposed two-phase ES strategy demonstrated a favorable trade-off, remaining within ~ 2 dB of ES-Ideal while significantly lowering complexity. Robustness analysis further confirmed that ES-based estimators maintain their advantage even under pilot contamination, noise mismatch, and residual SIC errors. At a residual SIC of − 20 dB, NOMA-STAR-RIS still provides around 30% higher ergodic sum rate compared to OMA. Overall, the results establish ES-based estimation as a robust and efficient solution for STAR-RIS-aided NOMA, paving the way for beyond-5G and 6G deployments. Future work may explore adaptive pilot scheduling, power control, and machine learning–based estimation to reduce overhead and improve robustness. Extensions to Multiuser-NOMA architectures and mmWave/THz bands offer further opportunities to enhance STAR-RIS performance.
