Evaluation of cerebrospinal fluid human epididymis protein 4 for leptomeningeal metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yong-juan Lin, Ming-yang He, Yu Xie, Zhen-yu Yin, Xia-wen Lin, Ling-li Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosing and monitoring leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) presents a significant clinical challenge. This study evaluated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Human Epididymis Protein 4 (HE4) as a novel diagnostic and monitoring biomarker for LM in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsA prospective cohort study enrolled 117 participants (including 61 NSCLC patients with LM and 56 controls without central nervous system malignancies) from March 2023 to December 2024. Paired CSF and serum levels of HE4, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and Cytokeratin 19 Fragment (CYFRA21-1) were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. Multivariable binary logistic regression, adjusting for clinical covariates (KPS, ICP, and symptom-to-diagnosis interval), was employed to identify independent diagnostic predictors. Diagnostic performance was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and compared via the DeLong test. Serial CSF HE4 monitoring was performed in a subset of patients to assess treatment response.ResultsCSF HE4 levels were markedly elevated in LM patients compared to controls (median 807.00 vs. 99.35 pmol/L, P < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression revealed that CSF HE4 was a robust, independent diagnostic factor for LM (Adjusted OR = 1.027; 95% CIs: 1.010–1.044; P = 0.001), along with the CSF-to-serum HE4 ratio (QHE4; P = 0.046). The multivariable model demonstrated excellent calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, P = 0.366). In ROC analysis, CSF HE4 (AUC = 0.895) significantly outperformed QHE4 (AUC = 0.769), CSF CEA (AUC = 0.701), and CSF CYFRA21-1 (AUC = 0.664) (all P < 0.05). At an optimal cutoff of 173.00 pmol/L, CSF HE4 yielded a sensitivity of 83.21% and specificity of 92.86%. Subgroup analysis indicated higher CSF HE4 in cytology-positive and progressive disease patients. Longitudinal monitoring showed that dynamic fluctuations in CSF HE4 closely mirrored clinical treatment response and disease progression.ConclusionCSF HE4 is a promising biomarker for the diagnosis of NSCLC related LM. For CSF HE4, its robust diagnostic performance and correlation with disease activity support its use for both initial diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring.

Background

Leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) is a severe complication of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), affecting 3–5% of patients with advanced disease and resulting in a poor prognosis [1, 2]. Its diagnosis remains difficult, as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology has a low initial sensitivity (~ 50%), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is non-diagnostic in a significant proportion of cases [3, 4]. This highlights an urgent need for reliable CSF biomarkers to enhance LM detection.

Human epididymis protein 4 (HE4), a secretory protein overexpressed in lung cancer, has emerged as a promising candidate. Beyond its established role in diagnosis, HE4 is increasingly recognized as a critical tool for dynamic disease monitoring and prognostic stratification across various malignancies [5, 6]. In high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, baseline serum HE4 levels correlate more strongly with surgical tumor burden than cancer antigen 125 (CA125), providing a more accurate initial assessment of disease extent [7]. Notably, longitudinal monitoring shows that HE4 levels during primary therapy are a significant predictor of platinum-resistant relapse, while serum HE4 concentration at first recurrence serves as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival [8]. This prognostic value is further refined in the context of modern targeted therapies. For instance, during poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitor maintenance treatment for epithelial ovarian cancer, serial HE4 measurement (using a ≥ 25% increase threshold) demonstrates significantly higher accuracy than CA125 in detecting disease progression, highlighting its superior reliability in this novel therapeutic setting [9]. The translational utility of HE4 dynamic monitoring is also evident in lung adenocarcinoma, where combining HE4 with Cytokeratin 19 Fragment (CYFRA21-1) in post-resection surveillance improves recurrence detection, outperforming either marker alone [10]. Taken together, serial HE4 assessment offers a powerful, integrative approach for evaluating real-time tumor dynamics, predicting therapeutic resistance, and detecting early recurrence, thereby supporting a more proactive and personalized cancer management strategy.

A recent landmark study by Li et al. established the diagnostic value of CSF HE4 for LM in lung adenocarcinoma, demonstrating its superior performance and synergistic effect when combined with carcinoembryonic antigen related cell adhesion molecule 6 (CEACAM6) [11]. These promising findings positioned CSF HE4 as a potent diagnostic biomarker. Notably, the study of Li et al. exclusively enrolled patients with cytologically positive CSF, which inherently confirms LM diagnosis [11]. To comprehensively assess the biomarker’s performance in a more representative clinical spectrum, our study included both CSF cytology-positive and cytology-negative patients. However, despite these advances, current evidence on the application of CSF HE4 for monitoring treatment response and predicting progression in LM remains limited. Thus, our study aimed to validate the diagnostic accuracy of CSF HE4 in an independent cohort and, more importantly, to explore its potential for serial therapeutic monitoring, addressing one of the key unresolved clinical challenges in LM management.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

A prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Department of Geriatric Oncology at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University, a tertiary comprehensive hospital (hospital information is concealed for peer review). The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University (No. 2024-544-02). Informed consent was obtained from the family members of all patients, and all procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

Between March 2023 and December 2024, 61 consecutive NSCLC patients with a confirmed diagnosis of LM were enrolled. The LM diagnosis followed the European Association of Neuro-Oncology (EANO)-ESMO criteria [12]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged 18 years or older; (2) a confirmed pathological diagnosis of NSCLC; (3) LM was definitively diagnosed according to the EANO-ESMO clinical practice guidelines. This included patients with cytologically confirmed disease (Type 1) or those with a clinical-radiological diagnosis (Type 2) based on pathognomonic MRI findings and typical neurological manifestations in the absence of positive cytology. Exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of encephalitis, significant craniocerebral trauma, neurosurgical intervention, or cerebral radiotherapy within the previous six months; (2) concurrent active malignancies; (3) incomplete clinical data or insufficient biological samples for study analysis. Due to the invasive nature of lumbar puncture, it is ethically restricted in NSCLC patients without neurological indications. Therefore, patients with non-malignant neurological disorders were utilized as a pragmatic and ethically sound control group to establish baseline CSF HE4 levels. The control group (n = 56) comprised patients diagnosed with non-malignant neurological disorders, such as cognitive impairment, benign intracranial tumors, or non-infectious/non-malignant meningitis, during the same study period. These individuals underwent lumbar puncture due to clinical indications requiring CSF analysis for differential diagnosis. Control participants were required to have undergone lumbar puncture yielding sufficient CSF volume, with paired serum samples collected on the same day. To minimize selection bias and establish a reliable baseline, we strictly excluded patients with a history of any malignancy, particularly Central Nervous System involvement or systemic metastasis.

Clinical data collection

Standardized initial diagnostic lumbar punctures were conducted within 24 h of admission, with CSF cytology and HE4 measurement completed within a median of 18 h (IQR, 14–21 h) to ensure high temporal resolution relative to clinical presentation. Patients were transferred to the procedure room, where an assistant prepared the necessary equipment and assisted with positioning. Lumbar puncture was performed under aseptic conditions, and CSF flow confirmation was followed by measurement of opening pressure. Approximately 6–8 mL of CSF was collected for laboratory analysis, including cytological examination, HE4 measurement, CEA, CYFRA21-1 and circulating tumor cell detection. Paired peripheral blood samples were collected simultaneously, and the CSF-to-serum HE4 ratio (QHE4) was calculated to evaluate the intrathecal synthesis of the biomarkers.

Paired CSF and peripheral blood samples analyzed using previously described methods, were collected during intrathecal chemotherapy for dynamic HE4 monitoring. For all patients in the LM cohort, CSF samples for HE4 measurement were obtained during the initial diagnostic lumbar puncture, ensuring that biomarker levels were assessed prior to the commencement of any intrathecal chemotherapy. Subsequent to the baseline assessment, CSF and serum samples were obtained on days 1 and 8 of each month, synchronized with the administration of intrathecal chemotherapy cycles. To avoid the immediate confounding effects of pharmacological agents on HE4 concentrations, all CSF samples were collected immediately prior to drug administration in each cycle. Monitoring continued until the documentation of disease progression or treatment discontinuation due to intolerance. Additionally, contrast-enhanced MRI was performed within one week of admission to provide clinical-radiological correlation, with a median completion time of 5 days (IQR, 2–4 days).

Clinical and demographic data, including age, sex, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), intracranial pressure (ICP), CSF routine parameters (white blood cell count, protein, glucose, chloride), and MRI findings, were extracted from electronic medical records.

Laboratory measurement of biomarkers

To maintain high analytical rigor and minimize pre-analytical variability, all biological samples were processed using a standardized protocol. CSF samples were subjected to immediate visual inspection to exclude macroscopically hemorrhagic specimens (traumatic punctures), followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min to effectively remove cellular debris and residual red blood cells. Supernatant CSF and paired serum aliquots were processed and moved to storage within 5 h of collection. For long-term stability, samples were stored at -80 °C and were restricted to a maximum of one freeze-thaw cycle prior to analysis.

Paired CSF and peripheral blood samples were analyzed for HE4, CEA, and CYFRA21-1 to evaluate their respective diagnostic utility. HE4 and CYFRA21-1 concentrations were measured using the Roche cobas e801 electrochemiluminescence immunoassay system (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), while CEA was assessed using the Siemens Atellica Solution chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc., New York, USA). The HE4 assay (Ref: 05950929190) has a reported analytical range of 15.0–1500 pmol/L and a limit of detection (LoD) of 5.0 pmol/L. In our laboratory, the intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was < 3.0%, and the inter-assay CV was < 4.5%. All assays were performed using instrument-specific reagent kits (Lot: 84899001) and calibrators (Lot: 768265) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures. Technicians were blinded to the clinical status and group allocation of the participants until data locking.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Paired comparisons between CSF and serum levels were made using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent diagnostic value of CSF biomarkers. Potential confounding clinical covariates were adjusted for in the model. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, where P> 0.05 indicated acceptable model fit and consistency between predicted and observed probabilities. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) values were compared using the DeLong test. The optimal diagnostic cutoff for CSF HE4 was determined using the Youden Index. To assess the robustness of our results and minimize optimism bias, internal validation was performed using a bootstrapping method with 1,000 resamples. This approach was used to calculate the optimism-corrected AUC and the 95% confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity at the selected cutoff. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Elevated CSF HE4 levels in LM patients

CSF HE4 levels were markedly elevated in the LM group compared to controls [807.00 (206.00-1580.00) vs. 99.35 (79.55–124.50), Z = 7.361, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A]. Similarly, serum HE4 levels were higher in LM patients than in controls [128.00 (98.10–182.00) vs. 61.05 (46.15–75.65), Z = 7.707, P < 0.001; Fig. 2B]. Notably, in LM patients, CSF HE4 levels significantly exceeded those in the paired serum samples (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C), with a weak but statistically significant correlation between the two compartments (r = 0.376, P = 0.003; Fig. 2D). Furthermore, QHE4 was significantly higher in the LM group than in the control group [5.25 (1.93, 10.94) vs. 1.68 (1.24, 2.08), Z = 5.014, P < 0.001; Table 1].

Diagnostic performance of CSF biomarkers

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of diagnostic factors after adjusting for baseline clinical characteristics (KPS score, ICP, and time from symptom onset to diagnosis) via multivariable binary logistic regression, CSF HE4 emerged as a highly robust, independent diagnostic factor for LM (OR = 1.027, 95% CIs: 1.010–1.044, P = 0.001). In addition, QHE4 was also identified as a statistically significant independent correlation factor (OR = 0.260, 95% CIs: 0.070–0.974, P = 0.046), demonstrating an inverse relationship with diagnostic risk. Notably, the KPS score (OR = 1.077, 95% CIs: 1.006–1.153, P = 0.032) and the time from symptom onset to diagnosis (OR = 1.183, 95% CIs: 1.023–1.367, P = 0.023) also retained independent statistical significance within the model (Fig. 3). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test confirmed that the multivariable model achieved excellent calibration and goodness-of-fit (χ2 = 8.730, P = 0.366).

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the three CSF biomarkers and QHE4 (Table 2; Fig. 4). CSF HE4 demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy, with an AUC of 0.895 (95% CIs: 0.830–0.960). At an optimal cutoff value of 173.00 pmol/L, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing LM were 83.21% and 92.86%, respectively. QHE4 also showed favorable diagnostic value, yielding an AUC of 0.769 (95% CIs: 0.677–0.861, P < 0.001); at an optimal cutoff value of 3.531, its sensitivity and specificity were 59.00% and 100.00%, respectively. In contrast, the diagnostic power of CSF CEA (AUC 0.701, sensitivity 63.96%, specificity 89.29% at 3.01 U/mL) and CSF CYFRA21-1 (AUC 0.664, sensitivity 73.77%, specificity 67.21% at 1.78 U/mL) was significantly lower (P < 0.05 for both comparisons with HE4).

Pairwise comparison of the ROC curves using the DeLong test demonstrated that the diagnostic accuracy of absolute CSF HE4 was significantly superior to that of QHE4 (AUC difference = -0.126, 95% CIs: -0.191 - -0.061, Z = -3.799, P < 0.001), CSF CEA (AUC difference = 0.194, 95% CIs: 0.104–0.284, Z = 4.217, P < 0.001), and CSF CYFRA21-1 (AUC difference = 0.172, 95% CIs: 0.088–0.257, Z = 4.016, P < 0.001). Interestingly, no statistically significant differences in diagnostic power were observed between QHE4 and the traditional markers, including CSF CEA (AUC difference = 0.068, 95% CIs: -0.038–0.173, Z = 1.261, P = 0.207) and CSF CYFRA21-1 (AUC difference = 0.047, 95% CIs: -0.046–0.139, Z = 0.990, P = 0.322). Similarly, the diagnostic performance between CSF CEA and CSF CYFRA21-1 was comparable (AUC difference = -0.021, 95% CIs: -0.124–0.081, Z = -0.405, P = 0.685) (Table 3).

Correlation of CSF HE4 with other parameters and clinical subgroups

To further evaluate whether CSF HE4 primarily reflects tumor burden or inflammatory response, we analyzed its relationship with CSF cellularity and biochemistry. Spearman correlation analysis in LM patients revealed that CSF HE4 levels showed a weak positive correlation with CSF white blood cell count (r = 0.385, P = 0.002), protein (r = 0.354, P = 0.005) and IL6 (r = 0.255, P = 0.048). Additionally, moderate positive correlations were observed with CSF CEA (r = 0.412, P = 0.001) and CYFRA21-1 (r = 0.623, P < 0.0001). In contrast, CSF HE4 showed a moderate negative correlation with CSF glucose levels (r = − 0.493, P < 0.0001). Figure 5 illustrates the details.

Subgroup analyses assessed CSF HE4 levels under different clinical conditions (Table 4). No significant differences were observed based on the presence of concurrent brain metastases (P = 0.318), specific MRI findings (P = 0.253), or the duration of LM symptoms (P = 0.658). However, CSF HE4 levels were significantly higher in patients with positive CSF cytology compared to those diagnosed clinically or radiographically with negative cytology [964.50 (320.50–1600.00) vs. 152.80 (77.80–214.00) pmol/L, P = 0.015]. Notably, patients with progressive disease (PD) had substantially higher CSF HE4 levels than those with non-PD [1189.00 (784.00-2120.00) vs. 352.50 (180.50-1162.50), P = 0.011].

Serial monitoring of CSF HE4 in guiding treatment and predicting progression

To explore the potential of CSF HE4 in therapeutic monitoring, serial measurements were performed in four LM patients undergoing intrathecal chemotherapy (Fig. 6). Evaluated by RANO-LM criteria, these longitudinal profiles demonstrated that serial CSF HE4 measurements could reflect dynamic changes in leptomeningeal tumor burden, offering valuable clinical insights. This suggests that CSF HE4 could be a useful tool for monitoring treatment response and providing early indications of LM progression.

Discussion

LM in NSCLC is associated with a poor prognosis, compounded by diagnostic delays and the complexity of predicting treatment response, posing significant clinical challenges [13–15]. This study identified CSF HE4 as a promising biomarker for both diagnosis and monitoring. Findings suggest that elevated CSF HE4 levels appear to show an enhanced diagnostic performance, providing a potential molecular basis to guide treatment decisions and predict clinical responses in this high-risk population.

The diagnostic challenge in LM is highlighted by the limited sensitivity of initial CSF cytology and the frequent non-diagnostic nature of MRI findings in a significant proportion of patients [16, 17]. Compounding this challenge is the established clinical consensus that positive CSF cytology, when detectable, often indicates a higher leptomeningeal tumor burden and is independently associated with a poorer prognosis [18, 19]. In this context, our study aimed to explore a novel biomarker across a broader clinical spectrum. Notably, unlike the pivotal work by Li et al., which focused exclusively on a cytology-confirmed cohort [11], our investigation included both cytology-positive and cytology-negative patients with high clinical/radiological suspicion of LM.

Our findings show that CSF HE4 achieves an AUC of 0.895, significantly outperforming CSF CEA and CYFRA21-1. Unlike neuron-specific markers such as neuron-specific enolase, which are intrinsic to CNS tissues, HE4 is an extrinsic biomarker in the context of the brain. Its presence in the CSF at levels exceeding systemic circulation indicates that the metastatic NSCLC cells themselves serve as the primary secretory source within the leptomeningeal microenvironment. Interestingly, our ROC curve and DeLong test analyses revealed a pragmatic nuance: while QHE4 demonstrated a statistically significant diagnostic value for LM (AUC = 0.769), its performance was significantly lower than that of absolute CSF HE4 levels (AUC = 0.895, P < 0.05). This discrepancy can be mathematically and biologically attributed to the ‘denominator confounding effect.’ Advanced NSCLC patients inherently exhibit elevated systemic serum HE4 levels due to extracranial tumor burden. Consequently, incorporating serum HE4 as the denominator mathematically attenuates the CSF-to-serum ratio in patients with concurrent high systemic disease, thereby introducing confounding noise and reducing standalone diagnostic purity. Furthermore, DeLong test indicated no significant difference between QHE4 and traditional markers (CSF CEA and CSF CYFRA21-1). This implies that while QHE4 remains a rigorous conceptual tool to confirm true intrathecal synthesis and rule out passive blood-brain barrier leakage, absolute CSF HE4 concentration remains the superior, more direct, and reliable surrogate for assessing real-time leptomeningeal tumor burden in clinical practice.

Importantly, we observed that HE4 levels were significantly higher in the CSF cytology-positive subgroup compared to the cytology-negative group, reinforcing its correlation with disease burden. Moreover, CSF HE4 retained promising diagnostic value even within the cytology-negative cohort, addressing a critical unmet need in patients with suspected LM but negative routine cytology. Furthermore, serial monitoring revealed statistically significant changes in CSF HE4 levels before and after treatment, suggesting its potential utility in therapeutic monitoring.

A key discovery in our study is the marked discrepancy between CSF and serum HE4 levels in LM patients, with CSF concentrations significantly exceeding those in the systemic circulation. A weak but statistically significant correlation was observed between CSF and serum HE4 (r = 0.376, P = 0.003). This suggests that elevated CSF HE4 is not merely a passive reflection of systemic levels but likely indicates disproportionate local secretion or accumulation within the subarachnoid space. It is hypothesized that HE4 is actively secreted or released following the lysis of metastatic tumor cells within the leptomeninges, where it becomes concentrated due to the relative containment of the CSF compartment. As a result, CSF HE4 levels may provide a more sensitive and direct indicator of leptomeningeal tumor activity compared to serum levels.

One potential concern is whether CSF HE4 levels are confounded by CNS inflammation or increased blood-brain barrier permeability. In our cohort, the correlation between HE4 and CSF WBC count or IL-6 was weak (r < 0.4), whereas its link to disease progression and cytological positivity was highly significant. This indicates that while inflammation may coexist with LM, it is not the primary driver of the observed HE4 elevation. Instead, the high local concentration likely reflects active secretion by metastatic NSCLC cells. Research in ovarian and endometrial cancers has demonstrated that HE4 promotes cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [20–22]. In the context of lung cancer-related LM, the high local concentration of HE4 may contribute to tumor cell survival and colonization within the leptomeningeal space by modulating the local microenvironment, potentially through interactions with proteolytic pathways or immune evasion mechanisms. While the exact molecular pathways in LM remain to be fully elucidated, the persistently high CSF HE4 levels observed in progressing disease support its potential pro-tumorigenic role, suggesting that HE4 may not only serve as a diagnostic marker but also be involved the aggressive pathophysiology of LM.

Beyond its diagnostic utility, the dynamic changes in CSF HE4 levels warrant further exploration regarding their role in disease monitoring. Our data suggest a potential association between CSF HE4 levels and clinical outcomes, with higher CSF HE4 levels observed in patients with PD compared to those with non-PD. This observation is consistent with serological trends in lung and ovarian cancers, where elevated serum HE4 levels have been linked to advanced disease stages [23–25]. The longitudinal case profiles in our study tentatively indicate that serial measurements of CSF HE4 could reflect alterations in leptomeningeal tumor burden and responses to intrathecal therapy. These findings highlight the potential of CSF HE4 as a candidate marker for monitoring therapeutic efficacy in LM management.

Interpretation of HE4 levels should be approached with caution, especially when considering the relationship between circulating HE4 and tumor biology. Evidence suggests that elevated serum HE4 concentrations may not always directly correlate with tumor tissue HE4 overexpression and may be influenced by factors such as tumor stage, necrosis, or malignant effusions [26–28]. This highlights the complexity of HE4 biology. In our study, the distinct behavior of CSF HE4, decoupled from serum levels in LM patients, supports the notion that its source is local. Therefore, in the context of LM, CSF HE4 is likely a more specific and reliable indicator of leptomeningeal disease activity than serum HE4, as it is less influenced by systemic tumor factors.

Despite these compelling findings, several limitations should also be considered. First, the single-center design of this study necessitates further validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts. Second, the influence of various systemic and intrathecal therapies on CSF HE4 kinetics remains to be fully understood. Clarifying these dynamics will be helpful in determining its specific role in monitoring therapeutic efficacy. Thirdly, we did not evaluate the HE4 index (normalized to albumin) to formally quantify the rate of intrathecal synthesis. While the observed CSF-serum gradient strongly supports local production, future studies should incorporate blood-brain barrier permeability markers to further refine the diagnostic specificity of CSF HE4. Finally, A limitation of the current study is the preliminary nature of the serial monitoring analysis. The longitudinal data presented are based on a small subset of patients and are primarily illustrative. Future research will focus on expanding this serial dataset to formally quantify the predictive value of HE4 kinetics for therapeutic response and overall survival.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study validates CSF HE4 as a sensitive and specific biomarker for diagnosing NSCLC-related LM and, more importantly, suggests the potential feasibility of its use in serial therapeutic monitoring. While further validation in larger cohorts is warranted, these findings suggest that CSF HE4 represents a plausible adjunct marker that could potentially assist clinicians in managing this complex condition.
