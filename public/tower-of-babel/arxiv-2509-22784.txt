Abstract
GRB 250702B was the longest gamma-ray burst ever observed, with a duration that challenges standard collapsar models and suggests an exotic progenitor. We collected a rich set of optical and infrared follow-up observations of its rapidly fading afterglow using a suite of telescopes including the W. M. Keck Observatory, the Gemini telescopes, the Magellan Baade Telescope, the Victor M. Blanco 4-meter telescope, and the Fraunhofer Telescope at Wendelstein Observatory. Our analysis reveals that the afterglow emission is well described by forward shock emission from a highly obscured relativistic jet. Deep photometric observations of the host galaxy reveal a massive ( 10 10.66 ​ M ⊙ 10^{10.66}M_{\odot} ), dusty, and extremely asymmetric system that is consistent with two galaxies undergoing a major merger. The galactocentric offset, host galaxy properties, and jet characteristics do not definitively distinguish between competing progenitor scenarios. We find that the afterglow and host are consistent with a range of progenitors including a collapsar, a merger between a helium star and a stellar mass black hole, the disruption of a star by a stellar mass compact object, and the tidal disruption of a star by an off-nuclear intermediate mass black hole.



I
Introduction

Relativistic jets can illuminate the entire electromagnetic spectrum, reaching very high luminosities from gamma-rays to the radio when the observer’s line of sight is fortuitously aligned with the jet axis. Among the plethora of high-energy transients, gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the most common. Progenitors of the majority of GRBs include collapsars and compact object mergers
(see e.g.
Piran 2004
, for a review)
. The prompt emission from GRBs is usually found to last from
\sim 0.01
s up to several minutes
(
Kouveliotou et al. 1993
)
, but a rare population of so-called ultralong GRBs exists
(
Levan et al. 2014
)
, where prompt emission can last for several thousand seconds. The progenitors of these systems are believed to be massive stars with particularly large radii, which is supported by the emergence of bright supernovae after some ultralong GRBs
(
Stratta et al. 2013
;
Levan et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
. An helium-star merger progenitor
(
Fryer & Woosley 1998
)
has also been proposed, for instance in the case of ultralong GRB 101225A
(
Thöne et al. 2011
)
.

Although exceptionally rare, the disruption of a star by a massive black hole may also produce relativistic jets, in which case the gamma-ray activity may last for a few days. This extended emission from a relativistic tidal disruption event (TDE) was observed for Sw J1644+57
(
Bloom et al. 2011
;
Burrows et al. 2011a
;
Levan et al. 2011
;
Zauderer et al. 2011a
)
, Sw J2058+05
(
Cenko et al. 2012
;
Pasham et al. 2015
)
, Sw J1112-82
(
Brown et al. 2015a
;
Brown et al. 2017a
)
, and AT2022cmc
(
Andreoni et al. 2022
;
Pasham et al. 2023
)
. The high-energy spectrum of these sources is softer than typical GRBs, thus the emission is long-lasting predominantly in the hard X–rays.

On 2025 July 2, the Gamma-ray Burst Monitor (GBM) onboard the
Fermi
satellite detected multiple GRBs likely originating from the same source
(
Neights et al. 2025b
)
. They were observed between 2025-07-02 13:56:00 UT and 2025-07-02 16:21:33 UT and designated GRB 250702B
(
Fermi GBM Team 2025a
)
, GRB 250702C
(
Fermi GBM Team 2025b
;
Neights et al. 2025a
, later retracted;)
, GRB 250702D
(
Fermi GBM Team 2025c
)
, and GRB 250702E
(
Fermi GBM Team 2025d
)
. Henceforth we will refer to the event as GRB 250702B, in accordance with standard GRB nomenclature. The transient was later reported to have been concurrently observed by other facilities including
Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory

(
Gehrels et al. 2004
, hereafter
Swift;
)
Burst Array Telescope’s Gamma-ray Urgent Archiver for Novel Opportunities (BAT-GUANO)
(
DeLaunay et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
, the
Monitor of All-sky X-ray Image
(
MAXI
)
(
Kawakubo et al. 2025
)
,
Konus-Wind

(
Frederiks et al. 2025
)
, and the
Space-based multi-band astronomical Variable Objects Monitor
(
SVOM
)
(
Svom/Grm Team et al. 2025
)
.
Konus-Wind
detected multiple over-lapping X-ray emission episodes over hours from 2025-07-02 12:40 UT to 2025-07-02 16:50 UT
(
Frederiks et al. 2025
)
, suggesting an extreme duration for the transient. A complete gamma-ray analysis combining
Konus-Wind
and
Fermi
finds a gamma-ray duration of
\sim 25
ks
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
.
This extraordinary duration was further extended by retroactive analysis of stacked
Einstein Probe

(
Yuan et al. 2025
, EP;)
Wide-field X-ray Telescope (WXT) images, which revealed emission as early as July 1st
(
Cheng et al. 2025
)
,
giving it a central engine duration of
\sim 100
ks
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
.
This makes GRB 250702B the longest-duration gamma-ray bursts ever observed - the previous record holder GRB 111209A lasted
\sim 15
ks in the gamma rays and had a central engine duration of
\sim 25
ks
(
Gendre et al. 2013
)
. The exceptionally long duration of GRB 250702B complicates explanation from traditional long GRB models.

The EP mission detected a soft X-ray counterpart (designated EP250702a) at 2025-07-02 02:53 UT, with a localization accuracy of 2.4  arcminutes. Follow-up observations with the
Swift
X-ray Telescope (XRT) further improved the localization to a radius of 2.0″ centered on J2000 coordinates RA =18h 58m 45.61s, Dec =
-
07d 52
′
26.9
′′

(
Kennea et al. 2025a
)
.
Following these initial reports, ultraviolet (UV) optical and near-infrared (nIR) follow-up observations ensued. No UV or optical counterparts were identified in these initial searches (see Table
1
).
At 0.746 days after the first
Fermi
detection, Very Large Telescope (VLT) HAWK-I observations identified a nIR counterpart
(
Levan et al. 2025
)
at coordinates RA=18h 58m 45.57s, Dec=
-
07d 52
′
26.2
′′
.
Subsequent follow-up observations confirmed that the nIR counterpart was rapidly fading and coincident with an extended nIR galaxy
(
Levan et al. 2025
)
. Neither the transient nor the host galaxy were initially detected in any optical photometric band during follow-up, suggesting significant extinction along the line of sight.

In this work, we focus on optical and nIR observations of GRB 250702B and its host galaxy. The paper is organized as follows. We describe our observations in Sec.
II
. Modeling of both the host galaxy and the transient emission is presented in Sec.
III
. We discuss our results and their implications for the progenitor in Sec.
IV
. We present our conclusions in Sec.
V
.

III
Analysis

III.1
Jet Modeling

We used the observed nIR detections obtained with Keck/MOSFIRE, FTW, VLT/HAWK-I, and
HST
for model fitting (see Table
1
and Figure
2
). We fit the
H
and
K
-band data out to
3
days after discovery. We neglected the last two VLT/HAWK-I
K
-band detections as they are likely contaminated by host galaxy flux
(
Levan et al. 2025
)
and require late-time host galaxy templates to fully capture the transient flux.
We first fitted a powerlaw,

F(\nu,t)=A\nu^{\beta}(t-t_{0})^{\alpha},

(1)

where
\nu
is the effective frequency associated with each filter,
\beta
is the spectral index,
\alpha
is the temporal index, and
A
is the normalization.While the exact start time of the afterglow (the jet launching time) for the forward shock could have been coincident with the
Fermi
B, D and E bursts
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
, here we assumed the time of the first burst, GRB 250702D, as the initial time
t_{0}
. From this joint temporal and spectral fit, we obtained a temporal index
\alpha=-1.83^{+0.05}_{-0.06}
and spectral index
\beta=-4.95^{+0.32}_{-0.32}
. In particular, we find that the late-time
HST
detection, after removing the majority of the host contamination (Section
II.7
) is consistent with the single powerlaw decay shown at earlier times. Overall, the near-infrared temporal index is consistent with the decay of the X-ray lightcurve
(
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
, but the observed spectral index is clearly inconsistent with the initial X-ray reports
(
Kennea et al. 2025b
,
\beta=-0.65\pm 0.20
;)
.

Assuming the observed near-infrared emission arises from synchrotron radiation produced by the forward shock (the afterglow) of a relativistic jet
(
Granot & Sari 2002
)
, we can infer the properties of the blastwave and its surrounding environment. Using the standard afterglow closure relations
(
Granot & Sari 2002
)
, we can infer the slope
p
of the electron’s powerlaw energy distribution
N(\gamma)\propto\gamma^{-p}
. Assuming the near-infrared emission lies below the cooling frequency
\nu_{\textrm{c}}
implies
p=1-2\beta=10.9\pm 0.64
whereas if the near-infrared is above the cooling frequency we have
p=-2\beta=9.90\pm 0.64
. Either of these values are significantly inconsistent with both the theoretical and observed values of
p
which lie closer to
p\sim 2.3

(
Achterberg et al. 2001
;
Ellison & Double 2002
;
Spitkovsky 2008
;
Curran et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2015
)
. This demonstrates that the spectral index is incredibly steep and is strongly indicative of intrinsic dust extinction
(
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
, see also)
in addition to the large component in the Milky Way which was already corrected for
(
Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011
)
.

Therefore, we consider the closure relations utilizing the temporal decay index. This depends on the slope of the surrounding environment’s density profile of
\rho(r)\propto r^{-k}
, where
k=0
refers to a homogeneous medium and
k=2
refers to a wind environment
(
Dai & Lu 1998
;
Chevalier & Li 2000
)
. We start by considering the case of a pre-jet-break afterglow. Assuming a homogeneous medium, if the UVOIR data satisfy
\nu_{m}<\nu<\nu_{c}
, where
\nu_{m}
is the injection frequency, this value of
\alpha
implies an electron powerlaw index of
p=3.44^{+0.07}_{-0.08}
. In a wind environment, this instead yields
p=2.77^{+0.07}_{-0.08}
. If the UVOIR data lies above the cooling frequency, instead we have
p=3.11^{+0.07}_{-0.08}
, independent of the surrounding environment. While these values of
p
are high, they are not inconsistent with the broad range of values found for GRB afterglows.

If we instead consider a post-jet-break afterglow from a non-spreading jet
(
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, for
\nu_{m}<\nu<\nu_{c}
, we find values of
p=2.44^{+0.07}_{-0.08}
and
p=2.11^{+0.07}_{-0.08}
for a homogeneous and wind environment, respectively. As it is unlikely that the near-infrared lies above the cooling frequency, we consider only this solution. As both of these values are typical of GRB afterglows, we cannot differentiate between these environments based on the simple temporal fit alone. Specifically, the inclusion of an intrinsic dust component is required to derive the reasonable range of intrinsic spectral slopes, and therefore aid in constraining
p
.

Therefore, we used
VegasAfterglow

(
Wang et al. 2025
)
to fit the observed UVOIR data to assess whether it is consistent with synchrotron emission arising from the forward shock of a top-hat jet
(
Sari et al. 1998
;
Granot & Sari 2002
)
. We do this using
bilby

(
Ashton et al. 2019
)
with nested-sampling using
dynesty

(
Speagle 2020
)
. We assumed a wind medium (
k=2
), as favored in other recent works
(
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
, and fit for
A_{*}
, the number density of the wind environment measured at a radius of
10^{18}
cm. We assumed an on-axis viewing angle,
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}=0
, and fit for the jet’s core half-opening angle,
\theta_{\textrm{c}}
. The free parameters in the fit are the isotropic-equivalent kinetic energy at the jet’s core
E_{\textrm{kin,iso}}
, the initial bulk Lorentz factor at the jet’s core
\Gamma_{0}
, density
A_{*}
, jet’s half-opening angle
\theta_{\textrm{c}}
, slope of the electron’s energy distribution
p
, and the electron (
\epsilon_{e}
) and magnetic field (
\epsilon_{B}
) energy fractions. We included an additional intrinsic dust component to our modeling using the Milky Way dust law reported in
Fitzpatrick & Massa 2007
, which is parametrized by
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}
with fixed
R_{\textrm{V}}=3.1
. This dust component
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}
was uniformly sampled between 0 and 10 magnitudes. We did not include UVOIR upper limits in the fit, and focused on the
H
and
K_{s}
-band detections (Figure
2
. We supplemented the UVOIR dataset (Table
1
) with additional X-ray and radio data
(
O’Connor et al. 2025
;
Levan et al. 2025
)
.

We performed three
VegasAfterglow
fits to the full multi-wavelength (X-ray to near-infrared to radio) lightcurve using
bilby

(
Ashton et al. 2019
)
with nested-sampling using
dynesty

(
Speagle 2020
)
. The first fit was ran with the exclusion of lateral jet spreading effects and the second fit included jet spreading
(see
Wang et al. 2025
, for details)
. A third fit for a non-spreading jet was performed with the added requirement that
\Gamma_{0}\theta_{\textrm{c}}>1
such that the spherical jet approximation holds during the deceleration phase
(
Rhoads 1997
;
Rhoads 1999
, e.g.,)
. The fit results are shown in the corner plots in Figures
8
,
9
, and
10
. The model comparison to the observed multi-wavelength lightcurve is shown in Figure
3
for both the non-spreading (without the requirement of
\Gamma_{0}\theta_{\textrm{c}}>1
) and spreading models. We find a Bayes factor (BF) strongly in favor of the model without jet spreading enabled of
\log_{10}\mathrm{BF}=26.8
. We discuss these solutions in §
IV.3
.

III.2
Host Galaxy SED Modeling

The host galaxy of GRB 250702B is extremely red and was only detected in the
zHK
filters (Figure
1
).
Due to the diffuse nature of the host galaxy (Figure
5
) the standard point source upper limits derived in other filters are misleading, thus we exclude the
i
and
J
band upper limits from the model fitting. We choose to include
g
and
r
band upper limits (Table
1
) as they are half a mag deeper and thus more likely to still be constraining on a highly extincted SED.
We modeled the spectral energy distribution (SED, top panel of Figure
4
) using the
zHK
host detection (Table
2
) with the flexible stellar population (FSPS) code
(
Conroy et al. 2009
)
. We utilized
prospector

(
Leja et al. 2017
;
Johnson et al. 2019
;
Johnson et al. 2021
)
in accordance
with the methods previously outlined in
O’Connor et al. 2022
. The model fit was performed with
emcee

(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
using 246 walkers for 2048 iterations.
We adopted a
Chabrier 2003
initial mass function, a
Calzetti et al. 2000
dust law, and a delayed-
\tau
star formation history.
We fit for the total mass
M_{\textrm{gal}}
, the age of the host galaxy
t_{\rm age}
, e-folding timescale
\tau
, metallicity
Z
, and intrinsic dust
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}
. We adopt uniform bounded priors with ranges of 0.01–5.71 Gyr for
t_{\rm age}
, 0.1–10.0 Gyr for
\tau
,
-1.8
to
+1.2
dex (relative to solar) for log(
Z/Z_{\odot}
), and 0–25 mag for dust optical depth. We then convert these into posterior distributions for total stellar mass, mass-weighted stellar age, and star formation rate (SFR, Fig
4
, bottom panels).
Due to the sparsity of our observations we poorly constrained all parameters except for total stellar mass and dust, for which we find a massive (
M_{*}=10^{10.66\pm 0.15}\ M_{\odot}
) and dusty (
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}=0.88^{+0.47}_{-0.45}
mag) host. A corner plot showing the posterior distributions is presented in Figure
11
. We note that without the addition of redder (rest frame) wavelengths in the fit the inferred mass may be lower than the true stellar mass of the galaxy.

III.3
Morphological Analysis of the Host Galaxy

HST
observations of the host galaxy of GRB 250702B revealed a peculiar, disturbed morphology. We performed a full morphological analysis using both parametric and non-parametric fitting techniques to investigate the underlying features in the galaxy’s light.

We began with a parametric analysis using
GALFIT

(
Peng et al. 2002
)
. We produced a bad-pixel segmentation map using
Photutils

(
Bradley et al. 2024
)
to mask out nearby objects that are unrelated to the host galaxy, and a PSF using the
Spike
software
(
Polzin 2025
)
. As the morphology of the galaxy clearly shows two separate components, we performed a fit using two Sérsic profiles (Figure
5
). We derive a half-light radius of the upper (North) Sérsic of
r_{50}=0.31^{\prime\prime}\pm 0.08^{\prime\prime}
and the lower (South) Sérsic of
r_{50}=0.30^{\prime\prime}\pm 0.11^{\prime\prime}
. The Sérsic indices are
0.56\pm 0.41
and
0.49\pm 0.44
, respectively, indicative of disky galaxies.

To further investigate the host’s nature, we performed a non-parametric analysis using the
Morfometryka
software
(
Ferrari et al. 2015
)
. This makes use of the three-dimensional “CAS” volume, representing the the concentration (C), asymmetry (A), and clumpiness (S) of a galaxy’s light profile, to classify a given galaxy’s morphology
(
Conselice et al. 2000
;
Conselice 2003

.
For this galaxy, we derive a concentration of C
=2.56_{-0.06}^{+0.05}
and an asymmetry of
A=0.90_{-0.05}^{+0.05}

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Constraints on rebrightening

Continued nIR observations extending to 40.67 (19.98) days in the observer (rest) frame post-
Fermi
detection show no evidence of rebrightening (Figure
2
).
Our reported
HST
F160W photometry is consistent with a continued power-law decay out to 12.53 (6.15) days in the observer (rest) frame (Figure
2
). This is in contrast to the non-power-law decay in the combined
H
and
F160W
lightcurve reported by
Levan et al. 2025
, where no attempt was made to remove contaminating host flux from the
HST
measurement. Similarly, the last two
K
-band VLT epochs in Figure
2
appear nonlinear in log-log scale but are similarly contaminated by host flux (see
Levan et al. 2025
for a discussion about removing host flux from the VLT photometry). These points are not included in our jet modeling (§
III.1
).
The final observation that we acquired, using the Magellan telescope, allows us to place only limited constraints on the rebrightening of the nIR transient. We are able to rule out rebrightening below an absolute magnitude of
K_{s}=-22.32
AB mag at
\Delta t\approx 20
days in the rest frame.
This is insufficiently deep to rule out rebrightening akin to either the well-studied optical jetted TDE AT2022cmc
(
Andreoni et al. 2022
;
Pasham et al. 2023
)
or the prototypical GRB supernova SN1998bw
(
Galama et al. 1998
)
.
AT2022cmc was observed in
K_{s}
at rest frame
\Delta t=

3.02
,
9.02
and
13.57
days after the first ZTF detections with observed absolute magnitudes of
K_{s}=-24.01
,
K_{s}=-21.88
, and
K_{s}=-21.54
AB mag respectively. Given GRB 250702B’s slightly lower redshift
(
Andreoni et al. 2022
, AT2022cmc occurred at
z=1.19325
;)
and assuming the SED of AT2022cmc’s slow component takes roughly the form of a 30,000 K blackbody around a rest wavelength of 1,000 Å, we would expect these values to be
\sim+0.3
mags dimmer, making our late time observations
\sim 1.1
mag too shallow to constrain this potentially slowly fading component of the UVOIR lightcurve.
SN1998bw occurred at a very low redshift of
z=0.0085
and peaked in the
I
band (the reddest wavelength for which photometry is available) at an absolute magnitude of
-18.82
AB mag
(
Blanton & Roweis 2007
, converted from Vega according to)
at
\Delta t\approx 19
days after the associated GRB. Accounting for redshift, but not extinction or K-correction, this is roughly equivalent to an
H
band observation of
H\approx 25.5
AB mag for an object at
z=1.036
.
Based on a spectrum obtained with the Sofi IR spectrograph on the New Technology Telescope
(
Patat et al. 2001
, NTT/Sofi,
\Delta t\approx 23
days after the associated GRB;)
and spectra from the Danish
1.54
-metre telescopes Danish Faint Object Spectrograph and Camera
(
Patat et al. 2001
, DFOSC,
\Delta t\approx 19
and
21
days after the associated GRB;)
we infer that a constraining
K_{s}
observation for a 1998bw-like supernova at the location of GRB 250702B would require observations deeper than
K_{s}\approx 25.5
AB mag.

IV.2
Dust extinction from jet modeling

Our observations and analysis of the nIR transient further support its relativistic nature (§
III.1
and
IV.3
), and therefore its association with the high-energy detections of GRB 250702B
(
Neights et al. 2025c
;
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
. The bright peak absolute magnitude, rapid fading, and color evolution not only are well fit by relativistic jet models (Section
III.1
), but are inconsistent with other common classes of extragalactic transients in the optical/nIR
(
Kasliwal 2011
;
Ho et al. 2022
, see peak magnitude vs. evolution time-scale plots for optical transients in, e.g.,)
.

The source is very reddened due to high extinction, as also discussed by
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
;
Gompertz et al. 2025
. After correcting for Galactic extinction, we find that both the host galaxy and the jet models need to account for a significant amount of dust. The estimate for the extinction that we infer from the jet modeling (
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{GRB}}=5.43^{+0.53}_{-0.47}
mag) is significantly larger than the value we obtain from host galaxy SED fitting (
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}=0.88^{+0.47}_{-0.45}
mag). This discrepancy suggests that the GRB was produced in a dense, dusty circumstellar environment, or a thick lane of dust is present in the host galaxy on the line of sight, between the observer and the transient
(
Levan et al. 2025
)
. The line of sight extinction is comparable to the dustiest of those observed in a set of eight optically dark GRBs discussed in
(
Krühler et al. 2011
,
A_{\textrm{V}}\gtrsim 3
mag;)
and to the heavily obscured relativisitc TDE Sw J1644+57
(
Burrows et al. 2011b
,
A_{\textrm{V}}\approx 4.5
mag;)
, see also
O’Connor et al. 2025
.

IV.3
Opening angle and collimation-corrected energetics

As discussed in §
III.1
, there exist two quantitatively different solutions for the afterglow model (see Figure
3
) depending on the inclusion or exclusion of lateral jet spreading effects.
The non-spreading jet fit (Figure
8
) requires a narrow jet such that the lightcurve is already in a post-jet-break phase prior to the start of the earliest observations at
\sim

0.5
days from discovery. This provides a better match to the observed temporal slope and the required value of
p
as noted above. This post-jet-break solution was also found by
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
;
Gompertz et al. 2025
. However, when including lateral jet spreading, the post-jet-break decay of
\approx

t^{-p}

(
Rhoads 1999
;
Sari et al. 1999
)
is too steep to describe the observed temporal decline of
\alpha

\approx

-1.8
found for both the optical and X-ray data. Therefore, the solution identified in the presence of lateral jet spreading (Figure
9
) requires that the jet-break occur either during or after our latest data point. The pre-jet-break temporal slope of the model is therefore slightly shallower (
\alpha

\approx

-1.45
for
p

\approx

2.3
) than the observed steep X-ray and near-infrared decay
\alpha

\approx

-1.8
. Instead of identifying a steeper value of
p
and placing the jet-break at
>65
days, the fit using a spreading jet places the jet break earlier (at
\sim

1-3
days) and underproduces the late-time
Chandra
X-ray detections
(
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
. In this case, while the jet is not extremely wide (as would be required for a
>65
day jet-break; see
O’Connor et al. 2023
), it is significantly wider compared to the non-spreading jet solution and therefore also requires a higher collimation-corrected energy. That said, the inferred opening angles and collimation-corrected energies (Figures
8
and
9
) are consistent with the range observed from classical long GRBs
(
Cenko et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
O’Connor et al. 2023
)
.

While at present the non-spreading post-jet-break solution provides a better fit to the data, there is no clear break observed in the lightcurve. As such, both solutions (wide jet versus narrow jet) for the afterglow are potentially valid (§
III.1
). While our afterglow modeling with a spreading jet (Figures
3
and
9
) does not identify the late jet-break solution, an additional fit requiring a late jet-break (
>65
days) was successfully able to model the lightcurve. This fit requires a steeper value of
p\approx 2.6
(with higher
A_{\textrm{V}}\approx 8.5
mag), but underpredicts the observed
H
-band flux, though a different dust law may account for this. Qualitatively the fit is viable and as the correct afterglow solution (wide jet versus narrow jet) has significant consequences for the progenitor’s requirements, additional late-time X-ray observations and continued radio monitoring can constrain the presence of the onset of any jet-break in the lightcurve and possibly differentiate between these two possible solutions.

IV.4
Constraints on the jet’s Lorentz factor

An additional critical constraint on the progenitor of GRB 250702B comes from the inferred bulk Lorentz factor
\Gamma_{0}
, which is found to be highly relativistic. Observing the end of the jet’s deceleration phase is required to determine the exact Lorentz factor
(
Sari & Piran 1999
;
Molinari et al. 2007
;
Ghisellini et al. 2010
)
, but an upper limit to the deceleration time can also provide useful constraints
(
O’Connor et al. 2020
;
O’Connor et al. 2024
)
. As we do not detect the deceleration of the afterglow (and therefore have an upper limit to the time of jet deceleration), the results of our fit can be considered a lower limit to the true Lorentz factor, as lower Lorentz factors will lead to later deceleration.
The posterior distribution of GRB 250702B’s initial Lorentz factor (
\log\Gamma_{0}=1.25^{+0.16}_{-0.12}
) in Figure
8
favors ultrarelativistic blastwave with Lorentz factor
\gtrsim

10
.
Enforcing the condition
\Gamma_{0}\theta_{\textrm{c}}>1
to prevent the jet from spreading laterally during the coasting phase requires a significantly larger Lorentz factor (
\log\Gamma_{0}=2.61^{+0.27}_{-0.41}
; see Figure
10
).
We note that the detection of high energy
>1
MeV photons by
Fermi
sets an independent requirement of
\Gamma_{0}\gtrsim 50-80

(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
.
Measurements of
\Gamma_{0}
of observed from relativistic jetted TDEs AT2022cmc and Swift J1644+57 lie around
\sim 8-20

(
Zauderer et al. 2011b
;
Metzger et al. 2012
;
Andreoni et al. 2022
;
Rhodes et al. 2023
)
, which depart from the observed population of GRBs which generally have far larger Lorentz factors
(
Liang et al. 2010
;
Ghirlanda et al. 2012
;
Ghirlanda et al. 2018
, e.g.,
\sim
hundreds;)
.
As we cannot rule out an earlier deceleration of the jet, the measurements can be treated as a lower bound to the Lorentz factor. Higher Lorentz factors clearly disfavor a TDE-like progenitor scenario and are more consistent with a GRB-like progenitor (§
IV.6
).

IV.5
Host galaxy properties and population comparisons

(
Lin et al. 2018
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Jin et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025

.
In either case, GRB 250702B is not an outlier and is potentially consistent with either a massive star or an offset IMBH progenitor.

Turning to the host parameters from SED modeling (Sec

) we compare the inferred stellar mass as a function of both redshift and extinction to long GRB hosts. In the left panel of Figure

In the right panel of Figure

.

In Section

As such, the morphology of GRB 250702B’s host is a significant outlier compared to observed GRB hosts.
The morphology of GRB 250702B’s host also differs significantly from the host of the relativistic TDE Sw J1644+57

.

.

IV.6
Summary of the possible progenitors

The gamma-ray duration of GRB 250702B suggest a similar origin to previously studied ultralong GRBs. While the core-collapse of a massive star could explain a
\sim 25
ks
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
prompt emission, the observed long-lived, multi-day X-ray emission is incompatible with the standard collapsar models
(
O’Connor et al. 2025
)
.
The off-center location of GRB 250702B is naturally consistent with stellar mass or intermediate mass black hole engines from several proposed scenarios: collapsars, compact object–helium star mergers
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
, micro-TDEs
(
Beniamini 2025
)
or IMBH-TDEs
(
Granot 2025
;
Levan et al. 2025
)
.
An association with a supernova would be expected in collapsars and some versions of micro-TDE origins involving a natal-kick-induced TDE after stellar collapse in one component of a field binary star system.
An IMBH-TDE would also potentially require a supernova association. In this scenario, in the classic ‘frozen-in’ approximation
(
Lacy et al. 1982
;
Rees 1988
)
energy dissipation takes place primarily at the tidal radius
2
2

2

If, instead, energy dissipation is dominated at the peri-center (for comparison of this with the ‘frozen-in’ assumption, see
Steinberg et al. 2019
), and the penetration factor
\beta
is rather large
\beta\gtrsim 5
, then the fallback time shortens significantly (as
\beta^{-3}
, see
Beniamini 2025
) and disruption of a main sequence star by an IMBH becomes possible (albeit geometrically fine-tuned).
and the duration of fallback from the most inner-bound orbit is
t_{\rm fb}\sim 10^{6}(M_{\rm BH}/10^{4}M_{\odot})^{1/2}R_{*}^{3/2}M_{*}^{-1}\mbox{ s}
. As a result, matching the duration with that of GRB 250702B requires a smaller physical size of the disrupted star which can be realized if the star is a white dwarf. In this situation, the compression of a white dwarf during the TDE process is expected to be sufficient to trigger a thermonuclear supernova
(
Rosswog et al. 2009
)
.
Helium star mergers are also expected to have associated supernovae, though significantly dimmer than those associated with collapsars
(
Neights et al. 2025c
)
.
Finally, the absence of a supernova would support a dynamically-induced micro-TDE.
At the time of writing, the large redshift and significant dust extinction in GRB 250702B preclude a strong conclusion regarding potential supernova association.

V
Conclusion

We presented deep optical and nIR observations of the high energy transient GRB 250702B – the longest duration GRB ever observed – whose extreme multi-wavelength properties cannot be placed firmly among known transient classes

(
Levan et al. 2025
;
O’Connor et al. 2025
;
Neights et al. 2025c
;
Gompertz et al. 2025
)
.
Deep observations from a suite of optical/nIR facilities — including Blanco, Gemini, Wendelstein, Keck, and Magellan telescopes — combined with additional photometry from
Levan et al. 2025
, revealed a highly-obscured, rapidly-fading transient detectable only in the near-infrared. Our late-time observations constrain a luminous rebrightening
\sim

20
days after the GRB in the rest frame, but deeper observations are needed to rule out rebrightening akin to the relativistic TDE AT2022cmc’s slow thermal component
(
Andreoni et al. 2022
)
or SN 1998bw
(
Patat et al. 2001
)
given the high redshift and large extinction in the line of sight.

Using a redshift of
z=1.036
obtained from JWST observations
(
Gompertz et al. 2025
)
we modeled the decline of the nIR transient and found it to be consistent with forward shock emission originating from a non-spreading top-hat jet with a Lorentz factor of
\log\Gamma_{0}=1.25^{+0.16}_{-0.12}
. After correcting for Galactic extinction, we estimated the intrinsic line of sight extinction to be
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{GRB}}=5.67^{+0.3}_{-0.33}
mag at
z=1.036
.
While this high extinction is consistent with both the most obscured GRBs
(
Krühler et al. 2011
)
and TDEs
(
Burrows et al. 2011b
)
, the Lorentz factor measurement remains model-dependent: the value derived both without lateral spreading and with lateral spreading (
\Gamma_{0}\approx 18
) aligns with relativistic TDEs
(
Zauderer et al. 2011b
;
Metzger et al. 2012
;
Andreoni et al. 2022
)
, while enforcing additional constraints (
\Gamma_{0}\theta_{\textrm{c}}>1
) on a non-spreading jet yields a value (
\Gamma_{0}\approx 400
) more typical of GRB collapsars
(
Liang et al. 2010
;
Ghirlanda et al. 2018
)
.

Keck, Gemini, and
HST
images enabled the detection of the host galaxy in the
z
,
H
,
K_{s}
, and
F160W
filters. GRB 250702B’s galactocentric offset is consistent with both the population of observed long GRBs and the three known off-nuclear TDEs.
Using the host galaxy SED modeling tools within
Prospector
, we found the galaxy to be massive (
10^{10.66\pm 0.15}\,M_{\odot}
), dusty (
A_{\textrm{V},\mathrm{host}}=0.88^{+0.47}_{-0.45}
mag), and highly asymmetric (
A=0.90
in CAS space).
The host’s inferred stellar mass is consistent with the masses of long GRB hosts and reminiscent of the
\sim 10^{10.9}M_{\odot}
host masses observed in all 3 known off-nuclear TDEs
(
Lin et al. 2018
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Jin et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025
)
. The differential between the extinction of the nIR counterpart and the host suggests that GRB 250702B originated in a dense circumstellar environment or was obscured by a prominent dust cloud within the host.
The galaxy’s high asymmetry makes it an outlier among long GRB hosts and suggests the possibility of a major merger which could support the TDE hypothesis.

Multiple scenarios remain viable to explain the nature of GRB 250702B. The jet modeling highlights the nIR emission accompanying GRB 250702B as consistent with both a collapsar and a TDE scenarios. Our data are also consistent with exotic models that could explain the origin of GRB 250702B, such as micro-TDEs
(
Perets et al. 2016
;
Beniamini 2025
)
, TDEs from IMBHs
(
Granot 2025
)
, and helium star merger
(
Fryer & Woosley 1998
;
Neights et al. 2025c
)
. The multi-wavelength signatures of the longest-duration GRB 250702B make it a unique puzzle which may originate from a novel progenitor.
