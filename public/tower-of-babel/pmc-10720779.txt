Perioperative and long-term survival outcomes of pancreatectomy with arterial resection in borderline resectable or locally advanced pancreatic cancer following neoadjuvant therapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kang Xue, Xing Huang, Pengcheng Zhao, Yi Zhang, Bole Tian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Pancreatic cancer frequently involves the surrounding major arteries, preventing surgeons from making a radical excision. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) can lessen the size of local tumors and eliminate potential micrommetastases. However, systematic and evidence-based recommendations for the treatment of arterial resection (AR) after NAT in pancreatic cancer are scarce.Method:A computerized search of the Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library databases, and Clinicaltrials was performed to identify studies reporting the outcomes of patients who underwent pancreatectomy with AR and NAT for pancreatic cancer. Studies that reported perioperative and/or long-term results after pancreatectomy with AR and NAT were eligible for inclusion. The quality of the evidence was assessed with Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Form of bias tool. Data were pooled and analyzed by Stata 14.0 software.Result:Nine studies with an overall sample size of 215 met our eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. All studies were retrospective studies, and the methodological quality was moderate. The pooled morbidity and mortality rates were 51% (95% CI: 41–61%; I²= 0.0%) and 2% (95% CI: 0–0.08; I²=33.3%), respectively. Meta-analysis showed that the overall R0 resection rate was 79% (CI: 70–86%, I²=15.5%). Comparative data on R0 rates of patients who underwent pancreatectomy with and without NAT showed a significant difference in favor of the former group with moderate statistical heterogeneity (Relative risk=1.21; 95% CI: 0.776–1.915; I²=48.0%). The median 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of patients who had AR were 92.3% (range: 72.7–100%), 64.8% (range: 25–78.8%), 51.6% (range: 16.7–63.6%), and 14% (range: 0–41.1%), respectively. Data on median progression-free survival ranged from 5.25 to 36.3 months, and the median overall survival ranged from 17 to 44.9 months.Conclusions:Pancreatectomy with major AR following NAT has the potential to enhance the survival rate of patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer involving the arteries by achieving R0 resection, despite a significant risk of postoperative complications. However, to validate the feasibility and effectiveness of this procedure, prospective controlled studies are necessary to address limitations arising from small sample sizes and potential biases inherent in retrospective studies.

Pancreatic cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer death in the United States, and the 5-year survival rate remains ~10%1. Pancreatic cancer frequently involves major arteries in its vicinity, such as the celiac axis, superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and/or common hepatic artery (HA). This presents a significant challenge for surgeons performing radical excision. Patients with affected vessels are typically diagnosed with borderline resectable pancreatic cancer (BRPC) or locally advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC)2,3. Researchers have explored various treatment methods for BRPC and LAPC4, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, ablation, targeted therapy, and immunological therapy. Nevertheless, the administration of the aforementioned innovative therapies did not result in a significant extension of patient survival.

Radical resection remains the only way to attain long-term survival5. Excision of veins and arteries is commonly considered essential for achieving complete resection (R0). Current literature indicates that patients who undergo venous resection have comparable mortality rates to those without this intervention. Moreover, venous resection can augment the R0 rate and enhance the long-term prognosis6. Several meta-analyses analyzed the pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) with venous resection and indicated that the combination mentioned above achieved a higher rate of R0 and a favorable prognosis6–8, which means that vein resection is not cited as a contraindication. However, physicians have always debated the resection and reconstruction of major arteries. Fortner and his colleagues first proposed a total pancreatectomy combined with resection of the celiac artery (CA) to complete a gross resection of the tumor9. However, poor long-term survival and increased morbidity have led to the withdrawal of support for arterial resection (AR) at the same time as PD. A meta-analysis also suggested that AR was associated with a significantly increased risk for perioperative mortality (odds ratio (OR)=5.04; 95% CI: 2.69–9.45) and poor survival at 1 year (OR=0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.78) and 3 years (OR =0.39; 95% CI: 0.17–0.86) compared with patients without AR10. Due to the challenges associated with extensive arterial and neural invasion beyond the scope of preoperative imaging and intraoperative exploration, aggressive surgical intervention may not result in a satisfactory outcome with complete tumor resection.

The researchers have observed that systemic neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has the potential to effectively reduce the size of local tumors and eradicate possible metastasis11. Recently, chemotherapy regimens, including nab-paclitaxel (NAB) and FOLFIRINOX, have significantly improved overall survival (OS), leading to an emphasis on NAT for pancreatic cancer patients12,13. Based on FOLFIRINX regimens, multiple centers have shown that up to 60% of BRPC and LAPC patients have access to surgery11,14–16. It is worth noting that a considerable number of patients who received NATs were still radiologically unresectable following re-evaluation through preoperative imaging17. The median survival time for these patients, who exclusively underwent chemoradiation, was a mere 13.4 months18. The study conducted by Liang et al. demonstrates that a combination of radical resection, AR, and small intestine transplantation following NAT yields a high R0 rate of 94.4% and OS durations of 21.4 months19. The median survival time of the patients was significantly extended16,20–26. Then, could patients with vascular invasion after the treatment of NATs (no progression and able to tolerate surgery simultaneously) be suggested to undergo aggressive surgery to achieve a better survival outcome? It is essential to evaluate the postoperative outcome and survival outcome of AR.

Over the past decade, a significant number of studies have been conducted on the AR of pancreatic cancer patients after NAT. However, systematic and evidence-based recommendations for the treatment of AR after NAT in pancreatic cancer are scarce. Herein, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the perioperative outcome and long-term survival of patients who underwent resection for pancreatic cancer with combined NAT and AR.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this review was defined a priori and registered online in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews with an amendment made on 13 January 2023. This work has been reported in line with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) (Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/B51) (Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/JS9/B52) and AMSTAR (Assessing the methodological quality of systematic reviews) Guidelines (Supplemental Digital Content 5, http://links.lww.com/JS9/B53)27,28.

Literature Search

A computerized search of the Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library databases, and Clinicaltrials was performed on studies published between May 1973 and October 2022. The following search terms were used: ʻarterial resectionʼ, ʻhepatic arterial resectionʼ, ʻSMA resectionʼ, ʻsuperior mesenteric artery resectionʼ, ʻproper hepatic artery resectionʼ, ʻceliac axis resectionʼ, ʻVascular resectionsʼ, ʻVascular involvementʼ, ʻvascular graftingʼ, ʻvascular transplantationʼ, ʻvascular transplantʼ, ʻvasotransplantationʼ, ʻneoadjuvantʼ, ʻpreoperativeʼ, ʻchemoradiotherapyʼ, ʻradiotherapyʼ, and ʻpancreatic cancerʼ. There were no restrictions on dates, sex, or age, and type of surgery. In addition, the reference lists of relevant articles were manually searched to find other potentially eligible studies. The search was restricted to studies published in English. The last search was performed on 1 January, 2023. The titles and abstracts of the search findings were screened for potentially eligible studies. Full articles, which were considered relevant, were obtained for detailed evaluation, and eligible studies were included.

Study selection criteria

All studies included in the meta-analysis were selected according to the following inclusion criteria based on the PICOS framework: P: Patients with a definite diagnosis of BRPC or LAPC, I: Patients with preoperative NAT underwent pancreatectomy and AR, with or without venous resection, C: Patients mentioned above without NAT or AR, O: Surgical and oncology outcomes of perioperative patients were reported, and S: Prospective or retrospective studies.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: abstracts, case reports, editorials, letters, systematic reviews, and comments, studies with incomplete data, studies that enrolled the overlapped or same population, non-English studies, and articles that dealt with resection of arteries other than the common HA, proper HA, CA, left and/or right HA, and the SMA.

Study selection

Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility that were identified in the electronic database searches. Trial registers and conference abstracts were hand searched and assessed for eligibility by one author. For records that were considered relevant according to title and abstract screening, full-text articles were obtained, and inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were defined in advance and included the following (1): perioperative morbidity and mortality (90 days) (2); the rate of R0; and (3) OS. The predefined secondary outcomes were reoperation rates, blood loss, operating time, and hospital stay.

Data collection and synthesis

Two authors independently extracted the following data from each identified study: first author, year of publication, study period, sample size, baseline characteristics of the study cohort, kind of vascular resections, vascular reconstruction techniques, NAT, response rate, preoperative therapy, and study design. We documented data on the following outcomes: perioperative morbidity and mortality, the rate of R0, OS, reoperation rates, blood loss, operating time, and hospital stay. The response rate was determined by calculating the percentage of cases where NAT resulted in either a partial or complete response, as indicated by preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans and evaluated according to the updated Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines version 1.1 (CR, complete response; PR, partial response; SD, stable disease; PD, progression disease)29. Data were extracted to an internally piloted data extraction spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft). If no information regarding the outcomes was clearly reported, outcomes were visually extracted from Kaplan—Meier curves whenever applicable and feasible.

Subgroup analysis

We performed subgroup analyses to evaluate the impact of institutional experience (sample size of patients with AR and NAT), type of vessel excision (CA vs. CA/SMA/HA resection) and study quality (risk of bias) on the outcomes. To facilitate these analyses, continuous data were dichotomized based on their median value.

Quality assessment

We used the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Form for Cohort Studies (NOS) to assess the quality of the included nonrandomized articles30. NOS includes three domains: selection, comparability of study groups, and assessment of outcomes. Each study was evaluated by two independent researchers and rated on a 0–9 scale. Afterward, a point was given to each accomplished item to classify the studies as high-quality (score 7–9), moderate quality (score 4–6), or poor quality (score 0–3)31.

Statistical analysis and analysis of heterogeneity

Meta-analysis was conducted using Stata 14.0 software (https://www.stata.com/stata14/). The heterogeneity of the results was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the Higgins I² statistic32. A Q test P-value <0.1 or I²>50% was considered significant heterogeneity, and a random-effects model was applied to estimate the pooled results. When heterogeneity was not significant (Q test P-value >0.1 or I²<50%), a fixed-effect model was adopted. To reduce and explain the heterogeneity of the results among studies, meta-regression analysis and sensitivity analysis were applied. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P-values less than 0.05 were defined as statistically significant. Publication bias was assessed by visually assessing the symmetry of funnel plots combined with Begg’s and Egger’s tests.

Result

Search results

Our initial literature search identified 1749 references. After removing duplicates, 1226 citations remained. Following title and abstract screening, 1202 references were not considered relevant because they did not meet our inclusion criteria and were consequently excluded. Hence, 24 records underwent full-text review (Fig. 1). Of these 24 records, 13 references were excluded because they did not report relevant data. One study was dismissed because of overlapping patients, and 1 study was dismissed due to an inadequate population. Finally, 9 retrospective studies with an overall sample size of 215 met our eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Table 1)16,21–26,33,34. The number of patients per study ranged from 10 to 57, with a median of 24 patients. No data from any RCT were identified. Preoperative therapy and resection strategy

In this study, the majority of patients received chemotherapy alone with gemcitabine being the predominant choice (Table 2). Only three studies reported concurrent use of radiotherapy, among which Amano et al. utilized radiotherapy13 and Baumgartner et al. reported the use of SBRT3 and RT8 in all patients. The tumor response to NAT was assessed according to RECIST 1.1 criteria, with the majority of patients demonstrating either a partial response or stable disease. Among the 112 patients who exhibited a response to neoadjuvant treatment, only five experienced progressive disease. Distal pancreatectomy with celiac axis resection (DP-CAR) was the most frequently utilized resection method. Portal vein removal rates reported in eight studies ranging from 30 to 69.2%. The duration of surgery was recorded in six studies, with the longest operation lasting 567±132 min and the highest amount of intraoperative blood loss reported by Amano being 1311±604 ml.

Main analysis

Perioperative morbidity

According to the Clavien–Dindo classification35, perioperative morbidity rates of patients with AR and NAT were reported in six studies including 116 patients and ranged from 30 to 100% (median 54%)16,22,24–26,33. The pooled morbidity rate was 60% (95% CI: 40–79%; I²=75.9%) (Fig. 2). The forest plot identified one study with an inclusion period of 1996–2007 as the primary source of heterogeneity22 (Fig. 3). The exclusion of the study conducted by Stitzenber et al. resulted in a reduction of the adjusted morbidity rate to 51%, which was accompanied by a significant decrease in heterogeneity (0.0%) and a slight improvement in the precision of the 95% CI (95% CI: 41–61%). (Fig. 4). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the results, while both the funnel plot and Egger’s test indicated no publication bias (P=0.774>0.05). Of the six studies, two studies reported the proportion of complications (Clavien—Dindo > grade III) to be 42 and 27%, respectively21,23. The pooled rate was 38% (95% CI: 23–54%; I²=0.0%) (Fig. 5). Eight studies reported the incidence of POPF, with rates ranging from 0 to 45%, and a pooled estimate of 25% (95% CI: 15–37%; I²= 53.8%) was obtained (Supplementary Material 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/B49). Six studies reported the incidence of postoperative delayed gastric emptying, with rates ranging from 0% to 30.8%, and a pooled estimate of 7% (95% CI: 0.0–18%; I²=47.3%) was calculated (Supplementary Material 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/B50). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings, while neither the funnel plot nor Egger’s test provided evidence of publication bias (P>0.05). Subgroup analyses indicated that studies with a sample size greater than 30 and study quality greater than or equal to 7 exhibited slightly lower morbidity rates (52.9%, 95% CI: 40.9–64.6%; 56.3%, 95% CI: 26.8–94.6%)16,24 compared to those with smaller samples and lower quality (64.3%, 95% CI: 26.8–94.6%; 61.1%, 95% CI: 34.1–85.2%)22,25,26,33 (Table 5).

Mortality

The mortality rate was defined as the total in-hospital death rate. The numbers of patients with 60-day or 90-day mortality were reported in seven of the nine studies16,21–25,33. Among 145 patients, only six patients died after pancreatomy and artery resection, and the exact reasons were not reported precisely. The highest mortality was 18%, as reported by Baumgartner33, and 90-day mortality did not occur in three studies16,23,25. The pooled mortality rate was 2% (95% CI: 0–0.08; I²=33.3%) (Fig. 6). In this study, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to further estimate the impact of combining mortality. However, no significant reduction in heterogeneity or substantial alteration in statistical outcomes was observed upon the gradual exclusion of each study, indicating the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. Furthermore, both the funnel plot and Egger’s test demonstrated no evidence of publication bias (P=0.234>0.05). In the subgroup analysis, four articles with a sample size greater than 30 was included to investigate postoperative mortality16,21,23,24 (Table 5). The results showed that the mortality rate (0.9%; 95% CI: 0.0–4.5%) was lower compared to those with smaller sample sizes (9.7%; 95% CI: 0.2–26.6%)22,25,33.

R0 rate

R0 rates ranged from 50 to 92% (median 81%) in the seven studies with available data on 148 patients with AR16,21–24,26. Meta-analysis showed that the overall resection rate of R0 was 79% (95% CI: 70–86%, I²=15.5%) (Fig. 7). Visual examination of the funnel plot showed basic symmetry, revealing potential evidence of publication bias, which was confirmed by both Egger’s test (P=0.0.876) and Begg’s test (P=0.879) (Fig. 8). Interestingly, the R0 rates reported in high-quality literature (78.7%, 95% CI: 68.2–87.8%)21,23,24 were comparable to those reported in low-quality literature (78.9%, 95% CI: 61.3–92.7%)16,22,26,33. Comparative data on the R0 rates of patients who underwent pancreatectomy with and without NATs showed a significant difference in favor of the former group with moderate statistical heterogeneity (RR=1.21; 95% CI: 0.776–1.915; I²=48.0%) (Fig. 9)16,21,23. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to further estimate the impact of combining mortality. However, no significant reduction in heterogeneity or substantial alteration in statistical outcomes was observed upon the gradual exclusion of each study, indicating the robustness of the meta-analysis findings.

Survival outcomes

Survival data were inconsistently reported; hence, meta-analysis was not feasible. The median 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of patients who had AR were 92.3% (range: 72.7–100%), 64.8% (range: 25–78.8%), 51.6% (range: 16.7–63.6%), and 14% (range: 0–41.1%), respectively. Data on median PFS were available from three studies16,24,33 and ranged from 5.25 to 36.3 months. Only one study reported that the 1-, 2-, and 5-year recurrence-free survival rates were 72.7, 49.9, and 18.7%, respectively 21. Data on median OS were obtained from seven studies16,21–24,33,34. Overall, the median OS ranged from 17 to 44.9 months.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current literature on pancreatic cancer surgery combined with AR and NATs for the first time. Our analysis showed that such extended surgery was associated with a high incidence of complications but low mortality. Moreover, patients undergoing AR had increased R0 rates and improved survival outcomes, which means that AR may not be a contraindication.

BRPC or LAPC has been considered late-stage by the guidelines36, and the tumor is locally aggressive, including the invasion of the important arteriovenous structure leading to radical resection difficulties. The review of the topic was done by Mollberg et al. and Malczak et al. 10,37. The review analyzed all available data on pancreatic resections with various ARs. AR was associated with a greater risk of mortality (fivefold increase) and morbidity (twofold increase) as shown by Mollberg et al. Moreover, a similar meta-analysis performed by Malczak and his colleagues showed a decrease in morbidity and mortality compared to previous studies. In this meta-analysis, the pooled morbidity of patients with NATs receiving AR was 60%. The heterogeneity observed may be attributed to the inclusion of a study that enrolled patients from the 1990s22. After excluding the study conducted by Stitzenber et al., the adjusted morbidity rate decreased by 51%. This is in line with data from some small simple studies from Japan, in which patients received resection and reconstruction of the hepatic artery, the SMA and en bloc celiac axis resection38,39. In the vast majority of reports, evaluation of perioperative outcome revealed that the risk of perioperative morbidity was lower than that in our analysis40,41. This may be due to local fibrosis caused by NAT, which increases the difficulty of surgery. One should mention that the assessment of morbidity in the present analysis was complicated due in large part to the differences in reporting and the lack of studies offering direct comparisons between patients with and without AR or NAT that left only 1 study for analysis16. Following AR, Yoshiya et al. documented two instances of ischemic gastropathy and Amano et al. reported one case of bleeding due to CA rupture. Christians et al. observed elevated levels of alanine aminotransferase in two patients as an indicator of hepatic ischemia resulting from insufficient arterial inflow; however, no occurrences of ischemic gastric disease or gastrointestinal hemorrhage were noted. The remaining articles did not report any cases of ischemic or hemorrhagic disease. Our subgroup analysis suggests that the reduction of morbidity may be closely linked to surgical expertise and optimization of perioperative management, which is consistent with the findings of the Heidelberg University study42.

The perioperative mortality rate of 2% obtained from pooled data suggests that AR assisted by NATs is associated with acceptable surgical safety, as confirmed by sensitivity analysis and recent studies. A study of 36 patients with superior mesenteric artery involvement demonstrated a slightly high mortality rate of 5% (2/36) following NAT and radical resection, which included small bowel grafting and AR43. Similar results were reported in a comparative analysis between LAPC and BRPC, where the 30-day mortality rate was also low at 3% (2/58), with an approximate neoadjuvant utilization rate of 80%20. Notably, the LAPC group exhibited a significantly higher rate of AR compared to the 30 BRPC controls (11 vs. 1; P=0.002). Moreover, clinical heterogeneity may arise from variances in vessel resection techniques, disparities among surgical teams involved, and discrepancies in NATs. In the multiple literature included in this paper, the majority of patients received resection of CA, while only a few received resection of HA or SMA. Our subgroup analysis revealed a mortality rate of 3.3% (95% CI: 0.1–9.3%) in the DP-CAR group. Based on the literature of patients with SMA and HA resection over the past 10 years (neoadjuvant rates of 43-94%), the mortality rate has ranged from 0 to 9.5%44–46. Mortality rates for patients undergoing CA-resection ranged from 0 to 10%, and their neoadjuvant rates ranged from 57.5 to 89%47–50. Upon retrospective analysis of AR conducted during the 1990s and 2000s without prior NAT, it was revealed that the surgical mortality rate ranged from 12 to 14%51,52. This implies that neoadjuvant post-AR can now be performed with a reduced mortality rate, which is considered more acceptable, given the high selection of patients and advancements in surgical techniques. However, further investigation by surgeons is imperative to ascertain the variances in mortality rates resulting from the excision of different vessels with greater precision.

After NAT, if the tumor does not respond satisfactorily, it is classified as unresectable pancreatic cancer according to existing criteria. The current R0 resection approach varies among medical centers and depends on the opinions and specialties of individual doctors, with no standardized application of specific technical methods for arterial divestment or AR. Our analysis has demonstrated a resection rate of 79% for R0, with further subgroup analyses revealing a positive correlation between improved rates and both surgical expertise and perioperative management. Additionally, this meta-analysis suggests that NAT can enhance the likelihood of achieving an R0 resection compared to non-NAT (RR=1.21; 95% CI: 0.776–1.915; I²=48.0%), underscoring the importance of NAT in radical surgery. Although NAT has contributed to a reduction in the morbidity and mortality rates of AR, as well as an impressive R0 removal rate, it is important to note that medical centers performing AR and reconstruction must possess robust vascular surgery and organ transplantation technologies to support the procedure. In the event of significant arterial damage during sheath dissection, reconstruction or autologous blood vessel transplantation may be necessary. Furthermore, if the reconstructed artery deviates from its expected length, autologous blood vessel transplantation becomes imperative. Therefore, it is crucial for medical centers to possess robust technical support in managing such scenarios. In the event of compromise to the superior mesenteric artery and vein, arterial reconstruction becomes challenging or time-consuming, necessitating organ perfusion and potentially autologous small intestine transplantation. Noteworthy, among the studies included in this review, patients who underwent AR remained unresectable after NAT16,17, unlike those with LAPC who became resectable after NAT25,26,34,39. This procedure may change the conventional understanding of what is ʻunresectableʼ and may offer survival benefits to the subset of patients, as evidenced by the 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates and median OS.

The Gastrointestinal Tumor Study Group (GITSG) initially reported that the median survival of patients undergoing pancreatoduodenectomy could be prolonged almost twofold by postoperative chemoradiation53. Other studies have also shown an advantage to adjuvant chemoradiation over observation after resection54. However, Zhang et al. reported that adjuvant therapy did not demonstrate a significant association with survival in patients who underwent NAT followed by surgical resection, except for those with positive margins55. Further substantiated evidence is necessary to validate the efficacy of adjuvant therapy in advanced pancreatic cancer. The potential adverse effects of NAT cannot be ignored. Although no mortality associated with FOLFIRINOX was reported in a meta-analysis included 689 patients, the combined rates of grade 3 or 4 adverse events per 100 patients were as follows: all grade 3 or 4 adverse events at a rate of 60.4, neutropenia at a rate of 19.6, thrombocytopenia at a rate of 5.9, diarrhea at a rate of 8.2, vomiting at a rate of 8.8 and fatigue at a rate of 11.756. Insights from a meta-analysis of gemcitabine-based treatments also revealed that adverse events were more prevalent in pancreatic cancer patients treated with GEM compared to 5-FU, and neutropenia (38.1%) was the most common grade 3/4 toxicity observed in both groups57. Currently, ongoing clinical trials are being conducted to compare the effectiveness of different chemotherapy agents. The majority of the studies we included employed NAT based on gemcitabine, with only one study providing information on neoadjuvant toxicity26. The incidence of adverse events was highest in relation to appetite loss (3/17). However, based on our experience with the potential for radical resection, only a small fraction of patients withdrew from the trial due to neoadjuvant complications. Furthermore, the increasing utilization of FOLFIRINOX is expected to have a significant impact on the pool of patients eligible for resection after NAT, potentially influencing postoperative outcomes in a meaningful way. Hence, it is plausible that the incorporation of these specific patients in future meta-analyses may result in disparate findings.

Although this review suggested potential benefits from extensive ARs, there are some important drawbacks that should be noticed: Our meta-analysis only included nine observational studies with small sample sizes, which may introduce some selection bias. The limited sample size may compromise the statistical power, and some operators are yet to overcome the initial learning curve. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the findings of this study; Publication bias that came from the process of literature searching was unavoidable and was hard to overcome; Among the nine studies included, four were conducted solely in Japan and without incorporating any literature from other languages, which may potentially compromise the reliability of these findings; Studies used different resectability criteria, chemotherapy regimens, additional treatment (e.g. CRT), and diverse surgical procedures. In our study of nine articles, NCCN guidelines were utilized in five, AJCC criteria in one, and the remaining three provided exclusive definitions for LAPC or BRPC within their text. Further clarification of resectable criteria could potentially yield future benefits;The level of evidence was moderate, indicating that further research is likely to have an important impact on confidence in the estimate of effect. Nearly half of the studies were single-arm studies, resulting in insufficient data for direct comparison. Finally, from a clinical perspective, we were interested in comparing the long-term survival of LAPC and BRPC with different arteriectomy and chemotherapy regimens. However, due to limited research or insufficient descriptive statistics, conducting an in-depth investigation is not feasible. Therefore, once AR has reached a level of maturity suitable for use in surgical facilities, it will be necessary to conduct a future multicenter randomized controlled trial to investigate the potential benefits of combining AR and NAT.

Conclusion

Pancreatectomy with major AR following NAT has the potential to enhance the survival rate of patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer involving the arteries by achieving R0 resection, despite a significant risk of postoperative complications. However, to validate the feasibility and effectiveness of this procedure, prospective controlled studies are necessary to address limitations arising from small sample sizes and potential biases inherent in retrospective studies.
